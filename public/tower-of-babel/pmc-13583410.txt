Mechanistic Origins of Pressure Reduction, Apparent Efficiency Gain, and Enhanced Detector Response in Radial Flow Stream Splitting Chromatography
Authors: Jake A Cravino, Ming Zhao, Torgny Fornstedt, Jorgen Samuelsson, Arianne Soliven, Richard Henry, Ross Andrew Shalliker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRadial flow stream (RFS) splitting has been reported to reduce operating pressure and apparent theoretical plate height (increasing column efficiency) while increasing detector response relative to conventional high‐performance liquid chromatography column operation. However, the mechanisms responsible for these effects have remained unclear. Here, pressure measurements, computational fluid dynamics, and reduced‐order outlet models were used to examine how radial outlet partitioning in the new end fitting alters column behavior. Most of the measured pressure reduction arose from post‐column effects, although a smaller in‐column contribution remained. Simulations showed that the radial outlet disrupted the conventional outlet‐flow funnel and preferentially captured wall‐adjacent flow. When the outlet models were applied to a published model of radial column heterogeneity, apparent column plate height decreased by approximately 19% and peak height increased by approximately 18%, consistent with typical experimental results. Experimental sensitivity improvements also persisted under detector conditions that were independent of flow rate. These results indicate that the reported benefits under RFS splitting mode arise from a combination of post‐column pressure effects and selective sampling of the radial solute distribution.Keywords: HPLC, outlet‐flow partitioning, pressure reduction, radial flow stream splitting, radial heterogeneity

Introduction

Historically, most approaches to improving high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) have focused on the packed bed, including particle morphology, packing structure, and extra‐column dispersion. Radial flow stream (RFS) splitting provides an alternative approach in which the column effluent is partitioned radially at the outlet [1, 2, 3, 4]. Compared with conventional operation, RFS has produced pressure reductions of approximately 20%–30%, reductions in apparent Height Equivalent to a Theoretical Plate (HETP) commonly around 10%–20% at the optimum flow rate and substantially greater under some conditions, and increases in ultraviolet (UV) detector response of approximately 20%–30% [1, 2, 3, 4]. Improvements in signal‐to‐noise ratio have also been reported for mass‐spectrometric detection [1].

Improved chromatographic efficiency is often accompanied by increased hydraulic resistance, particularly when achieved through smaller particles or more restrictive flow paths [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The RFS end fitting is therefore notable because it appears to improve several performance metrics while also reducing operating pressure—a critical advantage in modern HPLC where operating pressures are approaching 20 000 PSI and upwards in some cases [8]. Regardless of the increasing quality of pumping architecture, which can handle impressive pressures, in HPLC, lower pressures are almost always favorable to some extent. More often than not, lower pressure increases column lifespan, reduces instrument wear, and results in a greater throughput capacity as the system can be run at greater flow velocities.

The RFS design is unusual within HPLC column development because it targets the outlet flow path rather than the packed bed itself, altering the flow at the outlet of the column, rather than more traditional approaches to improving column performance, which historically have been targeted towards alteration of the column bed structure, and perhaps most notably, reducing the particle size within a particle‐packed column [9].

In the current work, we examine the importance of outlet‐flow structure and its impacts on column performance and pressure. Using analytical modelling, pressure experiments, and computational simulation, we examine how outlet geometry may contribute to the reported pressure, efficiency, and sensitivity changes observed in RFS operation.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

HPLC‐grade methanol and Naphthalene (>99%) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (Castle Hill, NSW, Australia), and ultrapure water was prepared in‐house using a Milli‐Q system (at 18.2 MΩcm).

An Avantor ACE C18 column (100 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm fully porous particles) was used. For the experiments concerning operating pressure and column performance, a single RFS end fitting was installed on the outlet of the column (with one central and one radial port, Chromaspeed Pty Ltd, Tonsley, South Australia). For the calculation of the packed‐bed resistance coefficient, identical RFS fittings were fitted to both the inlet and outlet of the column.

Instrumentation and Experimental Conditions

Experiments were performed at ambient temperature (∼20°C) using an Agilent UHPLC system comprising a 1260 autosampler, a 1290 binary pump, and a 1290 diode array detector.

Pressure Measurement Conditions

Pressure measurements were performed using an isocratic mobile phase of 50/50 methanol/water. No analyte was injected during pressure readings. In RFS mode, ∼70% of the total flow was directed out the radial port, leaving ∼30% through the center (measured by mass).

Detector‐Response Conditions

Preliminary experiments were performed to identify detector conditions under which peak height was independent of the flow rate entering the detector. Acquisition frequencies between 2.5 and 160 Hz were evaluated at flow rates of 1–5 mL/min. Peak height became independent of detector flow rate at an acquisition frequency of 160 Hz. Accordingly, this frequency was selected for the final comparison between conventional and RFS operation.

Optimized Final Method for Detector‐Response Comparison

Detector‐response experiments were performed using an 80:20 methanol/water mobile phase and 10 µL injections of approximately 250 ppm naphthalene. Detection was performed at 254 nm (at 160 Hz). The total column flow rate was 5 mL/min. In RFS mode, 35% of the total flow, equivalent to 1.75 mL/min, was directed to the detector through the central outlet.

Four injections were obtained in each mode using the sequence RFS × 3, conventional × 4, and RFS × 1 to account for possible detector drift.

Pressure Measurements and Hydraulic Calculations

Correcting for the Pressure Contributions of the Post‐Column Tubing

Pressure–flow relationships were measured from 0.5 to 3.5 mL/min for the pump alone, the isolated radial and central post‐column tubing paths, and the complete column assemblies. The central tubing path included the UV detector. Each component/configuration was measured in triplicate; for each replicate, all flow rates were tested sequentially before disconnecting and reconnecting the setup to obtain independent measurements.

Hydraulic resistance was obtained from the slope of pressure against volumetric flow rate. Uncertainty was estimated by randomly selecting one of the three pressure measurements at each flow rate, repeating the linear regression 2000 times, and taking the 99% interval of the resulting resistance distribution. This approach was used to determine whether the corrected RFS–conventional difference exceeded the propagated experimental variation.

Post‐column pressure contributions were corrected as follows. In conventional mode, the column and outlet tubing were treated as series resistances. In RFS mode, the central and radial outlet paths were treated as parallel resistances, with their equivalent resistance 1Rtotal=∑n=1i1Rn in series with the column. Pre‐column tubing was not corrected because it was identical between modes (and was in series with the column—therefore, subtracting it separately would add uncertainty without altering the difference between modes). Total uncertainty was estimated by combining the relevant 99% confidence intervals in quadrature.

Calculation of the Viscous Resistance Coefficient

Packed‐bed pressure was measured over 0.25–3 mL/min after allowing the system to equilibrate for several minutes at each flow rate. The RFS insert was then removed from the outlet to approximate an open‐ended column, allowing the pressure contribution of the end fitting to be estimated. The pump was also run to an open beaker to determine the system pressure. These measurements were used to correct the total pressure and isolate the pressure contribution of the packed bed only (Figure 1). Because identical RFS fittings were installed at both ends, the inlet‐fitting pressure contribution was assumed to equal the measured outlet‐fitting contribution. Both fitting contributions were therefore subtracted from the total pressure to isolate the packed‐bed contribution.

The packed‐bed resistance was calculated using Equation (1) [10, 11] (in Equation (1), α is the permeability, μ is the dynamic viscosity, Δp is the pressure drop along the column, and v⃗ is the superficial linear velocity in the column). The viscosity and density of the 50:50 methanol/water mobile phase were taken as 1.54 cP [12] and 926.4 kg/m3 [13], respectively. The resulting average bed resistance was 6.7 × 1012 m−2, corresponding to 1/α in Equation (1). Because the pressure–flow relationship was linear, the second‐order Darcy term was set to zero [10, 11].

Computational Methods

Software and Computing Resources

Simulations were performed in ANSYS Fluent 2025 on a Windows 11 Pro workstation with an Intel Core i7‐14700 CPU and 32 GB RAM.

Column Geometries

Geometries representing the outlet region of a 4.6 mm ID HPLC column were constructed in ANSYS SpaceClaim. Only the terminal 5 mm of the column was modelled, exceeding the estimated laminar entrance length of approximately 0.7 mm under the investigated conditions [14].

The conventional geometry consisted of a 5 mm long, 4.6 mm ID column with a single central outlet tube, 1 mm long and 0.135 mm ID. The RFS geometry used the same central outlet with an additional radial outlet of identical dimensions positioned 1.53 mm from the column axis. A third “wide‐outlet” geometry used a single central outlet of 0.191 mm ID, giving the same total outlet area as the two‐outlet RFS design. Additional open‐ended cylindrical columns were constructed to estimate the pressure contribution of the outlet‐flow contraction. These geometries were reduced‐order representations designed to isolate the relevant outlet‐flow effects and did not reproduce proprietary commercial details.

Each model comprised a porous column body and a separate outlet body so that packed‐bed resistance was applied only within the column region. Shared topology was used between the bodies, and the inlet was represented as an open column face (Figure 2).

Meshing

The bulk column region was meshed with a characteristic element size of 0.1 mm, while the column base and outlet‐tube walls were refined to 0.04 mm with a size ratio of 1.2. Mesh smoothing was set to high. Mesh independence was assessed using the conventional outlet geometry by comparing the inlet pressure across three mesh densities. Computational Fluid Dynamics Conditions

Steady‐state pressure‐based simulations were performed in double precision using the SIMPLE solver. Velocity‐inlet conditions corresponded to volumetric flow rates of 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/min, and all outlets were assigned a gauge pressure of 0 Pa. Water was used as the fluid, and the experimentally determined viscous resistance coefficient was applied to the porous column body.

Simulations were continued in 500‐iteration blocks until the inlet‐to‐outlet mass imbalance was ≤0.05%. Continuity and velocity residuals had plateaued by convergence, with continuity residuals of approximately 10−4. Inlet pressure was calculated as the area‐weighted average over the inlet surface.

In the RFS simulations, the central and radial outlets carried equal flow, giving a 50:50 outlet split.

Capture‐Boundary Calculations

Flow‐based Lagrangian Outlet Walk (FLOW) and Static Partitioning via Layered Iterative Tangential Reflections (SPLITR) (see section 3.1.2) outlet‐partitioning calculations were implemented in Python using a unit‐circle representation of the outlet plane, with port positions scaled to the simulated geometry. The capture boundary separated starting positions according to the outlet through which each fluid element was predicted to leave.

In the SPLITR method, points were sampled within the circular domain and assigned to either the central or radial outlet according to the partitioning criterion defined in Equation (3). The relative capture fraction of each outlet was calculated from the number of points assigned to each outlet.

In the FLOW method, a Cartesian grid was generated across the circular domain. For each point, the local velocity field was evaluated (using Equation 5), and the point was advanced iteratively (using a forward Euler timestep) until it reached either outlet. A point was counted as captured when it entered, or crossed into, a defined outlet capture radius. Points that did not reach an outlet within the maximum number of iterations were left unassigned. The final outlet capture fractions were calculated from the number of captured grid points terminating at each outlet.

Results and Discussion

Conceptual Foundations

Laminar Flow Assumptions

Analysis of the Reynolds number for flow in the porous bed (Re’) showed that under normal operating conditions, the bulk flow through the packed bed is expected to remain laminar (Re′∼0.01 based on a 4.6 mm column at 1 mL/min with a phase of 50/50 water‐methanol, and a lower limit of bed porosity of 0.36) [15, 16]. Typically, flow begins to transition from laminar to turbulent at Re’ > 1, and is fully turbulent at Re’ > 10 [15].

As shown throughout this work, as the fluid progresses toward the column outlet(s), there is an acceleration of linear superficial velocity, where the maximum velocity is reached at the outlet of the column. In typical RFS column geometries, the outlet diameter is approximately 30–40 times smaller than the main column diameter, and thus, at the exit, Re′∼13.5. However, this estimate applies the packed‐bed Reynolds number to a region where the flow is transitioning through the frit (which is more porous than the stationary phase bed) and (unpacked) outlet port rather than through the packed bed itself. For this reason, the open‐tubular Reynolds number at the outlet was also considered, which was calculated as Re ∼110, well within the traditional laminar region. On this basis, the flow was treated as laminar.

Models of the Outlet Flow

The performance benefit of the RFS end fitting is thought to arise from radial outlet partitioning. As shown throughout this work, the radial port preferentially removes flow from the wall region, leaving the central port to sample the central region of the column effluent, which is typically less affected by wall effects and packing heterogeneity.

Directly coupling a three‐dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solution to a chromatographic transport simulator would substantially increase model complexity and computational cost (especially since traditional HPLC simulators rely on a selected transport model, say the equilibrium dispersion model, rather than a full hydrodynamic simulation). Reduced‐order capture‐boundary models were therefore developed to assign regions of the outlet plane to the central or radial port.

The first model developed was the SPLITR model, which estimated the capture boundary by assigning regions of the column cross‐section to either the central or radial outlet.

A second method, the FLOW model, was developed to provide a dynamic estimate of the capture boundary. Unlike SPLITR, which was static, FLOW treated the outlet assignment dynamically from the velocity field. Accordingly, the sink description was changed from the distance form to the corresponding two‐dimensional sink potential equation, the first derivative of which gave the local velocity field.

The final linear term cancels the wall‐normal residual generated by this sink–image pair, thereby enforcing zero normal velocity at the circular boundary.

The capture regions were then obtained by following the points through the velocity field and assigning each point to the outlet reached by its trajectory. In this way, FLOW produced a dynamic estimate of the central and radial capture regions while remaining substantially simpler than a full CFD treatment.

Two models were developed because each had different advantages. SPLITR gave a stable static estimate of the capture boundary and allowed the central port to remain exactly at the column center, but its outlet‐strength parameter had to be tuned iteratively to obtain a desired flow split. FLOW gave a more direct trajectory‐based estimate by advancing points through a calculated velocity field, so outlet strengths, and therefore flow split, could be specified more directly. FLOW was also easier to extend to multiple radial ports and allowed a finite numerical capture radius for each outlet, but required the central port to be offset slightly from the origin and appeared most reliable at intermediate split proportions. Together, the two models provided complementary static and dynamic descriptions of approximately the same capture‐boundary behavior, and form the radial FLOW/SPLITR framework for modelling radial flow splitters.

Explanations of the RFS Pressure Reductions

Correcting for Post‐Column Tubing

With approximately 70% of the flow directed through the radial port, RFS reduced the total hydraulic resistance by 442PSIminmL−1(that is, pressure per unit volumetric flow rate), equivalent to an approximately 19% pressure reduction. After correcting for the post‐column tubing, the conventional and RFS column‐only resistances were approximately 1797 and 1722 PSIminmL−1, respectively. The remaining difference of approximately 75 PSIminmL−1 exceeded the estimated experimental uncertainty of approximately 49 PSIminmL−1.

Approximately 83% of the total pressure reduction was therefore attributed to the parallel post‐column flow paths, with the remaining 17% associated with the column assembly itself. Thus, the post‐column contribution dominated, although a smaller in‐column effect remained.

Regardless of its origin, a total pressure reduction of ∼20% remained important from an end‐user perspective, as it could enable faster operation, lower energy use, and reduced instrument wear.

In‐Column Pressure Relief

Although most of the measured pressure reduction was attributed to post‐column tubing, there was still an in‐column contribution. To examine this contribution, simulations were used to visualize the flow path near the column outlet. In the conventional column, flow converged radially toward the central outlet, producing a clear outlet “funneling” effect (Figure 4). This behavior was observed at all simulated flow rates. Although the flow rate changed, the streamline paths were essentially unchanged, indicating that the absolute flow path near the outlet was not strongly dependent on velocity over the range tested.

The path line graphics for the simulated conventional column at 0.1 mL/min (A), 0.25 mL/min (B), 0.5 mL/min (C), and 1.0 mL/min (D). ‘Particle ID’ refers to an identifier used to represent each particle, represented here by color.

The funnelling effect increased the pressure contribution of the outlet region. As flow converged toward the central outlet, the reduced flow area required the fluid to accelerate to maintain mass flow rate, assuming incompressible flow (Figure 5). Near the outlet, the superficial velocity increased by more than three orders of magnitude. In a real column, this acceleration contributed to the inlet pressure required to maintain the imposed flow rate, with the packed bed further increasing this resistance.

A heat map of the axial (X‐axis) velocity corresponding to the funnelling of flow out of the outlet at a bulk flow rate equivalent to 1 mL/min. The fluid exited from the central outlet at a velocity of ∼1.1 m/s, and the scale of the heat map was chosen to emphasise the acceleration of the fluid in the main column.

In contrast, the RFS simulation produced a different outlet‐flow pattern (Figure 6). Flow still funnelled toward the outlets, but the dual‐outlet geometry changed the pressure contribution in two ways. First, the larger total outlet area reduced the acceleration required through each outlet. Second, the radial outlet altered the conventional central funnel, reducing the velocity and pressure gradient near the column exit.

The path line graphics for the simulated radial flow stream (RFS) column at 1 mL/min. The other flow rates showed identical results (data not shown).

This effect was larger than would be expected from outlet area alone. When the single central outlet was widened to match the total outlet area of the RFS design, the pressure reduction (compared to the standard conventional design) at 1 mL/min was 36.7%, compared with 51% for the RFS geometry (Figure 7). This indicated that the RFS pressure reduction was not only an outlet‐area effect, but also reflected disruption of the conventional outlet funnel. The absolute pressure reduction was likely overestimated, because the simulation used the bulk bed resistance, whereas much of the funnel region in a real column occurs within the outlet frit, which has much higher porosity than the packed bed. Even so, the relative comparison supported the role of outlet‐flow structure in the in‐column pressure contribution.

The simulated inlet pressure plots for the 3‐column designs tested.

To estimate the pressure contribution associated with the outlet funnel, open‐ended columns with 4.6 mm ID and lengths of 5 and 10 mm were simulated. The corresponding inlet pressures were 48.1 and 97.8 PSI, respectively. By linear projection, the 5 mm outlet region containing the exit funnel produced a pressure contribution equivalent to an ∼85 mm open‐ended column, or ∼17 times its physical length.

This comparison should be interpreted cautiously, as the simulation applied the bulk bed resistance to the outlet region, whereas much of the funnel in a real column would occur within the outlet frit, which has a higher porosity than the packed bed. Nevertheless, the result indicated that the outlet‐flow structure could make a disproportionate contribution to the in‐column pressure, even though it was not the dominant source of the total RFS pressure reduction.

Mechanism of the Apparent Efficiency Improvement

Outlet Partitioning and Apparent HETP

The second reported benefit of the RFS end fitting is improved apparent column efficiency. It has previously been proposed that this gain arises because the dual‐outlet system directs wall‐adjacent flow through the radial port and away from the detector [3], so that the central port samples the more discrete central portion of the elution band. The column wall, and particularly radial packing heterogeneity near the wall in both particle‐packed and monolithic columns, is a well‐established contributor to column performance, as are radial temperature gradients [6, 7, 17, 18]. These effects are not discussed in detail here. Suffice it to say, however, the result of these radial effects is the distortion of the elution band, from an ideal cylindrical shape to a parabolic bowl, reducing the effective power of the separation as neighboring solutes eluting from the column are increasingly smeared together nearer to the wall. Selective detection of the radially central portion of the elution band can therefore improve apparent performance, because this region is typically less affected by wall‐adjacent flow effects.

In the RFS simulation, a substantial fraction of the wall‐adjacent flow was directed through the radial port (Figure 6). Although only two‐dimensional graphics are shown here, the simulated flow field indicated that this wall‐flow capture occurred in three dimensions. The extent of wall‐adjacent capture depended on the flow split between the radial and central ports: increasing the radial‐port fraction increased the amount of wall‐adjacent flow removed through the radial outlet.

The FLOW/SPLITR models reproduced the main outlet‐partitioning behavior observed in the CFD simulations. In both SPLITR and FLOW, the radial capture region expanded preferentially around the column wall as the radial‐port fraction increased (Figure 8). This showed that wall‐adjacent flow could be directed to the radial outlet, while the central outlet continued to sample the more central portion of the column cross‐section. FLOW and SPLITR reproduced the qualitative wall‐wrap capture pattern observed in the CFD simulations. The models were not validated against an experimentally measured capture boundary; their chromatographic plausibility was instead assessed below using an independently published model of radial column heterogeneity.

The FLOW model was tested by comparing the target radial‐port fraction with the fraction captured by the radial outlet. Across intermediate split proportions, the achieved radial fraction closely matched the target, supporting outlet strength as a practical way to prescribe flow split. At very low or very high split proportions, however, the low‐flow outlet developed a narrow (perhaps nonphysical) wall‐jet capture region extending from the port toward the wall (starting to form in Figure 8D), suggesting that FLOW was most reliable over intermediate split ranges, approximately 10%–90% radial flow in the single‐radial‐port case. Even so, FLOW was the more physically direct model, as it followed trajectories through a velocity field and used circle inversion to account for the wall. SPLITR, by contrast, was a static approximation and required iterative tuning of β, but did not show the same obvious wall‐jet behavior.

A preliminary assessment was undertaken using the radial‐heterogeneity model of Miyabe and Guiochon [19], which provided an established means of representing radial variations in mobile‐phase velocity and local column efficiency. Their model was extended to a three‐dimensional circular column, after which only those regions predicted to leave through the central port were included in the detector response according to either FLOW or SPLITR (in the conventional case, the entire outlet plane was included). Here we used a radial profile presented by Miyabe and Guiochon, selected to represent a typical, though not highly tailed, HPLC peak as a representative case (n=4,Nc=2.0×103,uw/uc=0.97,hw/hc=3.0 [20]).

The published parameter set generated a typical conventional peak shape (Figure 9), and application of FLOW reduced the apparent HETP by approximately 19% and increased the simulated detector response by approximately 18%, both of which agree well with the magnitude of the efficiency and sensitivity gains commonly reported for RFS operation [3, 4, 21]. SPLITR predicted substantially larger benefits and appeared to overestimate the degree of selective sampling under these conditions. The purpose of this analysis was therefore not to provide a complete validation of either model, but to determine whether the proposed outlet‐selection mechanisms could reproduce the characteristic RFS effects when coupled to a previously published model of radial column heterogeneity.

Radial Time Delay

A further effect of the outlet geometry was observed in the CFD simulations. In the conventional configuration, flow originating near the column wall followed longer, curved paths toward the central outlet, whereas centrally originating flow exited more directly. The contribution of this outlet‐induced delay to experimental band broadening was not quantified and may be small relative to established in‐column sources of dispersion. Nevertheless, the simulations demonstrate that conventional outlet funneling can introduce a systematic residence‐time difference between central and peripheral flow. RFS reduces this effect by directing part of the wall‐adjacent flow through the radial outlet rather than drawing it across the outlet region toward the central port.

Explanations for Detector Sensitivity Improvements

RFS operation has previously been reported to increase detector sensitivity by approximately 20% [3]. The proposed explanation is that the central port preferentially samples the more concentrated central region of the elution band, while part of the more dilute wall‐adjacent region is removed through the radial port. Consequently, the concentration presented to the detector is greater than that obtained when the complete column cross‐section is recombined under conventional operation.

This mechanism was supported by the preliminary radial‐heterogeneity simulation described above (Figure 9), where the preliminary simulation showed an approximately 19% increase in peak height.

A possible alternative explanation was that the sensitivity increase arose from the lower detector flow rate during RFS operation. At low acquisition frequencies, detector response was flow‐rate dependent, with peak height decreasing as flow rate increased. At 160 Hz, however, peak height was independent of detector flow rate.

The mean peak height increased from 35.48±0.14 mAU in conventional mode to 41.46±0.21 mAU in RFS mode (mean ± SD, n=4), corresponding to a 16.8% increase (Table 3). Because this increase exceeded the experimental uncertainty, persisted under flow‐rate‐independent detector conditions, and was to some extent reproduced by applying the FLOW selection model to a radially heterogeneous simulated column (though not this specific column), the sensitivity improvement was attributed primarily to preferential sampling of the concentrated central region of the band rather than to a detector‐flow artefact.

Conclusions

RFS altered both the hydraulic resistance and radial composition of the column effluent. Most of the measured pressure reduction arose from the parallel post‐column flow paths, although a smaller in‐column contribution remained. CFD indicated that this contribution resulted from disruption of the conventional central outlet funnel, reducing flow acceleration near the column exit.

The apparent efficiency and sensitivity gains were explained by preferential removal of wall‐adjacent flow through the radial port. FLOW reproduced the capture pattern observed in the CFD simulations and, when applied to a published radial‐heterogeneity model, predicted an approximately 19% reduction in apparent HETP and an 18% increase in peak height. The experimental sensitivity increase persisted under flow‐rate‐independent detector conditions. Together, these results identify outlet‐flow structure as a practical design variable through which pressure, apparent efficiency, and detector response can be modified without altering the packed bed.
