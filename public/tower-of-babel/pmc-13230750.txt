Hair and wool of ruminants and canines as environmental vectors of Bacillus anthracis and potential human exposure in anthrax-endemic regions
Authors: Muhammad Waqar Aziz, Nadia Mukhtar, Hamda Pervaiz, Tahir Yaqub, Kinza Khan, Alan J Wolfe, Daniel S Schabacker, Sara Forrester, Mark Khemmani, Waqas Ahmad, Amin A Aqel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthrax is an endemic and persistent zoonotic disease in Pakistan. This fact emphasizes the need for surveillance in high-risk endemic regions. The aim of this study was to assess the role of wool and hair of ruminants and canines in the transmission of Bacillus anthracis, the causative agent of anthrax, and to evaluate the exposure to anthrax in asymptomatic animals and humans. Samples from five districts were analyzed using microscopic methods, culture methods (on both PLET and blood agars) and PCR. Additionally, to check the exposure, ELISA was performed on animals and human samples. Interestingly, 114/441 (25.85%) samples exhibited Gram-positive rods and characteristic spore-forming bacilli. Culturing on selective media (PLET) and blood agar resulted in characteristic growth in 42 (9.52%) of samples. These 42 samples were confirmed for B. anthracis by PCR. The ELISA findings indicated exposure to anthrax in both animals and humans. The exposure rate in human samples varied across exposure groups, and the highest exposure was detected in humans exposed to the sheep (33%) and goats (33%), indicating zoonotic potential by small ruminants. The high degree of spore contamination indicated that the sole use of conventional microscopy may lead to an overestimation of anthrax prevalence. For accurate detection of anthrax, the present study suggests the need to integrate bacterial culture with molecular methods, e.g., PCR. These results highlighted the role of small ruminants as potential reservoirs and the need for strict biosecurity measures to mitigate the transmission of anthrax in endemic regions.Keywords: Bacillus anthracis, Anthrax, Canine, Ruminants endemic, Wool and hair, Emerging zoonotic diseaseSubject terms: Microbiology, Bacteria, Pathogens, Policy and public health in microbiology

Introduction

Anthrax is a serious zoonotic disease caused by the spore-forming bacterium Bacillus anthracis, which is prevalent worldwide1,2. Recognized as one of the oldest documented diseases, anthrax has long been associated with outbreaks in livestock and wildlife3,4. The resilience of B. anthracis is due to its ability to produce endospores, which survive in harsh environments, making the disease an endemic and persistent threat to both humans and animals5–7. Although anthrax primarily affects grazing ruminants, including cattle, buffalo, sheep and goats, it also can affect humans through direct contact with the infected animals, contaminated animal products, and by ingestion or inhalation of the spores present in soil1,8. The disease develops rapidly with a high mortality rate, causing substantial risk to both human and animal health9. Moreover, the potential use of B. anthracis as a bioweapon highlights its significance worldwide2,10.

In Pakistan, anthrax is endemic in many regions, particularly in rural areas where the livestock-reliant economy is vulnerable to outbreaks11,12. Poor veterinary infrastructure, limited vaccination programs, and inadequate public awareness exacerbate the situation13. Human cases, often involving cutaneous anthrax, typically arise from handling infected animals or contaminated products14. Grazing ruminants, including small ruminants (sheep and goats) and large ruminants (cattle and buffalo), play a central role in the disease’s transmission by ingesting B. anthracis spores from contaminated soil, water, or vegetation15. Infected animals further contaminate the environment through carcasses and secretions, perpetuating the disease cycle16. Although less susceptible, canines contribute to the spread by scavenging infected carcasses or dispersing spores through their fur, thereby enhancing the persistence and dissemination of anthrax in endemic areas17.

Effective detection of anthrax involves antigen and antibody identification. Staining techniques, such as Gram and spore staining, are used for morphological and spore detection, whilst molecular methods, such as PCR that targets virulence genes (pag, cya, and lef), provide high specificity and sensitivity18. Serological methods, particularly ELISA, are employed to detect antibodies against protective antigens, enabling sero-surveillance and evaluation of vaccination efficacy19. Combining molecular and serological approaches strengthens anthrax surveillance, aiding in outbreak management and the development of targeted control strategies to protect livestock, wildlife, and public health20.

Despite its endemic nature, anthrax remains underreported in Pakistan, leading to a limited understanding of the ecological and biological factors contributing to its persistence and spread. A previous study in Tharparkar district of Sindh, Pakistan reported that 25% of goat hair and 30% of sheep wool were contaminated with anthrax spores but this report was based on microscopic examination only21. In another study from endemic areas of Pakistan, anthrax spores were reported in soil. Whilst microscopic analysis revealed a high prevalence of spores suspected to be B. anthracis, further analysis using growth on selective media and molecular methods revealed that the actual prevalence was lower11. Therefore, effective detection of anthrax antigen involves both microscopic and molecular methods.

Data regarding the role of ruminant’s hair and/or wool and of canine hair are scarce, necessitating further investigation. The aim of the present study was to evaluate the role of these biological carriers in the transmission of anthrax in the endemic areas of Pakistan. Given the evidence presented above, we used microscopic, molecular, and serological methods to more accurately assess the true prevalence of B. anthracis. Our findings are critical to establish suitable methods for anthrax surveillance with the goals of avoiding false positive reporting and establishing better control strategies in the region, with the ultimate aim of reducing outbreaks of this lethal zoonotic disease.

Results

Microscopic examination

Microscopic examination of 441 hair and wool samples indicated that 239 (54.19%) and 114 (25.85%) contained Gram-positive rods arranged in long chains and spores, respectively (Fig. 1). Both are presumptive of B. anthracis in the hair and wool of the tested ruminants and canines.

(a) Gram-positive rods in long chains, suggestive of B. anthracis. (b) Endospores (red stain) in those long chains, also suggestive of B. anthracis.

District wise variation in gram-positive rods and spore detection rates

The prevalence of Gram-positive rods and spores exhibited significant variability across the South Punjab districts studied (Fig. 2). Bahawalpur exhibited the highest frequency of detection with Gram-positive rod prevalence in 50 of 93 samples (53.76%) and spore detection in 23 of 93 samples (24.73%). In Dera Ghazi (DG) Khan, Gram-positive rods and spores were detected in 44 of 90 samples (48.88%) and 25 of 90 samples (27.77%), respectfully. In Multan, Gram-positive rods and spores were detected in 34 of 70 samples (48.57%) and 16 of 70 samples (22.85%), respectfully. In Layyah, Gram-positive rods and spores were detected in 37 of 77 samples (48.05%) and 24 of 77 samples (31.16%), respectfully. Muzaffargarh demonstrated the lowest prevalence, as Gram-positive rods and spores were detected in only 47 of 111 samples (42.34%) and 26 of 111 samples (23.43%), respectfully. These findings underscore substantial regional differences in bacterial contamination and spore presence, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions in high-prevalence areas.

Prevalence of gram-positive rods and spore detection among different species

We also assessed the prevalence of Gram-positive rods and suspected anthrax spores in the different animal types, highlighting significant interspecies and regional variability (Fig. 3). For example, Bahawalpur exhibited the highest Gram-positive rod prevalence in sheep (72.22%) with spore detection rates of 33.33%. DG Khan showed moderate Gram-positive rod prevalence (48.88%) with spore detection peaking at 44.44% in dogs. Layyah and Muzaffargarh exhibited comparable results, with Gram-positive rod prevalence ranging from 42.11% to 57.14% and spore detection from 22.22% to 42.85%. In Multan, 38.89% of cattle and 56.25% of buffalo tested positive for Gram staining, with spore detection rates up to 27.27% in small ruminants.

Bacterial culturing, DNA extraction, and PCR

The 114 samples suspected by microscopy of containing B. anthracis were cultured on PLET agar, a selective medium for B. anthracis. Of these, 68 (59.64%) grew. For further confirmation, these 68 isolates were cultured on blood agar, a differential medium for B. species (Fig. 4).

B. anthracis growth on different media having characteristic growth. (a) Growth on a selective medium (PLET agar), showing the light-yellow hue and circular to comma shape growth characteristic of B. anthracis. (b) Growth of B. anthracis on blood agar, illustrating the characteristic non-hemolytic growth pattern of colonies with a medusa head appearance.

On blood agar, 42 of the 68 samples displayed non-hemolytic, light-yellow colonies with a ground-glass appearance and distinct medusa head-like edges, as observed under a microscope at 100× magnification, presumptive of B. anthracis (Fig. 5). As a positive control, a commercially available live spore vaccine (Sterne) strain was simultaneously cultured, its colony morphology was examined, and shown to be non-hemolytic.

Medusa head formation of B. anthracis colony edges. (a) Observation of B. anthracis colonies under microscope at 100X magnification, colony edges showing the characteristic medusa head formation.

Since Gram-positive rod morphology and spore formation are common features among multiple Bacillus species, they should be regarded as presumptive rather than confirmatory. Definitive identification of B. anthracis can only be achieved for samples that show characteristic growth on selective and differential media and are confirmed by PCR. Thus, from the 68 samples that exhibited characteristic growth on PLET agar, bacterial DNA was extracted and PCR performed (Table 1; Fig. 6). Only 42 samples (61.76%) were PCR-positive for the five targeted B. anthracis genes; these were the samples that exhibited the characteristic medusa head edges on blood agar. The remaining 26 isolates were PCR-negative for all five genes. In contrast, the Sterne strain (control) was positive for only four of the genes, the exception was the capsular gene, consistent with its attenuated profile. These findings provide evidence that B. anthracis spores from field isolates are transmissible via ruminant hair/wool and canine hair, establishing these materials as significant vectors for spore dissemination.

Discussion

This study was conducted in the regions that neighbor anthrax-endemic areas, where animal movement was observed, and sporadic anthrax cases were observed and/or suspected, but proper laboratory confirmation was not available. Our aim was to strengthen anthrax surveillance in these areas. The most important strength of our study lies in investigation of previously neglected factors in the transmission of anthrax disease.

Previously, most anthrax surveillance was focused on livestock and animal-human contact. In contrast, our study tested the hypothesis that ruminants and dogs function as potential anthrax spore carriers. Our data supports this novel hypothesis. Several behavioral and ecological factors play roles in the transmission of B. anthracis spores on the outer coats of these animals. In endemic areas, ruminants can become exposed by grazing and drinking in common areas contaminated with spores. Ruminants also may expose themselves to spores by contact with contaminated carcasses, which are often disposed improperly. Moreover, the manner in which ruminants move and groom themselves can contribute to mechanical dispersal of spores across herds and environments. This transmission of spores by passive contamination of ruminant outer coats is especially true for wool, which offers a fibrous surface on which spores can stick and remain protected.

Identification of canines as potential carriers of B. anthracis spores is another novel finding of our study. Canine–associated contamination and transmission has been neglected and thus has not been previously identified in Pakistan. Yet, this method of transmission is relevant for rural settings, where dogs can scavenge on improperly disposed carcasses, and become exposed to B. anthracis spores. Since dogs move freely among livestock and households, they can easily contribute to anthrax transmission. Thus, incorporating canines into routine anthrax surveillance may be critical in endemic regions.

This study also assessed exposure to B. anthracis of humans who have contact with potential carrier animals. The use of ELISA to detect anti-PA antibodies provides direct measurement of human exposure to B. anthracis spores. Indeed, our data reveals that individuals who have close contact with small ruminants and canines have significant exposure, confirming the risk for these individuals. These results align with those of previous studies that also reported elevated zoonotic disease transmission rates among humans in close animal contact in poor rural agricultural farming22,23. Interpretation of these human exposure findings is complicated by the overlapping nature of exposure categories in rural settings, where individuals frequently interact with multiple animal species and share common environments. As a result, exposure groups were not mutually exclusive, and the intensity and duration of contact varied substantially among participants. These factors, together with relatively small subgroup sizes, likely contributed to wide confidence intervals and unstable odds ratio estimates, despite observable differences in positivity rates.

The use of multiple detection methods is a methodological strength of our study. In many previous studies24,25, only microscopy was adopted for anthrax detection. In contrast, we combined microscopy with culturing on selective and differential media plus molecular detection by PCR as orthogonal methods to achieve a more accurate anthrax detection rate. We first performed Gram staining, identifying Gram-positive rods in a large number of samples. However, spore staining revealed that many of these samples did not contain spores. Only 114 (25.85%) of the 441 samples were both positive for both Gram and spore staining. Furthermore, only a subset (68/114, 59.64%) of the Gram-positive samples grew on the selective agar PLET. While all of these were non-hemolytic on blood agar, only a subset (42/68, 61.76)) were positive for B. anthracis by PCR; the other 26 are likely members of anthracoid species. Overall, only 42 out of 441 samples (9.52%) were confirmed as B. anthracis.

These results highlight that reliance only on microscopy and growth on selective media can result in misidentification. Growth on selective media (PLET), lack of hemolysis on blood agar, and PCR identification can provide definitive confirmation, confirming the necessity of combination of different method for more accurate detection, as also suggested by24,26. While Gram staining and spore staining can identify rod-shaped, spore-forming bacteria, these methods lack the specificity needed to distinguish B. anthracis from other non-hemolytic Bacillus species. This limitation explains why several samples that appeared suspicious under microscopic examination were later confirmed as negative by culture and PCR. Therefore, microscopy should be considered only a preliminary screening tool rather than a confirmatory method. Instead, the use of selective media (PLET), characteristic colony morphology, absence of hemolysis on blood agar, and PCR greatly improves diagnostic accuracy and helps to avoid both false-positive and false-negative results. These findings highlight the importance of multi-step diagnostic approaches, especially in field settings where sample quality and bacterial load may vary considerably.

Our ELISA results reveal the positivity exposure rates in both animals and humans, consistent with the human health risk due to exposure to infected animals. We detected regional variation of positivity but the differences did not differ significantly. This was true for exposure in animals across the different districts. We also did not detect a significant difference between the various human-animal exposure groups. Our findings are consistent with previous studies4,27, which also reported varied exposure rate in livestock.

Several previous studies in Pakistan also observed seroprevalence in cattle and sheep from endemic regions, showing the regional prevalence of infection11,12,28. The higher positivity rates observed in districts such as Multan, Bahawalpur, and Dera Ghazi Khan may be linked to their proximity to anthrax-endemic regions previously reported in Pakistan. These districts are characterized by frequent livestock movement, shared grazing areas, and active animal trading routes, which can facilitate the dissemination of B. anthracis spores across farms and communities. In addition, variability in veterinary services, vaccination coverage, carcass disposal practices, and environmental factors such as soil conditions may further influence regional differences in exposure and detection rates. Although these factors were not quantitatively assessed in the present study, they provide biologically plausible explanations for the observed spatial patterns and highlight the importance of region-specific surveillance and control strategies29–31.

Although anthrax disease is known for causing sudden death in ruminants, asymptomatic animals have been reported in various endemic regions12. This can occur when animals are exposed to sub-lethal or low levels of Bacillus anthracis spores that stimulate the immune system of animals but cannot cause clinical disease32. In regions with repeated anthrax outbreaks, livestock may develop an acquired partial immunity due to low level exposure33. Additionally, spores can adhere to the wool and hair of animals without any symptoms of disease and thus the animals appear healthy. In a previous study, sero-positivity in several asymptomatic animals was reported, supporting the possibility of exposure or sub-clinical infection of anthrax11.

Several potential confounding factors may have influenced the observed prevalence of B. anthracis and sero-positivity patterns. Importantly, none of the animals included in this study were vaccinated against anthrax, indicating that the detected anti-protective antigen (anti-PA) IgG antibodies reflect natural exposure rather than vaccine-induced immunity. Nevertheless, environmental factors such as soil contamination, grazing behavior, carcass disposal practices, and differences in farm management and veterinary access may contribute to spatial variation in exposure and detection rates. Although these variables were not quantitatively assessed in the present study, they represent important considerations for interpreting the findings and should be investigated in further studies.

A limitation of the present study is the relatively small number of canine samples and the limited size of some human exposure subgroups, which may reduce statistical power and limit broader generalization of the findings. Canine sampling was constrained by field accessibility, owner consent, and biosafety considerations in anthrax-endemic settings; however, samples were obtained from farms and households in areas with previously reported or suspected anthrax outbreaks. While the findings provide important preliminary evidence supporting the potential role of dogs as environmental carriers of B. anthracis, they should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In addition, the study was intentionally focused on individuals living in close contact with livestock and canines in high-risk settings, with the aim of evaluating exposure patterns among populations most likely to encounter B. anthracis. As a result, the observed seropositivity reflects exposure within at-risk groups rather than background levels in the general population, and needs future studies including larger cohorts and unexposed control groups.

In conclusion, the present study revealed new understandings in anthrax disease transmission, particularly the role of canine fur and the hair and wool of ruminants in the transmission of spores. The combination of traditional microscopic and culture methods combined with a molecular method is necessary for confirmative diagnosis. Moreover, the exposure rate in animals and humans also highlights the risk to human health. More research should be conducted in other regions and animal species. This integrated approach of anthrax detection and surveillance will be helpful in reducing the risk to public health by anthrax disease.

Information regarding the suspected anthrax outbreaks in previous years was acquired from field veterinary staff working in these respective endemic areas. The samples were collected from both the ruminant pastoral farming systems and livestock small scale farms.

Sample collection strategy

Blood samples were aseptically collected using a 5 mL disposable syringe after appropriately restraining the animals. For cattle, buffalo, sheep, and goats, blood was drawn from the jugular vein, while for dogs, it was collected from the cephalic vein with care. The blood was immediately transferred to gel/clot activator vacutainers for serum separation. Samples were appropriately labeled, stored in an icebox, and transported to the laboratory. None of the animals included in this study had a history of anthrax vaccination, as confirmed by animal owners and local veterinary authorities.

From humans, blood samples for serum isolation was collected from Median cubital vein located in the tuck of the elbow and cephalic vein.

Wool and hair samples were obtained under strict precautionary measures from the tail and abdominal regions. The collected samples were sealed in sterile, double-layered zip-lock bags and transported to the laboratory.

Sample processing

Microscopic and molecular identification of wool and hair samples

Microscopic examination of wool and hair samples

Spores were extracted from wool and hair following the OIE manual protocol and GABRI method, as described previously with minor modifications36. Briefly, a 1–1.5 g sample of wool and hair was suspended in 100 mL of sterile distilled water and incubated overnight. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm cellulose nitrate membrane (Whatman International Ltd., England). The membrane and its deposit were then suspended in 5 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 6.6 ± 0.1). The aliquot was heated at 70 °C for 20 min in a water bath to eliminate vegetative bacteria and organisms, leaving only spores. The treated aliquot was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of PBS for further use.

Gram staining was performed to detect vegetative bacterial forms, specifically Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacteria arranged in long chains. Spore staining, using the Schaeffer–Fulton staining method, was conducted to identify green, oval-shaped spores, either as separate entities or located centrally to sub-terminally within bacterial cells.

Bacterial culturing, DNA extraction, and PCR

For molecular confirmation of B. anthracis genes, samples suspected based on Gram and spore staining were cultured on differential media (blood agar) and selective media (PLET: polymyxin B, lysozyme, EDTA, thallous acetate). Suspected B. anthracis colonies from these media were processed for DNA extraction using the GeneJET™ Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Catalog No. K0721) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysozyme containing lysis buffer, followed by Proteinase K digestion, and RNA removal with RNase (A) The lysate was mixed with ethanol before transferring to DNA purification column. DNA washing was done by using wash buffer I and II, and DNA was eluted by using elution buffer according to kit’s instructions. Previously reported primers were used to amplify genes associated with (B) anthracis: the plasmid-encoded capsular (CAP) gene, edema factor (EF), protective antigen (PA), cya and bacterial chromosome37.

As a control, the live spore vaccine strain (Sterne strain) of B. anthracis, which is non-virulent and lacks the capsular gene, was cultured alongside field isolates. The absence of the capsular gene in the Sterne strain coupled with its presence in virulent field strains was used to validate the molecular identification.

Serum processing for anti-PA IgG antibody detection by ELISA

Serum samples from cattle (n = 98), sheep (n = 104) and human (n = 93) were processed to detect antibodies against the protective antigen (PA) of Bacillus anthracis. Commercially available ELISA kits were used: the Cow Anthrax Protective Antigen IgG (Anti-PA-IgG) ELISA Kit (abx055844) for cattle, the Sheep Anthrax Protective Antigen IgG (Anti-PA-IgG) ELISA Kit (abx055845) for sheep and the Human Anthrax Protective Antigen IgG (anti-PA-IgG) ELISA Kit (abx 055843) for human. ELISA was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions, with positive and negative controls included in each run to confirm the reliability of the results.

Briefly, Serum samples were diluted at 1:5 ratio using the kit-provided dilution buffer. Controls included one blank, two positive, and two negative controls, used as per the kit’s instructions. Diluted samples and controls (50 µL each) were added to wells, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and washed five times with 1:20 washing buffer. Subsequently, 100 µL of HRP enzyme conjugate was added, incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, and washed similarly. Then, 50 µL of substrate solutions A and B were added, incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, and stopped with 50 µL of stop solution. Using a spectrophotometer (Rayto ELISA Microplate Reader, Model RT-2100 C), optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm.

Prevalence and risk assessment of exposure in human

The data regarding human exposure to Bacillus anthracis through interactions with various animal species was collected, including demographic information such as gender, age and most importantly the details on contact with different animal’s species. On the base of human contact with specific animals, such as cattle, buffalo, sheep, goat, and dog, as well as combinations of these animals (cattle & buffalo, cattle & goat, goat & sheep, goat, sheep & dog, cattle & dog, and cattle & sheep & dog), samples were distributed in different groups. This approach allowed for a detailed risk assessment of B. anthracis exposure across different human groups.

Statistical analysis

To compare animal positivity rates (frequency of positive and negative cases) for cattle and sheep samples across five districts, Chi-square (χ²) test was performed. The test determined whether the positivity rates for cattle and sheep samples varied significantly between districts and p-value < 0.05 was considered significant statistically. For more accurate estimation for small sample size, Wilson score method was used and 95% confidence intervals38 for positivity was calculated.

A One-Way ANOVA was used to evaluate differences in positivity percentages among different human exposure groups (Cattle & Sheep & Dog, Cattle & Dog, Goat & Sheep & Dog, Cattle & Goat, Dog, Sheep, and Cattle. The ANOVA tested whether the infection rates based on exposure to various animal species showed significant variability. Additionally, binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the significant exposure predictors of human infection status, and the odds ratio along with the 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
