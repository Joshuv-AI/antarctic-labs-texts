Eccentric Accretion Disks in Active Galactic Nuclei
Hongping Deng 1,∗

Abstract
Active galactic nuclei (AGNs), among the universe’s most luminous objects [ 1 ] , radiate across the entire electromagnetic spectrum. They are powered by gravitational energy released from material feeding central supermassive black holes (SMBHs) [ 2 ] . However, current AGN theory remains largely phenomenological, relying on envisioned structures [ 3 ] beyond accretion disks [ 4 ] to produce broad optical emission lines [ 5 ] (broad line regions, BLRs) and X-ray emission [ 6 ] (corona). It struggles to explain accretion disk sizes [ 7 , 8 ] , optical line diversity [ 9 , 10 ] , BLR radial inflows/outflows [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] , and crucially, rapid X-ray [ 14 , 15 , 16 ] and optical variability (line width [ 17 , 18 , 19 ] , line shape [ 20 ] , inflow/outflow switch [ 21 , 22 ] , emitting region [ 23 ] ) on timescales significantly shorter than disk accretion [ 24 , 25 , 26 ] . Here we show that moderately eccentric flows around SMBHs—formed via circumnuclear gas accretion [ 27 , 28 , 29 ] or tidal disruption events [ 30 ] —generate eccentricity cascades in the BLRs (from 0.8 to 0.2 outwards), explaining multi-wavelength emission and variability. The flows’ non-axisymmetric temperature structures explain dust sublimation asymmetries [ 31 , 32 ] , different BLR components [ 9 , 33 ] and their radial motions [ 11 , 12 , 13 ] . The innermost BLR links to the SMBH vicinity through highly eccentric streams producing soft X-rays at periapsis [ 34 , 35 ] . General relativistic precession further compresses these flows, generating a hard X-ray continuum near the SMBHs [ 35 , 36 ] . Precession of the eccentric flow drives optical/X-ray variability, reproducing the observed X-ray power spectral density [ 14 ] and occasional X-ray quasi-periodic eruptions [ 15 ] . We thus propose eccentric accretion disks as a physical AGN model unifying the elusive BLRs and X-ray corona. This model enables detailed anatomy of AGNs and maximises their potential as cosmological standard candles.

The above static eccentric disk model provides some key scales and structures for interpreting multi-wavelength AGN emissions. However, eccentric disks are intrinsically unstable to a class of parametric instability when pairs of inertial waves are in resonance with orbital motion (see Methods). In an ideal eccentric disk precessing uniformly (Extended Data Fig. 7.8), we observe exponential growth of the instability, which leads to vigorous hydrodynamic turbulence (Extended Data Fig. 9) with an effective viscous
\alpha\sim 0.2

1

Thus far, the eccentric AGN accretion disk model is complete in both its structures and variability. The three BLRs for typically broad emission lines provide unique opportunities for AGN anatomy. Specifically, with one well-resolved broad line we can obtain three time lags of the BLRs via reverberation mapping

[
52
]
. Differentially precessing eccentric disks grow in twists with time and become vulnerable to orbital collision (large
q
parameter, see equation
3
) and dissipation, so that they probably evolve towards a uniformly precessing state. However, they may experience a long period of eccentricity oscillation before eventually reaching a uniformly precessing state (Extended Data Fig. 3).

Here we use uniformly precessing disks as tests (see ‘Hydrodynamical Simulations’) for our direct hydrodynamics simulations to find the required resolution for properly capturing disk precession and subtle turbulence due to the parametric instability in eccentric disks
[
39
]
. We precomputed dense tables for the geometric factor
F^{\mathrm{(3D)}}
with different
e
and
e_{a}
, and found the precession frequency for our specific disk model via shooting methods, forcing torque-free boundary conditions
[
52
,
55
]
. The Python scripts solving for the vertical scale height, the geometric factor, and the uniformly precessing disks are available at an online repository.

Hydrodynamic simulations

Three-dimensional hydrodynamic simulations of highly eccentric flow are challenging because the extreme vertical compression (by a factor over 100 even for
e\sim 0.6
, Extended Data Fig. 5) can easily go below the resolution scale of an Eulerian grid code
[
58
]
. As a result, we chose the meshless finite mass (MFM) scheme in the GIZMO code
[
59
]
, which is a Godunov-type Lagrangian method. MFM carries out effective volume partition from given fluid elements (particles) distributions and updates fluid variables from fluxes obtained with Riemann solvers. MFM can naturally follow complex flows in distorted accretion disks and resolve subtle parametric instability thanks to its hybrid nature and by discarding artificial viscosity
[
60
]
.

However, numerical tests are warranted to determine the resolution requirement for eccentric disk simulations, especially capturing potential parametric instability
[
61
]
. A similar parametric instability in a warped disk required a mid-plane resolution scale 1/8 of the disk scale height
[
62
]
. To save computational resources, we construct compact reference circular disk models with a short radial range covering
[a_{0},2a_{0}]
where
a_{0}
is the code length unit. The disk has a mass distribution
M_{a}\propto\tanh(\frac{a-a_0}{0.1a_0})\tanh(\frac{2a_0 -a}{0.15a_0})
and a total mass of
10^{-5}
in code units. The gas follows a simple polytropic equation of state,

P=A_{0}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho_{0}}\right)^{5/3},

(8)

with
A_{0}
a constant and
\rho_{0}
the code density unit, assuming no entropy gradient and instantaneous loss of energy due to shock heating.
A_{0}
is appropriately chosen so that the disk scale height is
\sim 0.033a
. The thick disk is chosen to allow a good vertical resolution. We generate a particle representation of the density profile via rejection sampling and relax the disk model by damping out radial and vertical velocity fluctuations due to numerical noise to achieve an equilibrium state
[
60
]
.

We employed 5 million or 42 million particles in the circular disk model (we often split particles in the low resolution model to reach a high resolution
[
60
]
), which gives a midplane resolution of
1/5H^{\circ}
and
1/10H^{\circ}
. A more extended disk of similar thickness but twice as massive covering
[a_{0},3a_{0}]
(with the same taper functions at the edge) is constructed with 51 million particles to allow a larger dynamic range (see Fig.
1
). We used code units of 1 au, 1 million solar masses and
G=1
so that
A_{0}=2.11
. We note that only the relative strength of gas pressure to orbital energy matters (disk thickness) in all the following simulations, even with GR precession. The results can be rescaled and applied to more extended and low-density gas.

We deform the reference circular disk to the desired eccentric disk models given specific eccentricity profiles (e.g., the uniformly precessing profile in the section above) by the following steps: 1) Divide the circular disk into 200 radial bins. 2) Rescale the vertical coordinate according to the solution of equation
4
with the desired eccentricity in each bin. 3) Add the vertical oscillation velocity. 4) Deform the circular bins to elliptical orbits. 5) Adjust the orbital velocity to the desired elliptical Keplerian motion. Similar approaches are taken in the construction of nonlinear steady warped accretion disks
[
60
]
. A Python script for this transformation is available at an online repository.

The central potential and inclusion of GR precession

We present two classes of simulations as summarised in Table
1
: scale-free simulations intended to reveal general eccentric disk evolution and simulations with GR precession to investigate black hole accretion.

In the scale-free simulations, only the Keplerian potential of a supermassive black hole of one million solar masses (code mass unit) is considered. These simulations reveal the evolution of eccentric disks far away from the black hole so that GR effects can be ignored. The results can be rescaled, regardless of the code units here, and apply to a wide range of radii in AGNs.

The high eccentricity scale-free simulation, A2E05-SF/A3E05-SF, forms highly eccentric flow in the inner disk, leading to orbital collision and fast accretion. When the flow reaches the vicinity of black holes (
\sim 100R_{g}
,
R_{g}=GM/c^{2}
is the gravitational radius), GR effects become important
[
63
]
. A good approximation for the GR precession is crucial since it largely determines the precession of eccentric flows (affected by gas pressure) and drives the disk variability. We adopt the post-Newtonian potential proposed in ref.
[
63
]
, which exactly predicts the periapsis advance of elliptical orbits. The correction terms, whose strength is characterised by
R_{g}
, are added to the central Keplerian potential. We confirm with numerical tests that our implementation reproduces the periapsis advance of test particles properly.

To mimic and accelerate the inspiraling of materials (Extended Data Fig. 4g-i), we increase
R_{g}
from 0 to
0.01a_{0}
within
10\pi\Omega^{-1}(a_{0})
following,

R_{g}=0.01a_{0}\sin^{2}\left(\frac{t}{20}\right).

(9)

On the role of radiation pressure and magnetic fields

The temperature is proportional to
(jh)^{1-\gamma}
, and the ratio of radiation pressure to gas pressure is proportional to
(jh)^{4-3\gamma}
, varying azimuthally in contrast with the standard circular accretion disk theory. The gas pressure always dominates over the radiation pressure along the reference orbit O2 in Fig.
2
. In contrast, the radiation pressure plays a dominant role for material within reference orbit O1. However, a reduction of the minimum temperature by a factor of 4 (can still form soft X-ray excess near periapsis, allowing higher eccentricity or twists, Extended Data Fig. 6) with an increase of density by a factor of 10 in this inner region can make the radiation pressure subdominant.

The current model is by no means complete due to limitations of GR treatment, the neglect of radiation pressure and magnetic fields, which are necessary for material accretion within
10r_{g}
in Extended Data Fig. 11 and potential jet formation. Nevertheless, the gravitational energy is much larger than both the radiation pressure and gas pressure and controls the orbital motion. Here, we provide valuable information on how materials are dynamically fed into the
<100R_{g}
region (along eccentric orbits featuring extreme compression), which is typically assumed to be an isolated torus in radiation magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) simulations
[
64
]
. The universal soft X-ray excess (Fig.
2
, Extended Data Fig. 6, 12) and characteristic X-ray power spectrum (Fig.
3
) are reasonably explained, which tends to suggest that the orbital motion of the eccentric flow largely explains the variability.

On the other hand, the only dedicated MHD simulations of eccentric disks
[
58
]
, to the best of our knowledge, find subsonic magnetorotational instability (MRI) turbulence weaker than the parametric instability turbulence in terms of the Mach number of the velocity fluctuation (Extended Data Fig. 9). This is in line with the subdominant role of MRI turbulence in warped disks
[
65
]
. These simulations also highlight the enhanced magnetic reconnection at the vertically compressed regions, though poorly resolved numerically, weakening the magnetic fields. Further GRMHD simulations, probably with aggressive adaptive mesh refinement at the periapsis (Extended Data Fig. 5), are warranted to reveal the role of magnetic fields in eccentric flow in the vicinity of black holes, and radiation should be included when possible.

Analysis of the X-ray emission regions

To better quantify the variability of the eccentric disks around SMBHs, we carried out time series analysis for the X-ray emitting regions. First and foremost, only the relative density matters in our simulations, and the denser regions are hotter due to our simple polytropic gas equation of state (
T\propto\rho^{2/3}
). The core region (with density [0.8, 1] times the peak density) is typically compact so that its position and precession can be easily determined ( Fig.
3
, Extended Data Fig. 13). We note that in rare cases the core regions become extended azimuthally embedded in low-eccentricity flows, leading to sudden changes in the determined phase angle of the core (Fig.
3
a around
250\Omega^{-1}(100R_{g})
).

The peak temperature reached near the black hole is largely determined by the supplied material along the reference O1 orbit (Fig.
2
), linking optical emission to X-ray emission
[
42
]
.
Although temperature along the O1 orbit is affected by irradiation from the central engine
[
8
]
and the assumed eccentricity (Extended Data Fig. 6), observationally, there appears to be a universal black body soft X-ray excess of
\sim 0.1
keV
[
34
]
. We link this soft X-ray excess to the hot gas near the periapsis of reference O1 orbit and the hard X-ray continuum emission (2-10 keV) to regions with a density up to 64 times higher (see Extended Data Fig. 12). However, the peak gas density varies randomly with time (Fig.
3
), as does the defined core density
\rho_{c}
(the average for particles with density [0.8, 1] times the peak density). To remove this randomness, we use the time average core density
<\rho_{c}>
as a reference to define the soft X-ray emission regions with a density between
<\rho_{c}>/64
and
<\rho_{c}>/32
and the hard X-ray regions with a density larger than
<\rho_{c}>/8
.

We calculated the time series of the mass of all particles within the defined density range as an approximation for the luminosity. We then performed Fourier analysis on the time series to determine the power (Fig.
3
) or find the phase difference between hard and soft X-rays for different frequencies (Extended Data Fig. 12). Despite that, we can explain the hard X-ray lag due to the propagation of perturbations (Extended Data Fig. 12) from large to small scales
[
66
]
, we lack reprocessing models to explain the leading hard X-ray variation at high frequency
[
67
]
.

We note that the above correspondence is made based on the assumption that orbit O1 is sufficiently eccentric (Extended Data Fig. 6) and can compress materials at its periapsis to a temperature high enough for soft X-ray black body emission, which should be common in AGNs (Fig
2
). This may not be true for post-TDE disks due to their limited dynamical range and AMD budget. In this case, the flow at the periapsis of O1 is not hot enough for soft X-ray emission, and even the final compression by GR precession can only bring it to a temperature high enough for soft X-ray emission (Extended Data Fig. 13, 14).

Angular momentum deficit and orbital energy

The angular momentum deficit (AMD) of a fluid element of mass
m_{k}
on eccentric orbits with semi-major axis
a_{k}
, eccentricity
e_{k}
and inclination
i_{k}
reads,

m_{k}\sqrt{GMa_{k}}(1-\sqrt{1-e_{k}^{2}}\cos(i_k)).

(10)

The total AMD of the eccentric disk is the sum of this quantity and is conserved (Extended Data Fig. 10) in the absence of shocks and turbulence
[
62
]
. Here we keep track of the AMD to show how the limited initial orbital excitation is damped out in the absence of external AMD supply.

For the gravitational potential we adopted, the orbital energy
[
63
]
of the fluid element reads,

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r_{k}}{r_{k}-2R_{g}}\right)^{2}m_{k}v^{2}_{r,k}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{r_{k}}{r_{k}-2R_{g}}\right)m_{k}v^{2}_{t,k}-\frac{GMm_{k}}{r_{k}},

(11)

where
r_{k},v_{r,k},v_{t,k}
are the distance to the central object, the radial and tangential velocities for the fluid element. The gas internal energy is negligible compared to the orbital energy, and the released orbital energy is efficiently lost at shock fronts due to our polytropic equation of state assumption.
