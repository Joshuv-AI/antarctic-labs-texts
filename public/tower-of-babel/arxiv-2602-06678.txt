Beyond Judgment: Exploring Large Language Models as Non-Judgmental Support for Maternal Mental Health
Shayla Sharmin, Sadia Afrin

Abstract
In the age of Large Language Models (LLMs), much work has already been done on how LLMs support medication advice and serve as information providers; however, how mothers use these tools for emotional and informational support to avoid social judgment remains underexplored. This study conducted a 10-day mixed-methods exploratory survey ( N = 107 N=107 ) to investigate how mothers use LLMs as a non-judgmental resource for emotional support and regulation, and for situational reassurance.Our findings show that mothers are asking LLMs various questions about childcare to reassure themselves and avoid judgment, particularly around childcare decisions, maternal guilt, and late-night caregiving. Open-ended responses also show that mothers are comfortable with LLMs because they do not have to think about social consequences or judgment. Although mothers use LLMs for quick information or reassurance to avoid judgment, over half of the participants value human warmth more than LLMs; however, a significant minority, especially those in joint families, consider LLMs to avoid human judgment. These findings help understand how LLMs can be framed as low-risk interaction support rather than a replacement for human support, and highlight the role of social context in shaping emotional technology use.

Introduction

Motherhood, the sweetest journey of a woman, unfortunately, also comes with strong social judgment. This is commonly known as the “intensive mothering” ideology
10
;
29
. Specifically, from pregnancy through the early years of raising children, mothers are judged on how they care for their children, show their feelings, how their bodies change, and how they balance work and family. In reality, it is not possible to be a perfect mother; mothers learn from experience on this journey. However, social pressure is greater in real life and on social media. The “Work-Life Paradox” to judge mothers’ actions is created by the “snap back” culture and the pressure to show off perfection
31
;
4
.

This kind of judgment can cause a lot of emotional stress. In South Asian contexts, people prioritize cultural norms and family expectations in communal reputation. In these situations, the idea of the “selfless mother”, the common fear of “What will people say?”, and being told they are overreacting, often keep mothers from talking about their struggles and make them conscious to seek help. In many South Asian countries, mental health concerns are still treated as an overreaction or a waste of time and money. It is not encouraged to seek professional help for emotional distress because mental health is not widely prioritized. Still, psychological struggles are not considered a health issue. It is considered a personal weakness or a lack of resilience
22
;
6
;
26
.

Most of the time, mothers face criticism from older women in the family and from more experienced mothers. Older women often describe their own emotional experiences of hardship as normal, which can make mothers from this generation feel that emotional suffering is simply part of motherhood. As more women handle both their jobs and household chores, these expectations to be a perfect mother become even harder to meet. When older family members respond with statements such as “we managed too,” without acknowledging changing social and economic conditions. This may increase guilt, self-doubt, and emotional strain in the mother.

These are the reasons mothers hesitate to seek help from their older and turn to alternative options like Artificial intelligence (AI). Before 2022, people used AI chat systems, which were built on predefined topics or scripts, for emotional support because they were anonymous, non-judgmental, and always available. These early AI systems and specialized chatbots were designed to provide informal, short-term emotional support or therapy-like conversations, not as a medical tool
20
;
25
.

From 2022, large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude have become easy for many people to use in daily life. LLMs are seen as more flexible, conversational, low-cost, and able to respond to different emotional situations compared to earlier rule-based systems.
People use them for many purposes, such as thinking about their feelings, handling stress, and coping with psychological vulnerability. Users increasingly turn to these systems not only for answers, but also for validation, comfort, and a sense of being heard without being judged
13
;
12
;
11
. Recent studies show that between 25% and 50% of users report engaging with LLMs for emotional support, therapy-like conversations, or companionship
30
;
11
. There are known risks with LLMs, such as biased responses, incorrect information, or poor crisis management, but people still use them because they feel anonymous and not judged
11
. The studies show that to avoid stigma and because of limited mental health care, the use of LLMs is increasing for reassurance and comfort
30
;
20
;
25
.

Most studies about LLMs and mothers’ mental health focus on medical screening tools or special AI chatbots with set tasks. These studies also show that being anonymous and avoiding stigma are important for pregnant and new mothers
28
. Although nowadays people are using LLMs for emotional support, to our knowledge, there is still little research on how mothers use LLMs for this purpose. Our objective is not to understand how mothers use LLMs for medical help but how they use them to avoid judgment, seek reassurance, or obtain quick information in daily life.

This understanding is needed not just for mothers’ well-being, but also to help design LLMs systems that support people who are emotionally vulnerable.
The research questions we asked are as follows:

RQ_{1}

How do mothers use LLMs as a judgment-free source of emotional support?

RQ_{2}

How does emotional safety shape mothers’ preferences for LLMs support in comparison to human warmth?

RQ_{3}

How and under what conditions do mothers rely on LLMs for emotional regulation during moments of vulnerability, such as guilt, anger, or isolation, when human emotional or practical support is limited or unavailable?

Contributions:

Unlike prior AI-for-maternal-health studies that focus on clinical screening or app-based interventions, this study examines LLMs as a non-judgmental source of emotional and informational support. Our contributions are as follows

•

This mixed-methods exploratory survey study (
N=107
) examined how mothers describe using LLMs for emotional reassurance and support in parenting contexts.

•

Findings suggest that some mothers turn to LLMs when they feel uncomfortable or hesitant to seek help from people around them, particularly in situations where they anticipate criticism or judgment related to child-caring practices.

•

Most mothers said they value emotional warmth from people more than from AI, but some pointed out that using LLMs is helpful because they do not have to worry about being judged. This was more common among mothers living in joint family settings.

•

Quantitative results show that mothers sometimes use LLMs when they cannot get help from others, such as late at night or when their partner is not around. In these times, LLMs offer quick comfort rather than long-term emotional care.

•

Overall, mothers did not see LLMs as a replacement for real relationships, but as something they use in certain situations to get information and comfort without being judged.

Related Work

2.1
Societal Scrutiny and the “Good Mother” Construct

The idea of the ’Good Mother’ is a strong sociological benchmark that dictates the moral and behavioral expectations of motherhood
26
. Earlier research shows how this idea works around the world, and how new digital tools, including large language models
11
, are increasingly shaping how mothers receive emotional support. We use this research to explain why mothers might seek “safer” places to ask questions that feel risky to discuss in public in the South Asian context.

Global Perspectives of Motherhood

Around the world, people have described motherhood using the idea of “Intensive Mothering”, which sees raising children as focused on the child, guided by experts, and very emotionally demanding
10
.
Mothers who do not meet the above standards may feel their identities are damaged because they feel guilty for being busy with work or not knowing everything about child care
7
. In these situations, mothers often become silent and stressed to avoid being judged
15
.

South Asian Contexts: Collective Surveillance and Maternal Endurance

In South Asian societies, the situation is more difficult for a mother. Generally, a mother cannot be a “Good Mother” alone. She needs support and help from the whole family. But in South Asia, sacrifice and endless hardship in motherhood are glorified, and mothers’ struggles are often seen as normal
22
. The family, especially the joint family, and the community closely watch how mothers act and link those with the family’s values, reputation, and morals. They often worry about what others think or ’What will people say?’
6
. In these situations, mothers’ daily choices, such as how they feed or care for their children, are scrutinized more closely. These affect mothers to talk about their true feelings and struggles.
As a result, maternal struggles are often framed as individual shortcomings or moral failures, rather than as outcomes of broader social expectations and family structures.

Digital Shifts: AI or LLMs and the Search for Non-Judgmental Spaces

Recently, the number of mothers looking for support online has increased.
Studies on AI-based emotional support show that people discuss sensitive issues more openly with these chatbots
13
;
2
. This is because these tools allow people to remain anonymous and do not judge them. Mothers use these AI tools as their private place of sharing their concerns and questions to avoid strict or judgmental family situations
11
.

But these tools have some limitations. AI tools are often built on Western culture, but these are also used in other cultures without changing
1
;
2
;
22
. Because of this, these systems may not provide adequate support in family situations, like in South Asian countries, where group values or unique cultural pressures on mothers are important.
Even though more people are interested in using AI for mental health, most research about AI and mothers still focuses on medical tests and finding problems
13
;
2
.
There are not as many studies examining the everyday emotional support mothers need beyond medical care. Also, recent studies have found that these AI systems can make traditional gender roles stronger. Xiu et al. show that LLMs consider the mother as the main caregiver rather than the father
32
.
Overall, the research shows that while more mothers are using these tools for emotional support, we still do not know much about how these systems fit with the experiences of mothers across cultures, especially in South Asia.

2.2
Maternal Trauma and Sub-clinical Distress

The Invisibility of Distress: Stigma Theory

Unnoticed distress of motherhood in many societies can be explained by “Stigma Theory”
7
. The pressure of being a “perfect mother” by society stops mothers from talking about their feelings, tiredness, sadness, or confusion about child care and parenting
2
;
4
. They often avoid talking openly about their struggles because they are afraid people will think they are not good enough. Self-blame and guilt also create anxiety in them.
In cultures where family and community are very important, like in South Asian societies, these feelings of stigma can be even stronger
6
;
22
.
In these situations, a mother’s distress might be seen as a personal or family weakness rather than as a need for help. This helps explain why distress can persist even when mothers are surrounded by people, yet still feel unable to disclose.
This situation creates a repeating pattern where the mothers who most need emotional support are often the least likely to ask for it, because talking about their struggles is seen as not living up to what a mother should be.

Role Strain Theories and Social Surveillance

Modern age mothers experience more tension and stress because most of the mothers now work both outside and also have to take care of the children. Goode
8
says in his “Role Strain Theory” that this mental distress happens because of overwhelming and unclear role demands of being a mother.
Mothers are constantly advised and criticized by the people around them, both in real life and social life. This “Social Surveillance Theory” says this creates anxiety and stress as well as weakens personal privacy
9
. In these times mothers do not need only medical care, but they also need emotional judgment-free support.

Social Support Theory (Non-clinical)

“Social Support Theory” is often used to understand the stress mothers feel that does not meet the level of a medical diagnosis, like Postpartum Depression or Postpartum Anxiety
14
;
5
. Emotional support, advice, and hands-on assistance can help people handle stress during major life changes
16
. For mothers, studies show that this kind of stress often manifests as feeling worn out, lonely, and self-blaming. Usually, this situation arises because they feel there is a “support gap” in which their needs exceed the help they receive
16
.
Mothers’ feelings of being alone could be so strong if they do not have proper emotional support from family and friends. In this case, it may happen that they are not showing any visible mental or physical health issues. Mothers may feel less noticed by others when they are dedicated and too much involved in taking care of their children
32
;
16
;
22
. In this situation, comfort and understanding from others become very important, and studies show that more support from friends or partners is linked to less stress for mothers and better feelings about their caregiving
4
;
16
. On the other hand, when these everyday support needs are not met, research shows that mothers may blame themselves for their stress rather than recognize the broader social or practical reasons
4
;
16
;
32
.

AI and LLMs as Support Proxies

Because of stigma and not having enough people to talk to, more research is looking at how mothers use AI and chatbots for emotional support. Studies say that chatbots like ChatGPT and Gemini can act as friendly conversation partners for people who worry about being judged or labeled at work
17
;
11
. Few studies show that mothers utilize these systems not only for informational purposes (e.g., pediatric guidance), but also for emotional reflection, reassurance, and alleviating loneliness
4
;
32
.
AI tools like LLMS are always available and free from judgment. This can make it easier for mothers to talk about difficult feelings like regret or burnout
24
;
2
. We do not know much about how mothers balance the safety of not being judged with the warmth of a real human connection in daily life.

2.3
AI as a Non-Clinical Emotional Support

Help-Seeking Behavior Theory

“Help-seeking Behavior Theory” is a set of ideas that explain how and why people decide to seek help for problems, often in health, mental health, or school settings
18
.
This theory says that people decide to seek help not just because they need it, but also based on how much it might cost, whether they might be judged, and how easy it is to get support
23
.

Preference for Low-risk, Low-cost Support Pathways:

AI-powered platforms make it easier to get emotional support by being available right away and costing very little. Being anonymous is especially important because research shows that private, anonymous conversations help people feel safe and comfortable. This encourages people who might avoid getting help because they worry about being judged. People may choose AI emotional support because it is a cheap and low-risk option compared to traditional mental health services
27
.

LLMs’ Role in Providing Anonymity, Immediacy, and Non-judgment:

Many people with mental health concerns avoid professional help because they fear their information will be leaked, cannot afford the cost in an emergency, or find services hard to access
27
. These barriers are stronger in places with fewer resources. LLMs lower these barriers because they offer immediate support, respond without judgment, and allow people to remain anonymous online
33
.
Many people feel safer talking to LLMs because they provide emotional support without the risk of personal judgment. LLMs do not get tired, do not hold personal opinions, and do not react based on past relationships
21
;
33
. Because of this, users do not have to worry that their words will be remembered, shared, or used against them. This helps explain why people often choose low-risk sources of support when they feel emotionally vulnerable. For mothers, this sense of safety can be especially important, since sharing personal struggles may lead to criticism or social consequences.

Human and LLMs Emotional Support

Support from other people can create a deep sense of connection. It is shaped by shared experience, thoughtful advice, and genuine care. At the same time, closeness also brings the risk of judgment. People have their own beliefs, limits, and emotional reactions, and these can affect how support is given
3
. Even well-meaning family members or friends may minimize concerns, express disappointment, or fail to notice emotional distress. These risks can make some people hesitate to open up, even when human support is available
3
.

2.4
Psychological Safety and Its Limits in LLMs Support

LLMs do not have personal experience and empathy, and thus, they cannot replace the depth and real understanding that humans bring to emotional support. Still, many people turn to AI because it offers support without judgment. In some cases, the lack of personal experience can be helpful, especially when people fear being judged or exposed.
This shows a basic conflict in how people look for emotional support. Feeling safe when talking to AI can happen in a few ways:

Evaluative privacy:
No risk of social consequences or relational fallout
19
.
Consistency:
Responses uninfluenced by provider mood or availability
19
.

Anonymity and transparency:
Users control disclosure and maintain anonymity
33
. LLMs can accept and support people, but they cannot offer the same kind of real connection that humans do. In some situations, the safety of not being judged makes people more willing to talk, but that same distance can also make the care feel less real.

Previous research has explored social expectations of motherhood, maternal stress, and the use of AI for emotional support, but these topics are often studied separately. We still know little about how they intersect in everyday life. We know less about how mothers use large language models when they feel judged, lack support from others, or face strong cultural expectations. This gap motivates our study to examine how mothers actually use LLMs to manage their emotions and seek support.

Method

3.1
Study Design

We conducted an independent, exploratory, cross-sectional online survey. Our goal was to examine how pregnant women and mothers use LLMs (e.g., ChatGPT, Gemini) to avoid social judgment for non-medical emotional support. We created the survey in Google Forms. We started our survey with a brief introduction describing the study’s purpose, followed by an informed consent page. Only participants who confirmed that they were 18 years or older and provided consent were allowed to proceed. We collected our data for ten days, from January 21, 2026, to January 30, 2026, after that we closed the data collection.

3.2
Participants

A total of 110 individuals responded to the survey. Of these, 3 respondents did not provide informed consent and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The final sample consisted of 107 participants. Participants were eligible if they were aged 18 or older and identified as pregnant or a mother (including first-time mothers and mothers of multiple children).

3.3
Recruitment

We used convenience and snowball sampling. The survey link was circulated through social media networks—primarily Facebook—across multiple community groups. Participants were encouraged to share the link with others (snowball distribution).

3.4
Survey Instrument

The questionnaire consisted of seven mandatory items, primarily presented as multiple-choice questions using five-point Likert scales (e.g., Never–Always, Strongly disagree–Strongly agree). One optional open-ended question to describe their experience, followed by the first question. All survey items were administered in English. The survey included questions that examined why mothers use LLMs for emotional and practical support. Items focused on perceived judgment from humans and LLMs, preferences for accuracy or empathy, and whether logical explanations from LLMs provided emotional relief. Additional questions explored the use of LLMs during moments of partner absence, anger toward children, feelings of guilt about parenting choices, and experiences of loneliness during late-night caregiving.

3.5
Data Collection and Cleaning

All survey questions were set as mandatory, ensuring that only complete responses were recorded. Partial or incomplete submissions were not possible. Responses were collected anonymously through Google Forms. We did not request names, email addresses, phone numbers, physical addresses, or any other identifying information.
Because no identifiers were collected, duplicate response detection was not possible.

Open-ended responses were reviewed before analysis. We had decided to remove any potentially identifying details (e.g., names of individuals, doctors, hospitals, or specific locations). All participants followed the instructions and did not write their personal information in the open-ended question.

3.6.1
Ethical Considerations

This was a minimal-risk, self-directed, independent study conducted outside a formal institutional research program. The survey began with an informed consent statement clarifying voluntary participation, the non-medical nature of the survey, anonymity protections, and the right to stop at any time. Participants confirmed they were 18 years or older before proceeding. We did not collect identifying information (e.g., name, email, phone number, address) and instructed participants not to include identifying details in open-ended responses.

In the open-ended questionnaires, participants said they use the LLMs to seek quick guidance to avoid criticism from relatives or friends. 4.3

A smaller proportion reported a clear preference for LLMs-generated logical explanations, with 26.2% (n = 28) stating that LLMs provided more peace of mind. In contrast, 19.6% (n = 21) reported that LLMs’ explanations provided no greater reassurance than human responses.

5
Discussion

Together, the above findings suggest that mothers engage with LLMs not as emotional substitutes, but as situational, low-risk emotional support embedded within everyday caregiving constraints.

5.1
RQ_{1}
: LLMs as a Judgment-Free Buffer

Our findings show that mothers use LLMs mostly when they do not feel safe sharing their concerns with people around them. Quantitative results show that almost half of the participants use LLMs sometimes or occasionally, and the qualitative report says that LLMs are used mainly in situations where mothers expect to be judged by others.
Mothers described situations where asking questions about their child’s health, feeding, or accidents led to criticism from relatives, especially in-laws. In those times, LLMs might become a non-judgmental space for them where they could ask questions without the fear of being blamed.

Our results can be explained by
Stigma Theory
. Stigma Theory explains how the fear of social judgment prevents people from sharing their sensitive problems and struggles
7
. LLMs do not judge or remember past mistakes, nor share secrets. These tools also do not follow any family power structure. Because of these reasons, mothers might turn to LLMs as a safer choice to avoid shame or criticism and to get quick emotional comfort and information during difficult times.

5.2
RQ_{2}
: Emotional Warmth and Safety

In our study, we tried to understand how mothers use this tool as their emotional support to receive reassurance, sensible information, and feel comfortable, rather than as a medical tool.
More than half of the participants said they missed human warmth when they needed help. They used LLMs as information tools for a quick reassurance or a piece of information to avoid judgment, not as a replacement for human support. This might suggest that mothers distinguish clearly when they need to feel safe and when they require human emotional support. Yet they seek help from LLMs because humans, such as family members or doctors, are not always available in an emergency situation. And sometimes there is a chance of being judged with or without explaining the whole situation. On the other hand, LLMs are a low-cost, low-risk choice, always available and free of social judgment. This aligns with
Help-Seeking Behavior Theory
. This theory suggests that people choose where to get help based on cost, risk, and ease of access
23
;
18
.

Family Context as a Moderator of Emotional Safety

Family structure also plays an influential role in mothers’ feelings about emotional safety. Most of the nuclear family mothers value the accurate information of LLMs, not because of the emotional safety. On the other hand, others living in joint families placed greater importance on LLM’s non-judgmental nature.
Social Surveillance and Role Strain Theory explain these differences. These theories suggest that being watched all the time and having many expectations adds emotional pressure
8
;
9
. In joint families, caregiving is very visible and often judged, making criticism more likely. LLMs let mothers step away from these family pressures for a while without going against family authority.

5.3
RQ_{3}
: LLMs for Emotional Regulation and Vulnerability

Emotional Regulation

The findings suggest that mothers used LLMs for emotional regulation and reassurance. They did not use them for deep emotional relationships. We asked them to compare LLMs’ logical explanation with a human’s generic reassurance. More than half did not view the two as interchangeable and agreed that LLMs and humans serve different emotional roles.
Only a quarter of the participants found LLMs’ logical explanations more calming than human reassurance. This might suggest that for some mothers, clear and organized answers help them feel better during uncertain times. Earlier research, such as Rubin et al., Ma et al., showed that as caregiving is a stressful task, people can feel better not just from empathy, but also if the opposite party understands and thinks clearly
24
;
17
.
At the same time, nearly one-fifth of participants said LLMs did not make them feel any better, which suggests that LLMs do not work for everyone when it comes to handling emotions.
Taken together, these results show that LLMs help people steady their emotions, rather than replace human comfort. Participants said they used LLMs to help them understand and think through things when they felt unsure.

Maternal Guilt and Self-Reassurance

Our result shows that only about one-quarter of participants said they sought reassurance from LLMs when they felt guilty about resting or not doing enough as caregivers. This suggests that guilt, which is closely connected to what people think mothers should do, may be harder to share with LLMs, and thus it was not a common pattern.
Stigma Theory and Social Support Theory say that guilt related to motherhood is often internalized and morally charged. This makes it less amenable to resolution through impersonal tools. For some mothers, LLMs offer a judgment-free space to normalize these feelings; for others, guilt may require human validation to feel legitimate.

Anger and Emotional Boundaries of LLMs Use

Most participants said they did not use LLMs to deal with anger; they would rather talk out with others or handle it on their own. Only a few used LLMs to handle their anger sometimes. Participants said they used LLMs for thinking things through, receiving reassurance, and understanding situations. But it was not used for dealing with feelings like fear of harm, a sense of losing control, or doing something wrong. This shows that mothers are careful and selective in their use of LLMs for emotional support.

Late-Night Loneliness and Temporal Availability

A small but notable group discussed with LLMs at night when they felt alone. But most participants reported they did not use LLMs for the company at night while taking care of the baby.
Our results show that late-night LLM use was strongly linked to participants’ employment status and education level. This suggests that factors such as work hours and caregiving responsibilities affect when and why LLMs are used for emotional support. This supports the idea that LLMs are most helpful for emotional support when people need it at certain times, especially when others are unavailable.

LLMs as an Immediate Resource

Our results show that people use LLMs only in certain situations. When partners were not around, almost half of the participants said LLMs were not helpful. However, nearly half said LLMs were at least sometimes helpful, which suggests that LLMs can be a backup in some situations but are not the main source of support.

These patterns show that technology can fill short-term gaps in help or emotional support, but it cannot take the place of real relationships. LLMs seem most helpful when people cannot get help from others right away and need quick reassurance, advice, or a way to steady their emotions, rather than long-term emotional care.

5.4
Limitations and Future Work

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sample size (
N=107
) is appropriate for an exploratory mixed-methods study, but it limits the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Some associations, particularly those involving student level or family context, should therefore be interpreted as suggestive rather than conclusive.
Second, the data are based on self-reported survey responses. Although the survey was anonymous, participants may have under-reported emotions such as anger or guilt due to social desirability or moral expectations associated with motherhood. As a result, reported frequencies of emotionally charged behaviors may be conservative estimates.
This means the results may not apply to everyone, since people who are comfortable with technology and LLMs might be included more often. The findings should not be seen as representing all mothers.
Fourth, the part of the study examining written answers was based on a small number of responses (20 in total). While these answers gave useful background, they are meant to show examples of patterns, not to show how common these patterns are or to represent larger trends.

Finally, culture plays an important role in understanding these results. The differences observed in joint family structures show common caregiving practices in South Asian families. In a joint family, caregiving is considered an easy task and is often judged by family members. These situations may differ in Western, or more individual-focused, families.
Overall, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than representative or causal.

Future Work

Our findings can be used to build future research in several important ways.
Longitudinal studies can be conducted to examine how mothers’ use of LLMs changes. And also whether support from LLMs helps reduce feelings of isolation, or makes them worse at different points in caregiving.
Journaling or using diaries can be helpful to observe mothers’ daily caregiving moments, such as nighttime feeding, when a child is sick, or when switching between work and care. As remembering this information later or getting time to write it down is not easy for a mother, applications with LLMs can be introduced, where this information will be automatically stored.

To see how different types of reassurance affect emotions, stress, and confidence in decision-making, experiments could also compare how LLMs provide logical answers with how people offer caring responses in real time.
Comparing caregiving in different cultures could help show which results are due to family setup, how much others watch over families, or cultural rules, and which results apply more widely.

Finally, our findings could be helpful to improve LLMs’ use of kind language, the right tone, and to be able to set limits when providing emotional support. This work would help guide the careful design of LLMs that help caregivers without taking the place of or hurting real human relationships.

6
Conclusion

In this study, we aimed to understand how mothers are using LLMs to get emotional support in daily life to avoid being judged and criticized. We conducted an online survey and
107
responses are considered for the result analysis. During our survey, we clearly mentioned that we were not looking for information on how mothers use LLMs as a medical tool, but rather if they are using these for judgment less, reassurance, a safe place, and quick information.
The results show that mothers do not see LLMs as replacements for real companionship. Instead, they use them when it is hard to get help from people because they are not around or they will be judged for sharing their struggle. They found LLMs safe and easy resources that offer comfort, information, and support. Mothers found LLMs as a private and non-judgmental space to work through feelings like guilt, fear, or not feeling good enough as a caregiver. And while sharing, they were not worried about being judged. The results also suggest that family structure, job status, education level, and the type of emotional need play a role in how helpful LLMs are to a mother. As LLMs become more common in daily life, it is important to learn when and why people use LLMs, and when they do not, so these tools can be designed to meet real needs.
