Development of a potential vaccine against dengue and chikungunya virus co-infection using immunoinformatics and structural evaluation
Authors: Sadeeq Ullah, Junhong Xie, Caixia Guo, Hizbullah Khan, Xiao Zou, Tianwen Lai, Abdullah Shah, Rehmat Islam, Jiahui Luo, Wenqi Xu, Amin Ullah Jan, Abdullah Khan, Jieming Xiao, Fang He, Lu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue virus (DENV) and chikungunya virus (CHIKV) co-infection poses a growing challenge in regions where both arboviruses co-circulate, highlighting the need for integrated vaccine strategies. Here, we designed a multi-epitope subunit vaccine candidate targeting conserved regions of the DENV NS1 protein and the CHIKV NSP1 protein using an immunoinformatics-based approach. Conserved CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes were selected on the basis of antigenicity, safety-related screening, and population coverage, then assembled with immunologically appropriate linkers and an N-terminal human β-defensin-2 adjuvant. The construct displayed favourable physicochemical features and acceptable structural quality after three-dimensional modeling and validation. Docking analysis indicated stable interaction with human TLR4, which was further examined by normal-mode analysis and 100-ns all-atom molecular dynamics simulations performed in AMBER v24 with the ff19SB force field. Trajectory analyses supported conformational stability of the vaccine-TLR4 complex, and MM/GBSA calculations suggested favourable binding energetics. Codon optimization supported expression feasibility in the E. coli pET-28a(+) system. In silico immune simulation further predicted induction of both humoral and cellular immune responses, with increased antibody production and immune-memory development. These results support the proposed construct as a rational candidate for further experimental evaluation toward a vaccine strategy against DENV-CHIKV co-infection.

Introduction

Co-infection occurs when one pathogen either suppresses or enhances the replication and pathogenicity of another, complicating the clinical progression.1 This interaction can significantly worsen health outcomes by increasing disease severity, prolonging illness, and complicating both diagnosis and treatment.2 Therefore, understanding co-infection dynamics is crucial for accurate surveillance, differential diagnosis, and the development of integrated vector control and management strategies.3 The co-infection of dengue virus (DENV) and chikungunya virus (CHIKV), both transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, poses a major public health concern in tropical and subtropical areas since the same insects spread both diseases.4 Such co-infection commonly causes fever, rash, and joint discomfort, increasing the risk of misdiagnosis and delayed treatment.5 The dual infection can complicate clinical management by combining symptoms such as severe arthralgia from CHIKV and hemorrhagic tendencies from DENV, potentially raising morbidity and hospitalization rates.6

Recent entomological research confirmed natural co-infection of DENV and CHIKV in wild Aedes mosquitoes, demonstrating transovarial transmission and indicating vertical viral passage from female mosquitoes to their offspring.7 This co-infection has also been observed in clinical cases. In Brazil, a 2023 report highlighted a simultaneous infection in a 77-year-old patient who experienced persistent arthralgia despite having mild acute symptoms.8 Additionally, a pediatric study in Myanmar found that 29.4% of febrile children were coinfected with DENV and CHIKV, with lower viral loads in co-infected cases compared to monoinfections, suggesting possible viral interference.9 On a global scale, the pooled prevalence of DENV-CHIKV co-infection is estimated at 2.5%, with higher rates in Asia and Latin America.10 Moreover, the absence of specific antiviral therapies or combined vaccines further underscores the significance of these co-infections in endemic regions.11

The Aedes aegypti mosquito is the main vector for transmitting the dengue fever virus, an infectious disease. The virus belongs to the Flaviviridae family and has four main types: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4.12 Dengue fever is a moderately severe disease that typically resolves on its own, but dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome are more serious and can be life-threatening.13 After the first major dengue fever pandemic in the Philippines in 1953–1954, dengue quickly became a worldwide public health issue, with outbreaks of dengue fever and dengue hemorrhagic fever occurring in different parts of the world.14 The CHIKV virus causes CHIK fever, an arthropod-borne viral infection that is mostly spread through the bite of the Aedes aegypti mosquito. The term “Chikungunya” comes from the Makonde language, meaning “that which bends up”, referring to the stooped posture of affected individuals due to severe joint pain and arthritis-like symptoms associated with the disease.15 CHIKV was first identified during a major epidemic in Tanzania between 1952 and 1953, marking the initial recognition of the disease as a distinct viral infection.16 In Asia, regions affected by CHIKV overlap with areas widespread for DENV, increasing the likelihood of mosquitoes becoming co-infected with both viruses1718;. Both diseases share several overlapping clinical symptoms, including high fever with chills, painful swelling of major and minor joints, restricted limb movement, nausea, headache, and vomiting. In some cases, patients may also develop characteristic skin rashes as part of the symptomatic presentation.19

Co-infections can significantly compromise vaccine effectiveness by altering host immune responses and exacerbating disease severity. Evidence from diverse systems, including helminth, influenza, COVID-19, and HIV co-infections, demonstrates that interacting pathogens can modulate B-cell and T-cell immunity, reduce antibody responses, and influence vaccine performance, thereby complicating disease control in endemic regions.20, 21, 22 These challenges highlight the need for integrated vaccination strategies and careful consideration of co-infection dynamics in vaccine development. Within this context, the co-infection of dengue virus (DENV) and chikungunya virus (CHIKV) represents an escalating global public health concern. Both arboviruses are transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes and frequently overlap in tropical and subtropical regions, increasing the likelihood of dual infection.23 Recent surveillance in Brazil has documented high levels of concurrent circulation, reflecting shared transmission ecology and sustained outbreak risk.24 Despite the substantial global burden of chikungunya and recurrent dengue epidemics, current vaccine strategies face limitations, including incomplete cross-serotype protection for dengue and safety or deployment constraints for CHIKV vaccines. These gaps underscore the need for innovative vaccine approaches, supporting the rationale for an immunoinformatics-guided, multi-epitope subunit vaccine designed to target conserved immunogenic determinants of both DENV and CHIKV and provide dual protective potential.25

The design of multi-epitope vaccines involves combining carefully selected immunogenic peptides that can stimulate both B-cell and T-cell responses. These vaccines are effective for preventing and treating viral infections.26 They activate the immune system to produce antibodies and memory cells, offering long-term protection against specific viral serotypes.27 To support the rational development of these vaccines, immunoinformatics tools have been created to predict and select suitable epitopes.28 Increasingly, these in-silico strategies are being used to screen and identify potential epitope candidates for vaccine formulation.29

Herein, in silico computational approaches were used to construct a promising epitope-based vaccine candidate aimed at stimulating a targeted and effective immune response.24, 30 A complete evaluation was conducted using known immunogenicity criteria to identify the most capable and immunologically relevant candidates among the predicted epitopes for cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), helper T lymphocytes (HTLs), and B cells. The finalized multi-epitope construct was then assessed for its potential to activate the immune system by performing molecular docking analyses with TLR4 and conducting in silico immune response simulations. These studies provide a rational and systematic framework for designing an innovative chimeric vaccine candidate.

Methodology

Retrieval of protein sequences and prediction of antigenicity

The complete proteomes of Dengue virus (DENV) and Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) in FASTA format. The protein sequences were subsequently analyzed using the VaxiJen server (http://www.ddg pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) to evaluate their antigenic potential. Among the analyzed proteins, the NS1 protein of DENV and the NSP1 protein of CHIKV exhibited the highest antigenicity scores and were therefore selected for the design of the final chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, highly antigenic NS1 sequences from DENV serotypes DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4, along with the NSP1 sequence of CHIKV, were downloaded using their respective accession numbers (Supplementary Figs. S1–S5 and Supplementary Tables S1–S5). All sequences were saved in FASTA format for subsequent analyses aimed at identifying and validating antigenic regions for chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine development. The individual protein accession numbers utilized in this study are provided in Supplementary Figs. S1–S5 and Tables S1–S5. Toxicity of proteins

NS1 and NSP1 were selected as vaccine targets due to their high sequence conservation, essential roles in viral replication, immune evasion, and proven immunogenicity.31 Importantly, as non-structural proteins, they minimize the risk of antibody-dependent enhancement compared with structural proteins, while enabling the induction of robust cellular and humoral immune responses, making them suitable candidates for multi-epitope vaccine design.32 The ToxDL server (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/ToxDL/index.html) was utilized to evaluate the potential toxicity of the protein. This tool applies an interpretable deep learning based algorithm that classifies proteins into two categories, i.e., toxic or non-toxic, based on their sequence features.

Identification of conserved region

The highly antigenic non-structural protein of Dengue virus (NS1) and Chikungunya virus (NSP1) were subjected to multiple sequence alignment using the Clustal Omega online server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/), to identify conserved regions. The predicted epitopes for B and T cells were investigated in the aligned retrieved non-structural protein of Dengue virus (NS1) and Chikungunya virus (NSP1) for conservation. Conserved epitopes exhibiting high sequence identity across all serotypes were prioritized for inclusion in the final construct to ensure broad cross-protective potential.33 Based on this analysis, highly conserved regions were selected for the final CTL, HTL, and BCL epitope selection.

Identification and evaluation of CTL-epitopes

CTL-epitopes play a crucial role in eliminating virus-infected cells, strengthening cellular immunity, and lowering viral loads in infected individuals. The NetCTL 1.2 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetCTL-1.2/) was used to predict CTL epitopes from the DENV and CHIKV. This server predicts MHC class I binding sites within the target protein, then assesses peptide transport efficiency via the Transporter Associated with Antigen Processing (TAP), and finally, identifies proteasomal C-terminal cleavage sites. For CTL epitope prediction, a threshold value of 0.75 was applied to ensure high specificity and immunogenic relevance.

Identification of HTL and B-cell epitopes

HTL epitope prediction using the NS1 protein of the DENV and the NSP1 protein of the CHIKV was evaluated by using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) server (http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/). Epitope selection was guided by a multi-parameter strategy including prediction scores followed by evaluation of antigenicity, immunogenicity, non-allergenicity, non-toxicity, conservancy, and population coverage. HTL epitopes with the lowest percentile rankings were selected for vaccine design due to their higher affinity for MHC class II alleles. The selected epitopes were assembled using appropriate linkers to preserve processing efficiency and minimize junctional immunogenicity. The identification of B-cell epitopes, which are essential for inducing protective antibody-mediated immune responses, was performed using the ABCPred server (http://crdd.osdd.net/raghava/abcpred/).34 Epitopes with the highest predictive scores were selected based on a stringent threshold cutoff of 0.80 to ensure optimal prediction accuracy.

Design and assembly of the multi-epitope vaccine construct

The selected CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes obtained from the non-structural proteins (NS1 of the DENV and NSP1 of the CHIKV) were assembled into a chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine construct. To ensure proper immunogenic presentation and structural flexibility, different linkers were used (AAY, GPGPG, and KK) to construct a chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine. AAY linkers were used between CTL epitopes to enhance proteasomal processing and MHC class I presentation. GPGPG linkers were employed between HTL epitopes to support MHC class II antigen processing. KK linkers were included to reduce junctional epitopes and improve antigen processing efficiency.35 Moreover, the human-derived β-defensin-2 was selected as an adjuvant for its potent antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, acting as a TLR4 agonist that promotes dendritic cell maturation and cytokine secretion crucial for adaptive immune activation. Its incorporation enhances antigen presentation and stimulates robust humoral and cellular immune responses. To further enhance the immunogenic potency of the designed construct, an appropriate adjuvant was conjugated to the N-terminal end of the vaccine sequence using an EAAK linker, promoting enhanced immune system activation and antigen presentation.30

Antigenicity and allergenicity of identification

The use of the VaxiJen v2.0 server (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) was employed to test the designed vaccine construct's antigenic potential. The threshold value was set at 0.4 to ensure the accuracy of the antigenicity prediction. Additionally, the allergenic profile of the designed construct was evaluated using the AllerTOP v.2.0 server (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/), enabling the identification of potential allergenic properties to confirm the vaccine's safety profile.

Physicochemical characterization and 3D structural validation

The ProtParam tool, available on the ExPASy server (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/), was used to determine the physicochemical properties of the final vaccine construct. These properties included: amino acid composition, theoretical isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight, instability index, projected half-life, aliphatic index, and the grand average of hydropathicity. The designed vaccine construct was submitted in FASTA format to the Robetta server (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/) for 3D structural modeling. The most reliable predicted model was then validated using ProSA-Web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) to evaluate the structural quality and the PROCHECK server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/) to analyze stereochemical properties through Ramachandran plot analysis.

Population coverage analysis

The population coverage of the selected epitopes was evaluated using the IEDB Population Coverage tool (https://tools.iedb.org/population/). Predicted HLA-binding epitopes were assessed to estimate their potential recognition among global and regional human populations. The analysis incorporated the frequency distribution of HLA class I and class II alleles across diverse ethnic groups. Overall and overlapping population coverages were computed to determine the vaccine construct's ability to induce broad immune responses worldwide.

Normal mode analysis-based dynamics

Normal mode analysis (NMA) of the docked vaccine receptor complex was performed using the iMODS online server (https://imods.chaconlab.org/). This server enables the assessment of protein deformability, structural stability, and collective motions of biomolecular complexes. It also allows visualization of possible conformational transitions between different structural states. In the present study, NMA was applied to examine the stability and dynamic behavior of the complex by evaluating parameters such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), residue–residue covariance, eigenvalues associated with molecular motions, and overall structural deformation patterns.22

Molecular docking analysis of the vaccine construct with TLR4

Molecular docking was performed to assess the structural compatibility between the designed vaccine construct and a host innate immune receptor. The 3D structure of Toll-like receptor 4 used in this study was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) as PDB ID: 2Z63. This structure was selected because it provides an experimentally resolved, high-resolution TLR4 ectodomain scaffold suitable for protein–protein docking and subsequent assessment of structural stability. TLR4 was evaluated because it is a major innate immune receptor involved in pathogen recognition, and because the vaccine construct contains a β-defensin-2 adjuvant, which has been reported to activate immature dendritic cells through a TLR4-dependent mechanism.54 However, 2Z63 does not represent the complete native human TLR4-MD2 receptor complex; therefore, the docking analysis was interpreted as an assessment of vaccine-TLR4 ectodomain structural compatibility rather than direct evidence of physiological TLR4-MD2 activation.36 Before docking, PyMOL software was used to remove all hetero-atoms and H2O molecules to generate the active receptor structure. The optimized TLR4 ectodomain scaffold and the designed vaccine construct were uploaded to the HDOCK web server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) to assess their binding interactions. The top-ranked docked complex, determined based on docking score, was selected for further structural and interaction analysis.

Molecular dynamics simulation and stability analysis of the vaccine-TLR4 complex

The AMBER v24 simulation software and the ff19SB force field for proteins were used to run atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. The TLR4 ectodomain scaffold and the vaccine construct were parameterized separately and then combined for complex simulations. A 12 Å buffer was maintained between each solute atom and the edge of the system by solvating it in a truncated octahedral box of specific OPC H2O molecules. Sodium ions (Na+) and chloride ions (Cl−) were added to balance the system and maintain a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M. Minimization involved two stages: first, a restrained minimization of solvent and ions (5000 steps), then an unrestrained minimization of the entire system (5000 steps). The system was gradually heated from 0 K to 310 K over 50 ps using Langevin dynamics, with a collision frequency of 2 ps−1. Equilibration was constructed for 500 ps in the NPT ensemble with positional restraints on the protein backbone (10 kcal mol−1 Å−2), using a Berendsen barostat. The molecular dynamics simulations were evaluated using an NPT ensemble at a fixed temperature of 310 K and pressure of 1 atm, employing a Monte Carlo barostat for pressure regulation throughout the simulation. A 2 fs time step was used with hydrogen-containing bonds constrained via the SHAKE algorithm. Long-range electrostatics were modeled with the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a non-bonded cutoff of 10 Å. Simulations were run on GPUs using the CUDA implementation of PMEMD. Trajectories were saved every 10 ps for downstream analysis. Each simulation was performed for 100 ns, and at least three independent replicates were generated to ensure convergence.

Trajectory analysis and binding free energy estimation

Post-simulation analyses were performed using cpptraj from AmberTools24. The radius of gyration (Rg), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and root mean square deviation (RMSD) were calculated for 100 ns production trajectories after aligning all frames to the starting structure using backbone atoms of the vaccine TLR4- ectodomain complex. RMSD is used to monitor overall stability, while RMSF assesses residue-level flexibility, and Rg evaluates conformational compactness. Native contacts were identified by identifying all heavy atom pairs between TLR4 and the vaccine construct within 4.5 Å at each frame, excluding intra-chain contacts. Contact frequency maps were created to highlight stable interfacial interactions. To assess convergence of the receptor-vaccine binding pose, RMSD-based cluster analysis was performed on the final 30 ns of the 100 ns production trajectory, corresponding to the 70–100 ns simulation window, using cpptraj in AmberTools24. Frames were aligned to the backbone atoms of the vaccine-TLR4 ectodomain complex before clustering. The most populated cluster was selected as the representative stabilized conformational state. MM/GBSA was used to estimate the binding free energy in MMPBSA.py. Snapshots from the last 25 ns of each trajectory were used to analyze the energetics of complex formation. The binding free energy (ΔG_bind) was calculated as:(A.1)ΔGbind=Gcomplex−(Greceptor+Gligand)

Using the GBneck2 model (igb = 8) with a monovalent salt concentration of 0.15 M. Calculations were performed without entropic contributions. All analyses were carried out on the entire TLR4-vaccine system without truncation. Atomic-level interface characterization of the representative vaccine-TLR4 ectodomain complex was performed using COCOMAPS v2.0.59 Chain A was defined as the TLR4 ectodomain scaffold, and chain B as the designed vaccine construct. The interface was analyzed using the default 5.0 Å contact cut-off to identify buried surface area, interacting residues, hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, polar and apolar van der Waals contacts, CH-O/N bonds, CH-π interactions, proximal contacts, steric clashes, and water-mediated interactions. The resulting interaction summary and contact map were used to define the stabilizing interface fingerprint.

Codon adaptation and in-silico optimization of the vaccine construct

In silico codon optimization is a computational-based genetic technique primarily used to optimize certain sequences of amino acids and accomplish maximum protein expression. The JCat (http://www.jcat.de/) was utilized to convert the amino acid sequence of the vaccine protein into a sequence of DNA suitable for cloning into the vaccine construct Escherichia coli (E. coli) K12 expression system. The JCat online server gives a CAI (codon adaptation index) value and GC content to reflect the level of expression in the E. coli expression system. To insert the mandatory gene into the E. coli pET-28a (+) expression vector, SnapGene was used with the Hand III and EcoR1 restriction sites added to the N and C terminal ends of the sequence.

In-silico immune simulation

The C-ImmSim web server (https://kraken.iac.rm.cnr.it/C-IMMSIM/) was used to analyze the immunological response that the proposed vaccination might cause in humans. The platform measures the vaccine's antigenic potential and its ability to generate humoral and cellular immune responses after the insertion. In silico simulation evaluated the levels of antibodies, cytokines, and interferons produced in response to the vaccine, as well as the activation and proliferation of key immune cell populations, such as type 1 and type 2 helper T cells (Th1 and Th2). It evaluated the immunostimulatory potential of the vaccine using computational analyses.

Results

Vaccines activate the immune system and are important for the protection against various infectious diseases. Instead of utilizing entire viral proteins, small antigenic determinants (epitopes) are often enough to trigger robust immune responses. Immunoinformatics provides a rapid and precise approach for identifying such immunogenic sites, speeding up rational vaccine design. In this study, the NS1 protein of the DENV and the NSP1 protein of the CHIKV were analyzed using a range of immunoinformatics tools to identify immunogenic, non-allergenic CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes with the potential to produce safe and effective immune responses. By combining these epitopes into chimeric, multi-epitope subunit constructs, recent progress aims to develop a vaccine construct having the potential of providing broad and effective protection against both DENV and CHIKV.

Identification of target proteins and conserved regions

The antigenicity of ten proteins of DENV and nine proteins from CHIKV was evaluated using the VaxiJen server. The non-structural proteins, such as NS1 and NSP1 from both viruses (DENV and CKIV), showed antigenicity scores of 0.61 for DENV and 0.50 for CHIKV, and were selected for constructing the final chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccines, as shown in (Supplementary Table S6). The protein sequences for the DENV and CHIKV were retrieved and saved in FASTA format. Multiple sequence alignment revealed conserved regions within the non-structural protein of each virus. The final selection of HTL, CTL, and BCL epitopes for designing the chimeric multiepitope subunit vaccine was limited to non-mutated and fully conserved regions of the protein. This approach ensures that the designed vaccine targets conserved viral regions, reducing the risk of immune escape due to mutations and increasing its effectiveness across different strains of both viruses.

Identification and evaluation of predicted epitopes

Cytotoxic T-cell (CTL) epitopes identified in DENV and CHIKV proteins

Adaptive immunity depends on the MHC class 1 glycoprotein present on all nucleated cells to transmit endogenous antigenic peptides to cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+ T cells). This process facilitates immune recognition and targeted elimination of infected or abnormal cells. These glycoproteins enable the display of both self-peptides from endogenous proteins and foreign peptides from intracellular pathogens such as viruses, which trigger a cytotoxic T cell response to eliminate the infected or abnormal cells. Besides all nucleated cells, MHC class I glycoproteins are also expressed on specialized antigen-presenting cells like B cells, monocytes, thymic epithelial cells, and dendritic cells, which present antigens and activate cytotoxic T lymphocytes. Given the critical role of CTLs in clearing invading pathogens, the protein sequences of NS1 and NSP1 from both DENV and CHIKV were uploaded to the NetCTL 1.2 web-based server to predict highly immunogenic CTL epitopes. Using this server, 346 and 527, epitopes were predicted for non-structural proteins of the DENV and CHIKV, respectively. From these predicted epitopes, eight proteins were selected based on their high binding affinity scores (Supplementary Table S7). The selected epitopes were further analyzed for their antigenicity profile, such as toxicity and allergenicity. Helper T-lymphocyte and B-cell epitopes and their antigenic profiles

Helper T-Lympocyte cells empower adaptive immune response against invading pathogens, including viruses. They contribute to immune defense by promoting both B-cell-mediated humoral responses and cytotoxic T-cell-mediated cellular immunity. Following viral infection, macrophages internalize and process viral particles, presenting the resulting epitopes to helper T cells, which then activate B cells to produce virus-neutralizing antibodies. Due to the important role of HTLs in modulating immune responses, the non-structural glycoproteins of DENV and CHIKV were analyzed for HTL epitope prediction, resulting in 341 potential epitopes for DENV and 1043 for CHIKV. From these predicted epitopes, 08 from each protein were selected based on their lowest percentile rankings, ranging from 0.08 to 1.00 (Supplementary Table S8). The identified epitopes were analyzed for antigenicity, toxicity, and allergenicity, as well as their presence within conserved regions of the corresponding DENV and CHIKV proteins, ensuring the selection of safe, immunogenic, and broadly protective candidates. Differentiated B cells function as the main antibody-producing cells in the human body and play an important role in connecting the adaptive and innate immune systems. In this study, the non-structural glycoproteins of DENV and CHIKV were analyzed using the ABCPred online server to identify potential B-cell epitopes capable of eliciting effective humoral immune responses. The ABCPred server predicted 26 and 25 BCL epitopes for non-structural glycoproteins of the DENV and CHIKV virus, respectively. From these predicted epitopes, eight from each protein were selected based on their high binding affinity scores (Supplementary Table S9). Construction and structural validation of the chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine

To design the final version of the multi-epitope subunit vaccine (CMESV), the selected immunogenic epitopes from the non-structural glycoproteins of DENV and CHIKV were joined using appropriate peptide linkers (Fig. 2A). The AAY, GPGPG, and KK linkers were used to connect CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes, respectively. These linkers not only provide structural flexibility but also enhance the immunogenicity of the vaccine construct. Specifically, the AAY linker supports efficient epitope presentation, the GPGPG linker aids in HTL activation and boosts antibody responses, while the KK linker improves the overall immunostimulatory capacity. Additionally, these linkers prevent steric hindrance and undesirable folding among adjacent epitopes, ensuring their independent function. An EAAK linker was used to conjugate an adjuvant to the N-terminal region of the construct, enhancing its ability to induce strong immune responses. Human β-defensin 2, known for its antimicrobial and immunomodulatory properties, was incorporated as the adjuvant to improve vaccine stability and antigen-specific immune activation. Molecular docking and normal mode analysis of the vaccine-TLR4 complex

For a vaccine to effectively activate an immune response, it must exhibit strong binding affinity toward the host's immune receptors. TLRs are key components of the innate immune system that regulate inflammatory signaling pathways and coordinate immune defenses against pathogens. Such kind of receptors are responsible for detecting pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which initiate intracellular signaling cascades, pathways, and gene expression changes that activate the immune system downstream. To assess receptor binding potential, molecular docking between the constructed vaccine and TLR4 ectodomain scaffold (PDB ID: 2Z63) was performed using the HDOCK server. The predicted binding energy for the chimeric multi-epitope subunit vaccine (CMESV) was −273.27 kcal mol−1, indicating a strong and stable affinity between the designed vaccine construct and TLR4 (Fig. 3A and B). Further interaction analysis with the PDBsum server identified seven hydrogen bonds between TLR4 (Chain A) and the vaccine construct (Chain B): Tyr38-Asn139, Ser62-Glu207, Gln39-Phe205, Arg234-Pro319, Gln578-Pro300, His431-Asn338, and Asn338-Gln430. These hydrogen bonds, together with one salt bridge and 307 non-bonded interactions, support the formation of a stable computational interface between the vaccine construct and the selected TLR4 ectodomain scaffold (Fig. 3C). Overall, the interplay of stabilizing van der Waals and solvation energies against electrostatic penalties resulted in a strongly negative ΔGtotal, consistent with persistent native contacts seen in the trajectory (Fig. 7). Computational simulation of the host immune response

A vaccination process stimulates protective immune responses in the host without causing the actual disease. When protein-based antigens are administered with adjuvants, they trigger localized innate immune activation, characterized by macrophage stimulation and the release of proinflammatory cytokines that initiate and amplify adaptive immune responses. The processes involve internalizing the antigen and presenting it on their surface using high-affinity major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, such as macrophages or dendritic cells. T-cells recognise the MHC-antigen complex through T-cell receptors, leading to adaptive immunity activation and the formation of memory T-cells. To assess the immune-enhancing effectiveness of the designed vaccines, immune response simulations were conducted. These simulations focused specifically on antibody production following vaccine administration. Interestingly, the immune system showed a boosted response with a significant increase in antibody production after insertion of each vaccine construct (Fig. 9). After administration of the chimeric multiepitope subunit vaccine construct, antigen titers increased suddenly and peaked by the third day. However, a rise in antibody titers was also observed starting from the seventh day. By the tenth day, complete antigen neutralization was achieved, along with activation of additional immune components. Post-vaccination, combined IgM and IgG levels, including IgG1 + IgG2, IgG1, and IgG2 titers, were similarly elevated (Fig. 9A). Interleukin (IL) and cytokine responses were also assessed, as shown in Fig. 9B, revealing significantly increased levels of IFN-γ, TGF-β, IL-12, and IL-10. These findings indicate a consistent and strong immune activation following vaccination. The cellular immune response upon re-exposure to the pathogen was notably robust, characterized by the formation of memory cells. Discussion

The development of vaccines targeting specific pathogens remains one of the most effective strategies for preventing infectious diseases. Conventional vaccine development methods have proven successful against many pathogens and continue to be a key part of global immunization programs.37 However, vaccines made through traditional methods often face several challenges, including long production times, high costs, potential safety issues, and limited effectiveness against rapidly mutating or newly emerging pathogens.38 In-silico approaches provide an efficient alternative to overcome these limitations.39 These computational methods are faster, more cost-effective, and highly reliable, allowing for accurate prediction and design of vaccine candidates with better safety and efficacy.29 The computational approach to vaccine development has gained significant interest from researchers because of its simplicity, high specificity, and improved safety, allowing for the rational design of targeted vaccine candidates with reduced experimental complexity4041;. This approach is especially useful in cases of viral co-infection, where complex interactions between circulating pathogens can complicate both disease progression and vaccine design. A major challenge in such situations is viral interference, where one virus affects or suppresses the replication of another co-infecting virus, potentially changing the severity of the disease, host immune responses, and complete infection outcome.37

In-silico approaches can help elucidate pathogen-pathogen and host-pathogen interactions, thus supporting the rational design of vaccines that address the challenges posed by viral interference during co-infection. This can lead to varied virulence and virus-mediated cytopathy, impacting disease severity.38 Both CHIKV and DENV are transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes and share considerable overlap in their seasonal patterns and geographical distribution.42 Consequently, regions for these vectors have a high risk of co-infection, adding complexity to diagnosis, clinical management, and vaccine development.37 DENV and CHIKV are known to interact and influence each other's replication dynamics at the cellular level. Both infections are frequently associated with hepatic complications, with acute liver involvement being common during infection with either virus. Notably, hepatic dysfunction is recognized as one of the key warning signs indicating progression to severe dengue disease.43 DENV and CHIKV often co-circulate in the same Aedes mosquito vectors and geographic regions; documented co-infection rates are significant during outbreaks, and their circulation has important epidemiological implications4445;. A combined vaccine could reduce logistical burdens, prevent simultaneous morbidity, and potentially decrease misdiagnosis and inappropriate management in endemic areas.

An innovative method in vaccine research and development is the epitope-based vaccination, offering increased safety, higher efficacy, and improved logistical practicality. Multi-epitope vaccinations trigger specific immune responses to conserved epitopes within the full antigen, while avoiding undesirable epitopes could cause harmful immune-modulating reactions.46 Currently, the diversification of methodologies in structural biology, bioinformatics, and computational biology has become essential for advancing efficient vaccines47.35 Therefore, developing new therapeutic strategies for the co-infection of CHIKV and DENV is very important. The availability of biological and proteomic data has improved vaccine development through reverse vaccinology.48 Epitope-based vaccinations represent a revolutionary therapeutic approach aimed at creating effective vaccines with heightened potency, logistical feasibility, and better safety. Multi-epitope vaccines show promise in inducing targeted immune responses by concentrating on conserved epitopes within full antigenic sequences.46 Population-coverage analysis using IEDB revealed high global and regional HLA representation driven by prevalent alleles such as HLA-A02:01, HLA-A24:02, and HLA-B07:02 (MHC-I), together with HLA-DRB104:01, DRB107:01, and DRB1*15:01 (MHC-II), which dominate antigen presentation across Europe, East Asia, South Asia, and the Americas.49 However, reduced representation of certain HLA-B and HLA-DR alleles common in West and East African and indigenous populations suggests a potential coverage gap, highlighting the need for additional epitopes to improve global equity.50

This work aimed to use immunoinformatics techniques to develop a novel multiepitope-based vaccine construct designed to induce immune responses in persons infected with the virus. The protein sequences of non-structural proteins were first retrieved from the NCBI database. The epitopes were selected based on their antigenicity, non-allergenic qualities, and non-toxic properties. These epitopes were then linked with linkers and adjuvant peptides to create an effective vaccine. This process ensured the structural integrity and effectiveness of the modified vaccine.51 In silico analysis indicated a high antigenic score and predicted non-allergenicity of the vaccine construct. Its design exhibits a positively charged characteristic, suggesting that the developed vaccine is highly suitable for commercial production, mainly because of its excellent solubility and efficient absorption properties.52 The immune simulation provided computational insights into potential cellular immune responses. Similarly, the repeated antigen exposure triggered a strong immune response, marked by significant increases in T-cytotoxic cells. T-helper cells and immunoglobulin production after the initial injection. Additionally, sustained increases in IFN-gamma and IL-2 levels following repeated antigen exposure indicated the presence of humoral responses to the vaccine.53

In silico cloning indicated the feasibility of expressing the proposed vaccine construct in a suitable host. Antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) represents a major challenge in dengue vaccine development, whereby non-neutralizing or sub-neutralizing antibodies may facilitate viral entry into Fc receptor-bearing cells, potentially exacerbating disease severity.34 As the present study is based exclusively on computational immunoinformatics analyses, the risk of ADE cannot be directly assessed. Nevertheless, the vaccine design strategy focused on conserved epitopes and balanced T-cell responses to reduce the likelihood of serotype-specific, non-neutralizing antibody production. Comprehensive in vitro and in vivo studies will be required to evaluate neutralization potency, antibody specificity, and ADE risk before further development3634;. An in-silico immunoinformatics study can improve laboratory assays, saving time and reducing costs.51

The receptor model used for docking and MD simulation requires careful interpretation. PDB ID 2Z63 provides an experimentally resolved, high-resolution TLR4 ectodomain scaffold and has been used in several immunoinformatics-based vaccine docking workflows, supporting its utility for evaluating structural compatibility between vaccines and TLR4 in silico.54, 55, 56, 57 However, 2Z63 is a TV8 hybrid structure containing human TLR4 and hagfish VLRB-derived segments and does not represent the complete native human TLR4-MD2 receptor complex. Since MD2 is required for canonical human TLR4 ligand recognition and receptor activation, the present docking, MD, and MM/GBSA results should be interpreted as evidence of structural receptor-binding stability in silico rather than direct proof of physiological TLR4-MD2 activation. Native human TLR4-MD2 structures, such as PDB ID 3FXI, provide more complete receptor models for subsequent confirmatory validation. The COCOMAPS v2.0 interface analysis further refined the interpretation of the vaccine-TLR4 ectodomain complex by identifying the atomic contacts contributing to interface stabilization.58 The interface area of 2005.95 Å2 and the mixed polar/non-polar surface composition indicate a broad protein-protein interface stabilized by both specific polar/electrostatic contacts and apolar packing. This result is consistent with the MD-derived stability profile and the favourable MM/GBSA binding-energy estimate, while remaining within the limitations of the selected TLR4 ectodomain scaffold. Importantly, the β-defensin-2 adjuvant remains biologically relevant for TLR4-associated immune engagement; however, its precise residue-level energetic contribution to the receptor interface should be quantified in future studies using per-residue MM/GBSA decomposition or comparable residue-level energetic analysis.59

Although the computational analyses offer strong initial evidence for the immunogenic potential of the structural potential of designed CMESV, in vitro and in vivo validation remains crucial. Experimental assessment should include antigen expression profiling, T-cell proliferation assays, cytokine measurement, and animal model immunization studies to verify immunoprotection efficacy. Furthermore, the significance of cross-reactivity among flaviviruses and alphaviruses requires an accurate investigation to ensure safety and prevent immunopathological effects. Nevertheless, the current study provides a rational computational basis for prioritizing the proposed DENV-CHIKV multi-epitope vaccine construct for experimental evaluation, including antigen expression, receptor-binding assessment, immune-activation assays, and preclinical immunization studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we designed a rational multi-epitope subunit vaccine candidate targeting DENV-CHIKV co-infection using an integrated immunoinformatics and structural evaluation framework. The selected conserved epitopes from the DENV NS1 and CHIKV NSP1 proteins exhibited favourable antigenicity, acceptable safety-related profiles, and broad predicted population coverage, supporting their suitability for a dual-target vaccine design. Structural modeling, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations collectively indicated a stable and energetically favourable in silico interaction between the designed vaccine construct and the selected TLR4 ectodomain scaffold, while immune simulations suggested the potential to encourage both humoral and cellular immune responses. Despite these promising computational outcomes, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as they remain predictive. Critical aspects such as epitope processing, immunodominance, potential cross-reactivity, and risks such as antibody-dependent enhancement cannot be fully resolved in silico. Therefore, comprehensive experimental validation, including immunogenicity, safety, and efficacy assessments in vitro and in vivo, is needed. This work provides a rational computational basis for the further development of a dual-target vaccine candidate against arboviral co-infection. Future work must focus on experimental expression, antigen characterization, immunogenicity testing, and protective evaluation in appropriate in vitro and in vivo systems.
