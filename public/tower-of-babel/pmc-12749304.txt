Using synthetic data for pretraining partial discharge detection in overhead transmission lines
Authors: Lukáš Klein, Jan Fulneček, Ondřej Kabot, Jiří Dvorský, Lukáš Prokop
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate detection of partial discharges (PDs) in medium-voltage overhead transmission lines is critical for preemptive maintenance and avoiding costly outages, yet it is challenged by scarce labeled data and pervasive electromagnetic interference. This paper investigates a hybrid simulation-and-data-driven framework in which synthetically generated PD signals are used to pretrain deep neural networks and are subsequently fine-tuned on a limited set of real overhead-line measurements. The synthetic pipeline systematically varies PD repetition rates, amplitude distributions, vegetation-contact scenarios, and noise conditions, producing diverse time-series and spectrogram-like representations that approximate real operating environments. We conduct a comprehensive ablation study across multiple architectures—Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), a Vision Transformer (ViT), and a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network—and analyze their sensitivity to granular sweeps of synthetic-data parameters. CNN-based models decisively outperform ViT and LSTM counterparts on the spectrogram-based classification task, while ViT and LSTM fail to learn meaningful representation. For the successful CNNs, pretraining on carefully parameterized synthetic datasets—particularly those reflecting higher PD activity, such as our Datasets 3 and 4—consistently improves downstream performance on real data, boosting the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) on imbalanced, cost-sensitive test sets by roughly 10–20% compared with training from scratch. At the same time, we show that poorly aligned synthetic data can degrade generalization, underscoring the need for accurate noise calibration and domain-aligned simulation. Overall, the results confirm that (i) architectural choice is pivotal for PD detection in overhead lines and (ii) well-designed synthetic data is a powerful, practical lever for achieving reliable and cost-effective PD monitoring when real labeled data are limited.

Introduction

Partial discharges are localized dielectric breakdowns that manifest in high-voltage insulation whenever the local electric field surpasses a critical threshold1,2. Though each individual discharge involves only modest energy release, their collective electrical, thermal and chemical effect can erode insulation over time and precipitate catastrophic system failures3,4. To mitigate vegetation-related outages and enhance safety, medium-voltage (MV) overhead distribution systems often use cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE)-covered conductors5. While this covering prevents direct short circuits from casual contact, it does not fully eliminate the possibility of PD under adverse conditions, such as contamination or sustained contact with branches3. Furthermore, the conductor covering can partially shield the radiated electromagnetic signals from PD events and may influence the discharge characteristics themselves, posing unique challenges for detection compared to bare conductors.

Challenges in PD detection

Early and reliable detection of PD is crucial for the timely identification and mitigation of high-impedance faults, reducing the risk of unplanned outages and potential fire hazards6. Conventional galvanic techniques (i.e., sensors that clamp directly onto energized lines) can yield accurate measurements yet typically require service interruptions for installation, incurring significant operational costs7. Non-contact or “radiometric” sensing via antennas has thus been proposed as a more practical alternative, as it monitors radiated electromagnetic signals from PD events without necessitating direct conductor access6,8. However, robust detection in the field remains difficult; signals can be masked by noise, corona discharges, and other transient electromagnetic phenomena, particularly in overhead configurations with variable environmental conditions9,10 and also distance of detection range (power lines span across many kilometers). Recent deep-learning pipelines have nevertheless achieved F-scores above 0.95 on laboratory benchmarks, e.g., Yang et al.’s parallel recognition ensemble on hybrid HV lines11 and Rauscher et al.’s data-augmented CNNs for electrical machines12.

Synthetic data for PD classification

One key obstacle hindering the development of accurate, machine-learning-based PD detectors is a general scarcity of labeled data. Two open-access antenna-based datasets now provide multi-year XLPE covered-conductor measurements13,14, yet each contains only a few hundred positively-labeled PD snapshots—far below what most deep models require. Field measurements tend to be costly, time-consuming, and limited in coverage—especially for sporadic events like partial discharges in overhead distribution systems13. As a solution, synthetic data generation has garnered increasing interest for augmenting or substituting real measurements in fault diagnosis pipelines15,16. They are also widely used in other fields of deep learning17. Traditionally, researchers have adopted two broad approaches to synthetic data generation.

On one hand, physics-based simulation employs first-principles models or digital twins to reproduce the underlying discharge processes and create waveforms consistent with known electromagnetic phenomena18,19. Such methods can systematically produce large datasets covering rare or extreme conditions. Comparable physics-based strategies have demonstrated value for mechanical fault detection20,21, allowing models to learn from a range of scenarios that might be prohibitively difficult to collect experimentally. However, ensuring high-fidelity simulations of PD is challenging, given the complex stochastic behavior of discharges and the partial shielding effects introduced by conductor coverings.

On the other hand, data-driven generative models offer a more empirical route. By learning statistical patterns from available real data and then sampling from the inferred distribution, these models can produce new, realistic training examples. This family of techniques includes Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)22 and, more prominently, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)23. In partial discharge research, GAN-based augmentation has been shown to improve classification accuracy, particularly when existing labeled datasets are limited24. Indeed, cGANs–GAN variants incorporating auxiliary information like operating conditions—have proven effective in synthesizing fault data with diverse amplitudes and noise conditions. However, purely GAN-driven approaches generally demand sufficient baseline datasets (i.e., some real PD examples to learn from), which may not always be feasible for overhead transmission power lines in early-stage monitoring25.

Conditional generative adversarial networks

Among data-driven approaches, cGANs stand out for their capacity to yield high-fidelity, labeled outputs tailored to specific environmental or operational contexts26,27. This strength is especially relevant for partial discharge classification, where signal amplitudes and spectral content vary widely depending on insulation health, voltage level, contamination, and vegetation interactions. Yet cGANs typically call for extensive tuning, extensive computation, and a sufficiently robust real-data foundation to ensure stable training. In practice, overhead distribution networks often lack the large annotated datasets necessary for purely data-driven pipelines25. The result can be synthetic outputs of questionable realism or coverage gaps that undermine classifier performance in real-world conditions. Physics-based simulation, conversely, offers a path to generate diverse data even without an extensive collection of initial real examples, making it potentially more suitable when real labeled data is severely limited.

Gap and motivation

Although both physics-based simulations and cGAN-based augmentation demonstrate promise, their application to medium-voltage overhead covered conductors remains comparatively underexplored. Generating synthetic PD signals under realistic wideband noise and partial shielding conditions—while also capturing sporadic events like vegetation contact—poses significant modeling and computational challenges. Moreover, it is unclear whether specific distributions of synthetic data (e.g., emphasizing high-rate PD events or wider amplitude variability) systematically yield more robust detectors, or how best to fuse synthetic pretraining with a small pool of real measurements.

Objectives and contributions

Motivated by these gaps, the present work proposes a hybrid simulation-and-data-driven approach to generate synthetic PD datasets that reflect the unique operational realities of XLPE-covered overhead transmission power lines. Visual comparison of time-domain signals and spectrograms

A qualitative visual comparison was conducted between both the time-domain waveforms and the corresponding spectrograms generated from the synthetic data pipeline and those derived from real-world overhead transmission power line measurements.

In the time domain, the synthetic signals replicate the presence of sharp, transient pulses characteristic of partial discharge (PD) superimposed on a noise floor (compare Figs. 5 and 6). Similarly, the synthetic spectrograms exhibit structural resemblances to real data spectrograms, capturing the broadband energy bursts associated with PD events (compare Figs. 4 and 4 Real Signal - Spectrogram). Domain expert assessment, considering both representations, confirmed that the synthetic data captures relevant PD characteristics deemed suitable for the intended pretraining application.Fig. 4Spectrograms of synthetic datasets (d1–d4) and a real partial discharge signal.Fig. 5Real Signal - Time-domain representation showing amplitude variation over time.Fig. 6Time-domain representations of synthetic signals under different sample configurations.

However, specific visual differences were noted across both domains. In the time domain, the baseline noise texture and amplitude in synthetic signals may differ from the real measurements, which sometimes exhibit specific periodic interference or distinct noise floor characteristics. Real signals occasionally show broader, low-amplitude oscillations (potentially related to power line harmonics or specific noise sources like switching power supplies) not fully replicated synthetically. In the frequency domain (spectrograms), these differences manifest as variations in background texture and the potential absence of certain continuous narrowband or broadband interference components present in the real data. Furthermore, synthetic datasets designed for severe conditions (Dataset 3, Dataset 4) intentionally feature more frequent and higher-amplitude PD pulses in both time-domain and spectrogram representations compared to average real samples.

Despite these visual differences, the success of pretraining, especially with Dataset 3 and Dataset 4, suggests the models effectively learned the core discriminative features of PD. We hypothesize that the CNN, particularly when processing spectrograms, focuses on the characteristic broadband, transient energy bursts associated with PD events. These key features were successfully replicated in the synthetic data (especially their intensity and frequency variations in Datasets 3/4), making the model robust to variations in background noise texture or the absence of specific narrowband interference components during classification.

Performance comparison across architectures

The models were assessed across a comprehensive suite of synthetic pretraining configurations, with performance quantified primarily by the Receiver Operating Characteristic Area Under the Curve (ROC AUC) and further validated using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that synthetic-data pretraining can substantially enhance partial discharge (PD) detection in overhead transmission lines, albeit with strong dependence on the quality and characteristics of the synthetic datasets. The results presented in Fig. 1–3 and the accompanying statistical analyses underscore two main findings: (1) not all synthetic datasets confer the same degree of performance improvement, and (2) datasets simulating higher levels of PD activity, specifically Dataset 3 and Dataset 4 in our experiments, stand out as the most effective resources for pretraining.

The superior performance associated with Dataset 3 and Dataset 4 can likely be attributed to their simulation parameters, which were designed to reflect higher PD activity (see Table 7). Specifically, the increased PD repetition rates (1600 and 3200 pulses/window, respectively, compared to 800 in Datasets 1/2) and higher amplitude variability (Veg. PD Amp. Sigma of 1.45 and 1.85, and higher mean in Dataset 4) likely exposed the pretrained models to a richer, more challenging set of discriminative features characteristic of significant or evolving fault conditions. This better prepared the models to identify such patterns within the noise and variability of the real test data.

Interpretation of visual fidelity and pretraining effectiveness

The visual discrepancies observed between synthetic and real signals, evident in both time-domain waveforms and spectrogram representations, require careful consideration regarding their impact on pretraining. While perfect visual replication across both domains was not achieved—particularly with respect to specific ambient noise signatures (e.g., noise floor texture, periodic interference) and the deliberate exaggeration of PD activity in datasets such as Dataset 3 and Dataset 4—the significant performance improvements obtained through pretraining strongly suggest that capturing the core functional characteristics of PD signals is more important than reproducing all visual details.

Our hypothesis is that the synthetic data pipeline successfully embedded key discriminative features relevant to PD physics, recognizable in both time and frequency domains. This includes the sharp transient nature and amplitude variations of pulses (visible in time-domain plots) and their corresponding broadband spectral signatures (visible in spectrograms), together with realistic variations in repetition rates and clustering. The neural network, particularly when using a spectrogram input representation derived from these time-domain signals, appears to leverage these fundamental learned features effectively during pretraining. This, in turn, facilitates successful transfer to the real-data domain during fine-tuning, despite the apparent visual domain gaps in both representations. The notable success of Dataset 3, characterized by more intense PD activity, further supports the idea that exposing the model to a diverse range of functionally relevant signal variations—even if visually imperfect or slightly exaggerated—is highly beneficial for robust learning.

This simulation-centric approach, which focuses on embedding physically meaningful characteristics, contrasts with generative approaches such as conditional GANs (cGANs) that often prioritize closer visual resemblance in the target domain25. In our own experiment with a cGAN trained on only 1,020 labeled real samples, the model was unstable and failed to generate high-fidelity samples; we attribute this behaviour to the acute scarcity of real data, which increases the risk of mode collapse and the appearance of unrealistic artifacts. By comparison, several studies reporting strong cGAN-based gains (e.g., MCC improvements from 0.701 to 0.795) rely on substantially larger and more diverse real datasets (often on the order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>10{,}000$$\end{document} samples) to learn a reliable data distribution12. Our setting is closer to the typical industrial scenario, where only a limited number of labeled real measurements is available (in our case, approximately 1,700 samples for fine-tuning), and under such conditions the physics-informed simulation route proves to be more stable and more controllable than purely data-driven generation.

Taken together, these findings highlight an important implication for simulation-based pretraining: achieving perfect visual fidelity across all signal characteristics (including precise background noise replication) may be less critical than accurately capturing the time–frequency signature of PD pulses—their location, bandwidth, intensity, and repetition patterns. A simulation framework grounded in first principles and domain expertise is not primarily constrained by the size of an existing real dataset and can systematically generate a wide spectrum of plausible PD scenarios. This makes it a practical and robust pathway for pretraining in data-scarce environments, such as overhead transmission power line PD monitoring, while still remaining compatible with GAN-based augmentation in regimes where ample real data is available.

Impact of suboptimal pretraining

Although synthetic pretraining is conceptually appealing for mitigating data scarcity, the results caution that it can be detrimental if the synthetic data fail to mirror key real-world characteristics. Our experiments show that models pretrained on certain datasets (Dataset 1, Dataset 2, All Datasets Combined) do not always outperform models trained de novo on limited real data (No Pretraining). In fact, Dunn’s post-hoc comparisons indicate that these synthetic datasets sometimes lead to statistically lower MCC than no pretraining at all. This observation implies that ineffective synthetic data may inadvertently induce biased or misleading feature representations, contrasting sharply with the benefits seen from the more intense PD simulations in Datasets 3 and 4.

From an operational standpoint, these findings underscore the importance of investing resources into the design or curation of synthetic datasets before field deployment. Rather than relying on a generic or off-the-shelf approach to synthetic PD waveforms, stakeholders should tailor the data-generation methodology to the specific conductor types, expected contamination levels, and vegetation interactions pertinent to the target environment. Such efforts can substantially reduce the risk of negative transfer, potentially prioritizing simulations that capture challenging or high-activity conditions.

Architectural considerations for spectrogram-based PD detection

The results of the architectural comparison indicate a clear preference for convolutional models in the context of spectrogram-based partial-discharge (PD) classification. The three CNN-based architectures consistently outperformed the recurrent and transformer-based variants. This outcome suggests that the most discriminative information for PD detection is encoded in the two-dimensional time—frequency structure of the spectrograms.

Convolutional networks are inherently well aligned with this representation: their localized receptive fields and hierarchical feature extraction enable them to identify characteristic PD signatures, such as short broadband energy bursts, their orientation, and their relative positioning within the spectrogram. In practical terms, convolutional filters act as learnable detectors of these time–frequency patterns and can robustly separate PD-related activity from background interference.

By contrast, the LSTM model processes the spectrogram as a temporal sequence of frequency vectors. Such a one-dimensional treatment is likely to discard or underutilize spatial correlations across the frequency axis, which appear to be essential for this task. The Vision Transformer model, while competitive in large-scale vision tasks, typically requires substantial pretraining (e.g. on ImageNet-scale corpora) to learn effective patch-level representations. Under the comparatively limited data conditions of this work (combining synthetic and real signals), the CNN backbone—notably the ResNet50V2 variant employed in the SOTA-Inspired configuration—benefits from strong inductive biases (locality, translation invariance) that make it more data-efficient and better matched to image-like inputs.

Refining the strategy for synthetic data generation

The granular ablation of synthetic-data parameters further refines the data-generation strategy. Slight but consistent improvements in MCC for higher vegetation-induced PD amplitudes (higher Veg Mean, cf. Fig. 8) indicate that pretraining on scenarios with more pronounced PD signatures is beneficial. This is in agreement with earlier experiments using synthetic datasets that emphasized higher PD activity: making the target phenomenon more salient during pretraining improves the model’s ability to recognize it under real, potentially noisier, conditions.

An equally important observation is the robustness of the top CNN architectures to variations in synthetic noise level (Fig. 8). Once a CNN has learned the core PD signature, moderate changes in the background noise do not substantially degrade performance. This suggests the following design guideline for synthetic-data pipelines: while a realistic noise model remains desirable, the fidelity, variability, and frequency of the target PD events in the synthetic data are more critical than an exact reproduction of every noise component present in the field.

Practical implications and FP/FN trade-offs

From an operational standpoint, the ROC AUC results are particularly relevant. High AUC values (exceeding 0.9 for the SOTA-Inspired architecture) imply that the classifier maintains good separation between PD and non-PD classes over a broad range of decision thresholds. This flexibility allows system operators to select a threshold that prioritizes either high sensitivity (low FN rate) or high specificity (low FP rate), depending on the maintenance policy and cost structure.

In power-distribution applications, false negatives are typically more costly, as undetected PD activity may lead to accelerated insulation degradation, unplanned outages, or safety risks. False positives, while undesirable, usually result only in additional inspection costs. A model with high ROC AUC thus enables a more favorable FP/FN operating point and supports economically viable predictive-maintenance workflows. Conversely, the inability of the LSTM and ViT models to achieve meaningful AUC scores indicates that these architectures, in the present setting, do not provide the level of discriminative control required for deployment in the field.

Limitations and future research directions

Residual synthetic–real domain gap

Despite the promising results, a persistent limitation is the domain gap between synthetic and real-world PD signatures. Even though Dataset 3 and Dataset 4 reduce this discrepancy, current synthetic pipelines still simplify several non-ideal, site-specific, and time-varying phenomena. Our generator already models multiple interference classes—colored and impulsive noise, power-line harmonics, and narrowband RF carriers—to approximate the electromagnetic environment of medium-voltage overhead lines, but real deployments often exhibit structured, nonstationary components from power-electronic equipment, periodic or stochastic switching events, harmonic distortion, and local electromagnetic coupling. Environmental effects (severe weather, multi-source corona, vegetative canopies) are likewise only partially represented.

Richer noise and interference modeling

Future versions of the synthetic generator should therefore incorporate more expressive disturbance models. Concretely, this includes: (i) stochastic switching events with variable rise times, durations, and repetition patterns to emulate power-electronic and switching surges; (ii) clustered harmonics and amplitude- or frequency-modulated carriers to mimic coupled RF and power-electronic activity from nearby infrastructure; and (iii) adaptive, site-derived noise profiles learned from measured background spectra so that diurnal load changes, weather-induced coupling, or maintenance operations are reflected in the synthetic signals. These additions would allow the synthetic dataset to better approximate the spectral texture and temporal variability of real antenna- or SDR-based PD measurements, further narrowing the synthetic–real gap.

Line- and asset-specific simulation and advanced domain adaptation

Another direction is to make the simulation step more line- and asset-aware. Even within overhead distribution systems, conductor geometry, phase spacing, grounding layout, and proximity to other infrastructure affect propagation, attenuation, and interference patterns. Encoding these features into line-specific models would yield more faithful PD waveforms and reduce the burden on downstream adaptation.

Furthermore, while our two-phase pretraining-and-fine-tuning workflow effectively transfers knowledge, performance could be further enhanced by incorporating more explicit domain adaptation techniques. A promising extension would be to introduce a lightweight domain-adversarial fine-tuning step following the initial pretraining. In such a setup, a domain classifier is trained to distinguish between features extracted from synthetic and real data, while the main feature extractor is trained to “fool” this classifier. This encourages the model to learn domain-invariant representations that are robust to the subtle differences between the two data sources28,29. Similarly, contrastive domain-mapping methods could be employed to pull feature representations of the same class (e.g., “PD present”) from both synthetic and real domains closer together in the embedding space30,31. Integrating these advanced adaptation strategies represents a compelling avenue for future research, potentially offering further performance gains by more explicitly and systematically closing the synthetic-real gap.

Topology-agnostic extension of the framework

Although the present study focuses on covered conductors, the proposed hybrid synthetic-data pretraining strategy is largely topology-agnostic. PD monitoring is routinely applied to power transformers, XLPE cables, rotating machines32, and GIS installations33. The same pretraining–fine-tuning workflow can be transferred to these assets by adjusting a limited set of parameters—discharge amplitude distributions, propagation and reflection coefficients, expected interference profiles, and background noise models—to match the target environment (including bare conductors or underground cables).

Benchmarking, deployment, and model drift

Progress in the community will benefit from structured benchmarks that combine synthetic and real measurements from diverse overhead-line configurations, enabling reproducible and comparable evaluations across modeling approaches. Our results already indicate that the achieved inference latency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}12.3 ms) is compatible with real-time stream processing, but long-term field deployment introduces model drift due to seasonal changes, conductor aging, or emerging interference sources. This motivates adaptive or continuous learning schemes—periodic fine-tuning on newly collected field data, semi-supervised exploitation of unlabeled streams, or online domain adaptation—to preserve accuracy over time. A practical path is to train a centrally maintained foundation model on a large, diversified synthetic corpus and to fine-tune it with small, site-specific datasets for rapid rollout. To increase operator trust and ease integration into SCADA or asset-management systems, future work should also integrate explainability (e.g., Grad-CAM on spectrogram inputs34) so that alarms can be traced back to the responsible time–frequency structures.

Computational cost and practical applicability

To assess the real-world feasibility of our proposed pipeline, we evaluated the computational costs associated with both synthetic data generation and model training.

The synthetic data generation process was performed on a multi-core CPU environment using 32 threads of an Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU E5-2630 v4 processor. The generation of 1,000 synthetic samples took approximately 96 minutes. It is important to note that this CPU-based generation python script was not performance-optimized; significant acceleration could be achieved through code vectorization or by porting the process to a GPU. As data generation is a one-time, offline task, this cost is considered manageable for creating large-scale pretraining datasets.

The model training and inference phases were conducted on a single, consumer-grade NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1070 GPU, demonstrating that the approach does not require high-end computational resources. The training process was highly efficient. On average, the synthetic pretraining phase for 1000 samples completed in approximately 117 seconds (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx 2$$\end{document} minutes). The subsequent two-phase fine-tuning on real data required an average of 144 seconds for head-only training and 353 seconds for full-model fine-tuning, culminating in a total fine-tuning time of under 9 minutes per run. The total training time for a single model, including pretraining and fine-tuning, was therefore just over 10 minutes.

For deployment, the inference speed is critical. Our trained models exhibited a mean latency of approximately 12.3 ms per spectrogram. This high throughput confirms the suitability of the models for near-real-time monitoring applications. Overall, the low training overhead and fast inference speeds underscore the practical applicability and cost-effectiveness of our synthetic pretraining framework for real-world PD detection systems.

The practical relevance of these performance improvements goes beyond achieving better statistical indicators. In routine grid maintenance, the consequences of misclassification are strongly asymmetric. A false negative (FN)—i.e., failure to detect an actual partial discharge—may precipitate severe equipment damage, unplanned outages, and elevated safety risks, all of which translate into substantial financial and reputational losses. By contrast, a false positive (FP)—i.e., incorrectly flagging a healthy asset—typically leads only to an unnecessary inspection, which is comparatively less costly, though still operationally undesirable.

In this setting, the higher Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) obtained by models pretrained on Dataset 3 and Dataset 4 is particularly meaningful, because MCC offers a balanced assessment of classifier performance under class imbalance and unequal error costs. A model that simultaneously suppresses FNs and limits FPs improves the reliability of discharge detection and, at the same time, lowers the expected operational cost. Consequently, such models support more efficient and economically sustainable predictive-maintenance policies for overhead distribution networks.

Concluding remarks

In summary, our results confirm that the careful design and use of synthetic data can be a powerful tool for overcoming data limitations in PD detection for overhead transmission lines. While not all synthetic datasets yield equally positive outcomes, the marked success of Dataset 3 and Dataset 4 underscores the pivotal role of aligning simulated conditions with real operational complexities. By strategically integrating synthetic pretraining and selective fine-tuning, utilities and researchers can achieve heightened classification accuracy, faster model convergence, and reduced costs for data acquisition and annotation. These findings pave the way toward broader adoption of data-driven PD monitoring systems, improving insulation health assessment, reducing the risk of service interruptions, and supporting more robust grid operation in the long term.

Methods

Data acquisition and real dataset

We employed a real-world, hourly sampled dataset introduced in13, collected using contactless antenna-based data acquisition systems installed on medium-voltage overhead transmission power lines. Each measurement was a 20 ms snapshot (one full mains cycle) sampled at 40 MS/s, yielding 800000 samples per snapshot6,7. The snapshot acquisition was repeated at hourly intervals. Expert-curated ground-truth labels (fault vs. no fault) were provided by an auxiliary galvanic contact sensor13, ensuring reliable annotations for model training and evaluation. The dataset is publicly available under:13.

To develop and assess our models, we partitioned the real dataset into three subsets: 60% for training, 20% for validation, and the remaining 20% for testing. This split preserved ample data for both model development and unbiased performance estimation on held-out samples.

To complement and augment the real data, we generated synthetic wideband signals resembling on-site measurements. These signals incorporated partial discharge (PD) events, corona activity, vegetation contact phenomena, and various noise and interference sources. The goal was to produce realistic time-domain waveforms that captured the complex electromagnetic environment observed in real overhead power lines. We modeled both partial discharge (PD) pulses and corona discharges as damped sinusoids. Corona discharge appeared as continuous, weaker pulses whose amplitude typically followed the line voltage cycle (50 Hz or 60 Hz). In the simulation, many low-amplitude corona pulses were generated over time, with statistical variability in repetition rate, amplitude, and damping parameters that depended on environmental factors such as humidity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta$$\end{document}) and altitude.

Partial discharges in high-voltage networks appeared as broadband, short-lived transients. We used the same damped sinusoid form in (5) but with distinct parameter ranges (e.g., higher damping rates and broader frequency content). The amplitude and time of occurrence were drawn from empirically motivated or log-normal distributions, reflecting the variability found in real-world PD signals.

Noise and interference

To replicate the electromagnetic interference environment around overhead transmission power lines, various noise sources were incorporated. One major source was power-line interference, which consisted of sinusoidal components at the fundamental line frequency (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_1 \approx 50\,\textrm{Hz}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$60\,\textrm{Hz}$$\end{document}) and its harmonics. Another important component was colored noise, characterized by a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1/f^\alpha$$\end{document} spectral profile to account for the stronger low-frequency components commonly observed in practical measurements. A sparsity model was introduced to emulate intermittent transmissions, adding another layer of complexity to the simulated interference environment.

Composite channel and data generation

The total transfer function for the line and antenna was given by\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} H_{\textrm{total}}(f) \;=\; H_{\textrm{line}}(f)\,H_{\textrm{ant}}(f). \end{aligned}$$\end{document}After summing all pulses, noise, and interference in the time domain, we transformed the signal to the frequency domain via an FFT, multiplied by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_{\textrm{total}}(f)$$\end{document}, and then applied the inverse FFT to simulate channel effects. Evaluation metric

For binary detection tasks such as identifying vegetation contact, we adopted the MCC to provide a balanced performance measure:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{MCC} \;=\; \frac{(TP \times TN) - (FP \times FN)}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)\,(TP+FN)\,(TN+FP)\,(TN+FN)}}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TP$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$TN$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FP$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FN$$\end{document} denoted true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. The MCC was particularly useful for imbalanced datasets, offering a single-value metric that captured all four confusion-matrix components.

Following our previous work35, we employed a well-established and reliable methodology for the classification of partial discharges. Specifically, we utilized a spectrogram-based classification approach leveraging a two-dimensional convolutional neural network (2D-CNN) with a ResNet50V2 backbone36. The classification heads differed primarily in how they processed extracted features. Inspired by state-of-the-art designs, the SOTA-Inspired head employed a deeper architecture, beginning with global average pooling, followed by batch normalization and two fully connected layers (1024 and 512 units) with L2 regularization, interleaved with dropout layers for improved stability.

To dynamically adjust the learning rate during training, a ReduceLROnPlateau learning rate scheduler was employed. Evaluation protocol

To ensure a rigorous assessment, model performance was evaluated using a dedicated real-world test set. The evaluation followed a structured approach that included several key strategies. First, we analyzed the confusion matrix, recording the counts of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN) for each model. This allowed us to gain insights into the types of errors made and their impact on overall performance. Additionally, we computed the MCC, a robust metric particularly suited for imbalanced binary classification tasks, providing a more reliable measure of predictive quality than simple accuracy.

Beyond these core metrics, we conducted a thorough sensitivity analysis to examine how various factors influenced model performance. Specifically, we investigated the impact of different synthetic dataset configurations, variations in classification head architectures, and the inclusion or exclusion of synthetic pretraining.

To ensure the validity of our measurements, we performed at least 31 random cross-validations with different seeds. This helped mitigate the impact of randomness in data splits and provided a more reliable estimate of model performance. By integrating these evaluation strategies, we ensured a comprehensive understanding of the proposed models’ robustness and generalization capabilities across diverse experimental conditions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the significant potential of synthetic-data pretraining to enhance the accuracy and robustness of deep neural networks for partial discharge (PD) detection in medium-voltage overhead transmission power lines. By carefully crafting simulation pipelines that incorporate realistic PD rates, amplitude variability, vegetation contacts, reflection artifacts, and wideband electromagnetic noise, we created multiple synthetic datasets with varying degrees of fidelity. Our empirical analysis reveals that pretraining on a well-balanced and representative synthetic dataset yields statistically significant gains in MCC, F\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_1$$\end{document} score, and accuracy upon subsequent fine-tuning with real-world measurements.

In particular, the dataset with higher PD repetition rates and broader amplitude distributions produced the most consistent improvements, underscoring the need to mirror actual overhead-line conditions as closely as possible. We also observed that pretraining on inadequately parameterized synthetic data can be detrimental, reinforcing the necessity for domain alignment and rigorous simulation design. From a practical standpoint, these findings highlight a path toward reducing field data requirements, lowering operational expenses, and strengthening PD detection pipelines for proactive grid maintenance.

Our comparative analysis unequivocally shows that CNN-based architectures are significantly more effective for this task than LSTM or Vision Transformer models when using spectrogram inputs, highlighting the primacy of spatial feature extraction for identifying PD time-frequency signatures. Among the synthetic data configurations, those simulating more intense PD activity—characterized by higher repetition rates and amplitudes—consistently produced the best results, better preparing models for real-world conditions. We also found that top-performing models are robust to varying levels of synthetic noise, suggesting that fidelity in modeling the PD signal itself is paramount.

Future work should focus on extending the proposed framework in two key directions. First, sophisticated domain adaptation techniques, such as domain-adversarial fine-tuning or contrastive mapping, should be investigated to more explicitly align the feature spaces of synthetic and real signals, especially under evolving environmental or loading conditions. Second, developing enhanced generative models capable of simulating extreme events, such as severe weather patterns, augmented vegetation encroachment, or heavy rime formation—would bolster real-world readiness.

Addressing these avenues will ultimately facilitate more reliable and scalable PD monitoring solutions, helping utilities and operators safeguard overhead transmission networks against unexpected faults and service interruptions.
