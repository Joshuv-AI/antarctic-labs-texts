Navigating luminal heterogeneity: etiology-based proteogenomic subtyping for targeted treatment strategies in breast cancer
Authors: Ya-Hsuan Chang, Yi-Ju Chen, Zhi-Jie Hong, Yi-Jing Hsiao, Kuen-Tyng Lin, Guo-Shiou Liao, Sheng-Fang Su, Ze-Shiang Lin, Huei-Wen Chen, Chia-Li Han, Eric Sheng-Wen Chen, Yin-Chen Hsu, Yan-Ming Chen, Hao Fang, Hao-Chin Yang, Yan-Si Chen, Chien-Yu Lin, Hsiang-En Hsu, Ching-Hung Lin, Pin-Lian Jiang, Pei-Shan Wu, Ching-Wen Chen, Chen-Ting Hung, Ethan Wu, Wei-Tzu Chiu, Fan-Ni Hsing, Tsai-Pei Liu, Chia-Yu Wang, Yu-Tai Wang, Chang-Wei Yeh, Ki-Hok Liao, Ana I Robles, Henry Rodriguez, Show-Ling Yang, Mien-Chie Hung, Yen-Shen Lu, Hsuan-Yu Chen, Sung-Liang Yu, Jyh-Cherng Yu, Yu-Ju Chen

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLuminal breast cancer is rising rapidly among East Asian women, particularly younger patients, with variable clinical outcome, yet current risk-stratification models inadequately predict early recurrence of under-studied young-onset cases. We hypothesize that the interplay between endogenous mutagenic processes and overlooked environmental carcinogen exposure drives molecular diversity, revealing novel etiologic and therapeutic insights.MethodsWe performed integrative proteogenomic profiling of 164 prospective, treatment-naïve early-stage Taiwanese breast cancer patients using whole-exome sequencing, transcriptomics, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics. Functional validation in luminal models confirmed therapeutic vulnerabilities and biomarkers, with an independent cohort (n = 270) used to stratify high-risk recurrence patients.ResultsOur integrative proteogenomic analysis revealed distinct molecular etiologies and actionable vulnerabilities. For the first time, mutation signature analysis revealed both environmental carcinogen exposure (DBAC) and endogenous APOBEC mutagenesis as key contributors to poor disease-free survival, particularly in younger patients. A high-mutation-burden, immune-evasive subgroup revealed immunoepigenetic vulnerabilities. DBAC-driven tumors exhibited overexpression of ROS-detoxifying enzymes, DNA-damage checkpoint activation, and suppressed DNA repair pathways, supporting an environmental–genomic cooperative mechanism. APOBEC-associated tumors exhibited upregulation of APOBEC3B/3F/3G, steroid hormone biosynthesis enzymes, and downstream oncogenic signaling, forming an immunotherapy-responsive subtype. Proteomic classification further resolved luminal heterogeneity beyond PAM50, identifying two clinically relevant groups: (1) a young DBAC-proteome subset with suppressed DNA-repair machinery and favorable chemotherapy response; and (2) a recurrence-prone subtype characterized by co-activating ER, PI3K–AKT–mTOR, and CDK4/6/9 signaling. Functional validation demonstrated that selective CDK9 inhibition targeting the p-POLR2A-Ser2 axis significantly outperformed CDK4/6 blockade. Furthermore, a companion panel (HDAC2, ALDH1L2, ARF4, SCAMP3) stratified high-risk recurrence patients in an independent cohort, supporting biomarker-guided therapy for aggressive luminal breast cancer.ConclusionsThis study uncovers environmental mutagenesis as a previously overlooked but critical driver of luminal breast cancer heterogeneity in East Asian patients. We establish a proteogenomics-transformative scheme to guide risk stratification and subtype-specific vulnerabilities, offering a precision oncology strategy for early-stage East Asian breast cancer management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-026-02701-x.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women globally [1]. Notably, the incidence of invasive BC, particularly in younger patients in Asia and some other regions, has witnessed a rapid and unexplained increase over the last three decades [2]. High heterogeneity and variable subtype-driven therapeutic outcomes present challenges for BC precision medicine. The tumor biology for BC in younger patients is significantly under-studied, partly because postmenopausal BC accounts for the majority of BC in Western countries [3]. When compared to Caucasian cohorts, BC in East Asia has a higher proportion of younger patients, with a peak incidence of 50–55 years, and a higher proportion of luminal subtypes in younger women [2, 4, 5]. Early-onset BC exhibits distinct clinico-pathological characteristics compared to postmenopausal BC. For example, younger luminal BC has higher risk of recurrence rate after conventional standard treatment with curative intent, yet current risk stratification method does not apply to young age luminal early breast cancer [6]. While gene expression signatures offer precise guidance for the use of adjuvant chemotherapy in luminal disease patients aged 50 and above, their effectiveness failed when applied to younger patients [7, 8]. Whether distinct mutational processes confer age-specific vulnerabilities and influence the oncogenic pathways in breast cancer remains poorly understood. Fundamentally, our evolving understanding of population-specific epidemiology and environmental carcinogenesis, along with their complex interplay, may provide clues to their critical role on cancer initiation and biology.

Previous genomics studies have established the mutational landscape and gene expression profiles that have shed light on the intrinsic molecular characteristics of BC [9, 10] advancing our understanding of genome evolution and identifying actionable targets. Prior BC proteomic [11, 12] and integrated proteogenomic [13–17] profiling studies have explored the molecular architecture of subtype classifications, tumor immunogenicity, and targetable pathways. However, the majority of key findings have focused on triple negative and HER2 + diseases. Beyond genomic studies, luminal disease is significantly less studied. Despite the use of a wide range of clinical, pathological and molecular approaches for patient care, relatively little is understood about mechanisms that contribute to the high heterogeneity observed in this patient subgroup. Notably, luminal disease accounts for more than 60% of BC, and with the advancement of multiple anti-HER targeted therapies, luminal disease no longer has a survival benefit over HER2 disease. Furthermore, traditional endocrine therapy does not sufficiently benefit all luminal patients [18]. Treatment regimens that include cyclin-dependent kinases CDK4/6 inhibitors significantly increase recurrence-free survival for hormone receptor (HR)+/HER2-early BC. However, primary resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors occurs in about 15–30% of patients, and nearly all initially responsive patients eventually develop acquired resistance leading to mortality; prediction biomarkers for resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors are still missing [19–22]. Due to the current paucity of biomarkers to predict success, as well as the lack of viable treatments for CDK-inhibitor resistant patients, major challenges to the development of long-term therapies persist.

In this study, we conducted a multi-omic profiling, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics of early-stage breast tumors and normal adjacent tissues (NAT) from a prospective East Asian cohort enriched for younger premenopausal, luminal-subtype patients. To our knowledge, this work provides the first proteogenomic evidence linking environmental carcinogen exposure (DBAC, a PAH compound) with endogenous APOBEC mutagenesis, both significantly associated with poor disease-free survival in younger patients. Through proteotyping, we established a novel luminal subtyping framework that resolves underappreciated heterogeneity beyond PAM50 and stratifies patients by etiology, therapeutic response, and recurrence risk. Specifically, we identified: (1) a young subtype with DBAC-associated DNA repair deficiency and favorable chemotherapy response; and (2) a recurrence-prone luminal subtype with co-activation of ER, PI3K–AKT–mTOR, and transcriptional CDK signaling. Functional assays further validated the superior efficacy of targeting CDK9–p-POLR2A-Ser2 axis as a selective and potent drug target that outperformed CDK4/6 blockade. Finally, companion biomarker candidates (HDAC2, ALDH1L2, ARF4, SCAMP3) enabled stratification of high-risk recurrence patients in an independent validation cohort. These findings show the translational value of proteogenomics-informed classification to guide prognosis, patient stratification, and targeted treatment strategies, and guiding tailored treatment strategies in East Asian populations in overcoming the limitations in standard care.

Results

A prospectively-collected cohort of 140 treatment-naive Taiwanese patient biospecimens was characterized proteogenomically to study the early-stage, younger, and luminal subtype-predominant in East-Asian BC. They comprised 27.9% luminal A, 40.0% luminal B1, 11.4% luminal B2, 9.3% HER2 (ER-, HER2+), and 11.4% triple negative (TNBC) disease according to pathology, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and HER2 fluorescence in situ hybridization. Their clinicopathological characteristics are summarized in Table S1A-B. All tumors were additionally classified by PAM50 subtyping using the PCA-PAM50 method [23, 24], which mitigates cohort-specific bias. This approach resulted in 60% agreement amongst clinical subtypes, with Luminal A demonstrating the highest concordance (84%)(Table S1C). This cohort (henceforth TW) (median age = 55.1 years) is distinct from the older and geographically non-Asian cohort in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, median age = 61.5 years) [25]. Furthermore, the TW ER+/PR+ subgroup was significantly younger than the average age of 55 (p = 0.006), whereas ER-/PR- patients were younger in TCGA (Figure S1A). We performed whole exome sequencing (WES), RNA-seq, proteomic, and phosphoproteomic characterization on tumor and matched normal-adjacent tumor (NAT) tissues. A total of 29,396 nonsynonymous somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs, Table S1D) were identified. At the RNA level, a total of 25,200 genes were quantified (Table S1E). Proteomics and phosphoproteomics were performed by tandem mass tag (TMT)-based 10-plex labeling [26] with coverage of 14,695 proteins and 48,599 phosphosites (FDR < 0.05, Table S1F-G). Unsupervised molecular classification of the patients was performed by their multi-omic profile and the corresponding enriched features in each group were detailed in the following sections (Figure S1B).

Distinct mutational landscape reveal endogenous and environmental carcinogen-induced mutagenesis associated with poor survival and germline risk

We first characterized the somatic mutation profiles of the TW cohort based on the Cancer Gene Census (COSMIC) and compared them with the TCGA cohort (Fig. 1A and Table S1H). While the most frequently mutated genes were common (PIK3CA at 29% and TP53 at 24%), the TW cohort harbored significantly higher mutation frequencies in KMT2C (20%), GATA3 (14%), FAT1 (10%), and FAT4 (Fig. 1A). Besides, in the TW cohort, tumors from older patients had higher PIK3CA mutation frequency compared to younger patients (Figure S1C), consistent with previous reports of increased PIK3CA mutations with age in BC patients [27]. Mutation burdens and specific driver frequencies varied significantly by PAM50 subtype. The TW HER2 + subtype exhibited the highest number of somatic mutations and tumor mutation burden (TMB) (Fig. 1A, Figure S1D). The highest TP53 mutation frequency was observed in the HER2 + subgroup (p < 0.001), consistent with higher protein expression compared to other PAM50 subtypes (Figure S1E). However, a striking population disparity was observed in TP53 mutations within specific subtypes. Although TP53 mutation rates in HER2 + tumors were comparable (TW: 62.5% vs. TCGA: 67.6%), its frequency in the basal/TNBC subtype was drastically lower in the TW cohort (28%) compared to TCGA (73.1%) (p = 0.002). Somatic copy number variation (CNV) landscapes were largely concordant between cohorts, with shared canonical driver amplifications at 11q13.3 (CCND1), 17q12 (ERBB2), and 20q13.2 (Figure S1F). Notable differences included a distinct 8p11.22 amplification in our cohort compared to the 8q24.21 (MYC) peak in TCGA. However, it is noted that the visual variations in G-Score amplitude and peak broadness between cohorts are likely influenced by technical factors, including platform resolution (WES versus SNP arrays) and differences in sample size and statistical power.

Interestingly, the mutational spectrum of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) also revealed population-specific signatures. While C > T transitions dominate in both cohorts, T > G transversions were second most frequent in the TW cohort but least frequent in TCGA, where C > A transitions ranked second (Fig. 1B). Notably, T > G transversions have been associated with exposure to polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), whereas C > A transitions are more commonly linked to tobacco-related mutagenesis [28]. This divergence suggests distinct environmental or etiological exposures shaping the mutational landscape of breast cancer in the TW cohort compared to TCGA. Thus, to explore the potential contribution of endogenous factors and environmental carcinogen to the oncogenesis of BC, non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) based on 96 types of nucleotide changes within trinucleotide motifs was performed to identify mutational signatures in TW and TCGA cohorts (Fig. 1C and Table S1I), followed by cosine similarity analysis against mutational signatures in human cancer [29–31] and environmental agents in vitro [28]. Based on highest cosine similarity, the mutational signatures best matching to those in the TW cohort were related to two endogenous sources, APOBEC cytidine deaminase (S1) and spontaneous deamination of 5-methylcytosine (S3), as well as one exogenous source from DBAC (S2) (Fig. 1C and Table S1I). Notably, the DBAC mutational signature was uniquely detected in the TW cohort. In contrast, the mutational signatures in the TCGA cohort were entirely attributed to endogenous processes. These differences likely reflect regional differences in environmental exposures and the relative contributions of distinct mutational mechanisms (Table S1I).

Based on the contributions of the three mutational signatures, tumors were classified into 10 clusters by hierarchical clustering [32] and consolidated into four genome groups (Fig. 1D). The largest group, G1, defined by a DBAC-like signature, provides the first genomic evidence linking polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) exposure to breast cancer in East Asia [33]. In contrast, G2 and G3 were dominated by endogenous processes, APOBEC-associated mutagenesis and 5-methylcytosine deamination signatures, respectively, while G4 exhibited mixed exogenous and endogenous signatures. To assess how genomic architecture relates to carcinogen-associated groups, we analyzed copy number alterations (CNAs) across genomic subtypes. Integration with RNA-seq data confirmed a strong cis-dosage effect, where DNA gains correspond to increased gene expression (Figure S1G). Notably, a co-amplified cluster of 53 genes at 1q21 (e.g., MCL1, PSMB4, SETDB1) was enriched in the G2 (APOBEC) and G4 (mixed) groups (p = 0.04), suggesting subgroup-specific CNA drivers.

Clinically, these genome groups were associated with patient phenotypes. The G1 exhibited the youngest age of onset, particularly among LumB (p = 0.040) and HER2 (p = 0.044) patients (Fig. 1E). Notably, patients in the DBAC-associated G1 subgroup had a significantly higher carrier frequency (34%) of rare germline mutations (allele frequency < 1%) in DNA repair genes (BRCA1/2, EP300, BRIP1, and FANCA). The G1 subgroup was also uniquely characterized by individual patients concurrently harboring multiple rare variants (up to three variants per patient). In contrast, carriers in G2-G4 subgroups carried at most a single rare variant, highlighting a distinct enrichment of rare variant burden specifically in G1 (p = 0.009, Fig. 1F). In addition, elevated allele frequencies of polymorphisms in DNA repair genes (MSH3, RAD23B, and RAD54B; p < 0.05), and mutations in carcinogen-metabolizing genes (ALDH2, NAT2, CYP1A1, UGT1A1, and CYP1B1; Figure S1H), further support enhanced susceptibility to environmental carcinogens. This combined metabolic–repair deficiency was more pronounced in younger patients (≤ 55 years; p = 0.031, Figure S1I). Importantly, the DBAC-enriched G1-3 and APOBEC-driven G2-1 subgroups were associated with significantly shorter disease-free survival (p = 0.002, Fig. 1G), suggesting that distinct mutational processes contribute to adverse clinical outcomes. Together, these findings support an etiology-based molecular taxonomy of breast cancer and highlight gene-environment interactions that may shape tumor evolution and prognosis in East Asian patients.

In summary, the analysis of tumor mutational signatures points to an environmental carcinogen, DBAC, as well as endogenous mutagenesis and germline risk variants as contributors to genomic profiles that can be used for stratification of BC patient outcome.

Dissecting age-linked divergent oncogenesis shaped by exogenous and endogenous mutagenesis

To evaluate the functional effect of exogenous and endogenous mutagens, differentially expressed RNAs or proteins (log2T/N values) across genome groups were analyzed. Pathway enrichment and activation scores were assessed using KEGG and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to identify affected molecular pathways and biological processes. Among these groups, G1 (exogenous) tumors exhibited the most pronounced differences at the RNA level, with 31 significantly enriched pathways, including metabolism, replication and repair, cellular processes, immune response, and signaling, compared to other genomic subtypes (p < 0.05; Figure S2A). Age stratification further revealed that younger patients (≤ 55 years) in G1 and G2 displayed enrichment of pathways related to cellular function, immune regulation, and cancer signaling, yet showed lower activation of replication and DNA repair pathways relative to older patients. These observations suggest that long-term carcinogen exposure may drive age-dependent functional consequences. To delineate the biological impact of mutational processes, we summarized RNA and protein-based enrichment of carcinogenesis and oncogenesis pathways in G1 DBAC-like (exogenous) and G2 APOBEC (endogenous) tumors (Fig. 2A and Table S2A), revealing distinct mechanisms that may drive early-stage breast cancer progression.

Patients with G1-DBAC-like signature, compared to non-DBAC cases, exhibit significant enrichment of chemical carcinogenesis-related pathways (Fig. 2A, right; Figure S2B and Tables S2A-B). Notably, key cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP1A1, CYP1B1, CYP2A6, CYP2B7P) show significantly upregulated expression in DBAC-enriched tumors with more profound elevation in older patients, supporting their role in the metabolic activation of environmental carcinogens such as DBAC into reactive intermediates (e.g., dihydrodiol) that form DNA adducts [34]. Regulatory factors of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) metabolism, including AHR and ARNT, are consistently upregulated in NATs regardless of age (Fig. 2A, top right). In tumors, overexpression of ROS-detoxifying enzymes (GPXs, SODs, CAT) and activation of the G2/M DNA damage checkpoint pathway further support oxidative DNA damage as a key carcinogenic mechanism in this group (Fig. 2A, top right, Figures S2B). Additionally, signaling components implicated in chemical carcinogenesis and cancer progression (GRB2, CREB1, MAPK1) were enriched in G1-DBAC tumors, including pathways related to reactive oxygen species (hsa05208) and receptor signaling (hsa05207) (Fig. 2A, bottom right).

In contrast, G2-APOBEC patients, particularly younger patients, are enriched for steroid hormone biosynthesis and multiple oncogenic signaling pathways (Fig. 2A, left). This group is characterized by elevated expression of APOBEC family enzymes (APOBEC3B, F, and G), which are known to induce endogenous mutagenesis and regulate estrogen receptor signaling, a feature associated with poor prognosis in ER+ breast cancer [35]. Within the G2-APOBEC group, we observed concomitant overexpression of APOBEC enzymes (APOBEC3B, F, and G), and components in steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway and downstream BC oncogenesis pathways (Fig. 2A, left; Figure S2B). Particularly, the younger G2-APOBEC patients exhibited coordinated overexpression of steroidogenic enzymes (CYP11A1, CYP17A1, CYP19A1, HSD3B7, HSD17B7, HSD17B6) and hormone receptors (ESR1, ESR2, AR), suggesting aberrant sex hormone metabolism as a driving force. Consequently, G2-APOBEC tumors show increased RNA and protein expression in key oncogenic pathways, including cell cycle, MAPK, FOXO, and Ras signaling, supported by multivariate linear regression analysis (p < 0.05; Figure S2C and Table S2C). Notably, younger patients in this group show upregulation of key components of the MAPK pathway (MAPK1, PIK3R1, PIK3CD, PLCG1, RASA1), along with other pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways such as aldosterone (ALD), HER2, EIF2, and sirtuin pathways. These findings uncover a distinct oncogenic program in younger G2-APOBEC patients, driven by APOBEC mutagenesis and amplified by dysregulated hormone metabolism and pro-tumorigenic signaling, and highlight a mechanistic link to early-onset breast cancer and potential therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In summary, our results reveal age-dependent, mutagen-specific oncogenic programs and highlight potential therapeutic vulnerabilities linked to environmental exposure and hormone dysregulation in early-onset breast cancer. G1-DBAC tumors in older patients show heightened susceptibility to environmental carcinogens, with activation of detoxifying enzymes and DNA damage pathways. In contrast, younger G2-APOBEC patients display increased vulnerability to endogenous mutagenesis, driven by APOBEC activity and dysregulated hormone signaling.

A hypermutated luminal subtype defined by epigenetic regulator mutations and HRD

High tumor mutational burden (TMB) is an emerging predictive biomarker for efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), as it increases neoantigen load and enhances tumor immunogenicity [36]. Within the 5-methylcytosine deamination subgroup (G3-1), we identified a unique cluster of tumors, designated as the high-TMB (H-TMB) subtype, characterized by markedly elevated somatic mutation burden and neoantigen load (Fig. 2B, denoted by *). This hypermutated phenotype extended beyond canonical oncogenes and tumor suppressors (e.g., PIK3CA, TP53) to include widespread mutations in epigenetic regulators, or “epigenes,” involved in DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling. Notably, key epigenetic modifiers such as TET1/2, DNMT1, KMT2C, DOT1L, HDAC4, and CHD7, are significantly enriched mutation targets in this subgroup. Alternation in these regulators has been reported to impair DNA replication and repair fidelity, potentially contributing to the genome instability and hypermutation [37–43]. To investigate whether the low fraction of expressed SNVs (eSNVs) was due to epigenetic silencing, we analyzed the transcriptional abundance of these methylated genes. Their total transcript levels (log2 CPM) remained stable and comparable to other clusters, with ARID2 showing significantly higher expression in G3-1 relative to others (7.67 vs. 6.97, p = 0.03). This discordance, high overall expression but low representation of mutant alleles, suggests selective degradation or instability of mutant transcripts (e.g., via nonsense-mediated decay), or preferential transcription of the wild-type allele to preserve essential epigenetic functions. Despite this lower transcriptomic representation of mutant alleles, the total number of predicted mutated peptides was the highest among all subgroups (Figure S2D), indicating enhanced immunogenic potential and possible responsiveness to immunotherapy [44], even within this non-TNBC enriched subset typically considered less ICI-responsive.

To explore functional consequences, proteome-wide network analysis using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) (log₂ tumor/NAT ratios; Pairwise Wilcoxon Tests, Benj.-Hoch. FDR < 0.05, Table S2D) revealed activation of both canonical breast cancer pathways (HER2, mTOR) and immune-related pathways (BCR, TCR, PD-L1, and immune cell signaling) (Fig. 2C). A particularly notable finding was the subgroup-specific silencing of mutant tenascin-C (TNC), an immunosuppressive extracellular matrix protein associated with breast cancer metastasis [45]. While TNC was overexpressed in ~ 70% of the full cohort, it was nearly absent at the protein level in the H-TMB subset (Fig. 2D), correlating with superior survival outcomes (p = 0.049, Fig. 2E). The TNC-low tumors exhibited increased expression of immune cell markers including γδ T cells, NKT cells, and B cells (Fig. 2C), suggesting enhanced immune infiltration and a more active tumor microenvironment, potentially improving immunotherapy response. Notably, prior studies have reported that TNC suppresses T-cell activation and contributes to immune evasion in cancer, providing a potential biological context for the observed association between reduced TNC expression and an immune-active phenotype [46].

Beyond immunogenicity, the H-TMB subtype also exhibited potential vulnerability to DNA damage repair-targeted therapies. Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), a loss of DNA repair function, has been shown to predict clinical response to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) in metastatic breast cancer patients [47]. The HRD scores were dichotomized into HRD-positive (HRD+) and HRD-negative (HRD-) groups using the established cutoff of 42 [48]. In our cohort, 25.4% (35/138) of patients were classified as HRD+. The G3 subgroup exhibited the highest proportion of HRD-positive tumors (50%, 13/26) and significantly elevated HRD scores (median = 39.5; p < 0.0001) compared to other groups (Fig. 2F and Table S2E). This enrichment was particularly pronounced in younger patients (< 55 yrs), suggesting a potential therapeutic window for PARP inhibitors in this subgroup. Furthermore, we observed PARP1-pSer782 phosphorylation, a site critical for PARP1 activation via CDK2 [49], in the majority of patients. This phosphorylation showed a weak but positive correlation with HRD scores in the proteome P1 cohort, which is enriched for luminal breast cancers with aggressive features such as high tumor grade, lymph-vascular space invasion (LVSI), and elevated MKI67 expression (Fig. S2E). These findings suggest that PARPi therapy, guided by HRD and PARP1-pSer782 as potential biomarkers, may be extended to a subset of high-risk luminal patients.

In summary, our study identifies a previously unrecognized H-TMB luminal breast cancer subtype defined by epigenetic dysregulation, elevated neoantigen load, and immune activation, potentially expanding the population of luminal patients eligible for immunotherapy. Concurrent HRD and PARP1-pSer782 phosphorylation further suggest this group may also benefit from PARPi, providing a dual therapeutic vulnerability that merits clinical investigation.

Proteogenomic stratification reveals immune subtypes beyond PD-L1

ICIs have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy, either alone or in combination with standard therapies. However, their clinical efficacy remains limited in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. The unexpected discovery of a high tumor mutational burden (H-TMB) subgroup among luminal tumors prompted us to investigate their immune microenvironment using CIBERSORT [50] to estimate tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) (Fig. 3A). This analysis revealed two distinct immunophenotypes. The active immunophenotype was characterized by elevated levels of cytotoxic and immune-effector cells—B cells, cytotoxic T lymphocytes, M1 macrophages, activated CD4 + memory T cells, and NK cells. In contrast, the inactive immunophenotype was enriched for immunosuppressive or less active immune components, including regulatory T cells, monocytes, M2 macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils.

Proteomic profiling reinforced these findings: tumors with active immunophenotypes exhibited significantly higher expression of immune cell–specific protein markers, including B cell markers (CD22, CD37, MS4A1, SELL) and T/NK cell markers (CD2, CD3E, CD5). In contrast, the inactive group showed enrichment for monocyte/macrophage/DC markers (CCL14, CCL18, CD209, MS4A6; p < 0.05; Fig. 3B). Notably, immune scores were significantly higher in the active group (p < 0.0001), whereas stromal scores showed no significant difference (Figure S3A). When comparing immunophenotypes with mutational signature-defined genome groups (G1–G4), we observed their strong association (p = 0.0038; Fig. 3C). A substantial proportion (73.3%) of G2-APOBEC tumors and 60% of G4-mixed tumors displayed an active immunophenotype. Only APOBEC-mediated mutagenesis showed a statistically significant association with the immunologically active subtype (p = 0.0128) compared to G4-mixed tumors (p = 0.0769). These findings suggest that APOBEC-mediated mutagenesis is more strongly linked to an immunologically “hot” phenotype and may serve as a potential biomarker for identifying immunotherapy-responsive tumors.

Although PD-L1 expression is commonly used to guide ICI treatment, it does not fully predict therapeutic response [51], highlighting the need for more robust predictive biomarkers [52, 53]. In our cohort, PD-L1 (CD274) levels varied across immunophenotypes (Fig. S3B): while PD-L1 was elevated in many TNBC and HER2-positive tumors, known to be more responsive to ICIs, within the active group, it was also unexpectedly high in some tumors with inactive immunophenotypes. This discordance underscores the limitation of PD-L1 as a standalone biomarker. Expression of T cells (CD2, CD3D/E/G, CD5, CD6, CD8A, CD38) and NK cells (APOBEC3G, CD247, LCK) markers positively correlated with PD-L1 (Fig. 3D), indicating immune cell infiltration is a critical determinant of tumor immunogenicity. Interestingly, in the inactive group, PD-L1 and ESR1 expression were negatively correlated (Spearman p = 0.0034, R² = -0.41; Figure S3C), consistent with previous reports that ESR1 represses PD-L1, suggesting that low ESR1 expression may favor increased immune activation [54].

To identify additional biomarkers predictive of an active immune state, particularly in tumors with low PD-L1 expression, we found 12 immune-related proteins significantly elevated in the PD-L1-low active group (p < 0.05; Fig. S3D). By integrating immunophenotype and PD-L1 expression levels (Fig. 3B and D) [55], we stratified the tumor immune microenvironment into four subtypes (Fig. 3E). The most favorable, Type I (PD-L1⁺, active immunophenotype), comprised > 85% of G2-APOBEC tumors (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR = 0.0255) and 65% of younger patients (FDR = 0.0182), suggesting strong ICI candidacy. In contrast, Type II (PD-L1⁺, inactive immunophenotype) tumors may represent a PD-L1-expressing subgroup that fails to mount an effective immune response and may contribute to resistance observed in clinical trials [56–58].

In summary, proteogenomic profiling of the tumor immune microenvironment revealed a refined classification of breast cancer immunophenotypes beyond PD-L1 expression alone. The identification of immune cell protein markers and functional immunotypes may better stratify patients for immunotherapy and identify those who may benefit from ICI treatment, particularly in luminal breast cancer subtypes previously thought to be immunologically inert.

Proteomic subtyping resolves phenotypic heterogeneity and early recurrence in luminal BC

To further dissect the tumor heterogeneity that critically influences disease progression, therapeutic response, and prognosis, we aimed to define proteome-based subtypes that capture molecular complexity beyond conventional IHC or PAM50 classifications. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of proteomic tumor-to-NAT ratios, using the top 50% most variably expressed proteins, revealed four distinct proteomic subtypes (P1-P4) (Fig. 4A). These subtypes exhibited stronger associations with clinical features and age compared to clusters derived from transcriptomic or phosphoproteomic data (Figures S4A-B). Age stratification was based on established cutoffs: 50 years as the threshold for younger age (reflecting average age of natural menopause [7, 8]) and 55 years as the cohort median for comparative genomic analyses.

While these proteomic subtypes partially overlapped with PAM50 categories, they revealed additional biological distinctions. Subtypes P1 (median age 51.0) and P2 (median age, 54.4 years) captured significantly younger luminal patients, corresponding primarily to luminal B/HER2 + and luminal A/B tumors, respectively (Figs. 4B-C). In contrast, P3 was enriched with an older patient population (median age, 63.1 years) and was enriched in HER2 and TNBC cases. P4 was enriched with lower-grade tumors and luminal A–like features. Notably, as the tumor clustering was performed on global proteomic profiles rather than conventional clinical subtype classification, tumors from distinct clinical subtypes (e.g., TNBC and luminal) may be grouped within the same cluster when they share similar molecular characteristics at the proteome level. Despite both P1 and P2 comprising younger luminal patients, they differed markedly in clinical features: P1 tumors displayed aggressive characteristics including high tumor grade (58% grade 3), the highest frequency of lymph-vascular space invasion (LVSI; 58%; Fig. S4C), and elevated Ki67 expression (p = 7.5e-7; Fig. 4D). Notably, LVSI in P1 occurred more frequently in younger patients (≤ 50 years, p < 0.001), highlighting an age-related susceptibility to aggressive tumor behavior.

Pathway enrichment analysis revealed shared upregulation of spliceosome and RNA processing pathways in both P1 and P2 subtypes (Fig. 4E, Fig. S4D; Table S3A), with the spliceosome consistently overexpressed across SR proteins, snRNPs, and SF3 components (Fig. S4E, Table S3B). In contrast, subtype P3 was characterized by enrichment of p53 signaling, apoptosis, and immune-related pathways, suggesting potential immune engagement and tumor suppressor activation. Subtype P4 exhibited upregulation of complement and coagulation cascades and reduced spliceosome pathway enrichment, which may be associated with reduced homologous recombination (HR) activity and may reflect a distinct genomic and immune microenvironmental context [59, 60] (Fig. 4E, Table S3B). Aberrant splicing has been implicated in malignancy via dysregulation of oncogenes and tumor suppressors. Among these splicing factors, six that were relatively highly expressed in the P1 or P2 subtypes were significantly associated with poor survival in an independent breast cancer cohort [61], four of which have established roles in angiogenesis, apoptosis resistance, invasion, and metastasis [62] (Fig. S4F and Table S3C).

Surprisingly, in our prospective cohort of stage I–III patients with median 3-year follow-up and standard-of-care therapy, early recurrence (≤ 2 years) was not observed in the aggressive luminal P1 (LP1) subtype, despite high nodal burden, Ki67, and LVSI. In contrast, nearly all early recurrences among luminal breast cancer patients occurred in the luminal P2 (LP2) subtype, which exhibited worse disease free survival, despite having relatively favorable clinical features but a younger average age (51.9 years)(Figs. 4F-G). This discordance suggests standard therapeutic responsiveness in P1 and underscores an underappreciated vulnerability in clinically low-risk but molecularly distinct, early recurrent P2 tumors. Proteomics and phosphoproteomic analysis revealed differential activation of oncogenic pathways: P1 tumors were enriched for SHC1/GRB2/RAS signaling, while early recurrence-prone P2 tumors showed elevated ESR1 expression (Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.0035; Figs. S4G-H) and activation of MAPK and PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathways—key drivers of proliferation and endocrine resistance [63] (Fig. 4H). This suggests that hormone and growth factor pathway crosstalk contributes to early recurrence in P2, despite less aggressive histopathology. Furthermore, within P2, patients harboring APOBEC signature (G2-1 tumors) exhibited even shorter median disease free survival (~ 2 years; p < 0.0001) (Figs. 4I and S4I), highlighting a high-risk molecular subtype not captured by traditional staging or PAM50 classification.

In conclusion, this novel proteome-based patient stratification reveals clinically unrecognized heterogeneity among luminal breast cancers. These subtypes are distinguished by splicing dysregulation, oncogenic pathway activation (e.g., ERBB2, PI3K-AKT-mTOR), and immune signaling differences, with clear prognostic and therapeutic implications. Most notably, the identification of a younger, early-recurrence luminal P2 subtype, undetectable by clinical or PAM50 classification, offers a new opportunity for molecular risk stratification and tailored intervention.

A young luminal subtype with DBAC carcinogen-linked DNA repair deficiency

To further explore the molecular features linking age, mutational processes, and proteomic subtypes, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) across genome-associated pathways. This analysis revealed a distinct clustering of younger patients in the G1-DBAC group, particularly those also classified in the P1 proteomic subtype, along the second principal component (PC2, 74.2%, p < 0.05), which captures unique pathway alterations (Fig. 5A). Notably, the majority of luminal A (92.9%, p = 0.0037) and luminal B (77.8%, p < 0.0001) patients within G1-DBAC were younger than the cohort median age, suggesting a convergence of environmental mutagenesis and age-specific molecular programs. Pathway contribution to PC2 highlighted enrichment of DNA repair pathways as key features (Table S4A). Indeed, REACTOME scores for DNA repair, double-strand break repair, and damage bypass were significantly lower in G1 patients younger than 55 years, compared to other groups (p = 0.036, 0.032, 0.028; Fig. S5A). Correlation analysis identified 11 DNA repair genes (CUL4B, PARP1, FEN1, RBBP8, MSH2, EP300, SUMO2, ERCC5, BLM, POLK and EYA2) with significant RNA–protein concordance as well as RAD54L with inverse correlation (Fig. S5B), indicating transcriptional and post-transcriptional dysregulation in repair machinery.

Network-based visualization revealed strong correlations among age, genome subtypes, and proteome subtypes, particularly in younger patients under the age of 50 (Fig. 5B). Nearly all younger patients were enriched in exogenous carcinogen–associated genome groups (G1 and G4), with a striking concentration in the P1 proteomic subtype. The results suggest a tight link between exogenous carcinogen exposure and the P1 subtype in young individuals.

Consistent with transcriptomic patterns, the proteomic data revealed a pronounced age-dependent effect of G1-DBAC mutagenesis, particularly involving DNA repair pathways. Proteins involved in major DNA repair mechanisms—including nucleotide excision repair (NER), mismatch repair (MMR), homologous recombination (HR), and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)—were broadly downregulated in the P1 subtype, in sharp contrast to the early recurrent P2 subtype, which exhibited upregulation of many of these components (Fig. 5C, Table S4B). Within the P1 group, younger patients (< 50 years, designated as P1Y) showed the most dramatic downregulation (Fig. 5D), suggesting a significantly impaired DNA repair capacity in this young, DBAC carcinogen-exposed subgroup. A closer comparison of protein expression across younger (< 50 years) and older patients (> 50 years) groups within the P1 and P2 subtypes revealed that the most consistently suppressed proteins in P1 were upstream regulators of NER—including CUL4A/4B, DDB1/2, XPC, RPA1, RECC2/3, CCNH, and GTF2H3/4—indicating NER as the most affected repair pathway in young P1 patients, caused by environmental carcinogen among young females. Similarly, proteins from other repair systems also showed marked downregulation in P1, including MMR components (RFC3/5, RPA1, MSH1/2/3), HR (ATM, RPA1, RAD50), and NHEJ (ATM, XRCC5/6, PRKDC) (Fig. 5D). Altogether, these results define a distinct subset of young luminal breast cancer patients with strong DBAC-associated signatures and widespread suppression of DNA repair pathways, revealing a mechanistic link between environmental mutagen exposure and impaired genome maintenance.

Biomarker-guided CDK9 targeting overcomes therapy resistance in early-recurrent luminal BC

Young-onset luminal breast cancer is associated with elevated recurrence and mortality risk [64], despite standard endocrine therapy (ET) and the addition of CDK4/6 inhibitors improving progression-free survival in HR+/HER2– patients. However, 15–30% of patients develop resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors through largely undefined mechanisms [19–22], underscoring the need to dissect luminal heterogeneity and identify predictive biomarkers to guide treatment. Beyond the clinically established role of CDK4/6, targeting transcriptional CDKs, such as CDK7 and CDK9, have recently emerged as promising therapeutic targets [65].

In our cohort, proteomic profiling revealed broad overexpression of multiple CDKs (CDK2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 12, and 15) in luminal tumors, with most prominent enrichment in the luminal P2 (LP2) subtype (Fig. S6A). To evaluate the prognostic impact of CDK expression, we computed hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and log-rank p-values for each CDK across all patients (n = 118). Among CDKs, CDK9 protein emerged as the top predictor of poor prognosis (HR = 11.21, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6A, Table S5A), highlighting its potential as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in high-risk recurrence-prone luminal BC. Consistently, analysis of independent cohorts across different geographic populations using the KM-plotter platform [66] showed that higher CDK9 gene expression was associated with worse post-progression survival (p = 0.036; Fig. S6B), reinforcing its potential as a clinically relevant prognostic biomarker. Interestingly, proteomic subtype-resolved CDK expression patterns reveal subtype-specific therapeutic vulnerabilities. To investigate CDK expression and associated biological features within luminal tumors, we performed unsupervised clustering of luminal patients, defining three luminal proteome subtypes (LP1–LP3) that largely recapitulate the global proteome clusters (P1–P3). Despite its aggressive clinical phenotype, the luminal P1 (LP1) subtype lacked CDK4/6 overexpression and showed reduced RB1 expression and phosphorylation, suggesting poor dependency on the CDK4/6–RB1 axis (Fig. S6A). In contrast, CDK4/6 overexpression was observed in LP2 and LP3, accompanied by coordinated downregulation of CDK inhibitors (p15, p16, p21, p27), increased RB1 expression and elevated RB1-pS249 phosphorylation, a direct CDK4/6 substrate, suggesting functional CDK4/6 activity in these subtypes [67] (Fig. S6C). These findings indicate that RB1-pS249 phosphorylation may serve as a functional biomarker of CDK4/6 activity, stratifying patients who could benefit from CDK4/6 inhibition.

Focusing on high-risk recurrence-prone LP2 subtype, CDK1, 2, 5, 7, and 9 were notably elevated, with CDK7 and CDK9 showing the most significant overexpression compared to LP1 and LP3 (p < 0.001; Fig. S6A). Hyperphosphorylation of RNA Polymerase II (Pol II) at Ser5 (S5) and Ser2 (S2) has been reported to be key events of the transcriptional activation of CDK7 and CDK9, respectively, induced by DNA damage [68]. Thus, we analyzed Pol II phosphosites. Although both CDK7 and CDK9 are overexpressed in LP2 subtype, only CDK9-specific Ser2 phosphosites (Fig. S6D), including pS1847, pS1875, pS1882, pS1889, pS1896, pS1910, and pS1917, were significantly elevated in LP2 (Figs. 6B-C), alongside with upregulation of downstream regulators (e.g., POLR2B, XRN2) and components of the transcriptional elongation complex (e.g., SEC proteins). This phosphorylation signature strongly correlated with CDK9 expression in LP2 (Fig. S6E), confirming CDK9 hyperactivation in this subtype.

Pathway enrichment by IPA and GSEA further revealed coordinated upregulation of CDK7/9 interactors and their phosphorylated targets in LP2, including CDK12, RB1, CCNH, and GTF2H2 (Fig. 6D, Table S5B). The expression patterns of CDK7/9 interactors align with their known binding preferences to Pol II phosphorylation, pS2 (DDX21, HNRNPU, PES1, RPL6, RPL18) and pS5 (PES1) [68], suggesting these in regulation of DNA repair, survival, metastasis, and metabolism [69, 70]. Prior studies show CDK9 activation can drive PARP inhibitor resistance via Pol II-Ser2–mediated induction of DNA repair genes such as RBBP8, RAD51, and RAD50 [71], reinforcing its role in therapy resistance. Collectively, these findings suggest that CDK9-mediated transcriptional activation in the LP2 subtype contributes to co-transcriptional recruitment of cancer-promoting factors, supporting disease progression and therapeutic resistance.

To evaluate whether CDK9 inhibition represents a promising strategy for the early-recurrent LP2 subtype, we tested the effect of pooled siCDK9 in luminal A cell lines (MCF7, T-47D). CDK9 knockdown significantly reduced cell viability and colony formation (Figs. S6F-G) and enhanced the cytotoxicity of the CDK9 inhibitor NVP-2 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. S6H), suggesting that reducing CDK9 protein levels may be more effective than inhibiting its activity alone. We next assessed the therapeutic potential of various CDK9 inhibitors in seven luminal breast cancer cell lines, including luminal A (MCF7, T-47D and HCC1500) and luminal B (17pe, MDA-MB-361, ZR75-1 and BT474), using MTT and immunoblot assays. Notably, 17pe, a primary cell line, was derived from the pleural effusion of a stage IV (pT3N2cM1) Taiwanese patient. It exhibited a luminal B-like phenotype characterized by moderate ER (30%), low PR (< 5%), and HER2-negative (IHC 1+) status, which represents the LP2 subtype defined in this study. MDA-MB-361 cell line is a well-established model for trastuzumab resistance [72] and has demonstrated cross-resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors, necessitating combination therapy for effective tumor clearance, including tucatinib (HER2 small-molecule inhibitor), palbociclib (CKD4/6 inhibitor) and fulvestrant (selective ER degrader) [73]. Treatment with pan-CDK inhibitor abemaciclib (ABE) revealed marked resistance in luminal B cell lines, 17pe, MDA-MB-361 and ZR75-1, with IC50 values of 5249, 14,351 and 14,543 nM, respectively, compared to MCF7 (666 nM) [74] (Figs. 6E and S6I). In contrast, the CDK9-selective inhibitor NVP-2 exhibited potent activity, achieving nanomolar IC50 values across all cell lines and a > 700-fold enhanced efficacy in MDA-MB-361 cells (IC50 = 18 nM), revealing that NVP-2 is highly effective in luminal subtypes that have developed resistance to CDK4/6-targeted therapies, as well as promising > 3000-fold enhanced sensitivity in the Asian-derived cell line 17pe. NVP-2 also significantly suppressed POLR2A-Ser2 phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner, while ABE required higher concentrations (200 nM) to achieve modest inhibition in 17pe, MDA-MB-361 and other luminal cells (Figs. 6F-G and S6J). Together, these findings define CDK9 as a therapeutically actionable vulnerability in luminal breast cancer, including CDK4/6-resistant disease, and nominate p-POLR2A-Ser2 as a clinically tractable biomarker to monitor target engagement and treatment response.

To further characterize subtype-specific kinase activity and additional pathogenic mechanisms, we analyzed differential phosphosites across luminal proteome subtypes and performed kinase motif enrichment analysis [75] (Fig. 6H, Table S5C). The LP1 subtype showed dominant activity of CMGC family kinases (CDK1, CDK2, MAPK1, MAPK3) and others (DYRK2, AURKA, AURKB, MAP3K10, and MTOR), with concurrent upregulation of splicing-related phosphosites (SRSF3_pS130, SRRM2_pS1329, pS1318), consistent with previous findings shown in Figure S4E. LP2/3 tumors exhibited activation motifs associated with CMGC and CAMK families, with PRKCI as a potential upstream regulator via CDK7-T170 phosphorylation. This activation may promote phosphorylation of downstream targets, including POLR2A and CDK11B, thereby linking transcriptional control with cell cycle regulation [76].

In summary, our findings reveal heterogeneous activation of CDK4/6 and CDK7/9 signaling across luminal subtypes. While standard therapies benefit DBAC-susceptible LP1 tumors, transcriptional CDK9 hyperactivation defines an early recurrence, therapy-resistant group. The CDK9-selective inhibitor NVP-2 and biomarker p-POLR2A-Ser2 offer a rational precision treatment strategy.

Proteogenomics-guided Patient Stratification and Actionable Pathways for Precision Treatment

This study presents a multi-layered proteogenomic analysis that refines molecular subtyping of breast cancer beyond clinical diagnosis and PAM50 classification. We proposed a proteogenomic subtyping scheme, particularly addressing the heterogeneity and distinct oncogenic processes in luminal BC, to guide therapeutic vulnerabilities across subtypes of early-stage breast cancer (Figs. 7A-B). Notably, G3-1 patients are characterized by both high tumor mutational burden (TMB)—a biomarker approved for predicting response to ICIs [53, 77]—and a high frequency of epigenetic mutations. These combined features suggest potential benefit from ICIs combined with epigenetic therapy or PARP inhibitors. Therefore, detection of mutational signatures may help identify G3-1 patients and guide more precise treatment strategies. Among proteome-defined subtypes, DBAC-susceptible P1 and early recurrent P2 represent two distinct luminal groups with different clinical and molecular profiles. Despite aggressive clinicopathological features, young P1 patients (average age, 45.8 years) exhibit strong enrichment of the G1-DBAC signature and marked DNA repair deficiency, a feature associated with chemotherapy sensitivity [78]. These patients may benefit from standard adjuvant chemotherapy. In contrast, P2 tumors represent a younger, high-risk luminal population with early recurrence, despite moderate clinical features. Notably, adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor treatment was not yet clinically available for this cohort. Compared to other luminal patients, molecular profiling of P2 tumors reveals hyperactivation of ER, PI3K-AKT, and CDK7/9 signaling pathways, which are implicated in tumor progression and endocrine resistance due to the interplay between the PI3K-AKT-mTOR with ER and CDK7/9 pathways [63, 79, 80].

To further aid stratification of high-risk patients, we identified a panel of candidate biomarkers uniquely upregulated in recurrent P2 tumors, which discriminates the aggressive P2 subgroup from all luminal BC patients (log2 T/N values, Kruskal-Wallis test p < 0.05, followed by Pairwise Wilcoxon Tests, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR < 0.05, Fig. 7C). Using annotations from FDA, HPA, DrugBank, DGIdb, ChEMBL, and IPA, we evaluated their druggability and clinical relevance. Among these, HDAC2 overexpression, validated using NanoString BC360 in an independent cohort of stage I-III luminal patients (n = 270), was associated with poor survival (Fig. 7D, Table S1J). In addition, ALDH1L2, ARF4, and SCAMP3 were also linked to poor prognosis based on public breast cancer proteomic datasets using KM-plotter [81, 82] (Fig. 7E). These genes are implicated in metastasis, immune modulation, and proliferation across multiple cancer types [83–87], suggesting their potential utility as biomarkers to identify high-risk recurrence in early-stage luminal breast cancer.

Discussion

Over the past four decades, breast cancer incidence has risen sharply among East Asian women, with nearly half of cases occurring before age 50 and a predominance of the luminal subtype [2, 88]. This epidemiologic pattern, distinct from Western populations, highlights the urgent need to uncover region-specific etiologies and precision strategies for early intervention. Our proteogenomic analysis provides the first evidence linking environmental carcinogen-associated mutational signatures—particularly DBAC—to luminal breast cancer, revealing a novel axis of exogenous DNA damage and impaired repair in younger Taiwanese patients [89, 90]. DBAC-enriched tumors displayed transversion-dominated mutational profiles and suppression of nucleotide excision repair (NER), a critical pathway for resolving bulky DNA adducts, such as those induced by environmental carcinogens like DBAC [91]. Within the luminal P1 proteome subtype, this NER deficiency likely contributes to chemosensitivity despite aggressive pathological features. These findings suggest that carcinogen-induced mutational stress and repair deficiency cooperate to drive genomic instability in young-onset luminal tumors. This environmentally imprinted molecular subtype may be underrecognized outside of East Asia, presenting new opportunities for prevention and etiology-informed patient stratification. Whether this profile is unique to Asian populations or previously overlooked warrants further investigation.

To address the population specificity, we compared our cohort with the predominantly Western TCGA dataset. Beyond clinical differences in younger age of onset and luminal enrichment, our East Asian cohort displays a distinct mutational landscape indicative of altered etiological exposures. Unlike TCGA, where endogenous processes predominate (reflected by higher transition-to-transversion (Ti/Tv) ratios driven by C > T transitions), our cohort is highly enriched for exogenous mutagen exposure that induced prominent transversion patterns (notably T > G and C > A). These patterns align with polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons–associated signatures, including DBAC identified in our analysis, supporting a carcinogen-driven etiology in this population. The reliability of detecting these often subclonal environmental signatures is supported by our high-depth whole-exome sequencing (> 150×) using Mutect2 for sensitive identification of low-frequency variants, following MAQC/SEQC2 recommendations [92]. Consequently, the exclusive identification of the DBAC carcinogen imprint in our cohort provides compelling evidence that breast carcinogenesis in this population is uniquely shaped by localized interactions between genetic and environmental factors, rather than shared universal pathways.

Due to the limited sample size of our prospectively collected TNBC cohort (n = 15), the genomic findings within this subset should be interpreted with caution. For example, the observed atypical features, specifically the lack of a high mutational burden and a lower frequency of TP53 truncating mutations, can be reasonably explained by intrinsic subtype heterogeneity. Our analysis indicated that this cohort was not exclusively Basal-like; rather, a substantial proportion (53%) was classified as non-basal (HER2-enriched/Luminal)(Fig. 1A). Since the traditional high-instability features are predominantly driven by the Basal-like phenotype, the presence of these non-basal variants cases naturally diluted the overall mutational burden. This observation is consistent with recent genomic profiling of East Asian TNBC populations [93], which identified a significantly higher prevalence of the luminal androgen receptor (LAR) subtype (23%) compared to Western cohorts. Given that the LAR subtype (classified as non-basal/HER2-enriched by PAM50) is characterized by a distinct mutational landscape with fewer TP53 truncating mutations and more missense events compared to Basal-like tumors [94], the enrichment of this subtype in our cohort logically explains the ‘atypical’ mutational features.

While immunotherapy has shown promise in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [95, 96], its efficacy remains limited in non-TNBC subtypes. We identified an unique non-TNBC genomic subtype, G3-1 (H-TMB), characterized by high tumor mutation burden (TMB), abundant neoantigens, frequent epi-mutations, and elevated HRD scores. These features implicate converging genomic and epigenomic instability as a driver of immune dysregulation. Prior studies have shown that epigenetic modifiers such as DNMT and HDAC inhibitors can restore antigen presentation and reverse immune evasion [97, 98], offering a mechanistic rationale for epigenetic priming to sensitize tumors to ICIs. Moreover, the prominent 5-methylcytosine deamination signature and high HRD burden suggest parallel vulnerabilities to PARP inhibitors and other DNA damage response (DDR)–targeted agents. Collectively, these features position G3-1 subtype as a compelling candidate for biomarker-guided combination therapies that combine ICIs, epigenetic agents, and DDR inhibitors in non-TNBC tumors. Further investigation into combination treatments could be valuable in developing precision medicine in this H-TMB subtype, including potential to overcome resistance observed with ICIs.

Although ER+ breast cancer is generally associated with favorable prognosis, younger patients face a disproportionately higher risk of recurrence [99, 100]. Our study uncovers previously unrecognized molecular heterogeneity within luminal disease, shaped by both age and environmental carcinogen exposure. A distinct subgroup of young patients in the luminal P2 proteome subtype experienced early recurrence within two years, despite low clinicopathologic risk and standard adjuvant therapy. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic profiling revealed coordinated upregulation of oncogenic signaling in ESR, PI3K/AKT/mTOR, and CDK signaling pathways, along with elevated HER2 expression, molecular features likely contributing to endocrine therapy resistance [101, 102]. Notably, recent clinical trials, including UNIRAD and SWOG S1207, failed to demonstrate survival benefit from mTOR inhibitors (everolimus) in unselected luminal cohorts [103, 104]. This may be attributed to high discontinuation rate (50%) and the lack of molecular stratification in selecting mTOR-activated subgroups, such as the luminal P2 subtype. However, improved outcomes were observed in premenopausal women receiving everolimus in the SWOG S1207 trial, highlighting the value of targeted interventions in specific subgroups. Our finding of enriched PI3K/AKT/mTOR activity in the high-risk P2 subgroup directly supports these clinical observations and highlights a path forward: targeted therapy based on molecular subtyping. The proposed biomarker panel for the P2 subgroup may improve patient stratification and therapeutic precision, particularly with emerging PI3K or AKT inhibitors.

CDK4/6 inhibitors have transformed the treatment landscape for advanced luminal breast cancer [77], yet their efficacy is often limited by tumor heterogeneity and acquired resistance [105]. Our proteogenomic analysis reveals CDK circuitry, with CDK7/9 most prominently upregulated in the early recurrent P2 subtype. Our findings may help explain the superior clinical efficacy of abemaciclib— a CDK4/6 inhibitor with additional CDK9 activity—observed in adjuvant trials, compared to the more selective palbociclib [77, 106]. Recent phase I trial combining CDK7 inhibitor (Samuraciclib) with endocrine therapy showed early promise in HR+/HER2 − patients post-CDK4/6 treatment [107], and preclinical studies have implicated CDK9-driven transcriptional deregulation of MYC in endocrine resistance [108]. In our early-stage cohort, CDK9 stood out as the strongest predictor of poor prognosis among CDKs, with marked high hazard ratios. Functionally, our CDK9 inhibition assays using the selective inhibitor NVP-2 showed ~ 3000-fold greater efficacy than pan-CDK inhibitors in resistant luminal cells. The observed highly activated CDK4/6/9 axis in P2 aligns with its emerging clinical relevance in HER2−/HR+ breast cancer, where the CDK4/6 inhibitor such as abemaciclib has demonstrated efficacy and approved as adjuvant therapy in HR+ metastatic breast cancer [77]. Despite these advances, resistance to CDK4/6-targeted therapy remains a major clinical challenge [109]. Our findings highlight CDK9, along with p-POLR2A-Ser2 as a functional downstream indicator, as a potential therapeutic vulnerability in recurrence-prone luminal tumors, including those resistant to current targeted therapies. Rather than proposing CDK9 as a novel target, our study provides a molecular basis for its deployment, particularly in patients who have progressed on CDK4/6 inhibitors. Together, these results support CDK9-targeted strategies as a rational extension of current treatment paradigms and a potential avenue for precision therapy in high-risk luminal breast cancer.

Methods

Data and code availability

Genomics and proteomics data referenced in this study will be available in publicly accessible NIH-designated data repositories and portals including the database of Genotypes and Phenotypes (dbGaP) (accession number: phs003150.v1) and the NCI Proteomics Data Commons (PDC), along with demographic and clinical data. Raw data files and the processed data for proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses reported in this paper are uploaded to the NCI Proteomics Data Commons and can be accessed at: https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/study/PDC000461 and https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/study/PDC000462.

Clinical specimens

The clinical cohort for proteogenomics characterization was collected from the Tri-Service general hospital (TSGH) from 29 June 2017 to 11 November 2019. All non-pregnant female patients who met the following criteria were eligible for this study: (1) age ≥ 20 years; (2) having invasive breast cancer as the first event of malignancy in her lifetime; (3) never receiving any local or systemic treatment before surgery, such as chemotherapy, target therapy, immunotherapy, radiotherapy and endocrine therapy. A total of 140 patients were included in this study and the information of follow up was updated up to March 24, 2022. Clinicopathological parameters, including age, smoking status, the condition of hormone replacement therapy, menopausal status, tumor stage, lymph node stage, tumor grading, hormone receptor status, HER2 receptor status, Ki-67 proliferative index, lymphovascular space invasion status, perineural invasion status, tumor infiltrating lymphocyte status, the regimen of adjuvant therapy, as well as recurrence and survival outcomes were collected. Breast cancer pathological and TNM (tumor, node, and metastasis) staging was done based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer 8th edition stage [110]. IHC-defined intrinsic subtypes, including luminal A (ER + and/or PR+, HER2-, and Ki-67 < 14%), B1 (ER + and/or PR+, HER2- and Ki-67 ≥ 14%), B2 (ER + and/or PR+, and HER2+, irrespective of Ki-67 expression), HER2-enriched (ER-, PR-, and HER2+) and TNBC (ER-, PR-, and HER2-) subtype, were classified according to the result of IHC staining of ER, PR, HER2 receptor status and Ki-67 proliferative index [111]. The surveillance of clinical outcomes includes recurrence and overall survival status at the end of follow-up. Their breast cancer tissues, adjacent normal tissues (NAT) and blood were subjected to the proteogenomic experiments. Clinical information including age, gender, stage, IHC results of clinical subtype, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and HER2 were listed in the Table S1A and summarized in the Table S1B.

The validation cohort for HDAC2 expression associated with overall survival was from the National Taiwan University hospital (NTUH). A total of 66 patients identified with distant metastasis and 204 non-relapse patients with matched age, ER, HER2 status selected from a retrospective hospital cohort of 1028 breast cancer patients with stage I-III breast cancer diagnosed in 2007–2009 and with median follow-up time of 13.7 years at National Taiwan University Hospital [2]. This study has been both approved by the Research Ethics Committees of Academia Sinica, TSGH and NTUH and informed consents have been received from all patients. Clinical information including age, gender, stage, IHC results of clinical subtype, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (ER) and HER2 was listed in the Table S1J.

For proteogenomics analysis, after gross examination, non-necrotic tumor and adjacent normal tissues were excised from resected tumor specimens. Only tissues with weight greater than 40 mg were divided into two cryotubes and stored in liquid nitrogen within 30 min after resection. Paired adjacent normal tissues were taken from the most distant site relative to the tumor bolder. Finally, the H&E staining and pathology reports were utilized to identify and qualify breast tumors with at least 60% tumor cell nuclei and less than 20% necrosis for this study.

Genomic and transcriptomic analysis

Whole exome sequencing and mRNA sequencing experiments

All of the procedures used in nucleic acid extraction and library construction were performed by manufacturers’ instructions. In brief, genomic DNAs and total RNAs of frozen tumor and adjacent normal tissues were co-isolated using AllPrep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal Kit (Qiagen) and genomic DNAs of peripheral blood buffy coat were extracted using QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen), respectively. DNAs and RNAs were quantified using Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the integrity of RNA was determined using Agilent TapeStation 2200 (Agilent Technologies). For whole exome sequencing (WES), DNeasy PowerClean Cleanup kit was used for DNA purification and 400 ng of purified DNAs were performed for Illumina paired-end library preparation. The hybrid capture-based exome enrichment was performed using SureSelectXT2 (Agilent Technologies) with 8 multiplexes. For RNA sequencing, the Illumina RNA sequencing library was prepared with 1 µg of total RNA using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina). The sequencing libraries were quantified by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the size of libraries was determined using Agilent TapeStation 2200 (Agilent Technologies). The exome-capture libraries and RNA sequencing libraries were performed for the 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing using NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) with S4 flow-cell. The average mappable depth was over 150X for each exome-capture library and the average mappable reads was over 50 million paired reads for RNA sequencing library.

Sequencing reads data preprocessing

Quality of sequencing raw data and adaptor/primer contamination were checked by FastQC (v0.11.7). In order to reduce noise in the sequencing data, reads were trimmed off if average sequencing quality within the 4-base sliding window was < 20 and removed out adaptor/primer sequences by Trimmomatic (v0.36). Trimmed reads with < 36 bases were dropped out for decreasing mapping error in the next processing steps.

Detection of germline variants and somatic variants

Trimmed paired-end reads were mapped to the human reference genome UCSC hg19 by BWA (v0.7.17) and transformed into the binary alignment/map file (BAM file) by samtools (v1.9). Because of the artificial effect from the PCR step during library preparation and systematic technical error from sequencing machines, marked duplicated and base quality recalibration were also applied through GATK (v4.0) and the finalized BAM files were used for further variants detection. Germline mutation and somatic mutation detections followed the GATK best practice recommended by the Broad Institute (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us). In brief, somatic mutations were detected through comparing paired NAT and tumor tissue using MuTect2 (embedded in GATK). Haplotype caller of GATK module was used to identify germline variants in genomic DNA specimens.

Cancer related rare germline mutations were identified according to the following criteria: (1) variants resulted in amino acid change; (2) mutation frequencies in population were less than 1%; (3) variants located in cancer related genes reported in the COSMIC database; and (4) variants annotated as “benign” or “unknown” in the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar/).

Somatic mutation frequencies in TW and TCGA cohorts

Somatic mutation frequencies of cancer related genes in the TCGA breast cancer cohorts were downloaded from the cBioPortal (source data from GDAC Firehose, PanCancer Atlas, PID:26451490, PID: 23000897). Two-proportion z test was applied to comparing mutation frequencies between TW and TCGA cohorts.

Detection of somatic copy number alterations (CNAs)

To identify somatic copy number alterations (CNAs) from the whole-exome sequencing (WES) data (n = 138), BAM files from paired tumor and NAT were processed using VarScan2 to calculate raw copy numbers and log2 depth ratios [112]. The resulting data were then segmented using the Circular Binary Segmentation (CBS) algorithm implemented in the DNAcopy R package to delineate genomic regions with consistent copy number states [113]. Focal and broad CNAs across the cohort were evaluated using GISTIC 2.0 (Genomic Identification of Significant Targets in Cancer) to identify recurrent amplification and deletion peaks, applying a false discovery rate (FDR) at q-value threshold of 0.25 [114]. The GISTIC 2.0 algorithm assigned a discrete somatic copy-number status to each gene, categorized as deep deletion (-2), shallow deletion (-1), diploid/neutral (0), low-level gain (1), or high-level amplification (2).

To contextualize the genomic architecture of our Taiwanese cohort and explore potential population-specific disparities, a comparative CNA landscape analysis was conducted against the reference TCGA Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA) dataset. GISTIC 2.0 peak files for the TCGA BRCA cohort (derived predominantly from Affymetrix SNP 6.0 array data aligned to hg19) were retrieved from the Broad GDAC Firehose. Global amplification and deletion landscapes between the two cohorts were visualized and contrasted using back-to-back G-score plots generated via the maftools R package [31].

To elucidate the etiological relevance of the detected CNAs, copy number profiles were integrated with matched transcriptomic data across the assigned carcinogen groups (G1–G4). A Jonckheere-Terpstra trend test was employed to evaluate the cis-regulatory dosage effect by statistical correlation between discrete DNA-level GISTIC statuses and corresponding RNA-seq expression levels. This approach identified genes with transcriptional upregulation driven by somatic copy number gains.

Gene expression analysis

Trimmed paired-end reads from RNA sequencing were mapped to the human reference genome GRC37/hg19 using STAR2 (STAR-2.6.0c). Based on the Ensembl GRC37 gene annotation, gene expression estimated by the htseq-count algorithm embedded in STAR2. In order to correct different sequencing throughputs across samples, scaling factors were estimated by the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) algorithm (edgeR, R package). Genes with expression of > 1 CPM (count per million reads) and expressed in at least 2 samples were included for further analysis. In order to apply statistical models to detect differential expressed genes or pathways, log2 transformation and quantile normalization were utilized to preprocess expression values before data analysis.

Cancer related mutational signature and carcinogen signatures detection

According to the 6-type single nucleotide substitution of somatic mutations and their adjacent bases, frequencies of 96 possible mutation types for each sample were calculated. Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) algorithm was used to deconvolute the matrix of frequencies of 96 mutation types among all samples into 3 signatures and contributions of signatures for each patient. In order to study the endogenous source and environmental carcinogen of these signatures, mutation patterns of these signatures were compared to the 30 cancer signatures in the COSMIC database and the 54 carcinogen signatures reported from Kucab et al. to identify cancer-related mutational signatures and carcinogen signatures [28]. Cosine similarity was calculated to assess the similarity between factors and signatures in databases, which ranged from 0 to 1 indicating maximal dissimilarity to maximal similarity, respectively [29, 31].

Gene expression analysis in the validation cohort

Gene expression of the validation cohort was analyzed by the NanoString®BC360 panel. From each sample, 100 ng of RNA was hybridized to the human nCounter® Breast Cancer 360™ (BC360) gene expression panel (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA), and processed on the nCounter® SPRINT Profiler (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA) according to manufacturer protocols. The BC360 panel consists of 776 individual genes, including 18 housekeeping genes for normalization, and 40-gene signatures associated with breast cancer signaling pathways and biological processes.

Sample preparation for proteomic and phosphoproteomic analysis

Preparation of reference tissue samples

To ensure the quality of the proteomics and phosphoproteomics datasets, each batch included two reference samples. Two types of reference samples were prepared for quality control in each TMT batch experiment and used for abundance normalization between different TMT10plex batches. The first reference sample was prepared by pooling tumor tissues from 28 patients and labels by TMT-131 channel. The second reference sample was pooled from a total of 28 pairs of tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues and labeled with TMT-126 (labeling details are described below). These internal controls showed a mean correlation of 0.87 and 0.73 in the batch-to-batch abundance for the proteome and phosphoproteome dataset, respectively, indicating high technical reproducibility.

Protein extraction and tryptic digestion

The quantitative comparison for the fresh frozen paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues was performed by the TMT 10-plex-based proteomic and phosphoproteomic experiments. The protein extraction and digestion experiment was carried out as described previously [26]. Briefly, fresh frozen paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue were sliced into small pieces and washed by ice-cold PBS. The sliced tissue specimens were re-suspended in 10-fold volume of lysis buffer and homogenized by Precellys 24 homogenizer (Bertin Technologies). The homogenized samples were heated at 95 °C with vortexing at 750 r.p.m. for 5 min and sonicated for 10 min (30s on, 30s off) using Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode, Denville, NJ). Residual debris was removed by centrifugation (16,200 x g for 30 min at 4 °C). Then, methanol/chloroform protein precipitation methods were performed on the supernatant. Finally, the protein precipitate was resuspended in digestion buffer containing urea. The protein concentration was determined using BCA Protein Assay following the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Scientific). For tryptic digestion, 450 µg proteins from individual sample spiked with yeast ALD4 protein as an internal standard at a ratio of 1:250 (w: w) was reduced by 5 mM dithiothreitol at 29 °C for 30 min and alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide at 29 °C for 45 min in the dark. The samples were diluted with 50 mM TEABC and reacted with Lys-C at a ratio of 1:100 (w: w, Lys-C: protein) at 29 °C for 3 h, and followed by trypsin digestion with a ratio of 1:50 (w: w, trypsin: protein) for 18 h at 29 °C. The proteolytic digestion was stopped by adding TFA. The digested peptides were desalted by SDB-XC StageTip protocol described previously followed by BCA assay for measuring peptide concentration [115].

TMT 10-plex labeling

Desalted peptides from each sample were labeled with TMT10plex™ isobaric label reagents according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific) with a ratio of 0.8 mg reagent to 100 µg peptides. Synthetic β-casein standard phosphopeptide (FQpSEEQQQTEDELQDK) was spiked into tryptic peptides with a ratio of 1:250,000 (w: w) before TMT 10-plex labeling. For each set of TMT 10-plex, 126 and 131 channels were used to label the reference samples as described above. Four pairs of tumor and adjacent normal tissues were labeled with the other eight channels (adjacent normal tissues labeled with 127 N, 128 N, 129 N, and 130 N; tumor tissues labeled with 127 C, 128 C, 129 C, and 130 C). Two units of each TMT channel were freshly dissolved in anhydrous acetonitrile (ACN) with a ratio of 0.8:41 (w: v, mg:µL); 82 µL of each TMT channel in ACN was further added into 200 µg peptides dissolved in 200 µL of 100 mM TEABC. After incubation for 1 h at room temperature, the reaction was quenched by adding 16 µL of 5% hydroxylamine and incubated for 15 min. Then, 10 channels of TMT-labeled peptides were pooled together and desiccated by Speed-Vac for High-pH RPLC fractionation.

Peptide fractionation by high-pH RPLC

To increase the profiling depth of proteome and phosphoproteome, peptide fractionation was performed by high-pH reverse phase liquid chromatography (RPLC). Pooled and dried TMT 10-plex labeled peptides were re-dissolved in 600 µL of 5 mM ammonium formate (pH 10) and 2% ACN, and loaded on a 4.6 mm x 250 mm Zorbax 300 Å Extend-C18 column (Agilent, 3.5 μm bead size) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min on Waters alliance e2695 LC instrument coupled with 2489 UV/Visible detector and fraction collector III. Solvent A (2% ACN, 5 mM ammonium formate, pH 10) and a nonlinear increasing percentage of solvent B (90% ACN, 5 mM ammonium formate, pH 10) were used for a 120-min LC gradient for peptide fractionation, starting with 100% solvent A for 10 min, increased linearly to 12% B in 4 min, 40% B in 63 min, 60% B in 7 min, 90% B in 15 min, and maintained at 90% B for 8 min. Peptides were collected every minute for a total of 96 fractions from 11 to 106 min and combined into 24 fractions with a stepwise concentration strategy. Following desalting with SDB-XC StageTip, 10% of each fraction were aliquoted and dried by vacuum centrifugation for proteome analysis. The remaining 90% of concatenated fractions were further combined into 12 fractions, dried with Speed-Vac and stored at -80 °C until phosphopeptide enrichment.

Phosphopeptide enrichment

The phosphopeptide enrichment was performed by home-made immobilized metal ion affinity chromatography (IMAC) StageTip. For the preparation of IMAC beads, 40 µl Ni-NTA superflow agarose beads (Qiagen) was used for each sample, washed with water for 3 times, and removed the supernatant. Then 250 µL of 100 mM EDTA was added in the mixture and incubated for 30 min, followed by washing the beads with water for 3 times. To generate the Fe-NTA beads for the phosphopeptide enrichment, 200 µL of 100 mM FeCl3 was incubated with above washed beads for 30 min, washed the mixture with water for 3 times and removed the supernatant. The iron exchanged activating beads were resuspended in 60 µL of reaction buffer with a ratio of 1:1:1 (100% methanol:100% ACN:0.01% Acetic Acid) and then add into 200 µg tryptic peptides for 30 min incubation. After the enrichment, replace the reaction buffer by resuspending in 80% ACN/0.1% TFA. Next, the StageTip was activated with methanol, washed with 50% ACN/0.1% FA and equilibrated with 200 µL 0.1% FA twice prior to sample loading. Enriched phosphopeptide sample was loaded onto the StageTip with centrifugation (1000 x g, 3 min). After successive washes with washing buffer A (80% ACN, 0.1% TFA) and washing buffer B (0.1% FA), the bound phosphopeptides were eluted twice with 150 µL of 500 mM potassium phosphate buffer and desalted using RP StageTips.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Proteome analysis

For proteome analysis, LC-MS/MS analysis was performed with a Thermo Scientific™ UltiMate™ 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The fractionated peptides were separated using Thermo Scientific™ PepMap™ C18 50 cm x 75 μm ID column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 6–30% ACN gradient in 0.1% FA over 200 min at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The spectra of full MS scan (m/z 400–1500) were acquired in the Orbitrap mass analyzer at 120,000 resolution for a maximum injection time of 50 ms with an AGC target value of 4e5. The data-dependent mode cycle time was set at 3 s with an isolation window of 0.7 Th. MS2 precursors were fragmented by HCD using a normalized collision energy of 38% and analyzed using the Orbitrap at 50,000 resolution for a maximum injection time of 86 ms with AGC target value of 1.3e5. Precursor ions with unassigned charge state as well as charge state of 1+, or superior to 7 + were excluded from fragmentation selection.

Phosphoproteome analysis

For phosphoproteome analysis, LC-MS/MS analysis was performed with a Thermo Scientific™ UltiMate™ 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Enriched phosphopeptides were dissolved in 1% formic acid and separated using Thermo Scientific™ PepMap™ C18 50 cm x 75 μm ID column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 5–28% ACN gradient in 0.1% FA over 200 min at a flow rate of 250 nL/min. The full MS spectra (m/z 400–1500) were acquired in Orbitrap at 120,000 resolution with an AGC target value of 4e5 for a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The data-dependent mode cycle time was set at 3 s with an isolation window of 0.7 Th. MS2 precursors were fragmented by HCD using a normalized collision energy of 36%. The AGC target value was set to 1.3e5 and the dynamic exclusion was set to 20s. MS2 spectra were acquired in Orbitrap with a maximum injection time of 86 ms at a resolution of 50,000. Precursor ions with the charge state of unassigned, 1+, or superior to 7 + were excluded from fragmentation selection.

Mass spectrometry data analysis

Identification of proteins and phosphosites by database search

All MS raw files from the same batch were processed together with Proteome Discoverer (ver. 2.5) by Mascot (ver. 2.3.2) and SequestHT engines against the Uniprot human protein database (version 2018.10, 20,410 entries), combined with the spiked internal standard ALD4 protein from yeast (SwissProt accession: P46367) for proteome analysis and with β-casein protein (SwissProt accession: P02666) for phosphoproteome analysis. The tolerance for spectra search allowed 10 ppm mass tolerance for precursor, 0.02 Da for product ions, and trypsin enzyme specificity with up to 2 missed cleavages. All search engines considered static carbamidomethylation (+ 57.022 Da) on Cys residues and TMT modifications (+ 229.163 Da) on the peptide N terminus and Lys residues, as well as dynamic oxidation (+ 15.995 Da) on Met residues, deamidation on Asn and Gln residues (+ 0.984Da), and acetylation on protein N terminus (+ 42.016 Da), while the phosphorylation on Ser, Thr and Tyr residues (+ 79.966Da) was additionally included as dynamic modification for phosphoproteome analysis. The minimal peptide length was set as 6 residues. The false discovery rate (FDR) of peptide and protein were all set as 1%. For phosphosite localization, the ptmRS was used to determine phosphosite confidence and the phosphosite probability > 0.75 is considered as confident phosphosites.

Quantification of proteome and phosphoproteome data

The quantification analysis for proteome dataset was performed at the protein level by Proteome Discoverer (ver. 2.5). The protein abundance was calculated from the sum of peptide abundance with quan value corrections and co-isolation threshold filtering < 50%. The protein-level TMT signal-to-noise (S/N) values of each batch were exported from Proteome Discoverer and unique protein groups were consolidated in a single table containing all samples (columns=samples, rows=proteins). Protein S/N values were normalized by column-median to correct for equal loading across samples. The normalized S/N values were log2-transformed and the row-mean was subtracted from each protein for each batch separately to obtain log2-scaled values. Additionally, log2 T/N values were computed using the normalized S/N values, which were further transformed to column z-scores and centered at zero (row-wise) to obtain the centered-log2T/N values. All normalization steps were performed in Perseus 1.6 [116].

The quantification analysis for phosphoproteome data was performed at phosphopeptide level by Proteome Discoverer. The TMT S/N values of phosphopeptides mapping to the same site were aggregated by median values. Phosphosite S/N values were normalized by column-median to correct for equal loading across samples and log2T/N values were computed. These were further transformed to column z-scores and centered at zero to obtain the centered-log2T/N values. All normalization steps were also performed in Perseus 1.6. To analyze the phosphorylation changes, the log2T/N value of each phosphosite was corrected for the respective protein log2T/N value using linear regression to obtain net phosphorylation changes.

Quality control evaluation

To evaluate the data quality of every batch of proteomics and phosphoproteomics datasets, we used proteins (n = 5,067) or phosphopeptides (n = 2,320) commonly identified in all proteomics or phosphoproteomics experiments for pairwise Pearson correlation coefficient analysis. The log2 (TMT126/TMT131) ratios derived from column z-score transformed intensity were used to generate pairwise scatter plots for Pearson correlation and histogram of each proteomic and phosphoproteomic analysis by using psych R package.

Identification of variant peptides

After somatic mutation detection for each patient, based on the UCSC hg19 gene annotations, mutations were predicted from the amino acid change by customProDB (R package). For each patient, we retrieved nonsynonymous mutations and generated patient-specific amino acid sequences with variant amino acids resulting from somatic mutations as the individualized database. Then we combined the individualized databases from patients within the same protein analysis batch into one reference database for variant peptide identification.

All MS raw files were processed using Proteome Discoverer (ver. 2.5) with SequestHT search engines against the SwissProt human protein database (version 2018.10, 20,410 entries), combined with a batch-specific mutated protein database derived from the WES data from 4 patients in the same batch. The tolerance of all spectra allowed +/- 10 ppm mass tolerance for precursor, +/- 0.02 Da for product, and up to 2 missed cleavages for trypsin enzyme specificity. All search engines considered static carbamidomethylation (+ 57.022 Da) on Cys residues and TMT modifications (+ 229.163 Da) on the peptide N terminus and Lys residues, as well as dynamic oxidation (+ 15.995 Da) on Met residues, deamidation on Asn and Gln residues (+ 0.984 Da), and acetylation on protein N terminus (+ 42.016 Da). Peptide identification stringency was set at a maximum FDR of 1% and a minimum peptide length as 7 residues. Peptide spectral matches (PSMs) were validated using a percolator based on q-values at 1%. Unique proteins with < 1% FDR were considered as positive identifications. The PSMs of identified variant peptides were manually confirmed. We also cross-checked the TMT reporter intensity in the variant PSM and the patient-derived variant database to identify the variant-carrying patient. The identified variant peptides that had isobaric substitutions were excluded.

Multi-omic data analysis

Unsupervised clustering by mutation, mRNA, proteome and phosphoproteome data

After deconvoluting frequencies of 96 trinucleotides motifs in all patients into mutation patterns of 3 signatures and signature contributions for each patient, Euclidean distance was used to estimate signature contribution similarity between samples. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering linkaged by Ward’s method was applied to cluster patients into 10 subgroups. We further classified patients into four genomic mutagenesis groups. Unsupervised clustering of the patient samples at the different molecular levels was performed with the ConsensusClusterPlus R package [117] using the top 70% most variable of common RNAs, proteins and phosphopeptides based on standard deviation ranking. For the proteomics and RNA data, genes with missing values were pre-excluded whereas for the phosphoproteomics data with less than 30% missing values, we imputed the missing data from the normal distribution within a range of 20% of the standard deviation of all values in Perseus 1.6. Patient clusters were derived based on k-means clustering, Euclidean distance and 1,000 resampling repetitions in the range of 2 to 7 clusters. The empirical cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot initially showed optimal separation between 3 and 4 clusters for all the molecular levels. To assess the stability of the k = 3 and k = 4 consensus clusters we made silhouette plots in RStudio using the “cluster” library. The statistically significant clinical features among patients were enriched by Fisher’s exact test (Benj. Hoch. FDR < 0.05).

Functional annotation and pathway analysis

In order to investigate expression profile of KEGG pathways between genomic groups, pathway scores for each patient were calculated using the log2 T/N ratio of gene expression for single-sample gene set-enrichment analysis (ssGSEA, GSVA package of R software). Multivariable linear regression with covariates, age and PAM subtypes was used to identify differentially expressed pathways and genes. Pathway enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes of genomic groups was performed (FDR < 0.05). The log2T/N values of proteins and phosphopeptides were considered with the differential expression in genomic groups, H-TMB, proteomic groups, and recurrent cohorts by statistical analysis (Kruskal-Wallis test, p < 0.05, Pairwise Wilcoxon Tests, Benj.-Hoch. FDR < 0.05). These targets were further analyzed by Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). The mean log2T/N values on the expression of proteins and phosphopeptides of individual patients was computed by 1D-annotation pathway enrichment analysis. Functional annotation and KEGG pathway of proteome and phosphoproteome was performed with Fisher’s exact test using the 1D-annotation pathway enrichment method in Perseus 1.6 [116, 118]. The enrichment score indicates whether the proteins in a given biological term tend to be systematically up- or downregulated based on Mann-Whitney U test. All enriched pathways were filtered for Benj.-Hoch. FDR < 0.05.

PAM50 subtyping

PAM (Prediction Analysis of Microarray) 50 gene expression profile is used to do intrinsic subtyping for luminal A, luminal B, HER2, basal, and normal breast cancer subtypes and reference for clinical treatment decision and prognosis prediction [23]. Therefore, normalized gene expression of the tumor part was subjected to the method of principal component analysis-based iterative PAM50 subtyping (PCA-PAM50 R package) for PAM50 subtyping.

Kinase enrichment analysis

The comprehensive kinase-substrate relationships annotated in the PhosphoSitePlus database [75] were used to perform the kinase enrichment analysis from the differentially changed phosphopeptide substrates Unique phosphorylation motifs were identified as a 15-mer sequence (± 7 amino acids surrounding the phosphorylation site). The median log2 T/N values of individual sites was implemented to rank the phosphorylation sites and was submitted to Perseus to annotate their corresponding upstream kinases.

HLA genotyping analysis and neoantigen prediction

HLA class I genotype with 4 digit was inferred by POLYSOLVER using whole exon sequencing (https://software.broadinstitute.org/cancer/cga/polysolver) [119]. Nonsynonymous somatic mutations were selected and translated into mutated peptide sequences. Binding affinity between mutated peptide sequences with 8 to 14 amino acids and HLA class I allele was predicted by the NetMHC (v4.0) (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetMHC/) [120]. The predicted binding affinity (IC50) < 50 nM was considered as strong binding and binding affinity between 50 and 500 nM was weak binding.

Tumor microenvironment profiling

CIBERSORT software was used to estimate immune cell fractions in the tumor environment using gene expression profiling of tumor part from RNA-seq data against the gene expression profile of 22 immune cell types [121]. According to fractions of 22 immune cells in each patient, we clustered patients using hierarchical clustering with euclidean distance linkaged by Ward’s method and divided them into active and non-active immunophenotypes. Besides, stomal scores and immune scores were also estimated using ESTIMATE package in RNA-seq data of tumor part to predict the level of tumor-infiltrating stromal cells and immune cells mainly composed of non-tumor constituents in the tumor samples [51]. The abundance of tumor and adjacent normal tissues as well as their log2 T/N values of a total of 547 immune-related proteins from 22 immune cells were further collected from proteomic data for each patient [121]. The statistical difference in active and non-active immunophenotypes was estimated for significant protein enrichment. Student’s t test or Welch’s t test, as the unequal variances of testing groups, was performed to test the difference in continuous variables. All tests were performed by two-tailed test and p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) scores

Copy number variation and tumor ploidy were determined first using paired tumor-normal WES data as input for sequenza R package. Then the results were submitted to the scarHRD R package to estimate the levels of telomeric allelic imbalance, loss of heterozygosity and large-scale transitions. The three measurements were combined into one summary measure of HR deficiency as HRD score [122].

Cell viability

The cell lines MCF7 and T-47D were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MDA-MB-361 was purchased from Bioresouces Collection and Reseach Cencer (BCRC; Hsinchu, Taiwan). The 17pe was primary cell line derived from the pleural effusion of a 45-year-old Taiwanese female patient with Stage IV breast cancer. The 17pe and MCF7 cells were maintained in DMEM media and MDA-MB-361, BT474, HCC1500, T-47D and ZR75-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 media. Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The MDA-MB-361 and BT474 cells were seeded at 8,000 cells/well, MCF7 cells were seeded at 5,000 cells/well, T-47D and ZR75-1 were seeded 4,000 cells/well, and 17pe and HCC1500 were seeded at 3,000 cells/well into 96-well plates to adhere overnight. Following media removal, the cells were provided with the CDK inhibitors, Abemaciclib (LY2835219) and NVP-2, as indicated concentration. After 72 h incubation, the thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was performed to determine the cell viability according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After subtraction of the media-only read (blank), cell viability (%) was calculated relative to untreated cells. Abemaciclib (LY2835219) was a highly selective pan-CDK inhibitor and NVP-2 was an ATP-competitive CDK9 inhibitor (Selleck Chemicals LLC). For silencing CDK9, the MCF7 and T-47D cells were transfected with siCDK9 using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermofisher) for 48 h and applied on the following experiments.

Immunoblot

The cells were collected and incubated in the RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150 mM sodium chloride, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) containing 1X Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Diagnostics). The protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore) which were blotted with indicated antibodies: anti-CDK9 Ab, anti-phosphoPOLR2A-S2 Ab, anti-POLR2A Ab (Sigma-Aldrich) and anti-beta-actin Ab (GeneTex). Proteins were detected by the Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore) and detected by FUJIFILM LAS-4000 ECL system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Methods of statistical analysis for all experiments and omic analyses were described in the Results, figure legends, and Method Details subsections.

Correlation between mRNA and protein expression was estimated by Spearman rank correlation coefficient. Additionally, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the mutation frequencies between genomic subtypes. For each subtype, the p-value for the age decreasing or increasing pattern across mutagenesis groups was estimated using the linear regression. The statistical difference in age distribution between TCGA cohort and Taiwan cohort was estimated from the interaction term of two-way ANOVA. Besides, a two-sample t-test was used to estimate the age difference between 2 cohorts within each subtype. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare the number of non-ref germline loci, protein expression, immune score, stromal score, HRD score, and pathway score between groups. For enrichment of significant proteins, Student’s t test or Welch’s t test, as the unequal variances of testing groups, was performed. All tests were performed by two-tailed tests and p-values < 0.05 were considered significance.

Survival analysis

The disease-free survival (DFS) analysis was performed to evaluate the correlation between the carcinogen genomic groups or protein subtypes and clinical outcome of patients. Besides, survival analysis was also performed for the independent validation cohort to evaluate the selected gene expression for potential utility of the recurrence biomarker candidate. Gene expression levels were classified as low or high based on the median cut-off value. Events were defined as disease relapse or death from any cause after operation date. Kaplan–Meier (KM) method was used to derive survival curves and log-rank test was applied to estimate p-values. A hazard ratio was calculated to evaluate the clinical outcome, that 1 means a lack of association and a hazard ratio > 1 suggests an unfavorable outcome. Furthermore, the association between protein expression and clinical outcomes observed in our cohort was validated in independent public datasets using the KM-plotter platform (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [66, 81, 82]. In the KM-plotter platform, the “Tang_2018” dataset (n = 118) from the breast cancer protein module was used for protein-level analysis, while breast cancer mRNA gene chip datasets (n = 343) were used for transcript-level analysis. Overall survival (OS) and post-progression survival (PPS) were evaluated separately based on expression levels, with statistical significance determined using the log-rank test. All statistical tests were executed with R software. P-values of two-tail tests < 0.05 were considered statistical significance.
