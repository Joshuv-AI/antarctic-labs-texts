Abstract
. Benchmarking is a common practice in software engineering to assess the qualities and performance of software variants, coming from multiple competing systems or from configurations of the same system. Benchmarks are used notably to compare and understand variant performance, fine-tune software, detect regressions, or design new software systems. The execution of benchmarks to get a complete picture of software variants is highly costly in terms of computational resources and time. In this paper, we propose a novel approach for reducing benchmarks while maintaining stable rankings, using test suite optimization techniques. That is, we remove instances from the benchmarks while trying to keep the same rankings of the variants on all tests. Our method, BISection Sampling, BISS, strategically retains the most critical tests and applies a novel divide-and-conquer approach to efficiently sample among relevant remaining tests. We experiment with datasets and use cases from LLM leaderboards, SAT competitions, and configurable systems for performance modeling. Our results show that our method outperforms baselines even when operating on a subset of variants. Using BISS, we reduce the computational cost of the benchmarks on average to 44% and on more than half the benchmarks by up to 99% without loss in ranking stability.

Benchmarking is a fundamental practice in software engineering that focuses on evaluating the qualities and performance of software systems.
It is also essential in computer science for tasks such as optimization of algorithms or evaluation of system performance
(

In computational science, scientists use benchmarking to explore, compare and validate the performance of complex variants of simulations and models, facilitating advances in research and development
(
Donoho 2024
;
Guyon and Viegas 2020
)
.
Benchmarks can be seen as a special kind of test that is used to measure software performance such as execution time, energy consumption, or accuracy.
Unlike unit or integration tests that return binary or ternary test results (i.e., Pass/Fail/inconclusive), benchmarking returns numerical evaluations, which makes them more challenging to work with.
This is why software system benchmarking usually involves comparisons with other software variants.
Hence, benchmarks are executed on different variants, whether they originate from different competing, alternative systems, or various configurations of the same system.

These benchmarks,
workloads, test sets, performance tests, inputs, or problems
typically consist of a set of test cases to measure and compare the software variants’ performance.
The ranking of software variants based on benchmark results provides an objective quantitative measure of their relative performance.
This ranking not only highlights discrepancies in variant performance, but also offers a concrete basis for comparing the diversity of software solutions across different problem domains.
In addition, rankings can reveal patterns and trends in performance across various types of inputs or problem characteristics, providing valuable, data-driven insights for software optimization and development strategies.

Benchmarks and performance comparisons are crucial for understanding the performance characteristics of software variants, configuring and fine-tuning software, detecting regressions, and guiding the design of new systems.
However, executing comprehensive benchmarks to achieve a complete understanding of software variants can be extremely costly in terms of computational resources and time.
For instance, LLM leaderboards
(

)
or SAT competitions
(

;

)
are two well-known examples of benchmarking competitions that require extensive computational resources to evaluate the performance of variants.
The need for more efficient methods to maintain accurate rankings of software variants while minimizing the computational load is evident in several concrete scenarios:

•

Comparison:
In competitive settings or for the sake of improvements, numerous software variants must be evaluated and ranked.
For instance, leaderboards are becoming more and more available to provide engineers with a variety of benchmarks and the associated performance of the variants.
Full benchmarks are often prohibitively expensive, especially with many entries.
A minimized benchmark significantly reduces computational costs while still accurately ranking top performers and revealing their strengths and weaknesses.

•

Regression testing
: Executing reduced benchmarks on each commit or release allows for frequent performance checks,
enabling quick detection of regressions’ rankings, for example, in continuous integration. This approach facilitates rapid iterations and more thorough exploration of alternatives in both software engineering and scientific computing, with better-informed decisions and continuous performance assessment;

•

Performance modeling, extrapolation, and configuration tuning:
Engineers often use mathematical models and simulations to predict software performance under various conditions or to find optimal configurations
(
Balsamo et al. 2004
)
. By selecting a subset of representative benchmarks, they can efficiently train models that capture essential performance distributions. When benchmarking is training
(

•

Benchmark design and elaboration:
In case benchmarks are repetitive or identical, a strategy to design better benchmarks can be to focus on the most informative tests and extend them;

•

Benchmarking a new system:
When new systems (variants) are considered, reduced benchmarks can be leveraged at a better cost to get a preliminary performance comparison with competitors.

It can boost innovation and productivity by enabling more efficient evaluation or simply reducing the cost of benchmarking.

Our optimization goal thus differs and consists in preserving the performance ranking of potentially independent software variants. In this paper, we propose a novel method to reduce the computational cost of benchmarks while still effectively ranking software variants using test suite optimization techniques.
Our method, called BISS for BISection Sampling, is based on the fact that some tests cannot be removed and form the essential core of the benchmark. Furthermore, once some tests are removed, other tests become crucial as well.

We couple it with a divide-and-conquer algorithm to reduce the variance and speed up the search, and with an automatic restart (iterative solving) as long as we find improvements. An artifact with all the datasets and benchmarks needed to reproduce the results.

3.
Problem Statement: Ranked Test Suite Minimization (RTSM)

In software testing, test suite minimization focuses on reducing the number of tests needed while still ensuring that all requirements are met. Traditionally, these functional tests check Boolean requirements, where a test either passes or fails, similarly to solving set-cover instances. However, many scenarios involve performance-related tests that have continuous and quantitative answers.

Definition 3.0.

Given a set of configuration variants
\mathcal{A}
, a
performance value
is a function
t:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
that associates a performance value (e.g. estimation, measurement, execution time, memory consumption, etc.) with each variant of a configurable system.

Individual performance values are usually difficult to get and verify. For example, checking that a system runs exactly in 2.42 seconds, as it has been registered from previous executions or predicted by an ML model, is very unlikely. Comparison of this with other observations coming from other variants’ performance estimations is usually needed.
Ranking performance estimations are a way to evaluate how a system operates and is widely used
(
Bartz-Beielstein et al. 2020
;
Lesoil et al. 2023
)
.

Given a set of configuration variants, we can typically estimate their performance on a set of test inputs, sum their performance over these inputs, and rank each variant accordingly.
Intuitively, given a full benchmark obtained from each variant’s performance estimation and its ranking of the variants, our goal is to compress the benchmark while preserving the ranking.

Definition 3.0.

The
rank of a variant

v\in\mathcal{A}
for a test suite
\mathcal{T}
is the rank
r
of its performance estimation
perf(v,\mathcal{T})=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}t(v)
, precisely:

r=Index(\argmax_{v\in\mathcal{A}}(perf(v,\mathcal{T}))

The variant with the maximal performance estimation is ranked
1
and so on.
Note that this ranking computes a total ordering over the variants in
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 3.0.

Given a set of variants
\mathcal{A}
and a test suite
\mathcal{T}
, the
Ranking Test Suite Minimization problem (RTSM)
aims to find a smallest
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}
and
W
such that the ranking of variants
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is preserved (i.e., the same as the ranking of variants
\mathcal{A}
on
\mathcal{T}
) where
W
gives a weight to each test in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
for ranking. Note that several subsets
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
can be solutions of RTSM.

Looking at the left part of the
Fig. 1

is determined by classifying the variants in ascending order with respect to the summed performance estimates. A solution to the RTSM problem is given on the right part of the figure. Keeping only 45 tests as a subset of

Note that test suite minimization is an instance of RTSM when one just ignores the ranking. As test suite minimization is NP-hard
(

, we can deduce from the previous remark that RTSM is also NP-hard.
In practice, for large instances, we will accept near-optimal solutions obtained by using
heuristics, anytime algorithms, or probabilistic approaches.

Definition 3.0.

Given a set of variants
\mathcal{A}
and a test suite
\mathcal{T}
with a cost associated with each test, the
Weighted Ranking Test Suite Minimization problem (WRTSM)
aims to find
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{T}
and

such that the ranking of variants
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is preserved (i.e., the same as the ranking of variants
\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{T}
) and that minimizes
cost(\mathcal{T}^{\prime})=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}cost(t)
where
W
gives a weight to each test in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
for ranking.

Error Metric

Unfortunately, there is no ground truth; therefore, we will compare the rankings obtained using the solution to the original solution.
There are multiple metrics to compare the rankings, the two most commonly used ones are Kendall’s
(
Kendall 1938
)
and Spearman’s
(
Spearman 1904
)
rank correlation coefficient.
Both give a score between -1, the two vectors offer opposite rankings, and 1, the two vectors offer exactly the same rankings.
We chose to use Kendall’s, which is directly proportional to the number of variants correctly ranked.
Choosing Spearman or Kendall does not matter when the aim is a score of 1, that is, the same ranking, because they both coincide for this data point.
Both tests are non-parametric hypothesis tests on the relations between the two rankings.
Kendall can be easily computed, assume that a pair of variants are concordant if they are ranked in the same order in both rankings and discordant otherwise, then we have:

\text{Kendall's }\tau=\frac{\#|concordant\ pairs|-\#|discordant\ pairs|}{\#|pairs|}

Special cases occur when there are ties, but in our scenarios, ties never occur.

Multi-metric

The current formulations in
Definition 3.2
and
Definition 3.3
use a single performance metric, but can be easily adapted to multiple metrics.
When multiple metrics are present, compute the Kendall for each metric independently and take the worst one.
For example, if we wanted a Kendall of .99 in the multi-metric case, then on all metrics the Kendall must at least be .99.

4.
Benchmark minimization with BISS

Novelty

As explained in
section 2
, most existing methods for minimizing the test suite require prior information such as test results, historical data, etc.
However, the scenarios for RTSM are very diverse, requiring handcrafted features by experts for their domain if we want to use a white-box approach, which are often not available or are too costly to acquire. Instead, we choose to look at black-box approaches.
We have no features about the variants; we have just an identifier to be able to identify them, and this is the same for the tests.
So, as input, we only have the performance matrix, that is, a matrix that contains the performance of each variant on each test.
Then techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) or racing techniques
(
López-Ibáñez et al. 2016
)
should solve this problem. We will see that we tested these techniques and they do not solve our problem.
Indeed, these classic techniques can work to some extent, but we are interested in objectives that are a loose proxy of our interest: the ranking of variants. Whereas most of these techniques are interested in more fine-grained information: the magnitude of the differences, they care if you are twice as fast or ten times as fast, whereas in RTSM it does not matter.

Intuition

The core idea is that we do not care about the individual performances of the tests; we only care about the relative performance of a variant to another across tests.

Definition 3.2
gives us the possibility of assigning weights to
W
: Given a subset of the test set, we can use a linear regression to optimize
W
to obtain the best coefficients.
Let
P\in\mathbb{R}^{|Variants\times|\mathcal{T}|}
be the performance matrix of the variants in the test, then, given
\mathcal{T}
’ of
\mathcal{T}
, we can optimize:

\displaystyle\forall i\in\text{Variants, }P_{i\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}W^{T}=\sum_{j=0}^{|\mathcal{T}|}P_{ij}

This is a linear regression problem where
W
is a vector that assigns a weight to each test in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, which is the set of parameters that we try to find for each variant, given their performance in the variants, to predict their performance on all tests.
Our goal is therefore to sample relevant subsets
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
Formally, we assume that we have the procedure
solves_{T}(T^{\prime})
which solves the linear regression and checks if the Kendall target is reached with
T^{\prime}
as a solution to the problem with the complete test set being
T
, if it is, then the procedure returns
true
otherwise, it returns
false
.

As we have explained earlier, the problem is NP-hard, and covering all possible solutions is intractable for small test suites; for a test suite of size 30, there are more than a billion possible solutions.
The process is a bit akin to dimensionality reduction; each test acts as a dimension, and we want to reduce the dimension while keeping the ranks intact. Taking inspiration from the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma, which states that a random projection in the "low" dimension has a high probability of keeping the original distances between the data points in the original high dimension. The dimension that the lemma gives us is dependent logarithmically on the original dimension and grows inversely proportional to the square of the minimum distance between two data points. In other words, the closer two variants have similar performance, the more likely it is that we are going to need more tests to rank them correctly.
Therefore, our objective is to sample subsets of tests intelligently that are likely to result in a solution.

4.1.
Bisection Sampling

Obviously, we would like to sample more cleverly than pure random.
For that, we need to improve on two points: reducing the sampling variance and eliminating tests faster than one at a time.

Variance reduction

The idea is to draw on the following fact: If we add more tests to a solution to the RTSM problem, then it is also a solution. Then, conversely, if we have a set of tests that is not a solution, none of the subsets is a solution.

4.2.

High-level overview of our divide and conquer with an iterative strategy used in our BISS algorithm
The figure illustrates a classic divide and conquer approach on random subsets of the input test set except that if the solution subset is smaller than the original the algorithm is restarted with the solution.

The idea is to use a divide-and-conquer approach and to use bisection sampling as a subroutine.
Fig. 2
illustrates at a high level our divide-and-conquer approach with our iterative strategy.
In the first step, we split
\mathcal{T}
into multiple smaller sets and solve these smaller instances of the RTSM problem.
From then on, at each iteration, we will merge two solutions of the previous step.
We do this simply by considering the instance of the RTSM problems, where we keep only the tests in either solution.
This is a new instance of the RTSM problem that we solve.
We keep iterating until there is only one instance left.
In the worst case, in all of the sub-instances, we could not find better solutions than including every test of the sub-instance; therefore, the last step is similar to not doing any divide and conquer, but in all other cases, we potentially solve smaller problems, enabling faster solving.
The initial split thus has a large variance and, therefore, can have a large impact on the performance of this approach.

The process is described in detail in
Algorithm 3
. But before diving into the explanations of the algorithm, let us convince ourselves that this process gives a valid solution.
Given two solutions
A
and
B
for the RTSM instances
T_{A}
and
T_{B}

S

also a solution to the entire RTSM instance

The answer is no, the reason being that when we build the predictions, we build them on the exact true data, so when we consider only
A\cup B
we may be able to remove the tests because we can predict them accurately up to their ranks but not accurately enough for use in the prediction of other tests.
To do this correctly at minimal cost, when a solution

A\cup B

T_{A}\cup T_{B}
, if it is we accept it and continue otherwise we try again a certain number of times until we arbitrarily set

to
A\cup B
.

Finally, we combine our divide-and-conquer with

Iterative solving solves the RTSM problem from an already existing solution and follows the idea that if a smaller cost test set is found, then we may try one more time to find another improvement until no improvement is found.

5.
Other Approaches

We describe a few methods inspired by related work and intuition that we consider as baselines.
They can also be used as divide-and-conquer subroutines, including iterative solving, algorithms, and will try solutions and test them with
solves
.

Our method is called BISS. All methods, unless specified, use divide-and-conquer and iterative solving.

Random Search RANDOM

Random search is a simple baseline that can be easily adapted to the RTSM problem.
The random search involves randomly sampling a set of tests and then checking if the set forms a solution that can be returned at any time.
Rather than sampling among all possible candidates, the process can be adapted to be iterative.
Initially, there are
n
tests in the suite, then we randomly search for a set of tests of size
n-1
.

Greedy Algorithm GREEDY

Another simple black-box baseline in a greedy search approach. At each step, we can try to remove the test that provides the least information; since all tests have the same weight, we chose to remove the test with the lowest metric variance because, on average, it should contribute the least to the ranking. Note that we also tried with the lowest mean metric, and the results were approximately the same, so we decided not to report this variant for brevity reasons.

Mixed Integer Linear Programming MILP

The WRTSM problem can be transformed into an MILP problem, except that since our decision variables are the presence or absence of a test in the solution, this disables the ability to also optimize
W
, which weights the tests in the subsets.
Let us use the notations introduced in
Definition 3.3
:
\mathcal{A}
is the set of variants,
\mathcal{T}
is the set of tests,
p_{k,i}
if the performance of variant
k
in the test
i
,
cost(t_{i})
is the cost of the test
t_{i}
and the weights of the solution
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for the test
t_{i}
.
Let us explain our encoding; the presence or absence of the test
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
in the solution is encoded as
{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\in\{0,1\}
.
Our constraints are encoded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{k,i}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked lower than variant
l

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{k,i}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked higher than variant
l

which translates to the ranking should be the same. Thus, our objective is to minimize:

\min\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times cost(t_{i})

However, this formulation does not take into account the weights of the tests in the solution. They can be included in the following way into our constraints for variant
k
and variant
l
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}\times p_{k,i}\leq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked lower than variant
l

\displaystyle\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}\times p_{k,i}\geq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked higher than variant
l

Notice that this is no longer a linear program, since both the
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}
and the
{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}
are decision variables.
Therefore, we use the previous linear version even though it solves a simpler version of the problem, but with less agency; therefore, the solutions of the MILP approaches are not optimal for the problem where the
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}
can be set, although they are optimal for the
{\color[rgb]{1,0.2656,0.1992}w_{i}}=1
cases.
Observe that this formulation also only enables us to consider a Kendall of exactly 1, if we wanted to consider a Kendall of
T
, then we would use the following formulation for variant
k
and variant
l
:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{k,i}\leq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked lower than variant
l

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{k,i}\geq\sum_{i}{\color[rgb]{0.1172,0.5352,0.8984}u_{i}}\times p_{l,i}

if variant
k
is ranked

with
{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}\in\{-1,1\}
, they are also decision variables and the additional constraint that:

\frac{1}{2|\mathcal{A}|^{2}}\sum_{k,l\in\mathcal{A}}{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}\geq T

since
{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}=1
for a concordant pair and
{\color[rgb]{0.2266,0.5859,0.0078}c_{k,l}}=-1
for a discordant pair, translating exactly the left-hand side into the definition of the Kendall.
This means that changing the Kendall also implies that this is not a linear program anymore, thus MILP is a very rigid approach.
Note that MILP is used as a standalone baseline without divide-and-conquer.
In our implementation, MILP uses the COIN (CBC) solver
(
John Forrest et al. 2024
)
.

Principal Component Analysis PCA

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a technique used to reduce dimensionality while minimizing loss in terms of variance.
We use PCA on the performance matrix and look at the most important vector, the one who explains most of the variance. Then we choose to remove the test that is least important for this vector, that are the one whose coefficient is the least correlated with this vector.

5.1.
Failed Attempts

In this section, we report other attempts to obtain some baselines that were not included in our experimental results.
We decided not to include them due to poor results compared to existing baselines but believe it is of scientific interest to report what we failed to make work.

Racing Algorithm

Based on racing algorithms such as
irace

(
López-Ibáñez et al. 2016
)
, we adapt their approach based on Friedman’s test
(
and 1937
)
and produce the same decisions
irace
would take.
In other words, it works iteratively and chooses a test to remove at each step.
The test chosen is based on Friedman’s test used in
irace
to detect the poorest performers; here, our intuition is that it should detect the test that has the least discriminative power and remove it.
We implemented it in such a way that if we run
irace
on our setup, except that it does not have new data at each step, then
irace
would remove the same tests in the same order.
Applying it in practice over all our benchmarks led to no cost reduction at all on all seeds; therefore, this baseline is discarded.

Genetic Algorithm

There is a large body of work on the minimization of the test suite that uses genetic algorithms
(
Harman and Jones 2001
;
Yoo and Harman 2012
)
.
However, most of these approaches are white- or gray-box approaches. Unlike these approaches, there are neither features nor associated code; therefore, we cannot adapt their approaches.
We can still adapt the approach of genetic algorithms to our case.
Our genes consist of zeros and ones; a one indicates that we keep a test in the test suite, and a zero indicates that it is discarded. Then, we can frame the RTSM problem as a multi-objective optimization problem by considering two independent objectives, namely 1) minimizing the size of the test suite and 2) minimizing the ranking error so that it stays below the desired threshold.
Genetic algorithms, unlike other methods, cannot guarantee that the ranking error stays below the desired threshold; that is why we have to consider it as an objective function to minimize it.
In order for genetic algorithms to work with our two objectives, we use the following formula:

score(T)=\begin{cases}accuracy(T)\ \text{if $T$ is not accurate}\\
10+(1-\frac{size(T)}{N})\end{cases}

When we say ‘
T
is not accurate’, we mean that it does not predict the classification of the variants to the required accuracy, that is, there is too much classification error.
The idea is that we favor the sets
T
that match the required accuracy threshold and then rank them according to their size, since
\frac{size(T)}{N}
describes the ratio of tests kept from the problem. It is close to a linear combination, but since we want a solution in a limited time, we observed that we need to bias for correct solutions with the desired accuracy, to always get solutions within the timeouts.

We tried changing the score to a linear combination or using a multi-objective genetic algorithm, and we tried different variations of the parameters of the genetic algorithm to no avail: we only found worse-performing versions.
Since we did not make genetic algorithms work correctly, they are not included in the experiments.

6.1.
Experimental details

We implemented our methods in Python; an archive of the code is available as supplementary material along with all additional figures and tables.
We will report Kendall’s
\tau
as mentioned previously (see
section 3
).
All experiments were run on a single core on Intel Xeon Gold 6130 (16 cores, 2017), and our algorithms returned the best result found so far.
Working with multiple cores can speed up the results almost linearly, and better results may be achieved with a longer timeout on a single core, since all methods except MILP are anytime algorithms.
Since our methods are stochastic, we conducted our experiments on 10 different seeds and reported mean performance along with the confidence interval 95% and performed statistical tests when necessary.
The timeouts were 50 minutes per task for all datasets.
As a reminder, all methods use divide-and-conquer and iterative solving except MILP.

6.5.
RQ3: How is minimization affected with only a subset of the variants?

For this RQ, as mentioned above, we only consider MILP, BISS and RANDOM.
We sampled a fraction of all the variants for each benchmark, then ran the minimization procedure, and measured the cost reduction and Kendall on the full set of variants.
Unlike RQ2, we only consider targeting a Kendall of 1, the same ranking, because we are already losing information by removing variants.
This experiment reproduces a natural competition using our approach. The benchmark is minimized with only a subset of all variants, then new variants are submitted by competitors, and the reduced benchmark is used.
We chose to keep 75% 50%, and 25% of all variants.
We sampled 10 random subsets for each percentage and report the mean performance with a confidence interval 95%.

However, there are two metrics to consider: the cost reduction and the Kendall.
Intuitively, if the cost is reduced by 10%, for an optimal trade-off, the Kendall should not be more than 10% lower.
Therefore, we introduce a single metric that mixes the two in order to easily compare and run statistical tests on the performance of our methods.
This metric is the score and gives equal weight to a cost reduction and a Kendall reduction:
score(c,k)=\frac{2c+(1+k)}{4}
where
c\in[0;1]
is the fraction of cost reduced and
k\in[-1;1]
is the Kendall coefficient.
By default, if no reduction is made, the obtained score is 0.5; another way to think about this is that if the score is above 0.5, then the trade-off starts to become interesting.

Since there are 50 different benchmarks, we produced 4 plots and a table for each benchmark, which are available in the supplementary material, since these would take more than 50 pages of PDF.
As a general trend, the lower the fraction of variants kept, the greater the variance observed among the results.
Surprisingly, we could expect the score to decrease with the lower fractions, but that is not always the case.
Generally, the MILP approach has a lower variance than our approaches.

Instead, we look at the summary offered by
Fig. 3
, where we plot the cumulative distribution of the scores between seeds and benchmarks.
The goal is to have a higher score, so the smaller the area under the curve, the better the method.
Also note that on average, if a proportion greater than 0.5 is reached before a score of 0.5, then on average it means that the method is not worth it: we lose more than we gain either in terms of Kendall or in terms of reduction.
Interestingly, this is the case for MILP, which is not worth it at all for different fractions of variants.
In contrast, BISS and RANDOM are below this threshold, and are generally worth it.

In all scenarios, it is clear that the area under the curve is minimal for BISS.
An interesting phenomenon occurs: the area under the curve tends to increase as the fraction of variants increases.
This may seem counterintuitive at first; however, what happens is that removing variants often enables our approaches to drastically reduce the cost while losing little to no Kendall, therefore increasing the score.
In other words, it is often the case that a benchmark cannot be reduced further because of a few variants that are hard to rank.

More generally, BISS offer scores of above:

•

0.9 in more than 10% of cases for 25% of variants, 20% of cases for 50% of variants and 25% of cases for 75% and 100% of variants;

•

0.8 in more than 40% of cases for all fractions of variants;

•

0.5 in more than 65% of cases for all fractions of variants.

The last item is not surprising indeed; with 100% of variants, only 66% of the benchmarks could be reduced.
Finally, in some scenarios, better solutions can be found when there are fewer variants than when given all variants, in other words, sometimes with less data, sometimes better solutions can be found.

7.2.
Case Study: SAT 18 and SAT 20: How does test features distribution change after minimization?

In this case study, we want to observe how the different statistical features of the tests evolve when we only select a subset of the tests.
We will use the 10 different solutions with 100% of variants obtained with BISS.
The minimization likely induces a significant change in the test set.
We use the features given in
(
Bischl et al. 2016
)
that were used for automated algorithm selection tasks.
Intuitively, if the variants are representative and the features relevant, then we should keep more or less similar distributional properties on the reduced test sets.
To check this, we look at the evolution of min, 1st quartile, median, 3rd quartile, max, mean, and std for all features.
We compute the median relative evolution of these characteristics across all features. The median has the interesting property that if only a few features vary wildly, this will not be seen in the median, and at the same time, if globally there are a lot of variations, the median will capture that.
We selected two datasets with varying minimization successes despite being from the same domain.
For SAT18, there are 54 features; we found cost reductions ranging from 45% up to 85%, which translates into keeping only between 38% and 85% of the original tests.
For SAT20, there are 108 features; we found cost reductions ranging from 38% up to more than 60% which translates into keeping only between 24% and 72% of the original tests.

The results are in
Table 5
for SAT 18 and
Table 6
for SAT 20.
Interestingly, in both cases, there almost always seems to be no change to the minimum,m indicating that perhaps it is almost always a good choice to keep it.
We could expect the same for max, but it is not as clear, it still seems to be very relevant since there are a lot of 0% but that is not always the case.
More precisely, the change from the 1st quartile to the median to the 3rd quartile is always increasing.
Intuitively, larger values for features usually translate into more complex instances, which are slower to solve; therefore, removing them provides greater cost reductions so it is likely that our approach focuses on removing these instances first.
The best minimization seems to have no relative changes in min or max.

For SAT 20, it is easy to observe that there are very few changes to the distributional property, with the highest relative changes never going over 9%.
For SAT 18, despite smaller changes to the test set than for SAT 20, there are more impactful changes up to 23% except for the 3rd quartile and max with changes of 36.87% and 26.43% respectively, this is likely due to greater cost reduction obtained for this benchmark.

8.
Threats to Validity

Internal threats.

Timeouts

The configuration of timeouts presents a potential threat to the internal validity of our study, but since most methods are anytime, this leaves as much time as needed for the different methods.

Stability

While it is theoretically true that adding more tests to a solution can only improve it, in practice, this is not always the case. We have observed that adding more tests can sometimes lead to instability. For example, our
FindNecessary
procedure (see
Algorithm 1
) may incorrectly identify certain elements as necessary when they are not. Unfortunately, we do not yet have a solution to this problem. However, our experimental results suggest that it is a minor issue. Our best experimental method is based on the
FindNecessary
procedure, and the results show that the impact of this issue is very low.

Generalizability

The effectiveness of our method relies on several hyperparameters, particularly within the divide-and-conquer part. These hyperparameters include the number of splits used to manage variance and the number of tests conducted during the iterative solving phase. While these parameters were selected manually, we avoided overfitting and extensive hyperparameter tuning to maintain the generalizability and robustness of our method across different datasets and scenarios. By selecting reasonable default values, we aimed to balance performance and applicability.
Besides, we repeated each experiment ten times to account for variability and random effects. This repetition did introduce variance in the results. We reported and analyzed the standard deviations across different runs as part of our findings.

External threats.
The applicability of our study to real-world situations is influenced by the types of data we used for the evaluation. We tested our method using specific benchmarks from well-known sources, but this may not be representative of all possible datasets and domains.
Our evaluation spans different domains (LLM, SAT, video, …), with variants being independent systems or configurations from the same system, and different scenarios (leaderboard, competitions, performance modeling).
An empirical question remains regarding the extent to which benchmarks can be minimized in each domain while still maintaining accurate rankings. Addressing this question is an ambitious task that would require extensive experiments. Our paper highlights the need for further exploration and assessment across broader datasets. We hope that other researchers will build on our work and conduct additional studies.

9.
Impact

In general, this analysis shows that some benchmarks are more amenable to minimization than others, highlighting the importance of domain specificities when using BISS.

Recommendations for benchmark design.
Benchmark designers, developers, creators can use our methods to design, understand, and refine their benchmarks. Our method automates this process by retaining the most relevant ones and, as shown, effectively removing tests that are too easy or repetitive.
It can be applied to existing benchmarks, effectively compressing them. We recommend using BISS since it generally outperforms other methods, and in case of bad results, to use MILP, which behaves quite differently. If benchmark designers want to test new variants on the reduced dataset (
e.g.,
to verify the hardness of the benchmarks), they should replicate the RQ2 experiments to ensure representativeness and control ranking error.
As experiments show, on average better results are achieved when most of the variants remain in the problem.

Recommendations for software developers
Developers consume benchmarks for performance comparison, nonregression, or tuning. Smaller benchmarks with the same discriminative power mean that consumers will maintain accuracy while spending less time executing their software on variants. For scenarios like tuning or nonregression, where benchmarks are run repeatedly, the gains are amplified with each run, resulting in substantial overall benefits. We thus offer the same recommendations as for benchmark design, and empirical results show that important gains can be achieved for reducing the computational cost.

In addition, our method enables strategic targeting of the most performance-critical tests.
While general recommendations remain, developers who need to focus on a very few instances can even reduce the target Kendall to find even smaller representative instances at the cost of accuracy. They can then improve their systems before testing on the larger benchmark. However, there is a risk of overfitting (
e.g.,
BISS may find one test case sufficient to rank) and of excessively aggressive tuning or non-regression assurance. Developers should balance these risks and gradually increase their confidence based on the computational budget.

Cost Saving Potentials

A highlight of our results is, for example, defects4j, which can be reduced to only 5% of the tests, saving up to hundreds of dollars per new LLM test.
These reductions can be observed on many different datasets at different scales.

Conclusions and future work

We have introduced the ranked test suite minimization (RTSM) and its solution. It is a new problem that aims to minimize a benchmark while keeping the performance ranking of the variants over the test suites intact.
This problem is highly relevant for practitioners, as it enables cost-effective benchmarking for variant comparison, rapid regression detection, performance modeling, benchmark design, etc.

Addressing this problem requires developing new approaches specifically designed to optimize benchmark selection while preserving variant ranking accuracy. We have introduced our approach BISS based on bisection sampling and supplemented with divide and conquer and iterative solving; and introduced baselines to compare to, including MILP, PCA, RANDOM, and others based on existing techniques.
Experiments on 50 different benchmarks show that divide-and-conquer enables a 4-times faster solving and that iterative solving always leads to improved results.
Iterative solving improves the solutions in 76% of the cases where a cost reduction was found.
Our experiments show that BISS outperforms all alternatives.
Furthermore, when reducing the target Kendall, we show that our approach highly benefits from it, by reducing the Kendall by 1% on average, we get a 10% improvement.
We also showed that our method behaves well even if all variants are not present, it often introduces some imprecision; however, we showed that in most cases, this is an interesting trade-off, we gain more cost reduction than we lose Kendall or Spearman.

In general, with BISS, more than half of the benchmarks could be reduced with a mean reduction over these reducible benchmarks of 67%.
This implies that quite extensive reductions are available for many benchmarks, including those used today.
Future work includes studying the applications and implications of our methods in different domains.
For example, the field of AI is driven by benchmark, standardizing such minimization approaches could drastically reduce the cost of research comparison, thus increasing research velocity.
Another direction is to work with partial data, that is, with some variants running only on a subset of the tests.
