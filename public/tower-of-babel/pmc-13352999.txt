Medication safety in emergency departments: a mixed methods study
Authors: Hande Tüzün, Fatma Cebeci, Songül Bişkin Çetin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication safety competence is a critical component of patient safety, particularly in emergency departments (EDs), where high-acuity conditions and workflow instability increase vulnerability to medication-related errors. Despite extensive research on medication safety, limited attention has been given to how medication safety competence is enacted within emergency department settings.MethodsA convergent mixed-methods design was employed. Data were collected between July and September 2024 from two large urban tertiary teaching hospitals. Quantitative data were obtained from 122 emergency nurses using the Medication Safety Competence Scale and were analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and one-way analysis of variance. Qualitative data were collected through structured written open-ended questions and analyzed using inductive content analysis. Integration was conducted through joint display analysis of quantitative and qualitative findings.ResultsStatistically significant differences were observed only in the patient-centered medication management subdimension, where nurses aged 30–39 years and those with ≥ 5 years of experience reported higher scores (p < .05). No significant differences were identified in the total scale score or other subdimensions. Mean item scores were generally high across all subdimensions, with the highest scores observed in patient-centered medication management, followed by responsibility in nursing. Qualitative analysis generated three themes: (1) self-assessment-based learning and development, (2) developing a medication safety culture, and (3) efficient organizational management. Integration revealed that although self-reported medication safety competence was high, its consistent enactment was influenced by cognitive workload, workflow interruptions, and the prevailing error reporting climate.ConclusionsMedication safety competence in emergency nursing is not solely an individual attribute but a context-dependent capacity shaped by professional development, collaborative culture, and organizational conditions. Strengthening medication safety competence therefore requires integrated educational and system-level interventions that enable its consistent enactment in emergency clinical practice.Keywords: Emergency department, Emergency nursing, Medication safety competence, Patient safety, Mixed methods research

Background

Medication administration constitutes one of the most safety-critical functions in emergency nursing practice and is directly linked to patient outcomes [1]. Emergency departments (EDs) operate in environments characterized by high patient acuity, compressed decision timelines, and continuous reprioritization—conditions that heighten susceptibility to medication errors [2]. The reported prevalence of medication errors in 22.6% of emergency care cases underscores the magnitude of risk embedded in ED medication processes [1].

In emergency care settings, medication administration occurs within complex and fragmented workflows involving multiple stages, including prescribing, transcribing, preparation, and administration, each associated with potential risks for error [3]. Evidence indicates that medication errors most frequently occur during prescribing and administration phases, particularly in high-acuity environments such as EDs [4].

In many settings, these risks are further exacerbated by the continued use of hybrid workflows, in which handwritten prescriptions are subsequently transcribed into electronic systems. Such processes have been identified as high-risk due to their susceptibility to transcription errors and communication breakdowns [5, 6].

Moreover, the absence or limited integration of system-level safeguards—such as clinical decision support systems (CDSS) and pharmacist-led medication review—has been identified as a key contributor to medication-related risks in emergency care [3]. Therefore, medication safety competence should be evaluated within the context of these system-level conditions rather than as an isolated individual attribute.

Within this high-pressure and system-constrained conditions, ED nurses assume primary responsibility for medication preparation, verification, administration, and monitoring [7, 8]. These responsibilities are executed under substantial cognitive load and time pressure, conditions associated with increased vulnerability to medication errors during administration in high-acuity settings [6, 9].

Although medication safety has been extensively studied, prior research has largely focused on error incidence, reporting behaviors, and organizational safety culture rather than on the clinical competence required to ensure safe medication processes [10–12]. While these studies have enhanced understanding of system-level risks, they provide limited insight into how medication safety competence is enacted in real-time clinical practice.

Importantly, organizational culture plays a critical role in shaping whether competence can be effectively enacted. In environments characterized by punitive responses to errors, healthcare professionals may avoid reporting mistakes or near misses due to fear of blame, thereby limiting opportunities for learning and improvement [13–15]. This suggests that even when competence is present, its translation into practice may be constrained by the absence of psychological safety.

However, despite its recognized role in influencing reporting behaviors, the relationship between safety culture—particularly non-punitive response to error—and the enactment of medication safety competence in emergency settings remains insufficiently explored.

Emerging evidence indicates that medication safety competence is associated with clinicians’ cognitive capabilities, including clinical reasoning and critical thinking, as well as with the characteristics of the nursing practice environment [16, 17]. These findings suggest that competence should be conceptualized not merely as an individual attribute but as a performance emerging through interaction with clinical work systems.

Although validated instruments have been developed to assess medication safety competence across domains such as knowledge, skills, and attitudes [18], such measures primarily capture perceived competence and may not fully reflect how competence is enacted in real-time clinical practice.

Accordingly, the present study employed a convergent mixed-methods design to (1) assess emergency nurses’ medication safety competence and (2) explore how this competence is enacted in practice, with particular attention to team dynamics, environmental conditions, and emotional regulation during medication processes in emergency care settings.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently, analyzed independently, and integrated during interpretation [19]. The quantitative component assessed nurses’ medication safety competence, while the qualitative component explored how this competence was experienced within ED practice. Integration aimed to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, and divergence between measured competence and contextual experiences.

The qualitative strand adopted a descriptive qualitative design with an inductive analytic orientation. This approach was selected to provide a direct and minimally theory-driven account of nurses’ experiences and perceptions related to medication safety practices, allowing themes to emerge from the data without imposing a predefined philosophical framework.

Quantitative data were collected using the Medication Safety Competence Scale for Nurses (MSCS) [20, 21]. Qualitative data were obtained through structured written open-ended questions, which participants completed in a self-administered format. These questions were developed in alignment with the study objectives and existing frameworks on medication safety competence [18, 20]. The structured format ensured consistency across participants while allowing respondents to elaborate on their experiences in their own words.

Integration of the quantitative and qualitative strands occurred during the interpretation phase through joint display analysis, whereby quantitative findings were systematically compared with corresponding qualitative themes to identify areas of convergence, complementarity, and discordance. This integrative process facilitated the generation of meta-inferences that extended beyond the results of either dataset alone [22].

The quantitative component was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [23] while the qualitative component adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [24]. The overall mixed-methods study was reported in line with the Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) to ensure methodological transparency and rigor [25].

Research setting and participants

The study was conducted in the adult EDs of two large urban tertiary teaching hospitals located in Türkiye. The first institution is a state training and research hospital serving as a regional referral center, managing approximately 1,300 emergency visits per day. The second is a university hospital with approximately 450 daily emergency admissions. Both institutions operate as academic teaching centers characterized by high patient acuity, rapid turnover, and substantial workload intensity. This context was considered appropriate for examining medication safety competency due to the complexity of medication administration processes and the high-risk nature of emergency care.

Medication management in the participating EDs follows a hybrid workflow integrating both paper-based and electronic processes. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the medication administration process, which comprises two distinct pathways: an emergency pathway and a non-emergency pathway.

Medication administration workflow in the emergency department. The medication administration process comprises two distinct pathways: an emergency pathway and a non-emergency pathway. In emergency situations, medication orders are frequently issued verbally (e.g., during code blue events), enabling immediate access to medications via emergency response carts. In this pathway, medications are rapidly prepared and administered by nurses under time-critical conditions, followed by patient monitoring. Documentation is typically completed retrospectively in the Hospital Information Management System (HIMS) by both nursing staff and pharmacy personnel. Due to the urgency of care, clinical decision support system (CDSS) functionalities are not consistently utilized during this phase, limiting the availability of system-based safety checks at the point of care. In contrast, the non-emergency pathway follows a more structured sequence, beginning with handwritten physician medication orders (prescribing stage). These orders are subsequently transcribed into the HIMS by nurses and verified in coordination with pharmacy personnel (verification stage). During this stage, integrated clinical decision support system (CDSS) functionalities are activated, including automated checks for dose limits, drug–drug interactions, and patient-specific allergy alerts. Following verification, medications are dispensed by the pharmacy and prepared and administered by nurses (preparation–administration stage). Finally, patient monitoring and documentation are completed within the system (monitoring and documentation stage). This workflow highlights that medication administration involves multiple sequential stages, each representing a potential point of risk, particularly in high-acuity emergency contexts where system-level safeguards such as CDSS may be variably applied

For the quantitative component, a census sampling approach was adopted, whereby all registered nurses actively employed in the adult EDs during the data collection period were invited to participate (N = 175). Nurses were eligible if they were directly involved in medication administration at the time of the study. Nurses who declined participation or were unavailable during the data collection period were not included. Additionally, nurses working exclusively in administrative roles or those on extended leave were excluded. In total, 122 nurses provided voluntary informed consent and completed the study instruments, resulting in a response rate of 62.6%. The qualitative strand included participants from the quantitative sample who provided responses to the structured open-ended questions.

Participation in both components was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. No prior relationship was established between the researchers and the participants before the study. This embedded qualitative strand enabled exploration of experiential perspectives alongside measured competency levels [19]. As all eligible participants were invited to respond to the open-ended questions, no additional sampling strategy was applied. Data adequacy was considered achieved when iterative analysis indicated thematic sufficiency, with no substantially new codes or categories emerging.

Data collection

Data collection commenced following institutional review board approval and administrative authorization. A pilot study involving five emergency nurses from non-participating institutions was conducted to assess clarity and feasibility of the instruments; pilot data were excluded from the final analysis. Consistent with the convergent mixed-methods design, quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently between July and September 2024. To minimize disruption to clinical workflow, participants completed the study instruments at flexible times prior to their shifts.

Quantitative data collection

Quantitative data were collected via the Turkish-validated version of the MSCS [20, 21]. The 36-item instrument assesses six subdimensions: patient-centered medication management, improvement of safety issues, management of influencing factors, safe risk management, multidisciplinary collaboration, and nursing responsibility. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating superior competence [21]. The scale demonstrated excellent reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.953).

The sample size was calculated via G*Power 3.1, assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and power (1–β) = 0.80. This calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 111 participants for a one-way analysis of variance. The final sample of 122 nurses exceeded this threshold, ensuring adequate statistical power.

Qualitative data collection

Qualitative data were obtained through four structured written open-ended questions designed to generate experiential insights into medication safety practices in ED settings. Written open-ended responses were selected to minimize disruption to clinical workflow in the high-acuity ED environment and to enable participation of nurses working across different shifts. The questions were developed based on a review of relevant medication safety literature and aligned with the study objectives to ensure conceptual coherence with the research aims [26, 27].

To enhance content validity and contextual appropriateness, the draft questions were reviewed by experts with academic and clinical expertise in ED nursing and patient safety. Revisions were made to improve clarity, comprehensibility, and alignment with ED practices. The revised set of questions was then pilot-tested with five non-participant ED nurses. Minor changes were implemented following pilot feedback to ensure clarity and applicability.

The final questions explored (1) experiences related to safe medication practices, (2) challenges encountered in medication administration, (3) suggestions for improving medication safety in the ED, and (4) additional comments related to medication safety. Participants were encouraged to provide detailed information in their written responses to enhance the depth and richness of the qualitative data.

Data analysis

Quantitative analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize medication safety competency scores across demographic and professional characteristics. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Independent samples t-tests were applied for two-group comparisons, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Bonferroni adjustments were performed for post hoc analyses where appropriate.

Qualitative data were analyzed using inductive content analysis consistent with the descriptive qualitative approach [23]. The analysis proceeded in three stages: (1) independent open coding of written responses by three researchers; (2) comparison and refinement of codes through consensus discussions; and (3) grouping of recurrent codes into subthemes and overarching themes.

To enhance the reliability of the coding process, coding decisions were compared across researchers and discrepancies were resolved through consensus. At this stage, the inter-coder consistency ratio (Cohen’s kappa (k) value) was found to be 0.88, and a kappa (k) value between 0.81 and 1.00 is interpreted as a almost perfect agreement [26].

Analytical rigor was enhanced through researcher triangulation and iterative review of the data. The analysis continued until thematic sufficiency was reached, defined as the point at which no substantially new codes emerged during successive reviews of the dataset.

Mixed methods integration

Integration of the quantitative and qualitative strands was conducted in accordance with the principles of convergent mixed-methods design. Following independent analyses of both datasets, integration occurred at the interpretation stage through joint display analysis [19, 28]. A systematic comparison was undertaken whereby quantitative findings were aligned with corresponding qualitative themes to examine patterns of convergence (confirmation), complementarity (expansion), and divergence (discordance) [28].

This comparison was facilitated through the development of a joint display table that visually mapped numerical results alongside related qualitative insights. The integration process was conducted collaboratively by the research team through iterative discussions to ensure analytical coherence. Through this comparative approach, meta-inferences were generated that extended beyond the findings of either dataset alone, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of how medication safety competence is enacted within ED contexts.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Methodological rigor was established through Lincoln and Guba’s criteria: credibility, dependability, transferability, and confirmability [29]. Credibility was supported by triangulating scale scores with open-ended responses and using direct quotations to reflect participants’ authentic voices. Dependability was ensured by maintaining a detailed audit trail of methodological decisions. To enhance transferability, we provided thick descriptions of the study setting and participant demographics [30]. Confirmability was achieved by submitting the coding structure to an external expert reviewer, who verified that the findings were grounded in the data.

To minimize researcher bias, the team engaged in reflexivity prior to and during the analysis. Researchers documented their personal assumptions regarding medication safety in reflexive journals to maintain neutrality during the coding process [27]. The study followed GRAMMS guidelines to ensure transparent reporting of the mixed-methods integration [25]. The research team consisted of two academic nursing faculty members and one doctoral-level PhD student with experience in qualitative and mixed-methods research.

Ethical considerations

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 25.04.2024, Decision No: TBAEK-286). All participants provided written informed consent, and data confidentiality was strictly maintained.

Sample characteristics

The sample comprised 122 emergency nurses, predominantly female (68.9%). The largest age cohort was 30–39 years (42.6%). Educational attainment was high, with 82.8% of participants holding a bachelor’s degree or higher. Regarding institutional affiliation, 76.2% worked at a Training and Research Hospital, while 23.8% were employed at a University Hospital. The workforce was experienced; 66.4% possessed five or more years of ED experience. Most participants (86.9%) worked 40 h or more per week, and 94.3% followed shift-based schedules.

Significant differences were observed only in the patient-centered medication management subdimension. Specifically, age and ED experience were significantly associated with this subdimension (p < .05), with nurses aged 30–39 years and those with ≥ 5 years of ED experience demonstrating higher scores. No statistically significant differences were identified for the total medication safety competence score.

Normality of the data distribution was assessed using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. The results indicated that the assumptions of normality were met (p > .05), supporting the use of parametric analyses.

Main Theme 1: Self-assessment-based learning and development

Participants emphasized that safe medication administration depends on continuous learning and self-assessment. This theme encompasses three critical sub-themes: strengthening professional knowledge through mentorship; developing multitasking capabilities; and supporting rapid clinical assessment.

Mentorship and leadership were identified as key mechanisms for enhancing professional judgment. Experienced nurses served as guides for less experienced colleagues, while structured leadership programs helped strengthen clinical reasoning.

…When experienced nurses guide the newcomers, it helps ensure medications are given correctly, lowers the chance of mistakes, and keeps the learning going through sharing knowledge. (P 90). Since the ED requires skills in both clinical decision-making and crisis management, I believe that individuals who can manage stress and make decisions with a clear head should be assigned to this unit.… (P 28).

Subtheme Strengthening Professional Knowledge, Skills, and Experience through Mentorship and Leadership Programs. Continuous, mentorship-supported training programs effectively reduced error risks. As one nurse expressed:

…an experienced nurse knows medication administration well; therefore, having an experienced nurse alongside newly started nurses both ensures correct practices and reduces the risk of error. Continuous training through knowledge sharing and guidance is very important. (P 94).

Subtheme Developing Multitasking Management Skills. Emergency nurses identified multitasking as a major source of distraction. Managing multiple responsibilities simultaneously often compromised focus and safety.

We have to do many things at the same time—prepare medication, answer questions, monitor patients, and check orders. This situation causes distraction and increases our risk of making mistakes. (P 56). The constant pressure to work quickly, essentially racing against the clock, creates stress which in turn leads to distractions. (P 66).

Subtheme Supporting Rapid Clinical Assessment and Decision-Making Skills. Strengthening clinical assessment is crucial in the time-pressured emergency environment, where constraints often jeopardize careful practice.

We have to race against time and be fast in the ED; sometimes this means medications are administered without proper consideration. (P 66).

Main Theme 2: Developing a medication safety culture

A robust safety culture is critical for minimizing errors. This theme includes fostering a team approach, structuring consultation systems, developing protocols, and encouraging error reporting.

Subtheme Fostering Team Approach, Collaboration, and Communication that Includes Patients and Relatives. Uninterrupted communication and collaboration foster trust and prevent errors.

…Establishing effective communication between the healthcare worker and the patient can reduce medication errors. (P 80). In preventing medication errors, a team approach, along with physician-nurse and nurse-nurse collaboration and communication, is very important. (P 87).

Subtheme Structuring Continuous Medication Consultation System. Ongoing expert support is crucial for clinical decision-making. Nurses highlighted the need for accessible guidance.

During the medication administration process, getting support from an expert with pharmacological knowledge can reduce the risk of error… (P 106). Delays in administration can occur due to reasons such as medications not being easily accessible… a system that provides rapid access to medications and offers information support should be structured. (P 38).

Subtheme Developing Safe Medication Administration Protocols. Standardized protocols and clear written communication are necessary to reduce errors.

To reduce the risk of error, it is important to prioritize the use of written orders and, when necessary, to prefer the use of verbal orders in accordance with instructions. (P 3). Orders should be written neatly and clearly… necessary protocols for medication orders and administration in the ED should be developed. (P 43).

Subtheme Fostering a Culture of Error Reporting. Effective reporting systems, particularly for near-miss events, are essential for identifying risks proactively.

Reporting near-miss events creates a safer environment. (P 129).

Main Theme 3: Efficient organizational management

Safety requires strong management that addresses physical and technical deficiencies. This theme covers environmental adaptation, scheduling preferences, technological systems, and performance evaluation.

Subtheme Continuously Adapting the Physical Environment to Meet Needs. Adequate equipment and well-designed workspaces enhance attention and reduce risk.

Having a safe environment and the right equipment really makes the whole process of giving medication more efficient. (P 91). Making sure there are no interruptions while preparing medication is key to minimizing errors. (P 56).

Subtheme Structuring Systems that Consider Individual Preferences in Scheduling Work Hours. Balanced workloads and structured shifts are vital for maintaining alertness.

For medication safety, it’s important to have regular working hours and to improve the patient-to-nurse ratios by hiring more staff. (P 50). Having reasonable work hours and enough nurses on staff could reduce medication errors. (P 45).

Subtheme Implementing Technological Systems for Medication Safety. Technological innovations play a crucial role in reducing error rates.

It makes the process safer when medication orders are integrated into the hospital information system, so the route, time, and amount are written out clearly. (P 42). …Getting medications from the pharmacy based on a barcode system could help prevent errors. (P 54).

Subtheme Establishing and Continuously Monitoring Competency-Based Performance Evaluation Systems. Regular evaluation and education help reduce errors and strengthen performance.

With updated and adequate training—and applying it in practice—you can also minimize the nurse error rate. (P 129). An experienced nurse always knows the right dose and the right time, and they guide others. (P 94).

Discussion

The present study employed a convergent mixed-methods design to examine medication safety competence among emergency nurses and to contextualize measured competency levels within clinical practice conditions. Although quantitative findings demonstrated relatively high self-reported competence across several domains, qualitative findings underscored the influence of cognitive workload, workflow instability, and the prevailing non-punitive error reporting climate on the enactment of this competence. Taken together, these findings suggest that medication safety competence in EDs extends beyond measurable performance and is shaped by clinical context.

Competency development and self-assessment

The observed association between experience and patient-centered medication management may be related to clinical exposure in practice, particularly in areas involving individualized care and patient interaction. Clinical experience has been linked to improved clinical reasoning and safer medication practices [16, 31]. In addition, prior research has emphasized that medication safety competence is shaped by both individual cognitive abilities and the characteristics of the clinical practice environment [17, 18].

However, the absence of significant differences in the total scale score suggests that experience alone may not fully explain overall medication safety competence, which may be influenced by multiple interacting factors, including workflow complexity and organizational conditions [6, 8].

These findings highlight the importance of interpreting medication safety competence at the subdimension level. As suggested in prior research, different components of competence may develop through distinct mechanisms and may not progress uniformly [18]. Therefore, relying solely on total scores may obscure important domain-specific variations.

These findings are further supported by the qualitative results. The enactment of medication safety competence appears to be influenced not only by individual characteristics but also by work-system factors such as cognitive workload, workflow interruptions, and organizational conditions. This is consistent with the SEIPS model, which emphasizes that performance outcomes emerge from the interaction between individual and system-level factors [17]. Accordingly, medication safety competence can be understood as a context-dependent construct rather than a fixed individual attribute.

A key finding of this study is the divergence identified between quantitative and qualitative results. While nurses reported high levels of competence in multidisciplinary collaboration in the quantitative analysis, qualitative findings revealed concerns related to fear of reporting errors in environments perceived as punitive. This discrepancy highlights that high levels of perceived collaboration may coexist with limited psychological safety for error reporting, particularly in environments characterized by punitive responses to errors [13, 15].

Safety culture and multidisciplinary collaboration

Quantitative findings indicated favorable perceptions of multidisciplinary collaboration across demographic groups, suggesting that teamwork is broadly embedded within emergency nursing practice [32]. A key and particularly important finding of this study is the divergence between high self-reported collaboration and hesitancy in error reporting within perceived punitive environments. However, qualitative findings pointed to a more complex picture of safety culture, with participants reporting reluctance to report near-miss events due to perceived punitive responses.

This divergence may reflect a distinction between structural teamwork and psychological safety [13]. While interprofessional communication may be functionally present, the presence of a punitive climate may limit open reporting and reduce opportunities for learning from errors. Similar patterns have been described in medication safety research, where punitive environments have been associated with lower reporting transparency [33].

These findings highlight that the enactment of medication safety competence is not solely dependent on the presence of collaborative practices, but may also be shaped by the broader organizational context in which care is delivered. In particular, the absence of a non-punitive environment may constrain the translation of competence into practice by discouraging open communication and feedback.

Accordingly, collaboration alone may not fully represent a mature safety culture; rather, the effective enactment of medication safety competence may depend on both functional teamwork and a psychologically safe environment that supports learning from errors [13, 34].

Organizational management and systemic support

Quantitative findings indicated relatively high levels of perceived competence in improving safety issues, whereas qualitative findings suggested that organizational conditions may play a role in shaping the sustainability of safe medication practices. Physical workspace design, equipment adequacy, and protection from interruptions emerged as important structural elements supporting focused medication preparation, consistent with evidence linking environmental design to medication error risk [6]. Workload intensity and scheduling practices were also identified as factors that may affect safe performance. In line with literature demonstrating that excessive workload and irregular shifts are associated with reduced cognitive functioning and increase medication-related risk [6, 8], these findings suggest that medication administration in emergency settings may be shaped by work system conditions that support attentional capacity and task continuity.

Technological integration, including electronic order entry and barcode-assisted medication processes, was associated with greater clarity in medication administration. Evidence from empirical studies suggests that computerized provider order entry and decision-support systems are associated with reductions in medication error rates [35], which may support recommendations advocating digital integration in medication safety processes [7].

Ongoing education and competency-based performance evaluation may contribute to maintaining safe medication practices. Evidence suggests that structured competency-based training is associated with improved clinical performance [36]. These findings indicate that medication safety competence in EDs may be shaped not only by individual capability but also by organizational systems that support continuous professional development.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the multi-stage medication workflow may involve different sources of risk at each step, highlighting areas that may warrant further investigation. These findings may also reflect the presence of multiple potential points of failure across different stages of the medication process, including prescribing, transcription, dispensing, and administration, although these stages were not directly examined in the present study [3, 5]. These stages may involve distinct types of risk, such as illegible or incomplete physician orders at the prescribing stage, misinterpretation during transcription, dispensing inaccuracies at the pharmacy level, and technique-related errors during medication administration [5].

Future research could benefit from providing a more granular analysis of medication safety processes by identifying error-prone stages within the workflow, including prescribing, transcription, dispensing, and administration. Such stage-specific analyses may help clarify where errors and near-misses most frequently occur and may offer more actionable insights for system-level improvements.

An important consideration in interpreting these findings is the reliance on self-reported measures of medication safety competence. While such measures provide insight into perceived competence, they may not fully capture actual clinical performance. In particular, self-assessment tools may not adequately identify individuals with limited awareness of their own performance gaps, including those who may overestimate their competence.

Accordingly, a more comprehensive evaluation of medication safety competence may benefit from integrating objective indicators, such as observed medication error rates, alongside collaborative assessments involving interdisciplinary teams, including pharmacists.

Integration of findings

The convergent mixed-methods design provided a comprehensive perspective on medication safety competence by integrating measured competency levels with experiential insights from emergency nurses. While quantitative findings indicated generally high levels of perceived competence, qualitative data highlighted contextual constraints—such as cognitive workload, workflow interruptions, and concerns about error reporting—that may influence how this competence is enacted in emergency clinical settings.

The integration suggests that medication safety competence may not be fully understood as a fixed individual attribute, but rather as a context-dependent capacity that emerges through the interaction of professional proficiency, collaborative practice, and organizational infrastructure. This synthesis extends understanding beyond descriptive competency scores and highlights the systemic conditions that may facilitate or constrain the translation of competence into consistently safe medication administration practices.

Implications for emergency nursing

The findings suggest that strengthening medication safety competence in EDs may benefit from interventions that address both individual and system-level conditions. A key implication of this study is the observed divergence between high levels of self-reported collaboration and reported hesitancy in error reporting within perceived punitive environments. This finding highlights that collaboration alone may not fully reflect a mature safety culture, and that efforts to improve medication safety should also consider the cultural conditions that influence reporting behaviors and learning from errors.

Interventions may therefore focus on both professional development and organizational context. Structured mentorship and feedback mechanisms may support clinical judgment in high-acuity settings, while training programs may benefit from incorporating realistic scenarios that reflect interruptions, multitasking, and time pressure. At the organizational level, optimizing workflow design, minimizing interruptions, and ensuring access to timely pharmacological consultation may further support safe medication practices. Promoting a non-punitive reporting environment may also enhance open communication and opportunities for learning.

Importantly, the reliance on self-reported measures indicates that perceived competence alone may not fully reflect actual clinical performance. In particular, self-assessment approaches may not adequately identify individuals with limited awareness of their own performance gaps. Accordingly, efforts to strengthen medication safety competence may benefit from integrating objective indicators, such as observed medication error rates, alongside collaborative evaluation processes involving interdisciplinary teams, including pharmacists. Such approaches may help reduce the gap between perceived and enacted competence and support more targeted system-level improvements.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, data were collected from two tertiary hospitals within a single urban region, which may limit transferability to other healthcare settings. Second, medication safety competence was assessed using self-reported measures, which may be subject to social desirability bias and perception–performance discrepancies. In addition, the study did not include direct observation of medication administration practices, limiting the ability to capture actual clinical performance.

Qualitative data were obtained through structured written open-ended responses rather than in-depth interviews. While this approach enabled inclusion of a larger and more diverse sample, it may have limited the depth and interactive probing typically achieved in interview-based qualitative designs.

The response rate of 62.6% also introduces the possibility of non-response bias. As data on non-participants were unavailable, comparisons between respondents and non-respondents could not be conducted. It is possible that participants differed systematically in terms of confidence, engagement, or perceived competence, potentially leading to overestimation of medication safety competence.

Finally, the use of univariate statistical analyses without adjustment for potential confounding variables limits the interpretation of independent associations. While these analyses identify relationships between variables, they do not allow conclusions regarding independent predictors. Future studies incorporating observational data and multivariable analytical approaches are needed to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of medication safety competence.

Conclusion

This study suggests that medication safety competence in EDs extends beyond measurable knowledge and skills. While nurses reported relatively high levels of perceived competence, the findings indicate that its enactment in practice may be shaped by cognitive demands, collaborative dynamics, and organizational conditions.

A key finding is the divergence between high self-reported collaboration and hesitancy in error reporting within perceived punitive environments, suggesting that collaboration alone may not fully reflect a mature safety culture. In addition, the reliance on self-reported measures indicates that perceived competence may not fully capture actual clinical performance. Future research may benefit from integrating objective indicators and examining how both professional development and organizational conditions influence the enactment of medication safety competence in practice.
