Ensemble-based feature fusion for accurate plant disease classification using pre-trained models
Authors: Tembelihle Apleni, Folasade Olubusola Isinkaye, Michael Olusoji Olusanya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Agricultural productivity remains seriously threatened by the attacks of plant diseases, even though it is the bedrock of global food security. These diseases, if ignored, can lead to massive crop losses and economic setbacks. Therefore, the development of accurate and efficient plant disease detection systems is essential to preserve plants and promote sustainable agricultural practices. This study examined the potential of ensembled deep learning models that incorporates feature-level fusion to enhance the robustness and accuracy of plant disease detection. Specifically, we utilized pre-trained deep learning architecture (VGG16, Residual Network 50 (ResNet50), and GoogleNet (InceptionV3)) to extract distinctive features representations from plant leaf images. These extracted features were combined to enhance the performance of our ensemble model. For disease classification, the fused features were passed through a Dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation, followed by a SoftMax classification layer to predict the probabilities of each plant disease class. Experiments were conducted using the New Plant Diseases Dataset. It contains 87,867 image samples of various plant disease species for 38 classes and 14 different crop species. The ensemble model achieved notable results, with an accuracy of 97.0%. This reveals the capability of feature-fusion ensembled learning in improving detection stability and accuracy. The knowledge and application of feature fusion in disease detection can help ensure more accurate, timely, and eco-friendly interventions. This, in turn, can support sustainable agricultural practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25927-z.

Introduction

The main source of nutrition for billions of people around the world is agriculture, which also serves as the pillar of global food security1. However, plant diseases present a significant threat to agricultural productivity, as they cause substantial losses in yield, quality, and market value2. In recent years, the increasing global population and changing climatic conditions have intensified the occurrence of plant diseases. This calls for an urgent need for more reliable plant disease detection approaches. Early and accurate disease detection is necessary to prevent the spread of diseases, reduce the use of pesticides, and guarantee the overall plant health3. Plant disease detection mostly relied on manual assessment by agricultural experts, which is a cumbersome and time-consuming process susceptible to human error. To overcome these limitations, there has been an increased demand to develop automatic systems for plant disease detection using advanced technologies like machine learning and computer vision4. These systems have the potential to substantially improve the accuracy, speed, and scalability of plant disease detection. It also provides real-time solutions to farmers and agricultural experts. Among these technologies, deep learning models, specifically, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have shown incredible success in many image classification tasks, such as plant disease detection5. They have the ability to automatically learn spatial features from leaf images and have shown promising results on public datasets like PlantVillage.

In the field of plant disease detection, deep learning models like VGG16, Residual Network 50 (ResNet50), and GoogleNet (Inception V3) have gained widespread attention due to their ability to learn complex patterns and features from images6. VGG-16, with its 16-layer deep architecture and 3 × 3 convolutional filters, is a benchmark model in image classification7. It is accepted for its simplicity, high performance, and ability to learn complex features effectively8,9. ResNet50, with its residual learning framework, enables deeper networks to be trained without suffering from the vanishing gradient problem10,11. GoogleNet (Inception V3) is known for its efficiency in model design. It encourages the extraction of diverse features at multiple scales through its inception modules12. Each of these architectures has its strengths and limitations, which make them applicable for different tasks and environments. Also, single deep learning models have delivered competent results in plant disease detection as shown in13–15. However, they have some limitations which include inability to generalize well in different crops and disease types in some situations. Apart from this, since each model learns different parts of the image features; using a single CNN may lead to suboptimal representation.

Although many deep learning models have achieved high classification accuracy in controlled environments, their practical utility is limited by poor generalization and feature representation issues. Summarily, the consensus in the recent literature confirms that existing standalone CNN models lack the necessary robustness to cope with the environmental variability and domain shifts prevalent in agricultural settings. This body of evidence establishes a clear research gap: the need for advanced, multi-architectural frameworks capable of delivering stable and generalized disease detection for practical, real-world deployment.

To achieve reliable and generalized plant disease detection in real-world agricultural settings, we must address the limitations of standalone Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). As demonstrated in the preceding literature review, CNNs, even high-performing ones like ResNet and Xception, struggle with domain shift and suboptimal feature representation. They usually overfit to specific laboratory conditions and lack the architectural diversity needed to capture the full spectrum of subtle disease patterns and environmental noise. To overcome the high variance and architectural bias associated with a singular deep learning approach, we adopt an ensemble learning approach that utilizes feature fusion as the foundational architectural decision for this work. Our proposed ensemble framework strategically integrates three distinct, high-performing, pre-trained base models (VGG16, ResNet50, and InceptionV3) to harness their complementary feature extraction strengths. This is to ensure superior robustness and cross-domain generalization.

Therefore, the study develops a feature fusion ensemble deep learning model for plant disease detection. Precisely, we focus on three state-of-the-art deep learning architectures (VGG16, ResNet50, and Inception V3) to examine how combining them into ensemble models can improve the robustness and generalization of plant disease detection. Proposed methodology for the ensemble model

This section introduces the methodology implemented in the study to examine the performance of deep learning model for plant disease classification. It details the steps involved in data preparation, which includes the selection, preprocessing, and augmentation of the dataset. Also, it explains the evaluation metrics used to analyze the performance of the model. Metrics, such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, are discussed to ensure a thorough evaluation of the model.

Dataset description

The dataset (New Plant Diseases Dataset)21 used in this study was sourced from a publicly available collection on Kaggle. It consists of 87,867 images of both healthy and diseased plant leaves (sample images of healthy and diseased plant leaves are shown in Fig. 1). These images are categorized into 38 distinct groups that represent different stages and types of plant diseases across 14 different crop species22. The distributions for training, validation, and test sets are 63,282, 17,572, and 7,013 images respectively. Table S1 in the Supplementary Material presents the detailed class-wise distribution of the dataset. To prepare the dataset for training, all images were resized to a uniform resolution23, which is vital to maintain consistency in the dataset and optimize model performance. The resizing also aids in improving computational efficiency during the training process. It helps to reduce processing time without compromising the quality of the images.

Data augmentation and splitting

Data augmentations were applied after resizing the dataset to enhance the dataset and address the issue of class imbalance and the limited size of certain categories24,25. Real-time data augmentation was applied during training. The process performed on-the-fly transformations such as rotation, zooming, horizontal flipping, and shearing without changing the size of the dataset. These operations helped to artificially increase the diversity of the training data, which improves the ability of the model to generalize to unseen data. Image rescaling was also applied to normalize pixel values to enhance uniformity in all input images to assist in faster convergence during training. Also, to ensure a robust evaluation, the dataset was split into three subsets: training, validation, and testing, using a 7:2:1 ratio. This partitioning approach is consistent with prior study, such as the study by26 which revealed high accuracy for plant disease detection using a similar distribution. The training set, which has 70% of the dataset, was used to train the model, while the validation set (20%) was employed to fine-tune hyperparameters and monitor overfitting. The testing set used the remaining 10%, to evaluate the final performance and generalizability of the models.

Model selection and training

In this study, three state-of-the-art deep learning models (VGG16, ResNet50, and Inception V3) were selected for feature extraction, while a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) was employed for the final classification. The choice of these models is justified based on their proven effectiveness in feature extraction, computational efficiency, and ability to capture varying levels of spatial and hierarchical representations. Each of the models are discussed in the following sections.

VGG16 model architecture

VGG16 is a deep convolutional neural network with 16 layers, adapted in this study for plant disease classification. The model processes input images of (224, 224, 3) through sequential convolutional layers with filter sizes of 3 × 3, starting with 64 filters, increasing to 128, 256, and 512 in subsequent block as depicted in Fig. 2. Each convolutional block is followed by 2 × 2 max pooling layers to reduce spatial dimensions while retaining essential features. To adapt the model for our study, the original fully connected layers trained on 1,000 ImageNet categories were removed, and Global Average Pooling (GAP) was applied to extract deep feature representations.

Resnet50 model architecture

We used the ResNet50 model, which consists of 50 layers, designed to address the vanishing gradient problem through residual connections (Fig. 3). This deep architecture incorporates five convolutional blocks, with each block containing multiple residual layers that enable the model to maintain efficient gradient flow, even with many layers. The model processes input images by first applying a convolutional layer with 64 filters and a 7 × 7 kernel. This is followed by a max pooling layer with a stride of 2 in both dimensions to reduce spatial dimensions while retaining important features. The residual connections allow the network to skip over certain layers to enhance performance and mitigate the difficulties often encountered in deeper networks. For our study, ResNet50 was used as a feature extractor. The original fully connected layers trained on ImageNet were removed, and Global Average Pooling (GAP) was applied to extract high-level feature representations.

GoogleNet (Inception V3) model architecture

We used GoogleNet (Inception V3) which is popular for its efficient use of computational resources and its ability to capture multi-scale features. Unlike conventional models where convolutional and pooling layers are stacked sequentially, Inception V3 adopts a more complex architecture, which incorporate Inception modules that enable the network to use multiple filter sizes within the same layer as depicted in Fig. 4. This design allows the model to efficiently process a wide range of feature sizes, to increase its capability to detect subtle patterns in plant leaf images, such as variations in texture, shape, and color indicative of plant diseases. The Inception V3 model in our study has 48 layers, and it notably reduces the number of parameters compared to other deep learning models. While AlexNet has 60 million parameters and VGGNet has 138 million, GoogleNet uses only 7 million parameters. This makes it computationally efficient for large datasets. The model harnesses the 1 × 1 convolutions for dimensionality reduction and includes global average pooling to further streamline computations while retaining essential feature information. In our study, InceptionV3 was used for feature extraction, its Inception modules were utilized to capture multi-scale features critical for plant disease detection. The original fully connected classification layers trained on ImageNet were removed. This ensures the model focused solely on extracting relevant features from plant leaf images.

The proposed feature-fusion model

The proposed model (Fig. 5) integrates VGG16, ResNet50, and InceptionV3 using a feature concatenation approach to enhance robust plant disease classification. These three pre-trained deep learning models, originally trained on ImageNet, serve as feature extractors. They capture distinct spatial and hierarchical information from the plant images.

After each of these architectures has extracted distinctive feature representations from plant leaf images, rather than using these models directly for classification, their extracted features are combined to create a more comprehensive feature set. These feature maps are integrated to ensure that the ensemble model captured richer, more discriminative information that helps to improve classification accuracy and robustness.

Plant disease classification using dense classification layer

The training of the fused features starts by passing the concatenated feature vector through a classification head designed to learn the mapping between the fused features and the plant disease classes. The classification head consists of a Dense layer with 128 units and ReLU activation to make it trainable. The Dense layer serves as a learnable transformation, which adjusts the weights during the training process to capture the complex relationships between the extracted features from different models. Through the training, the Dense layer fine-tunes the fused features to enhance their relevance and improve the ability of the model to distinguish between various plant diseases. After the features have been processed by the Dense layer, the output is sent to the SoftMax layer, which performs the final classification. The SoftMax layer converts the processed features into probabilities, it assigns each input sample to the most likely plant disease class based on the learned representations to enhance accurate and interpretable classification. Hyperparameter settings

For the individual models, VGG16, ResNet50, and Inception V3, training was carried out using a learning rate of 1e-4, a batch size of 32, and 30 epochs, with the Adam optimizer. These hyperparameters were selected to enhance efficient fine-tuning of the pre-trained models. The moderate learning rate allows for stable convergence, and the extended number of epochs ensures that the models have sufficient time to adapt to the specific classification task. Instead of applying external learning rate scheduling techniques, the models relied on the internal learning rate adaptation of Adam.

To train the ensemble model, standard training procedures and hyperparameter settings were adapted16,17. These parameters were selected based on prior studies and empirical tuning to ensure optimal convergence and generalization performance18,19. Specifically, the ensemble model was trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.00025 and a batch size of 32. Early stopping was applied with a patience of 5 epochs, and learning rate decay was handled using the ReduceLROnPlateau scheduler. The training was conducted for a maximum of 10 epochs, with the best model weights restored based on validation loss. A summary of the hyperparameters is presented in Table 2.

Model performance evaluation

To evaluate the performance of our models, metrics such as Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score were used. These metrics provide a complete evaluation of the ability of the model to classify plant diseases accurately. The values of four parameters (False Positive (FP), True Positive (TP), False Negative (FN), and True Negative (TN)) are vital to determine the performance of the model in classifying plant leaf images. These parameters are gotten from the confusion matrix and help measure the ability of the model to correctly identify diseased and healthy leaves.

Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions among all predictions. For the individual models, VGG16, ResNet50, and InceptionV3, we present the training, validation, and test accuracies to assess their baseline performance. The ensemble model combines features extracted from individual models (VGG16, ResNet50, and InceptionV3) using a fusion-based approach. The model uses a Dense layer with ReLU activation to learn complex relationships from the concatenated features, followed by a SoftMax layer for final classification. We present the training, validation, and test accuracies, along with other evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, and F1-score for the ensemble model. Theoretical implications of this study

This study unveils the benefits of integrating multiple deep learning architectures to enhance feature extraction and model performance. The study capitalizes on the distinct strengths of each model combined. The capacity of VGG16 to capture simple, structured features, the competence of ResNet50 in learning deeper, hierarchical features through residual connections, and the InceptionV3 multi-scale feature extraction via inception blocks. This ensemble approach enhances the potential of the model to learn a more comprehensive variety of features, from fine-grained details to broader patterns, which improves its overall performance in plant disease classification. Furthermore, the study emphasizes the strengths of ensemble learning in performance optimization, as evidenced by the strong validation and test accuracies of 97.0%. Ensemble learning reduces the risk of overfitting and enhances the robustness of the classifier by integrating diverse architectures. This aligns with prior research that stresses the advantage of combining models to utilize their unique strengths33–35. Also, the fusion of local and global features extracted from the individual models assists the classifier to capture both detailed and large-scale patterns. This allows it to be more adept at understanding the complexity of plant disease features. The feature fusion, therefore, contributes to the higher accuracy, precision, and overall robustness of the model, which shows its theoretical benefit in complex classification tasks.

Practical implications of the study

The practical significance of this research is its ability to change agricultural practices; it can provide a reliable and efficient tool for real-world disease detection. With a high recall and precision of 97.0%, the model ensures that diseased plants are accurately identified to minimize the risk of misclassifying healthy plants. This level of accuracy is essential for farmers and agricultural experts, as it helps prevent crop loss, reduces avoidable pesticide use, and promotes both increased crop yield and sustainability. The capacity of the model to detect plant diseases early facilitates proactive disease management, which can assist farmers to intervene promptly and prevent the spread of disease. This early detection is vital, as it minimizes the cost of treatment, and improves both financial and environmental sustainability. In addition, the strong performance of the model on unseen data, with a 97.0% test accuracy, implies that it is adaptable to various crops and environments without requiring extensive retraining. This further support its practical application in diverse agricultural contexts.

The scalability of the model and automation capabilities further increase its real-world utility. When it is included in automated systems, continuous, large-scale disease monitoring can be achieved. This can substantially reduce the labor intensity of manual inspections and enable consistent monitoring in massive agricultural fields. This is also useful for large agricultural enterprises that need efficient and reliable tools to manage plant health at scale. Also, the capability to detect diseases accurately has profound implications for broader disease management strategies and policies. Agricultural bodies could use the model to make data-driven decisions on disease containment, treatment methods, and resource allocation. Policymakers can optimize response strategies by utilizing this ensemble model. It will ensure that resources are efficiently allocated, and that interventions are timely and targeted.

Conclusion

This study presents a robust ensemble deep learning model for plant disease classification by combining the strengths of VGG16, ResNet50, and InceptionV3 for feature extraction, followed by feature fusion using concatenation. The ensemble approach significantly enhanced the model’s ability to capture intricate local and global patterns in plant leaf images, which were subsequently classified using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). This methodology demonstrated exceptional performance, with training, validation, and test accuracies of 98.0%, 97.0%, and 97.0%, respectively, reflecting the model’s high accuracy and generalization capability. The strong recall and precision values of 97.0% confirm its reliability in accurately identifying diseased plants while minimizing false positives and negatives, further validated by a balanced F1 score of 97.0%. Practically, the model shows strong potential for real-world agricultural applications that can facilitate early disease detection, decrease crop loss, and curtail pesticide use. Its scalability supports large-scale monitoring across different crops, and its accuracy can guide data-driven agricultural policies in optimizing disease management and resource allocation to support sustainable farming practices.

Future work

Future work will focus on examining more diverse feature fusion strategies and incorporating additional real-world datasets that capture noise, varying lighting conditions, and background interference typical of field environments. We also plan to investigate attention-based architectures such as DenseNet-SE and Vision Transformers (ViTs) to further enhance classification performance. Similarly, we intend to replace the existing concatenation-based approach with more advanced fusion techniques such as cross-model attention and adaptive feature selection to reduce possible feature redundancy and improve semantic coherence. These improvements aim to make the system more adaptable, scalable, and robust. This will further advance the development of precision agriculture solutions that support more sustainable farming practices.
