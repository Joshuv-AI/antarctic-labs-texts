Pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations increase the risk of antiretroviral treatment failure in HIV-1 naïve patients
Authors: Yifan Su, Rentian Cai, Yinyin Zhu, Mingli Zhong, Mingxue Qi, Chen Chen, Zi Ye, Hongying Zhang, Hongxia Wei, Yanjie Yin
Published: 2023

To the Editor: High-frequency resistance (typically ≥15–25% viral quasispecies) detected by conventional resistance assays has long been associated with failure of antiretroviral therapy (ART) to suppress viral replication,[1] but the impact of pre-existing low-frequency resistance on virological response is controversial. Previous studies have shown an increased likelihood of virological response failure in patients with pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations after initiation of antiviral therapy, especially primary ART regimens containing non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs).[2] However, some studies have concluded differently, suggesting that pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations do not correlate with viral response failure.[3] This may be related to the method of detection of pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations.

Detection platforms for second-generation sequencing include MiSeq, Ion Torrent, and Pacific Biosciences. Among them, the MiSeq platform adopts double-end sequencing for sequencing viral and bacterial genomes, which can achieve higher detection sensitivity and specificity,[4] and is more suitable for clinical implementation.

Therefore, in response to previous studies showing inconclusive effects of pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations on antiretroviral efficacy in human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1)-infected patients, the study was designed to detect pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations in HIV-1-infected patients using the MiSeq second-generation sequencing platform to investigate their impact on virological response in HIV-1 naïve patients.

HIV-1 ART-naïve patients attending the Second Hospital of Nanjing during January 1, 2018 to December 31, 2020, with drug-sensitive results from traditional pre-existing drug resistance test, were enrolled in this study. They received ART and were followed up for 12 months, with viral load monitoring at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 9 months, and 12 months of treatment. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing (No. 2018-LY-kt027). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Failure of virological response is defined as (1) HIV-1 RNA >200 copies/mL at 6, 9, and 12 months of treatment without any change in treatment regimen; (2) patients who changed drugs within 6 months of treatment because of high viral loads or slow decline in viral loads; and (3) patients with drug resistance detected after treatment. Definition of successful virological response: viral load of HIV-1 RNA <50 copies/mL at 6, 9, and 12 months of treatment. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Patients with poor adherence resulting in failure of virological response, but after correcting the adherence of the viral load significantly reduced; (2) treatment of 6, 9, and 12 months of the viral load test data is missing to determine the results of virological response; (3) treatment of 12 months due to allergies, economic reasons, and simplified treatment of the second combination of self-selected drug change.

Thirty-eight patients who failed antiviral therapy were defined as the failure group according to the study definition criteria, and 38 successful virological responders were matched as the control group based on the baseline characteristics of the failure group (baseline CD4+ T-lymphocyte counts and viral loads, 1:1 propensity score matching method, carboplatin value 0.05) [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B780].

All of the 76 HIV-1-infected patients with baseline plasma pre-existing low-frequency drug resistance mutations were detected by the MiSeq second-generation sequencing platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Count data were described by absolute numbers and rates. Non-parametric tests are used for data that do not conform to the normal distribution to compare differences in patient CD4+ T-cell counts, HIV-1-RNA, and mutation detection rates between two groups. All tests were two-sided and differences were considered statistically significant at P <0.05.

A total of 76 patients enrolled in this study had been tested for pre-existing resistance by conventional Sanger sequencing at baseline, with results suggesting that no resistance mutations were detected. Of the overall 76 included patients, CRF01-AE (40.8%, 31/76) was the predominant subtype, followed by subtypes B + C (26.3%, 20/76) and B (18.4%, 14/76). Both the control and failure groups were predominantly male (92.1% [35/38] vs. 97.4% [37/38]), and the predominant subtype was CRF01-AE (44.7% [17/38] vs.36.8% [14/38]). There was no significant difference in age, baseline CD4+ T-cell count, and baseline HIV-1 RNA between the two groups (P >0.05) [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B780].

Among the 76 patients who underwent second-generation sequencing, the success rate of amplification was 100%. Low-frequency resistance mutations were detected in a total of 18 loci, with a total number of 26 mutations, and the mutations were predominantly in the NNRTI class of resistance, which accounted for 53.8% (14/26) of the mutated loci, including V179 (19.2%, 5/26), Y188 (7.7%, 2/26), and E138 (7.7%, 2/26). The NRTI class resistance mutation rate was 38.5% (10/26), including K65 (7.7%, 2/26) and D67 (7.7%, 2/26). In addition, one HIV-infected patient with low-frequency resistance in the PI class and one with low-frequency resistance in the integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) class were detected, respectively [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B780]. Two or more mutation sites were present in 54.5% (6/11) of the patients.

The initial treatment regimen of patients in this study population was predominantly 2NRTI + NNRTI in 77.6% (59/76), while the remaining 17 patients were on an INSTI-based antiviral regimen and all of them had at least one viral load test result that could be used for determining the virological response after 6 months of antiviral therapy. The results of testing by second-generation sequencing showed that the overall pre-existing low-frequency resistance detection rate of the included population was 14.5% (11/76), in which the pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutation rate of the failure group was significantly higher than that of the control group (23.7% [9/38] vs. 5.3% [2/38]), and the difference was statistically significant (χ2 = 5.208, P = 0.022).

The study compared viral load levels at 48 weeks post-treatment in two groups of patients with and without low-frequency resistance, with viral load data from patients who switched therapy after changing their original antiviral regimen no longer included in the analysis. The results showed that there was no significant difference in viral load between the two groups at baseline, and after receiving antiviral therapy it was seen that the viral load level of patients with low-frequency resistance was consistently higher than that of patients without mutation, which showed no significant difference in viral load level between the two groups at 4 weeks, 12 weeks, and 48 weeks (P = 0.126, 0.233, and 0.059), but at 24 weeks of treatment the difference in viral load level between the two groups was statistically significant (P = 0.010) [Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B780].

In this retrospective study, the presence of HIV pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations was significantly higher in the post-ART virological failure group than in the successful virological response group, and resistance was predominantly to NNRTIs. A pooled analysis evaluating the association between pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations and the risk of first-line NNRTI antiretroviral. The study showed that pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations were associated with an increased risk of virological failure after controlling for patient medication adherence, race, ethnicity, baseline CD4+ T-cell counts, and plasma HIV-1 RNA levels, with low-frequency resistance mutations in the NNRTI class being the most strongly associated with an increased risk of virological failure.[5–7] Given that NNRTI-based ART regimens are still predominantly used by HIV-1-infected patients in many countries, it is important to speculate on the possible presence of pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations leading to resistance to NNRTIs after virological failure, and timely switching to an INSTI- or protease inhibitor (PI)-based ART regimen is likely to result in a return to a successful virological response.

We further analyzed the level of viral load changes in patients receiving antiviral therapy for 48 weeks. Comparison of viral load levels after 4 weeks, 12 weeks, 24 weeks, and 48 weeks of antiviral therapy, excluding the effect of baseline characteristics of patients with and without low-frequency resistance mutations, showed that patients with low-frequency resistance mutations only at 24 weeks of therapy had significantly higher viral load levels than patients without resistance mutations. According to the guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of AIDS,[8] the time point for determining whether antiretroviral treatment failure has occurred and the need for switching treatment is precisely 24 weeks after initiating treatment. Thus, low-frequency resistance mutations delayed the rate of viral load decline after ART and showed a single significant difference at 24 weeks of treatment, but with longer antiviral treatment, such as at 48 weeks of ART, there was no significant difference in viral load decline between the two groups. This may be due to the fact that after 24 weeks of treatment, some patients had already chosen to switch treatment regimens based on viral load levels or resistance testing, resulting in a smaller sample size.

In conclusion, we showed that HIV-1 patients may have pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations in both the failed and successful virological response groups, and that the prevalence of pre-existing low-frequency resistance was higher in the former than in the latter. There are some limitations of this study. First, this study was a single-center study and included a small sample size of patients. In addition, low-frequency resistance mutations occurring at different frequencies may not have a uniform impact on antiviral efficacy, and the frequency aspect of low-frequency resistance mutations was not analyzed in depth in this study.

In conclusion, pre-existing low-frequency resistance mutations are present at a higher rate in the HIV-1 primary treatment population with failed virological response than in patients with successful virological response.
