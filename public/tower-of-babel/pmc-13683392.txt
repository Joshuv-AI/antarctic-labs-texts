BH3 profiling analyses for functional precision medicine
Authors: Paula Morales-Sánchez, Joan Montero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvasion of apoptosis is a central hallmark of cancer and a major driver of therapeutic resistance. While genomic and transcriptomic profiling have reshaped our understanding of cancer, they often fail to predict whether a cell will die when treated. BH3 profiling and its variants solve this by directly interrogating mitochondrial apoptotic priming and its reliance on anti-apoptotic dependencies by directly exposing cells to defined BH3-only peptides. Over the past decade, researchers have successfully applied these assays across a wide range of hematologic malignancies, solid tumors, and resistant cancer models. This functional assay predicts treatment response, identifies rational drug combinations, and reveals mechanisms of both intrinsic and acquired resistance to apoptosis. In hematologic malignancies, functional dependence on BCL-2 measured by BH3 profiling strongly correlates with sensitivity to venetoclax, while reduced mitochondrial priming underlies resistance. In solid tumors, these analyses have uncovered previously unappreciated reliance on BCL-xL and MCL-1, enabling the design of effective combination strategies despite limited single-agent activity of BH3 mimetics. Recent methodological advances, including high-throughput and dynamic BH3 profiling, further highlight the changing and heterogeneous nature of apoptotic regulation within tumors. This review evaluates the preclinical and early clinical evidence for BH3 profiling in precision oncology. Beyond assessing its current performance, we examine the methodological requirements for its transition into clinical settings and its potential to address the limitations of current personalized cancer therapies.Subject terms: Cancer therapeutic resistance, Cell death

Introduction

Although genomic and transcriptomic profiling have transformed cancer classification and revealed actionable oncogenic drivers, they do not reliably predict responses to cytotoxic drugs [1–3]. Genetic data alone cannot explain why patients with identical mutations have different clinical outcomes [4]. While we know the molecular targets of most drugs, therapeutic success depends on whether the resulting cellular stress triggers mitochondrial apoptosis.

Many patients exhibit intrinsic resistance to these drugs or develop it over time [5]. Cancer cells are highly adaptable; they can survive severe damage and often acquire more aggressive phenotypes that lead to limited therapeutic options. This reality reinforces the need to move toward precision medicine strategies that directly test tumor vulnerabilities [2].

Mitochondrial apoptosis as a threshold-regulated vulnerability in cancer

Apoptosis occurs through two main signaling cascades: the extrinsic and the intrinsic pathways [6]. While the first responds to extracellular death signals (Fig. 1A), the latter integrates various intracellular stress stimuli (Fig. 1B). Both pathways eventually activate the caspase family of proteases, which carry out controlled cellular dismantling.

The intrinsic pathway relies on mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) [7–9]. MOMP is regulated by the BCL-2 family of proteins, which act as a coordinated network rather than isolated effectors (Fig. 1C) [6]. These family members are classified into different groups based on their structure and function [9]. The first group comprises multidomain anti-apoptotic proteins (BCL-2, BCL-xL, MCL-1, BCL-w, BCL-2A1 and BCL-B), which inhibit death by sequestering pro-apoptotic members. The second group consists of pro-apoptotic effectors BAX, BAK and BOK, which directly promote MOMP. The last group includes BH3-only proteins, which either directly activate BAX and BAK (BIM, BID, PUMA) or act as sensitizers (BAD, NOXA, HRK, BMF, and others) by specifically neutralizing anti-apoptotic proteins.

Cellular stresses, such as DNA damage or growth factor deprivation, activate BH3-only proteins, which then engage BAX and BAK to trigger MOMP [8]. This process releases cytochrome c and other proteins from mitochondria into the cytosol [6]. Cytochrome c binds to APAF-1 and pro-caspase-9 to form the apoptosome, initiating caspase-9 activation. Caspase-9 activates executioner caspases (caspase-3 and -7), which degrade critical cellular components such as cytoskeletal proteins and nucleases that fragment DNA, as reviewed elsewhere [9, 10]. This results in distinct morphological changes (Fig. 1D), including cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, membrane blebbing, and the formation of apoptotic bodies [11]. These features ensure that cellular components are packaged and removed efficiently by phagocytes without causing an inflammatory response [12].

Apoptosis operates on a threshold system [1, 2, 13]. At that specific point, the mitochondrial membrane breaches and the cell dies (Fig. 2A) [14]. Engagement of mitochondrial apoptosis does not proceed in a gradual or proportional manner; instead, cells remain viable until a critical balance of pro- and anti-apoptotic signals is surpassed, at which point MOMP occurs [14, 15]. This apoptotic threshold is dynamic, shaped by protein interactions [16], post-translational modifications [16–18], the specific mitochondrial context or the subcellular localization of the BCL-2 family of proteins [16, 19, 20].

A defining difference between cancer and normal cells is their ability to evade apoptosis [21]. While physiological apoptosis eliminates damaged cells to preserve tissue health, oncogenic transformation creates chronic stress that would normally trigger cell death [22]. To survive, tumors adapt by suppressing apoptosis, often by overexpressing anti-apoptotic BCL-2 members or impairing BAX and BAK [9]. These changes do not eliminate cells’ ability to die, but instead shift the apoptotic threshold, allowing cancer cells to tolerate higher levels of stress. This resistance not only facilitates tumor initiation and progression, but also represents a major barrier to effective chemotherapy, as most cytotoxic agents rely on the activation of apoptosis [1, 5, 23].

This state of constrained survival creates a specific vulnerability. Because cancer cells face persistent stress, they are often closer to the threshold than non-tumoral cells. In fact, many tumors become dependent on a small subset of anti-apoptotic proteins to stay alive. This dependence explains why some cancers are highly sensitive to drugs that disrupt the BCL-2 family interactions.

Given that the apoptotic threshold is dynamically regulated by shifting protein-protein interactions, single expression-based biomarkers are insufficient to capture real-time cellular changes [1, 2].

From static snapshots to dynamic apoptotic profiling

The gap between molecular characterization and the functional outcome has prompted an increasing interest in Functional Precision Medicine (FPM). This approach tests therapies directly on living cancer cells, functioning conceptually analogous to antimicrobial susceptibility testing to identify optimal therapeutic agents [24]. By integrating the downstream effects of genomic, epigenetic, and microenvironmental factors, these platforms complement molecular analyses and facilitate a shift from empirical treatment toward personalized strategies that optimize the balance between efficacy and toxicity [3, 25]. Unlike assays of dead cells or static biomarkers, FPM captures mechanisms of resistance that are invisible to conventional determinations [3].

Within this framework, BH3 profiling constitutes a foundational functional assay that directly interrogates mitochondrial readiness for apoptosis (Fig. 2). Developed by the Letai laboratory, this methodology directly measures how close a tumor cell is to the apoptotic threshold [26]. In its original implementation, it involves selective permeabilization of the plasma membrane followed by exposure of intact mitochondria to synthetic BH3 peptides derived from pro-apoptotic BH3-only (activators or sensitizers) BCL-2 family members. MOMP is quantified through loss of mitochondrial membrane potential or cytochrome c release. This approach yields two main parameters: overall apoptotic priming, reflecting the cell’s intrinsic proximity to death, and specific anti-apoptotic dependencies, determined from selective sensitivity to peptides such as BAD (BCL-2/BCL-xL), HRK (BCL-xL), or NOXA/MS1 (MCL-1) [14, 26, 27]. These functional dependencies often diverge from expression-based measurements, indicating that apoptotic susceptibility is governed by protein-protein interactions rather than abundance alone [14].

Standard BH3 profiling provides a snapshot of a mitochondrial state and does not show how therapy reshapes apoptotic signaling (Fig. 2B). This limitation prompted the development of Dynamic BH3 Profiling (DBP) (Fig. 2C), which quantifies changes in mitochondrial priming following short-term ex vivo drug exposure [15]. An increase in priming reflects early engagement of upstream death signaling pathways and reliably predicts cytotoxic responses that may manifest days/weeks later in conventional viability assays. DBP enables rapid prediction of therapeutic efficacy, minimizes artifacts associated with prolonged ex vivo culture, and sensitively detects adaptive shifts in anti-apoptotic dependence that underlie non-genetic mechanisms of drug resistance. These advantages have driven the continued evolution of BH3 profiling into scalable, high-throughput formats that overcome the technical and conceptual limitations of the original assay [28–30].

Our understanding of apoptosis has advanced alongside the development of BH3-mimetics, establishing a framework in which functional apoptotic profiling informs drug discovery and clinical translation (Fig. 1C). Early studies with navitoclax, a dual BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibitor, demonstrated robust apoptosis induction in BCL-2-dependent malignancies but revealed dose-limiting thrombocytopenia resulting from on-target inhibition of BCL-xL in platelets [31]. These observations directly motivated the development of venetoclax (ABT-199), a BCL-2-selective inhibitor designed to preserve antitumor efficacy while sparing platelets and BCL-xL-dependent non-tumoral tissues [32]. Subsequent efforts to extend BH3 mimetic activity to solid tumors uncovered a broader and more heterogeneous landscape of anti-apoptotic dependencies, frequently involving BCL-xL and MCL-1. However, clinical translation of inhibitors targeting these proteins has been constrained by undesired toxicities, including thrombocytopenia for BCL-xL and cardiotoxicity for MCL-1 [33, 34]. These limitations have driven the development of alternative strategies such as PROTAC-based degraders [35], antibody-drug conjugates [36, 37], and transcriptional suppression of MCL-1 via CDK9 inhibition [38–41].

These advances show how functional interrogation of mitochondrial apoptotic priming has not only guided the rational design of BH3 mimetics but continues to inform the safe and effective exploitation of apoptotic vulnerabilities across diverse tumor contexts. Research studies produced between 2015 and 2025 showed that BH3 profiling acts as a practical checkpoint in FPM. The assay captures how the cumulative effects of diverse cellular stressors combine to determine whether a patient will respond to therapy.

Revisiting the paradigm of BCL-2 dependence in hematologic malignancies

Early work established that sensitivity to BH3 mimetics is not only determined by the expression levels of BCL-2 family members, but by mitochondrial proximity to the apoptotic threshold. It is known that multiple hematologic malignancies evade apoptosis through overexpression of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members, particularly BCL-2 [42].

The development of the assay firmly established this paradigm in blood cancers. In multiple myeloma, BH3 profiling showed that what really determines sensitivity to venetoclax is not just how much BCL-2 is present, but how strongly tumor cells functionally depend on it [43]. Similar findings were later observed across other hematologic malignancies, like Blastic Plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell Neoplasm [44], Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL) [45], and subsets of Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) [46–48], among others. Tumor cells were highly primed for apoptosis and relied mostly on BCL-2 to sequester pro-apoptotic activators such as BIM [44, 45]. DBP accurately captured this “functional addiction” and outperformed genomic or proteomic biomarkers in predicting venetoclax response.

Expanding the spectrum to Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia

Functional profiling has broadened the range of malignancies identified as BCL-2 dependent. BH3 profiling and related preclinical methods have identified high-risk leukemias, including early T-cell precursor ALL, immature T-ALL, KMT2A (MLL)-rearranged ALL, Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL, and TCF3-HLF-positive ALL as functionally reliant on BCL-2 despite marked genetic heterogeneity [49–54]. Ex vivo sensitivity to venetoclax can be accurately predicted by functional BCL-2 dependence measured by BH3 profiling in B-cell precursor ALL (Table 1) [55]. In patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, leukemia-free survival following venetoclax treatment was determined by mitochondrial addiction to BCL-2, as assessed ex vivo [55]. This predictive capacity was validated across independent cohorts, including high-risk and T-ALL models, establishing functional BCL-2 dependence as a robust determinant of in vivo therapeutic response. These results provide strong evidence that BH3 profiling captures the integrated apoptotic state regulating drug sensitivity and supports its use as a stratification tool for guiding venetoclax therapy in ALL [55]. Adult Early T-Cell ALL is primarily BCL-2 dependent, whereas adult T-cell ALL demonstrates co-dependence on BCL-2 and BCL-xL [56]. BH3 profiling accurately predicted sensitivity to venetoclax and navitoclax, and immunoblotting confirmed differential BIM binding patterns (BCL-2 in Early T-cell ALL; by BCL-2 and BCL-xL in T-cell ALL). These functional dependencies were reflected in early clinical responses: venetoclax-based therapy achieved complete responses in a subset of adult patients and undetectable minimal residual disease in another subset, validating BH3 profiling as a functional stratification tool for guiding therapy in adult ALL.

Clinical applications and prospective validation in Acute Myeloid Leukemia

BH3 profiling represents a promising functional approach for therapeutic stratification in patients [57], including AML. It can identify patients unlikely to respond to standard induction chemotherapy and identify sensitivity to targeted therapies, even in the absence of actionable mutations. A proposed clinical framework integrates DBP results into multidisciplinary tumor boards, combining clinical history and genomic data with functional vulnerability maps to support personalized therapeutic decisions.

The predictive value of mitochondrial priming has remained consistent across diverse treatment regimens, despite the small cohorts and retrospective nature of early research (Table 1) [46–48, 58–60]. Initial studies using archived specimens [46, 60], established priming as an independent prognostic marker for survival and induction response. These retrospective insights have been corroborated by subsequent analyses of clinical trials’ data [48], and specifically for response evaluations to hypomethylating agents [47].

The clinical application of functional assays in AML is transitioning from retrospective validation toward prospective, real-time utility. Recent developments have demonstrated the feasibility of simplified protocols that adapt the traditional BH3 profiling assay to only three concentrations of BIM peptide and venetoclax [61]. By normalizing the apoptotic response of leukemic blasts against a patient’s own internal lymphocytes, researchers have achieved success rates of approximately 90% in routine settings (Table 1). This methodological refinement bypasses the need for limited healthy hematopoietic stem cells, providing a robust diagnostic tool that serves as an independent prognostic indicator for survival regardless of genetic background [61]. Further prospective evidence from cohorts using fresh samples confirms that functional profiling integrates complex biological variables into a superior biomarker [62]. This study reported high predictive accuracy for complete remission in both intensive chemotherapy and venetoclax-based regimens, with AUC values (0.88–0.91) significantly exceeding those of the European LeukemiaNet genetic risk classification (0.67).

In the relapsed/refractory setting, where treatment options are limited, these assays are particularly valuable for avoiding futile toxicities; in the VenEx trial, no patient identified as resistant ex vivo achieved a clinical response, while sensitive patients showed significantly improved survival (Hazard Ratio 0.33). While BH3 profiling measures immediate mitochondrial readiness, the prospective VenEx trial has further validated the field by measuring direct cell death via flow cytometry after 48 hours of venetoclax exposure [63]. DBP could predict clinical response to complex combinations, such as lenalidomide plus MEC chemotherapy, in relapsed/refractory AML patients [64, 65]. By measuring the increase in mitochondrial priming induced by lenalidomide, this functional assay served as a near-perfect binary predictor for achieving complete remission, reaching AUC values of 0.95 for the BIM peptide and 1.0 for the PUMA peptide (Table 1). These clinical trial findings confirm that lenalidomide-induced increased apoptotic priming in vivo enhances the vulnerability of myeloblasts to subsequent cytotoxic agents, providing a robust mechanism-based rationale for therapy selection [64].

A major clinical advantage of these assays is their high feasibility and rapid execution time, which allows physicians to receive actionable results far earlier compared to the one/two weeks required for comprehensive genomic profiling. DBP and early biomarkers predicted pro-apoptotic anti-leukemic drug combinations (Table 1) [66, 67]. Changes in ribosomal protein S6 phosphorylation and PUMA BH3 peptide-induced cytochrome c release could predict cytotoxicity at 48 hours to DNA-damaging agents (etoposide, vosaroxin) and FLT3 inhibitors [67].

Mechanisms of adaptive resistance and functional rewiring

As venetoclax transitioned into widespread clinical use, BH3 profiling again proved its critical use for explaining therapeutic failure. Although single-agent venetoclax induces deep and durable responses in CLL, responses in AML and other malignancies are frequently transient [42]. Demonstrating that standard-of-care therapies and targeted agents can induce rapid functional rewiring of the BCL-2 network, with surviving cells shifting dependence toward MCL-1 or BCL-xL, or reducing overall mitochondrial priming [68, 69]. These adaptive resistance mechanisms are largely masked in genomic sequencing but are detected by BH3 profiling-based functional assays.

Mechanistic insights into these processes have been investigated using PDX models. Bhatt et al. showed that acquired resistance to venetoclax or the MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 was rarely driven by new mutations, but instead by the redistribution of BIM from BCL-2 to MCL-1 and concomitant loss of apoptotic priming. DBP revealed that resistant cells often retained sensitivity to alternative targeted agents, including FLT3 inhibitors and SMAC mimetics, and that concurrent inhibition of BCL-2 and MCL-1 could overcome resistance when appropriately administered [59]. Standard BH3 profiling showed that patients with high-risk Myelodysplastic Neoplasms and secondary AML present varied dependencies on BCL-2 family members. High BCL-2 dependence predicted favorable response to azacitidine/venetoclax, whereas increased BCL-xL dependence correlated with primary resistance (Table 1) [70]. Longitudinal sampling revealed that treatment failure frequently arose through adaptive shifts, such as decreased apoptotic priming or a shift toward MCL-1 or BCL-xL dependencies. In vitro targeting of these acquired vulnerabilities, such as BCL-xL inhibition in resistant cases, effectively restored apoptosis [70]. This framework extends to cases of advanced resistance, including AML blasts that no longer respond to either venetoclax or navitoclax [71]. In these resistant cells, dynamic profiling has identified specific subgroups that rely entirely on MCL-1 for survival. These dependencies create a vulnerability to dasatinib, which induces MCL-1 degradation and triggers caspase-dependent apoptosis. DBP correlated strongly with ex vivo drug responses, and immunoblotting confirmed higher MCL-1 and BIM expression in sensitive samples [71]. These results show that profiling anti-apoptotic dependencies can effectively stratify resistant patients’ dependencies and guide the selection of novel therapeutic strategies when standard inhibitors fail.

Apoptotic priming to guide next-generation therapies

The scope of BH3 profiling has recently moved beyond identification of single-agent testing to guide rational combination strategies and new therapeutic modalities. This application is particularly relevant in T-cell lymphomas (TCLs), where conventional molecular profiling has struggled to identify effective targets [72, 73]. Evidence shows that the levels of anti-apoptotic proteins, measured by transcriptomics or protein abundance, correlated poorly with the tumor’s response to BH3 mimetics [74]. In contrast, DBP accurately predicted drug sensitivity across cell lines and PDX models (Table 1). This functional approach identified a subset of TCLs that depended primarily on MCL-1 for survival. In these models, the selective MCL-1 inhibitor AZD5991 significantly extended survival. When researchers combined AZD5991 with CHOP chemotherapy, the results were synergistic and outperformed the effects of either treatment alone [74]. These data indicate that mitochondrial dependency provides a logical basis for adding BH3 mimetics to existing chemotherapy protocols.

BH3 profiling has enabled the deployment of novel therapeutic modalities that extend beyond conventional small-molecule inhibition in hematological malignancies. In post-myeloproliferative neoplasm AML, Wang et al. used BH3 profiling to confirm dominant BCL-xL dependence in JAK2/CALR/TP53-mutant hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells [75]. This functional insight enabled the deployment of DT2216, a PROTAC that selectively degrades BCL-xL via the VHL E3 ligase, sparing platelets (and preventing thrombocytopenia) while achieving potent antileukemic activity alone or in combination with azacitidine, ruxolitinib, or venetoclax.

Functional screening has moved beyond direct BCL-2 modulation to identify how metabolic drugs can be repurposed for oncology treatment. Research by Lee et al. demonstrated that statins enhance venetoclax efficacy in AML and Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma. By inhibiting protein geranylgeranylation, statins trigger an upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein PUMA and increase mitochondrial priming [17]. Retrospective clinical data confirmed higher complete response rates among venetoclax-treated CLL patients also receiving statins. These results suggest that widely available non-oncologic drugs can be used to lower the apoptotic threshold. In parallel, DBP has also been used to track how standard chemotherapy changes the apoptotic state in Diffuse Large B-cell Lymphoma. Data show that CHOP chemotherapy causes surviving cells to shift their dependence toward MCL-1or BCL-xL [68]. This heterogeneity depends on both the drug and the specific cellular context. Because of these adaptive shifts, adding BH3 mimetics to standard regimens will likely require individualized functional testing to be effective in patients.

Integrating BH3 profiling assays with cellular immunotherapy has revealed new therapeutic opportunities. Research in T-cell lymphomas shows that resistance to CD37-directed CAR-T cell therapy is linked to higher apoptotic thresholds mediated by MCL-1 [76]. By mapping this dependency, pharmacologic inhibition of MCL-1 sensitized tumor cells to granzyme B/perforin-mediated cytotoxicity. This shows that functional profiling can be used not only to select targeted agents, but also to prime malignant cells for enhanced immune-mediated killing.

BH3 profiling studies have fundamentally changed the idea that hematologic malignancies only rely on BCL-2. The data reveal a complex landscape of anti-apoptotic dependencies that shift according to disease subtype, cell maturation, and therapeutic pressure. While BCL-2 remains a major target, studies show that MCL-1 and BCL-xL often act as primary dependencies or adaptive escape routes. These findings in blood cancers highlight the need to detect dynamic and heterogeneous apoptotic changes to improve therapy.

Overcoming apoptotic redundancy and adaptive rewiring in solid tumors

Mitochondrial survival in solid tumors is controlled by multiple co-dominant proteins. This differs from hematologic malignancies, which often mostly depend on a single family member. Pan-cancer mapping has revealed that while hematologic cells often rely on BCL-2, most of the solid tumors, such as breast, lung, colon, and kidney cancer, depend on BCL-xL, MCL-1, or a complex combinatorial buffering by multiple family members [77].

Adult solid tissues show low mitochondrial apoptotic priming and are largely refractory to genotoxic stress. In contrast, highly proliferative cells and certain pediatric tissues remain primed and more sensitive [78]. These differences in apoptotic threshold depend on the developmental context and the cellular lineage of the tissue. High c-Myc-dependent activity in pediatric tissues and progenitor cells promotes apoptotic priming, whereas lower levels in adult tissues lead to a more resistant state [78]. These dynamics may explain why pediatric cancers and rapidly proliferating malignancies respond better to therapies targeting the intrinsic apoptosis pathway than most adult solid tumors.

Translating the therapeutic successes observed in hematologic malignancies to solid tumors has taken a deeper understanding of their inherent apoptotic redundancy. Because multiple proteins regulate cell survival, inhibiting a single member is often insufficient to trigger MOMP. Therapeutic strategies have therefore shifted from seeking direct single-agent treatments to using targeted inhibitors as priming agents. Using DBP to track these pathways may help identify how chemotherapy or targeted therapies push mitochondria toward the apoptotic threshold. This functional shift renders the tumor cell acutely susceptible to subsequent perturbation, transforming a sub-lethal stress into a decisive pro-apoptotic event.

Breast cancer and subtype-specific dependencies

Functional BH3 profiling has revealed distinct vulnerabilities across breast cancer subtypes. In Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC), a subtype characterized by the absence of targeted hormonal receptors, the apoptotic threshold is frequently governed by oncogenic kinases. PIM1, for example, promotes survival by maintaining BCL-2 and MCL-1 levels. The inhibition of PIM1 increased mitochondrial priming and sensitized TNBC cells to chemotherapeutic agents like paclitaxel and eribulin [79]. This suggests that measuring mitochondrial effects can guide the use of kinase inhibitors. In PDX models of TNBC, combining BCL-2/BCL-xL inhibition with EGFR-targeted antibody-drug conjugates (ADC) led to significant tumor regression, particularly with high EGFR expression [80]. Microscopy-based DBP confirmed that mitochondrial signaling correlates with these in vivo responses. This illustrates how this assay can direct the design of rational combinations that maximize efficacy while mitigating systemic toxicity.

The Luminal (ER+) subtype exhibits a more complex regulation of the BCL-2 family. While endocrine therapy remains the standard for ER+ disease, sensitivity depends on mitochondrial estrogen receptor localization as well as nuclear ER activity [81]. Mitochondrial ER-β maintains high apoptotic priming, whereas a shift toward ER-α or the loss of ER-β raises the apoptotic threshold and leads to resistance. Mapping therapy-induced adaptations shows that ER+ cells utilize dual buffering by BCL-xL and MCL-1 to sequester BIM and prevent MOMP [82]. A sequential therapeutic strategy has proven effective: AKT inhibition with ipatasertib primes mitochondria, but apoptosis is triggered only when BCL-xL (A-1331852) and MCL-1 (S63845) are subsequently inhibited. This metronomic function-guided approach demonstrates how DBP can convert acquired resistance into a predictable, targetable vulnerability, offering a blueprint for rational BH3 mimetic combinations in refractory ER+ breast cancer [82].

Functional mapping is also necessary in HER2-enriched and Invasive Lobular Carcinoma, where adaptive rewiring can mask therapeutic targets. Research using DBP shows that BET inhibition induced a specific functional dependence on BCL-xL [83]. Although BET inhibitors are cytostatic as single agents, combining them with BCL-xL inhibitors (or FGFR1 inhibitors in resistant lines) created a synthetic lethality. These results also illustrate how functional profiling can identify rational combination partners for epigenetic therapies [83].

Melanoma: adaptive resistance and priming

Solid tumors possess a rapid adaptive capacity that complicates treatment. In melanoma, research using DBP identified a non-genomic resistance mechanism to MAPK pathway inhibition [84]. Treatment with BRAF or MEK inhibitors led to the rapid loss of the pro-apoptotic protein NOXA due to mRNA destabilization mediated by the RNA-decay protein ZFP36. Without NOXA, MCL-1 is free to sequester BIM, which reduces apoptotic priming and limits the efficacy of targeted therapy. DBP revealed that this adaptation created a distinct, acquired dependence on MCL-1. Sequential treatment with BRAF inhibitors followed by MCL-1 inhibition resulted in profound tumor regression in vivo, a strategy that would have been missed by genomic sequencing alone. Beyond these acute adaptations, this functional assay has also uncovered how melanoma cells survive therapy-induced senescence, a state typically resistant to apoptosis [85]. Senescence involves the downregulation of the sensitizer HRK, which increases the amount of BCL-xL available to sequester BAK and prevent MOMP. This survival state makes senescent melanoma cells sensitive to BCL-xL-targeted strategies. Navitoclax, the inhibitor A-1331852, and the PROTAC DT2216 have all shown potent senolytic activity in these models.

Lung cancer: plasticity and subtype divergence

Adaptive plasticity is also evident in lung cancer. In Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC), DBP predicts chemotherapy response and identifies heterogeneous dependencies on BCL-xL and MCL-1 [86]. In oncogene-driven disease, the assay accurately predicts sensitivity to ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors and uncovered rapid adaptive responses. These responses are characterized by NOXA downregulation and the emergence of MCL-1 survival dependencies (and in some cases BCL-xL-assisted) [87]. Blocking these adaptations with BH3 mimetics prevents the formation of drug-tolerant persister cells in vitro and in vivo.

Small Cell Lung Cancer (SCLC) shows a different apoptotic profile. Cells in SCLC are highly primed and unable to adapt to the rapid loss of MCL-1 and cFLIP induced by CDK9 inhibitors like dinaciclib [88]. This lack of plasticity makes SCLC sensitive to apoptosis induction, even in chemotherapy-resistant models. DBP highlighted this fundamental difference in apoptotic adaptability between lung cancer subtypes, supporting CDK9 as a potent therapeutic target for this disease.

Aggressive tumors: glioblastoma and pediatric malignancies

The clinical failure of conventional therapies in glioblastoma highlights the need for functional approaches such as DBP, which can uncover targetable apoptotic dependencies in tumors characterized by marked aggressiveness and limited therapeutic alternatives. Despite intensive multimodal treatment (including surgery, radiotherapy, and temozolomide) [89], patient outcomes remain poor, largely due to tumor persistence and recurrence driven by chemoresistant subpopulations.

Research suggests that in glioblastoma cells, BCL-xL and MCL-1 act as redundant apoptotic guardians [90, 91]. They are essential in glioblastoma stem-like cells, which are often more resistant to conventional treatments [90]. Research using BH3 mimetics to probe sensitivity has identified that these resistant subpopulations are highly primed for apoptosis and that they can be exploited for glioblastoma treatment [90]. Also, standard-of-care therapies rewire the intrinsic apoptotic network in a genotype-dependent manner, specifically in p53-wild-type tumors through PUMA induction [91]. To therapeutically exploit these vulnerabilities, a novel precision medicine methodology has been developed that combines an antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) targeting BCL-xL (ABBV-155) with standard therapy. This strategy utilizes the Glioma Apoptosis Vulnerability Analytics (GAVA) machine-learning framework, which integrates molecular characteristics with functional tumor data (specifically BIM AUC values derived from DBP) to robustly predict which tumors will respond to BCL-xL-targeted combinations across diverse patient-derived model systems (Table 1) [91].

In pediatric medulloblastoma, DBP identified BCL-xL as the primary adaptation against cisplatin-induced apoptosis [92]. Inhibiting BCL-xL alone is often insufficient because it triggers a compensatory reliance on MCL-1. DBP showed that BCL-xL inhibition creates acute MCL-1 dependence. Only the dual inhibition of BCL-xL and MCL-1 combined with cisplatin triggers significant cell death.

Other solid tumors

Functional profiling has also elucidated actionable dependencies in other solid tumor types. In metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer, docetaxel resistance in PTEN wild-type tumors is driven by the co-activation of PI3K/AKT and MEK/ERK pathways [93]. This study shows that single-agent inhibition fails to induce sufficient priming, while dual blockades effectively activate the mitochondrial pathway. Post-treatment adaptation analyses showed that certain combinations failed to overcome anti-apoptotic buffering. For instance, treatment with selumetinib and capivasertib resulted in significantly higher cytochrome c release than the combination of selumetinib with AZD8186. These results suggest an anti-apoptotic adaptation mediated by MCL-1, pointing to more effective strategies for this specific prostate cancer subtype [93].

In rhabdomyosarcoma, resistance often arises from MCL-1 adaptation [94]. Exposure to vincristine can cause BID and BAK to bind more effectively to MCL-1, increasing the apoptotic threshold. DBP-guided sequential treatment of the MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 after vincristine was shown to counteract this adaptation and successfully stop tumor progression in PDX models.

DBP also identified key dependencies in esophageal adenocarcinoma and malignant pleural mesothelioma. MCL-1 and BCL-xL act as survival factors that, when inhibited, sensitize these tumors to cisplatin [95]. High-throughput DBP assays in mesothelioma PDX models have validated the combination of navitoclax with mTOR inhibitor AZD8055 [96]. Similarly, rare tumors such as thymomas and thymic carcinomas depend on MCL-1 and BCL-xL, whereas dual inhibition overcomes resistance to single-agent therapy [97].

New platforms now integrate real-time BH3 profiling with cyclic immunofluorescence to measure apoptotic priming and protein expression in single cells [98]. In colon cancer models, this approach revealed marked intratumoral heterogeneity, identifying chemoresistant subpopulations characterized by low mitochondrial priming, elevated BCL-xL and MCL-1 expression, and distinct cell-cycle states and growth factor signaling. By directly linking proteomic states to functional apoptotic vulnerabilities, the study demonstrated that resistance emerges from specific cellular subsets that are hidden in bulk analyses [98].

BH3 profiling was used to functionally define apoptotic dependencies in chromophobe renal cell carcinoma, identifying BCL-xL as the dominant survival factor in this therapeutically underserved tumor subtype [99]. This type of tumor revealed a compensatory upregulation of MCL-1. Because inhibiting BCL-xL leads to a compensatory increase in MCL-1, effective treatment requires targeting both proteins or using BCL-xL degraders like DT2216 [99].

Overall, multiple studies in solid tumors highlight the importance of understanding these dynamic and heterogeneous apoptotic states. Shifts in anti-apoptotic dependencies arise early, indicating that apparent dependencies in bulk assays mask clinically relevant heterogeneity. In summary, while some hematologic cancers exhibit a simpler “addiction” to BCL-2, multiple studies demonstrate that solid tumors operate through a redundant network of BCL-xL and MCL-1 that provides a pro-survival buffer against therapy and enables swift adaptation. Together, these findings show that apoptotic vulnerabilities are determined by cellular state and lineage context and can be detected by functional determinations, supporting DBP as a critical tool for predicting response, guiding therapy, and anticipating resistance in solid tumors.

Key challenges of the BH3 profiling assays

Across preclinical models and early clinical studies, mitochondrial priming has consistently emerged as a central determinant of therapeutic outcomes. While BH3 profiling platforms successfully overcome the predictive limitations of static biomarkers by capturing real-time life-death decisions, several technical and biological challenges still limit their widespread clinical implementation.

A fundamental limitation is the strict dependence on fresh, viable tumor material because of the functional readout. Unlike genomic assays, BH3 profiling requires a single-cell suspension and cannot be performed on formalin-fixed or paraffin-embedded tissues. This requirement is problematic for solid tumors, where obtaining enough biological material is difficult. Although short ex vivo exposure minimizes culture-induced artifacts [28, 62, 67], collecting and processing samples remain difficult for many solid tumors. Biopsies are invasive, carry risks of patient injury, and are impractical for the continuous monitoring of disease progression.

The dissociation of solid tissues into single-cell suspensions introduces additional variability in BH3 profiling, as mechanical and enzymatic processing (often involving collagenase) can affect mitochondrial integrity and apoptotic protein levels prior to measurement [100]. Sample handling steps such as cryopreservation and thawing may induce artificial caspase activation and alter baseline apoptotic priming [101], further impacting assay readouts. Consistent with this, coefficients of variation (CV) are lower in minimally processed samples such as peripheral blood B cells (CV ~ 5.1%) and higher in tissue-derived populations such as tonsillar B cells (CV ~ 13.6%) [69]. While fresh samples generally provide higher viability and improved resolution compared to cryopreserved material, dissociation is not universally efficient across all tumor types. Highly fibrotic or irradiated specimens, for example in esophageal adenocarcinoma [95], often show poor survival during processing and subsequent ex vivo culture required for dynamic BH3 profiling. Given that priming is calculated relative to baseline controls, sufficient viability is essential, as primary tumor cells can rapidly lose functional integrity outside their native microenvironment, limiting the effective time window for functional drug exposure.

The requirement for high cell numbers presents a significant barrier for the implementation of BH3 profiling in solid tumor samples. Traditional assays often demand more material than standard clinical biopsies provide. To address this, newer BH3 profiling technologies address these constraints (Fig. 2B). For instance, high-throughput dynamic BH3 profiling (HT-DBP) [28], is a microscopy-based assay performed at single-cell resolution that enables the functional screening of hundreds to thousands of compounds directly in patient-derived tumor cells. Drug candidates identified by HT-DBP have demonstrated robust anti-tumor activity in PDX models, establishing this platform as a powerful and unbiased tool for therapy personalization. Microfluidic DBP (μDBP) [29] also reduces cell input requirements by using a microfluidic chip to generate BIM peptide gradients. While preserving sensitivity, μDBP produced results comparable to standard DBP and successfully identified an effective therapeutic combination in a patient with a refractory gastrointestinal stromal tumor, emphasizing its potential for clinical implementation. These advances represent an important step toward the deployment of microfluidic, automated, user-friendly functional assays for in situ precision medicine [29].

The goal of functional assays is to recapitulate the biological responses occurring within the human body [102]. Intratumoral heterogeneity also complicates data interpretation [62, 103], as rare subpopulations with distinct apoptotic thresholds may drive resistance and relapse. Recent advances in high-throughput and single-cell BH3 profiling begin to address this internal complexity [28, 98]; the tumor microenvironment (TME) presents an additional layer of regulation. As a complex and dynamic ecosystem that surrounds, nourishes, and modulates the tumor, the TME plays a decisive role by providing extrinsic signals that decrease mitochondrial priming [104]. CLL cells residing in the bone marrow niche are significantly less primed than matched cells in the peripheral blood [105]. When cancer cells are exposed to factors such as IL-10, CD40L, and CXCL12, the stroma can force a survival shift, upregulating alternative proteins like MCL-1 or BCL-xL and moving the cell further from its apoptotic threshold [105]. Overcoming this stroma-mediated rescue requires disrupting the protective architecture of the niche. De-adhesion strategies using PI3K or BTK inhibitors to mobilize CLL tumor cells from the bone marrow to the blood effectively restored their mitochondrial priming and increased their sensitivity to BH3 mimetics [105, 106]. Similar TME influences are observed in glioblastoma, where BDNF/TrkB signaling between differentiated tumor cells and cancer stem-like cells further dictates survival dependencies [90]. Beyond biological factors, physical and pharmacokinetic barriers limit clinical accuracy. DBP typically assumes direct drug exposure, which overlooks constraints such as the blood-brain barrier in gliomas or the dense spatial architecture of solid tumors.

Beyond shielding, the TME itself harbors targetable vulnerabilities. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) frequently express high levels of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX, a state that primes their mitochondria for death and makes them uniquely sensitive to the BH3 mimetic navitoclax [107]. Selective deletion of CAFs using BH3 mimetics can suppress tumor outgrowth and metastasis by depleting malignant cells of critical stroma-derived survival signals, as demonstrated in desmoplastic models like cholangiocarcinoma [107]. In breast cancer, the anti-apoptotic protein MCL-1 serves as a critical regulator of the myofibroblastic phenotype in CAFs. Inhibiting MCL-1 with a BH3 mimetic (S63845 or AZD5991) can reverse this activation, decreasing the fibroblasts’ pro-invasive capacities and their ability to promote cancer cell invasion in 3D environments [108]. This phenotypic reversal is driven by mitochondrial fragmentation following a groove-independent interaction between MCL-1 and the fission protein DRP-1.

Integrating BH3 profiling with organoids or sliced cultures could potentially overcome the limitation of TME signal loss that occurs in isolated cell cultures. These 3D models may offer a more robust platform for studying the rewiring of apoptotic machinery and stromal-induced resistance. Despite being a reliable technique for expanding the available material, PDO generation often requires weeks of cell culture. This delay might induce clonal selection and frequently alter the original epigenetic and phenotypic state of the tumor. DBP could provide a functional readout within 24 to 48 hours, aiming to preserve the immediate in vivo apoptotic state. Because it is typically performed on dissociated cells, it sacrifices the biomechanical context provided by 3D models to achieve higher speed and throughput. Despite these complementary strengths and limitations, further validation is required to establish how accurately these functional assays recapitulate apoptotic priming and anti-apoptotic dependencies observed in patients. In this context, patient-derived gliomaspheres have provided strong evidence that BH3 profiling can robustly identify clinically relevant vulnerabilities [91]. Across heterogeneous glioblastoma cohorts, a consistent dual dependence on MCL-1 and BCL-xL has been observed, which is functionally confirmed by the selective induction of apoptosis only upon their combined inhibition.

Beyond predicting immediate cytotoxic responses, DBP has emerged as a powerful functional framework for dissecting mechanisms of therapeutic resistance, particularly those associated with therapy-induced senescence (TIS) [85]. Senescent tumor cells frequently display resistance to conventional chemotherapeutics, including Aurora kinase and topoisomerase II inhibitors, yet recent studies demonstrate that these cells remain highly susceptible to senolytic strategies targeting BCL-xL [85, 103, 109]. Also, challenging the prevailing assumption that senescent cells are uniformly less “primed” for apoptosis [100, 109]. It is shown that TIS is often accompanied by a global reduction in mitochondrial priming relative to parental cells, while specific anti-apoptotic dependencies (especially BCL-xL) are retained or selectively amplified. This inheritance of survival dependency highlights the unique value of DBP over static genomic or proteomic biomarkers. Consistent with this model, in melanoma TIS models, survival of senescent cells was shown to be sustained through BCL-xL-dependent sequestration of BAK, thereby preventing MOMP [85]. BH3 profiling with BCL-xL and selective probes such as the HRK peptide accurately predicted senolytic sensitivity and outperformed transcriptional signatures [109], which often fail to capture dynamic protein-protein interaction networks governing apoptotic control. Moreover, DBP provides mechanistic insight into the emergence of secondary resistance. Jochems et al. identified MCL-1 upregulation as a principal compensatory survival pathway in TIS cells resistant to BCL-xL inhibition [103]. Incorporation of DBP into clinical workflows, therefore, enables discrimination between senescent cells that remain vulnerable to senolysis and those that have acquired alternative anti-apoptotic buffering. This level of functional resolution is critical for the rational deployment of BH3 mimetics, ensuring that senolytic interventions are directed toward patients most likely to benefit while minimizing unnecessary toxicity.

Looking forward, the greatest potential of DBP lies in its integration within unified precision medicine frameworks. Functional profiling is intended to complement, rather than replace, standard diagnostic assays, providing a more comprehensive test to guide treatment. Recent prospective studies demonstrating clinically feasible workflows represent important steps toward regulatory validation, but multicenter harmonization and large-scale trials will be required to establish DBP as a companion diagnostic. The standardization of peptide panels, normalization controls, and automated data analysis pipelines has improved the reproducibility and throughput of BH3 profiling techniques, paving the way for their clinical implementation. This evolution toward automated, user-friendly platforms ensures that the functional testing of candidate anti-cancer agents can be determined within clinically actionable timeframes, bridging the gap between bench-side discovery and bedside decision-making.

Conclusions

Ultimately, BH3 profiling assays exemplify a broader shift toward functional precision medicine, in which treatment selection is guided not only by tumor genotype but by cellular response under different perturbations and stressors. Wider adoption will require continued technical simplification, standardization, and validation across tumor types. As technologies mature and clinical infrastructures evolve, direct measurement of mitochondrial apoptotic priming may redefine how therapeutic vulnerability is assessed, enabling more rational combination strategies and more precise patient-centered cancer care.
