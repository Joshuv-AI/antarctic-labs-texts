Most people who agree with you won’t protest: Predictors of marches and demonstrations from six decades of research
Authors: Shelley Boulianne, Jennifer Earl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protest and activism are endemic and potent features of many nations’ political life and critical potential counters to democratic backsliding and authoritarianism. In this review article, we meta-analytically examine six decades of research findings on what explains participation, focusing particularly on two well-studied protest forms: marches and demonstrations. Focusing on these iconic and common forms of protest participation ensures accurate research comparison, allowing us to identify sets of variables that are consistently important, others that are not consistently important, and still others that need additional research. Our focus on survey-based studies also allows us to compare research that includes both participants and nonparticipants, which is critical for methodologically sound comparisons. Our analysis of 181 survey-based studies (with more than 1800 different estimates) shows that survey research strongly supports the relevance of political interest, political ideology, membership in organizations, and digital/social media use in explaining participation, as well as demographic variables such as education, age, and living in an urban center. Our review also offers important suggestions for future survey research and micromobilization research more broadly.

INTRODUCTION

Protest and social movements are important vehicles for voice and are arguably critical sources of social and political change (1). Protesters have challenged regimes around the world, revealing popular dissatisfaction in democracies and autocracies (2, 3) and sometimes igniting regime change (4, 5). Understanding what motivates participation is critically important in a world that has been pushing toward autocracy (6), facing democratic backsliding (7), and witnessing incredible antagonism to people challenging the conditions of their rule (e.g., Hong Kong student protesters) and working to improve their lives (e.g., climate crisis activists, Black Lives Matter activists, and gun safety activists). Protest and social movements also improve the lives and health of people in many other palpable ways, as, for instance, occurred when movements promoted anti-smoking legislation (8–10) and reduced “drunk driving” and related deaths in the US (11).

Across six decades of research on protest and social movements, thousands of studies have been published on what may lead to participation and/or activism. This research spans multiple disciplines, including sociology, psychology, political science, communication, and economics, among others. Each discipline highlights different aspects of protest participation (e.g., sociology emphasizes groups and organizations more than psychology, which focuses more on identity and efficacy). Many reviews of this sprawling literature are organized around disciplinary research lines, but a different path yields a robust and interdisciplinary state-of-the-art assessment: We meta-analytically compare results from surveys analyzing participation in two very specific, and important, forms of protest: participation in marches and demonstrations. The meta-analytic backbone of our review builds on the identification of more than 2800 academic outputs related to protest participation but focuses on a subset of 181 survey-based studies of participation in marches and/or demonstrations (with more than 1800 different estimates) using general population surveys, rather than surveys of protesters at events (see Meta-Analytic Methods section for more details on our methods). Instead of organizing our review around disciplines or their specific theoretical traditions, we shed that organizational scheme and organize our review around patterns in empirical findings across these 181 studies. In doing so, we reveal what is settled knowledge, identify areas of ongoing empirical contestation, and/or surface frontier areas that require more research.

Our focus on participation in marches and demonstrations allows us to identify patterns in research that may not otherwise be discernible across disciplinary boundaries. Since the 1970s, modern social science has acknowledged that social movements, and individual participation in them, is not an automatic result of the existence of grievances. As McCarthy and Zald famously pointed out, there are many more grievances than there are social movements, and some movements arise in the absence of objectively supportable grievances (e.g., anti-vaccination movements) (12, 13). This has made the study of the rise of social movements themselves (i.e., the study of mobilization) and the study of individual participation in social movements (i.e., the study of micromobilization, sometimes also referred to as differential recruitment) incredibly important topics of research and prolific areas of research.

Aggregating all forms of protest participation, collective action, activism, and social movement engagement into one dependent variable implies that these kinds of activities operate the same. Instead, we argue that the clearest synthesis of current empirical research is attainable by carefully controlling the comparison of research so that the dependent variable is always participation in these two common forms of protest participation, i.e., marches and demonstrations. Focusing on marches and demonstrations also allows us to synthesize empirical research that compares participants and nonparticipants, which is methodologically important. As discussed more shortly, comparisons between participants and nonparticipants are important in shedding light on what explains action versus attitudinal support for action. By doing this, our review surfaces three groupings of potential influences on actual protest participation: (i) explanatory factors that have clear and consistent effects on participation in survey research; (ii) explanatory factors that remain uncertain based on survey research; and (iii) explanatory factors that have great potential and need additional study in survey research.

In many cases, our findings are consistent with research drawing on a wide array of methodologies. For example, we show that membership in organizations, political interest, and various sociodemographic variables (e.g., education and age) are consistent predictors of protest participation. These factors are also supported by other styles of research.

As we review patterns in empirical results, we also review the claims and interpretations that accompanied the research we synthesize. That review shows that the mechanisms that are argued to connect these factors to protest participation are less clear and/or settled in the literature. We are not forwarding a specific theory of march and demonstration participation or introducing our own theoretical approach. Instead, we are reviewing others’ theoretical accounts as we review the empirical findings associated with those accounts. As a result, we spend much of the paper focused on theories that are supported with robust empirical evidence. However, where warranted, we also point to evidence that undermines existing causal accounts even when the empirical correlational findings are clear. In doing so, our goal is to foreshadow critical frontiers in research, which in many cases requires developing and/or resolving competing causal claims about why various explanatory factors are consequential. Also, much of the scholarship is correlational, which is weak for assessing causal claims. We address this issue in the Supplementary Materials (see section S6).

There is no existing theory that emphasizes the factors we find most consistently influence march and demonstration participation across the 181 studies we examine: political interest, political ideology, organizational membership, education, age, living in an urban center, and digital/social media use. We do not offer a new theoretical account that would do so but instead suggest ways for this interdisciplinary field to move constructively toward such an account.

In some cases, our findings stand in contrast to others’ meta-analyses, but that is due to important differences in the dependent variables used in the analyses. For instance, we find that the roles of grievances, deprivation, political efficacy, political trust, and traditional media use are not strong and/or consistent predictors of participation in marches and demonstrations, as many fields may have expected. Van Zomeren and colleagues (44, 46) have conducted a series of meta-analyses that conclude that people participate in protests because they are angry/experienced an injustice, identify with the group, and believe the group or the protest event will be effective in influencing the government or achieving their goals. We argue that the difference in findings results from our focus on survey research examining actual participation versus attitudes or reported willingness and our focus on two specific forms of participation—marches and demonstrations—versus the wider formats of participation they studied (e.g., signing petitions). More generally, this speaks to the importance of the specific dependent variable studied (e.g., intent to participate yields different results than actual participation, and participation in petitions may be explained by different factors than marches and demonstrations), as well as the impacts of different measurement approaches, political contexts, and/or historical periods on findings.

We also identify factors that show strong results but have not yet been studied extensively enough to allow robust conclusions. For instance, a series of political communication and interaction variables (e.g., being invited to participate, political discussion, and social media use) show great promise in explaining patterns of protest participation, but survey researchers have not studied them enough to date to reach firmer conclusions. Likewise, there is a need for survey research to consider more fully several factors that have received substantial attention in nonsurvey research. We discuss potential theoretical accounts of how these factors may matter causally but do not introduce or advance some wholistic theoretical account. Instead, we offer practical suggestions about the best paths forward to advancing research once patterns in findings are accounted for. Our meta-analytic approach provides a compelling and empirically meaningful backbone for our review, even while we also compare and connect our findings with findings from meta-analyses of different but related dependent variables and findings from different research traditions on micromobilization.

RESEARCH TRADITIONS IN THE STUDY OF MICROMOBILIZATION

Before introducing our meta-analytic approach, it is important to situate survey research within the broader scholarship on micromobilization, which has grappled with the need to compare participants and nonparticipants. A bedrock principle of social science research methods is that observational comparisons are more telling when there is a method of similarity and a method of difference (14). Applied to micromobilization, this means that comparisons are less likely to err when researchers can observe what is similar between protesters and what is different between those who did not participate and those who did. The second component—the method of difference—has been a difficult standard to satisfy because it requires identifying people who might have participated but did not and studying them too.

The most obvious way to solve this problem has been through survey research examining reported participation. Whether broad population studies, or population studies of more specific groups, like youth (15) or residents of a specific city (16), researchers from this tradition use the comparisons made possible from large samples of people—which invariably include people who have participated in protest and people who have not—to infer factors that may shape participation (see table S1). This includes surveys that ask retrospectively whether respondents have participated in protests over specific periods of time (e.g., in the last 6 months, in the last year, or in their lifetime). In the Supplementary Materials (sections S3 and S4), we include more details about how protest was measured and offer robustness tests related to key findings. Our review focuses on surveys about participation in marches and demonstrations as these represent the most researched and most iconic forms of protest participation. Our review, though, does note where research on other dependent variables may reach different conclusions. For instance, it is common in some areas to survey on the intention to participate, and this can yield different results.

Some survey researchers have focused on participation in specific major events or movements (17–19), also highlighting the important methodological advantages of comparing participants to nonparticipants. Oegema and Klandermans, a classic in the field, demonstrated the importance of surveying participants and nonparticipants. They studied participation in one of the largest mobilizations in Dutch history, which involved more than 35 million people in a petition against cruise missile deployment. They found wide-scale support for the goals of the movement, but even individuals who agreed with the goals of the movement and had initially intended to participate failed to do so if there were no attempts to directly mobilize individuals, or they faced barriers to participation, and/or faced unsupportive environments (20).

Other classic research in micromobilization earned its place in the canon by figuring out other ways to make comparisons between participants and nonparticipants without the benefit of a broad survey sample. However, unlike survey research, this often focused on participation in longer campaigns instead of in a march or demonstration specifically. For instance, several of the most famous publications on micromobilization focus on controlled comparisons of people who attempted to join protest campaigns but never did and those who attempted to join and ultimately participated. McAdam’s study of Freedom Summer participants in the US in the 1960s showed that organizational and network ties to other participants were important for participation (21). Later research using the same data, though, showed that network ties were not directly linked to mobilization but rather the pathway to identifying with a movement, which then drove mobilization. This method has been replicated in the study of participation in other major protest campaigns (22).

When interview-based studies engage participants and nonparticipants, comparisons can also be made, although the generalizability is less clear. For instance, interviews with young people actively involved in social movements and those that are not has shown that despite public concerns about the interest and engagement of young people, young people respond to mobilization attempts (e.g., invitations and/or network connections to social movements and information about movements), the (un)supportiveness of schools, families, and friendship networks, among other factors (23, 24). This research also often examines social movement participation in general, or activism more generally, as opposed to participation in specific events such as marches and/or demonstrations.

Social network scholars have used various tools to compare the networks of participants, although they often compare participants to those who participated and then abandoned or compare participants who engage at varying levels (versus comparisons to nonparticipants) (25). For instance, Diani (26) compared people of differing levels of engagement within the Italian ecology movement.

Experimental research has examined intent to participate and/or participation in fictitious events within experiments. While helping to elucidate what factors contribute to support of movements, many classic studies of micromobilization show that far more people intend to participate in protest than actually do (20, 21), making the ability to explain intentions notably different than the ability to explain participation. The distinction between intention and behavior also matters at a theoretical level. Below, we show that “biographical availability” (27), which refers to “the absence of personal constraints that may increase the costs and risks of movement participation” (27, 70) is repeatedly used in theoretical explanations. This theory cannot explain attitudinal support for collective action but appears to be important in theorizing actual participation.

Other research traditions either lack methods of difference entirely or focus on differences across time (28) or level of activity. For instance, research on dangerous or deadly forms of protest participation often lacks comparison sets (29). A growing body of research drawing on surveys of people who are present at protest events also lacks comparisons to nonparticipants (30, 31). Other kinds of variation may be substituted, such as comparing lower-risk activism to high-risk activism (27, 32), the decision to escalate tactics, including new uses of violence (33), or to continue participation in activism over time (34), especially in the face of repression (35, 36).

Ethnographic and/or interview-based research on activists and the social movements also has a long history of attempting to diagnose what led social movement activists to become involved (37). For instance, Blee has traced how women became involved in the KKK in the 1920s in Indiana (38) and in more contemporary hate movements (39), while Munson traced very different pathways to engagement for women involved in the pro-life movement (40). These approaches lack a method of difference but gain considerable insight into the features shared by participants. However, our meta-analysis is focused on studies of participation in marches and demonstrations, not broader or other forms of activism, social movement participation, or support or intentions to participate.

Our analysis is informed by insights from this broader literature but uses the comparison of survey findings that include participants and nonparticipants in two specific but common forms of protest participation—marches and demonstrations—as an organizing framework for identifying patterns from our meta-analytic investigation. In this way, our approach differs from prior reviews, which have either not been informed by a meta-analytic angle (41–43), have used meta-analytic techniques to examine only a small set of independent variables (44, 45), or have examined many different dependent variables together (44, 46). Also, many non–meta-analytic reviews have varied in how much they stress different predictors of micromobilization, largely based on the authors’ own theoretical background and commitments (47–49), while our review’s focus is guided by empirical results from existing research. We discuss theoretical accounts, including noting what may need greater development, but focus on identifying pathways for future empirical research as opposed to developing or forwarding our own theory of protest participation.

ESTABLISHED PREDICTORS

We consider established predictors to be explanatory factors that have been widely tested (at least 50 tests/studies) and for which the results have been in the same direction. This emphasis on shared direction follows Lorenz-Speen et al. (50). Figures report the frequency of relationships that were negative and positive and the number of results that were significant versus not significant, with precise values reported in table S2.

There are two categories of established predictors: non-sociodemographic predictors and sociodemographic predictors. Political interest, ideology, and membership in associations or organizations are the established predictors for which at least 85% of the relationships are in the same direction and about 70% are statistically significant and positive (see table S2). The other three established predictors are sociodemographic in nature: education, age, and urbanicity. While more than 70% of the results are in the same direction for education, age, and urbanicity, only half of the results achieve statistical significance in the expected direction.

Voluntary associations/memberships

As illustrated in Fig. 1, membership in voluntary associations or organizations, including unions, significantly increases the likelihood of participation in marches and demonstrations in about 69% of the studies that measured this variable (see Fig. 1 and table S2 for more details and the Supplementary Materials, section S5). While the importance of this factor is undeniable, there is considerable debate about the reason it is so important.

Number of tested relationships for political interest, memberships, and ideology.

First, organizational ties may be impactful in their own right. For instance, Somma (51) outlines a range of pathways from organizational involvement to protest participation and finds that these “time and money” contributions are the most relevant aspect of organizational engagement. Organizations have also invested in building ladders of engagement through which potential participants are encouraged to donate and/or take low-risk or low-cost action and build to larger and larger commitments. Since past action often predicts future action, this may facilitate participation. Alternatively, organizations may have larger numbers of members or connections and so even weak tendencies to mobilize members may generate strong turnouts. Since organizations can deploy “bloc recruitment” in which social movement organizations reach out to other organizations, such as unions, asking them to support their event by encouraging their members to participate, potential mobilization pools can be quite large. See the Supplementary Materials for additional clarification about the role of unions.

Another possibility is that organizational ties serve as proxies for more immediate mobilization predictors, such as invitations to participate or political discussion. Political discussion or talk (k = 28) tends to be a positive and significant predictor of participation in marches and demonstrations. As we discuss in the unresolved and new factors section, there is growing evidence that invitations matter too. It is possible that in surveys that are not directly measuring invitations, political discussion, identity alignment, and other factors, the apparent direct effect of organizational ties exists because the strong association membership has to do with other, more immediately mobilizing factors. For instance, surveys of only protesters (i.e., they do not include nonparticipants and are, hence, not part of our meta-analysis) substantiate the importance of organizational ties for learning about protest events (52) and offer nuances about the features of organizations that enable mobilization (53). Identity alignment may also be cultivated through organizational membership (47).

Whatever the specific route, a positive connection between organizational membership and micromobilization is consistent with the larger multimethod literature on micromobilization more broadly, particularly the large multimethod body of scholarship on social network effects in recruitment (54). That literature, though, points to many of the same potential mediators. For instance, network ties to activists embedded in organizations can lead to invitations to participate in events even before someone has beliefs or identities that are aligned with a movement, and it may actually be through participation that some people come to hear about and believe in movement claims (40).

An absence of ties does not eliminate the chances of participation. Research on digital media usage in movements, for instance, has shown that digital media can help to mobilize people who have little or no connection to existing movements (23, 24, 30). Looking at how individuals are mobilized who lack connections to movements may help to clarify the underlying causal mechanisms (e.g., invitations versus the more general effect of social capital).

Whether looking at only surveys and organizational ties, or the larger multi-method literature, the causality may have to do to direct action of these organizations, experiences people are more likely to have when connected to organizations (e.g., invitations, political discussion), it may have to do with a predisposition by participators to participate in both protests and organizations more than others, or it may have to do with the social capital and/or social embeddedness of the potential participant in a movement. The larger multimethod scholarship would lean toward more of a consensus around social embeddedness than ties to organizations (42, 47). However, even there, researchers measure embeddedness in a variety of ways (including in ways that do and do not align with organizational membership), making it difficult to agree about causal processes.

Given the importance of social network connections, social capital, and social embeddedness in the larger literature, we recommend that survey researchers incorporate a wider battery of questions to disentangle these potential mechanisms as well as a larger battery of questions on network connections to social movements instead of assuming that all connections occur through organizations (e.g., ties to friends or family or social media connections who participate, not just ties to organizations). It may be helpful for future survey research to draw on recent research on social networks and participation, using other methodologies in formulating these strategies (17, 55, 56). We also recommend that surveys attempt to control for other potentially mediating variables, like political discussion (47, 57) and invitations to participate. For instance, it is possible that the relationship is largely between political discussion and invitations, but since these are correlated with ties to organizations and not often measured, their effects are wrongly attributed to the general organizational tie as opposed to those more proximate causes. Alternatively, digital trace data could offer unprecedented access to network data to test how different types of social connections influence participation, such as connections to other protesters, degree of embeddedness within a network, and whether schools, families, and friends support (or discourage) protest.

Political interest

Survey-based studies offer strong affirmation that political interest is a consistent and positive predictor of participation in marches and demonstrations (Fig. 1). As reported in table S2, approximately 96% of tests are positive; of all 23 variables considered in this review, political interest has the highest percentage of significant effects in the expected direction. In short, political interest positively and significantly relates to protest participation. For example, one important study showed that there were two major pathways to participation—one driven by invitations to participate and another driven by political interest for those who did not report invitations to participate (58).

Verba et al. (59) explain the lack of citizens’ political participation as “they can’t, they don’t want to, and nobody asked” (p. 16); political interest measures don’t want to. Political interest is the motivation and is central to the civic voluntarism model. Politically interested people are more likely to participate because they have a stake in the outcome of the protest (60). As noted with other variables, political interest could be a proxy for a variety of mechanisms, including political ideology, political discussion, political efficacy, and political trust, which are all highly correlated with political interest (57).

There is some debate about the theoretical definition of political interest, which can be defined as a stable personal trait or as an ever-changing evaluation of politics (61). There is also some debate about whether political interest should affect participation in marches and demonstrations, since, for instance, participation in pride parades may not have explicit motives, whereas as participation in a No Kings march clearly has political motives. In any case, the results make clear that theoretical and empirical models of protest participation that exclude political interest are missing a critical variable. Considering that these results are drawn from surveys that include participants and nonparticipants, it is useful to underscore that this means that political interest does not just motivate attention to an issue but facilitates action specifically.

Political ideology

Political ideology, like other ideologies, serves to “describe or interpret the world as it is” and “to envision the world as it should be” (62). Results suggest that political ideology is important to micromobilization. The most popular way to conceptualize political ideology is the right-left distinction (62). Survey-based studies suggest that those who identify as left or liberal tend to be more likely to participate in marches and demonstrations (Fig. 1). As noted in table S2, 93% of the tests are positive, suggesting a consistency in the set of results. In addition, 71% of the tests are positive and statistically significant. The left-wing bias in protest may be due to the legacy of left-wing protests during the 1960s, the rise of the New Left in Europe in the 1970s (63), or a long-term legacy of protests being linked to workers’ rights. Using the European Social Survey (2002 to 2016), Borbáth and Gessler (63) show that left-wing ideology predicts participation in marches and demonstrations in Northwestern Europe. However, in Eastern Europe, communism was a more potent force and isolated these countries from the New Left movements (63). They also show that Eastern Europeans with extreme right-wing views are more likely to protest (63).

The relationship between broad political ideologies and protest may be changing over time. A 2021 survey conducted in the US, UK, France, and Canada shows that those on the right and left are more likely to participate in marches and demonstrations compared to moderates (64). In the US, right-wing protests are on the rise (65). Hence, the role of political ideology needs to consider historical and political context. Studies in Latin America (66) and Asia (67) do not include political ideology in their models, which means that we do not know as much about the relevance of political ideology in the motivation to protest in non-Western countries.

In thinking about these other ways of understanding ideology, one prominent model of micromobilization argues that participation is facilitated by building pools of potential participants through shared identities and ideology and then increasing motivation to participate in various ways. In this view, having more specific ideologies (e.g., different variants of feminism) that larger groups of people can connect with may drive up micromobilization levels because although not all adherents will participate, there are many potential people to be mobilized (28).

Education

Where education is specifically concerned, 51% found positive and significant coefficients, with more than 87% finding positive coefficients, whether or not they were significant (see Fig. 2 and table S2 for more detail). Education has consistently positive effects on participation in marches and demonstrations. This finding could reflect many mechanisms, from relationships between education and ideology to organizational ties to protest through campuses and/or social network effects. In particular, a handful of studies (k = 17) have examined whether being a student predicts participation, finding that being a student does increase the likelihood of participation. Researchers studying colleges and universities have noted that these environments are conducive to activism in a variety of ways (68, 69). However, Broćić (70) finds that attending university does not predict protest in a model that accounts specifically for majoring in liberal arts.

Number of tested relationships for education, age, and urbanicity.

Like other variables, education may also represent the effect of other unmeasured variables. For instance, better-educated people are more likely to be invited to participate in protests, which may lead to education appearing to drive participation when invitations are not measured (58). As we argue with each of these sociodemographic variables, it is important that researchers go beyond the strong association between these characteristics and try to understand the underlying mechanisms responsible for these associations.

How education works may also be context dependent. Using macrodata (thus not included in this meta-analysis) from 1960 to 2010 and covering 216 countries, Sawyer and Korotayev (71) explain that the effects of education depend on the country’s stage of sociopolitical development. In the early stages, formal education increases the intensity of antigovernment protests, but in later stages, education decreases participation in riots (71).

Age

Survey findings suggest a negative relationship between age and micromobilization. The survey measurement approach affects the pattern of age differences. When respondents are asked if they ever participated in a march or demonstration (a lifetime approach), e.g., the World Values Survey, age effects are rarely negative and significant. However, when age effects are assessed on the basis of participation during the past year, age effects are often negative and significant (see the Supplementary Materials, section S4). These age findings clash with popular anxiety about youth political disengagement, which persists despite a substantial and growing body of research showing that young people are politically active [see for a review of both concern about youth engagement and research showing youth engagement (72)]. Significant research reinforces young people’s integral role in a variety of social movements (73–76).

While traditional thinking within social movement scholarship tied young people’s notable engagement to biographical availability (15, 21), work on political engagement more broadly suggests that protest may be a preferred kind of political engagement for younger generations (77, 78). Some developmental psychologists would see biological and/or cognitive differences between young people and their elders as the cause (e.g., alleged heightened risk tolerance). However, this is unlikely to be accurate for a variety of reasons (79). First, adolescence and beyond is more of a socially constructed category than a biological or developmental category (80). There are no clear biological markers for “youth” and the definition of youth has been changing across societies in recent decades despite nonexistent changes in bodies across the same period, pushing back youth to include those 30 and younger in some places (81). Second, we can find no studies that test the relationship between alleged developmental differences and political engagement, let alone protest participation specifically. This is important because even developmental milestones and biological changes across the life course should vary among youth (e.g., not all young people hit the same markers for brain development at the same time), and some of those characteristics should also vary among older people too in ways that would allow correlations between characteristics to become evident (e.g., risk tolerance varies widely across the “adult” population and even within individuals across different domains, such as financial versus physical risks).

Instead of essentializing these participation differences, greater attention should be paid to how younger people are overcoming the many obstacles they face in becoming active (23). A large body of scholarship shows that activist organizations are often quite ageist spaces that are difficult for youth to navigate (82, 83), sometimes resulting in the formation of youth-led social movement organizations like the Sunrise Movement and the youth climate strike. Social movements also do not often attempt to directly mobilize young people (84, 85) despite the substantial assets young people can bring to activism (86). Thus, the strength of the age finding is unexpected given that in some ways, young people have the odds stacked against their engagement, making the routes through which young people are engaging important to study.

Researchers should also work to understand why adults (or people as they age more generally) participate less on average, including assessing whether this is linear versus curvilinear, as some research has found (15). Corrigall-Brown found that young people and seniors are more likely to participate than adults in midlife, when people are often spending time raising children, tending to aging parents, and growing their careers. These life cycle effects tip toward a biographical availability explanation at a mechanistic level. We recommend stronger testing of biographical availability by using more explicit questions about free time and commitments that might work against protest participation because there are more precise ways to measure biographical availability—which should vary across people even of the same age—so that the root causes of this age-based relationship can be better understood.

Urban

Living in urban areas may enable participation by reducing the time and effort required to participate, compared to when traveling to a protest event from outside the city. This hypothesis is well supported (see Fig. 2 and table S2). While some research has suggested that urban dwellers may have more grievances (87), it is also likely that people living in more urban environments have more network resources and other support for engaging in protest and/or may face lower costs or barriers for participating in protests. We argue this on the basis of an application of Klandermans’ supply-and-demand model of micromobilization (88).

Borrowing from economics, Klandermans (88) argued that micromobilization occurred when the demand for participation was met by a supply for participation and mobilization attempts. His focus on the demand side of participation is explicitly social psychological, as is work following from his original model (42, 47–49). On the demand side, factors like ideological alignment with a movement, identification with a movement, ties to current members, etc. all help to build a demand for participation (43). He notes that, “people participate not so much because of the outcomes associated with participation but because they identify with the other participants” (88). Demand may be more easily built in urban areas if there are more movement organizations, information circulation, and visible signs that other people are supportive and/or participating. Likewise, if rural areas had fewer actors trying to articulate and spread an ideology, or collective identity, or ties to current activists, the demand in rural areas might be lower. This would also be consistent with research showing that more conservative and populist movements have performed better in rural areas where there are more social supports for the growing demand for those movements (89).

On the supply side, Klandermans argued that opportunities for participation (e.g., protest events) must also exist in number and fit to allow people to engage. In other words, absent enough opportunities and opportunities that are appealing, even if there is substantial demand to protest, there may not be much protest. The appeal of events can be heightened by showing some likelihood of influence, or at least that others are also supporting the movement in increasing numbers. Research on college environments is aligned with this claim—When students are around many others who are also interested in protest and participating, it increases both demand for protest and also the appeal of the available supply (90). Framing opportunities to participate in appealing ways is also important to having an adequate supply of events (41). Appealing opportunities also align with important identities, which may help conservative and populist movements in rural areas (89), but opportunities to protest—however well-aligned with ideologies or framed—are certainly more abundant in urban areas. Urban areas may also benefit from more organizational and framing supports to make protests more appealing. This, too, could help to produce this urban effect.

Klandermans also argues that micromobilization results from demand and supply meeting as mobilization attempts occur to motivate people to participate in specific events. That is, while someone may want to protest, and there may be available opportunities, something has to catalyze the selection of and participation in a specific event. In some ways, one could think of supply and demand as factors that may affect geographic differences in the amount of protest because they affect the overall supply and demand; those geographic differences may be the large national differences that exist in protest participation, regional differences within countries, and urban versus rural differences. However, the selection and motivation to participate in specific events are more social-psychological and practical according to this model. Even still, there are ways in which urban dwellers may be differentially motivated, for instance, by seeing many others protesting, by having lower costs to participate in nearby and more abundant events, etc.

Whether this supply and demand model actually explains this urban effect is, of course, subject to more study. However, to do so through survey research, researchers need to move beyond just measuring the urban versus rural location of survey respondents and move toward asking questions that test these deeper causal reasons for the relationships.

COMPLICATED, NUANCED, AND UNCERTAIN VARIABLES

In this section, we highlight predictors that did not receive strong and consistent support in our meta-analysis but that have been described as theoretically important and may play a role in explaining other protest-related dependent variables (e.g., support for, or intentions, to participate). These variables may apply in some historical, political, or social context but are not empirically relevant across a variety of contexts; these findings are complicated and require nuances to explain. We sorted them into the broad categories of objective and subjective grievances and deprivation, political efficacy and trust, media, and gender.

Objective grievances and deprivation

Before McCarty and Zald (12, 13), and for many scholars since them as well, grievances, or appropriately framed grievances (37, 41, 91) have been thought to be integral to micromobilization. It would be accurate to say that grievances and deprivation have been traditional touchstones in the literature (42, 47, 49), although the field has lessened its focus over time (92). Scholars hotly debated whether grievances needed to be deprivation-focused, focused on relative deprivation, about suddenly imposed grievances, or about more general quotidian disruptions to daily life (93). Arguments have also considered whether objective conditions (i.e., objective grievances) were important to micromobilization, or whether it was the interpretation and meaning of these grievances that mattered, which drove the development of the larger literature on framing. Within economics, the key question is whether economic conditions (objective measures of grievances) predict protest activity. For instance, Cantoni et al. (94) did not find a relationship.

In the survey literature, grievances have largely been examined as objective conditions (i.e., objective grievances) that lead to micromobilization, without enough focus on the interpretation and meaning of conditions and how potential participants may differentially react to the framing of grievances. This is true to such an extent that some of the variables that have been used to measure grievances look similar to sociodemographic variables that may be used to study biographical availability. The key variables that have been analyzed are income, unemployment, and socioeconomic status (reported in Fig. 3), as well as dissatisfaction with personal finances; satisfaction with government; support for democracy; support or voting for the current ruler, president, or ruling party; and satisfaction with the economy (reported in Fig. 4). Survey results were rarely statistically significant.

More specifically, objective measures have not fared well in survey research (Fig. 3). While it is the case that much smaller comparisons of survey research and other methods have found support for factors that could be thought of as grievances (45), the findings for these objective measures of grievances are not supportive. For instance, income, dissatisfaction with finances, and support for democracy are almost equally likely to find a positive relationship as a negative relationship (see table S2).

Of course, one might argue that surveys are not doing a satisfactory job of measuring grievances. For instance, in Why Men Rebel, Gurr (95) outlined a theory of protest participation based on the tension between the current and desired state of affairs that results from comparing oneself to others. People develop a sense of entitlement to the same goods (welfare, power, and status) as their reference group. The key argument in this body of research is that it is perceptions that matter, not the objective measures of disadvantage (44, 95, 96), making it important to look at perceptions of grievances. Survey results affirm that common objective indicators of grievances (income, unemployment, and socioeconomic status) are weak predictors of protest participation; the most popular outcomes are statistically insignificant findings (Fig. 3). The same could be said of the weakness of these measures in relation to the voluminous literature on framing (97).

Subjective grievances

Where more subjective measures are concerned (Fig. 4), Van Zomeren, Postmes, and Spears (44) published a meta-analysis focused on three social psychological predictors of collective action, which was operationalized as “attitudinal support for protest as well as the protest intentions or behaviors of members of a social group that are directed at removing the perceived underlying causes of the group’s disadvantage or problem (e.g., signing a petition and participating in a demonstration).” For measures of injustice, they look exclusively at perceptions of injustice and exclude tests related to objective measures of injustice or inequality (44). They include measures of anger, dissatisfaction, procedural fairness, fairness related to the distribution of resources, and relative deprivation. While they are optimistic about the role of perceived grievances in collective action, the aggregate results across 181 surveys (focused on actual participation) do not validate the importance of grievances (table S2).

Klandermans and van Stekelenburg (98) outline the importance of different types of grievances for motivating protest participation. In particular, “suddenly imposed grievances” are “powerful mobilizers” (p. 167). The COVID-19 pandemic offered an opportunity to assess the effects of suddenly imposed grievances on the population’s propensity to protest. Within our database, a handful of studies explore protest in the context of the pandemic. Miller et al. (99) use a survey of more than 4000 respondents conducted in 2020, finding that almost 6% of Americans have participated in a protest against COVID-19 restrictions. Approximately 7% attended a protest about racism and police violence. While racism and police violence are ongoing issues in the US, COVID-19 was a sudden and unexpected event that caused grievance. What is distinctive about the two events is that the racism protests are connected to liberalism, whereas the COVID-19 protests are related to conservatism (99). In Chile, Donoso et al. (100) find that grievances were important for explaining protest participation, offering support that suddenly imposed grievances matter (98).

We recommend that survey researchers develop more compelling ways to measure the subjective motivations to participate in marches and demonstrations. For example, researchers could ask about the level of concern or worry about the topic(s) related to the protest event. This is important as it shifts the focus away from the individual and their personal experiences (of grievances or deprivation) toward a collective perspective. People may be motivated to participate because of concerns about the collective well-being of the community or country. For example, people may not believe that they are directly affected by climate change, but they may participate in environmental protests because they are concerned about the collective well-being of the planet (101).

Grievances are part of building demand for protest in Klandermans’ terms, but grievances themselves may not be enough motivation to secure participation. It may also be that other factors are simply better predictors, too. For instance, despite so many scholars’ (and lay people’s) beliefs that grievances might drive participation, existing scholarship suggests that sometimes, people act before having beliefs that are aligned with a movement (40). Alternatively, it may be that political control and/or repression make participation less possible and/or appealing (102).

Political efficacy and trust

Surveys suggest that of different conceptualizations of political efficacy, internal efficacy has more support as a positive predictor of micromobilization, but external efficacy, social trust, and political trust are not well-established explanatory factors. Political efficacy refers to a perceived ability to influence and participate in politics. In theory, participation in collective action reflects a rational choice when the potential exists to achieve goals and advance interests, despite incurring some costs related to participation (44, 103). If thought of as one’s own perceptions about one’s ability to participate in politics (104), internal efficacy has promising evidence related to its (positive) significant role in political protest (Fig. 5) but is not well-tested (also see table S2).

Number of tested relationships for efficacy and trust.

An important caution about internal efficacy involves its relationship to political interest. Political interest is usually highly correlated with internal political efficacy. Since political interest is a consistently important and positive predictor of micromobilization, it may be important to consider when the more stable trait for political interest is more likely to be relevant, versus internal political efficacy, which is more malleable, and thus could be used to understand participation in protest at different points in the life cycle. Research suggests that empirical models should be cautious in including both (57).

Instead of looking at internal efficacy, some scholars stress group efficacy. This definition ties efficacy to group identity. In particular, the “group” in group efficacy is linked to a perceived connection to a group (identity) that has experienced injustice, linking all three dimensions of social-psychological predictors of collective action (injustice, efficacy, and identity). van Zomeren et al.’s (44) meta-analysis results show that each of these three predictors has comparably sized coefficients in predicting collective action, including attitudes about collective action, willingness to engage, and actual participation in signing petitions and demonstrations. Klandermans and van Stekelenburg (98) suggest that “the more effective an aggrieved individual believes collective action participation to be, the more likely that person is to participate.” Other reviews of the literature that focus on collective efficacy reach similar conclusions (45). However, the survey data results are, instead, inconclusive (Fig. 5).

While we draw a distinction between collective and personal efficacy, and it is a conceptually and empirically important one, the broader literature on micromobilization—which does not focus on marches and demonstrations—does not always clearly delineate between these forms of efficacy. For instance, a variety of reviews touch on the importance of efficacy in explaining micromobilization, but seem to reference multiple forms of efficacy (42, 47), even if they acknowledge the conceptual difference (49). Other research is framed in terms of pessimism instead of efficacy. For instance, Oliver’s study of Detroit residents found that pessimism about the likelihood of others’ participation led to engagement (16). Future research should be more explicit about which form of efficacy is being studied (or compare forms of efficacy) and also consider whether pessimism can be mobilizing for some or in some situations. Future research should also consider how age and efficacy interact in influencing protest participation. This tactic may be particularly attractive for young people who cannot vote or feel that voting is not an effective way to influence politics.

The results on political trust are very inconclusive. A recent meta-analysis focused on political trust concluded that this variable did not consistently predict formal or informal political participation (k = 19), such as protest participation (105). We replicate this finding with our own meta-analysis (k = 56). Our greater number of studies, focusing exclusively on protest rather than the broader category of informal participation, suggest that political trust is not a consistent predictor (Fig. 5). The estimates are evenly split between positive and negative findings as well as significant versus nonsignificant findings. We also affirm that interpersonal or social trust does not predict protest participation.

Media

We examined the impact of different media on micromobilization in prior survey research, recognizing the important potential distinctions between media as divergent as newspapers and social media. Three important takeaways emerge. First, as we suspected, the modality of news media matters, as shown in Fig. 6, which compares the effects of newspapers and TV news versus newer forms of news media. This is consistent with recent research showing differences across modality and positive correlations with social media news (106).

Number of tested relationships for media uses.

Second, we assess non-news uses of social/digital media using a measure that includes the use of specific platforms/websites, posting to social media, and ties to online groups and find positive and significant effects with this measure: Half of the tests are positive and significant, which is comparable to the consistency observed for education. Approximately 80% of the tests showed consistency in the directionality, comparable to that observed for organizational memberships. However, we did not include this variable in the “settled list” because the tests do not use a consistent measure (various measures are combined).

Third, our examination of survey results does not support a tie between traditional media and micromobilization. The number of significant coefficients is very low compared to the number of tests. Survey-based studies suggest that TV news negatively relates, whereas print media positively relates to protest participation (Fig. 6). The overwhelming story is that it is not clear what role traditional news may have on micromobilization.

This may be quite unexpected for some since the study of traditional news media coverage of protest is a major industry in social movement studies. Many scholars seem to endorse the idea that traditional media’s coverage of ritual events (107) or protests of longer duration, such as the Occupy Movement or the Black Lives Matter Movement, informs citizens, and through that, contributes to downstream effects on participation. There are reasons to doubt this claim, such as retroactive coverage of protests in traditional media, which leads to no effects on participation (108) and biased or negative coverage (109). Our findings add additional doubt.

Research on digital media is more supportive of a significant, positive link to micromobilization, even if not enough tests have been done to place these findings on more solid footing. Digital media can help to mobilize people who have little or no connection to existing movements (23, 24, 30). It can also offer information about a protest quickly and widely without relying on traditional media as gatekeepers of information flow. Using digital media, organizations can redistribute online information about protest events to their members, combining organizations’ strong roles (see Fig. 1) and information effects into a powerful mobilizing force (Fig. 6).

Social media are also distinctive as a media form; these media combine information about an event and social networks (family and organizations). Jost et al. (110) highlight the roles of social media in providing information, motivation, and social networks supporting protest participation. Simonson et al. (111) examine eight different social media platforms’ roles in the Black Lives Matter protests, using data gathered in 2020. They find that mobile messaging apps, such as Snapchat and WhatsApp, are not significantly related to protest participation. “Friending apps” such as Facebook are negatively related, and “following apps” such as Twitter and TikTok are positively related to participation in these protests. YouTube is negatively related to participation in BLM protests (111).

Much of the research considers digital and social media as extensions of traditional media, adapting these theories to understand digital and social media uses. However, digital and social media could be considered in terms of the network features. Specifically, these digital tools connect individuals to one another and to groups and organizations. Given the strong and consistent role of organizational membership, it is logical that social media would also play a significant and consistent role in the mobilization process. Digital and social media are critical to sharing invitations to participate in events; these invitations may be sent from organizations or members of one’s social network. As described in the literature, many critical protest events began with invitations to participate circulated on social media platforms (108). Yet, we still do not know whether these online invitations are effective and what features of these invitations make them more or less effective in persuading people to participate. Future research could adapt Han’s (112) work on petitions and contacting officials to the study of marches and demonstrations.

These questions could be settled using a series of experiments that measure actual participation in protests. Conducting experiments to promote protesting has come under some scrutiny as different communities debate the implications of promoting protest. However, some economists have conducted experiments to that effect, providing Hong Kong citizens with a cash incentive if they provided documentation about the size of the event, which indirectly documents their participation in the event (113). They found that social interaction (e.g., making new friends) increased the persistence of protest participation in an ongoing movement in Hong Kong against the Chinese Communist Party.

Furthermore, we recommend that survey researchers use a broader definition of “information” rather than measuring “news.” Digital and social media are essential to learn how members of one’s network view the issues attached to the protest (versus the voices presented in traditional media). These platforms vary in the degree to which they support interactivity among network members. Some platforms enable this exchange of information with minimal interference (e.g., private messaging apps); others filter this information through algorithms and/or content moderation practices (114). These media are also critical for sharing updated and timely information to enable participation, such as logistical details about the protest event. Platforms differ in terms of the degree to which information is accessible outside the network. Open platforms enable a wider reach to potential protesters but also make it easier for police and governments to surveil and repress activities (115) as well as easier for countermovements to monitor and organize counterdemonstrations (64).

Gender

Surveys suggest that women are less likely to participate than men, but the empirical evidence does not support a clear gender difference (Fig. 7). The results may differ by type of event. Although most surveys ask about sex, our interpretation follows a gender analysis, pointing to socially constructed differences that lead to different resources and attitudes (116, 117).

Number of tested relationships for gender.

Gender could shape participation in marches and demonstrations for several reasons. First, in historical terms, women had lower levels of education, labor market participation, and political interest, which reduced political participation, including participation in protests (116). Liu (117) argues that resources attached to labor force participation continue to be important in explaining gender differences in protest participation in East and Southeast Asian countries. Second, the gender division of labor in the home can create greater demands on women, reducing the time available for political participation, especially time-intensive forms of participation such as marches and demonstrations. This taps the construct of biographical availability, which claims that protest participants must have the time and resources to participate (15). Third, protest participation may be considered too high-risk for women, consistent with the depressing effect of gender on Freedom Summer participation (21). Grasso and Giugni (118) argue that gender differences may be better understood in terms of gendered political opportunity structures and changes in social processes to undermine the current political establishment. For example, changes in government spending on public services might create a gendered opportunity for participation (118).

Future research should consider the net effect of gender once markers for different pathways for its effect are introduced. For instance, gender, education, biographical availability (which typically includes having a job, a family, and/or free time), and family or friendship ties to other participants could be compared in the same model to determine whether gender continues to matter on its own once these other factors are examined alongside it.

UNRESOLVED AND NEW FACTORS

Beyond the suggestions for future research that we already introduced, we conclude by offering pathways to untangling complicated findings to understand more fully emerging predictors of micromobilization.

Invitations as a research frontier

As noted earlier, there are a handful of survey studies measuring receiving an invitation to participate, including Schussman and Soule (58), which is a canon in the field. There is also reasonably strong support for invitations having an impact in survey research using nonrandom samples and from ethnographic fieldwork. For instance, a study of 2646 survey respondents who were connected to the key organizer of the Grand Parade protest in Brussels in 2016 (but representing both participants and nonparticipants in the protest) found that “being asked is by far the strongest variable in the model,” outperforming political interest, active membership in relevant organizations, social approval, ideology, framing, and individual efficacy (18). Other research has shown that being informed, specifically informed about the event, is critical to participation (19). Munson (40) also stresses the importance of invitations based on his qualitative study of the pro-life movement. He found that some very active participants, and even leaders, came to the movement through personal invitations to events.

Given the strength of this finding across multiple modalities of research, questions about invitations should be critical to all future micromobilization studies. Digital trace data and online tracking studies could offer critical nuances about the role of online invitations and also related questions like the amount and content of political discussion, which would enrich our understanding of the mobilization process. Another promising line of research related to invitations involves the supply of invitations, to borrow Klandermans’ framing. Further research could consider who is doing the inviting, the content of the request, and the modality (online versus offline) to better understand how this important variable operates to mobilize people to participate.

Future avenues related to organizations and social connections

While research has established that organizational ties matter, contemporary theories of protest focus on online networks (119) and question the importance of formal organizations in the mobilization process (120–122). Furthermore, generational elements may need to be considered. For older generations, formal memberships in organizations are often retained, making these organizations essential to their mobilization. In contrast, younger generations tend to have weaker ties to organizations and may be more easily mobilized by informal networks, particularly online ties to groups. These online ties to groups may be considered weak and perhaps temporary ties, but they are nonetheless crucial for facilitating discussions about grievances, distributing information about the issues, and sharing information about events (73). Furthermore, communication research has been at the forefront of mapping these online network dynamics and how these explain the rise and fall of offline protest activities [e.g., 123, 124]. Currently, this line of research has faced serious setbacks as platforms restrict access to data, particularly Twitter/X, a focal point of research on social media and protest (110).

Since nonparticipation in protest is more of a norm than participation worldwide, researchers should consider also how strongly supported factors, such as social networks and social media, may demobilize participation sometimes. As noted in early social movement scholarship, social networks can offer a push and pull effect, drawing people into and isolating them from movements. Simonson et al. (111) found that Facebook and YouTube uses are strongly and negatively related to participation in the Black Lives Matter movement; these are also the most widely used platforms in North America and Western European countries (125). These kinds of nuances are important to consider, and digital trace data offer opportunities to explore these nuances, if only researchers had access to these data. Which social media platforms might mobilize versus demobilize? How do these push-pull dynamics map to the types of networks that people create on these different platforms?

Future lines of research about political interest

As discussed, political interest is a consistently strong predictor of participation in marches and demonstrations. New lines of research could offer more nuances about generational differences in the role of political interest in political participation. For example, does political interest matter more for some age groups than others? To the extent to which some have argued that generational differences are more important than age differences in understanding participation in marches and demonstrations (126), it would be useful for surveys to test for generational differences in the relationship between political interest and micromobilization. For instance, Kim and McCarthy (127) explain the relationship between political interest and willingness to protest among high school seniors from 1976 to 2015; they find that political interest is the “strongest predictor” (p. 542) in their model, and this effect became stronger over time. Researchers studying the same dataset but focusing on actual participation, however, found that the role of political interest depended on risk preference (128). These processes have not been tested in autocracies where the risks of participation are significantly higher than in the United States.

Another line of research to consider is how political interest moderates the role of other key predictors in predicting participation patterns. For example, will someone participate if they are low in political interest but have strong social connections to others who are participating? In other words, family and friends mobilize people to participate in the absence of strong political interest. Surveys of protesters suggest that people attend these events with family or friends (30, 52); hence, group ties or group identity may be more important to mobilizing some citizens than their interest in current or political events. Within the more general study of political participation, scholars have examined inequalities in participation and how that affects whose voice counts in public policy (129); these same questions apply to protest participation. Walgrave et al. (56) offer a useful starting point for this question, exploring usual and unusual suspects in surveys of protesters at 71 events in nine countries; however, as they note, their conclusions are limited as they do not have nonparticipants in their sample.

Beyond left-wing political ideology

Research on protest participation has focused on those on the left and social causes associated with the left. For example, Walgrave et al. (56) look at 71 protest events and these events tend to, albeit are not exclusively, relate to left-wing causes, such as anti-austerity, the environment, valence (women’s rights and anti-racism), and ritual demonstrations such as pride events. Yet, in the past decade, there has been a rise in right-wing protests in the United States (65). We also see the rise of populist movements, such as the Pegida movement in Germany (130); these movements also organize marches and demonstrations (131). Just as surveys may work to detect generational differences in relationships, analyses should interrogate whether people on the right are mobilized using the same processes (64).

FACTORS OUTSIDE OF SURVEY RESEARCH

There are a number of other factors discussed in the broader micromobilization literature that simply do not make it into survey research. For instance, the alignment of personal identities with collective identities embraced by a movement is argued to be an important contributor to micromobilization in a range of existing reviews (42, 47, 88), but this is poorly studied in survey research. A thorough review of potential causal connections between identity and participation makes a strong case for the critical role of identity in micromobilization (49). The use of sociodemographic indicators in surveys (e.g., the use of gender and sexuality, race/ethnicity, etc.) is insufficient for this purpose. The larger multimethod literature would suggest that surveys should assess how closely respondents identify with the groups that protesters represent (or, how distant they feel from them). Broad partisan or ideological identifications may help to address this somewhat, but future research should work to determine whether more specific alignments in identities help to facilitate mobilization in the ways outlined in the multimethod literature on micromobilization.

Likewise, a range of authors have recently argued that emotions are generally important to understanding social movements and that anger in particular is important to understanding micromobilization (41, 44, 49, 132, 133). However, scholarship claiming anger mobilizes often do not have comparison sets of nonparticipants (who might also be angry) and/or also argue that other factors like grievances drive mobilization (42, 45, 47) despite the lack of support for a tie between grievances and micromobilization in our meta-analysis. Furthermore, some protests uplift positive emotions and the affirmation of a common collective identity. In addition, picking up the theme about the efficacy of protest, people may participate because they are hopeful.

Our review and synthesis of 181 survey-based studies (with more than 1800 different estimates) revealed that memberships in voluntary associations, political interest, political ideology, education, age, and living in an urban area are the most consistent predictors of participation in marches and demonstrations. There is no existing theory that focuses on all explanatory factors that we found to be consistently important, or even only the nondemographic variables that we found were consistently important. There was less consistent support in existing survey research for objective or subjective grievances, political efficacy and trust, and media effects.

Future research can benefit from this review in several ways. First, we recommend that survey researchers add subjective measures of grievances. This can be as simple as asking respondents how concerned or worried they are about different issues or how concerned they are about the issues represented in different opportunities to participate they experienced and/or in different invitations they may have received. We also recommend survey questions: (i) ask about invitations to protest; (ii) distinguish between different modalities of information and news and different forms of efficacy (internal versus collective) and trust (political and social); (iii) ask about collective identities and the alignment of individual identities with perceived collective identities; and (iv) ask about positive emotions such as hope, not just questions about anger.

We also show that there is a range of variables for which a relationship is clear, but the mechanism behind that relationship is not as settled. Subsequent research should focus on how these variables may be causally connected to micromobilization. For instance, we reviewed a range of ways that sociodemographic characteristics—education, age, and urbanity—are connected to micromobilization. It is imperative that future research adjudicate between alternative mechanisms, which may require new survey questions or more comprehensive batteries of questions so that alternative mechanisms can be more directly compared.

We also recommended that subsequent research focus on digging more deeply into the broadly supported and widely acknowledged relationship between social network ties and micromobilization. How do informal times with family and friends compare to organizational ties in mobilizing participants? Similar moves need to be made with respect to political interest. Are there generational differences in political interest, and does political interest operate differently across generations? Does risk offset interest or operate independently from interest? How do social ties influence political interest?

Last, we recommended an expansion beyond survey data to other kinds of digital trace and network data that may also offer methods of similarity and difference and help to deepen our understanding of micromobilization. For instance, digital trace data for online forms of protest may help elucidate the effects of different invitations’ framing and messaging and/or evaluate how important the sender of the invitation is to any effects on participation. Likewise, research could investigate media effects as operating through network relationships, not just information. Digital trace data could be used to measure ties to other participants, degree of embeddedness, messages of support/criticism from family and friends, etc. and differences in platforms in terms of openness, network ties, and interactivity could also be investigated.

META-ANALYTIC METHODS

This project involved searching Google Scholar and Scopus multiple times between December 2018 and October 2025. Using Google Scholar, we identified the most cited study using the keywords “political protest” and “protest participation.” The most cited study was Schussman and Soule (58). We downloaded all 844 citations to this article. In addition, we searched Scopus using political protest and protest participation, which produced another 1958 studies. We excluded non-English studies, unpublished studies, and dissertations. We screened abstracts and titles, excluding another 1877 records because of relevance. We downloaded the full-text records of 439 studies. However, we ultimately excluded 294 studies for various reasons (see fig. S1). From the database search, we identified 145 relevant records. We supplement these records with an additional 36 studies that the first author identified while preparing various manuscripts related to collective action.

Best practices in meta-analysis suggest using at least two databases, which we have done: Google Scholar and Scopus. However, to assess whether we missed any studies, in October 2025, we completed the search in Web of Science and EBSCO databases (SocIndex with Full text; Academic Search Complete, APA PsycINFO; and Communication & Mass Media Complete). We used HubMeta Ignite to remove duplicates across multiple databases, finding 2804 records. We coded 135 studies (training set) and then used the software’s built-in AI tool to identify the top 400 relevant studies. We downloaded all 400 studies to determine whether we had missed any relevant studies. We only found three new studies. We added these to the existing database. In the Supplementary Materials (section S1), we include a table summarizing the academic journals that appeared most often in our database.

The original meta-analysis tracked the results for 50 predictors. However, in 2021, we decided to drop race, ethnic identity, anger, experiencing discrimination, identity, life satisfaction, invitations to protests, interactions with family/friends, and trust in politicians from the meta-analysis because there were very few studies on these topics. Here, we focus on 23 variables that are theoretically important and have at least 10 tests of the relationship between the variable and protest participation. All data entry involved at least two coders working collaboratively, including the first author.

The analysis is based on 181 studies that included 273 samples; studies could include more than one sample if they were cross-national or longitudinal. Much of this research focuses on high-quality, representative samples, such as random-digit-dialing surveys or area probability sampling for face-to-face interviews. The World Values Survey, European Social Survey, regional Barometer studies, and Latin American Public Opinion Project (LAPOP) are popular data sources. The United States is the most popular country for analysis. Survey data were collected between 1965 and 2023. Almost all 273 samples were analyzed using logistic regression analysis because protest participation was measured as a yes/no question. Further details about the study collection process can be found in the Supplementary Materials. The list of 181 studies is also included in this documentation. To summarize the results of these regression models, we used the following system:

1) Relationship is positive and significant (usually reflected as * or P value that is less than 0.05).

2) Relationship is positive but not significant (P value that is greater than 0.05).

3) Relationship is negative and significant (usually reflected as * or P value that is less than 0.05).

4) Relationship is negative but not significant (P value that is greater than 0.05)

This approach replicates other meta-analyses (134, 135). We also include statistics about whether relationships were positive or negative (see table A2), following other meta-analyses (50). In the Supplementary Materials (section S2), we include an additional discussion of our meta-analysis approach.
