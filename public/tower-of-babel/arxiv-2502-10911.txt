Interfacial spin-orbit coupling in superconducting hybrid systems
A. A. Mazanik, Tim Kokkeler, I. V. Tokatly, F. Sebastian Bergeret

Abstract
We investigate the effects of interfacial spin-orbit coupling (ISOC) on superconductors, focusing on its impact on electronic transport and spin-charge conversion. Using a symmetry-based nonlinear sigma model, we derive effective boundary conditions for the Usadel and Maxwell equations that account for the spin-galvanic effect, spin relaxation, and spin precession. This approach allows for the analysis of various interfaces without relying on specific microscopic models. We apply these boundary conditions to derive ISOC-induced terms in the Ginzburg-Landau functional, which is then used to compute the critical temperature of superconducting films with ISOC subjected to an external magnetic field. Our findings show that, contrary to a recent prediction, the critical temperature of a film cannot be enhanced by an external magnetic field. Additionally, we demonstrate that the combination of ISOC and an external magnetic field leads to a superconducting diode effect. Its efficiency strongly depends on the interplay between the spin-galvanic and the spin relaxation terms. Our results provide a framework for understanding ISOC in superconducting systems and highlight the potential for optimizing diode efficiency through careful interface engineering.

To avoid dependence on a specific model of the interface and to capture all effects due to spin-charge coupling, phenomenological theories are best suited. Within superconductivity, the most well-known phenomenological theory is the Ginzburg-Landau functional. The Ginzburg-Landau functional in the presence of ISOC was used in different works; see
[

and references therein. For instance, the Ginzburg-Landau functional with an ISOC-induced term was recently utilized in
[
47

The advantage of this type of phenomenological approach is its independence from any specific microscopic model of the interface, which makes it broadly applicable.

However, the G-L approach comes with a few disadvantages. First of all, it can only describe superconductors close to their critical temperature and is therefore not suitable for the description at low temperatures, at which many experiments are carried out. Next to this, by going to the critical temperature, the superconducting coherence length diverges, and therefore the G-L theory should be treated with caution in systems of finite extent.
The phenomenological description of bulk spin-orbit coupling in the low temperature regime and on smaller scales was recently achieved by applying a phenomenological approach to the non-linear sigma model (NLSM)
[
48
]
. Because of the NLSM structure, such models are valid for all temperatures, while they are spatially accurate on scales much larger than the scattering length, which is a considerably weaker condition than for G-L theory. Meanwhile, by using phenomenological arguments for spin-orbit coupling, all possible types of spin-orbit-induced terms for a given material were identified, thereby automatically capturing the spin-charge conversion induced by spin-orbit coupling.

In this work, we use the method developed in
[
48
]
to derive the NLSM and describe normal metals and superconductors with ISOC at their boundaries. The latter is represented by constructing an effective boundary action that contains all symmetry-allowed effects, specifically spin precession, spin-galvanic effect, and spin relaxation. Using the saddle-point approximation of the derived NLSM, we find the bulk Usadel equation, and the boundary condition at the interface with an insulator with ISOC. With this, we provide the first description of low-temperature transport and spin-charge conversion in superconducting systems with ISOC.

Based on these equations we calculate the influence of ISOC and spin relaxation on the critical temperature and diode efficiency of superconductor/insulator junctions. We show that the critical temperature in such systems cannot increase with the application of a magnetic field, in contrast to the result of
[
47
]
.
To clarify this discrepancy, we derive the corresponding G-L functional and demonstrate that, to the second order, the spin-galvanic tensor increases the superconducting stiffness, that is, it becomes more costly to create phase gradients. This increase compensates any possible enhancement of the critical temperature in a magnetic field by the ISOC-induced Lifshitz invariant, thereby nullifying the effect. With decreasing thickness, the magnetic field dependence becomes weaker but, unlike in junctions without ISOC, the magnetic field dependence does not disappear, but instead converges to an ISOC strength dependent value. Thus, ISOC in fact makes thin materials more fragile against magnetic fields.

A consequence of the Lifshitz invariant that is not nullified, but only reduced by this increased stiffness, is the appearance of an equilibrium phase gradient for nonzero magnetic fields, which is usually indicative of a superconducting diode effects.
To explore this effect, we solve the Usadel equation for low temperatures for thin superconductors and show that the superconducting diode effect indeed exists.
We find that the strength of the superconducting diode effect crucially hinges on the relative strength of the spin-galvanic and spin relaxation terms, thereby emphasizing the importance of developing junctions with small relaxation.
In particular, its efficiency may approach
\approx 20\%
in thin films for reasonable intensity of the interface SOC in an in-plane external field of
\approx 0.7H_{c2}
for low enough values of spin relaxation. Here,
H_{c2}
is the second critical field of the superconductor.

To investigate whether such effects appear and to assess how strong they are, we derive the quantum transport equations in the diffusive limit. These equations can be obtained with the help of the so-called nonlinear sigma model (NLSM), which describes the soft-diffusion modes via a generating functional

. Moreover, since we consider a boundary action for a second-order differential equation, we keep terms up to first order in spatial derivatives. Keeping in mind the normalization condition
\check{Q}^{2}=\check{1}
, we construct all possible terms allowed by the charge conjugation symmetry introduced in Ref.
[
48
]
:

\displaystyle iS_{SOC}\left[\check{Q},\ \check{\bm{A}}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{S-I}}dS\ \frac{\pi\nu_{0}}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\Big\{\frac{i}{2}\alpha_{kj}\sigma_{j}\check{Q}\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{Q}

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{8}\gamma_{lk}\epsilon_{ijl}\sigma_{i}\check{Q}\sigma_{j}\check{Q}\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{Q}+\Gamma_{ab}\sigma_{a}\check{Q}\sigma_{b}\check{Q}\Big\}\;,

(16)

where
\mathcal{S-I}
is the boundary between the superconductor and the insulator. The first of these terms is the spin-precession term, and can be included as an SU(2) covariant derivative
[
10
]
, while the second term is responsible for the spin-galvanic effect
[
3
,
10
]
. Both terms require the existence of a second rank pseudotensor, that is, a gyrotropic tensor
\gamma_{lk}
. For boundaries, the condition of gyrotropy is satisfied, because the normal to the interface is a polar vector of the system
[
19
]
. Consequently, for an isotropic boundary the spin-precession tensor and spin-galvanic tensor need to take the form

\displaystyle\alpha n_{r}\epsilon_{kjr}\;,

(17)

\displaystyle\gamma n_{r}\epsilon_{lkr}\;,

(18)

that is, they have the same structure as for SU(2) covariant Rashba systems
[
10
]
. As shown in Appendix
A
with the help of the normal state equations,
e^{2}\nu_{0}\gamma
can be identified as the Edelstein conductivity, while
2D\alpha
corresponds to the spin-swapping coefficient in
[
43
]
. The third term describes spin relaxation. The spin-relaxation tensor
\Gamma_{ab}
may have contributions from many different processes, such as Dyakonov-Perel
[
55
]
or Elliott-Yafet
[
56
,
57
]
mechanisms or electron-electron correlations
[
58
]
. The spin-relaxation tensor is a second-rank symmetric tensor, and may exist for any crystal symmetry. For an isotropic boundary with normal vector
\bm{n}
, it takes the form,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\Gamma^{\perp}(\delta_{ab}-2n_{a}n_{b})+\Gamma^{\parallel}n_{a}n_{b}\;,

(19)

where
\Gamma^{\parallel}
denotes the spin relaxation for in-plane spins and
\Gamma^{\perp}
the relaxation for out-of-plane spins. All these tensors
\alpha_{kj}
,
\gamma_{lk}
and
\Gamma_{ab}
are real by the chronology symmetry
[
48
]
.

Introducing the forms of the tensors, Eqs. (
17
–
19
) in Eq. (
16
) we obtain the following boundary action for our theory:

\displaystyle iS_{B}\left[\check{Q},\ \bm{A}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{S-I}}dS\ \frac{\pi\nu_{0}}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\Big\{-\frac{i\alpha}{2}\bm{n}\times\bm{\sigma}\cdot\check{Q}\tilde{\nabla}\check{Q}

\displaystyle\ +\frac{1}{2}\Gamma^{\perp}\bm{\sigma}_{\parallel}\cdot\check{Q}\bm{\sigma}_{\parallel}\check{Q}+\left(\Gamma^{\parallel}-\frac{1}{2}\Gamma^{\perp}\right)(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\check{Q}(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\check{Q}\Big\}

\displaystyle\ -\frac{i\gamma}{8}\check{Q}\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}\check{Q}\left\{\bm{\sigma},\ \tilde{\nabla}\check{Q}\right\}\;.

(20)

Here,
\bm{\sigma}_{\parallel}=\bm{\sigma}-(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\bm{n}
.

The action. Eq. (
20
) is a central result of our work. It is the boundary action for any non-magnetic isotropic boundary between a superconductor and an insulator up to second order in the relativistic spin-orbit coupling, valid for any temperature. According to it, such boundaries are described by four independent quantities,
\alpha,\gamma,\Gamma^{\parallel}
and
\Gamma^{\perp}
. Generally, within our formalism there is no specific relation between the four independent coefficients, which may depend in an intricate way on the band structure and the type of spin-orbit coupling. Parameters like
\gamma
and
\Gamma^{\parallel,\perp}
can be extracted from experiments in the normal state
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
.

The total action functional of the superconductor is given by the sum of the bulk, Eq. (
14
), and boundary, Eq. (
20
), contributions

S\left[\check{Q},\ \check{\Delta},\ \check{\bm{A}}\right]=S_{\text{bulk}}\left[\check{Q},\ \check{\Delta},\ \check{\bm{A}}\right]+S_{B}\left[\check{Q},\ \check{\bm{A}}\right]\,.

(21)

To derive the transport equations we use the saddle point approximation.
At the saddle point,
the
\check{Q}
-matrix can be identified with the quasiclassical Green’s function
\check{g}
, which has the usual causality structure,

\displaystyle\check{g}=\begin{pmatrix}\check{g}^{R}&\check{g}^{K}\\
0&\check{g}^{A}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(22)

The saddle point equations can be determined by requiring that the functional derivatives with respect to these quantities have to vanish. First of all, we require the invariance under variations of the
\check{Q}
-matrix,
\frac{\delta S}{\delta\check{Q}}=0
, which leads to the Usadel equation:

D\tilde{\nabla}_{k}(\check{g}\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{g})=\left[\hat{\omega}_{t,t^{\prime}}\check{\tau}_{3}+\check{\Delta}+i(\bm{h}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]\;,

(23)

and the boundary condition at the interface with SOC:

\left.-D\check{g}\tilde{\nabla}_{z}\check{g}\right|_{z=0}=-\bm{\tilde{\nabla}}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{\check{J}}}^{S}+\mathcal{\check{T}}^{S}-2\Gamma_{ab}\left[\sigma_{a}\check{g}\sigma_{b},\ \check{g}\right]\;.

(24)

The terms on the right-hand side originate from the boundary action, Eq. (
20
), where
\bm{\mathcal{\check{J}}}^{S}
represents the matrix surface current and
\mathcal{\check{T}}^{S}
is the matrix surface torque, defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\alpha n_{r}\epsilon_{kjr}\left[\check{g}\sigma_{j},\ \check{g}\right]+

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{16}\gamma n_{r}\epsilon_{ijl}\epsilon_{lkr}\left\{\left[\sigma_{i},\ \check{g}\right],\ \sigma_{j}+\check{g}\sigma_{j}\check{g}\right\}\;,

(25a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\check{T}}^{S}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\alpha n_{r}\epsilon_{kjr}\left[\sigma_{j},\ \check{g}\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{g}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{8}\gamma n_{r}\epsilon_{ijl}\epsilon_{lkr}\left[\left\{\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{g},\ \check{g}\sigma_{i}\check{g}\right\},\ \sigma_{j}\right]\;.

(25b)

In Appendix
A
we show that in the normal state the boundary condition, Eq. (
24
), coincides with the one derived in Ref.
[
43
]
. In particular, the term proportional to
\gamma
describes the spin-galvanic effect, the
\alpha
term corresponds to the spin precession, and terms
\Gamma^{\parallel,\perp}
describe the spin-relaxation.

We note that the boundary conditions for the Usadel equation, Eq.(
24
), differ from those obtained in Refs.
[
59
,
60
]
. In fact, Eq.
(
24
) is formulated specifically for interfaces with non-magnetic heavy-atom insulators, whereas Refs.
[
59
,
60
]
focus solely on magnetic interfaces. In contrast to previous works, our boundary conditions incorporate ISOC which leads to a spin-dependent mixing of in-plane and normal derivatives, and generates the spin-galvanic effect.

Next, we consider the variation of the action Eq. (
21
) with respect to
\check{\Delta}
. We know that if there are no quantum components, that is, the fields take the same value on both branches, the contributions of the two branches cancel out each other exactly, and hence the action vanishes
[
52
]
. Thus, the saddle point conditions are satisfied by requiring that at the saddle point the quantum components vanish, i.e.
{\Delta}^{q}=0
,
\varphi^{q}=0
, which automatically guarantees that
\frac{\delta S}{\delta{\Delta}}=0
, while additionally imposing
\frac{\delta S}{\delta{\Delta}^{q}}=\frac{\delta S}{\delta{\varphi}^{q}}=0
. The latter condition results in the well-known self-consistency relation for the magnitude of the pair potential
\Delta

[
52
]
and its phase
\varphi
:

\displaystyle\Delta e^{i\varphi}=\frac{\pi\lambda}{8}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\check{\rho}_{1}(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2})\check{g}(t,t)\Big)\;,

(26)

where tr denotes tracing over matrix indices in Nambu-Keldysh-spin space, but without time integration.

We finally take the functional derivative of Eq. (
21
) with respect to the vector potential. The saddle point condition is satisfied by requiring that at the saddle point
\bm{A}^{q}=0
and
\frac{\delta S}{\delta\bm{A}^{q}}=0
, which leads to the Maxwell equation for the vector potential
\bm{A}
:

\nabla\times\left[\nabla\times\bm{A}\right]=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{j}_{S}\;,

(27)

where
j_{S,k}=\frac{e\pi\nu_{0}D}{4}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\rho}_{1}\check{\tau}_{3}\check{g}\tilde{\nabla}_{k}\check{g}\right\}
is the supercurrent density distributed in the film.
The boundary conditions for the Maxwell equation at the film edges are:

\displaystyle\bm{m}\times\Big(\nabla\times\bm{A}(d_{S}-0)-\bm{H}_{0}\Big)=-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;,

(28a)

\displaystyle\bm{n}\times\Big(\nabla\times\bm{A}(+0)-\bm{H}_{0}\Big)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{J}^{S}-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;.

(28b)

Here,
\bm{n}=(0,\ 0,\ 1)
and
\bm{m}=(0,\ 0,\ -1)
are the unit vectors perpendicular to the interfaces at
z=0
and
z=d_{S}
respectively.
J^{S}_{k}=-\frac{e\pi\nu_{0}}{4}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\rho}_{1}\check{\tau}_{3}\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{k}\right\}
is the surface anomalous current due to the ISOC, where
\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{k}
is defined in Eq. (
25a
). The term
\bm{I}_{0}
is usually imposed to fix the total current in the wire by fixing the magnetic field produced by the wire. It is given by
\bm{J}^{S}+\int dz\bm{j}_{S}=\bm{I}_{0}
. The appearance of this term in Eqs. (
28
) can be derived within the action formalism by adding a term
-\frac{1}{2c}\check{\rho}_{1}\bm{\check{I}}_{0}\cdot\check{\bm{A}}
to the action of both boundaries, Eq. (
20
), and zero action at the vacuum boundary.

Eqs. (
23
–
28
) is a complete set of equations determining the spectral and transport properties of superconducting systems with interfacial spin-orbit coupling in the presence of external fields and currents
In particular, the boundary condition, Eq. (
24
) is one of the main results of this work, generalizing the boundary conditions for ISOC in the normal state obtained in Ref.
[
43
]
to superconductors. In the next section, we introduce some approximations that will be used throughout the rest of the article to compute the critical temperature, anomalous current, and diode effect of thin superconducting films with ISOC.

III.1
Thin superconducting films

As stated above, the boundary value problem posed in Eqs. (
23
–
28
) describes the system in both normal and superconducting states, and for generic orientations and spatial dependence of the magnetic field and the applied current in the plane. In the following sections, we focus on equilibrium properties of the superconducting state. For this reason, it is convenient to use the Matsubara representation instead of the full Keldysh one.
This means that in all previous equations we have to make the substitution
\hat{\omega}_{t,t^{\prime}}\xrightarrow{}\omega_{n}=(2n+1)\pi T
, where
n
is an integer.
Moreover, it is convenient to solve Eqs. (
23
–
26
) in a gauge in which the superconducting phase
\varphi
in
\check{\Delta}=\Delta e^{i\check{\tau}_{3}\varphi}\check{\tau}_{2}
is eliminated. This is achieved via a gauge transformation which leads to the following transformations:

\displaystyle\check{g}\to e^{i\varphi\check{\tau}_{3}/2}\check{g}(z)e^{-i\varphi\check{\tau}_{3}/2}\;,

(29)

\displaystyle\check{\Delta}\to e^{-i\varphi\check{\tau}_{3}/2}\check{\Delta}e^{i\varphi\check{\tau}_{3}/2}=\Delta\check{\tau}_{2},

\displaystyle\tilde{\nabla}_{\parallel}\xrightarrow{}\frac{i\bm{p}^{S}}{2}\left[\check{\tau}_{3}\;,\ \check{\cdot}\right],\qquad\tilde{\nabla}_{z}\xrightarrow{}\nabla_{z}\;,

where
\bm{p}^{S}=\nabla\varphi-\frac{2e}{c}\bm{A}.

After this gauge transformation, instead of two gauge-dependent quantities
\varphi
and
\bm{A}
, we end up with only the superconducting momentum
\bm{p}_{S}
that is gauge invariant.

To maximize the effects of ISOC, we assume that the externally applied magnetic field and the current are homogeneous and that the magnetic field is in-plane. We define this direction as the
\hat{\bm{y}}
-direction, i.e.
\bm{H}_{0}=H_{0}\bm{\hat{y}}
, so that by symmetry of the spin-galvanic tensor in Eq. (
18
) the anomalous current flows in the
\hat{\bm{x}}
-direction. Therefore, the externally imposed current is always chosen as
\bm{I}_{0}=I_{0}\bm{\hat{x}}
. In this case, the symmetries of the Usadel equation imply that
\bm{p}^{S}=p^{S}\bm{\hat{x}}
and
\check{g}=\check{g}_{s}+\check{g}_{t}\sigma_{y}
. Following Eqs. (
25a
) and (
25b
) this has the consequence that all terms that depend on
\alpha
drop out of the equation. Indeed, in the normal state this leads to precession of spins around the magnetic field axis, while in the superconducting state it leads to a precession of the zero-spin polarization axis. In the setup here the zero-spin polarization axis is along the magnetic field and therefore does not precess.

Since interfacial effects are most prominent in thin films, we focus on a superconducting film with a thickness much smaller than the coherence length. Namely, we assume that

d_{S}\ll\xi=\sqrt{\frac{D}{2\pi T_{c0}}}\;.

(30)

Here
T_{c0}
is the BCS critical temperature corresponding to the coupling constant
\lambda
written in Eq. (
14
). Moreover, since we study the diffusive regime, the coherence length
\xi
is much shorter than the London penetration depth
\lambda_{L}
,

\xi\ll\lambda_{L}\;.

(31)

As derived in Appendix
B.1
, when Eq. (
31
) is satisfied, the Maxwell equations in Eq. (
27
–
28
) impose that the magnitude
p^{S}
of the superconducting momentum reads

\displaystyle p^{S}(z)=p^{S0}-\frac{2eH_{0}}{c}(z-d_{S}/2)\;.

(32)

Here,
p^{S0}
is the uniform part of the superconducting momentum. Within our calculations, we fix
p^{S0}
, solve the Usadel equation, find
\Delta
, and then we compute the total current corresponding to the given
p^{S0}
as

I_{0}=Wp^{S0}d_{S}+J^{S}\;,

(33)

so
p^{S0}
depends on the anomalous current
J^{S}
, the applied current
I_{0}
, and the superconducting stiffness
W
. The latter is given by

W=-\frac{e\pi\nu_{0}DT}{4}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\tau}_{3}\check{g}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]\right\}\;.

(34)

Using the approximation, Eq. (
30
), and the superconducting momentum
p^{S}(z)
from Eq. (
32
), we average the Usadel equation Eq. (
23
) over the thickness of the superconductor and obtain the following algebraic relation for
\check{g}
:

\displaystyle-\frac{d_{S}D(p^{S0})^{2}}{4}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\left(p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\right)\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{x}\right]+\mathcal{\check{T}}^{S}=

\displaystyle=d_{S}\left[\omega_{n}\check{\tau}_{3}+\Delta\check{\tau}_{2}+i(\bm{h}\cdot\bm{\sigma})\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]+8\Gamma^{\parallel}\check{g}_{s}\check{g}_{t}\sigma_{y}\;.

(35)

Only the singlet and a single triplet component – the one parallel to the external field – are finite. This allows for rather compact expressions for the matrix current and torque to be derived from Eqs. (
25a
-
25b
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{x}=-\gamma\check{g}_{t}\left(\check{1}-\check{g}^{2}_{t}\right)+\gamma\check{g}_{s}\check{g}^{2}_{t}\sigma_{y}\;,

(36)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\check{T}}^{S}=\frac{i\gamma}{2}\left(p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\right)\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}_{s}\right]\left(\check{1}-2\check{g}_{t}^{2}\right)\sigma_{y}\;.

(37)

We incorporated the fact that
\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{z}=0
due to the symmetry of our system, that is,
\alpha_{kz}=\gamma_{lz}=0
, and
\mathcal{\check{J}}^{S}_{y}=0
because
\bm{p}^{S}\parallel\hat{\bm{x}}
.

The self-consistency equation for
\check{\Delta}
in terms of Matsubara frequencies reads

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{\lambda\pi T}{4}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\tau}_{2}\check{g}(\omega_{n})\right\}.

(38)

In the following sections, we use Eqs. (
32
) –(
38
) to calculate the response of junctions with interfacial SOC to the externally tunable magnetic field
\bm{H}_{0}
and the current
\bm{I}_{0}
. Firstly, we present the G-L free energy functional with ISOC-related corrections. Then, we determine the critical temperature of the junctions using the G-L functional. Lastly, we estimate the superconducting diode efficiency and how it depends on the applied magnetic field and the intensities of the interfacial spin-charge coupling and the spin relaxation.

IV
Ginzburg-Landau functional with ISOC induced corrections

For temperatures close to the critical temperature, we can relate the action of the NLSM and the Usadel equation to the well-known G-L theory. With this, we determine the critical temperature of our junctions and make a connection to the results presented in
[
47
]
, where the G-L functional itself was determined based on symmetry arguments, and explain the similarities and differences between our results and theirs. We focus on how the G-L functional depends on
\gamma
,
\Gamma^{\parallel}
and
d_{S}
, and set the applied current to zero,
I_{0}=0
.

In this limit the Usadel equation reduces to a set of linear equations:

\displaystyle-D(p^{S0})^{2}f_{s}+\frac{i\gamma}{d_{S}}\left(p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\right)f_{y}\operatorname{sgn}\omega

\displaystyle=2|\omega_{n}|f_{s}+2ih\operatorname{sgn}\omega_{n}f_{y}+2i\Delta\;,

(39a)

\displaystyle-D(p^{S0})^{2}f_{y}+\frac{i\gamma}{d_{S}}\left(p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\right)f_{s}\operatorname{sgn}\omega_{n}

\displaystyle=2|\omega_{n}|f_{y}+2ih\operatorname{sgn}\omega_{n}f_{s}+\frac{8\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}f_{y}\;.

(39b)

The solution to this linear system is

\displaystyle f_{s}=-\frac{i\Delta\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)}{\left(|\omega_{n}|+X\right)\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}-h\right)^{2}}\;,

(40)

\displaystyle f_{y}=-\frac{\Delta\operatorname{sgn}{\omega_{n}}\left(g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{0}-\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}\right)}{\left(|\omega_{n}|+X\right)\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}-h\right)^{2}}\;,

\displaystyle h=g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{0}\;,\quad X=\frac{Dp^{2}_{S0}}{2}\;,\quad p^{S}(0)=p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\;.

The details of the derivation of the G-L functional from the pair amplitudes up to the second order in
\Delta
are shown in Appendix
B.4
. The final result reads:

F_{GL}=\frac{T-T_{c0}}{T_{c0}}|\Delta|^{2}+\frac{(\pi p^{S0}\xi)^{2}}{4}|\Delta|^{2}+2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left[\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\left(\chi_{{H}}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)-(p^{S0}\xi)\frac{\gamma}{2\pi T_{c0}\xi d_{S}}\right]^{2}|\Delta|^{2}.

(41)

Here,
\chi_{H}=\frac{g_{L}}{2mD}
,
M(x)
is defined as

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{2}(2n+1+x)}=

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi^{2}x-4\psi^{(0)}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)-4\gamma_{E}-4\log 4}{8x^{2}},

(42)

where
\gamma_{E}\approx 0.577
is the Euler constant,
\psi^{(0)}(x)
is the digamma function. This function monotonically decreases as a function of its argument. It has two important asymptotes,
M(0)={7\zeta(3)}/{8}
with
\zeta
being the Riemann zeta function, and
M(x\gg 1)\approx{\pi^{2}}/{8x}
.
Moreover, we introduce
\chi_{H}={g_{L}}/{2mD}
for the simplicity of notation. We identify four different contributions in
F_{GL}
, Eq. (
41
):

F_{GL}=F^{(0)}_{GL}+F^{(K)}_{GL}+F^{(H)}_{GL}+F^{(L)}_{GL}\;,

(43)

where
F^{(0)}_{GL}=\frac{T-T_{c0}}{T_{c0}}|\Delta|^{2}
is the usual BCS contribution that does not depend on the magnetic field or superconducting momentum and therefore represents the equilibrium free energy in the absence of a magnetic field.
F^{(K)}_{GL}
represents the kinetic energy of the superconducting condensate that reads

\displaystyle

\displaystyle F^{(K)}_{GL}=(p^{S0}\xi)^{2}\left[\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}+\right.\left.2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left(\frac{\gamma\xi}{Dd_{S}}\right)^{2}\right]|\Delta|^{2}.

(44)

The coefficient in square brackets corresponds to the superconducting stiffness
W
in Eq. (
34
), i.e. it describes how costly it is to impose a phase gradient. Meanwhile,
F^{(H)}_{GL}
denotes the contribution proportional to the magnetic field, which in the absence of ISOC determines the critical magnetic field,

F^{(H)}_{GL}=2\left(\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\right)^{2}M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)^{2}|\Delta|^{2}\;.

(45)

Finally, the free energy contains a term which is
first order in both the superconducting momentum and the magnetic field, the so-called Lifshitz invariant term
[
61
,
62
]
. For a thin superconductor with ISOC, this term takes the form

F^{(L)}_{GL}=-2\left(\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}p^{S0}\xi\right)M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma\left(\chi_{{H}}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)}{\pi T_{c0}\xi d_{S}}|\Delta|^{2}.

(46)

Both terms in Eq. (
41
) that depend on the superconducting momentum or magnetic field are squares, that is, they are positive. Thus the free energy is always larger than
F_{GL}^{(0)}
. From this we conclude that the critical temperature can not exceed
T_{c0}
. In other words, magnetic fields can not increase the critical temperature of the superconductor,as
wrongly predicted in Ref.
[
47
]
.
Specifically, as shown in Appendix
B.5
, the critical temperature can be written as

\frac{T_{c}}{T_{c0}}=1-\beta\left(\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\right)^{2}\;,

(47)

where
\beta
is the dimensionless coefficient that obeys
\beta>0
. The magnitude of
\beta
depends on the spin-charge coupling both directly through Eq. (
45
) and through the dependence of the equilibrium phase gradient
p^{\text{an}}
on
\gamma
.

This equilibrium phase gradient is determined by minimization of the free energy in Eq. (
41
). Due to the presence of the Lifshitz invariant, Eq. (
46
), such a phase may be nonzero.
In this case,
p^{\text{an}}
is called the anomalous phase gradient; it is a precursor for the superconducting diode effect at lower temperatures.
It reads

\displaystyle p^{\text{an}}\xi=\frac{8M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma}{D}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)}{\pi^{2}d_{S}/\xi+4M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\pi T_{c0}D\xi d_{S}}}\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\;.

(48)

This confirms that for a finite spin-galvanic coefficient,
\gamma
an external magnetic field
H_{0}
induces a phase gradient. This anomalous phase which is, according to Eq. (
48
), suppressed in the limit of strong boundary spin relaxation. Notably, the Lifshitz invariant in Eq. (
46
) and consequently the anomalous superconducting momentum in Eq. (
48
) change sign when
{\gamma}=2D\chi_{H}
. This value marks the boundary between the regimes in which the triplets are predominantly generated by the exchange field or by vector potential in combination with spin-charge coupling.

To compare our results with those of Ref.
[
47
]
, we focus on the limit
d_{S}\xrightarrow{}0
. In that case, the coefficient in Eq. (
45
) vanishes, that is,
T_{c}
may only depend on the external magnetic field through the generation of equilibrium gradients by
H_{0}
. On the other hand, the phase stiffness diverges, that is, imposing phase gradients becomes very costly.

Strikingly, the Lifshitz invariant converges to a finite value because
\lim_{d_{S}\xrightarrow{}0}\frac{\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}
, and therefore its coefficient is solely determined by the relative magnitude of the spin-galvanic and relaxation terms.

The optimal phase gradient
p^{\text{an}}
becomes

\displaystyle p^{\text{an}}\xi\approx\frac{1}{1+\frac{8Dd_{S}\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\gamma^{2}}}\frac{Dd_{S}}{\xi\gamma}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\;.

(49)

Thus, we find that, due to the increase of the superconducting stiffness, the optimal phase gradient is suppressed in very thin junctions.
The parameter
\beta
controlling the dependence of the critical temperature on the magnetic field in this limit reads

\displaystyle\beta=\left(\frac{\pi d_{S}D(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D})}{2\gamma\xi}\right)^{2}\;.

(50)

Thus, as
d_{S}\xrightarrow{}0
,
\beta
converges to
0
, just like in materials without ISOC.

The difference between our result and Ref.
[
47
]
is due to the suppression of the optimal phase gradient in our formalism, which can be traced back to the ISOC-dependent correction of the superconducting stiffness in Eq. (
44
).
The stiffness increases in the second order of
\gamma/D
. Usually,
\gamma/D
is small and therefore this term is negligible if one considers junctions whose thickness is of the order of the coherence length
d_{S}/\xi\approx 1
. However, if the junction becomes thin, the phase stiffness increases as
\left(\frac{\gamma\xi}{Dd_{S}}\right)^{2}
, so the phase gradients are distinctly harder to generate. This compensates for the free energy reduction by the Lifshitz invariant, by completing the square in Eq. (
41
).

Another interesting limit is
the opposite limit, where the thickness is much shorter than the coherence length but larger than
{\gamma\xi}/{D}
. We find to first order in
{\gamma\xi}/{Dd_{S}}

\displaystyle p^{\text{an}}\xi=8M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{s0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma\xi}{\pi^{2}Dd_{S}}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\;.

(51)

As expected, in this limit, even without spin relaxation (
M(0)=7\zeta(3)/8
), the optimal phase gradient is small because the triplet correlations exist only near the boundary, while spin relaxation suppresses the optimal phase gradient, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(a).
By substitution of this value in the free energy, we find that the coefficient
\beta
governing the
H_{0}
dependence of the critical temperature in this limit reduces to

\displaystyle\beta=2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{s0}d_{S}}\right)\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)^{2}\;.

(52)

We note that even in this limit
\gamma
has a strong influence on
\beta
, which is due to the vanishing of
\beta
for small
d_{S}/\xi
in the absence of ISOC. The size of
\beta
is controlled by the interplay between the generation of triplets by the exchange field and the vector potential in combination with spin-charge coupling. Indeed, if these effects balance, no triplets are generated, and therefore the magnetic field does not affect the junction. If there is no perfect balance, triplets are generated and therefore magnetic fields suppress the pair amplitudes. As expected, since spin relaxation hampers the generation of triplets, it improves the stability of the superconducting phase against magnetic fields, thereby increasing the critical temperature in the presence of a magnetic field, as shown in Fig.

(b).

V
Superconducting diode effect

From the appearance of the Lifshitz invariant in the G-L functional, we conclude that the system supports anomalous currents in the vicinity of the critical temperature. Therefore, for temperatures well below the critical one, we may expect the superconducting diode effect to appear. To investigate the dependence of this diode effect on the parameters that characterize the system, we numerically solve the nonlinear Usadel equation together with the self-consistency equation, specifically Eqs. (
33
–
38
) using the numerical procedure described in Appendix
C
. Subsequently, we find the diode efficiency as

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{I_{c+}-|I_{c-}|}{I_{c+}+|I_{c-}|}\;,

(53)

where
I_{c+}
and
I_{c-}
are the maximum supercurrents in the positive and negative directions. The results are shown in Figs.
3
(a–c) and indicate the presence of a superconducting diode effect.

As discussed in the previous section, the spin-charge coefficient
\gamma
is the key parameter that governs the intensity of ISOC-related effects.
Experimental results
[

30

on different insulator/normal metal systems indicate that
\sigma^{\text{sc}}
can reach values of the order of

\sigma^{sc}\sim 10^{5}\ {}^{-1}{}^{-1}

For a typical superconductor such as Nb, the normal-state conductivity is of order

\sigma_{D}\sim 10^{7}\;{}^{-1}{}^{-1}

[

\gamma/D=\sigma^{sc}/\sigma_{D}\sim 10^{-2}
.

Since the superconducting diode effect requires time-reversal symmetry breaking, a nonzero diode efficiency necessitates the presence of a magnetic field. The effect gradually increases with increasing magnetic field, regardless of the strength of
\Gamma^{\parallel}
. However, as
\Gamma^{\parallel}
increases, this growth in efficiency is significantly reduced, because spin relaxation suppresses the triplets and thereby weakens the effect of exchange fields.

The dependence of
\eta
on spin relaxation is further illustrated in Fig.

(b), where we observe that spin relaxation suppresses the diode efficiency.
Existing experiments yield values for the so-called spin mixing conductance
G^{\parallel}
of heavy insulator/normal systems
typically of order
10^{13}\text{ -- }10^{14}\ {}^{-1}{}^{-2}

[

Using a representative coherence length of

For the value of
\gamma/D=0.02
used above, and a small spin-relaxation coefficient
\Gamma^{\parallel}/(\pi T_{c0}\xi)\approx 10^{-2}
, we estimate from Fig.

(b) that the diode efficiency can reach
\approx 5\%
.
This value can be improved by properly selecting material combinations and carefully engineering the interface to minimize
\Gamma^{\parallel}
and maximize
\gamma

Finally, we show the dependence of the diode efficiency on the system temperature for different
\gamma
coefficients in Fig.

(c). We see that the diode effect decreases as the system temperature increases. This happens because with increasing temperature, the higher harmonics decay faster than the first harmonic, so that the diode efficiency goes down even if the
\varphi_{0}
-effect remains. We find that for
\gamma/D=0.1

VI
Conclusions

We have presented a transport theory for hybrid structures of superconductors in contact with heavy-atom insulators that generate interfacial spin-orbit coupling (ISOC). The boundary conditions, Eqs. (
2
)-(
4
), derived from the effective action, allow us to describe transport properties in both the superconducting and normal states. In the vicinity of the critical temperature, from the obtained kinetic equations we have derived the Ginzburg-Landau functional for superconducting films with ISOC. We show that for thin junctions, the ISOC significantly enhances the phase gradient stiffness. This enhancement prevents the increase of
T_{c}
by applying a magnetic field, in contrast to the suggestion of Ref.
[
47
]
based on the Ginzburg-Landau theory with a naive inclusion of a surface Lifshitz invariant.
We show how the combination of ISOC with an external magnetic field leads to anomalous currents and phase gradients. With the increase of the thickness of the junction, these effects are suppressed, because ISOC only affects the pair amplitudes close to the boundary, not throughout the junction.

We also study the superconducting diode effect in these systems well below
T_{c}
. We show that this effect is most clearly visible in thin films, in which the spin-galvanic effects dominate over the spin-relaxation effects.
Thus, our results may motivate experimentalists in the search and fabrication of heavy atom insulator/superconductor interfaces with stronger spin-charge coupling parameter (
\gamma
) but weaker spin relaxation (
\Gamma^{\parallel}
) to create superconducting diodes with conventional superconductors.

While in this paper we focused on isotropic boundaries, the developed theory can be used for non-isotropic interfaces. In such interfaces the spin-galvanic tensor
\gamma_{lk}
and relaxation tensor
\Gamma_{ab}
, see Eq. (
16
), will take different forms than those in Eqs. (
17
,
18
). In such a case, the dependence of the results on the magnitude of the spin-galvanic term will be qualitatively similar, but the dependence of the direction of the induced current on the direction of the magnetic field can be significantly altered, as was shown before for bulk spin-galvanic effects
[
8
,
48
]
.

Appendix A
Boundary Conditions in the normal state

In the normal state, superconducting correlations are absent, so
\Delta=0
and the retarded, advanced and Keldysh components of the Green function
\check{g}
in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
) take the following form

\displaystyle\check{g}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\check{g}^{A}(t,t^{\prime})=\check{\tau}_{3}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\;,

(54)

\displaystyle\check{g}^{K}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\check{\tau}_{3}\check{F}(t,t^{\prime})\;.

(55)

Therefore, we only need to solve for the distribution functions
F
. Moreover, because all the components commute with
\check{\tau}_{3}
, the resulting equations for
\check{F}(t,t^{\prime})
do not depend on the time difference coordinate
t-t^{\prime}
, and therefore the equations for
t=t^{\prime}
, which determine the physical observables, decouple from the equations for
t\neq t^{\prime}
.

The kinetic equation can in that case be directly written in terms of the excess charge and excess spin defined as

\displaystyle\delta n

\displaystyle=2\nu_{0}\mu=-\frac{\pi\nu_{0}}{2}\operatorname{tr}\check{\tau}_{3}\check{F}(t,t)\;,

(56)

\displaystyle\delta S_{a}

\displaystyle=2\nu_{0}\mu_{a}^{s}=-\frac{\pi\nu_{0}}{2}\operatorname{tr}\sigma_{a}\check{F}(t,t)\;.

(57)

Using these definitions in the boundary condition, we find that they can be written as

\displaystyle D\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\delta n

\displaystyle=-\gamma\bm{n}\times\bm{\nabla}\cdot\delta\bm{S}\;,

(58)

\displaystyle D\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\delta\bm{S}^{\parallel}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\bm{n}\times\bm{\nabla}\delta n+8\Gamma^{\parallel}\delta\bm{S}^{\parallel}-2\alpha(\bm{n}\times\bm{\nabla})\times\bm{n}\delta S^{\perp}\;,

(59)

\displaystyle D\bm{n}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\delta S^{\perp}

\displaystyle=8\Gamma^{\perp}\delta S^{\perp}-2\alpha\bm{n}\cdot\left((\bm{n}\times\bm{\nabla})\times\delta\bm{S}\right)\;,

(60)

where for simplicity of notation we denoted
S^{\perp}=\bm{n}\cdot\bm{S}
and
S^{\parallel}=\bm{S}-S^{\perp}\bm{n}
.
Comparing this with the result in
[
43
]
, we find that we may identify
-\gamma/D
with the spin-to charge conductivity
[
29
]

\sigma^{sc}
,
\frac{-2\alpha}{D}
with the spin-swapping coefficient
\kappa
and
\Gamma^{\parallel}
with the spin-loss coefficient
L/8
. Since we consider an interface with heavy atom insulators, in our model the conductance and spin conductance vanish.

Since none of the terms constituting the boundary action contains Pauli matrices in Nambu space, the linearized boundary conditions for the pair amplitudes
f_{s}+\bm{f}_{t}\cdot\bm{\sigma}
are obtained by substituting
\delta n\xrightarrow{}f_{s}
and
\delta\bm{S}\xrightarrow{}\bm{f}_{t}
.

B.1
Analytical solution of the Maxwell equations

In this subsection, we discuss how the Maxwell equation in Eq. (
27
) can be solved in the thin film limit, and with this, we derive Eqs. (
32
) and (
33
).

The Maxwell equation reads, according to Eq. (
27
) of the main text:

\displaystyle\nabla\times[\nabla\times\bm{A}]=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{j}_{s}\;,

(61)

where
\bm{j}_{s}
is the supercurrent in the material that depends on the quasiclassical Green’s function like discussed in the main text:

\displaystyle\bm{j}_{S}=W\bm{p}^{S}\;,

(62)

where
W=-\frac{e\pi\nu_{0}DT}{4}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\tau}_{3}\check{g}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]\right\}
is the superfluid stiffness of the superconductor, and
\bm{p}^{S}
is the superfluid momentum.
In the setup discussed within this paper, Eq.
61
is equipped with the boundary conditions presented in Eq. (
28
) in the main text:

\displaystyle\bm{m}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{A}(d_{S}-0)-\bm{H}_{0}\right)=-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;,

(63)

\displaystyle\bm{n}\times\left(\nabla\times\bm{A}(+0)-\bm{H}_{0}\right)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{J}^{S}-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;,

(64)

where
\bm{I}_{0}
is the total current flowing through the material, which can be externally imposed, while
\bm{J}^{S}
is the surface current, Eq. (
25a
), which in Matsubara space reads
J^{S}=-\frac{\pi\gamma}{8}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{\check{\tau}_{3}\left\{\left[\sigma_{i},\ \check{g}\right],\ \sigma_{j}+\check{g}\sigma_{j}\check{g}\right\}\right\}
,
depends on the quasiclassical Green’s function, but not on the vector potential or phase gradients.

The equation (
61
) may be written in terms of the local magnetic field
\bm{H}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
as
a first order differential equation

\displaystyle\nabla\times\bm{H}=\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{j}_{s}\;,

(65)

while its boundary conditions are

\displaystyle\bm{m}\times\bm{H}(d_{S}-0)

\displaystyle=\bm{m}\times\bm{H}_{0}-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;,

(66)

\displaystyle\bm{n}\times\bm{H}(+0)

\displaystyle=\bm{n}\times\bm{H}_{0}+\frac{4\pi}{c}\bm{J}^{S}-\frac{2\pi}{c}\bm{I}_{0}\;.

(67)

This is the differential equation of the first order with two boundary conditions; it only has a solution if

\displaystyle\bm{I}_{0}=\bm{J}^{S}+\int_{0}^{d_{S}}\bm{j}_{s}dz\;,

(68)

which reflects the fact that the bulk and the surface current should sum up to the total current.

As discussed in the main text, we consider
\bm{H}_{0}=H_{0}\hat{y}
, so that all currents flow in the
\hat{\bm{x}}
-direction. For each of them, like for
H_{0}
, we use plain notation to denote the magnitude of these vectors. Moreover, by choice of the gauge in Eq. (
29
), the local magnetic field and the bulk current only depend on the
z
coordinate. With this, Eqs. (
65
–
67
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\partial_{z}H=-\frac{4\pi}{c}j_{S}=-\frac{4\pi}{c}Wp^{S}\;,

(69)

and

\displaystyle H(d_{S})

\displaystyle=H_{0}-\frac{2\pi I_{0}}{c}\;,

(70)

\displaystyle H(0)

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\frac{2\pi I_{0}}{c}-\frac{4\pi}{c}J^{S}\;.

(71)

In the thin limit, where
d_{S}\ll\xi
, we know that the quasiclassical Green’s function is almost constant, and therefore, the superfluid stiffness
W
is almost constant. Moreover, in the chosen gauge, there is no spatial dependence of the superconducting phase, so that
\bm{p}^{S}
is directly related to
\bm{A}
via
\bm{p}^{S}=-\frac{2e}{c}\bm{A}
, so that the magnetic field and superfluid momentum are related by

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=-\frac{c}{2e}\partial_{z}p^{S}\;.

(72)

Because
W
is independent of position, Eqs. (
69
–
72
) can be solved analytically. The result is

\displaystyle p^{S}(z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2e\lambda}{c}H_{0}\frac{\sinh{\frac{z-d_{S}/2}{\lambda}}}{\cosh{\frac{d_{S}}{2\lambda}}}+\frac{4\pi e\lambda}{c^{2}}I_{0}\frac{\cosh{\frac{z-d_{S}/2}{\lambda}}}{\sinh{\frac{d_{S}}{2\lambda}}}-\frac{8\pi e\lambda}{c^{2}}J^{S}\frac{\cosh{\frac{z-d_{S}}{\lambda}}}{\sinh{\frac{d_{S}}{\lambda}}}\;,

(73)

\displaystyle H(z)

\displaystyle=H_{0}\frac{\cosh{\frac{z-d_{S}/2}{\lambda}}}{\cosh{\frac{d_{S}}{2\lambda}}}-\frac{2\pi}{c}I_{0}\frac{\sinh{\frac{z-d_{S}/2}{\lambda}}}{\sinh{\frac{d_{S}}{2\lambda}}}+\frac{4\pi}{c}J^{S}\frac{\sinh{\frac{z-d_{S}}{\lambda}}}{\sinh{\frac{d_{S}}{\lambda}}}\;.

(74)

Here, for simplicity of notation we defined

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{c}{\sqrt{8\pi eW}}\;,

(75)

which is the characteristic length scale over which the magnetic field decays, i.e. the temperature dependent London penetration depth. At zero temperature in a bulk superconductor, it reduces to the conventional expressions
W=\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma_{D}\Delta
and thus
\lambda=\frac{c}{2\pi\sqrt{\sigma_{D}\Delta}}=\lambda_{L}
. With increasing temperatures,
W
decreases and consequently
\lambda
increases.
In dirty materials, the London penetration length is usually much larger than the coherence length
[
71
]
, since unlike the coherence length it is not directly suppressed by disorder. Therefore,
d_{S}\ll\xi\ll\lambda
, which means we may keep only terms up to linear order in
p^{S}
and
H
:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\approx H_{0}-\frac{2\pi}{c}I_{0}\left(\frac{2z}{d_{S}}-1\right)+\frac{4\pi}{c}J^{S}\left(\frac{z}{d_{S}}-1\right)\;,

(76)

\displaystyle p^{S}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{2e}{c}H_{0}\left(z-\frac{d_{S}}{2}\right)+\frac{I_{0}-J^{S}}{Wd_{S}}\;.

(77)

Defining the constant term in
p^{S}
as
p^{S0}
, we find that

\displaystyle p^{S}\approx-\frac{2e}{c}H_{0}\left(z-\frac{d_{S}}{2}\right)+p^{S0}\;,

(78)

with which we have derived Eq. (
32
) in the main text. Moreover, comparing Eqs. (
77
) and (
78
), we find that the homogeneous component of the phase and the total current in the junction are related via

\displaystyle I_{0}=Wd_{S}p^{S0}+J^{S}\;,

(79)

which is Eq. (
33
) in the main text.

Lastly, we note that comparing the typical scales
H_{c2}=\frac{c\pi T_{c0}}{eD}
and
I_{c}\sim\frac{\sigma_{D}\pi T_{c0}}{e}=2e\nu_{0}D\pi T_{c0}
, we note that

\displaystyle\frac{cH_{c2}}{2\pi I_{c}}\sim\frac{1}{4\pi\nu_{0}}\left(\frac{c}{eD}\right)^{2}=1.76\left(\frac{\lambda_{L}}{\xi}\right)^{2}\gg 1\;.

(80)

Thus, in the magnetic field, we may safely neglect the current-induced field compared to the contribution of the external field. This allows us to write the exchange term in terms of the external field
\bm{H}_{0}
instead of the local field
\bm{H}(z)
in Eq. (
15
). In
p^{S}
we should keep the current dependent terms because they are of lowest order in
\frac{d_{S}}{\lambda}
.

B.2
The selfconsistency relation for
\Delta

The selfconsistency relation can be found by the variation of the action. Specifically, since the action vanishes for any causal configuration, we need to take the functional derivative of Eq. (
14
) with respect to the quantum component of
\check{\Delta}^{q}

[
52
]
. One finds the stationary point equation for
\Delta e^{i\varphi}
in a form:

\displaystyle\Delta e^{i\varphi}=\frac{\pi\lambda}{8}\operatorname{tr}\Bigg(\check{\rho}_{1}\left(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2}\right)\check{g}(t,\ t)\Bigg)\;.

(81)

This relation corresponds to Eq. (
26
) of the main text. In Matsubara domain, Eq. (
81
) reads as

\displaystyle\Delta e^{i\varphi}=\frac{i\lambda\pi T}{4}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2})\check{g}\right\}\;.

(82)

For the BCS uniform superconductor with the critical temperature
T_{c0}
, one can set
\varphi=0
everywhere, that gives the following equation:

\displaystyle\Delta=\lambda\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{n}+\Delta^{2}}}\;,

(83)

which means

\displaystyle 1=\lambda\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{n}+\Delta^{2}}}\;.

(84)

As
T\xrightarrow{}T_{c0}
we have

\displaystyle 1=\lambda\pi T_{c0}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{|\omega_{n}|}=\lambda\sum_{n>0}\frac{1}{n+\frac{1}{2}}\;.

(85)

This sum diverges if we leave
n
unrestricted. However, in conventional superconductors, in which the pairing interaction is mediated by phonons, there is only attractive interaction if
\omega_{n}<\omega_{D}
, where
\omega_{D}
is the Debye frequency. Consequently,
n
should be less than
\frac{\omega_{D}}{2\pi T_{c0}}
, and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda}\approx\log\left(\frac{\omega_{D}}{\pi T_{c0}}\right)\;.

(86)

Using this, we may write Eq. (
82

\displaystyle\Delta e^{i\varphi}\log\left(\frac{\omega_{D}}{\pi T_{c0}}\right)=\frac{i\pi T}{4}\sum_{\omega_{n}}\operatorname{tr}\left\{(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2})\check{g}\right\}\;.

(87)

With the help of the same trick as in Eq. (
85
) to restrict the summation over
\omega_{n}
up to
\omega_{D}
for any temperature
T
, we write:

\displaystyle\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{\Delta e^{i\varphi}}{|\omega_{n}|}=\Delta e^{i\varphi}\log\left(\frac{\omega_{D}}{\pi T}\right)\;.

(88)

Subtracting Eq. (
87
) from Eq. (
88
), we find

\displaystyle\Delta e^{i\varphi}\log\frac{T_{c0}}{T}=\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left(\frac{\Delta e^{i\varphi}}{|\omega_{n}|}-\frac{i}{4}\operatorname{tr}\left\{(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2})\check{g}\right\}\right).

(89)

Pair amplitudes in a thin superconducting film with ISOC

In the vicinity of the critical temperature,
T_{c0}-T\ll T_{c0}
, we linearize Eq. (

\displaystyle\check{g}\approx\operatorname{sgn}\omega_{n}\check{\tau}_{3}+\begin{pmatrix}0&f_{s}+f_{y}\sigma_{y}\\

(90)

and substitute this into Eq. (

(91a)

\displaystyle f_{s}=-\frac{i\Delta\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)}{\left(|\omega_{n}|+X\right)\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}-g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{0}\right)^{2}}\;,

(92)

\displaystyle f_{y}=-\frac{\Delta\operatorname{sgn}{\omega_{n}}\left(g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{0}-\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}\right)}{\left(|\omega_{n}|+X\right)\left(|\omega_{n}|+X+\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{d_{S}}\right)+\left(\frac{\gamma p^{S}(0)}{2d_{S}}-g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{0}\right)^{2}}\;,

\displaystyle X=\frac{Dp^{2}_{S0}}{2}\;,\qquad p^{S}(0)=p^{S0}+\frac{eH_{0}d_{S}}{c}\;.

B.4
Derivation of the Ginzburg-Landau functional

The pair amplitude
f_{s}
derived in Appendix
B.3
can be expanded up to the second order in
\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}
and
p^{S0}\xi
as follows

\displaystyle f_{s}

\displaystyle=-i|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}\left\{\frac{1}{|\omega_{n}|}-\left(\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{n}}\frac{\left(g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{c2}-\frac{\gamma\pi T_{c0}}{2D}\right)^{2}}{|\omega_{n}|+4\Gamma^{\parallel}/d_{S}}-(p^{S0}\xi)^{2}\frac{\pi T_{c0}}{\omega^{2}_{n}}\left[1+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2Dd_{S}^{2}}\frac{1}{|\omega_{n}|+4\Gamma^{\parallel}/d_{S}}\right]+\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(p^{S0}\xi\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\right)\frac{\gamma}{\xi d_{S}}\frac{1}{\omega^{2}_{n}}\frac{\left(g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{c2}-\frac{\gamma\pi T_{c0}}{2D}\right)}{|\omega_{n}|+4\Gamma^{\parallel}/d_{S}}\right\}\;.

(93)

We restore the global phase
e^{i\varphi}
in the order parameter,
\Delta=|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}
. By inserting this expansion into the selfconsistency equation, Eq. (
89
) we obtain:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}\log\frac{T_{c0}}{T}+\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left[\frac{|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}}{|\omega_{n}|}-if_{s}\right]\approx|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}\frac{T-T_{c0}}{T_{c0}}+\pi T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left[\frac{|\Delta|e^{i\varphi}}{|\omega_{n}|}-if_{s}\right]\;,

(94)

where we expanded the logarithm up to the first order in
T-T_{c0}
, consistent with the G-L limit.
We note that the resulting selfconsistency equation has to be the saddle point of the corresponding Ginzburg-Landau functional with respect to
\Delta^{\dagger}=|\Delta|e^{-i\varphi}
. Therefore, we obtain the G-L functional up to quadratic terms in
\Delta
:

\displaystyle F_{GL}=\frac{T-T_{c0}}{T_{c0}}|\Delta|^{2}+2|\Delta|^{2}\pi T_{c0}\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}\left[\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}-\frac{if_{s}}{\Delta}\right]=

(95)

\displaystyle=\frac{T-T_{c0}}{T_{c0}}|\Delta|^{2}+\frac{(\pi p^{S0}\xi)^{2}}{4}|\Delta|^{2}+2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left[\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)-(p^{S0}\xi)\frac{\gamma}{2\pi T_{c0}\xi d_{S}}\right]^{2}|\Delta|^{2}\;.

Here
M(x)
is the dimensionless function that is defined as follows,

M(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{2}(2n+1+x)}=\frac{\pi^{2}x-4\psi^{(0)}\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)-4\gamma_{E}-4\log 4}{8x^{2}}\;,

(96)

where
\gamma_{E}\approx 0.577
is the Euler constant,
\psi^{(0)}(x)
is the digamma function. This function monotonically decreases; it has two important asymptotics,
M(0)=\frac{7\zeta(3)}{8}
with
\zeta(3)\approx 1.202
being the corresponding value of the Riemann zeta function, and
M(x\gg 1)\approx\frac{\pi^{2}}{8x}
. With this, we have derived Eq. (
41
) in the main text.

B.5
The shift of critical temperature

We may now infer the change in the critical temperature caused by ISOC-induced corrections using the definition of the critical temperature as the highest temperature at which superconductivity appears. The quadratic terms of the Ginzburg-Landau functional always take the form

\displaystyle F_{GL}\left[\Delta\right]=\frac{T-T_{c}}{T_{c}}|\Delta|^{2}\;.

(97)

Comparing Eqs. (
95
) and (
97
), we infer that the critical temperature is given by

\displaystyle\frac{T_{c}(p^{S0},\ H)}{T_{c0}}=1-\frac{(\pi p^{S0}\xi)^{2}}{4}-2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left[\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)-(p^{S0}\xi)\frac{\gamma}{2\pi T_{c0}\xi d_{S}}\right]^{2}\;.

(98)

In equilibrium, the system acquires the phase gradient that minimizes the free energy, that is, that maximizes the critical temperature. It can thus be written as

\displaystyle\frac{T_{c}(H)}{T_{c0}}=\operatorname{max}_{p^{S0}}\left\{1-\frac{(\pi p^{S0}\xi)^{2}}{4}-2M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\left[\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)-(p^{S0}\xi)\frac{\gamma}{2\pi T_{c0}\xi d_{S}}\right]^{2}\right\}\;.

(99)

The maximum of the critical temperature, which corresponds to the minimum of the free energy, is reached at the optimal momentum
p^{\text{an}}
that is given by

p^{\text{an}}\xi=\frac{8M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma}{D}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)}{\pi^{2}d_{S}/\xi+4M\left(\frac{4\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}d_{S}}\right)\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\pi T_{c0}D\xi d_{S}}}\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\;,

(100)

where we introduce
\chi_{H}=\frac{g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{c2}}{\pi T_{c0}}=\frac{g_{L}}{2mD}
. At
\Gamma^{\parallel}=0
,
p^{\text{an}}
may be written as

\displaystyle p^{\text{an}}\xi=\frac{7\zeta(3)\frac{\gamma}{D}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)}{\pi^{2}d_{S}/\xi+\frac{7\zeta(3)\gamma^{2}\xi}{D^{2}d_{S}}}\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\approx\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)\times\begin{cases}\frac{Dd_{S}}{\gamma\xi}\;,\qquad d_{S}\ll\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}\;,\\
\frac{7\zeta(3)\gamma\xi}{\pi^{2}Dd_{S}}\;,\qquad d_{S}\gg\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}\;.\end{cases}

(101)

The presence of the maximum in
T_{c}(p^{\text{an}})
at a nonzero
p^{\text{an}}
suggests the existence of a helical phase, which in turn implies non-reciprocal transport. It is worth noting that
p^{\text{an}}
is suppressed in both the limits of very small and very large thicknesses, indicating a non-monotonic behavior. Consequently, the helical phase is most prominent when the thickness
d_{S}
satisfies
d_{S}\sim\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}
.

For
H_{0}=0
, we have
p^{\text{an}}=0
and consequently
T_{c}=T_{c0}
, as expected. For nonzero
H_{0}
, one may write the critical temperature as

\frac{T_{c}}{T_{c0}}=1-\beta\frac{H^{2}}{H^{2}_{c2}}\;,

(102)

where
\beta
is the dimensionless coefficient that obeys
\beta>0
. Specifically, in the very thin limit
d_{S}\ll\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}
, we have by direct substitution of the small
d_{S}
limit of Eq. (
101
) into Eq. (
98
) and keeping terms only to lowest order in
d_{S}
:

\displaystyle\beta=\left(\frac{\pi(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D})Dd_{S}}{2\gamma\xi}\right)^{2}\;.

(103)

This corresponds to Eq. (
50
) in the main text.

On the other hand, if we take
p^{\text{an}}
corresponding to the case of
\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}\ll d_{S}
, insert it into Eq. (
98
) and expand the result up to the second order in
\gamma/D
, then we find,

\displaystyle\beta=\frac{7\zeta(3)}{4}\left(\chi_{H}-\frac{\gamma}{2D}\right)^{2}-\left(\frac{7\zeta(3)\gamma\xi\chi_{H}}{2\pi Dd_{S}}\right)^{2}\;,

(104)

which in the limit of very large
d_{S}
reduces to Eq. (
52
) in the main text.
Interestingly, if we subsequently wrongly put the opposite limit
d_{S}\ll\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}
in Eq. (
104
) instead of the correct limit one,
d_{S}\gg\frac{\gamma\xi}{D}
, then we have the same dependence on
T_{c}(H_{0})
as reported in
[
47
]
using the G-L functional, where the positive contribution to
T_{c}
dominates.

C.1
Dimensionless variables

For numerical procedures, it is usually convenient to reduce the number of free parameters by using dimensionless parameters. The spin-galvanic term at the boundary has the same dimensions as the diffusion constant, and therefore, we define

\displaystyle\theta_{I}=\frac{\gamma}{D}=-2\frac{\sigma^{sc}}{\sigma_{D}}\;,

(105)

where
\sigma^{sc}
is the spin-charge conductivity
[
43
]
and
\sigma_{D}
is the normal state conductivity of the superconductor.

For the other quantities, we use typical scales of the superconductor.
For lengths and momenta, we use the superconducting coherence length
\xi=\sqrt{\frac{D}{2\pi T_{c0}}}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{d}_{S}

\displaystyle=d_{S}/\xi\;,\qquad\tilde{p}^{S}=p^{S}\xi\;.

(106)

For energies and temperatures, we use the BCS critical temperature
T_{c0}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{T}=\frac{T}{T_{c0}}\;,\qquad\tilde{\Delta}=\frac{\Delta}{\pi T_{c0}}\;,\qquad\tilde{\omega}_{n}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{\pi T_{c0}}=\tilde{T}(2n+1)\;.

(107)

For magnetic fields, we use the critical field
H_{c2}
of the superconductor:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{H(z)}{H_{c2}}\;,\qquad\tilde{H}_{0}=\frac{H_{0}}{H_{c2}}\;,

(108)

while for current densities and currents, we use the scales
j_{s0}=\frac{\sigma_{D}\pi T_{c0}}{\xi e}
and
j_{s0}\xi
, related to the critical current density:

\displaystyle\tilde{j}_{S}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{j_{S}(z)}{j_{s0}}\;,\qquad\tilde{W}=\frac{W}{j_{s0}\xi}\;,\qquad\tilde{J}^{S}=\frac{J^{S}}{\xi j_{s0}}\;,\qquad\tilde{I}_{0}=\frac{I_{0}}{\xi j_{s0}}\;.

(109)

The boundary spin-relaxation, which has units of velocity, is normalized via

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{\parallel}=\frac{\Gamma^{\parallel}}{\pi T_{c0}\xi}\;.

(110)

With this, the algebraic Usadel equation (
35
) reads as

\displaystyle-\frac{(\tilde{p}^{S0})^{2}}{2}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]\right]-\frac{i\tilde{p}_{S}(0)}{\tilde{d}_{S}}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{\tilde{J}}^{S}\right]+\mathcal{\check{\tilde{T}}}^{S}=\left[\tilde{\omega}_{n}\check{\tau}_{3}+\tilde{\Delta}\check{\tau}_{2}+i\chi_{H}\tilde{H}_{0}\sigma_{y}\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}\right]+\frac{8\tilde{\Gamma}^{\parallel}}{\tilde{d}_{S}}\check{g}_{s}\check{g}_{t}\sigma_{y}\;,

(111)

where for simplicity of notation we introduced

\displaystyle\chi_{H}=\frac{g_{L}\mu_{B}H_{c2}}{\pi T_{c0}}=\frac{g_{L}}{2mD}\;.

(112)

and the dimensionless surface current and torque read

\displaystyle\check{\tilde{J}}^{S}

\displaystyle=-\theta_{I}\check{g}_{t}(\check{1}-\check{g}_{t}^{2})+\theta_{I}\check{g}_{s}\check{g}_{t}^{2}\sigma_{y}\;,

(113)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\check{\tilde{T}}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\theta_{I}\tilde{p}^{S}(0)}{\tilde{d}_{S}}\left[\check{\tau}_{3},\ \check{g}_{s}\right]\left(\check{1}-2\check{g}_{t}^{2}\right)\sigma_{y}\;.

The selfconsistency relation, Eq. (
38
), within dimensionless units reads

\displaystyle-\tilde{\Delta}\log\tilde{T}=\sum_{n}\left[\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{|2n+1|}-\frac{i\tilde{T}}{4}\operatorname{tr}\{(\check{\tau}_{1}-i\check{\tau}_{2})\check{g}\}\right]\;.

(114)

Meanwhile, Eq. (
33
) that relates the total current to the surface current and the bulk current reads

\displaystyle\tilde{I}=\tilde{J}^{S}+\tilde{W}\tilde{d}_{S}\tilde{p}^{S}\;.

(115)

C.2
Parameterization

In order to solve Eqs.
111
,
113
, and
114
, we employ the angle parameterization:

\displaystyle\check{g}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{g}&\hat{f}\\
-\hat{\tilde{f}}&-\hat{\tilde{g}}\end{pmatrix}=\cosh\zeta\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\theta&\sinh\theta\\
-\sinh\theta&-\cosh\theta\end{pmatrix}+\sigma_{y}\sinh\zeta\begin{pmatrix}\sinh\theta&\cosh\theta\\
-\cosh\theta&-\sinh\theta\end{pmatrix}.

(116)

The gauge transformation, Eq. (
29
), makes
\check{\Delta}
real, so
\hat{\tilde{f}}=\hat{f}
,
\hat{\tilde{g}}=\hat{g}
, that is,
\zeta
is real and
\theta
is imaginary. In terms of this parameterization, the algebraic Usadel equation (
111
) reads:

\displaystyle\frac{i\theta_{I}}{\tilde{d}_{S}}\tilde{p}^{S}(0)\sinh\zeta\cosh^{2}\zeta\cosh\theta-\frac{{\left(\tilde{p}^{S0}\right)^{2}}}{2}\cosh 2\zeta\sinh 2\theta-\tilde{\omega}_{n}\cosh\zeta\sinh\theta-i\cosh\theta\left[\tilde{\Delta}\cosh\zeta+\chi_{H}\tilde{H}_{0}\sinh\zeta\right]=0\;,

(117)

\displaystyle\frac{i\theta_{I}}{4\tilde{d}_{S}}\tilde{p}^{S}(0)\left[\cosh\zeta+3\cosh 3\zeta\right]\sinh\theta-\frac{2\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}}{\tilde{d}_{S}}\sinh 2\zeta-

\displaystyle-\frac{{\left(\tilde{p}^{S0}\right)^{2}}}{2}\sinh 2\zeta\cosh 2\theta-\tilde{\omega}_{n}\sinh\zeta\cosh\theta-i\sinh\theta\left[\tilde{\Delta}\sinh\zeta+\chi_{H}\tilde{H}_{0}\cosh\zeta\right]=0\;.

These equations need to be solved for each
\tilde{\omega}_{n}
. We denote their solutions by
\theta_{n},

\zeta_{n}
. The dimensionless selfconsistency equation, Eq. (
114
), in this parameterization reads

\displaystyle-\tilde{\Delta}\log\tilde{T}=2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}\left[\frac{\tilde{\Delta}}{2n+1}-i\tilde{T}\cosh\zeta_{n}\sinh\theta_{n}\right]\;.

(118)

After the equation for selfconsistency is solved, the dimensionless superfluid stiffness and surface currents are calculated as

\displaystyle\tilde{W}=-\tilde{T}\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}\left[\cosh 2\theta_{n}\cosh 2\zeta_{n}-1\right]\;,

(119)

\displaystyle\tilde{J}^{S}=-2\theta_{I}\tilde{T}\operatorname{Im}\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}\sinh\zeta_{n}\cosh^{2}\zeta_{n}\sinh\theta_{n}\;.

C.3
Solution procedure

In order to solve Eqs. (
117
) and (
114
),
we first fix
\tilde{p}^{S0}
and
\tilde{\Delta}
. For fixed
\tilde{p}^{S0}
and
\tilde{\Delta}
, we solve the algebraic Usadel equations, Eq. (
117
), for every
\tilde{\omega}_{n}
and obtain their solutions
\theta_{n}
,
\zeta_{n}
. With the help of these solutions, we update
\tilde{\Delta}
for the given
\tilde{p}^{S0}
till the selfconsistency in
\tilde{\Delta}
is reached. Using
\theta_{n}
,
\zeta_{n}
, and
\tilde{\Delta}
found in the previous stage, we compute
\tilde{J}^{S}(\tilde{p}^{S0})
and
\tilde{W}(\tilde{p}^{S0})
according to (
119
). Then
\tilde{I}_{0}(\tilde{p}^{S0})=\tilde{J}^{S}(\tilde{p}^{S0})+\tilde{W}(\tilde{p}^{S0})\tilde{d}_{S}\tilde{p}^{S0}
is used to calculate the diode efficiency,
\eta=\frac{I_{c+}-|I_{c-}|}{I_{c+}+|I_{c-}|}
, where
I_{c+}=\operatorname{max}\tilde{I}_{0}(\tilde{p}^{S0})
and
I_{c-}=\operatorname{min}\tilde{I}_{0}(\tilde{p}^{S0})
. By using this code for multiple values of the input parameters
\theta_{I}
,
\tilde{H}_{0}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{\parallel}
, we generated Fig.
3
.

During our numerical procedure, we use root finding routines provided by the Python library scipy.optimize
[
72
]
to solve both the algebraic Usadel equation for each
\tilde{\omega}_{n}
and the selfconsistency equation. In order to achieve a good speed of convergence for the sums over the Matsubara frequencies
\tilde{\omega}_{n}
, we employ the Gaussian sum quadrature proposed in
[
73
,
74
]
,

\sum_{\tilde{\omega}_{n}>0}f(\tilde{\omega}_{n})\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}a_{j}f(\tilde{\omega}^{\star}_{j})\;.

(120)

Here, the coefficients
a_{j}
and the energies
\tilde{\omega}^{\star}_{j}
are chosen in such a way that the equality is exact for any functions
f(\tilde{\omega}_{n})
that are polynomials in
(1+n)^{-2}
of order less than
N/2
. Here,
N
is chosen so that
\tilde{\omega}_{N}
is much larger than any energy scale in the system.
