Influence of study climate and trust on knowledge sharing and knowledge hiding among Chinese medical students
Authors: Yuchen Long, Xueyan Cheng, Jing Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to explore the relationship between knowledge sharing (KS) and knowledge hiding (KH), and the influence of study climate (SC), interpersonal trust (IT) and sharing intention (SI) on KS and KH among Chinese medical undergraduates within the same framework.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted among 761 undergraduates from two medical colleges in Wuhan, China. Measurements included five scales on KS, KH, SC, IT and SI. Three path analysis was performed using a structural equation model (SEM) to examine the influence of SC, IT, and SI on KS and KH for medical students with different academic performance levels.ResultsThe mean scores for SC, IT, SI, KS and KH of medical students were 3.79 ± 0.67, 3.92 ± 0.62, 4.05 ± 0.64, 3.68 ± 0.68 and 1.64 ± 0.67, respectively. Path analysis showed that KS and KH were weakly related among three academic performance subgroups (coefficients ranging from -0.193 to -0.112). IT was significantly positively associated with KS (β = 0.268, 0.112 and 0.227, respectively, all P<0.05) among all three subgroups, while IT was negatively associated with KH among medical student with good and average academic performance (β = -0.200 and -0.117, respectively, all P < 0.05). SC could indirectly positively influence KS and negatively influence KH through IT and SI regardless of their academic performance; SC had no direct influence on KH, while only a weak negative influence on KS was identified in the average academic performance group (β=-0.132, P < 0.05). For students with good, below average, and average performance, the models explained 48.7%, 36.3% and 42.0% of the variance in KS, and 34.6%,17.0% and 26.3% of those in KH, respectively.ConclusionsKS and KH could coexist among medical students in China. The influence of SC and IT on KS and KH varied among medical students with different academic performance. The results demonstrated that educators could develop knowledge management strategies targeting students with different academic performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06364-4.

Introduction

Knowledge sharing (KS) among employees in an organizational setting have been paid much attention to in the past three decades [1–3], and previous research has indicated that KS plays a critical role in improving performance, innovation at the individual, team, and organizational levels [4–6].

KS in educational context is usually about the dissemination or exchange of explicit or tacit knowledge, ideas, experiences or even skills from one individual to another individual students or group of students [7]. The knowledge among medical students usually includes factual and procedural knowledge. Factual knowledge is the theoretical background of medical education, such as some free public health education and professional information, notes, resources, and study strategies, as well as access to information. Procedural knowledge is the practical part of medicine, including personal health experiences and treatments [8]. Medical students are continually acquiring vast amounts of information across various subjects and are often at the forefront of new medical research and practices [9]. Besides, for the comprehensive nature of health care, medical students are usually expected to leverage their expertise to collaborate with professionals from various fields (i.e., physicians, nurses, technicians) and to solve problems cooperatively in their future workplaces [10]. Therefore, KS should be an irreplaceable content of professionalism for future medical workers.

However, most studies about KS and KH were mainly conducted in organizational context [11], while only limited work was conducted for students [12, 13], even fewer for medical students. Previous studies had demonstrated that students in higher education were the most effective and efficient knowledge sharers during their study period in the academic institutions; however, knowledge hiding (KH) is also prevalent among them [14]. KH is an intentional attempt to withhold or conceal knowledge [15]. It suggests that KS and KH represent two different constructs with different underlying motivations and mechanism [16, 17], which can exist simultaneously [18]. Although some studies in organizational context took efforts on the association of KS and KH under the same framework [18–25], the relationship and mechanism between KS and KH in students are largely unknown. Considering the intrinsically relational behaviors of KS and KH, a social network approach was used to interpret the interplay associations between them, and to estimated the co-evolving relationships between KS and KH and environmental factors.

For medical students, research has explored some potential factors associating with KS, focusing on both internal and external determinants. Internal factors that are primarily related to student’s psychological processes (i.e., intentions and self-efficacy) [26, 27] can be influenced by their environmental context (i.e., interpersonal relationships and study culture in class) [28, 29]. Notably, it is also suggested that academic performance is one of the most concerns for medical students that could affect their KS behavior [30]. However, even though most studies have detected KH in organizational context [31–34], few studies had focused on medical students specifically, and none examined medical student’s KH in China so far.

An abundance of research had explored the internal and external influencing factors for individuals in organizational context to share their knowledge [35]. The internal factors are mainly associated with individuals’ personality (such as the Big Five and Dark Triad)[36] knowledge self-efficacy, goal orientation and enjoyment in helping others. By contrast, the external motivators are multifaceted [37], such as organizational climate [38, 39], trust [40, 41], and social relationship [1, 35, 42–45]. Compared with KS, studies about exploring the determinants of KH are scarce. Regarding medical students, the mechanism between influencing factors and KS and KH has not been fully studied. Although individual’s internal factors (i.e., personality and intelligence quotient) could influence KS and KH directly, external factors (i.e., study climate, interpersonal trust) may shape these associations [28, 29]. Therefore, this study aimed to summarize potential associations between influencing factors and KS and KH theoretically, and to verify the associations with a cross-sectional study among Chinese medical undergraduates.

Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

Social exchange theory

Social exchange theory (SET) states that individual exchange resources with others based on the principle that individual behavior is to maximize benefits and minimize costs. If people believe that they are able to extract more of a reward through a behavior than they lose by performing it, then they will perform the behavior [46]. It could be interpreted as the process of formation of sharing intention (SI). The exchanges are not limited to the organization but extended to the attempt to interact with society and environment. The SET has been adopted in many domains, including knowledge management [47].

Cost and benefits

Knowledge sharing and hiding in this study are the results of weighting perceived cost and benefit of medical students in their class. Based on SET, students may would like to share knowledge when the perceived benefits of their sharing behavior exceed the costs, or they may be hesitant to share knowledge and even lead to knowledge hiding [46].

KS can not only enhance individual learning but also foster collaborative spirit essential for medical profession. For individual learning, KS enables individuals to understand professional knowledge and skills, foster the feeling of importance, and boost innovation [5]. KS also encourages collaboration and promote effective communication, which is essential in building connections and establishing the foundation for future teamwork in clinical settings. For example, students can improve their leadership skills by organizing KS activities, creating KS environment, and to explore effective KS methods [48].

While the benefits of KS among medical students usually lead to positive outcome, its potential downsides are also essential to be mindful, and thus increase the probability of KH. First, potential of conflict may cause due to limited resources may increase the costs of KS (i.e., scholarship application, ranking in class). Second, from student’s perspective, strong psychological ownership of knowledge, especially specific knowledge and experiences, can increase the perceived costs of sharing knowledge, which may decrease the intention of KS [49]. At last, time costs on organizing and attending study sessions can detract from individual study or personal time, and preparation for sharing knowledge can also be time consuming [11]. Once the perceived cost of KS exceeds its benefits, individual’s intention to KS may decreased. Hypothesis about SI and KS and KH were made based on the principle of SET.

H1a:

There is a positive relationship between SI and KS.

H1b:

There is a negative relationship between SI and KH.

Co-evolving factors

As mentioned above, individual’s behavior of KS could be influenced by both internal and external factors. Considering that internal factors are usually associated with an individual’s educational background, personalized experience and personality, external factors are mainly discussed in this study. Interpersonal trust (IT), defined as confidence in and willingness to act based on another’s words, actions, and decisions [50–52]. Interpersonal knowledge sharing and learning are more likely to occur in strong trusting relationships [53], since high level of IT can increase students’ intention to cooperate [54]. However, existing studies only found the positive association between distrust and KH [15], the association between trust and KH has not been widely proved. Meanwhile, distrust was considered as one of the independent antecedents when studying KH phenomenon. Connelly et al. [15] found a positive association between KH and interpersonal distrust. However, the relationship between trust and KS and KH in the same framework among medical students remains unclear.

Moreover, individuals’ attitude and behaviors could be shaped by the environment. Organizational climate, encompassing subjective impressions, feelings, and perception of the actions of the members [55], holds significant sway over individuals’ intentions and actions [56]. Study climate, equivalent to organizational climate in the realm of education [57], has been demonstrated a mixed impact on knowledge exchange [38, 39, 58]. Collaborative study environments can promote KS, as students’ study behavior could mutually influence each other [59]. The situation could be conversed in high-intensity knowledge environments, as individuals may be inclined to withhold information under competitive surroundings [15]. It is also unclear how the extend of study climate influence an individual’s trust and then leading to different direction to KS or KH among students, and the associations between study climate and KS and KH are highly unknown. We made hypothesis based on this:

H2a:

There is a positive relationship between SC and SI.

H2b:

There is a positive relationship between SC and KS.

H2c:

There is a negative relationship between SC and KH.

H3a:

There is a positive relationship between IT and SI.

H3b:

There is a positive relationship between IT and KS.

H3c:

There is a negative relationship between IT and KH.

In fact, contextual factors, including situational and environmental factors (culture, communication, and shared mental models) can result in different levels of trust. Collaborating with other students to achieve a shared objective necessitates a certain level of interdependence to realize the mutual goals, and interdependence is key factor in fostering trust [60]. Supportive contextual premises help to shape and guide the development of interdependence among students. However, even though a few studies had worked on the association between IT and organizational climate on isolated KS [61, 62] and isolated KH [63] in organizational context, none addressed the specific role of IT in the pathway of study climate to KS and KH at the same time in educational context. We made hypothesis based on this:

H4:

There is a positive relationship between SC and IT.

Transactive memory system theory

Research on transactive memory system (TMS) has developed a multi-level theoretical framework to elucidate how knowledge is distributed, recognized, shared, and utilized across dyadic, group, organizational and inter-organizational context [64]. The fundamental process of TMS in a work team is the recognition of team member’s expertise [65]. Once experts are identified within a team, other individuals can access their knowledge without needing to become experts themselves. At the same time, non-experts could transform to experts in other knowledge domains. This integrated process of recognizing expertise, retrieving knowledge, and allocating information could lead to the development of TMS within a team. As a result, team members become more specialized in their respective domains, minimizing redundant efforts to learn and manage knowledge beyond their areas of expertise [66].

For medical students, it’s normal that students with good academic performance to be the expertise in their class, and this situation may be hard to transform regarding study climate when relating to professional knowledge sharing. But IT among students may have separate influence on their experiences in transferring knowledge.

H5a:

IT is positively associated with KS and negatively associated with KH, while SC is not associated with KS and KH among students with good academic performance.

Even though students with below-average academic performance have the intention of KS, it would be restricted by their classmate’s trust, owned professional knowledge, and perceived study climate. For example, students with below-average academic performance may spend time on other activities (could be expertise in other fields), they would have different perceived study climate with those having good grades, and they would not be the prioritized expertise for knowledge seekers, even for trusted members.

H5b:

SC and IT is not associated with neither KS or KH among students with below average academic performance.

It seems hard to summarize the potential influencing factors for students with average academic performance, as their motivations and barriers to KS and KH are not clear. Therefore, we assume all co-evolving factors we mentioned could influence their behaviors of KS and KH.

H5c:

SC a﻿nd IT is positively associated with KS and negatively associated with KH among students with average academic performance.

Methods

Study Design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in April 2020 at two medical schools in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China.

Participants and Procedures

A purposive sampling strategy was used to select two medical schools (A and B). School A belongs to a general comprehensive university, while school B belongs to a “Project 985” university. A Project 985 university in China refers to a higher education scheme of the Chinese central government for creating world-class higher institutions and is supposed to be the first-class universities in China. Students from “Project 985” university require higher grades in university entrance examinations.

Medical students across various majors were selected based on class of majors, including Basic Medicine, Clinical Medicine, Stomatology, Public Health and Preventive Medicine, Chinese Medicine, Pharmaceutical Science, Nursing and Imaging and Nuclear Medicine. An online survey ensuring anonymity was conducted and participants provided informed consent by clicking on the “yes” button. No compensation was offered. The questionnaires were distributed to 838 medical students, with 761 valid responses, yielding an overall response rate of 90.8%.

Measurements

Measurement of KS

A four-item scale about knowledge sharing took reference from Bock et al. [67] was used to ask respondents to indicate to what extent they would like share knowledge with their classmates. Each item was estimated using the five-point Likert categories of “1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Usually, 5 = Always”. The example items including “I can share the knowledge with my classmates when they need without concealing” and “I can exchange professional knowledge, experiences, and information with my classmates”. According to the result of exploratory factor analysis (EFA), only one factor was identified.

Measurement of KH

We referenced a twelve-item Knowledge Hiding Scale from Connelly et al. [15], using a five-point Likert ranging from “1 = Strongly disagree” to “5 = Strongly agree”. An example item is “I will pretend not to know what he/she is talking or asking for”. It is noteworthy that, to mitigate social desirability bias, we emphasized in the questionnaire instructions when measuring KH: “When the classmates inquire about important matters such as medical knowledge, information about awards, postgraduate studies, employment, experiences, etc., somebody are willing to disclose and somebody may be less inclined to do so, and both situations are considered possible and acceptable normal. When your classmates inquire you about above information…” This was to ensure that participants can answer the questionnaire based on the objective circumstances as much as possible.

Measurement of SC

A five-item scale about study climate were adopted from the subscale of Class Psychological Atmosphere Evaluating Scale for College Students developed by Li et al. [68]. Each item was estimated using the five-point Likert categories of “1 = Not at all like me; 2 = Not much like me; 3 = Neutral; 4 = Somewhat like me; 5 = Very much like me”. An example item is “The learning atmosphere in my class is positive”.

Measurement of IT

Affect-based trust scale (seven items) developed by McAllister was used to measure the interpersonal trust among medical students in China [50]. The reliability and validity of the Chinese version of the scale has been estimated [69] (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.841). Each item was estimated using the five-point Likert categories of “1 = Not at all like me; 2 = Not much like me; 3 = Neutral; 4 = Somewhat like me; 5 = Very much like me”. An example item is “I can share my thoughts, emotions, and aspirations with my classmates”.

Measurement of SI

The six-item knowledge sharing intention scale was designed based on the knowledge sharing attitude and knowledge sharing intention subscales in Bock et al. ’s 2005 study [70]. Each item was estimated using the five-point Likert categories of “1 = Not at all like me; 2 = Not much like me; 3 = Neutral; 4 = Somewhat like me; 5 = Very much like me”. An example item is “I benefit from sharing knowledge, experience, and information with my classmates”.

Before the final version of the measurements confirmed, interviews with medical students about the contents, language, items, and statements of the scales were conducted. Then the scales were modified according to the interview results. Reliability and validity of the included scales were tested in this study, and the results demonstrated the good reliability and validity of the measurements.

The final version of the questionnaires used in the the study were shown in Appendix 1.

Statistical analysis

Reliability and validity of the scales were tested using Cronbach’s alpha (α), composite reliability (CR), and average Variance Extracted (AVE). According to Hair et al., the rule of thumb for the strength of factor loading refers to a standard value larger than 0.40, and the threshold value for Crobach’s α is 0.70, and CR values that are greater than 0.7 denote good reliability. The validity indicator was average variance extracted (AVE) in this study. The standard value for AVE is 0.50.

A path analysis was used to examine the relationships between variables using SEM in STATA version 15.0. Missing data were handled using maximum likelihood methods with missing values. Model fit was assessed using root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) (< 0.05 indicates good fit), comparative fit index (CFI) (> 0.95 indicates good fit), and standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR) (< 0.05 indicates good fit). For multi-group analysis, we compared the model among three academic performance groups and tested the invariance of parameters across groups with a Wald test.

Results

Reliability and validity of measurements and descriptive statistics

The Cronbach’s alpha values for the KS scale, KH scale, SC scale, IT scale and SI scale were 0.743, 0.955, 0.873, 0.914 and 0.905, respectively, all of which were greater than the usually acceptable threshold value of 0.7. CRs of all scales (KS, KH, SC, IT, and SI) were greater than 0.7 and the AVEs were greater than 0.5 (Table 1).

Exploratory factor analysis for scales of KS (KMO = 0.702, Barlett’s test χ2 = 827.303, P < 0.001), KH (KMO = 0.948, Barlett’s test χ2 = 9617.354, P < 0.001), SC (KMO = 0.861, Barlett’s test χ2 = 2130.138, P < 0.001), and SI (KMO = 0.866, Barlett’s test χ2 = 2920.187, P < 0.001) detected only one factor in each scale. However, two factors were identified for the scale of IT (KMO = 0.881, and Barlett’s test χ2 = 4091.30, P < 0.001), which were labeled as emotional trust (ET) and cognitive trust (CT). Despite identifying two factors from the results of EFA, the IT scale has been validated as an independent tool to measure affect-based trust. Therefore, the overall score of IT was used in the analyses of this study.

The mean score of KS was 3.68 (SD = 0.68), with the mean score of KH, SC, IT and SI being 1.64 (SD = 0.67), 3.79 (SD = 0.67), 3.92(SD = 0.62), and 4.05(SD = 0.64), respectively.

Demographic composition of the participants

Table 2 outlines the demographic characteristics of the participants in this study. Of the 761 respondents, 60.97% were female, 24.05% in their first year of study, 69.91% majored in basic medicine, 46.91% reported average academic performance, 46.39% held a position as one of the class commissions.

Among the participants in this study, female students, those in their late stage of study and in school B, with below-average academic performance, and those who were class commission members reported higher level of KS (t/F=-3.19, 3.65, 2.70, 11.36, 3.78, respectively, all P < 0.05). Meanwhile, male participants, those in school B, and majored in pharmacy were more compatible to report higher level of KH (t/F = 3.05, 2.99 and 3.38, respectively, all P < 0.05).

Influence of SC, IT and SI on KS and KH

The scores of SC, IT, SI, KS, and KH were standardized. The SEM was conducted by subgroups based on self-reported academic performance (good, below average and average). Considering the significant associations of school type and gender on both KS and KH, we included these variables in the models. Given that whether a student holds a class commission position may be related to personal characteristics of the student, we also included it in the model. According to the tests for group invariance, SC showed significant differences among academic performance groups in IT (χ2 = 11.129, P<0.01) and KS (χ2 = 9.345, P<0.01). Only significant associations were shown in the models.

Regarding the relationship between KS and KH, they were weakly related in the good, below-average and average academic performance groups (coefficients=-0.193, -0.161 and-0.112, respectively, all P < 0.05).

The influence of SC, IT and SI on KS and KH varied in three academic performance subgroups. For all three groups of students, SI were significantly positively associated with KS (β = 0.555, 0.497 and 0.538 respectively, all P < 0.01) and negatively associated with KH (β=-0.549, -0.339 and -0.338 respectively, all P < 0.01), H1a and H1b were supported. For all three groups of students, IT had a direct significant positive association with KS (β = 0.268, 0.112 and 0.227 respectively, all P < 0.05) and SI (β = 0.265, 0.329 and 0.450 respectively, all P < 0.05).H3a and H3b were supported. For students with good and average academic performance, there was a negative association between IT and KH (β=-0.200 and -0.117 respectively, all P < 0.05). For students with below-average academic performance, there was no significant association between IT and KH.

For all three groups of students, SC was significantly positively associated with IT (β = 0.617, 0.251 and 0.555 respectively, all P < 0.01). H4 was supported. For students with below average and average academic performance, there was a positive association between SC and SI (β = 0.131 and 0.150 respectively, all P < 0.05). For students with good academic performance, there was no significant association between SC and SI. For all three groups of students, SC had an indirect significant positive association with KS through IT and SI, and an indirect significant negative association with KH. SC had no direct influence on KH and almost no direct influence on KS, with a weak negative association observed only in the average academic performance group (β=-0.132, P < 0.05). H5a and H5b were supported.

For good, below average, and average academic performance subgroups, the models explained 48.7%, 36.3% and 42.0% of the variance in KS and 34.6%, 17.0% and 26.3% of those in KH, respectively.

Discussion

This study investigated the influence of SC and IT on KS and KH within the same framework. Our findings provided a deeper understanding of the mechanisms behind KS and KH, and it could help medical educators to improve knowledge management strategies among medical students.

Co-existent Relationship Between KS and KH

Although correlation analysis revealed a moderate negative correlation between KS and KH (r=-0.431) in our study, which is consistent with previous studies in organizational context (r ranging from -0.44 to -0.23) [22, 23], the coefficients in SEM were even smaller (ranging from -0.193 to -0.112) in the three academic performance subgroups, suggesting the relative independence of KS and KH [15, 71]. Therefore, KS and KH could coexist in educational equivalent as within organizations [20].These behaviors may be directed at different individuals or exhibited differently toward the same individuals over time [71]. This underscores the need for future research to explore the antecedents and consequences of KS and KH simultaneously within the same analytical framework.

Relationships between IT and KS and KH

Our study revealed a significant positive association between IT and KS, which is consistent with previous studies on college students in other majors (e.g., Administration and Finance) [2, 72]and studies in workplace settings [70, 73–76]. These studies have highlighted that trust is one of the most important predictors of KS. A high level of trust could always lead to positive expectations [77], a strong sense of security [78, 79] and lower individual’s perceived costs (e.g., uncertainty) [80, 81], thereby fostering open communication [74] and enhancing medical students’ willingness to share knowledge. Accordingly, to build friendly and trusting relationships, educators could open expression channels for students (i.e., increase the frequency of class activities), and organize teamwork tasks to guide KS [82].

The association between IT and KH varied across the three academic groups in our study. For students with good and average academic performance, the negative association between IT and KH was small (β = -0.200 and -0.117, respectively, P < 0.05), while insignificant association was found in the subgroup of below-average academic performance. Previous studies also highlighted a diverse relationship between IT and KH within organizational context (coefficients ranging from -0.95 to -0.36) [20, 83].Therefore, the influence of IT on KH is more complex and less decisive compared with on KS [83]. More studies are warranted to clarify the relationship between trust and KH across diverse samples, and to explore the boundary conditions of this relationship by incorporating relevant moderators.

Relationships between SC and KS

In this study, SC could indirectly influence KS positively and KH negatively through IT and SI, but SC had almost no direct influence on KS. For students with good and below-average academic performance, the association between SC and KS was not significant, while for students with average performance, SC had a weak negative association with KS (β= -0.132, P<0.05). These findings were different from previous findings with a strong positive link between organizational climate and employees’ KS [44, 61, 67]. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in samples and measurements. Unlike employees working together toward organizational goals, college students may focus more on their individual learning goals rather than class’s goal [46]. Although SC could significantly contribute to enhancing students’ learning engagement [84], its influence on KS remains limited. Meanwhile, the measurement of SC in our study reflected not only the extent of students’ participation in class and learning, but also their emphasis on academic performance. And activities related to academic performance among college students always involve competition [85], intense peer competition may lead them to feel threatened [86, 87]. In this study, students with average academic performance may perceived higher threat than the other two groups, making them more reluctant to share knowledge. Previous research has also indicated that students hesitate to share knowledge in activities that related to grades or exams [88]. However, the measurement of organizational climate mainly focused on “fairness”, “innovativeness” and “affiliation” [37, 61, 67], reflecting a perception of an open and equitable environment where members are cooperative, trusting, and caring, rather than emphasizing effort and competition among colleagues. Our findings suggested that students with different academic performance may perceive the study climate differently. Educators and researchers should pay more attention to these differences and strive to create a supportive rather than competitive SC to improve interpersonal relationships, thereby promoting KS.

School and Sociodemographic Factors Influencing KS and KH

Apart from the influence of IT and SC on KS/KH, this study showed that school type, undertaking class commissions, and gender also significantly correlated with KS and KH. First, medical students in school B, labeled as a “Project 985” school, were more prone to KS compared with a “non-Project 985” school which may due to more support for students’ KS in the former, such as teacher’s power (punishment and legitimacy), technology availability (internal sharing system), and student’s attitudes toward teamwork [12]. Second, among medical students with good academic performance, those with class commissions reported a higher level of KS than those without class commissions. Students with good grades have better ability on KS, and being a class commission, they have a good foundation of trust from their classmates, and therefore have more opportunities to share knowledge [89].

At last, female reported a lower level of KH, which may attribute to femininity such as being caring, helpful, and attuned to communal needs or showing solicitude forfeelings of others [90] Pertiwi et al. figured out that both females and males may have the intention to hide what they know in practice [90]. Therefore, more studies are needed to explore the gender differences in KH.

Implications and Limitations

There are several important implications of this study that are worth noting. First, our study is the first attempt to investigate the relationship between KS and KH among medical students within the same analytical framework. We found their co-existent relationship in the educational context, suggesting that KS and KH deserve equal attention and more studies are needed to explore how these behaviors be impacted by different factors. Meanwhile, our study found that SC and IT had significant influence on students’ KS and KH, suggesting that educators could improve the quality of interpersonal interactions among classmates as much as possible by enhancing trust, cooperation, and open communication rather than emphasizing competitive relationships among classmates. In addition, an interesting finding of our study is that students in different academic groups have different perception of the classroom environment and interpersonal relationships, and their attitudinal and behavioral responses vary accordingly. This highlights the need for researchers to consider the characteristics of different students, enabling educators to propose targeted measures to improve classroom management effectively.

Our study has a few limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was a self-report questionnaire survey, and social desirability could hardly be avoided completely, although we tried to reduce it in possible ways, such as not mentioning the names of the four key variables (i.e., KS, KH, SC, and IT) in the questionnaire and providing special instructions when measuring KH. Second, this study was a cross-sectional study that explored the associations between SC, IT, and KS and KH at a single time point, and did not assess the causal relationships among the variables. More experiments or longitudinal studies are needed to contribute useful information towards causal inference. Third, this study was conducted in central China, and generalizing the findings to other regions of China and other countries should be done with caution.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study indicated that KS and KH could coexist in the context of medical education. The influence of SC, IT and SI on KS and KH varied across the three academic groups. The results implied that educators could develop knowledge management strategies that are targeted at students with different levels of academic performance.
