G.AI: an AI-driven platform for phenotype standardization, variant interpretation and structured clinical reporting in rare disease genomic diagnosis
Authors: Zhinong Wang, Xiaoning Chen, Liuqing Tang, Xiaokai Wu, Aiyu Huang, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe diagnosis of rare diseases increasingly relies on the interpretation of high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) data. As sequencing volume expands, the analytical burden grows substantially, and manual workflows become increasingly difficult to scale and prone to inconsistency. To address these challenges, we developed G.AI, an interpretable and traceable artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted genomic analysis platform that integrates automated phenotype standardization, variant pathogenicity ranking, and structured clinical reporting.MethodsThe platform uses a modular architecture comprising data parsing, AI-driven inference, and structured report generation. Performance was assessed using 39,156 multicenter whole-exome sequencing (WES)/ parent–child trio sequencing (WES Trio) cases from China, including 7,097 confirmed pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) positive cases. Key evaluation metrics included phenotype-model concordance, Top-1, Top-3 and Top-20 variant pathogenicity ranking accuracy and workflow efficiency.ResultsThe AI-Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) phenotype standardization model achieved 94% concordance with manual review. The pathogenicity-ranking model reached Top-1 95%, Top-3 98%, and Top-20 99.6% accuracy among positive cases, with metabolic disorders achieving 100% Top-3 accuracy. Additional analysis on non-diagnostic cases demonstrated low false prioritization rates and good model specificity. Total analysis time decreased from 4 to 6 h to 48 ± 12 min, demonstrating a significant improvement in efficiency.ConclusionBy integrating automated phenotype processing, variant annotation, and AI-driven pathogenicity evaluation, G.AI substantially enhances the accuracy, consistency, and scalability of rare disease variant interpretation. Its transparent and traceable workflow provides a robust foundation for large-scale clinical genomic applications.

Introduction

Rare diseases represent a major global public health challenge, with more than 7,000 conditions currently identified and approximately 80% having a genetic basis [1–3]. Although NGS is widely adopted, the time from symptom onset to molecular diagnosis often remains prolonged, contributing to substantial diagnostic delays [4, 5]. Contributing factors include multisystem phenotypes, incomplete documentation, and the complexity of variant interpretation [6]. Heterogeneous expertise across institutions further exacerbates these issues.

Traditional NGS interpretation workflows require bioinformatics teams to perform variant filtering and annotation, followed by manual review by genetic counselors and clinical geneticists who integrate literature evidence, database information, and patient phenotypes. This process is labor-intensive and experience-dependent, making consistency difficult to achieve in large-scale testing settings [7, 8]. Consequently, improving the efficiency and standardization of rare disease variant interpretation—while preserving clinical safety and interpretive transparency—has become an urgent need.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence, particularly in natural-language phenotype processing and neural network–based variant prediction, offer new opportunities for accelerating genetic diagnosis [9–11]. However, existing automated tools have limited phenotype extraction, minimal clinical validation, and poor integration into reporting workflows [12–14]. Exomiser relies on manually curated HPO terms, lacks natural language processing (NLP) capability, and cannot be directly applied to clinical report generation [15, 16]. Phenomizer only processes structured phenotype inputs, does not support NLP, and provides neither variant filtering nor prioritization functions [17, 18]. InterVar performs American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) rule-based variant classification but lacks phenotype integration and prioritization mechanisms [19, 20].

To address these gaps, we developed G.AI, a clinically AI-assisted interpretation platform. The platform incorporates an AI-HPO phenotype standardization model and a pathogenicity scoring model, combined with a traceable evidence framework and clinician-review mechanisms, enabling automated transformation from variant call format (VCF) data to structured clinical reports. The platform is designed to enhance the efficiency and accuracy of rare disease variant interpretation, reduce manual workload, and support transparent, clinically manageable, AI-assisted diagnostic workflows in real-world settings.

Methods

Platform design

The G.AI platform employs a three-tier architecture comprising a data processing layer, an AI inference layer, and a structured reporting layer. The platform ensures interpretability and traceability through a human-in-the-loop workflow.

1) Data Processing Layer

VCF files generated from NGS pipelines are ingested and processed through standardized quality control, multi-dimensional variant annotation, and integration of population databases, functional, and clinical evidence sources. These outputs serve as input for downstream AI analysis.

2) AI Inference Layer

Clinical narratives are normalized into Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms using the AI-HPO phenotype standardization model. The platform performs phenotype–genotype association and ranks candidate variants.

3) Structured Reporting Layer

The platform produces standardized, editable clinical interpretation reports using configurable templates that integrate AI-generated evidence summaries, variant classifications, and phenotype mappings. Users may review and revise the content to ensure diagnostic accuracy and regulatory compliance.

AI-HPO phenotype standardization model

With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) in medicine, their ability to encode clinical knowledge and perform reasoning has been well demonstrated [21–23]. We developed an AI-HPO model based on a hybrid framework that combines a large language model (LLM) with rule-based constraints for phenotype normalization. The LLM component is used to perform contextual semantic parsing of unstructured clinical text, including clinical notes, diagnostic descriptions, and examination records. It identifies phenotype-relevant information and maps these descriptions to standardized Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms. This process is designed to accommodate common characteristics of clinical narratives, such as medical abbreviations, synonymous expressions, and negation. To improve the accuracy and consistency of the results, rule-based constraints are applied after LLM inference. These include ontology validation to ensure valid HPO term mapping, hierarchical consistency checks to maintain logical relationships between parent and child terms, filtering of negated or nonspecific descriptions, and removal of non-phenotypic content. In addition, domain-informed rules are used to improve the clinical relevance of the retained phenotype terms.

The final output is a semantically disambiguated and standardized set of HPO terms that preserves essential clinical meaning while removing noise and redundancy, facilitating digital reconstruction of the patient’s phenotype.

To evaluate the performance of the AI-HPO module, expert manual HPO curation—prepared by clinicians experienced in genetic diagnosis—were used as the reference standard. Model performance was assessed using the harmonic mean of precision and recall (F1)-score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F1 = 2 \times Precision \times Recall/\left( {Precision + Recall} \right)$$\end{document}

Here, precision represents the proportion of predicted HPO terms that are truly correct, whereas recall reflects the proportion of all relevant terms that were successfully identified by the model [24–26]. Because phenotype standardization is sensitive to both false positives and false negatives, the F1-score provides a balanced and robust measure of overall performance. In addition, model robustness was evaluated across cases with varying numbers of phenotypic features to assess its stability in multi-phenotype scenarios.

Variant annotation

For each VCF file, the platform executes an automated standardized variant annotation workflow, integrating population databases: Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD),1000 Genomes, in-house cohort) [27, 28], functional prediction tools (Sorting Intolerant From Tolerant (SIFT) [29], PolyPhen-2 [30], Rare Exome Variant Ensemble Learner (REVEL) [31], Splicing Artificial Intelligence(SpliceAI) [32] and clinical databases: ClinVar [33], Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) [34]. Functional prediction tools are incorporated as features in the pathogenicity scoring framework, contributing to variant prioritization, and are also used for auxiliary pre-filtering to exclude variants with low likelihood of pathogenicity [35]. This multi-source annotation provides comprehensive evidence for downstream variant interpretation.

AI phenotype and variant pathogenicity ranking

We developed an AI pathogenicity ranking model that integrates patient clinical phenotypes (HPO/OMIM) with molecular characteristics of each variant. Candidate genes are first retrieved based on the patient’s HPO terms and their parent-level phenotypes, and further expanded using OMIM disease associations. Variants then undergo hard-threshold prescreening, including population frequency filtering, functional impact annotation, and integration of evidence from curated databases.

For phenotype–genotype matching, the system assigns cumulative scores according to the relevance between each gene and the patient’s clinical presentation. The cumulative score is defined as an integrated score combining multiple sources of evidence: Cumulative score = phenotype matching score + pathogenicity feature score + technical quality adjustment. The relative contributions of phenotype matching, pathogenicity features, and quality adjustments are controlled through a weighted integration scheme, in which each component is assigned a predefined contribution based on its relative importance in variant prioritization.

The phenotype matching score is calculated based on hierarchical HPO term matching, including exact HPO matches, weighted parent-level matches, and phenotype associations derived from OMIM disease descriptions. These contributions are aggregated across all matched phenotype terms. The pathogenicity feature score integrates evidence from population frequency data, functional prediction tools (e.g., REVEL, SIFT, PolyPhen-2, SpliceAI), and curated clinical databases. To increase the reliability of the final prioritization, down-weighting adjustments are applied to variants that may represent technical artifacts or low-confidence biological signals. These include low allele ratio variants, synonymous variants, single-allele findings in recessive disorders, splice-site variants lacking predictive support, and potentially noisy variants within the Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) region. Each category is assigned a down-weighting coefficient (0.1–0.8), empirically determined based on internal validation datasets to reflect domain-specific pathogenicity characteristics, thereby proportionally adjusting its contribution based on estimated pathogenic likelihood.

Model training and weight optimization

Model parameters (i.e., feature weights) were learned from the training data using regularized maximum likelihood estimation within a logistic regression framework. The relative contributions of different feature groups were therefore determined in a data-driven manner. Hyperparameters, including regularization strength, were selected through cross-validation to balance model complexity and generalization performance. Model development followed a standard training–validation–independent test split to ensure robustness and generalizability. Model selection and performance evaluation focused on ranking quality rather than overall classification accuracy, with Top-1, Top-3, and Top-20 ranking accuracy of pathogenic variants as the primary evaluation metrics. To mitigate overfitting, regularization constraints were applied, highly correlated or redundant features were removed, and the contribution of each feature group to the final score was monitored to prevent dominance by any single evidence source. Model performance was iteratively evaluated on validation data to ensure stable ranking behavior. In addition, the model incorporates a calibrated scoring scheme based on validation datasets to improve consistency across heterogeneous evidence types and enhance overall ranking stability.

Report template and clinical review workflow

The platform automatically assembles structured, customizable interpretation reports, incorporating patient information, prioritized variants, disease descriptions, and relevant literature. Users refine and finalize the diagnostic conclusion and may append comments or supplemental notes. Template sections support the inclusion of additional results: sanger/copy number variation (CNV) confirmations, informed consent statements, test limitations, and digital signatures.

Report issuance follows a closed-loop, version-controlled workflow—from AI-generated recommendations to user review and final approval—with all operations and revisions comprehensively logged to ensure regulatory compliance. Reports are exportable as PDF.

Dataset and ethical compliance

Model training was performed using 39,156 de-identified WES/WES Trio cases from multiple clinical centers in China, including 7,097 confirmed positive cases. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or legal guardians, and only de-identified data were used. A standard training–validation–independent test split and cross-validation were applied to ensure model robustness and generalizability. All procedures complied with institutional ethics requirements. In addition, a subset of non-diagnostic cases (negative and VUS) was randomly sampled for manual review to assess model specificity and false prioritization behavior.

Sample description

The G.AI platform was benchmarked on a large multicenter cohort of 39,156 de-identified WES/WES Trio cases. Following manual variant filtration and ACMG-guided pathogenicity assessment [36], 7,097 cases were confirmed to carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic SNVs, including both single-nucleotide polymorphisms(SNPs) and insertion/deletion variants (INDELs), representing 18.12% of all analyzed samples. Among these SNV-positive cases, singleton analyses accounted for 73.41% (5,210 cases), whereas trio-based analyses constituted 26.59% (1,887 cases).

This heterogeneous multicenter dataset provides a robust foundation for evaluating G.AI model performance.

Distribution across disease systems

Based on the primary clinical phenotypes, all SNV-positive cases were categorized into major disease systems. Neurological disorders accounted for the largest proportion (62.7%, 4,452 cases), including neurodevelopmental delay, epilepsy, intellectual disability and behavioral abnormalities. Other disease systems were distributed as follows: syndromic/multisystem disorders (7.7%, 546 cases), skeletal disorders (5.1%, 363 cases), cardiovascular disorders (4.8%, 340 cases), metabolic disorders (4.5%, 319 cases), renal/urological disorders (3.9%, 277 cases), sensory disorders involving hearing or vision impairment (3.7%, 263 cases), neuromuscular disorders (3.1%, 219 cases), digestive disorders (1.9%, 135 cases), hematologic disorders (1.3%, 94 cases), and endocrine disorders (1.3%, 89 cases).

The positive cases encompassed all major categories of hereditary diseases, demonstrating substantial diversity and representativeness.

Distribution of variant types

A total of 10,536 pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants were identified among all positive cases. SNPs constituted the majority, accounting for 76.2% (8,032 variants), whereas INDELs represented 23.8% (2,504 variants).

Within the SNP category, missense variants were the predominant subtype (42.2%, 4,448 variants), followed by canonical splice-site variants (13.4%, 1,416 variants), splice-region variants (11.2%, 1,180 variants), and nonsense variants (9.4%, 988 variants).

Within the INDEL category, frameshift variants were the most frequent (47.5%, 1,190 variants), followed by non-frameshift deletions (39.2%, 982 variants), non-frameshift insertions (9.2%, 230 variants), and non-frameshift delins (4.1%, 102 variants).

Overall, the observed variant spectrum—including the proportions of missense, nonsense, splice-site, and frameshift variants (Fig. 3)—is consistent with pathogenic variant distributions reported in gnomAD and ClinVar, providing a suitable baseline for evaluating the performance of the G.AI platform across different variant classes.

Performance stratification revealed that the mild complexity group achieved the highest concordance (F1-score 95–97%), while the moderate complexity group showed a slight decrease (F1-score 92–94%). In the high-complexity group, consistency declined to 88–90%, likely due to increased cross-system symptoms and text-derived noise; however, performance remained within an acceptable range and did not impair the recognition of diagnostically critical features.

Performance remained consistent in disorders with well-defined phenotypic signatures, such as neurodevelopmental conditions, metabolic diseases, and sensory system disorders. A minority of discordant cases (~ 7%) were primarily due to ambiguous terminology or overlapping multisystem manifestations. Manual review indicated that these discrepancies had minimal impact on downstream variant pathogenicity ranking, and corresponding issues have been addressed in subsequent model updates.

Collectively, these results indicate that AI-HPO phenotype standardization model achieves near-expert performance in semantic phenotype extraction, hierarchical mapping, and noise mitigation. The model provides consistent, high-fidelity phenotype representations across diverse case complexities and clinical settings, thereby strengthening the reliability and reproducibility of downstream variant interpretation.

AI variant pathogenicity ranking across systems

The pathogenicity-ranking algorithm of the G.AI platform demonstrated robust overall performance, achieving a Top-1 accuracy of 95% (6,742/7,097), Top-3 accuracy of 98% (6,955/7,097), and Top-20 accuracy of 99.6% (7,068/7,097) across the entire cohort. Among all positive cases (n = 7,097), the metabolic, sensory, hematologic, and endocrine systems showed the highest ranking accuracies, with Top-3 and Top-20 accuracy both reaching 100% for metabolic disorders, and Top-20 accuracy reaching 100% for sensory, hematologic, and endocrine disorders. These results may reflect stronger phenotype specificity and a higher concentration of well-characterized genes within these systems.

In contrast, neurological and neuromuscular disorders showed slightly lower Top-1 performance (93%–97%), consistent with their substantial phenotypic heterogeneity and large number of potential causative genes. Syndromic and multisystem disorders exhibited mid-to-high accuracy, likely benefiting from the aggregation of cross-system clinical features and relatively well-defined gene sets. Ranking accuracy of the G.AI algorithm across disease systems

To further evaluate model performance in real-world clinical scenarios, we conducted an additional analysis on non-diagnostic cases. We selected a representative subset to enable detailed manual review, as full-cohort manual verification would not be feasible in a real-world clinical setting. A total of 200 cases, including both negative and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) cases, were randomly selected from the full cohort, covering multiple disease systems.

For each case, the top 1–20 ranked candidate variants were manually reviewed. No variants were identified that simultaneously satisfied both strong pathogenicity evidence and high phenotype consistency within the Top-20 candidates.

The top-ranked variants generally fell into two categories: (1) variants with relatively high pathogenicity scores but lacking phenotype consistency, and (2) variants with partial phenotype relevance but insufficient pathogenicity evidence. Variants with both low pathogenicity and poor phenotype relevance were largely excluded during the pre-filtering stage and were rarely observed among the top-ranked candidates. These results indicate a low false prioritization rate and demonstrate good specificity of the model in avoiding incorrect prioritization of non-causal variants in non-diagnostic settings. Further evaluation on larger cohorts will be conducted in future work to comprehensively assess model performance.

Analysis efficiency

Traditional WES interpretation relies heavily on manual operations across multiple analytical steps, including phenotype normalization, variant annotation, database retrieval, evidence review, variant prioritization, and report preparation. This multistep workflow typically requires 4–6 h of analyst time and is susceptible to inter-operator variability due to differences in interpretation criteria, database usage patterns, and subjective decision-making [7].

In contrast, the G.AI platform streamlines the entire analytical pipeline by automating AI-HPO phenotype standardization, real-time variant annotation, AI-driven pathogenicity ranking, and structured evidence synthesis. These automated modules consolidate the major time-consuming components into a unified process and generate a structured draft report that requires only targeted clinical review. Benchmarking showed that total analysis time was reduced to 48 ± 12 min.

Discussion

This study presents a systematic evaluation of G.AI, an AI-assisted platform designed to support clinical genetic testing workflows. Using multicenter real-world datasets, we assessed its performance in phenotype standardization, variant prioritization, and workflow efficiency.

The AI-HPO phenotype standardization model achieved an F1-score of 94% across heterogeneous, unstructured clinical text and maintained stable performance across varying phenotype complexity. Its high recall of core diagnostic features highlights its suitability for routine rare-disease interpretation.

Across 7,097 positive cases, G.AI demonstrated robust variant pathogenicity ranking performance, achieving 95%, 98%, and 99.6% accuracy at Top-1, Top-3, and Top-20, respectively. Results were consistent across disease categories and inheritance modes, highlighting the strength of the multi-evidence fusion approach.

In terms of efficiency, automation of phenotype processing, variant annotation, ranking, and structured reporting reduced interpretation time from 4 to 6 h to 48 ± 12 min, providing a scalable solution for high-throughput clinical programs.

Discrepancies between AI pathogenicity ranking and final diagnoses were primarily attributable to real-world data quality—such as incomplete narratives, limited phenotype depth, ambiguous terminology, or uneven sequencing coverage — rather than systematic biases of the model [37, 38]. These discrepancies did not materially affect diagnostic outcomes due to the downstream clinician review within the human-in-the-loop workflow. Their low frequency indicates limited impact on overall workflow reliability.

Despite the strong performance observed in this study, direct benchmarking against existing tools such as Exomiser, Phenomizer, and InterVar was not performed, primarily due to differences in input requirements, phenotype processing, and output formats. These differences make strict head-to-head comparisons under identical conditions challenging. Future studies will incorporate standardized datasets and unified evaluation metrics to enable more systematic and controlled benchmarking comparisons.

A limitation of this study is that the current version of G.AI primarily focuses on SNVs and INDELs derived from WES data and does not yet systematically support the detection and interpretation of copy number variants (CNVs) and structural variants (SVs) [39, 40]. This limitation may affect the completeness of structured clinical reporting in cases where pathogenic variants arise from copy number alterations or complex structural rearrangements. The lack of CNV/SV support may lead to incomplete identification of causal variants in such cases, potentially resulting in negative or inconclusive clinical reports despite the presence of underlying genetic abnormalities. It should be noted that reliable CNV detection from WES data typically depends on batch-level baseline normalization and specialized algorithmic models, which are difficult to implement in single-sample analysis. In addition, accurate identification of structural variants (SVs) is more effectively achieved using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data [41].Despite this limitation, SNVs and INDELs still represent a major proportion of variants identified in WES-based clinical diagnostics. The current framework of G.AI remains effective for phenotype standardization, variant prioritization, and automated reporting in these cases. Future work will progressively integrate CNV detection modules and WGS-based SV analysis capabilities to further improve the completeness and clinical utility of structured clinical reporting.

A limitation of this study is that all data were derived from multicenter cohorts in China, which may limit the direct generalizability of the results to other populations. Differences in population genetic structure, variant frequency spectra, and clinical documentation practices across geographic regions may influence model performance.

However, the dataset includes a large sample size, multicenter sources, and multiple disease systems, providing a degree of heterogeneity that supports an initial evaluation of model robustness in real-world clinical settings. In addition, G.AI is designed based on a generally applicable framework, including HPO-based phenotype standardization, integration of widely used public databases (e.g., gnomAD, ClinVar, OMIM), and phenotype–genotype association modeling, which are not inherently population-specific. Future studies will incorporate independent international datasets and cross-population analyses to further evaluate the generalizability of the model.

Overall, our findings demonstrate that G.AI provides accurate, efficient, and scalable support for molecular diagnosis of rare diseases. With continued expansion of models and curated data resources, G.AI may serve as foundational infrastructure for future clinical genetics and rare-disease programs.
