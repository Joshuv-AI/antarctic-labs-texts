A classification of unitals in nearfield planes with maximal automorphism group
Robert S. Coulter, Alice Man Wa Hui, Randon Weaver

Abstract
. We classify the parabolic unitals in regular nearfield planes of odd order q 2 q^{2} whose linear collineation group has the maximal size of q 3 − q q^{3}-q . We also establish a number of more general results concerning parabolic unitals in regular nearfield planes under weaker assumptions.



1.
Introduction

Set
p
to be a prime and
q
a power of
p
. We use

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
to denote the finite field of order
q
and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\star}
to denote the
nonzero elements.
The squares and non-squares of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
are denoted by
\square_{q}
and

\boxslash_{q}
, respectively.

A
unital
of order
m
is a
2
-
(m^{3}+1,m+1,1)
design.
In other words, it has
m^{3}+1
points,
m+1
points in each block, and the property that there is a unique block through any two points.
There are
m^{2}
blocks on each point.
If a unital of order
m
is embedded in a projective plane of order
m^{2}
,
then each line must intersect the unital in either
m+1
points or
1
point.
Lines of the former type are called
secants
, and lines of the latter type are called
tangents
.
There are
m^{2}
secants and 1 tangent through each point of the unital, while
for any point not in the unital, there are
m^{2}-m
secants and
m+1
tangents.
If a line of the plane is chosen as the line at infinity,
then the unital is called
hyperbolic
or
parabolic
depending on
whether the line at infinity intersects the unital in a secant or a tangent.

There are a number of known constructions of unitals embedded in
projective planes of square order, see the monograph
[
4
]

of Barwick and Ebert.
An example is the
classical unital
given by the nondegenerate Hermitian
curve in the Desarguesian plane
\PG(2,q^{2})
over the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
. One of its canonical forms is

(1)

\{(x,y,z)\in\PG(2,q^{2})\,:\,x^{q+1}-y^{q}z-z^{q}y=0\}.

A generalization of the classical unital is the
orthogonal-Buekenhout-Metz
unital
(or orthogonal BM unital, for short) in a translation plane of dimension two over its kernel, which is obtained from
an elliptic cone under the Bruck-Bose representation of the plane, see
Buekenhout
[
5
]
, Theorem 3.
Orthogonal-BM unitals in
\PG(2,q^{2})
were studied and enumerated by Baker
and Ebert
[
2
]
and Ebert
[
9
]
.

In this article we will be principally concerned with unitals in nearfield
planes, which are particularly special examples of translation planes.
(More information on nearfields and their planes is given below.)
Our work is preceded by several articles on this topic. In particular,
Wantz
[
19
]
studied a class of orthogonal-BM unitals
in the regular nearfield planes of order
q^{2}
. Let
{\gothfamily N}
denote a regular
nearfield plane of order
q^{2}
. Wantz showed that the set

(2)

\{(x,ax^{2}+bx^{q+1}+t\epsilon,1)\,:\,x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}},t\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}\cup\{(0,1,0)\},

is a unital in
{\gothfamily N}
if and only if
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
and

b^{2}-a^{2}\in\square_{q}
.
Baker and Ebert enumerated the distinct orthogonal-BM unitals in regular
nearfield planes of order
q^{2}
in
[
3
]
.

In this article we are especially interested in the case where
q
divides
the order of
\Aut(U)
for a unital
U
in
{\gothfamily N}
.
Our main result is a full classification of such unitals in the largest
possible automorphism group case. In fact, we prove
all are isomorphic to the unitals of Wantz, see
26
.
We also prove several more general results of independent interest while
progressing towards this goal, see Theorems
8
,

12
, and
13
, in particular.

The article is structured as follows.
We first recall some results on nearfields, nearfield planes, and their
collineation groups in Sections 2 and 3. In Section 4, we prove

8
, which is a general statement concerning central collineations that fix a unital.
In Section 5 we study the structure of the collineation groups that fix a
unital in a nearfield plane, under the assumption that the order of the
automorphism group is divisible by
q
. Then, in Sections 6 and 7, we examine how
these collineation groups dictate the geometry of the corresponding unitals,
and in particular the absence of certain O’Nan configurations. In Section 9,
assuming that the collineation group of the unital has order
q(q^{2}-1)
, we
prove that the fixed unital is isomorphic to one the Wantz unitals using
information about the all-ones polynomials presented in Section 8.

2.
Nearfields

The algebraic structure we shall be principally concerned with is a nearfield.

Definition 1
.

A finite set
S
with two binary operations,
+
(addition) and
\star

(multiplication), is called a (right)
planar nearfield
if

•

\langle S,+\rangle
is an abelian group with identity
0
,

•

\langle S^{\star},\star\rangle
is a group, and

•

the right distributive law holds.

As with finite fields, the additive structure of a planar nearfield is
necessarily elementary abelian, so that every nearfield can be viewed as a
finite field with some new “twisted" multiplication.
Dickson
[
7
,
8
]
introduced the concept of a nearfield
in 1905 and constructed two types of planar nearfields, now known as the
regular and irregular nearfields.
In 1935, Zassenhaus
[
20
]
showed that these were the only ones.
For a modern treatment of this classification result, see Grundhöfer and
Hering
[
10
]
.

There are exactly 7 irregular nearfields, and we refer the interested
reader to Dembowski
[
6
]
, page 231, for the specifics.
On the other hand, there are infinitely many regular nearfields.
To construct any one of them, let
q
be a prime power
and
n
a natural number such that the prime divisors of
n
also divide
q-1
.
If
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then we also require
n\not\equiv 0\bmod 4
.
Let
\mathfrak{z}
be a primitive element of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
and let
C=\langle\mathfrak{z}^{d}\rangle
,
the group generated by
\mathfrak{z}^{d}
.
Note that the order of
C
is
o(C)=(q^{n}-1)/d
and has index
d
in

{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}^{\star}
.
It is an easy exercise to prove that a set of
coset representatives for
C
in the multiplicative group

{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}^{\star}
is
\{\mathfrak{z}_{i}\,:\,0\leq i\leq d-1\}
, where

\mathfrak{z}_{i}=\mathfrak{z}^{(q^{i}-1)/(q-1)}
.
Let
F
be the Frobenius group of automorphisms of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
over

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
; that is,
F=\langle\phi\rangle
where

\phi:{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
satisfies
\phi(x)=x^{q}
. It is clear that

o(F)=n
.
We define the function
\alpha:{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}^{\star}\rightarrow F
by

\alpha(y)=(x\mapsto x^{q^{i}})
if and only if
y\in\mathfrak{z}_{i}C
.
Define a new multiplication on
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}
by

x\star y=\begin{cases}x^{\alpha(y)}y&\text{ if $y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}}^{\star}$,}\\
0&\text{ if $y=0$.}\end{cases}

Set
\mathds{N}(n,q)=\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{n}},+,\star\rangle
, where
+
is the field addition.
Then Dickson showed
\mathds{N}(n,q)
is a
regular
planar nearfield of order

q^{n}
, with a right distributive law.

The construction of the multiplication of a regular nearfield is essentially
the construction of a semidirect product
C\xs F
. It is
easily seen that this group is necessarily metacyclic. This was proved by
Zassenhaus as part of his classification of nearfields
[
20
]
,

In this article, we will be particularly interested in the regular nearfields

\mathds{N}(2,q)
. In this situation, nearfield multiplication simplifies to

(3)

x\star y=\begin{cases}xy&\text{ if $y\in\square_{q^{2}}$,}\\
x^{q}y&\text{ if $y\in\boxslash_{q^{2}}$.}\end{cases}

With
x^{-1}
and
\frac{1}{x}
denoting the regular nearfield multiplicative inverse and of finite field inverse
x
respectively, we get
x^{-1}=\frac{1}{x}
if
x\in\square_{q^{2}}
and
x^{-1}=\frac{1}{x^{q}}
if
x\in\boxslash_{q^{2}}.

The multiplicative group
\langle\mathds{N}(2,q)^{\star},\star\rangle
is isomorphic to

C_{(q^{2}-1)/2}\xs C_{2}
, where
C_{d}
denotes the cyclic group of
order
d
. Let the representation of
C_{2}
be
\{1,-1\}
.
Under this representation, the elements of
C_{(q^{2}-1)/2}\times\{1\}

correspond to the squares of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\star}
while the elements of

C_{(q^{2}-1)/2}\times\{-1\}
correspond to the nonsquares. Since the only
subgroups of
C_{(q^{2}-1)/2}\xs C_{2}
are
C_{d}\times\{1\}
or

C_{d}\xs C_{2}
for a divisor
d
of
(q^{2}-1)/2
, we see that any
subgroup
S
will consist entirely of squares of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\star}
, or split evenly
between squares and nonsquares of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\star}
.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a fixed primitive element of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
.
In the former case,
S=\langle\mathfrak{g}^{(q^{2}-1)/d}\rangle
.
In the latter case, where
S
consists half of squares and half of nonsquares,

S=S_{d/2}\cup S_{d/2}h
, where
h\in\boxslash_{q^{2}}

and
S_{d/2}=\langle\mathfrak{g}^{2(q^{2}-1)/d}\rangle
is of order
d/2
.
Moreover,
h\star h=h^{q+1}\in S
, forcing
h^{q+1}\in S_{d/2}
.
We summarize this discussion with the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathds{N}=\mathds{N}(2,q)
and let
\mathfrak{g}
be a primitive element of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
.
If
S<\langle\mathds{N}^{\star},\star\rangle
, then either

(i)

S=\langle\mathfrak{g}^{(q^{2}-1)/d}\rangle
for some divisor
d
of
(q^{2}-1)/2
, or

(ii)

S=S_{d/2}\cup S_{d/2}h
, where
S_{d/2}=\langle\mathfrak{g}^{2(q^{2}-1)/d}\rangle
for some divisor

d
of
(q^{2}-1)/2
and
h\in\boxslash_{q^{2}}
satisfies
h^{q+1}\in S_{d/2}
.

Later we will find ourselves dealing with a surjective homomorphism mapping
one subgroup of
\langle\mathds{N}(2,q)^{\star},\star\rangle
to a cyclic subgroup of

\langle\mathds{N}(2,q)^{\star},\star\rangle
. Anticipating this, we prove the following.

Lemma 3
.

Let
G,H
be subgroups of
\langle\mathds{N}(2,q)^{\star},\star\rangle
with
H
cyclic, and let

\sigma:G\rightarrow H
be an onto homomorphism with
o(G)=r\,o(H)
for some
integer
r
.
The following statements hold.

(i)

Suppose
G
is cyclic. Then
H<G
and there exists an integer

1\leq j<o(H)
with
\gcd(j,o(H))=1
for which
\sigma(x)=x^{rj}
for all

x\in G
.

(ii)

Suppose
G
is not cyclic, so that
G=S_{l}\cup S_{l}h
with

h^{q+1}\in S_{l}
.
Set
S=\sigma(S_{l})
and
K=\Ker(\sigma)\cap S_{l}
.

(a)

If
\sigma(S_{l})=H
, then
r
is even,
H<G
,
o(K)=r/2
, and there
exists an integer

1\leq j<o(H)
with
\gcd(j,o(H))=1
for which
\sigma(x)=x^{rj/2}
for all

x\in S_{l}
.

(b)

If
\sigma(S_{l})\neq H
, then
o(H)
is even,
o(K)=r
, and there
exists an integer

1\leq j<o(H)/2
with
\gcd(j,o(H)/2)=1
for which
\sigma(x)=x^{rj}
for all

x\in S_{l}
. Furthermore,
\sigma(h)\in H\setminus\sigma(S_{l})
and

H=S_{l}\cup S_{l}\sigma(h)
.

Proof.

Since
G<\langle\mathds{N}(2,q)^{\star},\star\rangle
, we know the form of
G
from
2
.

If
G
is cyclic, then both
G
and
H
are necessarily subgroups of the
(cyclic) multiplicative group of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\star}
. Since
o(H)|o(G)
, it is
immediate that
H<G
. In this case,
\sigma
is now a group homomorphism
on the cyclic group
G
with a kernel of order
r
. It is immediate that

\sigma
has the form claimed.

Now suppose
G
is not cyclic, so that
G=S_{l}\cup S_{l}h
where
o(G)=2l
.
Note that
K
is the kernel
of the restriction of
\sigma
to
S_{l}
and
S\approx S_{l}/K
. This also means

S
is a subgroup of
S_{l}
, as both are cyclic subgroups of the groups of
non-zero squares of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
.

Suppose first that
S=H
. Then
H\approx S_{l}/K
, from which we find

o(H)|l
and so
H<S_{l}<G
. Since
2l=r\,o(H)
, we also have
2|r
and

o(K)=r/2
.
Again, we appeal to the known structure of homomorphisms on cyclic groups
to get that the restriction of
\sigma
to
S_{l}
satisfies

\sigma(x)=x^{rj/2}
for some integer
j
relatively prime to
o(H)
.

Now suppose
S\neq H
.
If
\sigma(h)\in S
, then
\sigma(G)=S\cup S\sigma(h)=S\cup S=S\neq H
,
contradicting
\sigma
being onto.
Thus,
\sigma(h)\notin S
, and
S\sigma(h)
is a nontrivial coset of
S
.
It follows that
H=S\cup S\sigma(h)
, from which
[H:S]=2
. This time we have

o(K)=r
, and the restriction of
\sigma
to
S_{l}
satisfies

\sigma(x)=x^{rj}
for some integer
1\leq j<o(H)/2
with

\gcd(j,o(H)/2)=1
.
∎

3.
Nearfield planes and their collineations

There are a number of ways in which to construct a projective plane from a
nearfield. We follow the construction and notation of Wantz
[
19
]
,
whose results motivated this work.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\mathds{F}=\langle\mathds{F},+,\star\rangle
be any nearfield.
We define a point set
P
and line set
L
by

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\{(x,y,1)\,:\,x,y\in\mathds{F}\}\cup\{(1,y,0)\,:\,y\in\mathds{F}\}\cup\{(0,1,0)\},

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\{[s,1,t]\,:\,s,t\in\mathds{F}\}\cup\{[1,0,t]\,:\,t\in\mathds{F}\}\cup\{[0,0,1]\},

respectively. If incidence
\Inc
is defined by

(x,y,z)\,\Inc\,[s,u,t]\text{ if and only if }x\star s+y\star u+z\star t=0,

then
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})=(P,L,\Inc)
is a projective plane of order
o(\mathds{F})
.

This construction is standard (and not limited to nearfields). Note that in
this construction the
line at infinity
is considered to be the line

[0,0,1]=\{(1,y,0)\,:\,y\in\mathds{F}\}\cup\{(0,1,0)\},

with all points not lying on
[0,0,1]
viewed as
affine points
.
Moreover, the line at infinity is a translation line, see Dembowski

[
6
]
, pages 127–131, and Section 5.1 for further
information on this.

With regard to the collineation groups of nearfield planes, we have the
following results from André
[
1
]
.

Lemma 5

(
[
1
]
, Satz 12)
.

Let
\mathds{F}=\langle\mathds{F},+,\star\rangle
be any regular nearfield of order

q=p^{e}\neq 9
, and let
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
be the corresponding nearfield plane.
The full collineation group
\Gamma
of
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F}))
is given by

\Gamma=\mathfrak{T}\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0}\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{0}\,\mathfrak{B}_{0},

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y,1)\mapsto(x+u,y+v,1)\,:\,u,v\in\mathds{F}\},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y,1)\mapsto(x\star c,y\star d,1)\,:\,c,d\in\mathds{F}^{\star}\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y,1)\mapsto(\tau(x),\tau(y),1)\,:\,\tau\in\Aut(\mathds{F})\},(replace\ \sigma\ by\ \tau)\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\mathpzc{id},(x,y,1)\mapsto(y,x,1)\}.

Furthermore, we have
o(\mathfrak{T})=q^{2}
,

o(\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0})=(q-1)^{2}
, and

o(\widetilde{\mathfrak{U}}_{0})=e
, so that
o(\Gamma)=2eq^{2}(q-1)^{2}
.

From this result, André immediately obtains the following very useful
description of
\Gamma
.

Lemma 6

(
[
1
]
, Satz 13)
.

Any collineation of the nearfield plane
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
is of the form

(x,y,1)\mapsto(\tau(x)\star c+u,\tau(y)\star d+v,1),

or

(x,y,1)\mapsto(\tau(y)\star d+v,\tau(x)\star c+u,1),

where
\tau\in\Aut(\mathds{F})
, and
c,d,u,v\in\mathds{F}
,
c,d\neq 0
.

A
linear collineation
of
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
is any collineation not
induced by an automorphism of
\mathds{F}
.
5
thus tells us
the group of all linear collineations is precisely

\mathfrak{T}\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0}\,\mathfrak{B}_{0}
. Following the notation of

[
19
]
, we define the following mappings to describe all linear
collineations: for all
c,d,u,v,s,t\in\mathds{F}
with
c,d\neq 0
,

•

Define
\phi(c,d,u,v)
on
P
and
L
by

\displaystyle(x,y,1)

\displaystyle\mapsto(x\star c+u,y\star d+v,1)

\displaystyle(1,y,0)

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,c^{-1}\star y\star d,0)

\displaystyle(0,1,0)

\displaystyle\mapsto(0,1,0)

\displaystyle[s,1,t]

\displaystyle\mapsto[c^{-1}\star s\star d,1,t\star d-u\star c^{-1}\star s\star d-v]

\displaystyle[1,0,t]

\displaystyle\mapsto[1,0,t\star c-u]

\displaystyle[0,0,1]

\displaystyle\mapsto[0,0,1]

•

Define
\gamma(c,d,u,v)
on
P
and
L
by

\displaystyle(x,y,1)

\displaystyle\mapsto(y\star c+u,x\star d+v,1)

\displaystyle(1,y,0)

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,(y\star d)^{-1}\star c,0),y\neq 0

\displaystyle(1,0,0)

\displaystyle\mapsto(0,1,0)

\displaystyle(0,1,0)

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,0,0)

\displaystyle[s,1,t]

\displaystyle\mapsto[c^{-1}\star s^{-1}\star d,1,t\star s^{-1}\star d-u\star c^{-1}\star s^{-1}\star d-v],s\neq 0

\displaystyle[0,1,t]

\displaystyle\mapsto[1,0,t\star c-u]

\displaystyle[1,0,t]

\displaystyle\mapsto[0,1,t\star d-v]

\displaystyle[0,0,1]

\displaystyle\mapsto[0,0,1]

Clearly, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}

\displaystyle=\{\phi(1,1,u,v)\,:\,u,v\in\mathds{F}\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\{\phi(c,d,0,0)\,:\,c,d\in\mathds{F}^{\star}\}.

We note that
\mathfrak{T}
is the translation group of the plane; it is easily
observed that it consists of all
q^{2}
elations of the plane with
axis
[0,0,1]
. Thus
\mathfrak{T}\approx\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{q},+\rangle\times\langle{\mathbb{F}}_{q},+\rangle
.
The following result will prove useful.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathds{F}
be a nearfield of order
q=p^{e}
for some prime
p
.
The following statements hold.

(i)

The group of all elations of
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
with center
(0,1,0)
and
axis
[0,0,1]
is given by

H_{0}=\{\phi(1,1,0,v)\,:\,v\in\mathds{F}\}.

Moreover, any subgroup of
H_{0}
is of the form

\{\phi(1,1,0,v)\,:\,v\in V\}
for some subspace
V
of

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, when viewed as a vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
.

(ii)

The group of all elations of
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
with center
(1,y,0)
,

y\neq 0
, and axis
[0,0,1]
is given by

H_{y}=\{\phi(1,1,u,u\star y)\,:\,u\in\mathds{F}\}.

(iii)

Any linear collineation of
{\gothfamily N}(\mathds{F})
of order a power of
p
must
lie in
\mathfrak{T}
.

Proof.

It is easily verified that
H_{y}
has the claimed center for all
y
. Since
there are at most
q
elations with any given center and axis, it is clear
we have all such elations in each case. All that
needs to be established to complete the proof of (i) or (ii) is the form of

V
for a subgroup of
H_{0}
, but this follows at
once from the observation that composition of elements of
H_{0}
acts in the
same manner as field addition in the 4th coordinate of the
\phi
involved.

Finally, it is easy to prove that

\mathfrak{T}
is normal in the group of linear collineations

\mathfrak{T}\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{0}\,\mathfrak{B}_{0}
. Since
\mathfrak{T}
is a Sylow-
p

subgroup and normal, it is unique. Thus, all elements of order a power of
p

must lie in
\mathfrak{T}
, proving (iii).
∎

4.
Central collineations that fix a unital

Before working with unitals in nearfield planes, we first prove a general
result concerning central collineations that fix a unital in any finite
projective plane.

Theorem 8
.

Let
U
be a unital of order
m
embedded in a projective plane

{\gothfamily P}
of order
m^{2}
.
Suppose
G=\Aut(U)
contains a non-trivial central collineation
\phi
with
axis
\mathpzc{l}
.
The following statements hold.

(i)

\phi
is an elation if and only if
\mathpzc{l}
is a tangent line of
U
.

(ii)

\phi
is an homology if and only if
\mathpzc{l}
is a secant line of
U
.

Proof.

Choose a point
\mathpzc{P}\in\mathpzc{l}\cap U
and let
\mathpzc{b}
be the unique
tangent line through
\mathpzc{P}
.
As
\phi\in G
, we must have
\mathpzc{b}^{\phi}=\mathpzc{b}
.
Recall that, under the action of
\phi
, the non-fixed lines through
\mathpzc{P}

must be partitioned into orbits of size
o(\phi)
.

There are two cases.

\boxdot

\mathpzc{l}
is a tangent line.

In this case, we have
\mathpzc{l}=\mathpzc{b}
.
Now, if
\mathpzc{P}
is the center of
\phi
, then
\phi
is an elation.
Otherwise, there are
m^{2}
lines through
\mathpzc{P}
that are not fixed by

\phi

6.
The non-existence of certain O’Nan configurations in unitals in
{\gothfamily N}

Definition 14
.

An
O’Nan configuration
in a unital is a collection of four distinct lines
meeting in six distinct unital points.

In 1972, O’Nan
[
14
]
noted that these configurations do not occur
in the classical unital. It was later conjectured by Piper
[
15
]

that any unital without such a configuration is the classical unital.
Recently, Stroppel
[
16
]
showed that
the conjecture is true if for each unital point, there is an automorphism of
the unital that fixes the point and every block through it.

Lemma 15
.

In
{\gothfamily N}
, three points
(x,y,1),(x_{1},y_{1},1),(x_{2},y_{2},1)
are collinear on a
line
[u,1,z]
with
u\neq 0
, if and only if

(x-x_{2})\star(x-x_{1})^{-1}=(y-y_{2})\star(y-y_{1})^{-1}.

If these products are in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, then

\frac{x-x_{2}}{x-x_{1}}=\frac{y-y_{2}}{y-y_{1}}.

Proof.

The line determined by
(x,y,1)
and
(x_{1},y_{1},1)
is

[-(x-x_{1})^{-1}\star(y-y_{1}),1,x\star(x-x_{1})^{-1}\star(y-y_{1})-y]
.
For
(x_{2},y_{2},1)
to be on this line, we must have

-x_{2}\star(x-x_{1})^{-1}\star(y-y_{1})+y_{2}+x\star(x-x_{1})^{-1}\star(y-y_{1})-y=0.

Simplifying gives
(x-x_{2})\star(x-x_{1})^{-1}=(y-y_{2})\star(y-y_{1})^{-1}
.
The final statement follows from the definition of
\star
.
∎

We next prove a lemma concerning O’Nan configurations in
{\gothfamily N}
containing

(0,1,0)

Lemma 16
.

Consider an O’Nan configuration in
{\gothfamily N}
whose six points are
(0,1,0)
,

\mathpzc{P}=(x,y,1)

\mathpzc{P_{ij}}=(x_{i},y_{ij},1)

for

.
Suppose
\Tr(y_{11})=\Tr(y_{12})

Then
\frac{x-x_{2}}{x-x_{1}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
if and only if
\Tr(y_{21})=\Tr(y_{22})

Proof.

Applying
\phi(1,1,-x,-y)
if needed, without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathpzc{P}=(0,0,1)
.
By assumption,
x_{i},y_{ij}\neq 0
and
y_{i1}\neq y_{i2}
for

.
Applying
15

(4)

x_{2}\star x_{1}^{-1}=y_{21}\star y_{11}^{-1}=y_{22}\star y_{12}^{-1}.

Suppose
\Tr(y_{11})=\Tr(y_{12})

and
\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
.
Since
\Tr(y_{11})=\Tr(y_{12})
, we have
y_{12}=y_{11}+a
for some
a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
with
\Tr(a)=0
.
Let

It follows from (

\frac{y_{21}}{y_{11}}=\frac{y_{22}}{y_{12}}=\frac{y_{21}+b}{y_{11}+a}

On simplification,

\Tr(b)=\Tr\left(\frac{y_{21}}{y_{11}}a\right)=\frac{y_{21}}{y_{11}}\Tr\left(a\right)=0
, where the second last equal sign holds because
\frac{y_{21}}{y_{11}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

So
\Tr(y_{22})=\Tr(y_{21}+b)=\Tr(y_{21})

Conversely, suppose
\Tr(y_{11})=\Tr(y_{12})

and
\Tr(y_{21})=\Tr(y_{22})

Then for
i,j=1,2

y_{ij}=s_{i}+t_{ij}\epsilon

Recalling

Consider the case when

y_{12}\in\boxslash_{q^{2}}

Equation (

\frac{s_{2}+t_{21}\epsilon}{s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon}=\frac{s_{2}-t_{22}\epsilon}{s_{1}-t_{12}\epsilon}

[s_{1}(t_{21}+t_{22})-s_{2}(t_{11}+t_{12})]-(t_{12}t_{21}+t_{11}t_{22})\epsilon=0

So

Substituting these two equations one by one, we get

\begin{split}y_{21}\star y_{11}^{-1}=\frac{s_{2}+t_{21}\epsilon}{s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon}=&\frac{\frac{s_{1}(t_{21}+t_{22})}{t_{11}+t_{12}}+t_{21}\epsilon}{s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon}\\
=&\frac{s_{1}(t_{21}+t_{22})+(t_{11}t_{21}+t_{12}t_{21})\epsilon}{(t_{11}+t_{12})(s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon)}\\
=&\frac{s_{1}(t_{21}+t_{22})+(t_{11}t_{21}+t_{11}t_{22})\epsilon}{(t_{11}+t_{12})(s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon)}=\frac{(t_{21}+t_{22})(s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon)}{(t_{11}+t_{12})(s_{1}+t_{11}\epsilon)}=\frac{t_{21}+t_{22}}{t_{11}+t_{12}}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}.\end{split}

Similarly,

can be shown to be in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
in the other 3 combinations of square or non-square of

is in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
from (

).
∎

We can now prove our main result of this section.

Theorem 17
.

Let

be a parabolic unital of order
q
in
{\gothfamily N}
with
(0,1,0)\in U
and where

\{\phi(1,1,0,t\epsilon)\,:\,t\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}<\Aut(U)
.

contains an O’Nan configuration through
(0,1,0)
, then one of the lines
in the configuration must be
[0,1,z]
for some

Suppose
U
contains an O’Nan configuration through
(0,1,0)
and none of lines in the configuration are of the form
[0,1,z]
.
Let the coordinates of the points of the O’Nan configuration be as in

16

Since
(x_{i},y_{i1},1)
is a unital point for
i=1,2
and
\{\phi(1,1,0,t\epsilon)\,:\,t\in\lx@nobreakspace{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}<\Aut(U)

(x_{i},y_{i},1)
is a unital point if and only if
\Tr(y_{i})=\Tr(y_{i1})

So
\Tr(y_{11})=\Tr(y_{12})
and
\Tr(y_{21})=\Tr(y_{22})
.
By
16

\frac{x-x_{2}}{x-x_{1}}\in\lx@nobreakspace{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

Let
S
be the set of unital points on the lines in the subpencil from
\mathpzc{P}
to the
q
unital points

where
\Tr(y_{1})=\Tr(y_{11})

has

Let

By

7.
The sets
B(a,b)
and the lines of
{\gothfamily N}

For
a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, we define

B(a,b)=\{(x,y,1)\in{\gothfamily N}\,:\,\Nm(x)=a\land\Tr(y)=b\}.

With the canonical form in (
1
), the classical unital is given by

\{(0,1,0)\}\bigcup_{a\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}B(a,a).

This partition of unital points was studied by Taylor
[
17
]
and
Hui and Wong
[
12
]
for the extrinsic and intrinsic
characterizations, respectively, of a unitary polarity.

Lemma 18
.

The following statements hold in
{\gothfamily N}
.

(i)

Any line
[1,0,z]
intersects
B(a,b)
in
0
or
q

points.

(ii)

For any
a\neq 0
, any line
[0,1,z]
intersects
B(a,b)
in
0
or
q+1

points.

(iii)

Any line
[0,1,z]
intersects
B(0,b)
in
0
or
1

point.

(iv)

Any line
[u,1,z]
, with
u\neq 0
, intersects
B(a,b)
in
0,1
or
2

points.

Proof.

Firstly, all points on the line
[1,0,z]
are of the form
(-z,y,1)
with

y\in\mathds{N}
. Clearly
[1,0,z]
does not intersect
B(a,b)
if
a\neq\Nm(-z)
.
If
a=\Nm(-z)
, then there are
q
choices of
y\in\mathds{N}
for which

\Tr(y)=b
. A similar argument deals with the lines
[0,1,z]
.

Now consider a line
[u,1,z]
with
u\neq 0
. The set of points of the form

(x,y,1)
lying on
[u,1,z]
is given by

\{(x,-z-x\star u,1)\,:\,x\in\mathds{N}\}.

Suppose
(x,-z-x\star u,1)\in B(a,b)
. Then

\Nm(x)=a
and
\Tr(-z-x\star u)=b
.
We may rewrite the trace identity as
\Tr(u^{\prime}x)=b+\Tr(z)
, where

u^{\prime}
is
-u
or
-u^{q}
depending on whether
u\in\square_{q^{2}}
.
Set
x^{\prime}=u^{\prime}x
. Then we have the two equations

\Tr(x^{\prime})=b+\Tr(z)
and
\Nm(u^{\prime-1}x^{\prime})=\Nm(u^{\prime})^{-1}\Nm(x^{\prime})=a
.
Thus, to count the number of points in
[u,1,z]\cap B(a,b)
it is enough to
count the number of solutions
x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
of
\Nm(x)=c_{1}
and

\Tr(x)=c_{2}
for fixed

.
This is a well-known problem in finite fields. For want of a reference, we
now show that this system has 0, 1 or 2 solutions.
Using the representation
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q}(\epsilon)
, we
set
x=x_{1}+x_{2}\epsilon
, with
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and have the following system
of 2 equations in
x_{1},x_{2}
:

\displaystyle x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\epsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{1}

\displaystyle 2x_{1}

\displaystyle=c_{2}.

Clearly, we may solve for
x_{1}
uniquely, leaving a quadratic over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

in
x_{2}
, which has 2, 1, or 0 solutions if

c_{2}^{2}-4c_{1}
is in
\boxslash_{q}
, 0, or in
\square_{q}
, respectively.
∎

8.
The all-ones polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
– an interlude

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define
h_{k}\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
to be
h_{k}(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{k}
.
For
x\neq 1
, it is clear
h_{k}(x)=(x^{k+1}-1)/(x-1)
. However, determining
necessary and sufficient conditions for when
h_{k}
is a permutation polynomial
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
remained open for many years. It was only in 1994 that
Matthews resolved the problem.

Lemma 19

(
[
13
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

If
q=p^{e}
is odd,
p
a prime, then
h_{k}
is a permutation polynomial over

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
if and only if
k\equiv 1\bmod(p(q-1))
.

It turns out we shall also be interested in the behavior of
h_{k}
, but we will
need to know slightly more about the case where
h_{k}
is not a permutation
polynomial.
If a non-constant polynomial
f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X]
is known to not be a permutation
polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
\deg(f)\leq q-1
, then Wan
[
18
]

showed

(5)

|f({\mathbb{F}}_{q})|\leq\left\lfloor q-\frac{q-1}{\deg(f)}\right\rfloor.

This bound can be used to prove the following statement regarding the all-ones
polynomial.

Lemma 20
.

Set
E={\mathbb{F}}_{q}\setminus\{0,1\}
.
If
1<k<q-1
and
\gcd(k,q-1)=1
, then
there exists
c_{1},c_{2}\in E
such that

h_{k}(c_{1})=h_{k}(c_{2})
.

Proof.

Clearly
k\not\equiv 1\bmod(p(q-1))
, so that
h_{k}
is not a permutation
polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
by
19
. Since
k<q-1
, Wan’s
bound, given in
Equation

5
, shows

|h_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})|\leq q-2.

Suppose
x\neq 1
satisfies
h_{k}(x)=1
. As we have

h_{k}(x)=\frac{x^{k+1}-1}{x-1},

we see
h_{k}(x)=1
if and only if
x=0
or
x^{k}=1
. Since
\gcd(k,q-1)=1
, there
are no solutions to
x^{k}=1
with
x\neq 1
.
Thus, there are at most 2 pre-images of 1
under
h_{k}
, namely
x=0,1
. Thus,
1\in h_{k}({\mathbb{F}}_{q})
and
1\notin h_{k}(E)
,
which proves

|h_{k}(E)|\leq q-3.

Since
|E|=q-2
, we must have two elements
c_{1},c_{2}\in E
with the same
image under
h_{k}
, as claimed.
∎

Theorem 26

(0,1,0)

\mathcal{V}(j)

\mathcal{U}(j)

\mathcal{U}(j+(q-1)/2)

Then
\mathcal{U}(j+(q-1)/2)^{\phi(1,-1,0,0)}=\mathcal{U}(-1,j+(q-1)/2)

\mathpzc{l}=[1,1,u]
be a tangent to
\mathcal{U}(j)

Observe that

\Tr(y)=2b\Nm(x)^{j}

\mathcal{U}(j)
,
\mathcal{U}(j+(q-1)/2)

and
\mathcal{U}(-1,j+(q-1)/2)

(x,y,1)
on
\mathpzc{l}

(7)

\displaystyle\Tr(-x-u)

(8)

\displaystyle\Tr(-x-u)
