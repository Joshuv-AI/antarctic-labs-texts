Geometry of the space of phylogenetic trees with non-identical leaves
MARÍA A. VALDEZ-CABRERA, AMY D. WILLIS

Abstract
Phylogenetic trees summarize evolutionary relationships. The Billera-Holmes-Vogtmann (BHV) space for comparing phylogenetic trees has many elegant mathematical properties, but it does not encompass trees with differing leaf sets. To overcome this, we introduce Towering space: a complete metric space that extends BHV space to trees with non-identical leaf sets. Towering space is a structured collection of BHV spaces connected via pruning and regrafting operations. We study the geometry of paths in Towering space and present an algorithm for computing metric distances. By addressing a major limitation of BHV space, Towering space facilitates the analysis of modern phylogenetic datasets such as multi-domain gene trees.



1
Introduction

Phylogenetic trees represent evolutionary relationships between organisms. While a single phylogenetic tree can describe the shared evolution of large, complex eukaryotes, the genes of simple organisms may meaningfully differ in their evolutionary histories. This is in part because
genes are more commonly transferred, duplicated, gained, or lost in bacteria, archaea, and viruses than in eukaryotes
(
Jain et al. 1999
;
Koonin et al. 2001
)
. In these organisms, different genes have distinct evolutionary histories, and a collection of “gene trees” offers a more complete summary of phylogenetic relationships than a single tree.

Unfortunately, analyzing collections of phylogenetic trees remains challenging in practice.
A major advancement in mathematical phylogenetics was the introduction of the BHV tree space
(
Billera et al. 2001
)
, a metric space for phylogenetic trees that accounts for differences between trees with respect to both their topologies and their branch lengths. Geodesics in BHV space are smooth, with trees along the path deforming in a locally continuous manner. The polynomial-time algorithm of
Owen & Provan 2011
cemented the impact of BHV space, facilitating the development of BHV-based tools for estimating means, variances, principal paths and densities
(
Barden et al. 2013
;
Benner et al. 2014
;
Willis & Bell 2018
;
Brown & Owen 2020
;
Nye 2011
;
Nye et al. 2017
;
Weyenberg et al. 2014
)
, constructing confidence sets
(
Willis 2019
)
, and clustering
(
Gori et al. 2016
)
.
Unfortunately, BHV space suffers a major limitation that precludes its applicability to modern datasets: the BHV distance is only defined between trees with the same leaf set. If an organism is missing even one gene (such as due to gene loss or data incompleteness), either the gene tree or the organism must be discarded from a BHV-based analysis.
To address this shortcoming, we introduce a metric space for phylogenetic trees with non-identical leaf sets. Our proposal is closely connected to BHV space, and shares its property that distances account for both topological and edge length differences between trees.

Previous attempts to extend BHV space to accommodate trees with non-identical leaf sets considered combinatorial methods to transition between BHV tree spaces
(
Ren et al. 2017
)
, and constructed representative sets within the largest possible BHV space for all trees
(
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
)
. However, these approaches yield dissimilarity measures, not metrics.
Our approach yields a metric, and paths have a biological interpretation reflecting gene gain and loss, in addition to sequence divergence. Because our proposed paths traverse nested BHV spaces of differing dimension, we call it the
Towering space
.

While most phylogenetic tree distances are defined only for trees with the same leaf set, our proposal is by no means the first distance that accommodates non-identical leaf sets. The Robinson-Foulds distance — a well-known and simple distance that reflects only topological differences — has multiple extensions to accommodate non-identical leaf sets (reviewed in
Li et al. 2024
). However, to our knowledge, no (proper) metric has been defined for trees with non-identical leaf sets that accounts for differences in both topology and branch lengths. Our work fills this gap.

Our paper is structured as follows: Section
2
introduces notation and summarizes key elements of BHV space. Section
3
discusses the geometry of connections between BHV spaces via leaf prunings and regraftings. Section
4
formally defines Towering space and describes the geometry of geodesics, which may traverse multiple BHV spaces. We conclude the paper in Section
5
with a discussion of the implications of the results, applications to broader biological studies, and future work. Proofs of all results can be found in the Supplementary Material.

Background

2.1
Preliminaries

Phylogenetic trees are labeled tree-graphs that describe the shared evolutionary history of a set of organisms. The branching of the tree reflects the structure of the organisms’ evolutionary relationships, and branch lengths represent the distance between divergence events. Our proposal is equivalent for trees with and without a “root” organism. For generality, we proceed by considering unrooted trees.

Definition 2.1
.

A
phylogenetic tree

T
on a set of organisms
\mathcal{L}
is a connected edge-weighted acyclic graph whose
leaves
(nodes of degree one) are labeled by the elements of
\mathcal{L}
, and all interior nodes have degree greater or equal to 3. All edge-weights are non-negative values referred to as
lengths
. The branching pattern of the graph gives the
topology
of the tree.

Removing an edge from a tree splits its leaves into two groups, and such a split of leaves uniquely identifies the edge in the tree.
Throughout we use the notation
\mathcal{G}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{G}
, with
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{L}
, to represent the
split
on
\mathcal{L}
that separates the leaves in
\mathcal{G}
from
\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{G}
. Edges that induce the same split on topologically distinct trees are considered to be the same edge. Therefore, we use the terms edge and split interchangeably. Edges connecting to leaves are called the
external
edges, which correspond to splits
|\mathcal{G}|=1
or
|\mathcal{L}|-1
, while all others, with
2\leq|\mathcal{G}|\leq|\mathcal{L}|-2
, are the
internal
edges. The topology of a tree is fully defined by all its internal splits
(
Pachter & Sturmfels 2005
, Theorem 2.34)
. We use
\mathcal{S}(T)
to refer to the set of internal splits in
T
(equivalently, the topology of
T
),
while
\mathcal{H}(T)
refers to the set of external splits and
\mathcal{P}(T)=\mathcal{S}(T)\cup\mathcal{H}(T)
the entire set of edges. Only certain combinations of splits can be present on a tree. We say two splits are
compatible

(
Billera et al. 2001
, Section 3.2)
if they can simultaneously be present on a tree.

2.2
The Billera-Holmes-Vogtmann tree space

Billera et al. 2001
introduced the BHV tree space
(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}},d_{\text{BHV}})
, where
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}
denotes the set of all trees with leaf set
\mathcal{N}
, and
d_{\text{BHV}}:\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}\times\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a distance function. This metric space is Hadamard
(
Bacák 2014
)
, which guarantees continuous paths connecting any pair of trees, as well as a unique path achieving the minimum length, called the
geodesic
. The space we define in Section
4
is comprised of connected BHV spaces.

Consider all possible topologies for trees with leaf set
\mathcal{N}
of cardinality
n=|\mathcal{N}|
. Equivalently, consider all possible subsets of internal splits on
\mathcal{N}
that are pairwise compatible. For one such subset
S
, assign an order to its elements (
S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{m}\}
) in any consistent way; for example, a lexicographic order can be assigned to the leaves and the edges may inherit this order. A tree with the topology given by
S
may be represented by a
(n+m)-
dimensional non-negative vector, where the first
n
coordinates represent the lengths of the external edges and the last
m
coordinates represent the lengths of the internal branches in the given order (Figure
1
). The vectors representing possible trees with topology
S
form an orthant in
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
, called the
topology orthant
and denoted by
\mathcal{O}(S)

(
Owen & Provan 2011
, Section 2.1)
.

(a) A phylogenetic tree with leaf set
\mathcal{N}=\{A,B,C,D,E\}

s_{1}=\{A,B\}\big|\{C,D,E\}

(e_{A},e_{B},e_{C},e_{D},e_{E},s_{1},s_{2})

T_{2}
corresponds to the vector
(1.4,1.4,2.1,2.1,2,3,1)
.

A tree
T
with an internal edge
s\in\mathcal{S}(T)
of length zero represents the same graph as the tree without
s
among its internal edges, provided all other edges remain unchanged (Figure
2
). These two trees are therefore considered to be the same. Furthermore, since two additional orthants contain this tree, these three orthants are connected at this point. More generally, given a set of internal splits
S
with
m
elements, any topology orthant
\mathcal{O}(S^{\prime})
corresponding to a proper subset
S^{\prime}\subset S
of these internal edges can be viewed as a
face
of the larger topology orthant
\mathcal{O}(S)

(
Billera et al. 2001
, Section 2)
, corresponding to the trees with length zero in all edges in the difference
S\setminus S^{\prime}
.
Two topology orthants
\mathcal{O}(S_{1})
and
\mathcal{O}(S_{2})
such that
S:=S_{1}\cap S_{2}
are glued along the face corresponding to the topology orthant
\mathcal{O}(S)
(Figure
2
). The only topology orthants that are not faces of another topology orthant are those of maximum dimension
2n-3
, corresponding to the topologies of binary trees.

Remark 2.2
.

For any phylogenetic tree, there is a unique representation of its topology corresponding to the subset of internal edges
S
with positive lengths in the tree; i.e. dropping any internal edge of length zero from the set of internal edges. Henceforth, when referring to a tree
T
,
S(T)
contains strictly positive edges unless indicated otherwise. We use
\mathcal{O}(T)
to refer to the lowest-dimensional orthant containing
T
.

A
continuous
path can be traced between any two trees, where a path is a piecewise connected curve, each piece fully contained within a single orthant, and contiguous pieces connected through common faces of these orthants. Since each piece is a curve in a
\mathbb{R}^{n+m}
subspace, the lengths of the edges of the trees along these pieces change smoothly. Meanwhile, the transition from an orthant to the next requires some of these lengths to reduce to zero, at which point those zero-length edges are replaced by others. Thus, a path in BHV space represents how to gradually morph one tree into another by slowly modifying the edge lengths and topology. BHV space is a complete geodesic space with non-positive curvature
(
Billera et al. 2001
, Lemma 4.1)
, and thus the shortest path between any two trees (the
geodesic
) is unique. The
BHV distance
between the two trees
d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})
is the length of the geodesic that connects them, as this geodesic describes the most efficient method to transform
T_{1}
into
T_{2}
.

2.3
Geodesics in BHV space

The geometrical properties of BHV space enabled the construction of an algorithm to compute distances that is polynomial-time in the number of leaves
n

(
Owen & Provan 2011
)
. This algorithm results in a closed-form expression for the length of a geodesic. Here, we describe the elements of this expression, as they will be relevant when discussing distances in Towering space.

Definition 2.3
.

(
Owen 2011
, Definition 3.3)
For trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, consider the sets of internal splits
S_{1}=\mathcal{S}(T_{1})
,
S_{2}=\mathcal{S}(T_{2})
and the set of “common” internal splits
C\subset S_{1}\cup S_{2}
such that

S_{1}\cup S_{2}
. For a sequence of subsets
S_{1}=G_{0}\supset G_{1}\supset\ldots\supset G_{k-1}\supset G_{k}=C

such that
G_{i}\cup F_{i}
is a set of pairwise compatible splits, denote by
O_{i}=\mathcal{O}(G_{i}\cup F_{i})
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
. The sequence of orthants,

\mathcal{O}(T_{1})=O_{0}\rightarrow O_{1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow O_{k}=\mathcal{O}(T_{2}),

is a
path space
from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
.

Definition 2.4
.

(
Owen & Provan 2011
, Section 2.3)
Consider a path space

. When transitioning from

to

A_{i}=G_{i-1}\setminus G_{i}
the set of dropped edges and by
B_{i}=F_{i}\setminus F_{i-1}
the set of added edges. For ordered sets
\mathcal{A}=\left\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\right\}
and
\mathcal{B}=\left\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\right\}
, the pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
gives the
support
of the path space. Each
(A_{i},B_{i})
is also called a
support pair
.

A path space describes an efficient way to move from the topology orthant of
T_{1}
to the topology orthant of
T_{2}
through connected orthants. By
Billera et al. 2001

L^{2}
norm of each
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
in the support and the difference in length of the common edges, including the external edges
H=\mathcal{H}(T_{1})=\mathcal{H}(T_{2})

|p|_{T}
the length of the edge
p
in tree
T
and by
||P||_{T}=\sqrt{\sum_{p\in P}|p|_{T}^{2}}
the
L^{2}
norm of the lengths in
T
of all edges in the set of splits
P

d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(||A_{i}||_{T_{1}}+||B_{i}||_{T_{2}}\right)^{2}+\sum_{s\in K}\left(|s|_{T_{1}}-|s|_{T_{2}}\right)^{2}}

(1)

where
K=C\cup H
(see details in
Owen & Provan 2011
and
Miller et al. 2015
).

We conclude by noting that once
the best way to group the uncommon edges between the two trees through the support pairs is found, the distance is fully determined. The length of each edge in both trees contributes to this distance depending on whether they are a common or an uncommon edge. As we will see in Section
4.3
, the mechanisms that determine the distance in our metric space will follow a similar pattern.

3
Transitions between BHV spaces

Although BHV space offers a practical and intuitive metric for comparing trees, it is only defined when trees have exactly the same leaves. This is a significant limitation for many important applications, including the study of genes shared by distantly related organisms and the origins of complex, multicellular life.
Ren et al. 2017
addressed the restriction of BHV space to trees with identical leaves by introducing structured subsets in a BHV space formed by adding leaves to trees with fewer leaves and studied their combinatorial properties.
Building on this,
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
introduced Extension spaces, subspaces of BHV space that represent trees with fewer leaves, and proposed a dissimilarity measure between trees based on these spaces. More recently,
Valdez Cabrera & Willis 2025
developed a linear-optimization-based algorithm to compute shortest paths between Extension spaces, extending the dissimilarity concept to arbitrary tree pairs. However, this dissimilarity is not a distance, precluding the development of sophisticated analysis tools that rely on the triangle inequality, including (Fréchet) averages and principal paths.

In this section, we propose a new approach to connecting BHV spaces. Our approach overcomes limitations of prior work, including that it leads to a true distance (rather than a dissimilarity measure) with favorable geometric properties. This new metric also supports straightforward, BHV-like computations, avoiding gradient-based optimization procedures like those in
Valdez Cabrera & Willis 2025
.

3.1
Maps between topologies

Removing and adding leaves from a tree is a common operation in phylogenetics and combinatorics, and forms the basis for the subtree prune-and-regraft (SPR) distance between tree topologies
(
Hein et al. 1996
)
.
As in
Ren et al. 2017
;
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
, we consider the

tree dimensionality reduction map
(TDR) from
Zairis et al. 2016
, which removes leaves from a tree graph (Figure
3
, right). The removal results in edges connected by two-degree nodes that must be merged to give a valid tree. In the TDR map, the lengths of edges resulting from merging are defined to be the sum of the adjacent edges, or equivalently, the
L^{1}
norm of the lengths of these edges. Using a norm to define the new lengths results in favorable properties, including consistent lengths under sequential leaf removals (a result of commutativity and associativity of norms).

We employ the modified version of the TDR map given in
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
, which presents the TDR map as a function on edges rather than trees. As we will see later (Definition
3.8
), we then employ the
L^{2}
norm to assign lengths to merged edges, instead of the
L^{1}
norm. The advantageous geometrical properties of
L^{2}
merging will become apparent in Section
4
.

Definition 3.1
.

For
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
and a split
s=\mathcal{G}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{L}
, the
tree dimensionality reduction
(TDR) map is the projection function
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
that returns the split on
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
that separates all leaves of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}
from those not in
\mathcal{G}
; that is,
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{G}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{G}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If either
\mathcal{G}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\varnothing
or
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{G}=\varnothing
, the projection
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)
is not a valid edge. This happens when this split is not part of any path inside the tree connecting leaves in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. In these cases we write
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\varnothing
.

Our proposed leaf pruning operation differs slightly from the literature in that we restrict the set of trees on which this operation can be performed. Similar to dropping zero-length
internal
edges when transitioning between
topology orthants
in BHV space, only zero-length
external
edges can be dropped to transition between BHV spaces. The trees where leaf pruning may be performed are defined below.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a subset of leaves
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{L}
and
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
, the leaves in
\mathcal{M}
are
prunable
from
T
if each external edge to a leaf in
\mathcal{M}
is of length zero, and every internal edge of
T
maps to an internal split of the leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{M}
under the TDR map. That is, for every
s\in\mathcal{S}(T)
,
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\mathcal{L}_{1}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{1}
such that
|\mathcal{L}_{1}|,|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}_{1}|\geq 2
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is not prunable from
T
, there is a set of edges (external edges of positive length and internal edges) preventing
\mathcal{M}
from being prunable from
T
. We denote this set as
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
:

P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)=\left\{p\in\mathcal{P}(T)\mid\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(p)=\varnothing\text{ or }\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(p)=\{\ell\}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\{\ell\}\text{ for some leaf }\ell\right\}.

Of note, all edges that belong to
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
are of the form
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\cup\{\ell\}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\left(\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\cup\{\ell\}\right)
for some non-empty subset
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.

This definition ensures that when leaves are removed, all internal edges map to internal edges. This restriction aids in maintaining a strictly positive distance between
non-deformed trees
(trees with strictly positive edges) that are clearly distinct from each other. Later, we will use leaf prunings and regraftings as zero-distance operations. That is, two trees connected through these operations will be at distance zero from each other. If a leaf could be removed from or regrafted onto an external edge, then leaves could be just “swapped.” For example, without the additional restriction, all trees in Figure

Given two BHV spaces
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
, we say
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
is a
higher level
than
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
(and
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
is a
lower level
than
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
) whenever
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{L}
. As trees with prunable leaves offer junctions between BHV levels, they comprise an important subspace, which we now consider, followed by some convenient properties.

Definition 3.4
.

Consider a subset of leaves
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{L}
and BHV space
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
. The
\mathcal{M}
-trimmable subspace
, denoted by
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
, is the subset of trees in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
where
\mathcal{M}
is prunable.

Lemma 3.5
.

For any BHV space
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and a subset of leaves
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
is a convex and closed subspace.

Later on, efficient moves towards trees in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
will be useful for finding short paths between trees in different BHV levels. The following result concerns the projection of a tree onto
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
(i.e. the point in the subspace that is closest to the tree), and follows directly from
Sturm 2003
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For every tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
there exists a unique tree
T^{\perp\mathcal{M}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
such that
d_{\text{BHV}}(T,T^{\perp\mathcal{M}})=\inf_{t\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}}d_{\text{BHV}}(T,t)
. Moreover,
d_{\text{BHV}}^{2}(T,t)\geq d_{\text{BHV}}^{2}(T,T^{\perp\mathcal{M}})+d_{\text{BHV}}^{2}(T^{\perp\mathcal{M}},t)
for every
t\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
.

The projection of any tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
onto the
\mathcal{M}
-trimmable subspace can be found directly by focusing on the edges in
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
(see Figure
3
). We now make this connection explicit and show how to compute the distance between trees and their projections.

Lemma 3.7
.

For
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and a subset of leaves
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{L}
, consider the set of edges
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
. The projection of
T
onto
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
is the tree
T^{\perp\mathcal{M}}
with topology given by internal edges

\mathcal{S}(T^{\perp\mathcal{M}})=\left\{s\in\mathcal{S}(T)\mid s\notin P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)\right\},

with the lengths of these edges coinciding with the corresponding lengths in
T
; i.e.,
|s|_{T^{\perp\mathcal{M}}}=|s|_{T}
for every
s\in\mathcal{S}(T^{\perp\mathcal{M}})
. Similarly, all external edges not in
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
are of the same length as the external edges in
T
, but all external edges
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)
are of length zero. Furthermore,
d_{\text{BHV}}(T,T^{\perp\mathcal{M}})=||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T)||_{T}.

3.2
Pruning and regrafting

Having introduced a mapping between tree topologies in different BHV spaces and the subspaces where leaves may be removed, we can now formally define leaf pruning and regrafting. These operations connect trees in
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
such that BHV distances across different BHV levels remain consistent and have nice geometric properties. To this end, the last step in the pruning operation assigns new edge lengths by using the
L^{2}
norm of the lengths of corresponding edges in the original tree.

Definition 3.8
.

Consider
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
with a prunable subset of leaves
\mathcal{M}
, and denote
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{M}
. The
pruning of
\mathcal{M}
from
T
is the operation
\psi(T,\mathcal{M})=T^{\prime}
where
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
is such that:

•

The topology of
T^{\prime}
is given by the TDR map applied to the internal edges in
T
; i.e.,

\mathcal{S}(T^{\prime})=\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\left(\mathcal{S}(T)\right)=\left\{\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)\mid s\in\mathcal{S}(T)\right\}.

•

The length of each edge
p
of
T^{\prime}
is the
L^{2}
norm of the edges in the pre-image of
\mathcal{P}(T)
under the TDR map, i.e.,

|p|_{T^{\prime}}:=\sqrt{\sum_{q\in\mathcal{P}(T):\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(q)=p}|q|^{2}_{T}}.

Pruning provides a precise method for removing leaves from a tree, and returns a unique, unambiguous tree. In contrast, adding leaves to a tree can be performed in several ways. Therefore, regrafting is not a function, and instead we must consider the set of trees produced by all possible regraftings. These sets, which we call sprouting spaces, play an important role in constructing distances in Towering space. We refer to leaf prunings and leaf regraftings by the more general term
leaf operations
.

Definition 3.9
.

Given
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
and a superset of leaves
\mathcal{L}\supseteq\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
,
the sprouting space
of
T
in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
, denoted by
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T)
, is the set of all the trees in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
from which
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
are prunable and that map to
T
under the pruning of
\mathcal{M}
. That is,

\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T)=\left\{t\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}\mid\psi(t,\mathcal{M})=T\right\}
.

Sprouting spaces are closely related to the Extension spaces of
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
: while Extension spaces are subsets of trees in a maximal BHV space that map onto the same tree under the TDR map (as originally defined in
Zairis et al. 2016
), sprouting spaces are trees in a higher-dimensional BHV space that map onto the same tree after a pruning operation. Thus, we can consider sprouting spaces as a modified version of Extension spaces that permit traversal of BHV spaces of different dimension.

4
The Towering Space

We now construct a space on a union of BHV spaces, and to study its properties. We begin by defining equivalence classes, which span trees in different BHV levels, and use them to construct a distance metric, thereby extending the BHV metric space
(\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}},d_{\text{BHV}})
to trees with differing leaf sets. We then explore geometrical results arising from the properties of these equivalence classes. We conclude this section with a characterization of geodesics in Towering space, and a preliminary algorithm to compute geodesics.

4.1
Towering distances

For a set of
n
leaves
\mathcal{N}
, consider all possible subsets
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{N}
with cardinality
|\mathcal{L}|\geq 3
, and their corresponding BHV spaces
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
. Towering space contains all trees in these BHV spaces, that is, all trees in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}\in\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
, where
\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})=\left\{\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{N}\big||\mathcal{L}|\geq 3\right\}
.
By applying leaf prunings and regraftings, we can traverse BHV levels through trees that we consider equivalent.

Definition 4.1
.

Two trees
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
belong to the same equivalence class, denoted
T_{1}\simeq T_{2}
, if there is a finite number of prunings and regraftings that can be applied sequentially to
T_{1}
that result in
T_{2}
.

Armed with this equivalence relation, we now construct the metric of Towering space by refining a preliminary metric on
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}
to a strictly finite-valued quotient pseudometric
(
Bridson & Haefliger 1999
, I.5, Definition 5.19)
. The preliminary metric is as follows:

d^{*}(T_{1},T_{2})=\begin{cases}d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})&\text{ if }\mathcal{L}(T_{1})=\mathcal{L}(T_{2}),\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Definition 4.2
.

The
Towering distance
between trees
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}
is the quotient pseudometric of
d^{*}
on
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}/\simeq
, equivalently, the size of the smallest possible path between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, allowing for a finite number of distance-zero leaf operations between trees in an equivalence class:

d(T_{1},T_{2})=\inf\left\{\left.\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})\right|T_{1}\simeq t_{1},t^{\prime}_{i}\simeq t_{i+1}\forall i=1,\ldots,k-1,t^{\prime}_{k}\simeq T_{2}\right\},

(2)

with the infimum taken over all finite sequences
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k},\{t^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
.

By definition,
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is a pseudometric, upholding symmetry and the triangle inequality. Moreover, it correctly distinguishes non-deformed trees, meaning that if
T
is a tree with strictly positive edge lengths, then
d(T,T^{\prime})=0
if and only if
T\simeq T^{\prime}
. This in turn implies
T^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(T)
for some set of leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. We can further refine
d
into a proper metric function on
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}/\simeq
by defining
T_{1}\simeq T_{2}
whenever
d(T_{1},T_{2})=0
, thus guaranteeing that distinct trees are differentiated by the Towering space metric (see Supplementary Section 2).

4.2
Geometry of Towering geodesics

Our first result is a consequence of the reverse triangle inequality
||x||-||y||\leq||x-y||
and the
L^{2}
norm for combining edge lengths following leaf prunings. It implies that
shorter paths cannot be obtained in higher BHV levels that involve leaves not originally present in the trees. This is a highly desirable property, as the addition of arbitrary extraneous leaves should not improve distances between trees.

Theorem 4.3
.

Given two trees
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
for a subset of leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}
, then
d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})\leq d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\uparrow}_{1},T^{\uparrow}_{2})
for any trees
T^{\uparrow}_{1}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})
and
T^{\uparrow}_{2}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2}).

A consequence of this is that when a set of leaves is prunable from two trees, a shorter geodesic can be found in the lower BHV level resulting from this prune.

Corollary 4.4
.

Given trees
T_{1},T_{2}
in the same
\mathcal{M}
-trimmable space
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
, the length of the geodesic between them is lower-bounded by the length of the geodesic between their respective prunings of
\mathcal{M}
; that is,
d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})\geq d_{\text{BHV}}(\psi(T_{1},\mathcal{M}),\psi(T_{2},\mathcal{M}))
.

The construction of the short paths through low BHV levels will involve leaf operations performed at optimal locations. The following results describe the path length obtained through a single optimized prune-and-regraft operation (see Figure
5
(a)).

Theorem 4.5
.

Consider
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
with
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{M}
. Define
T^{\prime}_{1}=\psi(T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{M})
, the pruning of
\mathcal{M}
from the projection of
T_{1}
onto
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
. Then:

i.

The BHV distance between
T_{1}
and
t\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})
is lower-bounded by the
L^{2}
norm of the distances between
T_{1}
and the
\mathcal{M}
-trimmable subspace, and between
T_{2}
and
T^{\prime}_{1}
; that is,

d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},t)\geq\sqrt{||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}}(T_{1})||^{2}+d_{\text{BHV}}^{2}(T^{\prime}_{1},T_{2})}\text{ for every }t\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})

(3)

ii.

There exists at least one tree in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})
that achieves this bound.

While this result describes an efficient way to go from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
(through pruning a tree in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})
),
the following two results further establish that this path is in fact the most efficient overall when the only transitions between levels are prunings.

Lemma 4.6
.

Given
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{L}
, consider all paths from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
where the only transition between BHV spaces is the leaf pruning of
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
.
The lengths of these paths are lower bounded by the length of the direct path from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
by pruning
\mathcal{M}
at a tree in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})
, with length given by (
3
).

Lemma 4.7
.

Consider
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
where
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{L}
, and partition
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
into
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{1}\sqcup\mathcal{M}_{2}
. Consider all the paths from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
where only two transitions are performed: pruning
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, followed by pruning
\mathcal{M}_{2}
at some point later. All of these paths are at least as long as the direct path from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is pruned in a tree in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{2})
, with length given by (
3
).

Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
show that any path between two trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
in
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}
can be shortened by consolidating portions of the path involving only pruning leaves into a single pruning operation, allowing a direct jump to a tree at the lowest level of that portion. Similarly, sections of a path that exclusively regraft leaves can be combined into a single regrafting operation. This culminates in the following theorem relating to a prune-and-regraft operation.

Theorem 4.8
.

Consider two trees
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
, with
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{M}_{2}
for some subset
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Among the paths from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
where the leaves in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are all pruned, followed by regraftings of leaves in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, the shortest paths have length

\sqrt{\left[||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}}(T_{1})||+||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}}(T_{2})||\right]^{2}+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2})},

(4)

where
T^{\prime}_{i}
is the pruning of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
from
T_{i}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{i}}
, for
i=1,2
.

Therefore, the shortest path through a common lower level depends entirely on the
L^{2}
norm of the edges that prevent leaf sets from being prunable in each tree, along with the BHV distance between the two trees at the lower level resulting from pruning the relevant leaves. A consequence of Theorem
4.5
is that the shortest path between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
via one common lower level is comprised of only two BHV geodesics, one completely contained in the BHV space of
T_{1}
and the second completely contained in the BHV space of
T_{2}
(see Figure
5
). These path segments are connected by pruning all leaves in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
from a tree in the sprouting space
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}(X)
for some tree
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
and regrafting all leaves in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
onto the same tree to obtain a tree in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}(X)
. Moreover, the optimal
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
falls on the BHV geodesic from
T^{\prime}_{1}
to
T^{\prime}_{2}
, and it is the tree located at the position proportional to the distances from the original trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
to the respective prunable spaces. Specifically,
X
satisfies

d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},X)=\frac{||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}}(T_{1})||}{||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}}(T_{1})||+||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}}(T_{2})||}d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2}).

We provide a detailed proof and a method for finding
X
in Supplementary Section 1.

4.3
Characterizing geodesics

While Theorem
4.8
provides insight into the geometry of paths in Towering space, in practice, geodesics will often involve repeated prunings and regraftings. From Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
, we know that for each transition between BHV levels, a candidate geodesic path may pass through up to three trees within the same equivalence class — one prior to a leaf pruning, one following a leaf regrafting, and one in the lowest BHV level. We can therefore describe a path from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
by considering trees
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}
where transitions between BHV levels occur. For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, we denote by
t_{i}^{\downarrow}
the tree prior to a pruning, and by
t_{i}^{\uparrow}
the tree following the corresponding regrafting (see Figure
6
).

We now refine the structure of these candidate paths and show that at least one geodesic between any pair of trees consists of segments fully contained in the upper level
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}
. We refine further by applying Theorem
4.8
to restrict which trees allow optimal connections between the segments, thereby characterizing Towering space geodesics. We proceed first by demonstrating that BHV geodesics can be matched in length by geodesics in higher BHV levels. This result allows paths between any pair of trees trees to be lifted to an equally long path in the upper level
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Consider
T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
and a superset of their leaves
\mathcal{L}\supset\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. There exists
T_{1}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T^{\prime}_{1})
and
T_{2}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T^{\prime}_{2})
such that
d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})=d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2})
.

Thus, any path can be mapped to a path where all positive-length segments are contained in the uppermost BHV level
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}}
(Figure
6
). Therefore, in order to find a geodesic, it is sufficient to find the path with minimum length among all paths of this shape. This result is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.10
.

Given
T_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
and
T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
, consider the set of common leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}_{1}\cap\mathcal{L}_{2}
. A geodesic between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
passes through trees
T^{*}_{1}=t^{\uparrow}_{0}
,
t^{\downarrow}_{1}
,
t^{\uparrow}_{1}
, …,
t^{\downarrow}_{r}
,
t^{\uparrow}_{r}
,
t^{\downarrow}_{r+1}=T^{*}_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
, where

•

T^{*}_{1}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
and
T^{*}_{2}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
, and

•

For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
t^{\downarrow}_{i}\simeq t^{\uparrow}_{i}
with
t^{\downarrow}_{i},t^{\uparrow}_{i}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(t_{i})
for some tree
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{i}},

for a partition
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{1}\sqcup\ldots\mathcal{K}_{r}
.
The length of the geodesic is given by

\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\uparrow}_{i-1},t^{\downarrow}_{i}).

Furthermore, Theorem
4.8
constrains our search for
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}
. Specifically, fixing the trees
t_{i-1}^{\uparrow}
and
t_{i+1}^{\downarrow}
, we know
t_{i}
is on the BHV geodesic from
\psi(t_{i-1}^{\uparrow\perp\mathcal{K}_{i}},\mathcal{K}_{i})
to
\psi(t_{i+1}^{\downarrow\perp\mathcal{K}_{i}},\mathcal{K}_{i})
. This indicates that along the path from tree
t^{\uparrow}_{i-1}
to
t^{\downarrow}_{i+1}
, the edges outside
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(t^{\uparrow}_{i-1})
and
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(t^{\downarrow}_{i+1})
change at the same rate as their counterparts along the geodesic at the lower level. This implies the rate of change of edges remains the same along the path from one BHV space to the next, until that edge is (possibly) dropped. Thus the path is a constant-rate morph of one tree into the other.

From this, and the optimized expression shown in Theorem
4.8
, we conclude the length of a geodesic will directly depend on the optimal partition of common leaves to be pruned and regrafted through the edges preventing these leaves from being pruned, similar to the way
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{i}}(T_{i})
contribute to the expression in (
4
). More explicitly, if we fix the endpoint trees
T^{*}_{1}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
,
T^{*}_{2}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
and the partition
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{r}
, the length of the shortest path through the endpoint trees is

\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left[P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{1})+P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{2})\right]^{2}+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2})},

(5)

where
P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{1})
and
P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{2})
are modified versions of the sets of edges preventing
\mathcal{K}_{i}
from being prunable, and
T^{\prime}_{1}
and
T^{\prime}_{2}
are the underlying trees resulting from pruning all leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{K}
after reducing all edges in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{1})
and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{2})
to zero. Note
P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{1})
and
P^{*\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{2})
may differ slightly from
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{1})
and
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}_{i}}(T_{2})
depending on where other common leaves are regrafted.

4.4
An algorithm to compute geodesics

Based on the above results, we propose an iterative algorithm for computing geodesics in Towering space. The algorithm consists of two main steps: initializing candidate paths between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
; and optimizing these paths using Theorems
4.5
and
4.8
. We begin by detailing the optimization step.

Choose any partition
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{r}
. Initialize a candidate path with arbitrary initial trees
T^{*}_{1}(0)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
and
T^{*}_{2}(0)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
, and transition trees
t^{\downarrow}_{i}(0)
,
t_{i}(0)
,
t^{\uparrow}_{i}(0)
, where
t_{i}(0)\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{i}
and
t^{\downarrow}_{i}(0),t^{\uparrow}_{i}(0)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(t_{i}(0))
. The algorithm iteratively improves the path length by updating the transition trees. For iteration
k
:

•

If
k
is odd
(forward pass from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
):

–

Apply Theorem
4.5
to update the initial tree
t^{\uparrow}_{0}(k)=T^{*}_{1}(k)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
, selecting the closest tree to
t^{\downarrow}_{1}(k-1)
.

–

For each
j=1,\ldots,r
, apply Theorem
4.8
to optimize the path from
t^{\uparrow}_{j-1}(k)
to
t^{\downarrow}_{j+1}(k-1)
via pruning and regrafting
\mathcal{K}_{j}
, obtaining updated trees
t^{\downarrow}_{j}(k)
,
t_{j}(k)
, and
t^{\uparrow}_{j}(k)
.

•

If
k
is even
(backward pass from
T_{2}
to
T_{1}
):

–

Apply Theorem
4.5
to update the final tree
t^{\downarrow}_{r+1}(k)=T^{*}_{2}(k)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
, selecting the closest tree to
t^{\uparrow}_{r}(k-1)
.

–

For each
j=r,\dots,1
, apply Theorem
4.8
to optimize the path from
t^{\downarrow}_{j+1}(k)
to
t^{\uparrow}_{j-1}(k-1)
via pruning and regrafting
\mathcal{K}_{j}
, obtaining updated trees
t^{\downarrow}_{j}(k)
,
t_{j}(k)
, and
t^{\uparrow}_{j}(k)
.

•

Repeat until no further improvements in the path length are observed.

The above algorithm conditions on a fixed partition
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\mathcal{K}_{r}
. To ensure convergence to a geodesic (rather than a local mimimum), as a preliminary approach, we suggest constructing a candidate path for each possible partition. Choosing appropriate partitions and candidate paths can be done as follows:

•

For each possible topology in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
and
\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
, choose initial trees
T^{*}_{1}(0)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
and
T^{*}_{2}(0)\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
.

•

For each
j=1,\dots,r
:

–

Project
t^{\uparrow}_{j-1}(0)
onto the trimmable space
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathcal{N}}_{\mathcal{K}_{j}}
, and set
t_{j}(0)=\psi\left(t^{\uparrow\perp\mathcal{K}_{j}}_{j-1}(0),\mathcal{K}_{j}\right)
.

–

For each possible topology in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(t_{j}(0))
, choose a representative regrafting
t^{\uparrow}_{j}(0)
.

By the completeness of Towering space, Theorem
4.8
and Theorem
4.10
, this procedure is guaranteed to find at least one geodesic. While this algorithm is guaranteed to find a solution, it is not efficient. We leave further refinements — especially in selecting efficient partitions of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
to reduce the number of candidate paths — to future work.

5
Discussion

The primary contribution of this paper is the introduction of Towering space, a complete metric space that is defined over
all
possible phylogenetic trees on a collection of leaves, regardless of whether their leaf sets are identical. This framework enables comparisons between any pair of trees, laying a foundation for extending data-analytic tools to fully general phylogenetic comparisons.

Towering space shares many desirable geometric properties of BHV space, including path traversal via smooth topological and edge-length deformations. This was made possible through two subtle but important innovations: ensuring that internal edges map to internal edges under prunings (Definition
3.3
), and using the
L^{2}
norm for merging edge-lengths (Definition
3.8
). Edge-length merging via
L^{2}
has a particularly interesting interpretation.
In BHV space, the origin tree is identical for every topology, corresponding to the tree with all branches of zero length. This tree represents no structured evolutionary divergence among the organisms labeling its leaves. The BHV distance of a tree to the origin represents the cumulative divergence represented by all the branches in the tree, thus, trees further from the origin reflect evolutionary processes with more substantial changes than those closer to the origin. The use of the
L^{2}
norm to combine branch lengths in leaf prunings makes the trees connected by them equidistant from the origin (in their respective BHV space):
d_{BHV}(0,T)=d_{BHV}(0,\psi(T,\mathcal{M}))
for any
T\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{M}}
, where
0
denotes the origin tree. Thus, a leaf pruning produces a new tree with a similar topology and reflecting a comparable amount of overall evolutionary change among the organisms in the leaf set. This is an appealing biological interpretation of distances in Towering space.
Furthermore, the BHV distance between
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
can be directly expressed in terms of the distance of each tree to the origin tree and differences among their topologies
(
Billera et al. 2001
, Section 4.2)
by

d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{2})=||T_{1}||^{2}+||T_{2}||^{2}-2||T_{1}||||T_{2}||\cos\left[\min\{\pi,\measuredangle(T_{1},T_{2})\}\right],

(6)

where
||T||=d_{\text{BHV}}(0,T)
is the distance from a tree to the origin tree and
\measuredangle(T_{1},T_{2})
is the angle between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
via the origin tree. This angle is primarily a reflection of differences in their topologies.
Since all trees in a sprouting space
\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T)
for a given tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
with
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{L}
are equidistant from the origin tree, (
6
) implies that the closest tree in the sprouting space to any other tree
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
will mainly depend on the similarity of their topologies. This concurs with the intuition that, when comparing trees with different leaf sets, trees with similar topologies should lie close to each other — for example, because they share an evolutionary history, but with some organisms unobserved.

We also showed that the BHV distance between two trees after pruning gives a lower bound on the Towering distance between their pre-images (Theorem
4.3
). This monotonicity property ensures that our metric remains coherent across different BHV levels by guaranteeing that the distance between trees is only as close as the most favorable distance in upper levels, after accounting for the loss of information through pruning. In addition, in Towering space, edges preventing leaf prunings are grouped and their contribution to the path length is identical to that of uncommon edges through support pairs in BHV space. Once such edges are accounted for, the remaining path proceeds through a lower-dimensional BHV space, where the geodesic length behaves identically to BHV distances.

Our results characterizing geodesics led naturally to an algorithm for distance computation. While the proposed algorithm will provably find a geodesic, it is a preliminary algorithm, and future work is required before the distance can be adopted for large-scale use. The structural similarity with BHV geodesics suggests the potential for adapting
Owen & Provan 2011
’s algorithm to efficiently classify edges into those preventing prunings and those that belong to the underlying lower trees. This would give the efficient partition of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. We see this as the most promising direction to pursuing a faster algorithm. We leave further algorithmic development to future work.

Another key feature of Towering space is that it is a
complete length space
, meaning all tree pairs are connected through continuous paths. However, in contrast to BHV space,
Towering space does not guarantee unique geodesic paths. We conjecture that geodesics will be unique almost surely (e.g., for trees drawn from an absolutely continuous measure with full support on
\mathcal{T}^{\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{N})}
), and believe that the completeness and path-connectedness of the space will facilitate the development of analytical tools in Towering space (such as Fréchet mean and principal path construction).

From a computational standpoint, Towering space also addresses limitations of BHV space generalizations that embedded trees in subspaces of the highest-dimension BHV space
(
Ren et al. 2017
;
Grindstaff & Owen 2019
)
. While BHV distances can be minimized between pairs of these subspaces, the resulting solution is a dissimilarity measure, not a true metric, and the fastest algorithms for computing the dissimilarity are computationally expensive
(
Valdez Cabrera & Willis 2025
)
. In contrast, Towering distance is a true metric, and avoids high-dimensional embeddings by transitioning between BHV spaces of different dimensions through pruning and regrafting operations. While no computational implementation yet exists for Towering distances, we conjecture that an implementation of even the preliminary algorithm given in Section
4.4
will be faster than the current fastest algorithms for computing distances between Extension spaces. Both approaches require outer loops over all topologies in
\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{1})
and
\Lambda^{\mathcal{N}}(T_{2})
, but Towering distances do not require computing gradients, and each local improvement is done through a closed-form operation (Theorem
4.8
) instead of an approximate solution. This dramatically accelerates computation.

Finally, from a biological standpoint, Towering space geodesics provide a natural interpretation for gene gain and loss: pruning corresponds to the loss of a gene by a leaf organism (i.e., genome streamlining), while regrafting corresponds to the gain of of gene (e.g., via horizontal gene transfer). By formalizing these transformations within a metric structure, we advance tools to study evolution in organisms that frequently experience these events.
Trees with non-identical leaf sets pose a particular challenge for reconstructing ancient evolutionary relationships, and we anticipate that the greatest potential benefit of Towering space is in the study of the emergence of complex life from its single-celled origins.

\left(\left.\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}^{-1}(A_{i})\right|_{\mathcal{S}(T_{1}^{\perp})},\left.\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}^{-1}(B_{i})\right|_{\mathcal{S}(T^{*})}\right)
to the length of the geodesic from

to

is the same as the contribution of the edges

to the length of the geodesic from

to

. Thus, the internal edges in

’s contribution to the geodesic from

Proof 6.6
(of Lemma

to

\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},X)}
, where

to
T_{2}
completely contained in

is simply the geodesic with length

. On the other hand, again by Theorem
4.5

to
T_{2}

\mathcal{M}

\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},T_{2})}

By the triangle inequality,
d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},X)+d_{\text{BHV}}(X,T_{2})\geq d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T_{2})

\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},X)}\geq d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},X),

d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},X)+2d_{\text{BHV}}(X,T_{2})\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},X)}+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(X,T_{2})\geq d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T_{2}).

Proof 6.7

Proof 6.8
(of Theorem
4.8
).

Any path of this form will pass through a tree
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
. Lemmas
4.6
and
4.7
imply that the shortest paths from

to
X
(for

) that exclusively perform leaf prunings are given by performing the pruning of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
at trees in the sprouting space

\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{i},T_{i}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{i}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{i}^{\prime},X)}
. Thus, the shortest paths from
T_{1}
to
T_{2}
, going strictly to lower levels, passing through

and then going strictly through higher levels to
T_{2}
are of length

\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},T_{1}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{1}})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},X)}+\sqrt{d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(X,T_{2}^{\prime})+d^{2}_{\text{BHV}}(T_{2}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{2}},T_{2})}.

The values

and

are independent from the choice of
X\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
. For simplicity, denote
\rho_{i}=d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{i},T_{i}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{i}})
. Since

are all in the same BHV space,

. Let
y_{1}=d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1}^{\prime},X)
,
y_{2}=d_{\text{BHV}}(X,T_{2}^{\prime})
and

that produces the shortest path between

and

\sqrt{\rho_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}}+\sqrt{\rho_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}}
subject to

. Consider a geometrical approach by considering points
\mathbf{a}=(z_{1},\rho_{1})
and
\mathbf{b}=(z_{2},-\rho_{2})
in

, where

. Consider the piecewise linear path from

to
\mathbf{b}
crossing the x-axis at a point

. But the shortest of these paths is the direct line from

to
\mathbf{b}

\left(\frac{\rho_{1}z_{2}+\rho_{2}z_{1}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}},0\right)

for

.
Equivalently, the value

. This, in turn, is reached on the tree

on the geodesic from

to

, at a distance
\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}d_{\text{BHV}}(T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2})
from

.

7
Supplementary Material: Proof that the Towering distance is a metric

We prove that
non-deformed trees
are correctly differentiated by the distance function in the Towering space. That is, if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are distinct trees with all edges of strictly positive length, then
d(T_{1},T_{2})>0
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Consider a tree
T\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathcal{L}}
and take
T^{\prime}=\psi(T,\mathcal{M})
. Taking a proper subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
of the leaves
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{M}
of
T^{\prime}
, then
||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})||^{2}=||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)||^{2}
and
\psi(T^{\perp\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M})=\psi(T^{{}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{K}},\mathcal{K})
.

Proof 7.2
.

By the definition of
T^{\prime}
, we know that
\mathcal{S}(T^{\prime})=\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{S}(T))=\{\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)|s\in\mathcal{S}(T)\}
. We start by showing that there is a natural partition between the edges that prevent
\mathcal{K}
from being prunable from
T
and
T^{\prime}
, and the edges that do not prevent these leaves from being prunable. That is, we prove
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T))=P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})
and
\Psi^{-1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime}))\cap\mathcal{P}(T)=P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)
.

Consider a split
s=\mathcal{G}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{G}\in P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)
. There are two cases:

1.

s=\{\ell\}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\ell\}
for
\ell\in\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}
and with positive length. Since
T\in\mathcal{Z}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{M}}
, all external edges to leaves in
\mathcal{M}
are of size zero. This implies
\ell\in\mathcal{K}
. Thus,
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\{\ell\}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\{\ell\}
and it belongs to
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})
.

2.

s
is an internal edge and we can assume w.l.o.g.
|\mathcal{G}\setminus\left(\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}\right)|<2
. But since
\mathcal{S}(T^{\prime})=\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{S}(T))
, then we know
s^{\prime}=\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\left(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\right)\in\mathcal{S}(T^{\prime})
. We note that
\left(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\right)\setminus\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{G}\setminus\left(\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}\right)
, and so conclude
s^{\prime}\in P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})
.

Similarly, take
s=\mathcal{G}\big|\mathcal{L}\setminus\mathcal{G}
such that
s^{\prime}=\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(s)=\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\big|\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\left(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\right)\in P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})
. Assume w.l.o.g that
|\left(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{M}\right)\setminus\mathcal{K}|<2
, which implies
s\in P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)
.

Since we have this correspondence between the sets
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)
and
P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})
through the TDR map, we conclude
||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})||^{2}=||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)||^{2}
.

Moreover, since
\mathcal{S}(T^{\perp\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}})=\left\{s\in\mathcal{S}(T)\mid s\notin P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}(T)\right\}
and
\mathcal{S}(T^{{}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{K}})=\left\{s\in\mathcal{S}(T^{\prime})\mid s\notin P^{\downarrow\mathcal{K}}(T^{\prime})\right\}
, all splits in
T
mapping to edges in
T^{{}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{K}}
under the TDR map
\Psi_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}
are still preserved when projecting onto
\mathcal{Z}^{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}
to give the tree
T^{\perp\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}}
with identical edge lengths to
T^{\prime}
. This implies
\psi(T^{\perp\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{M})=T^{{}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{K}}
, and in turn this implies
\psi(T^{\perp\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M})=\psi(T^{{}^{\prime}\perp\mathcal{K}},\mathcal{K})
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Consider a tree
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
with all external edges strictly positive. If
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L^{\prime}}
satisfies
T^{\prime}\simeq T
, then
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}
is prunable from
T^{\prime}
, and
\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{K})=T
.

Proof 7.4
.

Consider a sequence of trees connecting
T
and
T^{\prime}
,
T\simeq t_{1}\simeq\ldots\simeq t_{k}\simeq T^{\prime}
where each tree in the sequence is the result of a leaf operation in the previous tree, meaning
t_{i}=\psi(t_{i-1},\mathcal{M}_{i})
or
t_{i-1}=\psi(t_{i},\mathcal{M}_{i})
for some set of leaves
\mathcal{M}_{i}
for all
i
. Note that leaf operations cannot modify the length of non-zero external edges, which implies that for each tree in the sequence the external edges to the leaves in
\mathcal{L}
are of positive length (they are same length as in
T
) and are not prunable. Thus,
\mathcal{L}
is part of the leaves for each tree in the sequence, including
T^{\prime}
. We proceed by induction on
k
to prove all other leaves are prunable and that their leaf prune returns
T
.

For
k=1
, we have either
T^{\prime}=\psi(T,\mathcal{M}_{1})
or
T=\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{1})
. Since all external edges in
T
are strictly positive, none of the leaves in
\mathcal{L}
are prunable from
T
. Thus
T^{\prime}=\psi(T,\mathcal{M}_{1})
is not possible and we conclude
\mathcal{M}_{1}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}
is prunable from
T^{\prime}
and
T=\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{1})
.

Now, assume the lemma is true for any tree connected through a sequence of size
k
. Thus, for
t_{k}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{k}}
,
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{L}_{k}
,
\mathcal{K}_{k}=\mathcal{L}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{L}
is prunable from
t_{k}
and
T=\psi(t_{k},\mathcal{K}_{k})
. There are two options:

1.

If
T^{\prime}=\psi(t_{k},\mathcal{M}_{k})
, since none of the leaves in
\mathcal{L}
are prunable from
t_{k}
, we know
\mathcal{M}_{k}\subset\mathcal{L}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{L}
and this implies
\mathcal{K}_{k}=\mathcal{K}\cup\mathcal{M}_{k}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{K}_{k}
is prunable from
t_{k}
,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{k}
is prunable from
T^{\prime}
, and by Lemma
7.1
we may conclude
\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{K})=\psi(t_{k},\mathcal{K}_{k})=T
;

2.

If
t_{k}=\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{k})
then
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}_{k}\cup\mathcal{M}_{k}
, which implies
\mathcal{K}
is prunable from
T^{\prime}
and
\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{K})=\psi(\psi(T^{\prime},\mathcal{M}_{k}),\mathcal{K}_{k})=\psi(t_{k},\mathcal{K}_{k})=T
.

In other words,
T
can serve as a “representative” of its equivalence class in the towering space, and we can say this equivalence class
[T]
is the union of all sprouting spaces of
T
in BHV level above
\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
; i.e.
[T]=\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}}\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(T)
. For the purpose of the following results, we write
d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},[T_{2}])=\inf_{t\simeq T_{2}}d_{\text{BHV}}(T_{1},t)
. Note this value will be infinite if there is no element of
[T_{2}]
in the same BHV space as
T_{1}
. We use the notation
d_{\text{BHV}}([T_{1}],[T_{2}])=\inf_{t_{1}\simeq T_{1},t_{2}\simeq T_{2}}d_{BHV}(t_{1},t_{2})
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Fix
T\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}}
with strictly positive external edge lengths and any trees satisfying
X_{1}\simeq X_{2}
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
d_{\text{BHV}}([X_{1}],[T])<\varepsilon
implies
d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{1},[T])=d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{2},[T])
.

Proof 7.6
.

Let
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2}\min\{|s|_{T}:s\text{ is a external edge of $T$}\}
. Since the lengths of non-zero external edges in trees cannot be modified by leaf operations, any tree
T^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}(T)
has the same external edge lengths as
T
. For any such tree,
d_{\text{BHV}}(X^{\prime},T^{\prime})<\varepsilon
would imply that
X^{\prime}
has strictly positive lengths for external edges of leaves in
\mathcal{L}
as well. This also implies that any tree
X\simeq X^{\prime}
would also have these leaves, and with strictly positive lengths.

Consider
X_{1}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{1}}
and
X_{2}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
with
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
. From Lemma
7.3
we have
d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{i},[T])=d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{i},\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}_{i}}(T))
, which by Theorem
4.5
is

d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{i},[T])=\sqrt{||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{i}}(X_{i})||^{2}+d_{\text{BHV}}^{2}(X^{\prime}_{i},T)},

where
\mathcal{M}_{i}=\mathcal{L}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{L}
and
X^{\prime}_{i}=\psi(X_{i}^{\perp\mathcal{M}_{i}},\mathcal{M}_{i})
. Since
X_{1}\simeq X_{2}
, there is a sequence of trees
X_{1}=t_{0}\simeq t_{1}\simeq\ldots\simeq t_{k-1}\simeq t_{k}=X_{2}
, where each pair
t_{i-1}
and
t_{i}
is connected by a direct leaf operation such that either
t_{i-1}=\psi(t_{i},\mathcal{K}_{i})
or
t_{i}=\psi(t_{i-1},\mathcal{K}_{i})
for some set of leaves
\mathcal{K}_{i}
.

Since all trees in the sequence are part of the equivalence class of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, all have
\mathcal{L}
in their leaf sets, and these leaves have strictly positive external lengths. For each tree in the sequence, say
t_{i}\in\mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i}}
and take
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{L}
. For any pair
t_{i-1}
and
t_{i}
assume w.l.o.g
t_{i-1}=\psi(t_{i},\mathcal{K}_{i})
. In this case
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{K}_{i}\cup\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i-1}
and so by Lemma
7.1
we have
||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i-1}}(t_{i-1})||^{2}=||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i}}(t_{i})||^{2}
and
\psi(t^{\perp\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i-1}}_{i-1},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i-1})=\psi(t^{\perp\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i}}_{i},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}_{i})
.

Since this is true for each pair along the sequence between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, we have that
||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{1}}(X_{1})||^{2}=||P^{\downarrow\mathcal{M}_{2}}(X_{2})||^{2}
and
X^{\prime}_{1}=X^{\prime}_{2}
, which implies
d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{1},[T])=d_{\text{BHV}}(X_{2},[T])
.

Theorem 7.7
.

Given a tree
T_{1}
with all external edges of strictly positive length and
T_{2}
such that
d(T_{1},T_{2})=0
,
T_{1}\simeq T_{2}
.

Proof 7.8
.

Let
\varepsilon_{1}>0
be as in Lemma
7.5
for
T_{1}
. Since
d(T_{1},T_{2})=0
, we can find a finite sequence
t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1},t_{2},t^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,t_{k},t^{\prime}_{k}
with
T_{1}\simeq t_{1},t^{\prime}_{i}\simeq t_{i+1}\forall i=1,\ldots,k-1,t^{\prime}_{k}\simeq T_{2}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})<\varepsilon_{1}.

We upper-bound
d_{\text{BHV}}([T_{1}],[T_{2}])
by
\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})
by proving
d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{k},[T_{1}])\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})
. We proceed by induction.

Since
t_{1}\in[T_{1}]
, we readily have
d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{1},[T_{1}])\leq d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1})
. Now assume that for some value
j<k
it is true
d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{j},[T_{1}])\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i})<\varepsilon_{1}
. By Lemma
7.5
we have
d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{j+1},[T_{1}])=d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{j},[T_{1}])
, and so

\begin{split}d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{j+1},[T_{1}])&\leq d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{j+1},t^{\prime}_{j+1})+d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{j+1},[T_{1}])\\
&=d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{j+1},t^{\prime}_{j+1})+d_{\text{BHV}}(t^{\prime}_{j},[T_{1}])\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i}).\end{split}

From this we have

d_{\text{BHV}}([T_{1}],[T_{2}])\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\text{BHV}}(t_{i},t^{\prime}_{i}).

Since the sum can be made arbitrarily small, we have
d_{\text{BHV}}([T_{1}],[T_{2}])=0
. Since
[T_{1}]=\bigcup_{\mathcal{L}}\Lambda^{\mathcal{L}}(T_{1})
, which is a closed set, this implies
T_{2}\simeq T_{1}
.
