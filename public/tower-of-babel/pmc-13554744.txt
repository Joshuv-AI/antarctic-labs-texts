The 2 Faces of Takotsubo Syndrome
Authors: Zhen'ge Chang, Kefei Dou, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) has long been viewed as a close mimic of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Patients often present with chest pain, ischemic electrocardiographic changes, and elevated cardiac biomarkers. These features make it difficult to distinguish TTS from ACS at the time of presentation.1,2 However, this traditional view may overlook an important clinical reality. Many patients with TTS do not present with a typical ACS pattern. Instead, they may have dyspnea, hemodynamic instability, or cardiac abnormalities that are detected incidentally. This is especially common after physical stressors such as sepsis, surgery, or acute neurologic events.1,3 These non-ACS presentations have received far less investigative attention, and their prognostic significance has remained uncertain.

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Li et al4 address this critical knowledge gap through an analysis of the TAKOC (Takotsubo Syndrome of Chinese Patients) Registry, a multicenter study that enrolled 421 patients from 18 tertiary hospitals across China. The findings hold clear clinical significance. Patients with a non-ACS presentation had a nearly 2.3-fold higher risk of 30-day mortality and a 2.4-fold higher risk of long-term mortality compared with those presenting with an ACS-like phenotype, despite lower troponin levels in the non-ACS group. These results challenge the perception that TTS is a generally benign condition and indicate that risk assessment should incorporate the mode of presentation. The study delineates a distinct clinical profile of patients with non-ACS TTS. Compared with their ACS-like counterparts, these patients were more likely to be male, to have a physical rather than an emotional trigger, to present with dyspnea rather than chest pain, and to have a lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) at admission. They also exhibited markers of profound systemic illness, including higher heart rates, lower mean arterial pressure, markedly elevated C-reactive protein levels, and higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios. Collectively, the non-ACS phenotype appears to represent a high-risk subgroup characterized by systemic inflammation, hemodynamic compromise, and more severe myocardial dysfunction.

These findings are consistent with and extend observations from several large international registries. The InterTAK (International Takotsubo) registry showed that physical triggers are associated with significantly worse short- and long-term outcomes compared with emotional triggers.5 The GEIST (German-Italian-Spanish Takotsubo) registry similarly found that physical triggers are associated with higher rates of in-hospital complications and mortality.6 It shows that the mode of clinical presentation itself carries prognostic value, beyond trigger type alone. Indeed, the non-ACS phenotype remained significantly associated with mortality after adjustment for trigger type, LVEF, and natriuretic peptide levels, suggesting that it captures risk information not fully explained by other clinical variables.

Several mechanisms may explain the higher risk in patients with non-ACS presentations. First, the absence of classic ischemic symptoms may delay recognition of TTS, postponing definitive diagnosis and appropriate supportive care.7 Second, the non-ACS phenotype appears to cluster with physical triggers, such as sepsis, intracranial hemorrhage, or major surgery, that independently carry high mortality risk.7,8 Third, the lower LVEF and higher prevalence of cardiogenic shock in the non-ACS group suggest a more severe form of catecholamine-mediated myocardial stunning.9 The excess catecholamine levels documented in TTS, 3-fold higher than those in myocardial infarction, may provoke not only cardiac dysfunction but also systemic effects, including platelet activation, endothelial injury, and proinflammatory cascades.1,9,10 In patients with non-ACS presentations, this adrenergic response may be more intense and may not be recognized as a cardiac syndrome early enough.

A key finding of the TAKOC analysis is the mismatch between illness severity and treatment intensity. Patients with non-ACS presentations required significantly more in-hospital intensive support, including vasoactive drugs (51.4% vs 15.9%), mechanical circulatory support (13.6% vs 3.0%), invasive ventilation, and cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Yet, they were less likely to receive cardiovascular medications, including antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors. This treatment gap is understandable. Patients with chest pain and ST-segment elevation are usually treated through an ACS pathway. They often receive antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockers, and RAAS inhibition early in care. In contrast, patients who present with dyspnea, shock, or altered mental status during a noncardiac illness may not trigger the same response. Mild troponin elevation and less obvious electrocardiographic changes may further delay the diagnosis of TTS. As a result, high-risk patients may be less likely to receive potentially beneficial therapies.

The authors report that in-hospital use of antiplatelet agents and beta-blockers was independently associated with lower 30-day mortality among patients without ACS. These findings should be interpreted with caution. The study was observational, and treatment selection may have been influenced by patient stability and survival status.11 Nonetheless, the findings are biologically plausible. Beta-blockers may reduce catecholamine-related myocardial injury.12,13 Antiplatelet therapy may counteract the platelet activation and prothrombotic state induced by catecholamine surges.10 The benefit of RAAS inhibitors, by contrast, was confined to the ACS presentation group in this study, suggesting that different pathophysiological drivers or timing of drug initiation may influence therapeutic response.14,15

The TAKOC Registry is the largest cohort of Chinese patients with TTS to date, and its findings highlight both similarities and differences with Western populations.16 However, the high proportion of patients classified as non-ACS (61.0%) is notable and may reflect referral patterns in the Chinese health care system, where only the most severely ill patients reach tertiary centers. Prior studies have shown higher in-hospital mortality in Japanese TTS cohorts than in Western cohorts, attributable in part to a greater prevalence of physical triggers.17 The TAKOC data suggest that this pattern may extend to Chinese populations, supporting the need for ethnicity-specific risk assessment tools and management strategies.

This study carries several immediate implications for clinical practice. First, clinicians should consider TTS even when the presentation does not resemble ACS. Dyspnea, shock, or new left ventricular dysfunction after a physical stressor should raise suspicion for TTS, even without chest pain or marked troponin elevation. Second, non-ACS presentations should not be viewed as a mild form of TTS. In fact, these patients may have the highest risk. Third, cardiovascular medications may deserve careful consideration in patients who are hemodynamically stable and can tolerate these drugs, regardless of presentation type. However, treatment decisions should be individualized until stronger evidence is available.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. The retrospective observational design precludes causal inferences. The moderate sample size limited the ability to perform detailed subgroup analyses. Time-to-event data for nonfatal outcomes were unavailable, which precluded competing risk analysis. Data on long-term medication adherence and myocardial recovery were also lacking. Furthermore, the generalizability of these findings to Western populations may be limited, although the results are broadly consistent with international registry data.

The TAKOC analysis reinforces an evolving understanding of TTS: It is not a single disease but a syndrome that includes distinct clinical phenotypes with divergent trajectories.18 The ACS-like phenotype is often seen in women, is commonly linked to emotional triggers, and may have a more favorable course. In contrast, the non-ACS phenotype—more often male, physically triggered, with global or atypical wall motion patterns and reduced LVEF—carries a substantially higher risk of adverse outcomes. Future research should move beyond a simple ACS versus non-ACS classification. Risk models should integrate presentation type, trigger type, hemodynamic status, ventricular function, and biomarkers of inflammation and myocardial injury.

The treatment gap seen in this study also highlights the need for randomized clinical trials in TTS. Future studies should clarify whether beta-blockade should be used broadly or targeted to patients with the high-risk non-ACS phenotype, determine the net benefit and appropriate duration of antiplatelet therapy, and identify whether RAAS inhibitors are most effective in patients with persistent left ventricular dysfunction. These issues cannot be resolved by observational data alone, and prospective trials are needed to establish an evidence base for this complex condition. Until such data are available, the clinical message is clear: Presentation matters, and patients with TTS who do not present like typical ACS cases may require the closest attention.
