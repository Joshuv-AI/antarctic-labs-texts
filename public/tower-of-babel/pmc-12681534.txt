5-Methoxytryptophan attenuates hypobaric hypoxia induced acute lung injury by alleviating lipid peroxidation via targeting peroxiredoxin 6
Authors: Xiaoyue Lai, Xiaoqin Wan, Xinyu Bao, Qingyuan Yang, Wei Zhang, Yan Tan, Xiaoshi Cai, Ziyang Wang, Yujie Li, Chun Liu, Zhongfang Deng, Pan Zheng, Dingyuan Tian, Ming Chen, Hongjun Yin, Gang Xu, Qi Huang, Zhihui Zhang, Hongmei Xia, Fang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypobaric hypoxia (HH)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) is a growing concern. Tryptophan metabolite 5-Methoxytryptophan (5-MTP) is a recently identified endothelial protective factor. However, the role of 5-MTP in HH-induced ALI remains unclear.Methods and resultsForty healthy male human participants were enrolled in Chongqing (200 m) to travel to Golmud (4260 m). We found the decreased plasma 5-MTP level was correlated with HH-induced blood oxygen desaturation and acute mountain sickness. ELISA showed 5-MTP levels decreased in the plasma and lungs of ALI mice (male 6–8 weeks C57BL/6J), and also in the supernatant of mouse pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs) after hypoxia. Immunoblotting showed the synthetase Asmt was downregulated in mice lungs and PMVECs after hypoxia, which could be restored by si-Hif1α or NF-κB inhibitor. ChIP assays confirmed that hypoxia enhanced NF-κB p50 binding in Asmt promotor. Application of 5-MTP protected the hyperpermeability in ALI. The transmission electron microscope of mice lungs showed 5-MTP alleviated the endothelial barrier disruption and lipoperoxidation injury. Using limited proteolysis combined with mass spectrometry, we revealed Prdx6-Ser32 as the direct target of 5-MTP, consistent with molecular docking. Molecular dynamics simulation, cellular thermal shift assays and microscale thermophoresis confirmed the interaction. 5-MTP prevented the hypoxia-induced decline of Prdx6 and lipoperoxidation. By MG132 and BafA1, we found hypoxia promoted the lysosomal degradation of Prdx6. Immunoblotting and immunofluorescence showed 5-MTP could reduce hypoxia-induced increased lysosomal localization of Prdx6. Prdx6-S32A impaired the protective effects of 5-MTP on PMVECs, and also the lysosomal localization of Prdx6. Adeno-associated viruses were injected intratracheally to confirm Prdx6-S32A also impaired the therapeutic effects of 5-MTP on ALI mice.Conclusion5-MTP is a predictive biomarker of hypoxic maladaptation. The restoration of 5-MTP attenuated ALI by inhibiting lipoperoxidation and protecting endothelial barrier via directly interaction with Ser32 site of Prdx6 to prevent its lysosomal degradation.

Introduction

As altitude increases, the pressure of oxygen in the atmosphere decreases, known as hypobaric hypoxia (HH), which is a major health threat at high altitude [1]. Maladaptation to HH may lead to blood oxygen desaturation and acute mountain sickness (AMS), which consists of a series of clinic syndromes that occur after arrival in areas above 2500 m, including headache, gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue, and dizziness [2]. Severe cases may even develop into life-threatening acute lung injury (ALI) [3]. The treatment options for ALI are still limited [4]. Therefore, it is urgent to explore new, safe and effective therapeutic targets.

Although the exact mechanisms of ALI are not yet fully understood, it is believed to be related to hypoxia-induced inflammatory responses and oxidative injury [3,5,6], lead to dysfunction in pulmonary alveolar capillary barrier (PACB) [7], resulting in the pulmonary interstitial thickening and leakage of fluid into the alveolar cavities [8].

Tryptophan metabolite 5-Methoxytryptophan (5-MTP) is a newly identified endothelial protective factor [9,10]. it has been reported to be protective in endothelial barrier function, inflammation and oxidative stress [3,11]. More importantly, 5-MTP is regarded as a highly promising metabolite that can serve as a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target [10,12]. In endothelial cells. 5-MTP is synthesized from intracellular tryptophan by two enzymatic steps. First, tryptophan hydroxylase1 (Tph1) transforms l-tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP). Then acetylserotonin methyltransferase (Asmt) converts 5-HTP to 5-MTP. 5-MTP is transported extracellular by vesicles through exocytosis, and catabolized by indoleamine 2, 3-dioxygenase (Ido1), which is mainly expressed in immune cells [9,11]. It was reported that the levels of 5-MTP decreased both in the supernatant of endothelial cells and mice serum in Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induced ALI model [13]. Animal experiments demonstrated that 5-MTP restoration alleviated LPS induced ALI. Other pro-inflammatory mediators could also reduce the production of 5-MTP [14]. Despite the importance of 5-MTP in acute lung injury and other endothelial related diseases, the reason why it decreases under various pathological stimuli, and the therapeutic mechanism, remain unclear.

Hypoxia is known to disrupt normal cellular metabolism and gene expression, often through the stabilization of the hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (Hif1α) [15]. While the hypoxic suppression of various metabolic pathways has been documented [16,17], its specific impact on Asmt pathway remains poorly understood. Furthermore, the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, a master regulator of inflammation, has been implicated in closely cross-talk with Hif-1α signaling [[17], [18], [19]]. Studies have shown that NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation can inhibit Asmt expression [20,21], but the underlying mechanisms remain unexplored.

Peroxiredoxin 6 (Prdx6), a member of the peroxidase family [22], was found to be a potential target of 5-MTP in this study. Prdx6 has been proven to be important in ALI [23], by repairing the peroxidized cell membranes following oxidative stress and protecting pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells against peroxidative stress [24,25]. These abilities allow Prdx6 to play an important role in antioxidant defense and phospholipid turnover in lung, which is especially vulnerable of peroxidative stress [26]. According to previous evidence, the level of Prdx6 is 5 times higher than Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (Gpx4) in lung [27], suggesting Prdx6 may be a more significant peroxidase in lung.

In this study, we explored the causes of the decline in Asmt and 5-MTP after HH and investigated the protective effects of 5-MTP on ALI. In addition, we also explored the underlying mechanisms by searching for a direct target using limited proteolysis-small molecule mapping (LiP-SMap). The present study demonstrates that Hif1α promotes the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p50 to inhibit Asmt and 5-MTP synthesis. 5-MTP protects against HH-induced ALI and ameliorates lipid peroxidation injury. Our findings also provide an explanation of how hypoxia leads to the downregulation of Prdx6 and illustrate how 5-MTP functions in ALI by preventing this phenomenon.

Materials and methods

Human study

This research involved a secondary analysis of previously collected plasma samples and clinical data. The original sample and data collection were completed prior to the conception of the current study's specific hypotheses. All human procedures including the original collection and use of samples and data for the present analysis were carried out in accordance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines, and had been approved by Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University, PLA, China. The reference number of ethical approval was (A)KY2024053. Informed consent of human subjects has been obtained.

Forty healthy Han male participants were enrolled in Chongqing (230 m above sea level) and transported to Golmud (4260 m above sea level) by train. The subjects had never traveled to areas above 2500 m prior to this study. Subjects with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic headache, heart diseases or pulmonary diseases were excluded.

The participants were between 18 and 25 years old. The body mass index (BMI) was 22.43 ± 1.71 kg/m2. Twenty-four (60 %) subjects had a history of smoking, and 18(45 %) subjects had a history of drinking (Supplementary Table 1). The day before trip, vital signs including the arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2), body temperature (BT), blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), and respiratory rate (RR) were all recorded after the subjects rested for 10 min. Then, blood samples were also collected.

After 3 days of stay in Golmud, the vital signs were measured again and the blood samples were collected. On the same day, symptomatic scores of AMS were assessed according to the Lake Louise scoring system (2018 LLS) [28]. The questionnaire consists of 4 parts, including headache, gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue/weakness, and dizziness/light-headedness. Each symptom was scored 0 to 3 so that the total score ranged from 0 to 12. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms. The presence of headache plus a total score of 3 or above was considered positive for AMS. A total score of 3–5 was classified as mild AMS, 6–9 as moderate AMS, and 10–12 as severe AMS.

After 3 days of hypoxia, the systolic pressure increased from 115.83 ± 9.37 mmHg to 122.55 ± 9.64 mmHg and the diastolic pressure increased from 76.55 ± 8.40 mmHg to 87.13 ± 6.02 mmHg. The HR also elevated from 69.28 ± 11.20bpm to 83.28 ± 16.93bpm. There were no differences in BT and RR (Fig. S1A–E). The SaO2 significantly decreased from 97.10 ± 0.98 % to 83.58 ± 5.72 % (Fig. 1A). The results of AMS evaluation were positive in 36(90 %) subjects, with an average score of 4.68 ± 2.27. Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Sample perapation

Blood samples were collected in heparin sodium anticoagulant tubes to obtain plasma by centrifugation for 3000 rpm at 4oC for 20 min. The plasma samples were stored at −80oC before measurement.

For animal tissues, approximately 100 mg tissues were homogenized in 900 μl phosphate buffer solution (PBS) after treatment with Red Blood Cell Lysis Buffer (Solarbio, China). the homogenates were obtained as the supernatant by centrifugation for 3000 rpm at 4oC for 20 min. The results were normalized with the content of total protein measured by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assays.

For cell supernatant, the samples were collected and centrifuged before measurement to exclude any floating cell fragments.

5-MTP ELISA

The samples were measured by a competitive ELISA kit for 5-MTP (Meimian, MM-92702201, China). Briefly, the samples and HRP-conjugated 5-MTP were added into the wells precoated with antibody. After washing, TMB (Tetramethylbenzidine) substrate was added. The resulting signal intensity was inversely proportional to the concentration of the target antigen in the sample. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm wavelength with a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The content of the substance to be tested in each sample was calculated according to the standard curve. Pre-experiments were conducted for each type of sample before the formal test to determine appropriate dilution ratio. The key validation parameters of the 5-MTP ELISA kits were provided in the Supplementary Table 3.

Melatonin and N-acetylserotonin (NAS) ELISA

The samples were measured by commercial ELISA kits for melatonin (Elabscience, China) and NAS (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co.,Ltd, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. The methodology was similar with those described in 2.2.2.

Animal model

This study was approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (No. AMUWEC20223540) complying with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Research Council's Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old male, 20±2g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were all maintained with a 12 h light/dark cycle at a temperature of 23±2oC, a relative humidity of 60 ± 5 %, and free to standard water and food. Cage positions were rotated weekly to minimize environmental confounding. Animals were randomly assigned to groups using computer-generated randomization (n = 6). Group allocation was concealed from operators during assessment. The data analysts remained blinded until the statistical analysis was completed.

We established HH-induced ALI model referring to Liuyang Tian et al. [8]. Briefly, a hypobaric and hypoxic animal chamber from Guizhou Fenglei Aviation Ordnance Co., Ltd. (DYC-2842T, China) was utilized to simulate the atmospheric conditions at 5500 m altitude (48.5 kPa, about half of the oxygen content at sea level). The light, temperature and humidity condition in the hypoxic chamber were all consistent with the normoxic animal room (230 m, 98 kPa). Before entering the animal chamber, there was a transitional chamber to balance the pressure at an altitude of 3000 m (70 kPa), allowing the experimenters to perform operations like drug administration. This process was performed once a day within 1 h. The rate of decompression was 4 m/s for the transitional chamber and 3 m/s for the animal chamber.

The mice were housed in the chamber for 3 days to induce hypobaric hypoxia and create ALI. For 5-MTP supplementation, animals were divided into four groups: (1) Control + Vehicle group; (2) Hypoxia + Vehicle group; (3) Hypoxia+25 mg/kg 5-MTP group; (4) Hypoxia+100 mg/kg 5-MTP group. The mice were injected intraperitoneally with 25 mg/kg or 100 mg/kg 5-MTP per day, starting from the day before hypoxia, based on previous studies [13,29,30]. The vehicle groups were injected with 5 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in saline. The mice were anesthetized with 50 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium before they were sacrificed to obtain all the samples.

Histopathology

The lungs were obtained carefully after perfusion, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded and sectioned after gradient alcohol dehydration. Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining was performed following the manufacturer's instructions (AiFang, China). Finally, the pathological changes were imaged using Digital Pathology Slide Scanner (KFBIO, China).

Lung wet/dry weight ratio

Lung wet/dry weight ratio test was performed to indicate the degree of pulmonary edema. The lung tissues were harvested and weighed as wet weight after the mice were sacrificed. Dry the tissues in 24-wells plates under a 65oC heated vacuum chamber for 72 h and weigh the tissues again as dry weight. The lung wet/dry weight ratio was calculated by dividing the wet weight by the dry weight.

Evans blue assay

Evans blue (EB, Aladdin, China) was used to test the permeability of pulmonary endothelial barrier in vivo. Briefly, after three days of treatment, the mice were injected through the tail vein with 2 ml/kg of 2 % EB dye. One hour after the injection, the mice were anesthetized and perfused with saline to remove the dye in vessels. The lung tissues were harvested to dry under 65oC vacuum chamber for 72 h to constant weight. Weigh the dried tissues and add 30 μL formamide to every milligram of tissue to bathe in water at 37oC for 24 h. Centrifuge at 3000 rpm for 10min to acquire the supernatant to measure absorbance at 620 nm wavelength with a microplate spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Content of EB in each specimen was calculated according to the standard curve.

Bronchoalveolar lavage

The mice were anesthetized to perform tracheotomy. The lavage device was self-made by attaching nylon catheters with 0.7 mm outer diameter and 0.35 mm inner diameter to the needles of 2.5 ml syringes. Lavage was performed 3 times with 1 ml 4oC PBS in the device to obtain bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). The cell density of BALF was examined by automatic cell counter (Countstar, China). The collected BALF was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was carefully removed to detect the total protein content in BALF using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The cell pellet was resuspended in pre-cooled PBS to detect myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity with a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) and prepare the smear for Giemsa staining (Biosharp, China). At least 200 cells were randomly counted under an optical microscope. Based on their morphological characteristics, the concentration of neutrophils and mononuclear/macrophages were distinguished and calculated.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM)

The lung tissue samples were sectioned as 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 pieces, fixed in 2.5 % phosphate-glutaraldehyde and stored at 4oC in the dark overnight. The sections were rinsed with 0.1 M PBS for 3 times, fixed with 1 % osmic tetroxide for 2 h and rinsed again. The samples were then dehydrated and embedded in EPON812 resin and then sliced into 70-nm-thick sections with an ultramicrotome (Leica, German). Finally, the samples were stained with 2 % uranium acetate-lead citrate and viewed using a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, JAPAN).

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection

Animals were divided into three groups: (1) empty vector (EV) group; (2) Prdx6-WT group; (3) Prdx6- S32A group. The mice were intratracheally injected with 50 μl AAV2/6-Tie (about 2.0 × 1012 vg/ml) carrying Prdx6-WT, Prdx6-S32A or EV plasmids (see the sequences in Supplementary Table 4). The AAV were purchased from HanBio Tech (Shanghai, China). After 3 weeks, the mice were induced ALI with administration of 100 mg/kg 5-MTP for assessment of lung injury and lipid oxidation.

Cell culture

Mouse pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs, SAIOS, CL-209 m, China) were cultured in MCDB-131 medium (ZQXZBIO, China), supplemented with 10 ng/mL mouse endothelial growth factor (PeproTech, USA), 1ug/ml Hydrocortisone, 5 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin. Mouse lung fibroblasts (MLFBs) and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (Gibco, USA) with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin. Cells were cultured at 37oC in a controlled 5 % CO2 incubator.

After forming a confluent monolayer, the PMVECs were placed in a 1 % O2 hypoxic chamber (94 % N2, 5 % CO2) to establish an in vitro hypoxia-induced microvascular endothelial barrier injury model. Before hypoxia, 5-MTP treatment was conducted after starvation in serum-free medium for 1 h. The concentration of 5-MTP treatment was 25 μM, which was determined by the results of cell viability assay (Fig. 3J).

To investigate the effect of NF-κB p50 nulclear translocation on Asmt, the PMVECs were treated with 30 μM Pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate (PDTC, MCE, USA) for 30min before hypoxic treatment.

To investigate the degradation of Prdx6 under hypoxic condition, PMVECs were treated with 10 μM Z-Leu-Leu-Leu-al (MG132, MedChemExpress, HY-13259), 100 nM bafilomycin A1 (BafA1, MedChemExpress, HY-100558) or DMSO for 12 h. The living cell lysosomes were observed using 50 nM Lyso-Tracker Green (Beyotime) with Hoechst 33258 (Meilunbio, China).

Cell viability assay

PMVECs were seeded on 96-well plates (1.0 × 105 cells/well), incubated overnight, and treated with 5-MTP. We set up seven groups to determine the optimal 5-mtp concentration: blank (−), 1‰DMSO (0 μM 5-MTP), 6.25 μM 5-MTP, 12.5 μM 5-MTP, 25 μM 5-MTP, 50 μM 5-MTP, and 100 μM 5-MTP. Then, the cells were exposed to 1 % O2 hypoxia for 24 h and performed cell viability assay by incubating the cells with 100 μL fresh medium containing 10 % Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (CCK8, MeilunBio, China) at 37oC for 1 h. The absorbances were detected using microplate spectrophotometer at OD 450 nm to calculate the percentages of cell viability.

Trans-endothelial electrical resistance (TEER) and FITC-Dextran permeability assay

PMVECs were seeded on 6.5 mm-transwell inserts in 24 well plates (Jet Bio-Filtration, China). Before TEER assay, cells forming a confluent monolayer were incubated with Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) for 30min. The total resistance was measured by a Millicell ERS-2 Volt-ohm Meter (Merck Millipore, German). The resistance of HBSS in a blank well was recorded as the Blank resistance. TEER was calculated as follows:TEER(Ω·cm2)=(totalresistance−blankresistance)(Ω)×insertarea(cm2)

For permeability assay, 200 μl medium containing 1 mg/ml 70 kDa FITC-Dextran (Aladdin) was added onto PMVECs monolayers and medium in the lower chamber was replaced with 700 μl sterile PBS. After 1 h of incubation away from light, the inserts were carefully removed to collect the PBS in the lower chamber and measure fluorescent absorbance at 488 nm excitation and 520 nm emission wavelength using a fluorescent microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). The fluorescence intensity was indicated as arbitrary fluorescence units (AFU).

Knock down of Hif1α expression by siRNA

The negative control siRNA (si-NC) and si-Hif1α (5′-3’: GAUAACGUGAACAAAUACATT) were provided by Genepharma Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). The PMVECs were seeded in 12-well plates and transfected with 100 nM si-NC or si-Hif1α using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, USA).

The binding of Hif1α and NF-κB p50 to Asmt promoter was assessed by a commercial ChIP kit (CST, #9003). Briefly, the PMVECs were cross-linked with 1 % formaldehyde. Chromatin was fragmented by 5 sets of 20-sec pulses sonication to generate DNA fragments of 200-500bp. Fragment size was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. 10 μl sample was saved as input. The remaining chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 5 μg primary antibody against Hif1α (GeneTex, GTX127309), NF-κB p50 (Proteintech, 14220-1-AP), Histone H3 (CST, #4620) or rabbit IgG (CST, #2729). After the cross-links were reversed, DNA was purified using spin columns and analyzed by qPCR (see ChIP primers in Supplementary Table 6). Data were expressed as percentage of input.

LiP-SMap

Lip-SMap and data analysis were performed according to the standard protocols of Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Briefly, about 2.0 × 107 PMVECS cells were collected after hypoxia treatment with 24 h of 1 %O2. The samples were lysed with cold PBS for protein extraction and quantification. The cell lysates were incubated with 25 μM 5-MTP or vehicle (1 ‰ DMSO) for 30min at room temperature (RT). Limited proteolysis was conducted by incubation of Proteinase K and Trypsin. Then, liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was performed, which briefly was data independent acquisition mass spectrometry using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) after chromatographic separation performed by a nanoliter flow rate Vanquish Neo system Easy-1200 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The results were processed by Spectronaut software. The filtering parameter Q Value cutoff was 0.01.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA was conducted to examine the thermal stabilization of Prdx6 upon ligand binding to assess target engagement in a cellular context. Two 10 cm dishes full-grown PMVECs were digested, washed twice and resuspend with 1 ml PBS containing protease inhibitor. The suspension was divided evenly into two 1.5 ml tubes and incubated with 25 μM 5-MTP or vehicle, 1 ‰ DMSO, on a shaker at RT for 2 h. Each tube was then divided evenly to incubate separately on a metal bath at 10 gradient temperatures between 37 and 73 °C for 3 min, then at RT for 3 min, and immediately freeze in liquid nitrogen. The samples were frozen and thawed twice and centrifuged at 20,000g at 4 °C for 20 min. The supernatants were then prepared for immunoblotting.

Molecular docking

The compound structure files of 5-MTP (Compound CID: 119802) were retrieved from PubChem (http://pubchem/ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and turned into PDB files using the Open Babel 2.3.2 software. The structure files of receptor proteins were retrieved from the Uniprot database. AutoDockTools software was used to modify the receptor protein by hydrogenation and charge balance and convert the receptor protein and ligand molecule into pdbqt format respectively. AuotoDock Vina 1.1.2 was used for molecular docking between receptor protein and ligand molecule. The docking results were analyzed using Protein-Ligand Interaction Profiler (PLIP, https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index) and visualized using PyMOL 3.1 software. The chemical structure of 5-MTP was drew by ChemDraw 20.0.

Note that the number 124 amino acid of mice Prdx6 was Aspartate (D) according to the open sequence on Uniprot (O08709, last update on 23-Jan-2007), while it turned out to be alanine (A) according to the lately published transcript on NCBI (NM_007453.4, last update on 24-Dec-2024) and our results of mass spectrum. Therefore, subsequent plasmid and virus construction were both designed based on NM_007453.4 transcript with 124A.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The MD simulation was conducted using the GROMACS software package, based on the results of molecular docking. The protein and ligand structures were preprocessed, and their topology files were generated accordingly. The simulation system was solvated in an explicit water box, followed by sequential energy minimization, NVT (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) equilibration, and NPT (constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature) equilibration. Finally, a production MD simulation was conducted for 100 ns at 310 K and 1 bar.

The protein was described using the AMBER99SB-ildn force field, and water molecules were modeled with TIP3P. The small molecule parameters were assigned using the general AMBER force field (GAFF). Atomic charges for the ligand were derived via the restricted electrostatic potential (RESP) method, with geometry optimization performed in ORCA, charge calculations carried out in Multiwfn, and force field parameters generated using Sobtop. During the production run, no constraints were applied, and an integration time step of 2 fs was used. Trajectory frames were saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

MST was performed for in vitro molecular interaction experiments to obtain affinity data of Prdx6-WT and Prdx6-S32A with 5-MTP. The interaction between Prdx6-WT, Prdx6-S32A and 5-MTP was measured by microscale thermophoresis using the Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper, German). Lysates from HEK293T cells expressing GFP-Prdx6-WT and GFP-Prdx6-S32A were used as a source of fluorescently labeled Protein. HEK293T cells were lysed 24 h after transfection. To assess the binding of 5-MTP to Prdx6-WT and Prdx6-S32A, cell lysates were diluted 20-fold with TNT buffer (10 mmol/L Tris-HCl; 150 mmol/L NaCl; 0.05 % Tween 20; pH7.8–8.2) to provide optimal fluorescence levels. 5-MTP was employed as the ligand. For each assay, the lysates were incubated with varying concentrations (1000 μM–0.0305 μM) of the ligand in the TNT buffer at RT for 15 min. The samples were loaded into NT.115 standard capillaries. Analysis was performed at 25oC, 40 % excitation power, 60 % MST power. Apparent Kd value was calculated using MO Affinity Analysis V2.3. Data of at least three independently pipetted measurements were analyzed and Kd was expressed as mean ± SEM.

Measurement of peroxidase (POD) activity

To determine whether the activity of peroxidase was affected after the S32A mutation of Prdx6, the peroxidase activity was detected in PMVECs using a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, China). Based on the principle that POD catalyzes the reaction of H2O2, the enzyme activity was determined by measuring the change in absorbance at 420 nm.

Measurement of SOD, MDA and GSH content

Superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) levels in lung tissues or cell lysates were measured with commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) following the manufacturer's protocols. The results were normalized by total protein content measured by BCA.

Assays for lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by Diphenyl-1-pyrenylphosphine (DPPP) and Liperfluo probe. DPPP (MedChemExpress, HY-118159) was a fluorogenic probe that reacts with lipid hydroperoxides. The reagent was dissolved in DMSO (10 mM) and stored in separate packages at −80 °C. lung homogenates or cell lysates were incubated in all-black 96-well plates (Corning, 3916) with 10 μM DPPP at 4 °C for 30min in the dark. The fluorescence was determined at 352 nm excitation and 380 nm emission wavelength. The results were normalized with protein concentration determined by BCA, and reported as AFU/mg protein.

For Liperfluo detection, PMVECs were seeded in 8-well chamber slides (ibidi, German) and loaded with 10 μM Liperfluo (Dojindo, L248) in serum-free medium for 30min after specific treatment. The slides were washed by HBSS twice and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica) at 488 nm excitation and 550 nm emission wavelength. ImageJ software was used to quantify fluorescence intensity.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from 5 × 105 PMVECs using an RNA Extraction Kit (Abclonal, RK30156, China) with a Full-automatic Nucleic Acid Extraction System (Abclonal). RNA concentration and purity were measured with NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using ABScript Neo RT Master Mix for qPCR with gDNA Remover (Abclonal). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (Abclonal) on a fluorescence quantitative PCR detection system (Biorad, USA). All data were normalized to the mRNA expression level of Actb. The primers were listed in Supplementary Table 6.

Extraction of lysosomes

To separate lysosomes and cytoplasm, a lysosome extraction kit from Beibo Biotech (BB-3603, China) was used. For a single sample, 2 × 107 cells were lysed in a 2 ml Dounce homogenizer (Sigma-Aldrich) for 50 times. After gradient centrifugations, the pellet was washed resuspend with 40 μl RIPA lysis buffer. The supernatant was centrifuged one more time and reserved as cytoplasmic components by adding triploid volume of RIPA lysis buffer. The lysosomes and cytoplasm were quantified by BCA and normalized separately between groups for immunoblotting.

Plasmid transfection

The plasmids containing Prdx6-WT or Prdx6-S32A were provided by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in antibiotic-free medium for 24 h before hypoxia and 5-MTP treatment.

Immunofluorescence

PMVECs were seeded on 8 mm TC-treated glass coverslips and treated by groups. After treatment, cells were fixed with cold 1:1 mixed methanol and acetone for 10 min at −20oC, followed by permeabilization with 0.3 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with normal goat serum (Boster, China) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies against NF-κB p50 (Proteintech, 14220-1-AP, 1:500), Prdx6 (Proteintech, 13585-1-AP, 1:1000) and Lamp2 (Abcam, ab13524, 1:500) were diluted in Immunol Staining Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime) and incubated overnight at 4oC. PBS was used for negative control. After washing three times with PBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20 PBST, cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488-Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, 1:1000, Abcam, ab150077; Alexa Fluor 555-Goat Anti-Rat IgG, 1:1000, Abcam, ab150158) for 1 h at RT in the dark. Coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using Mounting Medium with DAPI (Abcam, ab104139, UK) and allowed to cure overnight. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Invitrogen) with a 40x objective. NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation was evaluated by Pearson's correlation coefficient between NF-κB p50 and DAPI using co-localization analysis in ImageJ software. The lysosome localization of Prdx6 was evaluated by Pearson's correlation coefficient between Prdx6 and Lamp2.

Immunoblotting

Tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail, and the protein concentration was calculated by BCA. Add 5x loading buffer and denaturate in a metal bath at 95oC for 10min. Proteins were then isolated through SDS‒PAGE and transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5 % nonfat milk at RT for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4oC with primary antibodies against VE-cadherin(Abcam, ab3368, 1:1000), Asmt (Abclonal, A6529, 1:1000), Tph1 (Invitrogen, MA5-32209, 1:1000), Ido1(Proteintech, 13268-1-AP, 1:1000), Prdx6 (Proteintech, 13585-1-AP, 1:1000),β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-AP, 1:10,000),Hif1α (Genetex, GTX127309, 1:1000), Lamp1 (Huabio, HA722827, 1:1000), p62(CST, #5114, 1:1000).

The next day, the membranes were washed for 3 times with Tris buffered saline-Tween (TBST), 5 min each, before incubated with a goat anti-rabbit HRP conjugated secondary antibody (Huabio, HA1001, 1:500,000) for 1 h at room temperate (RT). After the membranes were washed with TBST 3 times again and added Ultra-sensitive ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Biosharp, China), the images were captured using ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad) and analyzed with Image Lab software 6.1. For Asmt (38 kDa) and Ido1 (50 kDa), the blot was stripped and reprobed by β-actin antibody because their molecular weights are very close to β-actin (42 kDa). The original immunoblotting figures were provided in Fig. S6.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software was used for Statistical analysis. Student's t-test was performed between two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple comparisons among three or more groups, and two-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons with two independent variables. All tests were two-tailed. Pearson correlation analysis and receiver operator characteristic curve (ROC) analysis were used to analyze the relation between 5-MTP levels and clinical outcomes. Linear regression was used to delineate the results of correlation analysis. The cut-off value of ROC curve was determined by Youden index. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypoxia downregulated 5-MTP by reducing Asmt

After 3 days of hypoxia, the human plasma levels of 5-MTP decreased significantly (Fig. 1B). Pearson correlation analysis indicated that 5-MTP level before hypoxia, after hypoxia and the variation ratio (R2 = 0.75, p < 0.0001; R2 = 0.61, p < 0.0001; R2 = 0.27, p < 0.001) were all correlated significantly with SaO2 after hypoxia (Fig. 1C–S2A). 5-MTP level before hypoxia and after hypoxia (R2 = 0.22, p < 0.0001; R2 = 0.14, p < 0.001) were correlated significantly with and AMS score (Fig. 1D–S2B). ROC analysis showed that 5-MTP level before hypoxia had a significant area under curve (AUC) value with a cut-off value of 876.5 ng/L for SaO2<85, p < 0.0001 (Fig. 1E); and 431.5 ng/L for moderate and above AMS (AMS score>5), p < 0.05 (Fig. 1F). The results indicated that 5-MTP level is a potential biomarker for over mild hypoxia-induced responses.

A mice model of HH-induced ALI was established. HE staining showed interstitial thickening and edema in the lung tissues in the Hypoxia group (Fig. S2C). The lung wet-dry ratio significantly increased in the Hypoxia group, indicating lung edema (Fig. S2D). EB assays were used to further investigate the function of PACB and the results indicated that the residual EB was higher in the Hypoxia group (Fig. S2E), indicating albumin leaks and thus impaired PACB, which were further confirmed with higher content of protein in BALF (Fig. S2F). We also analyzed the level of VE-cadherin in mice lungs, a critical marker in PACB integrity, which was significantly decreased in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 1I). The results above suggested signs of ALI.

In mice, the plasma 5-MTP levels also decreased significantly in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 1G). To investigate the tissues responsible for the decline, the 5-MTP levels in multiple tissues were detected. The results showed that the 5-MTP level was highest in the lung, and notably decreased in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 1H). To explore the reason for the decline of 5-MTP in ALI lung, the metabolic enzymes were detected by immunoblotting, including the first step synthetase Tph1, the second step synthase Asmt and the catabolic enzyme Ido1. The results revealed that Asmt decreased in the Hypoxia group, while no significant changes were observed in Tph1 and Ido1 (Fig. 1I). Since Asmt could also catalyze the synthesis of melatonin, we also examined the change of melatonin and its precursor NAS in mice plasma and lung by ELISA. The results showed that the content of NAS did not change significantly in either the plasma or the lung (Fig. S2G and H). The plasma melatonin level decreased significantly in the Hypoxia group (Fig. S2I). However, the melatonin level in the lung tissues was below the detection limit of our assay (<7.81 pg/ml). Therefore, we focused our subsequent mechanistic discussion on 5-MTP as it was the key local mediator in the downstream of Asmt in HH-induced lung injury.

Since 5-MTP was previously reported to be mainly synthesized in fibroblasts and endothelial cells, the supernatant levels of 5-MTP in PMVECs and MLFBs were measured. ELISA detection of cell supernatant after 0, 6, 12 and 24 h of hypoxia in 1 % O2 indicated a trend that the 5-MTP level decreased progressively with the duration of hypoxia in PMVECs (Fig. 1J) but not MLFBs (Fig. 1K). After 24 h of hypoxia in 1 % O2, the 5-MTP level in the supernatant of PMVECs decreased significantly. TEER and FITC-dextran permeability tests showed that the barrier function of PMVECs was markedly impaired after 24 h of hypoxia (Fig. S2J–K). It was supported by the increase in Hif1α level and the decline in VE-cadherin level in PMVECs after 24 h of hypoxia (Fig. 1L). The results suggested an in vitro hypoxia-induced microvascular endothelial barrier injury model, and 24 h of 1 % O2 were carried out as hypoxia treatment in the follow-up cell experiments.

Then, the metabolic enzymes of 5-MTP were detected in PMVECs. Immunoblotting showed that Tph1 was increased in PMVECs after hypoxia, while Asmt and Ido1 decreased (Fig. 1L). It was indicated that the decline in Asmt level was responsible for the hypoxia-induced decline of 5-MTP in PMVECs.

Hif1α promoted the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p50 to inhibit Asmt

Next, we sought to explore the mechanism of Asmt downregulation after hypoxia. qPCR results showed the mRNA level of Asmt decreased in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 2A). Transfection of si-Hif1α reversed the hypoxia-induced decline in Asmt and 5-MTP in PMVECs (Fig. 2B–D, S3A), suggesting that Hif1α induced the decline in Asmt and 5-MTP in PMVECs after hypoxia. However, ChIP assays showed that Hif1α did not bind to the promoter region of Asmt both in the Control group and in the Hypoxia group (Fig. S3B–C). According to previous studies [20,21], the expression of Asmt could be inhibited by NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation. There were two possible binding sites of NF-κB p50 in Asmt promoter (Fig. 2E). Therefore, we performed ChIP assays again for NF-κB p50. It was suggested that NF-κB p50 bound to both of the two sites in Asmt promoter in the Control group. Interestingly, the content of NF-κB p50 binding to the Asmt promoter significantly increased at Site2 after hypoxia, while the content of NF-κB p50 at Site1 did not show any significant change (Fig. 2F). The results indicated that hypoxia induced increased binding of NF-κB p50 on Site2 in the Asmt promoter. Immunofluorescent staining showed that hypoxia led to an increase in the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p50, which could be reversed by si-Hif1α (Fig. 2G). This result verified that hypoxia induced the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p50 via Hif1α. What's more, the hypoxia-induced decline in Asmt and 5-MTP were both reversed by PDTC, an inhibitor of NF-κB activation (Fig. 2H–J). The above results suggested that Hif1α promoted the nuclear translocation of NF-κB p50 to inhibit Asmt and thus to affect 5-MTP synthesis.

The protective effects of 5-MTP on ALI

We assumed that 5-MTP supplementation may protect HH-induced ALI. Therefore, two doses of 5-MTP, 25 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg were used. In order to verify the plasma 5-MTP concentration after administration, we conducted ELISA tests. The results found that the plasma 5-MTP level of the 25 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg groups were higher than that of the Hypoxia + Vehicle group. There were no significant differences among the 25 mg/kg group, the 100 mg/kg group and the Control group (Fig. 3A). The lung wet to dry ratio were reduced in 5-MTP groups (Fig. 3B), indicating less severe lung edema. EB assays indicated less leaks of EB in 5-MTP groups (Fig. 3C). HE staining showed the interstitial thickening in lung were alleviated in 5-MTP groups (Fig. 3D). The 5-MTP groups also had lower total protein (Fig. 3E) and total cell density, less macrophages and neutrophils (Fig. 3F), and lower MPO activity in BALF (Fig. 3G). The immunoblotting results showed that VE-cadherin levels were improved in 5-MTP groups (Fig. 3H). The TEM showed scattered widening and dissolution in the capillary basement membranes of PACB in the Hypoxia + Vehicle group, together with decreased electron density in endothelial cells suggesting edematous degeneration, and increased electron density in shrunk mitochondria suggesting lipid peroxidation damage. There were no changes in tight junctions. In contrast, the 5-MTP groups displayed different degrees of improvement (Fig. 3I). These results demonstrated that 5-MTP alleviated the vascular injury and PACB disruption in HH-induced ALI in mice. According to the results of various assessment indicators of ALI, the 100 mg/kg dose group showed better therapeutic effects than the 25 mg/kg dose group, especially in the Evans blue experiment. Therefore, a dose of 100 mg/kg was adopted in subsequent experiments.

To determine the optimal therapeutic concentration of 5-MTP in vitro, the PMVECs were treated with various concentrations of 5-MTP. According to CCK8 tests, 24 h of 1 % O2 hypoxia significantly reduced cell viability, which was dose-dependently improved in the 5-MTP groups (Fig. 3J). We noticed that there were no differences in cell viability at concentrations above 25μΜ 5-MTP groups. Therefore, 25 μM 5-MTP was used in follow-up cell experiments. As expected, in the Hypoxia+25 μM 5-MTP group, the VE-cadherin level increased (Fig. 3K and L) the permeability of FITC-dextran decreased (Fig. 3M), and the TEER increased (Fig. 3N), indicating that 5-MTP alleviated hypoxia-induced endothelial barrier disruption in PMVECs.

According to the pathological changes observed in our model, PACB disruption and lipid peroxidation injury, we focused on Prdx6, a critical anti-peroxidase playing an important role in vascular injury by protecting cell membrane integrity and lipid peroxidation injury in lung. As shown in Fig. 4B, limited proteolysis identified Ser32 site of Prdx6 as the direct target of 5-MTP.

CETSA experiment was then carried out to confirm the combination of 5-MTP and Prdx6. The results showed altered thermal stabilization of Prdx6 in the 5-MTP groups, suggesting a direct interaction between 5-MTP and Prdx6 (Fig. 4C). Subsequently, molecular docking delineated that 5-MTP was hydrogen-bonded to Ser32, Val66 and Leu68 site of Prdx6, and was hydrophobically interacted with Ala62 site (Fig. 4D). Ser32 was assumed to be hydrogen-bonded to the carbonyl group of 5-MTP (Fig. 4D and E). The binding energy was −5.3 kcal/mol, suggesting a stable binding relationship. Interestingly, Ser32 is an active site of Prdx6 to protect against lipid peroxidation, which strongly suggested that the interaction between 5-MTP and Prdx6-Ser32 could be a therapeutic target. We also performed molecular docking between 5-MTP and human PRDX6 protein, which also indicated a direct interaction between 5-MTP and the Ser32 site of human PrRDX6 (Fig. S4D).

To further verify the direct interaction of Prdx6 and 5-MTP, we performed MD simulations. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of the protein-ligand complex reached a stable state after 40ns, earlier than the protein monomer (70ns), suggesting that 5-MTP binding enhances conformational stability. Moreover, RMSD fluctuations after 10ns were smaller in the complex than in the protein monomer, indicating that 5-MTP binding restricted the conformational freedom of the protein and reduced the overall structural dynamic fluctuations. Based on RMSD results, the last 10 ns of the stabilized trajectory were used for MM/GBSA analysis. The results showed the average binding free energy of was

−14.05 kcal/mol, indicating thermodynamically favorable binding. The root-mean-square fluctuation analysis (RMSF) revealed relatively low fluctuations in residues 28–32, 58–68, and 99–101 of the binding pocket. The radius of gyration (Rg) value of the complex is generally higher than that of the protein monomer. A quill diagram indicated that this expansion occurred mainly in regions outside the binding pocket, without compromising its stability (Fig. S4E). The increase in Rg may reflect a slight expansion of the overall flexibility or conformational space of the protein after ligand binding, but does not affect the stability of the binding pocket. The solvent accessible surface area (SASA) values were similar between the complex and the monomer, implying minimal change in solvent exposure upon 5-MTP binding (Fig. 4F). Finally, a free energy landscape generated from the last 10ns of RMSD and Rg trajectories identified the lowest free energy state (blue region in the 2D plot), corresponding to the most stable conformation of the protein–ligand complex during the simulation (Fig. 4G).

Next, the MST experiment was conducted to test the binding affinity of 5-MTP with Prdx6-WT, and to compare with Prdx6-S32A. In the Prdx6-WT group, the protein migration rate increased with the increase of 5-MTP concentration, and the fitting curve shows an inverted "S" shape. The upper and lower plateau periods are distinct and have a strong concentration dependence, indicating specific binding. The Kd value is 75.7 μM. In contrast, the results showed that 5-MTP did not bind to Prdx6-S32A or the GFP control (Fig. 4H).

Discussion

ALI, an acute hypoxia-induced disease, is getting growing concerns as more and more people travel to high altitudes [2,34]. However, available treatments fail to meet current needs. Therefore, it is urgent to explore new biomarkers and cost-effective therapeutic targets for its prevention and treatment. The present study demonstrated that the human plasma 5-MTP level significantly decreased after HH, which was closely related to HH-induced blood oxygen desaturation and AMS. The reason for the HH-induced decline in 5-MTP could be explained by to a decrease in Asmt level via Hif1α induced NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation, resulting in decreased synthesis of 5-MTP in lung endothelial cells. We found that 5-MTP protected the endothelial barrier function to attenuate ALI by ameliorating lipid peroxidation injury via interacting with the Ser32 site of Prdx6 to reduce the lysosomal pathway degradation of Prdx6 induced by hypoxia. Our study identified a novel molecular target of 5-MTP to provide new insights into the mechanisms of 5-MTP in the treatment of endothelial-related diseases.

5-MTP was recently considered as an endothelial protective factor with anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic functions [10,35]. Previous studies have proved its importance as a biomarker metabolite in sepsis, heart failure and renal failure [10,12,[36], [37], [38]]. In this study, we enrolled 40 self-contrast human participants to assess the impact of HH on 5-MTP. The plasma level of 5-MTP has been shown to be significantly decreased after HH and correlated with HH-induced blood oxygen desaturation and AMS, suggesting that plasma 5-MTP level could serve as a prognostic biomarker in HH-induced responses.

The level of 5-MTP seems to be decreased in various pathogenic conditions. In chronic kidney disease model induced by unilateral ureteral obstruction, 5-MTP was more significantly decreased in the kidney than in other organs [10]. In our study, 5-MTP was more significantly decrease in the lung, probably because it's the organ most vulnerable to hypoxia. In vitro studies have proved that pro-inflammatory mediators inhibit the expression of Tph1 in endothelial cells to reduce the production of 5-MTP [9,10]. While in our study, instead, Tph1 level increased in PMVECs after hypoxia. The consistent elevation of Tph1 in endothelial cells is considered to be one of the causes of pulmonary hypertension under chronic hypoxia [39,40]. Even when Tph1 increased significantly, the level of 5-MTP still decreased due to the decline of Asmt, the final enzyme in the synthesis pathway that produces 5-MTP. Asmt is more known as the synthetase of melatonin in pineal body, which was also reported to be reduced by hypoxia-ischemia [41]. In this study, we also found that the mice plasma melatonin level decreased significantly after hypoxia. However, the melatonin level in the lung was below the detection limit of our assay (<7.81 pg/ml). Moreover, the level of NAS, the precursor of melatonin, did not change significantly in mice plasma or lung. This suggests that the local melatonin concentration in the lung under physiological and pathophysiological conditions may be too low to exert significant biological effects, despite the known efficacy of exogenous administration [42]. This observation aligns with the understanding that ASMT has different isoforms and functions in pineal versus non-pineal cells [[43], [44], [45]]. Our results support that in mice lung, Asmt is primarily involved in 5-MTP production. Our study also proved that hypoxia downregulated Asmt by Hif1α induced NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation. There were studies reporting that the expression of Asmt could be inhibited by NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation after immune activation [20,21]. In this study, the hypoxia induced inhibition of Asmt could be reversed by si- Hif1α or NF-κB inhibitor. What's more, we confirmed the direct binding of NF-κB p50 in the promoter of Asmt and clarified the specific site by ChIP assays.

Based on previous research, Prdx6 is a bi-functional enzyme with two distinct active sites to reduce small molecule peroxides like H2O2 and phospholipids peroxides [26,46,47]. Among them, C47 reside is mainly in charge of the peroxidase activity, while S32, H26 and D140 form a catalytic triad together, which is responsible for the phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity [27,48]. It has been proved that H26 is the direct binding site of Prdx6 and lipids and D140 is enzymatic active site, but the specific function of S32 remains unclear [[49], [50], [51]]. By Lip-SMap, we found the direct interaction between 5-MTP and the S32 site Prdx6, which was consistent with the results of molecular docking, MD simulation and CETSA. Further, MST confirmed the affinity between 5-MTP and Prdx6 and showed no binding between 5-MTP and Prdx6-S32A.

Our findings also suggests that interaction with Prdx6-S32 is a therapeutic target in lipid peroxidation and endothelial barrier function disruption. Amino acids 31–40 of Prdx6 were reported to be involved in the localization of Prdx6 to acidic organelles like lung lamellar bodies and lysosomes, where the binding ability of Prdx6 to phospholipids can be greatly enhanced under lower pH to effect as phospholipase [11]. It is postulated that the subcellular localization of Prdx6 can affect the balance between its two enzyme activities [50]. Nevertheless, the biological processes that occur when Prdx6 is transferred to acidic organelles seems to be quite complicated. Another hypothesis is that the degradation of Prdx6 may also occur in lysosomes [52]. In our study, BafA1 restored the hypoxia-induced decline in Prdx6, indicating that hypoxia promotes the degradation of Prdx6 though the lysosomal pathway. The S32A mutation blocks the degradation of Prdx6 through lysosomes, but the peroxidase activity of Prdx6 was not affected. Therefore, our study demonstrated that Ser32 site of Prdx6 was indispensable in the localization and degradation of Prdx6 to lysosomes.

Interestingly, our findings suggests that 5-MTP could prevent the localization and degradation of Prdx6 to lysosomes, but not affect its anti-lipoperoxidation function. Previous study showed that a serine-to-threonine mutation at the S32 site of Prdx6 abolished its ability to transport to acidic organelles while retained its structure and enzymatic activity [[53], [54], [55]]. It is indicated that interaction with S32 could alter the localization of Prdx6 to lysosomes without affecting its enzyme activity. We assume that this might be the mechanism by which 5-MTP interacts with Prdx6. The CETSA results showed a decrease in Prdx6 levels following 5-MTP treatment. This could also be explained by reduced lysosomal localization of Prdx6 with 5-MTP treatment, because Prdx6 is reported to be more thermal stabilized under acid environment.

However, it requires more studies to explore the specific functions of the S32 site of Prdx6, which are very important for the functions of Prdx6 but still not fully explained. Another limitation of this study is the demographic homogeneity of our study cohort, which consisted exclusively of healthy young adult males (aged 18–25 years). This selection was methodologically advantageous for the initial exploration, as it effectively minimized confounding variables associated with age, sex hormones, and comorbidities. However, we acknowledge that this specificity consequently limits the immediate generalizability of our findings to other populations, such as females, older adults, or adolescents, in whom the pathophysiology of hypoxic responses may differ significantly. Therefore, the general applicability of our result requires further validation in more diverse and representative cohorts. Besides, we conducted the mutation experiments in wild type PMVECs and mice, with the presence of endogenous wild type Prdx6. It will be more rigorous introducing mutant proteins under knockout conditions. What's more, the primary objective of our study was to identify the functionally critical targets through which 5-MTP exerts its protective effects under pathological (hypoxic) stress. It is a valuable direction for future research to uncover more of the functions of 5-MTP and its binding partners under physiological conditions.

Conclusion

Hypoxia downregulated Asmt expression in PMVECs via Hif1α induced NF-κB p50 nuclear translocation to decrease the synthesis of 5-MTP, and the application of 5-MTP attenuated ALI by inhibiting lipoperoxidation and protecting endothelial barrier through interacting with Prdx6-S32 to reduce the lysosomal degradation of Prdx6. Our data suggest 5-MTP is a potential biomarker and therapeutic clue in HH-induced responses.
