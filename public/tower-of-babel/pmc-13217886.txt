ABSTRACT
Human rights research increasingly employs computational text analysis, but existing datasets provide either document-level aggregations or event-level extractions from news sources, limiting fine-grained analysis of primary organizational reports. We present the Human Rights Violation Reporting Dataset, a comprehensive paragraph-level corpus comprising reports from Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, the US State Department, and the Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances, spanning 1999–2023. The dataset contains 832,220 paragraphs processed using a custom pipeline combining PDF extraction, custom crawlers, language model-based text correction (reducing character-level errors from 8.3% to 1.7%), and comprehensive natural language processing (NLP) enrichment, including named entity recognition, sentiment analysis, text classification, and content moderation. Each paragraph is enriched with 23 metadata fields enabling entity network analysis, topic modelling, cross-organizational comparison, and machine learning applications. Data are provided in comma-separated values (CSV) format, with standardized country-year identifiers compatible with existing political science datasets. This dataset enables previously impossible fine-grained computational analysis of how major human rights organizations document violations across time, space, and organizational contexts.

Background

Human rights research has been constrained by fragmented data sources, limiting comprehensive analysis of violations across time and space [1,2]. Existing datasets typically provide either document-level aggregations unsuitable for fine-grained analysis [3] or event-level extractions from news sources rather than authoritative organizational reports [4]. This creates measurement challenges, as latent variable models estimating respect for human rights depend on document-level indicators that obscure organizational reporting practices and textual specificity [5,6]. Additionally, uneven source coverage introduces systematic bias, with underreporting in countries with restricted media access and overreporting in heavily scrutinized states [7,8].

Our dataset was developed to address these limitations by providing paragraph-level structured text from primary organizational sources spanning 1999–2023. Unlike recent machine-coded event extraction projects [9], our approach preserves complete textual content while applying comprehensive NLP enrichment. This can enable researchers to conduct both computational text analysis and granular examination of how major human rights organizations document violations, facilitating validation of measurement models, assessment of bias, and integration with existing political science datasets through standardized country-year identifiers.

Data formats and accessibility

Data are provided in CSV format, a good human-readable format, compatible with Excel, pandas, and R tidyverse. All files use UTF-8 encoding. Temporal data follow ISO 8601 format (YYYY). Country identifiers use ISO 3166–1 numeric codes enabling linkage with V-Dem, Polity, UCDP, ACLED, and other political science datasets.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

In this section, we explain the workflow we used to collect, process, and analyze human rights-related data from four main sources: Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch, the US State Department, and the Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances. The workflow comprised two main parts: first, collecting reports and transforming them into structured data; second, applying advanced NLP and NLU techniques to enrich the dataset with additional variables (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1Pipeline of data collection and processing.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Creating structured data

We created structured data from reports based on the format in which sources saved and distributed their reports. The main innovation was the technique we used to transform poorly preserved reports into clean, analyzable datasets. This required the development of custom NLP methods combining advanced text processing and cleaning techniques with state-of-the-art large language models, specifically, ChatGPT. The former were employed to remove extraneous content, such as page numbers, headers, and footers, and to merge broken lines. The latter were used to correct misinterpreted characters incorrectly extracted from the original reports (see Fig. 2).Fig. 2Transforming PDFs into Clean and Coherent Text.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Working group on enforced or involuntary disappearances and amnesty international

The Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances and Amnesty International publish reports as PDF documents.2 Extracting unstructured data from PDFs, some of which are poor-quality scanned documents, required custom and ad-hoc processing.

First, we used the Python fitz library (PyMuPDF) to extract text content from the PDFs, treating each newline character (\n) as a paragraph boundary. Then, we proofread the raw text to correct any errors arising from the parsing process, such as incorrect characters, formatting issues, or structural problems. We used ChatGPT-4 with default temperature settings to assist with this and to identify sections in the document. The prompt was:Can you proofread the following text WITHOUT removing any words or reducing the size of the text? Just find mistakes and misspellings in the text and fix them. If the text appears correct and no editing is required, return the text provided. Also, there are cases where sections have titles. These titles should be placed on a separate line that starts with “Title”. For example:Background Political confrontations between the… should become:Title: Background Political confrontations between the… and:Abuses by armed groups Abductions and killings Armed groups, including the Taliban… should become:Title: Abuses by armed groups Abductions and killings Armed groups, including the Taliba

After processing the extracted text, we identified and split sections and converted them into CSV format for uniformity and ease of analysis. A custom Python program implemented in-house took the proofread raw text files as input and produced CSV files as output. The process generated four CSV files, one for each source. All had a common structure.

Each row in the CSV represents a paragraph, considered the basic unit of analysis. Each row also contains additional information necessary to uniquely identify each paragraph and place it in the correct context. The structure of the CSV includes the following fields: id, a counter uniquely identifying each entry in the CSV; year, the year the report was published; iso3n, the ISO 3-digit country code; cname, the name of the country; section number, the number of the section (with section numbering reset each year); section, the name of the section; paragraph number, the number of the paragraph (with paragraph numbering reset each year); paragraph, the actual content of the paragraph.

Two CSV files, those from the US State Department and the Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances, have additional columns, as their corresponding reports contain more information. The US State Department reports have four section levels, and the Working Group on Enforced or Involuntary Disappearances reports have a paragraph id column, as each paragraph has a unique ID.

Data processing

After we transformed the reports into structured data in CSV format, we applied NLP techniques to extract insights from the text and facilitate future use of the dataset by other researchers. To that end, we used one of the most advanced models currently available, Google’s Natural Language3 AI. This service provides four natural language-understanding technologies: entity recognition, entity sentiment analysis, text classification, and text moderation. We employed these NLP tools as production-ready APIs with performance characteristics documented and validated by Google. All four methods are state-of-the-art models trained on large-scale datasets, with performance metrics reported in their technical documentation.

We applied all four techniques at the paragraph level. This resulted in the creation of four additional CSV files per source (16 in total), each containing the corresponding analysis output. Data integration and final dataset composition

We integrated the outputs from the four NLP analyses with the original structured data to create a comprehensive dataset for each source. This resulted in 16 CSV files (four analyses per source), each enriching the dataset with different dimensions of textual information. The combined dataset facilitates multifaceted analyses, allowing researchers to explore patterns in entity mentions, sentiment expressions, thematic categorizations, and the presence of sensitive content across human rights reports.

Throughout the data collection and processing stages, we remained cognizant of potential challenges and limitations. By addressing them, we aimed to produce a robust dataset that would contribute to the field of human rights research.

Limitations

Several constraints warrant acknowledgment. First, the dataset captures only four major organizations and excludes regional human rights bodies, specialized NGOs, and non-English language publications. The selection reflects practical considerations regarding data availability and processing resources rather than comprehensive global coverage. Geographic representation follows organizational reporting priorities: well-documented regions (Middle East, Latin America, Eastern Europe) receive substantially more paragraph-level coverage than under-documented areas (Central Asia, Pacific Islands). Researchers conducting cross-regional analyses should account for these systematic patterns when interpreting documentation intensity.

Second, automated NLP processing has inherent limitations. Our named entity recognition performs comparably to state-of-the-art systems, though accuracy drops in predictable ways: ambiguous entity boundaries, nested structures, and non-Western name transliterations prove consistently challenging. Users requiring higher precision should implement additional validation procedures. Sentiment analysis operates at paragraph-level granularity and cannot disaggregate emotional valence toward specific entities within multi-actor paragraphs. Additionally, the State Department NLP Processing was limited only to years 1999–2010 due to the massive nature of the dataset and budget limitations.

Third, organizational reporting practices evolved substantially through 1999–2023. Earlier reports from some organizations (1999–2005) typically contain less geographic specificity and shorter incident descriptions than later reports with more detailed legal analysis (2015–2023). This temporal heterogeneity affects interpretation of trends and requires careful modeling to distinguish genuine changes in human rights conditions from shifts in documentation practices.

Ethics Statement

This research was approved by the Cyprus National Bioethics committee (Protocol Number: ref2023.01.38, Date: 30/03/2023). The authors have read and followed the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. The work does not involve human subjects, animal experiments, or data collected from social media platforms.

Source data considerations: The dataset processes publicly available human rights reports documenting violence, repression, and rights violations. The reports contain descriptions of severe harms including torture, extrajudicial killings, sexual violence, and enforced disappearances. While the data are based on publicly available documents, researchers using this dataset should exercise appropriate care when displaying, citing, or analyzing content describing graphic violence. Content warnings are recommended when presenting findings to general audiences.

Secondary trauma considerations: Researchers conducting manual annotation or detailed textual analysis should be aware of potential secondary trauma from prolonged exposure to detailed violence descriptions. We recommend following established protocols for research involving traumatic content, including limiting continuous exposure time and providing access to support resources.

Privacy and attribution: Original reports occasionally include names of victims, witnesses, and alleged perpetrators. While these are publicly available in source documents, researchers should carefully consider privacy implications and local security contexts before republishing names or detailed incident descriptions, particularly in ongoing conflict zones. Aggregate statistical analysis poses minimal privacy risk; case-study approaches require greater ethical scrutiny.

Organizational attribution: The dataset enables comparative analysis of organizational reporting practices. Researchers should interpret differences in coverage or emphasis carefully, recognizing organizations operate under different mandates, resource constraints, and security environments. Findings suggesting organizational "bias" should be accompanied by appropriate methodological caveats, and researchers should respect the dangerous work of human rights monitors.
