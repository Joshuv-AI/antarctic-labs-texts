Rethinking the time-to-laparotomy indicator of regional trauma center quality metrics in South Korea: a multicenter retrospective study with propensity score matching
Authors: Youngmin Kim, Junsik Kwon, Hang Joo Cho, Gil Jae Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTimely surgical intervention is crucial in trauma care, particularly in patients with hypotensive abdominal injuries. In South Korea, the regional trauma center system has adopted a quality metric mandating initiation of emergency surgery within 1 hour of emergency department (ED) arrival for such patients. We evaluated the impact of ED-to-surgery time on clinical outcomes in hypotensive abdominal trauma and assessed the appropriateness of the 1-hour threshold.MethodsThis multicenter retrospective study included 425 trauma patients who underwent emergency abdominal surgery at 2 regional trauma centers between 2019 and 2022. Patients with an initial or lowest recorded systolic blood pressure (SBP) <90 mmHg were included. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality; secondary outcomes included complications, intensive care unit stay, and total hospital stay. Patients were categorized into early (≤1 hour) and delayed (>1 hour) surgery groups. Statistical analyses included logistic regression, propensity score matching (PSM), receiver operating characteristic curve, and complication analysis by 30-minute intervals.ResultsWe found that 243 patients (57.2%) underwent surgery within 1 hour. In-hospital mortality did not differ significantly between early and delayed groups. Age, Glasgow Coma Scale, lowest SBP, 4-hour RBC transfusion volume, and abdominal Abbreviated Injury Scale were independent mortality predictors. PSM confirmed no mortality difference by surgical timing. However, complication rates increased with surgical delays.ConclusionWhile surgical delay was not associated with mortality, it was linked to increased complications. Future trauma quality metrics should incorporate refined patient stratification and evaluate the entire process aimed at restoring physiological instability, rather than relying on a strict 1-hour threshold.Keywords: Abdominal injuries, Emergency treatment, Surgical procedures, Time-to-treatment, Trauma centers

INTRODUCTION

Trauma continues to be a major global public health concern and is a leading cause of death, particularly among individuals under 40 years of age [1,2]. Beyond its direct impact on individual health, trauma imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden by limiting workforce participation among the working-age population [3]. Furthermore, trauma-related mortality reduces national productivity, increases healthcare costs, and places significant psychological and social distress on affected families. These factors pose a substantial challenge to healthcare infrastructure [4].

To address this issue, the government of South Korea established the regional trauma center system in 2012, with 17 centers currently in operation nationwide [5,6,7]. These centers have enhanced the organization and specialization of trauma care, contributing to a notable reduction in the preventable trauma mortality rate from 35.2% in 2012 to 15.7% in 2019 [8]. Despite these improvements, South Korea’s preventable trauma mortality rate remains higher than that of other developed countries, highlighting the need for continued advancements in trauma care [9,10,11,12].

The “Golden Hour” concept is widely accepted in trauma management, introduced in the 1970s by R. Adams Cowley at the Baltimore Shock Trauma Center. It suggests that critically injured patients face a significantly higher risk of mortality if they do not receive definitive care within 1 hour of injury [13,14]. Although initially proposed without strong scientific validation, the concept has gained widespread acceptance in clinical practice because of its relevance in optimizing trauma care [15].

This principle has been particularly emphasized in the management of abdominal trauma. For patients who have sustained severe abdominal trauma with ongoing hemorrhage, delayed surgical intervention is linked to significantly worse outcomes. Several studies have highlighted the importance of prompt operative management in improving survival in such cases [16,17,18,19].

In South Korea, following the establishment of regional trauma centers, the Ministry of Health and Welfare implemented a set of quality assessment metrics to monitor and promote optimal trauma care. One such metric evaluates the timeliness of emergency surgery initiation in patients with abdominal trauma. Specifically, it assesses the time interval from emergency department (ED) arrival to operating room admission in patients with an initial or lowest recorded systolic blood pressure (SBP) below 90 mmHg. A higher proportion of cases exceeding the 1-hour threshold results in a lower score for this metric.

However, the clinical justification for the selection of target patients and application of a fixed 1-hour threshold remains unclear. Furthermore, little is known about the outcomes of patients identified by this criterion in actual clinical practice.

In this study, we aimed to assess the effect of time from ED arrival to emergency abdominal surgery on clinical outcomes in patients with hypotensive abdominal trauma. Additionally, we evaluated the appropriateness of the current regional trauma center quality evaluation metric, which emphasizes a strict 1-hour threshold.

METHODS

Study design and population

This multicenter retrospective study was conducted at Ajou University Hospital and Gachon University Gil Medical Center in South Korea. Data were collected from January 1, 2019 to December 31, 2022. The study population consisted of trauma patients who underwent emergency abdominal surgery at the participating hospitals. To be included in the study, patients must have undergone surgery for a traumatic abdominal injury and have a valid electronic data interchange (EDI) code corresponding to a procedure classified as abdominal surgery (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, patients were required to have an initial SBP <90 mmHg upon arrival at the ED or a lowest recorded SBP of <90 mmHg. Patients who exceeded 12 hours from ED departure to operating room admission were excluded. Patients younger than 15 years were also excluded (Fig. 1).

The primary outcome of this study was in-hospital mortality, and secondary outcomes included complications, length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, total hospital stay, and duration of mechanical ventilation (Supplementary Material 1).

Data collection

Data were retrieved from hospital electronic medical records and the Korea Trauma Database (KTDB) registries. The collected variables included demographic information, injury mechanism, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) for each anatomical injury site. Initial vital signs and preoperative resuscitation parameters, including blood transfusion volume, were also recorded. Blood transfusion volume was measured within 4 hours after ED arrival and within 24 hours, consistent with the critical time frames used in previous trauma studies and in the KTDB [20]. The time from ED arrival to operating room admission was recorded as the independent variable.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics. Patients undergoing emergency abdominal surgery within 1 hour (early surgery group) were compared with those who underwent surgery after 1 hour (delayed surgery group). Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, while continuous variables were assessed with the Student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the independent variables associated with in-hospital mortality. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to minimize the selection bias. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression after adjusting for relevant covariates. To achieve a better covariate balance, 1:1 optimal matching was performed instead of 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching, which initially resulted in a suboptimal balance. Conditional logistic regression was initially planned to evaluate the association between surgical timing and mortality within the matched pairs.

However, because only a small proportion of matched pairs were discordant for mortality (31 of 151 pairs, 20.5%), the model produced unstable estimates with wide confidence intervals (CIs). Therefore, multivariable logistic regression using Firth’s penalized likelihood method was employed to reduce small-sample bias and issues related to complete or quasi-complete separation [21,22]. This approach allowed more stable estimation of odds ratios (ORs) while maintaining covariate adjustment in the post-matching analysis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) analyses were conducted using both the pre-matching and post-matching datasets. These analyses compared the discriminative ability of time to surgery alone with that of the multivariable model including physiological and injury severity covariates.

Additionally, the time from ED arrival to operating room admission was categorized into 30-minute intervals to assess the complication rates for each group. To evaluate the presence of a linear trend in complication rates, the Cochran-Armitage trend test was performed.

Finally, a subgroup analysis was conducted among patients who were directly transported from the scene without interhospital transfer, applying the same analytical procedures. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 23.0 (IBM Corp.) and R ver. 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and Python ver. 3.13.5 (Python Software Foundation).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating hospitals (No. GAIRB2024-044). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the need for informed consent was waived. Patient confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing the data prior to analysis.

RESULTS

Demographics and clinical characteristics

In total, 425 patients who underwent emergency abdominal surgery for trauma were included in this study. Among them, 243 patients (57.2%) underwent surgery within 1 hour of ED arrival, while 182 patients (42.8%) underwent surgery after 1 hour (Table 1). Data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). The lowest recorded SBP was significantly lower in the early surgery group than in the delayed surgery group (67 mmHg [IQR, 53–77] vs. 70 mmHg [IQR, 58–81], P = 0.050). The total volumes of RBC (10 units [IQR, 6–14] vs. 8 units [IQR, 4–14], P = 0.005) and plasma transfusions (8 units [IQR, 4–12] vs. 4 units [IQR, 0–12], P = 0.002) within the first 4 hours were also significantly higher in the early surgery group. Furthermore, abdominal AIS scores were higher in the early surgery group (4 [IQR, 3–4] vs. 3 [IQR, 3–4], P = 0.021).

In contrast, the delayed surgery group had a higher proportion of blunt trauma (92.9% vs. 74.1%, P < 0.001) and direct arrivals to the trauma center (80.8% vs. 66.7%, P = 0.002). Except for the abdominal region, the AIS score for other anatomical regions and the total ISS were significantly higher in the delayed surgery group. Other initial vital signs, including pulse rate, respiratory rate, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, did not differ significantly between the groups.

No significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in terms of outcomes, including complications, hospital length of stay, ICU stay, total duration of mechanical ventilation, or in-hospital mortality rate (Table 2).

Factors associated with mortality

In the univariable analysis, several factors were significantly associated with mortality, including blunt injury, lower initial GCS score, lower lowest SBP, higher RBC and plasma transfusion volume within 4 hours, and higher abdominal AIS scores (Table 3).

In the multivariable analysis, older age (OR, 1.038; 95% CI, 1.016–1.060; P < 0.001), higher initial respiratory rate (OR, 1.072; 95% CI, 1.020–1.128; P = 0.007), lower initial GCS (OR, 0.853; 95% CI, 0.781–0.933; P < 0.001), lower lowest SBP (OR, 0.975; 95% CI, 0.951–1.000; P = 0.047), and increased RBC transfusion volume within 4 hours (OR, 1.114; 95% CI, 1.058–1.173; P < 0.001) remained significant predictors of mortality. Additionally, abdominal or pelvic organ injury was independently associated with increased mortality risk (OR, 1.699; 95% CI, 1.129–2.557; P = 0.011).

Propensity score matching analysis

Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the 2 groups in the lowest recorded SBP (P = 0.050), RBC transfusion volume within 4 hours (P = 0.005), and AIS abdomen scores (P = 0.021) (Table 4). After matching, the standardized mean differences for all covariates decreased to below 0.1, indicating adequate balance. The estimated effect of early surgery on in-hospital mortality did not reach significance (estimated treatment effect, –0.06; 95% CI, –0.132 to 0.005; P = 0.067).

Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was subsequently performed using the full matched cohort (n = 302) (Fig. 2). In this model, delayed surgery remained associated with higher odds of mortality (OR, 1.85; 95% CI, 0.86–4.08; P = 0.113), although this association did not reach statistical significance. Older age (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.05; P = 0.009), lower initial GCS (OR, 0.85; 95% CI, 0.77–0.93; P < 0.001), lower lowest recorded SBP (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95–1.00; P = 0.032), higher RBC transfusion volume within 4 hours (OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.06–1.20; P < 0.001), and higher AIS abdomen scores (OR, 2.20; 95% CI, 1.39–3.61; P < 0.001) were independently associated with increased mortality. The overall model fit was statistically significant (likelihood ratio test, P < 0.001).

Receiver operating characteristic and area under the curve analysis

In the pre-matching dataset, time to surgery alone demonstrated poor discriminatory performance for in-hospital mortality (AUC = 0.539), whereas the multivariable model showed excellent predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.899). A similar trend was observed in the propensity score-matched dataset (AUC for ‘ED arrival-to-operation time’ = 0.555; AUC for multivariable model = 0.895) (Fig. 3).

Complications analysis

The incidence of complications was analyzed according to time to surgery, categorized into 30-minute intervals from ED arrival to operating room admission. To assess whether complication rates increased with longer delays, the Cochran-Armitage trend test was performed, revealing a significant linear trend across the delay groups (Z = –2.47, P = 0.014) (Fig. 4).

Direct visit (subgroup analysis)

An additional analysis limited to patients directly admitted from the scene was performed. Descriptive statistics, PSM, OR analysis for matched pairs, and ROC/AUC evaluations for both ED arrival-to-OR admission time and multivariable models were conducted. The association between ED arrival-to-OR admission time and mortality remained non-significant (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found no significant association between the time from ED arrival to laparotomy and in-hospital mortality among patients with hypotensive abdominal trauma. Instead, initial physiological instability and abdominal injury severity emerged as the principal determinants of patient outcomes. These findings remained robust after PSM matching for key covariates, including initial SBP, transfusion volume, and abdominal AIS scores. Although delayed surgical intervention was not directly linked to increased mortality, a stratified analysis by 30-minute intervals revealed a trend toward higher complication rates with longer delays.

Previous studies have emphasized the critical importance of early operative intervention in trauma care. Clarke et al. [23] reported a 1% increase in mortality risk for every 3-minute delay in the ED, and Barbosa et al. [24] similarly demonstrated significant associations between prolonged time to surgery and increased 24-hour and 30-day mortality in abdominal trauma. Such findings have supported the concept of the Golden Hour, which has long functioned as a guiding yet somewhat mythical principle in the field of trauma care.

While the Golden Hour encompasses both prehospital and in-hospital phases, the present study focused solely on the in-hospital phases. This decision was based on the structure of South Korea’s trauma quality indicator, which specifically evaluates the interval from ED arrival to surgical intervention. Our study was designed to assess the validity of this national quality metric and, therefore, aligned with its inclusion criteria accordingly.

Our findings showed that the time from ED arrival to surgery did not significantly affect survival. This may be partly explained by several factors. First, there was heterogeneity in the surgical indications among patients who underwent emergent laparotomy for hypotensive abdominal trauma—the target group of the current quality metric. Not all of these patients presented with ongoing hemorrhage. A substantial subset of patients underwent surgery for hollow viscus injury or intra-abdominal contamination without major bleeding. While these cases required early surgical management, they did not necessitate immediate intervention within the first hour. This variation in surgical indication may have attenuated the influence of surgical timing on mortality. Unfortunately, the specific surgical indications, such as ongoing hemorrhage, hollow viscus injury, or other causes, were not included in the present analysis.

Second, the delayed surgery group had a higher proportion of patients with blunt trauma and direct arrivals, as opposed to interfacility transfers. These patients were more likely to require diagnostic evaluation, including computed tomography, than those with penetrating injuries or those transferred from other hospitals. Furthermore, the delayed group showed less physiological instability than the early group but had higher AIS scores in non-abdominal regions, which may have contributed to a longer preoperative ED stay. In other words, unlike the early group—where the need for laparotomy was immediately confirmed due to severe abdominal injury and physiological instability—the delayed group may have included patients who required additional diagnostic tests or interventions for extra-abdominal injuries prior to surgery. Even after PSM adjusted for other major determinants of mortality, the time difference between the 2 groups did not significantly affect mortality, whereas physiological instability and the severity of abdominal injury were the primary determinants of death.

These findings have important implications for updating and improving current quality assessment metrics for regional trauma centers in South Korea. This indicator aims to promote timely surgical intervention in potentially hemorrhagic abdominal trauma by discouraging unnecessary delays. However, our data suggest that applying a uniform 60-minute threshold to all hypotensive abdominal trauma cases may not be clinically meaningful. Instead, a more nuanced approach that incorporates refined, risk-based stratification and evaluates whether appropriate steps were taken to improve survival may offer a more valid and clinically relevant quality metric than simply measuring the time from ED arrival to operating room admission.

Of note, when patients were stratified into 30-minute intervals, we observed a trend toward increased complication rates with longer delays, particularly among those who underwent surgery more than 90 minutes after ED arrival. Although the time from ED arrival to operating room admission was not significantly associated with mortality, the observed trend of increasing complication rates with longer delays highlights that time remains a foundational element in optimal trauma care.

This study has several limitations. First, because this was a retrospective review of medical records, missing data and unmeasured confounders may have influenced our results. Although PSM was employed to control for confounders, prospective randomized trials would provide more robust evidence. Second, although the 2 participating regional trauma centers are major institutions in South Korea, hospital-specific and regional differences may have influenced the findings, limiting their generalizability. Future studies utilizing data from the entire KTDB or international multicenter analyses are required to enhance external validity. Third, this study did not account for preoperative interventions, such as intubation, imaging, chest tube insertion, catheter placement, resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (REBOA), or other resuscitative measures, nor did it consider angioembolization prior to laparotomy in polytrauma patients. Fourth, only the presence or absence of complications was evaluated, and severity grading based on standardized systems such as the Clavien-Dindo classification was not applied. Future studies should incorporate these variables for a more comprehensive analysis.

In conclusion, even when emergency abdominal surgery was initiated more than 1 hour after ED arrival, the in-hospital mortality rate among patients with hypotensive abdominal trauma did not significantly increase. Rather, physiological instability appeared to be the predominant factor contributing to mortality, whereas delayed surgery was associated with a higher incidence of complications. These findings suggest that while timeliness remains important in trauma care, a rigid 1-hour threshold may have limited relevance to mortality outcomes. Future trauma quality metrics should incorporate more refined patient stratification and evaluate the entire hemostatic process to ensure that physiological instability is corrected as rapidly as possible.
