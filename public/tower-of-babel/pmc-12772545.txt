Premier League Defibrillator Fund
Authors: Nick Perchard, Zafar Iqbal
Published: 2025

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a major cause of death in the UK. In January 2024, the Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest Outcomes Registry (OHCAO) published data from all of the English regional ambulance services, reporting that of the nearly 35 000 people treated by the National Health Service ambulance services, who suffered an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in 2022, just 7.8% survived to 30 days.1 This compares unfavourably with other countries, notably Norway, where survival rates of 25% are reported.1 Every week in the UK, around eight young people (aged <35 years old) die following an SCA due to undiagnosed cardiac conditions comprising heart muscle, electrical and structural diseases.2

The major factor limiting survival from SCA is the ability to provide timely and effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and defibrillation. The chances of surviving a cardiac arrest decrease by 7%–10% for every minute that CPR and defibrillation is delayed, but only by 2%–3% per minute if early bystander CPR is instigated.3 Research has shown that survival rates increase between 50% and 74% when patients receive defibrillation within 5 min of cardiac arrest with an automated external defibrillator (AED).2 However, of the 35 000 SCA cases reported by OHCAO, an AED was only used in 10.6% of cases when the incident was witnessed.1

Over 80% of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) occurs at home, approximately 10% in a public place, 1% in the workplace and 0.1% in a place of education.1 Although the occurrence of OHCA is low in schools, about one-third can occur within close proximity (300 m) of a school.4 There have been successful campaigns to raise awareness of the importance of CPR and AED, and the UK government has planned to equip all schools in England with AEDs from 2023.5 In 2021, the accessibility of AEDs in England was being tackled by the British Heart Foundation, whose data estimated that 70% of defibrillators were unknown to ambulance services and therefore less likely to be used in an emergency. Further, a study by the University of Warwick found that despite people from lower socioeconomic groups being more at risk of cardiac arrest, areas classified as most deprived by the Lower Super Output Areas (LSOA) Index of Multiple Deprivation had the lowest coverage of AEDs, with the least deprived having the highest.6

It was in this context that on the 12 June 2021, Christian Eriksen collapsed from SCA while playing for Denmark in the delayed UEFA Euro 2020 competition. Thankfully, through the quick reactions of his teammates, match officials and stadium medical professionals alongside Denmark’s doctor and physiotherapist, they were able to use an AED and resuscitate Eriksen. He has subsequently returned to playing with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator, including playing competitively in the Premier League (figure 1). In addition, the presenting features and signs of sports-related SCA are now well characterised and included in the Resuscitation Council UK best-practice guideline for management of SCA on the field-of-play.7 8

Premier League player Christian Eriksen has helped raise awareness of the Premier League Defibrillator Fund. At the installation of a new AED at Bedfont Sports Club in 2022, he spoke of the importance of the initiative, “From a negative it’s hopefully turning into something positive, that will save other lives also. It’s something that saved my life. And if it wasn’t there, I wouldn’t be here today. So, obviously, something that is very important to have. Football’s response

Within the professional game, players have received cardiac screening since 1997, enabling cardiac conditions to be identified and risk mitigation determined. Where SCA happens without prior signs, as in the case of Eriksen, the presence of highly trained medical professionals and equipment such as AEDs increases the probability of survival. However, every week in the UK, 12 young people die from SCA,9 and given the popularity of grassroots football, every week some young people and adults will play the game with an undiagnosed heart condition. The availability of an AED may well save their lives in the event of a cardiac arrest.

Since 2000, the Premier League, The Football Association (FA) and Government have invested over £1.2 billion via the Football Foundation to develop grassroots football facilities. By the summer of 2021, over 1000 facilities had been built or enhanced. With the partners focused on supporting those that needed it most, 40% of the investment had been distributed to facilities in the top 20% most deprived areas based on the LSOA Index of Multiple Deprivation. The facilities, often large multi-pitch sites, have high numbers of users and are often in public parks. As such, they could play an important part in tackling the inequality of AED provision identified by the University of Warwick.

The Premier League Defibrillator Fund

In July 2021, the Premier League committed to providing over 2000 AEDs to grassroots football sites across England and Wales through the Premier League Defibrillator Fund. The initial phase enabled all previously funded Football Foundation sites to apply for a new AED for their site. This was quickly followed by a second phase that enabled any grassroots club that operated its own site to apply. To ensure the AEDs were of maximum benefit, each site was provided with an external storage cabinet, enabling the AEDs to be used 24 hours a day. Together, it was estimated that these sites serve over 1.5 million people annually. In addition, the Football Foundation partners agreed that from that point forward, every new site funded by the Football Foundation would be provided with an AED.

This approach, using investment of £2.7 million, would significantly increase the coverage of defibrillators within the country and disproportionately benefit high-need areas. However, evidence suggests that thousands of AEDs are unknown to the emergency services, and so to maximise the impact of the new AEDs, all sites were asked to register with the British Heart Foundation’s ‘The Circuit’. The Circuit is a nationwide map of defibrillators that enables emergency services to direct the public to their nearest AED in the event of a cardiac arrest (https://www.thecircuit.uk/).

Research by the British Heart Foundation has shown that a third of adults in the UK have never learnt CPR. While AEDs are designed to be used without training, to maximise the opportunity, all sites receiving a new AED were required to ensure at least one person completed The FA’s free online SCA module.

Since its inception in July 2021, the Premier League Defibrillator Fund has provided 2014 free AEDs to grassroots sites. With these now registered with the emergency services and combined with CPR training for facility staff, they have significantly increased the capacity to respond to cardiac arrests, improving the chances of survival for any facility user.

Learnings

The delivery of this project was not without challenge. At the outset, there was very little data on where AEDs were located. With limited data, quantifying the shortfall of AEDs at football facilities was difficult and led to an open application process rather than a more needs-based approach.

This makes the British Heart Foundation’s The Circuit project a very important development in the understanding of AED accessibility. With over 87 000 AEDs now registered, the ability to strategically target investment has been greatly enhanced, and this data should be used to inform any future schemes.
