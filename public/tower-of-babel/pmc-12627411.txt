The influence of AI service robots’ humorous response strategies on consumer forgiveness following service failure
Authors: Zhaoji Yu, Hao Deng, Yun Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs technology advances, more AI entities are deployed in public service roles. However, AI service robot failures are increasingly frequent, causing negative behaviors such as customer dissatisfaction or complaints. Service recovery plays a crucial role in shaping consumer experiences following AI failures. At present, the interplay between AI robots’ humorous recovery strategies and core consumer motivations in influencing forgiveness behavior remain unclear. Integrating mental accounting theory and social exchange theory, this study investigates how this interaction affects forgiveness, examining the mediating roles of perceived warmth and perceived competence, and the moderating influence of AI relationship paradigm orientation. We collected data from 780 subjects across three studies. Results demonstrate that consumers driven by hedonic motivations exhibit significantly stronger forgiveness behavior towards humorous responses, mediated by enhanced perceived warmth. Conversely, utilitarian-motivated consumers demonstrate greater forgiveness in response to non-humorous strategies, mediated by heightened perceived competence. Furthermore, AI relationship paradigm orientation moderates these effects on forgiveness. These findings uncover the psychological mechanisms underlying effective AI service recovery and provide differentiated theoretical and practical implications for enterprises in designing AI service recovery strategies, thereby facilitating consumer forgiveness.Keywords: AI service robots, Humor, Consumption motivation, Consumer forgivenessSubject terms: Business and management; Business and management; Psychology; Psychology; Science, technology and society

Digitalization has profoundly reshaped consumer behavior and cognitive frameworks, thereby propelling artificial intelligence into a prominent role as a service provider1. AI service robots have gained widespread adoption across industries such as hospitality and food service, driven by their inherent advantages of efficiency, entertainment value, and cost-effectiveness2. Their deployment not only enhances human-robot interaction within online service contexts but also contributes to elevating the overall consumer purchasing experience3. However, as an emerging technology, AI service robots still confront significant market challenges: technical constraints and complex operational environments frequently give rise to service failures and malfunctions, which in turn may induce consumer dissatisfaction, trigger negative word-of-mouth, and potentially lead to customer churn. Consequently, understanding consumer behavior and identifying effective strategies to mitigate the adverse impacts of such service failures have become critical priorities for enterprises.​.

Among the diverse spectrum of service recovery strategies, the adoption of AI-powered humorous responses has exhibited promising potential in alleviating users’ negative emotions and improving their evaluations of AI services4. As a distinctive communication approach, humor can effectively mitigate negative affective states and the subsequent unfavorable behaviors. Furthermore, environmental factors and heterogeneous consumption motivations also exert a notable influence on consumers’ willingness to forgive5,6. While the deployment of AI service robots for service recovery can yield positive outcomes, it is equally important to recognize that variations in consumer behavior and attitudes, as well as the social dynamics between enterprises and consumers, play pivotal roles in shaping recovery effectiveness. Notably, as established drivers of consumer behavior7, perceived competence and warmth are crucial to generating consumer satisfaction and loyalty8.

Recent scholarly research has begun exploring the efficacy of AI-based humor strategies in the aftermath of service failures. For instance, Xu and Liu9 demonstrated that humorous responses generated by AI can exert a positive impact on consumer forgiveness, with perceived warmth functioning as a mediating variable. Nevertheless, their study predominantly focused on perceptions of warmth, leaving other potential mediating factors underexplored. Similarly, Yang et al.4 corroborated the positive role of humor in service recovery but provided limited insights into its specific effects on perceived competence and consumption motivation. Examining the dual mediating roles of perceived warmth and perceived competence can shed light on how response strategies influence consumer emotions and cognitions, ultimately shaping their forgiveness intentions, which lays a solid theoretical foundation for the optimization of AI service recovery strategies.​.

Drawing on social exchange theory, this paper examines whether humor can serve as an effective response strategy for AI service robots when confronted with service failures. Specifically, we investigate the interactive effect of response strategy (humorous vs. non-humorous) and consumption motivation on consumer forgiveness, unravel the mediating roles of perceived warmth and perceived competence, and test the moderating effect of AI relationship paradigm orientation. To address these research objectives, three empirical studies were conducted: Study 1 verifies the interactive effect of response strategy and consumption motivation on consumer forgiveness; Study 2 explores the mediating mechanisms underlying perceived warmth and perceived competence; and Study 3 tests the moderating role of AI relationship paradigm orientation.

Rationale and research hypothesis

Service recovery strategies for AI service robots

Service failures can trigger a diverse range of customer responses, among which forgiveness constitutes a significant outcome. Consumer forgiveness is defined as “the consumer’s willingness to give up retaliation, alienation, and other destructive behaviours and to respond in a constructive manner.” Existing recovery strategies employed when AI service robots fail can be broadly categorized into two types: intervention by human employees, and language-related recovery, such as apologies and explanations, that AI service robots can conduct independently10. Scholarly research in service recovery has shown that providing remedies like apologies can reduce consumers’ motivation for retaliation and enhance their motivation for reconciliation11,12. Moreover, when AI service robots exhibit a high degree of anthropomorphism, they are able to provide a more pleasurable recovery experience compared to human employees13.​.

In light of this, the present study selects AI service robots as the subject of service recovery research. Drawing on the customer value hierarchy theory, Zhu14 developed a model outlining how the perceived intelligence and perceived cuteness of service robots influence customer satisfaction. Research conducted by Lv15 demonstrated that AI service robots adopting cute response strategies elicit a higher willingness to forgive when issuing apologies. Additionally, Xu9 indicated that empathetic response strategies can alleviate consumers’ negative emotions through the mechanism of empathy. Building on these prior findings, this paper examines whether the use of a humorous language style in AI service robots’ apologies exerts a potential effect on enhancing consumer forgiveness.

Humor and its role in service recovery

Humor, as a key trait of individuals in modern civilized societies, is widely recognized as an effective communication tool and an essential component of social interactions16. When employees employ humor during consumer interactions, it often strengthens customers’ brand recall. Similarly, consumers who use humor in communications with service staff tend to experience greater relaxation, fostering a more congenial interaction atmosphere. An increasing body of scholarly research is now focusing on the utility of humorous response strategies in the context of service failures. Based on the goals of humor and its negative versus positive characteristics, Martin et al.17 categorized humor into four types: self-enhancing, affiliative, aggressive, and self-deprecating, and proposed that aggressive humor and self-deprecating humor could form a new research dimension within service failure contexts. However, given the highly subjective nature of humor, its applicability must be carefully assessed on a case-by-case basis.

Recent research in AI and service contexts has refined understanding of their distinct impacts on consumer outcomes. The effectiveness of self-deprecating humor in AI service recovery has received empirical support18. For instance, Zhang and Huang19 empirically showed that self-deprecating response strategies can effectively restore customer satisfaction and strengthen continuous usage intention after AI service failures20. In contrast, Cao et al.21 identified self-enhancing humor as a key coping strategy in negative social contexts; Zhang and Liu22 further demonstrated that it outperforms self-deprecating humor in promoting continued use of service robots, especially in standardized service settings and luxury hotels.

For other types of humor, Shin et al.23 demonstrated that affiliative humor serves as a powerful tool for humanizing AI chatbots. It enhances service satisfaction by fostering perceptions of anthropomorphism and making interactions more engaging. Oliveira24 further emphasized its effectiveness in interpersonal service encounters, showing that employees’ combined use of affiliative and self-defeating humor benefits service quality perceptions, Conversely, aggressive humor, which entails teasing or ridicule, poses reputational risks. Shin and Larson25 found that while it can increase a firm’s perceived excitement, it severely undermines trustworthiness in service recovery. Ning et al.26 showed that low-aggressive humor can promote consumer engagement more effectively than high-aggressive humor in social media.

This study specifically defines humorous response strategies as self-deprecating humor, wherein AI service robots mitigate consumers’ negative emotions by humorously highlighting their own shortcomings in the event of service failures.

Compared with other response strategies, research on the application of humor in AI service robot service recovery remains relatively limited. Existing studies primarily highlight the research value of humorous responses in this domain. For instance, Yang et al.4 found that AI service robots using humorous responses after failures can effectively improve consumer evaluations. Similarly, Zhang et al.27 verified that self-deprecating humor significantly enhances consumer tolerance in cases of service failures involving intelligent voice assistants. Nevertheless, there has been no in-depth exploration of how self-deprecating humorous strategies employed by AI service robots influence consumer forgiveness. Therefore, this paper takes this gap as a starting point for investigation, aiming to enrich the research system on the promotion and application of AI service recovery.

Application of social exchange theory in the field of human computer interaction

Social exchange theory has been widely applied in the fields of economics and social psychology.It posits that interpersonal relationships are largely governed by reciprocity norms, and that consumers are more inclined to engage in exchange behaviors, such as accepting compensation, when service failures occur. Scholars have also introduced this theory into the domain of human–robot interaction to explain consumers’ attitudes and behavioral decisions. Lv Xingyang28 indicated that during interactions with service robots, consumers offset the costs incurred by service failures through both material and psychological rewards, which in turn influences their intention to continue usage. The anthropomorphic features of service robots help facilitate human–robot relationships and enhance the consumer experience. Furthermore, human–robot interaction shapes consumers’ perceptions of robot credibility and their evaluations of robotic design attributes29.​.

This paper argues that during interactions between service robots and consumers, the anthropomorphic characteristics of service robots, such as humor, lead consumers to perceive them as social actors, thereby triggering resource exchange behaviors. Consumers evaluate this exchange process through mental accounting, which ultimately impacts their level of forgiveness.

Interaction of response strategy and consumption motivation on consumer forgiveness

Consumer purchasing motivations can be classified into various types. Among these, hedonic motivation is closely associated with sensory experiences and emotional fulfillment, and is regarded as highly personalized and subjective. In contrast, utilitarian motivation is evaluated based on more objective criteria: consumers rely more on cognitive reasoning than emotional responses to make judgments30 and tend to prefer efficient and precise services. Humorous responses can stimulate positive emotions and cater to the emotional needs of hedonic consumers, whereas non-humorous responses may better align with utilitarian consumers’ pursuit of efficiency. This suggests that service recovery strategies should be tailored to consumers’ underlying consumption motivations, and this distinction can also be explained through mental accounting theory.

Mental accounting theory posits that individuals do not treat resources such as time, money, and experience as interchangeable; instead, they allocate these resources into distinct “psychological accounts.” Decisions are made by evaluating the benefits and losses across these accounts. This compartmentalization shapes how individuals perceive gains and losses, thereby influencing subsequent decision-making31. In the context of service recovery, if consumers perceive a service failure as a loss in their hedonic account, humorous responses may elicit positive emotions and enhance recovery effectiveness. Conversely, non-humorous responses may be more effective for utilitarian consumers who prioritize efficiency and precision. Based on this reasoning, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1: The interaction between response strategy and consumption motivation has a significant effect on consumer forgiveness.​.

H1a: Under hedonic consumption motivation, humorous responses exert a more positive effect on consumer forgiveness than non-humorous responses.​.

H1b: Under utilitarian consumption motivation, non-humorous responses exert a more positive effect on consumer forgiveness than humorous responses.

Mediating role of perceived competence and perceived warmth

Perceived competence and perceived warmth constitute two core dimensions of social perception32. Perceived competence encompasses traits such as intelligence, skill, effectiveness, and creativity, while perceived warmth includes emotional attributes such as friendliness, sincerity, and trustworthiness. When individuals attribute human-like mental capacities to non-human entities, they tend to evaluate these objects using the same social perception dimensions applied to humans33. Consequently, perceived warmth and perceived competence are also relevant in consumer interactions with AI service robots.​.

The use of humor can shape consumer perceptions and enhance positive affect34. Wirtz et al.35 demonstrated that consumer responses to AI service robots depend not only on functional performance but also on social capabilities. Yang4 confirmed that humor mitigates perceptions of insincerity and exerts an influence on service evaluations. Additionally, the technological sophistication of service robots can further foster positive consumer assessments, and perceived competence positively impacts consumers’ perceptions of both the functional and emotional dimensions of service36.​.

Affect regulation theory posits that individuals manage negative events through emotional regulation processes. Humorous responses can attenuate negative emotions and foster positive affect, thereby enhancing interactional warmth and the memorability of the interaction. Given that consumers driven by hedonic motivation place greater emphasis on emotional experiences, humorous responses may increase forgiveness by elevating perceived warmth. Conversely, information processing theory indicates that individuals filter and evaluate information based on cognitive needs. Utilitarian consumers prioritize accuracy and effectiveness; thus, non-humorous responses that deliver clear and efficient solutions can strengthen perceived competence, rendering consumers more inclined to accept and forgive service failures. Accordingly, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H2: Perceived competence and perceived warmth mediate the interaction effect of consumption motivation and response strategy on consumer forgiveness.​.

H2a: Under hedonic consumption motivation, humorous responses influence consumer forgiveness through perceived warmth.​.

H2b: Under utilitarian consumption motivation, non-humorous responses influence consumer forgiveness through perceived competence.

The moderating role of the AI relationship paradigm orientation

This study adopts Aggarwal’s37 concept of the relationship paradigm, which refers to the norms and expectations governing interactions between enterprises and consumers, and influences subsequent behavioral decisions. Clark and Mills38 initially categorized relationship paradigms into two types: transactional and communal. The transactional paradigm resembles interactions between consumers and strangers, emphasizing immediate rewards and economic benefits. In contrast, the communal paradigm is analogous to relationships among friends or family members, focusing on mutual need fulfillment and social bonding that often extend beyond economic exchange.​.

Existing research indicates that consumers with a transactional orientation prioritize tangible benefits derived from services, whereas those with a communal orientation value intangible outcomes such as interaction enjoyment and emotional connection39. These relationship norms also extend to service scenarios involving AI service robots. According to interpersonal communication theory, emotional and social factors shape individual behavior. As a distinctive communicative tactic, humor can foster a relaxed service atmosphere, generating benefits for both enterprises and consumers. For communally oriented consumers, humorous responses from AI service robots may facilitate value co-creation and enhance forgiveness through emotional support. Conversely, transactionally oriented consumers prioritize efficiency and outcomes, rendering non-humorous responses more effective. Thus, communally oriented consumers seeking long-term relationships may respond more favorably to humorous responses under hedonic motivation, while transactionally oriented consumers expecting short-term efficiency may prefer non-humorous responses under utilitarian motivation. The following hypotheses are proposed:​.

H3

AI relationship paradigm orientation moderates the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategy.​.

H3a

For consumers with a transactional AI relationship orientation, non-humorous responses under utilitarian motivation result in higher consumer forgiveness.​.

H3b

For consumers with a communal AI relationship orientation, humorous responses under hedonic motivation increase forgiveness intention.​.

Study 1: interaction between consumption motivation and humorous response on consumer forgiveness

Study 1 sought to verify the interaction effect between response strategy (humorous vs. non-humorous) and consumption motivation (hedonic vs. utilitarian) on consumer forgiveness, with the goal of testing Hypothesis H1. To this end, a 2 × 2 between-subjects experimental design was employed, wherein the two independent variables were consumption motivation (hedonic vs. utilitarian) and AI service robot response strategy (humorous vs. non-humorous). Specifically, this design was utilized to develop distinct consumption scenarios aligned with the two types of consumption motivation, and to quantify consumers’ forgiveness toward AI service robots across these varied scenarios.

Methodology

This study employs only simulated scenario experiments and questionnaire surveys, and does not involve human clinical trials or animal experimentation. This study adhered strictly to ethical research principles, did not involve any sensitive personal data, medical information, or minors, posed no risks to participants, and implemented measures to ensure data confidentiality. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Science and Technology Research Institute, Shenyang University of Technology, China.

Subjects

A total of 328 subjects were recruited in Study 1 on the Credamo platform, After excluding 8 subjects who failed the attention check and logic validation test, the final 320 subjects were considered as valid subjects, including 189 (59.06%) males and 131 (40.94%) females, with a mean age of M = 33.77 years and SD = 8.13, and subjects were randomly entered into 4 experimental groups. All subjects gave written informed consent and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study strictly followed the principles of voluntary participation, anonymous completion, and confidentiality of information to maximize the protection of respondents’ privacy. Their participation was entirely voluntary, and upon completion of the experiment, each received a reward of $1. These same procedures for protecting participant rights and ensuring voluntary participation were adhered to in Studies 2 and 3.

Design and implementation

A 2 × 2 between-subjects design was employed, resulting in four experimental conditions: utilitarian motivation–humorous response, hedonic motivation–humorous response, utilitarian motivation–non-humorous response, and hedonic motivation–non-humorous response. Participants were randomly assigned to one of these conditions. All subjects first read contextual materials designed to induce specific consumption motivations, with manipulations adapted from Kim et al.40 and Whitley et al.41. The product category was consistent across conditions, while the purchase purpose differed, hedonic motivation was framed as fun-seeking, and utilitarian motivation as task-oriented.

Specifically, the utilitarian motivation scenario was described as follows: “Your company has arranged for you to stay at a hotel offering robot services for work. Your task is to experience the hotel’s Chinese restaurant service and write a report on the environment, food taste, and staff service afterward. You take this experience seriously and aim to record your feelings comprehensively and objectively. However, after ordering, you find that the service robot delivered the wrong meal.” The hedonic motivation scenario stated: “Imagine the following: During your holiday, you chose to stay at a hotel with robot services purely to relax and enjoy food. At the end of the day, you feel very hungry and decide to go to the hotel’s Chinese restaurant for a delicious dinner, looking forward to an enjoyable dining experience. But after ordering, you find that the service robot delivered the wrong meal.” The robot’s humorous response was: “I’m sorry, I seem to have short-circuited and inconvenienced you just now. Would you like to give me another chance? I will re-deliver your meal.” The non-humorous response was: “ I’m sorry I couldn’t complete your task and for the inconvenience caused. I will continue to work hard to improve and deliver your meal again”.

To ensure attention and comprehension, participants answered an attention-check question after reading the contextual materials. Those who failed were excluded from subsequent analyses and not compensated. Participants who passed then completed a measure assessing their perception of consumption motivation to verify the effectiveness of the experimental manipulation. Consumer forgiveness was measured using a 4-item scale adapted from Finkel & Rusbult42. The 5-point Likert scale is used to score (from “1 = strongly disagree” to “5 = strongly agree”). The scale demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.878 in Study 1.

The manipulation of consumption motivation was assessed based on methods adapted from Whitley et al.41. Subjects in both motivation conditions rated the extent to which the described consumption context aligned with four dimensions: fun, pleasure, efficiency, and utility. Each dimension was measured using a single item, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Higher scores on fun and pleasure indicated a hedonic orientation, whereas higher scores on efficiency and utility indicated a utilitarian orientation. This measure also showed good reliability, with Cronbach’s α = 0.813. After completing the manipulation check, subjects reported their willingness to forgive the service failure and their level of involvement in the experimental scenario.

Results

Manipulation test

A one-way ANOVA was conducted in Study 1 to examine the effectiveness of the consumption motivation manipulation. The results indicated that participants in the hedonic motivation condition rated hedonic attributes significantly higher (M = 3.56, SD = 1.01) than those in the utilitarian motivation condition (M = 2.41, SD = 1.03; F[1, 316] = 101.77, p = 0.000 < 0.05). Conversely, participants in the utilitarian motivation condition rated utilitarian attributes significantly higher (M = 3.37, SD = 1.13) than those in the hedonic motivation condition (M = 2.48, SD = 1.13; F[1, 316] = 49.109, p = 0.000 < 0.001). These findings confirm that the manipulation of consumption motivation was successful, with a clear distinction between the two motivational contexts. Additionally, ANOVA results showed that participants in the humorous response condition perceived significantly higher levels of humor (M = 3.29, SD = 1.11) than those in the non-humorous response condition (M = 2.82, SD = 1.07, F[1, 318] = 14.921, p = 0.000 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.0450), confirming that the humorous response manipulation was also effective.

Interaction of consumption motivation and response strategies on consumer forgiveness

Study 1 was analysed by ANOVA with response strategy (humorous response = 1, non-humorous response = 0) and consumption motivation (hedonic motivation = 1, utilitarian motivation = 0) as independent variables and consumer forgiveness as dependent variable. The results of the data showed that the score of consumer forgiveness in the humorous response group (M = 3.46, SD = 0.98, 95% CI [3.09, 3.612]) was significantly higher than that in the non humorous response group (M = 3.24, SD = 0.81, 95%CI [3.116, 3.367], F[1, 316] = 4.729, p = 0.030 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.015); The willingness to forgive score of consumers with hedonic consumption motivation (M = 3.54, SD = 0.88, 95%CI [3.408, 3.680]) is significantly higher than that of utilitarian consumers (M = 3.16, SD = 0.89, 95% CI [3.020, 3.296]), F(1, 316) = 15.243, p = 0.000 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.046. The main effects of consumption motivation [F(1, 316) = 21.360, p = 0.000 < 0.05] and response strategy [F(1, 316) = 6.953, p = 0.009 < 0.05] both exist, and the interaction between response strategy and consumption motivation is significant, F(1, 316) = 126.580, P = 0.000<0.05, ηp2 = 0.286, and hypothesis H1 was tested.

Simple effects analysis indicated that under hedonic motivation, forgiveness was significantly higher in the humorous response group (M = 4.12, SD = 0.50, 95% CI [4.012, 4.230]) than in the non-humorous group (M = 2.97, SD = 0.79, 95% CI [2.794, 3.140]), t= −11.036, P = 0.000 < 0.05; Among utilitarian motivations, consumer forgiveness in the non-humorous response group (M = 3.52, SD = 0.74, 95% CI [3.355, 3.678]) was significantly higher than that in the humorous response group (M = 2.80, SD = 0.89, 95% CI [2.604, 2.995]), t = 5.545, P = 0.000<0.05 (see Table 1; Fig. 1). These results provide support for H1b and H2b.

Discussion

Study 1 experimentally manipulated consumption motivation and identified a significant interaction effect between response strategies and consumption motivation on consumer forgiveness. The results revealed that humorous responses significantly enhanced consumer forgiveness under hedonic consumption motivation, whereas non-humorous responses were more effective under utilitarian consumption motivation. Additionally, demographic variables such as gender and age had no significant effect on consumer forgiveness. Despite successfully verifying this interaction effect, Study 1 did not elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which response strategies and consumption motivation exert an influence on forgiveness. To address this gap, Study 2 was designed to further investigate the mediating processes underlying this interaction.

Study 2: the mediating role of perceived warmth and perceived competence

Study 2 was designed to examine the mediating roles of perceived warmth and perceived competence, while further validating the interaction effect between humorous response strategies and consumption motivation. Consistent with Study 1, a two-factor between-subjects design was employed, with 2 (consumption motivation: hedonic vs. utilitarian) × 2 (response strategy: humorous vs. non-humorous), resulting in four distinct experimental scenarios.

Methodology

Subjects

Subjects were again recruited via the Credamo platform on a compensated basis. A total of 256 individuals were initially recruited. After excluding 16 subjects who failed the attention check, 240 valid responses were retained for analysis. The sample consisted of 133 males (55.41%) and 107 females (44.59%), with a mean age of M = 29.77 years. All subjects reviewed the experimental instructions and provided informed consent prior to participation. Compensation was provided upon completion of the study.

To ensure statistical validity, a priori sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software. The analysis indicated that a sample size of N = 201 would achieve 85% statistical power at a significance level of α = 0.05, with an effect size of f = 0.25 across four groups. The actual sample size of 240 exceeded this estimate, supporting the statistical validity of the experimental results.

Design and implementation

Study 2 adopted a 2 × 2 between-subjects design, forming four experimental groups, and all participants were randomly assigned to one group. First, all participants were required to read contextual materials describing consumption motivations. For the experimental context, this study selected a science and technology museum as the new setting, aligning with the technological characteristics of AI service robots. The manipulation of consumption motivation remained consistent with Study 1.

The scenario materials for Study 2 were adapted from Jiang13. The scenario corresponding to utilitarian motivation was described as follows: “Imagine the following: You decide to visit a science and technology museum on the weekend with the goal of acquiring knowledge and using your time efficiently. You aim to learn about the latest technologies and utilize your time effectively for self-improvement. You are very interested in the exhibits in Hall D but are unfamiliar with the museum’s hall layout. Fortunately, the museum’s AI service robot has a guidance function, so you ask it to show you the way. However, the robot mistakenly leads you to Hall B.”

The hedonic motivation scenario was: “You decide to visit a science and technology museum on the weekend for an experiential outing, with the purpose of relaxing and enjoying your time. You look forward to having a pleasant visit, engaging with various interesting exhibits and activities. You are very interested in the exhibits in Hall D but are unfamiliar with the museum’s hall layout. Fortunately, the museum’s AI service robot has a guidance function, so you ask it to lead you. However, the robot mistakenly leads you to Hall B.”

The manipulation of the robot’s response strategy was referenced from Yang’s4 research. After reading the scenario, all subjects answered an attention-check question, Those who failed the attention monitoring were excluded from analysis and not compensated. Following the scenario materials, subjects completed a manipulation check to assess the effectiveness of the consumption motivation induction, using the same measures as in Study 1. The measures for the constructs were adapted from established scales. Specifically, consumer forgiveness was assessed using a four-item scale developed by Finkel & Rusbult42 (Cronbach’s α = 0.856). Perceived competence was measured using a 3-item scale adapted from Hofstede43 and Song et al.44, which assesses three dimensions: skill, efficiency, and capability. Sample items included: “This AI service robot is competent,” “This AI service robot is efficient,” and “This AI service robot is skillful” (Cronbach’s α = 0.842). Perceived warmth was evaluated with a 3-item scale derived from Bufquin45 and Fiske32, focusing on the dimensions of sincerity, friendliness, and warmth. Example items were: “The robot’s response made me feel friendly,” “The robot’s response made me feel warm,” and “The robot’s response was sincere” (Cronbach’s α = 0.855) (see Table 4).

Results

Manipulation tests

An ANOVA was performed in Study 2 to assess the effectiveness of the consumption motivation manipulation. The results indicated that participants in the hedonic motivation condition rated hedonic attributes significantly higher (M = 5.63, SD = 1.05) than those in the utilitarian motivation condition (M = 4.22, SD = 1.82; F[1, 236] = 54.358, p = 0.000 < 0.05). And the subjects in the utilitarian motivation condition rated the utilitarian attributes (M = 5.40, SD = 1.24) significantly higher than the subjects in the hedonic motivation condition rated the utilitarian attributes (M = 3.83, SD = 1.25; F[1, 236] = 94.806, p = 0.000 < 0.05).These results confirm that the manipulation of consumption motivation was successful, with a clear differentiation between the two motivational conditions.

Moreover, consumers’ sense of humor perceptions in humorous responses were significantly higher (M = 5.31, SD = 1.31) than in non-humorous responses (M = 4.16, SD = 1.59, F[1, 238] = 37.845, p = 0.000 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.137), suggesting that the manipulation of humorous responses in the experimental context was effective.

Interaction of consumption motivation and response strategies on consumer forgiveness

Study 2 was still an ANOVA with response strategy and consumption motivation as independent variables and consumer forgiveness as the dependent variable, and the data results showed that consumer forgiveness scores in the humorous response group (M = 5.19, SD = 1.14, 95% CI [4.983, 5.392]) were significantly higher than in the non-humorous response group (M = 4.90, SD = 1.01, 95% CI [4.719, 5.081]), F(1, 236) = 4.273, p = 0.04 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.018; Similarly, consumer forgiveness was significantly higher under hedonic motivation (M = 5.20, SD = 1.17, 95% CI [4.989, 5.407]) than under utilitarian motivation (M = 4.89, SD = 0.98, 95% CI [4.715, 5.064], F[1, 236] = 4.928, p = 0.027 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.020). A significant interaction was found between response strategy and consumption motivation, F(1, 236) = 57.783, p = 0.000 < 0.05, ηp2 = 0.197, and hypothesis H1 was again tested.

Further simple effects analyses were conducted and the results indicated that among hedonic motivation, consumer forgiveness intention scores were significantly higher for humorous responses (M = 5.81, SD = 0.90, 95% CI [5.585, 6.040]) than for non-humorous responses (M = 4.58, SD = 1.08, 95% CI [4.310, 4.857]), t= −6.775, p = 0.000 < 0.05; In contrast, under utilitarian motivation, forgiveness was significantly higher in the non-humorous response condition (M = 5.22, SD = 0.83, 95% CI [5.007, 5.426]) than in the humorous response condition (M = 4.56, SD = 1.01, 95% CI [4.307, 4.818]), t = 3.877, p = 0.000 < 0.05 (see Fig. 3).These results provide further support for H1a and H1b. In addition, independent sample t-test for gender (t= −0.182, sig.=0.856 > 0.05) and one-way ANOVA for age (F [3.236] = 0.206, P = 0.892 > 0.05) showed that in study 2, gender and age also had no significant effect on consumer forgiveness.

Main effects tests

A two-way ANOVA was conducted on the data to explore the effects of consumption motivation and response strategy on consumer forgiveness. The results indicated that response strategy showed significance (F = 5.386, p = 0.021 < 0.05), suggesting the presence of a main effect, while the consumption motivation main effect was equally significant (F = 6.195, p = 0.014 < 0.05).

Mediated effects test

The preceding analysis demonstrates that when consumers with different consumption motivations encounter service failures, distinct response strategies exert differential effects on consumer forgiveness. To further elucidate the underlying mechanism, this study examines the mediating roles of perceived competence and perceived warmth in shaping consumer forgiveness.

Consumption motivation was firstly analysed as a criterion-differentiated sample, with response strategy as the independent variable and perceived warmth and perceived competence as the dependent variables, respectively, and the results showed that perceived competence scores of non-humorous responses (M = 5.07, SD = 1.26, 95% CI [4.747, 5.385]) were significantly higher than that of humorous responses in hedonic consumption motivation contexts (M = 4.11, SD = 1.39, 95% CI [3.754, 4.456], t = 3.970, p = 0.000 < 0.05); perceived warmth scores were significantly higher for humorous responses (M = 5.45, SD = 1.13, 95% CI [5.164, 5.737]) than for non-humorous responses (M = 4.82, SD = 1.24, 95% CI [4.503, 5.131]), t=−2.923, p = 0.004 < 0.05). In contrast, non-humorous response strategies elicited higher perceived competence than humorous response strategies in utilitarian consumption motivation contexts (Mnon−humorous=5.25, SD = 1.16; Mhumorous=4.52, SD = 1.47; t = 3.040, p = 0.003 < 0.05).

Next, Bootstrap method was applied to test the mediating effect. The results showed that under the drive of utilitarian consumption motivation, perceived competence acted as a mediator between non-humorous responses and consumer forgiveness, with a mediation effect value of −0.157 (LLCI = −0.156, ULCI = −0.022, excluding 0), and perceived warmth didn’t act as a mediator (LLCI = −0.055, ULCI = 0.035, containing 0), and the hypothesis H2b was passed the test; under hedonic consumption motivation, the mediating role of perceived competence was not significant (LLCI=−0.081, ULCI = 0.025, containing 0), perceived warmth mediated the relationship between response strategies and consumer forgiveness, with a mediating effect value of 0.235 (LLCI = 0.023, ULCI = 0.210, excluding 0), as the confidence intervals for both the direct and indirect effects did not contain zero, the results indicate partial mediation and support hypothesis H2a (see Figs. 4 and 5).

Discussion

Study 2 confirmed the interaction effect between consumption motivation and response strategy on consumer forgiveness, while further uncovering the underlying mechanisms driving this effect. The experimental results demonstrate that perceived warmth and perceived competence jointly mediate the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategy in influencing consumer forgiveness. Specifically, under hedonic motivation, the positive effect of humorous responses on consumer forgiveness is partially mediated by perceived warmth; conversely, under utilitarian motivation, the positive effect of non-humorous responses on consumer forgiveness is fully mediated by perceived competence.

Study 3: the moderating role of the AI relationship paradigm orientation

Existing research indicates that the type of service failure committed by AI service robots can also influence consumer forgiveness. To enhance the robustness of the experimental findings, Study 3 expanded the scope of service failure contexts by incorporating a process failure scenario that differs from those employed in Studies 1 and 2. This design facilitates a further exploration of the mechanism underlying the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategy in shaping consumer forgiveness. Additionally, this study integrates a consumer-centric variable, namely AI relationship paradigm orientation, to examine its moderating effect on the aforementioned interaction.

Methodology

Subjects

Subjects were recruited via the Credamo platform. Those who failed the attention check were excluded in real time during data collection. The final sample consisted of 220 valid responses, with a mean age of 32 years (SD = 9.56). Among the subjects, 127 (57.7%) were female. All subjects provided informed consent prior to the experiment, and those whose data were deemed valid received compensation upon completion.

Design and implementation

Study 3 also employed a 2 × 2 between-subjects design, with subjects randomly assigned to one of the four experimental groups. Each subjecet was first presented with contextual materials adapted from Belanche et al.46, designed to induce either utilitarian or hedonic consumption motivation. The two scenarios were differentiated as follows:

Utilitarian Motivation Scenario:“You are an employee in the business department of your company and have been assigned an important task: to select a suitable restaurant for a business dinner with a partner. You carefully choose a highly rated restaurant called ‘Huaguang Restaurant.’ In the evening, you go to the restaurant with your colleagues and the partner. The reserved private room is primarily served by an AI service robot. However, after being seated for a minute, the robot still does not acknowledge your presence. You call out to attract its attention; it does not respond immediately but does so after a brief pause. You order according to your company leader’s instructions. While confirming the menu, you discover that the AI service robot misidentified two dishes. It apologizes after you point out the error, makes a prompt correction, and eventually serves you correctly”.

Hedonic Motivation Scenario:“You are an employee in the business department of your company and are planning to have dinner, chat, and relax with friends you haven’t seen in a long time after work. You carefully choose a highly rated restaurant called ‘Huaguang Restaurant.’ In the evening, you go to the restaurant with your friends. The reserved private room is primarily served by an AI service robot. However, after being seated for a minute, the robot still does not acknowledge your presence. You call out to attract its attention; it does not respond immediately but does so after a brief pause. You and your friends order together. While confirming the menu, you discover that the AI service robot misidentified two dishes. It apologizes after you point out the error, makes a prompt correction, and eventually serves you correctly”.

After reading the contextual materials, all subjects were required to answer an attention-check question (e.g., “What is the name of the restaurant you chose?” with the options 1 = Huaguang Restaurant and 2 = Xinglong Restaurant). Subjects who passed this check first completed the manipulation test questions. They then responded to measures assessing their willingness to forgive (Cronbach’s α = 0.816), perceived warmth (Cronbach’s α = 0.879), and perceived competence (Cronbach’s α = 0.891), using the same items and 7-point Likert scales as those employed in Study 2. Subsequently, all subjects reported their general AI relationship paradigm orientation using a seven-point semantic differential scale adapted from Li et al.47. This scale included three items: one ranging from “strictly business-specific” to “getting along as family and friends,” another from “formal and professional” to “informal and friendly,” and a third from “purely transactional” to “friendship-based” (Cronbach’s α = 0.855). Responses to these items were averaged and mean-centered to form a composite index representing relationship norms orientation, with higher scores indicating a stronger preference for a communal rather than transactional AI relationship paradigm.

Results

Manipulation test

An ANOVA revealed that participants in the hedonic motivation condition rated hedonic attributes significantly higher (M = 5.63, SD = 0.92) than those in the utilitarian motivation condition [M = 3.50, SD = 1.57, F(1, 216) = 149.053, p = 0.000 < 0.05]. Conversely, participants in the utilitarian motivation condition rated utilitarian attributes significantly higher (M = 5.92, SD = 0.90) than those in the hedonic motivation condition [M = 4.05, SD = 1.72, F(1, 216) = 102.345, p = 0.000 < 0.05]. These results confirm that the manipulation of consumption motivation was successful.

Interaction of consumption motivation and response strategies on consumer forgiveness

The results of the one-way ANOVA showed that the consumer forgiveness willingness score for the humorous response (M = 5.27, SD = 1.10) was significantly higher than that of the non-humorous response group (M = 4.62, SD = 1.29), F(1, 218) = 15.796, p = 0.000 < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.068, and that consumer forgiveness willingness scores of hedonistic consumption motivation (M = 5.18, SD = 1.51) were significantly higher than utilitarian consumer forgiveness scores (M = 4.71, SD = 0.83), F(1, 218) = 8.176, p = 0.005 < 0.01, ηp2 = 0.036. A two-way ANOVA further showed a significant interaction effect between response strategy and consumption motivation on forgiveness, F(1, 216) = 74.891, p = 0.000 < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.257. Simple effects analysis demonstrated that under hedonic motivation, forgiveness was significantly higher with humorous responses (M = 6.10, SD = 0.51) compared to non-humorous responses (M = 4.26, SD = 1.63), t = −7.964, p < 0.01. In contrast, under utilitarian motivation, non-humorous responses led to significantly higher forgiveness (M = 4.98, SD = 0.68) than humorous responses (M = 4.44, SD = 0.88), t = 3.631, p < 0.01 (see Fig. 6). Discussion

Study 3 once again validated the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategy on consumer forgiveness and the mediating role of perceived warmth and perceived competence, and revealed the moderating role played by AI relationship paradigm orientation in the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategy. The experimental results reveal that consumers with a transactional AI relationship orientation favour non-humorous responses when driven by utilitarian consumption motivation, whereas this tendency is not significant when driven by hedonic consumption motivation.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusions and theoretical contributions

Robotic services are increasingly prevalent in the tourism and hospitality industry and are expected to grow substantially in the future. However, service failures remain inevitable due to technological limitations, underscoring the need to investigate effective response strategies for AI service robots during recovery processes. It is essential to identify recovery methods that can mitigate the negative impacts of such failures.

This study examines AI service robots as independent recovery agents and explores the interaction between consumption motivation and response strategies following service failures. Through three causal experiments based on questionnaire surveys, the findings reveal that consumers driven by hedonic motivation show a greater willingness to forgive when AI service robots use humorous response strategies. Humor enhances their perceived enjoyment and facilitates forgiving behaviors. In contrast, utilitarian consumers prioritize problem-solving efficiency and practicality. For this group, humorous responses do not significantly promote forgiveness and may even reduce the perceived sincerity of the apology, thereby attenuating forgiveness intention. These consumers clearly prefer direct and efficient non-humorous responses.The differential effects are mediated through perceived warmth and perceived competence, and further moderated by AI relationship paradigm orientation. These results provide theoretical insights into the mechanisms underlying consumer forgiveness in human–robot interactions and offer practical implications for designing context-dependent recovery strategies.

This study offers three principal theoretical contributions to the field of AI service robots and apology-based service recovery strategies:

First, adopting a human–robot interaction perspective, this research establishes AI service robots as active agents in service recovery and investigates how the interaction between consumer motivation and robot response strategies shapes consumer forgiveness. Existing studies on robotic service recovery remain limited, with only preliminary attention given to certain types of response texts—such as empathetic messages—while other forms, including humorous responses, are still underexplored. By uncovering the interplay between humorous response strategies and consumption motivation in failure contexts, this work provides empirical support for the use of humor in AI service recovery, thereby enriching the theoretical foundation of robotic service research. These insights aid scholars in identifying effective recovery behaviors for AI service robots and in mitigating the adverse behavioral outcomes of service failures.

Second, this study elucidates the psychological mechanism through which consumption motivation and response strategies jointly influence consumer forgiveness, identifying two distinct mediating pathways. Specifically, under utilitarian motivation, non-humorous responses enhance forgiveness by strengthening perceived competence; under hedonic motivation, humorous responses operate through perceived warmth. These findings help clarify the boundary conditions of humorous response strategies in AI service recovery, pinpoint the contextual factors that determine the efficacy of robotic humor, and extend the theoretical understanding of AI service recovery. Consequently, this research broadens scholarly perspectives on how consumers interact with AI service robots in failure scenarios.

Finally, this paper examines variation in forgiveness across consumer segments based on consumption motivation (hedonic vs. utilitarian). By analyzing how each motivation type reacts to humorous responses, it provides deeper and more nuanced insights into consumer behavior. Furthermore, by introducing AI relationship paradigm orientation as a key moderating variable, the study reveals meaningful group differences in the main effect: consumers with a transactional AI relationship orientation exhibit higher forgiveness when receiving non-humorous responses. This underscores the critical role of individual differences in shaping the effectiveness of humorous recovery efforts and offers a foundational roadmap for future research to develop more differentiated, consumer-centric models of robot–consumer interaction.

Management implications

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for service enterprises. First, the research indicates that humorous response strategies employed by AI robots play a critical role in service recovery, particularly for consumers driven by hedonic motivation. Therefore, when developing AI service robots, companies should strive to balance technical performance with human-like interaction qualities. It is essential that robots not only respond with accuracy and efficiency, but are also capable of incorporating humor and anthropomorphic elements to better align with diverse consumer expectations and behavioral patterns.

Second, the study reveals that utilitarian consumers prioritize problem-solving and tend to prefer direct and efficient non-humorous responses, often viewing humorous replies with skepticism. Thus, businesses must carefully account for the characteristics and preferences of their target consumer segments when designing recovery strategies, especially when considering the use of humor. Sincerity and adaptability are crucial to avoid creating a negative perception of service quality among utilitarian-oriented customers. This underscores the need for companies to flexibly adapt AI response strategies to different consumer profiles and service contexts, enabling personalized interactions that enhance both service quality and consumer satisfaction.

Third, based on the empirical results, enterprises should tailor the development of AI service robots to fit specific service settings. For example, in business-oriented hotels or restaurants, AI robots should emphasize non-humorous characteristics, focusing on efficiency and problem-solving. In contrast, resorts or leisure-oriented hospitality venues should prioritize training robots in humorous response techniques, enabling them to deliver contextually appropriate and engaging interactions. By aligning AI development with market positioning and target consumer behavior, companies can enhance service delivery and more effectively meet customer needs.

limitations and future research perspectives

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, while the research focused on service scenarios within hotels and science museums, which are contexts relevant to current applications of AI service robots, the efficacy of humorous responses in other service environments remains unexplored. Second, the investigation centered specifically on self-deprecating humor, other forms of humor, such as affiliative or Self-enhancing humor, warrant further examination to determine their impact on consumer forgiveness. Moreover, this study relied primarily on scenario-based experiments rather than field studies. While this approach aids variable control, it cannot fully simulate the emotional intensity, social pressures, and contextual specifics of real-world service failures. This may create gaps between participants’ simulated reactions and actual behaviors in genuine encounters, limiting the results’ ecological validity. Finally, the cultural generalizability of our findings may be limited, as the effectiveness of self-deprecating humor by service robots likely varies across different cultural contexts.For instance, in high power-distance cultures, self-deprecation from a service robot might be perceived as a loss of face or incompetence, thereby reducing its effectiveness.

Building upon these limitations, several promising avenues for future research emerge. First, to address the concern of ecological validity, future studies should endeavor to conduct field experiments in real-world service environments where AI service robots are deployed. Measuring consumers’ actual behavioral responses to robotic humor during genuine service failures would significantly enhance the external validity of the findings. Additionally, future research should extend investigations to a broader range of service contexts, such as healthcare or luxury retail, to examine whether the effectiveness of humorous response strategies varies with situational characteristics.

Furthermore, future inquiry should explore a wider repertoire of humor types and their applicability across diverse cultural contexts. The perception and effectiveness of humor are profoundly shaped by cultural norms; for instance, self-deprecating humor or other humor forms may be interpreted differently in collectivistic versus individualistic cultures, or in high- versus low-power-distance societies. Thus, a critical direction for future research involves conducting cross-cultural comparisons to test how cultural dimensions (e.g., individualism/collectivism) moderate the relationship between humorous response strategies and consumer forgiveness. Such research would provide vital theoretical guidance for global enterprises aiming to develop culturally adaptive and localized AI service robots.
