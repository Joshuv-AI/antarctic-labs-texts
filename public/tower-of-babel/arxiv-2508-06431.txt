Nonparametric Learning Non-Gaussian Quantum States of Continuous Variable Systems Preprint: APS/123-QED
Liubov Markovich, Xiaoyu Liu, Jordi Tura

Abstract
Continuous-variable quantum systems are foundational to quantum computation, communication, and sensing. While traditional representations using wave functions or density matrices are often impractical, the tomographic picture of quantum mechanics provides an accessible alternative by associating quantum states with classical probability distribution functions called tomograms. Despite its advantages, including compatibility with classical statistical methods, tomographic method remain underutilized due to a lack of robust estimation techniques. This work addresses this gap by introducing a non-parametric kernel quantum state estimation (KQSE) framework for reconstructing quantum states and their trace characteristics from noisy data, without prior knowledge of the state. In contrast to existing methods, KQSE yields estimates of the density matrix in various bases, as well as trace quantities such as purity, higher moments, overlap, and trace distance, with a near-optimal convergence rate of O ~ ​ ( T − 1 ) \tilde{O}\bigl(T^{-1}\bigr) , where T T is the total number of measurements. KQSE is robust for multimodal, non-Gaussian states, making it particularly well suited for characterizing states essential for quantum science.



Introduction.
Continuous-variable (CV) quantum states describe systems with observables taking values in continuous spectra
Serafini 2017
. In quantum optics, their realizations, known as qumodes, serve as CV counterparts to qubits, as light fields naturally exhibit continuous quadrature degrees of freedom. CV platforms support a wide range of quantum information tasks, including computation, communication, and sensing
Lau and Plenio 2016
;
Arrazola et al. 2019
;
Killoran et al. 2019
;
Bromley et al. 2020
;
Jia et al. 2025
;
Nielsen et al. 2025
.
As a result, significant effort has been devoted to learning CV quantum states
Wu et al. 2024
;
Mele et al. 2024
;
Holevo 2024
, with particular progress for Gaussian and mildly non-Gaussian states
Walschaers 2021
, establishing tight bounds on the trace distance in terms of first moments. Alternatively, shadow
tomography for truncated CV systems or energy constraint states is used
Becker et al. 2024
;
Aaronson 2018
;
Volkoff et al. 2021
.

Due to the continuous nature of qumodes, the density matrix formalism is often impractical. However, CV states can be described in phase space by joint quasiprobability distribution functions (QPDFs), such as the Wigner, Q and P‐functions
Wigner 1932
;
Husimi 1940
;
Cahill and Glauber 1969
. These QPDFs are related via one-to-one transforms
Man’ko and Markovich 2020
providing a complete description of the state. However, since position
\bm{q}
and momentum
\bm{p}
operators cannot be measured simultaneously, we lack a pair of observable random variables to reconstruct their joint QPDFs. Instead, quantum state tomography
Smithey et al. 1993
;
Lvovsky and Raymer 2009
;
Babichev et al. 2004
;
Wallentowitz and Vogel 1995
measures a finite number of rotated quadratures
\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}=\mu\bm{q}+\nu\bm{p}
at various phase-space directions
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
. Using these statistics, the desired QPDF is reconstructed.

The Wigner function is reconstructed by inverting the Radon transform of quadrature distributions estimated via histograms and maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE)
Leonhardt and Paul 1995
;
Vogel and Risken 1989
;
Banaszek 1998
. This process is sensitive to sampling noise and the inversion of the Radon transform can be ill-posed, necessitating the use of regularization
Vogel and Risken 1989
;
Lvovsky and Raymer 2009
. Detector inefficiencies blur nonclassical features such as Wigner negativity
D’Ariano et al. 2007
.
These propagate into the reconstructed density matrix, leading to loss of nonclassicality, reduced purity, or even nonphysical states
Lvovsky and Raymer 2009
;
Hradil 1997
.
To avoid such instabilities, modern experiments reconstruct the state directly from quadrature data using parametric methods
that guarantee physicality
Christ et al. 2009
;
Wu et al. 2024
;
Flühmann and Home 2020
;
Jia et al. 2025
. The quadrature
X_{\mu,\nu}
is a random variable dependent on the measurement settings
\mu,\nu
, and its PDF
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
, called the symplectic tomogram, is informationally complete
Amosov et al. 2008
, uniquely specifying the density operator
\bm{\rho}
. Hence, every CV state can be fully described by the classical PDF, i.e., the tomogram, of its quadratures
Mancini et al. 1996
;
Ibort et al. 2009
;
Man’ko and Man’ko 1997
;
Dodonov et al. 1974
;
Chernega and Man’ko 2023
;
Dudinets et al. 2024
;
Man’ko and Markovich 2020
.

In this Letter, we propose a state reconstruction and trace characteristic estimation method based on nonparametric kernel density estimation (KDE)

and a kernel characteristic function estimation (KCFE), called
kernel quantum state estimation

Kernel Quantum State Estimation.
We consider a quantum state in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, represented by the density matrix operator
\bm{\rho}
. By definition, it is Hermitian, positive semi-definite, and has unit trace.
The connection between
\bm{\rho}
and its symplectic tomogram is given by
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\mathrm{tr}\bigl[\bm{\rho}\,\delta(x\,\hat{1}-\mu\bm{q}-\nu\bm{p})\bigr]
and

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\!\iint\left[\int\!\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)e^{ix}\,dx\right]e^{-i(\mu\bm{q}+\nu\bm{p})}\,d\mu\,d\nu,

(2)

where
\delta(\cdot)
denotes the Dirac delta function of an operator.
The tomogram is a PDF (always nonnegative and normalized) of a continuous random variable
X_{\mu,\nu}
corresponding to the quadrature operator
\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}
(see Supplemental Material).

Consider identically prepared CV systems in an unknown state with density operator
\bm{\rho}
. For each system and a fixed setting
(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})
, performing
n
independent measurements yields independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables

\{X_{l,\mu_{i},\nu_{j}}\}
from an unknown PDF
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu_{i},\nu_{j})
.
We introduce the KDE of this tomogram as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{l=1}^{n}K_{\mu,\nu}\left(\frac{x-X_{l,\mu,\nu}}{h}\right).

(3)

Note that, following mathematical convention, in the following section we use hats for the estimators.
The kernel function
K_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot)
is typically chosen to be a PDF (e.g., Gaussian).
KDE estimates probability densities from finite samples without assuming any parametric form. By adjusting the bandwidth
h>0
, it can resolve complex and multimodal structures more accurately than histograms or MLE, which often suffer from binning artifacts or model bias
Chen 2017
. As KDE yields a valid PDF, it can be directly used to construct a physical estimator
\bm{\rho}_{n}
in (
2
).

The total variation distance
T(\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2})
between two tomograms is defined as half of the
L_{1}(\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2})=\int\left|\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{W}_{2}(x|\mu,\nu)\right|\,dx
.
Then:

Theorem 2
.

(Lower bound to closeness of any
states)
The trace distance
D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2})
between two CV quantum states is lower bounded by the total variation
T(\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2})
between their tomograms.

Unlike previous results limited to Gaussian states
Wu et al. 2024
;
Mele et al. 2024
;
Holevo 2024
, our inequality holds for arbitrary quantum states. The upper bound we derive is not tight in general and becomes formally infinite unless the total variation is either compactly supported or decays sufficiently fast at infinity, as is the case for PDFs with Gaussian tails. This implies that some prior knowledge about the PDF class is needed to obtain a tighter bound using our method. For Gaussian states, tighter upper bounds have been derived in recent works
Holevo 2024
;
Mele et al. 2024
based on the first two moments.

For the
L_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh},\mathcal{W})
distance, KDE achieves a convergence rate of
O(n^{-2/5})
. Then:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\bm{\rho}
be an unknown state.
The number of copies
n
of
\bm{\rho}
required to estimate it by the KDE method with precision
\varepsilon
in trace distance has to scale at least as
O(\varepsilon^{-5/2})
.

For the
L_{2}
distance (i.e., mean integrated squared error (MISE)), KDE achieves a rate of
O(n^{-4/5})
(see Supplemental Material), outperforming the optimal histogram rate of
O(n^{-2/3})
, but remaining slower than the parametric MLE rate of
O(n^{-1})

Silverman 2018
. This reflects the tradeoff for model flexibility: KDE requires only smoothness of the underlying density, without assuming a specific parametric form. The resulting tomograms can be directly used to reconstruct the quantum state via Eq. (
2
), but the convergence remains slower than
O(n^{-1})
. In the following, we show that incorporating the CF allows us to recover the optimal
O(n^{-1})
rate.

Kernel Characteristic Function Estimation.

One can rewrite (
2
) using the CF
\phi(t=1;\mu,\nu)
, which is the Fourier transform
e^{ixt}
of the tomogram. The CF always exists, fully characterizes the distribution, and satisfies
\phi(0)=1
,
|\phi(t)|\leq 1
, and
\phi(-t)=\phi^{\star}(t)
. In Ref.
Markovich et al. 2024
, we formulated conditions on the CF ensuring
\bm{\rho}
reconstruction. Here, we present its equivalent integral formulation:

Theorem 4
.

(Hermiticity, Normalization, Positivity)
The integral (
2
) defines the density operator corresponding to a quantum state if and only if the CF of the tomogram satisfies:

\displaystyle\phi(1;\mu,\nu)=\phi(-1;-\mu,-\nu)\quad\forall\mu,\nu,\quad\phi(1;0,0)=1,

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\phi(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{{\chi}}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu\leq 1,\quad\forall\ket{\chi}.

(4)

Similar result is provided in Ref.
Holevo and Ballentine 1984
in no connection to the tomographic formalism, and the function satisfying the left inequality in the condition (
4
) is named
\Delta
-positive defined.
We show that the density matrices
\bm{\rho}_{1}
and
\bm{\rho}_{2}
of two quantum states satisfy the noncommutative Parseval relation:

\displaystyle\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\phi_{1}(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu,

(5)

where
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are the CFs corresponding to the tomograms of the states.
If
\bm{\rho}_{1}=\bm{\rho}_{2}
the latter provides the purity, while

\text{tr}{\left(\bm{\rho}^{d}\right)}
and
\text{tr}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\bm{\rho}_{i}\right)}
are given in Supplemental Material.
Since the distance between two pure states is
D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2})=\sqrt{1-\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}}

Holevo 2024
, we conclude that it is fully determined by the CFs in Eq. (
32
).
Finally, in the coordinate representation, the elements of
\bm{\rho}
follow from the CF via Fourier transform

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\rho(y,y^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\phi\bigl(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime}\bigr)\exp\Bigl(-i\,\tfrac{\mu(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\Bigr)\,d\mu,

(6)

and similar expressions can be written in any basis.

Thus, knowing the CF of a state allows one to fully characterize it and efficiently estimate both the distance and overlap between states, forming the basis of our KQSE framework. To achieve optimal convergence rates, we introduce the nonparametric KCFE as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)=\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)

(7)

with the optimal bandwidth selection.
We see that the latter estimator is a product of two CFs:

\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp{(itX_{j,\mu,\nu})}

depends on the measured data, while

\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{\mu,\nu}(z)\exp{(ithz)}dz

depends on the kernel’s bandwidth parameter. The form of
\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)
is known analytically because the chosen
K_{\mu,\nu}(z)
has a known analytical expression.

Homodyne tomography typically neglects various losses in the detection process, such as mode mismatching and detector inefficiencies, which distort the observed distribution relative to the idealized case. In
Leonhardt and Paul 1995
, the following simplified error model is introduced:

\displaystyle Z\equiv^{d}\kappa X+(1-\kappa)Y,\quad 0<\kappa<1,

(8)

where
\equiv^{d}
denotes equality in distribution. Here,
\kappa
represents the overall detection efficiency, and
Y
is an independent noise variable, typically modeled as i.i.d. Gaussian. While the true signal-noise interaction may be nonlinear and the noise non-Gaussian, the analysis of such models lies beyond the scope of this paper.

Our method requires the CF of the signal variable
X
to be filtered from noise. If
X
and
Y
are independent, the PDF of their sum equals the convolution of the individual PDFs, and the CF of their sum is the product of individual CFs. Thus, if the CF of the noise
\phi_{Y}((1-\kappa)t)
is invertible, the CF of the target variable is given by
{\phi}_{X}(t)={\phi}_{Z}\left(\tfrac{t}{\kappa}\right)\phi_{Y}^{-1}\left(\tfrac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right)
,
\kappa\in(0,1)
,
where
\phi_{Z}(t)
is the CF of the observable
Z
.
A similar idea arises in the deconvolution of signal densities from noisy data
Stefanski and Carroll 1990
;
Fan 1991
;
Delaigle and Gijbels 2004
, where one typically assumes the CF of the noise satisfies the nonvanishing condition:
|\phi_{Y}(t)|>0
,
\forall t
. In this deconvolution formula, division by
\phi_{Y}(t)
is required, and zeros in the noise CF would render the inversion ill-posed or undefined. For instance, the CF of a normal distribution is strictly nonzero, ensuring stability, whereas distributions such as the uniform law have CFs that vanish at specific points, causing potential singularities.

Thus, the model (
78
) and the assumed noise distribution determine the form of the correction in the CF estimation. Using (
64
), we define the kernel estimate of the signal CF
\phi_{X}
filtered from the noise:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{X,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)=\hat{\phi}_{Z,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)\phi_{Y}^{-1}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{Z,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)\equiv\hat{\phi}_{Z,n}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa};\mu,\nu\right)\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right),

where
\phi_{Y}^{-1}
is known from the noise model, and
\phi_{K}
depends on the kernel choice.

Theorem 5
.

(Convergence of KCFE)
Let

be the KCFE of

The KCFE yields a discrete set of CF values, allowing only partial reconstruction of the integrals in Eqs. (
32

36

Conclusions.
We study CV quantum states within the framework of the probabilistic tomographic representation of quantum mechanics. Unlike QPDFs, the quantum tomogram is a genuine PDF, depending on a directly observable random variable. We prove that the trace distance between two quantum states is bounded from below by the total variation distance between their tomograms. We introduce a KDE for the tomogram and show that, given a single set of tomographic parameters
\mu,\nu
and
n=O(\varepsilon^{-5/2})
samples, one can obtain a lower bound on the trace distance with precision
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the CF of the tomogram fully determines the quantum state and all its trace-based quantities.
By proposing a novel KCFE, we introduce our KQSE method for reconstructing quantum states and their trace characteristics from noisy measurement data. Our estimator achieves a near-optimal convergence rate in squared distance of
\tilde{O}(1/T)
, where
T
denotes the total number of samples. The method is universal: it performs reliably for arbitrary states, including non-Gaussian multimodal ones, which are crucial for achieving universal quantum computation
Menicucci et al. 2006
;
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
;
Hanamura et al. 2024
, entanglement distillation
Yokoyama et al. 2013
, quantum error correction
Gottesman et al. 2001
;
Sivak et al. 2023
, and metrological advantage
Xu et al. 2022
.

In contrast to existing methods, our approach is independent of the shape of the tomographic distribution. While many non-Gaussian states retain Gaussian tails, our technique remains robust for any light-tailed PDF (and even for heavy-tailed distributions with a proper
K_{\mu,\nu}(\cdot)
selection
Markovich 2018
), which frequently arise in practical scenarios and are relevant for quantum modeling. Finally, the proposed nonparametric framework naturally extends to multivariate and dependent data settings, offering a promising direction for our future work.

S1. Operator Symbols and Tomographic Probability Representation of Quantum States

1.1. Quantizer and Dequantizer Formalism

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
the set of linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. In quantum mechanics, physical observables
A
correspond to Hermitian operators
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Such operator can be represented as a matrix with complex elements.
Consider a set of Hermitian positive definite operators
\bm{U}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, called the
dequantizer
, where
z
takes continuous values. We define the function
f_{A}(z)
as

\displaystyle f_{A}(z)=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{U}(z)).

(10)

This function, known as the
symbol
of operator
\bm{A}
, establishes a linear mapping from the operator space to the space of functions.
If (
10
) establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the symbol and the operator, the inverse transform is given by

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\int f_{A}(z)\bm{D}(z)dz,

(11)

where the
quantizer
operators
\bm{D}(z)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
enable the reconstruction of
\bm{A}
from its symbol
f_{A}(z)
.
If the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
has finite dimension
d
, the variable
z
is discrete, and
\bm{U}(z),\bm{D}(z)
are linear bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. If
z
takes
N
values (outcomes), (
11
) becomes

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}f_{A}(k)\bm{D}(k).

(12)

For the inverse map to exist, the set of dequantizers
\bm{U}(z)
must contain
d^{2}
linearly independent operators, with
N\geq d^{2}
.
It is easy to check that the pair of quantizer and dequantizer operators satisfy the condition

\displaystyle\text{tr}{(\bm{U}(z^{\prime})\bm{D}(z))}=\delta(z-z^{\prime}).

(13)

The choice of the quantizer-dequantizer pair for
\bm{A}
is not unique. In general, the only requirement for the dequantizer is to be Hermitian, but in this case, the symbol is not a probability density function (PDF). For example, it can take negative values, as the Wigner function. This does not allow for a direct probabilistic interpretation of measurements. Imposing the additional condition that
\bm{U}(z)
is positive semi-definite and form a resolution of the identity on
\mathcal{H}
makes the dequantizer a positive-operator valued measure (POVM). In this case, (
10
) defines the PDF of
z
.

1.2. Tomographic Probability Density Function

We consider the quantum state in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, represented by a positive Hermitian operator
\bm{\rho}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, known as the
density matrix operator
. By definition,
\bm{\rho}
is Hermitian, positive semi-definite, and has unit trace. The kernel of
\bm{\rho}
is given by

\rho{(\psi,\psi^{\prime})}=\bra{\psi}\bm{\rho}\ket{\psi^{\prime}}

where
\ket{\psi}
and
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}
are quantum states.

Let us define the dequantizer and quantizer as

\displaystyle\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)=\delta\left(\hat{1}x-\mu\bm{q}-\nu\bm{p}\right),\quad\bm{D}_{\mu,\nu}(x)=(2\pi)^{-1}\exp{(i(x\hat{1}-\mu\bm{q}-\nu\bm{p}))},

(14)

where
\mu,\nu\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\bm{q}
,
\bm{p}
are position and momentum operators. We verify that
\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)
is Hermitian, positive semi-definite, and satisfies

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)dx=\hat{1}.

(15)

Here, the Dirac delta function of an operator is given by

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{A})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{ik\bm{A}}dk.

(16)

Thus,
\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)
forms a POVM.
Using (
14
) in the correspondence (
10
), the mapping of
\bm{\rho}
onto a parametric set of PDFs, depending on two real parameters
\mu
and
\nu
, is given by
Mancini et al. 1996
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\text{tr}{\bm{\rho}\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)}=\langle x;\mu,\nu|\bm{\rho}|x;\mu,\nu\rangle,\quad\!\!\bm{\rho}=\iiint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)\bm{D}_{\mu,\nu}(x)dxd\mu d\nu,

(17)

where
|x;\mu,\nu\rangle
is an eigenvector of the Hermitian operator

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}=\mu\bm{q}+\nu\bm{p},

(18)

called the
quadrature
. A continuous random variable
X
corresponds to (
18
), with sample space
S_{X}\subset\mathbb{R}
.
The function
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
, known as the
symplectic tomogram
, represents the
conditional PDF
over
S_{X}
, dependent on
\mu
and
\nu
. As a PDF, the tomogram is nonnegative and normalized:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)dx=1.

(19)

Since the delta function is homogeneous,
\delta(\lambda y)=|\lambda|^{-1}\delta(y)
, for any constant
\lambda
, the tomogram inherits the homogeneity property:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\lambda x|\lambda\mu,\lambda\nu)=|\lambda|^{-1}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu),\quad\mathcal{W}(y|\lambda\mu,\lambda\nu)=|\lambda|^{-1}\mathcal{W}(y\lambda^{-1}|\mu,\nu),\quad y=\lambda x.

(20)

Since tomograms are PDFs, the
total variation distance
between
\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\mu,\nu)
and
\mathcal{W}_{2}(x|\mu,\nu)
over a continuous variable
x
is defined as

\displaystyle T(\mathcal{W}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\int\Big|\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\mu,\nu)-\mathcal{W}_{2}(x|\mu,\nu)\Big|\,dx.

(21)

By Scheffé’s theorem, the
L_{1}
-distance between two PDFs equals twice the total variation:

L_{1}(W_{1},W_{2})=2T(W_{1},W_{2})
. The total variation distance ranges from
0
to
1
, where
0
indicates identical distributions and
1
means no overlap.

Our result establishes a connection between the total variation of tomograms and the trace distance of the corresponding density operators. The following theorem holds:

Theorem 6
.

The trace distance
D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}{|\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}|}=1/2\|\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}\|_{1}
between the density matrices
\bm{\rho}_{1}
and
\bm{\rho}_{2}
of two quantum CV states is

\displaystyle T(\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\mu,\nu),\mathcal{W}_{2}(x|\mu,\nu))\leq D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2}).

(22)

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle T(\mathcal{W}_{1}(x|\mu,\nu),\mathcal{W}_{2}(x|\mu,\nu))=\frac{1}{2}\int|\text{tr}(\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)(\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}))|dx

(23)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int\text{tr}(\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)|\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}|)dx=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left(\int\bm{U}_{\mu,\nu}(x)dx|\bm{\rho}_{1}-\bm{\rho}_{2}|\right)=D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2}),

where we used the POVM properties of the dequantizer.
∎

Example 1
.

The wave function of the excited state of the harmonic oscillator (HO) is

\displaystyle\Psi_{m}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\pi}\sqrt{2^{m}m!}}e^{-y^{2}/2}H_{m}(y),

(24)

where
H_{m}(y)
is the Hermite polynomial. The tomogram and CF of the excited oscillator state are the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{m}(x|\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(x|\alpha)\frac{1}{2^{m}m!}H^{2}_{m}\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right),\quad{\phi}_{m}(1;\alpha)={\phi}_{0}(1;\alpha)L_{m}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\right),

(25)

where the tomogram and CF of the ground state are

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(x|\alpha)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha}\exp\!\Bigl[-\tfrac{x^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\Bigr],\quad{\phi}_{0}(t;\alpha)=\exp\!\Bigl(-\tfrac{t^{2}\alpha^{2}}{4}\Bigr),\quad\alpha\equiv\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}}.

(26)

Here the Laguerre polynomial series decomposition is

\displaystyle L_{m}(x)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k}m!}{(k!)^{2}(m-k)!}x^{k}.

(27)

We conclude that any quantum state formed as a superposition of HO eigenstates exhibits Gaussian tails, both in its wavefunction and in the corresponding CF with respect to the parameters
\mu
and
\nu
. A more detailed analysis of the families of probability distributions arising in experimental settings is provided in Ref.
Markovich et al. 2024
.

1.3. Characteristic Function of the Tomogram

In
Markovich et al. 2024
, the
characteristic function
(CF) of the tomogram
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
is defined as

\displaystyle\phi(t;\mu,\nu)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)e^{itx}dx.

(28)

Since the PDF is always integrable, the CF always exists. It is called the CF because it fully characterizes the distribution.
The function
\phi(t)
is continuous for all real
t
and satisfies
\phi(0)=1
,
|\phi(t)|\leq 1
, and
\phi(-t)=\phi^{\star}(t)
. By Theorem 1 in
Pólya 1949
, if the corresponding probability density is even and continuous everywhere except possibly at
x=0
, then
\phi(t)
has additional properties: it is real-valued,
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\phi(t)=0
, even, and convex for
t>0
.

From (
20
) one can derive the following property

\displaystyle\phi(t;\lambda\mu,\lambda\nu)=\phi(t\lambda;\mu,\nu),\quad\phi(t\lambda^{-1};\lambda\mu,\lambda\nu)=\phi(t;\mu,\nu).

(29)

Using the CF, one can rewrite (
17
) as follows:

\displaystyle\phi(1;\mu,\nu)=\text{tr}{\bm{\rho}e^{i\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}}},\quad\bm{\rho}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(1;\mu,\nu)e^{-i\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}}d\mu d\nu.

(30)

As noted earlier, for a symmetric random variable around the origin, the CF is real and even. Since the Fourier transform of a real even function remains real and even, the resulting operator also inherits these properties.

Using the fact that
\text{tr}{e^{-i\bm{X}_{\mu,\nu}}}=\text{tr}(e^{-i(\nu\bm{p}+\mu\bm{q})})=2\pi e^{i\frac{\mu\nu}{2}}\delta(\mu)\delta(\nu)
holds, one can derive the connections between the trace characteristics of the quantum state and the CF of the tomogram:

\displaystyle\text{tr}{\bm{\rho}}=\phi(1;0,0),

(31)

\displaystyle\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}^{2})}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\phi(1;\mu,\nu)|^{2}d\mu d\nu,

\displaystyle\text{tr}{\left(\bm{\rho}^{d}\right)}\!=\!\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\phi(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{i})\phi^{\star}\left(1;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\mu_{i},\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\nu_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\!\Biggl[-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d-1}(\mu_{j}\nu_{k}-\nu_{j}\mu_{k})\Biggr]d\mu_{1}\dots d\mu_{d-1}d\nu_{1}\dots d\nu_{d-1}.

One can see that the density matrices
\bm{\rho}_{1}
and
\bm{\rho}_{2}
of two quantum states satisfy the
noncommutative Parseval relation
:

\displaystyle\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\phi_{1}(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\text{tr}{\left[\bm{\rho}_{1}e^{i(\mu\bm{q}+\nu\bm{p})}\right]}\left[\text{tr}{\left[\bm{\rho}_{2}e^{i(\mu\bm{q}+\nu\bm{p})}\right]}\right]^{\star}d\mu d\nu,

where
\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{1},\nu_{1})
and
\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{1},\nu_{1})
are the CFs corresponding to the tomograms of the states.
This result can be easily extended to the product of
d
density matrices:

\displaystyle\text{tr}{\left(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}\bm{\rho}_{i}\right)}\!=\!\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d-1}\phi_{i}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{i})\phi_{d}^{\star}\Bigl(1;\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\mu_{j},\,\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}\nu_{j}\Bigr)

(33)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp\!\Biggl[-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq d-1}(\mu_{j}\nu_{k}-\nu_{j}\mu_{k})\Biggr]d\mu_{1}\,d\mu_{2}\cdots d\mu_{d-1}\,d\nu_{1}\,d\nu_{2}\cdots d\nu_{d-1},

where
\phi_{i}(t;\mu_{i},\nu_{i})
are the CFs of the corresponding states
\bm{\rho}_{i}
. The trace from the square rooted density matrices can be expressed in terms of CF as

\displaystyle\text{tr}{\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{1}}\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\text{tr}{\left(\!\Big[\!\!\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{1},\nu_{1})\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{2},\nu_{2})e^{-i(\nu_{1}\bm{p}+\mu_{1}\bm{q})}\,e^{-i(\nu_{2}\bm{p}+\mu_{2}\bm{q})}d\mu_{1}d\nu_{1}d\mu_{2}d\nu_{2}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}\!\right)},

providing the upper and lover bounds in the following inequality for the trace distance
Kholevo 1972
:

\displaystyle 1-\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{1}}\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{2}}\leq D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2})\leq\sqrt{1-\left(\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{1}}\sqrt{\bm{\rho}_{2}}\right)^{2}}.

(34)

The kernel element
\rho(\chi,\chi)=\langle\chi|\bm{\rho}|\chi\rangle=\text{tr}{[\bm{\rho}\bm{\rho}_{\chi}]}
of the density operator in any state
\ket{\chi}
representation can be written as

\displaystyle\rho(\chi,\chi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\phi(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{{\rho}_{\chi}}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu,

(35)

where
\phi_{{\rho}_{\chi}}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)
is the CF for the tomogram corresponding to the quantum state
\ket{\chi}
. Since
\bm{\rho}
is a density matrix, it is positive semidefinite and its matrix elements can not be bigger than one, then
0\leq\rho(\chi,\chi)\leq 1
, holds
\forall\ket{\chi}\neq 0
.

In the coordinate representation (
\rho(y,y^{\prime})=\langle y|\bm{\rho}|y^{\prime}\rangle
), the kernel elements of
\bm{\rho}
can be written as a Fourier transform of the CF:

\displaystyle\rho(y,y^{\prime})\!=\!\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})\exp{\left(-i\frac{\mu(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}d\mu\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\overline{\phi}\left(1;\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2},y-y^{\prime}\right),

(36)

where
\overline{\phi}(1;\tilde{\mu},{\nu})
denotes the Fourier transform of
\phi(1;\mu,\nu)
.
Hence, to reconstruct the latter kernel of the density matrix operator, one needs to estimate the CF for a fixed parameter
\nu=y-y^{\prime}
, varying the parameter
\mu
.

1.4. Tomograms for Pure States

For the density operator
\bm{\rho}=\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi}
of the pure state
\ket{\psi}
, the relation (
17
) converts into a simpler one

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2\pi|\nu|}|(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}\Psi)(x)|^{2},\quad(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}\Psi)(x)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(y)\exp{\left(\frac{i\mu}{2\nu}y^{2}-\frac{ix}{\nu}y\right)}dy.

(37)

Here
(\mathcal{F}_{\mu,\nu}\Psi)(x)
is a two-parameter set of unitary transforms in the space
\mathcal{H}
.
One can see that if
\mu=0
and
\nu=1
, the latter is a Fourier transform.

In the case of general pure states there is exact expression for the trace distance between two density operators
Holevo 2024
:

\displaystyle D(\bm{\rho}_{1},\bm{\rho}_{2})=\sqrt{1-\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint\phi_{1}(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu}.

(38)

In this case the knowledge of the CFs can provide the trace distance between two pure quantum states.

1.5. Optical Tomograms

The optical tomogram is a special case of the symplectic tomogram. Using the change of variables

\displaystyle\mu=r\cos\theta,\quad\nu=r\sin\theta,\quad x=ry,\quad r\in\mathbb{R}^{+},\quad\theta\in[0,2\pi],

(39)

we can write the density operator (
17
) as follows

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}r\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})\exp{(ir(y-\bm{q}\cos{\theta}-\bm{p}\sin{\theta}))}dydrd\theta

(40)

where
\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})
is called
optical tomogram
.

Experimentally one uses the homodyne detection for a state reconstruction. The quantum system to be measured is laser light with a fixed frequency
Wu et al. 1998
;
Lvovsky 2004
;
D’Ariano et al. 2007
. A coherent laser source provides the input light field. The key component of the setup is a homodyne detector. The input signal is mixed by a
50-50
beam-splitter with a strong local oscillator, which is coherent with the input field
and is in a strong coherent state. The relative phase
\theta\in[0,\pi]
between the signal and the local oscillator can be adjusted and is varied with uniform probability. Two photo-detectors with efficiency
\eta
measure the field and the resulting photo-currents are subtracted electronically yielding the output signal

\displaystyle I_{H}(\eta)=\langle\bm{Y}_{\theta}\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{1-\eta}{2\eta}}\langle u_{\theta}+v_{\theta}\rangle,

(41)

where the modes
u
and
v
are in the vacuum state
and

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{\theta}=\cos{\theta}\bm{q}+\sin{\theta}\bm{p},

(42)

are the field’s quadrature amplitudes. The goal is to reconstruct the quantum state of the light field based on measurements
I_{H}(\eta)
which are obtained by varying the relative phase
\theta
between the input signal and the local oscillator in the homodyne detector.
The phase is a parameter controlled by the experimenter.

By varying the local oscillator phase

\theta
continuously over a range that is typically
[0,\pi]
, one can obtain a series of measurements for different quadratures
Y_{\theta}
. Each fixed value of

\theta
gives a marginal PDF
\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})

for the quadrature
Y_{\theta}
.

One can see that the optical tomogram and the symplectic tomogram, are connected by the transformation (
39
), that gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|r|}\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})\equiv\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)

(43)

That is why all the results deduced for the symplectic tomogram are easily translated to the language of optical tomograms and vice versa. The CF of the optical tomogram
\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})
is introduced as

\displaystyle\phi(r;\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})e^{iry}dy.

(44)

Then, we can immediately write the density operator, using the optical tomogram, as

\displaystyle\bm{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}|r|\phi(r;\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})\exp{(-ir\bm{Y}_{\theta})}drd\theta,

(45)

where we use the fact that
\cos(\theta+\pi)=-\cos\theta
and
\sin(\theta+\pi)=-\sin\theta
, so
\bm{Y}_{\theta+\pi}=-\bm{Y}_{\theta}
. Moreover, from (
29
) we can see that
\phi(r;\cos{(\theta+\pi)},\sin{(\theta+\pi)})=\phi(-r;\cos{(\theta)},\sin{(\theta)})
holds. One can conclude that it is enough to vary the angle
\theta
only in
(0,\pi)
to reconstruct the state, that is exactly done in the experiment mentioned before.

1.6. From Characteristic Function to Wigner Function

In quantum optics, a widely used connection between the Wigner function and the quantum tomogram by Radon transformation exists:

\displaystyle W(q,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)\exp\left(i(x-\mu q-\nu p)\right)\,dx\,d\mu\,d\nu,

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathcal{W}(y|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})\exp\left[ir\left(y-q\cos{\theta}-p\sin{\theta}\right)\right]rdydrd\theta.

One can rewrite it in terms of the CF as follows:

\displaystyle W(q,p)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_{\mathcal{W}}(1;\mu,\nu)\exp\left(-i(\mu q+\nu p)\right)\,d\mu\,d\nu,

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\phi_{\mathcal{W}}(r|\cos{\theta},\sin{\theta})\exp\left[-ir\left(q\cos{\theta}+p\sin{\theta}\right)\right]rdrd\theta.

We can conclude, that the CF of a symplectic tomogram is connected to the Wigner function of a quantum state as follows:

\displaystyle W(q,p)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\int\phi(1;\mu,\nu)e^{-i(\mu q+\nu p)}d\mu d\nu.

(48)

Using the Wigner function, similar to (
36
), the kernel element can be written as:

\displaystyle\rho(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}W\left(\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2};p\right)\exp{\left(-ip(y-y^{\prime})\right)}dp\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\overline{W}\left(\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2};y-y^{\prime}\right),

(49)

where
\overline{W}(q,\omega)
denotes the Fourier transform of the Wigner function. One can conclude that the following connection of CF and the Wigner function holds

\displaystyle\overline{\phi}\left(1;\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2},y-y^{\prime}\right)=\overline{W}\left(\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2};y-y^{\prime}\right),\quad\forall y,y^{\prime}.

(50)

In many traditional methods, the reconstruction of the quantum state typically proceeds via an intermediate step, first obtaining the Wigner function. In contrast, our approach bypasses this intermediary by directly estimating the tomogram or its CF from the measured data. This direct reconstruction not only simplifies the overall procedure but also reduces potential sources of error that may accumulate during intermediate transformations.

S2. Nonparametric Kernel Tomogram Estimation

Let us have
n
i.i.d. data points
\{X_{j,\mu,\nu}\}_{j=1}^{n}
from an unknown PDF
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
.
The kernel density estimate (KDE) of the tomogram
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
is
Silverman 2018
;
Tsybakov 2009
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{nh}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{x-X_{j,\mu,\nu}}{h}\right).

(51)

In this section, we denote the estimates with hats and use the notation
\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x)=\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)
.
Here
K(x)
is the kernel function, which is generally smooth, and
h>0
is the bandwidth parameter, which controls the width of the kernel.
A kernel function is generally symmetric and normalised
\int K(x)dx=1
. Moreover

\lim\limits_{{x\to-\infty}}K(x)=\lim\limits_{{x\to+\infty}}K(x)=0
, holds.
These requirements are needed to guarantee that the KDE
\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x)
is a PDF.

The kernel
K(x)
is typically chosen from the set of PDFs. The three most common kernel functions used are the Gaussian, Epanechnikov, and rectangular (uniform) kernels. These kernels have different shapes and characteristics, and the choice of kernel depends on the specific characteristics of the data and the goals of the analysis.
The Epanechnikov is a special kernel that has the lowest (asymptotic) mean square error.

The mean squared error (MSE) of the KDE is
Silverman 2018
;
Tsybakov 2009
:

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x_{0}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x_{0})-\mathcal{W}(x_{0}))^{2}]=\text{Bias}^{2}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x_{0}))+\text{Var}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x_{0}))

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{h^{4}}{4}({\mathcal{W}}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}\mu^{2}_{K}+\frac{1}{nh}{\mathcal{W}}(x_{0})\sigma^{2}_{K}+o(h^{4})+o\left(\frac{1}{nh}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(h^{4})+O\left(\frac{1}{nh}\right),

where
\mu_{K}=\int y^{2}K(y)\,dy\neq 0
and
\sigma^{2}_{K}\equiv\int K^{2}(y)\,dy
.
The bandwidth
h
plays a crucial role in the balance between bias and variance in the estimation. The bandwidth minimizing the MSE is given by

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{opt}}(x_{0})=\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{{\mathcal{W}}(x_{0})\sigma^{2}_{K}}{({\mathcal{W}}^{\prime\prime}(x_{0}))^{2}\mu^{2}_{K}}\right)^{1/5}=O(n^{-1/5}).

(53)

For this choice of smoothing bandwidth the MSE has the rate

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\hat{f}_{nh_{\mathrm{opt}}}(x_{0}))=O(n^{-4/5}).

(54)

In the above analysis, we only consider a single point
x_{0}
. A straightforward generalization is the mean integrated square error (MISE):

\displaystyle\text{MISE}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh})=\int\text{MSE}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x))\,dx.

(55)

One can show that

\displaystyle\text{MISE}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh})=\frac{\mu^{2}_{K}}{4}h^{4}\int|\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{K}}{nh}+o(h^{4})+o\left(\frac{1}{nh}\right)=O(h^{4})+O\left(\frac{1}{nh}\right).

(56)

The optimal smoothing bandwidth is often chosen by minimizing the first two terms of the latter expression. Namely, we have

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{opt}}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{4\sigma^{2}_{K}}{\mu^{2}_{K}\int|\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}(x)|^{2}\,dx}\right)^{1/5}=O(n^{-1/5}),

(57)

providing the optimal rate

\displaystyle\text{MISE}(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh_{\mathrm{opt}}}(x|\mu,\nu))\equiv\mathbb{E}\left[\int\left(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh_{\mathrm{opt}}}(x|\mu,\nu)-{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu,\nu)\right)^{2}dx\right]=O(n^{-4/5}).

(58)

Note that the bandwidth depends on the unknown derivatives of the density. To overcome that difficulty, a variety of automatic, data-based methods have been developed for the bandwidth selection. Several review studies have been undertaken to compare their efficiencies, with the general consensus that the plug-in selectors and cross validation
Hall et al. 1992
selectors are the most useful over a wide range of data sets.

The classical result (
58
) is obtained minimizing the
L_{2}
- norm distance and gives little information about how close the estimate is to the true PDF in
L_{1}
- norm, that is required for total variation (
21
). However, in Ref.
Hall and Wand 1988
under condition on the PDF to be bounded and having at least
p
bounded, continuous and integrable derivatives,
the kernel estimate achieves the following rate with respect to
L_{1}
- norm:

\displaystyle T(\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh_{u}}(x|\mu,\nu),{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu,\nu))\equiv\frac{1}{2}\!\ \int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh_{u}}(x|\mu,\nu)-{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu,\nu)|\right]dx=O\left(n^{-\frac{p}{2p+1}}\right)\!,

(59)

with the optimal bandwidth
h_{u}=O\left(n^{-1/(p+1)}\right)
.
The kernel function used in (
51
) is selected to be of order
p
- that is
\int|z^{p}K(z)|dz<\infty
,
K(z)
is bounded and its
p-1
moments are zero, while its
p
-th moment is finite. For example, second-order kernels, e.g., Gaussian or Epanechnikov, has the property that it integrates to one and its first moment (mean) is zero, while its second moment (variance) is finite. For such kernels the rate of convergence in
L_{1}
- norm is
O(n^{-2/5})
. Higher-order kernels (e.g., biweight or triweight) can have more than two zero moments, approximating smoother density functions more effectively. However, they are less frequently applied due to their complexity and potential for negative values in the density estimate.

KDE can perform poorly at the boundaries of the domain, as kernels may be only partially within the data range. Moreover, for large datasets, KDE can be computationally intensive, as it requires summing over all data points for each evaluation of the
PDF. Techniques like Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) and kernel density trees help to mitigate this issue.

2.1. Nonparametric Characteristic Function Estimation

For our approach, the estimation of the CF is needed to characterize the quantum state (
30
), the kernel function (
36
) and the trace characteristics (
31
) mentioned in the previous section.
We define the kernel CF estimate (KCFE) as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)e^{itx}dx=\frac{1}{nh}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{\mu,\nu}\left(\frac{x-X_{j,\mu,\nu}}{h}\right)e^{{i}tx}dx

(60)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{itX_{j,\mu,\nu}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{\mu,\nu}(z)e^{ithz}dz.

The probability density estimate can be introduced as the sum of the Dirac delta functions built on a variational series of vectors of samples
X

Chentsov 1981
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta(x-X_{j,\mu,\nu}).

(61)

Its CF can be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu,\nu)e^{itx}\,dx=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta(x-X_{j,\mu,\nu})e^{itx}dx=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{itX_{j,\mu,\nu}}.

(62)

Assuming that the kernel function
K_{\mu,\nu}(z)
is also selected to be a PDF, we can introduce its CF

\displaystyle\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)\equiv\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}K_{\mu,\nu}(z)e^{ithz}dz.

(63)

Using this, we rewrite (
60
) as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)=\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu).

(64)

We see that the latter estimator is a product of two CFs, one dependent on the measurement data and one on the bandwidth parameter of the kernel function. Since we define the kernel function ourselves, the form of
\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)
is analytically known. In signal processing (
64
), this is called smoothed empirical CF
Stefanski and Carroll 1990
.
If
\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)
is absolutely integrable, one can estimate the PDF
\mathcal{W}_{X}(x|\mu,\nu)
of
X
by the inverse Fourier transform of the CF:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)e^{-itx}dt.

(65)

Since the empirical CF is always bounded by one, e.g.,
|\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)|\leq 1
,
it suffices that the kernel’s Fourier transform
\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)
is integrable. For example, if we choose a Gaussian kernel in the form

\displaystyle K_{\mu,\nu}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}}\exp{\left[-\frac{z^{2}}{\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}}\right]},

(66)

the CF of this kernel is

\displaystyle\phi_{K}(th;\mu,\nu)=\exp{\left(-\frac{t^{2}h^{2}(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}{4}\right)},

(67)

that is a Gaussian function in
t
and it is known to be integrable. Therefore, under this choice the inversion integral exists.

The KCFE with the Gaussian kernel can be written as

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{G}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)=\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t;\mu,\nu)\exp{\left[-\frac{t^{2}h^{2}(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}{4}\right]}.

(68)

Now we need to find the optimal bandwidth parameter minimizing the distance between the CF and its kernel estimate in some specific norms.

2.2. Asymptotic Properties of the Kernel Characteristic Function Estimator

Now we analyze the estimation error of the KCFE.
Only the sample CF
\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t)
depends on the observable.
By linearity of expectation we have
Tsybakov 2009
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{itX_{j}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{itX}\Big]=\int e^{itx}f(x)dx=\phi(t),

(69)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t)|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j,l=1}^{n}e^{it(X_{j}-X_{l})}\Big]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{j,l=1;j\neq l}^{n}e^{it(X_{j}-X_{l})}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{1}\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(n+n(n-1)\phi(t)\phi(t)^{\star}\right)=\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)|\phi(t)|^{2},

where we used that for
X
and
Y
which are i.i.d. random variables, the CF of their sum is given by:

\phi_{X+Y}(t)=\phi_{X}(t)\cdot\phi_{Y}(t)
. Then we can deduce the expectation of the KCFE (
64
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{K}(th)\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t)]=\phi_{K}(th)\phi(t),

(70)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t)|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\phi}_{X,n}(t)|^{2}]=|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)|\phi(t)|^{2}\right).

Using these results, we obtain the bias and variance of the estimate:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Bias}(\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t))=(\phi_{K}(th)-1)\phi(t),

(71)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t)|^{2}]-|\mathbb{E}[\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t)]|^{2}=\frac{|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}}{n}\left(1-|\phi(t)|^{2}\right).

(72)

One can see that if
h\rightarrow 0
, the bias is tending to zero. If
n\rightarrow\infty
, the variance is tending to zero. Since the CF can take complex values, we define the MSE for the absolute value as

\displaystyle\operatorname{MSE}(\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu))

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu)-{\phi}(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}\right)=|\operatorname{Bias}(\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t))|^{2}+\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\phi}_{nh}(t))

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\phi_{K}(th)-1|^{2}|\phi(t)|^{2}+\frac{|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}}{n}\left(1-|\phi(t)|^{2}\right).

To find the rate of convergence in
h
one has to specify the kernel function.

For the ground state of the HO,
the corresponding tomogram is given by (
26
), that is, a Gaussian PDF. Hence, the obvious choice of the kernel function is (
66
).
The corresponding CF (
67
) is also Gaussian in
\mu,\nu
.
Then the KCFE with the Gaussian kernel is (
68
).
The MSE of this KCFE is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\phi}^{G}_{nh}(t;\mu,\nu))=\left(\exp{\left[-\frac{t^{2}h^{2}(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}{4}\right]}-1\right)^{2}|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}+\frac{\exp{\left[-\frac{t^{2}h^{2}(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}{4}\right]}}{n}(1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}).

(74)

For
|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|<1
the optimal bandwidth parameter is

\displaystyle h_{\phi}=\frac{2}{t(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}\sqrt{-\ln\!\left(1-\frac{1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}{2n\,|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}\right)}\approx\frac{2}{t(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}{2n\,|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}}=O(n^{-1/2}).

(75)

Since for any number
y
with
0<y<1
, we have
\ln{(1-y)}<0
, the expression under the square root is nonnegative if

\displaystyle 0<1-\frac{1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}{2n\,|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}<1,

(76)

hold. In practice, for big enough

n
it is always the case.
Then the optimal MSE of the Gaussian KCFE is

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\hat{\phi}_{nh_{\phi}}^{G}(t;\mu,\nu))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}{n}-\frac{(1-|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2})^{2}}{4n^{2}\,|\phi(t;\mu,\nu)|^{2}}=O(1/n).

(77)

The KCFE is asymptotically as efficient as the parametric maximum likelihood estimator.

2.3. Filtering Measurement Noise via Characteristic Functions

The homodyne tomography does not take into account various losses (mode mismatching, failure of detectors)
in the detection process, which modify the distribution of results in a real
measurement compared with the idealized case. In
Leonhardt and Paul 1995
, the following error model is introduced:

\displaystyle Z\equiv^{d}\kappa X+(1-\kappa)Y,\quad 0<\kappa<1,

(78)

where
\equiv^{d}
is an equality in distribution.
Here,
\kappa
comprises the overall detection efficiency, including all kinds of losses, and
Y
is a sequence of i.i.d. noise independent from the target variable
X
.

If two random variables
X
and
Y
are independent, then the probability density of their sum is equal to the convolution of the probability densities of
X
and
Y
. The independence of the two random variables implies that their joint PDF is equal to the product of their marginals, namely

p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_{X}(x)p_{Y}(y)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{Z}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{X,Y}(x,y)\delta\left(\kappa x+(1-\kappa)y-z\right)dydx

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{X,Y}\left(x,\frac{z-\kappa x}{1-\kappa}\right)dx=\frac{1}{1-\kappa}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{W}_{X}(x)p_{Y}\left(\frac{z-\kappa x}{1-\kappa}\right)dx,

where
\mathcal{W}_{X}(x)
is a PDF (tomogram) of
X
,
p_{Y}(z)
is a PDF of the noise
Y
.

For our approach we need the CF of
X
. It is known that the CF of the sum of two independent random variables is the
product of individual CFs.
The CF of
Z
, denoted by
\phi_{Z}(t)
, is:

\displaystyle\phi_{Z}(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{itZ}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{it(\kappa X+(1-\kappa)Y)}]=\mathbb{E}[e^{it\kappa X}]\cdot\mathbb{E}[e^{it(1-\kappa)Y}]=\phi_{X}(\kappa t)\phi_{Y}((1-\kappa)t).

(80)

Here
\phi_{X}(\kappa t)
is the CF of a target PDF, while
\phi_{Y}((1-\kappa)t)
is the CF of
p_{Y}(z)
.
One can conclude, that if
\phi_{Y}((1-\kappa)t)
is invertible, then

\displaystyle{\phi}_{X}(t)={\phi}_{Z}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}\right)\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right),\quad\kappa\in(0,1).

(81)

Similar idea appears in the context of
deconvolution
of signals PDF from the noisy data
Stefanski and Carroll 1990
;
Fan 1991
;
Delaigle and Gijbels 2004
. One imposes the nonvanishing condition on the CF of the noise, i.e.,

|\phi_{Y}(t)|>0
,
\forall t
.
When performing deconvolution (
81
), one needs to divide by
\phi_{Y}(t)
. If
\phi_{Y}(t)
were to vanish at some points, the inversion process would become unstable or even impossible due to division by zero. For example, the CF of a normal distribution (
67
) is nonvanishing since it is never zero. In contrast, some distributions, such as the uniform distribution on an interval, have CFs that vanish at certain values of
t
.

Thus, the model (
78
) and the assumption about the noise distribution define the correction of the CF estimator.
Using (
64
), we can define the KCFE of the signal
X
filtered from noise
Y
:

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{X,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)=\hat{\phi}_{Z,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right),

(82)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{Z,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)\equiv\hat{\phi}_{Z,n}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa};\mu,\nu\right)\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right),

where
\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right)
is assumed to be known from the noise model and
\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right)
is dependent from the kernel function choice.
If
\hat{\phi}_{X,nh}(t;\mu,\nu)
is integrable, one can estimate the PDF
\mathcal{W}_{X}(x|\mu,\nu)
of
X
by the inverse Fourier transform of the CF:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{W}}_{nh}(x|\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{Z,n}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa};\mu,\nu\right)\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right)\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right)e^{-itx}dt.

(83)

In order to guarantee that this
estimator is well defined we need to impose the conditions
Delaigle and Gijbels 2004
:

\displaystyle|\phi_{Y}(t)|>0,\quad\forall t,

(84)

\displaystyle\sup_{t}|\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right)\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right)|<\infty,

\displaystyle\int|\phi_{K}\left(\frac{t}{\kappa}h;\mu,\nu\right)\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{(1-\kappa)t}{\kappa}\right)|dt<\infty.

The trace product (
32
) between two estimated states can be estimated as

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1,nh}\bm{\rho}_{2,nh})}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\phi^{-2}_{Y}\left(\frac{1-\kappa}{\kappa}\right)\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{Z_{1},nh}(1;\mu,\nu)\hat{\phi}_{Z_{2},nh}(1;-\mu,-\nu)d\mu d\nu,

(85)

assuming for simplicity that the noise has a similar PDF for both measurement procedures.

If
\bm{\rho}_{1}=\bm{\rho}
is a true state and
\bm{\rho}_{2}=\bm{\rho}_{nh}
is its estimate from the noisy data, then

\displaystyle\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}\bm{\rho}_{nh})}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{1-\kappa}{\kappa}\right)\iint\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\phi}(1;\mu,\nu)\hat{\phi}_{Z_{1},nh}(1;-\mu,-\nu)d\mu d\nu,

(86)

that shows the overlap between the sate and its estimate.
The latter estimate must tend to purity for
n\rightarrow\infty
. The rate of convergence of this estimate is provided in the following sections.
Other trace characteristics (
31
) can be estimated in the same fashion. The estimate of the kernel of the density matrix (
36
) is

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho_{nh}(y,y^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\phi^{-1}_{Y}\left(\frac{1-\kappa}{\kappa}\right)\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}_{Z,nh}(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})\exp{\left(-i\frac{\mu(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}d\mu

(87)

Thus, with a collection of i.i.d. data sets
\{Z_{\mu_{i},\nu_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for a finite set of parameters

\{\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
on the interval, e.g.,
\mu_{i}\in[-\mu_{\mathrm{max}},\mu_{\mathrm{max}}]
,
\nu_{i}\in[-\nu_{\mathrm{max}},\nu_{\mathrm{max}}]
,
\mu_{\mathrm{max}},\nu_{\mathrm{max}}>0
,
i=[1,N]
, we can estimate a finite number of CFs
\{\hat{\phi}(1;\mu_{k},\nu_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}
and tomograms
\{\hat{\mathcal{W}}(x|\mu_{k},\nu_{k})\}_{k=1}^{N}
. However, this is not sufficient for integration over the entire parameter space in (
85
)- (
87
). The solution to this problem is presented in the next section.

S3. Kernel quantum State Estimation

We start with the estimation of the kernel of the density operator (
36
) in the coordinate basis. Let us rewrite (
36
) as a sum of integrals

\displaystyle{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})={\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})+{\rho}_{\mathrm{tail}+}(y,y^{\prime})+{\rho}_{\mathrm{tail}-}(y,y^{\prime}),

(88)

where we introduce the notations

\displaystyle{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}\Big[{\phi}(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})\exp{\left(-i\frac{\mu(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\Big]d\mu,

(89)

\displaystyle{\rho}_{\mathrm{tail}\pm}(y,y^{\prime})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}\Big[{\phi}(1;\pm\mu,y-y^{\prime})\exp{\left(\pm i\frac{\mu(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\Big]d\mu.

(90)

We have KCFE
\{\hat{\phi}(1;\mu_{k},\nu=y-y^{\prime})\}_{k=1}^{N_{\mu}}
only to approximate the first integral. That means that no measurement data is available to directly estimate (
90
).
From
Markovich et al. 2024
we know that the family of tomographic PDFs
\mathcal{W}(x|\mu,\nu)
encountered in experiments typically exhibit a Hermite-Gaussian form. Such PDFs are bounded on infinity and have finite moments. Their multimodal structure makes them challenging to estimate using solely parametric methods. However, beyond a certain value of
x
, the Hermite modes decay, and the Gaussian tail becomes dominant. Therefore, it is reasonable to adopt a hybrid estimation approach: to estimate
{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})
nonparametrically from the measurement data while estimating the tail components
{\rho}_{\mathrm{tail}\pm}(y,y^{\prime})
parametrically using the prior knowledge of the theoretically prepared state. We use similar technique for
{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}}
estimation.

3.1. DFT-Based Approximation of Integrals Involving the Characteristic Function

We want to approximate (
89
) with a finite sum. Let us divide the interval of width
2\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
into
N_{\mu}
equally spaced sub-intervals with width

\displaystyle\Delta\mu=2\mu_{\mathrm{max}}/N_{\mu}.

(91)

We can approximate the Fourier integral (
89
) by the sum

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})\equiv\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}{\phi}(1;k\Delta\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{max}},y-y^{\prime})\exp{\left(-i\frac{(k\Delta\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{max}})(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}.

(92)

By dividing the measurement range into small intervals and assigning a constant value of the function for each interval, we do a similar thing that is done in signal processing, when one performs time-sampling of the signal and the sampling frequency is given by
F=\Delta\mu^{-1}
. Further we use KCFE to write the estimate of (
92
) as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi F}\exp{\left(i\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k]\exp{\left(-i\frac{k(y+y^{\prime})}{2F}\right)},

(93)

where we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k]\equiv\hat{\phi}_{nh}\left(1;\frac{k}{F}-\mu_{\mathrm{max}},y-y^{\prime}\right),

(94)

and the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of this function is

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{nh}[g]=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k]\exp{\left(-i2\pi\frac{gk}{N_{\mu}}\right)}.

(95)

We use square brackets to stress that the function takes discrete input.
Comparing (
93
) and (
95
), we conclude that the DFT calculates the Fourier transform only for discrete frequencies which are given by
\frac{y+y^{\prime}}{2}=2\pi\frac{g}{N_{\mu}}F
.
Then the final estimate of the integral (
89
) after truncation, discretization and CF estimation is

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi F}\exp{\left(i\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\hat{\Phi}_{nh}\Big[\frac{(y+y^{\prime})N_{\mu}}{4\pi F}\Big].

(96)

We use
L_{\infty}
norm as a measure of mean error of approximating the kernel (
36
) of the density matrix operator with (
96
):

\displaystyle{L}_{\infty}(\bm{\rho}_{nh})\equiv\sup\limits_{y,y^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left(|{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})-\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\right).

(97)

It quantifies the maximum pointwise squared deviation between the true kernel and the estimated kernel over the domain of
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
.

In our method we have three types of errors. First error is the one arising from the CF estimation. The second is the truncation of the integral over the infinite space by the integral over a final domain. The third is the discretization error arising from approximating the integral with the finite sum.

Suppose we want to approximate

\displaystyle I(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(\mu)e^{-i\mu\theta}d\mu

(98)

with the truncated integral on the finite interval
[-\mu_{\mathrm{max}},\mu_{\mathrm{max}}]
:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\theta;\mu_{\mathrm{max}})\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}\phi(\mu)e^{-i\mu\theta}d\mu

(99)

The truncation error is given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\theta;\mu_{\mathrm{max}})=\left|I(\theta)-I_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\theta;\mu_{\mathrm{max}})\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|\mu|>\mu_{\max}}f(\mu)\,e^{-i\mu\theta}\,d\mu\right|.

(100)

Let
\phi(\mu)
be analytic in the strip

\displaystyle S_{b}=\{\mu\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}(\mu)|<b\},\quad b>0.

(101)

Assume that on the real line it satisfies

\displaystyle|\phi(\mu)|\leq Ce^{-\tau\mu},\quad\mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad\tau>0,

(102)

and
C>0
is some constant. Then it is easy to see that the
truncation error is bounded by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\theta;\mu_{\mathrm{max}})\leq\frac{C}{2\pi}\,e^{-\tau\mu_{\max}},\quad\forall\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(103)

So the tail is exponentially small in
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
.
This indicates that for analytic functions that decay exponentially on the real axis (or faster), the truncation error decreases exponentially with the truncation limit
\mu_{\max}
. The exponential, sine and cosine functions, as long as polynomials are analytic because their Taylor series expansions converge to the function values on their entire domain.

When approximating a Fourier integral with a discrete sum, a discretization error arises from replacing the continuous integral by a numerical quadrature. We approximate
I_{\mathrm{trunc}}(\theta,\mu_{\mathrm{max}})
by

\displaystyle I_{N_{\mu}}=\frac{\Delta\mu}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}f(\mu_{j})\,e^{-i\mu_{j}\theta},

(104)

with sample points

\displaystyle\mu_{j}=-\mu_{\max}+j\Delta\mu,\quad\text{for }j=0,1,\dots,N_{\mu}-1,

(105)

and grid spacing
\Delta\mu
.
If
f(\mu)
is analytic in the strip

S_{b}
and is uniformly bounded
|f(\mu)|\leq M
,
\forall\mu\in S_{b}
the discretization error
decays as
Trefethen and Weideman 2014
:

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{dis}}=\left|I_{\mathrm{trunc}}-I_{N_{\mu}}\right|\leq\frac{M}{\pi}\frac{e^{-2\pi\tau/\Delta\mu}}{1-e^{-2\pi\tau/\Delta\mu}}=O\!\left(e^{-\pi\tau N_{\mu}/\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}\right).

(106)

Let us use these results to our case. Considering the exponential family of PDFs we are typically dealing with, the
\phi(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})
is analytic and we can use the result (
103
) to upper bound the truncation error introduced by limiting the infinite integration domain to a finite interval. Since
|\phi(\mu)|\leq 1
, we can find the constants
C
and
\tau
such that the condition (
102
) will be satisfied.
First we select:

\displaystyle|\phi(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})|\leq 1\leq Ce^{-\tau\mu_{\mathrm{max}}},\quad\mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad\tau>0.

(107)

Since
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}>0
, the inequality holds for
\tau\leq\ln{(C)}/\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
,
C\geq 1
. The smallest truncation error is achieved by taking the largest admissible

\tau
. Then the truncation error is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trunc}}=|{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})-{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}.

(108)

Choosing the maximal

\tau
makes the product
Ce^{-\tau\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}
exactly one, and drives the truncation error down to the universal ceiling
(
108
), independent of
C
and
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
. Lets observe

\displaystyle|\phi(1;\mu,y-y^{\prime})|\leq Ce^{-\tau\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq 1,\quad\mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad\tau>0.

(109)

If
0<C\leq 1
the inequality holds
\forall\tau\geq 0
. If
C>1
, then
\tau\geq\ln{(C)}/\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
and the truncation error is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trunc}}\leq\frac{C}{2\pi}e^{-\tau\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}.

(110)

The truncation error is tending to zero when
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\rightarrow\infty
. However, we are always limited to some finite value of
\mu_{\max}
, so the truncation error always exists. Further we show how to mitigate this error with parametric correction.

Using the result (
106
), the discretization error arising from replacing the continuous integral with a sum over discrete points, can be estimated as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dis}}=|{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})-\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|\leq\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{e^{-\pi\tau N_{\mu}/\mu_{\max}}}{1-e^{-\pi\tau N_{\mu}/\mu_{\max}}},

(111)

where we can select
M=1
since
|\phi(\mu)|\leq 1
holds.

Finally, we take into account the estimation error, arising from the imperfections of the KCFE method:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|\tilde{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})-\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\right)

(112)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\Bigg|\frac{1}{2\pi F}\exp{\left(i\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}({\phi}[k]-\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k])\exp{\left(-i\frac{k(y+y^{\prime})}{2F}\right)}\Bigg|^{2}\right).

We use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{k}a_{k}b_{k}\Bigg|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{k}|a_{k}|^{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k}|b_{k}|^{2}\right),

(113)

where we take
a_{k}={\phi}[k]-\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k]
and

b_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi F}\exp{\left(i\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}(y+y^{\prime})}{2}\right)}\exp{\left(-i\frac{k(y+y^{\prime})}{2F}\right)}
.
Then the upper bound is

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{K}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{N_{\mu}}{(2\pi F)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}\Big|{\phi}[k]-\hat{\phi}_{nh}[k]\Big|^{2}\right).

(114)

Since the exponential term is of a unit modulus, it does not affect the magnitude of the expression. We use the point-wise MSE of the kernel CF estimation (
77
) to write

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{K}^{2}\leq\frac{N_{\mu}}{(2\pi F)^{2}n}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{N_{\mu}-1}(1-|{\phi}[k]|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mu^{2}_{\max}}{\pi^{2}n}.

(115)

Collecting all the errors, the distance between the estimate and the exact integral is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})-\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\right)\leq 3(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{trunc}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{dis}}^{2}+\varepsilon_{K}^{2})=\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\Big[\frac{1}{4}e^{-2\tau\mu_{\max}}+\frac{e^{-2\pi\tau N_{\mu}/\mu_{\max}}}{(1-e^{-\pi\tau N_{\mu}/\mu_{\max}})^{2}}+\frac{\mu^{2}_{\max}}{n}\Big].

(116)

As we mentioned, we need to select the parameter
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
such that the tail behavior of the CF would be dominating the oscillations. In theory we can select any
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
that would fit this demand, but in practice it is dictated by the measurement setup restrictions. To guarantee

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}{n}=0,\quad\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{N_{\mu}}{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}=\infty,

(117)

we must check that
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
is such that

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{max}}=o\left(n^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

(118)

and
N_{\mu}
must grow strictly faster than
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
.
To this end, we can select

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\approx\log{n},\quad N_{\mu}\approx\mu_{\mathrm{max}}\log{n}.

(119)

Then (
117
) are satisfied.
The error (
116
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})-\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\right)=\begin{cases}O(n^{-2\tau}),&\quad 0<\tau<\dfrac{1}{2},\\[6.0pt]
O((\log n)^{2}\,n^{-1}),&\quad\tau\geq\dfrac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

(120)

The total amount of measurement that we must do for our reconstruction procedure per
(y,y^{\prime})
is
T_{\mu}=N_{\mu}\times n=n(\log{n})^{2}
. Since we deal with the Gaussian tailed PDFs, the
\tau
can be always selected bigger than
1/2
.
Using the Lambert

W
- function, we find
n=\exp{2W(\sqrt{T_{\mu}}/2)}
. For large
T_{\mu}
the
W
- function is
W(z)\sim\log{z}-\log{\log{z}}
. We can derive that
n\approx T_{\mu}/4
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(|{\rho}(y,y^{\prime})-\hat{\tilde{\rho}}_{\mathrm{max}}(y,y^{\prime})|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{3(\log{T_{\mu}/4})^{4}}{\pi^{2}T_{\mu}}=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mu}}\right).

(121)

In KDE estimation, the difference between pointwise MSE and supremum MSE (i.e., in
L_{\infty}
norm) does not change the convergence rate, provided the target function is smooth and the estimator is well-behaved
Tsybakov 2009
. Since the reconstructed function is smooth and bounded, taking the supremum on the bounded domain provides
(
97
)

\displaystyle{L}_{\infty}(\bm{\rho}_{nh})=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mu}}\right).

(122)

The upper bound (
115
) allows to see the rate of convergence in
n
of the estimation error
\varepsilon_{K}^{2}
. However, the actual error, calculated by (
112
) is much smaller, still preserving the rate
O(1/n)
, that can be seen in Fig.

. The reason of this difference is that the upper bound provided by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is not tight.

3.2. Estimation Error of the Trace Characteristics of Quantum States

To estimate the trace characteristic of the density matrix operators we do similar steps. However, in this case their is no need in DFT, it is a simple integral approximation with the finite sum. The tail correction can be done in the similar fashion.
We denote the product of two CFs as

\displaystyle A(\mu,\nu)=\phi_{1}(1;\mu,\nu)\phi_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu,\nu),

(126)

and its point‐wise product and the KCFE as:

\displaystyle A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})=\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})\phi_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j}),\quad\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})=\hat{\phi}_{1,nh}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})\hat{\phi}_{2,nh}^{\star}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j}).

(127)

We want to approximate the integral

\displaystyle I\equiv\text{tr}{\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\iint A(\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu.

(128)

Similarly to the previous section, we first truncate the latter integral:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{trunc}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\limits_{-\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\mu_{\mathrm{max}}}\int\limits_{-\nu_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\nu_{\mathrm{max}}}A(\mu,\nu)d\mu d\nu.

(129)

The product of two CFs (
126
) is a CF too. We assume it has a Gaussian tail and its modulus can be bounded by
Ce^{-\beta(\mu^{2}+\nu^{2})}
. The truncation error is

\displaystyle|I-I_{\mathrm{trunc}}|^{2}=O\left(e^{-4\beta\min{(\mu_{\mathrm{max}}^{2},\nu_{\mathrm{max}}^{2})}}\right).

(130)

Then we discretise the finite integral

\displaystyle\tilde{I}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})\Delta\mu\Delta\nu

(131)

For smooth integrands and uniform grid the discretization error is
|I_{\mathrm{trunc}}-\tilde{I}|^{2}=O(\Delta\mu^{4}+\Delta\nu^{4})
.

Finally, we use the KCFE:

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{I}}=\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1,nh}\bm{\rho}_{2,nh})}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})\Delta\mu\Delta\nu.

(132)

The error arising from the kernel estimation method is

\displaystyle|\tilde{I}-\hat{\tilde{I}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Delta\mu\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}(A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j}))\right|^{2}

(133)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Delta\mu\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}(A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j}))(A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j}))^{\star}

If we take the expectation, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\tilde{I}-\hat{\tilde{I}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Delta\mu\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}(A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j}))(A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j}))^{\star}\right]

(134)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\Delta\mu\Delta\nu}{2\pi}\right)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}\mathbb{E}\left[|A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right].

We use
|ab-cd|^{2}\leq 2|a-c|^{2}|b|^{2}+2|c|^{2}|b-d|^{2}
, to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|A(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{A}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]

(135)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[|{\phi}_{1}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{\phi}_{1,nh}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{\phi}_{2,nh}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}|\hat{\phi}_{1,nh}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathbb{E}\left[|{\phi}_{1}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{\phi}_{1,nh}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\left[|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})-\hat{\phi}_{2,nh}^{\star}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{\phi}_{1,nh}(\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Bigg[\left(\frac{1-|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}{n}-\frac{(1-|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})^{2}}{4n^{2}\,|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}\right)|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-|\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}{n}-\frac{(1-|\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})^{2}}{4n^{2}\,|\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}\right)|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}+\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}(1-|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})+|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}|{\phi}_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}(1-|\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

where we use the Gaussian kernel with the optimal bandwidth (
75
), using the approximation

\displaystyle|\phi_{K}(th)|^{2}\approx\exp{-\frac{2}{\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}}\frac{1-|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}{2n|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}}}=1+O(1/n).

(136)

We assume that
\Delta\mu=2\mu_{\mathrm{max}}/N_{\mu}
,
\Delta\nu=2\nu_{\mathrm{max}}/N_{\nu}
. Then

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\mathbb{E}|\tilde{I}-\hat{\tilde{I}}|^{2}\leq\frac{8\mu^{2}_{\mathrm{max}}\nu_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}}{\pi^{2}N_{\mu}^{2}N_{\nu}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\nu}}\left(|{\phi}_{2}^{\star}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}(1-|\phi_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})+|{\phi}_{1}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2}(1-|\phi_{2}(1;\mu_{i},\nu_{j})|^{2})\right)O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right).

(137)

In this case, the total amount of measurements is

T_{\mu,\nu}=nN_{\mu}N_{\nu}
, since we vary both
\mu
and
\nu
parameters. We select the parameters
\mu_{\mathrm{max}}
and
\nu_{\mathrm{max}}
to be of the rate (
119
).
Then the latter error is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\tilde{I}-\hat{\tilde{I}}|^{2}=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mu,\nu}}\right).

(138)

We also assumed that the truncation error is corrected similarly to the previous section with the parametric tail corrector. Then the final error is scaling as
\mathbb{E}|{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1}\bm{\rho}_{2})}}-\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}_{1,nh}\bm{\rho}_{2,nh})}}|^{2}=\tilde{O}(1/T_{\mu,\nu})
.

Example 2
.

Let us assume that
\bm{\rho}
is a true state and
\widehat{\bm{\rho}_{nh}}
is its KQSE. Then from the previous derivations we know that the MSE of the estimate is

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}\bm{\rho}_{nh})}})=\mathbb{E}|{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}^{2})}}-\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}\bm{\rho}_{nh})}}|^{2}=\tilde{O}(1/T_{\mu,\nu}).

(139)

If the state

The MSE of the estimate of the squared trace distance is exactly equal to the MSE of the overlap estimator:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{D^{2}(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}_{nh})})=\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{\text{tr}{(\bm{\rho}\bm{\rho}_{nh})}})=\tilde{O}(1/T_{\mu,\nu}).

(141)

Apply Chebyshev’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle P\left(|\widehat{D^{2}(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}_{nh})})-{D^{2}(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho})})|\geq\delta\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{MSE}(\widehat{D^{2}(\bm{\rho},\bm{\rho}_{nh})})}{\delta^{2}}=\tilde{O}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mu,\nu}\delta^{2}}\right),

since

\displaystyle|N_{c}|^{-2}=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\exp\Bigl(-|a|^{2}+|a|^{2}e^{2\pi i(k-j)/3}\Bigr).

(147)

The coordinate representation of the coherent state reads as

\displaystyle\psi_{a}(x)=\langle x|a\rangle=\pi^{-1/4}\exp{\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}-\frac{|a|^{2}}{2}+\sqrt{2}ax-\frac{a^{2}}{2}\right)},

(148)

and its tomogram is known to be equal to
Man’ko and Man’ko 1997
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(X|{a},\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(X|\alpha)e^{iXs}e^{d},

(149)

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\left((\nu-i\mu)a^{\star}-(\nu+i\mu)a\right)}{\alpha^{2}},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-|a|^{2}+\frac{(\nu+i\mu)^{2}a^{2}+(\nu-i\mu)^{2}(a^{\star})^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}},

where
\mathcal{W}_{0}(X|\alpha)
is given by (
26
),
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}}
.
The corresponding CF is

\displaystyle{\phi}(t;{a},\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(t+s;\alpha)e^{d}.

(150)

The coordinate representation of the cat state (
146
) is

\displaystyle\rho(y,y^{\prime})=|N_{c}|^{2}\sum\limits_{j,k=1}^{3}\psi_{ae^{2\pi i(j-1)/3}}(y)\psi^{\star}_{ae^{2\pi i(k-1)/3}}(y^{\prime}).

(151)

According to (
37
) the tomogram of the cat states (
146
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{CCS}}(X|a,\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}(X|\alpha)|N_{c}|^{2}\sum\limits_{j,k=1}^{3}e^{iXs(j,k)}e^{d(j,k)},

(152)

\displaystyle s(j,k)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\left((\nu-i\mu)a^{\star}e^{-\frac{2\pi i}{3}(k-1)}-(\nu+i\mu)ae^{\frac{2\pi i}{3}(j-1)}\right)}{\alpha^{2}},

\displaystyle d(j,k)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle-|a|^{2}+\frac{\left((\nu+i\mu)^{2}a^{2}e^{\frac{4\pi i}{3}(j-1)}+(\nu-i\mu)^{2}(a^{\star})^{2}e^{-\frac{4\pi i}{3}(k-1)}\right)}{2\alpha^{2}}.

One can see that if
k,j=1
then
s(1,1)=s
,
d(1,1)=d
and the tomogram coincides with the single coherent state tomogram (
149
). The PDF (
152
) is a mixture of the Gaussian distributions
Robertson and Fryer 1969

\displaystyle{\phi}_{\mathrm{CCS}}(t;{a},\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|N_{c}|^{2}\sum\limits_{j,k=1}^{3}\phi_{0}(t+s(j,k);\alpha)e^{d(j,k)}.

(153)
