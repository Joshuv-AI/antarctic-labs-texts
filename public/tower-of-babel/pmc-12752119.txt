Adjunctive bilateral vs. unilateral or sham repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation for major depressive disorder or bipolar depression: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled studies
Authors: Dong-Bin Cai, Ying-Yan Deng, Yao-Yin Tang, Xiu-De Qin, Can-Jin Deng, Qiu-Ling Lu, Xiao-Chang Lan, Jian-Jun Wang, Xing-Bing Huang, Xin-Hu Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (brTMS) has shown promise as a therapeutic approach for depression; however, evidence from existing studies remains inconsistent. To address the concern, a meta-analysis was conducted to examine the efficacy and safety of brTMS in comparison with unilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (urTMS) or sham repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (srTMS) in treating major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar depression (BD).MethodsA systematic search covering publications up to May 22, 2024, was performed across four main bibliographic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and PsycINFO) to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing the efficacy and safety of brTMS vs. urTMS or srTMS in patients with MDD or BD. Following Cochrane Handbook guidelines, random-effects meta-analyses were employed to derive pooled estimates for primary and secondary outcomes.ResultsThe analysis included 20 RCTs (8 comparing brTMS to urTMS, 6 comparing brTMS to srTMS, and 6 comparing brTMS to both urTMS and srTMS), encompassing a total of 1666 participants. No significant difference was observed between brTMS and urTMS regarding study-defined response (9 RCTs, 11 study arms, n = 778; RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.83–1.72; Chi2 = 29.76, I2 = 66%; P = 0.34) or study-defined remission (7 RCTs, 8 study arms, n = 684; RR 1.36, 95% CI 0.87–2.12; I2 = 57%; P = 0.18) in patients with MDD or BD. However, when compared to srTMS, brTMS demonstrated statistically significant improvements in study-defined response (10 RCTs, 12 study arms, n = 595; RR 2.69, 95% CI 1.58–4.57; Chi2 = 22.75, I2 = 52%; P = 0.0003) and study-defined remission (8 RCTs, 9 study arms, n = 553; RR 3.84, 95% CI 1.62–9.07; Chi2 = 14.42, I2 = 45%; P = 0.002). No significant differences were detected between the brTMS, urTMS, or srTMS groups regarding adverse events or treatment discontinuation rates (P > 0.05).ConclusionsThe meta-analysis indicates that brTMS is an effective, safe, and well-tolerated treatment for MDD and BD but does not provide significant antidepressant advantages over urTMS. Consequently, it actually suggested that brTMS could serve as an alternative treatment strategy in cases where urTMS treatment is unsuccessful. Further large-scale studies are needed to explore its efficacy across diverse patient populations, stimulation parameters and to define its role in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03447-w.Keywords: Bilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, Major depressive disorder, Bipolar depression, Systematic review, Efficacy

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar depression (BD) were the highly prevalent mental illness and were associated with decreased quality of life, increased suicidal rates, and remain the most burdensome disorders worldwide [1, 2]. Globally, depression affects over 280 million people (WHO, 2021), with MDD ranked as the leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in mental disorders (GBD 2019) [3]. About one in three patients with depression reach complete remission using first-line antidepressants; however, despite advancements in pharmacological therapies, nearly one-third experience no therapeutic response, leading to treatment-resistant depression (TRD) [4], defined by the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guidelines as failure to achieve remission after ≥ 2 adequate antidepressant trials of sufficient dose and duration (≥ 6 weeks)[5]. Moreover, patients with MDD often encounter adverse events, high discontinuation rates, and drug interactions during antidepressant therapy [6, 7]. Consequently, there is increasing recognition of the need for alternative therapeutic strategies to optimize outcomes for patients with MDD or BD [8], including interventions, such as electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) [9], transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) [10], magnetic seizure therapy (MST) [11], and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) [12, 13].

Over the past two decades, rTMS has gained recognition as an effective treatment for MDD and received approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for TRD in 2008 [14]. Large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that rTMS is effective, with most protocols utilizing high-frequency rTMS (HF-rTMS; 5–20 Hz) applied to the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) or low-frequency rTMS (LF-rTMS; 1 Hz) targeting the right DLPFC [15, 16]. For instance, a previous RCT has confirmed that HF-rTMS applied to the left DLPFC yields greater efficacy compared to sham treatments for MDD [17], as corroborated by meta-analyses [18, 19]. Similarly, LF-rTMS targeting the right DLPFC has shown superior efficacy over sham treatments in patients with MDD [16]. The therapeutic potential of bilateral rTMS (brTMS), which combines HF-rTMS to the left DLPFC and LF-rTMS to the right DLPFC, remains a subject of debate due to mixed findings regarding its efficacy and safety in MDD and BD. For example, one RCT reported that brTMS demonstrated superior efficacy compared to unilateral rTMS (urTMS; HF-rTMS to the left DLPFC) or sham treatment in patients with MDD [20]. However, other RCTs have reported no significant differences between brTMS and unilateral right-sided rTMS in patients with TRD [21–23].

A systematic review and meta-analysis of three RCTs involving 279 participants with MDD reported that 24.7% (40/162) of those receiving active brTMS and 6.8% (8/117) of those receiving sham rTMS were classified as responders, indicating a statistically significant advantage of active brTMS over sham rTMS [24]. In contrast, response and remission rates did not significantly differ between brTMS and urTMS. Similarly, another meta-analysis of seven RCTs comprising 509 participants revealed response rates of 47.2% for brTMS (117 of 248) and 46.0% for urTMS (120 of 261) among patients with MDD, further supporting the lack of a significant difference between these two approaches [25]. These findings have collectively established brTMS as an effective and safe treatment modality for patients with MDD or BD [24, 25]. However, the most recent meta-analysis by Chen et al. [25], which evaluated the effectiveness and safety of brTMS in treating MDD or BD, included only seven RCTs (n = 509) and was limited to comparisons between brTMS and urTMS. Since the publication of these earlier meta-analyses [24, 25], several new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating brTMS for depressive disorders have been published, contributing valuable data to the evidence base [23, 26–28]. For example, a new published RCT with 221 patients with MDD was reported that brTMS did not show statistical differences in the efficacy comparing to urTMS between 5 and 10 Hz for treating MDD [29].

To build upon these findings and provide a more comprehensive evidence base for the clinical application of brTMS in MDD or BD, the current meta-analysis broadens the scope to determine the therapeutic benefits and safety profile of brTMS compared to both urTMS and srTMS in these patient populations. The primary objectives are twofold: first, to determine whether brTMS offers superior therapeutic outcomes relative to urTMS, and second, to evaluate the comparative efficacy of brTMS against a placebo control. Based on previous research [24, 25], it is hypothesized that brTMS may exhibit superior efficacy under optimized stimulation parameters (e.g., higher intensity or session frequency) while maintaining significant advantages over sham interventions.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [30] and utilized inclusion criteria based on the PICOS framework. Participants: the participants included individuals over 18 years experiencing an acute depressive episode of MDD or BD. Intervention vs. Comparison: the intervention and comparison groups consisted of active brTMS plus antidepressants vs. sham brTMS with or without antidepressants, or urTMS with or without antidepressants. Outcomes: primary outcomes were defined as study-specific response rates (e.g., ≥ 50% reduction in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [HAMD] total scores) and remission rates (e.g., HAMD total scores ≤ 7) at the treatment endpoint for MDD or BD. Secondary outcomes included depressive symptom severity (measured by HAMD at treatment endpoint), adverse events, and treatment discontinuation for any reason. HAMD or MADRS were prioritized when multiple rating scales were utilized, owing to their frequent use in the included studies [31]. Study: only RCTs evaluating the efficacy and safety of brTMS vs. urTMS or brTMS vs. srTMS in acute depressive episodes of MDD or BD were included, while studies involving animals, observational research, review papers, non-English studies, abstracts, gray literature (unpublished data), and non-peer-reviewed sources were excluded. Search strategy

For each domain and reproducible search strategy, we developed a systematic approach guided by the PICOS framework and Cochrane Handbook recommendations. Initial key concepts encompassed intervention terms (bilateral repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, bilateral rTMS, brTMS, and bTMS), condition terminology (major depressive disorder, bipolar depression, depress*, dysphor*, and melanchol*), and study design criteria (randomized controlled trial, randomized, RCT, sham, placebo, control). Iterative pilot testing in PubMed (May 10–15, 2024) validated the strategy against five benchmark studies, refining MeSH/Emtree hierarchies through explosion analysis, expanding free-text terminology (e.g., adding "treatment-resistant depression" increased recall by 12%), and optimizing Boolean syntax. A systematic search of the Cochrane Library, PubMed, EMBASE and PsycINFO was conducted for published studies on May 22, 2024. The relevant reviews and the references of the included studies were also searched to capture the vast majority of eligible published RCTs. The search targeted RCTs comparing brTMS to urTMS or sham rTMS for MDD or BD with a depressive episode. The search terms included: (“bilateral transcranial magnetic stimulation” OR bilateral TMS OR bTMS OR bilateral rTMS) AND (depress* OR dysphor* OR melanchol* OR antidepress*) AND (random* OR sham OR placebo OR control). In addition, reference lists from previously published meta-analyses [12, 24, 25] were screened to identify relevant studies.

Data extraction

Data were extracted by three independent reviewers (DBC, YYD and YYT) using a standardized Excel form (Microsoft Office 2016, Redmond, USA), including participant characteristics (e.g., mean age, gender, and study design), intervention parameters (e.g., brTMS and urTMS settings), and study outcomes (e.g., response and remission rates). For crossover studies, data from the pre-crossover phase were extracted. Missing data were retrieved by contacting corresponding authors via email to request unpublished data, including results from completed but unpublished trials or negative findings, or by extracting values from graphs using Getdata software. When standard deviation (SD) values were missing, the average SDs from other included RCTs were imputed as replacements [32, 33]. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion or consensus involving a fourth reviewer (XHY).

Assessment of study quality

This meta-analysis employed three assessment tools to evaluate the quality of the included studies: the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [34], the Jadad scale [35], and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system [36, 37]. Using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, three reviewers independently assessed the quality of the included RCTs across seven domains, including random sequence generation and allocation concealment. For each domain, judgments (low risk, high risk, or unclear risk of bias) were based on explicit criteria outlined in the Cochrane Handbook [34]. When methodological details (e.g., specific methods for random sequence generation or allocation concealment procedures, such as sealed opaque envelopes or centralized systems) were insufficiently reported in the publication, we contacted the corresponding authors via email to request clarification or access to study protocols. Similarly, the Jadad scale was used to evaluate methodological quality based on five criteria, with high-quality RCTs defined as those scoring greater than 3 [35]. Finally, the GRADE approach was applied to assess the overall quality of evidence for all meta-analytic outcomes. Evidence quality was categorized as very low, low, moderate, or high based on six criteria, with judgments independently made by three reviewers.

Data synthesis

Data synthesis was conducted using Review Manager software (Version 5.3; Cochrane IMS, Oxford, United Kingdom) (http://www.cochrane.org), following established methodologies [38]. For continuous data, standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used, and dichotomous data were expressed as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs. As recommended by DerSimonian and Laird (DerSimonian and Laird, 1986), all meta-analyzable data were pooled using the random effects model due to the likely heterogeneity across studies, such as the discrepancy between studies in basic demographic and clinical characteristics [39]. To assess heterogeneity, the chi-squared test and I2 statistic were employed, with significant heterogeneity defined as a chi-squared test P < 0.10 or I2 ≥ 50% [40]. Sensitivity analyses were performed to identify potential sources of heterogeneity in study-defined response and remission rates by excluding one outlier study [41]. In addition, subgroup analyses were conducted to identify sources, when I2 > 50% of the primary outcomes. For all analyses, statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Assess publication bias, funnel plot inspection and Egger’s linear regression test were employed for outcomes involving more than 10 RCTs [42].

Following the methodology of a previous meta-analysis [43], when a RCT with ≥ 2 study arms was examined, categorical data of multiple study arms were separately extracted and analyzed with the control group. This was done to be able to include each of the monotherapy arms separately in the analyses. For continuous data, the number of patients was randomly assigned to each study arm to avoid inflating the number of patients in the study group, as per a previous meta-analysis [44, 45].

Results

Study selection

A comprehensive search across four major bibliographic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, and PsycINFO) yielded 860 records, which were reduced to 455 after the removal of duplicates (Fig. 1). Figure 1 illustrates the process used for selecting studies. Ultimately, 20 RCTs met the inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis [20–23, 26–29, 46–57].

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the baseline clinical features of the included RCTs. Collectively, these 20 studies randomized a total of 1666 participants (mean age: 44.6 years) into three groups: 856 received active brTMS, 464 underwent urTMS, and 346 received srTMS. Among the included studies, six RCTs compared brTMS to both urTMS and sham treatment [20, 22, 23, 49, 52, 54], six RCTs compared brTMS to urTMS alone [21, 26, 29, 47, 50, 57], and eight RCTs compared brTMS to sham treatment alone [27, 28, 46, 48, 51, 53, 55, 56]. In total, the meta-analysis included 12 RCTs examining brTMS vs. urTMS and 14 RCTs evaluating brTMS vs. srTMS for MDD or BD in the context of acute depressive episodes. All studies targeted the left DLPFC and the right DLPFC for brTMS stimulation. In addition, 39.7% of the participants across the included studies were male. To address the concern regarding the clinical equivalence of rTMS parameters and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the parameter differences, we conducted a thorough analysis of the rTMS parameters used across the included studies. We incorporated dose calculations using the formula: total pulses = frequency × train duration × number of sessions, which allowed us to contextualize the variations in rTMS parameters and enhance the comparability of the results.

Primary outcomes and secondary outcomes

BrTMS vs. urTMS

For the primary outcome of study-defined response (defined as a reduction in HAMD total score ≥ 50%), the meta-analysis of 11 comparisons (9 RCTs) yielded a risk ratio (RR) of 1.20 (44.8% vs. 34.4%; n = 778; 95% CI 0.83–1.72; I2 = 66%; P = 0.34; Fig. 2) [20–23, 26, 47, 49, 50, 57]. For study-defined remission (HAMD total score ≤ 7), the analysis of 8 comparisons (7 RCTs) produced an RR of 1.36 (32.2% vs. 23.6%; n = 684; 95% CI 0.87–2.12; I2 = 57%; P = 0.18; Fig. 2) [20–23, 26, 50, 57]. These results suggest that brTMS did not confer a significant advantage over urTMS for patients with MDD or BD. Sensitivity analyses further supported these results, with no significant differences detected between brTMS and urTMS groups in study-defined response (I2 = 65%; P = 0.47) or remission (I2 = 52%; P = 0.27) after excluding an outlier study [20]. In regard to the subgroup analyses of the above results, only study duration more than 2 weeks and more than 10 sessions of the brTMS show the significant advantage than urTMS of the study-defined response and no significant differences were presented between two groups in all subgroup analyses of the study-defined remission (Supplemental Table 2).

Regarding the improvement of depressive symptoms post-brTMS, no significant differences were observed between brTMS and urTMS, as measured by HAMD scores (9 RCTs, 12 study arms; n = 811; SMD = −0.05; 95% CI −0.19–0.09; I2 = 0%; P = 0.45; Table 2) [20, 21, 23, 29, 47, 49, 50, 54, 57].

The meta-analysis of discontinuation due to any reason revealed an RR of 1.25 (7 RCTs, 9 study arms; n = 894; 95% CI 0.91–1.73; I2 = 13%; P = 0.17; Table 2), indicating no significant difference between brTMS and urTMS groups [20, 21, 23, 29, 49, 50, 52]. Similarly, adverse events, including headache, neck pain, sleepiness, increased agitation, and pain, showed no significant group differences (RR 0.32–1.64; all P > 0.05; Table 2).

BrTMS vs. srTMS

For the primary outcome of study-defined response (defined as a reduction in HAMD total score ≥ 50%), the meta-analysis of 12 comparisons (10 RCTs with 12 study arms) revealed an RR of 2.69 (38.7% vs. 14.0%; n = 595; 95% CI 1.58–4.57; I2 = 52%; P = 0.0003; Fig. 3) [20, 22, 23, 27, 28, 48, 49, 53, 55, 56]. For study-defined remission (HAMD total score ≤ 7), the analysis of 9 comparisons (8 RCTs with 9 study arms) yielded an RR of 3.84 (22.5% vs. 4.9%; n = 553; 95% CI 1.62–9.07; I2 = 45%; P = 0.002; Fig. 3) [20, 22, 23, 27, 28, 48, 53, 56]. These results indicate that brTMS demonstrated significant advantages over srTMS in terms of response and remission rates for patients with MDD or BD. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these observations, with significant differences maintained between the brTMS and srTMS groups for study-defined response (I2 = 53%; P = 0.001) and remission (I2 = 43%; P = 0.006) after excluding an outlier study [48]. For the subgroup analyses of study-defined response, superiority of brTMS compared to srTMS was confirmed in 5 out of the 10 subgroups (Supplemental Table 2).

For the improvement of depressive symptoms post-brTMS, significant differences were observed between brTMS and srTMS, as measured by HAMD scores (9 RCTs with 10 study arms; n = 501; SMD =−0.50; 95% CI −0.81–−0.19; I2 = 60%; P = 0.001; Table 2) [20, 23, 27, 28, 46, 48, 49, 51, 54].

Regarding discontinuation due to any reason, the meta-analysis of 10 RCTs with 12 study arms (n = 643) showed no significant difference between brTMS and srTMS (RR 1.23; 95% CI 0.78–1.95; I2 = 0%; P = 0.37; Table 2) [20, 23, 27, 28, 48, 49, 52, 53, 55, 56]. Analysis of adverse events, including anxiety, pain, nausea, suicide attempts, and tremors, also revealed no significant group differences (RR 0.30–2.70; all P > 0.05; Table 2). However, brTMS was associated with a higher incidence of headache compared to srTMS (13.7% vs. 4.9%; n = 408; 95% CI 1.02–3.87; I2 = 0%; P = 0.04; Table 2).

Assessment of study quality

The results of the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment are presented in Supplemental Fig. 1. Among the included RCTs, 11 studies (55%; 11/20) provided detailed descriptions of randomization and allocation concealment procedures [20–23, 26–29, 48, 50, 53]. A total of 15 studies (75%; 15/20) reported methods for blinding participants and personnel [20–23, 27–29, 46, 48, 50–53, 56, 57], while outcome assessment blinding was clearly described in 16 studies (80%; 16/20) [20–23, 26, 27, 29, 47–53, 56, 57]. The methodological quality, assessed using the Jadad scale, classified 17 of the 20 RCTs as "high quality" (Jadad score: 3–5) (Table 1) [20–23, 26–29, 47–51, 53, 54, 56, 57]. According to the GRADE criteria, the quality of evidence for the 10 meta-analyzable outcomes comparing brTMS to urTMS in patients with MDD or BD ranged from "low" (20.0%; 2/10) to "moderate" (50.0%; 5/10) and "high" (30%; 3/10) (Supplemental Table 1). For comparisons of brTMS to srTMS, evidence quality ranged from "low" (10%; 1/10) to "moderate" (30%; 3/10) and "high" (60%; 6/10) (Supplemental Table 1).

Publication bias

To evaluate publication bias, funnel plots and Egger’s test were used as suggested by Sterne et al. [58]. The funnel plot for brTMS vs. urTMS was asymmetrical, and Egger’s test found no significant publication bias for study-defined response (P > 0.05). Similarly, for brTMS compared to srTMS, although the funnel plot was also asymmetrical, Egger’s test did not detect significant publication bias for study-defined response (P > 0.05).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the most updated and comprehensive meta-analysis to date, encompassing 20 RCTs with a total of 1666 participants diagnosed with MDD or BD, evaluating the antidepressant effects of brTMS compared to urTMS and srTMS. The analysis revealed significantly higher response rates (38.7% vs. 14.0%) and remission rates (22.5% vs. 4.9%) in patients receiving brTMS compared to srTMS. However, no significant differences in response rates (44.8% vs. 34.4%) or remission rates (32.2% vs. 23.6%) were observed between brTMS and urTMS. These results are consistent with recently RCTs [23, 26, 28, 29, 46, 54] and two prior meta-analyses [24, 25], which despite smaller sample sizes (n = 279 and n = 509, respectively), also reported superior antidepressant effects of brTMS over srTMS but no significant differences between brTMS and urTMS. For example, the result of a recent RCT with 61 MDD patients also reported that brTMS showed a significant difference than sham treatment but not urTMS in treating MDD [54]. In terms of safety, the meta-analysis found no significant differences in most adverse events or discontinuation rates between brTMS and either srTMS or urTMS, with the exception of a higher incidence of headaches associated with brTMS compared to srTMS. These results are in line with the safety profiles reported in previous meta-analyses [24, 25]. Overall, the results confirm that both brTMS and urTMS are effective and viable therapeutic approaches for patients with MDD or BD.

The significant advantage of brTMS over srTMS in study-defined response and remission rates aligns with prior meta-analytic findings [24]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the pathophysiology of depression is associated with hypoactivity in the left DLPFC and hyperactivity in the right DLPFC [59–61]. Previous studies have indicated that brTMS, which combines HF-rTMS to the left DLPFC and LF-rTMS to the right DLPFC, effectively enhances left DLPFC activity while suppressing right DLPFC activity, thereby addressing this imbalance in patients with MDD [62]. In conclusion, brTMS demonstrates a clear advantage over srTMS in terms of antidepressant efficacy for patients with MDD or BD, further supporting its utility as a robust therapeutic option in clinical practice.

However, our meta-analysis found that brTMS did not demonstrate a significant antidepressant advantage over urTMS for patients with MDD or BD. Of the 12 RCTs included in the meta-analysis, 11 studies reported no significant difference in antidepressant efficacy between brTMS (targeting both the right and left DLPFC) and urTMS [21–23, 26, 29, 47, 49, 50, 52, 54, 57]. In contrast, only one RCT involving 74 patients with MDD reported a significant antidepressant effect of brTMS over urTMS in terms of study-defined response [20]. A previous meta-analysis comprising seven RCTs comparing brTMS to urTMS in patients with MDD similarly reported no significant difference in efficacy between the two approaches [25]. These findings suggest that the comparative efficacy of bilateral and urTMS may be equivalent for treating MDD. The absence of superior efficacy of brTMS compared to urTMS in the treatment of depression may be attributed to several factors, including variability in stimulation parameters (such as frequency, intensity, duration, and total number of sessions) and inter-individual differences among patients, which extend beyond the targeted brain regions [62, 63]. Notably, protocols employing higher intensities [e.g., 120% MT [20]] or extended treatment courses (> 20 sessions [50]) demonstrated trend-level response advantages. This aligns with brTMS's proposed mechanism: simultaneous modulation of prefrontal hemispheric asymmetry requires precise parameter calibration. Optimal outcomes likely depend on frequency pairing (e.g., 10–20 Hz left-DLPFC excitation with 1 Hz right-DLPFC inhibition) to normalize prefrontal-limbic circuitry [63]. A previous meta-analysis with 52 sham-controlled trials was reported that the greater number of pulses and daily sessions were associated with improved rTMS efficacy, whereas other parameters, such as coil positioning method, number of treatment days, stimulation intensity, frequency, and total number of pulses were not association [64]. Another meta-analysis with 37 RCTs reported that brTMS and high frequency urTMS may be the most efficacious rTMS treatments protocols than sham treatment [65]. In addition, Oostra et al. systematically analyzed 87 RCTs of rTMS (67 on HF-rTMS, 11 on LF-rTMS, 9 on both) in treating TRD, which suggested that 1200–1500 HF-pulses/session and 360–450 LF-pulses/session were adequate, while at least 30 sessions were recommended for a higher response chance [66]. Hence, future trials should validate parameter-specific effects through standardized high-intensity and long-term treatment protocols.

Regarding safety, available meta-analyzed data of adverse events included anxiety, headache, nausea, pain, suicide attempt, and tremor. However, only headaches were observed in patients receiving brTMS compared to those undergoing sham stimulation. The elevated headache risk with brTMS may be linked to stimulation parameters, though our meta-analysis design precludes formal correlation analyses due to aggregated study-level data and limited reporting of adverse events by parameter subgroups. Existing protocols vary in left DLPFC high-frequency intensity and total pulses, yet the low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) in our headache analysis implies consistent reporting across these variations, suggesting headache is a common adverse effect likely related to direct stimulation rather than parameter intensity. Future studies should investigate parameter-specific adverse events to identify modifiable risk factors.

However, no significant differences in discontinuation rates were identified. The adverse events associated with brTMS were transient and did not result in persistent or severe consequences [67, 68]. Several studies [69–71] evaluating the safety of brTMS and srTMS in the treatment of MDD and BD similarly reported no notable differences in adverse events between the two modalities. Moreover, adverse events and discontinuation rates did not significantly differ between brTMS and urTMS, indicating comparable safety profiles. Notably, brTMS has demonstrated equivalent safety and efficacy to urTMS in treating other conditions, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [72] and subacute stroke [73], further supporting its consistent safety profile across diverse clinical applications. For instance, one study revealed that both bilateral and unilateral high-frequency right-sided rTMS outperformed sham rTMS in patients with PTSD but failed to confirm the superiority of brTMS over urTMS in efficacy [72]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the favorable safety profile of brTMS as a therapeutic intervention. The finding that brTMS demonstrates comparable efficacy and safety to urTMS, while significantly superior to sham stimulation, has important implications for its clinical application. Given the lack of clear superiority over the well-established urTMS approach, brTMS may not be recommended as a first-line neuromodulation strategy over urTMS for most patients with MDD or BD. However, its comparable efficacy profile positions it as a viable alternative option, particularly in cases where urTMS has been tried but proven ineffective (urTMS non-responders) or is poorly tolerated. This sequential approach aligns with the principle of offering alternative evidence-based interventions when initial treatments fail, as reflected in treatment guidelines for major depression. For instance, the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) guidelines [61] and other consensus statements on neuromodulation emphasize a stepwise strategy, often considering different rTMS protocols (e.g., switching hemispheres, changing frequencies, or moving from unilateral to bilateral stimulation) following an inadequate response to an initial rTMS course [63, 67]. Therefore, based on the current evidence and guideline recommendations, brTMS can be positioned as a reasonable second-line rTMS strategy following unsuccessful urTMS treatment in patients with treatment-resistant MDD or BD.

There are several limitations to this meta-analysis. First, publication bias and significant heterogeneity were evident in the primary outcomes, potentially influencing the results. Second, the study durations of the included RCTs ranged from 2 to 6 weeks, limiting the ability to assess long-term effects. Future research must prioritize well-designed longitudinal trials with extended follow-up periods to rigorously evaluate the durability of the clinical response and remission achieved with brTMS, as well as the long-term safety profile. Third, neurocognitive function could not be assessed due to a lack of available data in the included studies; consequently, future research should prioritize investigating the efficacy of brTMS in improving this domain. Fourth, our analysis was unable to identify predictors of response due to limited data; therefore, future studies should specifically investigate this in patients with MDD and BD. Finally, we attempted to get missing data by contacting corresponding authors and using Getdata software but not available data were got from the two above methods, which is likely to have exerted an influence on the reliability and robustness of our findings.

In conclusion, this meta-analysis confirmed that brTMS is an effective, safe, and well-tolerated treatment option for patients with MDD or BD. Nevertheless, it failed to demonstrate that brTMS confers significant antidepressant superiority relative to urTMS. Therefore, this finding suggest that brTMS may represent an alternative treatment strategy after failed urTMS treatment in clinical practice. Further multi-center, large-sample RCTs are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy of brTMS across diverse demographic and optimize stimulation parameters.
