The effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization: a systematic review and meta-analysis of animal studies
Authors: Monireh Askarzadeh, Rezvan Yazdian-Robati, Misagh Rajabinejad, Alireza Rafiei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveBased on the bidirectional relationship between sleep and macrophages in healthy and diseased states, this systematic review and meta-analysis investigated the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization.MethodsA literature search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WoS) databases up to October 2023, identifying English-language studies on disrupted sleep’s effects on macrophage polarization. After screening and data extraction, the standardized mean difference (SMD) with a 95% Confidence Interval (CI) was calculated for M1 pro-inflammatory and M2 anti-inflammatory markers. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I-square (I2) statistic, and subgroup analyses were performed. The methodological quality was assessed using SYRCLE’s RoB tool. Publication bias was examined through Egger’s test and the trim-and-fill method, and a sensitivity analysis was also conducted. The meta-analysis was performed using STATA version 14.2 software.ResultsThirteen studies were included. Results indicated that disrupted sleep significantly increased CD11c levels [SMD: 2.450, 95% CI: 1.517 to 3.383, I² = 11.7%], and decreased CD206 levels [SMD: -2.616, 95% CI: -4.911 to -0.321, I² = 83.3%]. The association between disrupted sleep and M1/M2 was uncertain, with sex identified as a potential source of heterogeneity. No publication bias or sensitivity was detected, which strengthens the reliability and robustness of the findings.ConclusionDisrupted sleep significantly influenced macrophage polarization towards a CD11c+ M1 inflammatory phenotype. Given the heterogeneity and limited number of studies, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Further research on the underlying mechanisms, particularly concerning biological sex, to better understand sleep’s impact on immune function and treatment strategies for inflammatory conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-025-00770-z.

Introduction

Disrupted sleep and sleepiness are common in developed societies and are associated with various health conditions, including depression, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and asthma [1–3]. Disrupted sleep is associated with disturbance in metabolic function. For example, disrupted sleep causes elevated food intake, disrupted lipid balance, adipose tissue inflammation, insulin resistance, obesity, and type 2 diabetes (T2D) [3–10]. Due to the existing relationship between sleep, the immune system, and metabolism [11], it is possible that disrupted sleep, by affecting immune cells, causes interruptions in metabolic function and results in a disease state. One of these immune cells is the macrophage.

The two main phenotypes of macrophages, M1 inflammatory and M2 anti-inflammatory, are characterized by their functional roles in inflammation and tissue repair, respectively [12–14]. The M1 classically activated macrophages express inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and NADPH oxidase. They destroy pathogens by producing nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS). This phenotype produces high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, and IL-12 and plays a vital role in host defense against pathogens, tumor cell killing, and pathogenesis of inflammatory disease [12, 13, 15, 16]. M1 macrophages also express markers such as HLA-DR, CD68, CD86, F4/80, CD40, CD38, G-protein coupled receptor 18 (Gpr18) and formyl peptide receptor 2 (Fpr2), and CD11c [17–20].

M2 alternatively activated macrophages are defined by their role in reparative processes and anti-inflammatory properties. Furthermore, M2 macrophages are recognized as the predominant phenotype of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are instrumental in promoting angiogenesis. Human and mouse M2 macrophages are distinguished by their elevated expression levels of specific markers, including CD163, CD206, and CD204, found in inflammatory zone-1 (FIZZ1), chitinase 3-like 3 (CHI3L3; also called Ym1), early growth response protein 2 (Egr2), c-Myc, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and cMAF. M2 macrophages produce anti-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-10 and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). Additionally, they express arginase-1 (Arg1), an enzyme that plays a significant role in arginine metabolism, thereby facilitating tissue repair and enhancing anti-inflammatory responses [18–21].

Macrophages are resident cells in different tissues and play different roles based on their phenotypes [22–25]. For example, a significant portion of the non-adipocyte cell population in white adipose tissue (WAT) [26, 27] is adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) [22, 28] that exhibit an M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype in a lean and metabolically healthy state and regulate the surveillance, remodeling, and insulin sensitivity in this tissue [26, 27, 29]. As obesity, insulin resistance, and T2D advance, ATMs undergo a shift in phenotype, becoming more abundant and making up to 50% of the cells in adipose tissue (AT), causing an imbalance between M1 and M2 macrophages [27]. These M1 inflammatory macrophages play a significant role in intensifying inflammation within the adipose tissue environment [22, 26–30] (Fig. 1).

Macrophages, comprising a substantial portion of the tumor microenvironment (TME), ranging from 30% to 50%, significantly influence the polarization of TAMs into M1 and M2 types. During the initial stages of tumor formation, there is an M1 phenotype of macrophages that exhibits an anti-tumor response. However, as tumors progress, they transition to an M2 phenotype, which supports tumor advancement [23, 31]. The effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization has been investigated. Some studies showed that disrupted sleep shifted macrophages toward an M1 inflammatory phenotype in AT [2, 3, 9, 10, 32]. However, some studies illustrated inverse results in AT and TME [2, 33–35]. Therefore, we designed this systematic review and meta-analysis to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization.

Methods

Information sources and search strategy

A literature search was performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WOS) databases to identify studies that investigated the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization using the search terms provided in Appendix A (Table A.1) up to October 2023. Subsequently, the reference lists of included studies were examined. Then, the full-text of potentially relevant articles was obtained.

Eligibility criteria

Studies that met the following eligible criteria were included in this review: (1) primary research studies, (2) studies that were available in English, (3) studies that reported at least one set of results investigating the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization in animals, as measured by surface or intracellular markers of macrophages or markers released in the conditioned media of tissue macrophage culture.

Study selection and study characteristics

AR and MA reviewed the titles and abstracts of the identified articles for eligibility and then obtained the full-text of potentially relevant articles.

Data extraction

A unified data extraction form was created to gather pertinent information from the studies. The subsequent data that were collected consisted of the following: author(s), publication year, country, sample size, age, sex, species, strain, weight, method of sleep fragmentation (SF) induction (directly in animals or indirectly in pregnant mice and their offspring), duration of SF, the device used to induce SF, the tissue in which macrophages were examined, name of the case group, name of the control group, the number of cases, the number of controls, M1 markers, M2 markers, and the technique used to investigate macrophage markers.

Risk-of-bias assessment

MA evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies and identified any potential biases present using SYRCLE’s RoB tool [36], a widely used tool for assessing the risk of bias in animal studies. The risk of bias assessment was visualized using Risk-of-bias VISualization (robvis) [37], a software tool that provides a graphical representation of the risk of bias assessment. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Effectiveness assessment

The effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization was evaluated by investigating its impact on the levels of CD11c as an M1 macrophage marker and CD206 as an M2 macrophage marker, or the M1/M2 ratio (e.g., CD11c/CD206 or CD86/CD206), as these were the most consistently reported across the included studies. The required information included the mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size (N). For studies that presented numerical data solely in graphical form, WebPlot Digitizer (Automeris LLC, Frisco, Texas) was utilized to extract the data points from the figures [38]. Among these studies, the statistical plots of one study were presented as box plots [3]. We employed the estimation method by Wan et al. [39] to obtain the mean and SD for this case. In some cases, the standard error of the mean (SEM) and N were employed to calculate SD (see Appendix A, Eq. (A.1)). The SMD using Hedges’ g, which applies a small-sample correction, with a 95% CI, was used as the effect size. All analyses were conducted under a random-effects model using the DerSimonian–Laird estimator for between-study variance (τ²). We assessed the heterogeneity of the included studies by conducting Cochran’s Q test and analyzing Q and I-square (I2) statistics. Furthermore, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Based on biological plausibility and prior research, we evaluated sex, macrophage tissue type, sleep fragmentation methods (direct or indirect), and the definition of the M1/M2 ratio (CD11c/CD206 or CD86/CD206). Due to the limited number of studies within each subgroup, these analyses were pre-specified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Meta-analysis was performed using STATA version 14.2 software (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Data obtained from different assay platforms were standardized into Hedges’ g effect sizes, thereby facilitating comparability across measurement methodologies. To maintain statistical independence, we adhered to the following a priori decision protocols. When both sexes were reported, male and female data were regarded as independent comparisons, as each sex was examined with its own case and control groups. For outcome measures, we extracted only CD11c, CD206, and the M1/M2 ratio when reported, as these markers were the most consistently assessed across studies and enabled quantitative synthesis. CD11c was used as the primary marker of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, while CD206 was used as the primary marker of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages. The M1/M2 ratio was considered an integrative index of macrophage polarization balance, reflecting the relative dominance of pro-inflammatory versus anti-inflammatory phenotypes. In the study conducted by Khalyfa A. (2015), macrophage polarization was evaluated in both visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue (VWAT and SCAT). Given that each depot was investigated using distinct independent experimental and control groups, both VWAT and SCAT were incorporated into the analysis as separate comparisons. This methodology ensured that biologically significant differences between adipose depots were captured while preserving the independence of effect sizes.

Publication bias assessment

The investigation of potential publication bias was conducted using Egger’s test [40] and the trim-and-fill method [41, 42].

Sensitivity analysis

In addition, to explore how a particular study affects the overall effect estimate, sensitivity analysis was performed using the one-out remove method in the meta-analysis results [43].

All statistical analyses were two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

Study selection

AR and MA searched, read, removed duplicates and unrelated studies, and selected studies according to the inclusion criteria. The initial search yielded a total of 5434 articles. After removing the duplicates (n = 1163), 4271 unique articles remained. Upon reviewing the titles and abstracts, an additional 3782 articles were excluded as not being relevant or non-English. Further studies were excluded for the following reasons: review or meta-analysis articles (n = 353); book sections, meeting reports, comments, editorials, errata, guidelines, notes, viewpoints, hypotheses, and regular articles (n = 79). Consequently, the full-text assessment was performed on the thirteen included articles that ultimately met the inclusion criteria. Study characteristics

Thirteen studies were included. The included studies were conducted in various countries, including the United States (n = 8) [1, 2, 9, 10, 32, 33, 35, 44], China (n = 2) [34, 45], Brazil (n = 2) [46, 47], the United States and Italy (n = 1) [3]. We focused on comparing various groups, explicitly examining those exposed to disrupted sleep with a control group experiencing normal sleep. Notably, the study by Gozal et al. did not include a control group for sleep [10]. Among the included studies, three studies induced SF in pregnant mice, and the offspring were observed [2, 32, 45]. The animal strain examined in two studies was rats [44, 45], whereas the rest of the studies used mice. In one study, the mice species was female Balb/c [34], and in another study, both male and female C57BL/6J [2] mice were evaluated, while other studies evaluated male C57BL/6J or male A/J mice. The investigated macrophages in these studies included TAMs in three studies [33–35], lung-derived macrophages in one study [46], microglia in two studies [44, 45], peritoneal macrophages [47], and adipose-derived macrophages in the rest of the studies. Risk of bias assessment

The SYRCLE’s RoB tool comprises six entries, each associated with a distinct type of bias: selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases [36]. Most studies were judged as “unclear” in sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinding, reflecting incomplete reporting. Apart from the two studies [2, 3], the remaining studies did not provide information regarding sequence generation. All included studies indicated that the baseline characteristics were similar across the groups. Except for two studies [3, 32], the remaining studies lacked information regarding allocation concealment. Only two studies [3, 32] reported whether the animals were randomized during the experiments. Furthermore, only three studies [1, 3, 32] implemented blinding for caregivers or researchers. Apart from the two studies [3, 32], the majority of the studies did not provide information on random outcome assessment. All the studies demonstrated complete outcome data and lacked selective outcome reporting. Furthermore, no other sources of bias were identified in any of the studies. Notably, one study explicitly stated its adherence to the ARRIVE guidelines [3]. Another study mentioned that the individual experimenter was blinded to the treatment group, although this information was not provided in the sections relevant to our intended outcome [44]. Importantly, none of the studies were judged to be at high risk of bias in any domain. The summary of the risk of bias assessment, visualized using Risk-of-bias VISualization (robvis) [37], is presented in Fig. 3. Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the M1/M2 ratio synthesis. The potential factors examined as potential sources of heterogeneity in the studies included the macrophage tissue type, sex, the method of SF induction (direct or indirect), and the M1/M2 ratio (CD11c/CD206 or CD86/CD206). These analyses were interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, considering the limited number of studies per subgroup. The results showed that sex statistically significantly modified the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization (interaction p = 0.002). In male animals, the pooled estimate suggested an increase in the M1/M2 ratio (SMD = 1.492, 95% CI: 0.254 to 2.729), but substantial heterogeneity remained (I² = 80.8%), making the reliability of this subgroup effect uncertain. In contrast, female animals showed a modest negative association (SMD = − 0.731, 95% CI: − 1.367 to − 0.094, I² = 0%). Tissue type (adipose or tumor) and M1/M2 ratio definition (CD11c/CD206 or CD86/CD206) did not yield statistically significant subgroup effects (interaction p = 0.051 for both). Nevertheless, these analyses were constrained by the limited number of studies conducted in the tumor tissue and CD86/CD206 subgroups, thereby diminishing statistical power to discern subgroupal disparities. Furthermore, the methodology employed for SF induction did not substantially alter the impact of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization (interaction p = 0.222). Nevertheless, heterogeneity persisted within the majority of subgroups. Finally, it should be noted that substantial heterogeneity was also present in the CD206 + M2 synthesis (I² = 83%). Due to the very small number of available studies (k = 3), subgroup analysis was not feasible for this outcome. The sensitivity analysis did not show any changes in direction in any of the results, suggesting that the overall findings of the meta-analysis are robust and stable. The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Appendix A (Figure A.1).

Discussion

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis study suggest valuable insights into how disrupted sleep affects macrophage polarization, with a particular focus on the M1 and M2 phenotypes. The rigorous selection process, which reduced 5434 articles to just 13 relevant studies, highlights the thoroughness of the review and the reliability of the findings presented.

Some studies have illustrated evidence that disrupted sleep polarizes macrophages towards an M1 inflammatory phenotype. For example, several studies have shown an increase in CD11c levels [1, 3, 9, 10, 32], as well as an increase in CD68 levels, which serve as M1 markers under the influence of SF [44, 45]. The increased expression of other M1 markers, including IL-1β [44, 45], IL-6, and TNF-α [1, 2, 45], as well as the p47phox subunit of the NOX2 complex, NOX activity [1], iNOS [44, 45], and MCP-1 [2] has been illustrated in other studies. Furthermore, evaluated levels of TNFα in VWAT-MQ culture supernatant under the influence of SF have been found [9]. The M1 metabolic phenotype (CD11c+CD36+ cells) was also increased [10], and an increase in Ly6chigh cells was observed due to SF [3, 32, 48]. This rise in proinflammatory monocytes in VWAT may suggest monocyte recruitment from the bone marrow to VWAT [32]. Allergic mice exhibited an increase in IL-6+ and TNFα+ producing macrophages following SF [46]. Several studies have indicated a decrease in CD206 expression [1, 3, 9, 10, 32, 45], as well as a decrease in the expression of other M2 markers, including Arg1, Ym1, IL-4, IL-10 [2, 45], and Fizz1 [2]. Additionally, an increase in the M1/M2 ratio [2, 3, 9, 10, 32] was observed, which reflects a macrophage pro-inflammatory polarity switch induced by SF. However, several studies have reported contradictory findings [2, 34, 35, 46]. These studies have shown that SF leads to an increased M2/M1 ratio [33, 35] or a decreased M1/M2 ratio [2, 34], as well as a reduction in NOX activity, decreased levels of ROS in TAMs, reduced gp91phox in tumor lysate, and suppression of M1 pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype in TME [3, 33, 35]. Furthermore, these studies have also demonstrated higher expression of other M2 markers, including Fizz1, Arg1, and Mrc1 [33, 35], and marked increases in IL-10 [2]. Additionally, impaired nitric oxide (NO) production was observed in peritoneal macrophages stimulated with LPS and IFN-γ [47]. Collectively, these results suggest SF promotes a shift in macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype.

The findings of this meta-analysis indicated that disrupted sleep has a considerable impact on macrophage polarization, particularly enhancing the M1 inflammatory phenotype, as evidenced by increased CD11c levels and the substantial decrease in CD206 levels, suggesting a reduction in M2 macrophage, highlighting the inflammatory diseases associated with disrupted sleep. However, the absence of significant results concerning the M1/M2 ratio introduced some uncertainty about the overall influence of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization.

Based on the literature, macrophages exhibit a strong circadian rhythm. Circadian rhythms are biological processes that follow a roughly 24-hour cycle and play a vital role in regulating various physiological functions, including the immune response. The activities of macrophages are regulated by the circadian clock, and disruptions in these rhythms can alter the macrophage phenotype, potentially leading to inflammatory conditions. At the molecular level, mouse models exhibiting circadian disruption illustrated a dysregulation of inflammatory gene expression in macrophages. The circadian clock is controlled by a complex network of components that rhythmically regulate the expression of various genes. Key components include Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Kaput (CLOCK), Brain and Muscle ARNT-Like 1 (BMAL1), Period (PER), Cryptochrome (CRY), Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 1 Group D Member 1 (NR1D1, also known as REV-ERBα), Retinoic Acid-Related Orphan Receptor Alpha (RORα). The interaction between CLOCK, BMAL1, PER, CRY, REV-ERBα, and RORα is essential for the regulation of macrophage polarization. The CLOCK: BMAL1 complex activates the transcription of circadian-controlled genes, including those involved in macrophage function and polarization, while PER and CRY proteins provide negative feedback to this activation. This feedback loop ensures that macrophage responses are tightly regulated according to the time of day. REV-ERBα suppresses the pro-inflammatory cytokine genes and promotes M2 polarization, while RORα enhances the expression of pro-inflammatory genes and leads to M1 polarization. The balance between these two factors is crucial for determining the macrophage’s functional state in response to circadian cues. The rhythmic nature of macrophage polarization suggests that the inflammatory response can fluctuate significantly throughout the day. In other words, macrophages show a more robust pro-inflammatory response during the active phase to fight infections, while exhibiting anti-inflammatory responses during the rest phase that assist in tissue repair and recovery. Some factors, such as shift work, sleep disturbances, and metabolic stressors associated with TME and aging, lead to disruption of these rhythms and alter macrophage polarization. Indeed, this misalignment is associated with an increased prevalence of chronic inflammatory conditions, as the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages becomes skewed, often favoring a pro-inflammatory state. Circadian disruption has been linked to various chronic inflammatory diseases, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and rheumatoid arthritis. In these conditions, the dysregulation of macrophage polarization can exacerbate inflammation, leading to tissue damage and disease progression. As it was mentioned, obesity and insulin resistance are associated with a chronic inflammatory state characterized by an increased presence of M1 macrophages in adipose tissue. Circadian misalignment can further aggravate this inflammatory state, contributing to metabolic dysregulation. Circadian rhythms influence tumor microenvironments and the behavior of TAMs. Disrupted circadian rhythms can lead to a skewing of macrophages towards a pro-resolution phenotype, which may promote tumor growth and metastasis. The understanding of the impact of circadian rhythms on macrophage polarization opens avenues for chronotherapy, where the timing of treatments is optimized based on the circadian cycle. This approach could enhance the efficacy of therapeutic strategies and improve patient outcomes in chronic inflammatory diseases. Interventions aimed at restoring circadian rhythms, such as maintaining regular sleep patterns, exposure to light, and time-restricted feeding, may be beneficial [49–54].

The considerable heterogeneity observed among studies emphasized the variability, likely due to differences in the methods of SF induction, animal sex, macrophage tissue type, and the specific markers examined for M1 macrophages (CD11c or CD86). This variability reflected the complexity of the relationship between sleep and immune function. Following subgroup analysis, results illustrated that sex significantly impacted the effect of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization, with male subgroups exhibiting unexplained heterogeneity. This finding is crucial, as it suggests that biological sex may play a fundamental role in how disrupted sleep impacts immune responses. Drawing from the preclinical studies, sex steroid hormones and their receptors impact macrophage polarization in various cell types, including murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), alveolar macrophages, THP-1, BV-2, and RAW 264.7 macrophage cell lines. The researchers employed agonist treatments and selective knockout techniques to evaluate the impact of these hormones on macrophage polarization. The results of multiple studies showed that estrogen facilitated the macrophage polarization towards the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype while reducing the macrophage polarization towards the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype. Indeed, estradiol (E2) treatment in LPS-stimulated macrophages resulted in an impaired NF-κB pathway, decreased levels of NO, iNOS, and COX expression, and elevated levels of IL-10. Additionally, E2 increased M2 gene expression in macrophages stimulated with IL-4. Studies utilizing models of cutaneous wound repair, tumor, psoriatic inflammation, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), and rheumatic diseases illustrated that estrogen receptor (ER) signaling in various macrophage populations caused a reduction in inflammation and immune responses. However, some studies suggested that low concentrations of estrogen showed pro-inflammatory but high concentrations showed anti-inflammatory effects [55, 56]. Androgens such as testosterone and dihydrotestosterone have been found to enhance the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages [55, 57]. Additionally, progesterone has been found to inhibit inflammatory responses of macrophages by decreasing the levels of NO and IL-12, blocking the activation of TLR4 and TLR9, and disrupting NF-kB activation [55]. Studies comparing male- and female-derived BMDMs, myocardial macrophages, and alveolar macrophages have shown a significant elevation in both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotypes in female-derived cells, compared to male-derived cells at the same time point. These results suggest that female-derived macrophages may exhibit heightened sensitivity to environmental stimuli [58–61]. Overall, the impact of biological sex on macrophage polarization should be regarded as a critical variable in future research endeavors.

Furthermore, meticulous analyses have contributed to the credibility of our meta-analysis. Indeed, the absence of significant publication bias in the analyses of CD11c and CD206 enhanced the reliability of these findings. Conversely, the detected potential bias in the M1/M2 ratio analysis necessitated caution when interpreting these results. The sensitivity analysis indicated the robustness of the overall findings that reinforced confidence in the conclusions derived from this meta-analysis. Our use of Hedges’ g with a random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird) helps account for small sample sizes and between-study variability, which strengthens the reliability of the pooled estimates. Additionally, it is essential to consider that CD11c is not a unique marker of M1 inflammatory macrophages, as dendritic cells or neutrophils also express CD11c, and their increased presence in tissue might contribute to the higher population of CD11c+ cells [26].

While the dynamics of macrophage polarization can significantly influence behavioral activities in humans [49], there is still a lack of research specifically examining macrophage effects on human sleep. Previous research found that acute sleep manipulations, prolonged periods of sleep restriction, or sleep deprivation all consistently increased blood monocytes in adults [62]. However, analyses of human blood monocyte subsets based on 24-hour rhythms revealed that non-classical monocytes decreased during sleep [63]. Tissue-specific single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has opened up new possibilities for analyzing macrophage polarity, mapping cell types, and characterizing subsets in both health and disease; therefore, this new technology has the potential to shed light on the physiological and pathological effects of disrupted sleep on macrophage polarization in humans, elucidating current inconsistencies in human studies. For example, Ling et al. employed scRNA-seq to show that the fraction of disease-associated M2 macrophages upregulated with the course of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) [64]. In another study by Casanova-Acebes et al., scRNA-seq was used to determine the various subpopulations of macrophages that are present inside the TME of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [65]. As a result, the development of new technologies and bioinformatic tools may help to address present knowledge gaps and methodological pitfalls in the field.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study revealed disrupted sleep’s significant effects on macrophage polarization, particularly in favor of a CD11c+ M1 inflammatory phenotype. Our findings illustrate the complexity of macrophage polarization in the context of disrupted sleep and highlight a need for future research to explore the underlying mechanisms, particularly concerning the impact of biological sex. The results also emphasize the role of circadian rhythms in regulating macrophage behavior. This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the number of studies available for some outcomes was very small (e.g., CD11c and CD206 with k = 3), which reduces statistical power and limits the reliability of publication bias tests. Another notable limitation is the insufficient documentation of risk-of-bias assessment. While funnel plots and Egger’s test were provided for descriptive purposes, such evaluations lack sufficient power, and no definitive inferences concerning publication bias can be made. The majority of the included studies were classified as “unclear” in key domains, including randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding processes. Despite the fact that no study was considered to have a high risk of bias according to the extracted data, the prevalence of “unclear” assessments suggests that the true risk of bias may be underestimated. Therefore, caution is warranted in interpreting the findings. Our exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that sex may influence the relationship between disrupted sleep and macrophage polarization, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of studies and substantial residual heterogeneity. Subgroup comparisons by tissue type, induction method, and marker definition did not yield consistent differences, further underscoring the exploratory nature of these analyses. In one included study (Khalyfa A, 2015), both VWAT and SCAT adipose depots were analyzed. Because each depot was studied using independent experimental and control groups, both were included in the analysis as separate comparisons. Although this approach is justified to preserve independence and capture biologically relevant distinctions, it may have marginally elevated the relative weight of this study within the pooled analysis. It should be mentioned that only three macrophage markers were consistently reported across studies (CD11c, CD86, and CD206). Differences in gating strategies, assay platforms, and tissue contexts may limit comparability and contribute to heterogeneity. Addressing the identified methodological weaknesses and ensuring comprehensive reporting will be crucial for advancing research in this area. Understanding these dynamics could lead to novel therapeutic strategies for treating sleep-related inflammatory conditions and enhancing overall health outcomes.
