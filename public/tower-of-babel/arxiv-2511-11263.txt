Multiple correlation lengths and type-1.5 superconductivity in U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) superconductors due to hidden competition between irreducible representations of nonlocal pairing
Anton Talkachov, Paul Leask, Egor Babaev

Abstract
A fundamental characteristic of a superconducting state is the coherence length ξ \xi . Multicomponent superconductors, particularly ones breaking multiple symmetries, are characterized by multiple coherence lengths. Here we show that even, nominally single -component superconductors under certain conditions are characterized by multiple coherence lengths. We consider nearest-neighbor pairing interactions on a square lattice that leads to s s -wave and d d -wave representations of link superconducting order parameter. We show that even if the subdominant order parameter is completely suppressed in the ground state, it results in multiple correlation lengths with nontrivial hierarchy, resulting in important physical consequences in inhomogeneous solutions. Under certain conditions, this leads to type-1.5 superconductivity, where magnetic field penetration length falls between two coherence lengths, leading to vortex clustering in an external magnetic field.



The coherence length, introduced by Ginzburg and Landau
17
is a key characteristic of a superconducting state.
It is defined as an exponent that characterizes the spatial recovery of the Cooper pairing field away from a perturbation, such as a defect, local density fluctuation, or vortex.
In fact, this length scale is the key characteristic not only of a superconducting state but also of the normal state at temperatures close to the phase transition, where it sets the correlation length due to local pairing fluctuations and is important, e.g., in the phenomenon of paraconductivity
22
.

Conventionally, superconductors were characterized by a single coherence (or, equivalently, correlation) length.
Then its ratio relative to another fundamental length scale, the magnetic field penetration depth
\lambda
, determines the magnetic response
21
;
1
;
18
.
This is the Ginzburg–Landau (GL) parameter
\kappa=\lambda/\xi
.
For
\kappa>1/\sqrt{2}
, one has a type-II superconductor that possesses stable vortices repelling each other and forming a vortex lattice.
In contrast, for
\kappa<1/\sqrt{2}
we have a type-I superconductor where vortices attract each other and are not stable.

The clearest exception to this type-I/II dichotomy is where a superconductor has two broken symmetries, necessitating the introduction of two coherence lengths
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}
.
For example, there are a plethora of superconductors that break
U(1)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
time-reversal symmetry, giving rise to multiple coherence lengths (see detailed discussion in
10
;
14
).
A new (termed in
26
, type-1.5) regime then arises when
\lambda
is an intermediate length scale
4
.
For a superconductor with
n
-broken symmetries the type-1.5 regime occurs when the magnetic penetration depth
\lambda
falls between coherence lengths
\xi_{i}
(
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<...\lambda...<\xi_{n-1}<\xi_{n}
).
In such a state, vortices have a composite core with several competing length scales.
As a result of this competition, vortices attract each other on the length scale of larger coherence lengths, due to the vortex outer-cores overlapping.
On length scales of the magnetic penetration depth, the vortices repel one another due to magnetic/current-current interaction, forming stable vortex clusters.
This long-range attraction and short-range repulsion behavior, characteristic of type-1.5 superconductivity, enhances the superconducting typology by breaking the type-I/II dichotomy.
A more subtle case is the appearance of multiple correlation lengths in multiband superconductors that break a single
U(1)
symmetry.
In this case, multiple well-defined correlation lengths are not guaranteed by symmetry, but instead require certain microscopic conditions to be met by interband interactions
9
;
36
;
37
;
41
.
For experimental research see
26
;
28
;
29
;
7
;
42
.

In this work, we return to the basic question of what the coherence/correlation lengths and typology of a superconducting system with a single broken symmetry are.
We consider superconductivity in a system with competing pairing channels that belong to two different irreducible representations of nonlocal pairing.
In this case, we specifically focus on the regime where one of the competing order parameters is suppressed.
At first glance, such a system represents a case of a single-component superconductor that neither breaks multiple symmetries nor has multiple electronic bands.
Nevertheless, we show that, under certain conditions, a suppressed competing pairing channel significantly alters correlations in the system.
We show that such a system, in general, can only be described by the introduction of multiple correlation lengths.
The origin of additional coherence lengths in this type of ground state comes from the structure of gradient terms.

The effect is partially related to the phenomenon that, in a normal state, a proximate superconducting phase gives rise to well-defined coherence lengths, despite a lack of any broken symmetries.
In our case, we find that a proximate, but non-developed superconducting phase with different broken symmetry imprints additional correlation lengths in superconducting condensates.
We show that this principally alters the type of magnetic response of the system.
Furthermore, we show that such a nominally single-component system cannot be a type-II superconductor in proximity to a continuous phase transition to a different pairing state with additional broken symmetry.

We start from a single electronic band microscopic model with nearest-neighbor pairing and compute the phase diagram as a function of doping.
Two superconducting components arise in this model from the mathematical concept of symmetry (irreducible representations).
Then we derive the corresponding Ginzburg–Landau functional and discuss the coherence lengths
\xi_{i}
and magnetic field penetration depth
\lambda
.
We estimate a region on the phase diagram where type-1.5 superconductivity arises.
Performing Ginzburg–Landau calculations, we show the existence of vortex clusters in a
U(1)\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry breaking state (
s+id
) and also report configurations with nonzero skyrmion charge.
Importantly, we find vortex clusters in the
U(1)
symmetry breaking state where
only one
component of the order parameter (
s
-wave or
d
-wave) is nucleated in the ground state.

Microscopic model.
Consider a mean-field Hubbard Hamiltonian on a square lattice with attractive interaction
V
(
V>0
) between nearest neighbors:

\begin{gathered}H_{\text{MF}}=-\sum_{i\sigma}\mu c_{i,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{i,\sigma}-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,\sigma}e^{i\frac{ea}{\hbar}A_{ij}}c_{i,\sigma}^{\dagger}c_{j,\sigma}\\
+\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\left(\Delta_{ji}c_{i,\uparrow}^{\dagger}c_{j,\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\Delta^{*}_{ij}c_{i,\downarrow}c_{j,\uparrow}+\tfrac{|\Delta_{ij}|^{2}}{V}\right)+\sum_{\text{plaqu-}\atop\text{ettes}}\tfrac{a^{2}L_{z}}{2\mu_{0}t_{xy}}B_{z}^{2},\end{gathered}

(1)

where
\Delta_{ij}=-V\langle c_{i,\downarrow}c_{j,\uparrow}\rangle
is the superconducting order parameter on a link
ij
.
Here
c_{i,\sigma}^{\dagger}(c_{i,\sigma})
is the creation (annihilation) operator for an electron with spin
\sigma
on site
i
,
\mu
is the chemical potential,
a
is the unit cell size in
xy
plane,
L_{z}
is the interlayer distance,
\mu_{0}
is the vacuum magnetic permeability, and
A_{ij}=\tfrac{1}{a}\int_{j}^{i}\boldsymbol{A}d\boldsymbol{l}
.
The energies, gap, chemical potential, and temperature are in the units of hopping integral
t_{xy}
, and
k_{B}=1
.

The superconducting order parameter living on nearest-neighbor links can be mapped to a linear superposition of two one-dimensional irreducible representations living on sites corresponding to extended
s
-wave (
A_{1g}
irreducible representation) and
d
-wave (
B_{1g}
)

\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta_{s}(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})+\Delta_{d}(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}).

(2)

Besides the overall
U(1)
gauge symmetry, the superconducting gap is characterized by three parameters: the amplitudes
|\Delta_{s}|
,
|\Delta_{d}|
, and the relative phase difference between components
\theta=\arg(\Delta_{s}\Delta_{d}^{*})
.
For a state where
|\Delta_{s}|\neq 0,\,|\Delta_{d}|\neq 0
, the phase difference is
\pi/2
for systems with the

rotational symmetry

Ginzburg–Landau description.
The Ginzburg–Landau free energy density, derived from the microscopic model as an expansion in several small parameters associated with two gaps Eq. (
1
) in the weak coupling limit, has the following form

\begin{gathered}F=\alpha_{1}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}+\alpha_{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{2}+\beta_{1}|\Delta_{s}|^{4}+\beta_{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{4}\\
+\beta_{3}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{2}+\beta_{4}(\Delta_{s}^{*2}\Delta_{d}^{2}+\Delta_{s}^{2}\Delta_{d}^{*2})\\
+\gamma_{1}|\boldsymbol{D}\Delta_{s}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}|\boldsymbol{D}\Delta_{d}|^{2}\\
+\gamma_{12}\left[(D_{y}\Delta_{s})^{*}(D_{y}\Delta_{d})-(D_{x}\Delta_{s})^{*}(D_{x}\Delta_{d})+\text{c.c.}\right]\\
+\tfrac{1}{2}(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}})^{2},\end{gathered}

(3)

where
D_{i}=\partial_{i}+iq\tilde{A}_{i}
is a covariant derivative,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}=\sqrt{\tfrac{L_{z}}{\mu_{0}t_{xy}}}\boldsymbol{A}
, and
q=2\frac{ea}{\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}t_{xy}}{L_{z}}}
.
The Ginzburg–Landau distance is in units of the unit cell size
a
.
Note that we expect the Ginzburg–Landau expansion to fail at low temperature.
Details of the microscopic derivation of the Ginzburg–Landau coefficients and characteristic length scales calculations below are presented in Appendices
B
and
C.1
, respectively.

The resulting Ginzburg–Landau model is (i) anisotropic and (ii) there is an interband potential coupling (
\beta_{3,4}
) and mixed gradient coupling
\gamma_{12}
.
In general, these types of couplings create a hybridization of normal modes
2
;
9
;
38
: that is, a small variation in one of the gap fields causes a small variation in another, and vice versa.
Furthermore, in an anisotropic case, the resulting length scales should also depend on the chosen direction, i.e. fields recover their asymptotic form at different direction-dependent scales, away from a local perturbation
43
.
Exact evaluation of length scales leads to a degree 6 polynomial equation (for amplitudes, phase difference, and three gauge field components) that determine all the length scales in the system, while all the normal modes are, in general, mixed in the models of the type of Eq. (
3
)
38
.
For our purpose here, it is sufficient to make a rough estimate, that will follow by a numerical analysis: we first neglect the fact that the relative phase
\theta
changes for small amplitude fluctuations and vice versa.
This assumption, in fact, breaks for vortex core analysis.
However, this method allows us to get a coarse estimate and to illustrate the principle.
For a precise description, we will resort to numerical calculations.
The magnetic field penetration depth
\lambda
and coherence lengths
\xi_{i}
are obtained by linearizing the free energy
F
with respect to variations in the order parameter amplitudes (
|\Delta_{s/d}|=|\Delta_{s/d}^{\text{GS}}|+\sigma_{s/d}
where
\sigma_{s/d}
is small)

\begin{gathered}F_{\text{lin}}=F_{\text{GS}}+\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}(D_{i}\sigma_{\alpha})^{*}(D_{j}\sigma_{\beta})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\alpha}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\beta}\\
+q^{2}|\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}|^{2}\left(\gamma_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}\right)+\frac{(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}})^{2}}{2}\end{gathered}

(4)

where
\mathcal{H}
is the Hessian matrix of potential energy about the ground state.

The magnetic field penetration depth
\lambda
in SI units can be
roughly estimated
as

\lambda^{-1}=2\frac{e}{\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}t_{xy}}{L_{z}}}\sqrt{2\left(\gamma_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}\right)},

(5)

Each amplitude eigenmode has a direction-dependent coherence length
\xi_{i}
which, within the approximation, are given by

\xi_{i}(\boldsymbol{n})=\frac{a}{\sqrt{\eta_{i}(\varphi)}},

(6)

where
\varphi
is the angle between the chosen direction
\boldsymbol{n}
and the
x
axis and
\eta_{i}(\varphi)
are eigenvalues of
\Gamma^{-1}(\varphi)\mathcal{H}
.
Here the matrix
\Gamma(\varphi)
is given by

\Gamma(\varphi)=2\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}&&-\gamma_{12}\cos 2\varphi\\
-\gamma_{12}\cos 2\varphi&&\gamma_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(7)

and describes the gradient energy for an amplitude perturbation in the direction
\boldsymbol{n}
.
Note that off-diagonal terms describing mixed gradient terms vanish for diagonal propagation directions.
In general, the coherence lengths
\xi_{i}(\boldsymbol{n})
are associated with linear combinations of
\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{d}
, which gives rise to a hybridization of eigenmodes.
The presence of off-diagonal terms in
\Gamma(\varphi)
is crucial: It ensures that the system has two coherence lengths for amplitudes even for a ground state with pure
s
-wave or pure
d
-wave, and suppressed subdominant component.

Conclusion.
In this letter, we studied correlation lengths and vortex physics in a system with
s
- and
d
-wave pairing.
The particularly interesting aspect, not discussed in previous studies, is arising of multiple correlation lengths for a state that not only has one broken symmetry, but also a single order parameter in the ground state, leading to an observable physical effect: Type-1.5 behavior.
Namely, we showed that a competing, although suppressed order parameter, belonging to a different gap irreducible representation of nonlocal pairing, can principally alter the magnetic response of a system.
The extra length scale arises from the suppressed subdominant order parameter, which only nucleates near the vortex core, but gives a nontrivial hierarchy of the length scales of the problem.

This situation that we discussed should be more generic than our particular microscopic example.
Multiple correlation lengths can also arise in systems that have a single phase transition but are parametrically close to different superconducting instabilities belonging to irreducible representations (e.g.
d+ig
state).

These effects can also be utilized in applications.
Instability due to vortex formation is utilized in single-photon detectors.
Vortex clustering alters instabilities of superconducting state and can
be utilized in vortex-based superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors.
For example, the recent study Ref.
6
proposed that
the type-1.5 hierarchy of the length scales can diminish dark counts in single photon detectors.

Appendix A
Microscopic description of phase diagram

Self-consistency equation for superconducting gap has form

\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}V(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\frac{\tanh{\frac{E}{2T}}}{2E},

(8)

with only spin-singlet pairing channel

\begin{split}V(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})=&V\left(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}\right)\left(\cos k_{x}^{\prime}+\cos k_{y}^{\prime}\right)\\
&+V\left(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}\right)\left(\cos k_{x}^{\prime}-\cos k_{y}^{\prime}\right),\end{split}

(9)

where
N
is the number of sites in the lattice,
E=\sqrt{\xi^{2}+|\Delta|^{2}}
is the quasiparticle excitation energy,
\xi
is the dispersion relation [
\xi(\boldsymbol{k})=-2(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})-\mu
].
The sum is computed for energies

smaller than the upper bound denoted
\omega_{D}
.

Self-consistency Eq. (
8
) can be separated to the following system

\begin{cases}\Delta_{s}=\Delta_{s}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}V(\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y})^{2}\frac{\tanh{\frac{E(\bk)}{2T}}}{2E(\boldsymbol{k})},\\
\Delta_{d}=\Delta_{d}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}V(\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})^{2}\frac{\tanh{\frac{E(\bk)}{2T}}}{2E(\boldsymbol{k})}.\end{cases}

(10)

Here,
s
and
d
-waves are still implicitly coupled through energy
E
.
The stationary solution of Eq. (
10
) is obtained when the sums in the RHS of Eq. (
10
) are equal to unity or an order parameter component (
\Delta_{s}
,
\Delta_{d}
or both) is 0.
For numerical calculations, the sum is presented as an integral over the first Brillouin zone (
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}=\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d\boldsymbol{k}}{S_{\text{BZ}}}
taking into account the limits of
\omega_{D}
).
However, for analytical calculations, we use constant density of states [
\rho(\xi,\varphi)\approx N_{F}

\Gamma_{d}\cos^{2}2\varphi

) for

\begin{cases}\Delta_{s}=\Delta_{s}\cdot VN_{F}\gamma_{s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_s^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{s}^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}},\\

(11)

[
\Delta_{d}=0
in the first of Eqs. (

\Delta_{s}=0
in the second equation],

\displaystyle VN_{F}\gamma_{s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\xi}{2T_s}}}{\xi}=1,

(12a)

\displaystyle VN_{F}\Gamma_{d}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\cos^{2}2\varphi\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\xi}{2T_d}}}{\xi}=1.

(12b)

The integral is a standard one with the result
\ln\frac{2e^{\gamma}\omega_{D}}{\pi T_{c}}
, where
\gamma
is Euler’s constant.
So the characteristic temperatures have form
45

\displaystyle T_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\omega_{D}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{VN_{F}\gamma_{s}}\right),

(13a)

\displaystyle T_{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{\pi}\omega_{D}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{VN_{F}\Gamma_{d}/2}\right).

(13b)

Remember that the result holds only for a normal metal to pure

-wave (or pure

-wave) transition.
Therefore, only

determines

However, the concept and notation of the two

temperatures that
do not influence each other
will be useful later in the section.

A.2
Pure
s
-wave to
s+id
state transition for all temperatures

Transition from
U(1)
broken symmetry state to
U(1)\crossproduct\mathbb{Z}_{2}
state is of the second order in the model at the mean-field level

;
34
;
30
;
24
;
15
;

We can assume

for
s+id

Self-consistency Eq. (
11
) on the transition line becomes

\begin{cases}1=VN_{F}\gamma_{s}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_s^2}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{s}^{2}}},\\
1=VN_{F}\gamma_{d}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_s^2}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{s}^{2}}}.\end{cases}

(14)

If the first equation is satisfied, we know
\Delta_{s}
for given

The second equation is satisfied when

(

) in the BCS limit.
This coincides with the conclusion of Ref.

The form factor

depends only on the chemical potential

).
Therefore, the transition is a vertical line (

) on the

,
5

;

A phase diagram with a sharp boundary between

-wave phase and nematic phase with broken time-reversal symmetry was discussed in connection of iron-based superconductors, see e.g.

.

Note that experimentally, one investigates electron density
n

phase diagram because the number of electrons in the sample is fixed during the measurement.
The relation between electron density and chemical potential is the following

n=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left[1-\frac{\xi}{E}\tanh\left(\frac{E}{2T}\right)\right],

(15)

where
\xi
depends on
\mu
and

\mu
and

[through

],

.
This electron density is a measure of the asymmetry between the positive and negative excitations of

.
However, the change of electron density for

is quite small.
Numerically, the change is of the order of

for

Hence,

A.3
Pure

In the subsection, we are interested in a region where

-wave just developed (

Superconducting gap for

\Delta_{d}(T)=4\pi\sqrt{\frac{2}{21\zeta(3)}}T\sqrt{-\ln{\frac{T}{T_d}}}\approx 3.537T_{d}\sqrt{1-\frac{T}{T_{d}}},

where

It follows from the second of Eq. (

The first of Eq. (

) can be simplified in a similar manner to Ref.

First, Taylor expand integrand up to the second order in

Then integrate over
\xi

\begin{gathered}1=VN_{F}\gamma_{s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}\\

\left.-\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\left(\frac{\tanh{\frac{\xi}{2T}}}{2\xi^{3}}-\frac{1-\tanh^{2}{\frac{\xi}{2T}}}{4T\xi^{2}}\right)\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi\right]\\

Combining Eqs. (

A.4
Pure
d
-wave to
s+id
state transition for

Self-consistency Eq. (
11
) becomes

\begin{cases}1=VN_{F}\gamma_{s}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}},\\
1=VN_{F}\Gamma_{d}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\cos^{2}2\varphi\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}.\end{cases}

(19)

Let us work with the first equation of Eq. (
19
).
Add and subtract term
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}
to the integral:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{VN_{F}\gamma_{s}}&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\left(\frac{\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}\right)\\
&=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}\left(\tanh{\frac{\sqrt{\xi^2 + \Delta_d^2 \cos^2 2\varphi}}{2T}}-1\right)+\ln\frac{4\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}}.\end{split}

(20)

where the second integral
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\Delta_{d}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\varphi}{2\pi}\ln\frac{2\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}|\cos 2\varphi|}=\ln\frac{4\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}}
is computed in the limit
\omega_{D}\gg\Delta_{d}(0)

45
.
In the first integral, we change coordinates to
x=\beta\xi
(
\beta=T^{-1}
) and
y=\beta\Delta_{d}\cos 2\varphi

\begin{split}\frac{1}{VN_{F}\gamma_{s}}=&\ln\frac{4\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}}+\frac{1}{2\pi}8\int_{0}^{\beta\Delta_{d}}\frac{dy}{2\sqrt{\beta^{2}\Delta_{d}^{2}-y^{2}}}\\
&\cdot\int_{0}^{\beta\omega_{D}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\left(\tanh\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{2}-1\right).\end{split}

(21)

The main contribution to the integral comes from the region where
x
and
y
are the smallest.
Therefore, we can use approximation
\sqrt{\beta^{2}\Delta_{d}^{2}-y^{2}}\approx\beta\Delta_{d}

\begin{split}\frac{1}{VN_{F}\gamma_{s}}=&\ln\frac{4\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}}+\frac{2T}{\pi\Delta_{d}}\int_{0}^{\beta\Delta_{d}}dy\\
&\cdot\int_{0}^{\beta\omega_{D}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\left(\tanh\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{2}-1\right).\end{split}

(22)

The upper bounds for both integrals can be approximated as infinite because
\beta\rightarrow\infty
and
\Delta_{d},\,\omega_{D}
are some not infinitesimal parameters.
The integral can be computed in the following way:

\begin{gathered}\int_{0}^{\infty}dy\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\left(\tanh\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{2}-1\right)\\
=\int_{0}^{\pi/2}d\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\left(\tanh\frac{r}{2}-1\right)=-\pi\ln 2.\end{gathered}

(23)

Let us substitute this result to Eq. (
22
) and substitute Eq. (
13a
) to the LHS:

\ln\frac{2e^{\gamma}\omega_{D}}{\pi T_{s}}=\ln\frac{4\omega_{D}}{\Delta_{d}}-\frac{2T\ln 2}{\Delta_{d}}

(24)

Therefore, temperature for the pure
d
-wave to
s+id
state transition is

T_{d\text{ to }s+id}=\frac{\Delta_{d}}{2\ln 2}\ln\frac{2\pi T_{s}}{\Delta_{d}e^{\gamma}}

(25)

The last step is to substitute the superconducting gap at zero temperature (
\Delta_{d}(0)=2\pi e^{-\gamma-1/2}T_{d}

45
;
27
) instead of
\Delta_{d}
:

\begin{gathered}T_{d\text{ to }s+id}=\frac{\pi e^{-\gamma-1/2}T_{d}}{\ln 2}\ln\frac{T_{s}e^{1/2}}{T_{d}}\\
=\frac{2\omega_{D}}{\sqrt{e}\ln 2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{VN_{F}\Gamma_{d}/2}-\frac{1}{VN_{F}\gamma_{s}}\right)e^{-2/VN_{F}\Gamma_{d}}.\end{gathered}

(26)

For zero temperature of the transition, we have

\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{VN_{F}}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{s}}-\frac{2}{\Gamma_{d}}\right)=\ln\frac{T_{d}}{T_{s}}.

(27)

So the transition happens when
T_{d}=T_{s}e^{1/2}
if one uses auxiliary characteristic temperature for

-wave (which does not correspond to a phase transition).
The left part of Eq. (
27
) was also obtained in Ref.
27
.

Appendix B
Derivation of Ginzburg–Landau equations from microscopic model

The derivation of GL equations is based on expanding Gor’kov’s (microscopic self-consistency) equations in powers of order parameter
18
.
Below we present a short version.
First, we derive potential terms and then kinetic (gradient) terms.
We start with introducing notations
f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})=\cos k_{x}+\cos k_{y}
,
f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})=\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}
.
Microscopic self-consistency equation Eq. (
8
) takes form

\begin{gathered}\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}V\left[f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})+f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\right]\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\\
\cdot T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})+|\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|^{2}}\\
\approx\frac{VT}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}}\left[f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})+f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\right]\\
\cdot\sum_{\omega_{n}}\left[\frac{\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}-\frac{\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|\Delta(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|^{2}}{[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})]^{2}}\right],\end{gathered}

(28)

where we used identity
T\sum_{\omega_{n}}(\omega_{n}^{2}+E^{2})^{-1}=\frac{\tanh(E/2T)}{2E}
,
\omega_{n}=(1+2n)\pi T
are Matsubara frequencies.
Next, equations for
s
and
d
-wave components of the order parameter
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta_{s}f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})+\Delta_{d}f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})
can be separated:

\begin{gathered}\Delta_{s}\left(\frac{1}{V}-\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega_{n}}\frac{f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}\right)-\Delta_{d}\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega_{n}}\frac{f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})}+\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime},\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})]^{2}}\\
\cdot\left[f_{s}^{4}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\Delta_{s}+2f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|\Delta_{d}|^{2}\Delta_{s}+f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\Delta_{d}^{2}\Delta_{s}^{*}\right.\\
\left.+f_{d}^{3}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})|\Delta_{d}|^{2}\Delta_{d}+f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})f_{s}^{3}(\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime})\left(2|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\Delta_{d}+\Delta_{s}^{2}\Delta_{d}^{*}\right)\right]=0,\end{gathered}

(29)

the equation for
\Delta_{d}
can be obtained by changing indices
s\leftrightarrow d
.
The sum over
\boldsymbol{k}^{\prime}
is zero for odd in
f_{s/d}
terms for the tetragonal system because they are odd w.r.t.
\pi/2
rotation.
Comparing terms from Eq. (
29
) and GL equations that follow from Eq. (
3
) one finds

\begin{gathered}\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{V}-\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})},\\
\alpha_{2}=\frac{1}{V}-\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})},\\
\{\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4}\}\\
=\frac{T}{2N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{\{f_{s}^{4}(\boldsymbol{k}),f_{d}^{4}(\boldsymbol{k}),4f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}),f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})\}}{[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})]^{2}}.\end{gathered}

(30)

Note, that
\beta_{4}=\beta_{3}/4.

Coefficients
\beta_{1-4}
differ by factor 4 from corresponding coefficients in Refs.
20
;
11
.
Origin of the difference roots in the gap definition: They use
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta_{s}\frac{f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2}+\Delta_{d}\frac{f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2}
.
Therefore, if quadratic in
\Delta
’s terms coincide (situation that we have), quartic terms coefficients should be multiplied by factor of
2^{2}
when transforming from
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta_{s}f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})+\Delta_{d}f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})
to
\Delta(\boldsymbol{k})=\Delta_{s}\frac{f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2}+\Delta_{d}\frac{f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})}{2}
.

Now, let us compute gradient terms.
Below we derive coefficients only for quadratic in gaps
\Delta_{s/d}
terms.
Self-consistency equation can be written in real space using Green functions
\mathcal{G}_{0}
in the absence of a magnetic field (
\boldsymbol{A}=0
):

\frac{1}{V}\Delta^{*}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{r})=T\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},\epsilon,\omega_{n}}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r}+\eta,-\omega_{n})\Delta_{\epsilon}^{*}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}+\epsilon,\boldsymbol{r},\omega_{n}),

(31)

where
\eta,\,\epsilon=\pm\hat{x},\,\pm\hat{y}
, and Green function is assumed to be translationally invariant

\mathcal{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{r},\omega_{n})=\mathcal{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r},\omega_{n})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})}/(i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}})
.
Superconducting gap that lives on a links can be expanded in powers of derivatives:

\begin{gathered}\Delta_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\Delta_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})+(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\Delta_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\\
+\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\mu}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r}).\end{gathered}

(32)

Here assumed summation over repeated indices.
Substituting Green functions and Eq. (
32
) to Eq. (
31
) one gets

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{V}\Delta^{*}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{T}{N^{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\epsilon,\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1}}}\frac{1}{i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}}\left[\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\underbrace{\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})}}_{N\delta_{\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\eta+i\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\epsilon}\right.\\
\left.+\nabla_{\mu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\mu}}_{(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{1\mu}}+\eta_{\mu})}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})-i\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\eta+i\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\epsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\sum_{\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}}\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\mu}}_{(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{1\mu}}+\eta_{\mu})}\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})_{\nu}}_{(-i\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{2\nu}}-\epsilon_{\nu})}e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}_{1}+\boldsymbol{k}_{2})(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{r})-i\boldsymbol{k}_{1}\eta+i\boldsymbol{k}_{2}\epsilon}\right].\end{gathered}

(33)

Next, we ”integrate by parts” terms containing
\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{1\mu}}
and
\frac{\partial}{\partial k_{2\nu}}
and then perform sum over
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
like in the first line of Eq. (
33
)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{V}\Delta^{*}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\epsilon,\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\left[\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})+\eta_{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})-\frac{1}{2}\eta_{\mu}\epsilon_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})+i\nabla_{\mu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\frac{\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\mu}}{-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right.\\
\left.-\frac{i}{2}\eta_{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\frac{\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\nu}}{i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}-\frac{i}{2}\epsilon_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\frac{\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\mu}}{-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}}+\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\frac{(\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\mu})(\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\nu})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right]e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}(\eta+\epsilon)}.\end{gathered}

(34)

All the terms except the first and the last vanish during summation over
\epsilon
and
\boldsymbol{k}
because they are odd w.r.t. space inversion (containing
\epsilon,\,\eta
) or
\pi/2
rotation (with
\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\mu}
).
The last term is nonzero only for
\mu=\nu
for the same reason.
These facts significantly simplify self-consistency equation (written up to linear order)

\begin{gathered}\frac{1}{V}\Delta^{*}_{\eta}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\epsilon,\omega_{n}}\frac{1}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}}\left[\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\right.\\
\left.+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{r}_{\mu}^{2}}\Delta^{*}_{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{r})\frac{(\partial\xi_{\boldsymbol{k}}/\partial k_{\mu})^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}_{\boldsymbol{k}}}\right]e^{-i\boldsymbol{k}(\eta+\epsilon)}.\end{gathered}

(35)

Final step is to introduce extended
s
-wave and
d
-wave order parameter [
\Delta_{s}=\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{+\hat{x}}+\Delta_{-\hat{x}}+\Delta_{+\hat{y}}+\Delta_{-\hat{y}})

\Delta_{d}=\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{+\hat{x}}+\Delta_{-\hat{x}}-\Delta_{+\hat{y}}-\Delta_{-\hat{y}})

\begin{gathered}\Delta^{*}_{s}\left(\frac{1}{V}-\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\frac{f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})}\right)\\

(36)

Here we kept only terms that do not vanish after summation over
\boldsymbol{k}
.
Comparing this equation to linearized GL equations from Eq. (

),
gradient coefficients take form

\begin{gathered}\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{12}\}\\
=2\frac{T}{N}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\sin^{2}(k_{x})\frac{\{f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}),f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k}),-f_{s}(\boldsymbol{k})f_{d}(\boldsymbol{k})\}}{[\omega_{n}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})]^{2}},\end{gathered}

(37)

Inclusion of gauge filed
\boldsymbol{A}

\partial_{i}

D_{i}=\partial_{i}+i\frac{2ea}{\hbar}A_{i}

\Delta

The free energy density Eq. (

which can be found by comparison of energies in GL model and microscopic model at
T=0
.
In the Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieffer (BCS) limit it is density of states on the Fermi level.

In the BCS limit (
T\ll\omega_{D}\ll 1

\begin{gathered}\alpha_{1}\approx\frac{1}{V}-2\langle N_{F}f_{s}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle_{\text{FS}}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}d\xi\frac{\tanh{\frac{\xi}{2T}}}{2\xi}\\

(38)

where

13a
).
Similarly
\alpha_{2}\approx\langle N_{F}f_{d}^{2}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle_{\text{FS}}\ln\frac{T}{T_{d}}
.
Coefficients
\beta_{1-4}

\gamma

\begin{gathered}\beta_{1}\approx\langle N_{F}f_{s}^{4}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle_{\text{FS}}\int_{0}^{\omega_{D}}\frac{d\xi}{2\xi^{2}}\left(\frac{\tanh{\frac{\xi}{2T}}}{2\xi}-\frac{1}{4T\cosh^{2}\frac{\xi}{2T}}\right)\\
\approx\langle N_{F}f_{s}^{4}(\boldsymbol{k})\rangle_{\text{FS}}\frac{7\zeta(3)}{16\pi^{2}T^{2}}.\end{gathered}

(39)

As a special case, one can obtain GL coefficients for local
s
-wave pairing derived by Gor’kov
18
.
Considering
f_{s}=1
,
f_{d}=0
we recover potential terms from Ref.
18
.

It is convenient to rescale space (and simultaneously all GL coefficients) for numerical calculations. Total free energy in two dimensions has form

\mathcal{F}=\int_{\mathcal{R}^{2}}F(x)d^{2}x,

(40)

where
F(x)
is given by Eq. (
3
).
Performing space
x=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}}{|\alpha_{1}|}}x^{\prime}
and field
\Delta_{s/d}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{|\alpha_{1}|}{\beta_{1}}}\tilde{\Delta}_{s/d}(x^{\prime})
,

rescaling one gets

+\frac{\beta_{4}}{\beta_{1}}(\tilde{\Delta}_{s}^{*2}\tilde{\Delta}_{d}^{2}+\tilde{\Delta}_{s}^{2}\tilde{\Delta}_{d}^{*2})+|\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}\tilde{\Delta}_{s}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}|\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}\tilde{\Delta}_{d}|^{2}\\
+\frac{\gamma_{12}}{\gamma_{1}}\left[(D^{\prime}_{y}\tilde{\Delta}_{s})^{*}(D^{\prime}_{y}\tilde{\Delta}_{d})-(D^{\prime}_{x}\tilde{\Delta}_{s})^{*}(D^{\prime}_{x}\tilde{\Delta}_{d})+\text{c.c.}\right]\\
+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}(\nabla^{\prime}\times\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\prime})^{2},\end{gathered}

(41)

where
D^{\prime}_{i}=\partial^{\prime}_{i}+\tilde{A}_{i}^{\prime}
is a rescaled covariant derivative,

\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{1}}}{\gamma_{1}q}
.
This form of free energy allows one to have all GL coefficients to be
O(1)
that is convenient for numerical methods.

C.1
Coherence lengths calculation

Exact evaluation leads to a degree 6 polynomial equation (for amplitudes, phase difference, and three gauge field components) that determine all the lengths scales in the system, while all the normal modes are in general mixed
38
.
Here we provide rough approximations to the coherence lengths corresponding to amplitude variations and relative phase variations.
This is done by treating both cases separately with no mixing: Phase difference is fixed for calculations of coherence lengths for amplitudes and vice versa.
Second simplification we make: Translation invariance orthogonal to perturbation direction.
Both these simplifications in fact break for vortex perturbation.
However, this method allows us to get first coarse estimate for coherence lengths to search for type-1.5 superconductivity regime.

Let us linearize
F
with respect to variations in the order parameter amplitudes (
|\Delta_{s/d}|=|\Delta_{s/d}^{\text{GS}}|+\sigma_{s/d}
where
\sigma_{s/d}
is small).
Assuming amplitude variation in the direction
\boldsymbol{n}=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)
free energy density becomes

\begin{gathered}F_{\text{lin}}=F_{\text{GS}}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\sigma_{\alpha})\Gamma_{\alpha\beta}(\nabla\sigma_{\beta})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\alpha}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}\sigma_{\beta}\end{gathered}

(42)

where
\Gamma(\varphi)
is given in Eq. (
7
), the Hessian
\mathcal{H}
is

\mathcal{H}=\begin{pmatrix}8\beta_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}&4\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\theta^{\text{GS}})|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}||\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|\\
4\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\theta^{\text{GS}})|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}||\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|&8\beta_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(43)

with
\tilde{\beta}_{3}(\theta)=\beta_{3}+2\beta_{4}\cos 2\theta
and
\theta^{\text{GS}}=\pi/2
.

Coherence lengths for amplitudes
\xi_{i}
within the approximation are given by

\xi_{i}(\boldsymbol{n})=\frac{a}{\sqrt{\eta_{i}(\varphi)}},

(44)

where
\eta_{i}(\varphi)
are eigenvalues of
\Gamma^{-1}(\varphi)\mathcal{H}
.
Coherence lengths
\xi_{i}(\boldsymbol{n})
are associated with linear combinations of
\sigma_{s}
and
\sigma_{d}
that is called hybridization.
Moreover, presence of off-diagonal terms in
\Gamma(\varphi)
ensures that system has two coherence lengths for amplitudes even for ground state with pure
s
-wave or pure
d
-wave.

Let us now consider the relative phase mode and ignore any mixed gradient couplings for simplicity.
That is, we consider phase-difference-only fluctuations.
Let us consider the fluctuation of the relative phase about the ground state,

\theta=\theta^{\text{GS}}+\phi,

(45)

where the small fluctuation is
\phi
.
The linearized free energy with respect to
\phi
is

\begin{gathered}F_{\text{lin }\phi}=F_{\text{GS}}+\frac{1}{2}K_{\textup{rel}}(\varphi,\theta^{\text{GS}})(\nabla\phi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\partial^{2}F_{p}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\right|_{\theta=\theta^{\text{GS}}}\phi^{2},\end{gathered}

(46)

where
F_{p}
is potential part of the free energy Eq. (
3
),

K_{\textup{rel}}(\varphi,\theta)=\frac{2|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}-\gamma_{12}^{2}\cos^{2}2\varphi\cos^{2}\theta)}{\gamma_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}-2\gamma_{12}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}||\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|\cos 2\varphi\cos\theta+\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}}.

(47)

When substituting
\theta=\theta^{\text{GS}}
, one gets

K_{\textup{rel}}(\varphi,\theta^{\text{GS}})=\frac{2\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}}{\gamma_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}}

(48)

and

\left.\frac{\partial^{2}F_{p}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\right|_{\theta=\theta^{\text{GS}}}=2\beta_{3}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}.

(49)

Therefore coherence length for relative phase variations

\xi_{\theta}(\varphi)=a\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}{\beta_{3}(\gamma_{1}|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}+\gamma_{2}|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2})}}

(50)

is independent of the direction
\varphi
.
Note that it is defined in the region with two nucleated gap components (
s+id
phase in our case) as can be seen in Fig.
2
.

C.2
Numerical calculation details

After making rough estimates for length scales we can move to numerical solutions that allow to calculate length scales without these simplified estimates.
The model consists of a two-component complex order parameter
\Delta_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}
with
\alpha=1,2
and a vector gauge field
\vec{\tilde{A}}=(\tilde{A}_{1},\tilde{A}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We identify
\Delta_{s}=\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{d}=\Delta_{2}
.
Let the local coordinate be
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and define the local frame
\partial_{j}=\partial/\partial x_{j}
for
j=1,2
.
The GL energy density functional in index notation is

F=\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{jk}^{\alpha\beta}\overline{D_{j}\Delta_{\alpha}}D_{k}\Delta_{\beta}+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}|\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{\tilde{A}}|^{2}+F_{p}(\Delta_{s},\Delta_{d}),

(51)

where
\alpha,\beta,j,k=1,2
and the gauge covariant derivative is defined by the vector
\vec{D}=\vec{\nabla}+i\vec{\tilde{A}}
, and
\vec{\nabla}=(\partial_{1},\partial_{2})
is the gradient operator.
Note that we define

\overline{D_{j}\Delta_{\alpha}}=\partial_{j}\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}-i\tilde{A}_{j}\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}.

(52)

The anisotropy matrices are given by

\gamma_{11}=2\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}&-\gamma_{12}\\
-\gamma_{12}&\gamma_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma_{22}=2\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{12}\\
\gamma_{12}&\gamma_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(53)

The potential energy we are considering is defined by

\displaystyle F_{p}(\Delta_{s},\Delta_{d})=\alpha_{1}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}+\alpha_{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{2}+\beta_{1}|\Delta_{s}|^{4}+\beta_{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{4}

\displaystyle+\beta_{3}|\Delta_{s}|^{2}|\Delta_{d}|^{2}+\beta_{4}\left(\Delta_{s}^{2}\bar{\Delta}_{d}^{2}+\bar{\Delta}_{s}^{2}\Delta_{d}^{2}\right).

(54)

The associated static GL field equations are found to be

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{p}}{\partial\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}}-\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}D_{i}D_{j}\Delta_{\beta},

(55a)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\tilde{A}_{i}}=\,

\displaystyle J_{i}+\kappa^{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\tilde{A}_{j}-\kappa^{2}\partial_{j}\partial_{j}\tilde{A}_{i}.

(55b)

where the supercurrent is

J_{i}=\frac{i}{2}\left(\gamma_{ji}^{\alpha\beta}\Delta_{\beta}\overline{D_{j}\Delta_{\alpha}}-\gamma_{ij}^{\beta\alpha}\bar{\Delta}_{\beta}D_{j}\Delta_{\alpha}\right).

(56)

To obtain vortex solutions in our model, we must minimize the GL free energy Eq. (
51
).
This amounts to solving the corresponding field equations Eq. (
55
).
We do this using an accelerated gradient descent method with flow arresting criteria.
We reformulate the minimization as a second order dynamical problem and solve the second order system

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}^{2}\Delta_{\alpha}}{\textup{d}t^{2}}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}D_{i}D_{j}\Delta_{\beta}-\frac{\partial F_{p}}{\partial\bar{\Delta}_{\alpha}},

(57)

\displaystyle\frac{\textup{d}^{2}\tilde{A}_{i}}{\textup{d}t^{2}}=\,

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}\partial_{j}(\partial_{j}\tilde{A}_{i}-\partial_{i}\tilde{A}_{j})-J_{i},

(58)

where
t
is a fictitious time.
This system can then be reduced to a coupled first order system, which we solve using a fourth order Runge-Kutta method.

In order for our method to converge to a
N
-vortex solution, we need some appropriate initial configuration
\{\Delta_{s/d}(0),\vec{\tilde{A}}(0)\}=\{\Delta_{s/d}^{0},\vec{A}^{0}\}
.
For the superconducting order parameter, we use the ansatz

\begin{split}\Delta_{\alpha}^{0}=\Delta_{\alpha}^{\text{GS}}\sigma_{\alpha}(r)e^{iN_{\alpha}\theta_{\alpha}},\quad\vec{A}^{0}=\frac{Na(r)}{r}\left(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta\right).\end{split}

(59)

where
N_{\alpha}
is the vortex number in each component,
N
is the total vortex number and
|\Delta_{\alpha}^{\text{GS}}|
is the ground state value of the associated component.
The total magnetic flux through the plane is given by

\Phi={2\pi}N,\quad N=\frac{|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}N_{s}+|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}N_{d}}{|\Delta_{s}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}+|\Delta_{d}^{\text{GS}}|^{2}}.

(60)

The profile functions are monotonically increasing functions that satisfy the boundary conditions
\sigma_{\alpha}(0)=a(0)=0
and

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{\alpha}(r)=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}a(r)=1.

(61)

Note that in general boundary conditions are more subtle
33
.
However, we choose simulation box large enough so that there is no vortex interaction with boundaries
within numerical accuracy.
Hence the simplest boundary conditions are sufficient for our case.

For our initial configuration, we simply set
\sigma_{\alpha}(r)=a(r)=\tanh(r)
.

C.3
Skyrmions

When integer flux vortices split into spatially separated fractional vortices in each component
\Delta_{\alpha}
, such that their cores (zeroes of the components
\Delta_{\alpha}=0
) are never coincident, skyrmions form.
That can be seen by representing superconducting gap fields in terms of a pseudospin vector
3
.
For such structures, one may construct a gauge invariant field
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow S^{2}
, given by

\phi=\frac{1}{|\Delta_{s}|^{2}+|\Delta_{d}|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{s}^{*}\Delta_{d}+\Delta_{s}\Delta_{d}^{*}\\
i(\Delta_{s}^{*}\Delta_{d}-\Delta_{s}\Delta_{d}^{*})\\
|\Delta_{d}|^{2}-|\Delta_{s}|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(62)

We can describe the skyrmion field using the gauge invariant quantities
\Delta
,
\rho=\sqrt{|\Delta_{s}|^{2}+|\Delta_{d}|^{2}}
, and the supercurrent
J
, where we are regarding the supercurrent as a 1-form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We can express the magnetic field (as a 2-form on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
) in terms of these gauge invariant quantities as
44
;
12

B=\frac{1}{2}\phi^{*}\omega-\textup{d}\left(\frac{J}{\rho^{2}}\right),

(63)

where
\omega
is the usual area form on the target
S^{2}
and
d
is the exterior derivative.
Then, using Stoke’s Theorem, we observe that the skyrmion number is precisely the number of magnetic flux quanta in the field configuration, that is

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}B=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi^{*}\omega=2\pi\mathcal{Q}(\phi).

(64)

The skyrmion number
\mathcal{Q}
can be computed using the following integral representation, see a detailed discussion in
12

\mathcal{Q}(\phi)=\frac{i\epsilon_{ji}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\textup{d}^{2}x\,\frac{1}{|\Psi|^{4}}\left(|\Psi|^{2}\partial_{i}\Psi^{\dagger}\partial_{j}\Psi+\Psi^{\dagger}\partial_{i}\Psi\partial_{j}\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\right),

(65)

where
\Psi=(\Delta_{s},\Delta_{d})
.
