Comparative structure–activity relationships of green-synthesized silver, zinc oxide, and selenium nanoparticles mediated by Peganum harmala: physicochemical characteristics and multifunctional biological activities
Authors: Nouf Alharbi, Madyha H Mahmoud, Eman Merghani A Mohammed, Soha Taymour, Karim Abdelsalam, Fatemah Refaei, Ibrahim Shanyah, Mona M Khalifa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn environmentally responsible route to sustainable nanomaterial production is provided by green synthesis, enabling multifunctional bioactive systems intended for potential biomedical applications. Plant-mediated processes can reduce cost and environmental burden relative to conventional nanoparticle fabrication methods; phytochemical surface capping may also strengthen biological functionality in the resulting materials. In this study, Peganum harmala L. seed extract, a medicinal plant rich in flavonoids, phenolics, and terpenoids, was used as the plant source for biosynthesis.MethodsSilver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs), zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs), and selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs) were synthesized using Peganum harmala extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized by UV–visible spectroscopy, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and zeta potential analysis. Their biological activities were evaluated through a series of in vitro assays, including antioxidant capacity (total antioxidant capacity, reducing power, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)(ABTS), and nitric oxide scavenging (NO), carbohydrate-metabolizing enzyme (α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and aldose reductase),ResultsThe three nanoparticle systems exhibited distinct, pathway-specific biological activities consistent with their physicochemical profiles. ZnO-NPs (PDI = 0.184; zeta = +23.1 mV) demonstrated superior antioxidant and anti-diabetic enzyme inhibitory activities, with the lowest IC₅₀ values across DPPH, ABTS, α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and aldose reductase assays. Se-NPs (PDI = 0.091; zeta = +19.8 mV) exhibited the strongest anti-inflammatory (cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) and anti-arthritic activities, with IC₅₀ values approaching those of standard drugs. All three nanoparticles showed moderate, comparable cholinesterase inhibition. Ag-NPs consistently showed the weakest performance across most pathways, potentially related to their lower zeta potential (−14.4 mV) and reliance on steric rather than electrostatic stabilization.ConclusionsThe synthesized nanoparticles exhibited distinct bio functional profiles governed by elemental composition and physicochemical characteristics. ZnO-NPs were predominantly associated with antioxidant and anti-diabetic activities, whereas Se-NPs demonstrated superior anti-inflammatory and anti-arthritic properties. These findings highlight P. harmala-mediated nanoparticle synthesis as a sustainable and versatile platform for developing multifunctional nanomaterials with promising biomedical applications.Keywords: Peganum harmala, Green synthesis, Selenium nanoparticles, Antioxidant activity, Anti-diabetic activity, Anti-inflammatory activity, Physicochemical–biological associations

Introduction

Oxidative stress is widely recognized as a key pathophysiological factor in several chronic diseases, including diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions.1 The excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leads to cellular damage, lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and impairment of enzymatic functions involved in glucose metabolism.2 Oxidative stress is broadly defined as a disturbance between oxidant generation and antioxidant defense, leading to the accumulation of reactive molecules beyond the capacity of endogenous scavenging systems. Since its introduction, this concept has been widely examined because of its contribution to disease development and progression through multiple pathogenic mechanisms. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) are among the major oxidant mediators implicated in these processes.3 This oxidative burden contributes significantly to the progression of diabetic complications and metabolic dysfunction.

The breakdown of carbohydrates depends on α-amylase and α-glucosidase, while aldose reductase functions in the polyol pathway, contributing to diabetic complications. This establishes carbohydrate digestion and polyol-pathway enzymes as treatment targets. To control postprandial hyperglycemia and avoid long-term complications, their inhibition is consequently considered an effective management approach.4, 5 Inhibiting them also represents a therapeutic strategy that can manage postprandial hyperglycemia and help prevent long-term diabetic complications.4

Beyond metabolic disorders, oxidative stress and chronic inflammation are key drivers in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases, where they act synergistically to promote neuronal injury. In Alzheimer's disease, these mechanisms are especially evident and contribute to progressive neurodegeneration, where enzymes such as acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) play critical roles in the breakdown of acetylcholine, leading to cognitive decline.6 Therefore, inhibition of these enzymes has been recognized as a key therapeutic strategy for improving cholinergic signaling and slowing disease progression.7

Chronic inflammation centrally drives arthritic and inflammatory conditions.8 Within the inflammatory cascade, cyclooxygenases (COX-1 and COX-2) and lipoxygenases (5-LOX) act as major mediators by generating pro-inflammatory prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Within arthritic disorders, protein denaturation and proteolytic enzyme activity also sustain tissue damage.9 Suppressing these enzymes and inflammatory pathways can therefore limit degeneration of tissue while bringing inflammation under control.10

A combination of surface reactivity and distinct physicochemical properties enables metal nanoparticles to modulate oxidative stress, enzyme activity, and inflammatory responses; these capabilities make them promising components of multifunctional therapeutic systems.11 New opportunities for designing advanced bioactive systems with improved therapeutic efficiency have accompanied the growth of nanotechnologies.12 Metal nanoparticles synthesized through environmentally benign green approaches have distinctive physicochemical properties, among them a high surface area-to-volume ratio, tunable reactivity, and intensified nanoscale interaction with biological systems.13

Green synthesis using plant extracts has attracted significant attention as a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to conventional chemical methods.14 Plant-derived phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and other secondary metabolites act as natural reducing and stabilizing agents, facilitating controlled nanoparticle formation while simultaneously enhancing their biological activity through surface functionalization.15

Peganum harmala L. (P. harmala) is a medicinal plant widely used in traditional medicine and characterized by a rich phytochemical profile, including flavonoids, phenolics, and terpenoids with well-documented antioxidant properties.16These bioactive constituents make it a promising candidate for green nanoparticle synthesis, potentially imparting enhanced biological functionality to the synthesized nanomaterials.17

Despite the increasing number of studies on plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis, there remains a lack of systematic investigations comparing multiple metal nanoparticles synthesized using the same plant extract, particularly in relation to associations between their physicochemical properties and multifunctional biological effects. Addressing this gap is essential for the rational design of nanoparticle-based therapeutic systems.

Unlike previous studies that focused on individual, green-synthesized nanoparticles, the present work systematically compares Ag-NPs, ZnO-NPs, and Se-NPs synthesized under identical experimental conditions using Peganum harmala extract, enabling direct assessment of associations between physicochemical properties and biological activity across multiple pathways. This comparative framework explores how physicochemical differences in particle size distribution, surface charge, and phytochemical functionalization may relate to pathway-specific biological performance a question that has received limited systematic attention in the green nanoparticle literature.

Therefore, the present study aims to synthesize silver (Ag-NPs), zinc oxide (ZnO-NPs), and selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs) using P. harmala extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent; characterize their physicochemical properties; evaluate their antioxidant and metabolic enzyme-inhibitory (α-amylase, α-glucosidase, aldose reductase) activities using in vitro assays; and examine the associations between their physicochemical properties and biological activities.

Materials and methods

Materials

Analytical-grade chemicals were employed throughout the study. Sigma supplied silver nitrate (AgNO₃), zinc acetate, sodium selenite, and ascorbic acid; all remaining reagents came from standard suppliers and underwent no further purification.

Preparation of Peganum harmala extract

After collection and cleaning, Peganum harmala samples underwent drying for 72 h at 50 °C before being milled to powder of fine consistency. Distilled water was combined with this powder, and extraction proceeded for 24 h with uninterrupted stirring. Whatman No. 1 filter paper was then used to separate the extract, which was retained at 4 °C until further use.18

Synthesis of Ag- NPs, ZnO- NPs, and Se-based nanoparticles

The three nanoparticle systems were synthesized using distinct chemical routes according to their precursor chemistry and physicochemical requirements. Although P. harmala extract was incorporated as a bio-derived component, the synthesis conditions, auxiliary reagents, and reaction parameters were independently optimized for each nanoparticle type Table 1.

Silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs)

The method of Zeroual et al.19 was followed to prepare silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) in a polyol system, with ethylene glycol (EG) reducing silver nitrate (AgNO₃). Initially, 10 mL of EG was refluxed for 25 min at 160 °C. During continuous stirring, two formulations were introduced simultaneously and dropwise over 10 min: 5 mL of AgNO₃ in EG and 10 mL of 0.15 M polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) in EG containing 0.03 mM MnCl₂. Subsequently, P. harmala extract was introduced as a bio-derived additive that may contribute additional reducing and surface-associated phytochemical components during nanoparticle formation, the reaction mixture was kept at 160 °C under reflux with vigorous stirring. After cooling to room temperature, the product was recovered by centrifugation for 5 min at 3000 rpm. Three washes were conducted using ethanol and acetone; the material was then dried in an oven set to 70 °C.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs)

A sol–gel procedure reported by Al-Luhaibi and Sendi20 was adopted to prepare zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs). Into 400 mL of double-distilled water under continuous stirring, 12.6 g of zinc acetate dihydrate was introduced. After heating the solution to 50 °C, 600 mL of absolute ethanol was introduced slowly while agitation remained constant. Next, 6 mL of hydrogen peroxide (47%) was delivered dropwise; stirring of the reaction mixture then continued until clarity was achieved. Incubation for 24 h preceded drying at 80 °C for multiple hours, producing white ZnO nanoparticles. P. harmala extract was incorporated during synthesis as a bio-derived component that may contribute to nanoparticle stabilization through phytochemical interactions. Several double-distilled water washes removed residual byproducts from the recovered ZnO-NPs, which underwent final hot-air oven drying at 80 °C.

Selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs)

A modified Vahdati and Tohidi Moghadam21 procedure generated selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs), using P. harmala extract as a bio-derived additive during selenium nanoparticle synthesis. First, continuous agitation accompanied placement of 30 mg of sodium selenite (Na₂SeO₃·5H₂O) in 90 mL of Milli-Q water. Ascorbic acid solution (10 mL, 56.7 mM) was delivered dropwise into the sodium selenite solution during vigorous stirring. To enhance stability, Polysorbate (10 μL) was added intermittently as the reaction proceeded. A red colour signaled Se-NPs formation. Every solution used double-distilled water and was handled under sterile conditions. Centrifugation at 12,000 rpm collected Se-NPs from the suspension that formed; washing and drying at 50 °C followed.

Note on comparative synthesis conditions: The three nanoparticle systems described above were not synthesized under identical reaction conditions. Each nanoparticle type was prepared using a synthesis route appropriate to its precursor chemistry, with differences in precursor, reducing/stabilizing components, reaction temperature, and processing conditions. Accordingly, the comparative analysis presented in this study refers to the resulting physicochemical and biological profiles of the three green-synthesized nanoparticle systems, rather than to a controlled comparison of identical synthesis conditions.

Physicochemical characterization

Nanoparticles were characterized using complementary physicochemical techniques, including UV–visible spectroscopy, TEM, DLS, zeta potential analysis, and FTIR spectroscopy. Nanoparticles were comprehensively characterized using multiple analytical techniques. UV–visible spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-2450) was performed to confirm nanoparticle formation and determine optical properties. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F) was used to investigate morphology and particle size distribution. Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Microtrac FLEX 12.0.1.0) measurements were conducted to determine hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution in aqueous suspension. Zeta potential analysis was performed using the same instrument to assess surface charge and colloidal stability. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was performed to identify functional groups associated with nanoparticle formation and potential interactions between phytochemical constituents of P. harmala extract and nanoparticle surfaces.

In vitro biological activities

Each biological assay used triplicate measurements at every concentration of nanoparticles to confirm reproducibility. Suspensions of the nanoparticles received UV irradiation in a laminar flow hood for 30 min before biological testing. A humidified incubator-maintained cell cultures at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and > 95% relative humidity. To limit aggregation, sonication in an ultrasonic bath was applied to the nanoparticle dispersions for 10 min directly before every assay.

Antioxidant activity

Prieto et al.22 provided the procedure for determining total antioxidant capacity (TAC). TAC was reported in milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of weight. With ascorbic acid serving as the reference, iron reducing power (IRP) was calculated in μg/mL according to Oyaizu.23 Dose–response curves supplied the IC₅₀ values.

Scavenging activity

With ascorbic acid serving as a positive control, the 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging activity was assessed using the method proposed by Rahman et al.24to determine the median inhibitory concentration (IC50) of each tested extract and the inhibition percentage (Inhib. %) of the DPPH radical.

The 2,2′-azinobis-(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) experiment was performed using the method recommended by Arnao et al.25in order to determine the IC50 of each extract under investigation as well as the Inhib. % of the ABTS radical.

The nitric oxide (NO) radical scavenging activity was measured through the Griess Illosvory reagent reaction described by Chakraborthy.26 For every examined extract, both NO-radical Inhib. % and IC50 were established.

Plotting radical inhibition percentages against sample concentrations produced the curve from which each sample's median inhibitory concentration (IC50) was obtained.

Carbohydrate-metabolizing enzyme inhibitory activity

With acarbose as the reference drug, the assay quantified inhibition percentages for α-amylase and α-glucosidase enzymes.27, 28 Dose–response curves were generated by plotting inhibition percentages across multiple sample concentrations, allowing IC₅₀ values to be derived. Quercetin served as the reference standard for the separately assessed aldose reductase inhibitory activity and IC₅₀ values, following the previously reported method.29

Cholinesterase inhibitory activity

Cholinesterase inhibitory activity was evaluated using Ellman's method30 with donepezil as the reference inhibitor. The inhibitory effects against acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) were determined by calculating the inhibition percentage and IC₅₀ values. For the BChE assay, 5 μL of S-butyrylthiocholine iodide (BTCh, 0.5 mM), 5 μL of 5,5′-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB, 0.03 mM), and 5 μL of sample solutions at different concentrations were added to a 96-well plate and incubated for 10 min at 30 °C. The reaction was initiated by adding 5 μL of BChE enzyme (0.3 U/mL), and absorbance was measured at 412 nm. A control reaction containing all components except the test sample was included. The inhibition percentage was calculated relative to the control, and IC₅₀ values were obtained from concentration–response curves. 29for each sample was determined by plotting sample concentration against percent BChE inhibition.

COX and 5-LOX enzyme inhibitory activity

The in vitro anti-inflammatory activities were evaluated by suppressing two isoenzymes, cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) (ovine/human), as well as the 5-LOX enzyme (human recombinant). The Inhib. % of COX-1 and COX-2 was evaluated using the COX-1 and COX-2 kit31, 32 recommended using the 5-LOX kit to assess the inhibitory percentage of 5-Lipoxygenase (5-LOX). To determine the IC50, linear regression was used.

Protein-denaturation and proteinase-inhibitory activity

In the assay, diclofenac sodium, a widely used non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, was synthesized following the protocol outlined by Das to assess its ability to inhibit protein denaturation33 and proteinase enzyme activity.34 By constructing a dose-response curve with different concentrations of the samples against the inhibition percentage of the proteinase enzyme, the IC50 value for each sample was calculated.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3 technical replicates) to ensure reproducibility. Results were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). IC₅₀ values were calculated using linear regression analysis from concentration–inhibition curves. The findings are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SE). Statistical comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-hoc test. Statistical significance was defined by a p-value <0.05.

The optical properties of Ag-NPs, Se-NPs, and ZnO-NPs were evaluated by UV–Vis spectroscopy between 200 and 800 nm (Fig. 1). In every spectrum, the UV edge declined sharply before transitioning into a broad, gentle slope across the visible region. None displayed an isolated narrow absorption maximum, indicating that a classical, well-defined surface plasmon resonance band was absent from all three systems. A small yet reproducible shoulder appeared near 267–270 nm for both Se-NPs and ZnO-NPs; overlapping electronic transitions involving the metal(loid) core and surface-adsorbed phytochemical species from the P. harmala extract may account for it. Thus, nanoparticle–phytochemical interactions govern the composition-dependent electronic differences observed in the spectra. Strong UV band-edge absorption by ZnO-NPs is characteristic of semiconductor electronic transitions. The excitation of electrons across the direct wide band gap, moving charge carriers between the valence band and conduction band, is primarily responsible. Their semiconducting nature and optoelectronic properties arise from that inherent behavior.35 In selenium nanoparticles (Se-NPs), broad absorption features were linked to electronic transitions associated with selenium species and surface-bound phytochemicals. The absorption maximum observed at 270 nm may reflect size-dependent quantum confinement effects and electronic transitions. Because absorption-peak shifts depend on particle size, reducing nanoparticle dimensions can alter effective band gap energy.36 Near ∼269 nm, Ag-NPs produced weak absorption, suggesting suppression of plasmonic behavior by extensive biomolecular capping and diminished interparticle coupling. The ∼269 nm band also points strongly to aromatic amino acids, such as tryptophan and tyrosine in proteins, or to particular functional groups contributed by the capping agent. Damping of collective electron oscillations is caused by this dense layer.37 Taken together, these spectral patterns indicate that plant-derived capping effects and surface chemistry govern the optical properties; classical plasmonic processes alone do not.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was performed to determine the hydrodynamic diameter and size distribution of the synthesized nanoparticles (Fig. 2, Table 2). DLS-derived particle sizes are generally larger than actual core dimensions because hydrodynamic diameter measurements include hydration layers and surface-associated phytochemical molecules surrounding the nanoparticle core rather than only the physical particle size.38 In addition, the composition of plant metabolites adsorbed on nanoparticle surfaces and the degree of surface homogeneity may influence measured hydrodynamic behavior and introduce variability relative to electron microscopy measurements.39 The synthesized Ag-NPs exhibited a number-mean diameter (MN) of 87.2 nm, with a dominant size distribution peak at 65.8 nm and a PDI value of 0.171. ZnO-NPs showed an MN of 107.9 nm with a peak diameter of 85.8 nm and a PDI of 0.184, whereas Se-NPs exhibited an MN of 155.9 nm with the lowest PDI value (0.091). PDI values below approximately 0.3 are generally considered indicative of relatively narrow particle size distributions.40 However, the volume-mean diameters were substantially higher than the number-based measurements for all nanoparticle types (Ag-NPs: 602.0 nm; ZnO-NPs: 263.0 nm; Se-NPs: 2015 nm). This difference reflects the high sensitivity of volume-based DLS calculations to a small population of larger particles or aggregates, since larger structures contribute disproportionately to scattering intensity and calculated volume distributions. In particular, the high volume-mean diameter observed for Se-NPs is interpreted because of aggregation behavior in aqueous dispersion rather than indicating that the primary nanoparticles are micron-sized. Therefore, DLS values were considered indicators of hydrodynamic behavior and colloidal stability, whereas TEM analysis was used to assess primary nanoparticle morphology and dimensions.

Zeta potential analysis was performed to evaluate colloidal stability through surface charge measurements. Electrostatic interactions generated between charged nanoparticle surfaces create repulsive forces that contribute to stabilization and reduce aggregation tendency.41 Ag-NPs exhibited a negative zeta potential value of −14.4 mV, whereas Se-NPs and ZnO-NPs displayed positive surface charges of +19.8 mV and + 23.1 mV, respectively (Table 2). Zeta potential magnitudes near or above approximately ±20 mV is commonly considered sufficient to provide electrostatic stabilization in colloidal systems.42 Accordingly, ZnO-NPs exhibited comparatively stronger electrostatic stability, whereas Se-NPs demonstrated moderate stabilization. The relatively lower zeta potential value of Ag-NPs suggests that steric effects mediated by surface-bound phytochemicals from P. harmala extract may contribute substantially to maintaining nanoparticle dispersion.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was further employed to directly evaluate nanoparticle morphology, particle dimensions, and spatial distribution, complementing the ensemble hydrodynamic information obtained from DLS measurements.43 As shown in Fig. 3, TEM analysis revealed distinct morphological characteristics among the three nanoparticle systems. Ag-NPs exhibited small, well-dispersed quasi-spherical particles, whereas Se-NPs displayed larger spherical particles with relatively smooth surfaces and rounded morphology. In contrast, ZnO-NPs showed a faceted polyhedral morphology with clearly defined edges, a feature commonly observed for zinc oxide nanostructures. The TEM-derived particle-size distribution analysis (Fig. 4) further confirmed the variation in nanoparticle dimensions among the three systems, with size distributions obtained from individual particle measurements. These morphological and size differences likely reflect variations in nucleation and growth processes during the green synthesis of the different nanoparticle systems and are consistent with previously reported observations for biologically synthesized metal and metalloid nanoparticles of similar composition.44

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (Fig. 5) was performed to identify surface-associated functional groups involved in nanoparticle stabilization. All nanoparticle systems exhibited a broad band at 3436–3450 cm−1 corresponding to O—H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl-containing phytochemicals, including phenolic and flavonoid compounds derived from P. harmala extract. Bands around 2920–2921 cm−1 observed in Ag-NPs and ZnO-NPs were assigned to aliphatic C—H stretching vibrations, whereas signals at 1633–1638 cm−1 were attributed to carbonyl/amide-related vibrations and aromatic C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="20.666667pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 20.666667 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.019444,-0.019444)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 480 0 480 0 0 40 0 40 -480 0 -480 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> C stretching of surface-associated organic molecules. These spectral features indicate the retention of phytochemical residues on nanoparticle surfaces, supporting their potential role in stabilization during green synthesis. Ag-NPs additionally showed fingerprint-region bands at 1446, 876, and 634 cm−1, while the peak near 2365 cm−1 was attributed to atmospheric CO₂ interference. ZnO-NPs displayed low-frequency bands at 615 and 430 cm−1, consistent with Zn—O lattice vibrations. For Se-NPs, the band near 611 cm−1 was observed; however, its assignment cannot be considered definitive without complementary elemental analysis. The broad feature near 1455 cm−1 exceeding 100% transmittance was considered a probable baseline/scattering artifact. Since Ag-NPs and Se-NPs are elemental/metalloid systems rather than oxide nanoparticles, FTIR primarily provides information regarding phytochemical surface association rather than direct confirmation of the inorganic core.

From a therapeutic-application perspective, the nanoscale dimensions, colloidal stability, and surface functionalization of all three nanoparticle systems are factors expected to influence their bioavailability, dispersion behavior, and interaction with biological targets, and are revisited below in relation to their comparative biological activity. These findings collectively highlight that zeta potential and PDI provide complementary rather than redundant information on colloidal behavior, and that both parameters should be considered together when evaluating the stability and biological potential of green-synthesized nanoparticle systems.

Overall, the characterization results confirm that the green synthesis approach using P. harmala seed extract yielded stable, well-defined nanoparticles with controlled. Physicochemical properties, supporting their suitability for subsequent biological activity evaluation.

Among the tested nanoparticles, ZnO-NPs exhibited the most pronounced antioxidant activity across all assays. Specifically, ZnO-NPs demonstrated significantly higher TAC (76.72 ± 0.44 mg gallic acid equivalents/g) and IRP (67.01 ± 0.44 μg/mL) compared to Se-NPs (56.00 ± 0.32 mg GAE/g) and Ag-NPs (43.75 ± 0.25 mg GAE/g). In agreement with these findings, ZnO-NPs showed superior radical scavenging activity, achieving inhibition percentages of 65.57 ± 0.38% (DPPH), 69.36 ± 0.38% (ABTS), and 65.61 ± 0.38% (nitric oxide) at 50 μg/mL.

Significantly lower IC₅₀ values further supported the enhanced activity of ZnO-NPs: 5.61 ± 0.06 μg/mL for DPPH, 4.70 ± 0.16 μg/mL for ABTS, and 6.38 ± 0.02 μg/mL for nitric oxide. These results indicate greater efficiency in radical neutralization. Se-NPs, by comparison, showed moderate antioxidant activity, whereas Ag-NPs performed less strongly across every tested parameter. The superior antioxidant potential of ZnO-NPs may arise from greater electron transfer capacity together with effective surface functionalization by phytochemicals originating from Peganum harmala L extract. Improved electron transfer capacity and phytochemical surface functionalization from Peganum harmala extract may likewise explain this enhanced antioxidant activity. Nanoparticle formation therefore appears to increase radical scavenging potential, consistent with earlier reports.45 Previous studies similarly describe superior antioxidant performance by ZnO-NPs and connect it to increased surface reactivity, which promotes electron transfer and removal of reactive oxygen species (ROS).46 By participating in several antioxidant pathways, the catalytic character of ZnO-NPs strengthens their effectiveness.47 Further antioxidant mechanisms probably result from interactions between ZnO-NPs and surface-bound phytochemicals in Peganum harmala L extract, particularly phenolic and flavonoid compounds.48

The inhibitory effects of the synthesized nanoparticles on key carbohydrate-metabolizing enzymes are presented in Table 4. Several physicochemical features may explain the greater inhibitory performance of ZnO-NPs. Their smaller particle size and larger surface area promote contact with enzyme active sites and thereby facilitate inhibition.49 Surface charge may also influence, under physiological conditions, how ZnO-NPs interact with enzymes and potentially alter binding affinity.50 Interactions with functional groups, including thiols, can enable ZnO-NPs to induce enzyme conformational changes that reduce catalytic activity. Enzyme inhibition may also reflect oxidative modification caused by their capacity to generate reactive oxygen species.51 Physiologically, delayed carbohydrate digestion and glucose absorption result when α-amylase and α-glucosidase are inhibited, lowering postprandial hyperglycemia. Inhibiting aldose reductase can simultaneously reduce the osmotic stress and oxidative damage associated with the polyol pathway, supporting their therapeutic relevance for diabetes management. The importance of aldose reductase inhibition follows from the oxidative stress and osmotic imbalance produced during diabetes by the polyol pathway. This intervention may help limit diabetic complications.52

These findings are consistent with previous reports emphasizing the enhanced enzyme inhibitory activity of ZnO-based nanostructures, driven by their nanoscale properties and surface reactivity. Statistical analysis confirmed that ZnO-NPs exhibited significantly higher enzyme inhibitory activity compared to other nanoparticles (p < 0.05).

Cholinesterase inhibitory activity

The neuroprotective potential of the green-synthesized nanoparticles was evaluated through their inhibitory effects on key cholinesterase enzymes, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), as presented in Table 5 and Fig. 8. Cholinesterase enzymes contribute to the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease through their role in the hydrolysis of acetylcholine (ACh), a neurotransmitter essential for cognitive function. In particular, AChE has been reported to accelerate amyloid-β (Aβ) aggregation through interactions with Aβ fibrils, thereby promoting neurodegeneration and disease progression.53, 54 Accordingly, inhibition of AChE and BChE represents a well-established therapeutic strategy for the management of Alzheimer's disease.

Donepezil, the standard drug, produced significantly stronger inhibition (IC₅₀ = 4.74 ± 0.08 μg/mL for AChE), confirming only moderate relative potency for the synthesized nanoparticles. Overall, these findings suggest that, unlike antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities where clear nanoparticle-dependent variations were observed, cholinesterase inhibition was less sensitive to nanoparticle composition, which may plausibly reflect a different mechanism of biological interaction; this proposed mechanism was not directly tested in the present study. Consequently, the combined moderate cholinesterase-inhibitory and antioxidant activities of these nanomaterials may be of potential relevance to neuroprotection, a possibility that would require confirmation in cell-based or in vivo models. This dual mechanism may enhance the translational potential of these nanoparticles as supportive agents in neurodegenerative disease management.

Protein-denaturation and proteinase-inhibitory activity

The anti-arthritic potential of the synthesized nanoparticles was assessed in vitro using protein denaturation and proteinase inhibition assays, as presented in Table 5 and Fig. 8. Protein denaturation is a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of inflammatory and arthritic disorders, as structural alteration of proteins can lead to autoantigen formation and subsequent immune activation.57 In parallel, proteinases contribute significantly to cartilage degradation and tissue damage through the breakdown of extracellular matrix components. Therefore, inhibition of these processes represents a critical therapeutic strategy for arthritis management.58

Our results revealed a clear nanoparticle-dependent variation in anti-arthritic activity. Among all tested samples, Se-NPs exhibited significantly superior activity compared to Ag-NPs and ZnO-NPs. Specifically, Se-NPs demonstrated a high inhibition of protein denaturation (72.45 ± 0.57%) at 50 μg/mL, approaching the effect of the standard drug diclofenac sodium (83.92 ± 0.16%). In contrast, Ag-NPs and ZnO-NPs showed considerably lower inhibition values (31.92 ± 0.25% and 32.23 ± 0.25%, respectively).

A similar trend was observed in the proteinase inhibition assay, where the synthesized nanoparticles showed comparable inhibitory activity patterns. Se-NPs exhibited a markedly lower IC₅₀ value (8.67 ± 0.14 μg/mL), indicating a stronger inhibitory effect compared to Ag-NPs (20.94 ± 0.35 μg/mL) and ZnO-NPs (20.71 ± 0.35 μg/mL). Although slightly less potent than diclofenac sodium (IC₅₀ = 7.44 ± 0.06 μg/mL), Se-NPs still demonstrated substantial anti-arthritic potential.

Se-NPs may perform better because they more effectively regulate oxidative stress and suppress proteolytic enzymes responsible for inflammatory tissue degradation.59 By entering antioxidant enzymes, selenium supports redox homeostasis and may stabilize proteins while protecting them against denaturation.60 Phytochemical surface functionalization derived from P. harmala extract could also strengthen nanoparticle–protein interactions and thereby increase inhibitory efficiency.61

A strong structure–activity relationship is therefore apparent: selenium-based nanoparticles achieved better anti-arthritic activity than the other metal nanoparticles. This performance supports their candidacy for nanotherapeutic strategies directed at inflammatory and arthritic diseases.

Both COX isoforms—COX-1 and COX-2—generate prostaglandins from arachidonic acid.62 Although they share this function, differences between COX-1 and COX-2 extend to physiological role, chemical composition, and cellular location.63

The anti-inflammatory potential of the synthesized nanoparticles was assessed through their inhibitory activity against key inflammatory enzymes, including cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), and 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX), as shown in Table 6 and Fig. 9. These enzymes play central roles in the biosynthesis of pro-inflammatory mediators, including prostaglandins and leukotrienes, which are directly implicated in the progression of inflammatory disorders.64 Therefore, their inhibition is considered a primary therapeutic target in inflammation control.

Multiple factors may underlie the superior anti-inflammatory performance of Se-NPs. Through incorporation into selenoproteins, selenium helps regulate oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling; these proteins participate in redox balance and immune modulation.65 The resulting effect includes lower production of pro-inflammatory mediators. Greater interaction between Se-NPs and enzyme active sites is also promoted by their nanoscale dimensions and high surface reactivity, improving inhibitory efficiency.66 Surface-bound phytochemicals derived from P. harmala extract may act synergistically with the selenium core, strengthening antioxidant as well as anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

Importantly, the anti-inflammatory activity of Se-NPs is consistent with their protein-denaturation and proteinase-inhibitory performance, which may plausibly reflect a shared underlying mechanism related to oxidative stress modulation and enzyme inhibition; this proposed mechanism was not directly tested in the present study. This coherence across biological assays is consistent with an association between physicochemical properties and biological activity, where selenium-based nanoparticles demonstrate enhanced in-vitro inhibitory activity in inflammation-associated assays.

Overall, these findings highlight Se-NPs as highly promising nanomaterials for targeting inflammatory pathways, with potential applications in the development of novel anti-inflammatory and anti-arthritic therapeutics.

Associations between physicochemical characteristics and biological activities

The differential biological activities observed among Ag-NPs, ZnO-NPs, and Se-NPs may be associated with variations in their physicochemical characteristics, including particle size distribution, surface properties, and possible phytochemical functionalization, rather than elemental composition alone. However, the present study does not establish quantitative structure–activity relationships, and these observations should be interpreted as associations rather than direct causal relationships.

ZnO-NPs exhibited higher antioxidants and several enzyme-inhibitory activities under the tested experimental conditions. This enhanced activity may be related to their physicochemical characteristics, including their particle size distribution and relatively higher zeta potential (+23.1 mV), which may influence dispersion behavior and interaction with assay components. However, the specific contribution of these properties to biological activity requires further investigation using correlation analysis and mechanistic studies.

Se-NPs demonstrated stronger anti-inflammatory and anti-arthritic activities despite exhibiting moderate antioxidant activity. This observation suggests that factors beyond general antioxidant capacity may contribute to the measured responses. The relatively narrow particle size distribution of Se-NPs (PDI = 0.091) and their distinct surface characteristics may influence nanoparticle–biomolecule interactions; however, the involvement of specific inflammatory signaling pathways was not directly evaluated in the present study and should therefore be considered a proposed mechanism.

The relationship between nanoparticle dispersion characteristics and biological activity is likely influenced by multiple interacting factors. PDI and zeta potential provide complementary information regarding nanoparticle stability and should not be interpreted independently. Se-NPs exhibited the lowest PDI value (0.091), indicating a relatively uniform particle population, whereas ZnO-NPs and Ag-NPs showed slightly broader distributions (PDI = 0.184 and 0.171, respectively). Although ZnO-NPs displayed the highest zeta potential (+23.1 mV), Se-NPs maintained high uniformity despite a lower zeta potential (+19.8 mV), suggesting that stabilization in plant-mediated nanoparticle systems may involve both electrostatic interactions and steric effects associated with adsorbed phytochemical components.

The possible contribution of P. harmala-derived phytochemicals to nanoparticle surface modification and biological activity should also be considered. However, because extract-only, synthesis-reagent, and nanoparticle blank controls were not included, the individual contribution of surface-bound phytochemicals versus the nanoparticle core cannot be fully distinguished. Therefore, the observed biological differences represent the overall activity of the synthesized nanoparticle systems under the conditions investigated.

Limitations

Although FTIR analysis provided evidence of surface-associated functional groups, further complementary techniques, including XRD and EDX/XPS analyses, would provide additional confirmation of nanoparticle crystallinity, elemental composition, and surface chemical characteristics. However, these analyses could not be performed in the present study due to the limited availability of nanoparticle material after completion of biological evaluations.

Furthermore, plant-mediated nanoparticle synthesis involves multiple contributing factors, including nanoparticle cores, surface-associated phytochemicals, and potential residual synthesis components, which may collectively influence the observed biological responses. Although nanoparticles were washed prior to biological assessment, the individual contribution of P. harmala-derived compounds, synthesis-associated reagents, and nanoparticle surfaces cannot be completely distinguished because extract-only, reagent-only, and nanoparticle blank controls were not included. Therefore, the biological activities reported herein should be interpreted as the overall in-vitro activity of the synthesized nanoparticle systems under the tested conditions rather than as effects exclusively attributed to the inorganic nanoparticle cores.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Peganum harmala seed extract can be used as a bio-derived component to synthesize Ag-NPs, ZnO-NPs, and Se-NPs with distinct physicochemical characteristics and biological activity profiles. ZnO-NPs showed the strongest antioxidant and metabolic enzyme-inhibitory activity, while Se-NPs showed the strongest anti-inflammatory and protein-denaturation/proteinase-inhibitory activity; all three showed moderate cholinesterase-inhibitory activity of potential biological relevance requiring further validation. These differences are associated with variations in particle size, surface charge, and phytochemical functionalization, though quantitative structure–activity relationships were not established here. Further work incorporating additional structural characterization, appropriate control groups, toxicity evaluation, mechanistic studies, and in-vivo validation is needed to confirm the therapeutic potential of these nanoparticle systems.
