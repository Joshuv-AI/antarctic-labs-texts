Benefits and complications: A systematic review and meta-analysis of serial casting in early onset scoliosis
Authors: Suhani Sharma, Arun Ramnarine, Karim Fouad, Ally Cheung, Vedika Khurana, Shreya Sankar, Michael O’Connor, George-Paul O’Byrne, Oliver Boughton, Patrick Kiely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEarly-onset scoliosis (EOS) presents a unique therapeutic challenge, requiring interventions that achieve deformity correction while preserving spinal and thoracic growth. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesise current evidence on serial casting outcomes in EOS, with a primary focus on treatment-related complications and secondary evaluation of radiographic, pulmonary, and quality-of-life outcomes.MethodsComprehensive searches were performed in MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library and CINAHL without date restrictions to identify eligible studies. The primary outcome was treatment-related complications; secondary outcomes included Cobb angle correction, thoracic height progression, pulmonary function, and patient-reported quality of life. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Meta-analyses were conducted using random-effects models, and narrative synthesis was applied where quantitative pooling was not feasible.ResultsFrom 578 records, 23 studies met inclusion criteria. Sixteen studies were rated as low risk of bias, six moderate, and one high. The pooled complication rate from the single-arm meta-analysis was 17% (95% CI 9–24%), with moderate heterogeneity. Dermatological complications predominated (skin irritation, ulceration), while gastrointestinal and transient respiratory symptoms were infrequent. Comparative studies demonstrated lower complication rates with casting compared to growth rods (1/27 vs 12/27), with outcomes comparable or superior to bracing. Mean annual thoracic growth ranged from 0.8 to 3.8 cm. Cobb angle correction was generally greater in non-congenital compared to congenital scoliosis. Pulmonary function may transiently decline during casting but improves following cast removal, while long-term outcomes remained acceptable when early surgery was avoided. Quality-of-life analysis demonstrated inverse associations between residual curvature and domains including daily living, pain, and emotional wellbeing.ConclusionSerial casting remains an effective, low-risk, and growth-preserving intervention for managing early-onset scoliosis. The predominance of minor, reversible complications underscores its safety, while radiographic and functional outcomes affirm its therapeutic value. Future research should prioritise prospective evaluation of pulmonary and psychosocial outcomes to optimise patient selection and refine treatment protocols.Keywords: Early-onset scoliosis, Serial casting, Treatment complications, Thoracic insufficiency syndrome, Growth-friendly surgery

1. Introduction

Early onset scoliosis (EOS) is a heterogeneous group of spinal deformities manifesting before 10 years of age, characterised by progressive three-dimensional curvature of the spine, and associated thoracic or torso anatomical structures. 1 The distortion of thoracic architecture during critical periods of lung growth alters chest wall morphology, mechanical function, reduces pulmonary compliance, and predisposes to thoracic insufficiency syndrome, associated with increased morbidity and mortality. 2 The delicate balance between correcting curvature and preserving growth and respiratory capacity underpins all management strategies in this population. 3

Management of EOS is uniquely challenging, as interventions strive to correct or control deformity while preserving potential for spinal and thoracic growth. 4 Surgical approaches such as traditional growing rods, magnetically controlled rods, and vertical expandable prosthetic titanium ribs may be somewhat effective in deformity control, but are accompanied by high complication and reoperation rates.5,6 Consequently, non-surgical methods remain a central component of therapeutic strategy, particularly in younger children where surgery may ultimately compromise growth potential. Among these, serial elongation–derotation–flexion (EDF) casting, popularised by Mehta, has emerged as one of the most effective modalities to harness growth for curve correction. 7

Serial casting is typically applied under general anaesthesia on a Risser, Mehta or spica table, where corrective forces guide spinal alignment while allowing continued growth.8,9 Marrache et al. describe the difference in techniques when using a Mehta, Risser and spica table. They found no significant differences between outcomes using the different tables in their retrospective multicenter study. 9 Evidence indicates that early initiation of casting, particularly before 18 months of age, yields superior outcomes, with a greater likelihood of curve resolution and reduced need for later surgery.10,11 Radiographic improvements are well documented: Cobb angle reductions exceeding 20° are commonly reported, with some patients achieving long-term correction to ≤ 15°.12,13 Beyond deformity control, serial casting has demonstrated potential benefits for thoracic growth and space available for lung, metrics that directly influence pulmonary development.14,15

Despite its therapeutic advantages, casting is not without risk. Adverse events range from relatively minor but distressing complications, such as skin irritation, pressure sores, and cast-related discomfort, to more clinically significant respiratory issues including impaired ventilation, aspiration, or reduced pulmonary function.8,16–18 Cast modifications or premature removal are sometimes required, reflecting the real-world burden of complications. 16 Rare but serious concerns have also been raised regarding thoracic “compartment syndrome,” where restrictive casts may compromise chest wall motion or cardiopulmonary function. 19 However, systematic evaluation of these complications remains limited, and reports are dispersed across small case series and observational studies.

To date, no comprehensive review has synthesised the spectrum of complications associated with serial casting in EOS. Existing literature has primarily focused on deformity correction and long-term surgical avoidance, with safety outcomes often relegated to secondary analyses. Yet for clinicians and families, understanding the risks, ranging from skin lesions to respiratory compromise, is essential for informed decision-making.

Moreover, systematic appraisal of complications alongside radiographic, growth, pulmonary, and quality-of-life outcomes may refine patient selection and highlight avenues for innovation in cast design and application.

Accordingly, the present systematic review aims to collate and critically evaluate the available evidence on serial casting in EOS, with a primary focus on complications. By integrating data on cast-related adverse events with outcomes including curve correction, thoracic growth, pulmonary function, adherence, and quality of life, this review seeks to provide a comprehensive appraisal of the benefits and risks of this growth-modulating intervention.

2. Methods

2.1. Selection process and eligibility criteria

A systematic review and meta-analysis were performed to assess serial casting as a treatment modality for EOS, as per our published protocol. 20 The inclusion and exclusion criteria for the study were predetermined and are as outlined in Table 1. Three reviewers blindly reviewed (SS, AC, and VK) paper titles and abstracts from the results of the search strategy. After initial screening, full text screening was performed for study inclusion. Any conflicts when reviewing studies for inclusion were resolved by the lead author SS.

2.2. Information sources and search strategy

A custom search strategy was developed for PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase and Cochrane Library in line with the research question and included keywords, key phrases and MESH terms. The complete search strategy can be found in Supplemental table 1.

2.3. Quality assessment and risk of bias

The Joanna Briggs Institute criteria 21 was used to assess the quality of the study design and risk of bias in the studies that met the eligibility criteria. Four reviewers (AC, VK, AR and KF) completed the risk of bias assessment. If there was disagreement, the lead author SS resolved any conflicts.

2.4. Data collection

An online data collection template was created in Microsoft Excel for data extraction of the studies that met the eligibility criteria. Full details of the data items collected can be found in the published protocol. 20

2.5. Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study are complications of treatment associated serial casting. Each complication was documented according to timepoint of occurrence, where reported, such as during casting (intra-operative or application phase) and during follow-up (subsequent cast changes or review period).

The secondary outcomes were number of patients cured, Cobb angle improvements, thoracic height changes, pulmonary function changes, QoL improvements and where applicable, comparison to surgical techniques.

2.6. Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis was used to combine the results from the studies that met the eligibility criteria, as per our published protocol. 20 Analysis was completed using R (version 4.5.1) using the package “meta”. Full details of the stastical analysis can be found in the published protocol. 20

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

A total of 578 records were identified across five electronic databases, including Embase (n = 348), PubMed (n = 211), Cochrane Library (n = 17), CINAHL (n = 1), and citation searching (n = 1) (Figure 1). After 147 duplicates were removed, 431 studies underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 84 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 61 studies were excluded due to reasons such as wrong outcomes (n = 2), wrong study design (n = 3), full text not available (n = 43), wrong patient population (n = 3), no outcome data on complications (n = 8), and clinical trial data unavailable online (n = 2). Ultimately, 23 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis.

PRISMA literature search flowchart.

3.2. Study characteristics

A total of 23 studies were included in this review, encompassing both retrospective and prospective case series. All studies focused on paediatric patients with EOS treated using serial casting techniques, with some comparing outcomes across different aetiologies such as idiopathic, congenital, neuromuscular, and syndromic scoliosis. The included studies were conducted across diverse geographic and clinical settings, including India, China, Germany, Switzerland, and the United States.

Sample sizes varied considerably, ranging from 16 to 243 patients, with a mean age at treatment initiation between 2 and 5 years. Most studies utilized plaster of Paris or fiberglass casts, applied under general anaesthesia using traction and rotation techniques, with casting durations typically spanning 12 to 24 months (with an average casting duration of 1 year and 4 months) and an average of 4–6 casts per patient. Follow-up durations ranged from 1 to 5 years, assessing outcomes such as Cobb angle correction, thoracic growth, and pulmonary function.

Detailed study characteristics can be found in Supplemental table 2.

3.3. Risk of bias in studies

Overall, using the corresponding JBI checklists for case series (Figure 2.) and cohort studies (Figure 3.), fifteen studies were rated low risk of bias and six as moderate. One included study was a case report which was appraised by applying only relevant checklist questions according to the design. The findings from this case report were not included in the results for patient complications as population-based comparisons were performed in this section. Lastly, a systematic review and a conference abstract without full text were excluded from the risk of bias assessment but included in full text screening for context as JBI tools were used only for primary research study designs. The most common issues were inadequate follow-up and lack of strategies to address confounding factors.

3.4. Complications

Seven of the nine case series studies reported serial casting complications.11,16,22–26 In these studies, 57.4% of the patients had idiopathic scoliosis (IS), 23.2% had syndromic scoliosis (SS), 3.9% had neuromuscular disease (NMS), and 1 patient had congenital deformity (CS), while the remaining 3.2% were unspecified and had either SS, NMS or CS. However, only six of these studies reported their findings, based on patient number and were included in the meta-analysis (Figure 4) (Table 2).11,16,22–25

From the seven studies, dermatological complications were either equal or greater in number compared to other complication types (Figure 5).11,16–19,22–26 Other reported complications included: repeated vomiting, increased lumbar lordosis, soiled cast, patient discomfort, humerus fracture, subclavian vein thrombosis, cardiac arrest, asthma attack and GI issues.11,16,22–26 One of the complications Johnson et al. 23 reported was cast modification due to patient falling off bike. This complication was omitted from the meta-analysis (Figure 4) as this was deemed to be extraneous to the intervention. The risk was determined by the complication rate (number of complications as a ratio of size of patient cohort) reported in the studies. Additionally, there was moderate heterogeneity between studies in relation to the published complication rates.11,16,22–25

Proportion of skin complications in case series studies.

Seven of the eight cohort studies reported complications for serial casting.9,27–32 In these studies, 70.2% had IS, 12.8% NMS, 9.3% had CS, and 7.7% had SS. Cao et al. 27 and Dhawale et al. 28 both compared congenital (CS) with non-congenital scoliosis (non-CS). Both studies reported a low complication rate for non-CS, 2/15 patients compared to 3/12 respectively. However, there was a significantly higher rate of complication for CS in the Cao et al. 27 study (5/8 patients vs 1/15). Most of these, were dermatological in nature and were due to the development of a frictional ulcer, yet elevated airway pressure and vomiting also contributed to the increased rate. 27 Johnston et al. 29 compared serial casting with growth rods in their study. The complication rate was much lower for casting (1/27 vs 12/27) with the only casting complication being dermatological in nature.

Li et al. 30 analysed casting compared to bracing as part of their cohort study. The casting group had a complication rate of 4/9, with three of the complications relating to skin breakdown and one because of soiling of the cast. The bracing group reported no complications.

Marrache et al. 9 compared the outcomes when using paediatric hip spica tables or Risser/Mehta tables for casting, they provided a combined complication rate of 7/76 for all the patients in the study. Their findings reported no significant difference in outcome between Risser, Mehta and Spica tables.

In their cohort study, Montgomery et al. 31 analysed Jackson table (MJ) vs Risser frame (RF) usage for casting. The complication rate from MJ usage was higher than the Risser frame (3/14 RF, 4/11 MJ) and were predominantly due to skin related issues.

Welborn et al. 32 reported rates based on final Cobb angle (Cobb < 10 °– ‘cured’ and Cobb ≥ 10° - ‘palliated’) after casting. The complication rate was low amongst the two groups; the only reported complication was respiratory related which occurred in the palliated group, this may well relate to the deformity, and thoracic asymmetry itself, rather than the casting. 30

3.5. Timing of complications

Thoracic/respiratory events were reported across several studies, these included post-casting clinical events such as reactive airway disease requiring overnight observation and a pulmonary complication leading to discontinuation, 33 respiratory distress/chest infection prompting conversion to bracing, 28 and CO2-retention–related intolerance. 32 Isolated serious events were also described in a mixed-etiology series (cardiac arrest on induction and a death attributed to acute asthma). 26 In these studies, 80.1% of patients had IS and 19.9% had NIS.

When timing was specified in the extraction sheet, 26% of studies reported complications during casting, while 43.4% were reported during follow-up; the remainder did not specify timing clearly. Skin/fit issues frequently co-occurred and often necessitated cast modification or early removal but were catalogued separately. Importantly, no study in our dataset reported thoracic compartment syndrome specifically,10,23,29,33 even though other thoracic/respiratory complications were observed.

3.6. Pulmonary function

Two out of the 23 studies reported pulmonary function outcomes18,34 Jensen et al. 18 compared tidal volume, dynamic compliance and positive inspiratory pressure (PIP), in relation to baseline, during casting and after cast removal. There was a statistically significant decrease in tidal volume and dynamic compliance as well as an increase in PIP (p < 0.01) from pre-cast to in-cast. 18 However, after cast removal there was an improvement in these pulmonary measures (compared to during casting), with a reduction in PIP and increase in tidal volume and dynamic compliance (p < 0.01). 18 This study did not report on the etiology of scoliosis. Goldberg et al. 34 reported on pulmonary function based on how successful the casting process was for infantile onset scoliosis (non-congenital and non-syndromic). Group 1 did not undergo any surgical intervention and had normal mean pulmonary function (mean FEV1 = 98.7% and FVC = 96.6%). 34 Group 2 underwent a surgical intervention after the age of 10 and had acceptable mean pulmonary function at follow-up (FEV1 = 79% and FVC = 68.3%). 34 Group 3 had early surgery and had poor pulmonary function at final follow-up (FEV1 = 41%, FVC = 40.8%). 34

3.7. Quality outcomes

Quality of life outcomes were reported in three out of the 23 studies.11,34,35 In these studies, 75.2% of patients had IS, 12.4% had SS, 7.8% suffered from CS while 4.7% had NMS. Matsumoto et al. 35 and Sleem et al. 11 both conducted EOSQ-24 questionnaires as part of their quality analysis. Masumoto et al. 35 reported a statistically significant decrease in the domains of transfer, physical function, daily living, emotion, finance and child-satisfaction (p < 0.05) in idiopathic scoliosis (IS) group. In the non-idiopathic scoliosis group (NIS), there was a statistically significant decrease in the domains of transfer, emotion and child-satisfaction (p < 0.05). 35 These outcomes were compared from pre-cast to in-cast for all participants. 35 Sleem et al. 11 reported an inverse relationship between residual Cobb angle and quality outcomes. Residual Cobb angle at final follow-up had a negative impact on the domains of daily living (p = 0.004), satisfaction (p = 0.0009), pulmonary function (p = 0.0006), general health (p = 0.0003), pain/discomfort (p = 0.0002) and emotion (p < 0.0001). 11

Goldberg et al. 34 assessed cosmesis as a proxy for quality-of-life post-treatment. The success of the casting process was associated with better cosmesis with patients requiring early surgery (<10 years) having a poorer outcome due to rib hump progression affecting cosmesis. 34 There is a real potential for selection bias here which must be recognised, as the cohort who get selected for surgical intervention are frequently affected by more severe deformity and are indeed likely to be less responsive to casting, thus being excluded from this treatment cohort. Age of onset, overall deformity magnitude and rigidity are likely the most important determining factors in treatment success or failure.

3.8. Radiological outcomes

Two studies reported corrections of Cobb angle % without a comparator group.11,22 Both studies had a high correction index, 65% ± 16.7 to last cast 22 and 54.68% ± 28.37 correction after final follow-up. 11 In Sleem et al. 10 all of the patients had IS, while in Conry et al. 21 the patients had a mixed aetiology of scoliosis.

Two studies compared the percentage correction outcomes between serial casting and other intervention methods for early onset scoliosis. Smith et al. 24 reported that casting had improved outcomes compared to bracing and VEPTR (59% correction casting, 51.2% brace and 33.8% VEPTR at final follow-up). Similarly, Li et al. 30 reported superior performance for casting compared to bracing (29% correction vs 9% regression respectively). In these studies, 84.8% had idiopathic scoliosis, 9.2% had NMS, while 6% had CS.

Two studies examined the percentage Cobb angle correction in relation to the type of casting table used. Marrache et al. 9 reported comparable correction regardless of table type used (spica or Risser/Mehta). Conversely, Montgomery et al. 31 reported a considerable difference in correction between Risser frame and Jackson table at final follow-up (2 % vs 22% respectively). In these studies, 76.6% of patients had IS, while 23.4% had NMS.

Li et al. 30 compared percentage thoracic height changes between casting and bracing. The two groups were similar in relation to age at start of treatment, duration of follow-up and diagnosis of scoliosis type. However, major curve was different between the groups, with average starting curve of 50° ± 20° for casting and 31° ± 10° for bracing. Bracing reported a substantial increase compared to casting (16% vs 8 %). 30 In this study, 77.9% of patients had IS, 13.2% had NMS, and 8.8% had CS.

4. Discussion

4.1. Complications of EOS management

Management strategies for early-onset scoliosis (EOS) can be broadly categorised into conservative and surgical approaches. 3 Conservative options include bracing and physiotherapy. However, serial casting remains the most effective non-operative modality for limiting curve progression while achieving meaningful correction of existing deformity. 36 Surgical interventions are typically reserved for more severe cases and include distraction-based, compression-based, and growth-guided techniques, all of which aim to control deformity while preserving spinal growth.

Traditional growing rods represent the generally preferred method for curves exceeding 60 degrees, applying distraction forces to permit continued spinal elongation. 36 Despite their effectiveness, these constructs are associated with a substantial complication burden, with reported median complication rates of up to 84%. 37 Implant failure, infection, and wound-related complications are common, frequently necessitating prolonged treatment courses, repeated surgical procedures, and increased healthcare costs. 37 Notably, more than half of patients require unplanned reoperations, imposing considerable psychological, financial, and administrative burdens. 37 These findings underscore the importance of prioritising and exhausting conservative management strategies, particularly in patients with mild to moderate deformity.

In this meta-analysis, serial casting demonstrated a cumulative complication rate of 17%, with most adverse events being minor, transient, and reversible. While not insignificant, this rate is substantially lower than that reported for surgical interventions. The most reported complications were dermatological, including pressure sores, rashes, and frictional ulcers.9,27–31 Less frequent adverse events included vomiting, transient lumbar hyperlordosis, cast soiling, discomfort, asthma exacerbation, and gastrointestinal symptoms.9,27–31 Importantly, these complications rarely resulted in treatment discontinuation. When considered alongside the demonstrated benefits of curve correction and preservation of thoracic growth, 14 these findings support serial casting as a safe and effective non-surgical treatment modality for EOS.

In contemporary practice, many specialised spine deformity centres now adopt serial casting as a first-line intervention in early-onset scoliosis, particularly in younger patients, to mitigate the risks associated with early surgical intervention and delay or avoid the need for growth rod constructs.

Early initiation of casting, particularly before 18 months of age, has been consistently associated with improved radiographic outcomes and a greater likelihood of curve resolution. This highlights the importance of early identification and referral, as timely intervention may maximise the growth-modulating potential of casting and reduce the need for subsequent surgical management. This has important implications for primary care clinicians and paediatricians, who play a key role in early detection and referral pathways for children with suspected spinal deformity.

This safety advantage is further underscored by comparative data, where casting was associated with markedly fewer complications than growth rod instrumentation (1/27 vs 12/27). Beyond absolute complication rates, an important distinction lies in the complication-free interval. Serial casting avoids the cycle of repeated surgical lengthening procedures inherent to growth rod strategies, which are frequently associated with unplanned reoperations due to implant failure, infection, or wound complications. As such, casting not only reduces overall complication burden but also minimises treatment-related interruptions and cumulative surgical morbidity.

However, important limitations in radiographic assessment remain. It is important to recognise that Cobb angle, the most reported radiographic outcome, reflects only the coronal component of a three-dimensional deformity. Additional parameters such as apical vertebral rotation (AVR), rib–vertebral angle difference (RVAD), and space available for the lung (SAL) may provide further insight into rotational deformity and thoracic development, which are closely linked to treatment response and pulmonary outcomes. However, these measures were inconsistently reported across included studies, limiting assessment of the impact of serial casting on three-dimensional correction. This is particularly relevant given that EDF casting is designed to achieve elongation, derotation, and flexion. Future studies should incorporate standardised reporting of these parameters to enable a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment efficacy.

Evidently, the variability and underreporting of complications in the existing literature further highlight the need for systematic evaluation, which this study sought to address.

4.2. Clinical implications

Casting has been shown to impose temporary psychosocial and functional burdens, including restrictions in mobility, daily activities, emotional wellbeing, and patient satisfaction. 35 However, these effects are generally transient and resolve following cast removal and are outweighed by the physical and psychological benefits of deformity correction. Routine monitoring using validated instruments such as the EOSQ-24 is therefore essential to assess quality-of-life impact and guide patient counselling throughout treatment.

Cardiopulmonary function may transiently decline during casting, likely due to external thoracic restriction. However available evidence suggests recovery and improvement following cast removal. 18 This temporary reduction may represent a necessary trade-off in achieving deformity correction and optimising thoracic development, thereby reducing the long-term risk of thoracic insufficiency syndrome.

Compared with growth rod surgery, serial casting minimises operative morbidity and hospital readmissions. 29 Given the predominance of minor, reversible complications, the benefits of casting substantially outweigh its risks. Nevertheless, risk mitigation remains essential. Careful padding, abdominal windowing and improved education regarding cast hygiene may reduce dermatological complications. The adoption of breathable or waterproof materials may further enhance tolerability.

Practical considerations for minimising casting-related complications include careful padding of bony prominences to reduce pressure-related skin injury, routine inspection for early signs of skin irritation, and optimisation of cast fit to prevent frictional ulcers. Abdominal windowing may improve comfort and reduce gastrointestinal symptoms such as reflux or vomiting, while ensuring adequate thoracic expansion may mitigate respiratory compromise. Caregiver education regarding cast hygiene, skin monitoring, and early reporting of complications is essential to facilitate timely intervention and prevent progression of minor issues.

5. Limitations

This systematic review and meta-analysis has several limitations. Most of the included studies were retrospective single-centre case series, with small sample sizes and a lack of control groups. This reduced the generalizability of the results and limited the ability to draw reliable conclusions regarding the safety of serial casting. Furthermore, significant clinical geographic and methodological heterogeneity was observed across the studies, including differences in patient characteristics, casting techniques, follow-up, and outcomes reported. Moderate heterogeneity observed in the meta-analysis likely reflects differences in geographic and clinical settings, including variations in patient populations across regions (e.g., India, China, and the United States), as well as differences in casting techniques and materials such as plaster of Paris versus fiberglass. The lack of standardized outcome measures and longitudinal data, particularly for pulmonary and quality-of-life parameters, limited direct comparisons across studies and the feasibility of quantitative pooling of results. Long-term follow up beyond skeletal maturity is limited, which hinders the ability to draw conclusions regarding the impact of serial casting on spinal growth and respiratory functions. Additionally, the possibility of publication and selective reporting biases due to the underreporting of adverse events cannot be excluded. While most studies were found to have a low risk of bias, those with a moderate risk of bias were largely due to inadequate follow-up and lack of strategies to address confounding factors.

5.1. Future directions

Future research should therefore prioritize prospective, long term multicentre studies to draw generalizable conclusions regarding long term outcomes and complication rates. Furthermore, future studies should adopt standardized reporting practices for outcomes such as complication rates, curve progression, Cobb angle correction, and pulmonary function, as these are often reported inconsistently with varying definitions and follow-up intervals.

Few studies investigated the long-term functional, psychosocial and pulmonary outcomes during and after undergoing serial casting, which should be prioritized to achieve a holistic understanding of the impact of casting on this patient population. Given the variability in access to healthcare globally, future research ought to conduct cost-effective analyses comparing casting, bracing and surgical interventions in low- and middle-income settings, to optimize resource allocation and help guide healthcare policies. Furthermore, the prolonged nature of serial casting can impose financial and psychosocial burdens on patients and families, therefore patient-reported outcomes and health economic analyses should be incorporated to evaluate these implications. Preventative strategies aimed at reducing cast-related complications to improve outcomes and reduce morbidity should be a key focus of future studies, with a particular emphasis on dermatological issues as they were the most prevalent. Serial casting typically involves using fiberglass or plaster materials, 8 which can contribute to complications, discomfort, and limit radiological monitoring. Future research should explore novel materials and technologies such as breathable casts to reduce discomfort.

6. Conclusion

Serial casting emerges from this review as an effective, growth-preserving intervention for early-onset scoliosis, achieving substantial curve correction and supporting thoracic development with a comparatively low incidence of predominantly minor and reversible complications. Dermatological issues constituted the most frequent adverse events, while serious cardiopulmonary complications were uncommon and no cases of thoracic compartment syndrome were identified. Nevertheless, the current evidence base is constrained by small, largely retrospective cohorts, heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting, and limited longitudinal data, particularly in relation to pulmonary and psychosocial domains. To strengthen the clinical evidence underpinning serial casting, future research should prioritise prospective multicentre studies with standardised reporting frameworks, core outcome measures, and systematic incorporation of patient-reported and health economic outcomes. Such efforts will be essential to refine patient selection, optimise complication mitigation strategies, and guide innovation in cast materials and application techniques.
