The evolution of ECCO₂R: rethinking pump selection
Authors: José Carlos Gasca-Aldama, Osvaldo Alexis Marché Fernández, Jesús Enrique Castrejón Sánchez
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the prospective observational study by Kryvenko et al. [1]. Their work demonstrates the feasibility and safety of minimally invasive extracorporeal CO₂ removal (miECCO₂R) via a continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) platform in mild-to-moderate ARDS.

Historically, lower blood flow in ECCO₂R was assumed to guarantee safety. However, as Barbič et al. noted, centrifugal pumps are designed for high flows (3–6 L/min). Forcing them to operate at low flows (350–500 mL/min) increases circuit resistance and blood trauma. Admittedly, the severe bleeding reported in major trials (e.g., SUPERNOVA, REST, VENT-AVOID) was multifactorial. These adverse events were heavily driven by systemic anticoagulation in high-risk populations, prolonged treatment durations, and varying weaning protocols. Nevertheless, centrifugal pump shear at restrictive low flows likely acted as a contributing factor to these outcomes [2].

As early as 2018, Schmidt et al. showed that CRRT platforms could be successfully adapted for ECCO₂R [3]. Building on this, recent studies by Kryvenko et al. [1] and Combes et al. [4] utilized CRRT platforms driven by peristaltic (roller) pumps, which are specifically designed to handle low flows. Across a combined cohort of 78 patients from these two recent prospective studies, the incidence of clinically significant hemolysis, major hemorrhages, or intracranial bleeding was zero. However, this promising signal requires methodological caution. Applying the rule of three, the upper 95% confidence interval for these adverse events still approaches 4%. Furthermore, comparing selected, short-duration observational cohorts to large RCTs is limited. Peristaltic pumps are also not flawless. They carry inherent mechanical risks at higher speeds. These include tubing spallation, segmental pressure peaks, and occlusion-dependent hemolysis. Crucially, the hemolytic and mechanical risks of peristaltic pumps scale with revolutions per minute (RPM) and run time, a relationship that is not yet characterized for prolonged ECCO₂R durations. Moreover, routine CRRT circuits typically operate for at least 72 h. Prolonged ECCO₂R support extending well beyond this period poses unknown risks regarding circuit longevity.

We must balance safety with clinical efficacy. Safely running a circuit is only meaningful if it achieves sufficient CO₂ clearance to effectively reduce driving pressure and ventilation intensity. Assuming that lowering blood flow is universally beneficial risks repeating historical errors in ECCO₂R. Achieving optimal flows above 350 mL/min necessitates larger-bore venous access. Therefore, we advocate a deliberate shift away from strictly “minimally invasive” approaches toward a “mid-flow” strategy to maximize clinical efficacy. Furthermore, we concede that regional citrate anticoagulation at flows > 350 mL/min is currently unfeasible due to toxic requirements.

We propose a multicenter RCT comparing a ‘mid-flow’ ECCO₂R paradigm against conventional ARDSNet ventilation. To maximize benefit, selection should target moderate ARDS patients with refractory hypercapnia, a ventilatory ratio > 3, and driving pressure > 15 cmH₂O. The intervention integrates peristaltic pumps to mitigate shear stress, highly efficient membranes (> 0.8 m²), and sustained flows of 350–500 mL/min managed with strictly protocolized systemic anticoagulation. The primary outcome should be ventilator-free days on day 28 (VFD-28).
