AI-Based model for site-selecting earthquake emergency shelters
Authors: Amirmasoud Amiran, Behrouz Behnam, Sanaz Seyedin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The objective of this research is to develop an AI-based model that provides a map of potential emergency shelters for seismic-prone regions. While the predominant approach for locating emergency shelters involves expert-answered questionnaires, their accuracy has often been criticized globally. To address this, machine learning can enhance the speed and accuracy of shelter site selection; the results can also be generalized to other regions. Support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), gaussian processes classifier (GPC), and artificial neural network (ANN) methods are used to develop the model here. These algorithms are trained using maps of emergency shelters in San Francisco, enabling the resulting model to automatically provide potential emergency shelter maps not only for the studied case but also for cities with similar criteria. Except for LR, the other algorithms achieved F1 scores between 0.7 and 1.0 in selecting emergency shelter sites. The model developed in this research can serve as a reliable tool for disaster management planners in managing emergency shelters for people affected by earthquakes.

Introduction

The fact that over 50% of the world’s population lives in large cities, covering less than 3% of the earth’s surface, has made many urban regions vulnerable to natural disasters. Earthquakes, in particular, can cause extensive damage and losses in a short while1,2. Over the last century, more than 10,000 destructive earthquakes have occurred worldwide, resulting in the loss of over 1.5 million lives and significant financial and spiritual damage; these earthquakes have often occurred in urban areas3,4. To respond properly and timely to earthquakes, it is essential to prepare comprehensive disaster management plans. Disaster management refers to a set of activities, solutions, and instructions designed to reduce the consequences of a disaster and control or eliminate its destructive effects. Disaster management aims to push progress into a controllable routine and restore normal life to pre-disaster conditions as soon as possible. Disaster management includes actions before the disaster (prevention and preparation), during the disaster (response), and after the disaster (reconstruction and recovery)5.

Disaster risk reduction is critical to prevent losses from future disasters. However, its success often remains obscured due to the inherent challenges of evaluating outcomes and recognizing effective interventions, for instance, because hazard events do not occur in a given region/time window6. Disaster preparedness involves several crucial factors, such as assessing the level of vulnerability, identifying possible risks, and prioritizing solutions to prevent and control them. One of the essential steps in disaster preparedness is to create an emergency evacuation plan that includes evaluating the vulnerability of urban elements related to evacuation and selecting a suitable site for the emergency settlement of affected populations. Therefore, if it is possible to determine safe emergency shelters, it is thus possible to increase the preparedness against earthquakes to a desired level7,8. However, some field research has shown that existing emergency shelters may not be reliable enough. For instance, a study conducted in South Florida found that %48 of existing shelters and %57 of located shelters were situated in inappropriate areas. Moreover, 15 existing shelters were located in unsuitable areas with no alternative options9.

It is worth noting that emergency shelters are places that have minimum living standards for a defined period during or after a disaster. On the one hand, such places should be well identified to the local people, and on the other hand, the distances to reach them should not be too long. Also, these spaces should preferably be close to existing urban facilities and infrastructures.

Site-selecting emergency shelters (SSES) involves combined analysis of their spatial information and relevant descriptive data to find a suitable site with the desired characteristics10. This claim is defensible as in most of the previous studies on SSES, questionnaires survey methods, and hierarchical analysis have been used to determine the weight of the effective criteria. While acknowledging the strengths of the mentioned methods, it is evident that they also have many limitations that can challenge the confidence of their results, such as the inherent limitations of qualitative methods11,12. It is worth mentioning that SSES is a highly complex activity that must be balanced between different criteria. Because different stakeholders have different understandings of the value of each criterion, further reliable techniques are needed to deal with such complex issues and make decisions. In the following, by examining previous studies in the field of SSES, their innovations and limitations are mentioned.

Research background

Site-selecting emergency shelters

Liu and Lim13conducted a study in Australia using GIS for spatial analysis. They found that it is possible to determine suitable locations for emergency shelters using analytical methods and to route them using network analysis in the GIS environment. Unal and Uslu14 used GIS to study factors such as distance from medical centers, fault lines, population density, and urban open spaces. They identified these factors as parameters that can influence the suitability of the emergency shelter location. In another study, Zangiabadi et al.15combined GIS and AHP to identify and prioritize suitable areas for temporary shelters in Isfahan, Iran. They used criteria such as distance from medical centers, fire stations, gas stations, building height, park area, and road conditions. Yu and Wen16used GIS, AHP, and population data to investigate the adequacy and desirability of existing emergency shelters in the Jing’an district of Shanghai. In Schwartz’s study17, GIS analysis was used to evaluate safe emergency shelters in New York City. The study found that the proximity to main roads, medical centers, and vehicles was the most important factor in establishing emergency shelters. In a similar study, Trivedi and Singh18 applied the fuzzy AHP method to evaluate emergency shelters in a disaster recovery program in the Gorkha district of Nepal. To locate temporary shelters in Sanandaj in Iran, Dabiri et al.19 determined the criteria for selecting suitable sites using brainstorming sessions. Then, they prioritized thirteen candidates using the Intuitive Fuzzy Hierarchy Analysis. In a similar study, Bakhshi et al.20 used the superposition of eighteen information layers, where the weight of each one was obtained during the collective decision-making process, to SSES. Soltani et al.21 investigated the shelter site selection criteria for earthquake victims. Reviewing numerous published studies, they found that criteria such as accessibility and proximity to the residence are much emphasized in most studies. These criteria were then classified into six groups: size and location, risk reduction, rescue and relief facilities, site capability, environmental, and social aspects. Xu et al.22investigated the location of earthquake evacuation shelters using a multi-criteria decision model. Their research method included selection of candidate locations, spatial coverage analysis, and determination of final locations using geographic information systems (GIS). Wang23 evaluated the suitability of emergency shelters in Tianjin, China using the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) to determine the weights of the indicators and the TOPSIS technique to build the model. The defined criteria were grouped into three aspects: effectiveness, safety, and accessibility. Hosseini et al.24 Using the AHP and considering various physical, social, economic, and environmental aspects, presented a model for selecting emergency accommodations in earthquake-prone areas. They showed that despite the existence of sufficient spaces, there was a need to identify new places to meet potential physical and social issues. Zhang et al.25 Using geographic data, K-means clustering, and particle swarm optimization algorithms (PSO), investigated the optimization of emergency shelter locations in Deyang City, China. The results showed that the spatial distribution of emergency shelters in the city is uneven and unreasonable; adding 10 new shelters could reduce the number of inaccessible areas by 43.31%, and, hence, increasing the urban resiliency.

By reviewing previous studies on SSES, it is evident that multi-criteria decision-making tools, such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) based on questionnaire data collection, are commonly used for ranking criteria, determining their weights, and finally selecting shelter sites. This issue can lead to errors such as the limitation of the statistical population of the respondents, the spatial limitation of the questionnaire results to the questioned area, the inability to change the results of the questionnaire method over time, and the low speed of updating the results11,12.

Another crucial aspect highlighted by previous studies is that, in many cases, the site-selection results for emergency shelters have been treated as definitive, without considering the vulnerability analysis of the roads leading to these shelters. This oversight can lead to human and economic losses, such as reduced shelter capacity compared to planned values when access to multiple centers is compromised after a disaster. Additionally, it can create confusion among relief forces and affected residents as they seek their way to emergency shelters. It is worth stating here that transport infrastructure is exposed to natural hazards all around the world. Almost 27% of all global road and railway assets are exposed to at least one hazard26. To address this risky mistake, the term “potential” is used in the current study for the outputs of the site-selecting model.

Over the last years, interest has grown in leveraging innovative technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) to reinforce natural disaster management. In many fields, such as medicine and finance, AI has already gained traction due to advances in algorithms, a growth in computational power, and the availability of large data sets27. Since natural disaster data are examples of big data, for proper management and making appropriate predictions in this field, employing machine learning (ML) can be very useful as a suitable alternative to traditional questionnaire methods as mentioned earlier. In this vein, ML-based methods have been developed to improve various components of risk modeling, such as exposure, vulnerability, hazards, and risks, as well as to prioritize resources during the response and recovery phase28. However, these methods have not yet entered the topic of SSES, which is the main innovation of the current research. Studies that have used ML methods for disaster management are examined in the following Section.

Machine learning in natural disaster management

Ma et al.29 reviewed previous research on shelter location-allocation models after natural disasters. They showed that using ML as an optimization algorithm for the final location can be helpful. However, previous studies do not mention the utilization of ML to assess the significance of site selection criteria. Using ML algorithms and GIS, Rahman et al.30tried to optimize the distribution of population evacuation centers in Bangladesh to reduce human losses and increase the speed in the response phase of flood disaster management. Ochoa and Comes31 proposed an ML model that estimates the amount of damage and casualties by receiving satellite images after an earthquake, so that these data can be used to build temporary shelters later. Although the accuracy of this model was estimated at 62%, it was considered an important step in post-disaster image processing. Geng et al.32 presented a model for SSES using the VIKOR method. However, they still used a survey among experts to determine the weight of the site-selecting criteria. In a recent study, Chowdhury et al.33 used logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and GIS-based decision tree ML algorithms with 16 criteria to prepare landslide risk maps for the Chattogram area of Bangladesh; they achieved accuracies higher than 90%. Abdalzaher et al.34 examined the use of earthquake early warning systems (EEWS) in smart cities, using the Internet of Things (IoT) and ML. IoT systems were used to collect the required data and ML to analyze them. Li et al.35 focused on the prediction of casualties in earthquake-prone areas and presented a method based on area support vector regression (Z-SVR). Their research method included evaluating the characteristics affecting casualties, dividing areas based on population data and geological faults, and developing the Z-SVR model with optimal parameters for each risk area. The results showed that the Z-SVR model can significantly improve the accuracy of loss prediction. Mangalathu et al.36explored the possibility of using ML techniques to quickly predict building damage caused by earthquakes. The 2014 Napa earthquake Data were used and building damage was classified according to red, yellow, and green labels. Various ML models, such as K-nearest neighbors (KNN), decision trees, and RF, were evaluated. The results showed that the RF algorithm has higher accuracy than other algorithms. Alruqi and Aksoy37 examined the use of AI in disaster management. The findings showed that the adoption of AI in disaster management faces challenges such as the need for high-quality data, high resources, and insufficient awareness, which highlights the need for improved awareness and required cooperation.

By reviewing previous studies on SSES, the use of hierarchical analysis based on data collection in the form of questionnaires is evident. While well-employed, these methods can lead to errors such as the limitation of the statistical population of the respondents, the spatial limitation of the questionnaire results to the area in question, the impossibility of changing the results of the questionnaires over time, and the slow speed of updating the questionnaire results.

From a different viewpoint, reviewing previous studies on the applications of ML in earthquake disaster management shows that they have mainly focused on research areas such as damage estimation, casualty forecasting, EEWSs, earthquake forecasting, and optimization of emergency evacuation processes. Notably, Zhang et al. ‘s study25, uses the K-means ML method to cluster and identify areas for which emergency shelters should be established, and the optimization algorithm. It uses PSO to optimize distances to shelters and locate them. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, ML-based models have not been used in the field of reverse engineering of a city’s macro data to reach a site-selecting model.

Reverse engineering is an approach to understanding the design, structure, system, or process. It involves deconstructing the subject to reveal its components, relationships, and operational principles. By working backward from the final form, researchers can gain insights into its creation, and comprehend its mechanisms38,39. ML plays an important role in reverse engineering by improving the analysis of intricate systems, including software and hardware. AI-assisted reverse engineering employs ML techniques to efficiently detect patterns, vulnerabilities, and system structures, surpassing human experts in both speed and accuracy. Supervised learning is particularly used to recognize system components through trial and error and handle high-dimensional data40,41. Therefore, the application of ML techniques for reverse engineering successful experiences in the domain of SSES represents a novel contribution from both the site-selecting and ML approaches.

Preparing maps of emergency shelters

These maps reflect the research and calculations of the organizations and researchers who are in charge of the matter, which is the conclusion of the research of many experts.

The next operation on the data is to remove additional land uses from the dataset. Here, land uses such as residential and office occupancy which are not suitable to be used as emergency shelters, are removed. In addition to reducing the volume of data and increasing the speed of modeling, this operation can also increase the accuracy of modeling by reducing the ratio of the number of shelters to the total number of parcels. As well, the weight of the number of shelters and non-shelters is equalized by data augmentation which is done due to the small number of shelters compared to the total data. This operation is called data resampling.

Developing a machine learning model for fitting the data

In this important part of the research, a model based on ML is designed to produce the map of emergency shelters as the output by analyzing the inputs. In the training phase, the model initially analyzes the input data based on the specified criteria. Then, during the test phase (using 25% of the data not employed in model training), the model is tested to determine its error and generalization. Considering that the shelters’ data are binary the purpose of the model is to recognize the class of points in a binary manner (being a shelter or not) and that the model’s input data has a label, the problem is the classification that is a subset of supervised learning. SVM, KNN, LR, GPC, and ANN are used for modeling as introduced hereafter.

Supervised learning

Supervised learning usually involves classification and regression problems. In supervised learning, the dataset consists of feature and target vectors (labels). The model should predict the target value corresponding to the given feature vector60,61.

KNN

The KNN algorithm is based on the principle that data points with similar characteristics belong to the same class or have similar output values60–62.

LR

LR is used for binary and multiclass classification problems. At its core, LR is a linear model that models the probability of a binary outcome based on one or more variables60,63.

GPC

GPC uses Gaussian processes a type of kernel method- for classification tasks. Unlike conventional classifiers, GPC provides well-calibrated class membership probabilities, which provide a possible measure of prediction uncertainty. It excels in scenarios with limited data and efficiently handles noisy data sets64,65.

SVM

SVM is a powerful ML algorithm for classification. Due to its ability to process linear and non-linear data, this method is very popular60,66.

ANN

ANN is a system for information processing that includes several simple processing elements called neurons. ANNs are composed of interconnected nodes that are organized into layers, allowing them to analyze and transform data through complex computations and to identify complex patterns and trends in data that traditional computing systems may miss67.

Results and discussion

As mentioned earlier, an eliminative criterion should first be applied, which is the buffer of prohibited areas on the city map, and those areas should be removed from the city map for modeling. Prohibited areas include protected areas such as military bases, and security-protected areas where ordinary citizens are not allowed to enter. In San Francisco, these areas did not exist within the city boundaries. Then, to prepare and adjust the input data of the model for SSES using the shape file of the city’s land use, the area of each part is calculated in ArcGIS software. By processing and correcting the satellite image of the city DEM, a raster map of the land slope is produced. After that, each part’s surface slope is assigned. Next, the maps of the locations of police stations, fire stations, emergency management centers, medical and healthcare centers, emergency water reservoirs, and population distribution are obtained. For each part of the city, the minimum distance to the mentioned places (Manhattan distance) is calculated and assigned to each part. Then, for each parcel, the population within a radius of 800 m is calculated and assigned to each parcel. Finally, after adjusting the data, the site-selecting model of emergency shelters is developed using the mentioned algorithms and implemented on the data of the city.

A correlation near 1 or -1 indicates that the two variables are strongly correlated. This means that while one variable increases, the other one either increases (correlation = 1) or decreases (correlation=-1). In ML, the preference for correlation between features is closer to 0, which indicates nonlinear relationships between features. While the correlation of two features is near 1 or -1, one should be eliminated. This is because two variables with heavy correlation, give the same information to the ML model.

A correlation close to 1 or -1 between a feature and the target value indicates a linear relationship. Consequently, for such cases, simpler and more linear ML models are preferred. On the other hand, a correlation close to 0 indicates a non-linear relationship, which necessitates the use of more complex and non-linear ML models or less complex non-linear models using kernel tricks. If the correlation between a feature and target is close to 1 or -1 and a nonlinear model is trained, the model variance will be high and model biases will be less but if correlation is near 0, and linear models are used, then bias will be too high.

The correlation values between the features, as shown in Fig. 6, indicate relatively weak relationships between them, which indicates that there is no need to remove any criterion and that all are correctly selected. However, the existence of a moderate correlation between some variables indicates that some patterns and relationships can be useful for predicting and interpreting the model results. These results indicate that while there is no strong or inverse relationship between the features, there are still connections that may contribute to the model results.

With the initial running of the algorithms, proper outputs were not obtained at first due to the unbalanced amount of data in labels 0 and 1. However, this method introduced a significant bias in the model results toward open spaces. The reason was an imbalance in the number of shelters concerning land use, resulting from the addition of numerous small shelters while consolidating larger area shelters. To address this, we resample the data by equalizing the weights of shelter data (label 1) and the remaining data (label 0). This approach led to successful model results. Limitations and future research directions

The limitations of this research as well as suggestions for future studies are as follows:

As the model developed in this study was applied to San Francisco data as a case study, it is hence more suitable for cities with seismic hazard levels, urban and building structures, and population density similar to the city studied. This limitation can be overcome by incorporating data from other cities with successful earthquake disaster management experiences, such as Los Angeles, Tokyo, and Kobe; thus, increasing the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the model.

This model is specifically designed with a focus on earthquake risk. To better approximate real-world conditions, it is useful to consider the effect of multiple hazards, such as fires that may occur after an earthquake, in the model.

Another limitation is that due to the large amount of data, the model requires high computational resources, especially when using methods such as ANN and GPC, especially in the training phase. Future research may include increasing the amount of input data for further training and employing more complex ANNs to improve model performance, which requires even more computational resources.

Summary and conclusion

This research was conducted to improve accuracy and speed in the processing of site-selecting emergency shelters (SSES). Examining the research background, it was found that SSES is generally based on completing questionnaires by experts to determine the weights of the criteria and then superimposing the layers of information corresponding to each criterion. Due to the limitation of the statistical population of the respondents, the spatial limitation of the questionnaire results to the questioned area, the lack of dynamics of the results obtained from the questionnaire, and the low speed of updating the results, the results of these methods may have significant errors. To minimize these errors and develop high-speed and reliable SSES models, an ML-based model was proposed in the current research which is a functional tool to be employed by disaster managers.

To increase the accuracy of the input data of the SSES model, the information of one of the successful cities in the field of earthquake disaster management, San Francisco, was used. Two main criteria mandatory and eliminative were defined where the first is defined for selecting the shelters and the former is defined for controlling whether their locations are not prohibited. Since the eliminative criterion layer here had no effect, i.e., the buffer of prohibited areas within the city area, no area of the city map was hence removed to carry out the modeling calculations. Then, the information layers corresponding to the required criteria of land use, land area, land slope, minimum distance from emergency water reservoirs, minimum distance from healthcare centers, minimum distance from emergency service centers, minimum distance from police stations, and population density for this city were prepared. After making adjustments, corrections, and necessary data pre-processing, the location of emergency shelters was given as input to the model.

According to the target data type, which was binary, and the desired output type, which was a classification problem, five classification algorithms— Support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), logistic regression (LR), gaussian processes classifier (GPC), and artificial neural network (ANN)—were considered. After repeatedly training the model and running it by changing the modeling parameters, the error (F1 Score, due to the high importance of False Positive and False Negative errors) of all algorithms, except LR, reached 0.7 to 1.0, which was a favorable level. Finally, among all of the algorithms, the GPC algorithm with an F1 score equal to 1.0 and due to the FP and FN error equal to zero, which did not have any life and economic risk, was identified as the best algorithm in this research.
