Reconstructing Graph Signals from Noisy Dynamical Samples
Akram Aldroubi, Victor Bailey, Ilya Krishtal, Brendan Miller, Armenak Petrosyan

Abstract
We investigate the dynamical sampling space-time trade-off problem within a graph setting. Specifically, we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for space-time sampling that enable the reconstruction of an initial band-limited signal on a graph. Additionally, we develop and test numerical algorithms for approximating the optimal placement of sensors on the graph to minimize the mean squared error when recovering signals from time-space measurements corrupted by i.i.d. additive noise. Our numerical experiments demonstrate that our approach outperforms previously proposed algorithms for related problems.

Contributions.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

First, in Section

Next, in Section

1.2
Basic definitions and notation.

Symbols
G=(V,E)
will denote a finite undirected graph, where
V=V(G)=\{1,\ldots,d\}
is the set of vertices,
d=|V|
is the cardinality of
V
, and
E=E(G)\subseteq V\times V
is the set of edges.

The degree of vertex
i
, denoted by
deg(i)
, is the number of edges that are connected to
i
.

Next, we define a few standard operators associated with a graph. In this paper, we routinely identify operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with their matrices in the standard basis.

For a graph
G=(V,E)
,
V=V(G)=\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the Laplacian
\Delta
of
G
is the
d\times d
matrix
\Delta=D-C
, where
D
is a diagonal matrix such that
d_{ii}=deg(i)
and
C
is the
d\times d
adjacency matrix of
G
, i.e.,
c_{ij}=1
if there is an edge between
i
and
j
and
c_{ij}=0
otherwise. Clearly, the Laplacian
\Delta
is a symmetric matrix. Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\Delta
are real and
\Delta
has an orthonormal set of eigenvectors.

In order for the IVP (
1
) to be associated with a graph
G
we must make restrictions on the possible operators
A
. To this end, we use the Laplacian operator
\Delta
on
G
to relate
A
to the topology of
G
similarly to the way it was done in
[
21
]
.

Definition 1
.

An IVP (
1
) is associated with a graph
G
if the operator
A
is positive semidefinite and has eigenvectors coinciding with those of the Laplacian
\Delta
. Such an operator
A
will be called a
graph operator
(associated with
G
).

In particular, a graph operator commutes with the Laplacian of
G
.
Decomposing
\Delta
as
B^{*}D_{\Delta}B
where
B
is unitary and
D_{\Delta}
is diagonal, we get that
A
is a graph operator if and only if
A
can be written as
A=B^{*}D_{A}B
, where
D_{A}
is diagonal with real non-negative entries. Thus, a graph operator
A
is orthogonally diagonalizable.

In the following definition, we introduce Paley-Wiener spaces on graphs as it was done in
[
33
]
.

Definition 2
.

The
Paley-Wiener space

PW_{\omega}(A)\subseteq\ell^{2}(V)\simeq\mathbb{C}^{d}
of a graph operator
A
on
G
is the span of the eigenvectors corresponding to its eigenvalues bounded above by
\omega
. In particular, if
A
has
n\leq d
distinct real eigenvalues
0\leq\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\ldots<\lambda_{n}
, then the space
PW_{\lambda_{k}}(A)
, where
k\leq n
, is the span of the eigenvectors of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\}
.

If there is no ambiguity regarding the graph operator, then we will write
PW_{\omega}
instead of
PW_{\omega}(A)
. Additionally, in the case where
\omega=\lambda_{k}
, for some eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
A
, we will denote the Paley-Wiener space
PW_{\lambda_{k}}
as
PW_{k}
.

2
Reconstruction in Paley-Wiener Spaces

The main result in this section is Theorem
2
which provides necessary and sufficient conditions on which vertices need to be sampled in time to recover any initial condition
u_{0}
. This result is based on Theorem
1
from
[
4
]
, provided below. Before we begin, we introduce the following definitions and notation.

Let
D=diag(\lambda_{1}I_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}I_{n})
be a
d\times d
diagonal matrix where
I_{i}
are identity matrices of various sizes. We denote by
P_{j}
the orthogonal projection in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
onto the eigenspace of
D
associated to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
. In particular,
P_{j}
is a projection onto a span of a subset of the standard basis
\{e_{i}:i=1,\ldots,d\}
.

For a
d\times d
matrix
A
and a vector

, the
A\text{-annihilator}
of a vector

is the monic polynomial
q_{b}(x)=a_{0}+a_{1}x+\cdots+x^{r_{b}}
of smallest degree such that
q_{b}(A)b=0
.

Theorem 1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
and
\{b_{i}:i\in\Omega\}
vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
D
be a diagonal matrix and
r_{i}
the degree of the
D
-annihilator of
b_{i}
. Set
l_{i}=r_{i}-1
. Then
\{D^{j}b_{i}:\;i\in\Omega,\,j=0,\dots,l_{i}\}
is a frame of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
if and only if
\{P_{j}(b_{i}):i\in\Omega\}
form a frame of
P_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
j=1,\dots,n
.

Before we introduce our result on the reconstruction of a functions in a Paley-Wiener space from space-time samples, we need the following definitions and notation.

Definition 3
.

Let
\Omega\subset V=\{1,\ldots,d\}
be a subset of vertices of a graph
G
. The sub-sampling matrix
S_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
is a diagonal matrix defined by

(S_{\Omega})_{jj}=\begin{cases}1,&j\in\Omega;\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
be a graph operator. Let
W=PW_{k}
for some
k
and let
I_{W}:W\hookrightarrow\ell^{2}(V)
be the natural inclusion. The symbols
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
will denote the
Ld\times d
matrix

\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}=[I_{W}^{*}S_{\Omega},I_{W}^{*}AS_{\Omega},...,I_{W}^{*}A^{L-1}S_{\Omega}]^{*}.

(2)

When
W=\mathbb{C}^{d}
, we will denote
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
as
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
.

For a given graph operator
A
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\ldots<\lambda_{n}
, we denote by
Q_{j}
the orthogonal projection in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
onto the span of the eigenvectors of
A
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
. Given a Paley-Wiener space
PW_{k}
we denote by
\Pi_{k}
the orthogonal projection onto
PW_{k}
.

Let
A
be a graph operator. Then
A=B^{*}DB
and every
f\in PW_{k}
can be written as
f=B^{*}_{1}x
(where
B_{1}
is the submatrix of
B
such that the columns of
B^{*}_{1}
are the eigenvectors associated with
PW_{k}
, and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
with
p=\dim PW_{k}
). Define
b_{1i}
as the
i
-th column of
B_{1}
.
Let
r_{1i}
be the degree of the
D_{k}
annihilator of
b_{1i}
, where
D_{k}=diag(\lambda_{1}I_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}I_{k})
, and
\ell_{1i}=r_{1i}-1
. Using this notation we have the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Let
A
be a graph operator on
G=(V,E)
,
\Omega\subset V=\{1,\ldots d\}
, and consider the Paley-Wiener space
PW_{k}
. Then the following are equivalent.

1.

Any
f\in PW_{k}
can be recovered from the space-time samples
\{f(i),Af(i),\dots,A^{l_{1i}}f(i):i\in\Omega\}
.

2.

The set of vectors
\{P_{j}(b_{1i}):i\in\Omega\}
form a frame for the subspaces
P_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
j=1,\dots,k
.

3.

The set
\{\Pi_{k}A^{l}e_{i}:l\in\{0,1,\ldots r_{1i}-1\}
,
i\in\Omega\}
, where
r_{1i}
is the degree of the
A
-annihilator of
e_{i}
, is a frame for
PW_{k}
. That is,
\{A^{l}e_{i}:l\in\{0,1,\ldots r_{1i}-1\}
,
i\in\Omega\}
is an outer frame for
PW_{k}
.

4.

The set of vectors
\{Q_{j}(e_{i}):i\in\Omega\}
form a frame for the subspaces
Q_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{d})
,
j=1,\dots,k
.

5.

The matrix
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
in (
2
) has a left inverse.

Proof.

We start with proving that (1) and (2) are equivalent. Let
D_{k}=diag(\lambda_{1}I_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}I_{k})
. Since
f\in PW_{k}
, we can write
f=B_{1}^{*}x
where
x\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
and
p=\dim PW_{k}
. Then,

\begin{split}A^{m}f(i)&=\langle A^{m}f,e_{i}\rangle\\
&=\langle D_{k}^{m}x,B_{1}e_{i}\rangle=\langle x,D_{k}^{m}b_{1i}\rangle.\end{split}

Thus, if
\{P_{j}(b_{1i}):i\in\Omega\}
form a frame of
P_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{p})
,
j=1,\dots,k
, then using Theorem
1
and the identity above,
x\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
can be recovered from
\{f(i),Af(i),\dots,A^{l_{1i}}f(i):i\in\Omega\}
. Hence,
f=B_{1}^{*}x
can be recovered from the space-time samples
\{f(i),Af(i),\dots,A^{l_{1i}}f(i):i\in\Omega\}
.

For the converse, assume that for some
j_{0}\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
\{P_{j_{0}}(b_{1i}):i\in\Omega\}
is not a frame for
P_{j_{0}}(\mathbb{C}^{p})
. Then, there exists a vector
x_{0}\neq\bf{0}
such that
x_{0}\perp span\{b_{1i}:i\in\Omega\}
and for
f_{0}=B_{1}^{*}x_{0}\in PW_{k}
we have
A^{m}f_{0}(i)=\langle A^{m}f_{0},e_{i}\rangle=\langle x_{0},D_{k}^{m}b_{1i}\rangle=0
for each
i\in\Omega
, yet
f_{0}\neq\bf{0}
.

We now prove (2) and (3) are equivalent. Let
f\in PW_{k}.
Then
f=B_{1}^{*}x
(where the columns of
B_{1}^{*}
form an orthonormal set and
x\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
). Observe that

\langle x,D_{k}^{m}b_{1i}\rangle=\langle A^{m}f,e_{i}\rangle\\
=\langle f,\Pi_{k}A^{m}e_{i}\rangle.

Since
\|f\|^{2}=\|\sum_{j=1}^{p}x_{j}b^{*}_{1j}\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}|x_{j}|^{2}=
\|x\|^{2}
and as it follows from the observation above that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r_{1i}-1}|\langle x,D_{k}^{m}b_{1i}\rangle|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r_{1i}-1}|\langle f,\Pi_{k}A^{m}e_{i}\rangle|^{2}

we have
\{\Pi_{k}A^{m}e_{i}:m\in\{0,1,\ldots r_{1i}-1\}
,
i\in\Omega\}
is a frame for
PW_{k}
if and only if
\{D_{k}^{m}b_{1i}:m\in\{0,1,\ldots r_{1i}-1\}
,
i\in\Omega\}
is a frame for
\mathbb{C}^{p}
.
Applying Theorem
1
, the equivalence of (2) and (3) follows.

We now show that (2) and (4) are equivalent. Let
V_{j}=P_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{d})=span\{e_{1},\dots,e_{m}\}
and
W_{j}=Q_{j}(\mathbb{C}^{d})=span\{B^{\star}e_{1},\dots,B^{\star}e_{m}\}
, where
m=\dim V_{j}=\dim W_{j}
.
Clearly,
W_{j}=B^{\star}V_{j}
. Assume that
\{P_{j}b_{i}:i\in\Omega\}
is a frame for
V_{j}
. Then, for any
v\in V_{j}
, using the fact that
P_{j}v=v
, we have that

\alpha\|v\|^{2}\leq\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle v,P_{j}Be_{i}\rangle|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle v,Be_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq\beta\|v\|^{2}.

For
w\in W_{j}
, let
w=B^{\star}v
for some
v\in V_{j}
. Using the fact that
\|w\|=\|B^{\star}v\|=\|v\|
and
Q_{j}w=w
, we get

\begin{split}\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle w,Q_{j}e_{i}\rangle|^{2}&=\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle w,e_{i}\rangle|^{2}\\
&=\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle B^{\star}v,e_{i}\rangle|^{2}=\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle v,Be_{i}\rangle|^{2}\geq\alpha\|v\|^{2}=\alpha\|w\|^{2}.\end{split}

Similarly, we get an upper bound estimate

\sum\limits_{i\in\Omega}|\langle w,Q_{j}e_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq\beta\|w\|^{2}.

This proves that (2) implies (4). Starting from the assumptions that
\{Q_{j}e_{i}:i\in\Omega\}
is a frame for
W_{j}
, the exact same steps as before prove that (4) implies (2).

Recovery of
f\in PW_{k}
from the space-time samples
\{f(i),Af(i),\dots,A^{l_{1i}}f(i):i\in\Omega\}
is equivalent to the existence of a left inverse for
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
since we can recover
f
from the vector
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}f
if and only if there is a left inverse for
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
. Thus, the equivalence of (5) and (1) follows.
∎

3
Optimal node sampling for space-time problem on graphs

3.1
Problem setup

Let
G
be a finite graph, and let the time measurements be taken at discrete uniform time intervals.
In this case, without loss of generality, assume that
t_{n}=n
. Then, for each fixed time
n\leq L
, the vector
u_{n}:=(u(n,v))_{v\in V(G)}
is given by

u_{n}=A^{n}u_{0},

(3)

where
A
is a graph operator as defined above in Definition
1
.

We can determine the mean squared error of reconstructing a vector
u_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
via noisy measurements obtained from space-time samples by applying Proposition 4.1 in
[
8
]
. In particular, letting
\eta_{n}
be random vectors whose entries are i.i.d random variables of mean zero and standard deviation
\sigma
, the noisy space-time samples are recorded as:

\ y_{\Omega}=\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}u_{0}+\eta

where
\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
is as in (
2
), when
W=\mathbb{C}^{d}

and
\eta=[\eta_{0}^{T},...,\eta_{L}^{T}]^{T}
.
Denoting the error in the least squares solution as
\epsilon_{\Omega}
, we have that
\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{\Omega}\|_{2}^{2})=\sigma^{2}tr(C_{\Omega}^{-1})=\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}1/\mu_{j}(\Omega)
where
\mu_{j}(\Omega)
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
C_{\Omega}=\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}^{*}\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}
.

It is therefore natural to consider the problem of minimizing the mean squared error
\text{MSE}(\Omega):=\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{\Omega}\|_{2}^{2})
in reconstructing
u_{0}
from
y_{\Omega}
across sets
\Omega
of sampling locations with a given size
|\Omega|=s
. This is captured by the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\Omega)=\sigma^{2}tr(C_{\Omega}^{-1})

(4)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle|\Omega|=s.

Remark 1
.

In this remark, we collect a few observations with regard to the above problem.

1.

The MSE
(\Omega)
decreases as
L
, the number of time steps, increases, as shown in
[
8
]
. Similarly, for any
v\in V=V(G)
,
\text{MSE}(\Omega\cup\{v\})\leq\text{MSE}(\Omega)
.

2.

For the case in which
u_{0}\in W=PW_{k}
for some
k
we may similarly construct
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}
as in (
2
)
noting that
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}v=\mathcal{A}_{\Omega}v

3.

When
\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}u_{0}

can be recovered from

as
u_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}u_{0}

\eta

The error of reconstruction is now
\epsilon_{(W,\Omega)}=\mathcal{A}_{(\Omega,W)}^{\dagger}\eta

\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{(W,\Omega)}\|^{2})=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(\mathcal{A}^{*}_{(W,\Omega)}\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)})^{-1}

Proposition 1

Let
W=PW_{\lambda_{k}}

C_{(W,\Omega)}=\mathcal{A}^{*}_{(W,\Omega)}\mathcal{A}_{(W,\Omega)}

\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{(W,\Omega)}\|^{2})=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(C_{(W,\Omega)}^{-1})

The proof follows immediately from the proof of Proposition 2.5 in

\mathbb{E}(\|\Psi^{\dagger}\eta\|^{2})=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(\Psi^{*}\Psi)^{-1}

\text{MSE}(\Omega)=\sigma^{2}tr(C^{-1}_{(W,\Omega)})

)
becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\Omega)=\sigma^{2}tr(C^{-1}_{(W,\Omega)})

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle|\Omega|=s.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose

has dimension

, and let
C_{\Omega}
and

be as above. Then

\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{(W,\Omega)}\|^{2})<\mathbb{E}(\|\epsilon_{\Omega}\|^{2})

Proof.

Let

\begin{split}C_{(W,\Omega)}&=\sum_{n=0}^{L}I_{W}^{*}A^{n}S_{\Omega}A^{n}I_{W}\\
&=I_{W}^{*}(\sum_{n=0}^{L}A^{n}S_{\Omega}A^{n})I_{W}\\
&=I_{W}^{*}C_{\Omega}I_{W}.\end{split}

(6)

Thus, if
0<\rho_{1}\leq...\leq\rho_{m}
are the eigenvalues of

, and

We end this section with an explicit matrix form for

and
C_{(W,\Omega)}
. Let

\displaystyle C_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}A^{n}S_{\Omega}A^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}BD^{n}B^{*}S_{\Omega}BD^{n}B^{*}

\displaystyle=U\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}\sum_{v\in\Omega}D^{n}B^{*}S_{\{v\}}BD^{n}\bigg)B^{*}.

Writing

is

(\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})^{n}b_{vi}\overline{b_{vj}}

Thus, the

B^{*}C_{\Omega}B
is

\begin{cases}\frac{1-(\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j})^{L}}{1-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}\sum_{v\in\Omega}b_{vi}\overline{b_{vj}},&\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}\neq 1;\\
L\sum_{v\in\Omega}b_{vi}\overline{b_{vj}},&\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}=1.\end{cases}

(7)

Note that
B^{*}C_{\Omega}B
is the matrix form of
C_{\Omega}
with respect to the basis of eigenvectors of
A
. If
W=PW_{\lambda_{k}}
has dimension
m
, then (
6
) shows that
C_{(W,\Omega)}=C_{\Omega}|_{W}
. Therefore, the matrix form of
C_{(W,\Omega)}
with respect to the basis of eigenvectors spanning
W
is the principal
m\times m
submatrix of
B^{*}C_{\Omega}B
.

In practice, we are often concerned with signals which decay in magnitude over time. This means the norm of the graph operator
A
has norm less than 1. To model such evolution processes, we will assume that
\sigma(A)\subset(0,1)
in all numerical experiments in the next section. Furthermore, it was shown in
[
8
]
that, in such a scenario, MSE
(\Omega)
converges for all subsets
\Omega\subset V
as
L\to\infty
. For all numerical considerations, we will assume to take infinite samples in time, in which case the formula for
B^{*}C_{\Omega}B
becomes:

\frac{1}{1-\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}\sum_{v\in\Omega}b_{vi}\overline{b_{vj}}

(8)

3.2
Numerical Algorithms

The optimization problem (6) may not be feasible to solve using brute-force methods when the number of vertices in the graph is
20
or more. In this section, we propose several numerical algorithms for obtaining approximate solutions to (
5
). We recast the optimization problem
(
5
)
as a binary optimization problem as follows.

Recall that
W=PW_{k}
and note that

C_{(W,\Omega)}=\sum_{v\in\Omega}C_{(W,\{v\})}.

Given a vector
x=(x_{v})_{v\in V}\in\ell^{1}(V)
, we use the notation

C_{W}(x)=\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}C_{(W,\{v\})}.

(9)

With this notation, problem (
5
) becomes equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle tr(C_{W}(x)^{-1})

(10)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\quad x_{v}\in\{0,1\},\quad v\in V.

In the following, we propose several numerical algorithms for solving the above problem (
5
). We begin with a fast greedy worst-out best-in algorithm, whose performance depends heavily on its initialization. We then explore several efficient algorithms based on relaxing the binary constraints. It was first proposed in
[
25
]
to simply relax the binary constraints to convex constraints, and round the output to the nearest binary vector. That is, to solve the optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle tr(C_{W}(x)^{-1})

(11)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\quad x_{v}\in[0,1],\quad v\in V.

We will show heuristically that this method does not effectively minimize the objective in the case of dynamical sampling on graphs. For this reason, we will propose modifications to this method which penalize the objective function to promote a binary solution. Heuristically, we show that these algorithms produce a good approximation of the solution. Using this approximation as an initialization for the greedy algorithm typically produces the optimal solution.

3.2.1
Greedy Algorithm

We begin with the algorithm based on the greedy selection of the graph nodes. In the pseudo-code given below, we use
e_{v}\in\ell^{1}(V)
to denote the
v
-th standard basis vector. Starting with a randomly selected binary vector
x_{0}
, which describes the initial selection of
s
nodes, the algorithm computes which node will lead to the smallest increase of the objective function when it is thrown out from the selection. In the next step, the algorithms finds a node to add back to the selection which will decrease the objective function as much as possible. In case of multiple minima in either of the two steps the algorithm will pick the smallest numbered node. The out-in procedure is repeated as long as the objective function improves.

with

.

,

with

Unsurprisingly, the accuracy of the approximation to the solution of (
10
) provided by the greedy method depends heavily on the initialization procedure. In what follows, we present algorithms that provide an initialization that significantly outperforms random selection. In fact, in many cases, the vector obtained from this initialization will itself be a solution of (
10
).

3.2.2
Norm Penalty

The algorithm of this section is based on the classical idea of relaxing the discrete feasible set of the binary program (
10
) to its convex hull and adding a penalty term that encourages binary solutions.

Let
\lambda\geq 0
be a parameter chosen by the user.
The problem (
10
)
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})+\lambda(\|x\|_{1}-\|x\|_{2}^{2})

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in\{0,1\},\;\;v\in V(G),

since
\|x\|_{1}=\|x\|_{2}^{2}
for binary vectors
x\in\ell^{1}(V)
. Relaxing the binary constraints yields

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(x)={\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})+\lambda(\|x\|_{1}-\|x\|_{2}^{2})

(13)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in[0,1],\;\;v\in V(G),

which is an example of a DC-program (“DC” stands for a difference of convex). DC-programming is a well-developed area of non-convex optimization
[
27
]
. Such programs are usually solved via a DC algorithm (DCA).
Given that the function
G_{\lambda}
is infinitely differentiable (on its domain), we chose to use the concave-convex procedure (CCCP
[
40
]
) as the version of DCA we apply. Specifically, this iterative process updates as:

x_{n+1}=\argmin[{\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})+\lambda(s-2\langle x,x_{n}\rangle)]=\argmin[{\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})-2\lambda\langle x,x_{n}\rangle].

Note that this process will, generally, not converge to a binary solution. For this reason, we round the output to the closest binary vector which satisfies the constraints.

Remark 2
.

We could also use a multiplicative penalty, which does not depend on a choice of parameter:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle G_{1}(x)={\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})\cdot\frac{\|x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}

(14)

\displaystyle\emph{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in[0,1],\;\;v\in V(G).

Taking the reciprocal of the objective, we have the equivalent optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle G_{2}(x)=\frac{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}{\|x\|_{1}\cdot{\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})}

(15)

\displaystyle\emph{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in[0,1],\;\;v\in V(G).

This is an example of a convex-convex quadratic fractional program. Using the methods discussed in
[
26
]
, we can suboptimally solve (
15
) via the iterative process:

x_{n+1}=\argmin[{\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})-2\lambda_{n}\langle x,x_{n}\rangle],

where
\lambda_{n}=\frac{F(x_{n})}{\|x_{n}\|_{2}^{2}}
,
F(x)={\mathrm{tr}}(C(x)^{-1}).
We note that the implementation of this algorithm is essentially the same as the method used to solve the DC-program (
13
).

Remark 3
.

Another interesting penalty uses the same objective function proposed in Remark
2
, but constrains the 2-norm of the input vector instead of the 1-norm. More precisely:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{2}(V)}

\displaystyle H_{2}(x)={\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})\cdot\frac{\|x\|_{1}}{\|x\|_{2}^{2}}

(16)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{2}^{2}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in[0,1],\;\;v\in V(G).

To obtain a feasible solution of (
10
), one may simply round the
s
largest coordinates of the output to 1 and set the rest to 0. The output of this algorithm is usually sparse, and if it has less than
s
nonzero coordinates, one may use greedy selection
[
15
]
to select for the remaining vertices. We have observed this to be an efficient algorithm when the objective is minimized using the standard SciPy optimizer. However, this objective function is nonconvex, so it is unclear why this method is sensible. For this reason, we have chosen to omit it from the numerical experiments.

3.2.3
Exponential Penalty

Another penalty method we propose is the use of exponential penalty terms. Specifically, we choose a small number
\delta>0
and solve

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\ell^{1}(V)}

\displaystyle G_{\delta}(x)={\mathrm{tr}}(C_{W}(x)^{-1})+\sum_{v\in V(G)}e^{-x_{v}/\delta}

(17)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\|x\|_{1}=s,\;\;x_{v}\in[0,1],\;\;v\in V(G).

This method has the advantage of being a single convex programming problem instead of an iterative procedure. Typically, this method does not produce a sparse solution. Nevertheless, the rounded solution almost always outperforms the unpenalized convex relaxation.

3.3
Numerical experiments

In this section we conduct a series of numerical tests which demonstrate the efficiency of the algorithms proposed in the previous sections. In the first test, we demonstrate that the performance of the algorithms, relative to one another, is stable under different distributions of eigenvalues of the graph operator
A
, and different types of graphs. This will justify the choice to use uniform random eigenvalues and random connected graphs for the remainder of the experiments. The next tests demonstrate the accuracy of each algorithm, comparing them to the convex relaxation proposed in
[
25
]
and greedily minimizing the trace inverse
[
15
]
. We also test the accuracy of the Worst-Out Best-In (Wo-Bi) greedy algorithm initialized with the output of each algorithm.

3.3.1
Consistency across various graph types and eigenvalue distributions

To show the consistency of the performance of the proposed algorithms under the type of graph and distribution of eigenvalues, we conduct a numerical test as follows. We choose four probability distributions: uniform distribution
\text{U}(0,1)
, triangular distribution
\text{Triangular}(0,\frac{1}{2},1)

The eigenvectors of the graph operator will correspond to the eigenvectors of the Laplacian of a random graph, a clustered graph, a path graph, and a graph with a circulant adjacency matrix. All graphs in this test have 45 nodes, the number of nodes to select is fixed to
12
, and the dimension of the Paley-Wiener space is fixed to
10
. By a clustered graph, we mean two complete graphs, one of size 38 and another of size 7, each with two random edges removed, connected by two edges (see Figure

).
