Modeling with quantities in calculus and physics: A conceptual framework of the fundamental theorem
Suzanne White Brahmia, Patrick Thompson

Abstract
There is a substantial curricular overlap between calculus and physics, yet introductory physics students often struggle to connect the two. We introduce a conceptual framework for the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) to help unify learning across both disciplines. We propose a consistent approach to teaching definite integrals, including shared vocabulary and symbolism, to help students recognize how concepts like change, rate, and accumulation show up in both calculus and physics. We argue that the typical interpretation of the FTC in calculus, focusing on antiderivatives in closed form, doesn’t align well with how physicists use these concepts. We advocate for an additional focus on Riemann sums and the underlying ideas of change, rate, products, and accumulation, which are fundamental in both fields. This approach can help students build a deeper, more integrated understanding of both mathematics and physics quantity. By aligning learning objectives across the disciplines, we argue that students can develop a stronger understanding of foundational mathematical principles.

Introduction

Physics is the science of change, quantified by an abundance of different physical quantities. Calculus describes how they are related to each other, and creates a structure for new ones to emerge. The two disciplines are inseparable. Physics and engineering majors are required to complete calculus and calculus-based introductory physics courses in their first year of study – ideally preparing them to use calculus in physically significant contexts. Nearly all STEM majors will take both calculus and physics as part of their curriculum.

Nonetheless, students perceive a gap between doing mathematics and reasoning with mathematics in physics, which limits the potential quantitative reasoning that they tap into when taking a physics course.
Taylor \BBA Loverude \APACyear2023
conducted a study of students’ conceptual understanding of the mathematics they use in the context of a physics course. One of their research subjects explained, “I have math and physics on different days, so I forget about math when I go to physics, I forget about physics when I go to math.”

Nearly every quantity in physics has the potential to vary. Yet, the number of functions describing their variation is limited to a few common functions. While there are over 100 new physical quantities introduced in a typical calculus-based introductory physics sequence, many of the quantities have the same covariational relationship to one and other. Being equipped to recognize these patterns would allow students to rely on their calculus reasoning as they learn physics.

This paper argues for an agreed-upon objective for calculus and physics courses that students understand
why they do what they do
when they invoke calculus, as well as being competent at executing its procedures. Using the language of
Gray \BBA Tall \APACyear1994
in the context definite integrals, a
proceptual
understanding of symbolic procedures should evoke quantitative meanings, just as quantitative meanings should evoke appropriate symbolic procedures. We provide an instructional framework that centers on the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) to help build a common experience for students in both physics and mathematics. We propose a common approach to definite integrals, including vocabulary and symbolizing, that is central to the FTC and its many appearances in physics contexts.

Conceptualizing physical quantities involves making meaning of its units and symbolizing its relationship to other quantities. Calculus can help students develop an understanding of important mathematical quantities, both in the abstract and in contexts they can imagine or visualize, and this knowledge will be valuable in their physics courses. The physical world creates a
need

Harel \APACyear2008
necessity principle – students must have an intellectual need for a topic to be able to learn it. This paper provides opportunity for calculus courses to take up several physics quantities in a way that is coherent with student learning of the FTC. We believe our framework will help facilitate this practice.

There are two ways we can consider the FTC — in the mathematical context of a standard calculus course and in its quantitative significance.
. A productive understanding of the FTC for physics is, in effect, similar to Newton’s ideas of change and variation—considering all quantities as flowing or having flowed. When quantities flow, they have a rate of change with respect to some other quantity. This rate of change is the rate at which the quantity accumulates.
In our characterization, a productive understanding of situations as embodying the FTC is based in these ways of seeing the world.

•

If you understand a quantity as varying, it occurs to you immediately that any value is an amount of accumulation.

•

If you understand two quantities as varying in relation to each other, it occurs to you immediately that each quantity’s value varies at some rate of change with respect to the other.

•

If you understand two quantities’ values as varying at some rate of change with respect to each other, it occurs to you immediately that their values accumulate with respect to each other.

Understanding quantity also involves effectively using mathematical symbols to represent quantity and their relationships to each other. Letters are used to represents variable and other types of quantity.
P. Thompson \BBA Carlson \APACyear2017
gave this clarification of parameters and variables: Some quantities’ values are taken as fixed in a situation, but with the understanding they can vary from situation to situation. Symbols standing for such quantities’ values are called

Quantities, change in quantities, rates of change and accumulation are at the center of every story that physics has to tell, through the countless models describing the relationships between physical quantities. Students are required to take calculus so that they are ready to understand these models at a profound mathematical level. Yet, the simplicity, and universality of the FTC eludes many students as they embark on learning physics. We argue that recognizing accumulation functions as antiderivatives is both better aligned with productive ways of physics thinking and more general than the meaning of anti-derivatives as functions defined in closed form.

Physics instruction involves additional mathematical cognitive load in symbolizing, and reconciling students’ prior experience with quantities uniquely as scalars, while many physics quantities are vectors. Instructors rely on students having a proceptual understanding of rate, change and accumulation and their relationship through the FTC, so that they are prepared to learn these new layers of mathematics in physics.

The time necessary to help prime students’ knowledge about definite integrals in order to use them in physics contexts has been shown to be so time-intensive, that many calculus-based physics courses avoid expecting students to do any calculus. Physics textbooks avoid expecting students to find products of varying quantities because it would require calculus tools, and most students in a calculus-based physics course don’t have facility with the ”why” of calculus
(
Loverude \BOthers. \APACyear2025
)
. Many quantities that students work with in physics are approximated as being constant or having a constant rate of change so that they can be multiplied together arithmetically to create new quantities – simplified to keep the physics story line moving forward.

Von Korff \BBA Rebello \APACyear2012
conducted an experiment with a set of physics activities informed by mathematics education research
(
Zandieh \APACyear2000
)
. They worked with a single student, providing essentially private instruction to help her understand the definite integral in the context of mechanics, assessing and documenting her progress along the way. The researchers spent 14 contact hours with the student, and were able to see some hopeful progress in her understanding of the meaning of simple function definite integrals in several physics contexts.

Fourteen hours of one-on-one interaction is a prohibitive model in any instructional context, so theirs is not a viable solution for scaling up in physics, but their careful research findings provide insight into where both calculus and physics can start. Many of those hours were spent helping the student develop ideas (e.g. rate, change, rate-of-change, summation of products) that could have readily been taken up in her prerequisite calculus instruction, but weren’t. The vision of ways to weave several hours of instruction into the physics curriculum to help bridge the enriched calculus experience of students is viable and is on the horizon. This research provides evidence that a coordinated effort is possible, and viable.

Avoiding student engagement in calculus while learning physics shortchanges everyone– especially the students. The disciplinary discontinuity for students between physics and mathematics can be smoothed through collective effort in both calculus and physics instruction. We hope this paper is a step toward bridging the gap.

In the next section we explore background research on knowledge construction to help frame the changes we’re advocating. We summarize evidence of resources students develop in calculus that can be taken up in physics contexts, as well as difficulties they encounter along the way. In Section III we propose a conceptual framework of physics and the FTC as a blended entity that demonstrate the various ways in which the FTC carries a profound knowledge structure in physics. This model demonstrates a need for some common language and ideas in calculus that can be reduce barriers for these students. Next we support the framework and recommended practices with evidence fro both mathematics and physics education research. Finally, we share implications for instruction describing specific ways in which calculus instruction could change for the benefit of its students who go on to take physics courses.

Background

2.1
Quantification and quantities

White Brahmia \APACyear2019
argues that quantification is at the root of modeling on physics, emphasizing the importance of quantity and its rate of change.
P. Thompson \APACyear2011
argues that quantification is at the root of all quantitative reasoning. Many physics quantities are vector quantities, and signed scalars, presenting an additional challenge for new learners. Many quantities emerge from multiplying and dividing other quantities (e.g., momentum = mass x velocity, density=mass/volume). Procedurally, the arithmetic involved in creating new quantities is not a challenge for most students, however understanding why the arithmetic makes sense can pose a significant challenge
(
P. Thompson \APACyear2011
)
. The operations of calculus are built on conceptualizing the operations multiplication, division, addition and subtraction, with quantity, beyond simply procedural competency.

Yet, students’ understandings of physical quantities and their quantification is given little to no attention in typical mathematical calculus courses. Quantities such as force, work, pressure, and density are typically stated in textbooks along with a formula to produce values for them, such
F=ma
to calculate a force,
p=mv
to calculate momentum,
v=d/t
to calculate a velocity,
K=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}
to calculate kinetic energy, or
W=Fd
to calculate work. The treatment of quantities quickly jumps to contexts that include a variable force, variable density, variable velocity, or variable mass—which is quickly taken as occasions to use calculus to calculate definite integrals (exact values approximated by a sum).

We are not implying that calculus instructors should teach physics in their courses. Instead, we recommend that more time be given to helping students conceptualize quantities in a way that their quantification is natural. Why is momentum quantified as mass times velocity? Why is speed quantified as distance divided by time? Proportional reasoning is at the heart of quantification. For example, consider what it would take to stop an object in motion that is coming at you, i.e. how do you get rid of its momentum? Which quantities matter? How heavy it is, and how fast its moving – each quantity will affect how much momentum it has. So the momentum must combine mass and speed.
k
times the velocity means
k
times the momentum, similar reasoning applies for mass, and the two proportional relationships operate simultaneously. In addition, you need to oppose its direction of motion in order to stop it, so direction matters as well. That considered, it makes sense that momentum is a vector quantity, and the product of mass and velocity.

In addition to helping students understand quantities’ quantification, we advocate attention to helping students develop productive imagery for how physical quantities are related. How is kinetic energy related to work? How is force related to momentum? How is area related to pressure? How is acceleration related to velocity? Attention to these matters need not be the focus of a lesson, but attention to them during the buildup to physical applications of calculus can be helpful for students to understand contexts in a way that the physical relationships point to rates of change or accumulations of changes. In a study involving algebra students,
Ellis \APACyear2007
compared two experimental groups, one that relied on emergent ratio quantities for reasoning, and the other that relied on numeric patterns and procedures. She observed that “the generalizations connected to reasoning with emergent-ratio quantities proved more productive for students in terms of developing correct extensions, creating correct global conclusions about the nature of linearity, and producing appropriate justifications.” In a study by
Moore \BOthers. \APACyear2009
, the authors found that attending to precalculus students’ construction of quantitative relationships in contexts promotes successful mathematical modeling of the situation.

In the context of calculus with quantity,
Bajracharya \BOthers. \APACyear2023
interviewed math majors as part of a study to uncover how they made sense of a negative definite integral. They observed that invoking a physics example of a stretched spring helped catalyze sense making about
dx
, because it represented a displacement. Although the physical context helped math majors conceptualize the accumulation, there was a perceived departure from the pure math world to make meaning, as articulated by one interviewee, “when you think about just, like, the pure math problems, that’s all you really think about — just the fact that
dx
is just telling you … what variable to use (in the integral) … but … here, it represents, it represents something…” (a small change in a quantity). In physics, every variable represents something physical, we’d like students to imagine the potential of
x
and
y
in calculus to represent a whole variety of quantities, even when they’re not prompted.

To retain a focus on quantification that is essential in students’ physics courses, we advocate a focus of calculus teaching that elevates Riemann-sum reasoning (summing up products of two quantities – a rate of change and an infinitesimal change) and de-emphasizes a focus on finding antiderivatives to solve a problem. As we noted in the introduction, antiderivatives achieved their prominence historically because they enabled physicists and mathematicians to hand-calculate definite integrals. Today this is a nicety, but computing devices today allow acceptable approximate solutions expressed as summations.
Wagner \APACyear2018
provides evidence for the cognitive dissonance students experience based on their educational experiences focused on the importance of the anti-derivative in solving definite integrals. He contends that Riemann sum- based reasoning doesn’t align with solution processes using anti-derivatives. The use of computing devices for calculating Riemann sums can produce acceptable approximations to definite integrals.

2.2
Symbolic forms and symbolic blending frameworks

Contrary to popular “separate world” models in mathematics education, in which mathematics and the rest of the world occupy separate mental spaces
(
Blum \BBA Leiß \APACyear2007
)
, mathematics education researchers studying student problem-solving report that students engage in a continuous contextual validation of their mathematization
(
Czocher \APACyear2016
;
Borromeo Ferri \APACyear2007
)
. These findings are consistent with other research that shows expert physics modeling as a tight blend of physics and mathematical worlds
(
Zimmerman \BOthers. \APACyear2024
)
, and that physics majors reason productively when they blend these worlds
(
Schermerhorn \BBA Thompson \APACyear2023
;
Van den Eynde \APACyear2021
)
. Even introductory physics students are more efficient when they blend these worlds
(
Kuo \BOthers. \APACyear2013
)
. The interplay between quantities and mathematics is inseparable in physics. We approach this work treating the mathematics that is used in physics as a conceptual blend of physics quantities and mathematical objects. The conceptual blending framework is a theory of cognition developed by Fauconnier and Turner in which elements from distinct scenarios are “blended” in a subconscious process
(
Fauconnier \BBA Turner \APACyear2002
)
.

We consider quantities and mathematical objects to be inseparable, nonetheless they are made up of small pieces of blended knowledge. Cognitive resources are the fine-grained pieces of knowledge that people use to create a thought, and mathematics with quantities is the grain size of thought in physics. Resources are knowledge structures a person draws upon to understand and solve physics problems, including elements like declarative, experiential and procedural knowledge, spatial reasoning, visual imagery etc. that are needed to effectively engage with the problem. The resources students combine when reasoning with definite integrals in physics contexts are not the techniques for solving an integral but consist of smaller components like concepts of rate, change, derivatives, summations, or differentials. Arguably, knowledge-in-pieces is consistent with the Riemann sum-based reasoning, and at best neutral regarding an anti-derivative representation.

According to coordination class theory of learning, resources are organized and reorganized over time; as students move through their course curricula, they develop coordinated sets of resources that represents repeated patterns in reasoning.
Sherin \APACyear2001
observed third semester calculus-based physics students collaboratively solving unfamiliar problems, and developed a framework to categorize a
symbolic forms
, which are small schemata of coordinated resources that emerge from students’ procedural and experiential knowledge with quantities and their representations.

Dorko \BBA Speer \APACyear2015
applied Sherin’s framework to analyze how calculus students reason about area and volume. They developed a “measurement” symbolic form that combines numeric value and unit as one. The authors observed that students who wrote correct units could explain dimensions of planar figures and solids, and connect this knowledge to the shapes’ units. In contrast, students who struggled with units also struggled with dimensionality.
White Brahmia \APACyear2019
extended the work of Dorko and Speer and developed a“quantity” symbolic form central to physics reasoning which establishes sign (
+
or
-
) as a feature of physics quantity, in addition to numeric value and unit because of the varied and essential information the sign carries about the physical quantity
(
White Brahmia \BOthers. \APACyear2020
)
.

One of the Sherin’s forms that is particularly relevant in the context of this paper is the“parts-of-a-whole” form, where a whole quantity is made up of several smaller, additive parts, typically indicated by multiple terms added together using plus signs (+).
Meredith \BBA Marrongelle \APACyear2008
investigated how students apply integration in solving electrostatics problems in introductory calculus-based physics, analyzed through Sherin’s symbolic forms framework. They found that students have many strong purely mathematical resources but often struggle to apply them in physics contexts. One set of resources that were productive involved Riemann sums; the researchers characterized students notions of summing up small contributions as Sherin’s ”parts-of-a-whole” form, and observed students used it frequently and productively to guide their work solving definite integrals in this context.

Jones \APACyear2013
examined symbolic forms related to integration in calculus by investigating how experienced calculus students understand and conceptualize the integral. Jones documented the importance of adding up pieces through the lens of the symbolic forms framework. The author adds his voice to other researchers affirming the utility of an adding up pieces approach for these caclulus students, and that emphasizing the concept of accumulation and the ”adding up pieces” interpretation could enhance students’ ability to apply integrals in diverse situations.
Oehrtman \BBA Simmons \APACyear2023
developed an emergent model of how introductory calculus students construct and interpret definite integrals to model physical quantities. The authors emphasize the importance of quantitative reasoning and symbolic forms in students’ understanding of definite integrals.

Many researchers in physics education agree that mathematics and physics are a conceptual blend, based on Fauconnier and Turner’s conceptual blending framework
(
Bing \BBA Redish \APACyear2009
;
White Brahmia \BOthers. \APACyear2021
)
.
Schermerhorn \BBA Thompson \APACyear2023
introduce a ”symbolic blending” theoretical framework, which combines Sherin’s symbolic forms and Fauconnier and Turner’s conceptual blending frameworks in physics. The symbolic blending model combines the mathematical structure of equations (symbolic forms) with the contextual understanding of physics concepts (conceptual blending). The authors argue for the benefits of a symbolic blending model for disentangling mathematical justification from contextual knowledge in physics, even though students and experts are holding both in mind at all times. Symbolic blending facilitates envisioning a framework in which student resources can be fostered in both calculus and physics courses, with each retaining their own disciplinary learning objectives.

Even though mathematics and physics are inseparable, mathematics courses can help to strengthen student understanding of mathematics quantities. That strengthening must happen in the contexts of physical quantities; we propose several in the Section

3
An FTC framing of physics modeling

Within the first weeks of the physics course that is typically taken concurrently with the integral calculus course where the FTC is first mentioned, physics students are expected to learn new abstract physical quantities through definite integrals. The integrals relate electric force and field to their sources in an intricate story of the interplay between these vector quantities. It is assumed that they immediately recognize that they are summing up products and quantifying the accumulation.

For example, Gauss’s law (see Fig.
3
), combines mathematical quantities, symbolizing and multiplicative structures involving both vector and scalar physical quantities, as well as a vector-valued differential. It equates the electric flux (the accumulation on the left-hand side of the equation) to the total amount of electric charge,
Q_{enc}
, which is enclosed by the imaginary shape. The arrows represent the vector values of the electric field and area differential at several points on the surface. The two sides of the equation are made equal by the inclusion of a physical constant,
\varepsilon_{o}
.

Gauss’s law demonstrates that a solid understanding of the integral as a sum of small products can help students begin understanding what is being said here, despite the heavy symbolizing. If students can rely on mathematics to help guide them here, they can immediately see the integrand is tiny bits of something that result from the dot product of an electric field vector and a a small area vector. The integral sums up the small bits to find the total flux. All of that reasoning can take place effortlessly without needing to think deeply about flux or electric field, freeing up their minds to focus on the physics notion that the net flux is proportional to the charge enclosed.

Singh reports that in practice, students tend to memorize the integrals for common charge distributions so they can pass the exams and bypass any meaning-making at all through the calculus. We see this all-too-common behavior in physics as a missed opportunity for a rich understanding.

Developing the ability to decode an equation that relates quantities, products, and its accumulation in this manner is challenging, and many students never make sense beyond picking a correct equation. For physicists, equations tell stories, but students don’t always recognize these stories even when they have had success in prerequisite mathematics courses
(
Kuo \BOthers. \APACyear2013
;
White Brahmia \BOthers. \APACyear2021
)
. Strategic foreshadowing of calculus concepts that play foundational roles in physics models and other STEM models that emerge from them can help STEM majors reason with the mathematics they have learned how to do in their calculus and precalculus courses.

Most students have never encountered vector quantities outside of physics. Most physics instructors assume that because students are taking integral calculus, they’ve mastered the basics of vector quantities, their rates of change, and their accumulated change in their math courses. The anticipated novel concept is that the differential is a vector-valued quantity that has a direction perpendicular to the surface. Learning that fact in physics may feel somewhat random if you don’t already perceive the differential as a quantity that represents a small amount of something.

In this section we describe connections that can be made in the context of the fundamental theorem of calculus (FTC), that can better equip students to conceptualize the relationships between rates of change and other quantities that are central to physics models. Gauss’s law is far out of scope as a context for a calculus course. We present here examples that are within the zone of proximal development for students and instructors in a calculus course, through the lens of symbolic blending.

A first course in calculus-based introductory physics typically spans kinematics, Newton’s laws and the conservation of energy and momentum. Most courses start by developing the ideas foundational to the
kinematics equations
– position and velocity equations as a function of time for constant acceleration motion. Our framework starts there as well.

3.1
Kinematic Equations

The first day of physics typically begins with a description of motion in one dimension at a steady speed. The concept of a time rate of change first appears in physics as:

x(t)=x_{o}+v_{o}t

(3.1.1)

The quantity
acceleration
,
a
, is soon introduced as the time rate of change of velocity. By analogy, a kinematics equation for constant acceleration motion is introduced.

v(t)=v_{o}+at

(3.1.2)

A non-zero
change
in the velocity vector,
\Delta\textbf{v}=\textbf{v}_{f}-\textbf{v}_{o}
, indicates that the system is accelerating and therefore, by Newton’s 2nd law, there is an unbalanced force acting on it.
\Delta\textbf{v}
is one of the most important quantities that guides thinking in introductory physics. Note that finding the difference between the initial and final values of the velocity is one way of determining its change, but the FTC provides another way using rate, multiplication and sum to determine an
accumulation
.

f(b)-f(a)=\int_{a}^{b}{f^{\prime}(x)dx}

(2.3.1)

Summing up the rate (the acceleration) multiplied by each (infinitesimal) time interval results in an
accumulation
that is exactly equal to
\Delta\textbf{v}
. In the case of constant acceleration, the integral is more machinery than is necessary. But the act of setting up this mathematical machinery in the context of learning about the FTC can set students up to see how rates – even non-constant ones– are used to calculate change. Quantifying rates of change and using them to help determine change is a recurrent pattern in physics (and other STEM) modeling that can be introduced through the use of quantities in calculus instruction.

An example of a changing rate students will encounter in their first week of physics is the constantly changing velocity described by the kinematic equations. The accumulated effect of the constantly changing speed is a displacement,
\Delta\textbf{x}
that can be found the FTC, allowing for a non-constant velocity,

x(t)=x_{o}+\int v(t)dt

\Delta x=\int v(t)dt

3.2
Conservation Laws

The conservation laws introduced in mechanics, describing change in the total mechanical energy and the total momentum of a system, provide guidance and structure to all of physics. The FTC provides a template for a symbolic representation of these conservation laws.

The total energy of a system is changed when an object from outside the system exerts a force on the system while it changes position along the line of the force. The change in the system energy is equal to the accumulated effect of a force acting over a distance. This accumulation is so important, it is a named quantity–
work
. Here, the force both causes and quantifies the rate at which work is done as the position varies.

The total momentum of a system is changed when an object from outside the system exerts a force on the system over
a time interval
. The change in the total momentum is equal to the accumulated effect of a force acting over a time interval. This accumulation is also so important, it is a named quantity–
impulse
. Here, the force both causes and quantifies the rate at which the momentum changes as time varies.

4
FTC physics framing: Research foundations

The symbolic blending of the FTC quantities in physics contexts presents a significant challenge each time students encounter new topics in physics. At the heart of this difficulty is sensemaking with quantities and operations, and their meaning both in a mathematical sense and a physical one. We’ll provide evidence here of obstacles to making mathematical meaning in the context of physically realistic contexts.
Specifically, the evidence will reveal obstacles:

•

conflating exactness and correctness – emphasis on antiderivative over a Riemann sum interpretation of the FTC

•

conceptualizing differentials as a quantity going to zero

•

making meaning of quantities and their symbols in the FTC

4.1
Summing up parts

As discussed in section II, there is a strong evidentiary base that the part-of-a-whole symbolic form, in the structure of Riemann sums, manifests itself in ways that can be both productive and accessible for students when reasoning about integration in research settings.

The concept of adding up small pieces is in students’ zone of proximal development. Yet, a large number of students complete their study of calculus without being able to interpret the definite integral as a sum, conflating the techniques they’ve learned to do with the meaning for what they are doing.
Jones \APACyear2015
argue this is possibly due to the overemphasis on area-under-a-curve and anti-derivative techniques and underemphasis on Riemann sum-based reasoning in their instruction.

4.2
Infinitesimals and differentials

Another substantial difficulty emerges with quantities that involve
dx
in the limit that it goes to zero, we refer to as the differential, and just before it gets to zero we refer to it as an infinitesimal. The differential is involved in all quantities being summed in the FTC. Here we’ll first discuss a framework within mathematics for rate to better understand difficulties with this central quantity.

Zandieh \APACyear2000
model of students understanding of derivatives is helpful in framing barriers to transfer from calculus to physics.The model uses the metaphor of “layers” and describes three hierarchical layers. The most fundamental the ratio layer, is the derivative as a ratio of two finite quantities. The next is limit layer, the student should be able to imagine the denominator of the ratio approaching zero. and the highest is the function layer at which the student conceives of the derivative of a function as a function itself. Understanding, beyond procedure competency, of each layer is essential for attaining understanding of the next.

Layer one is foundational to a Riemann-sum interpretation for the integral. Regarding layer two, there is a ample evidence that students struggle to make meaning of a differential, specifically in the context of it being infinitesimal—not zero, but not different from 0 by any positive real amount.
Oehrtman \APACyear2009
reports on student reasoning in which there is a collapse in dimension, “…corresponding to the independent variable in the limit … going to zero, this dimension was ultimately imagined to vanish.” We note that attributing an amount to a differential has no meaning mathematically, the differential is not a quantity.

Physics can, and does, accommodate an interval that is slightly greater than the zero that is essential in pure mathematics. While students can follow this reasoning, the heavy emphasis on
dx
being a symbolic form pointing to a variable of integration or
dx
being infinitesimally small in their math courses, with no alternative interpretation, leaves them feeling hesitant to engage in this kind of “sloppiness” in physics.
Meredith \BBA Marrongelle \APACyear2008
found that the notion of a limit going to zero can hinder students’ understanding of integrals as sums, especially when they fail to notice that
dx
is never 0 in
\lim\limits_{dx\to 0,\,dx\neq 0}
. They argue that students need guided instruction to reinterpret mathematical concepts in physics contexts, where summation of all pieces is a foundational idea.
Jones \APACyear2013
observed an unproductive resource of adding up the integrand, which supports Meredith and Marrongelle’s observation of confusion that can set in when considering limits of an infinitesimal quantity as it becomes a differential.
Nguyen \BBA Rebello \APACyear2011
asked students to interpret infinitesimal intervals of area,
\vec{dA}
, in a physics context. Even though they productively used a summing up reasoning to interpret the integral, they felt that
\vec{dA}
refers to a changing area (process), rather than a small element of area (quantity). The authors contend that helping students to make a distinction between a process and a quantity may require some instruction targeted at the student’s physical intuition about infinitesimals.
Von Korff \BBA Rebello \APACyear2012
state that, in their approach that has shown promise, “the integral can also be constructed by summing an “infinite”’ number of infinitesimal products, although a traditional calculus framework would not allow this.”

Increasingly, mathematics education researchers argue for the pedagogical value of treating differentials as infinitesimal change in Calculus
(
Ely \BBA Jones \APACyear2023
;
Ely \APACyear2017
;
P.W. Thompson \BBA Dreyfus \APACyear2016
)
Sullivan, 1976;. The concept of a differential has been shown to be perceived by calculus students as directive for the task of calculating an integral, that in math it just tells you what variable you’re integrating. While conceptualizing that change as a small quantity helps students to make meaning of the mathematics that they are doing.

There are arguments, as well, that differentials are pedagogically important to understanding calculus. A model of two covarying quantities that change continuously, rather than as a series of small changes, is more pedagogically beneficial to establishing notions of limits in mathematics. For example,
Castillo-Garsow \BOthers. \APACyear2013
present case studies of several high school students reasoning about variation, and argue from these data that smooth images of change (differentials) are more powerful than chunky images of change in contexts of covariation. They argue that: “Simply put, chunky thinking generates chunky conceptions of variation, whereas smooth thinking generates smooth conceptions of variation, with these conceptions producing different mathematics. Although a smooth conception of variation involves imagining a changing number or magnitude, it also involves attending to all states continuously without privileging any sort of unit value that would form the basis of counting. Therefore a smooth conception of variation does not immediately generate products that could be counted. In contrast, products of a chunky conception of variation are always countable, because no matter how small the chunk, discrete points always exist and can be counted.”

We note here that most work in mathematics on student thinking with quantities has been done in the contexts of independent variables that students experience – time, volume of water flowing through a pipe, etc. Students can readily visualize what appears to them to be a continuous process. But the physical world is not continuous in the limit of the very small, which is the realm where a lot of physics takes place. There are units that do privilege a basis for counting and they never go to zero. The notion of a differential is in disagreement with physics in the small limit. The objectives of physics and of mathematics are quite different. In physics, the mathematics that students encounter models the physical world. The continuous functions are approximations to the world they describe, and not the other way around. The physical world is a chunky place.

Roundy \BOthers. \APACyear2015
conducted an experiment with faculty in mathematics, physics, and engineering. The subjects were asked to measure a specific derivative
\frac{dx}{dF_{x}}
using a device that allowed them to make (and measure) changes in
F_{x}
(an interval
\Delta F_{x}
) and measure the resulting changes in
x
. They report that the physicists and engineers immediately set to task designing a way to measure a derivative by measuring the change in the dependent variable x over intervals of
\Delta F_{x}
and calculating the ratio. The mathematicians spent much of the interview making meaning of the symbols used, and eventually set to work collecting data to find a function that they could differentiate. Through interaction with the interviewer, they did not consider a computed average rate of change to be a derivative, regardless of its precision. The physicists and engineers knew that their computation was an approximation, but they also knew how to ensure that it was a good one. We argue that the mathematicians in this context were conflating exactness with correctness. Figure

4.3
Making meaning of the quantities in the FTC

In a study of calculus student understanding while problem-solving with definite integrals that involve physical quantities (velocity, force, energy and pressure),
ThompsonandSilverman2008;Sealey2014
report that “conceptualizing the product of
f(x)
and
\Delta x
proves to be the most complex part of the problem-solving process, despite the simplicity of the mathematical operations required in this step.” Students struggled to understand how to form the product of two quantities, such as velocity and time, pressure and area, or force and distance, and how this product contributes to the overall calculation as an accumulation. We suspect that students’ difficulties may stem from them not expecting the product to contribute to a quantity that accumulates
(
P.W. Thompson \APACyear1994
)
.

Sealey also notes that students did not struggle with the concept of a sum of elements going to infinity. Instead, it was the limit of the infinitesimal going to zero that was problematic, and not the notion of limits writ large. Sealey included an orienting layer to the her Riemann Integral Framework, which involves students making sense of the variables and quantities given in a problem before, and while, engaging in calculations. She found that students often revisited this layer throughout the problem-solving process, contributing to the growing body of evidence that the blending of physics quantity and the calculus was continual
(
Zimmerman \BOthers. \APACyear2024
;
Czocher \APACyear2016
;
Schermerhorn \BBA Thompson \APACyear2023
)
.

Von Korff \BBA Rebello \APACyear2012
emphasize the time it takes, and the significance, of symbolizing in the context of the FTC. In their experiment they found it necessary to provide direct instruction more than once to help the student understand the meaning of the symbols, at points when the student was ready to make meaning of those symbols. When the calculus symbolizing is combined with the many different letters used to represent both scalar and vector physical quantities, the representations require significant decoding, as was seen earlier with Gauss’s law.

5
Implications for instruction

A first step toward making the connections between calculus and physics explicit to the students is the inclusion of physical quantities in calculus. We include quantities associated with foundational physics only with which students have a kinesthetic sense (see Table
2
), focusing on their connections through calculus. These quantities can be framed through the FTC as change, rates of change and accumulations. A focus on quantities relies on Riemann sums of bits of accumulation made by a rate times a change as a sensemaking device for why we do calculus, not just how we do it.

In conjunction with modifications in calculus instruction, we envision careful, and mathematically correct, discussions in physics around select physical quantities, highlighting how calculus reasoning facilitates thinking about quantities as they change over a given interval, and the physical implications of that change. There is little time devoted in a typical physics course to helping students make these kinds of connections, and there should be.

We’ve presented findings from physics and mathematics education research suggesting that making symbolizing an explicit part of instruction will help students to better understand the quantities and operations they represent. In Table
3
, we provide a structure so that instructors from both disciplines can draw attention to symbolizing, and providing students adequate opportunity to fully comprehend sigma notation and indices, as well as
\Delta
and
d
. In addition to symbols, we’ve included language and explicit reasoning around the quantification of change, rate and accumulation, and their representations in calculus and physics, that can be particularly valuable to students taking both courses. In the remainder of this section we provide more detail for the structure of the the table.

5.1
Change as physical quantity

One of the beauties of careful mathematical formalism is its generalizabilty. Unless the variables are quantities, there is no particular preference for which quantity is the independent variable – designated by
x
– and which is the dependent variable – designated
y
. The rate of change is quantified simply as the change in
y
divided by the change in
x
— which has the quantitative meaning that the change in
y
is some number of times as large as the change in
x
.

The physical world adds a layer of constraint to the calculus it uses in that the
models must be testable
. That testability involves visualizing an experiment in which you manipulate the independent variable by changing its value, and predict the value of the dependent variable based on the function that relates them, and then measure the dependent variable to test the model. In addition, some quantities (e.g. position, time) are much easier to measure than others (e.g. energies). Nearly all of the functions students encounter in their first physics courses are functions either of position or of time.

In the context of actual measurement, the scientist chooses an interval size (
\Delta x
) and measures the change in the dependent quantity (
\Delta y
) over that interval. While both (
\Delta y
) and (
\Delta x
) are considered ”change” in mathematics, they are very different kinds of change. In the context of measurement one is manipulated, and the other is a response – even though they covary. This relationship in experimentation is not unique to physics, so is generalizable across other science and engineering contexts.

We suggest here that referring to the change in the independent variable, (
\Delta x
), as an interval of change, and that the change (
\Delta y
), is what depends on the interval. This framing is better aligned with experimentation and other aspects of physics, which may help catalyze productive blended reasoning.

We propose a structure that focuses on
change
in the dependent variable and an
interval
of the independent variable This is particularly useful in the context of physical quantity measurement, as described above. It is important to emphasize that change as a quantity that is
different
from the quantity itself. Examples include displacement as a change in position, impulse as a change in momentum, work as a change in system energy.

Regarding these quantities in the context of the ACRA framework for conceptual learning of the FTC in calculus, we’d like to advocate for the inclusion of
interval
as a fundamental mathematical quantity as well. It can serve both as a bridge between STEM models and prior calculus instruction, as well as giving more meaning in mathematics courses to the concepts of independent and dependent variables, beyond their symbolic representations and positions on the axes of a graph.

5.2
Rate as physical quantity

A rate of change, seen in mathematics as the change in
y
over the change in
x
, can be thought of as a change in
y
over an interval of change
x
—the change in
y
that occurs as the independent quantity’s value varies through an interval. This difference goes beyond semantics – it is how a physicist envision rate. The concept of unit rate,“a change in the numerator for every unit of the denominator” is helpful here.

In some cases, the models generalize to physically meaningful situations in the ways mathematics allows, with no attribution as to why one quantity or another changes. One could think of a bidirectional causality – a small change in time for an object in motion will result in a small change in position. Or, conversely, a small change in position implies that there must have been a change in time. Position and its rate of change, velocity, and its rate of change, acceleration are names given to rates that help quantify motion, and carry no information about why the quantities change.

The conservation laws are different from the kinematics quantities, even though they are structurally identical mathematically. They all represent models in which the measured change of the independent variable is a
physically caused
by the rate of change in the integral. But for the conservation laws, the change of momentum, and the change in the potential energy are due to the force exerted over a given an interval. The meaning that these quantities carry are central to the conservation laws of physics. By contrast, acceleration is not a cause of a velocity change – just a rate at which it happens. Unlike the kinematic quantities, manipulating energy or momentum intervals and finding the corresponding position or time intervals makes little physical sense.

We propose a structure that focuses on rate as a ratio of a change in the dependent variable over an interval of change in the independent variable, as described above.
It is important to emphasize that rate as quantity, is a different from the quantity itself. Examples include velocity as the time rate of change of position, acceleration as the time rate of change of velocity, and density as the volume rate of change of the mass.

5.3
Accumulation as physical quantity

Visualizing the integral as summing up small bits is valuable in physics. Accumulations connect graphical representations – which are foundational to expert reasoning
(
Zimmerman \BOthers. \APACyear2024
)
– with quantities that carry physical meaning. The accumulation of the product of changing rates over short time intervals is at the foundation of kinematics. Similarly, the area under a force-position graph can be taken to represent an energy change when
\frac{dU}{dx}
is taken to be a rate of change of system mechanical energy with respect to position. The area under the a force-time graph can be taken to represent a momentum change when
\frac{dp}{dt}
is taken as a rate of change of momentum with respect to time. The interplay between these quantities is at the heart of the conservation laws of physics.

The kinematics variables appear in most calculus textbooks, typically initially mentioning units and them leaving them out of the mathematics until all calculation is complete. This practice is pedagogically unsound for physics students, the units are essential to the quantity and facilitate thinking. We emphasize that including units throughout the calculation is valuable in students’ conceptual development of what calculus is doing. In addition, including practice in a calculus course that help students connect just these quantities – force, position, energy change and force, time, momentum change – while getting a sense of what mathematical role each quantity plays as a change, rate, interval or accumulation could help students quite a bit as they grapple with these ideas and their consequences in physics.

5.4
Meeting challenges

5.4.1
Approximating with Riemann sums

In a Riemann sum approach, the onus becomes“what is an acceptable approximation and how can you tell you have one?” In the study of
Roundy \BOthers. \APACyear2015
, how do engineers and physicists know that their approximation is good enough? Why do the mathematicians reject the approximation?

P. Thompson \BOthers. \APACyear2019
took an approach to quantifying accumulation based on Cauchy’s notion of convergence instead of on Weirstrass’ notion of limit. When successive terms in an approximation differ negligibly (according to locally defined tolerance levels), the integral’s approximation is taken to be ”essentially equal to” the desired exact value.
Jones \APACyear2015
examined students’s abilities to comprehend the meaning of definite integrals using area, antiderivative, or accumulation (adding up pieces) meanings of integral. He found that students having an accumulation meaning of integral were more successful at comprehending physics and engineering applications than students holding either antiderivative or area meanings .
Oehrtman \APACyear2008
developed an approximation structure that emphasizes the concept of limit in terms of approximations. For example, students approximate derivatives and instantaneous rates of change by finding average rates of change, and exploring the processes of improving these approximations.

We present as proof-of concept examples of calculus activities that can enrich the calculus syllabus with physics relevant activities in the contexts of definite integrals. Materials are available as part of the Clear Calculus set of lab activities from Oerhtman et al. at Oklahoma State University
(
Oehrtman \APACyear2012
)
, as well at the free online textbook written by Thompson, Ashbrook and Milner
(
P. Thompson \BOthers. \APACyear2019
)
. We anticipate that instructors will want to use these materials, and will develop their own activities as well, to meet the needs of their students.

5.4.2
Quantifying physics and conceptualizing mathematical operations

Quantifying ratio and product physics quantities can be daunting to take on in a mathematics course. Physics Invention Tasks
(
White Brahmia \BOthers. \APACyear2024
)
are designed to engage students in authentic quantification, in preparation for subsequent formal learning. Students use data from contrasting cases to invent ratio or product quantities to characterize a variety of physical systems. Students work through sequences of such tasks to ramp up from everyday contexts to more abstract physics contexts, including velocity, acceleration, work and momentum. These invention sequences have been field tested both at the pre-college level and college levels.

Precalculus: Pathways to Calculus
(
Carlson \BOthers. \APACyear2020
)
includes a textbook, workbook and supplemental materials that facilitate student construction of calculus ideas that are particularly relevant in physics, especially constant rate of change and linear function, and changing rates of change, using covariation. Includes vector quantities, sequence and series representation as approximation. Focusses on less breadth in the variety of functions in favor of building a deeper understanding of the functions themselves using multiple representations and many relevant applications, while students are constructing their knowledge, not as an afterthought.

5.4.3
Finding instructional time and coherence

It could be argued that the chunkiness of the physical world is not a concern worthy of allocating institutional time in a calculus course. One argument in favor of making time, is that the majority of compound functions students work with in mathematics have no relevance outside of mathematics. In a recent survey of an introductory calculus-based physics textbook, the occurrence of different functions in physics models were tallied up, see Fig.

6
Conclusion

We emphasized that quantifying in physics is enough of a cognitive challenge (Sealey’s pre-layer) that it can create a cognitive overload when students have unproductive meanings for the mathematics used to create their symbolic representations in physics.

While procedural competence in calculus is important, physics students see few connections between the calculus they understand and the physics they are asked to learn. Our proposal is not to simply add more into the already overloaded syllabus of a calculus course. We argue that instructional time could be liberated in order to develop understandings of integrals and derivatives in mathematics that actually support their understanding of physical quantities that arise from seeing the world through the lenses of rate of change and accumulation. One avenue would be to narrow the range of functions that students procedurally master in a calculus course. Another would be to give greater emphasis to integrals as accumulations and derivatives as rates of change of accumulations. Indeed, our emphasis on quantitative meanings of the FTC is exactly this: that rate of change and accumulation are two sides of a coin. The standard mathematical treatment of the FTC emphasizes a procedural connection between integrals and derivatives–that you can use closed-form antiderivatives to evaluate definite integrals. Our proposed approach to the FTC emphasizes their quantitative connection–that integrals are accumulations from two quantities varying at (perhaps non-constant) rates of change and that quantities involved in a rate of change are co-accumulating.

Physics involves additional mathematical cognitive load in symbolizing, and reconciling that many of the physics quantities are vector quantities, while students have encountered only scalar quantities in mathematics. It relies on students having both procedural understandings and quantitative understandings of rate, change and accumulation and their relationship through the FTC, so that they can learn these new layers of mathematics in physics. Having a shared learning objective in both disciplines, that students will be able to demonstrate what

called a proceptual understanding of change, rate and accumulation in the context of definite integrals–thinking of symbolic procedures evokes their quantitative meanings and thinking of quantitative meanings evokes symbolic procedures involving them. We believe fostering such learning will enrich students’ experiences in both disciplines
