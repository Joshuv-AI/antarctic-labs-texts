A complete classification of endomorphisms of Kiselman’s semigroup
Luka Andrenšek

Abstract
. Let K n K_{n} denote Kiselman’s semigroup. It was thoroughly studied in Kudryavtseva and Mazorchuk’s paper On Kiselman’s semigroup. The authors in that paper pose a question, whether is it possible to classify all endomorphisms of K n K_{n} (Question 21). In this paper we give a complete classification and provide a boolean matrix semigroup that is isomorphic to End ​ ( K n ) \text{End}(K_{n}) .



1.
Introduction

Kiselman’s semigroup was first introduced in
[
2
]
, having roots in convexity theory. It was later generalized and studied in
[
1
]
. It admits interesting combinatorial properties and is related to some graph dynamical systems
[
3
]
.

In
[
1
]
the authors show that the only automorphism which
K_{n}
admits is the identity and the only antiautomorphism is the unique extension of the map

a_{i}\mapsto a_{n-i+1}
. They leave the question regarding the classification of endomorphisms open and has to date not been conclusively answered.

2.
Main result

In the present paper we show that the monoid
\text{End}(K_{n})
is isomorphic to the following boolean matrix semigroup, equipped with the usual boolean matrix multiplication. We denote this monoid by
D_{n}
.

\displaystyle D_{n}=\{M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\mid\forall i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}.\hskip 2.84526ptj>i\Rightarrow(M_{xj}=1,M_{xi}=0,M_{yj}=0,M_{yi}=1\Rightarrow x>y)\}

Where
M_{kl}
denotes the entry in the matrix
M
in the
k
-th row and
l
-th column.
In plain language, this is the set of boolean
n\times n
matrices which do not admit the following
2\times 2
submatrix (pattern)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}

3.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
be a finite alphabet. We define
K_{n}
by

\displaystyle K_{n}=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\mid a_{i}^{2}=a_{i},\hskip 2.84526pta_{i}a_{j}a_{i}=a_{j}a_{i}a_{j}=a_{j}a_{i},\hskip 2.84526pt1\leq i<j\leq n\rangle.

Let
X\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. If
X=\emptyset
put
e_{X}=e
, the unit element in
K_{n}
. Otherwise, assume
X=\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}\}
, where
i_{1}>i_{2}>\dots>i_{k}
. Then put

e_{X}=a_{i_{1}}a_{i_{2}}\dots a_{i_{k}}
. Now put
E_{n}=\{e_{X}\mid X\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}\}
. In
[
1
]
it is shown that every element of
E_{n}
is an idempotent, they are all distinct, and every idempotent is of the form
e_{X}
, for some
X\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Consequently, the number of idempotents in
K_{n}
is precisely
2^{n}
.

Furthermore, in
[
1
]
the following proposition is proven.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
X,Y\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Then the following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

e_{X}e_{Y}
is an idempotent

(2)

e_{X}e_{Y}=e_{X\cup Y}

(3)

\forall x\in X\setminus Y.\hskip 2.84526pt\forall y\in Y\setminus X.\hskip 2.84526ptx>y

Also define the content map
c:K_{n}\rightarrow 2^{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}
, where
c(a)
is the set of all
i
’s, for which
a_{i}
appears in
a
. In
[
1
]
it is shown that this map is indeed well defined.

Furthermore the following proposition holds.

Proposition 3.2
.

The map
c
is an epimorphism from
K_{n}
to the semigroup
(2^{\{1,2,\dots,n\}},\cup)
.

4.
The braid relation for idempotents

We prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X,Y\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. The following two conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\forall x\in X\setminus Y.\hskip 2.84526pt\forall y\in Y\setminus X.\hskip 2.84526ptx>y

(2)

e_{X}e_{Y}
is an idempotent and
e_{X}e_{Y}e_{X}=e_{Y}e_{X}e_{Y}=e_{X}e_{Y}

Proof.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)
is immediate from (
3.1
).

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
Because of (
3.1
) we only need to show the equalities
e_{X}e_{Y}e_{X}=e_{Y}e_{X}e_{Y}=e_{X}e_{Y}
.

Since
e_{X}e_{Y}
is an idempotent, it is equal to
e_{X\cup Y}
. Notice condition
(3)
from (
3.1
) holds for the pairs of sets
X\cup Y,X
and
Y,X\cup Y
respectively. It follows that

\displaystyle e_{X}e_{Y}e_{X}=(e_{X}e_{Y})e_{X}=e_{X\cup Y}e_{X}=e_{(X\cup Y)\cup X}=e_{X\cup Y}=e_{X}e_{Y}

and furthermore

\displaystyle e_{Y}e_{X}e_{Y}=e_{Y}(e_{X}e_{Y})=e_{Y}e_{X\cup Y}=e_{Y\cup(X\cup Y)}=e_{X\cup Y}=e_{X}e_{Y}

This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Isomorphism to monotone sets

In this section, we will construct an isomorphism from
\text{End}(K_{n})
to a set of sets, satisfying the
monotonicity
condition.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
be a sequence of sets, where
X_{i}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
for
1\leq i\leq n
.
\mathbf{X}
is said to be monotone, if

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in\{1,2,\dots n\}.j>i\Rightarrow\hskip 2.84526pt\forall x\in X_{j}\setminus X_{i}.\hskip 2.84526pt\forall y\in X_{i}\setminus X_{j}.\hskip 2.84526ptx>y

We denote by
M_{n}=\{(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})\mid(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})\hskip 2.84526pt\text{is monotone}\}
.

On the set
M_{n}
we define a binary operation
\ast
in the following way. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{n})
be elements in
M_{n}
.
We put
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\ast\mathbf{Y}
, where

\displaystyle Z_{i}=\bigcup\limits_{j\in Y_{i}}X_{j}

One can easily show that
(M_{n},\ast)
is a monoid, with its unit element being the sequence
(\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{n\})
.

The following theorem holds.

Theorem 5.2
.

The map
\Phi:\operatorname{End}(K_{n})\rightarrow M_{n}
, defined as

\displaystyle\Phi(\varphi)=(c(\varphi(a_{1})),c(\varphi(a_{2})),\dots c(\varphi(a_{n})))

is an isomorphism of monoids
(\operatorname{End}(K_{n}),\circ)
and
(M_{n},\ast)
.

Proof.

First we must prove, that for each
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
,
\Phi(\varphi)
lies in
M_{n}
. Therefore, let
\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
.
Since
\varphi
is a homomorphism,
\varphi(a)
is an idempotent, whenever
a
is an idempotent. Hence,
\varphi(a_{i})
is an idempotent for

1\leq i\leq n
. It follows that there exists a sequence
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
of subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, for which

\displaystyle\varphi(a_{i})=e_{X_{i}}

for
1\leq i\leq n
and thus we have
\Phi(\varphi)=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
. Now notice that
a_{j}a_{i}
is an idempotent, whenever
j>i
, in which case we ofcourse also have
a_{j}a_{i}a_{j}=a_{i}a_{j}a_{i}=a_{j}a_{i}
. Apply
\varphi
to these equalities and again use the fact that
\varphi
must preserve idempotents, it follows

\displaystyle e_{X_{j}}e_{X_{i}}\hskip 2.84526pt

is an idempotent

and

\displaystyle e_{X_{j}}e_{X_{i}}e_{X_{j}}=e_{X_{i}}e_{X_{j}}e_{X_{i}}=e_{X_{j}}e_{X_{i}}

whenever
j>i
. From (
4.1
) it follows that whenever
j>i
, the condition

\displaystyle\forall x\in X_{j}\setminus X_{i}.\hskip 2.84526pt\forall y\in X_{i}\setminus X_{j}.\hskip 2.84526ptx>y

must be satisfied. This implies the monotinicity of
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
and hence
\Phi(\varphi)\in M_{n}
.

The fact that
\Phi
is injective follows easily, since for any endomorphism, its images of the generators are of the form
e_{X_{i}}
for some
X_{i}\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Notice also that an idempotent in
K_{n}
is uniquely defined by its content. And thus if two endomorphisms coincide on the generators, they must be the same.

Now we prove surjectivity. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})\in M_{n}
. Define a map
\psi:\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}\rightarrow K_{n}
by

\displaystyle\psi(a_{i})=e_{X_{i}}

Put
b_{i}
=
\psi(a_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq n
. It is apparent that
b_{i}
are idempotents. Furthermore let
j>i
. Then because of
4.1
and the monotinicity condition imposed on
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
it must follow that

\displaystyle b_{j}b_{i}b_{j}=b_{i}b_{j}b_{i}=b_{j}b_{i}

Thus elements
\{b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{n}\}
follow the defining relations of
K_{n}
, therefore we can uniquely extend
\psi
to an endomorphism of
K_{n}
. By construction it follows
\Phi(\psi)=\mathbf{X}
.

Now we prove that
\Phi
is indeed a homomorphism. Let
\psi,\varphi\in\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
. Let

\psi(a_{j})=e_{Y_{j}}
and
\varphi(a_{j})=e_{X_{j}}
for
1\leq j\leq n
. Also put
e_{X_{i}}=a_{m_{l}}a_{m_{l-1}}\dots a_{m_{1}}
. In particular
X_{i}=\{m_{l},m_{l-1},\dots,m_{1}\}
and
m_{l}>m_{l-1}>\dots>m_{1}
. We compute

\displaystyle(\psi\circ\varphi)(a_{i})

\displaystyle=\psi(e_{X_{i}})

\displaystyle=\psi(a_{m_{l}}a_{m_{l-1}}\dots a_{m_{2}}a_{m_{1}})

\displaystyle=\psi(a_{m_{l}})\psi(a_{m_{l-1}})\dots\psi(a_{m_{2}})\psi(a_{m_{1}})

\displaystyle=e_{Y_{m_{l}}}e_{Y_{m_{l-1}}}\dots e_{Y_{m_{2}}}e_{Y_{m_{1}}}

\displaystyle=e_{\cup_{1\leq k\leq l}Y_{m_{k}}}

The last equality holds because
(\psi\circ\varphi)(a_{i})
is an idempotent and because of (
3.1
). Now if we denote by

(\Phi(\psi\circ\varphi))_{i}
the
i
-th component of the sequence of sets
\Phi(\psi\circ\varphi)
, we get

\displaystyle(\Phi(\psi\circ\phi))_{i}

\displaystyle=c((\psi\circ\varphi)(a_{i}))

\displaystyle=c(e_{\cup_{1\leq k\leq l}Y_{m_{k}}})

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{k=1}^{l}Y_{m_{k}}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{j\in X_{i}}Y_{j}

Since
X_{i}=\{m_{l},m_{l-1},\dots,m_{1}\}
.

On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle(\Phi(\psi)\ast\Phi(\varphi))_{i}

\displaystyle=((c(\psi(a_{1})),c(\psi(a_{2})),\dots c(\psi(a_{n})))

\displaystyle\ast(c(\varphi(a_{1})),c(\varphi(a_{2})),\dots c(\varphi(a_{n}))))_{i}

\displaystyle=((Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{n})\ast(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n}))_{i}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{j\in X_{i}}Y_{j}

Since
i
was chosen arbitrarily, we indeed have

\displaystyle\Phi(\psi\circ\varphi)=\Phi(\psi)\ast\Phi(\varphi)

And since the identity map is the unit of
\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
, it follows

\displaystyle\Phi(\operatorname{id}_{K_{n}})=(\{1\},\{2\},\dots,\{n\})

This concludes the proof.

∎

6.
Isomorphism to a boolean matrix monoid

Definition 6.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
D_{n}
the following set

\displaystyle D_{n}=\{M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}\mid\forall i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}.\hskip 2.84526ptj>i\Rightarrow(M_{xj}=1,M_{xi}=0,M_{yj}=0,M_{yi}=1\Rightarrow x>y)\}

In plain language,
D_{n}
is the set of boolean
n\times n
matrices which do not admit the following
2\times 2
submatrix:

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}

On the set
D_{n}
we define a binary operation
\cdot
as follows. Let
A,B\in D_{n}
,
A=(a_{ij})
and
B=(b_{ij})
. Then
C=A\cdot B
, where

C=(c_{ij})
and

\displaystyle c_{ij}=\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}(a_{ik}\land b_{kj})

One can easily check that
(D_{n},\cdot)
is a monoid and its unit element is the identity matrix.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
(e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n})
be the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Define the map

\chi:2^{\{1,2,\dots,n\}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\chi(X)=\sum_{i\in X}e_{i}

The following theorem holds.

Theorem 6.3
.

The map
\Psi:M_{n}\rightarrow D_{n}
, defined as

\displaystyle\Psi(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})=(\chi(X_{1}),\chi(X_{2}),\dots,\chi(X_{n}))

is an isomorphism of monoids
(M_{n},\ast)
and
(D_{n},\cdot)
.

Proof.

We must first show that
\Psi
indeed maps into
D_{n}
. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})\in M_{n}
. Denote
M=\Psi(\mathbf{X})
. Notice

M_{xi}=1
if and only if
(\chi(X_{i}))_{x}=1
, which is equivalent to
x\in X_{i}
. Now assume

j>i
and
M_{xj}=1,M_{xi}=0,M_{yj}=0,M_{yi}=1
, which is equivalent to
x\in X_{j},x\notin X_{i},y\in X_{i},y\notin X_{j}

, which itself is equivalent to
x\in X_{j}\setminus X_{i}
and
y\in X_{i}\setminus X_{j}
. We apply monotonicity of
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
and get
x>y
. Thus
\Psi(\mathbf{X})\in D_{n}
.

\Psi
is obviously injective.

We now prove surjectivity. Let
M\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
. We put
X_{i}=\{x\mid M_{xi}=1\}
. First we check, that
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n})
is monotone.
Hence let
j>i
and
x\in X_{j}\setminus X_{i}
and
y\in X_{i}\setminus X_{j}
. Then it follows
M_{xj}=1,M_{xi}=0,M_{yi}=1,M_{yj}=0
. Since

M\in\mathcal{D}_{n}
, it must follow
x>y
, thus
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n})
is monotone. Furthermore

(\Psi(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n}))_{xi}=1
if and only if
x\in X_{i}
, which is equivalent to
M_{xi}=1
by construction of
X_{i}
. Hence

\Psi(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n})=M
and surjectivity follows.

Now we show that
\Psi
is indeed a homomorphism. Let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in M_{n}
, where

\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots X_{n})
and
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots Y_{n})
. We compute

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X}\ast\mathbf{Y})=\Psi(\cup_{j\in Y_{1}}X_{j},\cup_{j\in Y_{2}}X_{j},\dots\cup_{j\in Y_{n}}X_{j})

Henceforth:

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X}\ast\mathbf{Y})_{xi}=1

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow x\in\bigcup_{j\in Y_{i}}X_{j}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists k\in Y_{i}.\hskip 2.84526ptx\in X_{k}

And on the other hand

\displaystyle(\Psi(\mathbf{X})\cdot\Psi(\mathbf{Y}))_{xi}=\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}(\Psi(\mathbf{X})_{xk}\land\Psi(\mathbf{Y})_{ki})

From which follows

\displaystyle(\Psi(\mathbf{X})\cdot\Psi(\mathbf{Y}))_{xi}=1

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists k\in\{1,2,\dots n\}.\hskip 2.84526pt\Psi(\mathbf{X})_{xk}=\Psi(\mathbf{Y})_{ki}=1

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists k\in\{1,2,\dots n\}.\hskip 2.84526ptx\in X_{k}\land k\in Y_{i}

\displaystyle\leftrightarrow\exists k\in Y_{i}.\hskip 2.84526ptx\in X_{k}

And thus the following identity holds

\displaystyle\Psi(\mathbf{X}\ast\mathbf{Y})=\Psi(\mathbf{X})\cdot\Psi(\mathbf{Y})

Proving
\Psi
is a homomorphism. Furthermore, it also follows
\Psi((\{1\},\{2\},\dots\{n\}))=I
. This concludes the proof.
∎

And finally.

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\operatorname{End}(K_{n})\cong(D_{n},\cdot)
.

7.
Properties of
\operatorname{End}(K_{n})

Even though the following theorem has in fact been proven in
[
1
]
, we can yet again prove it with relative ease with the new knowledge of the structure of
\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The only invertible element of
\operatorname{End}(K_{n})
is the identity. Consequently,
\operatorname{Aut}(K_{n})
is trivial.

Proof.

First notice that the only invertible boolean matrices are the permutation matrices. Indeed let
A=(a_{ij})
and
B=(b_{ij})
be
n\times n
boolean mattices and assume
A\cdot B=B\cdot A=I
. It follows that for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
there
exist
k_{1},k_{2}\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
such that
a_{ik_{1}}=b_{k_{1}i}=b_{ik_{2}}=a_{k_{2}i}=1
. Which in plain language means that every row and evey column of
A
and
B
respectively include atleast one
1
.
Assume now column
k
of
A
containt two entries
1
. Then for
r<s
we have
a_{rk}=a_{sk}=1
. Since row
k
of
B
contains atleast
1
entry
1
, we have some
t
, such that

b_{kt}=1
. Then we have
(A\cdot B)_{rt}=(A\cdot B)_{st}=1
. It then must follow
r=t=s
, a contradiction. It follows analogously that every row and every column of
A
and

B
respectively includes exactly one entry
1
.

Since the only permutation matrix that does not admit the pattern
1
is the idenitity, it follows
A=B=I
and the claim follows.
∎
