Physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic modelling of uridine 5′‐diphosphoglucorosultransferase (UGT) substrate drugs in pregnant women
Authors: William Saffaf, Ymke van der Velde, Paul R V Malik, Jelmer R Prins, Sanne J Gordijn, Daan J Touw, Paola Mian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWhile pregnancy‐related changes in phase I enzyme activity are well‐documented, less is known about the impact on phase II enzymes. This study aimed to test the hypothesis that changes in the pharmacokinetics (PK) of uridine 5′‐diphosphoglucuronosyltransferase (UGT) substrates during pregnancy result from altered enzyme expression or activity. Physiologically based PK (PBPK) modelling was used to evaluate these changes in pregnant populations.MethodsInduction of UGT1A1 and UGT1A4 in pregnancy was modelled using hormone‐based equations, which account for the effects of 17β‐oestradiol and progesterone. UGT1A3, UGT2B7 and UGT2B15 were assumed unchanged due to a lack of hormone‐induced expression. The predicted‐to‐observed ratio is commonly used to assess the accuracy of PBPK models in predicting clinical data. PBPK model predictions are typically considered acceptable when they fall within a 1.5‐fold error, defined as the ratio of the predicted to the observed PK parameter being within a 1.5‐fold range.ResultsOverall, 82% of predicted to observed ratio of pregnant population were within 1.5‐fold error. P:NP ratios were as follows: UGT1A1: 88%, UGT1A4: 50%, UGT1A9: 100%, UGT2B7: 100%, and UGT2B15: 25%. The P:NP ratio indicates that the PK changes in the pregnant population were successfully predicted for UGT1A1, UGT1A4, UGT1A9 and UGT2B7 substrates.Conclusionsp‐PBPK models that incorporated pregnancy‐induced changes in UGT1A1, UGT1A4 and UGT1A9 expression/activity, or assumed no change for UGT2B7, accurately predicted the pharmacokinetics of most UGT‐metabolized drugs. These findings enhance our understanding of phase II metabolism during pregnancy and may support dose adjustment for those drugs in the future.Keywords: enzyme activity changes, phaseII metabolism, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, pregnancy, uridine 5′‐diphosphoglucuronosyltransferase (UGT)

INTRODUCTION

During pregnancy, women keep on using a variety of different drugs. A longitudinal cohort study showed that 97% of pregnant women use at least one type of medication during gestation. 1 Pregnancy is known to induce profound physiological changes affecting many organ systems. 2 Drug‐metabolizing enzymes are particularly influenced, as can be seen by substantially altered body clearance of various drugs. For example, the clearances of midazolam and clorazepate, both probe substrates for cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4, are significantly increased in pregnant women, 3 , 4 while the clearances of caffeine and theophylline, both substrates for CYP1A2, are markedly decreased. 5 , 6 While research on the effects of pregnancy on phase I enzyme expression/activity has been gained, little is known on the magnitude and potential mechanism behind changes in phase II enzymes, such as uridine 5’‐diphosph‐glucuronosyltransferase (UGT). Studies that shed light on the underlying mechanism(s) of pregnancy‐mediated changes in drug metabolism provide a better understanding of pregnancy‐mediated effects and pave the way to improve drug therapy during pregnancy. Previous in vitro experiments in human HepG2 cells have demonstrated that the expression/activity of UGT1A1 is up‐regulated by progesterone 7 and that of UGT1A4 by estradiol. 8 Furthermore, Jeong et al. showed that oestradiol has no influence on the UGT2B7 expression/activity in HepG2 cells. 7 Since the plasma concentrations of both hormones increase substantially during pregnancy, these in vitro findings may constitute a mechanistic link to the observed in vivo clearance of drugs metabolized via UGT. A study by Berezowska et al. 9 used a mathematical equation to predict the relative enzyme activity of UGT1A1 (for raltegravir) and UGT1A4 (for lamotrigine) during gestation based on the increase of pregnancy‐related hormones (17β‐oestradiol and progesterone) on the expression/activity of UGT1A1 and UGT1A4. The author successfully developed and validated physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models for raltegravir and lamotrigine in pregnant populations. 9 However, since more substances are metabolized by UGT1A1 and UGT1A4, further PBPK model development using the same approach is needed to validate their equation for different UGT substrates.

The aim of this study was to test the hypothesis that pregnancy‐related changes in the pharmacokinetics (PK) of multiple sensitive substrates of UGT1A1 and UGT1A4 can be predicted by the hormone‐dependent equation proposed by Berezowska et al. 9 In addition, this study explores pregnancy‐related PK changes in substrates of other UGT enzymes, namely UGT1A9, UGT2B7 and UGT2B15, using physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modelling for pregnant populations (p‐PBPK).

METHODS

Software

All PBPK models were developed (or re‐developed/re‐verified) using PK‐sim v11.3, which is part of the Open Systems Pharmacology suite [www.opensystemspharmacology.org]. To extract data from published figures, WebPlotDigitizer [https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer.html] was used. R version 4.2.2 [https://posit.co/download/rstudio-desktop/] was used for data visualization.

Modelling strategy

PBPK models for 11 sensitive substrates of UGT in healthy adults were developed or obtained from scientific literature. These 11 drugs were selected because they are primarily metabolized via UGT enzymes and are commonly used during pregnancy. Although labetalol is not primarily metabolized by UGT enzymes but mainly through CYP2C19 and a major fraction through UGT, 10 it was included in this study due to its clinical relevance in pregnant populations. The PBPK models were each scaled to pregnant women, accounting for anatomical, physiological and other enzymatic changes (relevant to the specific clinical pharmacology profile of each drug), including the induction of UGT expression/activity. Raltegravir, dolutegravir, paracetamol, and olanzapine PBPK models from healthy adults and pregnant women were obtained from the open‐access page Github [https://github.com/Open-Systems-Pharmacology/OSP-PBPK-Model-Library]. PBPK models obtained from Github were independent re‐verified to predict the PK in healthy adult populations. The non‐pregnant and pregnant lamotrigine PBPK model and the non‐pregnant labetalol PBPK model were re‐created from the publication by Yeung et al. 11 and by Liu et al., 10 and re‐verified as well for healthy non‐pregnant populations. The non‐pregnant and p‐PBPK models for zidovudine, indomethacin, propofol, oxazepam and lorazepam were developed and validated in non‐pregnant populations. For the developed PBPK models, model parameters were optimized to describe systemic PK profiles of UGT substrate drugs in a healthy adult individual based on the PK following (in general) single intravenous (IV) and/or oral (PO) drug doses. Thereafter, both the healthy adult individual and adult population PBPK models were evaluated with PK data from literature following (in general) single and multiple oral UGT substrate drug dosing in scenarios that were notably different from those used for PBPK model building. When the healthy adult PBPK models were established, the workflow of Dallmann et al. 12 was followed for the development of the p‐PBPK models. All drug‐specific parameters, except the fraction unbound (f u), were fixed, and models were extrapolated to pregnant women by substituting the standard model structure with the pregnancy model structure, which includes nine additional compartments. 12 Additional changes in enzyme expression/activity as relevant to the clinical pharmacology profile of each drug are described below. The relative enzyme activities are manually adjusted in the Parameters tab in PK‐Sim, based on the enzymatic changes during pregnancy as described in Section 2.2.1. The predictive accuracy of the p‐PBPK models was evaluated by comparing simulated PK to the observed PK drugs from literature from pregnant women in the first, second and/or third trimesters of pregnancy. Reports of the re‐creation, re‐verification and/or development and validation of the non‐pregnant or p‐PBPK models for each substrate are available in Supplementary Material I‐XI.

Enzymatic changes during pregnancy

UGT1A1 and UGT1A4

Previous in vitro experiments have shown that the expression/activity of UGT1A1 in human HepG2 cells relative to vehicle controls is up‐regulated by progesterone 7 and that of UGT1A4 by estradiol. 8 Since the plasma concentrations of both hormones increase progressively and substantially during pregnancy, it was hypothesized that these increases could be quantitatively translated into UGT1A1 and UGT1A4 induction. The approaches used for predicting changes in UGT1A1 and UGT1A4 tissue concentrations during pregnancy were obtained from the publication of Berezowska et al. 9

To develop a pregnancy population model for UGT1A4 substrates, changes in UGT1A4 activity over the course of pregnancy were incorporated into a mathematical formula (formula 1) which was obtained from Berezowska et al. 9 This approach was based on an in vitro study showing that 17β‐oestradiol, a hormone that increases during pregnancy, boosted UGT1A4 mRNA expression by 2.3 times. 8 Additionally, another study found that progesterone‐treated HEPG2 cells co‐transfected with PXR exhibited increased UGT1A1 expression compared to untreated control cells. 8

UGT1A3

Fashe et al. 13 observed no altered protein concentration of UGT1A3 in sandwich‐cultured human hepatocytes (SCHH) when exposed to pregnancy‐related hormones (PRHs). 13 Therefore, the UGT1A3 enzyme expression/activity was kept unchanged in the p‐PBPK model.

UGT1A9

Data on UGT1A9 expression in pregnancy are limited. Cho et al. 14 found that 17β‐oestradiol increases UGT1A9 expression in liver cells by activating oestrogen receptor α (ERα), which binds to the UGT1A9 gene promoter. This suggests that rising oestrogen levels during pregnancy may upregulate UGT1A9, potentially affecting drug metabolism. 14

Propofol, a substrate of UGT1A9, is primarily metabolized by this enzyme (70%) and to a lesser extent by CYP2B6 (29%). 15 Gin et al. 16 investigated the PK of propofol in women undergoing elective caesarean sections, highlighting how the drug's pharmacokinetic parameters, including clearance, is influenced by physiological changes during pregnancy. The study found that the clearance of propofol was significantly higher (P‐value < 0.05), with 33.74% in the pregnant group compared to the non‐pregnant group, which may necessitate adjustments in dosing during caesarean delivery. 16

Given that propofol is primarily metabolized by UGT1A9, it is assumed that the increase in total body clearance is reflective of a corresponding increase in the relative activity/expression of UGT1A9 during pregnancy. The pregnant women included in the study by Gin et al. 16 were at the terminal stage of pregnancy, assumed to be at gestational week 40. Based on this assumption, it is proposed that the relative activity/expression of UGT1A9 in pregnant women at gestational week 40 is increased by 1.3374‐fold compared to non‐pregnant women. Due to the limited available data, it is further assumed that the increase in UGT1A9 relative activity/expression is linear over gestational weeks, ranging from 0‐fold increase at gestational week 0 to 1.34‐fold increase at gestational week 40.

UGT2B7

Jeong et al. and Fashe et al. found no effect of pregnancy‐related hormones on the promoter expression/activity and protein concentration of UGT2B7 in HepG2 cells and SCHH. 7 , 13 Based on these findings, it was assumed that the UGT2B7 enzyme expression/activity is not affected by pregnancy in the liver. However, morphine clearance, which is primarily metabolized via UGT2B7, is higher in the pregnant population than in non‐pregnant controls. 17 Furthermore, oestradiol is primarily metabolized in the gut rather than in the liver. Oestradiol levels are also known to increase during pregnancy. 18 The intestine is the main site where oestrogen undergoes glucuronidation, and UGT2B7 plays an important role in the glucuronidation of oestrogen in the gut mucosa. 19 Some UGT2B7 substrates, such as morphine, show increased clearance during pregnancy, 17 whereas others, like zidovudine, exhibit similar zidovudine clearance compared to non‐pregnant populations. 20 Based on this, our hypothesis is that UGT2B7 activity does not change in the liver but increases in the gut mucosa, given its role in the glucuronidation of oestrogen—a hormone that rises during pregnancy. Therefore, due to the lack of reported studies on gut UGT2B7 expression during pregnancy or with pregnancy‐related hormones, it was assumed that UGT2B7 activity/expression is increased only in the gut mucosa, with a two‐fold increase in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy.

UGT2B15

UGT2B15 did not exhibit altered protein concentration in sandwich‐cultured human hepatocytes in response to PRH exposure. 13 Therefore, it was assumed that the expression/activity of UGT2B15 was not changed during pregnancy.

Sulfotransferase (SULT) 1A1

Rubin et al. proved that the SULT1A1 expression/activity does not change during pregnancy by studying the clearance of the substrate 4‐nitrophenol in pregnant women (median gestational age: 10 weeks). 21 SULT1A1 is proven to be the main enzyme responsible for 4‐nitrophenol sulfonation. 22 The clearance of the substrate did not differ between pregnant and non‐pregnant women. Furthermore, it was shown that SULT1A1 expression is not affected by increased levels of progesterone. 21 Therefore, it was assumed that SULT1A1 is not affected by pregnancy, and the enzyme expression/activity was kept unchanged in the p‐PBPK model.

CYP1A2

Caffeine is a probe drug for measuring the CYP1A2 expression/activity during pregnancy. Tracy et al. showed a decrease in CYP1A2 expression/activity in the first, second and third trimesters. 23 In a previously developed p‐PBPK model, a correlation between the caffeine clearance and CYP1A2 expression/activity change during pregnancy have been described, 24 this function expressing CYP1A2 expression/activity change throughout pregnancy has been used in our study.

CYP2C8

In the absence of data from literature, the expression/activity of CYP2C8 was assumed to be unchanged throughout pregnancy.

CYP2C19

CYP2C19 mediates the biotransformation of proguanil to cycloguanil. The change in plasma ratio of proguanil to cycloguanil is an indicator of an altered CYP2C19 expression/activity. 25 Ke et al. found, after back calculating, a decrease in CYP2C19 expression/activity of 68% in the second and third trimester. 25 Therefore, the CYP2C19 enzyme expression/activity was reduced by a factor of 0.32 in the second and third trimesters, respectively.

CYP2E1

Two studies suggest an increased expression/activity of CYP2E1 in late pregnancy. 26 , 27 Kule et al. reported an 80% increase in CYP2E1 expression/activity just 5 min before delivery. In the absence of additional data in earlier stages of pregnancy, it was assumed that CYP2E1 expression/activity increases linearly throughout pregnancy.

CYP3A4

Dallmann et al. 24 estimate a 60% increase in all trimesters of pregnancy based on various in vivo studies examining CYP3A4 expression and activity throughout gestation. However, there are conflicting reports regarding the extent of pregnancy‐induced changes in CYP3A4/5 activity. During late pregnancy, in vivo studies with various marker substances have reported increases in CYP3A4/5 activity ranging from 27% 28 to 99%. 4 , 29 Previous p‐PBPK models have estimated activity increases of 100% at 30–32 weeks of gestation 30 and 3.6% at week 39. 31 In this study, a 60% induction of CYP3A4/5 activity was assumed for the third trimester, representing the weighted average of the in vivo data available. 4 , 28 , 29 This assumption was applied consistently across all trimesters, as a longitudinal in vivo study found no significant differences in CYP3A4/5 induction between the first, second and third trimesters. 23

Flavin‐containing monooxygenase (FMO3)

The biotransformation of nicotine to nicotine N′‐oxide is mainly catalysed by FMO3. 32 The ratio of urinary excreted nicotine N′‐oxide to AUC of nicotine could be an indicator of the change in expression/activity of FMO3 during pregnancy, as proposed by Hukkanen et al. 32 Hukkanen et al. found a 58% increase in FMO3 expression/activity in the third trimester. 32 Like the case for CYP2E1, information about the change in FMO3 expression/activity in the first and second trimesters was not available. Therefore, it was assumed that FMO3 expression/activity increases linearly throughout pregnancy.

CYP2B6

The study by Ban Ke et al 25 used in vitro to in vivo extrapolation (IVIVE) of CYP2B6 induction during pregnancy to predict the fold increase of CYP2B6 during pregnancy. They found out that CYP2B6 increased by 1.9‐fold in the third trimester of pregnancy. A full explanation of the IVIVE of CYP2B6 can be found in the study by Ke et al. 25

Pharmacokinetic model evaluation

The development and validation of the non‐pregnant PBPK models for each drug can be found in the Supplementary Material I‐XII (dolutegravir (Supplementary Material I), Figure S1; olanzapine (Supplementary Material II), Figure S2; paracetamol (Supplementary Material III), Figure S3; raltegravir (Supplementary Material IV), Figure S4; propofol (Supplementary Material V), Table S1–S6 and Figures S5–S9; lamotrigine (Supplementary Material VI), Figure S10; labetalol (Supplementary Material VII), Figure S11; zidovudine (Supplementary Material VIII); Table S7–S10 and Figures S12–S16; indomethacin (Supplementary Material IX), Tables S11‐S14 and Figures S17‐S21; oxazepam (Supplementary Material X), Tables S16–S19 and Figures S22‐S24; lorazepam (Supplementary Material XI), Tables S19–S26 and Figures S25–S28).

The developed or optimized p‐PBPK models were evaluated through comparison of the predicted plasma concentration‐time profiles in pregnant women against observed in vivo data from the literature. Predicted to observed ratios were estimated for different pharmacokinetic parameters, and the numbers within a 1.5‐fold error range (i.e., 0.67 ≤ ratio ≤ 1.50) were given.

To understand further whether the PBPK models accurately predicted the degree of change between the pregnant and non‐pregnant states, the predicted pregnant to non‐pregnant (P:NP) ratios of pharmacokinetic parameters were compared to the observed P:NP. The proposed acceptance ranges by Guest et al. 47 for ratios were used to evaluate the predicted to observed P:NP ratios.

Model performance was evaluated using two complementary criteria. First, predicted PK parameters (AUC, Cmax, apparent clearance or Ctrough) in pregnant women were compared to observed clinical data, and prediction accuracy was quantified as predicted‐to‐observed ratios. Predictions were considered acceptable if ratios fell within a 1.5‐fold error range (0.67–1.5). Second, the ability of the PBPK models to predict pregnancy‐related changes was evaluated by comparing predicted and observed P:NP ratios of PK parameters. These ratios were assessed using the acceptance criteria proposed by Guest et al. 47 Thus, predicted‐to‐observed ratios were used to evaluate the accuracy of predicted pharmacokinetic parameters against observed values, whereas P:NP ratios were used to assess whether pregnancy‐related changes in pharmacokinetics were adequately captured relative to the non‐pregnant population. Throughout the manuscript, the term ‘successful prediction’ refers specifically to meeting these predefined quantitative acceptance criteria. The latter acceptance criterion proposed by Guest et al. 47 is more important, as we are primarily interested in whether the PBPK model adequately predicts pregnancy‐related changes in pregnant women compared with the non‐pregnant population. In this work, prediction performance was evaluated using the Guest criteria, 47 which were proposed to address limitations of the traditional two‐fold rule for ratio predictions. The Guest criteria apply ratio‐dependent acceptance limits, with stricter boundaries for ratios close to unity and wider limits for larger effects, providing a more meaningful and unbiased assessment of pregnant‐to–non‐pregnant exposure ratios.

DISCUSSION

The interest in developing p‐PBPK models has grown over the last decade. 12 However, the quantitative influence of pregnancy on the expression and activity of UGT enzymes, and how this affects the PK of UGT‐substrate drugs, remains poorly understood. Therefore, it is of utmost importance to develop new methods for integrating and scaling available information across multiple physiological levels within the PBPK modelling framework. Incorporating this information into PBPK models is essential for improving drug dosing predictions in pregnant women, reducing adverse effects and optimizing therapeutic outcomes for these drugs.

From this study it can be concluded that the p‐PBPK models successfully predicted the PK of UGT1A1, UGT1A4, UGT1A9 and UGT2B7 substrates in pregnant women. However, the p‐PBPK models were unable to accurately predict the PK of UGT2B15 substrates.

Overall, 82% of the predicted‐to‐observed ratio for all UGT metabolized drugs—except for lamotrigine across all trimesters, oxazepam in the first trimester, and indomethacin in the second trimester—fell within a 1.5‐fold error. Based on the Guest criteria, 47 72.41% of the observed‐to‐predicted P:NP ratios of the PK parameters fell within the acceptance range for all tested UGT drugs: UGT1A1 (87.5%), UGT1A4 (50%), UGT1A9 (100%), UGT2B7 (100%) and UGT2B15 (25%) (Figure 5). This indicates that our p‐PBPK models successfully predicted the PK changes of most UGT substrates in the pregnant population, with the exception of UGT2B15 substrates (lorazepam and oxazepam).

When going into more depth on the specific UGT isoforms, UGT1A1 activity is known to be upregulated by progesterone during pregnancy. 7 The implementation of the relative activity increase of UGT1A1, based on the equation developed by Berezowska et al., 9 successfully predicted the observed PK in the developed PBPK models for drug substrates mainly metabolized by UGT1A1. Overall, only the P:NP ratio of the AUC predicted for dolutegravir in the third trimester and the P:NP ratio of Cmax for paracetamol in the first trimester fell outside the acceptance range. However, the developed PBPK models did show some limitations. When focusing on dolutegravir, in the non‐pregnant dolutegravir PBPK model developed by Liu et al., 50 the fraction metabolized by UGT1A1 was set to 0.79. In the obtained non‐pregnant model for dolutegravir, the fraction of dolutegravir metabolized by UGT1A1 was combined with the fractions metabolized by UGT1A3 and UGT1A9 and set to 0.79. However, in the literature, the fraction metabolized by UGT1A1 has also been described as being less than 0.51. 51 The fraction metabolized by UGT1A1 was maintained at 0.79, consistent with the PBPK model developed by Liu et al. 50 Besides UGT1A1, UGT1A3 also plays a role in metabolizing dolutegravir. Also in that some limitations, namely Fashe et al. 13 found no effect of pregnancy‐related hormones on UGT1A3 activity, whereas UGT1A9 is upregulated by estradiol. 14 Combining the fraction of UGT1A3 into the fraction metabolized by UGT1A1 and inducing UGT1A1 activity with the induction factors may lead to an overestimation of clearance and an overestimation of the UGT1A1 activity changes that occur during pregnancy. In the p‐PBPK model for paracetamol, CYP2E1 activity was assumed to increase linearly with gestational age, alongside increased UGT1A1 activity/expression. However, the only data on CYP2E1 changes during pregnancy, from Kulo et al. and Miners et al., 26 , 27 report a 1.80‐fold increase in late pregnancy. Due to the lack of data for other trimesters, CYP2E1 activity was assumed to increase linearly, reaching a 1.80‐fold increase in the third trimester. This linear assumption may lead to over‐ or underprediction of paracetamol clearance in the first trimester in the p‐PBPK models.

UGT1A4 was scaled into pregnancy using the mathematical equation from Berezowska et al. 9 For olanzapine, all observed‐to‐predicted P:NP ratios fell just outside the acceptance range, whereas the predicted‐to‐observed Ctrough ratio remained within the 1.5‐fold error. The limited clinical PK data during pregnancy, particularly full concentration–time profiles, further restricts the reliability of PBPK model predictions. For lamotrigine, all predicted‐to‐observed P:NP ratios fell within the acceptance range across all trimesters, although apparent clearance was slightly underestimated. High interindividual variability in clearance 11 may explain the differences between observed and predicted populations. Gardner et al. 52 found that including UGT2B10 in the PBPK model improved clearance predictions, suggesting that adding UGT2B10 to the p‐ PBPK model would enhance lamotrigine clearance accuracy. This was not included in our lamotrigine model because the contribution of UGT2B10 to lamotrigine metabolism is not well understood. 53

The expression and activity of UGT1A9 during pregnancy remain uncertain. An in vitro study showed that increased 17β‐oestradiol upregulates UGT1A9 activity in HepG2 cells, 14 while other studies found no change in UGT1A9 protein concentrations in SCHH with PRH exposure. 13 In this study, a linear increase in UGT1A9 activity was assumed based on propofol PK in pregnant vs. non‐pregnant women. 16 All P:NP ratios fell within the acceptable range, and predicted‐to‐observed ratios for the pregnant population were 100% within the 1.5‐fold range, indicating that the p‐PBPK model for propofol accurately predicted PK changes using the UGT1A9 linear scaling method. UGT1A9 expression was assumed to increase linearly by a factor of 1.34 at term (GA = 40 weeks), as propofol clearance was 34% higher in non‐pregnant women. 16 However, this assumption did not account for the increased fraction unbound (fub) during pregnancy 46 or the 1.9‐fold increase in CYP2B6 activity in the third trimester. 25 Further studies are needed to clarify the role of UGT1A9 in pregnant women to improve predictions.

To predict changes in UGT2B7 activity during pregnancy, p‐ PBPK models were developed for zidovudine and indomethacin. Fashe et al. 13 and Jeong et al. 7 observed no effect of PRHs on UGT2B7 activity in sandwich‐cultured human hepatocytes or HepG2 cells. All predicted‐to‐observed P:NP ratios of UGT2B7 substrates fell within the acceptance range. Morphine, another UGT2B7 substrate, exhibits increased clearance during pregnancy, 17 in contrast to zidovudine and indomethacin, suggesting a potential role for intestinal metabolism in UGT2B7‐mediated drug clearance. Intestinal expression of UGT2B7, as well as UGT2B15 and UGT2B17, have been reported, 54 and functional UGT2B‐mediated metabolism in the gut has been demonstrated. 55 However, most studies investigating pregnancy‐related changes in drug‐metabolizing enzymes have focused on hepatic regulation, leaving intestinal effects largely unexplored. In the present study, assuming a twofold increase in intestinal UGT2B7 activity adequately predicted the PK of zidovudine and indomethacin, with predictions remaining within an acceptable error range. Other possible explanations for the increased clearance of morphine during pregnancy include increased placental P‐glycoprotein expression affecting maternal–fetal transport, 56 contributions from enterohepatic circulation and extrahepatic metabolism, 57 and its intermediate‐to‐high hepatic extraction, which is sensitive to pregnancy‐related changes in hepatic blood flow. 17 These complexities indicate that morphine PK in pregnancy involves dynamic processes besides the intestinal UGT2B7 during pregnancy, and appropriate parameterization remains a topic for future research.

UGT2B15 substrates were less accurately predicted, with 25% of the calculated P:NP ratios for lorazepam and oxazepam falling inside the acceptable range. While oxazepam PK were accurately predicted in the third trimester, predictions for the first trimester were underestimated. Variability in the clinical data 33 may have contributed to this discrepancy. The fraction unbound (fub) of lorazepam was set at 0.14 based on the literature, 58 but if it had been kept constant at 0.11, the model would have successfully predicted third‐trimester PK (see supplementary materials XI (Figure S26). Lorazepam exhibits a wide range of reported fub values in non‐pregnant individuals (7.7–11.4%), 59 highlighting the need for further research into its pregnancy‐specific protein binding.

Another limitation is that data on enzyme activity across all gestational ages are limited, so we assumed a linear increase during pregnancy (for UGT1A9 and CYP2E1). While this is a limitation, the model still performed well for drugs such as propofol (a UGT1A9 substrate). The expression of UGT2B15 in pregnancy is also poorly characterized, which could affect predictions. Future studies should aim to quantify enzyme activity in pregnant women, potentially in relation to pregnancy‐related hormones.

Besides the above‐mentioned limitations, this study has also several strengths. This study provides the most detailed analysis of UGT‐metabolized compounds in pregnant women, reporting data on 11 compounds. Multiple published PBPK models were used, evaluated and/or refined, emphasizing the importance of transparent reporting. Unlike single‐case analyses, this study examined 11 UGT substrates across various pregnant populations, offering a comprehensive PK profile that supports better dosing of UGT‐metabolized compounds during pregnancy.

In conclusion, PBPK modelling in pregnancy is well‐established for CYP enzymes but remains underexplored for UGT‐metabolized drugs. Overall, 82% of the predicted‐to‐observed ratios for all UGT substrates were within the 1.5‐fold error range, and 100% were within the 2‐fold error range. Moreover, 72.41% of the observed‐to‐predicted P:NP ratios of pharmacokinetic parameters fell within the acceptance range for all tested UGT enzymes. This study successfully predicted PK changes during pregnancy for UGT1A1, UGT1A4 and UGT1A9 substrates. UGT2B7 substrates were also well predicted by implementing a twofold increase in UGT2B7 activity in the gut only. However, UGT2B15 substrates, particularly lorazepam and oxazepam, exhibited greater variability, highlighting the need for further research into their pregnancy PK profiles to validate these models. Accurately predicting pharmacokinetic changes enables more precise plasma concentration‐time curve estimations, leading to improved dosing strategies for pregnant populations. However, effective optimization relies on sufficient research into the effects of pregnancy on metabolic enzymes and other drug‐specific factors to properly account for these physiological changes. Although p‐ PBPK models for UGT1A substrates already exist in the literature, less attention has been given to UGT2B isoforms. More research is needed to study the expression of UGT2B isoforms during pregnancy.
