Barriers to and facilitators of effective management of fever episodes in hospitalised Kenyan children with cancer: protocol for convergent mixed methods study
Authors: Charles Nathaniel Nessle, Festus Njuguna, Julia Dettinger, Raphael Koima, Lenah Nyamusi, Evelynn Kisembe, Sarah Kinja, Mercy Ndung’u, Dennis Njenga, Sandra Langat, Gilbert Olbara, Cheryl Moyer, Terry Vik
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFebrile neutropenia is an oncological emergency in children with cancer, associated with serious infections and complications. In low-resourced settings, death from infections in children with cancer is 20 times higher than in high-resourced treatment settings, thought to be related to delays in antibiotic administration and management. The barriers to effective management of fever episodes in children with cancer have not previously been described. This convergent mixed-methods study will provide the evidence to develop fever treatment guidelines and to inform their effective implementation in children with cancer at Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital (MTRH), a level 6 referral hospital in western Kenya.Methods and analysisProspective data collection of paediatric patients with cancer with new fever episodes admitted to MTRH will be performed during routine treatment. Clinical variables will be collected from 50 fever episodes, including cancer diagnosis and infectious characteristics of the fever episode, and elapsed time from fever onset to various milestones in the management workflow. Semistructured qualitative interviews with healthcare providers (estimated 20 to reach saturation) will explore the barriers to and facilitators of appropriate management of fever episodes in children with cancer. The interview guide was informed by a theoretical framework and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. A mixed-methods analysis use of joint display tables and process mapping will link and integrate the two types of data with meta-inferences.Ethics and disseminationInstitutional review board approval was obtained from the MTRH (0004273) and the University of Michigan (HUM0225674), and the study was registered with National Commission for Science Technology and Innovation (P/23/22885). Written consent will be obtained from all participants. Results will be formally shared with local and national policy leadership and local end users, presented at relevant national academic conferences and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal.Keywords: ONCOLOGY, Clinical Decision-Making, Protocols & guidelines, INFECTIOUS DISEASES, PAEDIATRICS, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

In the paediatric patient with cancer, fever and neutropenia (FN), defined as an absolute neutrophil count <500 cells/µL and a fever 38°C for >1 hour or single fever >38.3°C, is an oncological emergency with high risk of serious bacterial infections and subsequent mortality.1–3 Blood stream infections (BSIs) in children with cancer have a higher incidence during FN episodes compared with patients without neutropenia (11–24% vs 5%), and 0.9–11% with intensive care unit admissions during FN episodes.4 5 Through advances in supportive care with appropriate empirical and prophylactic antibiotic regimens, the current estimated mortality from FN episodes ranges from 0% to 1.5% in high-resourced settings, a marked improvement from above 85% at its initial description in the 1960s.2 6–8

Death from infections in FN episodes of paediatric patients with cancer in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is at least 20 times higher than the USA and Europe.9 Regional and country-specific descriptions highlight drastic variability of prevalence of BSI episodes (14.1–29.4%), predominant bacterial isolates, antibiotic-resistant patterns and approaches to treatment in paediatric patients with cancer.10–15 Patients with cancer do not receive timely antibiotic administration and infectious diagnostic evaluations, which may contribute to high mortality.11–13 16 Tertiary institutions in SSA countries, Kenya included, have documented variable clinical approaches to paediatric patients with cancer with fever; 14.1–16% of patients with fever get blood cultures, and only 23% have antibiotics given within 3 hours of fever onset.9 12 While multidrug-resistant bacterial isolates were documented in immunocompetent children in Kenya,10 17 few have focused on antimicrobial resistance patterns of isolates in children with cancer, who are at high risk of BSIs from unique bacteria.10 12 17 There are no publications describing non-neutropenic fever (NNF) episodes in children with cancer in SSA.

Given the high disparity of infection-related mortality of fever episodes in children with cancer in SSA,9 12 13 urgent attention should be given to improve the timely diagnosis and management of fever episodes in children with cancer.9 16 Treatment guidelines have largely relied on evidence from high-income countries,3 unpublished data from treatment centres and anecdotal experience. The barriers to and facilitators of effective management which contribute to the high death rate of fevers in paediatric patients with cancer in SSA are currently unknown.9 18

Aims and objectives

This study aims to comprehensively describe the fever episodes and management approaches to support the development of evidence-based clinical guidelines and to inform and improve effective clinical guideline implementation. We will examine both FN and NNF episodes as the barriers to and facilitators of effective fever management are hypothesised to be similar in both clinical scenarios during hospitalisation based on local experience. More specifically, the study is intended to achieve the following aims:

To describe the clinical characteristics, management workflow and antibiotic-resistance pattern of bacterial isolates in paediatric patients with cancer younger than 14 years old admitted to the cancer ward with new fever episodes.

To describe the clinical characteristics, management workflow and antibiotic-resistance pattern of bacterial isolates in paediatric patients with cancer younger than 14 years old admitted to the cancer ward with new fever episodes.

To explore the barriers and facilitators of effective diagnosis and treatment of fevers in paediatric patients with cancer encountered by healthcare providers (eg, nursing, physician, phlebotomy, pharmacy, microbiology laboratory).

To explore the barriers and facilitators of effective diagnosis and treatment of fevers in paediatric patients with cancer encountered by healthcare providers (eg, nursing, physician, phlebotomy, pharmacy, microbiology laboratory).

We hypothesise the results of this study will improve the management of fever episodes: (1) provide insight into the factors which negatively and positively influence effective management of fever episodes; (2) obtain evidence to support empirical antibiotic recommendations and guideline development; (3) facilitate effective delivery of care to febrile paediatric patients with cancer by identification of barriers to implementation which will (4) eliminate the high infection-related mortality rate disparity in paediatric patients with cancer in low-resourced settings.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This convergent mixed-methods study will be conducted at Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital (MTRH) in Eldoret, Kenya as a collaborative effort between Moi University, University of Michigan (UM) and Indiana University (IU). To achieve the study aims, the design is informed by a theoretical framework around the management of fever episodes in children with cancer (figure 1). As a convergent mixed-methods study design, both types of qualitative and quantitative data will be prospectively collected over a similar time period in parallel, allowing for more accurate linking and integration during the mixed-methods analysis (figure 2). Following collection of both types of data, a mixed-methods analysis will be conducted to inform the diagnostic/management workflow and clinical characteristics of fever episodes in children with cancer. More specifically, we will use connecting integration during the mixed-methods analysis,19 20 meaning the thematic analysis of the qualitative data (interviews with healthcare workers) will explain the results of the quantitative data (fever episodes in children with cancer). This study report followed the Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials checklist.21 The study is active at time of publication.

Setting

MTRH is a level 6 referral hospital, the largest government referral hospital in western Kenya and serves a population of over 20 million (figure 3).22 The paediatric oncology programme at MTRH, which collaborates with Princess Maxima Pediatric Oncology Center (Netherlands) and the Division of Pediatric Hematology-Oncology, Indiana (IU, USA), boasts of the only paediatric oncology clinical fellowship training programme in Kenya and has advanced radiation oncology capabilities. The study site is a primary location for the Academic Model for Providing Access to Healthcare (AMPATH), led by IU, which has worked to support patient care and research productivity and capacity through a longstanding partnership with MTRH.23 The collaborative oncology team improves patient survival through multiple programmatic outreach activities24 and currently treats over 300 new paediatric patients with cancer each year. The clinical microbiology laboratory at MTRH performs identification of bacterial isolates and antibiotic susceptibilities, as well as diagnostic infectious evaluations such as rapid diagnostic testing, PCR and genetic tuberculosis testing. The paediatric oncology fever guidelines at MTRH were adapted from society recommendations: administration of empirical, broad-spectrum antibiotics and an infectious disease diagnostic evaluation in all paediatric patients with cancer with fevers.3

Study planning

Regularly, scheduled team meetings were held prior to institutional review board submission and became more frequent after submission. The research team leadership consisted of a senior and junior paediatric haematologist-oncologist from MTRH, a senior and junior paediatric haematologist-oncologist from IU and UM, and a senior qualitative research methodologist from UM. The team recruited a highly qualified research assistant, previously trained as a clinical officer, who had prior research experience with data collection of quantitative variables through manual abstraction of medical records and qualitative data through semistructured interviews. Several consultants were used to refine study methodology: paediatric infectious disease specialist, doctoral-level mixed-methods research methodologist and doctoral-level qualitative researcher. The theoretical framework assisted in the identification of stakeholders for data collection: microbiology laboratory, paediatric pharmacy and healthcare providers on the inpatient cancer ward. Meetings were then conducted with various key stakeholders within each cadre to discuss project aims, feasibility and strategies for data collection, relationship building and proposed study timeline. Members of the study team, including the research assistant, also participated in training sessions focused on overall methodology, data collection plan and practices, qualitative interviewing practices, monitoring and evaluation plan, and bias mitigation strategies. Additional planning meetings were held with AMPATH paediatrics research working group and AMPATH qualitative research core.

Data management

Both types of data will be stored in secure environments accessed by the study team. Quantitative data will be collected via pen and paper by the trained research assistant, who will then enter the data into REDCap.25 Qualitative data will be collected on digital voice recorders, transcribed full verbatim, and then audio recording and transcriptions will be stored in the secure cloud-based software for use by the qualitative research team. This short-term, minimal risk study without interventions does not require a data monitoring committee. Data will be made available upon request.

Patient and public involvement

The patients and caregivers have minimal involvement in this study and will experience minimal burden. This group will only be directly engaged in the consenting process for quantitative data collection of fever episodes. Patients and caregivers were not directly involved in the planning or design of the study. The research objective and study aims were indirectly informed by the observations of patients/caregivers by clinicians involved in the study. The results will be directly provided to patients/caregivers on the cancer ward by written handouts to coincide with the steps the paediatric oncology division will take to improve the management workflow. Regional, national and local medical stakeholders will receive the study results by way of direct communication, policy brief and medical conference.

Study populations, recruitment and data collection

Quantitative arm: fever episodes

Aim 1: to describe the clinical characteristics, management workflow and antibiotic resistance pattern of bacterial isolates in paediatric patients with cancer younger than 14 years old admitted to the cancer ward with new fever episodes.

Secondary aim: to describe severe comorbidities and severe outcomes (death, BSI) in children with cancer during the management of FN and NNF episodes.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

The participants will be located in the inpatient paediatric cancer ward of MTRH. Inclusion criteria for quantitative data collection: diagnosis of a new fever episode by a documented temperature of 38°C in two consecutive readings at least 1 hour apart or single fever >38.5°C obtained by thermometer (MTRH definition); cancer diagnosis; admission on paediatric oncology ward; age ≤14 years. Exclusion criteria are as follows: first fever was detected outpatient, the patient was transferred from an outside facility for fever management or fever episode is documented within 7 days of another fever. Participants may have multiple fever episodes that occur during the study period provided the participant is afebrile for 7 days or longer between fever episodes.

Recruitment and consenting process

Study recruitment for fever episodes began on 15 January 2023. Screening for eligible participants will be conducted by the study team and research assistant by review of clinical data in medical chart, observation of ward rounds and daily discussion with healthcare providers on the ward. Eligible participants and caregivers will be approached in person by study investigators or research assistants to discuss participation either on the day of the fever episode or prior if high risk of developing a fever. An eligible participant meets the study inclusion criteria or may be a paediatric patient with cancer at risk of developing a fever. A member of the study team will obtain written consent or assent, when appropriate, prior to data collection.

Quantitative data collection

Collection of clinical course variables by manual abstraction of the medical chart will be performed. During study planning, the team discussed the data collection plan, including the feasibility and location of the clinical variables for the study prior to collection. Several locations of the clinical variables were identified: electronic medical record, internal microbiology laboratory specimen registry, internal pharmacy computer documentation and more than five hard copy medical records located on the inpatient ward. Due to the nature and aims of the study, the team will incorporate a standardised strategy of data collection if the variable may not be documented in the record. For example, the time of antibiotic administration may not be consistently documented, so the research assistant will inquire with the bedside nurses two times per day and estimate the administration time to the nearest half hour. Data integrity will be monitored and evaluated continually by notes and formal weekly meetings. The quantitative data will include categorical and continuous variables to describe the fever episode such as oncological history, infectious diagnostics and comorbidities present during the episode. Importantly, data focus on elapsed time from fever onset, a required variable to collect for analysis, to milestones within the routine workflow of the blood culture and antibiotic management for the fever episode. Paired with the analysis, data collection continues until enough observations describe trends in the workflow. As explained below in study analysis, the quantitative data collected will describe the ‘what’ of the fever episodes and workflow. Qualitative arm: healthcare worker interviews

Aim 2: explore the barriers and facilitators of effective diagnosis and treatment of fevers in paediatric patients with cancer encountered by healthcare providers (eg, nursing, physician, phlebotomy, pharmacy, microbiology laboratory).

Interview participants

Semistructured interviews will be conducted with healthcare providers who provide direct or indirect care for paediatric patients with cancer with fevers. The healthcare providers will be purposefully selected to reflect a wide range of experience, roles and job descriptions (eg, nurses, oncologists, registrars, pharmacists, pharmacy technologists, laboratory technicians).

Recruitment and consenting process for healthcare workers

A mixed purposive sampling method will be used for interview participant recruitment. Study recruitment for interviews began on 15 March 2023. First, we will use purposive sampling of key healthcare providers identified by the study’s theoretical framework. The study will then use a snowball sampling technique within each thematic clinical area until data saturation is determined. Eligible participants will be approached in person to schedule the interview. Written informed consent will be collected from interview participants.

Qualitative data collection: semistructured interview

The semistructured interview guide was informed by the theoretical framework and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research,26 adapted to local context. The interview guide will be subjected to iterative evolution throughout the study to incorporate observations from quantitative and qualitative data collection. Prior to data collection, pilot interviews will be performed with transcription review to ensure rich data collection, improve interview flow and increase the research assistant’s confidence in using the tool. Interviews and quantitative data collection will be conducted by the same study research assistant with past experience in conducting semistructured interviews and further qualitative training for the study. Although deidentified transcripts will be collected, participants will state their specific cadre and years of experience. Field notes are documented during the interview process 45–90 min to inform data analysis. While the quantitative data collected describe ‘what’, the qualitative data will explain the ‘why’ behind the described barriers within the antibiotic and blood culture workflow. Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis

Quantitative clinical data will be cleaned and imported into Stata V.16.0 for analysis; statistical significance is defined as a p value less than 0.05. Informed by our team’s local experience and study feasibility, it is assumed each fever episode has a blood culture and antibiotic administered; we estimate a convenience sample of 50 fever episodes is required to observe trends in the respective workflow to link to the qualitative results during mixed-methods analysis. Since the barriers preventing effective management are not yet known, the study team will carefully monitor key indicators for the study and adjust sample size accordingly: the number of fever episodes, the number of episodes which have an antibiotic administered and the number of episodes in which a blood culture is obtained. Data collection should be reflexive to ongoing study monitoring and evaluation of key indicators to ensure ample observations and workflow description are achieved. Process mapping will be used for the blood culture and antibiotic workflow, incorporating the mean elapsed time from documented fever onset to each milestone in the workflow.27 We will also explore the impact which severe comorbidities during both FN and NNF episodes may implicate the blood culture and antibiotic workflow. Predetermined process indicators descriptively measure markers of quality management, such as proportion of episodes with a blood culture obtained prior to antibiotic administration and proportion of antibiotics administered within 1 hour of fever detection.3 Descriptive statistical analysis will be performed with continuous demographics summarised with medians and ranges, while categorical demographics will be summarised with proportions.

Qualitative thematic analysis

Interviews will be digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim, then entered into NVivo V.12.0 for coding. The coding strategy is informed by the theoretical framework, semistructured interview guide and study aims as the codebook will be made a priori, but open to exploratory methods. The transcription of audio files will be conducted by a trained qualitative researcher, who will also conduct iterative thematic coding of transcripts. Together with leaders from the research team, they will perform the thematic analysis using the Attride-Sterling Qualitative Analysis Method.28 Rapid thematic analysis will be performed at the beginning of data collection. Interviews are to be conducted until thematic saturation,29 estimated at 20 participants. As the barriers to and facilitators of the effective management are not yet described, our thematic analysis will remain flexible to continued data collection recognising the sample size, determined prior to data collection, may be insufficient.

Mixed-methods analysis

After the separate analysis of both types of data, a mixed-methods analysis, including split-frame analysis, will be applied to the collected data to identify agreement and discrepancies across the two types of data.30 Connecting integration of the mixed data, meaning the qualitative results will explain the quantitative results, will be conducted using joint display tables during analysis19 31; quantitative results describe the ‘what’ of the problem, while the qualitative results explain the ‘why’ behind the described problem. After the joint display tables are constructed to display data origin and linkages, the data will be collectively interpreted to describe meta-inferences and their significance.20 Meta-inferences represent extended interpretations of the linked data by joint display, which provides insight beyond the conclusions of the separate individual results.32 The joint display table of results will show the two data types, data origins, relationship of the data, meta-inferences and potential impact to change the workflow.30 A type of joint display used for the mixed-methods analysis will be a modified process map.27 A joint display process map will be constructed for the blood culture workflow and one unique to the antibiotic workflow, which will include elapsed time from fever onset to the milestone in the workflow (from the quantitative results) and the explanation of that elapsed time period (from the qualitative interviews). For an example of a joint display table to improve clarity of the mixed-methods analysis, the quantitative results may show an average of 500 min from fever onset to first blood culture draw, and qualitative thematic analysis of the interviews connected to this observation explains majority of blood cultures ordered during a certain time window and a low nurse-to-patient ratio are contributing factors (table 1).

Ethics and dissemination

This is a minimal risk observational study. Written informed consent will be obtained from the study participants in both qualitative and quantitative arms. The diagnosis and management of fever episodes are a complex, multifactorial clinical process and, therefore, the study team will give collective feedback after study analysis as opposed to case-by-case feedback prior to analysis of all study data.

The institution ethics and research boards from the UM (HUM0225674), MTRH (0004273) and MTRH chief executive officer (CEO) reviewed the research study protocol and all related study documents to either approve (MTRH research committee, MTRH CEO) or deferred review (UM) to the primary research institution site (MTRH). This study was registered with National Commission for Science Technology and Innovation (P/23/22885).

Results will be formally shared with local and national policy leadership and local end users, presented at relevant national and international academic conferences, and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal. Data will only be available to approved study team members, but deidentified data set may be available upon request.

Discussion

This observational study proposal takes a novel approach in the initial step towards decreasing severe outcomes from infections in children with cancer; the comprehensive description of fever episodes and barriers to effective diagnosis and management will inform the future treatment guideline and its effective implementation. The qualitative assessment of barriers and facilitators from healthcare providers will serve as a foundational component to inform the effective implementation of the fever treatment guideline. Additionally, the comprehensive clinical description of the fever episodes allows for the future development of an evidence-based, paediatric oncology clinical guideline adapted to the local context from the existing hospital guideline, and process measures during future implementation. The mixed-methods results importantly improve etiological understanding of the barriers to management when the connected qualitative results expand the quantitative results, facilitating the identification of implementation interventions when the fever guideline is implemented. This study will not only facilitate an impactful change of local clinical practice at future implementation of the fever guideline, but the study methodology may provide a framework upon which other institutions in low-resourced treatment settings may locally adapt and build upon.

Although our research team carefully planned this convergent mixed-methods study, the results are subject to biases which we will attempt to control. We identified the study is at risk of the Hawthorne effect33 and observer bias during data collection, and steps will be taken to mitigate these potential biases.34 Regarding the Hawthorne effect, our team benefits from established rapport with the healthcare providers on the cancer ward, but the study team will motivate the ward staff to improve infection outcomes, assure we are evaluating the workflow and not their performance, encourage honest responses and will include them in our mission.34–36 Due to fragmented medical records and a new hospital electronic medical record, quantitative data collection of the fever episodes may be incomplete which would prevent extensive description of the fever management workflow. To address this and observer bias, we attempted to comprehensively construct a data collection plan, including a standardised method for collecting the time-specific workflow variables.34 To mitigate the potential response bias of interview participants and staff during fever episode data collection, the research assistant will ensure that responses are confidential, deidentified, and not shared with managers or supervisors.34 Since the primary aims of this study focus on describing the management of fever episodes, we do not anticipate sufficient sample size to detect statistically significant differences in the episode characteristics between the FN and NNF groups, nor ability to accurately determine risk factors for severe outcomes in the analytical group. While the unique results of this study may be centre specific, this methodology may be generalisable to other centres in low-resourced treatment settings. The barriers and facilitators to effective management may change with evolution of hospital policies/procedures and should be subjected to ongoing assessments.
