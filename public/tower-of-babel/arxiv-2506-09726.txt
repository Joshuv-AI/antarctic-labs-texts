Don’t be Afraid of Cell Complexes
Josef Hoppe, Vincent P. Grande, and Michael T. Schaub rwth Aachen University { hoppe, grande, schaub } @cs.rwth-aachen.de

Abstract
Cell complexes ( cc s) are a higher-order network model deeply rooted in algebraic topology that has gained interest in signal processing and network science recently. However, while the processing of signals supported on cc s can be described in terms of easily-accessible algebraic or combinatorial notions, the commonly presented definition of cc s is grounded in abstract concepts from topology and remains disconnected from the signal processing methods developed for cc s. In this paper, we aim to bridge this gap by providing a simplified definition of cc s that is accessible to a wider audience and can be used in practical applications. Specifically, we first introduce a simplified notion of abstract regular cell complexes ( arcc s). These arcc s only rely on notions from algebra and can be shown to be equivalent to regular cell complexes for most practical applications. Second, using this new definition we provide an accessible introduction to (abstract) cell complexes from a perspective of network science and signal processing. Furthermore, as many practical applications work with cc s of dimension 2 and below, we provide an even simpler definition for this case that significantly simplifies understanding and working with cc s in practice.

Cell Complexes: Intuition and Motivation

Intuitively, graphs may be thought of geometrically as a collection of points (or
vertices
) connected by lines (or
edges
).
Cell complexes extend graphs to higher dimensions by allowing the use of building blocks (
‘cells’
) of arbitrary dimension in addition to the
0
- and
1
-dimensional vertices and edges of ordinary graphs.
Most prominently,
2
-dimensional cells are polygons that are attached to a cycle of edges (
1
-cells) of the graph.
In general, a
k
-dimensional cell or
k
-cell is represented by a
k
-dimensional object (a
k
-disc).
The intuition behind this is that cell complexes model real-world structures with
geometric
or
topological
properties,
i.e., in physical networks where
2
-cells correspond to areas, or in flow systems where we can measure the flow
around
a part of the system represented by
2
-cells.
In this regard, cell complexes inherently differ from other network models like hypergraphs which are more flexible but do not enforce an underlying geometry in the network.

1

Center: The same flow, represented with respect to a reference orientation of the edges induced by a lexicographical ordering of the vertices.

We will use cell complexes in practice to
model
complex data and facilitate geometry- and topology-informed computations.
Thus, we need an abstract computational model of cell complexes.
In particular, we are interested in the combinatorial and algebraic structure on cells enforced by the geometry and
not
in the point-set topology itself, which we will abstract away.
For abstract cell complexes, we will thus consider the combinatorial structure of cells induced by the geometry, but not the infinite continuous euclidean space itself.

Cells in a cell complex are related via the
boundary
relation (or
boundary map
). A
k
-cell is enclosed by multiple
(k-1)
-cells which together form its
boundary
.
This boundary relation is the central combinatorial structure of the abstract cell complex.
Like on graphs, discrete geometry, global structure and global phenomena emerge from this local relation.

Data on spaces with geometric or topological structure usually has a direction:
For instance, we are not only interested in the absolute value of an edge flow, but also in its direction.
To represent such data, cells are thus endowed with a reference orientation which is arbitrarily chosen at construction.
For edges, the orientation is often also discussed in terms of a direction and is represented by an arrow.
The orientation is not a property of the underlying space, but rather a means of defining a reference direction for the data.
Hence, changing the orientation of a cell only changes the sign of the associated data.

Figure

2
illustrates the bookkeeping of flows w.r.t. the reference orientation of edges.
For polygons, we may think of the orientation as identifying an ’upper’ and ’lower’ side of the polygon; or the ’inside’ and ’outside’ of a volume etc.
The boundary of a volume consists of polygons oriented s.t. their upper side points outwards.

Boundaries also express
how
a cell relates to cells it is attached to.
The boundary of a polygon can either contain an edge in accordance with or opposite to its reference orientation.

4
On How to Find a Cell Complex in the Wild

In many cases, we do not get access to data that is already in the form of cell complexes.
However, a closer look often reveals underlying topological and geometrical structures which are representable by cell complexes.
In this section, we will introduce the most important techniques by revealing the hidden cell complex structure in different data sets and modalities.

Simplicial complexes are cell complexes

Cell complexes, and in particular
arcc
s generalise simplicial complexes.
In short, simplicial complexes only permit the simplest possible
k
-cells, that is edges for
1
-cells, triangles for
2
-cells, tetrahedra for
3
-cells and so on.
This means that
cc
s are able to model more complex data, whereas simplicial complexes enjoy a simple definition and combinatorial structure.

Definition 4.1

(Simplicial Complex)
.

A
finite simplicial complex

\mathcal{S}=(V,S)
is a pair of a finite set
V
called vertices and a set
S
of non-empty finite subsets of
V
called
simplices
such that

1.

S
is closed under taking non-empty subsets and

2.

the union over all simplices recovers
V
, i.e. we have
\bigcup_{\sigma\in S}\sigma=V
, or in other words,
\forall v\in V:\{v\}\in S
.

We call the set of subsets of
V
in
S
with
k+1
elements the
k
-simplices
S_{k}
.
Given an ordering of
V
, we can order the
k
-simplices for all
k
lexicographically.
An important example of simplicial complexes are triangular meshes known from computer geometry.

Vietoris–Rips complexes

The Vietoris–Rips complex is a standard way to build a cell complex, in particular a simplicial complex, on a given point cloud and is widely used in topological data analysis:

Definition 4.2
.

Given a real number
\varepsilon
and a point cloud
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the associated Vietoris–Rips complex
VR_{\varepsilon}
is the simplicial complex with vertex set
X
and simplex set

S_{\varepsilon}=\{\emptyset\not=\sigma\subset S:\lVert x_{1}-x_{2}\rVert_{2}\leq\varepsilon\quad\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\sigma\}.

Products of graphs and simplicial and cell complexes

Cell complexes naturally arise as the product of easier structures:
Let us consider two very simple graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
with

G_{1/2}=(V_{1/2},E_{1/2})=(\{v_{1/2},u_{1/2}\},\{(v_{1/2},u_{1/2})\})

both corresponding to a single edge with nodes.
It could be that the graphs correspond to two different directions in physical space.
The vertex set of their cartesian graph product
G_{1}\square G_{2}
now consists of pairs of vertices
(u,v)\in V_{1}\times V_{2}
.
The edges set of
G_{1}\square G_{2}
is the union

E_{G_{1}\square G_{2}}=V_{1}\times E_{2}\cup V_{2}\times E_{1},

where the edge
(v_{1},(u_{2},v_{2}))
connects the vertices
(v_{1},u_{2})
and
(v_{1},v_{2})
in
G_{1}\square G_{2}
.
After having assigned
0
-dimensional objects to pairs of vertices and
1

2

In the case of the graph product, we are bound by the language of graph theory not to do this.
However, the world of cell complexes allows for the generalisation of graph products by assigning a
2
-cell to every pair of edges.

7

More generally, we can construct a product of two given cell complexes, using the following definition which we will explain below.

Definition 4.3

(Products of cell complexes)
.

We let
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{*},B_{*})
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{*},B^{\prime}_{*})
be two cell complexes.
Their product
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is given by the cells

(\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime})_{\hat{k}}=\bigcup_{k+k^{\prime}=\hat{k}}\mathcal{C}_{k}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}.

The boundary matrices are defined via their action on the basis elements:

B^{\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}_{k+k^{\prime}}\left(c_{k}\times c^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}\right)=(B^{\mathcal{C}}_{k}c_{k})\times c^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}+(-1)^{k^{\prime}}c_{k}\times(B^{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}_{k^{\prime}}c^{\prime}_{k"})

But what does this actually mean?
For every pair of cells
c_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
and

Filling cycles to lift arbitrary graphs

Filling all simple cycles is not generally feasible as, in the worst case, the number of possible simple cycles on a graph may be exponential in the number of nodes.
Theoretically, chordless cycles have the same problem: Any graph can be made chordless by splitting each edge into two edges with a node in between.
However, in practice, the number of chordless cycles is often much smaller than the number of simple cycles.
Thus, chordless cycles have been used and proven to be useful to capture relevant structural information

5
Topology and Geometry on Cell Complexes

One of the key strengths of modelling data as and on cell complexes is the close connection between
cc
s and topology and geometry.
In this section, we will highlight some of the most important connections.

5.1
Homology

Homology is one of the key concepts of algebraic topology.
Intuitively, the
k
-th homology of a topological space or a cell complex encodes the
k
-dimensional loops/holes of the space.
This is most natural for
k=1
, where
1
-homology just encodes the ordinary loops of the complex.
For
k=0
, homology measures the connected components of the cell complex.

Definition 5.1

(Homology of a cell complex)
.

Given a cell complex
\mathcal{C}
with chain spaces
C_{i}
and boundary maps
B_{i}
, we denote by the
k
-th (real-valued) homology of
\mathcal{C}
the
\mathbb{R}
-vector space
H_{k}(\mathcal{C})
given by

H_{k}(\mathcal{C})=\ker B_{i}/\Ima B_{i+1}.

More generally, if we consider chain spaces
C_{i}^{\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}
with coefficients in an abstract ring
\mathcal{R}
and view
B^{\mathcal{R}}_{i}
as a map
B^{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\colon C_{i}^{\mathcal{R}}\to C_{i-1}^{\mathcal{R}}
, we define the
k
-th homology in
\mathcal{R}
-coefficients as the
\mathcal{R}
-module
H_{k}(\mathcal{C};\mathcal{R})
given by

H_{k}(\mathcal{C};\mathcal{R})=\ker B_{i}^{\mathcal{R}}/\Ima B^{\mathcal{R}}_{i+1}.

5.2
Hodge Laplacians and the relation to geometry

While
topology
and in particular
homology
deals with global information on cell complexes,
geometry
allows us to relate the structure to local properties on individual cells.
As we can view cell complexes as discretisations of manifolds, many geometrical notions naturally carry over to cell complexes.
The manifold Laplacian
\Delta
of a function
f
encodes for every point
x
the difference of the value of
f
on
x
and on a small neighbourhood of
x
.
Analogously, the
0
-Laplacian
L_{0}
of a cell complex
\mathcal{C}
takes a function
f\in C_{0}
on
0
-cells, i.e. in the
0
-th signal/chain space of
\mathcal{C}
and returns, for every
0
-cell
c^{i}_{0}
, the difference of
f
on
c^{i}_{0}
and its neighbours:

\displaystyle L_{0}(f)(c_{0}^{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c_{1}^{h}\in\mathcal{C}_{1}:\partial(c_{1}^{h})=\{c_{0}^{i},c_{0}^{j}\}}f(c_{0}^{j})-f(c_{0}^{i})

(5.4)

\displaystyle=B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}c_{0}^{i}.

(5.5)

As a matrix, we can thus write the
0
-Laplacian in terms of the boundary operators
L_{0}=B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}
.
The
0
-Laplacian corresponds to the ordinary graph Laplacian
L=D-A
on the underlying graph of
\mathcal{C}
.
The associated quadratic form

C_{0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\quad f\mapsto f^{\top}Lf=\langle B_{1}^{\top}f,B_{1}^{\top}f\rangle_{1}=\lVert B_{1}^{\top}f\rVert^{2}

is the
total variation
of the node signal
f
and can be viewed as some form of energy where
\langle-,-\rangle_{k}
denotes the inner product on the space on the
k
-th signal space
C_{k}
.

While the
0
-Laplacian operates on
0
-cell signals, we now look for a generalisation
L_{1}\colon C_{k}\to C_{k}
sending signals on
k
-cells to signals on
k
-cells.
The naive generalisation of
Equation

5.5
would be what we call the Up-
k
-Laplacian,

L_{k}^{\text{up}}=B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}.

The Up-Laplacian, together with the
Down
-Laplacian forms the Hodge Laplacian or Combinatorial Laplacian:

Definition 5.6

(Hodge Laplacian on Cell Complexes)
.

Given a cell complex
\mathcal{C}
with boundary matrices
B_{i}
, the
k
-th Hodge Laplacian
is the matrix given by

L_{k}=\underbrace{B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}}_{L_{k}^{\text{up}}}+\underbrace{B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}}_{L_{k}^{\text{down}}}.

Again, we can interpret the Hodge Laplacian as a quadratic form
L_{k}\colon C_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,

L_{k}\colon f_{k}\mapsto f_{k}^{\top}L_{k}f_{k}=\langle B_{k+1}^{\top}f_{k},B_{k+1}^{\top}f_{k}\rangle_{k+1}+\langle B_{k}f_{k},B_{k}f_{k}\rangle_{k-1}=\lVert B_{k+1}^{\top}f_{k}\rVert^{2}+\lVert B_{k}f_{k}\rVert^{2}

measuring some notion of variation or smoothness of the signal on
k
-cells.

The spectrum of the Hodge Laplacians

The eigenspectra of the Hodge Laplacian and their corresponding eigenvectors have a meaningful underlying structure and are useful for various tasks.
Let us first assume that
v
is an eigenvector for eigenvalue
\lambda>0
of the up-Laplacian.
We then have that

\lambda v=L_{k}^{\text{up}}v=B_{k+1}(B_{k+1}^{\top}v)

and hence
v
is in
\Ima B_{k+1}
.
We remember that we constructed the boundary matrices of cell complexes in such a way that the composition of two boundary matrices returns 0, i.e.
B_{k}B_{k+1}=0
for all
k>1
.
When we now evaluate the down-Laplacian on
v
, we obtain

L^{\text{down}}_{k}v=B_{k}^{\top}B_{k}v=\frac{1}{\lambda}B_{k}^{\top}\underbrace{B_{k}B_{k+1}}_{=0}B_{k+1}^{\top}=0

and any non-zero eigenvector of
L_{k}^{\text{up}}
is in the kernel of
L^{\text{down}}_{k}
and thus an eigenvector for the same eigenvalue of the Hodge Laplacian
L_{k}
.
Furthermore, there is a correspondence between the non-zero eigenpairs
(v,\lambda)
of
L_{k}^{\text{up}}
and non-zero eigenpairs
(B_{k+1}^{\top}v,\lambda)
of
L_{k+1}^{\text{down}}
:

L_{k+1}^{\text{down}}B_{k+1}^{\top}v=B_{k+1}^{\top}\underbrace{B_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{\top}}_{L_{k}^{\text{up}}}v=\lambda B_{k+1}^{\top}v.

All of the above holds analogously for the down-Laplacian.
Given two eigenvectors
v
and
v^{\prime}
with eigenvalues
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}>0
of
L_{k}^{\text{down}}
and
L^{\text{up}}_{k}
respectively, we can see that they are orthogonal by computing their inner product

\langle v,v^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{1}{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}v^{\top}B_{k}^{\top}\underbrace{B_{k}B_{k+1}}_{=0}B_{k+1}^{\top}v^{\prime}=0.

Combining the above observations gives rise to the following structure theorem of the signal spaces.

Theorem 5.7
.

Given a cell complex
\mathcal{C}
with Hodge Laplacians
L_{k}
, signal/chain spaces
C_{k}
and boundary matrices
B_{k}
, there is an orthogonal decomposition

\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}\cong C_{k}=\underbrace{\Ima B_{k+1}}_{\text{curl}}\oplus\underbrace{\Ima B_{k}^{\top}}_{\text{gradient}}\oplus\underbrace{\ker L_{k}}_{\text{harmonic}}

and there exists a basis of
C_{k}
of eigenvectors of
L_{k}
respecting this decomposition.

For an illustration of this, see
Figure

.

The total chain space and the Dirac operator

Given an
n
-dimensional cell complex
\mathcal{C}
, we will call the direct sum of the individual chain spaces of
\mathcal{C}
the
total chain space
or
topological spinor
of
\mathcal{C}

\mathbf{C}=C_{0}\oplus C_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus C_{n}

The total chain space encodes all possible combinations of signals on the nodes, edges,
2
-cells and so on.
We can view every element
x
of
\mathbf{C}
as a vector
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{\top}
where
x_{0}\in C_{0}
is a node signal,
x_{1}\in C_{1}
an edge signal, and so on.
We can define the total boundary operator
\mathbf{B}\colon\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
acting on the total chain spaces:

\mathbf{B}=B_{1}\oplus B_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&0&B_{2}&0&0\\
0&0&0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\ddots&0&B_{n}&\\
0&0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}

This leads us to the construction of the total Hodge Laplacian
\mathbf{L}\colon\mathbf{C}\to\mathbf{C}
as the direct sum of the individual Hodge Laplacians, or simply as
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
:

\mathbf{L}=L_{0}\oplus L_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus L_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}L_{0}&0&0&0\\
0&L_{1}&0&0\\
0&0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&0&L_{n}\end{pmatrix}

Analogously
This leads us to the definition of the Dirac operator
[
9
]
as the of the sum of the total boundary and its transpose:

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}^{\top}=B_{1}^{\top}\oplus\left(B_{1}+B_{2}^{\top}\right)\oplus\dots\oplus\left(B_{n-1}\oplus B_{n}^{\top}\right)\oplus B_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&B_{1}&0&0&0\\
B_{1}^{\top}&0&B_{2}&0&0\\
0&B_{2}^{\top}&0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\ddots&0&B_{n}&\\
0&0&0&B_{n}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix}

We have that
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}
because of the individual relations
B_{k-1}B_{k}=0
.
Thus, we can rewrite the total Hodge Laplacian as follows:

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}+\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\top}=\left(\mathbf{B}^{\top}+\mathbf{B}\right)^{2}=\mathbf{D}^{2}.

We see that the Dirac operator thus acts as some form of generalised
‘square root’
of the total Hodge Laplacian.
Such a relation is only possible by passing to the total chain spaces.
The total Dirac operator and its
k
-dimensional counterparts
D_{k}=B_{k}\oplus B_{k}^{\top}
can be used for processing of joint signals on
k
-cells and
(k-1)
-cells, analogously to how the Hodge Laplacian is used in topological signal processing
[
9
]
, cf.
Section

7
.

6
Weights and/or Non-Oriented Data

So far, we have assumed all cells in the same dimension to be equal.
However, in many cases, cells are not uniform and carry the same importance, but have different
weights
.
Given different applications and dimensions, these weights can represent very different quantities.
For example, on graphs, edge weights can represent a distance or node similarity measure.

In this work, we will look at weights as a similarity or strength measure (rather than a distance): As a weight trends to zero, the cell vanishes – which is exactly what we see in e.g. the adjacency matrix.
To weigh a complex by distance, we use the inverse of the distance as a weight.

While weight vectors would have the same shape as signal vectors, they are not conceptually elements of the chain space as weights do not have an orientation — instead, they are part of the structure of the cell complex itself.
To avoid confusion, we represent weights as a diagonal weight matrices
W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}\times\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
.

Weighted boundary matrices and Hodge Laplacians

For signal processing and to relate the chain spaces, we will define weighted boundary matrices and weighted
k
-Hodge Laplacians.
After giving the definition, we will explain the intuition that motivates it.
In doing so, we will also explore the interpretation of chains in the unweighted case.

The following definition is a generalization of the special case introduced in
[
42
]
.

Definition 6.1

(Weighted Boundary Matrix)
.

Given a weighted cell complex
\mathcal{C}
with boundary matrices
B_{i}
and weight matrices
W_{i}
, the
k
-th
weighted boundary matrix

B_{k}^{W}
is given by

B_{k}^{W}=W_{k-1}^{-\frac{1}{2}}B_{k}W_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Definition 6.2

(Weighted Hodge Laplacian)
.

Given a weighted cell complex
\mathcal{C}
with weighted boundary matrices
B_{k}^{W}
, the
k
-th
weighted Hodge Laplacian
is given by

L_{k}^{W}=\underbrace{B_{k+1}^{W}B_{k+1}^{W\top}}_{{L_{k}^{W}}^{\text{up}}}+\underbrace{B_{k}^{W\top}B_{k}^{W}}_{{L_{k}^{W}}^{\text{down}}}.

With these definitions, we generalize both the weighted graph Laplacian and the unweighted Hodge Laplacian and boundary maps.

The weighted graph Laplacian
L^{W}=D^{W}-W
can be obtained by setting
W_{0}=I
and
(W_{1})_{(u,v),(u,v)}=W_{u,v}
.
The unweighted case can be obtained by setting all weights to
1
.
Furthermore, the relationship between the weighted Hodge Laplacian and the weighted boundaries is the same as in the unweighted case.
Finally, like in the unweighted case, the boundary of a boundary is
0
, i.e.,
B_{k}^{W}B_{k+1}^{W}=0
.

Now that we have confirmed that the weighted Hodge Laplacian is, indeed, a generalization of the unweighted Hodge Laplacian, we will discuss the intuition behind weights and signals in the weighted case.

Intuition behind the weighted Hodge Laplacian

We consider the intuition of electrical systems, beginning with the unweighted case
W_{1}=I_{n}
.
Given a set of electrical potentials
v\in C_{0}
(in Volt) in a circuit where the edges are resistors, we can calculate the power
P
lost on the resistors (in Watts; voltage multiplied with current) via the Hodge Laplacian:
The voltage (difference in potentials)
U
(in Volt) along an edge and the electrical current
I
(in Ampère) along an edge are both proportional to
B_{1}^{\top}v
, i.e.
\alpha_{U}U=\alpha_{I}I=B_{1}^{\top}v
for constants
\alpha_{U}
and
\alpha_{I}
.
However, this means that we can interpret the
1
-chains
C_{1}
both as current as well as voltage on the individual edges, only differing by the scalar factors
\alpha_{U}
and
\alpha_{I}
.
We can calculate the power using the inner product induced by the Hodge Laplacian, i.e.,

\alpha_{U}\alpha_{I}v^{\top}L_{1}v=\alpha_{U}v^{\top}B_{1}B_{1}^{\top}v\alpha_{I}=U^{\top}I=P.

This means that we can interpret the element-wise product of
U
and
I
as the power lost over each individual edge,
U\cdot I=P_{e}
.
Taking the element-wise square root, we thus get
\sqrt{\alpha_{U}\alpha_{I}}B_{1}^{\top}v=\sqrt{U\cdot I}=\sqrt{P_{e}}
yielding a third interpretation of elements of the chain space.

This translates well to the weighted case:
We now decide that we want to interpret the space of
0
-chains as potentials and the space of
1
-chains as voltages.
Then, the weight matrix
W_{1}
represents the conductivity of the edges and translates voltage into current,
I=W_{1}U
.
Given node potentials
v
, we obtain the edge-wise voltage and current as

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle=B_{1}^{\top}v

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=W_{1}B_{1}^{\top}v.

Consequently, with our definition of the weighted boundary matrix from
Definition

6.1
, we get
\sqrt{U\cdot I}=B_{k+1}^{W\top}v
and the weighted Laplacian then indeed computes the power of the system:

P=U^{\top}I=v^{\top}B_{1}^{\top}W_{1}B_{1}v=v^{\top}Lv.

Note that we could decide to furthermore scale the potentials of the individual nodes using
W_{0}
.
This shows that in physical applications, considering the weighted Laplacian is very natural and allows us to drop the scalars
\alpha

6.1
Weights in geometric cell complexes

In geometric graphs, the size of cells (lengths, areas, volumes) constitutes a relevant property.
In particular, a cell complex and a subdivision of the same cell complex into more and smaller cells represent identical geometric objects and thus should be
‘similiar’
in terms of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the associated Hodge Laplacians.
For the unweighted Hodge Laplacian, this is not the case:
Figure

12
(a) shows the harmonic eigenvector of the unweighted Hodge Laplacian.
There, we witness that longer edges will get assigned comparably larger eigenvector entries than shorter edges at similar positions.

This is correct in the sense that these edges contribute more to the harmonic flow.
However, if we want to interpret
1
-chains as the
speed or flow rate of the flow
, the length should not matter.

Naively, we can try to account for this by setting the weights to the inverse of the edge lengths and then compute the eigenvectors of the associated weighted Hodge Laplacian.
This is shown in
Figure

12
(b), but longer edges still have a larger flow value.
To understand why this is the case, we need to consider the geometric relationships expressed by the weighted Laplacian.

Let
c^{W}_{H}
be a harmonic flow on the weighted complex, i.e.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=L^{W}_{1}c^{W}_{H}=B^{W\top}_{1}B^{W}_{1}c^{W}_{H}+B_{2}^{W}B_{2}^{W\top}c^{W}_{H}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow B^{W}_{1}c^{W}_{H}=0\quad\text{and}\quad B^{W\top}_{2}c^{W}_{H}=0.

First, note that the node weights
W_{0}
and the
2
-cell weights
W_{2}
do not influence the harmonic flow:
For a flow to be harmonic, its upper and lower differential have to be
0
, which is not influenced by the weights of the
0
- and
2
-cells.

With that in mind, we first consider the lower differential.
If we consider the values of the vector to be flow rates (e.g. in litres/second), we can directly calculate the net flow into each node
\text{flow}=B^{W}_{1}c^{W}_{H}
.
In other words, for the lower differential to be
0
, we require
B^{W}_{1}c^{W}_{H}=0
.

Now, we consider the upper differential.
Intuitively, the up-Laplacian penalises flow that circles around polygons and incentivises flow which passes around both sides of the polygon equally.
In slightly technical words, in the harmonic case this enforces the flow around the polygon to induce no net rotational momentum.
For this, we multiply the flow rates by the length of the edges and require
B_{2}^{T}W_{1}c_{H}=0
.

We now want to construct a Hodge Laplacian with
c_{H}
as a harmonic eigenvector and obtain a non-symmetrical Hodge Laplacian
{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}
:

{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}=B_{1}^{\top}W_{0}B_{1}+W_{1}^{-1}B_{2}W_{2}B_{2}^{\top}W_{1}^{-1}

(6.3)

There is a straightforward relation between the above construction
{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}
and the symmetric Hodge Laplacian
L_{k}^{W}=W_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}W_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Thus, for
\lambda=0
, every eigenvector
v
of
L_{k}^{W}
has a corresponding eigenvector
v^{\prime}=W_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}v
of
{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}
.

Figure

12
(c) shows the harmonic eigenvector of
{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}
, where the values now correspond to the flow speed.

Thus, the eigenvectors of the weighted symmetric Hodge Laplacian (and thus the
k
-chains) are, again, an intermediate between the lower-order interpretation of flow speed and the higher-order interpretation of flow mass.
A higher cell weight will make the cell have more influence in computing the down-Laplacian and the associated down-energy and less importance in computing the up-Laplacian.
This matches the intuition that potential differences along short edges (and thus close nodes) and round flow along long edges will result in a high Hodge Laplacian energy.

Note that the asymmetrical weighting of the non-symmetrical Hodge Laplacian
{L^{\prime}}_{k}^{W}
leads to a Hodge decomposition that is not orthogonal with the standard inner product.
However, we can define an inner product that is weighted accordingly and for which the Hodge decomposition is orthogonal.

Graph Signal Processing

Topological Signal Processing is based on the Eigendecomposition of the Hodge Laplacian:

L_{k}=U^{\top}_{k}\Lambda_{k}U_{k}

where

The Eigenbasis

Consequently, we can define filters based on the eigenvalues that are defined by a filter function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

Given a signal

\hat{x}=U_{k}f(\Lambda_{k})U_{k}^{\top}x

(7.2)

In many cases, the exact eigendecomposition is difficult to compute.
However, common filtering operations can be performed more efficiently.
For example, a low-pass filter with
f(\lambda)=1-\lambda

\hat{x}=(I-L_{k})x

Moreover, the

\Ima B_{k}^{\top}

Toplogical trajectory classification

Many observations in the real world can be represented as trajectories, e.g. the movement of taxis

Current topological trajectory classification methods embed trajectories into a simplicial complex and use the homology or harmonic projection

For spaces without much structure, such as the ocean, the triangulation works well.

For example, in a city, the road network can be represented by intersections (nodes), edges (roads), and blocks (polygons).
This principle transfers well to other geographic networks such as internet cables, power grids, or water pipes; and to regional and national scales.

Except for some special cases such as tunnels, the blocks (polygons) are simply the windows in the geographic embedding of the network.
If these cases do not represent a hole, they are usually small and can be spanned by properly configured cells.
In a simplicial complex, the blocks have to be triangulated, adding spurious edges.

Topological pattern
here refers to loops, holes and voids across different scales, regions of low density surrounded by high-density regions, and complex connectivity patterns.

By tracking the homology of the complex across the sequence, we end up with something called the

Outlook and Challenges

In this section, we will identify five key challenges for the future of research on computational methods on cell complexes:

1.

Bringing data in cell complex form.

2.

Creating a generative cell complex model zoo like we have for graphs.

3.

Controlling the computational complexity and overhead with respect to graphs.

4.

Finding meaning in the higher-dimensions of cell complexes.

5.

Providing benchmark data sets and a standard data format.

We will now discuss the above identified points one-by-one:

Obtaining cell complexes from data

Cell complexes are powerful models that can abstract many real-world relationships.
However, data in the wild often does not come in the form of cell complexes.
While there are many methods to turn data sets into graphs, simplicial complexes or even hypergraphs, similar techniques for cell complexes are underdeveloped.
This presents a significant challenge for practitioners.
Thus, a very important problem remains how to meaningfully abstract data to take the form of cell complexes.
The challenge in obtaining cell complexes lies in the sophisticated structure presented by the boundary maps.
While there is a single possible hyperedge connecting all
n
nodes of a fully connected graph, there are
(n-1)/2!
different possibilities for
n>2
for attaching a single
2
-cell connecting all nodes.

Generative models for cell complexes

There is a wide variety of generative models for graphs that capture different aspects, such as community structure, degree distribution, or local neighborhoods.
However, for cell complexes, there exists only a model to capture the number of cells without further structure
[
27
]
(analogous to the Erdős–Rényi model), or generalisations of Delaunay triangulations of random point clouds.

Part of the challenge to create a meaningful generative model for cell complexes is identifying the
right properties to capture
.
As
cc
s are more complex than graphs, there are more possible properties to choose from.
Locally, we can look at the number of faces and cofaces of cells, the number of lower- and upper-adjacent cells, and the size of the boundary of
2
-cells.
If possible, preserving local neighborhood structure would be very interesting as a more nuanced null model.
Globally, we have topological properties such as the Betti numbers, and (upper) connectedness in every dimension, and geometric properties like curvature.

Some straightforward properties on graphs have multiple possible generalizations to cell complexes: For example, a block structure could be modeled via cells spanning an individual block vs. multiple blocks, but the combinatorial complexity means the number of cells that possibly span multiple blocks is, in general, much larger than the number of cells that span a single block.

Thus, a key challenge for future research is to create a zoo of generative cell complexes models like we have for graphs. This will make it easier and more transparent to benchmark current and future computational cell complex methods on flexible and meaningful synthetic data.

Conquering computational complexity

A major challenge for computational methods on cell complexes is their complexity.
While many methods behave nicely on graphs, increasing the dimension to higher-order cell complexes potentially introduced an exceedingly large number of possible cells and possible combinations to consider.

This challenge has to be addressed at two separate steps:
First, focus has to be put on extracting meaningful
sparse
higher-order cell complexes.

This is often not easy, as methods either produce very few, or far too many higher-order cells:
Whereas the possible number of edges on a graph is quadratic in the number of nodes, the number of possible
2
-cells (simple cycles) is exponential.
While there already exist some methods for
2
-cell complexes,
[
2
,
6
,
26
]
, extracting sparse cell complexes of dimension
3
and above is still virtually uncharted territory.
Second, once a cell complex is constructed, computational methods have to take in account the possible large size of the cell sets and thus make limited use of expensive computation steps polynomial in the number of cells.

Finding meaning in higher dimensions

Cell complexes are incredibly versatile tools that can model relationships in arbitrary dimensions.
However, it is not always clear what these relations would mean on real-world datasets.
While
0
-cells have a clear interpretation as single data points, locations, or points of interest.

1
-cells represent semantic, local, or geometric connection between
0
-cells.
In some applications dealing with flows,
2
-cells can keep track of the flow around a certain object or area.
It is a challenge for future work to unlock the full potential of the cell complex model by finding meaningful interpretations of the
true
higher-order nature of cell complexes beyond dimension
2
.
This would mean being able to leverage the multi-dimensional interactions on data sets through the powerful structure in the form of boundary operators.

Benchmark data sets and standard data formats

Standard benchmark data sets are common in areas like image processing (ImageNet,
[
39
]
), point cloud learning (ShapeNet,
[
12
]
or graph learning (TUDataset
[
32
]
).
Having benchmark datasets is important because a) they provide a way to meaningfully assess the strength of a newly proposed method and b) they stimulate competition and encourage development of novel and innovative methods in the relevant fields.
Despite all of this, there are no benchmark data sets yet for computational methods on cell complexes.
Even for the comparatively popular simplicial complexes, general benchmark sets are lacking and most of the time just taken from graph learning.
The introduction of benchmark sets for cell complex data would thus be an important next step for the computational cell complex community and provide the cornerstone for promising future research.

Closely related is the adoption of a common data structure for cell complexes.
While packages like TopoX
[
21
]
or cell-flower
[
26
]
have provide formats for cell complexes, there is no commonly adapted computational representation further hindering availability of standard cell complex data sets.
Thus, providing specifications of a universal cell complex data format is another task to be solved by a future comprehensive computational cell complex benchmark set.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced abstract regular cell complexes with a self-contained definition.
We have also given a comprehensive overview of state-of-the-art methods, some of which we generalized from simplicial complexes to cell complexes.

In conclusion, cell complexes are a powerful abstraction that allow for the representation of geometrically or combinatorially structured data and provide meaningful computational tools.
Methods on and for cell complexes cover a wide variety of applications, but are still underexplored and underdeveloped.
Consequently, there is a plethora of problems and methods to explore in future research.

Appendix A
Abstract Regular Cell Complexes of Arbitrary Dimension

In this section, we will give a purely combinatorial and algebraic definition of what we call abstract regular cell complexes.
For this, we first need to introduce some very basic notions from homological algebra.
The underlying idea is that we will associate an algebraic object to each dimension of the regular cell complex.
This will later allow us to express the relation between the
k
-cells and
(k-1)
-cells in terms of a homomorphism between the associated algebraic objects.

Definition A.1

(Abstract regular cell complex)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a positive integer and
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{*},B_{*})
a pair of a finite sequence of ordered non-empty sets
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\{c_{k}^{1},c_{k}^{2},\dots\}
for
0\leq k\leq n
called
cells
and matrices
B_{k}\in\{0,\pm 1\}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k-1}|\times|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
for
1\leq k\leq n
called
boundary matrices
. Given a cell
c_{k}^{i}
, we call the set of cells
c_{k-1}^{j}
associated to the non-zero entries
B_{k}^{j,i}
of the
i
-th row of
B_{k}
the
boundary

\partial c_{k}^{i}
of
c_{k}^{i}
and write
\partial S=\bigcup_{c_{k}^{i}\in S}\partial c_{k}^{i}
for a set of cells
S
.
We now call
\mathcal{C}
an
abstract regular cell complex
if

1.

All columns of
B_{1}
have exactly one positive and one negative entry.

2.

For every cell
c^{i}_{k}\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
and sets of cells
\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{k-i}=\partial^{i}\{c^{i}_{k}\}
for
0\leq i\leq k
,
the associated pair
(\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{*},\hat{B}_{*})
with boundary matrices
\hat{B}_{l}\colon\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{l}|}\to\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{l-1}|}
restricted from
B_{l}
fulfills

(a)

\ker B_{k}=0
and
\ker\hat{B}_{l-1}=\Ima\hat{B}_{l}
for all
2\leq l\leq k
,

(b)

\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{0}|}/\Ima\hat{B}_{1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark A.2

(On the definition of abstract cell complexes)
.

We will now try to give an intuition behind the above definition.
The integer
n
denotes the dimension, i.e. the highest dimension of cells of the
cc

\mathcal{C}
.
The set
\mathcal{C}_{k}
is the set of abstract
k
-cells which we assume is ordered and finite.
The order on
\mathcal{C}_{k}
gives a canonical association between entries
B_{k}^{i,j}
of the boundary matrix
B_{k}
and pairs of cells
(c_{k-1}^{i},c_{k}^{j})\in\mathcal{C}_{k-1}\times\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
The entry
B_{k}^{i,j}
then encodes whether
c_{k-1}^{i}
is not contained in the boundary of
c_{k}^{j}
(i.e.
B_{k}^{i,j}=0
), or is contained in the boundary with matching orientation (i.e.
B_{k}^{i,j}=1
) or opposite orientation (i.e.
B_{k}^{i,j}=-1
).
We can view this definition as building a cell complex iteratively:
We start with
0
-cells, which are just abstract points without any conditions.
In the next step we will attach
1
-cells
c_{1}^{j}
according to the entries
B_{1}^{-,j}
, which assigns a
\pm 1
to the cells on the boundary of
c^{i}_{1}
.
This then continues for arbitrary
k
-cells which are attached to
(k-1)
-cells.

However, the definition does not allow for arbitrary attachments.
In the usual definition of topological regular cell complexes, we allow only for the attachment of
k
-disks, i.e.
D_{k}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:|x|\leq 1\}
.

k
-disks are contractible spaces with vanishing homology in degree above
0
.
Condition
2
is the equivalent of this in purely algebraic terms:
For every cell
c_{k}^{i}
, we consider the minimal cell complex
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
containing
c_{k}^{i}
, i.e.
c_{k}^{i}
, its boundary, the boundary of its boundary, etc.
To this, we can associate a cellular chain complex

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{0}|}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{1}|}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{k-1}|}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{Z}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{k-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{k}}

Condition
2a
now says that the homology of the above chain complex vanishes in degree above
0
, which corresponds to the associated space being contractible.
Condition
2b
,
\mathbb{Z}^{|\hat{\mathcal{C}}_{1}|}/\Ima\hat{B}_{1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
now asks for the
0
-homology to be
\mathbb{Z}
, which corresponds to the associated space being connected.
Because it is not computationally possible to algorithmically determine whether a given simplicial complex is a
n
-sphere for general
n
s
[
49
, Section 10, a theorem of S.P. Novikov]
, this condition cannot be equivalent to
cw
complexes.
However, it is indistinguishable for the computational aspects discussed in this papers.
Finally, condition
1
is a bookkeeping condition: There is no canonical orientation for
k
-cells for
k\leq 1
and we can thus flip their orientation by multiplying the associated entries by
-1
in
B_{k}
and
B_{k+1}
.
However, the
‘orientation’
of
0
-cells corresponds to a scalar and we can enforce a consistent orientation by requiring every edge to have one head (entry
+1
) and one tail (entry
-1
). (Condition
2
already ensures all
1
-cells to connect precisely two
0
-cells.)

Definition A.3

(Signal/Chain space of a cell complex)
.

Given an abstract regular cell complex
(\mathcal{C}_{*},B_{*})
of dimension
n
, we have an associated sequence of
signal/chain spaces

C_{k}=\mathbb{R}^{|C_{k}|}
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{n}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{n-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{n}}

The
k
-th signal space

encodes real-valued signals on the
k
-cells of
\mathcal{C}
.
I.e., we can think of
C_{0}
as node signals, elements of
C_{1}
represent oriented signals on the edges like flows, and so on.
We will identify
\mathcal{C}_{k}
with the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
and thus think of
c_{k}^{i}=e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}=C_{k}
being the

-th basis vector.
The matrices
B_{k}
are then boundary maps between the signal spaces
C_{k}\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
.
When looking at the combined chain space

, we can view
\mathbf{B}=B_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus B_{n}
as the combined boundary matrix.
The above sequence suffices the conditions of being something called a

, namely that
B_{k-1}\circ B_{k}=0
for
1\leq k\leq n
, or equivalently
\mathbf{B}^{2}=0
.
The following lemma verifies this condition.

Lemma A.4
.

The above definition of chains on dimensional
cc
s together with the boundary maps forms a chain complex
(C_{*},B_{*})
.

Proof.

We will identify a cell
c_{k}^{i}
with the associated basis element in
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{C}_{k}|}
.
It suffices to show that
B_{k-1}B_{k}c_{k}^{i}=0
for arbitrary cells
c_{k}^{i}
for
k\leq 2
.
Because of the definition, we can work in the smallest subcomplex
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
containing
c_{k}^{i}
with associated chain spaces

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{C}_{k-2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{C}_{k-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{C}_{k}=\langle c_{k}^{i}\rangle\cong\mathbb{R}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{k-2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{k-1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hat{B}_{k}}

Because of condition
2a
of
Definition

A.1
, we have that

\ker\hat{B}_{k-1}=\Ima\hat{B}_{k}

and thus
0=\hat{B}_{k-1}\hat{B}_{k}c_{k}^{i}=B_{k-1}B_{k}c_{k}^{i}
which concludes the proof.
∎

We will now show that our above construction of abstract regular cell complexes corresponds to
cw
complexes in a precise way.
This means that topological objects correspond to
arcc
without the need for a detour to point-set topology, making them an ideal fit for computational applications in signal processing, network science, and so on.
A full correspondence between a computational definition (as ours) and a topological definition is not possible to to some results on non-decidability of some sphere homeomorphism problem in higher-dimensions,
[
49
, Par. 10,
‘A theorem by Novikov’
]
.
These possible exceptions are however not relevant from a computational perspective.

Theorem A.5
.

For every regular
cw
complex
X
there is an abstract regular cell complex
\mathcal{C}_{X}
with the same boundary matrices. For every abstract regular cell complex
\mathcal{C}
of dimension up to
2
there is a regular
cw
complex
X_{\mathcal{C}}
with the same boundary matrices.

Proof.

We first assume that we are given a regular
cw
complex
X
with cells
\mathcal{C}^{X}_{k}
.
We can choose an arbitrary orientation and ordering of cells to obtain boundary matrices
B_{k}^{X}
.
We now construct our abstract regular cell complex by setting
\mathcal{C}_{k}=\mathcal{C}^{X}_{k}
and use the same ordering as the boundary
B_{k}^{X}
.
Furthermore, we set
B_{k}=B_{k}^{X}
.
We claim that
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{*},B_{*})
is an abstract regular cell complex.
In regular cell complexes, all attaching maps are homeomorphisms onto their image. Thus, their mapping degrees are
\pm 1
and hence the entries of the boundary matrices are in
\{0,\pm 1\}
.
Condition
1
of
Definition

A.1
means that every
1
-cell is connected to exactly one
0
-cell with degree
1
and one
0
-cell with degree
-1
, which holds by convention.
Thus condition
2
remains.
Because every
k
-cell is contractible, its associated homology is trivial except in degree
0
where it is
\mathbb{Z}
.
This is equivalent to the second condition and we conclude the first part of the proof.

Now we assume we are given an abstract regular cell complex
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}_{*},B_{*})
of dimension up to
2
and we want to construct a regular
cw
with the same boundary matrices.
For the first two dimensions, this is straight-forward: For every 0-cell in
\mathcal{C}_{0}
we add a point to the
0
-skeleton of
X
, and for every
1
-cell
c_{1}^{i}
of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
attached to the two
0
-cells specified by the column
B^{-,i}_{1}
of the boundary matrix.
Now we consider a
2
-cell
c_{2}^{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
.
We have that
B_{2}c_{2}^{i}=\sum\pm c_{1}^{j}
and
B_{1}B_{2}c_{2}^{i}=B_{1}\sum\pm c_{1}^{j}=0
.
Thus we know that this collection of oriented
1
-cells corresponds to a collection of cycles in
X_{1}
. From condition 2 of
Definition

A.1
we even know that it is a single simple cycle.
We can then attach a cell
\bar{c}_{2}^{i}
to
X_{1}
by an attaching map to this simple cycle.
Doing this for all cells
c_{2}^{i}
gives us a
X
with the desired cells and boundary matrices.
∎

Remark A.6

(Non-regular abstract cell complexes)
.

We note that the above definition is for abstract
regular
cell complex, i.e. an algebraic version of
regular
cell complexes.
This is not a problem for the goal of this paper, as both applications in signal processing as well as in network science call for
regular
cell complexes.
We do not know an analogous abstract yet concise definition of (non-regular) cell complexes.
The main obstacle to this is that while the chain complex of regular cell complexes determines its homotopy type, this is not the case for general (non-regular) cell complexes.
For example, the standard
cw
representation of a
2
-torus contains one
0
-cell, two
1
-cells, and one
2
-cell and is, on the level of chain complexes, indistinguishable from the wedge of a
2
-sphere with two
1
-spheres.
However, as every cell complex is homotopy equivalent to some regular cell complex (with potentially more cells), this does not present a problem in practice.

Appendix B
Regular
cw
Complexes and Combinatorial Complexes

We will now introduce and discuss the two most commonplace definitions of
cw
complexes and discuss their drawbacks for computational topology.
In particular, we propose that a definition of regular
cw
complexes for computational purposes must fulfil the following two requirements:

1.

The algebraic structure of the
cw
complex in terms of its boundary matrices and associated Hodge Laplacians must be easily obtainable from the definition.

2.

Checking whether a given object
X
satisfies the definition of
cw
must be computationally feasible.

3.

The definition must align with the underlying geometric intuition of cells representing real-world lines, areas, volumes, etc.

For the first point, all computational methods introduced or discussed in this paper rely on the boundary matrices or the Hodge Laplacians in some form or another.
Hence a definition which does not give us access to these matrices cannot be used in practice.
For the second point, it is very useful to be able to check whether a constructed object actually satisfies the given definition.
If this is not the case, we cannot give any theoretical guarantees for the correctness of the applied methods.
The standard modern definition uses the concept of pushout squares from category theory and builds the cell complexes iteratively over each dimension:

\bar{X_{i}}\cap X_{j}\not=\emph{}
only if
X_{j}
is a subset of
\bar{X_{i}}
,
X_{j}\subseteq\bar{X_{i}}

2.

For every subspace
X_{i}
, there is a homeomorphism from
\bar{X_{i}}
to a closed disk
D_{n_{i}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}:\lVert x\rVert\leq 1\}
restricting to a homeomorphism between
X_{i}
and the open disk
\mathring{D}_{n_{i}}
.

While this definition is very concise, it provides us with little insight on how to
construct
the cell complex.
In particular, using the first condition together with the dimensions
n_{i}
can help us piece together the face relations of the cell complex.
To compute the boundary maps, we would further need to extract some kind of orientations, which is made even more difficult by a direct application of the definition.

In summary, while the above definitions work well from a theoretical perspective of algebraic topology or category theory, they are not geared towards computational applications.

While the above definition is very easy to verify, it does not allow for a geometric intuition and does not have the structural properties needed for computational applications requiring the boundary matrices.
