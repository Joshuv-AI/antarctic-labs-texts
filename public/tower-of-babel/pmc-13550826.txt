Perioperative Hypophosphatemia Following a Fixed High-Dose Ferric Carboxymaltose Protocol in Staged Bilateral Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jaemin Lee, Minseok Jeong, Doo Sup Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeHypophosphatemia is an established effect of ferric carboxymaltose (FCM). We describe the perioperative phosphate trajectory across two staged operations under one fixed dose, and the completeness of replacement. The uncontrolled design cannot attribute the changes to FCM.Patients and MethodsSingle-center retrospective cohort of 216 patients undergoing short-interval staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty (TKA) under a fixed 1,500 mg FCM protocol, tranexamic acid not being routinely used, with ten perioperative serum phosphate measurements. The preoperative sample preceded the first knee by a median of 37 days, and by at least eight days in 202 patients (93.5%). The primary outcome was a nadir below 2.0 mg/dL; below 1.0 mg/dL was biochemically severe.ResultsA nadir below 2.0 mg/dL occurred in 196 patients (90.7%) and 62 (28.7%) reached the severe range. Phosphate fell biphasically, troughing two days after each knee. Among the 126 patients untreated before the second operation, the second-period nadir was deeper (1.60 versus 1.71 mg/dL, p < 0.001), as in the 98 threshold-matched patients. Higher FCM dose per kilogram was associated with a lower nadir, attenuating the female-sex association. Intravenous phosphate reached 111 of the 196 affected patients (56.6%); in 108 evaluable recipients it rose 0.33 mg/dL after the first dose against 0.21 mg/dL spontaneously in untreated patients, and 88 (81.5%) stayed below 2.0 mg/dL. A predischarge value existed in only 95 of 216 patients, leaving recovery unknown for over half.ConclusionHypophosphatemia under this fixed high-dose FCM protocol was near-universal, often severe, and biphasic; replacement reached just over half the affected patients and usually left phosphate below threshold at the next measurement. The uncontrolled design, absent symptom data, and atypical setting preclude causal inference; these hypothesis-generating findings motivate prospective evaluation of stage-wise phosphate monitoring and of lower-risk iron formulations.Keywords: ferric carboxymaltose, hypophosphatemia, total knee arthroplasty, fibroblast growth factor 23, patient blood management, intravenous iron

Introduction

Ferric carboxymaltose (FCM) has become a preferred intravenous iron across medical and surgical settings because a single infusion delivers a large dose and raises hemoglobin quickly, including before major lower-limb arthroplasty, where iron-deficiency anemia is common.1,2 In total knee arthroplasty (TKA) it improves hemoglobin recovery,3 and perioperative intravenous iron lowers the transfusion requirement.4 The same formulation carries a distinctive metabolic cost: it raises intact fibroblast growth factor 23, which drives renal phosphate wasting, suppresses calcitriol, and lowers serum phosphate.5–7 This effect is far stronger than with other agents,8 and its severity and persistence are well documented.9 Reported incidence reaches 50 to 75% in dedicated studies and up to 92% in pooled analyses, while lower-risk formulations such as ferric derisomaltose produce single-digit rates;10–12 severe and persistent hypophosphatemia can cause osteomalacia and fragility fracture.13,14

Most of this evidence comes from medical outpatients with inflammatory bowel disease, heart failure, or chronic kidney disease, who receive iron without surgical stress; the orthopedic perioperative picture is poorly described. A patient who undergoes staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty15,16 receives two surgical insults within a short interval, each carrying its own catabolic and inflammatory stress, on top of a fixed high cumulative iron dose. That configuration—two separated operations, one fixed dose given entirely around the first, and the same patient serving as the reference for both periods—is a natural model for how phosphate behaves when surgical stress is repeated without further iron. Perioperative iron trials rarely measure phosphate at all: a network meta-analysis of 34 such trials counted hypophosphatemia among its prespecified safety outcomes but reported no pooled estimate,1 and an orthopedic trial that did measure it sampled only near discharge, where the delayed nadir is easily missed.17 The single orthopedic series that examined it reported hypophosphatemia in 23% of hip-arthroplasty patients at the more lenient 2.5 mg/dL threshold,18 and in the perioperative DeFICIT trial prophylactic supplementation reduced the number of affected patients but did not prevent hypophosphatemia.19 The cohort described here also departs from contemporary blood-management practice in one important respect: tranexamic acid, whose adoption in Korea accompanied a national decline in transfusion after total knee arthroplasty20 and which reduces perioperative blood loss by either the intravenous or the topical route,21 was not routinely used, so the transfusion exposure reported below is higher than a contemporary reader would expect.

The incidence of hypophosphatemia after ferric carboxymaltose is already well established, and it is not the contribution of this study. What is missing from the orthopedic literature is a dense within-patient description of the phosphate trajectory across two short-interval operations under a single fixed dose, and an account of how completely the standard intravenous remedy reaches, and how far it corrects, the patients who qualify for it. We therefore examined patients undergoing short-interval staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty under a uniform 1,500 mg ferric carboxymaltose protocol, characterizing the incidence and severity of postoperative hypophosphatemia, the trajectory across ten perioperative timepoints, the nadir after each knee and its relation to length of stay, and the delivery and biochemical effect of intravenous phosphate replacement. Because the cohort is uncontrolled and every patient received the same protocol, the design cannot separate the contribution of ferric carboxymaltose from that of surgery, transfusion, perioperative fluids, or the second operation; every result below is descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study at Wonju Severance Christian Hospital, Yonsei University Wonju College of Medicine. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations (STROBE Statement, Supplementary Material). The Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved the study and waived informed consent for this record-based analysis. Because the cohort is uncontrolled, we frame every result as descriptive rather than causal.

Two previous analyses from our institution draw on the same consecutive staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty series: one compared spinal with general anesthesia for acute kidney injury and transfusion,22 the other described the persisting transfusion burden of the second knee under the same fixed high-dose iron protocol used here.23 All 207 patients of the first and all 210 of the second are contained within the present cohort, overlaps of 207 of 216 (95.8%) and 210 of 216 (97.2%). What differs is the outcome domain. Neither companion study measured, modeled, or reported serum phosphate, calcium, or any other index of mineral metabolism, and no phosphate-related hypothesis, outcome, model, table, or figure of the present report appears in either. Drawing on one source population, the baseline, transfusion, and length-of-stay descriptors of Table 1 necessarily resemble those already published; every phosphate and mineral-metabolism result here is new. The three cohorts were assembled under different eligibility and exclusion criteria and differ in size, and the present study holds a separate IRB approval.

Participants

We assessed consecutive patients aged 55 years or older who underwent elective staged bilateral primary total knee arthroplasty for osteoarthritis and received the institutional perioperative ferric carboxymaltose protocol between January 2020 and April 2026 (Figure 1). Eligibility required a cemented Persona knee implant (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA) and completion of both procedures within 14 days during a single admission. The implant restriction was applied because it defines the surgical exposure the institutional protocol was built around and keeps the operative insult homogeneous; it mirrors the source cohort rather than a post hoc selection but narrows the population. Of 301 patients assessed, we excluded 85—12 with a long-stem or non-cemented implant, 23 with an interval exceeding 14 days or surgery staged across separate admissions, 18 with incomplete operative records, and 32 with insufficient postoperative laboratory data—leaving 216. The two procedures were a median of seven days apart (range 7 to 14 days). Per-timepoint denominators are reported with the phosphate results.

Participant flow. Of 301 patients undergoing staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty assessed for eligibility, 85 were excluded (long-stem or non-cemented implant, 12; interval greater than 14 days or separate admissions, 23; incomplete operative records, 18; insufficient postoperative laboratory data, 32), leaving 216 in the analytic cohort.

Iron and Transfusion Protocol

The institutional protocol prescribed the same regimen for every patient: ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject; Vifor Pharma, St. Gallen, Switzerland) 500 mg about seven days before the first knee and 1,000 mg on the day of the first knee, a fixed cumulative dose of 1,500 mg, with no further iron for the second knee. Exposure is therefore the prescribed protocol rather than a verified per-patient administration record. The dose was fixed as a maximal perioperative blood-management regimen for staged bilateral surgery, not titrated to weight, hemoglobin, or a documented iron deficit; its consequences for exposure per kilogram are examined below. Tranexamic acid was not part of the institutional blood-management protocol and was not routinely used in this population. The stated reason was concern about thromboembolic risk in an older, predominantly female group with a high burden of cardiovascular comorbidity, but that rationale is not supported by contemporary evidence; the practice departs from current standards, and we treat it as a feature of the setting rather than a justified choice. Red-cell transfusion was triggered by a hemoglobin of 8.0 g/dL or lower, or by symptoms of anemia.

Administration was ascertained from the uniform institutional protocol and not from a per-patient medication or pharmacy field, which the research dataset does not contain for the iron infusions; we therefore cannot report how many patients received the full 1,500 mg, whether any infusion was delayed or incomplete, or whether any infusion reaction occurred.

Perioperative Timeline and Phosphate Measurement

The sequence was fixed: 500 mg ferric carboxymaltose about seven days before the first knee, the first knee with 1,000 mg on the same day, the second knee a median of seven days later with no further iron, and discharge a median of 15 days after admission. Supplementary Figure S1 shows the full timeline with the position of every scheduled blood sample and of intravenous phosphate administration.

Serum phosphate was measured at ten timepoints: before the first knee; on postoperative days 0, 1, 2, and 5 after each knee; and before discharge. All samples were analyzed in the hospital’s central clinical chemistry laboratory against the same reference intervals throughout (serum phosphate 2.5 to 4.5 mg/dL; calcium 8.6 to 10.0 mg/dL; potassium 3.5 to 5.1 mmol/L). The laboratory operated two automated platforms, assigned by ordering route rather than by any study procedure: the general chemistry service used a Roche cobas c702 (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) and the stat chemistry service a Siemens Atellica CH 930 (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), both measuring inorganic phosphate photometrically by the ammonium molybdate ultraviolet method. Platform use was therefore structured: the preoperative panel was analyzed on the general-chemistry platform in 212 of 216 patients (98.1%), 1,718 of the 1,722 in-hospital postoperative measurements (99.8%) on the stat platform, and the 95 predischarge samples divided between the two (42 and 53). An instrument difference therefore cannot be separated from the fall measured from the preoperative baseline, and only four patient-days carried a result from both platforms, too few to quantify their agreement. The postoperative comparisons are almost entirely insulated from it: the nadir defining the primary outcome was measured on the stat platform in 214 of 216 patients (99.1%), and both postoperative periods, on which the biphasic pattern rests, in all but four measurements.

Phosphate formed part of a standing perioperative panel drawn at protocol-defined timepoints for every patient, not ordered at the treating team’s discretion when a disturbance was suspected. A value was obtained at 2,033 of the 2,160 scheduled timepoints (94.1%), and at each of the eight in-hospital postoperative timepoints in 213 to 216 patients, so the incidence reported here is not inflated by preferential sampling of patients who appeared unwell. The predischarge sample is the exception, drawn only where a discharge panel was ordered, in 95 of 216 patients. Samples were drawn, transported, and processed under the laboratory’s standard procedure for this panel; tube type, transport time, and delay to centrifugation are not recorded.

The preoperative sample was the preadmission clinic panel; its date is reported in the Results, because its position relative to the first ferric carboxymaltose dose determines whether it is a pretreatment baseline. Postoperative samples came from the routine daily morning panel, except the day-0 sample after each operation, drawn once the operation had finished; clock times are not recorded, so the order of the first day-0 sample relative to that day’s 1,000 mg infusion cannot be established. Where a nominated day had no sample but an adjacent day did, the adjacent measured value was carried onto that timepoint—a last- or next-observation-carried-forward substitution of a real though differently dated measurement, affecting 15 of the 2,160 scheduled timepoints (0.7%). No value was model-based or statistically imputed, and no missing value was filled in any other way. Serum calcium, albumin, potassium, and creatinine-derived estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) were recorded on the same panels at the same ten timepoints.

Intravenous Phosphate Replacement

The institutional treatment indication was a measured serum phosphate of 2.0 mg/dL or lower at any timepoint, prompting intravenous monobasic potassium phosphate (Phosten; JW Pharmaceutical, Seoul, Republic of Korea; 136.1 mg/mL, 20 mL per ampoule, about 20 mmol per ampoule). We recorded the date and ampoule count of every administration relative to the operations and to the phosphate measurements. All phosphate replacement in this cohort was intravenous: no oral phosphate preparation was carried on the hospital formulary at any point in the study period, so oral supplementation could not have occurred and is not an unmeasured route of delivery. Orders written but not carried out, refusals, and contraindications were not recorded, so the delivery figures below describe administered treatment rather than clinician intent. Severe injection-site vascular pain interrupted some infusions; never counted and documented only qualitatively in free-text records, it is reported as an unquantified clinical observation with no analytic weight.

Variables and Definitions

The primary outcome was postoperative hypophosphatemia, defined as a per-patient postoperative phosphate nadir below 2.0 mg/dL, the nadir being the lowest of the nine postoperative values. Biochemically severe hypophosphatemia was a nadir below 1.0 mg/dL, and a milder threshold of 2.5 mg/dL was also tabulated to permit a like-for-like comparison with the orthopedic benchmark. The first- and second-period nadirs were the lowest values across the four timepoints after each knee. Two thresholds bear on the replacement analysis: the institutional indication was a real-time trigger—any single measured value of 2.0 mg/dL or lower—whereas for the cohort-level analysis we also classified patients retrospectively by a per-patient nadir below 2.0 mg/dL; both denominators are reported with the replacement results. Covariates included age, sex, body weight, body mass index, ferric carboxymaltose dose per kilogram (1,500 mg divided by body weight), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status, comorbidity, baseline phosphate, baseline eGFR, baseline hemoglobin, and operative time. Anemia followed World Health Organization thresholds (hemoglobin below 12 g/dL in women, 13 g/dL in men). Length of stay ran from admission to discharge for the single admission spanning both procedures, and major complications (reoperation, symptomatic venous thromboembolism, surgical-site infection) were ascertained from the same chart review.

Statistical Analysis

We summarized continuous variables as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. All analyses used observed values. Incidence was reported with 95% Wilson confidence intervals. We did not test the incidence against the external 23% orthopedic figure, which came from a different dose, surgery, sampling scheme, and threshold; instead we compare the two descriptively at a common 2.5 mg/dL definition. We modeled the ten-timepoint trajectory with a linear mixed-effects model with timepoint as a categorical fixed effect and a random patient intercept, which imposes a compound-symmetry covariance structure and uses all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption without imputation; because that structure assumes a constant within-patient correlation across time lags, we based the trajectory inference mainly on a prespecified family of paired contrasts—the nine adjacent timepoints and the matched second postoperative day—tested with Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and adjusted by the Holm method. The two period nadirs, and the baseline and predischarge values, were compared with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, reporting the paired sample size for each; with no prespecified equivalence margin, a non-significant comparison indicates no detectable difference rather than equivalence. To test whether the partial-recovery finding reflected selective availability of the predischarge value, we compared patients with and without it on nadir severity, length of stay, age, and last recorded phosphate.

Predictors of severe hypophosphatemia were examined with logistic regression on a parsimonious set of six covariates, chosen to keep at least ten events per variable and to avoid the collinearity between chronic kidney disease and eGFR, and confirmed with Firth penalized logistic regression; predictors of the continuous nadir were examined with linear regression on the same covariates. We did not model the primary 2.0 mg/dL outcome multivariably because it had only 20 non-events, so the regression results pertain to the severe 1.0 mg/dL outcome and to the continuous nadir; these coefficients are exploratory and unadjusted for multiplicity, and transfusion and operative time were left out to preserve events per variable, leaving their potential confounding uncontrolled. As exploratory analyses we related nadir severity to length of stay and transfusion, and described intravenous phosphate use across nadir-severity strata, testing the trend with the Cochran–Armitage test. Unpaired comparisons used the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables; nadir and length of stay were related by Spearman rank correlation. The sample comprised all eligible patients treated during the study period, and we performed no a priori sample-size calculation; the study is adequately sized for incidence estimation and for the repeated-measures trajectory, but underpowered for uncommon complications, weaker predictor associations, and subgroup comparisons, so estimates near the conventional significance threshold should be read with corresponding caution.

Seven analyses were added after peer review. They are post hoc, and identified as such again wherever reported in the Results: a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients whose preoperative sample preceded the first knee by at least eight days; a sensitivity analysis excluding pre-existing hypophosphatemia, separating prevalent from incident disturbance; a comparison of the two period nadirs restricted to patients untreated before the second operation, repeated in the subset who had nonetheless crossed the treatment threshold during the first period, matching on threshold crossing and not only on treatment; substitution of ferric carboxymaltose dose per kilogram and of body weight for body mass index in the regression models, with a further model entering body mass index and dose per kilogram together to document their collinearity; a paired comparison of the phosphate values bracketing the first replacement dose, with the equivalent contrast in untreated patients as a non-randomized comparator; and calendar-year trends, tested by Spearman rank correlation and the chi-square test. None was adjusted for multiplicity, and together they form a family in which some nominally significant results are expected by chance; they test the robustness of the primary description rather than support new claims. All computations were performed in Python 3 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) using the pandas, SciPy, and statsmodels libraries; all tests used a two-sided alpha of 0.05.

Results

A total of 216 patients met the eligibility criteria. Table 1 summarizes their baseline characteristics. Mean body weight was 63.9 ± 11.0 kg, so the fixed 1,500 mg dose corresponded to 24.2 ± 4.2 mg/kg, ranging from 15.8 to 41.7 mg/kg; women received a higher dose per kilogram than men (24.7 versus 21.5 mg/kg, p < 0.001). Anemia by World Health Organization criteria was present in 47 patients (21.8%) before surgery, and iron studies had been performed in only 26 patients (12.0%), among whom the median transferrin saturation was 31% (interquartile range 24 to 47) and 5 values fell below 20%; ferritin was not recorded for any patient. Ferric carboxymaltose was therefore given as a fixed institutional blood-management regimen rather than for individually confirmed iron deficiency.

The preoperative phosphate sample was drawn at the preadmission clinic a median of 37 days before the first knee (interquartile range 28 to 44 days; range 1 to 87 days), and at least eight days before it in 202 of 216 patients (93.5%). Since the protocol scheduled the 500 mg dose about seven days preoperatively, those 202 samples are very likely to precede any ferric carboxymaltose exposure, although infusion dates are not recorded per patient; in the remaining 14 the sample fell within seven days of surgery and may have followed the first dose. The postoperative day 0 sample after the first knee was drawn on the calendar day of that operation in all 216 patients, after the operation itself; because sampling times are not recorded, whether it preceded or followed the 1,000 mg infusion given the same day cannot be determined.

The phosphate nadir fell below 2.5 mg/dL in 207 patients (95.8%, 95% Wilson confidence interval [CI] 92.3 to 97.8), below 2.0 mg/dL in 196 (90.7%, 86.1 to 93.9), and below 1.0 mg/dL in 62 (28.7%, 23.1 to 35.1) (Supplementary Table S1). Five patients were already below 2.5 mg/dL and one below 2.0 mg/dL before surgery, so the disturbance was overwhelmingly incident rather than prevalent. Neither post hoc restriction materially changed the estimate (Supplementary Table S1): excluding those five gave 191 of 211 patients (90.5%, 85.8 to 93.8) below 2.0 mg/dL and 61 (28.9%, 23.2 to 35.4) severe, and restricting to the 202 patients whose preoperative sample preceded the first knee by at least eight days gave 188 (93.1%, 88.7 to 95.8) and 59 (29.2%, 23.4 to 35.8).

Serum phosphate followed a biphasic course across the ten timepoints (Figure 2, Table 2), the mean falling to a first trough two days after the first knee, recovering partially by the day of the second operation, then falling again two days after the second knee. Every prespecified Holm-adjusted contrast across adjacent timepoints and the matched second postoperative day was significant, and the omnibus mixed-effects test concurred (χ2 = 2,774.5, df = 9, p < 0.001; 2,033 observations). The second postoperative trough fell below the first (1.54 versus 1.64 mg/dL, Holm-adjusted p = 0.02), yet across the whole cohort the per-patient first- and second-period nadirs did not differ significantly (1.47 versus 1.45 mg/dL; Wilcoxon p = 0.18, n = 216). Categories are defined by that nadir as severe (below 1.0 mg/dL, 62 patients), moderate (1.0 to below 2.0 mg/dL, 134 patients), mild (2.0 to below 2.5 mg/dL, 11 patients), and no hypophosphatemia (2.5 mg/dL or above, 9 patients). Cumulatively, the nadir was below 2.0 mg/dL in 196 patients (90.7%) and below 2.5 mg/dL in 207 patients (95.8%).

Because 90 patients received intravenous phosphate before the second operation, the observed trajectory is a treated trajectory rather than untreated natural history, and the whole-cohort comparison is correspondingly attenuated. In a post hoc comparison restricted to the 126 patients who had received no intravenous phosphate before the second operation, the second-period nadir was lower than the first (1.60 versus 1.71 mg/dL; mean difference 0.11, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.18; Wilcoxon p < 0.001), and the matched second postoperative day showed the same pattern (1.72 versus 1.90 mg/dL, p < 0.001); 22 of these 126 patients received a first dose at or after the second operation, which could only have raised the second-period nadir and therefore biases against the difference observed, and 104 were never treated.

That comparison could in principle be produced by regression to the mean, because treatment was triggered by an extreme first-period value and the two subgroups are therefore selected on the outcome. Two observations argue against that reading. First, the subgroups are separated less by whether a patient reached the threshold than by whether clinicians acted on it: 98 of the 126 untreated patients (77.8%) had in fact recorded a first-period value at or below 2.0 mg/dL, as had all 90 treated patients. Second, repeating the post hoc comparison in those 98 untreated but threshold-eligible patients preserved the finding (first-period nadir 1.47 versus second-period nadir 1.37 mg/dL; mean difference 0.10, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.17; p < 0.001). In patients not treated between the operations, then, the second operative period was at least as severe as the first. We do not compare the two period nadirs within the treated subgroup, because those patients entered it by recording an extreme first-period value and any apparent improvement would be uninterpretable.

Serum calcium fell along with phosphate and recovered before discharge (Table 3), the paired fall from baseline to the second postoperative day being significant (p < 0.001). Serum albumin fell over the same period and accounts for most of that decline: albumin-corrected calcium fell only from 9.05 to 8.77 mg/dL, though this smaller fall was also significant (p < 0.001). The distinction matters: the per-patient postoperative nadir of total calcium fell below the lower reference limit of 8.6 mg/dL in 205 patients (94.9%), below 8.0 mg/dL in 128 (59.3%) and below 7.5 mg/dL in 41 (19.0%), whereas after correction for albumin it fell below 8.6 mg/dL in 146 patients (67.6%) and below 8.0 mg/dL in 27 (12.5%). The albumin-corrected figures are the more meaningful and we use them in interpretation.

Serum potassium showed no signal of potassium loading: the per-patient postoperative peak exceeded 5.1 mmol/L in 10 patients (4.6%) and 5.5 mmol/L in 4 (1.9%), two in each of the recipient and non-recipient groups, with no difference in the median peak (4.5 versus 4.6 mmol/L, p = 0.25); with only four events, this comparison is uninformative about rare hyperkalemia. Hypokalemia was commoner, with a postoperative value below 3.5 mmol/L in 69 patients (31.9%). Estimated glomerular filtration rate dipped on the first postoperative day after each knee and exceeded 80 mL/min/1.73 m2 at every other postoperative timepoint (Table 3); because creatinine-based estimates rise with the loss of muscle mass and with dilution, this should not be read as improved renal function.

A predischarge phosphate was available in 95 of 216 patients, so recovery before discharge is unknown for the other 121 (56.0%). Patients with and without that value had similar nadir severity (median nadir 1.16 versus 1.23 mg/dL, p = 0.45; severe rate 31.6% versus 26.4%, p = 0.50) and similar age (median 76 versus 75 years, p = 0.49). Those with a predischarge value stayed longer (median 15 versus 14 days, p < 0.001) and had a higher last recorded phosphate than the unmeasured patients (median 2.46 versus 1.71 mg/dL, p < 0.001), so the measured subgroup was selected by a longer stay and its values overstate recovery for the full cohort. Among the 95 measured patients, the mean predischarge phosphate was 2.55 mg/dL, below the baseline value (p < 0.001, n = 95), and 30 remained below 2.0 mg/dL.

In the logistic model for severe hypophosphatemia, higher baseline phosphate and higher body mass index were associated with lower odds, and female sex with higher odds (Table 4). In the linear model for the continuous nadir, female sex, lower body mass index, lower baseline phosphate, and higher baseline hemoglobin were each associated with a lower nadir (Table 4). Because every patient received the same absolute dose, body mass index, body weight, and dose per kilogram carry overlapping information (r = −0.80 between dose per kilogram and body mass index), so the sex association may partly reflect exposure per kilogram. In a post hoc model substituting dose per kilogram for body mass index, dose per kilogram was associated with severe hypophosphatemia and with a lower continuous nadir, and the sex association was attenuated but not abolished: the odds ratio for female sex fell from 2.76 to 1.92 and was no longer significant, whereas in the linear model it remained significant with a coefficient reduced by about 30% (Table 4). Substituting body weight gave a closely similar pattern, except that the linear-model association with female sex fell just short of significance (p = 0.057); because dose per kilogram is 1,500 mg divided by weight, that model is a reparameterization rather than an independent check. Entering both together left neither significant, as their collinearity predicts, and dose per kilogram also correlated with the nadir directly (Spearman rho −0.20, p = 0.003). The pattern of the primary model was concordant under Firth penalized logistic regression, although the sex and body mass index estimates sat near the null boundary (female sex 2.56, 95% CI 1.01 to 6.53; body mass index 0.92, 0.84 to 1.00). In an exploratory unadjusted comparison, the 62 patients with a nadir below 1.0 mg/dL had a longer hospital stay than the other 154 (median 15 versus 14 days, p = 0.008), and a deeper nadir correlated with a longer stay across the cohort (Spearman rho −0.26, p < 0.001). Major complications were rare: four reoperations, and no symptomatic venous thromboembolism or surgical-site infection.

Clinicians gave intravenous phosphate to 112 of the 216 patients (Table 5). The two eligibility definitions gave almost identical denominators: 197 patients (91.2%) had at least one measured value at or below the real-time treatment threshold of 2.0 mg/dL, of whom 112 (56.9%) were treated, and 196 had a retrospective nadir below 2.0 mg/dL, of whom 111 (56.6%) were treated. The treatment rate fell across nadir-severity strata, from 74% in the severe group to none above 2.5 mg/dL (Table 5; trend p < 0.001); the two mildest strata sat at or above the 2.0 mg/dL threshold and were largely ineligible. Phosphate measured after the first dose was higher than the value that preceded it, but usually still below the treatment threshold. In a post hoc paired comparison restricted to the 108 recipients with an evaluable phosphate value on both sides of the first dose, the mean rose from 1.22 to 1.55 mg/dL (mean difference 0.33 mg/dL, 95% CI 0.24 to 0.42, p < 0.001, median interval between the bracketing samples 3 days), yet 88 of these 108 patients (81.5%) were still below 2.0 mg/dL at the next measurement and only 5 reached 2.5 mg/dL. This change cannot be attributed to the infusion, because phosphate also rebounds spontaneously: in the 85 untreated patients with a nadir below 2.0 mg/dL the values bracketing their first sub-threshold measurement rose by 0.21 mg/dL over the same median interval, and the difference between the two rises was not significant (0.12 mg/dL, p = 0.07) (Table 5). Recipients received a median of about 60 mmol of phosphate, rising to about 90 mmol in those with a severe nadir. Because completed doses, orders, contraindications, and refusals were not recorded, and because the untreated comparison is itself confounded by the higher starting value of that group, these figures describe the biochemical trajectory around administered treatment rather than its efficacy.

In a post hoc analysis, severity and treatment were not stable across the seven calendar years of enrollment (Supplementary Table S3): the median nadir rose from 1.15 to 1.75 mg/dL (Spearman rho 0.26, p < 0.001), the severe rate fell from 28.2% to 0% (p = 0.004), and the proportion treated varied from 15.4% in 2020 to 83.3% in 2022 before falling to none of the 13 patients operated in the partial 2026 year (p < 0.001). Because the iron protocol was fixed throughout, these shifts most plausibly reflect changing measurement and treatment behavior rather than changing exposure; they are unexplained by our data and temper the pooled estimates.

Discussion

Postoperative hypophosphatemia was almost universal in this cohort, biphasic across the two knees, and deeper after the second in patients untreated between them; among the minority measured before discharge phosphate had not returned to baseline. Intravenous replacement reached little more than half of eligible patients and usually left phosphate below the treatment threshold at the next measurement, the rise being similar to the spontaneous rebound in untreated patients. Because there was no comparator group, the design cannot attribute the disturbance to ferric carboxymaltose rather than to surgery, transfusion, perioperative fluids, or the second operation.

The incidence sits at the upper edge of the published range. At the common 2.5 mg/dL definition, our 95.8% rate is far higher than the 23% reported as a secondary finding in a hip-arthroplasty blood-management study;18 because that study focused on transfusion rather than phosphate, the comparison is descriptive context rather than a matched contrast.

At the identical 1,500 mg cumulative dose, a randomized trial reported hypophosphatemia in 39% at the 2.0 mg/dL threshold,24 well below our 90.7%; a dose-matched observational cohort with serial sampling reported about 88% overall and about a quarter severe,25 close to our 95.8% at the same 2.5 mg/dL threshold and our 28.7% severe rate. The prevalence itself is therefore not the new contribution; the dense within-patient trajectory across two short-interval surgical insults and the quantified account of replacement delivery and effect are. Three features of our setting plausibly contribute to the high rate: a fixed cumulative dose of 1,500 mg; two surgical insults within a short interval; and ten-point sampling that captured transient nadirs sparser schedules may miss.

The dependence of the nadir on baseline phosphate echoes the risk profile described after ferric carboxymaltose in medical cohorts.9,26 Female sex and lower body mass index also marked the patients who fell furthest, and our post hoc models suggest a simpler partial reading: because the dose was fixed in absolute terms, smaller patients received more iron per kilogram, and once dose per kilogram entered the model the association with female sex lost significance in the logistic analysis and was attenuated by about a third, without disappearing, in the linear analysis. Body size and exposure per kilogram cannot be separated in a single-dose cohort, so we present this as plausible rather than demonstrated. The fixed dosing has a practical corollary: the 1,000 mg component exceeded 20 mg iron per kilogram, the single-dose ceiling in the product information, in the 14 patients weighing under 50 kg, and exceeded 15 mg/kg in 143 patients (66.2%), with a mean single-dose exposure of 16.1 mg/kg and a maximum of 27.8 mg/kg. Weight-banded dosing would remove that excess. Higher baseline hemoglobin was associated with a deeper nadir as well, reported here as an association only.

The biphasic trajectory invites a mechanistic question that our data can raise but not answer. Both iron doses were given around the first operation, and the second knee received none, yet phosphate fell again after the second procedure, and in treatment-naive patients it fell slightly further than after the first. Three explanations are possible and cannot be separated here. The first is pharmacologic: ferric carboxymaltose raises fibroblast growth factor 23 and drives a delayed phosphaturic effect5,7,27 whose serum-phosphate nadir falls weeks after dosing,28 so phosphate may still have been falling from the first-day dose when the second operation took place. The second is that the second surgical insult lowered phosphate on its own, through mechanisms described after major abdominal operations.29 The third, which does not involve renal loss, is transcellular redistribution: dextrose-containing fluids, resumed enteral intake, and the respiratory alkalosis and catecholamine surge of surgery all drive phosphate into cells and would produce exactly the day-2 trough and day-5 partial rebound we observed; because fluid and nutritional intake were not recorded, we cannot weigh it against the other two. All three remain speculative here, because we measured neither fibroblast growth factor 23, nor urinary phosphate excretion, nor parathyroid hormone, nor vitamin D; the concurrent fall in albumin-corrected calcium is consistent with a hormonally mediated mineral disturbance but equally consistent with the systemic response to two operations.

Whether the disturbance matters clinically is the harder question, and this study cannot answer it. Biochemically severe hypophosphatemia was associated with a longer hospital stay, but the absolute difference was about one day in a cohort whose stay was already long and tightly clustered, a compressed range that limits the correlation. Length of stay is multifactorial and the comparison was unadjusted, and because symptoms, cardiorespiratory events, and functional recovery were not systematically assessed, we cannot connect the biochemical nadir to patient harm and do not describe the laboratory abnormality as clinically important. External postoperative studies link hypophosphatemia to a longer stay, prolonged ventilation, and organ-specific complications;30,31 a secondary analysis of a surgical iron trial associated lower phosphate with a longer stay;32 and severe hypophosphatemia can cause neuromuscular dysfunction.33 We therefore treat the length-of-stay association as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of harm.

The replacement data carry the most directly actionable message, and one not confined to orthopedics: managing hypophosphatemia in this setting requires both detecting a falling phosphate and repleting it, and our cohort shows how readily each step can fall short. The shortfall is not an artifact of classifying eligibility retrospectively: clinicians acted on real-time values, and eligibility defined that way gives 112 of 197 treated (56.9%) against 111 of 196 (56.6%) on the retrospective nadir, the two nearly coinciding because a nadir below 2.0 mg/dL is itself a measured value at or below the trigger. That pooled figure conceals a large, unexplained variation between calendar years (Supplementary Table S3) rather than a stable practice. Nor did it reflect transient dips: most untreated patients had a sustained indication spanning both operative periods, and nearly one in five had reached the severe range. When treatment was given it was given promptly, so the shortfall lay in whether patients were treated rather than in how quickly. Why treatment was withheld cannot be established from the record, which holds no orders, refusals, or contraindications. The treating physicians’ recollection, offered here as clinical context rather than as a measured variable, is that ward nurses frequently called to report that a patient could not tolerate intravenous phosphate because of vascular pain, and that the infusion or the order was discontinued on that account—a pathway that would leave a sustained indication untreated or incompletely treated; the free-text documentation of pain interrupting infusions concerns patients who did receive one and cannot corroborate the reason for non-treatment. Neither was counted, and whether the shortfall reflects tolerability, recognition, or delivery cannot be determined.

Where treatment was given, four in five recipients were still below the threshold at the next measurement, and the rise that followed a dose was only 0.12 mg/dL greater than the spontaneous rebound in untreated patients, a difference that did not reach significance. The delivered amount may be part of the explanation: a median of about 60 mmol, and 90 mmol in the severe stratum, over a median of two doses. We cannot say whether the regimen was ineffective or simply under-dosed, and with no randomized comparator, no record of completed doses, and no systematic response assessment we neither estimate the efficacy of intravenous phosphate nor conclude that replacement failed. Injection-site pain attributed to potassium phosphate was noted in free-text records as a reason some infusions were stopped early; because it was never counted, we report it only as an observation. Peak postoperative potassium did not differ between recipients and non-recipients, but with only four patients above 5.5 mmol/L occasional hyperkalemia cannot be excluded.

The perioperative DeFICIT trial points the same way.19 When a biochemical disturbance is this common and repletion this incomplete, the practical question shifts from reactive treatment toward prevention and a more tolerable repletion route. The following are research proposals rather than clinical guidance, and none is validated by these data: phosphate monitoring after each operative stage; a defined treatment threshold and recovery target; more tolerable repletion strategies such as sodium glycerophosphate, greater dilution, a slower infusion rate, central administration, or oral bridging; and weight-banded rather than fixed iron dosing. Lower-risk formulations such as ferric derisomaltose carry a fraction of the phosphate risk in randomized comparisons, including a total knee arthroplasty trial in which no hypophosphatemia was observed;8,10,34 whether substituting them removes most of this burden in bilateral arthroplasty requires a head-to-head trial. Reviews of current guidance similarly support monitoring around high-risk or repeated ferric carboxymaltose exposure, with supplementation for severe or symptomatic hypophosphatemia.35

This study has limitations. The retrospective single-center design and the absence of a comparator group preclude causal inference. Major surgery itself can lower phosphate, as documented after major abdominal operations in which renal phosphate wasting was implicated,29 and this cohort also had a high transfusion exposure and no routine tranexamic acid use; without an iron-free or low-risk-iron comparator, the incidence cannot be apportioned to ferric carboxymaltose alone. Individual iron administration was inferred from the uniform institutional protocol rather than confirmed from a per-patient medication or pharmacy record, the single largest gap in exposure ascertainment. Phosphate was also assayed on two analytical platforms whose use tracked the ordering route, so an instrument difference cannot be separated from the fall measured from the preoperative baseline, though the postoperative comparisons, including the primary outcome and the biphasic pattern, were made almost entirely within a single platform. The dose was fixed rather than weight-banded, so exposure per kilogram varied 2.6-fold and is confounded with body size and sex, which is why the corresponding associations are presented as exploratory.

We did not capture baseline iron indices in most patients—iron studies were available in only 26 of 216 and ferritin in none—so we could not confirm that ferric carboxymaltose was prescribed for measured iron deficiency, and only about one in five patients was anemic by World Health Organization criteria. We excluded 32 patients for insufficient postoperative laboratory data and could not characterize them, because the research dataset holds records only for the 216 analyzed; if laboratory completeness tracked clinical course, sparsely sampled patients most likely had shorter, less complicated admissions and shallower unobserved nadirs, making our incidence estimate the higher bound rather than the lower one. Fibroblast growth factor 23, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D, and urinary phosphate excretion were not measured, so we rely on published mechanistic evidence and cannot evaluate the cited osteomalacia and fracture risks. Serum magnesium was measured in only 22 of 216 patients, so hypomagnesemia—which contributes to refractory hypophosphatemia and to hypocalcemia—could neither be excluded nor quantified, and perioperative fluid composition and nutritional intake were likewise not recorded, leaving transcellular redistribution unevaluable. The mixed-effects model assumes that missing observations are missing at random, an assumption the predischarge data contradict, since availability of that sample tracked length of stay. The preoperative sample was drawn a median of 37 days before surgery and at least eight days before it in 93.5% of patients, which under the protocol schedule places it before any iron exposure; because infusion dates are not recorded per patient this cannot be confirmed individually, and its distance from surgery means it reflects the preadmission rather than the immediately preoperative state.

The observed trajectory is a treated trajectory: 90 patients received phosphate before the second operation, which is why the treatment-naive subgroup is reported separately. Predischarge phosphate was available in only 95 of 216 patients, so recovery could not be reliably assessed and remains unknown for 56.0% of the cohort; that subgroup stayed longer and had a higher last recorded phosphate, so its apparent partial recovery probably overstates recovery in the whole cohort.

The treatment shortfall reflects delivered care and the timing of laboratory information rather than clinician decision-making alone, but it cannot be attributed to replacement by an unmeasured route, because the hospital formulary carried no oral phosphate preparation. Symptoms attributable to hypophosphatemia, postoperative functional recovery, and long-term skeletal outcomes were not assessed, and the ten-point perioperative window cannot address osteomalacia or fracture. Nadir severity and treatment rates varied significantly across the seven years of enrollment despite an unchanged iron protocol, adding unexplained uncertainty to the pooled estimates.

The absence of routine tranexamic acid use departs from contemporary patient blood management practice20,21 and may have increased the transfusion exposure, and the restriction to a single cemented implant keeps the surgical exposure homogeneous but narrows the population. The median stay of 15 days was unusually long; given the low complication rate, it reflects local perioperative practice rather than a complicated course. Together with this transfusion practice and the older, predominantly female, single-center population, it limits how far the incidence and the length-of-stay association generalize to standard practice.

A perioperative comparison of ferric carboxymaltose against ferric derisomaltose in bilateral arthroplasty, with phosphate and fibroblast growth factor 23 measured prospectively, would clarify whether formulation choice reduces this burden; a prospective study capturing orders, completed doses, reasons for non-treatment, structured symptom assessment, and infusions stopped for pain would establish whether the shortfall reflects recognition, delivery, or tolerability, and would allow the efficacy of replacement to be estimated rather than described.

Conclusions

Under a fixed 1,500 mg ferric carboxymaltose protocol in short-interval staged bilateral total knee arthroplasty, hypophosphatemia was near-universal, biochemically severe in more than one-quarter of patients, and biphasic across the two operative periods. Intravenous phosphate replacement was documented in only a little more than half of the patients who met the treatment threshold, and among those treated phosphate remained below 2.0 mg/dL at the next measurement in 88 of 108 evaluable patients, having risen by an amount close to the spontaneous rebound in untreated patients. This uncontrolled cohort cannot determine how much of the phosphate decline is attributable to ferric carboxymaltose rather than to surgery, transfusion, perioperative fluids, or the second operation, and it does not establish causality, clinical harm, the efficacy of intravenous phosphate, or the superiority of any treatment strategy; the preoperative value may not represent a true pretreatment baseline, recovery before discharge was unknown for more than half of the cohort, and symptoms were not systematically assessed. These descriptive, hypothesis-generating findings support the prospective evaluation of phosphate monitoring after each operative stage, of weight-adjusted rather than fixed iron dosing, and of lower-risk iron formulations, rather than any change to current practice.
