Effects of radiotherapy dose and application time on the load-to-failure values of teeth filled with different sealers
Authors: Ozgun Gulderen, Esma Saricam, Sedef Gökhan Açikgöz, Yılmaz Tezcan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe materials used in root canal filling and radiotherapy (RT) application can affect the load-to-failure of the teeth. This study aimed to compare the load-to-failure of the teeth filled with AH Plus (AHP) or AH Plus bioceramic (AHPB) before or after 60 Gy- and 70 Gy-dose radiotherapy.Materials and methodsOne hundred and ten maxillary incisors were endodontically prepared up to ProTaper Next X4 instruments. The teeth were randomly divided into 5 main groups as: non-irradiated, root canal treatment (RCT) before 60 Gy radiotherapy, RCT before 70 Gy radiotherapy, RCT after 60 Gy radiotherapy, RCT after 70 Gy radiotherapy. These groups were divided into 2 subgroups for filling material: AHP ve AHPB. After filling and radiotherapy procedures, the teeth were fixed on the thermoplastic mask plane. The plane were placed in the middle of a rectangular prism shaped glass phantom, and irradiated via a linear accelerator device. The teeth were then embedded in cylindrical acrylic blocks and the force (N) that caused the fracture was recorded under the Universal testing device. Student t-test was used for statistical comparisons. Statistical significance level was determined as p < 0.05.ResultsAfter 70 Gy dose radiotherapy, the group filled with AHPB showed statistically significantly lower fracture strength than the group filled with AHP (p < 0.05). In the control group, before 60 Gy RT group, after 60 Gy RT group, and before 70 Gy RT group, the sealers had statistically significantly similar load-to-failure. (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe group filled with AHPB caused a lower fracture strength than the group filled with AHP following a 70 Gy dose of RT. The sealers in the control group, before 60 Gy RT group, after 60 Gy RT group, and before 70 Gy RT group exhibited comparable load-to-failure values.Keywords: Bioceramic root canal sealer, Materials testing, Radiotherapy

The goal of endodontic treatment is to eradicate infection of the root canal and entirely fill the root canal space in three dimensions to stop microorganisms from penetrating in either apical or coronal directions. An effective root canal sealer should tightly seal accessory canals and apical foramen and provide adherence between the core material and the root canal wall [1]. Root canal sealers are classified according to their main chemical components as follows: zinc oxide eugenol, calcium hydroxide, glass ionomer, silicone, epoxy resin, and bioceramic based [2].

Epoxy resin-based sealers are preferred in endodontics due to their superior physio-chemical properties [3]. An epoxy resin–containing root canal sealer, AH Plus (AHPB; Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA), is a common reference material for comparing newly marketed root canal sealers [4]. The superior flowability and long working time of AHP ensure deep penetration of the sealer into the dentinal tubules and provide good apical sealing features [5, 6].

Bioceramics-based root canal sealers have recently been introduced to the market and have become increasingly popular. These hydrophilic sealers exhibit minimal toxicity, high levels of three-dimensional hermetic sealing, and no dimensional changes during the setting process, and they provide bioactivity by inducing hard tissue formation [7]. They can be used either by themselves or with gutta-percha to fill root canals. AH Plus Bioceramic (AHPB) root canal sealer (Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte, NC, USA) is a recently introduced calcium silicate-based sealer in premixed form. It is biocompatible, stimulates tissue development, has a fast-setting time, and is dimensionally stable and very resistant to dissolution. It does not cause discoloration of the teeth. According to the manufacturer’s statement, it has a higher radiopacity than EndoSequence® BC Sealer™, and TotalFill® BCSealer™ [8].

The term “head and neck cancers” refers to malignancies that affect the lips, mouth, oropharynx, tonsils, salivary glands, nose, paranasal sinuses, and middle ears [9]. Head and neck cancers are the seventh most common type of cancer worldwide, with 890,000 new cases and 450,000 deaths in 2018 [10]. In addition to surgery and chemotherapy, radiotherapy is an important treatment method that is commonly used in the management of head and neck cancers [11]. Radiotherapy doses range from 70 Gy for gross disease to 60-66 Gy for high-risk areas and 50-54 Gy for low-risk areas [12].

According to previous studies, following head and neck radiotherapy, pulp necrosis [13], caries [14], alterations in pulpal blood circulation [15], and a decrease in dentin microhardness [16] may appear. It was found that irradiated root dentin had microcracks, fractures, and fewer collagen fibers [17]. The incidence of apical periodontitis is also influenced by the radiation dose [18]. It has been claimed that the bonding strengths of root canal sealer to dentin [19] and of the resin cement used in fiber posts to dentin [20] have been affected in irradiated teeth.

Endodontic treatment involves steps such as mechanical preparation, irrigation, and root canal filling that can weaken the root structure, making it prone to fractures [21–23]. The fracture resistance of teeth is also influenced by the root canal sealer type used for fillings [24, 25]. In a previous study, the lowest fracture strength in teeth filled with Biodentine or AHP before or after 60-Gy radiotherapy was observed in teeth with root canals filled with Biodentine after radiotherapy [26]. Overall, radiotherapy has been found to significantly reduce the fracture resistance of teeth [27].

After a literature review, it was observed that there was no study evaluating the effect of radiotherapy dose and bioceramics-based root canal sealers on the fracture resistance of the teeth. The aim of this in vitro study was to evaluate the effect of 60- and 70-Gy dose radiotherapy applications before and after root canal treatment (RCT) on the fracture resistance of teeth filled with resin-based (AHP) or bioceramics-based (AHPB) sealers. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference in load-to-failure value between teeth filled with AHP and AHPB in the control group before and after 60-and 70-Gy doses of radiotherapy.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was received for this in vitro study from the Ankara Yıldırım Beyazıt University, Ethical Committee of Health Sciences (April 13, 2023, number 2023/142). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study. As a result of the power analysis performed with the G-Power program (Version 3.1, Heinrich Heine Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany), the number of samples was 110 for 10 groups, 11 per group (effect size = 0.50; = 0.05; power = 0.95). One hundred and ten permanent maxillary human incisor teeth extracted for periodontal reasons were included in the study, as were teeth with a length between the anatomical apex and the incisal edge of 19–22 mm. Teeth with caries, defects, restorations, cracks, fractures, and calcified root canals were excluded from the study. The measurements of the teeth were as follows: mesiodistal widths, 8 ± 0.5 mm; labiolingual widths, 5.5 ± 0.5 mm; lengths of crowns, 9.5 ± 0.5 mm; and lengths of the roots, 11 ± 1 mm. The measurements were made using a digital caliper. Within the scope of the study, five basic groups were created according to the application time for RCT: nonirradiated (control), RCT before 60-Gy radiotherapy, RCT before 70-Gy radiotherapy, RCT after 60-Gy radiotherapy, and RCT after 70-Gy radiotherapy. These groups were divided into two subgroups for the sealers: AHP and AHPB (Table 1). A total of 10 groups were created, and the teeth were randomly divided into groups.

The total doses applied to the teeth were determined to be 60 and 70 Gy. Irradiation was performed with the applied dose being 2 Gy/fraction, 1 fraction per day, 5 days per week. The radiotherapy dose was applied for 6 weeks to the 60-Gy dose radiotherapy groups (Groups 3–6) and for 7 weeks to the 70-Gy dose radiotherapy groups (Groups 7–10). In Groups 5, 6, 9, and 10, RCT was applied first, followed by radiotherapy, while in Groups 3, 4, 7, and 8, radiotherapy was applied first and then RCT. Groups 1 and 2, the control groups, did not receive any radiation.

Endodontic access cavities were prepared for all teeth. A #15 K-file was inserted into the canal until it was just visible at the apical foramen under the dental loop with 2.5× magnification. Using this file, the endodontic working length was determined by taking the incisal edge as the coronal reference point 1 mm shorter than the apical foramen. Preparation of the root canals was performed with ProTaper Next rotary nickel titanium files (Maillefer Instruments, Ballaigues, Switzerland) having an apical diameter of X4 (#40). Preparation was performed with ProTaper Next X1, X2, X3, and X4 files. Rotary files were passively inserted into the canal. The files were used in a rotational motion with an endodontic motor (AI motor; Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument, Guilin, Republic of China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with a rotation speed of 300 rpm and a torque of 2.5 N/cm. For irrigation of the canals at each file change, 2 ml of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite solution was used through a 27-gauge, 2.5-ml dental syringe needle (Berika Teknoloji Medikal, Konya, Turkey). The final irrigation solution consisted of 2 mL of 17% ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid, 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite, and 2.5 mL of distilled water.

After the root canals were dried with ProTaper X4 paper points (Dentsply-Sirona), they were filled with AHP or AHPB root canal sealer with gutta-percha (Sure Dent Corporation, Sagimakgol-ro, South Korea; #40 main cone and #20-#25 accessory cones) using the cold lateral condensation technique. Access cavities were restored with composite resin (CeramX One SphereTEC/CO; Dentsply Sirona, Konstanz, Germany).

Radiotherapy applications to the teeth were performed by a physical technician under the supervision of a radiation oncologist using the EDGE linear accelerator (Varian, Palo Alto, CA, USA) in the Radiation Oncology Department of TC Ankara Bilkent City Hospital. A flat plane was obtained by heating a thermoplastic face mask, which is generally used clinically during head and neck radiotherapy. The teeth were fixed on the plane with a fishing line and silicone adhesives. This plane, on which the teeth were fixed, was placed in the center of a 40 × 40 × 20 cm glass phantom parallel to the two sides and at an equal distance. The glass phantom was filled with distilled water so that all the teeth were inside, and the distilled water was refreshed at the end of each day (Fig. 1). After the glass phantom containing the teeth was placed on the treatment table, the device irradiation area was opened to 40 × 40 cm, and the set-up position was created using the skin axis distance method, with the distance between the source isocenter being 100 cm (Fig. 2). The gantry angle was calculated at 90° and 270° (both sides) by a medical physicist using 10 MV of energy.

Radiotherapy application to the teeth via a linear accelerator device

After the radiation process, the root surfaces of the teeth were first covered with aluminum foil at a thickness of 0.2–0.3 mm, up to 2 mm below the cementoenamel junction, to mimic the periodontal ligament. Then, the teeth were embedded in cylindrical acrylic blocks (2.5 cm long and 2 cm in diameter) up to 2 mm below the cementoenamel junction using cold acrylic (Takilon, WP Dental, Germany). After the initial polymerization of cold acrylic, the teeth were removed from the acrylic, and their aluminum foil was removed. Light body impression material (Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) was applied to the acrylic, and the teeth were mounted back into the acrylic. Excess impression material was removed with a scalpel and left at normal room temperature for 24 h to complete polymerization.

After that, the samples were set up on the Universal Testing Machine (Lloyd LRX; Lloyd Instruments Ltd., Fareham, United Kingdom) using a unique holder positioned at a 45° angle to perform the fracture resistance tests [28, 29]. A special stainless steel apparatus tapering toward the tip area was fixed 1 mm above the cingulum of the tooth. Force was applied at a speed of 1 mm/min until the teeth broke. The force required to load the teeth to failure was recorded in newtons (N).

SPSS v.22 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) program was used to analyze the obtained data. Normality distribution analysis of the data was performed with the Kruskal–Wallis test. The test of homogeneity of variance and Levene’s statistic were performed. The variances were homogeneous. Student’s t test was used to compare paired groups. The statistical significance level was p < 0.05.

There was no statistically significant difference in fracture strength between the two root canal sealers when radiation was applied at doses of 60 Gy (p > 0.05) and 70 Gy (p > 0.05) after root canal filling. The teeth filled with AHP and AHPB canal sealers showed no statistically significant difference in fracture strength following a 60-Gy dose (p > 0.05). After receiving 70 Gy of radiation therapy, the group filled with AHPB had a lower fracture strength value than the group filled with AHP sealer (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, the effect of different doses of radiation applied before and after root canals were filled with epoxy resin-based AHP and bioceramics-based AHPB on the load-to-failure value of teeth was assessed. In the literature review, no similar research was found evaluating the effect of radiotherapy doses on the load-to-failure value of endodontically treated teeth. According to the results of this study, in teeth filled with these two sealers, there was no difference in fracture strength between them before or after radiation doses of 60 Gy or before a dose of 70 Gy. Following 70 Gy of radiation therapy, the group filled with the AHPB bioceramic sealer had lower fracture strength than the group filled with AHP epoxy resin-based sealer. The leading load-to-failure values of the teeth filled with AHP and AHPB root canal sealers did not significantly differ in the control groups without irradiation. Therefore, the null hypothesis was partially rejected.

A previous study investigated the load-to-failure value of teeth filled with gutta-percha and epoxy resin-based root canal sealer or Biodentine before and after a 60-Gy dose of radiotherapy [26]. However, the effect of the dose was not investigated in that study. Based on a literature review, doses of 60-70 Gy are commonly preferred in investigations to mimic the doses used in the treatment of head and neck tumors. In some previous studies, a dose of 60 Gy was applied to examine the effects of radiation therapy on the dislocation resistance of root canal filling sealers [19, 30], resin cements [17], and fiber posts [31]. Using radiation doses of 60 Gy, the effect of radiotherapy on the sealers’ apical sealing was investigated [32, 33]. In studies examining the push-out bond strength of resin cement to root dentin at the time of radiotherapy application, a dose of 70 Gy was given to the teeth [20]. In the study by Suri et al., a total dose of 70 Gy of radiotherapy was applied to the teeth to investigate the changes in the surface properties and permeability of human enamel after radiotherapy [34]. In contrast to earlier in vitro investigations, both 60-Gy and 70-Gy dosages were used in this study. It appears that there is no other study in the field of endodontics that compares and uses these two of the most frequently used radiation doses in tandem.

It was found in this study that following radiotherapy at a dose of 70 Gy, there was a difference in the load-to-failure values of the two sealer groups. In the group filled with AHPB, a lower load-to-failure value was observed. Depending on the radiation dose administered before the RCT, choosing the right sealer is crucial. The load-to-failure value was not influenced by the sealer used in RCT before radiotherapy. In cases in which RCT is indicated following radiotherapy for patients who have received 70-Gy doses of head and neck radiation, epoxy resin-based sealer may be chosen over bioceramics-based sealer. The effects of radiation therapy on dental tissues are dose related. In a clinical trial, dose-related damage to dental tissues was classified into three stages: minimal tooth damage after 0–30 Gy, 2- or 3-fold increase in tooth-dose damage between 30 and 60 Gy, and a 10-fold increase in this connection at 60 Gy or more [35]. In a systematic review, it was reported that in radiotherapy applications at a dose of 70 Gy, there was a significant decrease in calcium, phosphate, phosphorus, the protein/mineral ratio in the enamel structure, and the microhardness of enamel; coronal and root dentin structures also decreased, and the tubular structure in the dentin disappeared [36]. In this study, fracture resistance decreased in teeth filled with AHPB after 70 Gy of radiotherapy. It was thought that the altered mineral structure of the dental tissues following radiotherapy at 70 Gy could have a detrimental effect, hence influencing the fracture strength.

There is no research available evaluating the load-to-failure value of teeth filled with the AHPB root canal sealer that was used in this investigation. A prior study comparing AHP with another bioceramics-based sealer, TotalFill® BC, in terms of load to failure found that the two sealer groups were similar [37]. In a different investigation, no difference was found in terms of fracture resistance between AHP and the bioceramics-based sealers MTA Fillapex and EndoSequence BC Sealer [38]. There was no discernible difference between the fracture resistance of AHP and the bioceramics-based BioRoot RCS root canal sealer in another investigation conducted by Yusufoglu et al. [39]. This study demonstrated no difference in fracture strength between the AHPB and AHP in the control groups, supporting the findings of other investigations.

According to the reviewed literature, there were differences in the conditions in which the teeth endured radiation treatment. It was observed that there were differences regarding the medium in which the teeth were kept during the irradiation procedure. Previously, radiation was administered after teeth were fixated on wax patterns in Styrofoam filled with brass [40]. In another investigation, the teeth in distilled water inside the phantom were exposed to radiation [41–43]. The studies showed that during radiotherapy, the teeth were preserved in distilled water or rice. To avoid interfering with radiation during radiotherapy, it is crucial that the teeth be positioned in the sagittal plane and not overlap. Furthermore, the teeth need to be maintained in a humid environment for the load-to-failure test to be carried out properly. Thus, for this study, the teeth were secured in a glass phantom and radiation in distilled water was used. The teeth were fixed in the same sagittal plane on a melted thermoplastic mask, which is routinely used to stabilize the patient’s head during clinical head and neck radiotherapy procedures. The mechanism was created by fixing the apparatus on the glass phantom via an orthodontic wire.

In several previous studies evaluating the load-to-failure values of endodontically treated teeth, the coronal parts of the teeth were not removed [26, 28, 29]. To mimic clinical conditions, the teeth used in this study were not decoronated. In this study, 2.5% NaOCl was used for the irrigation procedure. To eliminate E. faecalis biofilms, 2.5% and 5% NaOCl were used, and the results were similar [44]. Studies carried out in vitro with different NaOCl concentrations have revealed that higher concentrations of NaOCl are more toxic than lower concentrations [45]. For these reasons, many recent studies used lower concentrations of NaOCl (< 3%) [46, 47]. The teeth in this study were filled using the cold lateral condensation technique. A previous study showed that there was no statistically significant difference in fracture resistance between teeth filled using lateral condensation and those filled using the single cone technique [48]. The most widely preferred root canal filling method is the cold lateral condensation technique, which is regarded as the gold standard and the method against which to compare other filling methods [49]. A micro-computerized tomography study comparing the filling area of teeth filled using cold lateral condensation and single cone techniques found the cold lateral condensation technique superior to the single cone technique [50].

Standard in vitro conditions were designed for this investigation. One limitation of this study could be its inability to replicate clinical conditions precisely. This study only evaluated the effects of resin-based and bioceramics-based sealers on the load-to-failure values of teeth. Further studies evaluating the effects of different sealers on the load-to-failure values of teeth are recommended.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, the results showed that AHP root canal sealer provided a higher load-to-failure value than AHPB root canal sealer in RCT applications after 70-Gy radiotherapy. The sealers showed similar load-to-failure values in the control group before and after 60-Gy radiotherapy and before 70- radiotherapy.
