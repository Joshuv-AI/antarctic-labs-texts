Comparative in vitro evaluation of sonic, passive ultrasonic, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation on Enterococcus faecalis reduction and smear layer/debris removal in root canals
Authors: Rehab Ali Farag, Abd El Rahman O El mekkawi, Khaled Beshr, Mohamed Sayed, Mohammad Rayyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent intraradicular infection remains a major cause of endodontic treatment failure because conventional syringe irrigation may not adequately reach the apical third, dentinal tubules, and canal irregularities. The aim of this in vitro study was to compare the antimicrobial efficacy and smear layer/debris removal ability of sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation using photon-induced photoacoustic streaming (PIPS), relative to conventional syringe irrigation, in prepared root canals infected with a mono-species Enterococcus faecalis biofilm.MethodsSixty extracted single-rooted mandibular second premolars were prepared, sterilized, inoculated with E. faecalis, and randomly allocated to conventional syringe irrigation, sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, or Er: YAG/PIPS activation (n = 15). All groups received 2.5% sodium hypochlorite. Post-irrigation bacterial load was quantified as log10 CFU/mL, and smear layer/debris removal in the apical, middle, and coronal thirds was assessed by scanning electron microscopy using a five-point score. CFU values were analysed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, and SEM scores by the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple-comparison test. Inter-examiner agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsBacterial load differed significantly among groups (F(3, 56) = 85.11, p < 0.001). Mean log10 CFU/mL values were 6.41 ± 0.30 for conventional irrigation, 5.37 ± 0.26 for sonic activation, 4.12 ± 0.24 for passive ultrasonic irrigation, and 4.28 ± 0.27 for Er: YAG/PIPS activation. Passive ultrasonic and Er: YAG/PIPS activation produced significantly lower bacterial loads than sonic activation and conventional irrigation, with no significant difference between the ultrasonic and laser groups (p = 0.452). SEM scores also differed significantly (H = 38.9, p < 0.001). Overall median scores were 4.5 (IQR 4–5), 3 (IQR 3–4), and 2 (IQR 2–3) for conventional, sonic, and both ultrasonic and laser activation, respectively.ConclusionsAll tested activation methods improved root canal disinfection and smear layer/debris removal compared with conventional syringe irrigation. Passive ultrasonic irrigation and Er: YAG/PIPS activation were more effective than sonic activation and showed comparable performance. These methods may be useful adjuncts to sodium hypochlorite irrigation, although clinical studies are needed to confirm their clinical effectiveness.Keywords: Endodontic irrigation, Enterococcus faecalis, Sonic activation, EndoActivator, Passive ultrasonic irrigation, Er:YAG laser, PIPS, Smear layer, Sodium hypochlorite

Background

Persistent intraradicular infection continues to challenge the long-term success of root canal therapy, despite advancements in mechanical preparation and root-filling materials [1–3]. When biofilms persist in dentinal tubules or other instrumented areas, they can lead to post-treatment flare-ups or treatment failure, underscoring the need for an effective irrigation strategy [4]. Although NaOCl remains the gold standard irrigant due to its broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity and tissue-dissolving capability, conventional needle-and-syringe delivery often proves insufficient for penetrating the complex anatomy of the canal system. These anatomical intricacies, including isthmuses and lateral canals, can harbor residual debris and resistant bacteria, which are key contributors to persistent infections [5, 6].

To address these limitations, a variety of irrigation activation methods have been introduced, with the overarching goal of improving irrigant flow, penetration, and biofilm disruption [7]. Among these innovations, three principal approaches are the focus of much investigative work: sonic, ultrasonic, and laser activation [8]. Each approach aims to enhance the physical effects of irrigation (via acoustic streaming, cavitation, or photoacoustic shockwaves) beyond what is achievable by standard syringe irrigation alone.

Sonic activation typically employs lower frequencies (roughly 150–300 Hz) through devices such as the EndoActivator system [9]. When placed in the canal, these polymer tips oscillate to stir the irrigant, thereby increasing contact between the solution and the canal walls [10]. While sonic activation has demonstrated advantages over static irrigation, some research suggests that its lower frequency may not generate the same degree of turbulence and shear forces evident in higher-frequency methods [11].

By contrast, ultrasonic activation operates at frequencies between 25 kHz and 40 kHz, creating robust acoustic streaming and, under optimal conditions, cavitation [12]. Passive Ultrasonic Irrigation (PUI) involves placing a non-cutting ultrasonic file into an irrigant-filled canal. The resulting vibrations increase fluid velocity and aid in detaching microbial aggregates from the canal walls [13]. Numerous in vitro experiments have documented superior smear layer removal and bacterial reduction with ultrasonic activation compared to conventional syringe irrigation or sonic agitation, especially in the apical third [14, 15].

A more recent entrant is laser activation, leveraging photon-induced photoacoustic streaming (PIPS) to generate shockwaves within the irrigant [16]. Diode, Nd: YAG, Er: YAG, and Er, Cr: YSGG lasers can produce localized photoacoustic effects and significant microstreaming, potentially removing debris even from complex canal topographies [17]. Preliminary studies suggest that laser activation may match or exceed ultrasonic irrigation in certain scenarios. However, practical concerns such as the high cost of laser equipment, operator learning curve, and the risk of thermal damage if protocols are not strictly followed can limit widespread adoption [18–20].

Despite the burgeoning literature on irrigation activation, direct in vitro comparisons of sonic, ultrasonic, and laser-based methods (using the same bacterial strain and standardized protocols) are relatively scarce. Enterococcus faecalis has been extensively studied in this context, due to its capacity to survive harsh environmental conditions, invade dentinal tubules, and withstand high pH [21]. Understanding how effectively these activation modalities eradicate E. faecalis biofilms in vitro can inform clinical practice and guide endodontists in optimizing treatment protocols [22].

Accordingly, the aim of this in vitro study was to compare the antimicrobial efficacy and smear layer/debris removal ability of sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation using photon-induced photoacoustic streaming (PIPS), relative to conventional syringe irrigation, in extracted human mandibular second premolars infected with a mono-species Enterococcus faecalis biofilm. Antimicrobial efficacy was assessed by quantitative colony-forming unit analysis, while smear layer/debris removal was evaluated using scanning electron microscopy in the apical, middle, and coronal thirds.

Although irrigation activation has been widely investigated, direct comparison among sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation remains affected by methodological variability among published studies. Differences in tooth type, irrigant concentration, activation time, canal preparation size, bacterial model, and outcome assessment can make direct interpretation difficult. Therefore, the novelty of the present study lies in its standardized comparative design, in which sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation were evaluated under identical experimental conditions using extracted human teeth. By combining quantitative CFU analysis with SEM-based assessment of smear layer and debris removal, this study provides an integrated evaluation of both antimicrobial efficacy and canal cleanliness.

Current study primary null hypothesis H0 was; mean post-irrigation bacterial load (log10 CFU/mL) is equal across groups (control, sonic, ultrasonic, laser); irrigant activation has no effect. While secondary null hypothesis H0 was Smear-layer/debris SEM scores do not differ among groups at any canal, and there is no intragroup interaction.

Materials and methods

Ethical clearance

The investigation was conducted over a seven-month period in 2025 at the Endodontic Department, Faculty of Dentistry, Misr University for Science and Technology (MUST), Egypt. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Misr University for Science and Technology (IRB #2026/0004). The extracted mandibular premolars used in this study were collected after extraction for therapeutic reasons unrelated to the study. All specimens were anonymized and coded before use, and no identifiable patient data were collected. Therefore, the requirement for informed consent to participate was waived by the Institutional Review Board (IRB). All procedures were carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the applicable institutional and national guidelines governing the ethical use of human biological specimens [23].

Sample size and allocation

An a priori sample size calculation was performed for the primary outcome, post-irrigation bacterial load expressed as log10 CFU/mL. The calculation was based on one-way ANOVA for four independent groups, using α = 0.05, power = 80%, and an anticipated medium-to-large effect size. Cohen’s effect size for ANOVA was estimated according to the formula f = √[η² / (1 − η²)], where η² is the expected variance explained by the treatment group. A minimum of 12 specimens per group was required. To improve precision, maintain balanced allocation, and compensate for possible specimen loss during sterilization, inoculation, microbial sampling, sectioning, or SEM preparation, the sample size was increased to 15 specimens per group, giving a total sample size of 60 teeth.

Tooth selection

A total of 60 extracted human mandibular second premolars were collected from oral surgery clinics. Only single-rooted mandibular second premolars with a single canal configuration were included to standardize canal anatomy across all experimental groups. Teeth were inspected clinically and radiographically to confirm the presence of one root and one canal. Teeth with more than one canal, canal bifurcation, severe curvature, calcification, internal or external resorption, cracks, open apices, previous root canal treatment, caries or restorations extending to the root, or root fractures were excluded. Extractions were performed for orthodontic or periodontal reasons unrelated to this study. This standardization was performed to minimize anatomical variability and ensure that differences in antimicrobial efficacy and smear layer/debris removal were mainly related to the irrigation activation method rather than canal configuration.

Initial inspection and storage

After extraction, teeth were rinsed with sterile saline to remove blood and soft tissue debris. They were then stored in 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C for no more than two months prior to experimentation [24].

Specimen preparation

Standardization of root length

The coronal portions were sectioned at the cemento-enamel junction using a water-cooled diamond disc, standardizing each tooth to a root length of 15 mm (± 1 mm).

Working length and canal preparation

A size 10 K-file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was introduced into the canal until just visible at the apical foramen and then withdrawn 1 mm to establish the working length. Canal patency and negotiability were confirmed before preparation. Mechanical preparation was performed using rotary nickel-titanium instruments up to a standardized final preparation size of 35/0.04. During instrumentation, each canal was irrigated with 2 mL of 2.5% NaOCl between files.

The final preparation size was standardized for all specimens to reduce methodological variability among groups. Although clinical apical preparation is commonly individualized according to the initial binding file and canal anatomy, this in vitro study required a uniform canal size to allow controlled comparison of the different irrigation activation methods. Standardizing the final preparation size helped ensure comparable canal dimensions, irrigant exchange, activation tip movement, microbial sampling, and SEM assessment across the control, sonic, ultrasonic, and Er: YAG laser groups.

After instrumentation, the canals were flushed with distilled water to remove residual NaOCl, then dried with sterile paper points (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland).

Sterilization and verification

All roots were placed in sealed autoclave pouches and subjected to steam sterilization at 121 °C for 15 min at 15 psi. Sterile conditions were confirmed by including biological indicators (spore strips) in each autoclave cycle [25].

Bacterial inoculation and biofilm formation

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

An Enterococcus faecalis (ATCC 29212) strain was used to model endodontic infection. A mono-species biofilm model was selected to standardize the microbial challenge across all groups and to allow controlled comparison of the antimicrobial effects of the tested irrigation activation techniques. Stock cultures were grown overnight at 37 °C in brain-heart infusion (BHI) broth. The bacterial suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1.5 × 10^8 CFU/mL) for canal inoculation.

Inoculation protocol

Each sterile root canal was injected with 20 µL of the standardized E. faecalis suspension, using a sterile micropipette, ensuring that all specimens received the same inoculum volume and bacterial concentration.

Irrigation protocols

Irrigant and volume

In all groups, 2.5% NaOCl was the primary irrigant. Each canal received a total of 5 mL across the entire procedure. The 5 mL total volume was standardized in all groups as follows: 1 mL initial irrigation, 3 mL divided into three activation cycles with 1 mL fresh NaOCl used for each cycle, and 1 mL final rinse. In the control group, the same 5 mL volume was delivered by syringe irrigation over 60 s without activation. In the sonic and ultrasonic groups, activation was performed for three 20-second cycles with fresh irrigant replenishment between cycles. In the Er: YAG laser/PIPS group, activation was performed for three 10-second cycles with 5-second pauses and fresh NaOCl replenishment between cycles.Between aliquots, the fluid in the canal was replaced with fresh NaOCl to maintain its potency. Irrigation timing was standardized according to each activation protocol. The control, sonic, and ultrasonic groups received 60 s of irrigation/activation, while Er: YAG laser activation was performed in three 10-second PIPS cycles with 5-second pauses according to the recommended laser protocol. In all groups, irrigant volume, NaOCl concentration, and replenishment between cycles were standardized.

Statistical analysis

Microbial reduction

CFU values were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to compare the means among the four groups. Tukey’s post hoc test identified pairwise differences (p < 0.05).

SEM scoring

The Kruskal-Wallis test was employed for non-parametric smear layer/debris scores, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison if significant differences were detected.

Inter-examiner reliability

Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated to ensure consistency between the two blinded SEM evaluators, with kappa ≥ 0.75 indicating good agreement.

Discussion

The present in vitro study aimed to compare the antimicrobial efficacy and smear layer/debris removal ability of sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation against a mono-species Enterococcus faecalis biofilm model in prepared root canals. The primary null hypothesis was that post-irrigation bacterial load would not differ among the control, sonic, passive ultrasonic, and Er: YAG laser groups. The secondary null hypothesis was that SEM smear layer/debris scores would not differ among groups or canal thirds. Both null hypotheses were rejected, as significant differences were found among the groups for CFU reduction and SEM smear layer/debris scores. The findings should therefore be interpreted as controlled in vitro comparative evidence, while recognizing that a mono-species E.faecalis model does not fully reproduce the polymicrobial nature of clinical endodontic infections.

Mechanistic interpretation of irrigation activation

The improved antimicrobial and cleaning performance observed with activated irrigation can be attributed to enhanced irrigant movement, increased contact between sodium hypochlorite and canal walls, and greater disruption of the bacterial biofilm structure. Conventional syringe irrigation depends mainly on needle delivery and irrigant diffusion, which may limit irrigant exchange in the apical third, dentinal tubules, fins, and canal irregularities. In contrast, activation techniques increase fluid movement within the canal space and may improve irrigant penetration, replacement, and mechanical disruption of biofilm and debris.

Sonic activation produces low-frequency oscillation of a flexible polymer tip, creating hydrodynamic agitation and improving irrigant circulation compared with conventional syringe irrigation. However, because sonic devices operate at lower frequencies, the generated acoustic streaming and shear stress are generally less intense than those produced by ultrasonic activation. This may explain why sonic activation improved bacterial reduction and smear layer/debris removal compared with the control group but remained less effective than passive ultrasonic and Er: YAG laser activation.

Passive ultrasonic irrigation produces high-frequency oscillation of a non-cutting file within the irrigant. This generates stronger acoustic streaming, increased fluid velocity, and possible cavitation-related effects. These forces may detach bacterial aggregates, disrupt the extracellular polymeric matrix of E. faecalis biofilms, improve NaOCl exchange, and remove loosely attached debris from instrumented canal walls. This mechanism supports the lower CFU counts and improved SEM cleanliness observed in the ultrasonic group, particularly in the apical third, where irrigant penetration is usually more difficult [6, 8].

Er: YAG laser activation using PIPS acts through photon-induced photoacoustic streaming. Short laser pulses produce rapid vapor bubble expansion and collapse within the irrigant, generating shockwave-like fluid movement. Since the laser tip is positioned coronally rather than advanced to working length, the antimicrobial and cleaning effects are mainly produced by fluid dynamics rather than direct thermal or ablative contact with dentin or bacteria. This explains why the Er: YAG laser group showed antimicrobial and smear layer/debris removal outcomes comparable to passive ultrasonic irrigation [9, 10].

Conventional syringe irrigation versus activated irrigation

All three activation techniques produced significantly greater bacterial reduction than conventional syringe irrigation. This finding is consistent with previous reports indicating that mechanical agitation improves irrigant distribution, canal-wall contact, and penetration in anatomically challenging areas [7]. Conventional syringe irrigation showed the highest bacterial load and smear layer/debris scores, which can be attributed to limited fluid exchange and weaker mechanical disruption, especially near the apical third.

Sonic activation versus passive ultrasonic irrigation

Sonic activation showed a significant improvement compared with conventional syringe irrigation, confirming its value as an adjunctive irrigation method. However, passive ultrasonic irrigation produced lower CFU values and lower smear layer/debris scores than sonic activation. This difference is most likely related to the higher frequency of ultrasonic file oscillation, which generates stronger acoustic streaming and greater shear forces than sonic agitation. These findings agree with Gu et al., who reported that sonic systems improve irrigant agitation but may be less effective than ultrasonic activation in disrupting established biofilms and debris in complex canal areas [28].

Passive ultrasonic irrigation versus Er: YAG laser activation

Passive ultrasonic irrigation and Er: YAG laser activation produced comparable antimicrobial and SEM outcomes, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Ultrasonic activation showed a slight numerical advantage in CFU reduction and smear layer/debris scores, but this difference was not statistically significant under the present protocol. Therefore, the results support the interpretation that both high-frequency acoustic streaming and laser-induced photoacoustic streaming can effectively enhance NaOCl activity and canal cleanliness. However, no clinical superiority of one method over the other should be assumed from the present in vitro findings alone.

Relationship between CFU reduction and SEM cleanliness

The microbiological and SEM findings showed a consistent pattern. Groups with greater bacterial reduction also demonstrated lower smear layer/debris scores. Ultrasonic activation produced the lowest mean log10 CFU value, followed closely by Er: YAG laser activation, whereas sonic activation showed an intermediate effect and conventional syringe irrigation showed the highest bacterial load. This trend was paralleled by the SEM findings, where ultrasonic and laser activation produced the lowest overall smear layer/debris scores, while sonic activation showed moderate improvement and the control group showed the greatest smear layer retention. This agreement suggests that the enhanced antimicrobial effect of ultrasonic and laser activation may be partly related to their greater ability to improve irrigant exchange, disrupt biofilm structure, and remove debris from instrumented canal walls.

Comparison with previous studies

The present findings are consistent with previous studies showing that activated irrigation improves root canal disinfection and smear layer/debris removal compared with conventional syringe irrigation. The superior performance of passive ultrasonic irrigation observed in this study agrees with reports attributing its effectiveness to acoustic streaming, increased irrigant velocity, and cavitation-related shear forces [6, 8]. Similarly, the comparable performance of Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation supports previous findings that photoacoustic streaming can enhance irrigant penetration and biofilm disruption [9, 10].

Bao et al. (2024) reported comparable advantages for Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation and passive ultrasonic irrigation over sonic-powered irrigation in a multispecies biofilm model using SEM and CLSM analysis. The agreement between their findings and the present results supports the interpretation that ultrasonic and Er: YAG laser activation may provide stronger biofilm disruption than sonic activation. However, unlike Bao et al., the present study used extracted human mandibular premolars, standardized canal preparation, CFU quantification, and SEM scoring across canal thirds. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as complementary standardized comparative evidence rather than as an entirely new concept [29].

Clinical relevance

Smear layer removal is relevant because open dentinal tubules may allow deeper irrigant penetration and may support improved sealer adaptation [5, 30]. In the present study, ultrasonic and Er: YAG laser activation produced better smear layer/debris removal than sonic activation and conventional irrigation, particularly in the middle and coronal thirds. Nevertheless, the apical third remained the most challenging region in all groups, supporting the need for careful activation protocols and adequate irrigant replenishment. The clinical use of ultrasonic or laser activation should also consider operator training, equipment cost, and protocol sensitivity, particularly for laser activation, where pulse energy, tip design, and activation time must be carefully controlled to avoid thermal or procedural complications [31, 32].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, a mono-species E. faecalis biofilm model was used. Although this model is commonly applied in endodontic research, it does not fully reproduce the polymicrobial nature of clinical root canal infections [2, 33]. Second, the study was conducted under controlled in vitro conditions using extracted mandibular premolars. While this allowed standardization of tooth type, canal preparation, irrigant concentration, and activation protocols, it cannot fully simulate clinical variables such as complex anatomy, tissue fluids, host response, irrigant extrusion risk, postoperative symptoms, or long-term healing [22]. Third, all canals were prepared to a standardized final size of 35/0.04 to allow controlled comparison among groups. Although this reduced methodological variability, it does not fully reflect clinical practice, where final apical preparation size is usually determined according to canal anatomy, initial canal diameter, and the initial binding file. Finally, CFU analysis and SEM evaluation provide useful microbiological and surface-level morphological data, but they do not directly measure fluid dynamics, cavitation, acoustic streaming, photoacoustic shockwaves, temperature changes, or irrigant penetration depth.

Future directions

Future studies should investigate multispecies biofilms, curved and multi-rooted teeth, and more clinically complex canal anatomies. Additional methods such as CLSM, micro-CT, and fluid-dynamic analysis may provide more detailed information on biofilm disruption, irrigant penetration, and three-dimensional canal cleanliness. Sequential or combined activation protocols, including sonic-ultrasonic or ultrasonic-laser approaches, may also be investigated to determine whether combined activation can provide additional benefits over single-technique protocols [34–36].

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this in vitro mono-species Enterococcus faecalis biofilm study, sonic activation, passive ultrasonic irrigation, and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation improved bacterial reduction and smear layer/debris removal compared with conventional syringe irrigation. Passive ultrasonic irrigation and Er: YAG laser-activated irrigation demonstrated greater antimicrobial and cleaning efficacy than sonic activation, with no statistically significant difference between the ultrasonic and laser groups. Further studies using multispecies biofilms, complex canal anatomies, and clinical outcome assessment are required to confirm these findings.
