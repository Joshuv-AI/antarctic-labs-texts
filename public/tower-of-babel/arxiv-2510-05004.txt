Quantitative Limit Theorems for Cox-Poisson and Cox-Binomial Point Processes
Hamza Adrat, Laurent Decreusefond

Abstract
This paper establishes quantitative limit theorems for two classes of Cox point processes, quantifying their convergence to a Poisson point process (PPP). We employ Stein’s method for PPP approximation, leveraging the generator approach and the Stein-Dirichlet representation formula associated with the Glauber dynamics. First, we investigate a Cox-Poisson process constructed by placing one-dimensional PPPs on the lines of a Poisson line process in ℝ 2 {\mathbb{R}}^{2} . We derive an explicit bound on the convergence rate to a homogeneous PPP as the line intensity grows and the point intensity on each line diminishes. Second, we analyze a Cox-Binomial process on the unit sphere 𝕊 2 \mathbb{S}^{2} , modeling a system of satellites. This process is generated by placing PPPs on great-circle orbits, whose positions are determined by a Binomial point process. For this model, we establish a convergence rate of order O ⁡ ( 1 / n ) O(1/n) to a uniform PPP on the sphere, where n n is the number of orbits. The derived bounds provide precise control over the approximation error in both models, with applications in stochastic geometry and spatial statistics.



Keywords:
Stein’s method , Poisson approximation

1.  Introduction

The Poisson point process (PPP) is a cornerstone of stochastic geometry and spatial statistics, serving as a fundamental model for phenomena characterized
by complete spatial randomness. Its analytical tractability and elegant properties make it a natural benchmark for more complex spatial patterns.
A common theme in the theory of point processes is the emergence of Poissonian behavior in the limit of superpositions or transformations of other,
often non-Poisson, processes. While many results establish such convergence in law, a crucial aspect for practical applications is to quantify
the rate of this convergence.

Stein’s method, originally developed for normal approximation by Charles Stein
[
14
]
,
offers a powerful and versatile framework for obtaining explicit error bounds in probabilistic approximations.
The generator approach to Stein’s method is particularly well suited for point process settings,
built upon the theory of spatial birth-and-death processes
[
12
]
. This approach relies on characterizing the target distribution (in our case, the PPP)
via a differential operator of a stationary Markov process, typically the Glauber dynamics. B
y solving the associated Stein equation through its semigroup, one can obtain integral representations for the difference between expectations,
leading to explicit error bounds. This technique has been successfully adapted to various settings,
including infinite-dimensional spaces and Brownian approximations
[
13
,
1
]
.

Our contribution

This paper applies the Stein-generator framework to derive quantitative limit theorems for two important classes of Cox point processes,
which are doubly stochastic processes where the intensity measure itself is random.

1.

The first model is a Cox-Poisson process built upon a Poisson line process in the Euclidean plane, where points are scattered along these random lines.
Such processes are instrumental in the modeling of wireless and vehicular networks
[
6
]
.

2.

The second model is a Cox-Binomial process on the unit sphere, designed to represent satellite constellations where satellites are distributed
along great-circle orbits determined by a finite number of parent bodies.

3.  Preliminaries

3.1.  Poisson and Binomial point processes

Definition 3.1

(Poisson point process)
.

Let
\sigma
be a Radon measure on
\mathbb{X}
. The Poisson point process (PPP)
\Phi
with intensity measure
\sigma
is defined
as the unique point process on
\mathbb{X}
with intensity measure
\sigma
such that, for any disjoint relatively compact subsets

K_{1},K_{2}
, the random variables
\Phi\left(K_{1}\right)
and
\Phi\left(K_{2}\right)
are independent.

Moreover, if there exists
\lambda\in[0,+\infty)
such that
\sigma(\operatorname{d}x)=\lambda m(\operatorname{d}x)
,
then
\Phi
is said to be homogeneous with intensity
\lambda
.

Let
\Phi
be a marked point process, we denote by
\Phi^{0}
the unmarked point process on
\mathbb{X}
, and we suppose that
\mathbb{M}
is equipped with measure
\nu
.
If
\Phi^{0}
is a PPP with intensity
\sigma
, and the marks are stochastically independent,
then the marked point process
\Phi
is also a PPP on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{M}
with intensity
\sigma\otimes\nu
, called a marked Poisson point process (marked PPP).

Similarly, we define a Binomial point process (BPP) and a marked BPP as follows.

Definition 3.2

(Binomial point process)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{X}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
.
A Binomial point process with parameter
N
and supported by
\mu
has exactly
N
points drawn independently according to
\mu
.

And if
\Phi^{0}
is a BPP with parameter
N
and supported by
\mu
, being the unmarked point process of
\Phi
, then
\Phi
is also a BPP with
parameter
N
and supported by
\mu\otimes\nu
, where
\nu
is the measure of the marks space
\mathbb{M}
.

One of the fundamental formulas for PPPs is the following (see
[
4
]
).

Theorem 3.3

(Campbell-Mecke formula for a PPP)
.

Let
\Phi
be a PPP on
\mathbb{X}
with intensity measure
\sigma
.
Then, for any measurable function
F\colon\mathbb{X}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{X}}F(x,\Phi)\sigma(\operatorname{d}x)\right]<\infty,

we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi}F(x,\Phi\ominus x)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{X}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(x,\Phi)\right]\sigma(\operatorname{d}x).

The identity given below provides an analogous integral representation to the Campbell-Mecke formula for a PPP,
specifically adapted to the finite-sample nature of the Binomial point process, see
[
5
]
.

Theorem 3.4

(Campbell-Mecke formula for a BPP)
.

Let
\Phi_{N}
be a BPP on
\mathbb{X}
with parameter
N
and supported by
\mu
.
Then, for any measurable function
F\colon\mathbb{X}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}^{(N)}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{N}}F(x,\Phi_{N})\right]=N\int_{\mathbb{X}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(x,\Phi_{N-1}\oplus x)\right]\mu(\operatorname{d}x),

where
\Phi_{N-1}
is a BPP with
N-1
points drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
.

If
\Phi
is a PPP with intensity measure
\sigma
and
p\in[0,1]
, then the point process denoted
p\circ\Phi
and built by keeping with probability
p

and deleting with probability
1-p
each point
x
of
\Phi
independently is also a PPP with intensity measure
p\sigma
, called the
p
-thinning of
\Phi
.
In particular, a PPP
\Phi
verifies the following invariance property:

Theorem 3.5

(Invariance property)
.

For any
t\in[0,1]
,

t\circ\Phi^{(1)}\oplus(1-t)\circ\Phi^{(2)}\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}\Phi,

where
\Phi^{(1)}
and
\Phi^{(2)}
are independent copies of
\Phi
, and
\overset{\mathcal{D}}{=}
denotes the equality between probability distributions.

Using this invariance property, we can define the semigroup and the infinitesimal generator associated with a PPP through the Glauber dynamics,
which describe a birth-death type Markov process on the configuration space.
The Glauber process evolves by randomly adding and removing points according to certain stochastic rules designed to preserve a given invariant measure.
In the case of the homogeneous PPP of intensity
\lambda
, the dynamics are particularly simple:
each point in the configuration is removed independently at rate
1
(death mechanism),
and new points are added according to a homogeneous PPP of intensity
\lambda
(birth mechanism).
This gives rise to a Markov semigroup
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
acting on functions
F\in\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,\pi^{\sigma})\cap\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{X})
by

\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\omega)={\mathbb{E}}\left[F({\mathcal{G}}_{t})\,|\,{\mathcal{G}}_{0}=\omega\right],

where
({\mathcal{G}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the Glauber dynamics starting from
\omega
.
The process is ergodic with the PPP as its unique invariant measure,
and the semigroup captures the temporal evolution of observables under this stochastic dynamics, see
[
7
,
4
]
.

Definition 3.6

(Glauber semigroup)
.

Let
\Phi
be a PPP with intensity measure
\sigma
. We set

\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\omega)={\mathbb{E}}\left[F\left(e^{-t}\circ\omega\oplus(1-e^{-t})\circ\Phi\right)\right],\;t\geq 0,\;\omega\in{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}},

(1)

for
F\in\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,\pi^{\sigma})
.

Theorem 3.7

(Semigroup of a PPP)
.

The family of operators
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given in Equation (
1
) is a semigroup on the Bannach space

\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,\pi^{\sigma})
, whose stationary measure is the distribution of
\Phi
, i.e.

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\Phi)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Phi)\right],\;\forall\,F\in\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,\pi^{\sigma}).

Moreover,
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an ergodic semigroup, i.e.

\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\omega)\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Phi)\right],\;\forall\,F\in\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\,\pi^{\sigma}).

Theorem 3.8

(Infinitesimal generator of a PPP)
.

Let
\left(\mathcal{P}_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
be the semigroup given in Equation (
1
),
associated to the PPP
\Phi
with intensity measure
\sigma
.
Then, its infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
has its domain containing the set of measurable functions

F\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{X})\cap\operatorname{Dom}D
, and is equal to

\mathcal{L}F(\omega)=\sum_{x\in\omega}\left(F(\omega\ominus x)-F(\omega)\right)+\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(F(\omega\oplus x)-F(x)\right)\sigma(\operatorname{d}x),

for
\omega\in{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}
.

\operatorname{Dom}D
is the domain of the discrete gradient of the PPP
\Phi
, given by:

\operatorname{Dom}D\coloneq\left\{F\colon{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\;{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left|F(\Phi\oplus x)-F(\Phi)\right|\sigma(\operatorname{d}x)\right]<\infty\right\}.

3.2.  Stein’s method

In this section, we consider a topological space
E
and we first recall the definition of
1
-Lipschitz function, which is an important notion for our work.

Definition 3.9

(
1
-Lipschitz function)
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a subset of
E
and let
\operatorname{d}_{\mathbb{L}}
be a distance on
\mathbb{L}
.
We say that a function
h\colon\mathbb{L}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
1
-Lipschitz according to
\operatorname{d}_{\mathbb{L}}
if for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{L}
,

\left|h\left(x_{1}\right)-h\left(x_{2}\right)\right|\leq\operatorname{d}_{\mathbb{L}}\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right),

and we denote by
\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{L},\operatorname{d}_{\mathbb{L}})
the set of all these maps which are measurable.

Stein’s method, first introduced initially by Charles Stein in a seminal paper
[
14
]
in the context of normal approximation,
is a powerful and versatile technique developed for deriving probabilistic approximations and proving limit theorems.
More precisely, let
\eta
be a target probability measure (e.g. Gaussian, Poisson, etc.), and suppose we wish to comapre
a given probability distribution
\mu
to
\eta
, both being probability distances on the same topological space
E
.
The distances adapted to Stein’s method are the ones of the form:

\operatorname{d}_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\mu,\eta)\coloneq\sup_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[{h}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[{h}\right]\right|,

where
{\mathcal{H}}
is a space of test functions from
E
to
{\mathbb{R}}
.

The main metrics used in Stein’s method are the Total variation distance
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}}
and the Wasserstein distance
\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{W}}
defined as follow:

Definition 3.10

(Total variation distance)
.

Let
\mu
and
\eta
be two probability measures on a measurable space
(E,{\mathcal{B}}(E))
. We define the total variation distance on
E
by:

\begin{split}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}}(\mu,\eta)&\coloneq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{B}}(E)}\left|\mu(A)-\eta(A)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[{f}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[{f}\right]\right|.\end{split}

If
X,Y
are two random variables with law
\mu
and
\eta
respectively, then the total variation distance writes also as:

\begin{split}\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}}(X,Y)&\coloneq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{B}}(E)}\left|\mathbb{P}(X\in A)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in A)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{E}\left|f_{X}(z)-f_{Y}(z)\right|\operatorname{d}(\mathbb{P}_{X}+\mathbb{P}_{Y})(z),\end{split}

where
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
are respectively the densities of
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y}
with respect to
\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}+\mathbb{P}_{Y})
.

In particular, if
\mu,\eta\in{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}
, then we have

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}}(\mu,\eta)=\left|\mu\ominus\eta\right|+\left|\eta\ominus\mu\right|.

Definition 3.11

(Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
\mu
and
\eta
be two probability measures on a measurable space
(E,{\mathcal{B}}(E))
, and
\operatorname{d}_{E}
a metric on the space
E
.
We define the Wasserstein distance on
E
by:

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{W}}(\mu,\eta)\coloneq\sup_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(E,\operatorname{d}_{E})}\left|{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[{f}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[{f}\right]\right|.

Similarly, if
X
,
Y
are two random variables with law
\mu
and
\eta
respectively, then the Wasserstein distance writes also as:

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{W}}(X,Y)=\sup_{f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}(E,\operatorname{d}_{E})}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(X)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(Y)\right]\right|.

These metrics are well-suited to different approximation problems.
For instance, the Wasserstein distance is particularly natural when dealing with functionals of metric spaces,
while total variation distance is more stringent and sensitive to rare events.

In our case, the target object is a finite Poisson point process.
So, we consider a functional operator
\mathcal{L}
which, at a formal level, satisfies for a finite point process
\Phi
on
\mathbb{X}
the identity

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{L}F(\Phi)\right]=0\text{ for a class of functions }F\colon{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

if and only if,
\Phi
is a Poisson point process with finite intensity measure
\sigma
.

Then, we solve the Stein’s equation, which means that we ne need to find, for any test function
F\colon{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
a function
H_{F}\colon{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that, for any
\phi\in{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}
, we have

\mathcal{L}H_{F}(\phi)={\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\zeta)\right]-F(\phi),

where
\zeta
is a Poisson point process with finite intensity measure
\sigma
.

So, we use the so-called generator approach applied in
[
13
,
1
]
and based on theory of spatial birth-and-death processes
[
12
]
.
In our case,
\mathcal{L}
is built as an infinitesimal generator of the Glauber process seen in the previous section, with the
distribution of
\zeta
as its invariant meausre. And given the Glauber semigroup
(\mathcal{P}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
mentioned in Definition
3.6
,
we can show that we have for any
\phi\in{\mathfrak{N}\textsuperscript{f}}_{\mathbb{X}}
,

\mathcal{L}H_{F}(\phi)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\phi)\,\operatorname{d}t,

which leads us to the well-known Stein-Dirichlet representation formula for PPPs:

Theorem 3.12

(Stein-Dirichlet representation formula for PPPs)
.

For any functional
F\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{X})\cap\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}};\,\pi^{\sigma})
, and any
\omega\in{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}}
,
the Stein-Dirichlet representation formula for the PPP
\Phi
is given by:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Phi)\right]-F(\omega)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}_{t}F(\omega)\,\operatorname{d}t.

4.  Quantitative Limit Theorem for Cox-Poisson Point Processes

The set of lines generated by a PPP on
\mathbb{X}\equiv{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times[0,2\pi)
is called a Poisson line process.
And a Cox-Poisson point process, as illustrated in Figure
1
, is constructed by populating points on the lines of Poisson line process
such that the locations of points on each line form an independent 1D PPP.

Figure 1:
Cox-Poisson point process.

In other words, a Cox-Poisson point process, denoted by
Y
, can be seen as the image of a marked PPP constructed by first drawing a PPP on
\mathbb{X}

to construct a line denoted
D(r,\theta)
(characterized by the perpendicular distance
r
of the line from the origin,
and the angle
\theta
subtended by the perpendicular dropped onto the line from the origin with respect to the positive
x
-axis in counterclockwise direction).
And then the mark of each line is a locally finite Poisson point process on the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Our goal is to quantify the convergence of a sequence of such processes
(Y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
given by the following transformation:

\displaystyle T\colon{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{X}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{{\mathbb{R}}}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{N}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\delta_{(r_{i},\theta_{i},\zeta_{i}^{\mu_{n}})}

\displaystyle\longmapsto Y_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{(r_{i},\theta_{i})}\left(\sum_{x\in\zeta_{i}^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{\Pi_{(r_{i},\theta_{i})}(x)}\right),

where
\Phi_{n}
is a marked PPP on
\mathbb{X}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{{\mathbb{R}}}
with intensity

\sigma_{n}\coloneq\lambda_{n}\ell^{+}\otimes\frac{1}{2\pi}{\mathbf{1}}_{[0,2\pi)}\otimes\pi^{\mu_{n}}
,
(
\mu_{n}
is the intensity of the PPP
\zeta^{\mu_{n}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}
),
and
\Pi_{(r_{i},\theta_{i})}(x)
is the projection of the point
x
from the PPP
\zeta^{\mu_{n}}_{i}
onto the line
D(r_{i},\theta_{i})
.

This analysis is rooted in the modeling principles laid out in
[
6
]
,
and adapts technical tools from
[
13
,
1
]
to our setting in order to address the mathematical challenges
posed by the random line structure and marked point process representation.
Following the generator approach developed in these works,
we leverage the Stein-Dirichlet representation formula (Theorem
3.12
) as a key tool
to bound the Wasserstein distance between the law of
Y_{n}
and the target Poisson distribution.

The following Lemma is important to prove our main result.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbf{L}^{1}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+},\;\ell^{2}\right)
. We denote by
D_{r,\theta}
the line of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
characterized by
(r,\theta)
.
Then, for a fixed value of
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, we have:

\int_{A}f(x)\operatorname{d}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\left(\int_{A\cap D_{r,\theta}}f(s)\operatorname{d}H^{1}(s)\right)\operatorname{d}r,

(2)

where
A
is a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
H^{1}
is the one-dimensional Hausdorff measure.

Here is the main theorem of this section, stated as follows:

Theorem 4.2

(Convergence of the Cox-Poisson Point Process)
.

We suppose that
\lambda_{n}\longrightarrow+\infty\text{ and that }\mu_{n}=\dfrac{c}{\lambda_{n}}
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}^{*}_{+}
.
We consider a compact subset
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Then, we have:

\sup_{F\in\operatorname{Lip}_{1}({\mathfrak{N}}_{K},\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{TV}})}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(Y_{n})\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(N)\right]\right|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\left(c^{2}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(H^{1}(K_{r,\theta})\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}r\frac{\operatorname{d}\theta}{\pi}\right),

(3)

where
N
is a PPP on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with intensity
c\,\ell^{2}
, and
K_{r,\theta}=K\cap D_{r,\theta}
.

Another way to present the previous result is to use the following inequality

\operatorname{d}_{\operatorname{W}}(Y_{n}\cap K,N\cap K)\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}\left(c^{2}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(H^{1}(K_{r,\theta})\right)^{2}\operatorname{d}r\frac{\operatorname{d}\theta}{\pi}\right).

5.  Quantitative Limit Theorem for Cox-Binomial Point Processes

The BPP offers a simple yet powerful way to describe a finite set of independent points sampled according to a reference measure.
One particularly interesting application arises in the modeling of satellites orbiting the Earth,
where their spatial distribution can be seen as a transformation of a marked BPP.

In this setting, each satellite is initially associated with a point sampled uniformly on the unit sphere,
representing a random position on the Earth’s surface. Given such a point, we define its associated orbit as the great circle orthogonal to its position.
This reflects the natural fact that satellites often move along nearly circular trajectories centered around the Earth.
Furthermore, in order to capture additional randomness in their motion, we associate a mark to each point
x
.
This mark is modeled as a PPP defined on the unit circle, whose points are then projected onto the orbit of
x
through the corresponding rotation.

Let the state space be the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
endowed with a reference probability measure
\nu
(i.e. the normalized surface measure).
We first sample a Binomial configuration of “orbits”
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
with i.i.d. locations from
\nu
.
To each base point

\displaystyle T\colon{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{i},\zeta_{i}^{\mu_{n}})}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\Psi_{n}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{y\in\zeta_{i}^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x_{i}}(y)}\right).

Here is the main theorem of this section:

Theorem 5.1

(Poisson approximation for the satellite model)
.

for some

, and

\mathbb{S}^{2}

.
Then, we have:

(4)

Intuitively, as the number of sampled orbits increases while individual orbits become sparsely populated,
the superposition of independent Poisson-distributed marks leads to a spatial configuration that asymptotically exhibits the properties of a Poisson process on
\mathbb{S}^{2}

The intensity measure

reflects the uniform distribution of initial positions weighted by the limiting expected number of satellites per orbit.
The proof follows the Stein-generator approach adapted here to the Binomial setting.

In particular, the Wasserstein-
1

6.  Conclusion

In this paper, we have successfully derived quantitative limit theorems for two distinct classes of Cox point processes using Stein’s method.
By leveraging the generator approach and the Stein-Dirichlet representation formula for the Poisson point process, we established explicit,
non-asymptotic bounds on the distance to a limiting PPP.

Our first result provided a convergence rate for a Cox-Poisson process on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, constructed from a Poisson line process.
The derived error bound highlights a clear dependence on the line intensity and the geometry of the observation window,
offering a precise measure of approximation quality.
Our second result focused on a Cox-Binomial model for satellite constellations on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, for which we established a sharp convergence rate of order
O(1/n)
.
Together, these findings demonstrate the robustness of the Stein-generator framework in handling complex dependencies
arising from transformations of both Poisson and Binomial point processes.

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{(x,\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\in\Phi_{n}}\tilde{F}\left(\Phi_{n}\ominus\delta_{(x,\zeta^{\mu_{n}})}\right)-F(\Phi_{n})\right]\\
=n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\tilde{F}(\Phi_{n-1})-\tilde{F}\left(\Phi_{n-1}\oplus\delta_{(x,\zeta^{\mu_{n}})}\right)\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\\
=n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\\
=n\int{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right){\mathbf{1}}_{U=1}\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\\
+n\int{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}}).

(11)

From Equations (
10
) and (
11
), we obtain:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\left[\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n}))\right.\right.\\
-\left.\left.\left(F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right)\right]\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\left[\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n}))\right.\right.\\
-\left.\left.\left(F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right)\right]{\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\left(F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right].

A second use of the Mecke formula and the use of the intermediate functional
\tilde{F}
yield:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}F\left(\Psi_{n}\ominus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
-n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}}).

Combining these results, we get:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{y\in\Psi_{n}}F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right]\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
+n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right){\mathbf{1}}_{U=1}\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
+n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left(F(\Psi_{n-1})-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{y\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(y)}\right)\right)\right|U=1\right]\\
\mathbb{P}(U=1)\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
+n\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1})-F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)\right]\mu_{n}e^{-\mu_{n}}\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{U\geq 2}\right]\\
-c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n-1})\right]e^{-\mu_{n}}\nu(\operatorname{d}y).

Therefore,

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{L}F(\Psi_{n})\right]=I_{1}+I_{2}\implies\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{L}F(\Psi_{n})\right]\right|\leq\left|I_{1}\right|+\left|I_{2}\right|,

with

I_{1}\coloneq c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right)\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
-c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n-1})\right]e^{-\mu_{n}}\nu(\operatorname{d}y),

and,

I_{2}\coloneq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}(F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})){\mathbf{1}}_{\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\geq 2}\right].

The difficulty we face now is that we do not have the same configuration
\Psi_{n}
in the expressions of
I_{1}
.
Instead, we have
\Psi_{n}
and
\Psi_{n-1}
.
To bridge the expectation over the law of
\Psi_{n}
with an expression involving
\Psi_{n-1}
,
we leverage the constructive definition of the Binomial process.
Specifically, the
n
-orbit configuration
\Psi_{n}
can be viewed as the superposition of an
(n-1)
-orbit configuration
\Psi_{n-1}

and the satellite process generated from a single, independent
n
-th orbit.
This translates to the following expression:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n})\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\right].

Therefore,

I_{1}=c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(F(\Psi_{n}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right)\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
-c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n-1})\right]e^{-\mu_{n}}\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
=c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\left(F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right.\right.\\
-\left.\left.F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right)\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
-c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n-1})\right]e^{-\mu_{n}}\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
=c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}\left[e^{-\mu_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right.\right.\right.\\
-\left.\left.\left.F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right)\right|U=0\right]\\
+(1-e^{-\mu_{n}})\mathbb{E}\left[\left(F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right.\right.-\left.\left.\left.F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right)\right|U\geq 1\right]\\
\left.-e^{-\mu_{n}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[F(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y)-F(\Psi_{n-1})\right]\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\nu(\operatorname{d}y)\\
=c\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times{\mathfrak{N}}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}}(1-e^{-\mu_{n}})\mathbb{E}\left[\left(F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right.\right.\\
-\left.\left.\left.F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right)\right|U\geq 1\right]\nu(\operatorname{d}x)\pi^{\mu_{n}}(\operatorname{d}\zeta^{\mu_{n}})\nu(\operatorname{d}y).

Now, using the fact that the functional
F
is
1
-Lipschitz, which means in particular that we have

\left|F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus y\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)-F\left(\Psi_{n-1}\oplus\sum_{z\in\zeta^{\mu_{n}}}\delta_{R_{x}(z)}\right)\right|\leq 1,

we deduce that

\begin{split}|I_{1}|&\leq 1c(1-e^{-\mu_{n}})\\
&\leq c\mu_{n}\\
&=\frac{c^{2}}{n}\cdotp\end{split}

Again by
1
-Lipschitzness,

\begin{split}|I_{2}|&\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\sum_{y\in\Gamma_{x}}\left|F(\Psi_{n}\ominus y)-F(\Psi_{n})\right|{\mathbf{1}}_{\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\geq 2}\right]\\
&\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{x\in\Phi_{n}^{0}}\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1}){\mathbf{1}}_{\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\geq 2}\right]\\
&\leq n\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1}){\mathbf{1}}_{\zeta^{\mu_{n}}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\geq 2}\right]\\
&\leq n\,\mu_{n}(1-e^{-\mu_{n}})\\
&\leq n\,\mu_{n}^{2}\\
&=\frac{c^{2}}{n}\cdotp\end{split}

So as a conclusion, we have shown

\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathcal{L}F(\Psi_{n})\right]\right|\leq|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|\leq\frac{2c^{2}}{n}\cdotp

By Equation (
9
) and the contraction property of
\mathcal{P}_{t}
, the same bound transfers to the integrand, yielding the claimed estimate in
(
4
). ∎
