Commutation principles for optimization problems involving strictly Schur-convex functions in Euclidean Jordan algebras
Pedro G. Massey, Noelia B. Rios *, David Sossa

Abstract




Abstract
. In this work we establish several commutation principles
for optimizers of shifts of spectral functions in the context of Euclidean Jordan Algebras
(EJAs). For instance, we show that under certain assumptions, if
\bar{x}
is a (local) optimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
, where
\Omega\subset\mathcal{V}
is a spectral set of an EJA
\mathcal{V}
,
a\in\mathcal{V}
and

F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a strictly Schur-convex spectral function, then
a
and
\bar{x}
operator commute. We make no further assumption on the smoothness of
F
; instead, we take advantage of the smoothness
(Lie structure) of the Automorphism group of
\mathcal{V}
and make use of majorization techniques for the eigenvalues of elements in EJAs. Our approach allows us to deal with several problems considered in the literature, related to strictly convex spectral functions and strictly convex spectral norms. In particular, we use our commutation principles to analyze the problem of minimizing the condition number in EJAs.

Mathematics Subject Classification
: 17C20, 15A27, 15A42, 90C26

Keywords
: Euclidean Jordan algebra, commutation principle, Lidskii’s inequality, strictly Schur-convex function, condition number.

1
Introduction

Commutation principles in Euclidean Jordan algebras (EJAs) consist of a class of optimization problems involving (weakly) spectral sets and functions where it is ensured that each (local) optimizer operator commutes with some element related to the objective function. For instance, let
\mathcal{V}
be a EJA, consider the map

x\in\Omega\mapsto\Theta(x)+F(x),

(1)

where
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, and
\Theta,\,F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
. In
[
17
]
, Ramírez, Seeger and Sossa have proved that when
\Omega
is a spectral set,
\Theta
is differentiable, and
F
is a spectral function (see definitions in the next section), if
\bar{x}
is a local optimizer (minimizer or maximizer) of (
1
), then
\nabla\Theta(\bar{x})
and
\bar{x}
operator commute. Gowda and Jeong
[
5
]
further developed this commutation principle by assuming that
\Omega
and
F
are weakly spectral (i.e., they are invariant under automorphisms, which is a weaker condition than spectrality). These commutation principles were successfully applied in some variational problems like obtaining a meta-formula for generalized subdifferential of spectral functions
[
12
]
, and deducing bounds for the determinant of the sum of two elements in EJAs
[
20
]
. It has also been investigated strong commutation principles for this class of problems, which consist of commutation principles that provide strong operator commutation. For instance, Gowda
[
4
]
proved that under the assumption that
\Omega
and
F
are spectrally defined, and by setting
\Theta(x)=\langle x,a\rangle
, with
a\in\mathcal{V}
,

\bar{x}
is a global maximizer of (
1
)
\Rightarrow

a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute.

(2)

This class of results allows us, for instance, to simplify the problem of minimizing (
1
) to a minimization problem formulated in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n
being the rank of
\mathcal{V}
, via the eigenvalue map.

In this paper, we study commutation principles for the problem of optimizing the map

x\in\Omega\mapsto F(x-a),

(3)

where
a\in\mathcal{V}
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a spectral function. Observe that, in general, the maps (
3
) and (
1
) are not related; so, we can not use directly the commutation principle discussed above to characterize the optimizers of (
3
). Indeed, despite the fact that
F
is spectral, the shifted spectral function
F(\cdot-a)
is no longer spectral. Moreover, we are not imposing a differentiability assumption over
F
. Some particular cases of (
3
) have been studied in the literature. For instance, Jeong and Sossa
[
9
]
have recently proved that when
\Omega
is weakly spectral and
F
is a strictly convex spectral function or a strictly convex spectral norm, if
\bar{x}
is a local optimizer of (
3
) then
a
and
\bar{x}
operator commute. Their proof relies on a commutation principle developed for (
1
) by using nonsmooth analysis. Massey, Rios and Stojanoff
[
14
]
have also obtained a commutation principle for (
3
)
in the context of Hermitian matrices, which are examples of (simple) EJAs. Their result states that in case
\Omega
is an orbit of some element of
\mathcal{V}
and
F
is a strictly convex spectral norm, if
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer of (
3
) then
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute. Their approach is based on majorization techniques and on the smooth manifold structure of unitary orbits of Hermitian matrices. Similar results have also been applied in some other optimization problems
[
1
]
.

Inspired by the works of Massey, Rios and Stojanoff
[
14
]
, and Jeong and Sossa
[
9
]
, our aim is to develop commutation principles and strong commutation principles for the problem of optimizing (
3
) with
\Omega
and
F
as general as possible. Our proof techniques are mainly inspired by those techniques developed in
[
14
]
; that is, we will use majorization techniques in EJAs, see
[
8
]
. The spectral function
F
will be assumed to be strictly Schur-convex. Observe that this class of functions is quite broad and covers classes like spectral functions that are strictly convex, strictly quasi-convex, and strictly convex spectral norms. We also introduce a new strictly Schur-convex function which is used to deal with the problem of minimizing the condition number (see Section
7
).

Our main results are the following commutation principles: Let
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly Schur-convex spectral function;

(i)

Suppose that
\Omega
is a spectral set. If
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer (resp. global maximizer) of (
3
), then
a
and
\bar{x}
(resp.
-a
and
\bar{x}
) strongly operator commute (see Theorem
4.1
).

(ii)

Suppose that
\Omega
is a weakly spectral set. If
\bar{x}
is a local optimizer (minimizer or maximizer) of (
3
), then
a
and
\bar{x}
operator commute (see Theorem
5.1
).

(iii)

Suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is simple, and set
\Omega
as the eigenvalue orbit of
b
. If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer (resp. local maximizer) of (
3
), then
a
and
\bar{x}
(resp.
-a
and
\bar{x}
) strongly operator commute. Moreover,
\bar{x}
is a global optimizer of (
3
) (see Theorem
6.1
).

Regarding item (i) above, we remark that in this case, if we replace the hypothesis that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer (resp. global maximizer)
by the weaker hypothesis that
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer (resp. local maximizer)
of (
3
)
then
a
and
\bar{x}
(resp.
-a
and
\bar{x}
) do not necessarily strongly operator commute
(see Example
6.4
). We also point out that if we replace the assumption that
\Omega
is a spectral set for the (weaker) assumption that

\Omega
is a weakly spectral set and assume that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer (resp. global maximizer) of (
3
)
then,
a
and
\bar{x}
(resp.
-a
and
\bar{x}
) do not necessarily strongly operator commute (see Example
4.2
below).

Regarding item (ii), notice that weakly spectral sets are rather small domains (as opposed to spectral sets, that are larger domains) for an spectral function in an arbitrary EJA. Hence, the hypothesis that
\bar{x}
is a local optimizer within weakly spectral sets is a rather weak assumption. In particular, the result also applies to local optimizers within spectral sets (see Corollary
5.7
).

Regarding item (iii), we point out that if we remove the hypothesis that
\mathcal{V}
is simple and assume that
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer (resp. local maximizer) of (
3
) then,
a
and
\bar{x}
(resp.
-a
and
\bar{x}
) do not necessarily strongly operator commute (see Example
4.2
below). Also, notice that we have set
\Omega
to be a rather minimal set, and hence, the hypothesis that
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer in
\Omega
is quite weak.

As a consequence of the previous comments, notice that the results in items
({\rm i})
-
({\rm iii})
are sharp.

The organization of the paper is as follows: In Section
2
, we provide a background of the concepts and tools from EJAs that we require for developing our results. In Section
3
, we review majorization concepts and provide examples of strictly Schur-convex spectral functions. Sections
4
,
5
, and
6
are mainly devoted to proving the respective statements (given above) (i), (ii), and (iii). In Section
7
, we illustrate the applications of our commutation principles to the analysis of the problem of minimizing the condition number in EJA.

2
Preliminaries

Euclidean Jordan algebras

Let
(\mathcal{V},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\circ)
be
Euclidean Jordan algebra
(EJA) of rank
n
and unit element
e
. For any
x,y\in\mathcal{V}
,
\langle x,y\rangle
denotes the inner product and
x\circ y
denotes the Jordan product. We refer the reader to the book of Faraut and Korányi
[
3
]
as a general reference for the theory of EJAs.

Here, we recall some definitions and properties of EJAs that will be used later.
The Jordan product in
\mathcal{V}
satisfies the commutation law (
x\circ y=y\circ x
, for
x,\,y\in\mathcal{V}
),
but it is not associative in general; still, the Jordan product satisfies the Jordan identity:
(x\circ y)\circ(x\circ x)=x\circ(y\circ(x\circ x))
, for
x,\,y\in\mathcal{V}
.
Recall that an EJA is said to be
simple
if it is not a direct product of nonzero EJAs. It is well known
[
3
, Corollary IV.1.5, Theorem V.3.7]
that any EJA can be decomposed into a direct product of simple EJAs and there are only five simple ones up to isomorphism. One of them is the algebra
\mathcal{S}^{n}
of
n\times n
symmetric matrices with Jordan and inner products given by

X\circ Y=\frac{XY+YX}{2},\quad\left<X,\,Y\right>=\operatorname{tr}(XY)\;\mbox{(trace inner product)}.

An element
c\in\mathcal{V}
is an
idempotent
if
c^{2}=c
. An
idempotent
c
is
primitive
if it is nonzero and cannot be written as a sum of two nonzero idempotents. A
Jordan frame
is a collection
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
of primitive idempotents satisfying

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=e
, and
c_{i}\circ c_{j}=0
when
i\neq j.

The spectral decomposition theorem asserts that any element
x\in\mathcal{V}
can be written as

x=\lambda_{1}(x)e_{1}+\lambda_{2}(x)e_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}(x)e_{n},

where
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\lambda_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(x)
are real numbers called the eigenvalues of
x
, and
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
is a Jordan frame. We denote
\lambda(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(eigenvalue map) to the vector whose components are the eigenvalues of
x
arranged in nonincreasing order. The
trace
of
x\in\mathcal{V}
is defined as the sum of its eigenvalues. That is,
{\rm tr}(x):=\lambda_{1}(x)+\cdots+\lambda_{n}(x).
The inner product of
\mathcal{V}
is scaled so that we can assume that
\langle x,y\rangle={\rm tr}(x\circ y)
which is the
trace inner product
. From now on,
\|\cdot\|
will denote the norm on
\mathcal{V}
induced by the trace inner product; that is,
\|x\|=({\rm tr}(x\circ x))^{1/2}
. When
\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\|\cdot\|
coincides with the Euclidean norm (2-norm) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
p
be an idempotent element of
\mathcal{V}
. It is known that
\mathcal{V}
can be decomposed as the direct sum

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(p,1)\oplus\mathcal{V}(p,0)\oplus\mathcal{V}(p,1/2),

(4)

where
\mathcal{V}(p,\alpha):=\{x\in\mathcal{V}:x\circ p=\alpha x\}
, for
\alpha\in\{1,0,1/2\}
. This decomposition is called the
Peirce decomposition
of
\mathcal{V}
with respect to
p
. It is also known that
\mathcal{V}(p,1)
and
\mathcal{V}(p,0)
are subalgebras of
\mathcal{V}
, and
\mathcal{V}(p,1/2)\neq\{0\}
whenever
\mathcal{V}
is simple and
p\notin\{0,e\}
.

Two elements
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{V}
are said to
operator commute
if
L_{a}L_{b}=L_{b}L_{a}
, where
L_{a}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
is a linear map defined as
L_{a}(x)=a\circ x
for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
. The operator commutativity property in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
coincides with the usual matrix commutativity property. That is,
A
and
B
operator commute in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
if and only if
AB=BA
. It is known that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
operator commute if and only if both
a
and
b
can be
simultaneously decomposed
by the same Jordan frame. That is, there exist a Jordan frame
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
and real numbers
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
such that

a=\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\alpha_{2}e_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}e_{n},\quad b=\beta_{1}e_{1}+\beta_{2}e_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{n}e_{n},

(5)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are some rearrangements of
\lambda(a)
and
\lambda(b)
, respectively. We say that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}

strongly operator commute

if there exists a frame
\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
for
\mathcal{V}
such that the representations
in Eq. (
5
) hold, in such a way that
\alpha=\lambda(a)
and
\beta=\lambda(b)
.
Then, one can see that
a
and
b
strongly operator commute if and only if
\left<a,\,b\right>=\left<\lambda(a),\,\lambda(b)\right>
.

Orbits, spectral sets, and spectral functions

A linear map
X:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}
is an
(Jordan algebra) automorphism
if
X
is bijective (invertible) and
X(a\circ b)=Xa\circ Xb,
for all
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
. The (Lie) group of all automorphisms of
\mathcal{V}
is denoted by
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V})
. As an example, the automorphism group
{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{S}^{n})
of the EJA of
n\times n
symmetric matrices can be identified with
\mathcal{O}(n)
i.e. the orthogonal group in dimension
n
.

For
b\in\mathcal{V}
, there are two kinds of orbits of
b
. The first one is the
eigenvalue orbit
which consists of the elements of
\mathcal{V}
with the same eigenvalues of
b
; that is

[b]:=\{x\in\mathcal{V}:\lambda(x)=\lambda(b)\}.

The second one is the
Automorphism orbit
(or weak orbit), which is defined as

[b]_{w}:=\{Xb:X\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V})\}.

We have that
[b]_{w}\subseteq[b]
and the equality holds whenever
\mathcal{V}
is simple.

The above orbits are the basic form of sets known as spectral and weakly spectral sets, respectively. We say that a set
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
symmetric
if it is permutation invariant which means that if
u\in Q
then
Pu\in Q
for every permutation matrix
P
of order
n
. A set
\Omega\subset\mathcal{V}
is called
spectral set
if there exists a symmetric set
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\Omega=\{x\in\mathcal{V}:\lambda(x)\in Q\}
.
\Omega
is called
weakly spectral set
if it is invariant under automorphisms which means that if
x\in\Omega
and
A\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V})
then
Ax\in\Omega
. In particular,
[b]
is a spectral set and
[b]_{w}
is a weakly spectral set. It is known that any spectral set is weakly spectral, because of
\lambda(Ax)=\lambda(x)
for all
A\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V})
and
x\in\mathcal{V}
. Moreover, the concepts of spectral and weakly spectral coincide whenever
\mathcal{V}
is simple.

We say that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
symmetric
if it is permutation invariant which means that if
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
f(u)=f(Pu)
for every permutation matrix
P
of order
n
. A function
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
spectral function
if there exists a symmetric function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F(x)=f(\lambda(x))
for all
x\in\mathcal{V}
.

3
Majorization and strictly Schur-convex functions

For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
u^{\downarrow}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote the vector obtained from
u
by arranging its entries in non-increasing order. We denote
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\downarrow}:=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:u=u^{\downarrow}\}
. For
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one says that
u
is
majorized
by
v
, in symbol
u\prec v
, if

u^{\downarrow}_{1}+\cdots+u^{\downarrow}_{k}\leq v^{\downarrow}_{1}+\cdots+v^{\downarrow}_{k}\quad\text{ for every }\quad k=1,\ldots,n\,,

(6)

and
u^{\downarrow}_{1}+\cdots+u^{\downarrow}_{n}=v^{\downarrow}_{1}+\cdots+v^{\downarrow}_{n}
. If only the conditions in (
6
) hold, we say that
u
is submajorized by
v
, and write
u\prec_{w}v
.
We say that
u
is
strictly majorized
by
v
if
u\prec v
and
u^{\downarrow}\neq v^{\downarrow}
(
u
is not a rearrangement of
v
). For
x,y\in\mathcal{V}
, we say that
x
is majorized by
y
in
\mathcal{V}
and write
x\prec y
if
\lambda(x)\prec\lambda(y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
x
is strictly majorized by
y
in
\mathcal{V}
if
\lambda(x)
is strictly majorized by
\lambda(y)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
Schur-convex
if for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

u\prec v\;\Rightarrow\;f(u)\leq f(v).

If, in addition,
f(u)<f(v)
whenever
u
is strictly majorized by
v
, then
f
is said to be
strictly Schur-convex
(cf.
[
13
]
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a spectral function given by
F(x)=f(\lambda(x))
. We say that
F
is a
Schur-convex
(resp. strictly Schur-convex) spectral function if its associated symmetric function
f
is Schur-convex (resp. strictly Schur-convex).

Since the commutation principles that we developed in this work require considering strictly Schur-convex spectral functions, we list some examples of them below. Other examples can be found in
[
13
, Chapter 3]
and
[
21
]
.

Example 3.2
.

Any strictly convex spectral function
F
is strictly Schur-convex. Indeed, if
f
is the associated symmetric function of
F
, in
[
9
]
was proved that
F
is strictly convex if and only if so is
f
. Then, the result follows by recalling that any strictly convex symmetric function is strictly Schur-convex.

Example 3.3
.

We say that
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
strictly convex spectral norm
if it is a spectral function and a strictly convex norm in
\mathcal{V}
. Recall that
F(\cdot)
is a strictly convex norm in
\mathcal{V}
if it is a norm in
\mathcal{V}
satisfying
F(x+y)<2
for any distinct nonzero
x,y\in\mathcal{V}
such that
F(x)=F(y)
. For instance, the Schatten
p
-norm of
x\in\mathcal{V}
given by
||x||_{p}:=(|\lambda_{1}(x)|^{p}+\cdots+|\lambda_{n}(x)|^{p})^{1/p}
is a strictly convex norm for
p>1
. In
[
9
]
, it is proved that any strictly convex spectral norm
F
in
\mathcal{V}
is strictly Schur-convex. Indeed,
F
is a strictly convex spectral norm in
\mathcal{V}
if and only if its associated symmetric function
f
is a strictly convex symmetric norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, any strictly convex symmetric norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is strictly Schur-convex.

Example 3.4
.

Recall that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
strictly quasi-convex
function if
f(\alpha u+(1-\alpha)v)<\max\{f(u),f(v)\}
for all
u\neq v
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, see
[
2
, Definition 2.2.2]
. By using induction, one can prove that
f
is strictly quasi-convex if and only if for all integer
k\geq 2
, and for all distinct vectors
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
f(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}u_{i})<\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}f(u_{i})
for all
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly quasi-convex symmetric function. We claim that
f
is strictly Schur-convex. Indeed,
let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
u\prec v
and
u^{\downarrow}\neq v^{\downarrow}
. Since
f
is strictly quasi-convex, then it is quasi-convex. It is known that a quasi-convex symmetric function is Schur-convex
[
13
]
. Then,
f(u)\leq f(v)
. We will prove that
f(u)<f(v)
when
f
is strictly quasi-convex. In fact, suppose that
f(u)=f(v)
. Since
u
is strictly majorized by
v
, then
u
is in the convex hull of
\{Pv:P\text{ is a $n\times n$ permutation matrix}\}
but it is not an extreme point of that set. Hence,
u=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}P_{i}v
, where
k\geq 2,\alpha_{i}>0
for all
i
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=1
,
P_{i}
is a permutation matrix for all
i
, and
P_{i}v\neq P_{j}v
whenever
i\neq j
. Thus, we arrive to the following contradiction:

f(v)=f\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}P_{i}v\right)<\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}f(P_{i}v)=f(v),

where the strict inequality is because of the strict quasi-convexity of
f
, and the right equality is because
f
is symmetric. The claim follows.

Therefore, a spectral function is strictly Schur-convex whenever its associated symmetric function is strictly quasi-convex.

Example 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an EJA of rank
n
and let
\mathcal{V}_{+}=\{b\in\mathcal{V}\ :\ \lambda_{n}(b)>0\}
.
Given
b\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
, we can consider the condition number
\mathfrak{c}(b)=\lambda_{1}(b)/\lambda_{n}(b)>0
, which is a measure of the spread of the eigenvalues of
b
(see
[
19
]
for some optimization problems related with the condition number in EJAs). Similarly, if

c\in\mathcal{V}

we can consider the spectral spread of
c
,
\text{Sprd}(c)=\lambda_{1}(c)-\lambda_{n}(c)\geq 0
, which is also a measure of the spread of the eigenvalues of
c
. For the particular case when
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{S}^{n}
is the EJA of real symmetric
n\times n
matrices,
\mathfrak{c}(b)
and
\text{Sprd}(c)
play a role in determining the stability properties of
b\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
and
c\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
. It is well known that
\mathfrak{c}(\cdot):\mathcal{V}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\text{Sprd}(\cdot):\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are Schur-convex functions (since
u\prec v
implies that

\lambda_{n}(v)\leq\lambda_{n}(u)
and
\lambda_{1}(v)\geq\lambda_{1}(u)
) but not strictly Schur-convex. In order to get the corresponding strictly Schur-convex function associated with

\mathfrak{c}(\cdot)
and
\text{Sprd}(\cdot)
we consider the functions

\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}(b)=\|(\lambda_{i}(b)/\lambda_{n-i+1}(b))_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\|\quad\text{and}\quad\text{Sprd}_{\|\cdot\|}(c)=\|(\lambda_{i}(c)-\lambda_{n-i+1}(c))_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\|\,,

where
b\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
,
c\in\mathcal{V}
, and
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
(
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
n/2
).
The case
\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}
is analyzed in Section
7
. The analysis for the case
\text{Sprd}_{\|\cdot\|}
is analogous. See
[
10
]
for the introduction of the vector valued spread of selfadjoint matrices and
[
15
,
16
]
for some of its basic properties and applications in
\mathcal{S}^{n}
.

One of the main tools used to develop our analysis is the
Lidskii’s inequality
. As mentioned in
[
11
]
, the Lidskii’s inequality is one of the central tools for studying perturbation theory for the eigenvalues of symmetric matrices. For the general case of Euclidean Jordan algebras, the proof of the Lidskii’s inequality can be found in
[
8
, Theorem 5.1.]
. The Lidskii’s inequality says that for
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{V}
, the following majorization is satisfied:

\lambda(a)-\lambda(b)\prec\lambda(a-b).

(7)

We will also use the
Ky Fan’s inequality
which says that for
a
and
b
in
\mathcal{V}
:

\lambda(a+b)\prec\lambda(a)+\lambda(b).

(8)

It is not difficult to see that the Ky Fan’s inequality can be obtained from the Lidskii’s inequality.

Below, we provide a characterization for the strong operator commutation property by using the Lidskii’s inequality.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

a
and
b
strongly operator commute.

(b)

\displaystyle\lambda(a+b)=\lambda(a)+\lambda(b).

(c)

\displaystyle(\lambda(a)-\lambda(b))^{\downarrow}=\lambda(a-b).

Proof.

The equivalence
(a)\Leftrightarrow(b)
is proved in
[
6
, Proposition 2.6]
. Let us prove
(a)\Leftrightarrow(c)
. Suppose that
a
and
b
strongly operator commute. Then there exist a Jordan frame
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
such that
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(a)c_{i}
and
b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(b)c_{i}
. Hence,

a-b=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}(a)-\lambda_{i}(b))c_{i}
which implies the desired result
\lambda(a-b)=(\lambda(a)-\lambda(b))^{\downarrow}
. Suppose now that
(c)
holds. Let
x\in[a]
. Then, by observing that
\lambda(x)=\lambda(a)
, and by using the Lidskii’s inequality we obtain

\lambda(a-b)=(\lambda(x)-\lambda(b))^{\downarrow}\prec\lambda(x-b).

(9)

Since the 2-norm function
\|\cdot\|
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex and symmetric, we have that it is Schur-convex. Then, from (
9
) we obtain
\|\lambda(a-b)\|\leq\|\lambda(x-b)\|
. Hence,

\|a-b\|\leq\|x-b\|
for all
x\in[a]

where the norm on
\mathcal{V}
is the one induced by the trace inner product. Hence,
a
is global minimizer of
\frac{1}{2}\|x-b\|^{2}
for
x\in[a]
. Note that this is equivalent to say that
a
is a global maximizer of
\langle x,b\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
for
x\in[a]
. Thus, since
-\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
is a spectral function, we can use the strong commutation principle (
2
) to deduce that
a
and
b
strongly operator commute.
∎

4
On global optimizers and strong commutation principles

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly Schur-convex spectral function with
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as its associated symmetric function, let
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{V}
be a spectral set with
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as its associated symmetric set, and let
a\in\mathcal{V}
. Then, the following statements are satisfied:

(a)

If
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
, then
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute. Moreover,

\min_{x\in\Omega}F(x-a)=\min_{u\in Q}f(u-\lambda(a))=f(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a)).

(10)

(b)

If
\bar{x}
is a global maximizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
, then
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute. Moreover,

\max_{x\in\Omega}F(x-a)=\max_{u\in Q}f(u+\lambda(-a))=f(\lambda(\bar{x})+\lambda(-a)).

(11)

Proof.

(a)
. Let
\bar{x}\in\Omega
be a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
, for
x\in\Omega
. Suppose that
a
has the spectral decomposition
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(a)c_{i}
for some Jordan frame
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
. Let us take
\hat{x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})c_{i}
. Observe that
\lambda(\hat{x})=\lambda(\bar{x})
by construction. Hence,
\hat{x}\in[\bar{x}]\subseteq\Omega
since this last set is spectral. Then,
\hat{x}-a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})-\lambda_{i}(a))c_{i}
which implies

\lambda(\hat{x}-a)=(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))^{\downarrow}\prec\lambda(\bar{x}-a),

(12)

where the majorization is because of the Lidskii’s inequality (
7
).
Let
f
be the symmetric function associated with
F
. Since
F
is a strictly Schur-convex spectral function, then
f
is a strictly Schur-convex symmetric function. Then, from (
12
), we obtain

F(\hat{x}-a)=f(\lambda(\hat{x}-a))\leq f(\lambda(\bar{x}-a))=F(\bar{x}-a).

(13)

Recall that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
. Then, this fact and (
13
) imply

f(\lambda(\hat{x}-a))=f(\lambda(\bar{x}-a))
. Hence, due to the strict Schur-convexity of
f
we deduce that

\lambda(\hat{x}-a)=\lambda(\bar{x}-a).

(14)

Indeed, otherwise,
\lambda(\hat{x}-a)
is strictly majorized by
\lambda(\bar{x}-a)
and the inequality (
13
) should be strict. Now, by combining the equality in (
12
) with (
14
) we deduce that

\lambda(\bar{x}-a)=(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))^{\downarrow}
. Thus, from Proposition
3.6
, we conclude that
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute.

To prove (
10
), observe that since
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute, there exists a Jordan frame
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
such that
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(a)e_{i}
and
\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})e_{i}
. Then,

\min_{x\in\Omega}F(x-a)=F(\bar{x}-a)=f(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))\geq\min_{u\in Q}f(u-\lambda(a)).

(15)

We claim that the equality is attained in (
15
). Indeed, let
\hat{u}
be a global solution of the right-hand side problem of (
15
). If
f(\hat{u}-\lambda(a))<f(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))
, then we can take
\hat{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{u}_{i}e_{i}
, which is in
\Omega
since
\lambda(\hat{x})=\hat{u}^{\downarrow}\in Q
, obtaining

F(\hat{x}-a)=f(\hat{u}-\lambda(a))<f(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))=F(\bar{x}-a),

contradicting that
\bar{x}
is a global solution of the left-hand side problem of (
15
).

(b)
. Let
\bar{x}\in\Omega
be a global maximizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
. We consider the representation
of
a
as
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\lambda_{i}(-a)c_{i}
, and we set
\hat{x}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})c_{i}
. Then, from the Ky Fan inequality (
8
) we get

\lambda(\bar{x}-a)\prec\lambda(\bar{x})+\lambda(-a)=\lambda(\hat{x}-a).

Thus,
F(\bar{x}-a)\leq F(\hat{x}-a)
, and since
\bar{x}
is a global maximizer we have
F(\bar{x}-a)=F(\hat{x}-a)
. Therefore, by reasoning as in item
(a)
, we deduce that
\lambda(\bar{x}-a)=\lambda(\bar{x})+\lambda(-a)
, which implies that
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute because of Proposition
3.6
. The proof of (
11
) is analogous to the proof of (
10
) with the difference that in this case we have
a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(-a)e_{i}
and
\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})e_{i}
, for some Jordan frame
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, because
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute.

∎

It is natural to consider whether the above Theorem is still valid if
\Omega
is assumed to be weakly spectral. The following example shows that this is not the case.

Example 4.2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{S}
is a simple EJA of rank
2
, and consider the nonsimple EJA given by
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}
. For
b\in\mathcal{V}
, recall that
[b]_{w}
is weakly spectral (but not necessarily spectral since the algebra is not simple). Below we construct
a,\,b,\,\bar{x}\in\mathcal{V}

such that
\bar{x}\in[b]_{w}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b]_{w}
(for any continuous function
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
) and such that
a
and
\bar{x}
do not strongly operator commute.

Recall from
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
that in this case, each
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{V})
has the form

\phi=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\circ\sigma
, with
\phi_{j}\in{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{S})
,
j=1,\,2
and where
\sigma
denotes the identity of
\mathcal{V}
or the transposition

\sigma((b_{1},b_{2}))=(b_{2},b_{1})
, for
(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{V}
.
As a consequence, we conclude that given
b=(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{V}

[b]_{w}=\{\,x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathcal{V}:\ (\lambda(x_{1}),\lambda(x_{2}))=(\lambda(b_{1}),\lambda(b_{2}))\ \text{ or }\ (\lambda(x_{1}),\lambda(x_{2}))=(\lambda(b_{2}),\lambda(b_{1}))\,\}\,,

where
\lambda(x_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the eigenvalue vector of
x_{j}\in\mathcal{S}
, for
j=1,2
.
Hence, the structure of
\mathcal{V}
imposes certain restrictions on
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{V}
so that this element can be an element of the orbit
[b]_{w}
, beyond merely spectral conditions on
\lambda(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Indeed, consider
b=(b_{1},b_{2})\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\lambda(b_{1})=(4,1)
and
\lambda(b_{2})=(3,2)
. Let
\{e_{1},\,e_{2}\}
be a Jordan frame for
\mathcal{S}
and consider

a_{1}=4\,e_{1}+3\,e_{2}
and
a_{2}=2\,e_{1}+1\,e_{2}
. Hence,

\lambda(a_{1})=(4,3)
and
\lambda(a_{2})=(2,1)
so that
a=(a_{1},a_{2})\notin\mathcal{[}b]_{w}
, even when
\lambda(b)=\lambda(a)=(4,3,2,1)
.

We now show that
a
and
x
do not strongly operator commute, for any

x\in[b]_{w}
. Indeed, if there is
x\in[b]_{w}
such that
a
and
x
strongly operator commute
then there exists a Jordan frame
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{4}\}
for
\mathcal{V}
such that

x=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}(b)\,f_{j}
and
a=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\lambda_{j}(a)\,f_{j}
. Since all eigenvalues of
a
are simple
(have multiplicity one) then we get that
f_{1}=(e_{1},0)
,
f_{2}=(e_{2},0)
,
f_{3}=(0,e_{1})
and
f_{4}=(0,e_{2})
.
Hence, we now see that
x_{1}=4\,e_{1}+3\,e_{2}=a_{1}
and
x_{2}=2\,e_{1}+1\,e_{2}=a_{2}
(thus,
x_{j}
and
a_{j}
strongly operator commute in
\mathcal{S}
, for
j=1,2
). Notice that this last fact contradicts that
x\in[b]_{w}
.

Let
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Since
[b]_{w}
is compact, we conclude that there exists
\bar{x}\in[b]_{w}
which is a minimizer
of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b]_{w}
; by the previous arguments, we get that
a
and
\bar{x}
do not strongly operator commute.

The next result shows the converse of Theorem
4.1
when
\Omega
is an eigenvalue orbit.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Schur-convex spectral function. Let
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
, and let
\bar{x}\in[b]
. Then, the following statements are satisfied:

(a)

If
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute, then
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b].

(b)

If
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute, then
\bar{x}
is a global maximizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b].

Proof.

(a)
. Suppose that
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute, then there exists a Jordan frame
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
such that

a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(a)c_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\bar{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})c_{i}.

Then
\bar{x}-a=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}(\bar{x})-\lambda_{i}(a))c_{i}
which implies that for all
x\in[b]
,

\lambda(\bar{x}-a)=(\lambda(\bar{x})-\lambda(a))^{\downarrow}=(\lambda(x)-\lambda(a))^{\downarrow},

where the second equality is because
\lambda(x)=\lambda(b)=\lambda(\bar{x})
since
x
and
\bar{x}
remain in the orbit
[b]
. Then, From the Lidskii’s inequality (
7
), we get

\lambda(\bar{x}-a)=(\lambda(x)-\lambda(a))^{\downarrow}\prec\lambda(x-a),

(16)

for all
x\in[b]
. By definition, the fact that
F
is a Schur-convex spectral function means that its associated symmetric function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is Schur-convex. Then, from (
16
) we obtain

F(\bar{x}-a)=f(\lambda(\bar{x}-a))\leq f(\lambda(x-a))=F(x-a),

for all
x\in[b]
. It means that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b].

(b).
The proof is analogous to the previous item. Indeed, by assuming that
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute we can deduce that for all
x\in[b]
,

\lambda(\bar{x}-a)=\lambda(\bar{x})+\lambda(-a)=\lambda(x)+\lambda(-a).

Then, the Ky Fan’s inequality (
8
) implies that

\lambda(x-a)\prec\lambda(x)+\lambda(-a)=\lambda(\bar{x}-a)
,
for all
x\in[b]
. Thus, since
F
is Schur-convex we conclude that
F(x-a)\leq F(\bar{x}-a)
for all
x\in[b]
. It means that
\bar{x}
is a global maximizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b].

∎

Since
[b]_{w}\subseteq[b]
, the above result is still valid if we substitute
[b]
by
[b]_{w}
. This is proved in the next corollary.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
F:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
be a Schur-convex spectral function, and let
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
. Let
\bar{x}\in[b]_{w}
be such that
a
(respectively,
-a
) and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute. Then,
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer (respectively, maximizer) of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b]_{w}.

Proof.

Let
\bar{x}\in[b]_{w}
be such that it strongly operator commutes with
a
. Since

[b]_{w}\subseteq[b]
we have that
\bar{x}\in[b]
, and by Theorem
4.3
we deduce that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b].
Furthermore, the inclusion
[b]_{w}\subseteq[b]
also implies that
\bar{x}
is a global minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b]_{w}.
The proof for the case
\bar{x}
and
-a
strongly operator commute is analogous.
∎

5
A general commutation principle

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an EJA and let
a\in\mathcal{V}
. Let
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a strictly Schur-convex spectral function, and let
\Omega\subset\mathcal{V}
be a weakly spectral set. Let
\bar{x}
be a local optimizer (minimizer or maximizer) of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\Omega
. Then
a
and
\bar{x}
operator commute.

The proof of Theorem
5.1
will be divided into several lemmas. Essentially, we will show that the assumption that
a
and
\bar{x}
do not operator commute will lead us to the conclusion that
\bar{x}
can not be a local optimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in[b]_{w}
.

Let
a,b\in\mathcal{V}
. Recall that the property that
a
and
b
operator commute is equivalent to the property that
a
and
b
are simultaneously (spectral) decomposed; that is, there exists a Jordan frame
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\}
such that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}(c_{1},1)\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{V}(c_{n},1)
. If
a
and
b
do not operator commute, in some cases, they can still be simultaneously decomposed but in blocks; that is, there exist non-zero idempotents
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
(for some
2\leq k\leq n
) such that
p_{i}\circ p_{j}=0
if
i\neq j
,
p_{1}+\ldots+p_{k}=e
(which means that
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\}
is a complete system of orthogonal idempotents) and such that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}(p_{1},1)\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{V}(p_{k},1)
. If this is not the case, we say that
a
and
b
are totally noncommuting. This concept will be key in our forthcoming lemmas. Below, we give a precise definition.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an EJA and let
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
. We say that
a
and
b
are
simultaneously block decomposable
if there exists an idempotent
p\notin\{0,e\}
such that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}(p,1)\oplus\mathcal{V}(p,0)
. We say that
a
and
b
are
totally (operator) noncommuting
if they are not simultaneously block decomposable.

We also need to recall some tools from differential geometry. Let
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{M}
be smooth manifolds embedded into finite dimensional real inner product spaces
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
, respectively. For
u\in\mathcal{N}
, we denote by
T_{u}(\mathcal{N})
to the tangent space of
\mathcal{N}
at
u
. A smooth map
\Psi:\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{M}
is a
submersion
at
u\in\mathcal{N}
if its differential
D\Psi_{u}:T_{u}(\mathcal{N})\to T_{\Psi(u)}(\mathcal{M})
is a surjective linear map.

It is known that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V})
has a smooth manifold (Lie group) structure whose tangent space at the identity (Lie algebra) is
\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})
. Recently, in
[
9
, Proposition 2]
, it has been proved that the operator commutativity property can be characterized as follows:

\text{$a$ and $b$ operator commute}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\langle Da,b\rangle=0\text{ for all }D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V}).

(17)

In what follows we consider
\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\,:\ {\rm tr}(x)=t\}
, for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Notice that

\mathcal{V}
is a translation of an hyperplane in
\mathcal{V}
and has a natural sub-manifold structure; moreover, we have that
T_{a}(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\mathcal{V}_{0}=\{x\in\mathcal{V}\,:\ {\rm tr}(x)=0\}
, for every
a\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an EJA. Fix
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
and set
t={\rm tr}(a-b)
. Let
\Psi=\Psi_{(a,b)}:\text{Aut}(\mathcal{V})\times\text{Aut}(\mathcal{V})\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{t}
be given by
\Psi(X,Y)=Xa-Yb
. Then
\Psi
is a submersion at
(I,I)
if and only if
a
and
b
are totally noncommuting.

Proof.

Observe that
T_{(I,I)}(\rm Aut(\mathcal{V})\times\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{V}))=\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})\times\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})
and
T_{\Psi(I,I)}(\mathcal{V}_{t})=T_{a-b}(\mathcal{V}_{t})=\mathcal{V}_{0}
. Then, the differential map of
\Psi
at
(I,I)
is given by

D\Psi:\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})\times\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})\to\mathcal{V}_{0},\;D\Psi(D,E)=Da-Eb.

By definition,
\Psi
is a submersion at
(I,I)
if and only if the differential map
D\Psi
is surjective.
Hence,
\Psi
is not a submersion if and only if there exists

f\in\mathcal{V}_{0}
,
f\neq 0
such that
f
is orthogonal to the range of
D\Psi
.
In turn, this last condition is equivalent to the fact that

\langle Da,f\rangle=\langle Db,f\rangle=0\quad\text{for every}\quad D\in\text{Der}(\mathcal{V})\,,

(18)

which means, because of (
17
), that
f
operator commutes with
a
and
b
simultaneously. Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{k}
be the distinct eigenvalues of
f
. We know from the first version of the spectral theorem
[
3
, Theorem III.1.1]
that there exists a unique complete system of orthogonal idempotents
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
such that

f=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}c_{i}.

Notice that since
f\neq 0
and
{\rm tr}(f)=0
then
f
should have at least two different eigenvalues which implies that
c_{i}\neq e
for all
i
. Thus, since
f
operator commutes with
a
and
b
, we deduce that
c_{1}
also operator commutes with
a
and
b
. Therefore,
a,b\in\mathcal{V}(c_{1},1)\oplus\mathcal{V}(c_{1},0)
with
c_{1}\neq 0
and
c_{1}\neq e
. This proves that
a
and
b
are simultaneously block decomposable, which means that they are not totally noncommuting.

Conversely, suppose that
a
and
b
are simultaneously block decomposable. Then, there is an idempotent
p\notin\{0,e\}
such that
a,b\in\mathcal{V}(p,1)\oplus\mathcal{V}(p,0)
. If we let
f={\rm tr}(p)^{-1}\,p-{\rm tr}(e-p)^{-1}(e-p)
then
f
is a nonzero element in
\mathcal{V}_{0}
. Furthermore, relation (
18
) holds because
\langle Da,p\rangle=\langle Db,p\rangle=0
, since
p
operator commutes with
a
and
b
, and
\langle Da,e\rangle=\langle Db,e\rangle=0
, since
e
operator commutes with each element in
\mathcal{V}
. Therefore,
f
is orthogonal to
D\Psi(\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V})\times\operatorname{Der}(\mathcal{V}))
. The converse now follows from these facts.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an EJA, and let

6
Strong commutation principles for local extrema in simple EJAs

In this section, we present the following strong commutation principle for local minimizers in automorphism orbits of simple EJAs. We remark that, unless stated otherwise, all EJAs
\mathcal{V}
considered in this section will be simple; in particular,
[b]=[b]_{w}
, for
b\in\mathcal{V}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a simple EJA, let
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a strictly Schur-convex spectral function, and let
a,\,b\in\mathcal{V}
.

(a)

If
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\mathcal{[}b]
then
a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute.

(b)

If
\bar{x}
is a local maximizer of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\mathcal{[}b]
then
-a
and
\bar{x}
strongly operator commute.

Moreover, if
\bar{x}
is a local minimizer (resp. maximizer) of
F(x-a)
for
x\in\mathcal{[}b]
then it is a global minimizer (resp. maximizer).

will require the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let

is strictly majorized by

and

.

Let

. By re-arranging the coordinates of

and
v
, we can assume that

and

v=v^{\downarrow}
. If
v_{1}\leq u_{1}
then the hypothesis
u_{1}+u_{2}=v_{1}+v_{2}
implies that
v\prec u
. As a consequence we get that
\|u\|^{2}\geq\|v\|^{2}
, since

,

, is a convex function. This last fact contradicts our assumption

7
An application to the problem of minimizing the condition number in EJA

In this section, we assume that
\mathcal{V}
is an EJA of rank
n\geq 2
. Recall that
\lambda_{i}(x)
are the eigenvalues of
x\in\mathcal{V}
arranged in nonincreasing order:
\lambda_{1}(x)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(x)
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{+}
denote the interior of the cone of square elements of
\mathcal{V}
(symmetric cone of
\mathcal{V}
). It can be characterized as
\mathcal{V}_{+}=\{x\in\mathcal{V}:\lambda_{n}(x)>0\}
.

The condition number of
x\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
is defined as

\mathfrak{c}(x)=\frac{\lambda_{1}(x)}{\lambda_{n}(x)}.

Given
a\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
, there is interest in studying the problem of perturbing
a
in such a way as to diminish its condition number as much as possible, see
[
19
,
7
]
. This problem can be formulated

\min_{x\in\Omega}\mathfrak{c}(x+a),

(30)

where
\Omega
is some set of admissible perturbations. It is known that
\mathfrak{c}(\cdot)
is Schur-convex on
\mathcal{V}_{+}
, but not necessarily strictly Schur-convex. Thus, we cannot apply our results directly to study this problem since we require strict Schur-convexity.

To overcome the above drawback, we consider a vector containing not only the condition number but also the ratio between the
i
th eigenvalue and the
(n-i+1)
th eigenvalue, for
i=1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. This is specified in the next definition.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
, and let

\kappa_{i}(x):=\frac{\lambda_{i}(x)}{\lambda_{n-i+1}(x)}\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor.

The
condition vector
of
x
is defined as the vector
\kappa(x)=\left(\kappa_{1}(x),\ldots,\kappa_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(x)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
.

It is straightforward to see that the entries of
\kappa(x)
are arranged in nonincreasing order and that

\kappa_{1}(x)=\mathfrak{c}(x)
. Because of those facts, it can be proved that

\lfloor n/2\rfloor^{-1/2}\|\kappa(x)\|\leq\mathfrak{c}(x)\leq\|\kappa(x)\|,

for every
x\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
, where
\|\kappa(x)\|
denotes the euclidean norm of
\kappa(x)
. This shows that the behavior of
\|\kappa(x)\|
is comparable to the behavior of
\mathfrak{c}(x)
. That is, if
\|\kappa(x)\|
is small or large, then
\mathfrak{c}(x)
is small or large, respectively. Thus, we can analyze the problem (
30
) by solving the problem

\min_{x\in\Omega}\|\kappa(x+a)\|.

(31)

Our goal is to describe some fundamental properties of the solutions of (
31
) by relying on the tools we developed in the previous sections. For
x\in\mathcal{V}_{+}
, let us denote
\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}(x):=\|\kappa(x)\|
. The next proposition shows that
\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}
is a strictly Schur-convex spectral function on
\mathcal{V}_{+}
. Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
denote the interior of the positive orthant of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

The following statements are satisfied.

(a)

\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}:\mathcal{V}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a spectral function with associated symmetric function
\phi_{\|\cdot\|}:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\phi_{\|\cdot\|}(u)=\|\phi(u)\|
, with

\phi(u)=\left(\frac{u^{\downarrow}_{1}}{u^{\downarrow}_{n}},\frac{u^{\downarrow}_{2}}{u^{\downarrow}_{n-1}},\ldots,\frac{u^{\downarrow}_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}}{u^{\downarrow}_{n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor+1}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

(b)

\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}
is a strictly Schur-convex spectral function on
\mathcal{V}_{+}
.

Proof.

(a) It is clear from the definition of
\kappa_{\|\cdot\|}
.

(b) We first show that
\phi_{\|\cdot\|}
is Schur-convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. Indeed, let
u,\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
be such that
u\prec v
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
u=u^{\downarrow}
and
v=v^{\downarrow}
. Hence, by the relations between majorization and convex functions (see
[
13
, Theorem 5.A.1]
), we get that

(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})^{\downarrow}\ni(u_{n}^{-1},\ldots,u_{1}^{-1})\prec_{w}(v_{n}^{-1},\ldots,v_{1}^{-1})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})^{\downarrow}\,.

As a consequence, we get the following relations for the entry-wise product vectors (see
[
13
, Theorem 5.A.4.f]
)
(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})^{\downarrow}\ni(u_{i}/u_{n-i+1})_{i=1}^{n}\prec_{w}(v_{i}/v_{n-i+1})_{i=1}^{n}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n})^{\downarrow}\,.
In particular,

(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})^{\downarrow}\ni(u_{i}/u_{n-i+1})_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\prec_{w}(v_{i}/v_{n-i+1})_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})^{\downarrow},

(32)

which implies that

\phi_{\|\cdot\|}(u)\leq\phi_{\|\cdot\|}(v)
.

We now show that
\phi_{\|\cdot\|}
is strictly Schur-convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. As before,
we consider
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
such that
u\prec v
, and assume that
u=u^{\downarrow}
and
v=v^{\downarrow}
.
Notice that, in particular, these vectors satisfy the normalization condition
{\rm tr}(u):=u_{1}+\cdots+u_{n}=v_{1}+\cdots+v_{n}:={\rm tr}(v)>0
.
Suppose further that
\phi_{\|\cdot\|}(u)=\phi_{\|\cdot\|}(v)
. Notice that
\|\cdot\|
is a strictly Schur-convex function, and it is a strictly increasing function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
(for the componentwise ordering in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
). Hence, from
[
13
, Theorem 3.A.8]
and the submajorization (
32
) we have that

u_{i}/u_{n-i+1}=v_{i}/v_{n-i+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. In particular, we get that
u_{1}/u_{n}=v_{1}/v_{n}
; now, using that
u\prec v
implies that
v_{n}\leq u_{n}
and
v_{1}\geq u_{1}
, we see that
u_{i}=v_{i}
for
i\in\{1,n\}
. We can continue in this way and show that
u_{i}=v_{i}
, for
i\in\{j,\,n-j+1\}
and
1\leq j\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Thus, we get
u=v
when
n
is even. When
n
is odd, the previous fact together with the normalization condition
{\rm tr}(u)={\rm tr}(v)
imply that
u=v
.
∎
