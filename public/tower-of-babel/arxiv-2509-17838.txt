Abstract
. Let ℳ ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{M}(n) be the subgroup of G ​ L ​ ( n , ℤ ) GL(n,\mathbb{Z}) generated by the particular involutions that are identical to the identity, except for a single line where − 1 -1 and + 1 +1 alternate. We study the properties of ℳ ⁡ ( n ) \mathcal{M}(n) , and then find several notable characteristics of the unions of trajectories obtained by iteratively applying a fixed sequence of such involutions to elements from ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} .

1.
Introduction

In the quest for distinguished patterns in tiling problems or partitioning the space with integers, certain involutions arise and prove to have a directive role.
Depending on their destined play, they can function in a lower dimension, as in
[
1
]
, where they were used to indicate location and content
by changes in direction and size, or by keeping the dimension as in
[
5
,
4
]
, where they also take on the property of being invariant under translations.

Within the same class, the type of operators we are considering are elements of
GL(n,\mathbb{Z})
,
which act on elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}

by replacing one component with an alternating sum of all components to which an additive constant is added while the other components remain unchanged.
Nonetheless, that constant added to the alternating sum
entangles a straightforward generalization of these transformations
to higher dimensions.
In this case, by selecting a feasible option, we have opted to put the constant aside,
along with the requirement of invariance under translations,
keeping only the one of being involutions.
Moreover, in the most basic considered version, we can replace
\mathbb{Z}
with
\mathbb{F}_{2}
,
but to emphasize the reflective property, we will keep the entries in
\{-1,0,1\}
instead.

Let
n
be a positive integer.
For any integer

j
, with
1\leq j\leq n
,
we let
K_{j}=K_{j}(n)
denote the modified identity matrix such that the
j
-th line
starts with
(-1)^{j}
and then alternates
\pm 1
’s.
To be precise, let

\bm{e}_{j}=\bm{e}_{j}(n)
be the
j
-th standard basis
(column) vector
and let

\bm{r}_{j}=\bm{r}_{j}(n):=\big((-1)^{j},(-1)^{j+1},\dots,(-1)^{j+n-1}\big)
.
(We will omit the reference to dimension
n
as the dimension will be clear from the context.)
Thus

\displaystyle K_{j}:=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}.

(1)

We also consider the corresponding operators

\K_{j}:\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that transform any vector

\bm{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
into the product
(K_{j}\bm{x}^{T})^{T}
.
(Further on, in this context, we will drop the indication
of transpositions by writing simply
\K_{j}(\bm{x})
or just
K_{j}\bm{x}
.)
Iteratively applying operators
\K_{j}
, with indices selected from
a given sequence of positive integers,
creates a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, called
trajectory
or
orbit
, when it is closed.
These trajectories vary based on the chosen initial point, called
trajectory
generator
.
In the case of non-trivial sequences,
excluding generators belonging to certain special
locations that are part of sets with zero measure in the
working environment (such as the origin, specific axes,
planes, or hyperplanes), the trajectories share the same
class of shape indicated by the same underlying topology.
For any fixed sequence of indices of the operators,
the union of the associated trajectories forms a set
partition of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
The mentioned additive constant, which is
0
in the case
of operators
\K_{j}
, causes the trajectories generated by
their iterated application to be bounded.
This is in contrast to, for instance, the trajectories
that were unbounded parallel parabolas
or intricately looking circles that partition the plane

\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in
[
5
,
4
]
.

Applying the

There are orbits that also have notable arithmetic properties
such as those related to squares and primes.
Thus, an orbit that

1.1.
Main results

Our work has two main parts.
In Sections
2
–
5
, starting from the observation that
all
K_{j}
with
j=1,2,\dots,n
are involutions,
we follow step by step on their properties and
arrive at a comprehensive description of the group they
generate (see Theorem
6
).
Lately, we find that, as a Coxeter group,

\langle K_{1},K_{2},\dots,K_{n}\rangle
is isomorphic
with the symmetric group
S_{n+1}
(see Section
5
).
In the second part, we turn our attention specifically to the characteristic
aspects of sets formed by unions of orbits, particularly in the two-dimensional
case.
This proves to be essential for understanding the general phenomenon,
which will be the topic of a subsequent work.

Starting from an arbitrary
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

In each orbit

By letting

The following theorem shows the common property of all orbits to be
specifically colored based on the positioning of their nodes in the plane.

Theorem 1
.

In the Euclidean distance, the diametrically opposite nodes of
the bounding boxes enclosing the two-dimensional
orbits project onto the unit circle in two opposite arcs of length

\arctan\left(2\right)-\arctan\left(1/2\right)

Then,
(\arctan\left(2\right)-\arctan\left(1/2\right))/\pi\approx 0.204833
is,
in this sense,
the limit probability that a lattice point belongs to the Euclidean diameter of the orbit to which it belongs.

2.
The involutions
K_{j}

Let us begin by noticing that the difference

Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}

sets the
j
th line of the identity matrix to zero
and
\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}
is the modified-zero matrix with the
j
th line replaced by
\bm{r}_{j}
,
so that the operators

K_{j}=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}
defined
by (
1
)
indeed satisfy the intended setup.

For example, if
n=3
, the operators are:

\displaystyle K_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1&-1\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\ K_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
1&-1&1\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\text{ and }K_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
-1&1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Here, we list the main operations that drive the grammar in the group

\langle K_{1},K_{2},\dots,K_{n}\rangle
.

Remark 2.1
.

Let
n,j
, and
\ell
be integers and suppose
1\leq j,\ell\leq n
.
Then:

(1)

The following products are
1\times 1
matrices:

\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}=(1)
;

\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}=-(1)
;

\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{r}_{j}^{T}=-(1)
;
and
\bm{r}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}^{T}=n(1)
.
Also, we have:

\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}=\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}=(-1)^{j+l-1}(1)
.

(2)

The
n\times n
matrix
\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}
has
1
at position
(j,\ell)

and zeros elsewhere.

(3)

The matrix
\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}
replaces the
j
-th line of the zero matrix
with
\bm{r}_{j}
.

(4)

If
j\neq\ell
, then
\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{\ell}=(0)
.

(5)

If
j\neq\ell
, then

\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}=-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}
.

The next lemma verifies that
K_{j}
is an involution.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
n
and
j
be integers, and suppose

1\leq j\leq n
.
Then
K_{j}^{2}=Id
.

Proof.

The definition (
1
) implies

\begin{split}K_{j}^{2}&=\big(Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}\big)\big(Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}\big)\\
&=Id-2\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+2\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j})\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j})\bm{r}_{j}-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{j})\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{j})\bm{r}_{j},\end{split}

where we have associated the matrices in the middle whose product is
1
-dimensional.
Then, observing by Remark
2.1
.
(1)
that
\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}=(1)

and
\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}=-(1)
, it follows that

\displaystyle K_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=Id-2\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+2\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}=Id.

∎

Next we calculate the product of two distinct
K
-operators.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
j,\ell
, and
n
be integers and suppose
1\leq j\neq\ell\leq n
.
Then

\displaystyle K_{j}K_{\ell}=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}.

(2)

Proof.

By definition (
1
), we have:

\begin{split}K_{j}K_{\ell}&=\big(Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}\big)\big(Id-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\big)\\
&=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\\
&\quad+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{r}_{\ell}-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{r}_{\ell}.\end{split}

(3)

According to Remark
2.1
.
(4)

j\neq\ell
implies
\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{\ell}=(0)
, so that
the first two terms on the last line of (
3
) are
\mathbf{0}
(the
n\times n
zero matrix).
Thus

\begin{split}K_{j}K_{\ell}&=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\\
&\quad-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{r}_{\ell}.\end{split}

(4)

By Remark
2.1
.
(1)
, the last two terms above are:

\displaystyle-\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}(\bm{r}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell})\bm{r}_{\ell}=(-1)^{j+\ell-1}(-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{\ell}).

Replacing this expression in (
4
) and rearranging the terms, we obtain

\displaystyle K_{j}K_{\ell}

\displaystyle=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\big(\bm{r}_{j}+(-1)^{j+l-1}\bm{r}_{\ell}\big).

(5)

By checking all possible parity cases, we find that the last term cancels out
yielding (
2
) and concluding the proof.
∎

Thus, Lemma
2.2
shows that the product

K_{j}K_{\ell}

\ell

\bm{r}_{\ell}
,
and on line

\displaystyle K_{1}K_{4}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&-1\\

Following the sequential transformations in the proof
of Lemma
2.2

step by step, we see that
in the first line of relation (

in the identity matrix the lines

and

Then, after the cancellation of the last term in (

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
j,\ell
, and
n
be integers and suppose
1\leq j\neq\ell\leq n
.
Then:

(i)

K_{j}K_{\ell}K_{j}=K_{\ell}K_{j}K_{\ell}=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\left(\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{l}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\right)
;

(ii)

(K_{j}K_{\ell})^{3}=Id
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
and (
1
), we have:

\begin{split}K_{j}K_{\ell}K_{j}&=\left(Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\right)\left(Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}\right)\\
&=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\\
&\quad-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+(-1)^{j+\ell+1}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\\
&\quad+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}.\end{split}

(6)

Since
\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}=(1)
by Remark
2.1
.
(1)
, and

\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}=(0)
by Remark
2.1
.
(4)
,
expression (
6
) reduces to

\begin{split}K_{j}K_{\ell}K_{j}&=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}\\
&\quad-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}.\end{split}

Here we observe that
\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}

is the zero matrix with the
j
-th element on line

replaced by
(-1)^{l+j-1}
, so that it coincides with

(-1)^{l+j-1}\bm{e}_{l}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}
. Therefore

\begin{split}K_{j}K_{\ell}K_{j}&=Id-\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}-\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+(-1)^{j+\ell}\left(\bm{e}_{j}\bm{e}_{\ell}^{T}+\bm{e}_{l}\bm{e}_{j}^{T}\right)+\bm{e}_{\ell}\bm{r}_{\ell}\left(Id+\bm{e}_{j}\bm{r}_{j}\right).\end{split}

In the previous equality, the last term cancels out according to Remark
2.1
.
(5)
and, since it is invariant under the interchange of

, the equalities in
(i)
follow.

In order to establish

,
we start with the equality

and multiply it successively on the right

,
K_{j}
, and
K_{l}
, noting that the involution shown in Lemma

leads to the right-hand side of the outcome being equal to

.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

5.
Completion of the proof that

\mathcal{M}(n)\cong S_{n+1}

Let us view
\mathcal{M}(n)
in a more abstract framework, that of the Coxeter groups (see
[
7
,
8
,
2
,
10
]
).
As a finite group generated by
K_{1},\dots,K_{n}
,
the associated matrix of the orders of the product of any two generators is

(m_{jk})
, where
m_{jk}=m_{kj}:=3
, if
1\leq j\neq k\leq n
,
according to Lemma
2.3
, and
m_{jj}:=1
if
1\leq j\leq n
,
according to Lemma
2.1
.
Then, the pair group-generators

(M(n),\{K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\})
, together with the presentation given by all relations
(K_{j}K_{k})^{m_{jk}}=Id

for
1\leq j,k\leq n
forms a Coxeter system,
and
M(n)
is called a Coxeter group.
Being a symmetric matrix that describes the relations
between the involutions that generate the group
M(n)
,

(m_{jk})
is a Coxeter matrix.

In an equivalent way, the Coxeter matrix can be represented
as an unordered graph, called Coxeter diagram,
whose nodes are the generators
K_{j}
, and the edge
between any two distinct nodes
K_{j}
and
K_{k}
has weight
m_{jk}
for
1\leq j\neq k\leq n
.
The convention is that no loop
K_{j}K_{j}
is included in the diagram, and that the arcs are labeled by the weights only if they are strictly larger than
3
.
Hence, the Coxeter diagram of
M(n)
is a polygon with

n
vertices along with all its sides and diagonals.

Viewed as linear operators over an
n
-dimensional vector space,

K_{j}
also verifies the conditions for being a reflection (see
[
10
,
2
]
),
so that
M(n)
is also a reflexion group.

The symmetric group
S_{n+1}
is also a reflection group
as it identifies with a subgroup of

O_{n+1}(\mathbb{Q}):=\left\{O\in GL(n+1,\mathbb{Q}):O^{\mathsf{T}}O=OO^{\mathsf{T}}=Id\right\}

of orthogonal
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrices,
in which permutations act on
\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
by permuting
the subscripts of the basis vectors

e_{j}=(0,\dots,0,1,0\dots 0)

(the
n+1
-tuple with all components equal to 0,
except the
j
th, which is
1
).
Moreover,
S_{n+1}
is also a Coxeter group, but the presentation
provided by the sequence of ordinary set of transpositions

(j,j+1)
leads to a linear Coxeter diagram, which is fundamentally distinct from the above Coxeter diagram of
M(n)
.

In order to see that
M(n)
is indeed isomorphic to
S_{n+1}
,
a different system of generators for
S_{n+1}
is needed.
One that proves suitable is the set of transpositions

(1,j)
with
2\leq j\leq n+1
.
These transpositions also generate
S_{n+1}
, because
(with the multiplication of transpositions written from left to right)

(1,j)\cdot(1,j+1)\cdot(1,j)=(j,j+1)
shows that the ordinary transpositions
(j,j+1)
can be obtained as products as the one from the new set.
The transpositions
(1,j)
are also involutions and, since
(1,j)\cdot(1,k)=(1,j,k)
, it follows

\big((1,j)\cdot(1,k)\big)^{3}=\id
(the identity permutation).
Therefore, with this new set of transpositions as generators,
the associated matrix of the orders coincides with the
Coxeter matrix of
\mathcal{M}(n)
.
Thus, in this setting,
S_{n+1}
is a Coxeter group whose
Coxeter diagram is an

n
-polygon together with all its edges and diagonals
un-labeled, exactly as that of
M(n)
.
Since the sets of generators of
\mathcal{M}(n)
and
S_{n+1}
have the same cardinality, their associated Coxeter systems are
equivalent.

In conclusion, these imply that
\mathcal{M}(n)\cong S_{n+1}

(see
[
10
, Section 2.1]
for the general argument).
We remark that the requirement for the groups in question to be reflection groups can be bypassed, since, according
to
[
10
, Theorem 6.4]
, any Coxeter group is
a finite group if and only if it is a reflection group.
The overall result still requires identifying how the isomorphism works, as we have seen that the choice of generator systems is essential.

For the sake of illustration,
let
F:\{K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\}\to\mathcal{M}(n)
be the natural embedding that maps
K_{j}

to
F(K_{j}):=K_{j}
for all
j
.
Then, let
\psi:\{K_{1},\dots,K_{n}\}\to S_{n+1}

be defined by
\psi(K_{j}):=(1,j+1)
.
Note that

\displaystyle\left(\psi(K_{j})\psi(K_{k})\right)^{m_{jk}}=\left((1,j+1)\cdot(1,k+1)\right)^{m_{jk}}=(1,j+1,k+1)^{m_{jk}}=\id

(38)

for all
j,k
with
1\leq j,k\leq n
.
Then, according to the universality property
of the groups obtained by presentations,
there is a unique extension of

d
