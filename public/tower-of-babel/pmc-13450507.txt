Integrating rain classroom with case-based learning in teaching dental hard tissue non-carious diseases: a comparative study
Authors: Yu Bo, Zou Huiru, Li Mohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental hard tissue non-carious diseases pose a growing clinical challenge, yet traditional lecture-based dental education often fails to effectively teach their complex diagnosis and management. Considering the limitations of conventional methods in engaging students and promoting practical application, this study explored the potential of integrating Rain Classroom with Case-based Learning (CBL) to enhance teaching effectiveness. The objective was to evaluate whether this combined approach could improve students’ comprehension, retention, and application of knowledge, thereby boosting their clinical competence in this field.MethodsA comparative study was conducted at the School of Medicine, Nankai University, involving two cohorts of dental students. One cohort received traditional lecture-based instruction, while the other was exposed to an integrated methodology that combined Rain Classroom and CBL. For assessments, a multi-dimensional approach was adopted. Quantitative tests were used to measure students’ knowledge acquisition, and surveys were conducted to collect qualitative feedback on student satisfaction and their perceived learning efficacy.ResultsStudents in the integrated group demonstrated significantly higher performance on subjective questions assessing clinical reasoning, but lower performance on objective questions measuring factual recall, with no significant difference in total scores. Additionally, they also displayed a more pronounced capacity to apply theoretical knowledge effectively in practical clinical scenarios. Furthermore, these students expressed greater satisfaction with the interactive features of Rain Classroom and the practical relevance of case studies. They believed that these aspects of the course better equipped them for real-world clinical practice.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the integration of Rain Classroom and CBL offers a promising educational strategy for enhancing the teaching and learning of dental hard tissue non-carious diseases. Background

Dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, including enamel hypoplasia, dentinogenesis imperfecta, dental fluorosis, abfraction, erosion, and attrition, are vital in Endodontics and Operative Dentistry. Despite being frequently underestimated, they significantly impact oral health, highlighting the need for early diagnosis, effective treatment, and comprehensive patient management strategies [1, 2]. Mastering these diseases is essential for improving patient outcomes and advancing oral health.

However, their intricate nature poses challenges in diagnosis and instruction. Traditional lecture-based teaching often fails to inspire creativity, nurture critical thinking, or fully engage students, making it hard for them to grasp the nuances of these disorders. To address this, this study aims to integrate Rain Classroom with the Case-based Learning (CBL) model to create a more dynamic and effective learning environment.

Rain Classroom, co-developed by Tsinghua University and XuetangX, is a widely used e-learning platforms that seamlessly integrates Microsoft PowerPoint with WeChat [3]. Teachers can share course QR codes in WeChat groups for students to join sessions. By embedding the software into PowerPoint, teachers can launch real-time quizzes, monitor responses, and gather feedback instantly, enabling them to adapt teaching strategies promptly. Its core mission is to foster seamless teacher-student interaction through smart devices, enhancing learning experiences across pre-class, in-class, and post-class activities, and promoting educational reform [3, 4].

Case-based Learning (CBL), also known as case-oriented learning, is a modern educational methodology that focuses on examining and discussing clinical cases. It involves analyzing real-life clinical scenarios where students interact with “clinical patients” in a conducive learning setting [5, 6]. This approach integrates foundational theoretical knowledge with practical clinical experiences, enhancing realism. However, conventional face-to-face CBL is often constrained by time limitations, hindering in-depth case analysis [7].

Our research hypothesis is that integrating Rain Classroom with CBL can create a dynamic and interactive learning experience. The specific objective of this study is to verify whether this integrated approach can significantly enhance students’ understanding of dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, effectively foster their critical thinking skills, and substantially increase their engagement in the learning process. The findings of this study are anticipated to provide valuable insights and guide future teaching practices in dental education, ultimately contributing to the improvement of oral health care.

Methods

Experimental design and ethical approval

In 2022, a cohort of fourth-year dental students from Nankai University majoring in stomatology embarked on a course focused on dental hard tissue non-carious diseases. They were instructed using a combined teaching model that integrated Rain Classroom with CBL. To evaluate the effectiveness of this innovative approach, the theoretical exam scores of these students were compared to those of a cohort from 2019, who were taught using traditional teaching methods. Notably, the instructors for both the 2019 and 2022 student groups remained consistent, ensuring a fair comparison. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from Tianjin Stomatological Hospital, the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nankai University (Approval ID: PH2022-B-011).

Conversely, the exclusion criteria for participation in the combined teaching approach were: Students who were not in their fourth year of dental studies at Nankai University, and any student who declined to participate or was unable to comply with the study requirements due to personal or academic commitments.

Teachers and students interaction via the rain classroom

Once students completed their learning tasks on the Rain Classroom platform, teachers were able to instantly access comprehensive data analysis reports on their mobile phones, as shown in Fig. 1. Clinical cases were presented through a combination of pictures, short videos, and text descriptions, which were then discussed by the participants. During the implementation of this teaching segment, it was amidst the normalized phase of COVID-19 prevention and control, necessitating occasional switches between online and offline teaching modes. Students could engage in real-time interactions, answer questions, participate in barrage-style (rapid-fire) exchanges, and showcase their work within the “Rain Classroom”. This dynamic setup ensured that teacher-student interaction remained continuous, thereby enhancing the overall interactivity and engagement of the learning experience. Furthermore, Rain Classroom recorded the entire teaching process, encompassing pre-class preparation, in-class activities, and post-class follow-up. It generated digital analysis reports in real time, providing a clear picture of the teaching and learning effectiveness. The platform also utilized a task reminder function integrated with WeChat, facilitating the monitoring of post-class learning activities and ensuring that students remained on track with their assignments and coursework.

Application of the CBL teaching method

In our teaching endeavors, we employed the CBL methodology to facilitate a more intuitive and appropriate understanding among students regarding the clinical manifestations, diagnosis, differential diagnosis, treatment plans and prognosis of dental hard tissue non-carious diseases. This was achieved by presenting a selection of typical clinical cases during teaching sessions. Compared to the monotonous recitation of textbook entries, the incorporation of real-world cases significantly enhanced students’ receptiveness to new knowledge. For example, during a lesson on dentin hypersensitivity, we initially guided students to revisit their prior learning through the examination of four typical clinical cases. This approach effectively bridged the gap between previously acquired knowledge and the new material, leading to a shared understanding: “dentin hypersensitivity can manifest as a common symptom across various dental conditions”. Subsequently, we introduced a fifth clinical case involving a patient who, despite the absence of obvious dental defects, presented with complaints of dentin sensitivity. This scenario prompted students to ponder: “Can dentin hypersensitivity be considered an independent disease entity?” Through this exploration, students were encouraged to cultivate a habit of critical thinking, fostering a mindset that valued independent inquiry and discouraged blind adherence to established norms. In essence, the CBL method empowered students to “think beyond the obvious” and engaged in more profound and reflective learning.

Student evaluation and survey

This study compared two teaching methods: the experimental group used Rain Classroom with a CBL approach, while the control group followed traditional instruction. To evaluate effectiveness, we compared the control group’s scores from the first semester of 2019–2020 with the experimental group’s final exam scores in the first semester of 2022–2023, covering various question types and total scores.

A comprehensive questionnaire survey was conducted to understand students’ views on the experimental group’s teaching approach in dental hard tissue non-carious diseases (see Supplementary File). It explored students’ satisfaction, impact on memory and understanding, learning interest, classroom engagement, self-directed learning, problem-solving, clinical thinking, and teacher-student relationships. To ensure survey validity and reliability, we first reviewed literature to identify key learning experience dimensions. An expert panel then reviewed the questions. A pilot test with a small student group was carried out, and questions were revised based on their feedback to improve face validity. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to assess internal consistency, with values above 0.7 deemed acceptable. For test-retest reliability, a small student sample took the survey twice with a two-week interval; a high correlation coefficient (0.95) indicated reliability. Scenario-based questions were designed to measure problem-solving and clinical thinking skills. Responses were evaluated using a predefined rubric considering step logic, knowledge use, and complication handling. Clinical thinking scenarios focused more on clinical data analysis and interpretation. To ensure anonymity and honesty, students completed the survey at the end of the term for an unbiased evaluation of the blended teaching method in dental education. Statistical analysis

All data were collected and analyzed with SPSS statistical software (version 22.0, IBM Corp). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to evaluate the normality of the sample distribution. The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. The results of Final exam analysis expressed as scoring rate, and the data of the 2 groups were analyzed by Mann-Whitney U test. P < 0.05 indicated that the difference was statistically significant.

Results

Participant analysis

The study encompassed two distinct groups of students. The control group, consisting of 21 students, received instruction through traditional teaching method. In contrast, the experimental group, made up of 29 students, was taught with an innovative approach that combined Rain Classroom with CBL. In the control group, there were 10 male and 11 female students, with ages ranging from 18 to 22 years old. Similarly, the experimental group comprised 13 male and 16 female students, also within the age range of 18 to 22 years old. Prior to the commencement of the course on dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, a statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in age or gender distribution between the two groups. Additionally, all participating students had successfully completed the required credits before entering the study, ensuring a comparably academic baseline.

Students’ final test scores analysis

In the final examination of the first semester during the 2019–2020 academic year, students in the control group achieved a median scoring rate of 100.00 in the objective section of the dental hard tissue non-carious diseases test. Conversely, during the final examination of the first semester of the 2022–2023 academic year, students in the experimental group achieved a median scoring rate of 75.00 for the identical objective section (Fig. 3). It was worth highlighting that a statistically significant difference was observed between these two groups, as evidenced by a P-value of less than 0.05(P = 0.001) (Table 1).

Student evaluation survey results

All 29 students in the experimental group returned their completed questionnaires, resulting in a 100% response rate. The integration of Rain Classroom with the CBL teaching method yielded substantial positive impacts on students’ learning experiences. This innovative teaching approach not only significantly enhanced students’ interest in learning and their active participation in classroom activities but also boosted a greater sense of enthusiasm and initiative for academic pursuits. It had the potential to contribute to a more harmonious and interactive relationship between teachers and students, which could create an environment that might support open communication and mutual respect to a certain extent. In addition to these social and motivational benefits, the combined use of Rain Classroom and CBL also enhanced students’ problem-solving abilities, and improved their clinical thinking skills. Notably, the satisfaction rate among students regarding the Rain Classroom-integrated CBL teaching mode reached an impressive 100% (Table 4; Fig. 4).

Discussion

Blended learning has gained widespread recognition as a valuable supplement to traditional methods in clinical education, effectively tackling issues such as superficial learning, limited classroom engagement, and inadequate understanding [8]. Rain Classroom, in particular, fosters enhanced teacher-student interaction by enabling real-time feedback and instant quizzes. These features enable instructors to assess learning progress promptly, adjust teaching plans accordingly, and enhance student participation. Zhu et al. assessed a progressive blended case-based teaching model using Rain Classroom for medical students, finding that it significantly improved post-test scores and self-perceived competencies in most core domains compared to traditional lectures [9]. This approach effectively stimulated learning initiative and enhanced clinical competency development among medical students.

Case-based learning (CBL) rooted in problem-based learning (PBL), stimulates independent learning and practical application, thus bridging the gap between theory and practice [10, 11]. When applied to complex topics like dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, the Case-based Rain Classroom teaching method uses real clinical cases to simplify understanding. According to the questionnaire results, all students expressed an increased interest in learning, with not a single negative response, indicating widespread acceptance. To further boost student engagement, it would be beneficial to diversify the cases presented and conduct a thorough evaluation of the pedagogical effectiveness. Sellitto et al. found that the intervention of CBL significantly enhanced general clinical knowledge and overall competence scores among students from multiple Italian universities, fostering positive perceptions about content relevance and inter-university collaboration, though case-specific knowledge showed no change and some organizational and technical concerns persisted [12]. Li et al. combined case-based learning with Rain Classroom and found that it significantly improved undergraduate interns’ Q&A participation and theoretical test scores in the complete denture prosthesis clinical course compared to traditional lecture-based teaching. Additionally, a high proportion (94.54%) of interns in the test group preferred the new teaching mode, indicating its effectiveness in enhancing both theoretical understanding and clinical practice enthusiasm [13].

While the overall examination performance, as measured by total scores, did not differ significantly between the CBL-integrated and traditional teaching methods (P = 0.517), a closer examination of question-type performance reveals distinct patterns. The experimental group demonstrated significantly weaker performance on objective questions (P = 0.001), which primarily assessed factual recall and basic knowledge consolidation. Conversely, they outperformed the control group on subjective questions (P = 0.012), which required higher-order cognitive skills such as synthesis, analysis, and clinical reasoning. These findings suggest that the integrated approach did not simply improve learning outcomes but rather shifted the emphasis of student learning toward deeper conceptual understanding and critical thinking, potentially at the expense of rote memorization of foundational facts. This trade-off underscores the importance of aligning assessment methods with intended learning outcomes when evaluating pedagogical innovations. The results of this study show that there is no significant difference in the total scores on the final exam between the experimental group and the control group. This indicates that the two teaching models have similar effects in terms of improving overall academic performance, and both are able to achieve the basic teaching objectives of the course.

From the perspective of score differences across different question types, the experimental group scored significantly higher than the control group on subjective questions. This result indicates that the teaching mode combining Rain Classroom with CBL is more conducive to stimulating students’ active thinking and promoting the development of their abilities in knowledge understanding, integration, logical reasoning, and written expression. It demonstrates distinct advantages in enhancing students’ higher-order cognitive abilities, such as comprehension, application, analysis, and interpretation. The Rain Classroom combined with CBL teaching model places greater emphasis on students’ independent inquiry, meaning construction, and comprehensive application. As a result, students perform better in open-ended and comprehensive subjective questions, where they can more clearly demonstrate their thinking processes and levels of knowledge understanding.

Meanwhile, the study found that the experimental group scored lower on objective questions than the control group. Objective questions primarily assess the memorization of basic knowledge, concept differentiation, detail judgment, and answer rigor. This result reflects certain limitations of online teaching in the precise implementation of foundational knowledge, in-class immediate feedback, supervision of the learning process, and repetitive reinforcement. Compared to offline classrooms, which allow for face-to-face guidance, immediate error correction, in-class exercises, and repeated reinforcement, the online environment is more prone to issues such as student distraction, vague mastery of knowledge points, untimely correction of common errors, and insufficient answer standardization, all of which contribute to a lower accuracy rate on objective questions. This indicates that while the online teaching method combining Rain Classroom with CBL promotes deep understanding, it is relatively insufficient in the fine-grained consolidation of basic knowledge points and the training of exam-taking skills. In contrast, traditional offline teaching still holds irreplaceable advantages in solidifying foundational knowledge and standardizing study habits [14].

Overall, there is no significant difference in the total scores between the experimental and control groups, indicating that the variation in teaching effectiveness is not reflected in overall academic performance, but rather in the structure of students’ knowledge acquisition and the pathways of their ability development. In subsequent teaching practices, it is not advisable to simply determine one model as superior; instead, teaching approaches should be integrated and optimized by considering teaching objectives, content, and student characteristics. In online teaching, efforts should be made to strengthen the mastery of foundational knowledge, specialized training for objective questions, real-time assessment, and feedback on incorrect answers, thereby reinforcing the implementation of details and rigorous training. In traditional offline teaching, elements of inquiry-based and interactive teaching can be appropriately incorporated to enhance students’ comprehension and comprehensive application skills, thereby achieving a synergistic improvement in both foundational consolidation and cognitive development.

While the survey showed 100% student satisfaction and uniformly positive responses with no disagreements, response biases like social desirability bias and ceiling effects are possible. Social desirability bias may make students give positive feedback to please instructors or conform to educational expectations, fearing negative consequences like poor grades or strained teacher-student relations. This can overestimate satisfaction. The ceiling effect implies the questionnaire may lack sensitivity to detect subtle perception differences. Items might be too easy to agree with, or the response scale too limited, masking true variability and presenting an overly optimistic view of teaching effectiveness. To reduce these biases in future studies, ensure response anonymity and confidentiality via non-identifying online platforms or independent third-party survey administration. Refine the questionnaire with more neutral and challenging items for better discrimination. Triangulate survey data with student performance, classroom observations, or focus groups for a more comprehensive assessment. Also, though neutral responses are small, they should not be ignored as they may reflect mixed feelings or uncertainty about the teaching method, offering insights for improvement.

A notable limitation of this study is the small sample size (control group: n = 21; experimental group: n = 29), which may restrict the generalizability of our findings. While post-hoc power analysis confirmed adequate statistical power for observed effect sizes, future research with larger, more diverse cohorts is essential to validate these results and explore their applicability across different educational contexts. To a certain extent, teachers could consider making efforts to enhance interaction and practical elements within teaching activities. This may help encourage more active participation and potentially offer tangible benefits to students.

The other limitation of this study is the use of unequal group sizes (experimental group: n = 29; control group: n = 21) and historical controls from different academic years (2019 cohort vs. 2022 cohort). This design introduces potential confounding due to unmeasured differences in cohort characteristics, exam conditions, or external events (e.g., the COVID-19 pandemic). Future studies should employ prospective randomization or crossover designs to strengthen causal inference.

This study contributes to the discourse to dental education, showing that the Rain Classroom integrated CBL method enhances understanding and practical application. Qualitative feedback reveals high satisfaction with interactivity and case relevance. The findings suggest this method offers a promising educational strategy for dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, fostering deeper engagement and improving competence. However, further research with larger samples, longer follow-up, and comparisons with other strategies is needed to validate and explore limitations.

Conclusion

The integration of the Rain Classroom with CBL teaching method in dental education, specifically for the study of dental hard tissue non-carious diseases, demonstrates significant potential. This combined approach not only fosters enhanced teacher-student interaction and boosts student interest, but also contributes to the development of students’ comprehensive abilities and strengthens interpersonal relationships. However, the effectiveness of this approach in larger class sizes remains to be determined.
