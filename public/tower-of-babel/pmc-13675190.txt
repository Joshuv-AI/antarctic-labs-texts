Super-enhancer landscape regulated by CDK12 drives retinoblastoma progression
Authors: Caiyao Guo, Wei Dai, Jingxuan Li, Xuan Zhou, Jianqiong Yang, Fenfen Xie, Wan Liang, Jingfeng Zhou, Wen Hou, Jinxia Lan, Hai Liu, Shenglan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRetinoblastoma (RB) lacks effective targeted therapies. Super-enhancers (SEs) are crucial for tumorigenesis, yet their role in RB was unclear.MethodsWe profiled active SEs in RB cells using chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) and RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). qRT-PCR, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were employed to identify the critical regulator of SE activity. The anti-tumour effect of targeted blockade of SE regulator, achieved via pharmacological degradation and specific lentiviruses, was investigated in vitro and in preclinical models of subcutaneous, intraocular, and central nervous system (CNS)-metastatic RB.ResultsCyclin-dependent kinase 12 (CDK12) was identified as a key SE regulator. SE-driven oncogenic transcription was suppressed by CDK12 inhibition, leading to eliminated tumour outgrowth and metastasis. In addition, SE-associated oncogenes BIRC5 and PTPRC were identified as essential for RB cell survival and stemness. Furthermore, PTPRC overexpression rescued the anti-tumour effect of BSJ-4-116.ConclusionsThis study defines CDK12 as a master regulator of oncogenic SEs in RB. Targeting the CDK12-SE axis, via CDK12 or downstream effectors like BIRC5/PTPRC, presents a novel therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Retinoblastoma (RB), the most prevalent and devastating intraocular malignancy in children, has an annual incidence of 1 in 15,000 to 1 in 20,000 live births worldwide [1]. RB impairs vision and exhibits metastatic potential, spreading either via direct extension along the optic nerve to the central nervous system (CNS) or hematogenously to bone and lung [2]. Current management employs a multimodal approach, including enucleation, chemotherapy, and laser photocoagulation, to maximise globe salvage rates. However, these treatments are associated with significant long-term retinal and systemic adverse effects, and a subset of tumours develops chemoresistance. Therefore, developing novel targeted therapies with enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity is imperative.

Growing evidence indicates that transcriptional alterations contribute to RB development, driven by dynamic epigenetic landscape [3]. Super-enhancers (SEs) are vital regulatory elements composed of dense clusters of enhancer regions, which are characterised by high occupancy of transcription factors (TFs), cofactors, and mediator complexes, along with elevated levels of epigenetic histone modifications, notably histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) and histone H3 monomethylated at lysine 4 (H3K4me1). These cis-regulatory elements play a pivotal role in activating gene transcription that essential for cellular identity and functions, such as proliferation [4], survival [5] and stemness [6]. Aberrant SEs have been implicated in the development and progression of various malignancies, such as colorectal cancer [7], breast [8], and cervical cancer [9]. However, the landscape and potential role of SEs in RB progression remain largely unexplored. Comprehensive identifying of the genome-wide SE landscape and elucidation of their regulatory mechanisms in RB are urgently needed.

Cyclin-dependent kinase 12 (CDK12), a transcription-associated cyclin-dependent kinase, is alternatively referred to as CRKRS, CRKR, or CRK7 [10]. CDK12 plays essential roles in embryonic development, tissue homoeostasis, and regeneration [11]. However, dysregulation of CDK12 exhibits distinct function in different types of cancers [12]. Extensive studies have revealed the multifaceted roles of CDK12 in promoting oncogenesis and cancer progression, including sustaining proliferation, activating invasion and metastasis, conferring cancer stem-like traits, inducing metabolic reprogramming, stimulating angiogenesis and vasculogenic mimicry, and enabling immune evasion [12]. Paradoxically, CDK12 also possesses tumour-suppressive functions. Loss of CDK12 activity can lead to homologous recombination deficiency, genomic tandem duplications, and ferroptosis, which may further drive tumour progression [13, 14]. Although different or even paradoxical regulatory mechanisms and functions of CDK12 in cancers have been reported based on cell experiments or xenograft tumours in mice, the role of CDK12 in RB development and its underlying mechanism remain elusive.

In this study, we characterised SE landscape via chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-Seq) in RB and found that SEs were closely associated with the tumour progression. Through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) and functional experiments, CDK12 was found to overexpressed in RB cells and clinical specimens, acting as a crucial regulator of SE activity and SE-driven transcription. CDK12 suppression alleviated subcutaneous, orthotopic and CNS metastatic RB in multiple preclinical models. Integration of the SE landscape and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq), BIRC5 and PTPRC were identified as critical SE-associated oncogenes, which were exceptionally vulnerable to SE interruption by CDK12 inhibition, which are essential for cell proliferation, survival, and stemness maintenance in RB. These findings underscore the pivotal role of CDK12-SEs axis in driving tumour progression in RB.

Materials and methods

BSJ-4-116 and CDK12-IN-3 were obtained from TargetMol (Shanghai, China), prepared as a 20 mM stock solution in DMSO, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C. Propidium iodide (PI) and Annexin V-FITC were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). The following primary antibodies were used for Western blotting: RNAP II CTD Ser2 (Bethyl Laboratories, #A300-654A), Total RNAP II (Bethyl Laboratories, #A300-653A), CDK12 (Proteintech, #26816-1-AP), CDK13 (BOSTER, #A05292-1), Poly ADP-Ribose Polymerase (PARP) (Cell Signaling Technology: CST, #9532), Caspase-3 (CST, #9662), Survivin (Proteintech, #10508-1-AP), Cleaved Caspase-3 (CST, #9661), Bcl-XL (Proteintech, #10783-1-AP), Bcl-2 (Abmart, #333569), Mcl-1 (Proteintech, #16225-1-AP), CD45 (Proteintech, #20103-1-AP) and β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich, #A1978).

Cell culture

The RB cell lines Y79 and WERI-Rb1 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA). The SO-Rb50 cell line was established at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Sun Yat-Sen University (Guangzhou, China), as previously described [15]. Y79, WERI-Rb1, and SO-Rb50 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, NY) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The normal human adult retinal pigment epithelium cell line (ARPE-19) and the highly transfectable human embryonic kidney cell line (293T) were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ and harvested during the logarithmic growth phase. In all cultures, the DMSO concentration was maintained below 0.1% (v/v). All cell lines used in this study have been recently authenticated by STR and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Lentivirus packaging and generation of stable cell lines

The lentiviral shRNA plasmids targeting human CDK12, BIRC5 and PTPRC were constructed using the pLKO.1 backbone. For lentivirus production, 293T cells were co-transfected with the indicated lentiviral plasmids, the packaging vector pCMV-dR8.2 and the enveloping construct pCMV-VSVG using polyethyleneimine (Polysciences, USA). After 48 or 72 h transfection, lentiviral supernatants were collected and purified with a 0.45 μm sterile membrane filter. For stable cell line generation, RB cells were infected with lentivirus supernatants with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin (MCE, USA) for 7 days. Corresponding shRNA sequences for enhancer targeting are listed in Table S1.

CRISPRa and CRISPRi assay

Guide RNAs (gRNAs) were cloned into the lenti-sgRNApuro or lenti-sgRNA-MS2-Zeo (msgRNA) backbone plasmids. For gene activation experiments, CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) assay with synergistic activation mediator (SAM) complex was employed [6, 16]. In brief, RB cells were infected with lentiviral supernatants of dCas9-VPR (MiaoLing, P10265, Guangzhou, China) and specific individual msgRNA or non-enhancer targeting Scramble msgRNA. For gene repression experiments, CRISPR inhibition (CRISPRi) assay with dCas9-KRAB repressor complex was employed [6]. RB cells were infected with lentiviral supernatants of dCas9-KRAB-MeCP2 (Addgene, 110824, MA, USA) and specific individual sgRNA or non-enhancer targeting Scramble sgRNA. The transduced cells were then incubated with 1 μg/ml puromycin or 0.3 mg/ml G418 (MCE, USA) to selected stable clones. The detailed target sequences of gRNAs g are listed in Table S2.

RNA-Seq

RNA-Seq analysis was conducted on RB cells following treatment with BSJ-4-116 or vehicle control for 6 h. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN) following the previous study [17]. Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Transcript expression was quantified as FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) against Ensembl v75 annotations using Cufflinks [18]. Transcripts with mean FPKM > 1 were considered as actively transcribed and included for subsequent analysis. Integrated sequencing metrics of RNA-Seq data are listed in Table S3.

ChIP-Seq assay

ChIP assays were performed using the EZ-ChIP Kit (EMD Millipore) following our previously reported protocol [17]. Briefly, 1 × 10⁷ RB cells were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and quenched with 1.25 mM glycine. Cells were washed and lysed in buffer containing 1% SDS and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Lysates were sonicated to shear DNA into fragments of 100–1000 bp. Sheared chromatin was diluted in dilution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.1, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100) and precleared by incubation with 60 µL Protein G agarose for 1 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, 5 µg of anti-H3K27ac (Abcam, #ab4729), anti-CDK12 (Proteintech, #26816-1-AP), anti-RNAP II (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-47701), or IgG (CST, #2729S) antibody was added to the diluted lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Protein-DNA complexes were reverse-crosslinked in 1% SDS and 0.1 M NaHCO3 at 65 °C for 7 h. Both immunoprecipitated and input DNA samples were treated with RNase A and Proteinase K, followed by purification using a DNA clean and concentrator kit. DNA immunoprecipitated with anti-H3K27ac was used for library preparation with NEBNext ChIP-Seq Library Prep Reagent (NEB #E7370S). Integrated sequencing metrics of ChIP-Seq data are listed in Table S4. DNA enriched by anti-CDK12, anti-RNAP II, or IgG antibodies was analysed by qPCR using primers listed in Table S5.

ChIP-Seq analysis and SEs identification

ChIP-Seq reads were first subjected to remove adaptor sequences with Trim Galore and aligning them to the GRCh38/hg38 reference genome using Bowtie 2 [19]. PCR duplicates were eliminated via Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Significant peaks were called against genomic regions with MACS2 (v2.1.2) using parameters -m 5 50 and an FDR value threshold of 5% [20]. After filtering out peaks overlapping the repeat-sequence blacklist database [21] (https://github.com/Boyle-Lab/Blacklist/tree/master), the remaining peaks underwent ROSE (https://bitbucket.org/youngcomputation/rose) analysis to identify SEs per sample [22].

In the ROSE algorithm, the key parameters were set as follows: a stitching distance of 12.5 kb (i.e. constituent enhancers within 12.5 kb were stitched into a single region), and peaks within ±2.5 kb of annotated transcription start sites were excluded from stitching to avoid inclusion of promoter regions [23]. The ranked enhancers were plotted as rank (x‑axis) versus H3K27ac signal (y‑axis). SEs were distinguished from TEs by identifying the inflection point where the slope of the cumulative signal versus rank equals 1, which served as the rank cutoff for SE assignment [22]. SE intensities per sample were quantified using feature counts and normalised to reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM) [24]. Both SEs and TEs were assigned to the nearest annotated genes via ROSE. Bigwig files were produced with deepTools2 [25], and data visualisation was conducted using Integrative Genomics Viewer [26].

Bioinformatics analysis

For published microarray datasets, SEs identified in human retina pigmented epithelial RPE-1 cells were downloaded from SE analysis 2.0 database (http://licpathway.net/SEanalysis/search) [27]. GSE11116 [28] and GSE229598 [29] were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). For the scRNA-Seq analysis, datasets were analysed using Seurat v4.4.0. The five healthy human retinal datasets (E-MTAB-7316) [30] were retrieved from OmicsDI (https://www.omicsdi.org/dataset/biostudies-arrayexpress/E-MTAB-7316), and the seven RB dataset (GSE168434) [31] were downloaded from the GEO dataset (GEO: GSE168434). CreateSeuratObject was used to construct single-cell Seurat objects for each dataset, which were then merged using the merge function. Quality control metrics were calculated with PercentageFeatureSet, including the proportions of mitochondrial, erythrocyte, and ribosomal genes per cell. High-quality cells were retained based on the following criteria: 200 < nCount_RNA < 5000, mitochondrial gene proportion <20%, erythrocyte gene proportion <3%, and ribosomal gene proportion <40%. The expression matrix was normalised using NormalizeData, and the top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified with FindVariableFeatures. HVGs were scaled via ScaleData, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) on the first 50 principal components using RunPCA. Batch effects between datasets were corrected using Harmony. Based on the corrected first 50 principal components, the nearest neighbour graph between cells was constructed using the FindNeighbors function, followed by unsupervised cell clustering with FindClusters at a resolution of 1. Finally, dimensionality reduction for visualisation was performed using RunUMAP. Differential gene expression between retinoblastoma-like cells and normal intraocular cells was then assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Preclinical in vivo studies

For all animal experiments, male 4–6-week-old immunodeficient NOD-SCID mice (GemPharmatech, Jiangsu, China) were used and randomly assigned into experimental groups using a random number table. The investigators were not blinded to group allocation during the experiment or outcome assessment. The procedures received approval from the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Gannan Medical University (Approval number: 2022181). Subcutaneous Y79 xenografts were established by injecting 2 × 10⁷ cells into the flanks of mice. Tumour volume was measured every other day using calipers and calculated as 0.4 × a × b² (a = long axis, b = short axis). Once tumours reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and treated orally with either BSJ-4-116 (20 mg/kg, 5 days on, 2 days off weekly) or vehicle [10/90 dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)/30% hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin in water]. Two weeks later, mice were sacrificed and the tumour were excised. The tissues were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining.

To establish the intraocular models, 2 × 105 Y79 cells tagged with luciferase (Y79-Luc) in 2 µL of PBS were inoculated into the vitreous bodies of the eye of NOD-SCID mice using a dull 33-gauge needle as previously described [32]. Intravitreal BSJ-4-116 treatment (4 μg per eye in 2 μL, every other day) was performed in tumour-bearing eyes after tumour implantation for 21 days. Tumour burden was weekly measured using a Xenogen IVIS-Spectrum system (PerkinElmer, USA) in tumour-bearing eyes. Three weeks later, mice were sacrificed and the eyeballs were excised, weighed and photographed.

To establish the CNS retinoblastoma models, 1 × 106 cells Y79-Luc cells in 5 µL PBS were inoculated into the fourth ventricle of NOD-SCID mice as previously described [33]. Tumour burden of brain was measured weekly using a Xenogen IVIS-Spectrum system. Four weeks later, mice were sacrificed and the brain were excised. The tissues were subjected to H&E and IHC staining.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variances. Comparisons between two groups were analysed by two-tailed Student’s t tests. Two-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons in tumour growth studies. For multi-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was applied. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

The SE landscape in RB

To delineate the role and function of SEs in RB, ChIP-Seq was performed using an antibody against H3K27ac [34]. Analysis identified 1278 SEs in the Y79 cell line and 658 in WERI-Rb1 cells (Fig. 1a). The sequencing data identified RB cell fate-defining TFs, CRX [35] and OTX2 [36] as SE-driven genes, as well as novel SE-associated genes of unknown function, including the non-coding RNAs MIR11400 and LINC00426 (Fig. 1a). Furthermore, the top SE-associated genes, ranked by signal intensity, included known oncogenic factors HIPK1 [37], SYK [38], BIRC5 [39] and PTPRC [40] (Fig. 1c). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of SE-associated genes revealed their significant enrichment in biological processes relevant to RB, such as growth cone, as well as critical pathways involved in tumour progression, including transcriptional regulation, anti-apoptosis, proliferation, migration, and stemness maintenance (Fig. 1d). These findings indicate that SEs are active in RB cell lines and may contribute to pathogenesis and progression, but RB specificity cannot be determined without normal retinal SE data.

CDK12 is overexpressed in RB

Key SE-associated factors, including the Mediator complex, BRD4, EP300, and transcriptional CDKs, cooperatively establish long-range SE–promoter interactions to drive robust transcription of SE-associated genes [9, 41]. Specifically, MED1 scaffolds the Mediator complex for RNAP II transcription [41]. BRD4 marks SE regions [7]. EP300 catalyzes H3K27ac at SEs [42]. CDK7 initiates transcription via RNAP II CTD Ser5 phosphorylation [43], while CDK12 and CDK13 promote elongation through Ser2 phosphorylation [44]. To determine whether these factors are dysregulated in human RB, we profiled their expression.

To systematically identify key SE complex components in RB, we analysed 12 scRNA-Seq datasets (5 normal retina, 7 RB). Cells were clustered and, by combining copy number variation (CNV) analysis with curated cell-type markers, we identified 11 cell types (Figs. 2a–c and S1A–E). Genes upregulated in RB cells were enriched in pathways related to transcriptional regulation, anti-apoptosis, proliferation, migration, and stemness (Fig. S1F), paralleling SE functions. Among the six profiled SE-associated factors, CDK12 was consistently the most highly expressed in RB samples at both mRNA and protein levels, as shown by scRNA-Seq (Fig. 2d–g) and qRT-PCR in cell lines (Fig. 2h). Western blotting further confirmed that CDK12, but not CDK13, was highly expressed in RB cells (Fig. S1G). Additionally, CDK12 mRNA levels were significantly elevated in RB tumour tissues compared with normal controls and were further increased in invasive RB (Fig. 2i, j), findings that were corroborated at the protein level by IHC, which showed markedly higher CDK12 expression in tumour tissues relative to adjacent normal retina (Fig. 2k, l). CDK12 is required to sustain SE activity and function in RB

Treatment of RB cells with the CDK12-selective PROTAC degrader BSJ-4-116 [45] selectively degraded CDK12 and caused RNAP II CTD Ser2 phosphorylation (Ser2P) inhibition (Fig. 3a). To avoid toxicity response, cells were treated with either vehicle control (DMSO) or low dose of BSJ-4-116 (125 nM) for 6 h for sequencing experiments, with the low-dose treatment partially inhibiting Ser2P to 50–70% of the control level (Fig. S2A–D). ChIP-Seq analyses showed that CDK12 inhibition significantly impaired SE activity with reduced H3K27ac enrichment at SE regions (Fig. 3b–d). Notably, oncogenic genes, such as BIRC5, OTX2 and PTPRC, exhibited a remarkable decreased enrichment of H3K27ac occupancy at SE locus upon BSJ-4-116 treatment (Fig. 3e). Moreover, results revealed that high dose (500 nM) of BSJ-4-116 abolished RNA Pol II CTD Ser2P causing global mRNA decline, whereas a low dose (125 nM) partially inhibited Ser2P and selectively downregulated a subset of transcripts (Figs. 3f and S2A–E). GO analysis showed that these low-dose–sensitive genes were enriched in processes critical for tumour progression (Fig. 3g), which is similar to the functional signature of SE-associated genes (Fig. 1d). Importantly, SE-associated transcripts were downregulated more severely than those associated with TEs upon low dose of BSJ-4-116 treatment (Fig. 3h). Further, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that the sensitive genes were predominantly SE-associated following low dose of BSJ-4-116 treatment (Fig. 3i), indicating that SE-associated transcripts were selectively vulnerable to transcriptional disruption. CDK12 inhibition suppresses the tumorigenic potential of RB

To investigate the therapeutic potential of CDK12 suppression, we treated RB cells with BSJ-4-116. CCK-8 assays showed potent inhibition of RB cell viability (IC₅₀ 30–125 nM) with markedly reduced cytotoxicity in normal ARPE-19 cells (IC₅₀ >1000 nM) (Fig. 4a). BSJ-4-116 also significantly suppressed clonogenic growth in soft agar (Fig. 4b). To confirm that these effects were specifically mediated by CDK12 inhibition, we performed CDK12 depletion experiments and observed consistent impairments in both cellular proliferation (Fig. S3A–D) and clonogenicity (Fig. S3E, F). To evaluate whether CDK12 suppression abrogates tumour growth in vivo, we established subcutaneous Y79 xenograft model. Compared with the vehicle-treated group, BSJ-4-116 administration significantly reduced both tumour volume (Fig. 4c) and tumour weight (Fig. 4d, e). IHC revealed decreased CDK12 and Ki-67 expression (Fig. S4A, B), and Western blotting confirmed reduced CDK12 and RNAP II CTD Ser2P (Figure S4C). No significant weight loss or tissue damage was observed in BSJ-4-116-treated mice (Fig. S4D, E). The anti-tumour effect of BSJ-4-116 was further corroborated in mice subcutaneously implanted with CDK12-silenced Y79 cells (Fig. S4F–K).

To further validate the efficacy of CDK12 inhibition in a more physiologically relevant context, we established an orthotopic RB model by injecting Y79-Luc cells into the vitreous bodies of NOD/SCID mice (Fig. 4f). CDK12 silencing significantly reduced intraocular tumour burden, as assessed by serial in vivo bioluminescence imaging (Fig. 4g, h) and ophthalmological monitoring (Fig. 4i). Enucleated eye weights were significantly lower in the CDK12-knockdown group (Fig. 4j, k). H&E staining showed that Scramble control tumours extensively filled the vitreous cavity and invaded the optic nerve, whereas shCDK12 tumours were largely confined to the posterior retina, indicating restricted growth and invasion (Fig. 4l, m). IHC confirmed that CDK12 silencing dramatically suppressed proliferation (Figs. 4n, o and S4L, M). Collectively, the results from multiple in vitro and in vivo models consistently demonstrate that CDK12 inhibition effectively restrain RB tumour outgrowth, thereby supporting a critical pro-tumorigenic role for SEs in RB.

To elucidate how CDK12 regulates Survivin, H3K27ac ChIP-Seq profiling identified an acquired SE at the BIRC5 (gene symbol Survivin) locus in RB cells compared with normal RPE1 cells (Fig. 6f). This SE gain correlated with increased BIRC5 mRNA expression in RB tissues (GEO dataset, Fig. 6g), and with the high sensitivity of BIRC5 transcription to low-dose BSJ-4-116 in RB cells (Fig. 6h), positioning BIRC5 as an SE-driven oncogene. CRISPRa-SAM targeting of BIRC5 SE with three independent gRNAs strongly increased BIRC5 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6i, j), confirming SE-dependent transcriptional control. Functionally, SAM-mediated Survivin overexpression enhanced proliferation (Fig. 6k), whereas Survivin knockdown induced apoptosis (Fig. 6l, m) and impaired proliferation (Fig. S5H) and colony formation (Fig. 6n). Ectopic Survivin expression partially rescued BSJ-4-116-induced apoptosis, shown by reduced cleaved PARP (Fig. S5I) and decreased trypan blue‑positive death (Fig. S5J). In addition, ChIP‑qPCR showed that BSJ-4-116 significantly reduced CDK12 and RNAP II enrichment at the BIRC5 SE, but not at the non-enhancer control region (NEG) (Fig. 6o), indicating that CDK12 and RNAP II were recruited at the SE loci. Collectively, these results support a model in which CDK12 sustains BIRC5 SE activity, facilitating RNAP II occupancy and driving high Survivin expression to promote RB cell survival.

To determine whether PTPRC is an SE-associated oncogene, we analysed H3K27ac ChIP-Seq signals in normal RPE1 and RB cells. This revealed an acquired SE at the PTPRC locus in RB cells (Fig. S7C). Consistent with SE-driven expression, both PTPRC mRNA and protein levels were elevated in RB cell lines (Fig. S7D, E) and patient samples (Fig. S7F, G). PTPRC encodes CD45, an early hematopoietic marker [48]. To rule out the possibility that CD45 originates from infiltrating host immune cells, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) co‑staining for CD45 and the RB tumour marker CRX [49] in cultured RB cells, away from host cells. The results confirmed that CD45 protein is intrinsically produced by human RB tumour cells (Fig. 8d). This finding was validated in vivo, where CD45 co‑localised with CRX in subcutaneous, orthotopic, and CNS metastatic xenografts (Fig. S7H). Additionally, confirming its dependence on active transcription, PTPRC expression was markedly reduced upon CDK12 inhibition by BSJ-4-116 or shCDK12, as shown by RNA‑Seq data (Fig. S7I), Western blotting (Fig. S7J, K), and IHC (Fig. S7L, M).

To demonstrate that PTPRC is directly controlled by its SE, we employed CRISPRa and CRISPRi with three independent gRNAs targeting the individual SE peaks within the locus, along with a non-SE Scramble gRNA (Fig. 8e). All three SE-targeting gRNAs consistently induced PTPRC expression in the CRISPRa system, while the same gRNAs in the CRISPRi system suppressed expression compared to Scramble (Figs. 8f and S7N), with no effect on another SE-driven gene BIRC5 (Fig. S7O). This confirms SE-dependent, on-target transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, ChIP-qPCR showed that BSJ-4-116 significantly reduced CDK12 and RNAP II enrichment at the three SE loci, but not at NEG (Fig. 8g), indicating that CDK12 directly engages the SE to drive PTPRC expression. To strengthen causality, we performed genetic rescue experiments. shCDK12-mediated reduction of CDK12 and RNAP II occupancy at the PTPRC SE was restored by re-expression of CDK12 (Fig. 8h). In an orthogonal approach, the chemically distinct inhibitor CDK12-IN-3 phenocopied the reduced occupancy, and CDK12 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. S7P). Collectively, these data establish PTPRC as an SE-driven oncogene directly regulated by CDK12 in RB.

The SE-associated oncogene PTPRC drives RB tumorigenesis

To systematically dissect CD45 function in RB, we silenced PTPRC (Fig. S8A) and observed markedly suppressed proliferation (Fig. S8B). In contrast, CRISPRa‑mediated PTPRC overexpression enhanced proliferation and rescued BSJ‑4‑116‑induced proliferation defects (Fig. S8C, D), whereas CRISPRi‑mediated knockdown inhibited proliferation (Fig. S8C). PTPRC knockdown also impaired tumorsphere formation and serial replating (Fig. S8E, F) and reduced the Aldefluor⁺ CSC population (Fig. S8G, H). Forced PTPRC overexpression enhanced CSC self-renewal and counteracted BSJ-4-116-mediated stemness inhibition (Figs. 8i, j and S8I).

To definitively assess the impact of PTPRC on RB tumorigenesis in vivo, we employed a CRISPRa system to drive PTPRC overexpression in orthotopic xenograft models. PTPRC overexpression robustly increased bioluminescence intensity and eye weight, while reversing BSJ-4-116-induced suppression of both parameters (Fig. 8k–n). Histologically, CD45 overexpression promoted vitreal infiltration and optic nerve invasion and antagonised BSJ-4-116-mediated tumour suppression (Figs. 8o and S8J), with corresponding increases in Ki-67 (Fig. S8K, L). Furthermore, CD45 levels positively correlated with CDK12 levels in primary RB specimens (Fig. S9A, B), highlighting CD45 as a critical oncogenic driver in RB.

To explore the downstream mechanisms through which CD45 drives RB progression, we turned to β‑catenin, a known effector of CD45 in colorectal and lung cancers that promotes proliferation and stemness [50, 51]. Analysis of GEO datasets revealed a significant positive correlation between CD45 and β‑catenin in RB patients (Fig. S9C), and CD45 knockdown markedly reduced β‑catenin protein levels in RB cells (Fig. S9D). Importantly, overexpression of β‑catenin in CD45‑silenced RB cells partially rescued the proliferation and stemness defects caused by CD45 loss (Fig. S9E–G). Together, these results indicate that CD45 enhances tumour cell proliferation and stemness at least in part by promoting β‑catenin expression.

Discussion

Elucidating the mechanisms of RB and identifying novel candidate therapeutic targets are essential for improving patient outcomes. This study establishes the presence of active SEs in RB and demonstrates their contribution to intraocular and CNS metastatic disease. We identified CDK12 as an essential component of the SE machinery. Targeting CDK12 suppressed the SE-driven oncogenes BIRC5 and PTPRC, which were found to be key drivers sustaining tumour cell survival, proliferation, and CSC properties (Fig. 8p). Together, our work establishes the CDK12-SE signaling axis as a key driver of RB progression.

Transcriptional addiction represents an exploitable vulnerability in cancer cells. Among the SE complex components, we demonstrated that CDK12 is highly expressed in human RB. RB cells displayed exceptional sensitivity to CDK12 inhibition, attributable to the disruption of SE-driven transcriptional programmes. These findings support a preclinical rationale for CDK12 as both a therapeutic target and a potential biomarker. However, further pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies are required before clinical translation.

Increased survival is one of the hallmarks of cancer. Survivin, encoded by the gene BIRC5, belongs to the inhibitor of apoptosis family, which is a bifunctional regulator of cell death and cell proliferation expressed during embryonic development but undetectable in healthy adult tissues and re-expressed in many cancers [52, 53]. Its destruction increased apoptosis and inhibited tumour growth, and studies have established BIRC5 as a prognostic marker in multiple cancers [54, 55]. Here, we identify BIRC5 as a prime SE-associated gene in RB. The profound sensitivity of BIRC5 transcription to CDK12 inhibition underscores its dependency on the continuous, high-level transcription orchestrated by SEs.

CSCs, are considered a major driver of therapy resistance, disease relapse, and mortality in multiple malignancies. In the present study, we identified PTPRC (encoding CD45) as a novel SE-associated oncogene critical for maintaining cancer stemness in RB. Further, β‑catenin functions as a critical downstream effector of CD45, consistent with a previous report showing that CD45 promotes colorectal cancer progression by regulating β‑catenin [50]. However, the mechanism by which CD45 regulates β‑catenin expression remains unclear. One possibility is that CD45, as a protein tyrosine phosphatase, reduces phosphorylated (degradable) β‑catenin, thereby stabilising the protein and leading to its accumulation [50]. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which CD45 regulates PTPRC.

There are limitations in the present study. First, the mechanisms driving CDK12 upregulation in RB remain unknown and require exploration of genomic alterations, protein stability, or post-transcriptional modifications. Second, the present study used ARPE-19 cells as the non-malignant control. Future validation in lineage-matched normal cells is necessary to confirm the tumour selectivity of CDK12 targeting.

In conclusion, our work defines the SE landscape in RB and identifies a mechanistic pathway whereby CDK12 sustains oncogenic transcription through SE-mediated regulation of BIRC5 and PTPRC. Disrupting this SE-driven transcriptional addiction concurrently impairs tumour cell survival and CSC maintenance. These preclinical observations suggest that selectively targeting the CDK12-SE axis could represent a novel concept for simultaneously inducing apoptosis and eradicating CSCs in RB.
