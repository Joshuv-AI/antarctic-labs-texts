Effective treatment coverage for sick young infants with possible serious bacterial infection when referral not feasible in low-and middle-income countries: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Wassie Negash Mekonnen, Gizachew Tadele Tiruneh, Adugnaw Berhane, Wubegzier Mekonnen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBacterial infections are major causes of neonatal deaths globally and about 6.9 million episodes occur among young infants in low-and middle-income countries every year. A comprehensive cascade of care from identifying the target population to outcome-adjusted coverage is essential to identify service delivery bottlenecks and take corrective action; however, the majority of studies included single treatment coverage rates. In this study, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to estimate pooled coverage levels across stages of the care cascade, using a standardized framework for treatment coverageMethodsWe searched studies using a population, concept, and context framework combined with the Boolean terms ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ on PubMed/Medline, Cochrane, EMBASE, and Web of Science databases and grey literature. Two independent reviewers appraised eligible studies for their quality using the JBI appraisal checklist. We used a random effect model with a 95% CI in STATA 14 to estimate the treatment coverage along care cascades. We evaluated the publication bias through funnel plots and Egger's test, both of which indicated the absence of publication bias. However, we observed significant heterogeneity; consequently, subgroup analyses were conducted, taking into account the year of publication, setting, design, and location of treatmentResultthe overall pooled estimates of contact coverage, intervention coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence-adjusted coverage, and outcome-adjusted coverage from 16 studies were 71% (95% CI: 60%–82%; I² = 99.85%), 67% (95% CI: 55%–78%; I² = 99.75%), 61% (95% CI: 46%–76%; I² = 99.84%), 60% (95% CI: 43%–76%; I² = 99.4%), and 56% (95% CI: 43%–68%; I² = 99.0%), respectively.In the subgroup analysis based on study settings, studies conducted in India reported lower coverage levels compared to those in Ethiopia. Specifically, the contact coverage was 65% (95% CI: 53%–77%; I² = 97.4%), intervention coverage was 64% (95% CI: 49%–79%; I² = 98.3%), quality-adjusted coverage was 60% (95% CI: 41%–79%; I² = 98.9%), and outcome-adjusted coverage was 58% (95% CI: 38%–78%; I² = 99.02%)ConclusionThe pooled estimates indicate suboptimal levels of contact coverage, intervention coverage, and quality-adjusted coverage, highlighting challenges in delivering essential services. The lower levels of user adherence-adjusted coverage and outcome-adjusted coverage suggest potential issues with treatment effectiveness and patient outcomes. Therefore, targeted interventions and appropriate resource allocation to improve healthcare access, quality, and outcomes are crucial in low-resource settings.The review protocol has been registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), (ID: CRD42024525174).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25934-9.

Background

Possible serious bacterial infection (PSBI) commonly referred to as severe infection in neonates are leading cause of mortality in young infants. In 2018, of 5.3 million estimated child deaths 2.5 million occurred among neonates [1]. Serious bacterial infections are a leading cause of neonatal mortality, contributing to 2.9 million neonatal deaths annually worldwide and accounting for 37% of neonatal deaths in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) [2]. Most of these deaths occur in resource-limited settings and are primarily due to preventable causes such as sepsis (36.7%) and pneumonia (24.8%) [3, 4]. Infections were identified in the causal chain of 86.9% of deaths in these regions [3, 5, 6].

Lack of access to effective treatment for sick children is one of the main causes of child mortality [7]. Crude coverage, estimated using household survey data, is a less precise indicator of health system performance compared to effective coverage. Crude treatment coverage alone has limited impact on reducing child mortality, whereas effective treatment coverage serves as a more valid and meaningful indicator of how well the health system is functioning [8] Improving effective coverage results in quality services directly or indirectly [9]. Effective coverage ideally measures outcome-adjusted coverage in the health service coverage cascade; and is defined as the proportion of a population in need of a service that had a positive health outcome from the service [10]. Outcome-adjusted coverage is not always an ideal measure of effective coverage, whereas quality-adjusted coverage is often more appropriate.

Quality-adjusted coverage measures whether healthcare is delivered according to guidelines, serving as a proxy for effective coverage. Mortality measures are limited substitutes but may be used in specific cases. Whether measured directly(through outcome-adjusted coverage or indirectly (through quality-adjusted coverage), effective coverage is typically represented at a single point along the service delivery cascade rather than encompassing the entire service delivery processes and on all steps of treatment cascades [10].

There were 6.9 million neonates with PSBI needing treatment [11]. Many sick young infants (SYIs) require community-based treatment, as they often do not receive inpatient care due to the lack of affordable, accessible, or acceptable services. Studies indicate that hospital referrals are not feasible for many families, with referral refusal rates of 83.3% in rural Bangladesh, 80% in Uttar Pradesh (India), 61.3% in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), 87.7% in Nigeria, and 53.3% in Ethiopia [12–17]. There are inconsistent reports on treatment coverage for sick young infants (SYIs) with signs of PSBI across different contexts. Reported coverage rates include 53% in the rural Lucknow district, 57% in Pune district of Maharashtra, 70% in Palwal district of Haryana, and 80% in Himachal Pradesh [14, 18–20].

Relatively higher treatment coverage was reported in two rural districts of Sindh in Pakistan (95%) and, in Ibadan, South-west Nigeria (97.3%) and in DRC (83.9%) [13, 14, 21]. In Ethiopia, treatment coverage was reported to be 87% in Raya and 78% in Jimma Zone [16, 22].

Only 21% of sick children in eight high-mortality countries received effective treatment coverage [9]. Outcome-adjusted coverage was consistently lower than intervention coverage across various LMICs. For example, in India, outcome-adjusted coverage was 36% compared to 49% intervention coverage [14], in Ethiopia, 39% vs. 52% [23], in Bangladesh 56% vs. 64% [24],in DRC 64% vs. 84% [13], in Nigeria 89% vs. 96% [25], and in Pakistan 55% vs. 90% respectively [21]. In line with this treatment was successful in 91% of babies on 7 days of treatment who were treated with penicillin plus gentamicin [26]. About 854 (4%) of young infants with PSBI, were died [22]. And 412 (95.0%) completed all 14 doses of oral amoxicillin [16]. Among all PSBI cases who accepted simplified treatment, 885(92.4%) were successfully treated, whereas 25(2.6%) died. Moreover, of the 255 PSBI cases that refused treatment from government facilities, 50(25.4%) were dead [14].

There is no uniform indicator for effective treatment coverage for sick young infants (SYIs) across literatures [22]. Some used crude coverage as a proxy indicator and few others used effective coverage by adjusting the crude coverage for the quality or effectiveness of services; and the remaining others used the proportion of a population in need of a service that has a positive health outcome [8, 10]. Although effective treatment coverage through quality-adjusted or outcome-adjusted coverage without reporting the additional steps of care cascades are acceptable, full cascade is most relevant for subnational and facility levels to identify bottlenecks in service provision and to considered appropriate remedial actions [10, 27]. Effective coverage is often criticized for overestimating care and overlooking the quality of interventions. Using a standardized framework with a full cascade of care for treatment coverage helps optimize contact, intervention, quality, and outcome coverage. This approach provides a more accurate indication of the overall performance of the health system and contributes to reducing infant morbidity and mortality. The full cascade of care consists of a seven-step framework that measures both the coverage and effectiveness of health services. These steps include; identifying the target population, service contact coverage, input-adjusted coverage, intervention coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence-adjusted coverage, and outcome-adjusted coverage [10, 28]. Full care cascades provide detailed information at national, subnational, and facility levels, enabling the identification of bottlenecks in service delivery. In contrast, quality-adjusted or outcome-adjusted coverage primarily reflects performance at national or global levels.

Whether quality measured indirectly through quality-adjusted coverage or directly through outcome-adjusted coverage, assessing the full cascade of care provides a more comprehensive understanding of service quality. Examining the full cascade reveals processes across different stages of treatment and highlights effectiveness at specific points in the service delivery pathway, particularly in relation to effective treatment coverage. To our knowledge, no previous study has systematically synthesized the level of effective treatment coverage across the full cascade of care for managing sick young infants in LMICs. Generating such evidence is crucial for policymakers, practitioners, and researchers to address gaps in effective treatment coverage for SYIs with possible serious bacterial infections. This systematic review therefore aimed to determine the effective treatment coverage for SYIs with PSBIs, offering a comprehensive understanding of coverage across the full cascade of care in LMICs.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and Meta-analysis following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram (PRISMA) guidelines [29].

Eligibility criteria

Quantitative studies (such as cross-sectional descriptive studies, nonrandomized trials, and randomized control trials) and quantitative findings from the mixed methods research carried out in LMICs were among the papers considered in this review. Additionally, research outputs that included the treatment coverage of either of the care cascades for SYI suspected of acquiring a serious bacterial infection was included. We used filters like free full text; human subjects; studies published or reported in English; infants aged from birth to 2 months; and studies published from 2000 to 2025/09/30.

Research that has been published in peer-reviewed journals and grey literatures were considered.

Search strategy

​ We systematically conducted a thorough search across multiple sources for pertinent studies, following a three-step process. In the initial phase (phase one), we identified terms and keywords, concepts, colloquial terms, and relationship words. The new terms added, piloted, and assessed to see whether we get relevant research outputs. In the second phase (phase two), we used the identified initial terms and keywords, concepts, colloquial terms, and relationship words to search for literature across major databases including PubMed/Midline, Cochrane, EMBASE, and Web of Science. Additionally, we searched for grey literature using Google, Google Scholar, and institutional websites such as UN, UNICEF, and donor websites (USAID), as well as other resources like Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), and Ministry of Health (MOH), Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI), and University web portals.

The search terms were organized using the Population, Concept, and Context (PCC) framework and combined by using the Boolean terms ‘AND’ and ‘OR’. Study selection

To select studies, we employed the 2020 PRISMA approach using a three-step process (i.e., identification, initial screening of abstracts, and final eligibility assessment of full text). Two reviewers (WN and TL) independently screened the titles of studies retrieved from electronic databases using the following inclusion criteria. (i) infants aged from birth to 2 months; (ii) diagnosis of possible serious bacterial infection (PSBI); and (iii) the study addressed the management of PSBI or reported at least one treatment coverage indicator across the care cascade (i.e., contact coverage, intervention coverage, input-adjusted coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence–adjusted coverage, and/or outcome-adjusted coverage). We imported the identified articles to the EndNote X7 library, and then duplicate records removed. After eligible articles archived in the EndNote X7 library, we independently scanned the abstracts of the identified studies to exclude ineligible studies. Finally, full-length articles reviewed using the inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria of this systematic review. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion among them and with other authors.

Population and outcome measurements

The population of interest for this study included a sick young infant aged 0–59 days. The study assessed the effective treatment coverage for SYIs with PSBI, adopting the standardized cascade of care proposed by WHO and UNICEF in 2019 [10].

Data extraction

Two authors (WN, and GT) extracted data independently using an Excel file developed for the purpose of this study to document the treatment coverage across the cascade of sick young infants care. The extraction form consists of author and year of publication, study design, population(s), study setting, place of treatment, sample size, and the type of treatment coverage used across care cascades (i.e., contact coverage, intervention coverage, input-adjusted coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence-adjusted coverage, and outcome-adjusted coverage). If these coverage indicators not reported, we calculated from the reported data by taking the numerator and the denominator of each specific coverage indicators.

Subgroup analysis

Heterogeneity between studies was detected using forest plots for overlapping of confidence intervals and statistically using the standard chi-squared and I-squared tests (I2) to check the presence of observed differences between studies due to heterogeneity. The value of I2 statistics results higher heterogeneity.

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity in the pooled prevalence of treatment coverage, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on the following characteristics: year of publication (2020, 2021, and 2022), study setting (Ethiopia and India), and place of treatment (community-based vs. hospital-based). Heterogeneity improved to some extent with the year of publication and study setting. However, the possible source of heterogeneity not detected.

Sensitivity analysis

We did sensitivity analysis by excluding specific studies, with the largest and small sample size, and studies with larger or small effect sizes, from the pooled analysis. However, when such studies omitted, the results of the meta-analysis did not substantially change.

Assessment of risk of bias and methodological quality

Two reviewers (WN and GT) independently assessed the risk of bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and randomized controlled trials. The JBI had a maximum of 8 points for cross-sectional studies, 11 points cohort studies, and 13 points for randomized controlled trials. The reviewers graded the quality of each study by calculating the total percentage quality score. Accordingly, the mean quality assessment scores were 89.3% for cross-sectional studies 73% for cohort studies, and 83% for the randomized controlled trials respectively.

Publication bias and small study effects

Potential publication bias assessed through visual inspection of a funnel plot and statistically tested using Egger’s regression test. A sensitivity analysis conducted to assess the impact of individual studies on the pooled estimate. While the funnel plot exhibited visual asymmetry, Egger’s test indicated no statistically significant evidence of small-study effects (p > 0.05), suggesting the absence of publication bias.

Data synthesis, analysis and reporting the results

We first entered the selected studies into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and then imported into STATA version 14.0 for analysis. We used a random-effects meta-analysis model with a 95% confidence interval (CI) to estimate the pooled magnitude of PSBI treatment coverage across the care cascade. The heterogeneity among studies was substantial, with I² values ranging from 96.0% to 99.8% for different components of treatment coverage and corresponding p-values < 0.001. These I² values, all exceeding 75%, indicate a high level of heterogeneity across the included studies. To address heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses based on year of publication, study design, and study setting. We used Funnel plots and Egger’s test to evaluate potential publication bias and the effects of small studies. We detected no evidence of publication bias, as all p-values exceeded 0.108 across the treatment coverage components of the care cascade. Given the limited number of studies included (n = 16) and the limitations of the I² and chi-squared tests in effectively detecting bias under these conditions, it is crucial to interpret the observed heterogeneity with caution. We presented results using tables, narrative synthesis, and descriptive summaries; using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.

This systematic review and meta-analysis protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), University of York Centre for Reviews and Dissemination (ID: CRD42024525174).

Results

Descriptions of studies

The systematic search provided 6963 entries from the databases and grey literature, of which 6863 remained after removing duplicates. After screening titles and abstracts, we included 91 papers for further selection. About 70 studies excluded since they did not report outcomes of interest. In addition, 21 eligible full-text articles further assessed. Among them, five studies excluded due to neither of the treatment metrics reported; they report only the acceptability of referrals from all PSBI cases, which do not have the required content for the review. Therefore, 16 full articles with sufficient quality were included for this systematic review and meta-analysis. These articles reported 21,944 expected cases of PSBI among newborns, suggesting that approximately 10% of newborns may develop PSBI. Treatment coverage across the care cascades for sick young infants

We used random-effects to pool estimates of the effective treatment coverage across the care cascades. The pooled estimates of contact coverage were 71% (95% CI: 60% − 82%) (I2 = 99.85%, p < 0.001); intervention coverage 67% (95% CI: 55% −78%) (I2 = 99.75%, p < 0.001); quality adjusted coverage 61% (95% CI: 46% −76%) (I2 = 99.84%, p < 0.001), user adherence adjusted coverage 60% (95% CI: 43% −76%) (I2 = 99.4%, p < 0.001), and the outcome-adjusted coverage was 56% (95% CI: 43% − 68%) (I2 = 99.0%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Pooled estimates of treatment coverage across cascade of care for sick young infants with PSBI (n = 16 studies and 15406 identified and treated SYIs)

Pooled estimates of treatment coverage across cascade of care for sick young infants with PSBI (n = 16 studies and 15406 identified and treated SYIs)

The pooled estimate of treatment coverage shows a significant statistical heterogeneity. The included studies showed significant variation in treatment coverage was witnessed along the care cascades; for instance, contact coverage ranged from 16.3% to 99.6%, intervention coverage from 14% to 96%, and quality-adjusted coverage from 7% to 85% in rural Bangladesh and Ibadan in South-West Nigeria, respectively [12, 25],. Similarly, the outcome-adjusted coverage varies from 36% in India to 89% in Nigeria [14, 25] (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Forest plots showing the treatment coverage cascades for sick young infants with PSBI

In this review, significant heterogeneity was recognized. Therefore, we did sensitivity analysis and publication bias assessment to investigate the source of this heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted sensitivity analysis by excluding specific studies, such as the study by Nasir et al. with the largest sample size, the study by Hailemicahel et al. with RCT design, and studies with larger or small effect sizes, from the pooled analysis. However, when such studies omitted, the results of the meta-analysis did not substantially change, indicating that there were no individual study effects contributing to the observed heterogeneity.

Assessment of publication bias and small study effects

We assessed publication bias using funnel plots and Egger’s regression test. Visual inspection of the funnel plots revealed asymmetrical distributions across care cascade outcomes, suggesting potential bias. However, Egger’s test yielded p-values greater than 0.05 for all components: contact coverage (p = 0.108), intervention coverage (p = 0.520), quality-adjusted coverage (p = 0.984), user adherence–adjusted coverage (p = 0.383), and outcome-adjusted coverage (p = 0.989). These findings indicate no statistically significant evidence of publication bias. The observed asymmetry may instead reflect true heterogeneity in study characteristics such as methodology or population differences. Funnel plots showed scattered plots suggestive of sampling variability not due to publication bias. However, it does not indicate that studies with non-significant or negative results may be less likely to be published, leading to an asymmetrical funnel plot, so that it does not indicate publication bias (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Funnel plots of included studies across cascades of coverage for sick young infants

Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis was done, by year of publication, study setting, and the place of treatment given, since this meta-analysis displayed considerable heterogeneity to estimate the pooled treatment coverage across the care cascades (i.e. contact coverage, intervention coverage, quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence-adjusted coverage and outcome-adjusted coverage).

Contact coverage

For pooled estimates of contact coverage, subgroup analyses done by year of publication that results in pooled estimates of 83% (95% CI: 72% − 95%) (I2 = 99.03%, p < 0.001) for studies published in 2021, subgroup analysis based on the study setting results in 65% (95% CI: 53% −77%) (I2 = 97.4%, p < 0.001) for studies done in India.

Intervention coverage

for pooled estimates of intervention coverage subgroup analysis were done by year of publication, that results in a pooled estimate of 78% (95% CI: 70% − 85%) with (I2 = 96.1% p < 0.001) for studies published in 2021; based on the study setting results in 64% (95% CI: 49% −79%) I2 = 98.3%, p < 0.001) for studies done in India and by study design.

Quality adjusted coverage

Subgroup analysis also done to estimate the pooled quality adjusted coverage by year of publication, which resulted in a pooled estimate of 75% (95% CI: 66% − 84%) (I2 = 97.32%, p < 0.001) for studies published in 2021; based on the study setting resulted in 60% (95% CI: 41% − 79%) (I2 = 98.9%, p < 0.001) for studies done in India.

User adherences adjusted coverage

Subgroup analyses were conducted to estimate the pooled user adherence adjusted coverage by year of publication, resulting in a pooled estimate of 40% (95% CI: 16%–64%; I² = 99.72%, p < 0.001). As we progress from contact coverage to user adherence adjusted coverage, the number of studies reporting at each stage of the care cascade decreases from 16 to seven. Although conducting subgroup analysis with only seven studies is underpowered to detect meaningful differences, it is still useful for exploring potential sources of heterogeneity. However, care must be taken to avoid making strong conclusions.

Outcome adjusted coverage

subgroup analysis also done to estimate the pooled outcome adjusted coverage by year of publication, which resulted in a pooled estimate of 56% (95% CI: 38% −75%) (I2 = 97.4% p < 0.001) for studies published in 2020, whereas based on the study setting it resulted in 58% (95% CI: 38% −78%) (I2 = 99.0%, p < 0.001) for studies done in India.

The pooled estimates of treatment coverage across cascades of care and the subgroup analysis by year of publication, study setting, and place of treatment reduced the heterogeneity index to a smaller extent (Table 6). Discussions

In this study, the overall pooled estimates of contact coverage were 71% intervention coverage 67%, quality adjusted coverage 61%, user adherence adjusted coverage 60%, and outcome adjusted coverage was 56%. Although the studies showed heterogeneity, they clearly demonstrate a significant drop-off between contact coverage, intervention coverage, and other levels along the care cascade. Pooling coverage estimates of heterogeneous studies provides a broader picture of where the major drop-offs occur in the care cascade; this helps highlight system-wide bottlenecks that are common across settings.

The drop-off along the care cascade may be due to several factors. Inadequate healthcare infrastructure or limited resources can hinder the ability of health facilities to deliver services to all sick infants who make contact with the system. Additionally, shortages of trained healthcare professionals such as doctors, nurses, and community health workers may lead to delays or the inability to provide timely and appropriate care [32]. Geographical barriers, in remote areas, may make accessibility to health facilities challenging, leading to lower intervention coverage despite contact. Socioeconomic factors like financial constraints, lack of transportation, or cultural beliefs may deter careers from seeking or accessing healthcare services for sick infants [33] Moreover, even if sick infants reach health facilities, the quality of care provided may vary, affecting intervention coverage. This could include factors like the availability of necessary medications and equipment and adherence to treatment protocols [32, 34].

The subgroups analysis by study setting and publication year suggests that these factors contribute to heterogeneity in treatment coverage estimates. However, the persistence of heterogeneity within each subgroup indicates that other unmeasured or uncontrolled factors may be influencing the variability between studies. Even when subgroup differences are statistically significant, they may only partially explain heterogeneity. Hence, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, and future research should aim to standardize definitions and improve reporting quality to reduce unexplained variation. The subgroup analysis indicates higher treatment coverage in studies conducted in Ethiopia compared to those in India across all care cascade levels. For example, contact coverage was 65% in India versus 71% in Ethiopia; intervention coverage was 64% in India and 68% in Ethiopia; quality-adjusted coverage was 60% in India and 67% in Ethiopia; and outcome-adjusted coverage was 58% in India compared to 60% in Ethiopia. This may be due to Ethiopia’s strong Health Extension Program, which improves contact and intervention coverage. Ethiopia may also have better implementation of Community-Based Newborn Care (CBNC) and Integrated Community Case Management (iCCM) strategies. Additionally, stronger community acceptance of and trust in local health workers in Ethiopia may encourage greater care seeking and adherence.

This review found significant variability in treatment coverage across different studies and settings, contact coverage for example, ranging from 16% in Malawi to 99.6% in Nigeria [12, 25] and outcome adjusted coverage ranges from 36% in India to 89% in Nigeria [14, 25]. Moreover, the outcome-adjusted coverage used to measure the effectiveness of coverage lacks consensus on the definition, measurement, and interpretation. There are also inconsistencies within the same country. Most of the included studies (81.3%) were implementation research conducted at both community and facility levels.

The study reveals a substantial gap between crude treatment coverage and quality-adjusted treatment coverage, with pooled contact coverage of 71%. This level is lower than expected, as all SYIs should ideally have contact coverage given that treatment for SYIs with possible PSBIs is both feasible and recommended at the community level [12, 14, 24]. While community-based treatments for SYIs with PSBIs can be as effective as hospital-based care, their success depends on timely contact with health facilities that often do not occur [23, 35].

In this meta-analysis, the pooled intervention coverage was 67%, while the pooled quality-adjusted coverage was 60%, indicating a lower proportion of SYIs receiving care that met quality standards. This gap reflects deficiencies in treatment effectiveness, adherence to clinical guidelines, and recommended follow-up practice. This finding is higher than one single study conducted in Kenya on inpatient care showed 45.0% of SYIs receiving high to medium quality-adjusted coverage care in health facilities [36]. The variation in quality-adjusted coverage across studies may be due to differences in definitions, measurement methods, and quality standards. This meta-analysis considered adherence to the minimum recommended day 4 follow-up, while other studies included both day 4 and day 8 follow-ups or treatment completion. There is also inconsistency in how effectiveness is measured. Mostly quality is measured using criteria like: safety, effectiveness, efficiency, timeliness, and people-centered care [37]. The other reason may be that our study is based on community-based treatment coverage, whereas the study in Kenya was facility-based. Community-based healthcare workers play a crucial role in identifying and treating sick infants.

The pooled user adherence adjusted coverage was 60%. This measures how users comply with standard treatment protocols, this suggests that while the treatment may be effective in terms of quality, adjusting coverage, users’ adherence to treatment protocols could be a challenge. The pooled outcome-adjusted coverage was 56% this indicates the quality and effectiveness of this care is low; due to the care provided might not meet the necessary standards for effective treatment. It points out that many health systems in low-income countries suffer from quality issues, leading to ineffective treatments even when healthcare is accessed [38]. The drop from contact coverage to outcome-adjusted coverage indicates gaps in the healthcare system that must be addressed to improve outcomes for SYIs with PSBI. Enhancing each stage of the care cascade can boost treatment coverage and health outcomes. However, crude coverage alone is insufficient, as it ignores the quality of care and its impact on health improvement [8]. Unlike contact and intervention, the WHO suggests effective coverage as a measure of the performance of the national health system as a whole or for a specific health intervention individually [27]. Measuring effective coverage through quality-adjusted or outcome-adjusted coverage without reporting the full cascade of treatment coverage is best for global and national monitoring however, a complete cascade is more pertinent for monitoring at the subnational and facility levels, where specific data may be required to identify service delivery bottlenecks and take appropriate corrective action [10].

In this review treatment coverage across all care cascades is high, may be due to the majority of studies (81.3%) are implementation research that involved engagement of communities and promoting behaviour change as integral components of efforts to improve treatment coverage. This is supported by evidence from implementation research from Pradesh, India, showed that the treatment coverage in the launch phase was 33%, while it was 85% in the implementation phase and supportive supervision of the community health workers improved the identification of PSBI [14]. Community-based healthcare workers (health extension workers in the Ethiopian context) play a crucial role in identifying sick infants, being able to treat PSBI cases at the community level, and facilitating prompt referral to appropriate healthcare facilities. In line with this, 83% of families with sick infants did not accept referral advice and received outpatient injectable gentamicin plus oral amoxicillin treatment at the community level [16].

Community-led initiatives such as the availability of mothers’ support groups, the women’s development army (for the Ethiopian context), and male involvement programs foster positive healthcare-seeking behaviour and increase treatment coverage. Because they are able to use culturally sensitive communication strategies tailored to the local context, this can facilitate acceptance and adoption of recommended practices and enhance treatment coverage. Of 1,011 sick newborns presenting at intervention health posts, 57% were identified by HEWs, and 90% of PSBI of these cases were refused referral and were treated at the health post by HEWs [23]. Moreover, in rural Zaria, Nigeria, most families (97%) refused referral and were treated at a primary health care center [25].

In this review we identified different effectiveness measures used across studies. The majority of the studies reported the effectiveness of treatment using different measures of effectiveness, like completing antibiotics, completing follow-ups on days 4 and 8, the case fatality rate, and both the treatment outcome and completing the recommended follow-ups. One of the studies did not report any of the effectiveness measures [39]. Among them, 18.8%(3/16) of studies reported both treatment outcome and completing the antibiotics [13, 16, 23],.And 31.3%(5/16) of studies reported both completing the follow ups and treatment outcome [14, 15, 18, 24, 31]. One of the study reported only completing follow-up at day 4 and day 8 [12], and one other study reported the case fatality rate [30]. Various treatment effectiveness measures provide valuable insights into the complex nature of treatment efficacy and patient outcomes.

In this review, about 80.7% of SYIs completed both day 4 and day 8 facility follow-up visits in Malawi [24], 93.6% in DRC [13], 93.6% in Pradesh, India [14],95.0% in Jimma, Ethiopia [16],and 79% in South-west Nigeria [15]. Assessing both treatment outcomes and adherence to follow-up protocols offers a comprehensive view of treatment effectiveness. Timely follow-ups, especially on days 4 and 8, enable monitoring of disease progression and early detection of complications. Effective treatment depends on both the quality of care and patient adherence. The completion of antibiotic regimens serves as a fundamental indicator of treatment success, emphasizing the importance of patients adhering to prescribed medication schedules. Studies consistently demonstrate that patients who complete their antibiotic courses experience better outcomes, including reduced symptom severity, lower rates of recurrence, and a decreased risk of antibiotic resistance development. Inadequate treatment coverage is also a sign of ineffectiveness. Accordingly, at the hospital in Ray, Ethiopia, around 4% [35] of the SYI with PSBI passed away [22]. Comparably, 6.9% (118) in Pakistan [21], 40 SYIs in South-West Nigeria [15], 8.4% [29] in rural Zaria, Nigeria [25],, 9% [14] in Pradesh, India [19] and 8.3% of SYI with PSBI in the DRC had treatment failer that included death [13] (see Table 5). The collective evidence from the reviewed studies underscores the importance of employing multiple measures of effectiveness to assess the impact of treatments comprehensively. Researchers and healthcare practitioners can obtain a comprehensive picture of treatment efficacy and make well-informed decisions to optimize patient care and results by taking into account aspects including antibiotic completion rates, follow-up adherence, case fatality rates, and overall treatment outcomes. To bridge the remaining knowledge gaps and improve treatment options’ efficacy across a range of patient demographics and healthcare settings, more investigation and cooperative efforts are necessary.

Strength and limitation of the study

We conducted a comprehensive search using the JBI critical appraisal instruments to enhance methodological quality. Additionally, we included both published and unpublished studies to minimize selection and publication bias. For studies that did not report treatment coverage, we used raw data to calculate estimates and appropriately assigned reported coverage across the care cascade. This review may be the first to establish a baseline measurement of treatment coverage across the cascade for SYIs with possible serious bacterial infection PSBI.

However, this review has some limitations. In some studies, we used proxy indicators to measure quality-adjusted coverage for example, we used adherence to at least a minimum recommended day four follow-up, rather than using the completion of the full 5–7-day treatment or follow-ups on days 8 and 14; this may overestimate the quality-adjusted coverage indicator. While pooled estimates from heterogeneous studies can be acceptable, they need to be interpreted with caution, other ways, they may be misleading. The proposed health-service coverage cascade has four important cautions, as is also suggested by the Think Tank group [10]. These include the care cascade does not capture the underlying reasons for gaps between identifying the target population and delivering care; measuring user adherence is difficult and influenced by factors beyond the health system; and it overlooks user experience as a distinct component of care quality. Patients may have better adherence to poor quality services than objectively measured standard services. Fourth, the cascade assumes that each step must occur to have the maximum positive health outcome. However, a positive health outcome might occur in the absence of any health service.

Conclusion

The review showed treatment coverage is highly variable across studies, and there is no consensus on the definition, measurement, and interpretation of effective coverage indicators. The results of this study showed a significant drop-off from crude treatment coverage to quality-adjusted coverage, user adherence adjusted coverage, and outcome adjusted coverage.

The declining percentages from contact coverage to outcome-adjusted coverage along the cascades of care suggest potential gaps in the healthcare system. Therefore, policymakers, program implementers, and health workers should make concerted efforts to improve each stage of the care cascade and consider quality aspects of services along with accessibility. Moreover, future research should aim to standardize definitions and enhance reporting quality to reduce unexplained variation across studies.
