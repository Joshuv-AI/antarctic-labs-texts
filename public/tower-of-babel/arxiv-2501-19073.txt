Pareto-frontier Entropy Search with Variational Lower Bound Maximization
Masanori Ishikura, Masayuki Karasuyama

Abstract
This study considers multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) through the information gain of the Pareto-frontier. To calculate the information gain, a predictive distribution conditioned on the Pareto-frontier plays a key role, which is defined as a distribution truncated by the Pareto-frontier. However, it is usually impossible to obtain the entire Pareto-frontier in a continuous domain, and therefore, the complete truncation cannot be known. We consider an approximation of the truncated distribution by using a mixture distribution consisting of two possible approximate truncations obtainable from a subset of the Pareto-frontier, which we call over- and under-truncation. Since the optimal balance of the mixture is unknown beforehand, we propose optimizing the balancing coefficient through the variational lower bound maximization framework, by which the approximation error of the information gain can be minimized. Our empirical evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed method particularly when the number of objective functions is large.



1
Introduction

Multi-objective optimization (MOO) of black-box functions is ubiquitous in a variety of fields such as materials science, engineering, drug design, and AutoML.
Evolutionary algorithms have been classically studied for MOO, but they require a large number of function evaluations, which is often difficult for practical problems.
On the other hand, Bayesian optimization (BO) based approaches to MOO, which use a probabilistic surrogate model (typically, Gaussian process), have been widely studied recently
(
Knowles,, 2006
;
Emmerich,, 2005
;
Ponweiser et al.,, 2008
;
Belakaria et al.,, 2019
;
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
;
Qing et al.,, 2022
;
Tu et al.,, 2022
, e.g.,)
.

This study focuses on multi-objective BO (MOBO) based on the information gain of the Pareto-frontier. Since the optimal solution of an MOO problem is not unique in general, the optimal values are represented as a set of output vectors, called the Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
.
Pareto-frontier entropy search (PFES)
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
considers the mutual information between the Pareto-frontier and a candidate point as an acquisition function of MOBO.
The effectiveness of the basic idea of PFES has been repeatedly shown
(
Qing et al.,, 2022
;
Tu et al.,, 2022
)
.

In the information-theoretic approaches considering the information of the Pareto-frontier
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
;
Qing et al.,, 2022
;
Tu et al.,, 2022
)
, the predictive distribution given the Pareto-frontier
p(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\mid{\cal F}^{*})
plays a key role in the information evaluation, where
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
is a vector of objective function values at the input
\boldsymbol{x}
.
In this distribution,
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
cannot be better than
{\cal F}^{*}
because
{\cal F}^{*}
should be the Pareto-frontier.
Therefore,
p(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\mid{\cal F}^{*})
becomes a truncated distribution (see Fig.
1
(a) for which details will be discussed in Section
3.2
).
However, it is practically impossible to obtain the entire
{\cal F}^{*}
in a continuous space, and we only obtain a finite size subset
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}\subseteq{\cal F}^{*}
(red stars in Fig.
1
(a)).
This means that the exact truncation by
{\cal F}^{*}
shown in Fig.
1
(a) cannot be calculated.
To avoid this issue, all the existing studies use approximations based on an overly truncated distribution created by
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
(Fig.
1
(d)).
A drawback of this approach is that the effect of truncation by
{\cal F}^{*}
on
\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})
is always estimated stronger than the true truncation, because of which we call it over-truncation.

In this study, we introduce the variational lower bound maximization approach into the mutual information (MI) estimation of information-theoretic MOBO, which is called Pareto-frontier Entropy search with Variational lower bound maximization (PFEV).
In addition to the conventional over-truncation, we also consider a conservative approach, called under-truncation (shown as Fig.
1
(c)).
The under-truncation estimates the effect of truncation by
{\cal F}^{*}
weaker than the true truncation.
Therefore, to balance two opposite approaches, we combine the over and the under-truncation in such a way that a mixture of them (Fig.
1
(e)) defines a variational distribution of the MI approximation.
We show that the mixture weight can be optimized through the variational lower bound maximization.
This means that the optimal balance of the over and the under-truncation can be determined so that the MI approximation error is minimized.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

PFEV is the first approach to continuous space MOBO that is based on a general lower bound of MI (existing work only shows a lower bound under a restrictive condition of two objective problems, for which we discuss in Section
4
).

•

We newly introduce an under-truncation approximation for
p(\boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})\mid{\cal F}^{*})
.
Further, we define variational distribution as a mixture of distributions with the over and the under-truncation, and show how to optimize the mixture weight.

•

We also discuss properties and extensions of PFEV.
For example, we show that our MI lower bound can be further lower bounded by PI (probability of improvement).
We also discuss a Monte-Carlo approximation tailored to our lower bound.
We further discuss several extended settings such as parallel querying.

•

Through empirical evaluation on Gaussian process generated functions, benchmark functions, and an application to machine learning hyper-parameter optimization, we demonstrate effectiveness of PFEV.
We empirically observed that PFEV shows a particular difference from existing over-truncation based methods when output dimension
\geq 3
, in which the difference of two truncation becomes more apparent.

2
Multi-Objective Optimization and Gaussian Process Model

We consider Bayesian optimization (BO) for a multi-objective optimization (MOO) problem of maximizing
L\geq 2
objective functions

f^{l}:{\cal X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(l=1,\ldots,L)
,
where

{\cal X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

is an input space.
Let

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\coloneqq(f^{1}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\ldots,f^{L}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{\top}
,
where
f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\coloneqq f^{l}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
The optimal solutions of MOO are characterized by the Pareto optimality.
For given
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
,
if

f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\geq f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
for
\forall l\in\{1,\ldots,L\}

and there exists
l
that satisfies

f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}>f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
,
then “
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
dominates
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
”, written as
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\succ\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
.
When
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is not dominated by any other
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}
, then
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is Pareto-optimal.
The Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
is a set of Pareto-optimal
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
that can be defined as

{\cal F}^{*}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal F}_{{\cal X}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\not\succ\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\forall\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\in{\cal F}_{{\cal X}}\}
,
where

{\cal F}_{{\cal X}}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}\}
.

Each objective function is represented by the Gaussian process (GP) regression.
The observation of the
l
-th objective function is

y_{i}^{l}=f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}}+\varepsilon
,
where

\varepsilon\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\rm noise})
.
The training dataset with
n
observations is written as

{\cal D}\coloneqq\{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
where

\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=(y_{i}^{1},\ldots,y_{i}^{L})^{\top}
.
We use independent
L
GPs with a kernel function
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
.
Let

\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq(k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}),\ldots,k(\boldsymbol{x}_{,}\boldsymbol{x}_{n}))^{\top}
,

\boldsymbol{y}^{l}\coloneqq(y_{1}^{l},\ldots,y_{n}^{l})^{\top}
,
and

\boldsymbol{K}
be the matrix whose
(i,j)
-element is
k(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
.
The posterior

p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal D})

of the
l
-th GP (with
0
prior mean) is written as

{\cal N}(\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}),\sigma^{2}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))
,
where

\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{K}+\sigma^{2}_{\rm noise}\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}^{l}

and

\sigma^{2}_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})=k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{K}+\sigma^{2}_{\rm noise}\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
From independence, we have

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal D})=\prod_{l\in[L]}p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal D})
.
For notational brevity, conditioning on
{\cal D}
is omitted (e.g.,
p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal D})
is written as
p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
).

3
Pareto-frontier Entropy Search with Variational Lower Bound Maximization

We consider multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) based on mutual information

{\rm MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})

between an objective function value
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and the Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
(Note that, throughout the paper,
{\cal F}^{*}
is a random variable determined via the predictive distribution of
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
).
An intuition behind this criterion is to select
\boldsymbol{x}
that provides the maximum information gain of the Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
.
The effectiveness of this approach is shown by
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
, but it is known that accurate evaluation of
{\rm MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
is difficult.
Our proposed method is the first method introducing the variational lower bound maximization to evaluate
{\rm MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
.
We call our proposed method Pareto-frontier Entropy search with Variational lower bound maximization (PFEV).

3.1
Lower Bound of Mutual Information

A lower bound
\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x})
of
{\rm MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
can be derived as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int p({\cal F}^{*})\int p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\log\frac{p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathrm{d}{\cal F}^{*}

\displaystyle=\int p({\cal F}^{*})\left[\int p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\parallel q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\right)\right]\mathrm{d}{\cal F}^{*}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\left[\int p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right]\eqqcolon\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}
is Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, and
q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
is a density function called a variational distribution.
A similar lower bound was first derived in the context of constrained BO
(
Takeno et al.,, 2022
)
.
This lower bound holds for any distribution
q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
that satisfies the following support condition:

\displaystyle\mathrm{supp}(q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}))\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})),

(2)

where
\mathrm{supp}
is a set of non-zero points of a given density, defined as

\mathrm{supp}(p(\boldsymbol{f}\mid{\cal F}^{*}))=\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid p(\boldsymbol{f}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\neq 0\}
.
In addition to this condition, the lower bound

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x})

is derived by using the convention
0\log 0=0

(
Cover and Thomas,, 2006
)
.
We call the condition (
2
) variational distribution condition (VDC).
Note that if VDC is not satisfied,

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x})

is not defined,
because then, the probability measure induced by

p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

is not absolutely continuous
with respect to those of

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
.

3.2
Variational Lower Bound Maximization by Combining Over- and Under-Truncation

If

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
for
{\cal F}^{*}
almost everywhere, the lower bound

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x})

is equal to

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
.
However, the analytical representation of

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

is not known.
This stems from a common well-known difficulty of information-theoretic BO, and effectiveness of the truncated distribution based approximation has been repeatedly shown
(
Wang and Jegelka,, 2017
;
Takeno et al.,, 2020
;
Perrone et al.,, 2019
;
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
, e.g.,)
not only for MOBO, but also for a variety contexts of BO problems (such as standard single-objective problems, constraint problems, and multi-fidelity problems).

Define

\boldsymbol{f}\preceq{\cal F}^{*}

as that
\boldsymbol{f}
is dominated by or equal to at least one element in
{\cal F}^{*}
.
The truncation-based approximation replaces the conditioning in

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

with

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq{\cal F}^{*}
,
by which we obtain

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq{\cal F}^{*})
:

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})=\begin{cases}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})/Z^{{\cal F}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}^{{\cal F}^{*}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where

{\cal A}^{{\cal F}^{*}}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid\boldsymbol{f}\preceq\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime},\exists\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}^{*}\}

and

Z^{{\cal F}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}^{{\cal F}^{*}})

is the normalization constant.
As shown in Fig.
1
(a),

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
is a truncated normal distribution in which only the region dominated by
{\cal F}^{*}
remains.

Suzuki et al., (2020)
call this distribution PFTN (Pareto-Frontier Truncated Normal distribution).
PFTN is derived by the fact that if
{\cal F}^{*}
is given, any
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
dominating
{\cal F}^{*}
cannot exist.
However,
{\cal F}^{*}
cannot be obtained in practice in the continuous domain, and we only obtain limited discrete points, such as those indicated by the red star points in Fig.
1
(a).
A subset of
{\cal F}^{*}
defined by these discrete points is written as
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}\subseteq{\cal F}^{*}
(we discuss how to calculate
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
in Section
3.3
).
In this study, we consider combining two truncated distributions derived from

{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
, instead of using
{\cal F}^{*}
that is not obtainable.

Figure 1:

(a) Predictive distribution truncated by
{\cal F}^{*}
.
(b) Examples of possible truncation given
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
.
(c) Under-truncation based on
\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}
.
(d) Over-truncation based on
\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}
.
(e) Mixture of

q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})

and

q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})
.

In Fig.
1
(b), the orange dashed lines are examples of possible truncation behind
{\cal F}_{S}^{*}
and the red dot line is the true
{\cal F}^{*}
, which is unknown.
The first truncated distribution is based on the most conservative truncation shown in Fig.
1
(c).
This truncation is defined by removing “the region that dominates
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
” (any point that dominates
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
cannot exist).
The remaining region is written as

{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{L}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\preceq\boldsymbol{f},\exists\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}^{*}_{S}\}
.
The resulting truncated normal distribution is

\displaystyle q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})\coloneqq\begin{cases}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})/Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where

Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})
.
We call this distribution PFTN-U (PFTN with under-truncation).
PFTN-U has a larger support

\mathrm{supp}(q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S}))\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}))
,
and thus, VDC is satisfied.
This truncation is conservative in the sense that the region where the density function becomes zero is smaller than

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
,
by which it underestimates the effect of the condition
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq{\cal F}^{*}
on
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

The second truncated distribution is based on the over-truncation shown in Fig
1
(d).
This is “the region dominated by
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
”, defined as

{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}\mid\boldsymbol{f}\preceq\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime},\exists\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}\in{\cal F}^{*}_{S}\}
.
The resulting truncated normal distribution is

\displaystyle q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})\coloneqq\begin{cases}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})/Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where

Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})
.
In contrast to
q_{U}
,
q_{O}
overly truncates the distribution in the sense that the region where the density function value becomes zero is larger than
q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
, by which it overestimates the effect of the condition
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq{\cal F}^{*}
on
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
We call this distribution PFTN-O (PFTN with over-truncation).
It is important to note that PFTN-O may not satisfy VDC (
2
) because

\mathrm{supp}(q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S}))\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}))
.

Figure 2:

An example of truncation in three-dimensional output space.
The resulting truncate regions have a large difference.

As we have already discussed,
q_{O}
and
q_{U}
over and under estimates the truncation by the true
{\cal F}^{*}
.
Therefore, instead of using one of them, we consider the mixture defined as follows:

\displaystyle q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})=\lambda q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})+(1-\lambda)q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S}),

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
\lambda p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})/Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

(3)

where

{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\coloneqq{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\setminus{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}
,
and

\lambda\in(0,1]
is a weight of the mixture.
Because of

\lambda\neq 0
,
we have

\mathrm{supp}(q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S}))\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(p({\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}))
,
meaning that the mixture satisfies VDC.
Figure
1
(e) shows an example of the mixture.
The effect of PFTN-U and PFTN-O can be controlled by
\lambda
.
In the 2D illustration in Fig.
1
, the difference between the two truncations might appear small.
However, as shown in Fig.
2
, in three or more dimensions, the two truncations are significantly different, obviously (further discussion is in Appendix
L
).
Therefore, we conjecture that the balance of them can have a strong effect, particularly for problems with
L\geq 3
.

By substituting

q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})

into

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
we define

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

as

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\lambda q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})+(1-\lambda)q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})}{p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\bigl[\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}\bigr],

(4)

where

\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}
,

\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}
,
and

\mathbb{I}
is the indicator function.
Importantly, the weight parameter
\lambda
can be estimated by maximizing the lower bound:

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in(0,1]}\rm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

(5)

This maximization implies the following property, which is a well-known advantage of the variational lower bound maximization
(
Bishop and Bishop,, 2023
)
:

Remark 3.1
.

From (
1
),

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\parallel q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\right)\right].

Therefore, (
5
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda\in(0,1]}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\parallel q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\right)\right].

As a result, (
5
) can be seen as the minimization of difference between true
p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
and
q_{\lambda}
.

Further, we have the following property:

Remark 3.2
.

(
5
) can be bounded from below (the proof is in Appendix
A
):

\displaystyle\max_{\lambda\in(0,1]}\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\left[p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}})\right]>0.

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}})

can be seen as the probability of improvement (PI) from the region
{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
, from which positivity of (
5
) is also directly derived.

While MI has a trivial lower bound
0
in general, this remark guarantees that (
5
) is a larger lower bound than this trivial bound.
Further, we can also see that if the candidate
\boldsymbol{x}
is promising in a sense of PI, (
5
) should have substantially larger value than
0
.

As a result, the selection of
\boldsymbol{x}
is formulated as

\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{x},\lambda\in(0,1]}\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda),

in which
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\lambda
can be simultaneously optimized (
(d+1)
-dimensional maximization).

3.3
Computations

We employ the Monte-Carlo (MC) estimation to calculate the expectation in

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}\log\left\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left.\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\right\},

(6)

where
F
is a set of sampled pairs of
({\cal F}^{*}_{S},\boldsymbol{f})
, for which a sample pair is denoted as

(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})
, and
K=|F|
is the number of samples.
Henceforth, variables with ‘
\tilde{}
’ indicate sampled values.
For
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
, the same sampling strategy can be used as existing information-theoretic MOBO methods
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
;
Hernandez-Lobato et al.,, 2016
)
, in which a sample path

\tilde{f}^{l}

is approximately generated by using random feature map (RFM)
(
Rahimi and Recht,, 2008
)
.
We obtain
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
by solving the MOO maximizing sampled

\tilde{f}^{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

for

l=1,\ldots,L
.
This maximization can be performed by general MOO solvers such as the well-known NSGA-II
(
Deb et al.,, 2002
)
.
In the case of NSGA-II,
|{\cal F}^{*}_{S}|
can be specified, typically less than
100
.
Computations of
Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
and
Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
can be easily performed by the cell (hyper-rectangle) decomposition-based approach as shown by
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
for which details are in Appendix
B
.
This decomposition is only required once for each iteration of BO because it is common for all candidate
\boldsymbol{x}
.

We also consider another way to numerically approximate

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

based on the following transformation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

\displaystyle\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\!\left[\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\!+\!\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\log\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\left[p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\left.+p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\log\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt+p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})\log\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(7)

Note that the second line is from

\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})=1-\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})
, and the third line is obtained by replacing

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}
with

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}}
.
Since (
6
)
can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\log\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
by comparing this re-written expression with (
7
),
we can interpret that (
6
) performs one-sample approximations

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})\approx\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})

and

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})\approx\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
.
We consider improving this approximations by introducing prior knowledge about

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})

and

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})
.

Let
\theta\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})
.
We use an approximation

\theta\approx p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})=Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})/Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\eqqcolon\hat{p}

as our prior knowledge.
The approximation is replacement of the conditioning by

\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}

with the under-truncation

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
.
Note that
Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})
are required even in the naïve MC (
6
),
\hat{p}
can be obtained without additional computations.
A prior distribution having the mode at
\hat{p}
is introduced to estimate
\theta
.
We use the beta distribution by which MAP (maximum a posteriori) becomes

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\hat{p}+\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})}{2}.

The detailed derivation is in Appendix
D.1
.

\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}

can be seen as an average of

\hat{p}

and

\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
.

As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{LB}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle+(1-\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}))\log\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\eqqcolon\widehat{\mathrm{LB}}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda).

(8)

We empirically observe that the estimation variance can be improved by the prior.
Instead, the bias to
\hat{p}
can occur, but because the number of samples is usually small (our default setting is
K=10
in the experiments, which is same as existing information-theoretic BO studies such as
(
Wang and Jegelka,, 2017
)
), variance reduction has a stronger benefit in practice.
Further discussion on the accuracy of this estimator is in Appendices
D.2
and
D.3
.

The procedure of PFEV is shown in Algorithm
1
.
Assume that we already have the posterior mean and variance of the GPs.
Then, computations of (
8
) is
O(KCL)
, where
C
is the number of hyper-rectangle cells in the decomposition.
For the cell decomposition, we employ the quick hypervolume (QHV) algorithm
(
Russo and Francisco,, 2014
)
as indicated by
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
.
Sampling of
F
requires
O(D^{3})
for RFM with
D
basis functions and the cost of NSGA-II is also required.
We empirically see that, for small
L
, the computational cost of NSGA-II is dominant, and for large
L
, QHV becomes dominant, both of which are commonly required for several information-theoretic MOBO
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
;
Qing et al.,, 2022
;
Tu et al.,, 2022
)
.
Compared with them, the practical cost of the lower bound calculation in
CalcPFEV
of Algorithm
1
is often relatively small (see Appendix
K.3
for details).
As discussed in the end of Section
3.2
, the acquisition function maximization is formulated as
(d+1)
-dimensional optimization (line 10 of Algorithm
1
).
On the other hand, it is also possible to optimize
\lambda
for each given
\boldsymbol{x}
as an inner one-dimensional optimization.
In the latter case, efficient computations can be performed by considering (
8
) (or (
6
)) is concave with respect to
\lambda
.
Further, we can show that the maximizer of
\lambda
exists though the candidate
\lambda
is defined as a left open interval
(0,1]
.
Details of these discussions about the maximization problem of
\lambda
are shown in Appendix
C
.

4
Related Work

For MOBO, a variety of approaches have been proposed, typically by extending single-objective acquisition function such as expected improvement and upper confidence bound
(
Emmerich,, 2005
;
Shah and Ghahramani,, 2016
;
Zuluaga et al.,, 2016
;
Daulton et al.,, 2020
;
Ament et al.,, 2023
, e.g.,)
.
Information-theoretic approaches
(
Hernandez-Lobato et al.,, 2016
;
Belakaria et al.,, 2019
, e.g.,)
also have been extended from its counterpart of the single-objective BO
(
Hennig and Schuler,, 2012
;
Hernández-Lobato et al.,, 2014
;
Wang and Jegelka,, 2017
)
.
Among these, the most closely related method to the proposed method is PFES
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
.
PFES can be seen as a multi-objective extension of the max value based information-theoretic BO, called max-value entropy search (MES)
(
Wang and Jegelka,, 2017
)
.
Information-theoretic MOBO before PFES and other approaches are reviewed in
(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
.
Here, we mainly focus on information-theoretic MOBO methods after PFES.
A more comprehensive related work including other information-theoretic methods and other criteria are discussed in Appendix
I
.

The MI approximation with PFTN was first introduced by PFES.
On the other hand, the MI approximation is based on a decomposition into difference of the entropy, which is a classical approach in information-theoretic BO
(
Hernández-Lobato et al.,, 2014
)
.
In the context of constrained BO,
Takeno et al., (2022)
revealed that the entropy difference based decomposition can cause a critical issue originated from a negative value of the MI approximation.
The positivity of PFES has not been clarified.
Further, PFES only uses over-truncation (PFTN-O).
Therefore, effect of the truncation by
{\cal F}^{*}
on
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is overly estimated.

After PFES, Joint Entropy Search (JES)
(
Tu et al.,, 2022
)
was proposed in which the joint entropy of the optimal
\boldsymbol{x}
and
{\cal F}^{*}
is considered.
This approach is also only uses over-truncation.
Another approach considering the lower bound of MI is Parallel Feasible Pareto Frontier Entropy Search (
\{\text{PF}\}^{2}\text{ES}
)
(
Qing et al.,, 2022
)
, which also pointed out the problem of over-truncation.
However,
\{\text{PF}\}^{2}\text{ES}
is still based only on over-truncation.
A shifting parameter
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
of the Pareto-frontier is heuristically introduced to mitigate over-truncation, for which theoretical justification is not clarified.
Further, their criterion is not guaranteed as a lower bound in general (only when
L=2
with the assumption
|{\cal F}_{S}^{*}|\to\infty
).

PFEV is a general framework so that we can drive extensions for the following four scenarios:

Parallel querying:

In parallel querying, we consider querying multiple points at one iteration, which is an important practical setting.
Suppose that we consider
Q>1
points selection.
Let

{\cal X}_{q}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(q)}\}

and

{\cal H}_{q}=\{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(q)}}\}

for
q\leq Q
.
The MI for all
Q
points

\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{Q};{\cal F}^{*})

represents the benefit of selecting
{\cal X}_{q}
, but this results in a
(Q\times d)
-dimensional optimization problem, which can be unstable.
Instead, we follow the approach in
(
Takeno et al.,, 2022
)
, which is a greedy selection based on conditional mutual information (CMI).
When we select the
(q+1)
-th
\boldsymbol{x}
after determining
\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(q)}
, the MI can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{q}\cup\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})\!=\!\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{q};{\cal F}^{*})\!+\!\mathrm{CMI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q}),

where

\mathrm{CMI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{q}}[\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})]

is the MI conditioned on
{\cal H}_{q}
.
Since the first term does not depend on
\boldsymbol{x}
, we only need to optimize
\mathrm{CMI}
to select
\boldsymbol{x}
.
The calculation of
\mathrm{CMI}
can be performed by adding sampled
{\cal H}_{q}
into the training dataset of the GPs, after which evaluation of the lower bound is almost same as the single querying.
See Appendix
E
for details.

Decoupled setting:

In the decoupled setting, we can observe only one selected objective function instead of querying all
L
objective functions simultaneously
(
Hernandez-Lobato et al.,, 2016
)
.
In PFEV, the criterion for the decoupled setting can be defined as

\mathrm{MI}(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
,
which is the information gain from only one objective function
f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The lower bound of
\mathrm{MI}(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
can be derived by the same approach shown in Section
3
.
In this case, we need to approximate
p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
instead of
p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
, for which we can use the marginal distribution of

q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
.
See Appendix
F
for detail.

Joint entropy search:

For PFEV, not only the information gain of the Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
but also the corresponding input
\boldsymbol{x}
can be considered as

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})
,
where

{\cal X}^{*}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal F}^{*}\}
.
This can be seen as a JES extension of PFEV.
When we derive the lower bound for

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})

by the same approach as Section
3
, a variational approximation for
p(\boldsymbol{f}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})
is required.
A basic idea of JES
(
Tu et al.,, 2022
)
is to simply add

({\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})

into the training data of the GPs.
In the case of PFEV,
we can define the variational distribution

q(\boldsymbol{f}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})

by using the same mixture as (
3
).
The only difference is that
({\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})
is added to the training data of the GPs.
Since we have not observed particular performance improvement by this additional conditioning, we employ
\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
as the default setting of PFEV.
See Appendix
G
for detail.

Noisy observations:

It is also possible to derive the information gain obtained from noisy observation

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
,
where

\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

with

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}\boldsymbol{I})
,
though we mainly consider
\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
for brevity.
In this case, instead of

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
,
we need to consider

p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
.
Through the relation

p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})=\int p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
we can derive the lower bound based on the same approximation of

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

by using

q_{\lambda}
.
See Appendix
H
for detail.

(
Emmerich,, 2005
)

(
Suzuki et al.,, 2020
)
,

7
Discussion on Gradient-based Optimization of Acquisition Function

We employed DIRECT as an optimizer of the acquisition function because it does not require ‘initial points’ unlike the gradient descent that requires the appropriate setting of the initial points (the number of initial points and locations).
Our purpose is to focus more on differences of the acquisition functions and to reduce the other factors affecting the performance.
On the other hand, evaluation using gradient-based approaches is also important future work because it is widely used in BO and should have high performance in general.
We partially show comparison with a baseline using the gradient optimization (qLogNEHVI in BoTorch) in Appendix
M
.

Note that the proposed acquisition function (
8
) is mostly differentiable, except for the
indicator

\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})

in

\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
We consider that possible approaches are simply ignoring this term in the gradient (regarding the gradient of the indicator as 0) or using a continuous approximation of the gradient.
In the continuous approximation, we replace

\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\approx p(\boldsymbol{f}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
,
where

\boldsymbol{f}\sim{\cal N}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\rho\boldsymbol{I})

in which

\rho>0

is a fixed smoothing parameter.
The right hand side of the approximation is differentiable with respect to

\boldsymbol{x}

through the similar decomposition to (
9
) in Appendix
B
(note that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is differential because it is generated from RFM).
This can be interpreted as a counter-part of the standard CDF-based smoothing approximation of an indicator function, extended to the indicator of the Pareto dominated region.
For the calculation, although the cell-based decomposition is required for

{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}_{S}^{*}}
,
we can reuse the cells created in line 7 of Algorithm
1
.

8
Conclusions

We proposed a multi-objective Bayesian optimization acquisition function that is based on variational lower bound maximization.
By combining two normal distributions, defined by under- and over-truncation of Pareto-frontier, we introduced a variational distribution as mixture of these two distributions based on which a lower bound of mutual information can be constructed.
Performance superiority was shown by GP-generated functions and benchmark functions.
A current limitation includes theoretical guarantee of the MI approximation and convergence, which is important open problems for information-theoretic BO.
Another possible future direction is to use a more complicated variational distribution.
We employ the simple one parameter (
\lambda
) distribution because
q
should be estimated based on the
K
samples, which is usually quite small (
10
in the experiments).
However, to make the lower bound tight, a more flexible distribution may be required.

Appendix B
Computations of
Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
and
Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}

First, we consider the normalization constant of PFTN-O

Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})
.
We decompose

{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}

into disjoint hyper-rectangles, denoted as

{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}={\cal C}_{1}\cup{\cal C}_{2}\cup\ldots\cup{\cal C}_{C}
,
where

{\cal C}_{i}
is a hyper-rectangle and
C
is the number of hyper-rectangles.
Algorithms decomposing a dominated region have been studied in the context of the Pareto hyper-volume computation.
We use quick hypervolume (QHV)
(
Russo and Francisco,, 2014
)
, used also in PFES.
Each hyper-rectangle
{\cal C}_{i}
is written as

\displaystyle{\cal C}_{i}=\left(\ell_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{1}\right]\times\left(\ell_{i}^{2},u_{i}^{2}\right]\times\ldots\times\left(\ell_{i}^{L},u_{i}^{L}\right],

where
\ell_{i}^{l}
and
u_{i}^{l}
are the smallest and largest values in the
l
-th dimension of the
i
-th hyper-rectangle.
From the independence of the objective functions,
Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
can be easily computed by

\displaystyle Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\int_{{\cal C}_{i}}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\int_{\ell_{i}^{l}}^{u_{i}^{l}}p(f_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l})\mathrm{d}f_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\Phi(\bar{\alpha}_{i,l})-\Phi(\underline{\alpha}_{i,l})\right),

(9)

where

\underline{\alpha}_{i,l}=(\ell_{i}^{l}-\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))/\sigma_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

and

\bar{\alpha}_{i,l}=(u_{i}^{l}-\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))/\sigma_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})
,
and
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

Next, we consider
Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
that can be re-written as

\displaystyle Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}

\displaystyle=p\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-p\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-p\left(-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq\{-\boldsymbol{f}\mid\boldsymbol{f}\in\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}\}\right).

In the last equation, the region

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}

is re-written by flipping the sign of
\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}
in such a way that the region is written as a “dominated region”.
This enables us to use QHV, which decomposes a “dominated region” into hyper-rectangles, for the computation of

p(-\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\preceq\{-\boldsymbol{f}\mid\boldsymbol{f}\in\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}\})

based on almost the same way as (
9
).

Appendix D
Combining Prior Knowledge in MC Estimator

D.1
Derivation of MAP

We use the beta distribution for the prior
\theta\sim\mathrm{Beta}(a,b)
.
The density function is

\displaystyle p(\theta)=\frac{\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}}{B(a,b)}.

Using the approximation
\theta\approx\hat{p}
, we set
a-1=r\hat{p}
and
b-1=r(1-\hat{p})
, where
r\geq 0
is a parameter.
This sets the mode of
p(\theta)
as
\hat{p}
.
As shown in Fig.
9
, a larger
r
has a stronger peak at
\hat{p}
.
When
r=0
,
p(\theta)
becomes uniform in
[0,1]
.

Figure 9:

Density functions of the beta distribution when
\hat{p}=0.8
.
For all
r
, the mode is
0.8
.

The posterior of the beta distribution with the Bernoulli distribution likelihood is

\mathrm{Beta}(a+m,b+\ell)
,
where
m
is the number of ‘success’ and
\ell
is the number of ‘fail’ in Bernoulli trials.
We can interpret that

\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

is a sample from the Bernoulli distribution with the probability

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
.
Therefore, we set

m=\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

and

\ell=1-\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})
,
from which the mode of the posterior
\mathrm{Beta}(a+m,b+\ell)
can be derived as

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}

\displaystyle=\frac{a-1+m}{a-1+m+b-1+\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{r\hat{p}+\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})}{r\hat{p}+\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})+r(1-\hat{p})+(1-\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{r\hat{p}+\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})}{r+1}.

In the main text, we employ
r=1
.

The lower bound estimation by MAP (
8
) can have an estimation bias caused by the approximation
\hat{p}
, though it is almost negligible when
K
is a typical setting (such as
K=10
).
This bias occurs because we only have one
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
for each corresponding

\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}
.
Therefore, in each
\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}
, the effect of the prior remains even when
K
is large.
We can easily avoid this bias by setting
r
so that it decreases
r\rightarrow 0
when
K
increases, by which the estimator (
8
) converges to the usual MC estimator (
6
).
In Appendix
D.3
, we show that, in practice, the MAP-based approach has an advantage for small
K
setting.

D.2
Analyzing Variance

We re-write the estimator of the lower bound (
8
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}).

where

a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})=\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)-\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)
,

b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})=\log\left(\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})}\right)
,
and

\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\in[0,1]

is
\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})
when (
8
)
and is

\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})

when
(
6
).
By using independence of the MC samples and law of total variance, the variance of this estimator is decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\frac{1}{K}\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\biggl\{\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})^{2}\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\right]\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\right]+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]\biggr\}

(10)

In the first term of (
10
), only

\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\right]

changes depending on
\xi
.
From

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\right]=\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\right]/4,

we see that MAP makes the variance of the first term
1/4
.
The second term in (
10
) does not change depending on
\xi
.
In the case of the MAP estimate, the third term in (
10
) is

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})\right]+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[\frac{\hat{p}+\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})}{2}\right]+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\frac{\hat{p}+p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})}{2}+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right].

If

\hat{p}\approx p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})
,
this should be similar to

\mathbb{V}_{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}}\left[a(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})+b(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S})\right]
,
which is the variance in the case of

\xi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}})=\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
.
Therefore, under the assumption of

\hat{p}\approx p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*})

the variance reduction is expected because of the variance reduction in the first term of (
10
).

Appendix E
Parallel Querying

We here consider parallel querying in which
Q
queries should be selected every iteration.
Let

{\cal X}_{q}\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(q)}\}

and

{\cal H}_{q}=\{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}^{(q)}}\}

for
q\leq Q
.
Then,

\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{Q};{\cal F}^{*})

can be a selection criterion for determining
Q
points simultaneously.
However, this leads to
(Q\times d)
-dimensional optimization.
Instead, we employ a greedy strategy shown by
(
Takeno et al.,, 2022
)
, in which the MI approximation can be reduced to a similar computation to the case of single querying.

Assume that we have already select
q<Q
points
{\cal X}_{q}
, for which observations are not obtained yet, and consider determining the
(q+1)
-th point
\boldsymbol{x}
.
In this case,

\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{q}\cup\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})

should be maximized with respect to the additional
\boldsymbol{x}
.
We see

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{q}\cup\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})=\mathrm{MI}({\cal H}_{q};{\cal F}^{*})+\mathrm{CMI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q}),

where

\mathrm{CMI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{q}}\left[\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})\right]

is the MI conditioned on
{\cal H}_{q}
.
Since the first term does not depend on
\boldsymbol{x}
, we only need to consider the second term

\mathrm{CMI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})
.

The lower bound of

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})

is derived in the same way as the lower bound of

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
, i.e., (
4
).
The only difference is that the GPs have additional training data consisting of
{\cal X}_{q}
and
{\cal H}_{q}
.
Let

\zeta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

and

\eta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

be

\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

and

\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

in which the GPs with additional
q
observations are used to calculate
Z_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
Z_{O}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}(\boldsymbol{x})
as follows:

\displaystyle\zeta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{q})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{q})},

\displaystyle\eta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{q})},

where

Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{q})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal H}_{q})

and

Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{q})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal H}_{q})
.
Then, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{q}}\left[\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*}\mid{\cal H}_{q})\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{q}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal H}_{q}}\biggl[\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}},{\cal H}_{q}}\biggl[\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda,q}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}\biggr]

(11)

Since the expectation

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal H}_{q}}\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal H}_{q}}

can be seen as the joint expectation

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}},{\cal H}_{q}}
,
the MC approximation can be performed by using samples from the joint distribution of
{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\cal H}_{q}
.
Let
F_{q}
be a set of samples

(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\cal H}}_{q})

from the joint distribution.
Then, the MC approximation of the lower bound (
11
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\cal H}}_{q})\in F_{q}}\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda,q}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda,q}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}

The sampling of
F_{q}
can be performed by almost the same procedure as the single querying.
First, we generate the “entire function
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
” by using RFM.

\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}
is obtained by applying NSGA-II to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
.

\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
and

(q+2)
-th point selection, we can reuse

Appendix F
Decoupled Setting

For the decoupled setting, MI and its lower bound are

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\parallel q(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right]\eqqcolon L_{\rm Dec}(\boldsymbol{x},l,\lambda).

We define

q(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

as the marginal distribution of

q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})
:

\displaystyle q(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int\lambda q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})+(1-\lambda)q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\int p(\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\int p(\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\bigr\}

\displaystyle=p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr\},

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{-l}
is the
(L-1)
-dimensional subvector of
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
in which
f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is removed.
Then, the lower bound and its MC approximation is obtained as

\displaystyle L_{\rm Dec}(\boldsymbol{x},l,\lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\biggl[\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr\}\biggr]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr\}.

The probabilities

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

and

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

can be analytically calculated.
Let

{\cal C}_{i}=(\ell_{i}^{1},u_{i}^{1}]\times(\ell_{i}^{2},u_{i}^{2}]\times\cdots\times(\ell_{i}^{L},u_{i}^{L}]

be the cell partitions of the over-truncated region
{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
.
Then, from the independence of
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, we see

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{I}(f_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}\in(\ell^{l}_{c},u^{l}_{c}])\int_{{\cal C}_{c}^{-l}}p(\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}^{-l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{I}(f_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}\in(\ell^{l}_{c},u^{l}_{c}])\prod_{l^{\prime}\neq l}\left(\Phi(\bar{\alpha}_{c,l^{\prime}})-\Phi(\underline{\alpha}_{c,l^{\prime}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb{I}(f_{\boldsymbol{x}}^{l}\in(\ell^{l}_{c},u^{l}_{c}])\prod_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Z_{cl^{\prime}},

(13)

where

Z_{cl^{\prime}}=\Phi(\bar{\alpha}_{c,l^{\prime}})-\Phi(\underline{\alpha}_{c,l^{\prime}})

and

{\cal C}_{c}^{-l}
is the
(L-1)
-dimensional hyper-rectangle created by removing the
l
-th dimension of
{\cal C}_{c}
.
For

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
,
we use the following relation:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=1-p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})-p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Since

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}
in the last term
can be written as “dominated region”, shown in the end of Appendix
B
.
We can calculate

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\notin{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{f}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

by the same dominated region decomposition as (
13
).

Appendix G
Joint Entropy Extension

Let

{\cal X}^{*}=\{\boldsymbol{x}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal F}^{*}\}
.
The lower bound is

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right].

In practice, we only obtain a subset
{\cal X}_{S}^{*}\subseteq{\cal X}^{*}
, by which

{\cal F}_{S}^{*}=\{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\}_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}_{S}^{*}}

and

{\cal H}_{S}=\{(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\}_{\boldsymbol{x}\in{\cal X}_{S}^{*}}

are defined.
In the variational distribution

q(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})
,

{\cal H}_{S}
can be seen as an additional training data of the GPs
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
Then, a natural extension of the variational distribution (
3
) is

\displaystyle q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal X}^{*}_{S},{\cal F}^{*}_{S})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}\right)p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal H}_{S})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal H}_{S})&\text{ if }\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}},\\
0&\text{ otherwise, }\end{cases}

where

Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal H}_{S})

and

Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})\coloneqq p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal H}_{S})
.
Note that here the conditioning on
{\cal H}_{S}
is interpreted as a simple addition of the training data, and does not impose the conditions that “
{\cal H}_{S}
is the optimal solutions”, which is represented by the truncation (Because of this reason, we do not give ‘
*
’ to
{\cal H}_{S}
).
The resulting lower bound is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\biggl[\log\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}\biggr],

(14)

where

\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}+\frac{1-\lambda}{Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}

and

\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})=\frac{\lambda}{Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})}
.
The same MC approximation as (
6
) can be used to evaluate (
14
).

The MAP-based approximation is also possible based on transformation of (
14
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*}}\biggl[p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})\log\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})\log\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})\biggr].

Based on the same idea shown in Section
3.3
, we approximate

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid{\cal X}^{*},{\cal F}^{*})\approx Z_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})/Z_{U}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x}\mid{\cal H}_{S})
,
from which the MAP estimator can be defined.

Appendix H
Noisy Observation

Let

y^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\varepsilon

and

\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=(y^{1}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\ldots,y^{L}_{\boldsymbol{x}})^{\top}
,
where

\varepsilon\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2})
.
The mutual information for noisy observation is

\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})
,
for which the lower bound can be derived as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MI}(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}};{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\parallel q(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\frac{q(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})}{p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right].

Since
{\cal F}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
are independent if
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
is given, we define

q(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

by using

q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

as follows.

\displaystyle q(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})q_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})(\lambda q_{U}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1-\lambda)q_{O}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid{\cal F}^{*}_{S}))\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}})\bigr\}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}}p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\biggl\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\int_{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})\int_{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\biggr\}

\displaystyle=p(\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\left\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\}.

Then, the MC approximation becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\left[\log\left\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.+\eta_{\lambda}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{{\cal F}^{*}_{S}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{KK_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{(\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}})\in F}\sum_{\tilde{\varepsilon}\in E}\log\left\{\zeta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\eta_{\lambda}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}(\boldsymbol{x})p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\right\}.

where
E
is a set of
L
-dimension noise samples
\tilde{\varepsilon}
from
{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\rm noise}I)
,

\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}+\tilde{\varepsilon}

is a sample of
\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}
, and
K_{\varepsilon}=|E|
.
Note that, in this approximation, we transform

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{y}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}

to

\mathbb{E}_{{\cal F}^{*},\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
, which is possible because of the independence of the noise term.
To evaluate this MC approximation, we need the conditional distribution

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
, written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim{\cal N}(\boldsymbol{\nu}(\boldsymbol{x}),\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{s}(\boldsymbol{x}))),

where

\displaystyle\nu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})+\frac{\sigma_{l}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sigma_{l}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}}(\tilde{y}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}-\mu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})),

\displaystyle s_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sigma_{l}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{\sigma_{l}^{4}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\sigma_{l}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2}}.

Therefore,

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

can be calculated by using the same procedure as

Z_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
shown in (
9
):

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\int_{C_{i}}p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\int_{\ell_{i}^{l}}^{u_{i}^{l}}p(f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\tilde{y}^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{d}f^{l}_{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{C}\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\Phi(\bar{\beta}_{i,l})-\Phi(\underline{\beta}_{i,l})\right)

where

\underline{\beta}_{i,l}=(\ell_{i}^{l}-\nu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))/s_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})

and

\bar{\beta}_{i,l}=(u_{i}^{l}-\nu_{l}(\boldsymbol{x}))/s_{l}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
For

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
,
we can use a relation

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U\setminus O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})-p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
The last term

p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{O}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}\mid\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}_{\boldsymbol{x}})

can be calculated by the same approach as

Z_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}}=p(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\cal A}_{U}^{\tilde{{\cal F}}^{*}_{S}})
, described in the end of Appendix
B
.

Figure
14
shows the empirical evaluation for which the setting is same as in Section
6.1
.
Here, we used the
(d,L)=(3,3)
GP derived functions for three length scales.
We added the independent noise
{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{\rm noise}^{2})
with
\sigma_{\rm noise}=0.1
to all the observations.
The GPs have the same value of the noise parameter
\sigma_{\rm noise}=0.1
.

Appendix I
Additional Discussion on Related Work

Although our main focus is on information-theoretic approaches, here, other criteria are also reviewed.
A classical approach is the scalarization that transforms multiple objective functions into a scalar value, among which ParEGO
(
Knowles,, 2006
)
is a well-known method based on a random scalarization.
However, the information of the Pareto-frontier may be lost by the scalarization.
The standard expected improvement (EI) has been extended based on measuring the improvement of the hyper-volume called EHVI (expected hyper-volume improvement)
(
Emmerich,, 2005
;
Shah and Ghahramani,, 2016
)
.
The GP upper confidence bound (UCB) is another well-known general approach to BO
(
Srinivas et al.,, 2010
)
.
UCB based MOBO methods have been studied
(
Ponweiser et al.,, 2008
;
Zuluaga et al.,, 2013
;
Zuluaga et al.,, 2016
)
, but the setting of the confidence interval sometimes becomes practically difficult.
SUR
(
Picheny,, 2015
)
is based on the reduction of PI after the querying, which is computationally quite expensive.
A hypervolume-based multi-objective extension of knowledge gradient (KG) is considered by
(
Daulton et al.,, 2023
)
.
Naïve computations of the hypervolume KG are computationally intractable, and several approximation and acceleration strategies have been studied.
For example, so-called one-shot strategy transforms the nested optimization into simultaneous optimization which makes computation much faster, but the dimension of the acquisition function optimization becomes high.

We focus on the information-theoretic approach, which was first proposed for single-objective BO
(
Hennig and Schuler,, 2012
;
Hernández-Lobato et al.,, 2014
;
Wang and Jegelka,, 2017
)
.
Recently,
(
Cheng and Becker,, 2024
)
proposed a different variational lower bound approach to single-objective BO, which is only for single-objective problems.
A seminal work in the information-theoretic approach to MOBO is the Predictive Entropy Search for Multi-Objective Optimization (PESMO)
(
Hernandez-Lobato et al.,, 2016
)
.
PESMO defines an acquisition function through the entropy of the set of Pareto-optimal solutions
\boldsymbol{x}
, which is based on complicated approximation by expectation propagation (EP)
(
Minka,, 2001
)
.
On the other hand,
Belakaria et al., (2019)
proposed using the individual max-value entropy of each objective function, called max-value entropy search for multi-objective optimization (MESMO).
This largely simplifies the computations, but obviously, information of the Pareto-frontier is lost.
Another JES based approach has been recently proposed
(
Fernández-Sánchez and Hernández-Lobato,, 2024
)
, which is in a more general formulation including multi-fidelity and constrained problems.
Their computations are based on the Gaussian based entropy approximation, whose validity remains unclear.

Appendix L
Difference of Over- and Under-Truncation

Here, we discuss approximation error caused by replacing the dominated region by the entire Pareto-frontier
{\cal A}^{{\cal F}^{*}}
with its counter-part created by
{\cal F}_{S}^{*}
, i.e.,
{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
or
{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
.
The difference actually can be small if
S
is sufficiently large, but practically intractable size of
S
is required even for reasonable size of
L
.
To demonstrate this claim, we evaluate differences of hyper-volumes of
{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
and
{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
.
If the difference of
{\cal A}_{O}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
and
{\cal A}_{U}^{{\cal F}_{S}^{*}}
are small, it suggests that the true Pareto-frontier
{\cal F}^{*}
can be accurately approximated by
{\cal F}_{S}^{*}
.

In Fig.
28
, we empirically investigate the ratio of the volume of under- and over- truncations compared with the true volume.
We assume that the Pareto-frontier is the simplex (
\sum_{i\in[L]}f^{i}=1
and
f^{i}\geq 0
), and approximation points
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
are sampled uniformly in the simplex as shown in Fig.
28
(a).
The examples of the volume ratio is shown in Fig.
28
(b).
In Fig.
28
(c), we see that, when
L=2
, the difference between the truncations rapidly decreases, but for
L\geq 3
shown in Fig.
28
(d) and (e), large differences remain even when the population size is 1,000 (Note that in this example, over-truncation is closer to the true value than under-truncation, but in general, it depends on the shape of the true Pareto frontier which is unknown).
The continuous Pareto-frontier is the
(L-1)
-dimensional space, and we conjecture that the required number of points to keep the sufficient density of approximation points
{\cal F}_{S}^{*}
increases exponentially when
L
increases.

About the difference between
{\cal F}^{*}
and
{\cal F}^{*}_{S}
, increasing the number of MC samples cannot mitigate the essential problem in principle.
In our formulation, the over-truncation corresponds to
\lambda=0
. Therefore, the over-truncation based lower bound is biased toward a smaller value, because the bound is defined as the maximizer with respect to
\lambda
.
This discussion holds even in the population

\mathrm{LB}(\boldsymbol{x},\lambda)

(i.e., before introducing the sample approximation), and therefore, increasing the number of samples from the GPs does not solve this intrinsic bias.
