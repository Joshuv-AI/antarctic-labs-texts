From legislation to actual health service: evaluation of health provisions in the disability law of Ghana by adolescents with mobility and visual impairments and their families
Authors: Ebenezer Mensah Gyimah, Ebenezer Dassah, Maxwell Peprah Opoku, William Nketsia, Afua Ntoaduro, Clement Osei Tutu, Cecilia Opoku, Zakia Issaka, Philip Atta Mensah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715) making provisions for access to healthcare services for adolescents with disabilities and their families, a corpus of literature has reported that persons with disabilities continue to face challenges in accessing healthcare services. However, the voices of adolescents with disabilities and their families are very scarce in such discourse. This study explored the experiences of adolescents with disabilities and their families in accessing healthcare services as per the provisions described in Ghana’s Act 715.MethodsThis study involved 45 participants, including 25 adolescents with disabilities and 20 family members from a municipality in Ghana. Employing a qualitative descriptive design, semi-structured interviews were conducted which was then analyzed thematically and interpreted using Critical Disability Theory.ResultsThe study identified two major categories of barriers to healthcare access: environmental (socio-economic difficulties, poor built environment, unavailability of rehabilitation services) and systemic (cultural beliefs, poor support at healthcare facilities and inadequate healthcare legislative provisions for families of children with disabilities). Despite legal provisions for free healthcare, participants faced significant financial barriers, with specialist services often not covered by the National Health Insurance Scheme.ConclusionsThe study calls for policy adjustments to fully cover specialist care under Ghana’s National Health Insurance Scheme, establishment of local-level health assessment and resource centers, educational campaigns to change cultural perceptions, and training of healthcare workers to promote quality access to healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11611-x.Keywords: Adolescents with disabilities, Healthcare access, Legal provisions, Family, Barriers

Background

Persons with disabilities constitute 15% of the global population, with the majority residing in low-income contexts, such as Sub-Saharan Africa [1]. At the turn of the 21st century, many countries developed laws to promote the rights of persons with disabilities [2]. For instance, systemic barriers, subjugations and poverty informed global actions toward developing laws and policies to advance the lives of persons with disabilities [3]. However, the climax of global effort towards improving the living conditions of persons with disabilities was the enactment of United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with disabilities (CRPD) and its optional protocol which offer useful guidelines to member States [4]. The CRPD [5] is a foundation on which most countries are expected to build national disability laws and policies. Indeed, countries such as Ghana, ratified the convention and prior to that developed domestic laws to promote participation and equal access to essential services in the society [6]. Unfortunately, persons with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities) continue to suffer discrimination and unequal access to basic services such as healthcare [7, 8]. As part of advocacy towards mainstreaming and encouraging policy reforms, it is essential for persons with disabilities and their families to be engaged on specific issues such as access to healthcare to afford them the opportunity to respond to provisions made in legal instruments. The study reported here provided a rare opportunity to adolescents with disabilities and families to share their perspectives on access to health provisions made in the existing disability law of Ghana.

The Ghanaian Parliament passed Ghana’s Persons with Disability law (Act 715) in 2006, which outlines the rights of persons with disabilities and their families. It is widely known that the Ghana Disability Law is influenced by the social model approach to disability management [6]. Moreover, the enactment of Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715) was a result of collective advocacy efforts by Disabled Peoples Organizations, human rights activists and Civil Society Organizations. The law provides rights such as unrestricted access to public places, free medical care, economic empowerment, education and transportation among others, for persons with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities). The law also establishes the National Council on Persons with Disability and includes administrative and financial provisions to support implementation of same [6].

However, among the key challenges with implementing Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715), is the lack of political will by successive governments to approve a Legislative Instrument that would operationalize the law [9–11]. Insufficient funding and resource constraints have further hindered the implementation of the Act [12]. The National Commission on Civic Education (NCCE) and the Commission on Human Rights and Administrative Justice (CHRAJ), responsible for promoting the rights of all individuals regardless of disability, have remained silent on these issues, raising questions about their dedication to disability rights. The ineffectiveness of Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715) to enhance access to healthcare services for this vulnerable social unit (i.e., adolescents with disabilities and their families) contradicts the common notion that “civilized societies do not fail the vulnerable” [13]. Consequently, it is incumbent to understand the healthcare access experiences of adolescents with disabilities and their families as described in the Act 715, in order to push advocacy and enhance removal of barriers.

Access to health is a fundamental right [14], grounded in several international human rights instruments (i.e., Principles of the Constitution of the World Health Organization and Sect. 25 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights). Right to health access is becoming increasingly important due to the intricate relationship between healthy population and economic productivity [15]. Consequently, there is advocacy that persons regardless of their disability/impairment, gender, sex, race, beliefs and wealth, among other characteristics are expected to be given conducive platform to enjoy their rights to health [16]. In particular, right to health is essential to individuals with disabilities who usually live with other health or comorbid conditions [17]. Healthcare services for adolescents with disabilities encompass specialized care tailored to suit their unique needs [18] as well as related health conditions. Thus, guaranteeing equal access to healthcare to adolescents with disabilities is crucial towards addressing not only their general health problems but specific health needs associated with their disability which might compound their marginalization in society [19]. Therefore, stakeholders including families and health policymakers are expected to adopt a more inclusive and comprehensive approach that promotes the participation of adolescents with disabilities in healthcare.

Families play a crucial role in the lives of adolescents with disabilities [20]. In Ghana, family systems consist of blood relatives and may also include adopted members [21]. Family-centered approaches to the delivery and access to social support services, such as healthcare, have been found to significantly enhance the wellbeing of vulnerable social units. Per this approach, there is active involvement of families and there exist capacity-building opportunities [22–24]. Considering the caregiving duties played by families especially in Sub-Saharan Africa where formal structures are non-existent, policies supporting the emotional and physical health of families should be advocated [25]. For instance, families undertake the daily self-care activities of adolescents with disabilities, accompany them to schools, hospitals as well as ensure their safety and well-being [26, 27]. Families also play a crucial role in advocating for and safeguarding the rights of CWDs, especially for younger adolescents who are reliant on their families for support [28]. In order to improve access to healthcare for adolescents with disabilities, services should be family-centered and friendly, from their design to implementation [29]. Further, the United Nations Convention on the Rights of a Child acknowledges the duty of families as guardians and aims to maintain the balance between family rights and the rights of children [28]. However, families of persons with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities) often experience limited access to social support services, such as appropriate healthcare services [30]. Therefore, the perspectives of families are very crucial in developing systems that seek to enhance access to inclusive healthcare services for adolescents with disabilities.

Effective healthcare access for adolescents with disabilities is a step towards creating more inclusive societies [31]. This implies that effective healthcare access for adolescents with disabilities is pivotal in fostering inclusive societies. This approach acknowledges the unique challenges adolescents with disabilities face and aims to address systemic barriers in healthcare provision. By ensuring adolescents with disabilities receive appropriate, timely, and respectful care, societies must demonstrate a commitment to equality and human dignity [32]. Moreover, inclusive healthcare systems tend to promote wider societal awareness and sensitivity towards the needs and rights of persons with disabilities, thereby cultivating an environment of acceptance and support [33]. However, achieving this requires not only infrastructural adjustments and policy reforms but also a shift in societal attitudes to recognize and value diversity [34]. While progress in this area can significantly contribute to the well-being of individuals with disabilities, it also serves as a testament to the maturity and humaneness of society at large.

Previous studies on healthcare access for persons with disabilities conducted in Ghana and globally have focused on adult populations of persons with disabilities [35–39]. Most of these studies have also focused on the perspectives of practitioners and health professionals, such as doctors, nurses and social workers [40, 41], in understanding the healthcare access experiences of right holders’ (adolescents with disabilities and their families). Little research has been shown in understanding the healthcare access experiences for adolescents with disabilities and their families. Moreover, there appears to be limited motivation to evaluate the policy-practice gap, particularly concerning access to healthcare provisions for these right holders, described in Ghana’s Act 715. Soliciting the voices of adolescents with disabilities and their families (right holders) in understanding their healthcare access experiences seeks to bridge the inclusion, knowledge, and contextual gaps established in the literature.

The overarching goal of this study was to explore the experiences of adolescents with disabilities and their families in accessing healthcare services, as described in Ghana’s Act 715. Successive governments have addressed these access barriers with the enactment of relevant policies, including the National Health Insurance Scheme and the Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty programme. Although these social policies and initiatives exist, there are peculiar challenges in their implementation and operation, hence adolescents with disabilities and their families still experience limited access to healthcare services. For instance, while most Ghanaians (over 60%) are enrolled in the National Health Insurance Scheme [42], there is a problem with the utilization of the National Health Insurance Scheme among the general population [43]. Understanding the nuanced access experiences of adolescents with disabilities and their families who are also enrolled in the National Health Insurance Scheme would be helpful to both policymakers and implementers.

Access to health for persons with disabilities in Ghana

The gains associated with healthcare access underscore the need for health policymakers and providers in countries such as Ghana to promote its accessibility to adolescents with disabilities and their families. The healthcare system in Ghana is comprehensive and complex, encompassing faith-based healing services, private not-for-profit, private for-profit, and public [44]. The Ministry of Health performs oversight responsibilities to all healthcare services across the country that serve the population, whilst the Ghana Health Services is an implementing agency, managing healthcare services provision and utilization at the District and Sub-District levels [45]. In collaboration with other relevant ministries, the Ministry of Health takes charge of coordinating health policy implementation, whilst the Ghana Health Services manages the day-to-day delivery of healthcare services to the population at the various communities within the Districts and Sub-Districts. In Ghana, healthcare services are practically provided at three principal levels within Districts and Sub-Districts: primary, secondary, and tertiary [40]. District hospitals, polyclinics, health centers, clinics, and community-based health planning and services compounds, constitute the primary healthcare level. The regional hospitals are the secondary care whilst the teaching hospitals associated with universities, that handle referrals for more advanced and specialized treatments constitute the tertiary care levels [46]. These levels have been developed to facilitate accessible healthcare to all persons including adolescents with disabilities and families at the various communities within Districts and Sub-Districts levels.

In this study, disability was operationally defined as cognitive, physical or sensory impairment which interacts with the day-to-day living experiences of individuals [1]. According to the 2021 population housing census, 8% out of the estimated 30 million people in Ghana are living with a form of disabilities [47]. However, in Ghana and most developing countries, there is an intersection between disability and local culture [48]. Disability is often viewed as a generation curse, sin committed by a family and also linked to supernatural forces such as witchcraft or divine retribution [49, 50]. Adolescents with disabilities, including their families, especially those with conditions like visual, speech, and hearing impairments, are subjected to stigmatization and social isolation [51]. These individuals and their families are frequently marginalized as well as ostracized from traditions/activities like marriage and are largely ignored or overlooked in selections for leadership roles [52]. More so, the onset of a disability in a family become a label for the child and their families [53]. Consequently, adolescents with disabilities are not given the needed space to develop in society.

The stereotype associated with disability derailed their participation in societal activities and national policies and productive activities. For instance, over the years, literature has reported the barriers faced by adolescents with disabilities in their effort to access healthcare [35, 54]. Environmental barriers constitute the external factors that adolescents with disabilities have little control. For instance, the distance from a location, lack of transportation or dangerous terrain influences accessibility for healthcare services for persons with disabilities. In particular, adolescents with disabilities living in remote or rural areas may have limited access to healthcare services due to distance, poor built-environment and lack of transportation to healthcare facilities [35, 55]. The financial burden associated with disabilities, particularly, childhood (including adolescents) disabilities can also lead to significant financial hardship for families [56, 57]. More so, there is lack of proper diagnosis and treatment for impairments [58]. Other studies have reported that healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitudes and beliefs towards adolescents with disabilities and their families can be discriminatory and thus, serve as a barrier to healthcare [35, 54]. However, available literature on health access has mainly focus on adults with disabilities and thus, raising fundamental questions about health access to children and their families.

Article 2 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child consistently regard children (including adolescents) as right bearers [59]. Consequently, adolescents could comment on important public goods, such as access to healthcare. In this study, adolescents were operationalized as individuals who are below the age of 18 years who are dependent on adults in the society. While previous studies are yet to capture the voices of adolescents when it comes to access to health, the onset of disability have adverse impact on families [53]. Indeed, the global wave of development of policies swept through Ghana with the formulation of the Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715), to safeguard the rights of adolescents with disabilities and their families. However, the violations and denial of rights to health of adolescents with disabilities continue in the face of these legal frameworks. This study extends previous literature by giving adolescents with disabilities and their families the opportunity to comment on health provisions guaranteed in Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act.

Critical disability theory

This study employs a Critical Disability Theory as to achieve the objectives. According to Critical Disability Theory, disability phenomenon is a social construct stemming from a complex interaction among various factors such as impairments and society [60–62]. This means that social participation is significantly influenced by the interaction between adolescents with disabilities and their social surroundings. It is noteworthy to mention that critical disability theorists believe that Persons with Disabilities, rather than society, rarely control the social disadvantages they experience. Critical disability theorists maintain that the limitations to societal participation result from society’s failure to cater to the unique and nuanced needs of adolescents with disabilities [62–64].

This study adopts four principal components of Critical Disability Theory: advocating for individual rights, echoing the voices of the marginalized, appreciating diversity, and fostering independence [65, 66]. To begin with, the first principal component focuses on upholding the rights of adolescents with disabilities to access healthcare services. Ethically, it’s imperative to ensure that healthcare systems are equitable and just for all members of society, inclusive of persons with disabilities, particularly adolescents with disabilities [67]. This suggests that adolescents with disabilities, like all other human(s) are entitled to a level of living that supports their health and overall well-being. Highlighting the need for policies aimed at enhancing availability, acceptability, affordability of healthcare services for this social unit [35]. It is more important for policy makers to create an accessible environment because adolescents with disabilities are frequently subject to rights violations, particularly regarding their access to healthcare services and dignity [68]. It is, therefore, incumbent on states to ensure that normative and positive actions aimed at reforming existing healthcare delivery systems are put in place to enhance inclusivity and comprehensive access to healthcare services for persons with disabilities, particularly adolescents with disabilities [69].

The second tenet is understanding the voices of the marginalized. Critical disability theorists believe in the importance of giving marginalized populations a platform to express their views on matters affecting their lives [62, 64]. Indeed, both adolescents with disabilities and their families are directly affected by the condition of limited healthcare access [70]. Consequently, these right holders are in a better position to detail their experiences which has the potential to impact policy reforms and practices. Meaning, advocacy efforts by these policy makers are essential in mainstreaming disability considerations in broader health policies, to enhance inclusivity and responsiveness to the needs of all persons with disabilities. In view of this, adolescents with disabilities and their families are both better placed to share their perspectives on health provisions in the Ghanaian national disability law.

The third principal component of Critical Disability Theory is the recognition of diversity. Disability is an intricate and multidisciplinary phenomenon [71]. However, in Ghana and most African countries, there is a skewed perspective on disability emanating from the traditional lens, with its origins linked to superstition (witchcraft and ancestral curses) [72, 73]. Such beliefs contribute to the marginalization and exclusion of Persons with Disabilities. Consequent to this cultural understanding of disability, adolescents with disabilities encounter barriers in accessing healthcare services, for instance, negative attitudes from healthcare professionals [35]. Meanwhile, global advocacy efforts aim to eliminate discrimination and promote acceptance of adolescents with disabilities [74]. In view of this, proponents of Critical Disability Theory call for the recognition of diversity in understanding matters on disability. By adopting the tenets of the Critical Disability Theory in the context of the current study, it is expected that adolescents with disabilities have access to holistic healthcare services without any disparities [75]. It is essential for Persons with disabilities, particularly adolescents with disabilities, to have access to comprehensive healthcare due to the diverse nature of impairments and the unique challenges experienced in accessing healthcare [76], which can lead to poorer health outcomes compared to the general population [77].

The fourth principal component of Critical Disability Theory is independence. Enhancing independence in access to healthcare services for adolescents with disabilities and their families is intricately linked to catering for dignity, autonomy and, privacy and confidentiality [78]. It is evident that most adolescents with disabilities and their families who access healthcare services experience poor dignity, limited autonomy and unsatisfactory privacy and confidentiality [79]. The limitation to the enjoyment of “independence” by this social unit is due to the intricate interaction between adolescents with disabilities’ condition(s) and the absence of relevant accommodations [80]. The limited accommodations posed by barriers such as poor infrastructure and unskilled human resources impedes access to healthcare services for this social unit. For instance, the built environment in most societies are unfriendly towards adolescents with disabilities and their families [81]. Public places such as the physical edifice of hospitals are built with little consideration given to the uniqueness of adolescents with disabilities [82]. Also, most healthcare professionals, especially in Ghana and within the African contexts lack the skills to communicate in sign language to benefit deaf clients [36]. In most healthcare facilities within these settings, efforts made at establishing communication results in reduced autonomy and the lack of privacy and confidentiality in healthcare decision making [83].

Method

Study design

The qualitative descriptive design was employed to understand the experiences of participants [84]. This design offered the opportunity for the researchers to examine and describe the where, what, who, and how of an event or experience [84, 85]. The qualitative descriptive design was deemed the most appropriate for this study involving adolescents with mobility and visual imapirments and their families because we sought to reveal information on their experiences in accessing healthcare services, very relevant and practicable data for utilization by policymakers and practitioners [84].

Data was gathered using an interview guide (i.e., Appendix A) designed after a thorough literature review [35–40, 48, 54] between March and April 2023. Also, a comprehensive review of appropriate sections of Ghana’s Act 715 was conducted, taking cognizance of participants’ responses, to better understand the key themes identified and to establish meaningful connections.

Ethics

The study obtained ethical approval from the Committee for Human Research, Publication, and Ethics (CHRPE) at Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (CHRPE/AP/155/23), reflecting adherence to ethical standards. With this ethical clearance, the office of the Ghana Federation of Disability Organizations and the Department of Social Welfare at the Municipality were subsequently contacted to seek their permission to locate potential participants across the seven zonal councils within the jurisdiction. Informed consent was gathered from each participant via their signatures or thumbprints, detailing their right to participate and withdraw at any point during data collection and the inclusion of their data in subsequent publications. To appreciate participants for their time spent in the interview, they were compensated with airtime worth Twenty Ghana Cedis (20GH).

Research settings

Ghana is a West African state with a population of around 30.8 million as of September 2021, constituting 0.4% of the global human population [86]. The World Bank in 2021 reported that Ghana’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) was approximately $77.59 billion, with a growth rate of 5.1%. It is worth noting that in Ghana the rate of people living under the international poverty line (set at $1.90) dropped to 11.3% from 47.4% in 2011, signifying a remarkable progress towards poverty reduction [87]. Notwithstanding this progress, around 3.4 million people living primarily in rural areas, continue to live in extreme poverty, with child (including adolescents) poverty rates also increasing [86]. Particularly affected are persons with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities) and their families, who face ongoing poverty-related challenges [88].

In terms of disability statistics, Ghana reported that 3% of its population were persons with disabilities in 2010, with approximately 50,904 children (6.9% of the persons with disabilities) being identified as children with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities) [89]. This percentage is relatively modest compared to the World Health Organization’s 2022 estimate of over a billion individuals living with disabilities.

According to the 2019 Annual Health Report from the Mampong Municipal Health Directorate, the Municipality operated twenty-four (24) health facilities, encompassing one (1) Municipal Hospital, 7 private hospitals, 7 Health Centres, one Christian Health Association of Ghana facility, two quasi-government facilities, and six Community-Based Health Planning and Services compounds [90]. The healthcare workforce consists of 473 staff, including eight doctors, fifty-seven midwives, fifty community health nurses, seventy-four other nurses, and one public health nurse, among others. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census, the municipality is home to nearly 116,632 people, amongst whom 743 are registered persons with disabilities and 154 are children with disabilities (including adolescents with disabilities). We selected this study area because of the first author’s familiarity with the socio-cultural context (i.e., language, cultural values, and practices), which is key to gathering rich data [91].

Sampling and recruitment of participants

Purposive sampling was adopted to recruit study participants. Purposive sampling was the most appropriate technique to help recruit participants who could contribute to the study [92]. The inclusion criteria for purposively sampling adolescents with disabilities consisted of: (a). persons between 13 and 18 years of age; (b). having either mobility or visual impairment(s) (the most predominant forms of disabilities/impairments in the study area, as per information gathered at the Ghana Federation of Disability Organizations); (c). ability to communicate in Akan and English languages (the widely spoken languages in the study area) and; (d). willingness to participate. Practicably, to establish access points for recruiting potential participants, detailed information concerning the study was communicated through the office of the Ghana Federation of Disability Organizations at the Municipality, to all persons with disabilities and their families residing within the jurisdiction. Participants who met the eligibility criteria were then identified and recruited by the first author. Additionally, families of adolescents with disabilities were purposively sampled based on the following inclusion criteria (a). being over 18 years old; (b). serving as caregivers to adolescents with disabilities who spent/are spending a minimum of 20 h per week with these children with disabilities [93]; (c). ability to communicate in Akan and English languages (the widely spoken languages in the study area) and; (d). willingness to participate. In order to achieve diverse perspectives, these families were sampled based on the kind of relationship they had with the adolescents with disabilities, the type of disability of the adolescents, age, educational qualification, and employment type. Subsequently, permission was obtained from the office of the Department of Social Welfare at the Municipality before, identification and recruitment of eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria by the first author. Overall, forty-five participants (consisting of 25 adolescents with disabilities and 20 families of adolescents with disabilities who were unrelated) with diverse characteristics (see Tables 1 and 2), were sampled and interviewed based on the principle of “information power” [94]. This “information power” principle informed the sample size of this study on the basis of the study’s objectives, the specificity of the sample, the application of established theories, the quality of dialogue, and the strategy employed in the analysis [94].

Data collection

With the participants’ consent, the first author conducted and recorded 45 in-depth interviews. Most (over 70%) of these interviews were conducted in the Akan language, with a few in the English language. The field data collection exercise occurred at participants’ and the first author’s convenience, within homes, health facilities and community centres/durbar grounds across the Municipality, over extended period of five consecutive weeks. The duration of interviews varied, ranging from 45 to 60 min. Interview questions were centered around the experiences of participants relative to accessing healthcare services described in Ghana’s Act 715. During the interviews prompts and probes were adopted to elicit clarifications. After each interview, the interviewer made detailed field notes reflecting his opinion of the interview and identifying emerging themes. These notes provided additional context to the results, allowing for a broader understanding beyond what was explicitly mentioned by the participants during the interviews.

Data management, analysis and trustworthiness

The interviews were transcribed verbatim and those conducted in the local language was subjected to back-to-back translation. Data trustworthiness was enhanced through member checking, reflexivity, and consensus on the findings [95, 96].

Data was subjected to content analysis as prescribed by Wirihana [97]. This method of analysis was selected to extract more precise and meaningful interpretations from the data [97]. The processes for the content analysis encompassed transcribing the audio-recorded data into texts and familiarizing with the texts. Subsequently, key statements were extracted from the texts to formulate meanings (themes). The identified themes were grouped into like terms through clustering of the themes. Exhaustive meanings were established from the clustered themes. A framework/basic structure for the themes was established to represent the results. The results were subsequently subjected to validation from participants. To maintain transparency and reduce bias, the processes of analysis were conducted as a collaborative exercise among the authors and a detailed audit trail was also kept [98].

After validation of the themes, they were compared with specific sections of Ghana’s Act 715 to ascertain their appropriateness or alignment or otherwise.

Results

The analysis revealed five key themes, including socio-economic difficulties, poor built environment, unavailability of rehabilitation services, cultural beliefs, and poor support at health facilities. These key themes were categorized under two domains: environmental (i.e., socio-economic difficulties, poor built environment and unavailability of rehabilitation services) and systemic barriers (i.e., cultural and poor support at healthcare facilities) to accessing healthcare services. Themes were subsequently compared with specific sections of Ghana’s Act 715 to determine their alignment or otherwise.

Environmental barriers

The environmental barriers faced by adolescents with disabilities and their families in accessing healthcare services included socio-economic difficulties, poor built environment and unavailability of rehabilitation services.

Socioeconomic challenges

Section 31 of Ghana’s disability law stipulates as follows:

The Ministry of Health in formulating health policies shall provide for free general and specialist medical care, rehabilitative operation treatment, and appropriate assistive devices for persons with total disability.

The Ministry of Health in formulating health policies shall provide for free general and specialist medical care, rehabilitative operation treatment, and appropriate assistive devices for persons with total disability.

Notwithstanding this provision, there was a consensus among participants (adolescents with disabilities and their families) on the financial barriers they encountered in accessing healthcare services. Participants (n = 43) emphasized that although adolescents with disabilities were enrolled in the National Health Insurance Scheme at no cost, specialist medical services such as surgeries, procurement of some medicines/drugs, and health assessment meant for disability classification and placement, had to be paid for, from caregivers’ coffers. Meanwhile, most families of adolescents with disabilities could not afford to pay these extra expenses, hence, impeding access to healthcare services for this social unit. Statements shared in support of these assertions were as follows:

We have been registered in [with] the National Health Insurance Scheme by the government; however, some services like operation [surgery] and physiotherapy are not covered by the scheme. Sadly, poor families like mine, who cannot afford to pay those significant costs, just have to forgo these life-changing procedures. That is the sad reality. (Edward, IN).

…. When his magnifying glasses need to be changed, it requires a significant amount of money, whether you have [are registered with the] insurance or not. This is an amount of money I don’t have. Unfortunately, such services are cash and carry. Because of poverty, my child’s condition is getting worse. (Addae, IN)

The authorities should do something about the situation because most poor families like us cannot afford the cost of providing certain medical treatments of our children with disabilities. Up till now I am still in debts after selling everything I had just to support pay[ing] part of one eye surgery. (Mawutor, IN)

Poor built environment

Section 6 of Ghana’s Act 715 mentions that:

The owner or occupier of a place to which the public has access shall provide appropriate facilities that make the place accessible to and available for use by a person with disability.

The owner or occupier of a place to which the public has access shall provide appropriate facilities that make the place accessible to and available for use by a person with disability.

Section 7 also denotes that:

A person who provides any service to the public shall put in place the necessary facilities that make the service available and accessible to a person with disability.

A person who provides any service to the public shall put in place the necessary facilities that make the service available and accessible to a person with disability.

Nonetheless, participants (n = 38) revealed that the built environment in most health facilities within the Municipality were seldomly accessible. Participants mentioned that they experienced difficulties navigating the built environment within healthcare facilities because of the poor state of sidewalks, lack of ramps, and inadequate curb cuts. Participants emphasized that most adolescents with disabilities and their families were accompanied by their “significant others,” to assist them in surmounting these architectural-access barriers at the facilities. Supportive statements shared by participants included:

There is a private health facility in town where we once sought healthcare. Unfortunately, the wards of this facility are located on a [multi-]storey building. My mother and I once embarrassed ourselves when we fell on the staircase because the stairs are very narrow and quite steep. We were both hurt after the incident, and hence, we vowed not to go there again… (Fiifi, IN).

Nobody cares about how a child using a wheelchair is able to move around the built environment of the government hospital in town. The healthcare workers don’t care how we get to the treatment area. Because there is nobody to assist us, I usually have to let my elder son accompany us so he can help lift the wheelchair and even push him around. I don’t have that energy and no nurse or doctor will support you. (Esi, IN)

Unavailability of rehabilitation services

Section 35 of Ghana’s disability law outlines that:

The Ministry of Health in collaboration with District Assemblies and the Ministry responsible for Social Welfare shall establish and operate health assessment and resource centres in each district and provide early diagnostic medical attention to mothers and infants to determine the existence or onset of disability.

The Ministry of Health in collaboration with District Assemblies and the Ministry responsible for Social Welfare shall establish and operate health assessment and resource centres in each district and provide early diagnostic medical attention to mothers and infants to determine the existence or onset of disability.

Meanwhile, it was evident in participants’ (n = 32) responses that, certain specialist healthcare services, particularly rehabilitative services, were unavailable for “children with disabilities” at the various localities. Participants mentioned that rehabilitative services capable of delivering tailored care that addressed their unique and individual healthcare needs were unavailable for patronage in the various communities. Consequently, participants highlighted the urgent need for rehabilitation specialists and centres in order to enhance access to this all-important healthcare service.

Participants’ comments indicative of these assertions were as follows:

I learned from my parents that there is no resource and assessment center for the entire municipality. It is pathetic that in a whole municipality, there is neither a disability assessment and resource center nor rehabilitative professionals for mothers and infants to detect and address impairments earlier. Perhaps, if there were such a center and professionals, my impairment could have been identified earlier and treated before things worsened, degenerating into my current condition. (Fynn, IN).

When my ward was to undergo a surgical procedure at about one and a half years old, we had to travel to Duayaw Nkwanta. They told us that the procedure could not be done here due to the absence of an expert orthopedic surgeon at our hospital. The costs I incurred for the services, transportation, and accommodation because of the unavailability of this service in our community were needless. (Owusu IN).

There are no vision therapists at the government hospital in the municipal capital, so how do you expect such cases to be detected early for treatment? By the time parents notice the condition, most children are already blind. (Abena IN)

It is clear from the illustrative quotations presented above that participant commonly experienced these environmental barriers which impedes access to healthcare services outlined in Ghana’s Disability Act 715.

Systemic barriers

The systemic barriers to accessing healthcare services faced by adolescents with disabilities and their families included cultural beliefs and poor support at healthcare facilities.

Cultural beliefs

Ghana’s Act 715 in Sect. 34 espouses that:

The Ministry of Health in collaboration with the Ministries responsible for Education and Social Welfare shall provide for the periodic screening of children in order to detect, prevent, and manage disability.

The Ministry of Health in collaboration with the Ministries responsible for Education and Social Welfare shall provide for the periodic screening of children in order to detect, prevent, and manage disability.

However, a substantial number of participants (n = 29) still held the traditional belief that their impairments were “caused by evil spiritual forces,” and they could not benefit from orthodox medical procedures of screening and managing the same. Participants further mentioned that families who had adolescents with disabilities were often ostracized by the society because of the perception that they had brought forth “demons/devils.” Comments passed in support of this assertion included:

…. My impairment is spiritually inflicted, so I don’t go to the hospital. I continue to seek religious healing even though I experience excruciating pain day in and day out. I believe I will be healed by God one day, not by orthodox medicine. (Felix, IN)

I know the causes of my child’s impairment are more than what meets the naked eye. There are evil forces fighting against him. I am sure a powerful spiritualist can turn this predicament around, not those treatments at the hospital. It is a waste of time. (Gyabeng IN)

People still believe that adolescents with disabilities are demons who come to hunt humans. Therefore, nobody, including most healthcare workers, wants anything to do with families like my own, where we have such children. Hence, we mostly resort to traditional medicine for the healthcare needs of adolescents with disabilities. (Ama, IN)

Poor support at Healthcare Facilities

Section 33 of Ghana’s Act 715 mentions that:

The Ministry of Health shall include education on disability and disability issues in health care programmes.

The Ministry of Health shall include education on disability and disability issues in health care programmes.

Nonetheless, participants (n = 40) unanimously mentioned that the attitude of some healthcare workers impeded access to healthcare services by adolescents with disabilities and their families. Participants recounted how indifferent attitudes and disrespect at heath facilities impeded access to healthcare services. Supportive claims shared by participants included:

Due to my poor vision, I can hardly maneuver the health facility independently. Unfortunately, the health workers look on unconcerned whenever I struggle to access specific locations for essential healthcare services, such as where we draw the facility cards, the OPD, the eye clinic, and even the washrooms. It is sad. Therefore, when my mother is not there to accompany me, I prefer to miss my appointment at the facility. (Sampson, IN)

I was humiliated by a health worker at the physiotherapy unit, and since then, I decided not to go to the hospital again. She was pressing her phone and talking to her colleague after giving my child some exercise schedules to perform. I realized my child was not breathing well about three minutes into the exercise, so I told her to come and look at the situation. She insulted me and drove me away from the gymnasium. That day was the last time I decided to go to the facility. After all, the exercises can be done here at home…(Gladys, IN).

Inadequate Healthcare Provisions for Families of Children with disabilities

Participants expressed their dissatisfaction about the failure of Ghana’s Disability Act, 715 (2006) to adequately acknowledge the unique healthcare needs of families of adolescents with disabilities, despite the Act’s provisions aiming to improve healthcare access for adolescents with disabilities.

Sadly, even though my child enjoys free healthcare from the National Health Insurance Scheme provided by the government, I cannot also benefit from it. I have to pay for my healthcare even though I don’t work. Therefore, I cannot go to the hospital when I am sick and don’t have money to pay. I have been told several times by the social workers at the council [Municipal Assembly] that I don’t qualify for the free National Health Insurance Scheme card meant for free healthcare services, which my child enjoys. I wonder who put together these laws and arrangements concerning the National Health Insurance Scheme registration for disabled children alone, excluding their mothers. We often get sick even more frequently than the children. Did they consult families with adolescents with disabilities before implementing such laws? (Nana, IN)

These illustrative quotations, emanating from interviews with adolescents with disabilities and their families, highlight the systemic barriers experienced by this social unit in accessing healthcare services.

Discussion

The objective of this qualitative study was to explore healthcare services access provisions for adolescents with disabilities and their families described in Ghana’s Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715). Generally, it was evident in participants’ responses that environmental (i.e., socio-economic difficulties, poorly built environment, and unavailability of rehabilitation services) and systemic barriers (i.e., cultural and poor support at healthcare facilities) impeded access to healthcare services for this social unit [99]. Therefore, under the lenses of the Critical Disability Theory framework, the current study seeks the transformation of existing societal structures to adequately address the unique and intricate healthcare access barriers experienced by adolescents with disabilities and their families [62–64].

In addressing the challenge of a poor built environment in healthcare facilities, it’s crucial to understand the implications of this barrier through the Critical Disability Theory framework. On one hand, the inaccessible built environment at health facilities directly contravenes the diversity principle of the Critical Disability Theory. This lack of accommodation for the varied physical needs of adolescents with disabilities not only prevents these right-bearers from accessing essential medical services but also contributes to exacerbating health conditions due to delayed care [35]. Such oversight in infrastructure planning reflects a systemic failure to recognize and meet the diverse needs of these adolescents, reinforcing societal inequalities and feelings of frustration and marginalization [100]. On the other hand, the same poor built environment significantly impedes equitable and just healthcare access, a critical tenet of the Critical Disability Theory. This non-adherence to the principle of equitable access is evident in the physical barriers posed by poorly designed healthcare facilities [35, 54, 72]. These barriers not only contravene legal requirements, such as those in Sects. 6 and 7 of Ghana’s Act 715 but also violate the fundamental right to healthcare equality for adolescents with disabilities [101]. The lack of accessible infrastructure, including ramps, adequate restroom facilities, and appropriately designed medical spaces, limits the ability of these adolescents to receive care, further contributing to a broader sense of exclusion and inequality [102]. Several studies have highlighted the extent to which poor built environments at healthcare facilities hinder access to healthcare for persons with disabilities [35]. Despite this, there is a noticeable delay in retrofitting these infrastructures, likely due to the lack of political will of successive governments, misconceptions about childhood and disability, and poor implementation of policies designed to support vulnerable social units [9–11, 54, 103, 104]. It is imperative that healthcare facilities’ infrastructure be planned and executed with an inclusive approach, recognizing the diverse and complex needs of adolescents with disabilities.

The financial barriers to healthcare access experienced by persons with disabilities have been widely published [40, 105, 106]. Despite the availability of a national health insurance scheme, the limited coverage for essential rehabilitation services stands out as a key concern. This limitation aligns with Critical Disability Theory’s focus on acknowledging the marginalized voices, as it underscores the unique challenges faced by families of adolescents with disabilities in accessing necessary healthcare services. These challenges can hinder adolescents’ development [107] and lead to poorer health outcomes [108], emphasizing the need for policies that specifically address these barriers. It is known that individuals with disabilities could live with co-morbid conditions requiring specialist services [105]. The discrepancy between policy and practice is evident from the studies by Owusu-Ansah et al. [109], Badu et al. [110], and Pandey and Chattoraj [55], where legal provisions for free healthcare do not translate into effective access for persons with disabilities. This gap violates the Critical Disability Theory tenet of ensuring equitable and just healthcare systems. The limited coverage under national health insurance schemes, particularly for rehabilitation services, denies these adolescents crucial support for their development and well-being [106]. This calls for government and health policymakers to invest more in the health sector, specifically to define and integrate rehabilitation services within Ghana’s healthcare system, ensuring that the rights of adolescents with disabilities and their families to equitable and just healthcare are upheld. There is an urgent need for a multi-prong approach in addressing the financial barriers to healthcare for adolescents with disabilities, guided by the principles of Critical Disability Theory. This includes policy reforms, increased investment in healthcare, and a nuanced understanding of the diverse needs of individuals with disabilities and their families, to create a more inclusive and equitable healthcare system.

Deep-rooted cultural beliefs about disability are at ‘odds’ with Critical Disability Theory’s principles of social participation and a rights-based approach to managing disability. Such beliefs lead to stigmatization and social exclusion, which create psychological barriers for adolescents with disabilities and their families in seeking healthcare [111]. This situation is further aggravated when disabilities are viewed through a lens of superstition or as a result of malevolent forces, leading to a preference for traditional or spiritual remedies over conventional medical treatment [112]. This preference not only delays appropriate medical interventions but also undermines the effectiveness of healthcare policies designed to provide equitable access [40]. Such beliefs might prompt families to discuss personal health issues within the community or with spiritual leaders, rather than medical professionals, potentially exposing sensitive health information. Furthermore, these cultural beliefs can influence the attitudes and behaviors of healthcare providers, potentially leading to discrimination or substandard care for adolescents with disabilities [113]. This systemic bias in healthcare delivery exacerbates the challenges faced by adolescents with disabilities in accessing necessary health services [114]. From a dignity perspective, such beliefs undermine the dignity of adolescents with disabilities and their families, making them feel less respected and valued in healthcare settings [115]. Reliance on cultural beliefs often overshadows evidence-based medical treatments, hindering the ability of individuals to make autonomous decisions about their health based on accurate information and medical advice [116]. Addressing these deep-rooted cultural beliefs is crucial for creating a more inclusive and equitable healthcare system. Upholding the rights of adolescents with disabilities and their families in this context means actively challenging and changing these harmful cultural narratives and practices.

The limited support at health facilities directly impacts the independence and dignity of adolescents with disabilities and their families. Miller et al. [117], and Gibson and Martin [118] emphasize that the absence of adequate support in healthcare settings severely hinders the autonomy of adolescents with disabilities and their families. This lack of autonomy and dignity, as noted by Epstein [119], is further exacerbated by the disrespectful behavior of healthcare workers. Such behavior not only diminishes adolescents with disabilities and their families’ self-worth but also violates the principle of treating all patients with inherent respect and value. The indifference of healthcare workers significantly undermines the autonomy of adolescents with disabilities and their families, as underscored by Emond Pelletier and Joussemet [120]. This autonomy, crucial for making informed decisions about care, is a human right that must be upheld in healthcare settings [121]. However, the disrespectful and indifferent attitudes of healthcare workers, as discussed by Entwistle et al. [122] and Bekteshi et al. [123], clearly contravene the Critical Disability Theory’s tenet of acknowledging marginalized voices. Such behavior erodes trust, hinders effective communication, and leads to reluctance to seek medical care, delayed treatment, and exacerbation of health conditions, as noted by Soltani et al. [124]. This finding calls for urgent policy interventions aimed at transforming healthcare facilities into more inclusive, respectful, and just environments. Health training institutions may consider developing training programs for healthcare workers that incorporate human relationships and create a conducive environment for the participation of all patients with disabilities.

The unavailability of specialized rehabilitation services is a critical issue reflecting two major tenets of Critical Disability Theory: the diversity of disabilities and the recognition of marginalized voices (i.e., adolescents with disabilities and their families). The diversity tenet, emphasized by Nuri et al. [100], Hussain and Raihan [114], and Adugna et al. [106], points out the intricate and multidisciplinary nature of disabilities, underscoring a failure to recognize the diverse medical needs of adolescents with disabilities. This lack of localized and specialized services forces families, particularly those in rural or underserved areas, to travel long distances, incurring extra costs and delays in receiving care, leading to poorer health outcomes and increased dependence on caregivers [35, 125]. Simultaneously, the tenet of recognizing marginalized voices, as explored by Dassah et al. [40] and Novak et al. [126], reveals that adolescents with disabilities, despite being enrolled in national health schemes, often face limited access to healthcare, especially rehabilitation services. The absence of early intervention and adequate rehabilitation services is not just a medical oversight but also a systemic failure to listen to and address the needs of marginalized groups within the healthcare system. Therefore, it is imperative for health policymakers to redefine the scope and integration of rehabilitation services within Ghana’s health system. This approach should not only recognize the diverse needs stemming from the multifaceted nature of disabilities but also ensure that the voices and needs of marginalized groups, particularly adolescents with disabilities, are heard and adequately addressed. The convergence of these Critical Disability Theory tenets highlights the critical need for a more inclusive and responsive healthcare system that caters to the varied and specific needs of all individuals with disabilities.

Despite the Ghanaian Disability Act’s provisions aiming to improve access for adolescents with disabilities, families, who play a crucial role in the well-being of these adolescents with disabilities, are often overlooked [30]. This situation contravenes the Critical Disability Theory tenet advocating for equitable healthcare access as a fundamental right for all, including caregivers of adolescents with disabilities [67, 69]. The results bring to light the hardships and health challenges faced by caregivers, who are directly affected by limited healthcare access. Such first-hand accounts are vital for informing policy reforms and ensuring that health policies are inclusive and responsive to the needs of all persons affected by disability. The study underscores the importance of involving families with adolescents with disabilities in the formulation of laws and policies to ensure that their unique needs are recognized and adequately addressed.

Study limitations and direction for future research

The limited scope and methodology of the study suggests that the results of the current study ought to be interpreted with caution. For instance, adolescents with disabilities (visually impaired and mobility impaired) and their families residing in one municipality were studied qualitatively. Future studies may draw on adolescents with cognitive disabilities such as autism and their caregivers. Also, future studies should use quantitative methods and recruit participants from diverse communities to develop a broad understanding of health access. Secondly, the study relied on the accounts of adolescents with disabilities and their families only. Especially, health professionals were not given the chance to comment on assertions by the study participants. Future studies could capture the voices of policymakers regarding how health provisions in legal frameworks are being implemented for individuals with disabilities.

Conclusion and implication for practice

The Ghanaian government’s commitment to improving the health outcomes of adolescents with disabilities and their families through the enactment of appropriate policies and to some extent implementation of Ghana’s Disability Act 715 aligns with the aspirations of the Sustainable Development Goals, particularly Goal 3, which calls for ensuring equal access to health for all. The study identifies significant environmental and systemic barriers that prevent adolescents with disabilities and their families from accessing the healthcare services outlined in Ghana’s Disability Act 715. Environmental barriers, including socio-economic challenges, poor built environment and unavailability of healthcare services limit adolescents with disabilities access to specialist care. Systemic barriers including cultural beliefs related to spiritual causality, and neglect of support for adolescents with disabilities and their families at facilities, further restrict access to healthcare services. These results highlight the need for comprehensive policy revisions, societal attitudinal change, and increased oversight of services such as the National Health Insurance Scheme.

Ghana’s Act 715 provides a framework for comprehensive healthcare access rights of adolescents with disabilities and their families in Ghana. However, the healthcare provisions within the Act 715 seemed not to be enforced by governmental institutions and health services providers. Firstly, Ghana’s Act 715 promises free general and specialist medical care for adolescents with disabilities. However, the research uncovers a gap between policy and practice, where families bear the cost of specialized services. This calls for policy adjustments to fully cover specialist care under the National Health Insurance Scheme, reducing the financial burden on families. Secondly, as per the results there is limited coverage for specialized services such as surgeries, rehabilitative treatments, and assistive devices. Practices should be revised to ensure National Health Insurance Scheme inclusivity and comprehensiveness, aligning with Act 715’s commitment to holistic care for persons with disabilities, including adolescents with disabilities and their families. The results further call for systemic strengthening of healthcare systems to be more inclusive and responsive to the unique needs of adolescents with disabilities and their families, in line with the Sustainable Development Goals. Thirdly, the absence of local-level health assessment and resource centers influences early detection and treatment of disabilities. Implementing Sect. 35 of the Act to establish such centers can improve early intervention and localized care. Fourthly, beliefs in spiritually-inflicted impairments hinder the utilization of medical services. Consequently, educational campaigns and collaborations with cultural and religious leaders are necessary to alter these perceptions and encourage acceptance of medical interventions. Generally, there is a need for widespread public education to dispel myths about disabilities and promote understanding of the rights and needs of adolescents with disabilities and their families. Finally, the reported poor support in healthcare facilities for adolescents with disabilities and their families necessitates training and sensitization of healthcare workers. Enhancing facility accessibility and staff responsiveness can significantly improve healthcare experiences for adolescents with disabilities and their families.
