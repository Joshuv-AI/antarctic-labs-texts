HERVH-derived eRNA activates a super-enhancer–driven ALDH1A3/SAT1 axis to promote ferroptosis escape and pancreatic cancer development
Authors: Lili Yuan, Jiahui Yu, Xiaoli Xu, Yiwen Huang, Dianhui Wei, Zhenbo Hou, Huizhen Nie, Tiantian Jing, Yizhen Liu, Boshi Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs), widely distributed in the human genome, are usually epigenetically silenced but can be reactivated in cancer. Activated ERVs may act as regulatory elements or produce noncoding RNAs that modulate gene expression and affect tumor biology. Their role in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), particularly in ferroptosis regulation, remains unclear. Here, we identify human endogenous retrovirus-H (HERVH) derived enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) aberrantly overexpressed in PDAC by integrating gene expression and histone modification data. Silencing HERVH markedly suppresses PDAC proliferation and tumor growth. Mechanistically, HERVH transcription is driven by the super-enhancer–associated transcription factor Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5), and HERVH eRNAs cooperate with KLF5 to transactivate a distal super-enhancer upstream of aldehyde dehydrogenase family 1 member 3 (ALDH1A3), increasing its expression. ALDH1A3 inhibits the reactive oxygen species/specificity protein 1/spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1 (ROS/Sp1/SAT1) cascade, reducing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Disrupting this axis induces ferroptotic death and impairs PDAC development. Our study reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism in which HERVH-derived eRNAs regulate ferroptosis via super-enhancer–mediated transcriptional reprogramming and highlights the KLF5/HERVH/ALDH1A3 pathway as a potential therapeutic target in PDAC.

INTRODUCTION

Endogenous retroviruses (ERVs) are remnants of ancient retroviral infections that have stably integrated into the host genome over millions of years of evolution (1, 2). Comprising about 8% of the human genome, ERVs are a major subclass of transposable elements (TEs) and are characterized by the presence of long terminal repeats (LTRs) (3). Under physiological conditions, most of ERVs remain epigenetically silenced through DNA methylation and repressive histone modifications such as di- and trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9me2/3). However, recent studies have revealed that subsets of ERVs can escape this silencing in a cell type–specific or disease-specific manner, particularly in cancer (3, 4). Once reactivated, ERVs can reshape gene regulatory networks, contribute to immunogenicity through double-stranded RNA formation, or give rise to tumor-associated neoantigens (5, 6). Despite growing interest in ERV reactivation across various malignancies, the transcriptional and functional landscape of ERVs in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains largely unexplored.

ERVs can exert regulatory effects through both cis-acting and trans-acting mechanisms (7). In the cis context, LTR elements may serve as alternative promoters or enhancers that influence neighboring gene expression by recruiting specific transcription factors (TFs) or epigenetic modifiers. For instance, ERVs with signal transducers and activators of transcription 1 or interferon regulatory factor 1 motifs participate in interferon responses (6). ERVs may also engage pluripotency regulators such as octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4), sex-determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2), and NANOG to sustain stem-like features in tumor cells (8). Moreover, some ERVs transcribe noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) that locally modulate gene activity, such as human endogenous retrovirus-H (HERVH) derived ncRNAs in embryonic stem cells that reinforce OCT4-driven transcriptional programs (4, 9). In the trans context, ERV-derived long ncRNAs (lncRNAs) can modulate distal gene expression, often through interactions with chromatin-associated regulatory proteins. For example, HERVH lncRNA has been shown to interact with the coactivator bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) in colorectal cancer, promoting oncogenic transcriptional programs (10). These findings suggest that ERV transcripts may serve as both structural and functional regulators of chromatin dynamics and transcription.

Emerging evidence supports that some ERVs are embedded within or coincide with active enhancer, leading to the production of enhancer RNAs (eRNAs) (11, 12). eRNAs are a class of noncoding transcripts originating from active enhancers that facilitate enhancer-promoter looping, transcriptional cofactor recruitment [e.g., E1A-binding protein p300 (EP300) or mediator complex], and chromatin remodeling (13). In particular, clusters of enhancers can form super-enhancers (SEs), which are large regulatory domains that drive the high-level expression of genes critical for cell identity or disease progression (14–16). These SEs are typically enriched for the acetylation of lysine 27 on histone 3 (H3K27ac) and are densely occupied by transcriptional coactivators such as BRD4, EP300, and Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5), a member of the KLF family (17–20). SEs may also give rise to SE RNAs, a specialized class of eRNAs that further potentiate the activity of both local and distal SEs (21). Notably, cancer cells often become dependent on SE-driven transcriptional networks to sustain their growth and malignancy (22).

Despite the growing recognition of ERVs as components of regulatory enhancers, the identity and function of ERV-derived eRNAs (ERVDERs) in PDAC remain undefined. Our integrative transcriptomic and epigenomic analyses revealed that members of the HERVH family produce eRNAs in PDAC tissues, and these HERVH-derived eRNAs are aberrantly up-regulated in tumor samples compared to adjacent normal tissues. Functional studies demonstrate that silencing HERVH leads to the remarkable suppression of PDAC cell proliferation in vitro and tumor growth in vivo, highlighting its oncogenic role.

Mechanistically, we found that HERVH engages in a reciprocal regulatory loop with the SE-associated master TF KLF5 and the activation of SEs. Transcription of SE-embedded HERVH loci is directly driven by KLF5, while the resulting HERVH-derived eRNAs cooperate with KLF5 to transactivate a distal SE upstream of aldehyde dehydrogenase 1A3 (ALDH1A3). This activation initiates the antioxidant ALDH1A3/reactive oxygen species/specificity protein 1/spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase 1 (ROS/Sp1/SAT1) signaling cascade, promoting resistance to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent accumulation of ROS and subsequent lipid membrane damage (23, 24). Ferroptosis is characterized by metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress and has emerged as a potent tumor-suppressive mechanism, particularly in PDAC (25–27). Resistance to ferroptosis represents a key adaptive feature of aggressive tumors, enabling them to evade oxidative cell death and sustain malignant growth (28). The progression of ferroptosis can be tightly regulated by several metabolic enzymes, including ALDH1A3, which modulates intracellular aldehyde levels and ROS production (29, 30), and SAT1, which is involved in polyamine catabolism and lipid peroxidation (31). Dysregulation of these enzymes may alter ferroptotic signaling, contributing to tumor cell survival.

In this study, our findings highlight a previously unrecognized epigenetic mechanism by which HERVH-derived eRNAs orchestrate a ferroptotic program through enhancer-mediated transcriptional reprogramming. This sheds light on the potential of targeting ERV-associated SE networks to restore ferroptosis sensitivity and suppress tumor progression.

RESULTS

High expression of HERVH-derived eRNAs in PDAC

To identify ERVs with enhancer-like characteristics and ERVDERs that are highly expressed in PDAC, we integrated and analyzed the high-throughput epigenomic and transcriptomic sequencing data from multiple public databases (fig. S1A). Using the ChromHMM algorithm to profile the histone modifications patterns, we identified the active enhancer regions in PDAC (Fig. 1A). The ChromHMM model segmented the genome into four types: enhancer, promoter, heterochromatin, and quiescent chromatin. By incorporating RepeatMasker annotation data from the UCSC Genome Browser (hg38), we mapped the genomic locations of ERVs and identified those situated within PDAC-associated active enhancer regions.

To validate the enhancer activity of these ERVs, we performed cleavage under targets and tagmentation sequencing (CUT&Tag-seq) in PDAC cell lines and assessed histone modifications at the identified ERV loci. These ERVs were enriched for active histone marks, including H3K27ac and H3K4me1, and were devoid of the repressive mark H3K9me3, indicating active enhancer activity (Fig. 1B). We further analyzed the distribution of ERVs across distinct chromatin states and found that the majority were located in quiescent or heterochromatic regions, while only a small subset resided within active enhancer regions (Fig. 1C). Chromosome-wide expression profiling revealed that ERVs in enhancer regions exhibited markedly higher transcriptional activity compared to those in other chromatin contexts, with particularly high expression observed on chromosomes 13, 19, and 20 (fig. S1B).

To identify ERVs that are substantially up-regulated in PDAC tissues and determine their chromosomal distribution, we obtained whole-transcriptome RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets of PDAC and adjacent nontumor tissues from public repositories (PRJNA487487 and PRJNA632931). We quantified the expression levels of each enhancer-localized ERV and compared differential expression between tumor and normal tissues (table S1). This analysis revealed 20 enhancer-associated ERVs with notably elevated expression in PDAC (Fig. 1, D and E), among which 10 belong to the HERVH family.

These up-regulated enhancer HERVH typically exhibited a full-length structure, consisting of a HERVH internal sequence (HERVH-int) flanked by LTR sequences (Fig. 1, F and G). These HERVH loci showed strong enrichment of enhancer-associated histone marks H3K27ac and H3K4me1 (Fig. 1G). Compared to nonenhancer-localized HERVH elements, enhancer-localized HERVH elements exhibited markedly higher expression levels (Fig. 1H). Notably, expression levels of the 10 up-regulated enhancer HERVH loci were substantially and positively correlated with their enhancer activity (fig. S1C), indicating that their transcription is likely regulated by the activity of the enhancers. Furthermore, to evaluate the potential cis-regulatory activity of eRNA-producing HERVH loci, we examined the expression of neighboring genes and found that several were down-regulated upon HERVH knockdown in PDAC cells (fig. S1D). These findings indicate that HERVH loci may function as local enhancers, which positively regulate the transcription of proximal genes.

Strand-specific RNA-seq data further confirmed that these HERVH loci were transcribed unidirectionally in PDAC tissues (fig. S2A). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that the combined expression of the 10 enhancer HERVH loci, as well as several individual loci, was notably associated with poor overall survival in patients with PDAC (fig. S2B), suggesting that these elements may contribute to PDAC initiation and progression.

HERVH-derived RNA is required for the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells

To investigate the biological functions of HERVH in PDAC, we established PDAC cell lines with stable HERVH knockdown via lentiviral infection. Knockdown efficiency was validated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using six independent pairs of primers (Fig. 2A and fig. S3A). Visualization of RNA-seq data using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) browser revealed differential expression across 10 enhancer-associated HERVH loci, with global HERVH eRNA levels markedly decreased upon knockdown (Fig. 2B). Lentiviral-mediated HERVH knockdown consistently suppressed cell proliferation in multiple PDAC cell lines (SW1990, MIA PaCa-2, PANC-1, and SW1990 GemR), as demonstrated by colony formation assays (Fig. 2C). In vivo experiments further showed that HERVH knockdown impaired tumor growth in nude mice, underscoring its essential role in maintaining the tumorigenic capacity of pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 2, D and E).

To better recapitulate the characteristics of primary tumor cells within their native microenvironment, we used patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to assess the functional relevance of HERVH. Stable knockdown organoids were generated via lentiviral transduction, followed by puromycin selection and serial passaging, with knockdown efficiency confirmed by RT-PCR (fig. S3B). Organoid growth assays demonstrated that specific knockdown of HERVH greatly reduced the number of organoids compared to controls, indicating a potent inhibitory effect on PDAC progression (Fig. 2F).

HERVH suppresses SAT1 expression to alleviate intracellular lipid peroxidation

To elucidate the mechanistic role of HERVH in pancreatic cancer cells, differential gene expression analysis following HERVH knockdown identified 11 commonly up-regulated genes in both SW1990 and MIA PaCa-2 cell lines, including nine protein-coding genes (Fig. 3, A and B, and table S2). Among these genes, SAT1 exhibited the most pronounced increase in expression (Fig. 3C), suggesting that it may serve as a key effector in HERVH-mediated tumor growth. Subsequent validation by RT-PCR and Western blot confirmed that HERVH knockdown substantially up-regulated SAT1 at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3, D and E).

SAT1 encodes spermidine/spermine N′-acetyltransferase, a key polyamine-catabolic enzyme that accelerates lipid peroxidation and drives ferroptosis (31). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq data following HERVH knockdown SW1990 cells revealed pronounced enrichment of ferroptosis-associated gene signature in HERVH-deficient cells (Fig. 3F). Functional assays further demonstrated that HERVH knockdown substantially increased phospholipid peroxidation levels, as measured by BODIPY 581/591 C11 fluorescence (Fig. 3G). Consistently, HERVH down-regulation also resulted in elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, a marker of oxidative lipid–induced ferroptosis (Fig. 3H), as well as increased intracellular ferrous ion (Fe2+) concentrations (Fig. 3I). To determine whether the growth inhibition induced by HERVH knockdown in pancreatic cancer cells is mediated by ferroptosis, we treated HERVH-depleted cells with well-established ferroptosis inhibitors Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC). Both treatments largely rescued the growth impairment caused by HERVH silencing, suggesting that ferroptosis is a key mechanism underlying the growth-suppressive effects of HERVH depletion (Fig. 3J and fig. S3C). Collectively, these findings indicate that HERVH suppresses SAT1 expression to attenuate intracellular lipid peroxidation and limit ferroptotic cell death, thereby promoting pancreatic cancer cell proliferation.

HERVH negatively regulates SAT1 expression via the ALDH1A3/ROS/Sp1 axis

To clarify the molecular mechanism underlying HERVH-mediated regulation of SAT1, we drew upon previous studies characterizing HERVH as a positive transcriptional regulator (4, 9, 10), which raised the possibility that it modulates SAT1 expression through indirect mechanisms. To explore this, we reanalyzed transcriptomic profiles following HERVH knockdown, focusing specifically on remarkably down-regulated genes involved in ferroptosis resistance and oxidative stress response. Notably, the key antioxidant gene ALDH1A3 was notably down-regulated upon HERVH depletion (Fig. 4A). This result was validated by both qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses, which consistently confirmed the reduced mRNA and protein levels of ALDH1A3 in SW1990 and MIA PaCa-2 pancreatic cancer cells after HERVH knockdown (Fig. 4, B and C). Consistently, CRISPR activation (CRISPRa)–mediated activation of HERVH expression resulted in a markedly up-regulation of ALDH1A3 and a concomitant down-regulation of SAT1 (fig. S4A), further confirming the regulatory role of HERVH in controlling ALDH1A3 expression.

ALDH1A3, a member of the aldehyde dehydrogenase family, is a critical antioxidant enzyme known to confer resistance to ferroptosis (29, 32). To determine whether ALDH1A3 mediates the regulatory effect of HERVH on SAT1 expression, we performed ALDH1A3 loss-of-function experiments in pancreatic cancer cells. Silencing ALDH1A3 led to a pronounced up-regulation of SAT1 expression, mirroring the effect observed upon HERVH knockdown (Fig. 4D), and concurrently suppressed pancreatic cancer cell proliferation, thereby recapitulating the biological effects of HERVH depletion (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that HERVH may suppress SAT1 expression via ALDH1A3.

Given that ALDH1A3 functions as an antioxidant protein, it is plausible that it influences intracellular ROS levels. Literature reports and our assays have shown that the ROS negatively regulated TF Sp1 can act as a direct transcriptional repressor of the SAT1 gene (33–36) (fig. S4, B and C). On this basis, we hypothesized that ALDH1A3 might regulate SAT1 expression through the ROS/Sp1 axis. Supporting this, we found that ALDH1A3 knockdown markedly increased intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 4F) and led to a marked decrease in Sp1 protein expression (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, direct knockdown of Sp1 resulted in SAT1 up-regulation (fig. S4C), consistent with the effects observed upon HERVH and ALDH1A3 silencing.

Integrating previous literature with our current findings, we propose that HERVH suppresses SAT1 expression via the ALDH1A3/ROS/Sp1 signaling pathway. In support of this model, HERVH knockdown not only reduced ALDH1A3 expression (Fig. 4, B and C) but also decreased levels of reduced form of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (oxidized form) (NAD+) (NADH), a key catalytic product of ALDH1A3 (Fig. 4H), and reproduced the biological effects of ALDH1A3 depletion, including elevated ROS levels (Fig. 4I) and reduced Sp1 expression (Fig. 4J). Analyzing expression patterns of ALDH1A3 and Sp1 in PDAC RNA-seq datasets revealed that both ALDH1A3 and Sp1 were substantially overexpressed in HERVH-high PDAC samples (fig. S4D). Together, these results indicate that HERVH regulates SAT1 expression through the ALDH1A3/ROS/Sp1 axis, thereby contributing to the oxidative stress response and ferroptosis resistance in pancreatic cancer cells.

KLF5 is a key TF driving HERVH expression and downstream signaling

To investigate the mechanisms underlying HERVH overexpression in pancreatic cancer, we screened for TFs potentially responsible for activating HERVH eRNA. Using ReMapEnrich, we analyzed TF enrichment at enhancer regions associated with highly expressed HERVH loci in pancreatic cancer. This analysis revealed significant enrichment of several KLFs, particularly KLF4, KLF5, and KLF6 (fig. S5A). Consistent results were obtained from TF binding motif analysis using Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif Enrichment (HOMER), which further confirmed significant enrichment of recognition motifs for these KLFs, with KLF5 exhibiting the highest frequency (Fig. 5A). Given that KLF5 and related factors are known master TFs at SE elements, we also observed enrichment of SE-associated coactivators, such as BRD4 and mediator complex subunit 1 (MED1), at these regions. These findings suggest that HERVH loci may reside within SEs and be regulated by key transcriptional regulators.

To test this hypothesis, we used ROSE (rank ordering of SEs) software in combination with H3K27ac chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data (ERP023823 dataset) to identify SEs in pancreatic cancer samples. The analysis demonstrated that most of highly expressed HERVH loci overlapped substantially with SE regions, supporting the notion that HERVH is an SE-driven element (Fig. 5B). To functionally validate the role of KLF5, we performed loss of function assays in pancreatic cancer cells, which resulted in a marked reduction in HERVH RNA levels (Fig. 5C). KLF5 knockdown also diminished enhancer activity at HERVH loci (fig. S5B), indicating that KLF5 is a critical upstream regulator of HERVH. In addition, KLF5 knockdown suppressed tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 5D and fig. S5C), decreased ALDH1A3 expression, and increased SAT1 expression (Fig. 5E), leading to elevated levels of lipid peroxidation (Fig. 5F). These findings demonstrate that KLF5 regulates the HERVH/ALDH1A3/ROS/Sp1 signaling axis and thereby contributes to the suppression of ferroptosis.

Treatment of pancreatic cancer cells with ML264, a selective small-molecule inhibitor of KLF5, clearly inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 5G). Further analysis confirmed that ML264 treatment also reduced HERVH expression (fig. S5D), with downstream effects on ALDH1A3 and Sp1 protein levels consistent with those observed following HERVH knockdown (Fig. 5H). These results highlight the functional role of KLF5 in mediating the HERVH-dependent signaling cascade. Moreover, analysis of PDAC RNA-seq datasets showed that KLF5 expression was significantly higher in HERVH-high samples compared with HERVH-low samples (fig. S5E), supporting a regulatory relationship between KLF5 and HERVH. Consistently, KLF5 overexpression in PDAC cells led to increased HERVH expression (fig. S5F), further confirming KLF5 as an upstream driver of HERVH activation.

To assess the therapeutic potential of targeting KLF5 in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using SW1990 pancreatic cancer cells in nude mice. Intraperitoneal administration of ML264 (25 mg/kg) remarkably suppressed tumor growth and reduced tumorigenic capacity, without resulting notable weight loss (Fig. 5, I and J, and fig. S5G).

Collectively, our findings identify KLF5 as a master regulator of SE-driven HERVH expression and its downstream antiferroptotic signaling in pancreatic cancer. Pharmacological targeting the KLF5-HERVH axis with pharmacological inhibitors such as ML264 not only impairs tumor growth but also reactivates ferroptosis, highlighting its promising translational potential for therapeutic intervention in pancreatic cancer.

The HERVH interacts with KLF5 to transactivate ALDH1A3 expression through SE activation

A well-established mechanism of eRNAs involves transcriptional activation through interactions with TFs. Given that HERVH-derived eRNA expression is regulated by the SE-associated master TF KLF5, we hypothesized that HERVH eRNA may interact with KLF5 to enhance the activity of its associated SEs. To test this hypothesis, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays and revealed that HERVH associates with KLF5 protein (Fig. 6A).

Data are presented as means ± SD. Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided t test. (F) IGV browser visualization of the SE region upstream of the ALDH1A3 gene. (G and H) CRISPRi was used to target and repress the SE upstream of ALDH1A3. ALDH1A3 mRNA expression was assessed by RT-PCR (G), and protein levels of ALDH1A3 and SAT1 were evaluated by Western blotting (H). n = 3 biological replicates. Data are shown as means ± SD, and statistical significance was assessed using a two-sided t test. (I and J) IGV browser visualization of the ALDH1A3-upstream SE, showing occupancy peaks of BRD4, EP300, and KLF5 within the enhancer region and their changes following HERVH knockdown in SW1990 cells (I). Changes in H3K27ac peak intensity were analyzed in SW1990 and MIA PaCa-2 cells with or without HERVH knockdown to assess SE activity dynamics (J).

Further analyses demonstrated that HERVH modulates the function of KLF5 and its bound SEs. Specifically, HERVH knockdown markedly reduced KLF5 occupancy at SE regions, while the binding of other coactivators such as BRD4 and EP300 remained unaffected (Fig. 6B). Consistent with the loss of KLF5 binding, HERVH depletion also led to a substantial decrease in H3K27ac levels at these regions, indicating reduced enhancer activity (Fig. 6, C and D). Notably, the affected SEs included the HERVH loci themselves, supporting the classical cis-regulatory role of eRNAs (fig. S6A). However, our data also suggest that HERVH exerts trans-regulatory effects by interacting with KLF5 to modulate the activity of distal SEs and their target genes (Fig. 6, B to D).

To investigate whether HERVH regulates ALDH1A3 expression via KLF5-mediated SE activation, we treated pancreatic cancer cells with the bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) inhibitor JQ1. This treatment markedly altered the expression of ALDH1A3 and its downstream targets Sp1 and SAT1 (Fig. 6E), indicating that ALDH1A3 is under SE regulation. On the basis of SE mapping in SW1990 cells (fig. S6B), we identified an SE upstream of ALDH1A3 (Fig. 6F). CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) using catalytically dead Cas9 fused to the Krüppel-associated box domain (dCas9-KRAB) and single guide RNA (sgRNAs) targeting this region substantially suppressed ALDH1A3 expression (Fig. 6, F to H), while concomitantly increasing SAT1 levels (Fig. 6H and fig. S6C), further supporting a functional regulatory role for this enhancer.

CUT&Tag-seq analysis revealed strong KLF5 binding within the ALDH1A3-upstream SE. HERVH knockdown remarkably reduced KLF5 occupancy at this site without affecting the binding of BRD4 or EP300 (Fig. 6I and fig. S6D). In addition, HERVH depletion resulted in decreased H3K27ac levels, indicating reduced enhancer activity (Fig. 6J), consistent with the effects observed upon KLF5 depletion (fig. S6E). These results suggest that HERVH can promote KLF5 binding and transactivate the ALDH1A3 SE.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that HERVH collaborates with KLF5 to activate the ALDH1A3 SE, thereby promoting ALDH1A3 expression. This, in turn, activates downstream signaling pathways that suppress ferroptosis and support pancreatic cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 7).

Molecular mechanisms underlying HERVH-mediated PDAC development.

A subset of HERVH elements is highly expressed in PDAC, driven by TF KLF5 and SE activation. HERVH-derived eRNAs interact with KLF5 to transactivate an SE located upstream of ALDH1A3, leading to its overexpression. Elevated ALDH1A3 expression suppresses ROS/Sp1/SAT1-mediated lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, thereby promoting ferroptosis resistance and contributing to PDAC progression.

DISCUSSION

The malignant progression of pancreatic cancer is driven by a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic alterations (37–40). While extensive research has focused on gene mutations, growing evidence highlights the pivotal role of epigenetic regulators in tumor initiation and progression. Among these, ERV-derived transcriptional regulatory elements, including enhancers, and eRNAs are emerging as critical components of the epigenetic regulatory network. These elements influence tumor biology by remodeling chromatin states and modulating gene expression (41–44). This study systematically analyzed the epigenetic features of ERVs in PDAC and the expression patterns of ERVDERs, revealing the marked overexpression of specific ERVs, particularly HERVH, in PDAC and their potential functional roles. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the transcriptional regulatory network in PDAC but also provide previously unknown evidence for the role of ERVs in cancer.

ERVs regulate gene expression through both cis- and trans-acting mechanisms, thereby influencing cellular behavior. In cis-regulation, ERV sequences function as regulatory hubs enriched with TF binding sites, enabling them to recruit transcriptional or epigenetic regulators to nearby chromatin. This facilitates changes in local chromatin states and directly modulates the transcriptional activity of adjacent genes (6, 8). One important aspect of the cis-regulatory activity involves the production of eRNAs transcribed from ERV loci, which is driven by the recruited TFs, reinforces enhancer activity, and promotes local gene expression (4, 9, 45). In contrast, trans-regulatory mechanisms involve ERV-derived ncRNAs that modulate the expression of genes located at distant genomic loci.

Notably, cis and trans mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, and they can function synergistically and sequentially. Our study uncovers such a cooperative regulatory model. Specifically, transcription of HERVH eRNAs is initiated in a cis-like fashion via recruitment of the master TF KLF5 to HERVH loci that are enriched in KLF5 binding motifs. As a pivotal regulator of SE activity, KLF5 directly drives HERVH transcription as part of the local enhancer activation process. We further demonstrate that HERVH eRNAs contribute to the maintenance and amplification of enhancer activity at their sites of origin. Beyond local regulation, previous studies have shown that HERVH eRNAs also exert trans-acting functions by interacting with SE components such as BRD4 (10). In the present study, we identify KLF5 as a major interacting partner of HERVH. In contrast to BRD4, whose occupancy at SEs is not notably affected by HERVH, HERVH enhances the chromatin-binding activity of KLF5 at both local and distal SE regions. Through this interaction, HERVH-derived eRNAs extend their regulatory influence to distal genomic targets, including a key SE upstream of ALDH1A3. Using both the well-established SE inhibitor JQ1 and a CRISPRi system specifically targeting this SE, we demonstrated that ALDH1A3 expression is driven by the upstream SE. Moreover, HERVH depletion reduced KLF5 occupancy at this region, indicating that HERVH regulates the ALDH1A3 SE primarily through KLF5 recruitment. The regulatory events occurring in this locus thus provide a clear example of the trans-acting mechanism by which HERVH modulates gene expression.

SEs, defined as large clusters of enhancers with unusually high densities of TFs and coactivators, are key regulators of cell identity and are frequently co-opted in cancer to drive the expression of oncogenes (14–16). In PDAC, aberrant activation of SEs contributes to tumor cell proliferation, survival, and resistance to therapy, positioning them as central nodes in oncogenic transcriptional networks (22, 46–48). Our study advances the current understanding of SE regulation by revealing a role for HERVH-derived eRNAs as key mediators of interlocus communication within the SE network. Specifically, HERVH eRNAs are transcribed from a subset of SEs containing HERVH elements and not only reinforce the activity of their parent loci but also engage in transactivation of other distant SEs, regardless of whether these distal elements contain HERVH sequences. By bridging distinct regulatory hubs across the genome, HERVH eRNAs may facilitate the spatial coordination and functional coupling of SE-mediated oncogenic transcriptional programs.

To elucidate how HERVH contributes to PDAC proliferation, we performed knockdown experiments using two independent short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) in two PDAC cell lines. This led to the up-regulation of nine protein-coding genes, among which SAT1 exhibited the most robust and consistent increase. As a rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine metabolism, SAT1 accelerates the depletion of spermidine and spermine, compromising cellular antioxidant capacity. During polyamine metabolism, ROS such as hydrogen peroxide is generated, which can exacerbate phospholipid peroxidation and disrupt redox homeostasis (31, 49, 50). Thus, HERVH may negatively regulate SAT1 expression to reduce phospholipid peroxidation, helping pancreatic cancer cells evade ferroptosis and maintain viability.

Further investigation of the regulatory mechanisms of HERVH revealed that knockdown of HERVH also substantially down-regulated ALDH1A3, a key antioxidant enzyme that detoxifies ROS and maintains redox homeostasis (51, 52). This was accompanied by increased intracellular ROS, suggesting that HERVH positively regulates ALDH1A3 to limit ROS accumulation. In turn, reduced ROS levels inhibit SAT1 expression via Sp1-mediated transcriptional repression, forming a regulatory cascade: HERVH↑ → ALDH1A3↑ → ROS↓ → Sp1↑ → SAT1↓. Overall, this defines a previously unknown HERVH/ALDH1A3/ROS/Sp1/SAT1 signaling axis modulating ferroptosis resistance in PDAC.

Given the high expression and oncogenic role of HERVH eRNA in PDAC, we explored its potential as a therapeutic target. As direct inhibition of ncRNAs in vivo remains challenging, we focused on identifying clinically actionable upstream regulators. Our analysis pinpointed KLF5 as a key transcriptional activator of HERVH eRNA. KLF5, a member of the Krüppel-like TF family, regulates proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Previous studies have shown that KLF5 is highly expressed in advanced and metastatic PDAC (53, 54). Here, we show that RNA interference–mediated or pharmacological inhibition of KLF5 notably reduces HERVH eRNA levels and suppresses PDAC cell growth, implicating KLF5 as a crucial upstream driver of HERVH-mediated oncogenesis and a viable therapeutic target. We used ML264, a small-molecule inhibitor of KLF5 originally shown to suppress colorectal cancer growth via inhibition of KLF5 and its upstream activator early growth response protein 1 (EGR1) (55). ML264 has also demonstrated potent antitumor activity in various cancers (56–58) and was recently shown to inhibit PDAC progression (59). Our work confirmed that ML264 suppresses HERVH eRNA expression and induces ferroptosis in PDAC cells, supporting its therapeutic potential. Notably, ML264 was well tolerated in vivo, as evidenced by the absence of marked weight loss under our experimental conditions, suggesting no obvious systemic toxicity. Nevertheless, comprehensive assessments of toxicity and off-target effects are necessary to fully define its safety profile and translational potential. Future work will also evaluate ML264 in spontaneous PDAC mouse models, PDOs, and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, and explore combination therapies to address tumor heterogeneity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines and organoids

SW1990 (CRL-2172), AsPC-1 (CRL-1682), MIA PaCa-2 (CRM-CRL-1420), PANC-1(CRL-1469), BxPC-3(CRL-1687), and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T (CRL-3216) cells were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling. Cells cultured in RPMI 1640 or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China, catalog no. F101-01) at 37°C and 5% CO2 incubator. The establishment and culture of PDAC PDOs were described previously (60).

Reagents and antibodies

JQ1 (HY-13030), Fer-1 (HY-100579), and ML264 (HY-19994) were sourced from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; AC11L054) was purchased from Life-iLab Biotech Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RhoNox-6 (S1070S) used for the ferrous ion assay was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Puromycin, penicillin/streptomycin and NAC (50303ES05) were procured from Yeasen Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The primary antibodies for Western blot assays were as follows: anti-SAT1 (Proteintech, 83319-1-RR; 1:1000), anti-ALDH1A3 (Proteintech, 25167-1-AP; 1:1000), anti-KLF5 (Proteintech, 21017-1-AP; 1:1000), anti-Sp1 (Selleck Chemicals LLC, Houston, TX, USA, F2151; 1:1000), and anti–glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA, sc-32233; 1:1000). Second antibodies for Western blot were as follows: goat anti-rabbit (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA, 926-3221; 1:10,000) and goat anti-mouse (LI-COR Biosciences, 926-68020; 1:10,000). The primary antibodies used in CUT&Tag-seq assays were as followed: anti-H3K27ac (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab4729; 1:100), BRD4 (Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Danvers, MA, USA, #13440; 1:50), EP300 (Abcam, ab275378; 1:100), and KLF5 (Proteintech, 21017-1-AP; 1:100). The antibodies used in RIP assays include anti-KLF5 (Proteintech, 21017-1-AP; 5 μg) and anti–immunoglobulin G (IgG) (Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Danvers, MA, USA, #2729; 5 μg).

Animal studies

BALB/c nude mice were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed under strictly pathogen-free conditions. All animal experiments were conducted the approved guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and the Shanghai Jiaotong University Animal Care Commission. The maximal permitted tumor volume is 2000 mm3. To ensure humane end points, mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide (CO2) asphyxiation, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of preliminary pilot studies and power analysis using tumor volume as the primary outcome measure. We aimed to detect a 30% difference in tumor volume between groups with 80% power and a significance level of 0.05. On this basis, at least five mice per group were required. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size beyond these criteria. All mice were age and sex matched. Animals were excluded from the study only if they developed unrelated health issues or failed to form measurable tumors. To minimize potential biases, mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups using a random number generator. The order of treatments and measurements was counterbalanced between cages and groups to control for positional effects. Investigators conducting tumor measurements and end-point analyses were blinded to group allocation. Group assignment was concealed until all data collection and primary analyses were completed.

In vivo tumorigenesis assays

Six-week-old male nude mice were received subcutaneously injected with 2 × 106 SW1990 cells expressing either vector control or shRNA. Tumor volume was measured and documented. At the end point, tumors were excised, harvested, weighted, and photographed. For the in vivo pharmacological study, SW1990 cells were subcutaneously injected into nude mice. Two weeks postinjection, mice were randomly divided into two groups. One group received intraperitoneal injections of vehicle [10 μl/g; 2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in 98% corn oil]. The other group received ML264 (10 μl/g; 25 mg/kg, intraperitoneally). Treatments were administered once daily for 1 week.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were extracted from cells using radioimmunoprecipitation (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Bimake, Houston, TX, USA, B14001) on ice for 1 hour. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000g for 20 min at 4°C. The protein concentrations of the supernatants were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0011). Subsequently, the obtained lysates were subjected to separation by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose (NC) membranes (Pall Corporation). The NC membranes were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The membranes were incubated with goat anti-rabbit or goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies and visualized using the Odyssey imaging system.

Plasmid construction and cell transfection

The specific shRNA sequences were cloned into pLKO.1 plasmid. For CRISPRi assays, the sgRNAs targeting the SE region of ALDH1A3 were inserted into pLV-hU6-sgRNA-hUbC-dCas9-KRAB-T2a-Puro (Addgene, #71236) plasmid. For the CRISPRa assays, we used the CRISPR-based synergistic activation mediator system, which includes two plasmids, one expressing dCas9-VP64 (lenti dCas9-VP64_Blast, Addgene, #61425) and the other expressing the MS2-P65-HSF1 and gRNAs expressing cascade (pXPR_502, Addgene, #96923). The sequences of shRNAs and sgRNAs are provided in table S1. The KLF5 coding sequence was cloned into pLVX-IRES-Puro plasmid.

Lentiviruses were generated in HEK293T cells using the packaging plasmids (psPAX2 and pMD2.G). PDAC cells were incubated with the viral particles in the presence of polybrene. After 48 hours, stable cell lines were selected using the puromycin-containing medium.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted with the RNAiso Plus kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with reverse transcriptase (Vazyme, Nanjing, China, R323-01). qRT-PCR was performed with ChamQ Blue Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312-02) and analyzed by Viia7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The sequences of primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in table S3. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

NADH level measurement

The intracellular NADH level was measured using the NAD+/NADH Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0175) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The control cells or HERVH knockdown in SW1990 and MIA PaCa-2 cells were treated as indicated and collected for analysis.

MDA assay

The relative MDA concentrations in the cells were assessed with a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, D799762), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed in the provided lysis buffer and incubated with thiobarbituric acid at 100°C for 1 hour. After incubation, samples were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 532 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Lipid peroxidation measurement

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using BD LSRFortessa Cell Analyzer excitation at 488 nm. Cells (1 × 106 cells per well) were seeded in 12-well plates before 24 hours before analysis. Then, the cells were harvested, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and stained with 10 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, D3861) at 37°C for 30 min. A minimum of 10,000 single cells per sample were analyzed.

Intracellular ROS production assays

PDAC cells (1 × 106 cells per well) were seeded in 12-well plates 24 hours before the assay and incubated with fresh medium adding containing 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (Beyotime, S0033S) at 37°C for 30 min. Cells were washed three times with PBS, resuspended in 200 μl of PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry for evaluating the 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF) fluorescence intensity.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assay

For proliferation assays, cells (2000 cells per well) were seeded in 96-well plates overnight. Cell proliferation was measured at specific time points using 100 μl of DMEM containing 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, with a reference wavelength of 600 nm.

For the cell colony formation assays, cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates or 12-well plates and incubated for 7 to 10 days. The resulting colonies were stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, C0121).

RIP assay

For RIP analysis, cells (2 × 107 per group) were cross-linked in 5 ml of DMEM containing 132 μl of formaldehyde (37.7%) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by quenching with 270 μl of glycine (2.5 M). Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and pelleted (2000 rpm for 5 min), and nuclei were isolated using a commercial extraction kit (Solabio, SN0020) with ribonuclease (RNase) inhibitor–supplemented lysis buffer. Nuclear pellets were lysed in 2.3 ml of RIP lysis buffer, sonicated (5-s on/off for 10 min). Five micrograms of KLF5 antibodies and nonspecific rabbit IgG were attached to protein A/G magnetic beads (CYTOCH, PM0004) through 3 hours of incubation at 4°C and washed twice. Lysates were incubated with antibody-bound beads overnight at 4°C. Beads were washed six times with RIP buffer, and protein was digested with Proteinase K (55°C for 30 min). RNA was extracted using TRIzol, precipitated with glycogen, NaAc, and ethanol (−80°C overnight), centrifuged (13,000 rpm for 30 min), washed with 80% ethanol, and resuspended in 30 μl of diethyl pyrocarbonate water. qRT-PCR was performed using the reverse transcription kit and the PCR kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA integrity was evaluated using Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) to obtain RNA integrity number (RIN). RNA with RIN values of >7 was purified using the RNAClean XP Kit (Beckman Coulter Inc. CA, USA) and RNase-Free Deoxyribonuclease Set (QIAGEN GmBH, Germany). RNA libraries were prepared for sequencing with VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 system.

Raw RNA-seq data were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v0.11.9) and trimmed using Trim Galore (0.6.5-1). SortMeRNA (4.2.0) was used to remove ribosomal RNA reads to generate clean data. The clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using the HISAT2 (2.2.1) aligner to generate BAM files. The specifically mapped counts were obtained using StringTie (v2.2.3). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using EdgeR (3.30.3), and GSEA and gene function enrichment were performed using the ClusterProfiler package (3.16.1) based on the EdgeR results. The aligned reads (BAM files) were transformed into bigwig files using deepTools software (v3.5.6) for visualization in IGV.

Cleavage under targets and tagmentation sequencing

We used the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, TD903) to prepare DNA library. Briefly, 5.0 × 104 cells were gently resuspended in nuclear extraction (NE) buffer and incubated on ice for 10 min. The nuclei were isolated and then conjugated to 10 μl of preactivated concanavalin A–coated magnetic beads. Then, the nuclei bound to the beads were incubated overnight at 4°C in 50 μl of antibody buffer containing antibiotin rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:100 dilution). Subsequently, the nuclei were resuspended in 100 μl of dig-wash buffer containing antibody (1:100 dilution) to bind with the primary antibody. The nuclei were washed three times with 200 μl of dig-wash buffer before being tagmented with the pA/G-Tn5 adapter complex. The tagmented DNA was collected using DNA extract beads for library preparation. PCR was performed for library amplification for 15 cycles, and the library was subsequently purified using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411). The tagmented DNA was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

The sequencing reads obtained from the Illumina platform underwent quality control using FastQC (v0.11.9) to assess the quality of the reads. Adapter trimming and read filtering were performed using Trim Galore (0.6.5–1) with the following parameters: -phred33 –length 35 –stringency 3. The filtered reads were then aligned to the human genome (UCSC hg38) using Bowtie2 version 2.2.6 with parameters: -local –very-sensitive –no-mixed –no-discordant –phred33. The aligned reads were processed by MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) to call peaks and transformed to bigwig formatted files using deepTools (3.5.6) software. Processed data were visualized using the IGV browser.

Public epigenomic and transcriptomic data collection

To identify ERVDERs in PDAC, we integrated high-throughput epigenomic and transcriptomic sequencing data from multiple publicly available databases. Raw ChIP-seq data for histone modifications and TFs in PDAC cell lines, including H3K4me1, H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, and KLFs, were downloaded from the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) under the accession number PRJNA271302. Histone modification ChIP-seq data and matched RNA-seq data from PDAC tissues were retrieved from datasets ERP023823 and ERP023824 (61). Raw RNA-seq data were also retrieved from ENA dataset PRJNA694728. Furthermore, our previously published CUT&Tag-seq data for H3K4me1, H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and H3K9me3 (GSE247169) were also included to provide additional histone profiling in PDAC cell lines (60). The whole-transcriptome RNA-seq datasets were downloaded from ENA datasets PRJNA487487 and PRJNA632931 (62).

Identification of the enhancer and SE regions

Chromatin state annotation was performed using ChromHMM (v1.22), a hidden Markov model–based tool for segmenting the genome into functional chromatin states. ChIP-seq datasets for histone marks were binarized and input into ChromHMM to annotate the genome into promoter, enhancer, heterochromatin, and quiescent states. Active enhancer regions were defined on the basis of coenrichment of H3K4me1 and H3K27ac and absence of the repressive mark H3K9me3. Signal density plots and heatmaps depicting the enrichment patterns of histone marks at the ERV regions within enhancers were generated through the computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions in the deepTools package (v3.5.6).

To identify SE regions, we applied the ROSE algorithm to H3K27ac ChIP-seq or CUT&Tag data. Peaks were first called using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1), and constituent enhancers within 12.5 kb were stitched together. The stitched enhancers were then ranked based on H3K27ac signal intensity, and SEs were defined as those above the inflection point on the curve.

Mapping ERVs to enhancer regions

To determine the genomic distribution of ERVs within the identified enhancer regions, we retrieved RepeatMasker annotations for the human genome (hg38) from the UCSC Genome Browser. Using bedtools (v2.30.0), ERV coordinates were intersected with ChromHMM-defined active enhancers. ERVs located within these regions were classified as enhancer-localized ERVs or SE-associated ERVs.

Expression profiling and differential analysis of ERVs

To quantify ERV expression in PDAC, we downloaded whole-transcriptome RNA-seq datasets from the ENA (PRJNA487487 and PRJNA632931). Reads were aligned to the hg38 reference genome using HISAT2 (2.2.1), and ERV expression was quantified using featureCounts (1.5.3) with RepeatMasker-based ERV annotations. Differential expression analysis between PDAC and adjacent nontumor tissues was performed using edgeR (v3.30.3). ERVs showing remarkable up-regulation [false discovery rate < 0.05 and log2 fold change (log2FC) >1] and located within active enhancer regions were identified as candidate ERVDERs. Aligned BAM files were converted to BigWig format using the bamCoverage function in deepTools (v3.5.6), with normalization to reads per kilobase per million mapped reads (RPKM), and visualized using the IGV.

The prognostic value of specific PDAC–up-regulated HERVH loci were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with overall survival data from ERP023824 dataset. Survival curves were generated using the survival (3.8-3) and survminer (0.5.0) R packages, and differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

To determine the transcriptional orientation of ERVs, strand-specific whole-transcriptome RNA-seq data from the PRJNA632931 dataset were analyzed. HISAT2-aligned BAM files were separated by strand using SAMtools (1.7, SAMtools view function) with the following flags: -f 128 -F 16 or -f 80. The resulting strand-specific BAM files were then converted to BigWig format for IGV visualization.

Correlation analysis between enhancer activity and ERV expression

To assess the relationship between enhancer activity and ERV expression, we calculated the correlation between H3K27ac ChIP-seq signal intensity (from dataset ERP023823) and ERV transcript levels (from RNA-seq dataset ERP023824). H3K27ac signals and ERV expression values were quantified using the multiBigwigSummary function in deepTools (v3.5.6), with fragments per kilobase per million mapped fragments (FPKM) normalized BigWig files as input. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated in R (v4.4.1).

TF enrichment and motif analysis

The TF enrichment and motif analysis were performed as previously described (63). To identify TFs potentially involved in the activation of enhancer-localized ERVs in pancreatic cancer, we conducted enrichment analysis using ReMapEnrich (v0.99.0). Genomic coordinates of the selected ERV loci were converted into BED format and imported using the bedToGranges function. Enrichment analysis was performed using the “enrichment” function from the ReMapEnrich package against the ReMap2020 hg38 catalog, with the following parameters: fractionQuery = 0.01, fractionCatalog = 0.01, shuffles = 100, and tail = both.

To further evaluate TF binding motif enrichment at these loci, we used the HOMER software (v5.1). The findMotifsGenome.pl script was applied to the BED-formatted file containing the regions of interest, using the hg38 reference genome and default parameters. Background regions were automatically selected by HOMER to match guanine-cytosine (GC) content and region length.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0) and R (v4.4.1). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical significance between groups was assessed using Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
