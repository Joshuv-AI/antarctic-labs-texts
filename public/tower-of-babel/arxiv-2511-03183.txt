Random Schrödinger operator with singular potentials
Travis Kwan

Abstract
We survey the localization theory of random Schrödinger operators with singular single-site distributions, focusing on two regimes: (i) Hölder-continuous laws, where quantitative Wegner estimates enable the classical multiscale analysis (MSA); and (ii) purely atomic (Bernoulli) laws, where the failure of spectral averaging is overcome via quantitative unique continuation principles (UCP). Our discussion covers both lattice and continuum settings and highlights the analytic and combinatorial mechanisms that replace regularity of the single-site measure.

Introduction

Physics background

In his seminal 1958 paper, Anderson proposed that random on–site energies cause
absence of diffusion
by producing exponentially localized eigenstates
[
3
]
. Two decades later, the “one–parameter scaling theory” of Abrahams–Anderson–Licciardello–Ramakrishnan suggested that at zero temperature the conductance obeys a universal renormalization flow in the system size, predicting (among other things) that in
d=1,2
all states tend to localize while in
d\geq 3
a localization–delocalization transition (the Anderson transition) may occur
[
1
]
; see the comprehensive physics review
[
10
]
.

Universality in this context means that the large–scale (infrared) behaviour is determined by symmetry class, dimension, and coarse disorder strength rather than the fine details of the randomness. From this vantage point,
singular
randomness (e.g. Bernoulli) should localize under the same conditions as absolutely continuous laws whenever analytic obstructions can be overcome. Rigorous mathematics confirms this picture in several regimes: early multiscale proofs at large disorder or near spectral edges
[
12
,
11
]
, fractional–moment methods, and—for singular laws—quantitative UCP replacing spectral averaging
[
5
]
.

The universality motif also resonates with classical disordered media, such as percolation and kinetic Lorentz gases. Results on sharp phase transitions and enhancement in percolation
[
15
,
14
]
and studies of geometric scattering models (e.g. Lorentz mirror and Manhattan pinball) furnish instructive analogies for transport suppression and combinatorial control in discrete UCP arguments; see
[
20
,
21
]
.

Model and scope

We consider the Anderson Hamiltonian

H_{\omega}=-\Delta+V_{\omega},

on either the continuum
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
(V_{\omega}(x))_{x}
are i.i.d. single–site random variables. When the common law has a bounded density, the combination of a Wegner estimate and MSA (or the fractional–moment method) yields localization near spectral edges or at large disorder
[
12
,
11
]
. The focus here is the
singular
setting:

•

Hölder–continuous laws
(no density required): one can still derive quantitative Wegner estimates with exponent equal to the Hölder index, hence run MSA
[
7
,
13
]
.

•

Bernoulli/atomic laws
: spectral averaging fails; nevertheless, localization can be proved by replacing it with a quantitative UCP. In the continuum this goes via Carleman estimates (Bourgain–Kenig
[
5
]
; see also
[
16
,
17
,
25
]
); on the lattice the UCP must be reimagined to account for lower–dimensional supports of solutions and relies on discrete geometry and Boolean/combinatorial tools (Li–Zhang in
d=3

[
19
]
, and Li in
d=2
at large disorder
[
22
]
).

Organization.

[

,

]
. Section

Motivation from universality

Percolation theory exemplifies universality: macroscopic connectivity undergoes a sharp transition controlled only by dimension and local symmetries, with critical behaviour insensitive to microscopic details
[
15
]
. In disordered quantum systems, the Anderson model provides a quantum analog: wave interference in random media yields phase diagrams and criticality governed by
d
and symmetry class
[
10
,
18
,
2
]
. Foundational scaling arguments and finite-size scaling numerics support the universality picture across dimensions and boundary conditions
[
1
,
24
,
26
]
. Thus, singular single–site laws should not, by themselves, destroy localization mechanisms. In practice, the mathematical route is:
quantitative UCP
\Rightarrow
Wegner estimate (without densities)
\Rightarrow
MSA
. This was made explicit in the continuum Bernoulli model
[
5
]
and adapted, with new geometry, to the lattice in three dimensions by Li–Zhang; see Section
3
. We also note instructive classical analogs such as the
Manhattan pinball
and
Lorentz mirror
models, where geometry and combinatorics govern long–time transport
[
21
,
20
]
. On the experimental side, wave-localization observations with light and ultracold atoms further illustrate universality of the mechanism beyond electrons
[
28
,
4
,
8
]
. Finally, the original physics derivation of the (mathematical) Wegner bound has roots in random-matrix and field-theoretic treatments of disordered media
[
27
]
.

2
Hölder–continuous single–site laws: Wegner
\Rightarrow
MSA

In this section we work on the lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
(the continuum case requires additional unique continuation input but the MSA structure is the same). We write the Anderson Hamiltonian in finite volume as

H_{\omega,\Lambda}\;=\;H_{0,\Lambda}\;+\;V_{\omega,\Lambda}\;=\;(-\Delta)_{\Lambda}\;+\;\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\omega_{x}\,\Pi_{x},

where
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a cube with Dirichlet boundary condition,
\Pi_{x}
is the rank-one projector onto
\delta_{x}
, and
\{\omega_{x}\}_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are i.i.d. with common law
\mu
.

2.1
Multiscale framework and initial length scale

We fix the (deterministic) scale sequence
L_{k+1}=L_{k}^{1+\eta}
with
L_{0}
large and
\eta\in(0,1/10)
, and the geometric cubes

\Lambda_{L}(x):=x+\{-\lfloor L/2\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor L/2\rfloor\}^{d}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Write
\partial_{-}\Lambda_{L}(x):=\{y\in\Lambda_{L}(x):\mathrm{dist}(y,\,\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\Lambda_{L}(x))=1\}
for the inner boundary. For
E\in\mathbb{R}
we denote the resolvent

G_{\Lambda_{L}(x)}(E):=\bigl(H_{\omega,\Lambda_{L}(x)}-E\bigr)^{-1},\quad\text{whenever }E\notin\sigma\bigl(H_{\omega,\Lambda_{L}(x)}\bigr).

Definition 2.1

((E,
m
)-good/nonresonant boxes)
.

Fix parameters
m\in(0,1)
and
\zeta\in(0,1)
.
A cube
\Lambda_{L}(x)
is called:

•

E
-nonresonant
if
\mathrm{dist}\!\bigl(E,\sigma(H_{\omega,\Lambda_{L}(x)})\bigr)\geq e^{-L^{\zeta}}
.

•

(E,m)
-good
if it is
E
-nonresonant and

\max_{y\in\partial_{-}\Lambda_{L}(x)}\bigl|G_{\Lambda_{L}(x)}(E;x,y)\bigr|\;\leq\;e^{-mL}.

Otherwise it is called
(E,m)
-bad
.

Remark 2.2

(Combes–Thomas control)
.

If
\mathrm{dist}\!\bigl(E,\sigma(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\bigr)\geq\delta>0
, then the (standard) Combes–Thomas estimate yields exponential off-diagonal decay

\bigl|G_{\Lambda}(E;u,v)\bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{2}{\delta}\,\exp\!\bigl(-c\,\delta\,|u-v|\bigr),

for some dimension–dependent
c>0
; cf. its use in
[
12
,
11
]
.
Thus,
E
-nonresonance implies
(E,m)
-goodness as soon as
\delta\gtrsim L^{-1}
.

We record the probabilistic inputs used in the MSA:

Definition 2.3

(Wegner bound)
.

We say that a
Wegner estimate
holds on an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
if there exist constants
C_{W}>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that for every cube
\Lambda
and every subinterval
J\subset I
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{J}\bigl(H_{\omega,\Lambda}\bigr)\bigr]\;\leq\;C_{W}\,|\Lambda|\,|J|^{\alpha}.

Definition 2.4

(Initial length scale (ILS))
.

An
initial length scale
at energy
E\in I
is a pair
(L_{0},m_{0})
such that for some
\kappa>2d
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Lambda_{L_{0}}(x)\text{ is $(E,m_{0})$-good}\right)\;\geq\;1-L_{0}^{-\kappa}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

The next two theorems codify the MSA as used in this survey.

Theorem 2.5

(Induction step of MSA)
.

Fix
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and suppose a Wegner estimate (Definition
2.3
) holds on
I
with exponent
\alpha>0
. Let
L_{k+1}=L_{k}^{1+\eta}
and assume that for some
m_{k}\in(0,1)
and
\kappa>2d
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Lambda_{L_{k}}(x)\text{ is $(E,m_{k})$-good}\right)\;\geq\;1-L_{k}^{-\kappa}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\text{ and all }E\in I.

Then there exist
m_{k+1}\in(0,m_{k})
and
\kappa^{\prime}>2d
such that

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Lambda_{L_{k+1}}(x)\text{ is $(E,m_{k+1})$-good}\right)\;\geq\;1-L_{k+1}^{-\kappa^{\prime}}\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\text{ and all }E\in I.

Moreover,
m_{k+1}\to m_{\infty}>0
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof sketch.

The proof follows the bootstrap scheme (cf.
[
13
]
): cover
\Lambda_{L_{k+1}}
by well-separated subcubes of side
L_{k}
, use independence at distance to control the number of
(E,m_{k})
-bad subcubes, apply the geometric resolvent identity to connect the center to the boundary through a chain of good boxes, and bound the (rare) resonant events via the Wegner estimate. The probability improvement and a small loss in
m
come from union bounds and boundary–layer combinatorics.
∎

Theorem 2.6

(Initial length scale)
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an energy interval near the spectral bottom or fix any compact
I
and assume the disorder is sufficiently large. Then there exist
L_{0}
and
m_{0}>0
such that the ILS condition in Definition
2.4
holds for all
E\in I
(cf.
[
12
,
11
]
).

Remark 2.7
.

The ILS is obtained by Lifshitz-tail methods near the bottom (giving high probability of nonresonance and hence
(E,m_{0})
-goodness), or by large-disorder resolvent expansions; the specific mechanism is not needed here but is standard in the literature on MSA
[
12
,
11
]
.

Combining Theorems
2.5
and
2.6
yields spectral/dynamical localization on
I
. The remainder of this section proves a Wegner bound for Hölder–continuous
\mu
, which is the essential input (Definition
2.3
).

2.2
Wegner estimate under Hölder continuity

Set

s(\mu,\varepsilon):=\sup_{E\in\mathbb{R}}\mu([E-\varepsilon,E+\varepsilon]),\qquad\varepsilon>0.

We assume throughout that
\mu
is non-degenerate and Hölder with exponent
\alpha\in(0,1]
, i.e.
s(\mu,\varepsilon)\leq C\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}
for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.

Theorem 2.8

(Wegner for the discrete Anderson model with Hölder law)
.

Let
H_{\omega,\Lambda}
be the finite-volume discrete Anderson Hamiltonian on
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with i.i.d. single–site law
\mu
. Then for every interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\bigr]\;\leq\;C\,|\Lambda|\,s(\mu,|I|).

In particular, if
\mu
is Hölder of order
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\bigr]\;\leq\;C\,|\Lambda|\,|I|^{\alpha}.

The constant
C
depends only on
d
and on an a priori energy bound for
I
.

Proof.

We give a streamlined proof exploiting the rank–one structure (see also
[
7
,
13
]
for related approaches and consequences).

Step 1 (single–site variation and spectral counting).

Fix
x\in\Lambda
and freeze all
(\omega_{y})_{y\neq x}
. Consider

H(t):=H_{\omega,\Lambda}+(t-\omega_{x})\,\Pi_{x},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

which is a rank–one perturbation path. Let
N_{I}(H):=\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H)
denote the finite-volume eigenvalue counting function. Since a rank–one perturbation changes
N_{I}
by at most
1
when
t
is varied across a point where an eigenvalue crosses the endpoints of
I
, the spectral shift function
\xi_{x}(\lambda;t,t^{\prime})
along this path satisfies
|\xi_{x}(\lambda;t,t^{\prime})|\leq 1
for all
\lambda
and
t,t^{\prime}
.

Step 2 (averaging in
t
against a singular measure).

Let
I=[E-\varepsilon,E+\varepsilon]
with
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
. Fix
t^{\prime}<t
and write

N_{I}\bigl(H(t)\bigr)-N_{I}\bigl(H(t^{\prime})\bigr)\;=\;\int_{I}\xi_{x}(\lambda;t,t^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda,\qquad|\xi_{x}|\leq 1.

Hence
\bigl|N_{I}\bigl(H(t)\bigr)-N_{I}\bigl(H(t^{\prime})\bigr)\bigr|\leq|I|
.
Let
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\cdot]
denote expectation with respect to
\omega_{x}
alone (keeping the other sites fixed). Choose
t^{\prime}<t
so that
\mu((t^{\prime},t])=\mu([t-\varepsilon,t+\varepsilon])
and integrate in
t
against
\mu
by the layer-cake formula:

\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigl[N_{I}(H(\omega_{x}))\bigr]\;\leq\;\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\mu([u-\varepsilon,u+\varepsilon])\;=\;s(\mu,\varepsilon).

(The point is that
N_{I}(H(\cdot))
has total variation at most
|I|
along the rank–one path, and integrating this variation against
\mu
can only pick up mass in a window of width
\varepsilon
.)

Step 3 (summation over sites).

By spectral decomposition,

\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\;=\;\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\!\bigl\langle\delta_{x},\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\,\delta_{x}\bigr\rangle\;\leq\;\sum_{x\in\Lambda}N_{I}\bigl(H_{\omega,\Lambda}^{(x)}\bigr),

where
H_{\omega,\Lambda}^{(x)}
denotes
H_{\omega,\Lambda}
regarded as a function of the single variable
\omega_{x}
with the others frozen (this estimate is standard: the spectral projection is positive, and each diagonal matrix element is bounded by the number of eigenvalues in
I
). Taking full expectation and using the single–site bound from Step 2,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathrm{Tr}\,\mathbf{1}_{I}(H_{\omega,\Lambda})\bigr]\;\leq\;\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\,\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigl[N_{I}\bigl(H_{\omega,\Lambda}^{(x)}\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr]\;\leq\;|\Lambda|\;s(\mu,\varepsilon).

This is the claimed bound with
C=1
(up to an immaterial energy–dependent constant if one first truncates to a bounded energy window).
∎

Remark 2.9

(About proofs and variants)
.

The argument above is a distilled version of standard proofs of Wegner estimates for singular laws on the lattice; see
[
7
,
13
]
for comprehensive treatments (including continuum/alloy models) and further consequences such as continuity of the IDS.

Corollary 1

(Wegner estimate
\Rightarrow
MSA)
.

Assume
\mu
is Hölder with exponent
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Then Theorem
2.8
yields Definition
2.3
with the same
\alpha
, and Theorems
2.5
–
2.6
imply spectral/dynamical localization on any interval
I
where an ILS holds (near spectral edges or at large disorder)
[
13
]
.

3
Bernoulli potentials: quantitative unique continuation and antichains

We work with the discrete Anderson–Bernoulli Hamiltonian on a finite box

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with Dirichlet boundary conditions,

H_{\omega,\Lambda}\;=\;-\Delta_{\Lambda}\;+\;V_{\omega}|_{\Lambda},\qquad V_{\omega}(x)=\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\omega_{z}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{z\}}(x),\quad\omega_{z}\in\{0,1\}\ \text{i.i.d.}

(Everything below adapts verbatim to the alloy/bump versions.) We denote by

\sigma(H_{\omega,\Lambda})
its spectrum and by
G_{\omega,\Lambda}(E)=(H_{\omega,\Lambda}-E)^{-1}

its resolvent when defined.

Throughout, if
Q\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a discrete cube we write
\ell(Q)
for its side length and

\frac{1}{2}Q
for the concentric subcube with half side length. On
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
we will also use the

45^{\circ}
“tilted” coordinates

(s,t)=(x+y,\ x-y),\qquad D_{k}^{\pm}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\ x\pm y=k\},

and the associated tilted rectangles/squares (Definition
3.3
below).

3.1
Continuum quantitative unique continuation

The Bernoulli Wegner mechanism in the continuum relies on a
quantitative unique continuation principle
(UCP), which forbids an eigenfunction to be too small on every mesoscopic ball once it is nontrivial on a nearby ball. One convenient form is a three–ball/doubling inequality
[
16
,
17
,
5
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Balls and norms)
.

For
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
let
B_{r}(x_{0})
be the open Euclidean ball.
For a function
u
and set
A
write
\|u\|_{L^{2}(A)}^{2}=\int_{A}|u|^{2}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Quantitative UCP in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

Fix
d\geq 2
and
M\geq 1
. There exist
C,\theta\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
d
and
M
such that:
if
u\in H^{2}(B_{R}(x_{0}))
solves
(-\Delta+V)u=\lambda u
in
B_{R}(x_{0})
with
\|V\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\leq M
and
|\lambda|\leq M
, then for any
0<r<R/2
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{r}(x_{0}))}\ \geq\ C\,\Big(\frac{r}{R}\Big)^{\!\theta}\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R}(x_{0}))}.

Equivalently, there are
C,\eta\in(0,\infty)
so that for any balls
B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})\subset B_{r_{2}}(x_{0})\subset B_{r_{3}}(x_{0})\subset B_{R}(x_{0})
with
r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{2}})}\ \leq\ C\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{1}})}^{\eta}\,\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{r_{3}})}^{1-\eta}.

Idea of proof.

A Carleman estimate with a logarithmic/convex weight yields a weighted
L^{2}
inequality for
u
against
(-\Delta+V-\lambda)u=0
.
After a standard cut–off/commutator argument one obtains a three–ball inequality, from which the stated doubling follows by interpolation and a covering argument; see
[
16
,
17
,
5
]
. Sharp obstructions to stronger decay are due to Meshkov
[
25
]
.
∎

In Bourgain–Kenig’s localization scheme this UCP is combined with a selection of
free sites
in each box: if
u
is an almost–eigenfunction and it has nontrivial mass on many of those sites, then flipping the Bernoulli variables on a subfamily moves the eigenvalue by a definite amount, which gives a Wegner bound at scale; the multiscale analysis (MSA) then proceeds as usual (see Subsection
3.3
below for the combinatorial ingredient). For an overview contextualizing the UCP within the BK scheme, see
[
5
]
.

3.2
Discrete UCP on the lattice and the role of frozen/free sites

A
continuum
UCP is false on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in general: one can build
u\not\equiv 0
with
(-\Delta+V)u=0
supported on a lower–dimensional set when
V
is tailored appropriately. The Ding–Smart approach develops a
probabilistic
discrete UCP, robust enough for MSA, which we restate here in a deterministic–probabilistic form after introducing the geometry in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

[
9
]
.

Definition 3.3

(Tilted rectangles/squares and diagonal sparsity)
.

For intervals
I,J\subset\mathbb{Z}
, the
tilted rectangle
is

R_{I,J}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\ x+y\in I,\ x-y\in J\},

and a
tilted square

Q
has side length
\ell(Q)=|I|=|J|
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, the
\pm
–diagonals are
D_{k}^{\pm}=\{(x,y):x\pm y=k\}
.
Given
F\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we say
F
is
(\delta,\pm)
–sparse in
R
if
|D_{k}^{\pm}\cap F\cap R|\leq\delta\,|D_{k}^{\pm}\cap R|
for all
k
, and
\delta
–sparse
if both
(\delta,+)
and
(\delta,-)
hold.
We say
F
is
\delta
–regular in
E\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
if whenever
F
is not
\delta
–sparse in each of a pairwise disjoint family of tilted squares
\{Q_{j}\}\subset E
, then
\sum_{j}|Q_{j}|\leq\delta\,|E|
.

Definition 3.4

(Frozen and free sites)
.

Fix a tilted square
Q\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Let
F\subset Q
be the (possibly empty) set of
frozen
sites whose Bernoulli values are fixed; the complement
S=Q\setminus F
are the
free
sites.
A configuration
v:F\to\{0,1\}
induces the conditional law on
S
and an operator
H_{\omega,Q}
with Dirichlet boundary on
Q
.

Theorem 3.5

(Discrete quantitative UCP with frozen sites)
.

For every small
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\alpha>1
such that the following holds.
Let
Q\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be a tilted square with
\ell(Q)\geq\alpha
, and let
F\subset Q
be
\varepsilon
–regular in
Q
.
Define the event
E_{\mathrm{uc}}(Q,F)
that
for any
energy
\lambda
within
e^{-\alpha(\ell(Q)\log\ell(Q))^{1/2}}
of a spectral value and any (Dirichlet) eigenfunction
H_{\omega,Q}\psi=\lambda\psi
normalized by
\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\infty}(Q)}\leq 1
on a
1-\varepsilon(\ell(Q)\log\ell(Q))^{-1/2}
fraction of
Q\setminus F
, we have the growth bound

\|\psi\|_{\ell^{\infty}(\tfrac{1}{2}Q)}\;\leq\;\exp\big(\alpha\,\ell(Q)\log\ell(Q)\big).

Then, for each fixed
v:F\to\{0,1\}
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left(E_{\mathrm{uc}}(Q,F)\mid V|_{F}=v\right)\ \geq\ 1-\exp\big(-\varepsilon\,\ell(Q)^{1/4}\big).

Idea of proof.

The argument organizes
Q
into a Vitali–type family of tilted subsquares on which
F
is sparse on most diagonals; on such subsquares a deterministic “expansion” estimate controls the
\ell^{\infty}
growth of
\psi
from a small exceptional set to a larger set. A multi–scale pigeonholing then yields the claimed growth in
\frac{1}{2}Q
with high conditional probability; see the geometric covering and expansion steps in
[
9
, Sec. 3]
. For discrete-harmonic inspiration behind “large portion
\Rightarrow
rigidity,” compare
[
6
]
.
∎

Eigenvalue displacement under a single-site flip

We formalize the quantitative effect on eigenvalues when flipping a Bernoulli variable at a free site.
Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be finite with Dirichlet boundary, fix
x\in\Lambda
, and set

H(t)\;:=\;H_{0}+\sum_{y\in\Lambda\setminus\{x\}}\omega_{y}\,\Pi_{y}\;+\;t\,\Pi_{x},\qquad t\in[0,1],

where
\Pi_{x}
is the rank–one projector onto
\delta_{x}
, and the
\omega_{y}\in\{0,1\}
are fixed.
Thus
H(0)
and
H(1)
correspond to the two Bernoulli values at
x
with all other sites frozen.

Definition 3.6

(Spectral data along the rank–one path)
.

Since
H(t)
depends analytically on
t
and acts on a finite–dimensional space, its eigenvalues can be labeled by analytic branches

\{\lambda_{j}(t)\}_{j=1}^{|\Lambda|}
with associated normalized eigenvectors
\psi_{j}(t)\in\ell^{2}(\Lambda)
(piecewise analytic across possible crossing times). We write
\psi_{j}(t;x):=\langle\delta_{x},\psi_{j}(t)\rangle
for the amplitude at
x
.

Lemma 3.7

(Hellmann–Feynman for rank–one flips)
.

Whenever
\lambda_{j}(t)
is simple at
t=t_{0}
, the branch is real analytic near
t_{0}
and satisfies

\lambda_{j}^{\prime}(t_{0})\;=\;\big\langle\psi_{j}(t_{0}),\,\Pi_{x}\,\psi_{j}(t_{0})\big\rangle\;=\;|\psi_{j}(t_{0};x)|^{2}.

Consequently, on any open subinterval
J\subset[0,1]
on which
\lambda_{j}(t)
remains simple,

\lambda_{j}(t_{2})-\lambda_{j}(t_{1})\;=\;\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}|\psi_{j}(s;x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\qquad(t_{1}<t_{2}\in J).

Proof.

This is the classical Hellmann–Feynman identity for analytic eigenbranches of a self–adjoint analytic family.
∎

Definition 3.8

(Target window and blocking mass)
.

Fix an energy window
I=[E-\eta,E+\eta]
with
\eta\in(0,1)
.
Given
m_{*}\in(0,1)
we say that
x
is an
m_{*}
–blocking site for
I
at level
t
if

\text{for every normalized eigenpair }H(t)\psi=\lambda\psi\ \text{with}\ \lambda\in I,\quad|\psi(x)|\geq m_{*}.

If this holds for every
t\in[0,1]
we say
x
is
m_{*}
–blocking for the path
t\in[0,1]
.

Lemma 3.9

(Flip ejects an eigenbranch from a short window)
.

Assume
x
is
m_{*}
–blocking for the path
t\in[0,1]
.
Let
j
be such that
\lambda_{j}(0)\in I
and suppose
\lambda_{j}(t)
is simple on
[0,1]
(or piecewise simple with no net multiplicity exchange within
I
).
Then

\lambda_{j}(1)-\lambda_{j}(0)\;=\;\int_{0}^{1}|\psi_{j}(t;x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\ \geq\ \int_{0}^{1}m_{*}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\;=\;m_{*}^{2}.

In particular, if
\eta<m_{*}^{2}/2
, then
\lambda_{j}(1)\notin I
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.7
and the blocking property,
|\psi_{j}(t;x)|\geq m_{*}
for all
t\in[0,1]
, hence the integral lower bound.
If
\lambda_{j}(1)\in I
, then
|\lambda_{j}(1)-\lambda_{j}(0)|\leq 2\eta<m_{*}^{2}
, a contradiction.
∎

Remark 3.10

(Simplicity and window separation)
.

In finite volume, simplicity of
\lambda_{j}(t)
on
[0,1]
holds except for finitely many
t
’s; one can either exclude those
t
by a negligible enlargement of
\eta
or argue piecewise on intervals of simplicity. A standard alternative is to impose a
gap condition

\min(\lambda_{j+1}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t),\lambda_{j}(t)-\lambda_{j-1}(t))>2\eta
for all
t
, which is typical once
I
is chosen sufficiently small. Either route is sufficient for applications below; see also the min–max uses in
[
9
]
.

Proposition 3.11

(Blocking sets from UCP and ejection under a flip)
.

Let
Q\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a finite box (tilted in
d=2
if desired), and let
F\subset Q
be the frozen sites. Suppose a quantitative (discrete) UCP holds on
Q
at scale
\ell(Q)
with parameters that imply:
there exist constants
c_{0},C_{0}>0
and, for each configuration of the frozen variables, a set
B\subset S:=Q\setminus F
with
|B|\geq c_{0}|S|
such that for every
x\in B
and every normalized eigenpair
H(t)\psi=\lambda\psi
with
\lambda\in I
(for any
t\in[0,1]
along the flip path at
x
) one has

|\psi(x)|\ \geq\ m_{*}(Q)\ :=\ \exp\!\big(-C_{0}\,\ell(Q)\,\log\ell(Q)\big).

Then each
x\in B
is
m_{*}(Q)
–blocking for the path at
x
, and therefore by Lemma
3.9
every eigenbranch starting in
I
is ejected from
I
after flipping
x
provided
\eta<\tfrac{1}{2}m_{*}(Q)^{2}
.

Proof.

The hypothesis is precisely the pathwise blocking property at all
x\in B
, with a uniform
m_{*}(Q)
; the conclusion follows from Lemma
3.9
.
∎

Remark 3.12

(From blocking sets to a
\rho
–Sperner family)
.

Fix the frozen pattern on
F
and define

\mathcal{A}_{I}\ :=\ \{A\subset S:\ \sigma(H_{F\to v,\ S\to A})\cap I\neq\varnothing\}.

For each
A\in\mathcal{A}_{I}
choose an eigenbranch
\lambda_{j}^{(A)}(t)
with
\lambda_{j}^{(A)}(0)\in I
.
Let
B(A)\subset S\setminus A
be the UCP–blocking set given by Proposition
3.11
(constructed for the box
Q
at the same scale).
If
A^{\prime}\supset A
and
A^{\prime}\cap B(A)\neq\emptyset
, then there is an
x\in B(A)
with
x\in A^{\prime}\setminus A
; flipping at
x
moves
\lambda_{j}^{(A)}
by at least
m_{*}(Q)^{2}>2\eta
, hence
\sigma(H_{F\to v,S\to A^{\prime}})\cap I=\varnothing
.
Thus
\mathcal{A}_{I}
is
\rho
–Sperner with
\rho\geq c_{0}
(up to the small fraction of exceptional cases where the UCP fails, which occur with tiny probability and can be union–bounded). This is the combinatorial bridge to a Bernoulli–Wegner estimate (cf.
[
9
]
).

3.3
A combinatorial antichain bound: Sperner’s theorem and its
\rho
–Sperner variant

Let
S\subset Q
be the free sites and identify each
\omega|_{S}
with a subset
A\subset S
(the set where
\omega_{x}=1
).
Fix a target energy window
I=[E-\eta,E+\eta]
and consider the property

\mathcal{A}\ :=\ \bigl\{A\subset S:\ \sigma\bigl(H_{F\to v,\ S\to A}\bigr)\cap I\neq\emptyset\bigr\}.

Monotonicity of
A\mapsto\lambda_{k}(H_{F\to v,S\to A})
generally
fails
on the lattice (the eigenvalue can move both ways under flips), so the set
\mathcal{A}
need not be an antichain in the classical sense.
What
does
hold under the UCP is a
forbidden–augmentation
property: for any
A\in\mathcal{A}
there is a large set
B(A)\subset S\setminus A
such that
none
of the supersets
A^{\prime}\supset A
with
A^{\prime}\cap B(A)\neq\emptyset
belong to
\mathcal{A}
.
This leads to the following notion.

Definition 3.13

(
\rho
–Sperner family)
.

A family
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{S}
is
\rho
–Sperner
if for every
A\in\mathcal{A}
there exists a
blocking set

B(A)\subset S\setminus A
with
|B(A)|\geq\rho\,(|S|-|A|)
and such that

A\subset A^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\ \Rightarrow\ A^{\prime}\cap B(A)=\varnothing
.

Theorem 3.14

(Generalized Sperner bound)
.

If
\mathcal{A}\subset 2^{S}
is
\rho
–Sperner with
\rho\in(0,1]
and
|S|=n
, then

|\mathcal{A}|\ \leq\ 2^{n}\,n^{-1/2}\,\rho^{-1}.

Sketch.

Adapt Lubell’s counting method over all permutations of
S
: for each
A\in\mathcal{A}
the
\rho
–Sperner condition limits the expected number of times
A
appears as an initial segment in a random permutation by a geometric decay
(1-\rho)^{j}
after the first hit, which sums to
\rho^{-1}
. Combine with
\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{n}n^{-1/2}
uniformly in
k
to get the bound.
∎

How UCP produces
\rho
–Sperner.

Fix
A\in\mathcal{A}
and choose a normalized eigenpair
(\lambda,\psi)
of
H_{F\to v,S\to A}
with
\lambda\in I
.
By the discrete UCP (Theorem
3.5
), there is a subset
B(A)\subset S\setminus A
with
|B(A)|\geq c\,|S|
such that

many
sites in
B(A)
have pointwise mass
|\psi(x)|\geq e^{-C\ell(Q)\log\ell(Q)}
. The min–max principle and first–order
eigenvalue perturbation (via rank–one updates) imply that flipping
\omega_{x}
at any such
x
moves
every
eigenvalue of

H_{F\to v,S\to A}
by at least
\kappa\,|\psi(x)|^{2}\gtrsim e^{-C\ell(Q)\log\ell(Q)}
, uniformly in
x\in B(A)
(cf.
[
9
]
).
Hence, for
\eta
sufficiently smaller than this scale,
any
augmentation of
A
that includes at least one element of

B(A)
ejects
\lambda
from
I
after re–labeling. This is exactly the
\rho
–Sperner condition with
\rho\asymp c
.

Wegner bound for Bernoulli.

Applying Theorem
3.14
to
\mathcal{A}
and dividing by
2^{|S|}
(the number of Bernoulli configurations on free sites) yields

\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sigma(H_{\omega,Q})\cap I\neq\emptyset\ \big|\ V|_{F}=v\right)\;\leq\;C\,|S|^{-1/2}\,\rho^{-1},

uniformly in the frozen pattern
v
as long as the discrete UCP holds (which it does with high conditional probability by Theorem
3.5
). This is the Bernoulli–Wegner estimate at scale
Q
, which is the key input for the MSA in the Bernoulli setting.

3.4
Remarks

On
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, the Ding–Smart UCP is probabilistic and exploits the polynomial structure of discrete harmonic functions in tilted coordinates together with a multi–scale covering and expansion argument; the
\rho
–Sperner bound above is proved in a form that suffices for their scheme
[
9
]
. In higher dimensions, the 3D lattice case requires a
deterministic
discrete UCP and a refined geometric understanding of the nonzero set (pyramids/cones) to prevent lower–dimensional support: this is carried out in the work of Li–Zhang (3D)
[
19
]
and Li (2D at large disorder)
[
22
,
23
]
, whose multiscale inputs integrate with the same Sperner-type combinatorics to furnish a Wegner bound and hence localization.
