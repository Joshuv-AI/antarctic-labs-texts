High-dimensional sliced inverse regression with endogeneity
Linh H. Nghiem, Francis K.C. Hui, Samuel Muller, A.H. Welsh

Abstract
Sliced inverse regression is a popular sufficient dimension reduction method that identifies a small number of linear transformations of the covariates without losing regression information with the response. In high-dimensional settings, it can be combined with sparsity penalties to achieve simultaneous sufficient dimension reduction and variable selection. Both classical and sparse SIR estimators assume the covariates are exogenous. However, endogeneity can arise in a variety of situations, such as when variables are omitted or are measured with error. In this article, we show that such situations invalidate SIR and lead to inconsistent estimation of the true central subspace. To address this challenge, we propose a two-stage lasso SIR estimator, which first constructs a sparse high-dimensional instrumental variables model to obtain fitted values of the covariates spanned by the instruments, and then applies SIR augmented with a lasso penalty to these fitted values. We establish theoretical bounds for the estimation and selection consistency of the true dimension reduction subspace for the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator, allowing the number of covariates and instruments to grow exponentially with the sample size. Simulation studies and applications to two real-world datasets in nutrition and genetics illustrate the superior empirical performance of the proposed estimator, compared with existing methods which ignore endogeneity and/or nonlinearity in the outcome model. Keywords : Dimension reduction, high-dimension, instrumental variable, lasso, measurement error, statistical genomics

Introduction

Sufficient dimension reduction
(
Ma and Zhu,, 2013
;
Li,, 2018
, SDR)
refers to a class of methods that assume the outcome
y\in\mathbb{R}
depends on a set of random covariates
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
only through a small number of linear combinations
\bm{\beta}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{d}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
, with
d\ll p
. These linear combinations are known as sufficient predictors, and retain the full regression information between the response and covariates. More specifically, letting
\mathbf{B}=[\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{d}]
denote a
p\times d
matrix, we can formulate SDR as a multiple index model

y=f(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\varepsilon),

(1)

where
f
denotes an unknown link function, and
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
is random noise. The target of estimation is then the column space of
\mathbf{B}
, also known as the central subspace.
Since the pioneering work of
Li, (1991)
, a vast literature has developed on different sufficient dimension reduction approaches, ranging from inverse-moment-based and regression-based methods
(
Cook and Forzani,, 2008
;
Bura et al.,, 2022
, e.g.,)
, to forward regression
(
Xia et al.,, 2002
;
Dai et al.,, 2024
, e.g.,)
and semiparametric techniques
(
Zhou and Zhu,, 2016
;
Sheng and Yin,, 2016
, e.g.,)
. Among the various SDR methods, SIR
(
Li,, 1991
)
remains the most widely used due to its simplicity and computational efficiency, and as such continues to be the subject of much research
(see
Tan et al.,, 2018
;
Xu et al.,, 2022
, among many others)
. When the number of covariates
p
is large, it is often (further) assumed each linear combination depends only on a few covariates, i.e., the vectors
\bm{\beta}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{d}
are sparse. Recent literature has thus sought to combine SIR (and other SDR methods) with variable selection to achieve sparse sufficient dimension reduction
(
Lin et al.,, 2018
;
Lin et al.,, 2019
;
Nghiem et al., 2023c,
, e.g.,)
.

Much of the literature dedicated to SIR and many other SDR methods, including those mentioned above, has focused on the setting where all covariates are exogenous, i.e. in model (
1
),
\mathbf{X}
and
\varepsilon
are independent,
implying
y\perp\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
, where ‘
\perp
’ denotes statistical independence. However, this assumption may not be reasonable in settings where the random noise
\varepsilon
is dependent on
\mathbf{X}
, as in the case where the covariates are endogenous. Little has been done to tackle sufficient dimension reduction with endogeneity, with the following two common scenarios. First,
omitted-variable bias
occurs when one or more (truly important) covariates is not included in the model. In Section
7.1
for instance, we consider a genomic study relating a biological outcome (e.g., body weight) to gene expressions of mice. Outside of gene expressions, there can be potentially unmeasured confounding factors, such as those related to environmental conditions, which influence both gene expression levels and the outcome.
Second,
covariate measurement error
arises when one or more of the covariates are not measured accurately. For instance, in a second data application in Section
7.2
, we consider a nutritional study relating an individual’s health outcomes to their long–term diet, collected through 24–hour recall interviews that are well-known to be subject to measurement errors
(
Carroll et al.,, 2006
)
. Let
\mathbf{X}
denote the long-term dietary covariates. The observed data can then be modeled as
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{E}
, with
\mathbf{E}
denoting an additive measurement error independent of
\mathbf{X}
. In this situation, even when in the true model (
1
) it holds that
\varepsilon\perp\mathbf{X}
, a naive analysis which replaces
\mathbf{X}
by
\mathbf{W}
results in endogeneity, since
y=f(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{W}+\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{E},\varepsilon)
and
\mathbf{E}
is correlated with
\mathbf{W}
. This causes naive estimators that ignore measurement errors to be inconsistent.

In this article, we show under general conditions that endogeneity invalidates SIR, leading to inconsistent estimates of the true column space of
\mathbf{B}
. To address this challenge, we propose a two-stage lasso SIR method to estimate the central space in the presence of endogeneity, when valid instrumental variables are available. In the first stage, we construct a sparse multivariate linear model regressing all the covariates against the instruments, and obtain fitted values from this model. In the second stage, we apply a sparse SIR between the response and the fitted values from the first stage to perform simultaneous SDR and variable selection.

Addressing endogeneity via instrumental variables has been extensively studied in the econometrics and statistics literature; see
Angrist and Krueger, (2001)
for a review.
However, classical instrumental variable estimators are mostly studied with a linear model for the outcome, i.e.,
y=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\varepsilon
, and usually compute a two-stage least squares or a limited information maximum likelihood estimator.
Hansen et al., (2008)
showed in this setting that these estimators are consistent for
\bm{\beta}_{0}
when the number of instruments
q
increases slowly with the sample size. More recently, a number of methods have combined instrumental variables with regularization techniques on the covariates and/or instrumental variables to improve their performance in high-dimensional settings e.g., the lasso-type generalized methods of moments
(
Caner,, 2009
;
Caner et al.,, 2018
)
, focused generalized methods of moments
(
Fan and Liao,, 2014
)
, and two-stage penalized least squares
(
Lin et al.,, 2015
)
; the last of these methods inspires this article. Another line of research has focused on the use of sparsity-inducing penalties to estimate optimal instruments from a large set of a-priori valid instruments or some variation thereof
(
Belloni et al.,, 2012
;
Chernozhukov et al.,, 2015
;
Windmeijer et al.,, 2019
, e.g.,)
.
To the best of our knowledge though, none of these methods allow for the presence of non-linear link functions in the model for the response e.g., the multiple index model in (
1
). While there is some related literature on single index models with endogeneity
(
Hu et al.,, 2015
;
Gao et al.,, 2020
)
, their approach requires approximation of the link function and is not easy to generalize to multiple index models.

Under a setting where both the number of covariates
p
and the number of instrumental variables
q
can grow exponentially with sample size
n
, we derive theoretical bounds on the estimation and selection consistency of our proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator. Simulation studies demonstrate the proposed estimator exhibits superior finite sample performance compared with a single-stage lasso SIR estimator when endogeneity is present but ignored, and the two-stage regularized estimator of
Lin et al., (2015)
which accounts for endogeneity but assumes the outcome model is linear in nature. We apply the proposed method to two aforementioned real data applications studying the effects of different nutrients on the cholesterol level (covariate measurement error), and for identifying genes that are relevant to the body weight of mice (omitted variable bias).

We establish some notation to be used throughout the paper. For any matrix
\mathbf{A}
with elements

, let
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{1}=\max_{j}\sum_{i}|a_{ij}|
,
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}\sum_{j}|a_{ij}|
, and
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{\max}=\max_{i,j}|a_{i,j}|
denote the matrix
1
-norm,
\infty
-norm, and element-wise maximum norm, respectively. Also, let
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}
denote the
\ell_{2}
-operator norm, i.e. the largest singular value of
\mathbf{A}
. For a square matrix
\mathbf{A}
, we use
\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{A})
to denote the
j
th largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{A}
, whose corresponding eigenvector is given by
\bm{\eta}_{j}(\mathbf{A})
. When
j=1
, we suppress the subscript and write
\lambda(\mathbf{A})
and
\bm{\eta}(\mathbf{A})
. For any vector
\mathbf{v}
with elements
v_{i}
, let
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{i}v_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}=\sum_{i}|v_{i}|
, and
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|v_{i}|
denote its Euclidean norm,
\ell_{1}
norm, and
\ell_{\infty}
norm, respectively. Finally, for an index set
I
, let
\mathbf{v}_{I}
denote the subvector of
\mathbf{v}
formed with the elements of
\mathbf{v}
whose indices
i\in I
,
|I|
denote its cardinality, and
I^{c}
denote its complement.

2
SIR and endogeneity

In this section, we first establish the setting of interest, namely a high-dimensional multiple index model for the response along with a high-dimensional linear instrumental variable model for the covariates characterizing endogeneity. We then review SIR and a version augmented with the lasso penalty (Section
2.1
), and then demonstrate inconsistency of SIR under endogeneity in Section
2.2
.

2.1
Review of SIR and lasso SIR

Consider a set of
n
independent observations
\{(y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top});i=1,\ldots,n\}
where,
without loss of generality, we assume
E(\mathbf{X}_{i})=E(\mathbf{Z}_{i})={0}
. Each triplet
(y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top})
follows the multiple index model (
1
) coupled with a linear instrumental variable model. That is, the full model is given by

\displaystyle y_{i}=f(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{B},~\varepsilon_{i}),~\quad\mathbf{X}_{i}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\mathbf{U}_{i},~\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(2)

where
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
,
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times p}
,
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the random noise for the outcome model, and
\mathbf{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the random noise for the instrumental variable model. The vector
(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top},\varepsilon_{i})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
is assumed to have mean zero and a finite covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
whose diagonal elements are denoted by
\sigma_{j}^{2}
for
j=1,\ldots,p+1
. To capture endogeneity between
\mathbf{X}
and
\varepsilon
, the off-diagonal elements in the last row and column of
\bm{\Sigma}
are generally non-zero. The instrumental variable
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
satisfies
\mathbf{Z}_{i}\perp(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top},\varepsilon_{i})^{\top}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.

We aim to estimate the column space of
\mathbf{B}
when the dimensions
p
and
q
grow exponentially with the sample size
n
. A common assumption in such settings is that sparsity holds. Specifically:
(i) each covariate
X_{j}
depends upon only a few instrumental variables, such that in the instrumental variable model the
j
th column of
\bm{\Gamma}
is a sparse vector with at most
r_{j}<q
non-zero elements, where
r=\max_{j=1,\ldots,p}r_{j}
is bounded, and (ii) the multiple index model is sparse, such that
y
depends only on
s<p
covariates i.e., at most
s
rows of
\mathbf{B}
are non-zero row vectors. Equivalently, if
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B})=\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}
denotes the corresponding projection matrix, then only
s
diagonal elements of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B})
are non-zero. Without loss of generality, we assume
S=\{1,\ldots,s\}
i.e., the first
s
covariates indexes this set of non-zero elements, or equivalently the support set of the column space of
\mathbf{B}
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
denote the
n\times p
matrix of covariates, whose
i
th rows is
\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}
. Similarly, let
\mathcal{Z}
denote the
n\times q
matrix of instrumental variables whose
i
th row is
\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}
. Also, let
\mathbf{x}_{j}
and
\mathbf{z}_{j}
denote the
j
th column of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Z}
, respectively. For the remainder of this article, we use
y,\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathbf{Z}
to denote the random variables from which
(y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top})
is drawn.

The SIR estimator
(
Li,, 1991
)
of the column space of
\mathbf{B}
was developed under the assumption that the random noise
\varepsilon
is independent of the covariates
\mathbf{X}
.
Furthermore, a key prerequisite of SIR is the following linearity condition, which is satisfied when the covariates
\mathbf{X}
follow an elliptical distribution.

Condition 1
.

The conditional expectation
E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
is a linear function of
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
.

Under this condition, the population foundation for SIR is that the inverse regression curve
E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)
is contained in the linear subspace spanned by
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}

(
Li,, 1991
, Theorem 3.1,)
. Consequently, if we let
\{\bm{\eta}^{*}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\eta}^{*}_{d}\}=\{\bm{\eta}_{1}(\bm{\Lambda}^{*}),\ldots,\bm{\eta}_{d}(\bm{\Lambda}^{*})\}
denote the set of eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
\Lambda^{*}=\Cov\left\{E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)\right\}
, then
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\beta}_{k}\propto\bm{\eta}^{*}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Since
\bm{\Lambda}^{*}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}-E\left\{\Cov(\mathbf{X}\mid y)\right\}
, we can estimate
\bm{\Lambda}^{*}
from some estimates of
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}
and
T=E\left\{\Cov(\mathbf{X}\mid y)\right\}
: this approach to SIR is recommended by
Li, (1991)
, and used by many authors in the literature
(
Tan et al.,, 2018
;
Zou,, 2006
)
, although we acknowledge it is not the only approach possible
(
Lin et al.,, 2019
)
.

In more detail, suppose we estimate
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}
by the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{X}}=n^{-1}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}
. When
n<p
, this sample covariance matrix is not invertible, but critically the lasso SIR estimator defined below does not require such invertibility, as we will see shortly. Next, we partition the data into
H
non-overlapping slices
S_{1},\ldots,S_{H}
based on the order statistics of
y
, and compute the sample covariance matrices within each slice and average them as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{{T}}=\dfrac{1}{H}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left\{\frac{1}{c-1}\sum_{i\in S_{h}}(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{h})(\mathbf{X}_{i}-\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{h})^{\top}\right\},\text{where}\;\bar{\mathbf{X}}_{h}=\dfrac{1}{c}\sum_{i\in S_{h}}\mathbf{X}_{i},

where
c
denotes the slice size and we have assumed
n=cH
to simplify the notation.
Zhu et al., (2006)
showed that
\widehat{{T}}
can be rewritten in the form

\displaystyle\widehat{{T}}=\dfrac{1}{H(c-1)}\mathcal{X}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{H}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{c})\mathcal{X},\text{where}\;\mathbf{P}_{c}=\mathbf{I}_{c}-\dfrac{1}{c}{\bm{e}}_{c}{\bm{e}}_{c}^{\top},

and
{e}_{c}
denotes a
c\times 1
vector of ones. We can then estimate
\bm{\Lambda}^{*}
by
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}=\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{X}}-\widehat{{T}}=n^{-1}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{I}_{n}-\{c/(c-1)\}(\mathbf{I}_{H}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{c})
.
It follows that the eigenvector
\bm{\eta}^{*}_{k}
can be estimated by
\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}=\bm{\eta}_{k}(\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*})
, that is, the eigenvector associated with the
k
th largest eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}^{*}_{k}=\lambda_{k}(\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*})
of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
.

For settings where
p
is large and
\bm{\beta}_{k}
is sparse, consider replacing the population relationship
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\beta}_{k}\propto\bm{\eta}^{*}_{k}
by its sample counterpart. Then we have
\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}\bm{\beta}_{k}\propto\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}=(\hat{\lambda}^{*}_{k})^{-1}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathcal{X}\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}
, from which
Lin et al., (2019)
proposed the lasso SIR estimator as

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}=\arg\!\min_{\bm{\beta}}\frac{1}{2n}\lVert\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}^{*}_{k}-\mathcal{X}\bm{\beta}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\mu_{k}\|{\beta}\|_{1},

(3)

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}^{*}_{k}=(\hat{\lambda}^{*}_{k})^{-1}\mathbf{D}\mathcal{X}\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}
is a pseudo-response vector and
\mu_{k}>0
denotes a tuning parameter for the
k
th pseudo-response. Note the lasso penalty can be replaced by a more general sparsity-inducing penalty
(
Nghiem et al., 2023c,
, e.g., the adaptive lasso penalty in)
, but such generalizations are outside the scope of this article. More importantly, with an appropriate choice of
\mu_{k}
the solution
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}
is sparse, thus facilitating variable selection for each dimension of the estimator. Finally, variable selection for the estimated dimension reduction subspace is obtained by taking the union of the estimated support sets across all dimensions
k=1,\ldots,d
.

2.2
Inconsistency of SIR under endogeneity

When endogeneity is present, the inverse regression curve
E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)
is no longer guaranteed to lie in the subspace spanned by
\bm{\Sigma}_{X}\mathbf{B}
, even when Condition
1
holds for the marginal
\mathbf{X}
.
The following proposition establishes this result in the case
(\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)^{\top}
follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution.

Proposition 1
.

Assume
(\mathbf{X}^{\top},\varepsilon)^{\top}
follows a
(p+1)-
dimensional Gaussian distribution, and let
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}=\Cov(\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)
. Then the inverse regression curve
E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)
is contained in the linear subspace spanned by
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{B}
if and only if
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\in\text{col}(\mathbf{B})
, where
\text{col}(\mathbf{B})
denotes the column space of
\mathbf{B}
.

In Proposition
1
the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}
is the coefficient vector of the best linear predictor of
\varepsilon
from
\mathbf{X}
. Hence this proposition implies that, when endogeneity is present, the population foundation for SIR can
only
hold when the random noise can be predicted from the linear combination
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
itself. This condition is obviously restrictive, as it requires the same set of linear combinations to be used to predict both the response
y
and the random noise
\varepsilon
.

Unsurprisingly, when the condition in Proposition
1
is not satisfied, then the lasso SIR estimator in (
3
) is also generally not a consistent estimator of the column space of
\mathbf{B}
. The following proposition makes this precise in the simple setting where the number of covariates
p
is fixed, and the penalization is asymptotically negligible.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
denote the lasso SIR estimator formulated in (
3
), with tuning parameters
\mu_{k}\to 0
for all
k=1,\ldots,d
as
n\to\infty
. Then,
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{*})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B}^{*})
, where
\mathbf{B}^{*}=\bm{\Sigma}_{X}^{-1}\bm{\eta}^{*}
, where
\bm{\eta}^{*}
is a
p\times d
matrix of eigenvectors of
\Lambda^{*}=\Cov\left\{E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)\right\}
. It follows that if the covariance vector
\Sigma_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\notin\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\text{col}(\mathbf{B})
, then
\|P(\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{*})-P(\mathbf{B})\|\neq o_{p}(1)
as
n\to\infty
and
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}^{*}
is inconsistent for the column space of
\mathbf{B}
.

Propositions
1
and
2
imply that when the covariance vector
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\notin\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\text{col}(\mathbf{B})
, then endogeneity invalidates (lasso) SIR as an estimator of the overall dimension reduction subspace. Note this does not necessarily imply selection inconsistency, i.e. inconsistency in estimating the support set
S=\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
Indeed, selection consistency can still be achieved if the column space of
\mathbf{B}^{*}
has the same support as that of
\mathbf{B}
; see Appendix A for an empirical demonstration.
On the other hand, when the number of covariates
p
diverges with
n
, then it is unlikely
\mathbf{B}^{*}
will share the same support as
\mathbf{B}
. This motivates us to propose a new SIR method to handle endogeneity in high-dimensional settings.

3
A two-stage lasso SIR estimator

To overcome the inconsistency of the SIR estimator when endogeneity is present, we propose a two-stage approach for SDR and variable selection. Let
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Z})=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
denote the conditional expectation of the covariates on the instruments. We first impose a new linearity condition to ensure the conditional expectation
E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)
behaves similarly to
E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)
when no endogeneity is present.

Condition 2
.

The conditional expectation
E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
is a linear function of
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
.

Despite being stronger than Condition
1
, Condition
2
is satisfied when the linear instrumental variable model holds and the
p+q
-dimensional random vector
(\mathbf{X}^{\top},\mathbf{Z}^{\top})^{\top}
follows an elliptical distribution. In our simulation study in Section
5
, we show the proposed method can still exhibit reasonable performance even when this condition is not satisfied, for example, when
\mathbf{Z}
is binary. We then have the following result.

Lemma 1
.

Assume Condition (
2
) holds. Then the quantity
E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)
is contained in the linear subspace spanned by
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\mathbf{B}
.

The covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}
in Lemma
1
need not be of full-rank and invertible e.g., we permit the number of endogenous covariates
p
to exceed the number of instruments
q
.
To be precise, while theoretically we need to understand the behavior of its inverse, computationally
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}
does not have to be inverted in what follows.

Lemma
1
motivates a two-stage lasso SIR estimator for the column space of
\mathbf{B}
based on the observed vector
\mathbf{y}
, covariate matrix
\mathcal{X}
, and instrument matrix
\mathcal{Z}
. The two stages are detailed as follows:
In stage 1, we identify and estimate the effects of the instruments
\bm{\Gamma}
, and subsequently obtain the predicted values of the covariates
\mathbf{X}
. To accomplish this, we fit a multivariate instrumental variable model given by the second equation in (
2
), with the covariates
\mathbf{X}
as the
p
-dimensional response and instruments
\mathbf{Z}
as the
q
-dimensional predictor. Moreover, because we assume each endogenous covariate depends only on a few instrumental variables,
then a sparse first stage estimator
\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}
is obtained by computing in parallel for
j=1,\ldots,p

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}=\arg\!\min_{\bm{\gamma}}\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathcal{Z}\bm{\gamma}\|_{F}^{2}+\mu_{1j}\|\bm{\gamma}\|_{1},

where
\mu_{1j}>0
denotes the tuning parameter for the
j
th covariate in the first stage. Afterwards, the estimator
\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}=[\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{p}]
is formed along with the fitted value matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{Z}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
.

In stage 2, we compute a sparse estimator for the column space of
\mathbf{B}
by applying the lasso SIR estimator (
3
) with response vector
\mathbf{y}
and the fitted value matrix
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
,
Specifically, let
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}=(nc)^{-1}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=(nc)^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\top}{\mathcal{Z}}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\mathcal{Z}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
, and define the pseudo-response vector
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}=(\hat{\lambda}_{k})^{-1}\mathbf{D}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}
, where
\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}=\bm{\eta}_{k}(\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}})
is the eigenvector associated with the
k
th largest eigenvalue
\hat{\lambda}_{k}=\lambda_{k}(\hat{\bm{\Lambda}})
, and
\mathbf{D}
is defined as in Section
2.1
. The two-stage lasso SIR estimator for the
k
th column of
\mathbf{B}
then is defined as

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}=\arg\!\min_{\bm{\beta}}\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{{\mathbf{y}}}_{k}-\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2k}\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1},\;k=1,\ldots,d,

(4)

where
\mu_{2k}>0
is a tuning parameter for the
k
th pseudo-response in the second stage.

Computationally, both stages can be implemented straightforwardly using coordinate descent algorithms e.g., we apply the
R
package
glmnet

(
Friedman et al.,, 2010
)
in both stages.

4
Theoretical analysis

We study the properties of the two-stage lasso SIR estimator for the column space of
{\mathbf{B}}
, under the multiple index model (
2
) and when the dimensionality of the covariates
p
and instruments
q
can grow exponentially with sample size
n
.
Compared with the theoretical results for the linear outcome model in
Lin et al., (2015)
, the major challenge here lies in the nonlinear nature of the conditional expectation
E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)
, and subsequently the behavior of the eigenvectors corresponding to the covariance matrix
\Lambda=\Cov\{E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)\}
which require more complex technical developments.
In what follows, we will use
C
,
C^{\prime}
,
C^{\prime\prime}
to denote generic positive constants.

Following
Wainwright, (2019, Chapter 7,)
, we say an
n\times m
matrix
\mathbf{A}
satisfies a restricted eigenvalue condition over a set
S
with parameters
(\kappa,\nu)
if

\|\mathbf{A}^{1/2}\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\kappa\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2},

for all
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}(S,\nu)=\left\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:~\|\bm{\delta}_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\nu\|\bm{\delta}_{S}\|_{1}\right\}
. Also, for a vector
\mathbf{v}
let
\text{supp}(\mathbf{v})=\{j:v_{j}\neq 0\}
denote the support set of
\mathbf{v}
, and
\sigma_{\max}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p}\sigma_{j}
where we recall
\sigma^{2}_{j}
are the diagonal elements of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
corresponding to
(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top},\varepsilon_{i})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
. We assume each
\sigma_{j}^{2}
and hence
\sigma_{\max}
to be a constant with respect to
n
.

The following technical conditions on the distribution of the
\mathbf{Z}
and the conditional expectation
E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Z})=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}
are required.

Condition 3
.

The instrument random vector
\mathbf{Z}
is sub-Gaussian with a
q\times q
covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}
, and for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
there exists a constant
C
such that
\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}\leq C
, where

\lVert\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=1}\lVert\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}\rVert_{\psi_{2}},

and
\lVert u\rVert_{\psi_{2}}=\sup_{m}m^{-1/2}\left(E|u|^{m}\right)^{1/m}
.

Condition 4
.

There exist positive constants
L
and
C
such that
\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{1}\leq L
, and the largest singular values
\|\bm{\Gamma}\|_{2}\leq C
.

Condition 5
.

The quantities
p,q,r,s
satisfy
rs\left\{(\log pq)/n\right\}^{1/2}=o(1)
.

The sub-Gaussian condition is common in high-dimensional data analysis
(
Tan et al.,, 2018
;
Wainwright,, 2019
;
Nghiem et al., 2023b,
, e.g.)
, with a direct consequence being that the instrumental matrix
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies the restricted eigenvalue condition with parameter
(\kappa,3)
with high probability when
n>Cr\log q
, for some finite
\kappa

(
Banerjee et al.,, 2014
)
; we will condition on this event in our development below. Furthermore, letting
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{Z}}=\mathcal{Z}^{\top}\mathcal{Z}/n
denote the sample covariance of
\mathcal{Z}
data, then

\|\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{Z}}-\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}\|_{\max}=C\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}},

(5)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)

(
Ravikumar et al.,, 2011
;
Tan et al.,, 2018
, see also)
.
Condition
4
is also imposed in the theoretical analysis of the two-stage penalized least square methods in
Lin et al., (2015)
, and is necessary to bound the estimation error from the first stage.
Finally, we allow the number of covariates
p
, instruments
q
, and the sparsity levels
s
and
r
to depend on the sample size
n
, subject to the rate of growth of these parameters dictated by Condition
5
. For example, if
r
and
s
are bounded, then
p
and
q
can grow exponentially with
n
.

4.1
First stage results

In the first stage of the two-stage lasso SIR procedure, each column of the matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
is a lasso estimator from the regression of
\mathbf{x}_{j}
on the design matrix
\mathcal{Z}
. For completeness, we re-state the following result from
Lin et al., (2015)
, which itself is a direct application of
Banerjee et al., (2014)

Theorem 1
.

Assume Conditions
3
and
4
are satisfied, and set

\mu_{1j}=C\sigma_{j}\sqrt{\dfrac{\log pq}{n}},~j=1,\ldots,p.

(6)

Then with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)
, the columns of
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
satisfy

\lVert\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}-\bm{\gamma}_{j}\rVert_{1}\leq C\sigma_{\max}r\{\log(pq)/n\}^{1/2},

and with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log pq)
, we obtain

\left\|\widehat{{\bm{\Gamma}}}-{\bm{\Gamma}}\right\|_{1}\leq C\sigma_{\max}r\sqrt{\frac{\log pq}{n}}.

(7)

Theorem
1
allows us to establish estimation consistency for the column space of
\mathbf{B}
in the following section.

4.2
Second stage results

In the second stage, we compute the lasso SIR estimator using the outcome
y
and the fitted value matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{Z}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
obtained from the first stage. Since
\mathbf{X}
is related to the instruments
\mathbf{Z}
via (
2
), then we have
E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)={\bm{\Gamma}}^{\top}E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)
, where
E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)
is related to the dimension reduction subspace
\mathcal{S}_{y\mid\mathbf{Z}}
of
y
on
\mathbf{Z}
. It follows that we can impose technical conditions similar to those in
Lin et al., (2019)
to ensure this subspace can be estimated consistently in high-dimensional settings:

Condition 6
.

There exist constants
C_{\min}
and
C_{\max}
such that
0<C_{\min}\leq\lambda_{q}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{1}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}})<C_{\max}<\infty
.

Condition 7
.

There exist positive constants
c
and
\kappa\geq 1
such that
0<c<\lambda_{d}(\bm{\Omega})\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{1}(\bm{\Omega})\leq\kappa\lambda_{d}(\bm{\Omega})<\lambda_{1}(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}})
, where
\bm{\Omega}=\Cov\left\{E\left({\mathbf{Z}}\mid y\right)\right\}
, and
n\lambda_{1}(\bm{\Omega})=q^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1/2
.

Condition 8
.

The quantity
{m}_{\mathbf{Z}}(y)=E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)
satisfies the sliced stability condition introduced in
Lin et al., (2018)
.

Condition
6
ensures the loss function corresponding to the second stage of the proposed lasso SIR estimator does not have too small curvature, and is similar to a condition imposed by
Lin et al., (2015)
for the two-stage penalized least square method.
Condition
7
is analogous to the coverage condition imposed in the SIR literature for
\bm{\Lambda}

(
Li,, 1991
, e.g.,)
.
Finally, Condition
8
controls the smoothness of the conditional expectation
{m}_{\mathbf{Z}}(y)=E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)
. Such a condition is similarly used in
Tan et al., (2018)
when no endogeneity is present, with analogous conditions on the total variation having been employed earlier by
Hsing and Carroll, (1992)
;
Zhu et al., (2006)
for other sufficient dimension reduction methods.

We next establish the consistency of the two-stage lasso SIR estimator for
\text{span}(\mathbf{B})
.
First, consider the case of
d=1
i.e., the single index model version of (
2
). Here, Lemma
1
implies the covariance matrix
\bm{\Lambda}=\Cov\{E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)\}
has rank
d=1
and hence can be written in the form
\bm{\Lambda}=\lambda\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}
, where
\lambda>0
denotes the non-zero eigenvalue of
\bm{\Lambda}
and
\bm{\eta}
is the corresponding eigenvector. Without loss of generality, assume
\|\bm{\beta}\|=1
. For convenience below, we omit the subscript
k
and use
\hat{\lambda}
and
\hat{\bm{\eta}}
to denote the largest eigenvalue and its corresponding eigenvector of
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}
, respectively, noting both were defined above (
4
). Furthermore, we write
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{1}=\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
. With this notation, the two-stage lasso SIR estimator for the column space of
\bm{\beta}

is given by

\hat{\bm{\beta}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\widehat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\right\}.

(8)

By Lemma (
1
), we have
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\beta}=\xi\bm{\eta}
, where
\xi=\lVert\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\beta}\rVert_{2}>0
is a constant. Letting
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}=P(\bm{\eta})\hat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
, where
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}=\xi^{-1}\bm{\beta}{\bm{\eta}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}
, then it is straightforward to see
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
has the same direction as
\bm{\beta}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}})=\mathcal{P}(\bm{\beta})
, when
\bm{\eta}^{\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}\neq 0
, which occurs with probability one. With this quantity, we obtain the following deterministic result.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}=n^{-1}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies an restricted eigenvalue property with parameters
(\kappa_{2},3)
; see Proposition 11 in Appendix B that shows the latter holds with high probability. If the tuning parameter satisfies
\mu_{2}\geq 2\left\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}-n^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right\|_{\infty}
, then any solution
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
defined in (
8
) satisfies

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{\bm{\beta}}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\rVert_{2}\leq\dfrac{12}{\kappa_{2}}\mu_{2}\sqrt{s}.

Following Lemma
2
, by selecting a value of
\mu_{2}
so that the condition
\mu_{2}\geq 2\left\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}-n^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right\|_{\infty}
is satisfied with high probability, we obtain the consistency of the two-stage lasso SIR estimator for the single index model.

Theorem 2
.

Assume Conditions
3
-
8
are satisfied, and choose the first stage tuning parameters
\mu_{1j}
as in (
6
). Let
\bm{\eta}
be the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{\Omega}=\text{Cov}\{E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)\}
. Let
\rho_{1}=L^{2}+\lambda^{-1/2}{L(1+\Psi_{1})}
and
\rho_{2}=L\sigma_{\max}+\lambda^{-1/2}(1+\Psi_{1})\Psi_{2}
, where
\Psi_{1}=\left\|\mathbf{P}\right\|_{1}
and
\mathbf{P}
is the projection matrix associated with the space spanned by
\bm{\eta}\bm{\Gamma}
and
\Psi_{2}=\|\bm{\eta}\bm{\eta}^{\top}\|_{1}
. If we select the second stage tuning parameters as

\mu_{2}=C\left(\rho_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}+\rho_{2}r\sqrt{\frac{\log pq}{n}}\right),

(9)

then
with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)-\exp(-C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
, the two-stage lasso SIR estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|P(\hat{\bm{\beta}})-P({\bm{\beta}})\right\|_{F}\leq C\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\kappa_{2}}\left(\rho_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}+\rho_{2}r\sqrt{\frac{\log pq}{n}}\right).

Compared with the bound for the one-stage lasso SIR estimator, which is established by
Lin et al., (2019)
, the theoretical bound for the two-stage lasso SIR estimator has an additional factor of order
\left\{\log(pq)/n\right\}^{1/2}
, reflecting the consequences of endogeneity. Also, compared with the result for the two-stage penalized least squares estimator in the linear outcome model of
Lin et al., (2015)
, the bound in Theorem
2
involves an additional term
\rho_{1}\left(\log q/n\right)^{1/2}
, which
reflects the consequences of non-linearity.
Intuitively, the estimation error of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
depends on the estimation error of
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}\bm{\Gamma}
, which in turn depends upon the estimation error of both the coefficients
\hat{\bm{\Gamma}}
in the first stage and the covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}
.

Next, we establish the selection consistency of the two-stage lasso SIR estimator.
Let
\hat{S}=\{j:\hat{\beta}_{j}\neq 0\}
, and partition the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}\bm{\Gamma}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{C}_{SS}&\mathbf{C}_{S^{c}S}\\
\mathbf{C}_{SS^{c}}&\mathbf{C}_{S^{c}S^{c}}\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\phi=\|(\mathbf{C}_{SS})^{-1}\|_{\infty}
, and note in this case we have
\xi=\lVert\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\beta}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\mathbf{C}_{SS}\bm{\beta}_{S}\rVert_{2}
. We require an additional condition as follows.

Condition 9
.

There exists a constant
0<\alpha\leq 1
such that
\|\mathbf{C}_{S^{c}S}(\mathbf{C}_{SS})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\alpha
.

Condition
9
is similar to the mutual incoherence condition for the ordinary lasso estimator in the linear model without endogeneity
(
Wainwright,, 2019
, e.g., see Section 7.5.1,)
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume Conditions
3
-
9
are satisfied, and choose the first stage tuning parameters
\mu_{1j}
as in (
6
). If there exists a constant
C
such that

CsL\left\{L\sqrt{\dfrac{\log q}{n}}+\sigma_{\max}r\sqrt{\dfrac{\log pq}{n}}\right\}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\phi(4-\alpha)},

(10)

and the second stage tuning parameter is selected to satisfy

\min_{j\in S}|\beta_{j}|>\frac{2\xi\bm{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}}{2-\alpha}\varphi\mu_{2},

(11)

then
with probability at least
1-\exp(C^{\prime}\log q)-\exp(C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
, the support of the two-stage lasso SIR estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies
\hat{S}=S
.

Since the mutual incoherence condition
9
is imposed for the population quantity
\bm{\Sigma}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}
, the additional assumption in (
10
) is necessary to ensure that this condition is satisfied by the sample covariance matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}
with high probability.
Also, (
11
)
has an additional factor
\xi\bm{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
in the numerator,
reflecting another consequence of non-linearity. On the other hand, the lower bound is small when
\alpha
is small i.e., the lower the correlation between the important and non-important variables, the smaller the magnitude of non-zero components which can be identified.

4.3
Extension to multiple index models

We generalize the above results to multiple index models. For
k=1,\ldots,d
, let
\widehat{{S}}_{k}=\{j:|\hat{\beta}_{jk}|\neq 0\}
denote the estimated support set for the vector
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}
, and
\widehat{{S}}=\cup_{k}{\mathcal{S}_{k}}
. Recall
\mathbf{B}
is only identifiable up to rotational transformation, and the latter only preserves the zero rows of
\mathbf{B}
. As such, selection in the multiple index model refers only to selecting non-zero rows of
\mathbf{B}
. Equivalently, the set
\hat{{S}}
should be a consistent estimator of the support set
S
for the diagonal elements of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B})
.

Theorem 4
.

Assume Conditions
3
-
8
are satisfied, and choose the first stage tuning parameters
\mu_{1j}
as in (
6
). Let
\rho_{1}=L^{2}+\lambda_{d}^{-1/2}L(1+\Psi_{1})
and
\rho_{2}=L\sigma_{\max}+\lambda_{d}^{-1/2}(1+\Psi_{1})\Psi_{2}
, where
\lambda_{d}
is the
d
th largest eigenvalue of
\Lambda=\Cov\{E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)\}
. If we select the second stage tuning parameters to satisfy

\mu_{2k}=C\left(\rho_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}+\rho_{2}r\sqrt{\frac{\log pq}{n}}\right),\;k=1,\ldots,d,

(12)

(a)

Then with probability at least
1-d\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)-d\exp(-C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
, the two-stage lasso SIR estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{d})
, whose columns
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}
are defined by (
4
), satisfies

\displaystyle\left\|P(\widehat{\mathbf{B}})-P({\mathbf{B}})\right\|_{F}\leq C\frac{\sqrt{s}}{\kappa_{2}}\left(\rho_{1}\sqrt{\frac{\log q}{n}}+\rho_{2}r\sqrt{\frac{\log pq}{n}}\right).

(b)

If Condition
9
also holds and at least one tuning parameter
\mu_{2k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\min_{j\in\mathcal{S}^{k}}|\beta_{jk}|>\frac{2\xi_{k}{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}}{2-\alpha}\varphi\mu_{2k},

where
\xi_{k}=\|\Sigma_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\beta}_{k}\|_{2}
, then
\widehat{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}
with the same probability,.

Parts (a) and (b) in Theorem
4
for multiple index models essentially require Theorems
2
and
3
, respectively, to hold for every dimension. As such, the upper bound on the estimation error of
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\mathbf{B}})
depends on the lowest non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda_{d}
of
\bm{\Lambda}
, and requires all sample eigenvectors
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}
to not be orthogonal to their corresponding eigenvector
\bm{\eta}_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Under these conditions, we can establish bounds for the estimation and selection consistency for the multiple index model (
2
) under endogeneity.

5
Simulation studies

We conducted a numerical study to evaluate the performance of the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator for recovering the (sparsity patterns of the) dimension reduction subspace.We simulated data
\{(y_{i},\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i});i=1,\ldots,n\}
, from a linear instrumental variable model
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\mathbf{U}_{i}
coupled with one of the following five models for the response:

\displaystyle\text{(i)}:\;y_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{i},~~\text{(ii)}:\;y_{i}=\exp(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{i}),\quad\text{(iii)}:\;y_{i}=\sinh(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{i})

\displaystyle\text{(iv)}:\;y_{i}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{2})\exp(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{i}),\quad\text{(v)}:\;y_{i}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{1}+\varepsilon_{i})}{3/2+\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{2}+\varepsilon_{i}}.

Model (i) is the linear model, while the single and double index models (ii) - (v) are similar to those previously used in the literature to assess SDR methods
(
Lin et al.,, 2019
, e.g.,)
. The true sparse matrix
\mathbf{B}=\bm{\beta}_{1}
in the single index models (i) and (ii), and
\mathbf{B}=[\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2}]
in the multiple index models (iii) and (iv) are constructed as follows: We randomly selected
s=5
rows of
\mathbf{B}
to be nonzero and generated each non-zero entry in these rows from a uniform distribution on the two disjoint intervals
[-1,-0.5]
and
[0.5,1]
, which we denote as
U([-1,-0.5]\cup[0.5,1])
. The elements in all other rows were set to zero. We then used a Gram-Schmidt process to ensure
\bm{\beta}_{1}
and
\bm{\beta}_{2}
were orthogonal to each other in the case of the double index model. The covariate
\mathbf{X}_{i}
vector was generated from the instrumental variable model
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\mathbf{U}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where we followed a similar generation mechanism to that in
Lin et al., (2015)
. That is, we simulated each of the
q
elements of
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
from either: (1) a standard normal distribution, or (2) a Bernoulli distribution with success probability
0.5
. To generate
\bm{\Gamma}
, we randomly set
r=5
nonzero entries in each column from the uniform distribution
U([-b,-a]\cup[a,b])
with
(a,b)=(0.75,1)
. Finally, we generated the random noise vector
(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top},\varepsilon_{i})^{\top}
from a
(p+1)
-variate Gaussian distribution with zero mean vector and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}=(\sigma_{ij})
as follows: We set
\sigma_{ij}=(0.2)^{|i-j|}
for
i,j=

1,\ldots,p
, so that
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{U}}
has an AR(1) structure. For the last column/row of
\bm{\Sigma}
characterizing endogeneity, we set
\bm{\Sigma}_{S,p+1}=-\bm{\Sigma}_{S,S}\bm{\beta}_{1S}
, where
S
denotes the index set of non-zero elements in
\bm{\beta}_{1}
, and randomly selected another five elements and set them equal to
0.3
, while the remaining elements were set to zero. The last element
\sigma_{p+1,p+1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{U},\varepsilon}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{U},\varepsilon}+U(0,0.2)
. This construction of
\bm{\Sigma}
ensures it is positive definite, and that
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\epsilon}
does not belong to the column space of
\mathbf{B}
.

We considered both low-dimensional and high-dimensional settings, where for the former we set
p=q=40
and varied
n\in\{200,500\}
, while for high-dimensional settings we fixed
n=200
and varied
p=q\in\{500,1000\}
. For each configuration, we simulated
100
datasets, where in each dataset we applied the one-stage lasso, lasso SIR, two-stage lasso, and the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimators.
Note both the one-stage lasso and two-stage lasso estimates assume
d=1
, while the dimension
d
is assumed to be known for both the lasso SIR and two-stage lasso SIR. For the latter two SIR-based estimators, we also set the number of slices
H=10
.
To reduce the computational cost, for the two-stage lasso and two-stage lasso SIR estimators, we selected the tuning parameter for the penalized regression of each covariate on instruments to minimize the Bayesian information criterion (
BIC
). We also found that using other information criterion, such as extended BIC of
chen2008extended
, led to similar results in the second stage in both low and high-dimensional settings.
All other tuning parameters were selected via an appropriate ten-fold cross-validation approach. We assessed performance of all methods based on the error on the estimated projection matrix
\|\mathcal{P}(\hat{\mathbf{B}})-\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B})\|_{F}
, along with the area under the receiver operating curve (
AUC
) score for variable selection performance.

Table
1
demonstrates that in low-dimensional settings, the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator performed best in terms of both estimation error and variable selection. Indeed, it is the only method among the four considered whose estimation error consistently decreased when sample size increased from
n=200
to
n=500
. The one-stage lasso estimator, which ignores both endogeneity and non-linearity in the outcome model, performed the worst as expected. Comparing the lasso SIR and two-stage lasso, the former tended to perform better except in model (i), suggesting the consequence of ignoring non-linearity and/or underestimating the dimensions were more serious than ignoring endogeneity in this simulation design.
Turning to the variable selection results, the lasso SIR estimator has overall good performance, while the two-stage lasso estimator only achieves an AUC close to
1
in the linear model (i). The proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator consistently had the (equal) best selection performance.

For high-dimensional settings, Table
2
shows that the proposed two-stage lasso SIR still had the best performance overall. For the linear model (i), the two-stage lasso performed well as expected, but its performance was worse than the two-stage lasso SIR estimator in single index models (ii)-(iii) where the link function is non-linear. The proposed two-stage lasso SIR also had a much smaller estimation error compared with the one-stage lasso SIR in all single index models (i)–(iii), although the two methods had quite similar estimation error in the double index models (iv)-(v).
Finally, the performance of all methods in both low- and high-dimensional settings was similar for both continuous and binary cases of
\mathbf{Z}
. In particular, this suggested the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator was not overly sensitive to violation of Condition
2
in this simulation design.

6
Selection of dimensions

An important problem in applying any sufficient dimension reduction method in practice is to estimate the dimension
d
for the dimension reduction subspace. When there is no endogeneity, this can be estimated by examining the eigenvalues of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}
, which is an estimate of
\bm{\Lambda}^{*}=\Cov\{E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)\}
. In classical settings with
p
fixed and
n\to\infty
, it can be shown that the
d
largest estimated eigenvalues of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}
are separated from the last
H-d
eigenvalues, and hence we can estimate
d
via an asymptotic test
(
shao2007marginal
)
or a permutation/bootstrap test
(
ye2003using
, e.g.,)
. The problem is more challenging in high-dimensional settings where
p/n\rightarrow 0
, since all non-zero eigenvalues of
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}
are of the same order
p/n
and no longer separated.
To overcome this issue,
Lin et al., (2019)
proposed selecting the structural dimension for the lasso SIR estimator by clustering the adjusted eigenvalues
\tilde{\lambda}^{*}_{k}=\hat{\lambda}_{k}^{*}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}\|_{2}
, where
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}
is a solution of (
3
) for
k=1,\ldots,H
.
Other sparse SIR methods, such as those proposed by
Tan et al., (2018)
;
zeng2024subspace
, select the structural dimension
d
by augmenting an objective function with a nuclear norm constraint.

In our setting where endogeneity is present, the selection of dimensions in high dimensions is made more challenging by the complex eigenstructure of
\bm{\Lambda}=\Cov\{E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)\}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\Cov\{E(\mathbf{Z}\mid y)\}\bm{\Gamma}
. A first natural solution to this problem is to apply the clustering method in
Lin et al., (2019)
in Stage 2 of the proposed two-stage estimator, when we compute the lasso SIR estimator of the outcome data
\mathbf{y}
on the first stage fitted value matrix
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}=\mathcal{Z}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
.
On the other hand,
we can also write (
2
) as
y_{i}=f(\mathbf{Z}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{C},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{i})
, where
\mathbf{C}=\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{B}
is a
q\times d
matrix. It follows that the central subspace of
y
on
\mathbf{Z}
also has dimension
d
, assuming
\mathbf{C}
is full rank. Therefore, we also consider selecting
d
simply by computing a lasso SIR estimator of the outcome
y
on the instrumental variables
\mathbf{Z}
.

We conducted a small simulation study to compare the performance of different methods to estimate
d
based on applying the lasso SIR estimator of
y
on either
\mathcal{Z}
,
\mathcal{X}
, or
\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
. Specifically, after computing the lasso estimator on each dimension
k=1,\ldots,d
, we computed the adjusted eigenvalues as in
Lin et al., (2019)
and applied
K
-means clustering with
K=2
on these adjusted eigenvalues. Finally, we estimated
\hat{d}
as the size of the cluster containing the largest adjusted eigenvalues. To reduce the sensitivity of the final results to the choice of other tuning parameters, we performed
50
repeated
5
-fold cross-validation (i.e. 50 random splits of the data) where within each repeat, we obtained the lasso SIR estimator for each dimension at the tuning parameter selected as in the simulation, performed the clustering, and recorded the estimated
d
. The final estimator is then recorded as the value of
d
selected with the highest frequency across 50 repeats.
We simulated data using the same setup as in Section
5
, but only considered settings with continuous instruments
\mathbf{Z}
for brevity.

7
Real data applications

We demonstrate our proposed method on two examples with endogeneity, one due to omitted variables and the other due to measurement error.

7.1
Omitted variables: Mouse obesity data

We applied the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator to perform simultaneous SDR and variable selection on a mouse obesity dataset from
Wang et al., (2006)
, and compared the results obtained to those from
Lin et al., (2015)
who analyzed the same data using their two-stage lasso estimator, as well as results based on applying the lasso SIR estimator. Briefly, the study comprised
334
mice fed a high-fat diet, and who were then genotyped using
1327
single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with gene expressions profiled on microarrays that include probes for
23,388
genes. We followed the same pre-processing steps as in
Lin et al., (2015)
, and obtained a complete dataset with
p=2825
genes and
q=1250
SNPs on
n=287
mice. We treated the SNPs as instruments (
\mathbf{Z}
), and focused on identifying important genes (
\mathbf{X}
) related to the body weight (
y
). As mentioned in Section
1
, the relationship between gene expression covariates and the outcome is likely confounded by omitted variables, experimental conditions, and environmental perturbations. As such, endogeneity due to omitted variable bias is expected to be a critical issue in this application.
Lin et al., (2015)
argued that genetic variants as represented by SNPs are valid instruments because the mice in this study come from the same genetic cross.

To select the number of structural dimensions
d
, we applied the three approaches described in Section
6
, and all selected
\hat{d}=1
. Next, for each of the two-stage lasso, one-stage lasso SIR, and two-stage lasso SIR estimators, we applied stability selection to compute the empirical selection probability
(
Meinshausen and Bühlmann,, 2010
)
for each gene over 100 random subsamples of size
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
at each value of the tuning parameter. The upper bound for the per-family error rate was set to one, while the selection cutoff was set to
0.75
, meaning up to 75% number of components can be included in the estimates in each subsample. The results in Figure
1
present the selection path for each gene as a function of the second-stage tuning parameter
\mu_{2}
. With the maximum selection probability across all the tuning parameters set to
0.5
, the lasso SIR estimator selected seven genes, the two-stage lasso selected nine genes, and the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator selected six genes. Only one gene was chosen with a very high selection probability across all the three methods, while one additional gene was commonly selected by both the two-stage lasso SIR and two-stage lasso estimators.

With the maximum selection probability of at least 0.5, the gene selected by all the three methods is displayed in red, while the additional gene only selected by both two-stage methods is displayed in blue.

7.2
Measurement error: NHANES data

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) aims to track the health and nutritional status of individuals in the United States over time
(
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,, 2018
)
. During the 2009-2010 survey period, participants were interviewed and asked to provide their demographic background, nutritional habits, and to undertake a series of health examinations. To assess the nutritional habits of participants, dietary data were collected using two 24-hour recall interviews, wherein the participants self-reported the consumed amounts of a set of food items during the 24 hours before each interview. Based on these recalls, daily aggregated consumption of water, food energy, and other nutrition components such as total fat and total sugar consumption were computed. In this section, we revisit the data application in
Nghiem et al., 2023a ()
, seeking to understand the relationship between total cholesterol level (
y
) for
n=3343
women and their long-term intakes of
p=42
nutrition components, particularly a linear combination/s of these components carrying the full regression information, and whether these combinations are sparse in nature.

Let
\mathbf{W}_{ij}
denote the vector of 42 nutritional covariates at the
j
th interview for
j=1,2
. Dietary recall data are well-known to be subject to measurement errors, so we assume
\mathbf{W}_{ij}=\mathbf{X}_{i}+{E}_{ij}
, where
\mathbf{X}_{i}
represents the
i
th latent long-term nutritional intake, and
{E}_{ij}
denotes an independent additive measurement error term following a multivariate Gaussian distribution with zero mean and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{u}
.
We used an instrumental variable approach to account for this measurement error. Specifically, following
Carroll et al., (2006, Section 6.5.1,)
, we treated the first replicate
\mathbf{W}_{i1}=\mathbf{W}_{i}
as a surrogate for
\mathbf{X}_{i}
and the second replicate
\mathbf{W}_{i2}=\mathbf{Z}_{i}
as an instrument. Note in this case, the number of instruments satisfies
q=p
.
We then applied the linear instrumental variable model
\mathbf{X}_{i}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\mathbf{U}_{i}
, where
\bm{\Gamma}
is allowed to be a
q\times p
column-sparse matrix. It follows that we can write
\mathbf{W}_{i}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{i}+\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{i}
where
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{i}=\mathbf{U}_{i}+\mathbf{E}_{i1}
. We then applied the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator replacing
\mathcal{X}
with

\mathcal{W}
. All three methods for selecting the structural dimension described in Section
6
selected
\hat{d}=1
; this was also consistent with the analysis in
Nghiem et al., 2023a ()
.

We first examined the estimated matrix coefficients
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
from the first stage of the two-stage lasso SIR estimation procedure. Figure 4 in Appendix D shows that the largest element in each column of
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
is typically the diagonal element, so the most important instrument to model the consumption of any dietary component in the second interview is, unsurprisingly, the consumption of the same component in the first interview. Overall, each column of
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
is relatively sparse, with the number of non-zero coefficients in each column ranging from 3 to 20.
Next,
we applied the same stability selection approach as in Section
7.1
to the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator, and compared the resulting paths with the lasso SIR and two stage lasso estimators.
With the maximum selection probability across all the tuning parameters being at least 0.5, the lasso SIR estimator selected five nutritional covariates, the two-stage lasso estimator selected five nutritional covariates, while the proposed two-stage lasso SIR estimator selected six nutritional covariates. Figure
2
presents the selection path as a function of the tuning parameter
\mu_{2}
for each nutrition component. The two components commonly selected by all the three estimators were vitamin D and vitamin K, while both the two-stage lasso SIR and two stage lasso estimators additionally select one more common component in folic acid. Vitamin D is the component with the largest estimated coefficients in both the two-stage lasso SIR and two-stage lasso estimators, and was the second largest in the lasso SIR estimator.

With a maximum selection probability of at least 0.5, the components that are selected by all the three methods are displayed in red, while the additional component only selected by the two-stage methods is displayed in blue.

8
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a two-stage lasso SIR estimator for simultaneous sufficient dimension reduction and variable selection in the presence of endogeneity. We establish estimation and selection consistency of the proposed estimator, allowing the dimensionality of both the covariates and instruments to grow exponentially with sample size. Numerical studies demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed estimator compared with existing methods that ignore non-linearity and/or endogeneity in the outcome model.

Future research can extend the proposed method to accommodate a possible non-linear relationship between the covariates and the instruments in the first stage. Different sparsity structures on the coefficient matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
, such as a low-rank matrix, can also be considered
(
reinsel2022multivariate
)
. We can also examine endogeneity in other sufficient dimension reduction methods, such as inverse regression models
(
Bura et al.,, 2022
)
, and finally, we can adapt the methods to more complex settings involving endogeneity, such as SDR for clustered or longitudinal data
(
hui2022sufficient
;
Nghiem and Hui,, 2024
)
.

Performance of Lasso SIR under endogenity

In this subsection, we consider a simple setting to demonstrate the performance of Lasso SIR when endogeneity is present. Suppose the
(p+1)-
random vector
(\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)^{\top}
has the multivariate Gaussian distribution with zero mean, marginal covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{X}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
, and the marginal variance
\Var(\varepsilon)=1
. In this case, it follows that

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\varepsilon\\
\end{bmatrix}\sim N_{p+1}\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{p}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}\right),

(13)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}
is the correlation between
\mathbf{X}
and
\varepsilon

y

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\

B.1
Proof of Proposition 1

Without loss of generality, assume the expectation of
\text{E}(\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)=0
, the marginal covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{X}=\mathbf{I}_{p}
, and the marginal variance
\Var(\varepsilon)=1
. In this case, it follows that

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\varepsilon\\
\end{bmatrix}\sim N_{p+1}\left(\bm{0},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{p}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}&1\end{bmatrix}\right).

By the law of iterated expectation, we have

E(\mathbf{X}\mid y)=E\left\{E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\varepsilon,y)\mid y\right\}=E\left\{E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)\mid y\right\}.

Next, by the property of the multivariate Gaussian distribution we obtain

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\varepsilon)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}&\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B}&1\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\\
\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}&\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B})^{-1}&-(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B})^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\\
-\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B})^{-1}&1+\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}^{\top}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{B}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\\
\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}

which is in the subspace spanned by
\mathbf{B}
if and only if
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}
can be written in the form
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}
for a
d\times d
matrix
\mathbf{A}
, i.e
\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X},\varepsilon}\in\text{col}(\mathbf{B})
.

B.2
Proof of Proposition 2

Let
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}=\bm{\eta}_{k}^{*}\bm{\eta}_{k}^{*\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}
, so each element of
\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}
always has the same sign as that of
\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}
. Let
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}=\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}=\bm{\beta}_{k}^{*}\bm{\eta}_{k}^{*\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}^{*}_{k}
. Since
p
is fixed, the cross product
{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*\top}\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}\neq 0
with probability one for all
k=1,\ldots,d
. As a result, we have
\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{*})=\mathcal{P}({\mathbf{B}}^{*})
with probability one.

From the definition of the Lasso-SIR estimator and writing
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}=\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}+\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\lVert\tilde{\bm{y}}^{*}_{k}-\mathcal{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}-\mathcal{X}\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\rVert^{2}+\mu_{k}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}+\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{n}\lVert\tilde{\bm{y}}^{*}_{k}-\mathcal{X}{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}\rVert^{2}+\mu_{k}\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}\|_{1}.

Equivalently, we have

\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*^{\top}}\left(\frac{\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}}{n}\right)\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}-2\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*^{\top}}\left(\dfrac{\mathcal{X}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{y}}^{*}_{k}}{n}-\dfrac{\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}}{n}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}\right)\leq\mu_{k}\left(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}\|_{1}-\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}+\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\|_{1}\right).

By construction, we have
n^{-1}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{*}=\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}
, the eigenvector associated with the
k
th largest eigenvalue of
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}
. By the law of large numbers, we have
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}^{*}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\Lambda}^{*}
, so
\lVert\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}\rVert=o_{p}(1)
. Furthermore, as
n\to\infty
, we have
n^{-1}{\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}}\xrightarrow{p}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}

so
n^{-1}\mathcal{X}^{\top}\mathcal{X}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{k}^{*}\xrightarrow{p}\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}
.
Therefore, as
n\to\infty
. the vector
\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}
satisfies

\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*^{\top}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\leq\mu_{k}\left(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}\|_{1}-\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}^{*}_{k}+\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\|_{1}\right)+2\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}\right).

Since
\mu_{k}=o(1)
and
\lVert\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}-\tilde{\bm{\eta}}_{k}^{*}\rVert=o_{p}(1)
, we have
\lVert\bm{\delta}_{k}^{*}\rVert=o_{p}(1)
, so
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B}_{k}^{*})\xrightarrow{p}\mathcal{P}(\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}^{*})=\mathcal{P}({\mathbf{B}}^{*})
as
n\to\infty
.

B.3
Proof of Lemma 1

The instrument
\mathbf{Z}
is independent of both
\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{U}
, so the conditional expectation
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}=E(\mathbf{X}\mid\mathbf{Z})=\bm{\Gamma}\mathbf{Z}
is also independent of both
\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{U}
. By the properties of conditional expectation, we have

\displaystyle E(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y)

\displaystyle=E\left[E\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},y\right)\mid y\right]

\displaystyle=E\left[E\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X},\varepsilon\right)\mid y\right]~\quad\text{(since $y$ is a deterministic function of $\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{B}$ and $\varepsilon$})

\displaystyle=E\left[E\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right)\mid y\right]~\quad~~~\text{(since $\varepsilon$ and $\widehat{\mathbf{X}}$ are independent)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\Cov(\widehat{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{X})\mathbf{B}\left[\Var(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{X})\right]^{-1}\mathbf{B}^{\top}E(\mathbf{X}\mid y),

The developments below closely follow the work of

. Note

\bm{\Lambda}=\Cov\left\{E\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}\mid y\right)\right\}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\Cov\left\{E\left({\mathbf{Z}}\mid y\right)\right\}\bm{\Gamma}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Z}}-{\bm{\Xi}}\right)\bm{\Gamma},

where

. Similarly, we can write
\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}=\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\top}(\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{Z}}-\widehat{\bm{\Xi}})\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}

\widehat{\bm{\Xi}}=\dfrac{1}{H(c-1)}\mathcal{Z}^{\top}(\mathbf{I}_{H}\otimes\mathbf{P}_{c})\mathcal{Z},

(14)

with
\mathbf{P}_{c}=\mathbf{I}_{c}-\bm{e}_{c}\bm{e}_{c}^{\top}
,
\mathbf{e}_{c}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
being the

\mathbf{I}_{c}
denoting the

, and

We now bound the elementwise maximum norm
\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Lambda}}-\bm{\Lambda}\rVert_{\max}

j,k=1,\ldots,q
, we have

\displaystyle|\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}|

(15)

\displaystyle=|(\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\gamma}_{k})+(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\bm{\Omega}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k})+(\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k})|

\displaystyle\leq|\bm{\gamma}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}|+|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}|+|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}-\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}-\bm{\gamma}_{j}\|_{1}\|{\bm{\Omega}}\bm{\gamma}_{k}\|_{\infty}+\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\|_{1}\|\bm{\Omega}-\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\|_{\max}\|\bm{\gamma}_{k}\|_{1}+\|\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\|_{\infty}\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}-\bm{\gamma}_{k}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}-\bm{\gamma}_{j}\|_{1}\lVert\bm{\Omega}\rVert_{\max}\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{k}\rVert_{1}+\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\|_{1}\|\bm{\Omega}-\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\|_{\max}\|\bm{\gamma}_{k}\|_{1}+\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Omega}}\rVert_{\max}\lVert\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}\rVert_{1}\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{k}-\bm{\gamma}_{k}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}.

From Theorem 1 in the main text we have
\|\hat{\bm{\gamma}}_{j}-\bm{\gamma}_{j}\|_{1}=C\sigma_{\max}r\sqrt{\log(pq)/n}
with probability at least

\lVert\bm{\gamma}_{k}\rVert_{1}\leq L
and

(16)

For the second term

with probability at least

Lemma 3
.

Under the Conditions 4.1-4.5 in the main text, we have

The proof for Lemma

.
Combining Lemma 1 and union bound then, we obtain

(17)

with a probability at least

Finally for the term

(18)

(19)

Proof of Lemma 3

By the optimality of
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
, we obtain

\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}\leq\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}\|_{1}.

Letting
\bm{\delta}=\hat{\bm{\beta}}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
and substituting into (
22

\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|\bm{\delta}+\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}\leq\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|{\tilde{\bm{\beta}}}\|_{1}.

Expanding the square, cancelling the same terms on both sides, and rearranging terms, we have

\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\hat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{n}\bm{\delta}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}(\tilde{{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}})\leq\mu_{2}\left(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}-\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}+\bm{\delta}\|_{1}\right).

\hat{\bm{\eta}}=n^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\tilde{y}

) is equivalent to

\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\hat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\bm{\delta}^{\top}\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right)+\mu_{2}\left(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}-\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}+\bm{\delta}\|_{1}\right).

Applying Hölder’s inequality and the condition on
\mu_{2}

\displaystyle\bm{\delta}^{\top}\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\|\bm{\delta}\|_{1}\left\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\mu_{2}\|\bm{\delta}\|_{1}.

It follows that

\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\delta}\|_{1}+\left(\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}-\|\tilde{\bm{\beta}}+\bm{\delta}\|_{1}\right)\geq 0.

(24)

Furthermore, applying the (reverse) triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\beta}}}\rVert_{1}-\lVert\tilde{\bm{\beta}}+\bm{\delta}\rVert_{1}=\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\beta}}}_{S}\rVert_{1}-\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\beta}}}_{S}+{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}-\lVert\bm{\delta}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}-\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}.

Since
\lVert{\bm{\delta}}\rVert_{1}=\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}+\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\beta}}}\rVert_{1}-\lVert\tilde{\bm{\beta}}+\bm{\delta}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(iv)}}{{\leq}}\dfrac{1}{2}{\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}+\dfrac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}}+\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}-\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}=\dfrac{3}{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}-\dfrac{1}{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(v)}}{{\leq}}\dfrac{3}{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}.

It follows from step (iv) that
\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S^{c}}\rVert_{1}\leq 3\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{S}\rVert_{1}
, or
\bm{\delta}\in\mathcal{C}(S,3)
.

Next, we apply the following result which establishes the sample covariance matrix
n^{-1}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathcal{X}}
satisfies the RE condition with a parameter
\kappa_{2}
with high probability.

Proposition 4
.

Assume Conditions 4.1-4.2, 4.5 and the requirements of Theorem 1 are satisfied. Then there exists a constant
\kappa_{2}>0
such that
\lVert\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}^{1/2}\bm{\delta}\rVert_{2}^{2}\geq\kappa_{2}\lVert\bm{\delta}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, for any

, with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)-\exp(-C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
, for constants
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
.

Conditioned on this RE condition, step (v) implies

\begin{split}\dfrac{1}{2}\kappa_{2}\lVert\bm{\delta}\rVert_{2}^{2}{\leq}\dfrac{1}{2n}\lVert\hat{\mathcal{X}}{\bm{\delta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}&{\leq}\dfrac{1}{2}\mu_{2}\lVert{\bm{\delta}}\rVert_{1}+\mu_{2}\left(\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\beta}}}\rVert_{1}-\lVert\hat{{\bm{\beta}}}\rVert_{1}\right)\\

As a result, we obtain

as required.

B.7
Proof of Theorem 3

The proof of this theorem has the same steps as the primal-dual witness technique, which are used in the proof for variable selection consistency of the Lasso estimator in the regular linear model
(
Wainwright,, 2019
, see Section 7.5.2,)
, as well as in the two-stage Lasso estimator of
Lin et al., (2015)
. In our context, the proposed two-stage Lasso SIR estimator is the minimizer of

L(\bm{\beta})=\dfrac{1}{2n}\|\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\bm{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{2}\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}.

Applying the Karush–Kuhn-Tucker optimality condition, the estimator
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
has to satisfy

\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{\hat{S}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}})=\mu_{2}\text{sgn}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\hat{S}}),

(35)

and

\left\|\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{\hat{S}^{c}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}})\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mu_{2}.

(36)

It suffices to find a solution
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
such that both (
35
) and (
36
) hold. To achieve this, the idea of the primal-dual witness technique is first let
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S^{c}}=0
, then construct
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}
from (
35
), and finally show that the obtained
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies (
36
). Central to this development is the following lemma regarding the bound of the sample covariance
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}
.

Lemma 4
.

Assume Condition 4.7 is satisfied. If there exists a constant
C
such that

CsL\left\{L\sqrt{\dfrac{\log q}{n}}+\sigma_{\max}r\sqrt{\dfrac{\log pq}{n}}\right\}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\phi(4-\alpha)}

(37)

then with probability at least
1-\exp(C^{\prime}\log q)-\exp(C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
, the matrix
\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{X}}}=\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Sigma}}_{\mathbf{Z}}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
, where
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}
is defined from the first stage with the tuning parameters
\mu_{1,j}
as stated in Theorem 1, satisfies

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4-\alpha}{2(2-\alpha)}\varphi,\quad\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{S^{c}S}\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha}{2}.

(38)

Using a similar argument as in the proof of Theorem 2,
there also exist constants
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
such that

\left\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leq\dfrac{\alpha}{4-\alpha}\mu_{2},

(39)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-C^{\prime}\log q)-\exp(-C^{\prime\prime}\log pq)
. Hence, we will condition on both events (
38
) and (
39
) in the remaining part of the proof.

Next, recall
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}=n^{-1}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{S}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}
, and by definition of
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}
we have

, which is the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue of
\hat{\bm{\Lambda}}
. Since
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S^{c}}=0

35
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mu_{2}\text{sgn}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\hat{S}})=\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{\hat{S}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}

\displaystyle=(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}),

where
\tilde{\bm{\beta}}
is defined previously. Hence, we obtain

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}=\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}^{-1}\left\{(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})-\mu_{2}\text{sgn}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{\hat{S}})\right\},

and therefore

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}^{-1}\|_{\infty}\left\{\left\|\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}\right\|_{\infty}+\mu_{2}\right\}.

(41)

Substituting (
38
) and (
39

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4-\alpha}{2(2-\alpha)}\varphi\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{4-\alpha}\mu_{2}+\mu_{2}\right)=\dfrac{2}{(2-\alpha)}\varphi\mu_{2}<\min_{j\in S}|\tilde{\beta}_{j}|,

\min_{j\in S}|\tilde{\beta}_{j}|=k^{-1}|\bm{\eta}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\eta}}|\min_{j\in S}|\beta_{j}|>k^{-1}\hat{\rho}\min_{j\in S}|\beta_{j}|>\dfrac{2}{(2-\alpha)}\varphi\mu_{2},

and by the choice of
\mu_{2}

\text{sgn}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S})=\text{sgn}(\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S^{c}}=0

\hat{S}=S

It remains to show that
\hat{\bm{\beta}}
satisfies (
36

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{\hat{S}^{c}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}})

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S^{c}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S^{c}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S^{c}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{S^{c}}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{S}-\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S^{c}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}

\displaystyle=\left(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S^{c}}-\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S^{c}}^{\top}\hat{\mathcal{X}}_{S}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S}\right)-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{S^{c}S}\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}^{-1}\left\{(\hat{\bm{\eta}}_{S}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}}_{SS}\tilde{\bm{\beta}}_{S})-\mu_{2}\text{sgn}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{{S}})\right\},

\displaystyle\left\|\dfrac{1}{n}\hat{\mathcal{X}}^{\top}_{\hat{S}^{c}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\hat{\mathcal{X}}\hat{\bm{\beta}})\right\|_{\infty}

Proof of Theorem 4

Consider the multiple index model with
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}

\bm{\eta}
and

\bm{\eta}

5

Appendix D

D.1
Mouse obesity data

In this subsection, we further interpret the biological consequences of the genes selected by different estimators as outlined in Section 7.1 of the main paper. To that end, we conducted a gene ontology enrichment analysis for the genes chosen by each method. By annotating these genes for their participation in gene ontology, this analysis aims to identify which biological functions are over-represented in the selected genes
(
Bleazard et al.,, 2015
)
. This over-representation is measured by the
p
-values, adjusted for multiple comparisons, of the null hypothesis that this gene set is randomly associated with the GO. A smaller
p
-value thus indicates a more significant biological function. Furthermore, the gene ontology is divided into three main groups: biological processes (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC).
Figure
3
indicates that all the three methods capture a strong association between the obesity and lipid-related processes, including lipid transport, metabolism, and regulation. The one-stage lasso SIR estimator focuses on genes regulating lipid transport and metabolic processes (e.g., lipid transporter activity, monocarboxylic acid transport).

By contrast, both the two stage lasso and two-stage lasso SIR estimators emphasize plasma lipoprotein metabolism (e.g., plasma lipoprotein particle clearance, protein-lipid complex), with the former putting more emphasis on the interplay between lipid metabolism and immune regulation (e.g., immunoglobulin binding) while the latter focuses more on lipid catabolism and extracellular lipid transport (e.g., extracellular matrix structural constituent, lipase activity).
