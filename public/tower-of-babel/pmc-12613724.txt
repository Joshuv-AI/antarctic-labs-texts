Associations of ever breastfeeding with risk of all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality among women with and without diabetes: a cohort study
Authors: Wenjuan Zhou, Lingdan Zhou, Dan Li, Hua Li, Le Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough breastfeeding history is recognized as a potential protective factor against all-cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in women, its impact on these outcomes remains underexplored, particularly in the context of diabetes. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the association between ever breastfeeding and mortality risks from all causes and CVD in adult women with and without diabetes.MethodsThis prospective cohort analysis utilized nationally representative U.S. data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2012). Mortality follow-up through December 31, 2019, was established via linkage to the NHANES Public-Use Linked Mortality File using probabilistic matching to National Death Index records. To estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), we utilized Cox proportional hazards regression models. We evaluated interactions between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status using both multiplicative and additive scales. The stability of the results was assessed using sensitivity analyses.ResultsAmong the 10,965 parous women studied (mean age 53.2 years), 1,938 had diabetes and 6,310 reported ever breastfeeding at baseline. During median follow-up of 142 months (range: 7–20 years), 2,178 all-cause and 673 CVD deaths occurred. No significant associations were observed for CVD mortality in either group. Additive interaction analyses revealed a relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) of -0.41 (95% CI -0.69, -0.14), indicating subadditive joint effects. Background

Breastfeeding supplies infants with essential energy and immune protection, thereby supporting their healthy growth and development [1–3]. Concurrently, an increasing number of studies suggest that breastfeeding may positively influence the mother’s long-term health, potentially reducing the risks of cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, metabolic syndrome, and certain cancers [3–11]. Several studies have documented an association between breastfeeding and reduced risks of mortality from all causes and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in women [12–15].

Individuals with diabetes, characterized by insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, exhibit an elevated risk of CVD and premature mortality compared with the general population [16]. Breastfeeding has been shown to improve maternal insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism, with these beneficial effects potentially persisting for many years and thereby enhancing long-term maternal health [17–19]. Thus, elucidating the relationship between ever breastfeeding and the risks of all-cause and CVD mortality in women with diabetes is crucial for refining public health strategies in this vulnerable population. However, data in this area are limited, and it remains unclear whether and to what extent these associations differ between individuals with and without diabetes.

To address this research gap, we leveraged a large, long-term cohort from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to investigate the relationship between ever breastfeeding and the risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among both diabetic and non-diabetic women. Additionally, we aimed to determine whether diabetes status modifies the association between ever breastfeeding and these outcomes, and if so, to what extent.

Methods

Data source

The NHANES, conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (a division of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention), is a stratified, multistage probability cross-sectional survey of the non-institutionalized U.S. population. Demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related interviews were conducted in participants’ homes, whereas medical examinations, additional interviews, and laboratory assessments were performed at mobile examination centers (MECs) [20]. For sensitive topics, participants were interviewed privately at the MEC. Approval for the NHANES study protocol was granted by the research ethics review board of the National Centre for Health Statistics, with written informed consent obtained from participants during enrollment (available online) [21]. For the current analysis, ethical approval and participant consent were waived since it utilized publicly accessible, deidentified data.

In this cohort study, we included data from adult women aged ≥ 20 years across NHANES cycles spanning 1999–2012 (where each cycle represents a two-year survey period, e.g., 1999–2000, 2001–2002), as these were the only cycles containing breastfeeding information in the MEC questionnaire. After excluding participants with missing data on breastfeeding (n = 6,548), follow-up (n = 10), diabetes status (n = 806), and other covariates (n = 1,429), a total of 10,965 adult women were eligible for analysis. Ever breastfeeding and mortality

Ever Breastfeeding was assessed via a structured Reproductive Health questionnaire. From 1999 to 2006, the questionnaire asked, “Did you breastfeed any of your children?” (yes/no). From 2007 to 2012, the question was revised to, “Did you breastfeed any of your children for at least one month?” (yes/no). Participants who answered “yes” were classified into the self-reported breastfeeding group [9].

We utilized the NHANES Public-Use Linked Mortality File [22], which employs a probabilistic matching algorithm to link with National Death Index (NDI) records for mortality ascertainment [23], with data current through December 31, 2019. The NDI constitutes the National Center for Health Statistics’ (NCHS) centralized database encompassing all U.S. mortality events. Cause-specific mortality data within the NDI have been validated to accurately classify deaths with minimal potential for misclassification [24]. All-cause mortality was identified as death from any cause. CVD mortality was defined using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) criteria, encompassing these conditions: acute/chronic rheumatic heart diseases (I00-I09), hypertensive disorders (I11, I13), ischemic heart diseases (I20-I25), pulmonary heart diseases (I26-I28), other specified cardiovascular disorders (I29), additional cardiac diseases (I30-I51), and cerebrovascular diseases (I60-I69).

Identification of diabetes

Diabetes status was assessed during NHANES survey participation using the following diagnostic criteria [25]: ①fasting glucose levels ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, ②two-hour oral glucose tolerance test blood glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, ③random blood glucose levels ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, ④glycohemoglobin (HbA1c) levels ≥ 6.5%, ⑤doctor told you have diabetes, or ⑥the use of glucose-lowering medications.

Other covariates

Drawing from prior research, the covariates considered included age, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, family poverty-income ratio (PIR), smoking and drinking status, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), number of live births, and hyperlipidemia. Covariate definitions primarily utilized raw NHANES variables, with transformations applied where required for clinical relevance or model parsimony: ①Categorical variables (race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, smoking and drinking status, number of live births) maintained original NHANES coding but were recategorized; ②Continuous variables underwent transformation: PIR, Continuous ratio categorized according to the U.S. Department of Agriculture poverty guidelines; Physical activity, time of weekly activities calculated according to PAQ variables; ③Composite definitions: Hyperlipidemia, based on the raw coding of medication use or lipid parameters from NHANES, we derived relevant indicator using the criteria established by the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) and the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines. Marital status was categorized into four groups: married, never married, living with a partner, and other. Consistent with a U.S. government report, family income was stratified into three levels based on PIR: low (PIR ≤ 1.30), medium (PIR=1.31 - 3.50), and high (PIR >3.50) [26]. Educational status was classified into three categories: below high school, high school or equivalent, and beyond high school. Smoking status was categorized into never smokers (< 100 cigarettes in their lifetime), current smokers, and former smokers (>100 cigarettes before quitting), consistent with prior research [25]. Participants were classified into three categories of alcohol consumption: never drinkers (< 12 drinks in their lifetime), former drinkers (≥ 12 drinks in 1 year but no alcohol consumption in the past year, or did not drink last year but drank ≥ 12 drinks in their lifetime), and current drinkers (≥ 12 drinks in any 1 year and alcohol consumption in the past year) [26]. Physical activity was defined as the weekly time allocated to various activities, including walking or biking, household chores, occupational activities, and recreational activities [25, 27]. BMI was measured by trained health technicians at the MECs. The number of live births was assessed using the following questions: “How many of your pregnancies resulted in a live birth?” (1999–2004 NHANES cycles) and “How many of your deliveries resulted in a live birth?” (2005–2012 NHANES cycles). Hyperlipidemia was identified by the presence of any of the following: ①Lipid-lowering medication use; ②Elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dL); ③Elevated cholesterol (total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, Low-Density Lipoprotein ≥ 130 mg/dL, or High- Density Lipoprotein < 40 mg/dL).

Statistical analyses

This study constitutes a secondary analysis of publicly available data. For the NHANES dataset, incorporation of sampling weights and design variables is recommended in all analytical procedures, as failure to account for sampling specifications may yield biased national estimates and artificially inflate significance levels. Sampling weights were adjusted according to NHANES analysis guidelines to account for survey design changes across cycles. Data for our study were derived from household interviews and MECs during the NHANES survey. Consistent with the NHANES guidelines for the use of survey sample weights, we utilized MEC-specific weights [28]. A 4-year MEC exam weight (4-year MEC weight) was only used for the 1999–2002 data collection years. For the 2003–2012 data collection year, a 2-year MEC weight was used. According to NHANES analysis guidelines, sampling weights were determined as follows: for the 1999–2002 period, the weights were 2/7 × the 4-year MEC weight; for the 2003–2012 period, the weights were 1/7 × the 2-year MEC weight. As breastfeeding data were collected in the MEC, follow-up duration for each participant was calculated as the interval between the MEC completion date and the earliest occurrence of either the date of death recorded in mortality data or the final follow-up date (December 31, 2019).

Normality was evaluated using quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots (Additional Fig. 1). Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas skewed continuous variables were described using median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as unweighted counts and weighted percentages. Statistical analyses involved comparing continuous variables among groups using either the independent samples t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test, depending on the distribution of the data. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test when appropriate. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models to evaluate the association between ever breastfeeding and risks of all-cause and CVD mortality among adult women with and without diabetes. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, with no indication of violations. Three models were constructed for the analysis. Model 1 accounted for age, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, PIR, and NHANES cycle. Model 2 incorporated additional adjustments for smoking and drinking status. Model 3 included further adjustments for physical activity, BMI, number of live births, and hyperlipidemia.

To determine whether diabetes modifies the relationship between ever breastfeeding and the risks of all-cause and CVD mortality, we evaluated interactions on both multiplicative and additive scales. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated by comparing models with and without an interaction term between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status, utilizing a likelihood ratio test. Additive interaction was assessed using three indices: the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (SI). For additive interaction, the 95% CIs of RERI and AP would not include 0, and the CIs of SI would not include 1. To assess the joint associations, we further classified participants into four groups based on breastfeeding history (ever vs. never) and diabetes status (with vs. without). We then calculated HRs for all-cause and CVD mortality in each group, using participants without diabetes and who had never breastfed as the reference group.

Additionally, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we excluded participants with a history of cancer at baseline, as this condition might have increased mortality rates. Second, to minimize potential reverse causality, we excluded participants who died within 2 years of recruitment. Third, we used multiple imputation with five replications to handle missing data on covariates. Fourth, we additionally accounted for potential confounding effects from CVD, hypertension, history of cancer, and chronic kidney disease on the outcomes.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and the Free Statistics analysis platform version 1.9.2 (Beijing, China). Results with a two-sided P value < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. Data were analyzed from August to October 2024.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 10,965 eligible participants (mean age, 53.2 years), of whom 1,938 had diabetes. Among 10,965 participants, 6,310 (57.55%) reported ever breastfeeding at baseline. Table 1 presents the baseline clinical characteristics of the participants and compares those with ever breastfeeding to those without, stratified by diabetes status. All participants were parous women. Individuals with diabetes were more likely to be older and obese compared with those without diabetes. Among both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals, those with ever breastfeeding were more likely to be highly educated, non-smokers, and to engage in more prolonged physical activity compared with those never breastfeeding.

Ever breastfeeding affects the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality in adult women with diabetes

During the 7- to 20-year follow-up period of NHANES (1999–2012), we documented 2,178 all-cause deaths and 673 CVD deaths. The median follow-up duration was 142 months. After multivariable adjustment for age, race/ethnicity, marital status, education level, PIR, NHANES cycle, smoking and drinking status, physical activity, BMI, number of live births, and hyperlipidemia, ever breastfeeding was significantly associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.63, 0.92; p = 0.004) but not with CVD mortality (HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.72, 1.30; p = 0.804) among participants with diabetes. In contrast, no significant associations were observed among participants without diabetes for all-cause mortality (HR 1.05; 95% CI 0.93, 1.18; p = 0.425) or CVD mortality (HR 1.17; 95% CI 0.94, 1.46; p = 0.162). A significant interaction was observed between ever breastfeeding and all-cause mortality in participants with and without diabetes when stratified by diabetes status (Pinteraction for the likelihood ratio test = 0.012). In contrast, no interaction was observed for CVD mortality (Pinteraction= 0.259) (Table 2). A significant negative additive interaction was observed between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status on the risk of all-cause mortality. Based on the three indices of additive interaction (RERI, AP, and SI), the relative excess risk (RERI) of all-cause mortality due to additive interaction was − 0.41. Furthermore, the additive interaction accounted for 30% of the all-cause mortality among individuals exposed to both risk factors. SI of 0.49 (95% CI 0.31, 0.78) indicates that the observed joint risk of all-cause mortality among women with both diabetes and ever breastfeeding was 51% lower than expected under additive effects (i.e., less than the sum of individual risks). No significant additive interaction was observed for CVD mortality (RERI − 0.19; 95% CI -0.74, 0.36; AP -0.10; 95% CI -0.39, 0.20; SI 0.83; 95% CI 0.49, 1.41) (Table 3). Fig. 2 illustrates the joint associations of breastfeeding history (never vs. ever) and diabetes status (without vs. with) with all-cause and CVD mortality. Among individuals with diabetes, ever breastfeeding was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.400; 95% CI 1.219, 1.609).

Sensitivity analyses

Table 4 presents the results of sensitivity analyses. After excluding participants with a history of cancer at baseline, the adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 0.77 (95% CI 0.62, 0.95; p = 0.015; Pinteraction=0.010) among individuals with diabetes who had a history of breastfeeding compared with those who had never breastfed. After excluding participants who died within 2 years of follow-up, the adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 0.75 (95% CI 0.62, 0.91; p = 0.003; Pinteraction=0.005). Among the 13,200 participants, diabetes status was missing for 806 individuals (6.11%). We performed multiple imputations for missing covariates among the remaining 12,394 participants. Relative to individuals with never breastfeeding, those who had breastfed exhibited an adjusted HR of 0.76 for all-cause mortality (95% CI 0.65, 0.90; p < 0.001, Pinteraction=0.008). In addition, after adjusting for potential confounders including cardiovascular disease, hypertension, history of cancer, and chronic kidney disease, the association between ever breastfeeding and all-cause mortality as well CVD mortality remained consistent with the initial observations (Additional Table 1).

Discussion

Utilizing NHANES data (1999–2012), we performed a longitudinal cohort study involving adult women in the United States (U.S.). Our analysis revealed effect modification by diabetes status on the association between ever breastfeeding and all-cause mortality. Specifically, ever breastfeeding was associated with a 24% lower all-cause mortality risk among women with diabetes (HR 0.76; 95% CI 0.63, 0.92), but showed no significant association in non-diabetic women (HR 1.05; 95% CI 0.93, 1.18; Pinteraction=0.012). No mortality associations were observed for cardiovascular outcomes in either subgroup. Various sensitivity analyses corroborated the robustness of these findings. Moreover, our study revealed significant multiplicative and additive interactions between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status on all-cause mortality risk, offering quantitative evidence of the impact of these interactions on the outcome.

To date, the relationship between breastfeeding and risks of all-cause and CVD mortality has exhibited substantial heterogeneity across studies. Critical methodological differences may account for these inconsistencies, particularly regarding exposure definitions, diabetes stratification, and confounder adjustment. Significant variations in how breastfeeding history was operationalized in previous investigations have contributed to divergent findings. The Nurses’ Health Study [15] demonstrated a nonlinear dose-response relationship between total lifetime breastfeeding duration and mortality. Conversely, the Australian cohort [14] revealed that breastfeeding for 12 months per child was associated with reduced CVD mortality risk among women aged over 45 years. In contrast, studies employing binary ever/never breastfeeding definitions reported conflicting outcomes: European [12] and Japanese [13] cohorts found breastfeeding associated with decreased all-cause mortality, whereas the Norwegian cohort [29] documented elevated CVD mortality among parous women under 65 years who never breastfed—though without significant all-cause mortality association—potentially reflecting threshold effects obscured by dichotomous exposure classification.

Diabetes mellitus, particularly type 2 diabetes, is characterized by insulin resistance and chronic hyperglycemia - core pathophysiological features that promote endothelial dysfunction, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation. These conditions that subsequently elevate the risk of CVD and premature mortality. Notably, the majority of cited studies [12–15, 29] did not stratify analyses by diabetes status despite established pathophysiological differences. Only the Nurses’ Health Study [11] focused on diabetic subgroups (type 2 diabetes or gestational diabetes mellitus), reporting an association between longer breastfeeding duration and reduced CVD risk—though lacking data on all-cause and CVD mortality risks. Our stratified analyses revealed that ever breastfeeding was associated with decreased all-cause mortality exclusively in diabetic women while showing no association in non-diabetic individuals.

Variability in confounder adjustment was also evident across prior studies. Early research [29], adjusting for age, education, marital status, physical activity, smoking, and parity, found no association with all-cause mortality. Subsequent investigations incorporating additional adjustments for race/ethnicity, BMI, alcohol consumption, occupational status, socioeconomic factors, medical history, and reproductive history [12, 13, 15] consistently demonstrated protective associations. In the present study, after comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, ever breastfeeding was associated with reduced all-cause mortality among diabetic women, yet exhibited no benefit for CVD mortality. Among non-diabetic women, no significant associations were observed for either outcome—a finding that potentially explains population-level discrepancies in prior literature. Critically, our analysis identified a statistically significant multiplicative interaction between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status regarding all-cause mortality risk. We further quantified additive interactions between these exposures to assess their public health implications. The RERI and AP were both below zero, while the SI did not include 1, collectively indicating that the combined effect of diabetes status and ever breastfeeding was less than the sum of their individual effects. These findings substantiate that failure to stratify by diabetes status in previous cohorts obscured differential effects of breastfeeding. Our study advances this field by: demonstrating survival benefits of breastfeeding in diabetic women; quantitatively establishing how breastfeeding attenuates diabetes-related excess mortality through additive interactions.

This study has several strengths. First, we used a nationally representative sample of adult women in the U.S. Second, we adjusted for baseline characteristics and important potential confounders. Third, we examined these relationships across strata of diabetes status and conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the association between ever breastfeeding and all-cause mortality risk. Additionally, we quantified the additive interaction between ever breastfeeding and diabetes status to assess the association of ever breastfeeding with all-cause mortality in both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

Breastfeeding, as a potential modulating factor, may influence mortality risk in women with diabetes through several mechanisms. First, both animal studies and observational research suggest that breastfeeding plays a crucial role in resetting maternal metabolism through mobilizing stored fat, improving glucose uptake, modulating insulin resistance, and enhancing postpartum lipid metabolism [30, 31]. These effects may persist for many years, providing long-term benefits to maternal health and reducing the risk of mortality in later life [6, 17, 19]. Second, breastfeeding promotes oxytocin secretion, which not only facilitates milk ejection but also exerts multiple beneficial effects, including lowering blood pressure, dilating blood vessels, enhancing glucose uptake and lipid metabolism in skeletal muscle and fat cells, improving β-cell function, and inhibiting inflammation [32, 33]. These physiological effects may help explain the association between ever breastfeeding and reduced mortality risk in diabetic patients. However, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of these associations.

However, several limitations should be considered in this study. First, self-reported interview data may introduce recall bias or misclassification. Second, as an NHANES secondary analysis, our study lacked data on total breastfeeding duration, precluding dose-response assessment between breastfeeding duration and all-cause mortality. Previous evidence indicated a nonlinear association between total breastfeeding duration and reduced all-cause/CVD mortality (Pnonlinearity<0.01), with minimal risk observed at 16–24 months’ duration [15]. Third, despite multivariate adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Fourth, our study population appeared younger in the non-diabetic group, with some baseline characteristics differing between the two groups. However, we used a large sample size, particularly in the non-diabetic group, and constructed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. Additionally, we conducted sensitivity analyses. Fifth, NHANES’ cross-sectional design precluded longitudinal diabetes onset data, preventing definitive determination of breastfeeding-diabetes temporality. Although chronological patterns suggest breastfeeding typically precedes diabetes diagnosis (reproductive peak preceding typical diabetes onset age), residual confounding remains possible. Sixth, absent biomarkers (C-peptide, diabetes-associated autoantibodies) precluded type 1/type 2 diabetes differentiation, limiting subtype-specific effect assessment. Given these limitations, future multicentre randomised controlled trials should be conducted, incorporating systematic collection of longitudinal lactation metrics (e.g., exclusive breastfeeding and breastfeeding duration) and diabetes-related data to validate our findings, investigate threshold effects, and elucidate effects across distinct diabetes subtypes.

Conclusions

Drawing from a large prospective cohort study, we observed that ever breastfeeding significantly correlates with reduced all-cause mortality among women with diabetes but not among non-diabetic individuals. This finding suggests that ever breastfeeding has significant health effects on women with diabetes. Further research is warranted to clarify the causal link between ever breastfeeding and all-cause mortality risk in this population.
