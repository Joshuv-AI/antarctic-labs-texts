An evaluation of the surveillance system for monitoring and reporting adverse events following immunization in Kebbi State, Northern Nigeria: a mixed method approach
Authors: Semeeh Akinwale Omoleke, Laurent Cleenewerck de Kiev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite the benefits of periodic evaluation of the vaccine safety surveillance system, no formal assessment, to our knowledge, has been conducted in Nigeria. Hence, this study evaluated the surveillance system for adverse events following immunization (AEFI) to ascertain the system’s functionality to inform vaccine safety considerations and guide communication strategies for demand generation.Materials and methodsThe study employed a mixed-method approach. Survey questionnaires were administered to 274 routine immunization service providers in Kebbi State, Northern Nigeria, and data were analyzed descriptively using SPSS. In this study, 10 Key Informant Interviews and two Focus Group Discussions were conducted with senior officers and managers at sub-national and national levels within the immunization and surveillance landscape in Nigeria. The interview recordings were cleaned minimally, transcribed, and manually analyzed thematically. Finally, methodological triangulation was done to improve research rigor and provide a better understanding of the phenomena under investigation.ResultsOf the respondents, 201(73.4%) reported that the surveillance system can inform vaccine safety considerations while 170(62%) reported that the AEFI surveillance system can determine the magnitude of AEFI within the population. Further, 173(63%) reported that the surveillance system can provide timely feedback about causality assessment. However, 158(58%) of the respondents stated that the surveillance system is competent in informing communication strategies to improve immunization demand. Triangulation was done which showed dissonance in AEFI surveillance and vaccine safety considerations but partial agreement in immunization demand generation. Further, AEFI surveillance system attributes’ triangulation revealed agreements (convergence) on simplicity and timeliness; partial agreements on acceptability, data quality, sensitivity, flexibility, and completeness; dissonance on representativeness and silence on stability, indicating a sub-optimal performance of the AEFI surveillance system in the study setting. Finally, the study unearthed some underlying health system factors impeding the AEFI surveillance system from fully fulfilling its objectives.ConclusionThe AEFI surveillance system in Northern Nigeria is well established but functioning sub-optimally. Based on the study findings, the capacity to provide information on vaccine safety exists but it is not robust enough to generate sufficient and convincing vaccine safety data and guide communication strategies for vaccine demand generation, especially for new vaccines and those under emergency authorization use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20356-5.Keywords: AEFI Surveillance System, Evaluation, Vaccine Safety, Demand Generation, Kebbi State, Nigeria

Introduction

In the last 100 years, the profound role of vaccines in the prevention and control of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) remains incontrovertible [1–4]. Vaccines are complex biological products that may include live or attenuated organism(s), toxins of pathogens or their surface proteins, preservatives, and adjuvants [4, 5]. Each of these components may impact vaccine safety, most importantly, allergens [6]. Even alterations in manufacturing processes may impact the quality, safety, and efficacy of vaccines. Hence, adverse reactions following immunization may occur despite the scrutiny during vaccine development and licensure by national regulatory agencies and global health bodies.

An Adverse Event Following Immunization (AEFI) is any untoward medical event that occurs following immunization and does not necessarily have a causal relationship with vaccine usage [6]. Consequently, a system has been put in place- AEFI surveillance- to monitor and report vaccine safety issues and was designed to be an integral part of the national immunization program and disease surveillance system in many countries, including Nigeria [7, 8]. Further, AEFI surveillance and documentation is a component of the pharmacovigilance system. However, vaccine pharmacovigilance commences from the preclinical stage to clinical trials through large-scale administration either for emergency use or post-licensure surveillance and to the point of disseminating vaccine profiles, including the documentation of adverse events and reactions [9, 10].

In Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) is saddled with the Implementation of the National Policy on Pharmacovigilance and the provision of scientific guidance and information on the safe use of medicines, including vaccines; medicine safety communication; sourcing, collating, preserving, storing, retrieving and dissemination of information on vaccines, food, and other regulated products [11]. Functionally, the generation of vaccine safety data essentially takes place at the health facility level (routine services) and vaccination delivery sites during mass immunization (campaigns) under the overall coordination of the National Primary Health Care Development Agency, NPHCDA (which also has its sub-national level counterpart performing a similar function across the 36 States and Federal Capital Territory). Both NAFDAC and NPHCDA are critical members of the National Expert Committee that reviews AEFI cases- analyses of AEFI clustering, classification of events, etc.

A report published in 2018 by WHO colleagues reviewed cases of AEFI reported by countries in the WHO/UNICEF Joint Reporting Form of the WHO African Regional Office (AFRO) between 2010 and 2015, highlighting low reporting and its causes and explored how countries can rely on new opportunities and systems to improve their reporting rates and vaccine safety [7]. The analysis of performance from 2010–2015 showed that only five countries have fully implemented vaccine safety monitoring and pharmacovigilance plans in compliance with the Global Vaccine Safety Initiative (GVSI) blueprint [7]. In addition, many African countries have not been able to meet the standard reporting rate of at least 10 per 100,000 surviving infants per year as adopted by WHO [12]. According to the global data analyzed from 2000 to 2015, Africa had the second lowest case reporting rate of 74 per 100,000 surviving infants, which is much lower than the global average of 549 per 100,000 surviving infants, while the Eastern Mediterranean Region had the highest rate of 2,740 per 100,000 surviving infants [12]. Indeed, the improvement in the implementation of vaccine safety monitoring and pharmacovigilance is slow despite new vaccine introduction in many of these poor-performing countries. Nigeria has an established pharmacovigilance system with structures and AEFI guidelines in place. However, Nigeria is one of the countries within the African region with low or sub-optimal reporting of AEFI, pointing to issues around the functionality or agility of the system to detect, report, and, where applicable, investigate AEFI cases to inform public health and regulatory actions [13–15].

A well-established and effectively functioning AEFI surveillance system can measure the background rate of AEFI, monitor AEFI trends by antigens (vaccines), detect changes in immunization practices, and guide immediate public health action [16, 17]. By inference, it can guide vaccine and immunization policy formulation and implementation, provide information about vaccine safety, build confidence in immunization service delivery towards improved coverage, and ultimately contribute to reducing morbidity and mortality from VPDs.

Following the literature review, we found that very few AEFI surveillance system evaluation studies were conducted within the African region using the CDC Surveillance System Attributes. The CDC surveillance system attributes include sensitivity, flexibility, acceptability, data quality, timeliness and completeness, representativeness, and stability [18]. Before this study and to our knowledge, no formal assessment or evaluation has been conducted in Nigeria to assess the functionality of the AEFI surveillance system. Hence, we evaluated the AEFI surveillance system in Kebbi State, Nigeria, to ascertain the functionality and capacity of the system to generate robust and reliable data that will inform vaccine safety considerations and guide communication strategies for immunization demand generation.

Materials and methods

Study area

Kebbi State is one of the northwestern States of Nigeria with both local and international borders [13]. Locally, it borders Zamfara, Sokoto, and Niger States. It has international borders with Niger and Benin Republics. There are 21 Local Government Areas (LGAs) in the State with 225 political wards and four traditional Emirates. Based on the 2006 census population, the State has a projected population of 5,119,659 (2021) and an under-one population of 204,786.

Kebbi State was selected as the investigation site (please see Fig. 1 showing the map of Kebbi State indicating the LGAs and the borders) because of the apparent research gaps, ease of generating data, and access to some relevant records, and the findings in the State may reflect the status of AEFI surveillance systems in other states in Nigeria.

Map of Kebbi State showing the local government areas and borders

Routine immunization service delivery at the health facility level in Kebbi State

Regarding the RI service provision in the State, 23 out of the 31 General Hospitals (secondary healthcare facilities) offer RI services, while 619 of the 660 Primary Health Centers (PHCs) and Health Clinics offer routine immunization (RI). However, only five private health facilities (HFs) out of 18 offer routine immunization services in the State. Three of these private health facilities are in the State capital (Birnin Kebbi), while the rest are hosted by rural LGAs of Koko Besse and Dandi. Essentially, at the sub-national level, the PHC services are managed by the State Primary Health Care Development Agency (SPHCDA), with some oversight by the top management of the Local Government Health Authority. This is in line with PHC system reform, i.e., PHC Under-One-Roof in Nigeria, to reposition the SPHCDA by implementing a unitary management system, staff recruitment and promotion, and monitoring and accountability framework [19]. The implementation of PHC-Under-One-Roof is yet to fully materialize in Kebbi State, though progress has been made [20].

An overview of the AEFI surveillance reporting system in Kebbi State, Nigeria

In practice at the health facility level, a trained RI provider is in the custody of the various AEFI data tools and does the documentation and archiving. The RI provider line lists all the AEFI cases irrespective of the classification. In the event of a serious AEFI, the RI provider fills the line list and AEFI reporting form and transmits it to the LGA Disease Surveillance and Notification Officer (DSNO) and LGA Immunization Officer (LIO), or the Routine Immunization Officer (RIO) usually within 24 h, using a short messaging service (SMS) or any other telecommunication medium. The alert should also be sent to the NAFDAC, and this triggers the investigation by the State Investigation Team. Every month, a line list of all AEFI cases is submitted to LGA DSNO. The summary record is also submitted to the LGA Monitoring & Evaluation (M & E) officer for data entry in the District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2). If there is no detected AEFI case for the month, then zero reporting can be done using the appropriate data tool. It should be noted that the forms or data tools are normally supplied by the NPHCDA with the support of the health partners (WHO and UNICEF). Occasionally, the State Governments may also supply data tools through the SPHCDA, when funds are available and in the event of a shortage.

There are two channels for the AEFI data management system- Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) and District Health Information System 2 (DHIS2). The LGA DSNO captures the monthly summary of the AEFI report onto IDSR 003 and submits the line list to the State DSNO. The LGA M & E officer enters the summary into the DHIS2 platform. The State DSNO enters the AEFI data into the AEFI database for storage and decision-making at this administrative level. The State DSNO does the final transmission of the AEFI data to the National level. Similarly, the State M & E officer transmits the collated DHIS2 data to the national level.

In our working experience at the subnational level, we found significant discrepancies between IDSR and DHIS 2 data even though the primary data emanates from the health facility level. This gap may be explained by differences in the data management systems (different tools and chain of data transmission), varied importance attached to these datasets at different levels, and failure to harmonize data between the M & E and Disease Surveillance Units. Normally, all the private health facilities offering RI services and treating AEFI cases should report all AEFI to their respective LGA Immunization Officer within the catchment area of the facilities who immediately shares with the LGA DSNO. Historically, our experience within the immunization landscape in the State suggests gross AEFI under-reporting from private and public HFs and limited involvement of private healthcare providers in immunization and AEFI surveillance.

Nigeria set up the AEFI National Expert Committee (NEC) in 2012 with the membership drawn from the Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH), NPCHDA, NAFDAC, Experts from academia and clinicians -Pediatricians and Infectious Diseases Physicians, Immunologists/Vaccinologists, as well as Field Epidemiologists, Surveillance Officers and Public Health Officers from partner agencies (essentially WHO and UNICEF). Routinely, the NEC on AEFI meets every quarter to review and classify AEFI cases, especially serious events. However, ad-hoc NEC meetings may be scheduled based on public health exigencies. The classification and causality assessment guide critical public health actions, such as affirming the vaccine safety and continuous use in the field or stoppage and withdrawal of the vaccine from the field or clinical settings, if implicated.

Study design

A mixed-method design was adopted- quantitative and qualitative techniques were used for the study. The qualitative technique, based on the grounded theory approach, was adopted using key informant interviews and focus group discussions. The authors conducted 10 key informant interviews (KIIs) and two sessions of focus group discussion (FGD) to generate useful and quality information on the status and performance (evaluation) of the AEFI surveillance system in Kebbi State, Nigeria, using standard system attributes.

Further, the authors also reviewed the existing timeliness and completeness data for AEFI reports from the LGAs in the State over three years (the administrative datasets can be found in the supplementary files). This provided empirical evidence of sub-optimal reporting in many LGAs and guided LGA selection for FGDs.

From the quantitative design, the researchers collected the respondents’ socio-demographic data related to the study participants’ social and professional backgrounds. This may be relevant to a better understanding of the health workforce in the study setting, the health systems, and its bearing on the AEFI surveillance system under investigation. Overall, the author administered survey questionnaires and conducted KIIs and FGDs to answer the research questions about the functionality of the AEFI surveillance system.

Triangulation

Triangulation in research simply means using more than one research approach to answer research questions [21]. Characteristically, triangulation is associated with research methods and designs. However, there are other variations in the use of this terminology- could be considered as the use of multiple data sources, theories, methods and investigators in researching a phenomenon under investigation [21]. The main objectives of triangulation in research are to establish rigor i.e., consistency and validity of findings, and to provide a comprehensive understanding of the study results (compared to results from a single approach) [21, 22]. Indeed, it ensures the completeness of study results.

Based on the above merits and strengths, the authors adopted essentially methodological triangulation, the commonest type of triangulation, to answer specific research questions in this study. It allowed the authors to transcend the limitations of each of the methods- qualitative and quantitative methods [21, 22]. Combining both methods in this study resulted in four possible outcomes: agreement (convergence), partial agreement, dissonance (divergence), and silence.

Study population

The study population included the RI service providers at the HF level, RIOs/LIOs, DSNOs and M & E officers at the LGA level. At the State level, this included the State Director of Immunization, State Epidemiologist, and State Partners, such as WHO officers- Surveillance Focal Point, Routine Immunization Officer, State Data Assistant, and UNICEF’s Communication for Development Officer. These are key officers whose responsibilities impact immunization service delivery, AEFI monitoring, reporting, documentation and communication.

To achieve robustness and high-quality outputs, a mixed method approach involving interviews and discussion with diverse participants operating at the various levels of the health system who have a role in the operationalization and management of vaccine deployment, immunization and disease surveillance systems.

Specifically, the quantitative data was collected by administering questionnaires to the RI service providers. The RI service providers are the health workers responsible for operationalizing the AEFI surveillance system.

Validity was ascertained before the field survey by conducting content and face validity. The content validity was ascertained by field testing (piloting) of 20 questionnaires. The pretesting of the questionnaires was carried out at the Community Medicine of a Tertiary Health Institution and a PHC in the study area. The value of the Cronbach’s alpha test was 0.89 which showed internal consistency and reliability of the data collection instrument. Face validity was done by subjecting the instrument to a critical review by experts in the field and experienced quantitative researchers.

For the qualitative data, two FGD sessions were conducted among the different LGA-level Managers at the LGA Primary Health Care Department, whose job functions impact the AEFI surveillance and immunization service delivery. These include the Immunization Program Managers, DSNOs, and M & E Officers. Conducting FGDs bolstered findings from the administered questionnaires to the RI service providers (quantitative data) and provided a broader perspective on the subject of investigation. The authors selected the three LGA-level Managers from the three distinct categories (by job functions) from the 21 LGAs. The selection of the three individuals from each category for the FGD was guided by performance based on the routine (secondary data) completeness data for AEFI by LGA in Kebbi State (see Supplementary Tables 4, 5a, 5b, and 5c in the Supplementary file). Specifically, three officers from each distinct category (by job function) from three good-performing LGAs and ditto for the poor-performing LGAs were recruited. The selected LGAs by classification were Augie, Koko-Besse, and Sakaba (good performing) and Aliero, Yauri, and Zuru (poor performing), respectively. Consequently, a total of 18 officers from the three categories for the FGD at the LGA level were selected. Implying that two FGDs were conducted involving nine participants per FGD.

The KIIs were conducted with the State-Level Officers from the health development partners, such as the WHO officers- RI and Surveillance Focal Points, and Data Support Officer and Communication for Development Officer for UNICEF. Similarly, KIIs were conducted on the government side with the State Director of Immunization, State Director of Public Health, and State Pharmacovigilance Desk Officer from NAFDAC, to provide an in-depth understanding of the phenomenon of interest (AEFI surveillance and documentation). The KIIs involving the State-level Officers were reinforced by interviews with senior officers (pharmacovigilance/surveillance focal points) from NAFDAC and NPHCDA.

Overall, a total of 10 KIIs and 2 FGDs were conducted for this study. Combining FGD and KII offers the benefit of complementarity and diversity, and it allows more robust triangulation. The KIIs and FGD sessions were either virtual or physical, depending on the convenience and availability of digital technology on the side of the participants. However, the lead investigator took advantage of the State-level quarterly integrated review meeting involving all the above-listed LGA-level Managers to conduct the FGD sessions. In contrast, physical visits were paid to State-level officers, such as those with WHO, UNICEF, and SPHCDA, for the interviews. A few (two) virtual interviews (for the key informants) were conducted with the NPCHDA and NAFDAC Directors.

Field investigations were conducted between 10th May and 23rd July 2021. Each FGD session lasted for a maximum of an hour, while the KII lasted for a maximum of 50 min. The quantitative instrument took not more than 30–45 min for each health worker to fill. Recruitment into the qualitative survey was halted when data saturation was reached.

Measurement of system’s attributes

This subsection presents the measurement of AEFI surveillance system attributes that were analyzed. The attributes are simplicity, flexibility, data quality, acceptability, representativeness, timeliness, completeness, and stability. Respondents were asked eight questions/items on simplicity, six questions on flexibility, three questions on data quality, timeliness, and stability, four questions on acceptability, and two questions on representativeness and completeness. These questions/items were related to the AEFI surveillance system. For each item under each aspect of the AEFI surveillance system, a score of 1 was assigned to a correct response, while 0 was assigned to an incorrect response. Thereafter, each AEFI surveillance system domain was constructed based on the summation of all items under each domain. In other words, the number of relevant questions assessed for each of the system attributes informed the different ranges used. Simplicity score ranges between 0 to 6, flexibility range between 0 to 6, data quality range between 0 to 3, acceptability range between 0 to 6, representativeness range between 0 to 3, timeliness range 0 to 3, completeness range between 0 to 2 and stability range between 0 to 3 scores. Thereafter, each domain score was divided by three. This resulted in the categorization of each AEFI domain response into three levels or categories (good, fair or poor).

Hence, the responses and reclassification are proxies informing the performance of the AEFI surveillance system. Bearing in mind the limitations of this approach, a qualitative technique was also employed to strengthen and validate the quantitative outputs/findings.

Qualitative data analysis

We cleaned the data, and processed and analyzed it manually for the qualitative survey using a thematic framework approach. The process involved verbatim transcriptions of returned audio by professional transcribers who were not part of the investigation. The transcripts were screened, edited, and double-checked independently by one of the researchers (SAO) for accuracy, consistency, and clarity. Further analyses involved sorting, coding, and thematic identification. The codes were developed based on the objectives of the study, the research tools, and the responses elicited from the study participants.

We generated themes and sub-themes that were consistent and relevant to a better understanding of the research objectives. Subsequently, we used the content analysis method to generate qualitative data. Content analysis typically compresses texts into replicable, fewer content categories based on clear coding rules [23]. Consequently, we compressed responses to each theme and sub-theme and ensured that relevant and important expressions like quotations by the respondents were reported verbatim to enrich the study outputs (See the Result section and Supplementary Table 1).

Quantitative data analysis

The data collected was cleaned and entered into the SPSS version 25 software for analysis. The frequency distribution of all variables was determined.

Ethical considerations

The authors secured an ethical clearance for this study before the commencement of the fieldwork. The Kebbi State Health Research Ethics Committee (KSHREC) approved the study with registration number 106:20/2021 (see the supplementary file for the official approval letter). Further, participants consented before the quantitative instrument was administered on an open data kit (ODK). The quantitative data collection ended by default (as programmed on the ODK) once consent was not granted by the participant(s) to proceed with the data collection. For the qualitative aspect of the study, verbal consent was taken before the commencement of the recordings for the FGD sessions and KIIs. Recordings and a qualitative interview tool guide have been provided as additional evidence (see the supplementary file) underscoring the verbal consent taken after clearly explaining the background and the aim of the study.

Overall, informed consent was taken from participants for both quantitative and qualitative aspects of the study after participants were duly informed about the study. Also, the participants were informed that the findings of the study shall be used for research and surveillance system improvement only. They were also informed that they were free to opt out of the study at any point in time. Further, none of the participants interviewed was a minor. No invasive procedure was conducted, and indeed, the researchers ensured that the Helsinki Declaration principles of sound ethical research practices were upheld [24].

Results and discussion

Results

The results section presented key findings from the qualitative and quantitative data generated from the study. An additional table summarizing some other key results of the qualitative analyses was presented. Surveillance system attributes assessment

From Table 2, the simplicity of the AEFI surveillance system was assessed using some variables as proxies, scored, and categorized into three possible outcomes- good, fair and poor. Approximately 96% of the respondents reported that the case definition for AEFI is easy for case detection by all health professionals. Also, the current surveillance system allows health professionals to fill the data tools as reported by 88.3% of the respondents. An overwhelming 96.7% of the respondents reported that the surveillance system allows easy recording and timely data reporting, while 87.2% of them reported that the system allows updating of AEFI cases. The majority (92.3%) of the respondents reported that the AEFI surveillance system has the necessary information for investigation and establishment of causality. Regarding the time it takes the respondents to complete serious AEFI investigation forms, 69.8% reported less than 15 min, 24.5% reported 15-30 min and 16.1% reported more than 30 min. For causality assessment duration, roughly half (48.5%) of the respondents did not know the time taken to complete causality assessment from the time of reporting, while only 10.2% mentioned quarterly (every three months). Overall, the submission of the respondents based on good scoring indicated that the AEFI surveillance is simple.

Table 2 is about the flexibility of the AEFI surveillance system employing six variables. Approximately 86% of the respondents reported that the current reporting formats were useful for reporting other newly occurring health events, while 56.2% of respondents reported that change(s) in the existing procedure of case detection and reporting formats would be difficult to implement. About 82% of the respondents reported that adding new variables to the current formats is easy. Similarly, 77.4% of the respondents indicated that they could easily be integrated with others. Also, 74.1% of the respondents indicated that the surveillance system could accommodate the report of a new disease, while 72.3% of the respondents indicated that the system could be linked easily with new information technology/platforms. The overall assessment suggests a flexible surveillance system.

In Table 2, 75.9% of the respondents reported complete form (documentation) at the health facility. However, virtually all the respondents (98.5%) have reported at least one missing (unfilled) cell in the past year’s reports. In contrast, 95.3% of the respondents indicated the clarity of recorded data to read and understand. The data quality can be best described as fair.

From Table 2, 77.4% of the respondents accepted and are well-engaged in supporting AEFI surveillance activities at the health facilities. The reasons suggested for poor participation in AEFI surveillance and documentation included “work overload” (60.6%); lack of understanding of the relevance of the data to be collected (55.7%)”; no feedback/or recognition given by the higher bodies for their contributions” (33.6%); “report formats are time-consuming (30.3%)” and “reporting formats are difficult to understand” (17.5%). As reported, 81.4% of the respondents indicated that all the health workers use the standard case definition to identify cases, and 80.3% of the respondents reported that all health professionals are aware of the surveillance system. Also, 76.3% of the respondents reported that all the health workers send their reports using the current and appropriate surveillance reporting format. Overall, the surveillance system can be said to be acceptable based on the responses given by the participants.

From Table 2, 88.7% of the respondents reported that the current AEFI surveillance system is enabled to detect the occurrence of AEFI in all communities. Regarding differentials in AEFI case presentations, 51.6% of respondents reported that rural areas are more disadvantaged. This is closely followed by 41.6% of respondents who reported that both rural and urban areas are disadvantaged in terms of AEFI case presentation by the current surveillance system. Regarding the relevant socio-demographics in the surveillance reporting forms, 99.6% of the respondents reported the availability of relevant socio-demographics. The surveillance system is largely representative of the AEFI cases.

From Table 2, 92% of the respondents indicated that their respective health facilities report on time (for the current year 2021). In contrast, approximately 60% of the respondents reported tracking the timeliness of reports sent to the next level. Less than half (42.7%) of the respondents achieved greater than 75% timeliness of the report sent to the next level in 2020. Around a quarter (26.3%) of the respondents achieved 51–75% timeliness of reporting in 2020, while the remaining 31% of the respondents achieved 50% or less reporting rates in 2020. Based on the analyses, the timeliness of reporting as reported by the participants at the HFs is poor.

From Table 2, 82.5% of the respondents reported sending all reports to the next level in the current year (2021), while just over half (52.9%) of the respondents achieved more than 75% completeness of reporting for the previous year (2020). Roughly 19% (19.3%) achieved 51–75% completeness of reporting in 2020, while slightly over a quarter (27.7%) of the respondents had 50% or less completeness of reporting in 2020. The overall assessment can be described as fair.

From Table 2, only 47.4% of the respondents reported that a new restructuring affected the procedures and surveillance system, while 88.7% reported that lack of resources interrupted the AEFI surveillance system. About the type of resources that were lacking when the AEFI surveillance system was not fully operating, 82.8% of the respondents reported financial resources (leading), followed by human resources (59.1%), then logistics such as data tools (43.4%, and others (6.9%). The stability or resilience of the AEFI is weak (poor) based on the participants’ responses.

Results from qualitative analysis

The subsection detailed the outputs from the qualitative analyses of interviews and FGDs conducted on AEFI surveillance system evaluation and its performance.

Theme: current AEFI surveillance system and vaccine safety considerations

Sub-theme- system evaluation using CDC surveillance system attributes

Sensitivity

There is a convergence of opinions of the participants that the surveillance system is sub-optimal regarding sensitivity given that there is a high degree of under-reporting, delayed or non-transmission of reports (data) to the next level and incomplete documentation of AEFI cases. Evidence from the qualitative interviews also indicated that health workers have lackadaisical attitudes towards AEFI reporting and community awareness about AEFI and its reporting is low.

“It is not sensitive enough to track the AEFI that are occurring within the system” … we begin to see within the routine system of the vaccine operations in-country, the reporting of AEFI has not been well entrenched in the system. Reason being that they feel they are minor AEFI that can just be treated without actually reporting it. The data on minor AEFI are not transmitted up, then even with the occurrence of serious AEFI which are usually seen during the campaigns, the investigation reports are not properly done and not complete”- NAFDAC NPVFP (IDI).

“…we have an LGA that vaccinated more than 20,000 persons (for COVID-19), and not a single AEFI case was reported from such LGAs, LGAs like Danko-Wasagu- even the largest and most populated LGA, then you know that the system is not sensitive enough to report all the AEFI for now.” WHO SFP(IDI).

“Somehow, it is sensitive enough; somehow, it is not. But the problem is with the people who are supposed to conduct this particular AEFI to see how severe it is, either minor or major, that is where the problem lies at the health facility system”- UNICEF Comm FP (IDI).

“Talking about the sensitivity of AEFI surveillance in my LGA, we are under-reporting any AEFI case because only surveillance focal persons are recording the AEFI, other health workers in the health facility do not care about AEFI reporting, and the communities are not aware of the AEFI surveillance. That’s why we are under-reporting the AEFI cases.”- LGA-level POs, Batch B (FGD).

Simplicity

There was a consensus among interviewees that the surveillance system is simple, but functionality remains a challenge due to poor health workers’ attitudes to reporting and documentation. The functionality of the system is affected by the level of education of the health workers operating the surveillance system at the health facility level.

“The system based on documentation is good, simple and precise, but for its functionality, that is where the problem is. Regarding functionality, there is no willingness on the part of the health workers; there are so many lapses. One issue of documentation, they don’t take it seriously”- UNICEF Comm FP (IDI).

“…the simplicity is affected by the educational status at the operational level because there is a low level of education of the operators at the primary health care level”- DoI, SPHCDA.

Flexibility

The flexibility of the current AEFFI surveillance system can be best described as partial, given the need to introduce new tools for the operationalization of AEFI for new vaccines while leveraging the existing AEFI surveillance system.

“…the flexibility is not really there because in the conventional system, for any new vaccine introduction, e.g., novel oral polio vaccine (nOPV2), AESI surveillance that is being done recently, they have to introduce new data tools for reporting which negates the flexibility of the system”- WHO SFP (IDI).

“I can say that it is flexible because it allows new vaccine introduction or new vaccination exercise to benefit from the existing system”- WHO SDA (IDI).

Data quality

The findings suggest that the AEFI data quality is poor and inadequate to support classification and causality assessment. There is an agreement among interviewees that it is not robust enough and there is a need to improve AEFI data quality.

“…for example, during the supplementary immunization activities, there were reports of serious AEFIs from a couple of LGAs, because I went there for investigation and we found out that it was data quality error and we also found out that a lot of data that are not being reported is because there are so much data quality issues, because some people felt like this is not what they are supposed to report, so the data quality is not very robust for the present system”- WHO SFP (IDI).

“…you will see a health facility immunizing more than a hundred persons/clients for RI but report only two AEFI. And after that, when you check the line-list sent to the DSNO, you will see more than ten children that showed they had AEFI”- LGA-level POs, Batch B (FGD).

Timeliness, completeness, and usefulness

There is a consensus across all the categories of the interviewees (State and national level participants) that there is a delayed detection, reporting and transmission of AEFI data to the next administrative level which negatively impacts its usefulness for vaccine safety consideration. There is also an opinion by a regulatory expert who tacitly opined that the various data management (collection) tools are not robust enough to provide any key deduction of statistical analysis to guide public health action.

“…For example, at the LGA level, the DSNO or the reporting officer might sit on the data for a particular exercise for two to three months, and then at the state level, they also sit on it so by the time the data is being transmitted, it is not useful for vaccine safety consideration, so I don’t think it is timely. So, if the data is not timely, I don’t think it can be useful”- WHO SFP (IDI).

“I don’t think it is timely. The AEFI reporting might not be timely because the caregiver was not told to report immediately after being vaccinated at the service provider level. At times, it is when they come back for the subsequent vaccination that they will report to the health worker about the AEFI they had”—LGA-level POs, Batch A (FGD).

“You cannot make any important deduction or statistical analysis from the report generated from IDSR or DHIS 2 or SORMAS and all that”—NAFDAC NPVFP (IDI).

Representativeness

The interviewee posited that AEFI surveillance, as it is currently operated, is not representative enough as health facilities providing vaccination services are geographically limited, and detection and reporting are not representative of the true AEFI within the communities.

“Honestly, it is not because you found out that across the State, it is not all the facilities that are providing RI services. Then also, it is not only RI service providers that are supposed to be reporting AEFI. Communities are not well-sensitized unless during SIAs about AEFI. So, with this, you find that many cases are supposed to be reported. Overall, the AEFI data that you have is not the true representative of what is supposed to be obtainable on the ground”- LGAF (IDI).

“…the cases reported are not representative because the cases are sub-optimal- not all facilities are conducting routine immunization, not all facilities are reporting, and not all the cases are captured in the documentation”—LGA-level POs, Batch A (FGD).

Theme: current AEFI surveillance system and immunization demand generation

Sub-theme: vaccine demand generation and communication strategies

The sub-section presented findings regarding the system’s capacity to inform communication strategies and drive demand for immunization. The syntheses from the interviews indicated the limitations of reporting and sub-optimal data generated that could guide communication strategies aimed at driving demand for vaccines and immunization services. Most of the interviewees indicated that the system is ineffective in driving demand for AEFI reporting, but a few individuals opined that the system is good, regarding demand generation, especially the new vaccine introduction and mass immunization program, but requires strengthening for EPI vaccine demand generation. Some of the key responses are highlighted below:

“The current system is effective to drive demand for vaccination, but driving demand to drive AEFI reporting is not the case”- NAFDAC NPVFP (IDI).

“It cannot guide communication strategies because what we are seeing in the society if you go for community survey is higher than what is reported in the facility once you use what inside the facility we will never go anywhere in terms of mobilization”—UNICEF Comm FP (IDI).

Discussion

AEFI surveillance system and vaccine safety considerations

From the evidence available while drawing from the evaluation of system attributes and examining the operationalization of the AEFI surveillance and documentation in its current mode, the capacity to provide information for vaccine safety exists but it is sub-optimal and not robust enough to generate sufficient and convincing vaccine safety data, especially for new vaccines and those under emergency authorization use.

This is the first study conducted in Nigeria to evaluate the functionality of the current surveillance system to inform vaccine safety considerations. To the best of our knowledge, little or no study within sub-Saharan Africa has comprehensively evaluated the effectiveness of the AEFI surveillance system’s capacity to inform vaccine safety considerations. In 2016, a study conducted in Harare City, Zimbabwe, only evaluated the AEFI surveillance system without any specific reference to vaccine safety considerations. The study concluded that the system is useful, simple, acceptable, flexible, and stable but costly [25]. However, the system remained bedeviled with under-reporting, weak feedback, and inadequate AEFI management drugs [25]. These are implicit indications of the weak capacity of the system to guide vaccine safety considerations due to the paucity of credible data that lacks the power to detect rare adverse events.

Another study conducted in Guruve district, Mashonaland Province in Zimbabwe, evaluated the AEFI surveillance system using the updated CDC surveillance system attributes, complemented with a review of AEFI surveillance records from January 2016 to November 2017 [26]. The study concluded that the surveillance system was performing poorly and was not useful [26]. The study found that no AEFI was reported during the period under review [26]. The non-reporting of encountered AEFI suggests that the system could not provide vaccine safety data and thus, is incompetent to inform vaccine safety considerations. However, a study was conducted in Ghana in 2019 to evaluate the system’s attributes, usefulness, and performance [27]. The study concluded that the AEFI surveillance system is useful but only partially meets its objectives due to under-reporting [27].

AEFI surveillance system and communication for immunization demand

Given the findings from the health workers’ survey and the interviews of higher-level stakeholders, the functionality of the current structure of the AEFI surveillance is sub-optimal and inadequate to generate sufficient high-quality data to inform communication strategies toward improved immunization demand. This submission is essentially premised on the sub-optimal functionality of the current AEFI surveillance system, which impacts the capacity of the system to drive AEFI reporting. In other words, the current AEFI surveillance system is not agile and sensitive enough to elicit or detect all the AEFI cases or potential AEFI cases from the health system and within the community that could be pooled for safety data analysis and in turn, boost the confidence of caregivers and health workers to drive immunization demand.

In the study setting, not all the health facilities in the State offer routine immunization, but theoretically, all the health facilities should report IDSR, including AEFI. However, the challenges associated with the operationalization of AEFI in Nigeria include human resource deficit, comparatively less attention being paid to AEFI surveillance as against a focus on Acute Flaccid Paralysis and VPDs surveillance driven by partners and donor funding, data tool inadequacy, and limited knowledge of case definitions of AEFI and the procedures for causality assessment. The aforementioned issues have actively undermined the effectiveness of AEFI detection, reporting, and documentation, thus impeding the capacity of the system to generate robust and high-quality data to inform vaccine safety considerations and generate sufficient demand for immunization.

Overall, the reports from the few published studies in Africa showed gross under-reporting of AEFI [25–29], which is in agreement with our study findings, thus impacting the capacity and functionality of the system to inform vaccine safety considerations and generate sufficient demand for immunization.

Evaluation of AEFI surveillance system attributes

Regarding the simplicity of the current AEFI surveillance system, we found a concordance between the views of health workers who are the real operators of the AEFI surveillance and documentation system from the quantitative data and the qualitative interviews. Generally, the current AEFI surveillance system was perceived as simple, as it is paper-based with an easy-to-use standard case definition and has the necessary information for investigation and establishment of causality. However, there is an indication of a knowledge gap among the surveillance operators (health workers) as only 10% of the health workers know about the procedure and the feedback mechanisms for causality assessment. Further, both qualitative and quantitative findings suggest issues with the operationalization of the surveillance system rather than an inherent deficit in the structure of the AEFI surveillance system. Findings from this study suggest attitudinal issues and the relatively low educational status of the surveillance operators at the health facility level could partly explain the observed knowledge deficits. Similar to our study, other studies have reported that the AEFI surveillance system was perceived to be simple, but often challenged by knowledge and resource deficits at the health facility level [25–27].

In a slightly contrary finding, a study in Ghana similarly reviewed surveillance data and procedure and interviewed key stakeholders found a level of simplicity (fairly simple) that was challenged by longer turn-around time (especially for a coroner’s case), higher level of expertise to review serious AEFI cases and difficulty in granular characterization of AEFI cases often due to absence of pathognomonic signs and weak laboratory capacity for diagnostic confirmation [27].

Regarding the flexibility of the current AEFI surveillance system, the preponderance of opinions by critical stakeholders at the State and National levels suggests partial flexibility from the qualitative interviews. However, the respondents working at the health facility largely considered the system flexible. The slight divergence in findings from both research approaches could be a result of differences in the level of understanding of the flexibility domains being investigated, while the participants in the qualitative interview are diverse with a better understanding of the conceptual and functional status of the AEFI surveillance system in Nigeria given their levels of responsibilities. However, the qualitative interview provided a richer contextual meaning indicating that the current conventional AEFI surveillance system is not flexible enough, given that new data tools were introduced to monitor AEFI (implement AEFI surveillance) for a new vaccine, such as nOPV2. In addition, the use of paper-based documentation makes it difficult to make changes and additions to the existing reporting tools. These negate the principle of flexibility and adaptability of an effective surveillance system. Hence, the finding of this study adds to the clamor for the widespread use and improvement of the piloted electronic AEFI surveillance and documentation system (Med Safety app) in Nigeria. A couple of other studies conducted in a few countries largely corroborate our findings [25, 27, 30].

As for the data quality, evidence from the respondents at the health facility level suggests sub-optimal data quality as the occurrences of missing data were rife. Indeed, virtually all the respondents have reported at least one missing (unfilled) cell in the past year’s reports. Similarly, most of the interviewees at the State and National levels described the generated AEFI data as not of high quality. They asserted that most of the cases (data) were often under-reported, incomplete, and lacked internal consistency (discrepancies found). The poor-quality data makes causality assessment difficult for the national expert committee. This study’s finding is consistent with the evaluation results of the AEFI data quality assessment conducted in Ghana, where there were inconsistencies in the number of cases reported at all levels, data entry errors found in DHIS, and incomplete documentation [27]. An extreme situation was reported from the Kwekwe district of Zimbabwe, where data quality could not be assessed as there was no filled form for the reviewed period in the study [30]. Thus, the finding showed that the AEFI surveillance and documentation were moribund or non-existent in the Kwekwe district of Zimbabwe. On the contrary, the finding in this study is at variance with the report from Harare City, where data quality was excellent- all the sections of the AEFI forms were filed and reported on time [25].

One of the possible suggestions to minimize data quality issues is using an electronic data management system for AEFI cases from the HFs level to the national level. This triggered the recent effort by NAFDAC to develop a new electronic reporting tool (Med Safety app) that will provide real-time AEFI data to minimize the identified challenges. Similarly, the e-reporting innovation has been embraced in Ghana. For example, it was reported that some notifications from the community or the health facility were sent to the Ghana Food and Drug Authority (FDA) through the electronic reporting platform [27].

Regarding the acceptability of the AEFI surveillance system, evidence from this study suggests sub-optimal (fair) acceptability of the system. Despite a high level of awareness among these health professionals and the high capacity to use the case definition to identify AEFI cases, more than half of the respondents reported a lack of understanding of the relevance of the data to be collected. This may be related to the attitude of the health workers (having had training) and the overall functionality of the AEFI surveillance system. Evidence from interviewees at State and national levels indicate issues with acceptability (i.e., problematic) premised on the established fear of reporting by health workers, poor flow of data from bottom to the top, and gross under-reporting. The acceptability of the current AEFI surveillance system- its outputs- is very limited, especially at the regulator’s end (NAFDAC) and wider stakeholders, such as, donors, and international health development agencies.

When compared to studies in other African countries, this study’s finding, regarding acceptability, is somewhat different from the Harare City report, where the system was deemed (fully) acceptable. This is based on a high sense of responsibility to report, willingness to participate, timeliness and completeness of reporting, and good documentation [25]. A report from the Kwekwe district of Zimbabwe reported that the majority of the health workers are willing to participate in the surveillance and considered the completion of the AEFI reporting forms as part of their duties [30]. However, the study did not explore the views of the health development partners (WHO, UNICEF) and the regulators within the immunization and surveillance space across the strata of the health system. The evaluation report from Ghana indicated high acceptability at the national level and among partners (WHO, UNICEF and Gavi), but not the case with the health workers at the district level who expressed concerns about victimization for reported events [27]. Another study conducted in Zimbabwe at Guruve district, Mashonaland, found that health workers are willing to participate in the surveillance system and deemed it as part of their routine duty to report AEFI cases, though there was no evidence of documentation for AEFI cases encountered by the health workers during the study period [26]. In addition, the caregivers were willing to report “severe” AEFI cases to the health facilities but would not report if they perceived the AEFI as not severe, and this is evident by the proportion of those found not to have reported AEFI experienced by their children [26]. Hence, the surveillance system was not acceptable [26]. However, there was no evaluation of the perspective of other players within the surveillance system at Guruve district, Mashonaland Province of Zimbabwe.

Assessing the representativeness of the AEFI surveillance system, evidence suggests the system may not be fully representative. However, the respondents at the health facilities reported that the system is enabled to detect the occurrence of AEFI in all communities. From among those health workers who believed the system is not representative, slightly more than half of them reported that rural dwellers might be disadvantaged in terms of case representation, though closely followed by those who opined that both rural and urban populations are disadvantaged in terms of representativeness of the surveillance. This may be a pointer to the overall sub-optimal sensitivity of the current surveillance system in the study setting and a likelihood of under-representation of AEFI data (cases) from rural communities. However, the analysis of the qualitative outputs unpacked the discourse. It suggests a generalized under-representation of the true burden of AEFI in the study setting because not all the health facilities in various communities are delivering routine immunization services. Hence, access to immunization remains a challenge, how much more, AEFI case detection and reporting. This scenario might have also impacted community awareness (except during supplementary immunization activities). The interviews also suggested under-representation across the board without any other demographic bias among the eligible groups taking immunization services outside the possible differences between rural and urban populations. The finding of this study is different from the report from Harare, Zimbabwe, where the system was deemed representative [25]. The difference in findings may be explained by the narrow gauge (participation of 21 health facilities in surveillance) used to measure representativeness in the Harare city study by Zvanaka and colleagues. Like our study, which used a more robust yardstick to assess the representativeness, the report from Ghana found that the representativeness of the system was threatened as the system did not sufficiently capture AEFI data from travelers and pregnant women. In addition, there were poor reporting rates from the districts to the regions and from the HFs to the districts for all the years assessed [27]. At variance with the reports from Ghana and Nigeria, private health facilities were not participating in the surveillance system in the Kwekwe district, Midland Province of Zimbabwe [30]. This suggests a likely under-representation of the AEFI cases in the surveillance system.

Concerning the timeliness of reporting AEFI data, evidence from this study suggests sub-optimal timeliness. The result of this study further showed that system performance in terms of timeliness was not well-tracked as roughly 40% of the respondents were not routinely tracking the timeliness of reporting in their respective health facilities. The qualitative interviews corroborated the poor timeliness of reporting, which is worse at the LGA levels, where the DSNOs tend to sit on the data until being prompted by the higher levels. This calls to question the usefulness of such AEFI data for vaccine safety considerations. Contrary to the finding in this study, Zvanaka and colleagues in Zimbabwe reported acceptable timeliness of the surveillance system as forms were completely filled and shared with the district and province within 24 h [25]. Our study finding is in tandem with the sub-optimal timeliness of reporting in Ghana. The Ghana study also showed inconsistencies across the years and across the levels of administration assessed [27]. In sharp contrast to Zvanaka and colleagues, Constantine et al. could not objectively evaluate the timeliness of the system as there was no notification being done in all the health facilities [26]. This is the same finding reported by Chimusoro and colleagues in the Kwekwe district, Midland Province of Zimbabwe [30].

Similarly, the completeness of the AEFI data generated from the surveillance system was assessed in this study. Evidence from the health facility suggests sub-optimal completeness of AEFI reporting. However, some improvement was observed when compared to the previous year (2020), where just over half of the respondents reported achieving at least 75% completeness of reporting. This finding of sub-optimal completeness was corroborated by the qualitative interviews conducted. However, this study’s finding was at variance with the evaluation of the AEFI surveillance system in Harare City of Zimbabwe which found data completeness during the review period [25]. A sharp contrast was reported in the Kwekwe district, Midland Province of Zimbabwe, where investigators could not assess the timeliness and completeness of the AEFI surveillance system due to the absence of completed forms/reports [30]. Similar to the finding in the Kwekwe district’s study, the study from Ghana indicated poor data completeness at the district level (where HFs operate) but a high level of completeness at the higher levels, i.e., regional and national [27].

In this study, there are serious concerns about the stability of the current AEFI surveillance system and documentation, as most respondents at the health facilities reported that a lack of resources, such as financial, logistics and human resources, interrupts AEFI surveillance. Similarly, roughly half of the respondents reported that new procedures and restructuring affect AEFI surveillance. This study’s finding is contrary to the finding by Zvanaka and colleagues, who reported a stable surveillance system premised on the availability of human resources and logistics and the flow of communication [25]. However, the study did not evaluate the impact of new procedures and restructuring on the system’s stability. In contrast, in the Kwekwe District of Zimbabwe, there were issues of shortage of human resources, logistics (tools), and very limited AEFI data source and documentation [30], thus threatening the stability of the surveillance system. Constantine et al. reported that all the health facilities had a functional EPI fridge (for vaccine storage), AEFI guideline and notification forms, a trained health worker on AEFI surveillance, and a telephone for AEFI notification [26], suggestive of the system’s stability.

In spite of the strengths and uniqueness of this study, there are a few limitations which include the following:

Personal bias from the health workers who work at the primary health centers where AEFI surveillance and documentation are being conducted before being transmitted to the next administrative levels. They might have sentiments that could have biased their judgment of the surveillance system attributes which are proxy indicators of the system’s functionality and effectiveness. However, the personal bias was mitigated through the qualitative interviews conducted among other key stakeholders in the operationalization and management of Nigeria’s pharmacovigilance architecture. Further, the authors triangulated the data from multiple sources to ensure rigor, validity, and completeness of study findings.

Conclusion

The AEFI surveillance system in Kebbi State, Northern Nigeria, is functioning sub-optimally. To some extent, it can inform vaccine safety considerations and guide communication strategy for vaccine demand generation, especially for new vaccines and those under emergency authorization use. The surveillance system is simple, partially sensitive and flexible, fairly stable, not representative, and somewhat useful. Further, the surveillance system is challenged by sub-optimal data quality and poor timeliness and completeness. Addressing the health system challenges and specific operational bottlenecks would improve AEFI surveillance and documentation in Kebbi State and possibly, the entire Nigeria.
