Introduction

Living in rural areas has consistently been associated with poor health outcomes, including higher mortality,1 2 reduced access to healthcare services3,5 and shortages in the healthcare workforce.6,8 To quantify rural–urban differences in healthcare quality and inform both research and policy, many countries and regions have developed context-specific rurality indices.9 10 As rurality is a multidimensional concept encompassing geography, healthcare access and public service availability, no single universal measure exists; consequently, rurality indices are inherently shaped by local contexts, such as healthcare systems and policy priorities.9

In Japan, our team previously developed and validated the rurality index for Japan (RIJ) in 2023 to address the lack of standardised measures of rurality for healthcare research.11 Since its publication, RIJ has been cited by approximately 20 studies and applied across a broad range of research domains, including healthcare workforce distribution (structure),12 service delivery and utilisation (process)13 14 and population health outcomes (outcomes).15,17 Beyond academic research, RIJ has also attracted growing interest as a potential tool for informing healthcare policy, including regional planning and resource allocation for health disparities.18

The original RIJ was developed under practical constraints, with several designs made to ensure its feasibility and nationwide applicability.11 These designs included the use of Euclidean distance rather than road-based distance or travel time, as well as a binary classification of remote islands based on the absence of secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals.11 Although these decisions were appropriate at the time of development, subsequent policy discussions have underscored the need to more accurately reflect real-world healthcare access, particularly with respect to travel distances along transportation networks.19 To address these considerations, this study aimed to refine RIJ and develop multiple variants (the ‘RIJ family’) that incorporate alternative distance metrics and a modified classification of remote islands. We also sought to systematically compare these RIJ variants in terms of their interrelationships and validity to provide practical guidance on their appropriate use in both research and policy making.

Methods

Study design and overview

This study conducted a nationwide methodological comparison of refined RIJ variants. In addition to evaluating the robustness and validity of the alternative operational definitions, we also updated the RIJ using the most recent data available for 2024. We constructed multiple RIJ variants (the ‘RIJ family’) at the postal code level and systematically compared their interrelationships, convergent validity with physician distribution11 and criterion validity using life expectancy.11 These indicators were selected because they were employed in the assessment of the validity of the original RIJ11

The overall analytical framework comprised the following three steps: (1) updating and recalculating RIJ scores using 2024 data while incorporating alternative distance metrics and island classifications; (2) standardising rurality scores to a 0–100 scale to facilitate comparability across geographical units and (3) evaluating the concordance and validity across RIJ variants at multiple spatial levels, including municipalities and secondary medical areas.

Data sources and units of analysis

We used nationwide administrative and geographic datasets corresponding to 2024, including population data,20 healthcare facility locations21 22 and geographic boundary files.20 Road network information was obtained from the ArcGIS Geo Suite 2026, which incorporates road network data updated in 2021.23 These datasets provide near-complete nationwide coverage across Japan, including all postal code areas, and are based on official or widely used national sources, ensuring a high level of completeness and consistency for geographic and health services research. The primary unit of analysis for the index construction was the postal code, which is consistent with the original RIJ, as postal codes provide geographically precise units available across Japan. For validation analyses, postal code-level RIJ scores were aggregated into higher administrative levels, including municipalities, secondary medical areas and prefectures, depending on the availability of external reference indicators.

Construction of RIJ variants and score standardisation

Updating RIJ components and creation of RIJ variants

All RIJ variants were constructed using the same core components as the original RIJ11—population density, distance to the nearest secondary or tertiary emergency hospital, island status and special heavy snowfall areas based on The Act on Special Measures Concerning Countermeasures for Heavy Snowfall Areas—updated with the 2024 data. Consistent with the original RIJ, we constructed all RIJ variants using the same core components.11 Each component was standardised using min–max normalisation, and exploratory factor analysis was conducted.11 Factor loadings derived from this analysis were used as coefficients to calculate the composite RIJ scores, following the methodology of the original development study.11 To address the previously identified limitations, we created refined variants by incorporating alternative distance metrics (direct distance, road-based distance and estimated travel time using a car) and a modified classification of remote islands that distinguished islands with secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals from those without.

Road-based distances and estimated travel times by car were calculated using ArcGIS Pro (version V.3.3; Esri, Redlands, California, USA) and the ArcGIS Network Analyst extension, incorporating road network data and highway segments to reflect realistic travel conditions. Travel time estimates incorporate road network characteristics, including the use of expressways, to more accurately reflect real-world motorised access to secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals. Distances were calculated from the centroid of each postal code area to the geographic coordinates of the nearest secondary or tertiary emergency hospital, and this definition was consistently applied across all distance metrics. For remote islands without road networks, the road distance could not be calculated directly. In these cases, the direct (Euclidean) distance was used as a proxy for road distance. We estimated travel time for such islands by applying the ratio of road distance to direct distance observed in mainland areas to the corresponding direct distance between the island and the mainland, thereby approximating the relative travel time under constrained data availability. Although this method does not capture all aspects of real-world travel for island populations, it represents a transparent and reproducible compromise in the absence of comprehensive multimodal transport data and is consistent with the exploratory and comparative objectives of this study.

In the original RIJ, remote islands without secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals were assigned a value of 1, whereas all other areas were assigned a value of 0. In the modified RIJ (mRIJ), we refined this classification by assigning a value of 1 to remote islands without secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals, 0.5 to islands with such hospitals and 0 to all other areas. The island classification variable was incorporated as one of the components of the index and standardised using min–max normalisation together with other components before being combined using factor loadings derived from exploratory factor analysis. The intermediate value (0.5) was introduced to represent a graded level of healthcare accessibility among island areas, reflecting the presence of secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals, rather than assuming a binary distinction. To define the remote islands, we followed a widely recognised geographic categorisation in Japan that treats Hokkaido, Honshu, Shikoku, Kyushu and the main island of Okinawa as the five principal islands, with all other islands classified as remote islands for analytical purposes.24 This conceptual distinction is consistent with official descriptions in Japanese geographic statistics and policy documents, where the five main islands constitute the core landmass and the remaining 14 000-plus islands are referred to as remote islands.24

Standardisation of RIJ scores to a 0–100 scale

To ensure interpretability and comparability, the RIJ scores were standardised to a 0–100 scale, with higher values indicating greater rurality. In the original RIJ, standardisation was conducted using a two-step quantile-based approach, which was first converted into 100 quantiles. Subsequently, these quantile scores were averaged within municipalities or secondary medical areas and re-quantiled to generate area-level indices.

Percentile ranks were defined such that higher values indicated greater rurality, with 0 representing the most urban areas and 100 representing the most rural areas. Area-level RIJ scores (including municipalities, secondary medical areas) were then derived by averaging the postal code–level scores within each area and retaining one observation per area.

Rationale for adopting a percentile rank-based approach

A percentile rank-based approach was adopted for three main reasons. First, it preserves the continuous relative position of each geographic unit within the national distribution, avoiding the discretisation inherent in repeated quantile transformations. Second, it improves the interpretability of policy applications by directly representing rurality as a relative percentile within Japan, facilitating comparisons across administrative levels.

Statistical analysis

Overview of analytical strategy

Statistical analyses were designed to evaluate the robustness, concordance and validity of alternative RIJ variants constructed using different operational definitions. Analyses were prespecified and organised hierarchically, with a primary focus on concordance and reclassification across RIJ variants, followed by assessments of convergent and criterion validity using established external indicators. Concordance and reclassification analyses were primarily conducted at the postal code level, as RIJ variants were originally defined at this granularity. Moreover, rank instability was most evident before spatial aggregation. We conducted validation analyses at higher administrative levels to align them with the spatial resolution of external reference indicators. Analyses were conducted at the municipality or secondary medical area level to align with the spatial resolution of the external reference measures. Concordance and robustness among RIJ variants (primary analyses)

To examine the robustness of the rurality rankings across alternative RIJ variants, we first assessed pairwise associations using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. These analyses evaluated the extent to which relative rurality rankings were preserved when distance metrics (including Euclidean distance, road-based distance and travel time by car) and island classifications (RIJ vs mRIJ) were modified. To further characterise the impact of alternative definitions on regional classification, the RIJ scores were categorised into quintiles. We constructed reclassification matrices by comparing RIJ and mRIJ variants. Significant reclassification was defined a priori as movement of two or more quintile categories between variants. As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the reclassification analysis using deciles to assess finer categorisation. We quantified the proportions of geographic units that shifted by two or more and four or more categories between variants. To identify regions where operational definition changes had a disproportionate impact, we calculated rank differences (Δrank) between RIJ variants. Geographic units with absolute rank changes in the top 10% of the distribution are considered to exhibit large rank shifts. These regions were mapped to assess whether the rank instability was geographically clustered or randomly distributed.

Convergent validity with physician distribution indicators

Following the development of the original RIJ,11 convergent validity was evaluated by examining the associations between each RIJ variant and the national physician distribution index published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.25 Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the monotonic associations between rurality and physician maldistribution. In addition to correlation coefficients, we evaluated policy-relevant agreement by identifying regions ranked in the highest 10% of rurality for each RIJ variant and calculating the proportion of these regions that also ranked in the lowest 10% of the physician distribution index (maldistribution). These analyses were conducted to reflect policy-relevant classification thresholds, rather than relying solely on continuous correlations.

Criterion validity using life expectancy

Further, we assessed criterion validity using average life expectancy at birth for both men and women, which was consistent with the original RIJ validation process.11 Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated for each RIJ variant and sex-specific life expectancy to examine whether the established gradients between rurality and population health outcomes were preserved under alternative operational definitions. Municipal-level life expectancy data were available only for 2020, representing the most recent officially published estimates at this spatial resolution.26 Therefore, a temporal mismatch exists between life expectancy outcomes and RIJ scores updated using 2024 data.

Statistical considerations

All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was assessed at a p-value threshold of <0.05. 95% CIs for Spearman’s correlation coefficients were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 1000 repetitions. Given the descriptive and comparative nature of this methodological study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons; however, emphasis was placed on effect sizes, consistency of findings across analyses, and interpretability for research and policy applications. Data management and analysis were performed using the Stata Statistical Software Release V.19 (StataCorp).

Patient and public involvement

This study was conducted without any patient involvement. The patients were not invited to comment on the study design, consulted for the development of relevant patient outcomes, or asked to interpret the results. Moreover, they were not asked to contribute to the writing or editing of the documents for readability or accuracy.

Discussion

This study updated and evaluated RIJ variants using the most recent national data. Despite modifications to the distance metrics and island classification, all RIJ variants exhibited very high concordance and comparable validity profiles. Regarding convergent validity, correlations with physician distribution were moderate across variants (Spearman’s ρ ≈ −0.52). For criterion-related validity, associations with municipal-level life expectancy were weaker but consistent, ranging from −0.29 to −0.27 for males and from −0.19 to −0.17 for females. These findings are broadly consistent with those of the original RIJ study. Overall, the comparable validity evidence observed across the RIJ variants indicates that they capture similar underlying dimensions of rurality, supporting their flexible selection according to specific research questions or policy applications.

Summary of the findings

Despite substantive methodological differences in distance metrics and island classification, all six RIJ variants demonstrated remarkably high concordance in rurality rankings and similar validity profiles. This finding suggests that rurality in Japan is a structurally robust construct that is not highly sensitive to reasonable variations in operational definitions. Several factors likely contributed to this observed stability. First, key components of rurality in Japan—including population density, geographic isolation, and healthcare resource distribution—are strongly spatially correlated at the national level. Regions characterised by a low population density tend to be geographically remote and have fewer healthcare resources, resulting in a dominant underlying gradient of rurality. Consequently, alternative distance metrics—such as direct distance, road-based distance, and estimated travel time—capture largely overlapping spatial patterns, leading to minimal changes in relative rurality rankings. In addition, previous studies have reported that direct distance and road-based distance are relatively correlated,27 and the travel time in this study was calculated based on the road-based distance. Therefore, the choice of distance metric appears to have only a minimal impact on rurality rankings. Second, the use of percentile rank-based standardisation possibly contributed to the robustness of the rankings across RIJ variants. By expressing rurality as a relative position within the national distribution, this approach reduces sensitivity to absolute scale differences and extreme values. Consequently, modest variations in distance estimation or island classification are unlikely to produce large shifts in rank ordering, particularly when analyses focus on relative comparisons rather than absolute thresholds. Because both the original quantile-based approach and the percentile rank-based method are rank-based transformations that preserve the relative ordering of geographic units, the impact of this change in standardisation on rank-based analyses is expected to be minimal. Third, the mRIJ represents a refinement rather than a redefinition of the original index. Although the mRIJ introduces a graded classification for islands based on the presence of secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals, the remaining components of the index—population density, distance to emergency care, and climatic constraints—continue to exert substantial influence on overall scores. Notably, mRIJ preserves the overall rurality structure while allowing more nuanced differentiation among island settings. Although these areas represented the largest relative rank changes, the absolute magnitude of reclassification remained limited and was mostly confined to adjacent categories. In addition, differences between RIJ variants were not random; they were concentrated in specific geographic contexts, particularly islands with some degree of emergency hospital capacity. Reclassification was largely confined to adjacent quintiles, and substantial shifts were rare. This pattern suggests that the observed differences reflect notable adjustments in the boundary cases, rather than instability or measurement noise, supporting the interpretability of the variant-specific refinements. Criterion validity analysis further reinforced this interpretation. All RIJ variants showed comparable associations with physician maldistribution and municipal-level life expectancy, with consistent correlation magnitudes across distance metrics and island definitions. The absence of notable differences in validity across variants indicates that the refinements introduced in the mRIJ do not compromise the ability of the index to capture health-relevant aspects of rurality. Furthermore, the modest strength of associations—particularly for life expectancy—highlights the multifactorial nature of population health outcomes and underscores that rurality represents only one of the contributing determinants. Overall, these findings indicate that the RIJ framework is both robust and flexible: robust because core rurality patterns remain stable across reasonable methodological choices and flexible because alternative variants can be selected to better align with specific research questions or policy contexts without materially altering conclusions. This balance between stability and adaptability is a desirable property for rurality indices intended for both empirical research and practical applications. The aim of this study was not to identify a superior specification, but to evaluate whether reasonable methodological refinements materially alter the underlying rurality construct.

Comparison with existing literature

The findings of this study should be interpreted within the broader international literature on rurality measurements. Numerous countries have developed context-specific rurality indices, such as the Modified Monash Model28 in Australia, the Rural-Urban Continuum Codes,29 Rural-Urban Commuting Areas in the United States, and the Rurality Index for Ontario in Canada.30 Despite the differences in construction, these indices share common conceptual foundations, including geographic accessibility and population distribution, underscoring the multidimensional nature of rurality. Thus, the RIJ family aligns well with international practice in adopting a continuous or quasi-continuous representation of rurality rather than a simple binary classification.9 10 The incorporation of alternative distance metrics, including travel time, mirrors the approach used in other countries to better approximate real-world access to care. However, our findings demonstrated that, in Japan, relative rurality rankings are largely insensitive to such methodological variations, reflecting the strong spatial coupling of population density, geographic isolation, and healthcare resource distribution at the national level. Similarly, the RIJ family incorporates several features that distinguish it from many existing international indices and respond to the specific geographic and healthcare context of Japan. These include nationwide coverage at the postal code level, explicit consideration of remote islands, and standardisation using percentile ranks to ensure interpretability and internal consistency across administrative levels. Japan’s combination of dense metropolitan areas, sparsely populated mountainous regions, and numerous inhabited islands, alongside its universal health insurance system and free access to care,31 limits the direct transferability of rurality indices developed in other countries. Rather than aiming for direct international comparability, the RIJ framework prioritises contextual relevance and practical applicability within Japan while remaining conceptually consistent with global approaches to measuring rurality.

Implications of the study

The findings of this study have several important implications for healthcare policy and planning in Japan. First, the high concordance observed across the RIJ variants indicates that the rurality classification is robust to reasonable methodological choices regarding distance metrics and island definitions. From a policy perspective, this robustness provides reassurance that decisions informed by the RIJ may not be sensitive to minor technical specifications, thereby supporting its use as a stable reference indicator in regional healthcare planning. Second, the mRIJ, which distinguishes remote islands from those without secondary or tertiary emergency hospitals, may better reflect the contemporary reality of healthcare access in island settings. Although the overall impact on national rurality rankings was modest, reclassification analyses suggest that mRIJ provides more nuanced differentiation in boundary regions where healthcare capacity is present but limited. This refinement may be particularly relevant for policy initiatives targeting island communities, where binary classifications risk oversimplifying heterogeneous access. Third, given the minimal differences observed among distance metrics within both RIJ and mRIJ frameworks, practical considerations such as data availability, computational burden, and update feasibility become central to indicator selection. In this regard, direct distance-based variants—particularly mRIJ-direct—offer advantages in terms of simplicity, nationwide applicability, and ease of routine updating, including island areas where road-based distances cannot be directly calculated. These properties make mRIJ-direct a pragmatic candidate for standard use in policy monitoring and administrative applications. In addition, the availability of multiple RIJ variants enables flexible use tailored to specific policies or research questions. For example, road-based distance or travel time variants may be preferable in analyses focusing on emergency transport systems or time-sensitive access to care, whereas direct distance variants may be sufficient for a broader assessment of structural healthcare disparities. In addition to immediate policy applications, the RIJ family provides a foundation for future research on geographic health inequalities in Japan. Potential directions include longitudinal analyses of changes in rurality and healthcare access over time; evaluation of policy interventions aimed at physician distribution or service consolidation; and integration of RIJ variants into causal frameworks to examine the relationship between healthcare structure, processes and outcomes. Moreover, the RIJ framework may facilitate comparative methodological research by offering a transparent and empirically tested example of how rurality can be operationalised in a densely populated, island-rich country with universal health coverage. Overall, these policy and research implications highlight the value of a rurality index that is both methodologically robust and operationally adaptable. By balancing stability with contextual refinement, the RIJ family is well-positioned to support evidence-based decision-making and future empirical work in Japan’s evolving healthcare landscape.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several notable strengths. First, we conducted a comprehensive and systematic comparison of multiple refined rurality definitions within a single, coherent analytical framework. By evaluating six RIJ variants that differed in distance metrics and island classification, we were able to directly assess the robustness of rurality rankings to methodological choices while sharing identical core components. Such within-index comparisons are rarely performed in rurality research, and provide important evidence for the stability and interpretability of rurality measures. Second, the use of nationwide data with full geographic coverage, including remote islands, enabled the evaluation of rurality at multiple spatial scales, from postal codes to municipalities, secondary medical areas, and prefectures. The adoption of percentile rank-based standardisation further ensured internal consistency across these spatial aggregations and enhanced interpretability for both research and policy applications. Overall, these features strengthen the generalisability and practical relevance of the RIJ framework. Third, we assessed the validity using both structural (physician distribution) and outcome-related (life expectancy) indicators, reflecting the different dimensions of healthcare access and population health. The consistency of the validity findings across the RIJ variants supports the conclusion that the refinements introduced in the updated index do not compromise its ability to capture health-relevant aspects of rurality.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although we incorporated alternative distance metrics, including road-based distances and estimated travel times, these measures inevitably rely on assumptions regarding transportation networks and travel conditions. For islands without road connections, travel time was approximated using proportional adjustments based on mainland distance relationships. Although this approach was chosen to maintain nationwide coverage and internal consistency, some degree of measurement error was unavoidable. However, the high concordance observed across distance metrics suggests that such uncertainty is unlikely to materially affect the relative rurality rankings. Second, criterion validity analyses using municipal-level life expectancy data were based on data from 2020, whereas the updated RIJ variants were constructed using data from 2024. This temporal mismatch may have attenuated the observed associations, particularly because life expectancy reflects cumulative long-term exposure rather than contemporaneous conditions. Moreover, the modest correlation coefficients observed should be interpreted as conservative estimates rather than as evidence of limited validity. Third, rurality is inherently a multidimensional construct, and no single index can fully capture all the relevant aspects of geographic disadvantage. Factors such as socioeconomic conditions, transportation availability, and service organisation were not explicitly included in the RIJ framework. Consequently, associations between RIJ variants and health outcomes should not be interpreted causally but rather as descriptive indicators of the structural context. Finally, although this study focused on the internal robustness and validity within Japan, the RIJ framework was not designed for direct international comparison. Its strength lies in contextual relevance rather than in cross-national harmonisation, and caution is warranted when extrapolating the findings beyond the Japanese healthcare system.

Despite these limitations, this study supports the conclusion that the RIJ family represents a methodologically sound and policy-relevant approach for measuring rurality in Japan. By explicitly examining the impact of alternative operational definitions, this study provides transparent empirical grounds for the use of rurality indices in both research and policy-making.

Conclusions

In this nationwide methodological study, we systematically examined the robustness and validity of refined RIJ variants that incorporate alternative distance metrics and a modified classification of remote islands. Across all six RIJ variants, rurality rankings showed consistently high concordance, with minimal reclassification and comparable associations with physician maldistribution and municipal-level life expectancies. These findings indicate that rurality in Japan is a structurally stable construct that is not highly sensitive to reasonable variations in operational definitions. Similarly, refinements introduced in the mRIJ enable a more nuanced representation of island settings without compromising overall validity or interpretability.
