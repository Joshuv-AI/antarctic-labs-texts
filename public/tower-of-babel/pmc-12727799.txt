Risky sexual behavior and its associated factors among employees in industrial park, Northeast, Ethiopia
Authors: Yihenew Sewale Bizu, Kassahun Dires Ayenew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRisky sexual behavior is a workplace concern that particularly affects young adults in the working-age grou Among factory employees, it poses challenges for both private and government sectors, hindering progress toward sustainable development. This study aimed to assess risky sexual behavior and its associated factors among employees in industrial parks in Northeast Ethiopia. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 410 participants using a systematic sampling technique. Data were entered into EpiData version 4.2 and exported to STATA version 16 for analysis. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, with a p-value > 0.05 indicating a good fit. Variables with a p-value < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model, and associations with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The prevalence of risky sexual behavior among industrial park employees was 24.4%(95% CI of 20.8–28.3). Prior residence (AOR = 3.53; 95% CI: 1.51–8.27), alcohol consumption (AOR = 3.41; 95% CI: 1.10–10.58), watching pornography (AOR = 3.02; 95% CI: 1.07–8.49), peer pressure (AOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 1.30–26.14), and being 21–24 years old (AOR = 4.93; 95% CI: 1.10–22.13) were significantly associated with risky sexual behavior. Nearly one-fourth of the participants engaged in risky sexual activity, indicating a high level of risky practices within industrial park settings. Therefore, there is an urgent need for targeted sexual and reproductive health interventions, including awareness programs, improved access to preventive services, and the promotion of safe sexual practices among industrial park employees.Keywords: Risky sexual behavior, Employees, Industry park, EthiopiaSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Health care, Health occupations, Risk factors

Introduction

Background

Risky sexual activity, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), refers to behaviors that increase the likelihood of negative sexual and reproductive health outcomes, such as unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV/AIDS. These behaviors include unprotected intercourse with same-sex or commercial partners, frequent partner changes, multiple sexual partners, inconsistent or unreliable contraceptive use, sex with older partners, and engaging in sexual activity under the influence of alcohol or drugs1.

The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates 333 million new STI cases annually, with 222 million in adults, mainly from risky sexual practices2. Risky sexual practices negatively affect both public and private sectors, undermining development3–5. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the highest burden of HIV globally. Approximately one in 20 individuals (4.9%) in SSA are living with HIV, accounting for 69% of all HIV cases worldwide. Risky sexual behaviors such as having multiple sexual partners, unprotected sexual intercourse, and concurrent sexual partners are associated with increased risk of HIV transmission in the region6,7.

Young adults (15–29 years) represent 22% of the global adult population, half of them female. HIV disproportionately affects this group, with 59% of new infections in women. In the U.S., many sexually active youths are affected by STIs/HIV, and most remain at high risk8. World Health Organization and UNAIDS report 498 million new STI cases yearly and 36.9 million people living with HIV, 66% of whom are in SSA9–11. A study found that 41% of adults had ever had sex; among them, 43% did not use condoms in their last encounter, 14% used no contraceptives, and 21% consumed alcohol before sex3.

A study in Vietnam, among factory workers in industrial zones reported low condom use during sexual encounters with sex workers or casual partners, highlighting the prevalence of RSB in such settings. Factors associated with increased risky sexual behaviors included being an immigrant, binge drinking, and mobility-related challenges12. Similarly, in Ethiopia, a study among daily laborers at the Arjo-Didessa sugar factory revealed a high prevalence of risky sexual behaviors, with 57% of participants engaging in such behaviors with associated factors of age, marital status, income level, and substance use9.

In the context of industrial parks in Ethiopia, a study conducted in Kombolcha Industry Park assessed sexual and reproductive health service utilization among youth workers. The study found that only 45.5% of youth workers utilized reproductive health services, with factors such as, marital status, proximity to health facilities, and knowledge and attitude toward sexual and reproductive health services influencing utilization rates.

Ethiopia is one of the countries with construction enterprises, industrial parks, and various small and large companies that engage a significant portion of the young adult population. Migration from rural to urban areas exposes individuals to new environments and interactions with diverse cultures, increasing the risk of engaging in unstable sexual partnerships and fragmented social networks13.

Risky sexual behavior among young adults in Ethiopia may result in sexual intercourse with commercial sex workers, transactional sex, multiple sexual partners, and unprotected sexual intercourse. Studies have shown that sexually active young adults who engage in risky sexual behaviors, such as participating in organized events in factory settings, are at high risk for outcomes such as early sexual activity and pregnancy during their early years, as well as unwanted life disorders later in life. Ultimately, through risky sexual behaviors, the country loses educated manpower14. The prevalence of risky sexual behavior varies across regions in Ethiopia, ranging from 13% in the Humera and Mecha districts to as high as 40% in Nekemte15–17.

Investigating risky sexual behavior among factory workers in industrial parks is essential for understanding the sexual and reproductive health challenges faced by this emerging workforce. Such insights are critical for designing effective strategies to mitigate the negative consequences of risky sexual practices.

Furthermore, this study also considers additional factors, such as social influences and the role of social media, which have been underexplored in previous research. The findings may hold significant public health value by providing evidence-based insights for stakeholders, policymakers, program planners, and health service providers. Ultimately, a research may serve as a baseline for future studies seeking to explore risky sexual behavior. Therefore, this study aims to assess the prevalence of risky sexual behavior and identify associated factors among employees in the Industrial Park.

Methods

Study design and setting

An institutional-based cross-sectional study was conducted in Industrial Park (IP) of Northeast Ethiopia. The industrial Park (IP) is situated in Northeast of Ethiopia 130 km away from the capital city of Addis Ababa. The industrial park was inaugurated on March 02, 2019 by the prime minister of Ethiopia in northeast, Ethiopia. This project is initially planned to create 13,000 jobs that has eight industrial sheds ready to accommodate prospective investors. However, there are three actively working factories of which two are textile factories and one malt processing (bikil) factory. The factories are producing many types of textiles and apparel ranging from socks, underwear, and shirts up to sweaters in addition to malt processing. The study was conducted in 1,778 workers of which, 1,533 are females and 245 are males.

Data collections

Data were collected a self-administered questionnaires. We adapted a standardized questionnaire previously used in studies of sexual health and risk behaviors9,18–21. The adaptation process followed internationally recognized guidelines of world health organization for cross-cultural adaptation of psychometric tools to ensure semantic, conceptual, and technical equivalence in the Ethiopian context. The original questionnaire was translated from English into Amharic, the local working language, by two bilingual public health experts with experience in sexual and reproductive health research. A third independent bilingual expert blinded to the original instrument, back-translated the Amharic version into English. The questionnaires have components of socioeconomic, individual, substance use, social media and behavioral related factors were addressed. The pre-final version of the tool was pilot-tested among 5%20 industrial park employees from a site not included in the main study. Participants provided feedback on clarity, comprehension, and sensitivity of the questions. Items that were found ambiguous or culturally inappropriate were revised accordingly. Based on the pretest findings all the necessary modification and correction measures were applied. Data collection was carried out under close supervision and collected through trained data collectors. Informed consent was secured prior to questionnaire administration from each individual’s participants.

Study variables

In this study, the primary outcome of interest was whether individuals engaged in risky sexual behaviors, categorized as either “Yes” or “No.” A range of independent variables was examined to understand their potential influence on this outcome. These predictors were organized into several domains, beginning with socio-demographic characteristics, including age, level of education, marital status, monthly income, and occupation. Additional demographic factors such as ethnicity, family size, and prior residence in urban or rural settings were also considered relevant.

Beyond demographics, the study assessed individual behavioral and knowledge-related factors. These included practices related to condom use, participants’ knowledge of sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and the influence of peer pressure on sexual decision-making. These factors were considered critical in understanding personal choices and awareness that might contribute to risky sexual behavior.

Substance use was also included as a category of influencing variables, encompassing behaviors such as chewing khat, smoking cigarettes, and consuming alcohol. These behaviors have been linked in previous literature to increased risk-taking, including risky sexual activity, making them important areas for investigation.

Finally, the study examined the role of social media and internet use, with particular attention to exposure to sexually explicit content. This included watching pornography and frequent use of digital platforms such as Facebook, Telegram, YouTube, and TikTok.

Operational definitions

Risky sexual behavior

have sex with more than one sexual partner or have unprotected/non-consistent condom use with non-regular sexual partner in life time.

Consistent condom use

Using condom during or at every sexual encounter.

Substance use defined as current use refers to participants who consumed a substance within the past 30 days, former use includes those who have used a substance in the past but not within the last 30 days, and ever use encompasses individuals who have used a substance at least once in their lifetime at least any one of the following substances: alcohol, khat cigarette, shisha.

Peer pressure

is a Pressure that one feels as a member of social groups to behave in a certain way or believe in certain things in order to be accepted by peers in the same group22.

Extended absence was defined as any absence from work or school lasting more than 30 consecutive days due to illness, injury, or other personal reasons.

Prior residence refers to the place where a participant lived before the time of the study typically classified as urban or rural.

Data quality management

Before commencing the main data collection, a pre-test of the study questionnaire was conducted to assess its clarity, reliability, validity, and cultural appropriateness. The pre-test was helping identify ambiguous, sensitive, or redundant questions and ensure that the wording, flow, and response options are understandable to participants. It was also provide an estimate of the average time required to complete the questionnaire. The pre-test was carried out among employees 5%20 participant of the study sample size that were working in another industrial park of super eagle factory that were far part from the study places to avoid contamination information in the study population. An effort was made to include participants of different genders, age groups, job categories, and educational levels to ensure that the questionnaires were tested across a range of perspectives. During the pre-test, the questionnaire was administered under the same conditions as those planned for the main survey, whether self-administered or interviewer-administered. Instructions were given exactly as they were in the actual study. The time taken to complete the questionnaire was recorded. Upon completion, participants were invited to provide feedback on the clarity, sensitivity, and relevance of the questions, the appropriateness of the language, and the logical flow of the items. They were also being encouraged to point out any questions that cause discomfort or were difficult to answer. Two days training was given and close supervision was carried out. The collected data was reviewed and checked for completeness and consistency by the principal investigator daily.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were first checked for completeness and coded. Then, data were entered in to Epi-data version 4.2 and exported to STATA version 16 software for statistical analysis.

The dependent variable was recoded to dichotomous out comes as risky sexual behavior who had one sexual partner or who had used consistent condom use were coded as “0’ and those who had more than one sexual partner or who had inconsistent condom use were coded as ‘1’. Independent variables were coded based on previous related studies and the distribution of responses to the data.

Bi-variable logistic regression analyses were used and Crude Odd Ratio (COR) with 95% CI was computed to assess the association between each predictor and the outcome variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 during the bi-variable analysis were included in the multi-variable logistic regression analysis. Multicollinearity between independent variables were checked using variable inflation factor (VIF), and no significant (VIF < 10) collinearity was detected. Model goodness of fit was checked by Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and the final model was fitted (p-value = 0.11) for risky sexual behavior. Adjusting odds ratio (AOR) with 95% CI was estimated to identify the associated factors and statistical significance was declared at p value less than 0.05.

Prevalence and associated factors of risky sexual behavior

The prevalence of risky sexual behavior among employees in industrial parks, Northeast Ethiopia, was 24.4% (95% CI: 20.8–28.3). In bi-variable analysis, factors such as gender, age, prior residence, alcohol consumption, watching pornography, peer pressure, knowledge of HIV/AIDS, transactional sex, fidelity to a partner, condom use, sexual abstinence, friends’ engagement with sex workers, and knowledge of STIs and HIV prevention were associated with risky sexual behavior (p < 0.25). These variables were entered into multivariable logistic regression, which identified prior residence, age, alcohol use, watching pornography, and peer pressure as significant predictors (p < 0.05).

Participants from rural areas were 3.53 times more likely to engage in risky sexual behavior compared to urban residents (AOR = 3.53; 95% CI: 1.50–8.27). Participants who consumed alcohol were 3.41 times more likely to engage in risky sexual behavior than non-drinkers (AOR = 3.41; 95% CI: 1.10–10.58). Participants who watched pornography were three times more likely to engage in risky sexual behavior than those who did not (AOR = 3.02; 95% CI: 1.07–8.48), while those experiencing peer pressure were 5.83 times more likely than their counterparts (AOR = 5.83; 95% CI: 1.30–26.14). Additionally, employees aged 21–24 years were 4.93 times more likely to engage in risky sexual behavior than other age groups (AOR = 4.93; 95% CI: 1.10–22.13) (Table 7).

Discussion

The study revealed that the prevalence of risky sexual behavior was 24.4% (95% CI of 20.8, 28.3) which indicating nearly one out of four employees had risky sexual behavior. This finding is congruent with studies in Addis Ababa (26.7%)23, Haramaya (25.3%)24, Wolaita (24.7%)25, Aksum (23.7%)26, Arba Minch, (22.4%)27. However, the findings lower than the study in Nigeria (48%), Turkey 47.9%, Kombelcha (35.5%), Assosa (47%), Bahir Dar (44.9%), Nekemete (32.9%), Tanzania (40%) and Togo (34.6%)10,16,18,22,26,28–30. This variation may be attributed to differences in socio-demographic characteristics, cultural norms, and sexual education programs across countries and regions. Other possible explanations include differences in study populations, such as age distribution, marital status, and type of employment, as well as variations in access to sexual and reproductive health services, exposure to media and pornography, peer influence, and substance use. Moreover, methodological differences including data collection techniques, definitions of risky sexual behavior, and sample sizes may also contribute to the observed discrepancies. Moreover, the finding of study was also higher than a study conducted in Aksum, (19.6%)26, Western Tigray (13.7%)31, and Sri Lanka (12.4%)32. These differences might be in socio-demographic characteristics, such as age, educational level, and marital status, could influence sexual behavior patterns. Additionally, employees in industrial parks are often young, mobile, and exposed to urban environments, which may increase opportunities for risky sexual encounters. Greater access to alcohol, peer networks, and digital media, including pornography, may further contribute to higher engagement in RSB. Cultural norms, attitudes toward sexual health, and the availability and utilization of sexual and reproductive health services may also differ, influencing the likelihood of engaging in risky sexual practices.

Prior residence is statistically significant factors with risky sexual behaviors among Industry park employees who lived in rural areas were three times more likely to engage in RSB as compared to those who lived in urban areas. The finding is supported a study in South Africa33. The possible explanations could be urban areas may offer more opportunities and exposure; rural populations may face unique challenges due to limited access to information and resources. However, a finding contradict with a study in Arba Minch34, Gondar35. The possible discrepancy of the findings might be that a study in Arba Minch highlighted that khat chewing, alcohol use, and peer pressure were strongly associated with RSB in this population and in Gondar a study identified peer pressure, alcohol consumption, and pornography viewing as significant factors contributing to RSB among urban youth.

A participant who was alcohol consumption was a significantly associated with RSB. The participants who drank alcohol were more than three times than those who do not drink alcohol. This study finding is supported by a lot of studies that done in and out of Ethiopia. A finding has already been observed conducted at Wolaita, Gondar, Sri Lanka and Tanzania16,25,36,37. The possible reason could Alcohol consumption may increase risky sexual behavior (RSB) by causing intoxication, which heightens sexual arousal and impairs judgment. It can reduce awareness of the risks associated with unprotected sex and compromise sexual health decision-making. Additionally, drinking before sexual activity used as a socially acceptable excuse for not using condoms, further increasing RSB. Supporting evidence suggests that individuals are more likely to consume alcohol before sexual encounters in social contexts—such as dates, parties, or bars—to feel less anxious and more comfortable about their sexuality, thereby increasing the likelihood of engaging in risky sexual practices.

The finding also revealed that watching pornography was about three times more likely to engage in RSB as compared to those who did not watch pornography. This finding is consistence with a study in Arba Minch, Taiwan and Indonesia respectively26,27,38. The possible reason could be influence of pornography on risky sexual behavior can be explained scientifically. Exposure to pornography stimulates the brain’s reward system, flooding it with high levels of dopamine, which may increase the likelihood of engaging in risky sexual behavior. This overstimulation can lead to structural and chemical changes in the brain. Additionally, the act-out phase where individuals imitate what they see in pornographic content may prompt experimentation with new sexual behaviors.

Moreover, a study that had a peer pressure which was almost six times more likely to engage an individual to have RSB. The findings is supported a studies in Tanzania and Wolaita25,30. The possible explanations might be increased trust and close relationships with peers can lead to sharing information, including pornography, which may heighten curiosity about sexual matters and promote RSB. Individuals experiencing peer pressure are more likely to access and engage with pornographic content, which contains explicit sexual material designed to arouse desire, and the widespread availability of pornography through digital platforms and the Internet further amplifies this effect, making peer pressure a key determinant of risky sexual behavior.

Lastly, ages 21–24 years were five times more likely to be engaged in risky sexual behavior as compared to the younger age grou The finding was supported a study in Addis Ababa, Bahir Dar, Western Africa, China and Sri Lanka respectively22,30,39–41. The possible reason might be higher likelihood of risky sexual behavior among employees aged 21–24 years may be due to their classification as sexually active or “prime” age group, during which physical and physiological maturity increases sexual desire. Additionally, individuals in this age group with anxious attachment styles may feel undeserving of love, making them more willing to compromise their safety to maintain close relationships. Consequently, they may be more likely to consent to unprotected sex when requested by a partner.

Conclusions

This study revealed that one- fourth of study participants engaged in risky sexual behavior. Prior residence, alcohol use, watching pornographic movies, peer pressure and ages from 21 to 24 were statistically significant associated factors with risky sexual behaviors. Therefore, health education, counseling, awareness creation, and peer education is essential for risky sexual behavior groups. Special attention should be given to high-risk groups, including employees aged 21–24, being from rural areas, and exposed to behavioral risk factors. For researcher a strong study designs is recommended to explore causal relationships and guide more effective interventions.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. There might be social desirability and recall bias, particularly in the reporting of past sexual risky behaviors. The study areas that might be limited to generalization of the findings for the whole population.
