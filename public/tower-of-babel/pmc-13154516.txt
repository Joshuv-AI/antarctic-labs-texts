High INHBB expression in colorectal cancer is associated with poor prognosis and drives malignant phenotypes in tumor cells
Authors: Huiyan Chen, Yao Wu, Zongxuan Huang, Di Luo, Ziming Wang, Junhong Wu, Tianyuan Zhou, Hu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) represents a prevalent global malignancy, with its progression intimately associated with biological processes such as oxidative stress and anoikis. This study aimed to evaluate a prognostic model derived from oxidative stress and anoikis-related genes (OARGs) in CRC and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the core gene, INHBB.ResultsWe constructed a 12-genes prognostic model that retained independent predictive power for overall survival. Among the identified genes, INHBB was significantly upregulated in CRC tissues and cell lines, correlating with poor overall survival. Functional characterization revealed that INHBB overexpression markedly promoted malignant phenotypes, including proliferation, migration, and invasion. INHBB accelerates cell cycle progression, confers resistance to anoikis, exacerbates oxidative stress, and activates the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway.ConclusionBeyond validating a novel OARGs-based prognostic model, this study highlights INHBB as a critical malignant regulator driving colorectal cancer aggressiveness, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and precision therapeutic target.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00794-4.Keywords: INHBB, Colorectal cancer, Anoikis, Oxidative stress, Prognostic models

Introduction

As a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide, colorectal cancer (CRC) poses a persistent global health challenge [1–2]. CRC stands as the third most frequent malignancy in men and the second in women [3]. While the clinical paradigm has shifted with the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors and refined surgical techniques, the prognosis for advanced disease remains dismal. Approximately half of patients experience recurrence or metastasis within three years, resulting in a 5-year overall survival rate of merely 13% for metastatic cases [4–7]. Metastasis not only limits therapeutic efficacy, but also drastically degrades quality of life [8]. Therefore, dissecting the molecular architecture of metastatic progression is critical for establishing precise risk stratification and uncovering vulnerable therapeutic targets to improve patient outcomes.

Metastasis is defined by the dissemination of primary tumor cells and their subsequent colonization of distant organs. Under physiological conditions, non-malignant cells undergo apoptosis upon detachment from the extracellular matrix (ECM), a phenomenon known as anoikis. In contrast, metastatic tumor cells rapidly acquire adaptive mechanisms to resist this form of cell death, facilitating their survival in the circulation and eventual proliferation at secondary sites [9]. The regulatory networks governing this resistance are multifaceted, involving interplay between inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagy. Emerging evidence highlights a critical interplay between oxidative stress and cell death machinery as a potent driver of metastasis across various cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer [10]. A notable example is observed in hepatocellular carcinoma, where IQGAP1 orchestrates anoikis resistance and metastatic spread by coupling Rac1-dependent ROS accumulation with Src/FAK pathway activation [11]. However, the precise regulatory architecture of this synergy within CRC remains elusive. Moreover, while these pathways are intrinsic to CRC dissemination, a robust prognostic signature derived from oxidative stress and anoikis-related genes (OARGs) lacks systematic validation.

INHBB (a TGF-β superfamily member) is a critical regulator of cellular proliferation and differentiation, and its contribution to tumorigenesis is complex: while it fuels glioblastoma progression by enhancing stemness and EGFR signaling [12], it appears to constrain metastatic potential in nasopharyngeal carcinoma [13]. Beyond these opposing roles [14–15], recent studies in gastric cancer have highlighted its capacity to reprogram the tumor microenvironment via paracrine NF-kB activation. Similarly, INHBB has emerged as a key factor in oral squamous cell, renal clear cell, and hepatocellular carcinomas [16–18]. Despite this, the specific molecular mechanisms by which INHBB might exploit oxidative stress and anoikis resistance to drive CRC progression are currently unknown.

In this study, we integrated bioinformatics analysis with in vitro experimental validation to construct a prognostic model based on OARGs in CRC. Furthermore, we focused on characterizing the expression profile of the core gene, INHBB, and elucidating its regulatory role in malignant phenotypes, particularly metastasis. Our findings provide novel experimental evidence supporting the utility of INHBB as a biomarker for prognostic evaluation and a potential candidate for targeted therapeutic intervention in CRC.

Materials and methods

Data sources and ethics statements

Transcriptome data (FPKM format) and corresponding clinical information including age, gender, T/N/M stage, pathological stage and overall survival of 398 CRC tissues and 51 normal colon tissues were downloaded from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The dataset GSE39582 was downloaded from the Gene Expression Synthesis (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo).

Oxidative stress-related genes (ORGs) were derived from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp), and anoikis-related genes (ARGs) were extracted from the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/) and Harmonizome database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome/).

35 pairs of CRC and adjacent tissues from the 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistics Support Force were collected, and all patients gave informed consent and were approved by the hospital ethics committee (Project No. 2025-010). The study was conducted in full compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell lines

The human CRC cell lines SW620, SW480, HT-29, HCT116, Caco-2 and normal colonic epithelial cell lines NCM460 were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and the cells were identified by STR without cross-contamination.

Bioinformatics analysis

The Limma package in R language (version 4.3.0) was used to analyze the DEGs of between CRC and normal samples in the TCGA database, and the screening criteria was |log₂FC|>1 and adj. P<0.05. OARGs associated with overall survival (OS) of CRC patients were screened by univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.05). We randomly divided the set of single-factor significant gene expression data and clinical survival data into training group (train) test group (test) group and constructed the regression model of lasso algorithm of gene expression, survival time and state (least absolute shrinking and selection operator, Lasso), with which we conducted cross-validation. The gene corresponding to the point with the smallest error was selected as the significant gene of the Lasso regression model. Combined with the significant gene and its expression, we constructed a Cox regression model, with risk score = Σ (gene expression× regression coefficient). The patients were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups according to the median risk score, and the Kaplan-Meier method was used for survival analysis, and the Log Rank test was used to compare the survival differences between groups. The time-dependent ROC curve was drawn to evaluate the prediction performance of the model.

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of prognostically-related OARGs was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), and was visualized by Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to screen the independent prognostic factors of OS in CRC patients, and the prediction accuracy and clinical application value of the Nomogram were evaluated by calibration curve and decision curve analysis (DCA).

According to the expression of OARGs, the “consensuclusterplus” of R language was used to cluster and type the OARGs of colon cancer cells, in which the clustering algorithm was K-means, and the distance was Euclidean distance. Cluster number evaluation: k values from 2 to 9 were tested. Subtypes were validated by principal component analysis (PCA) and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed using R-pack “clusterProfiler”.

Experimental methods

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

35 CRC tissues and corresponding normal tissue specimens were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime, China), embedded in paraffin and serially sectioned (4 μm). After dewaxing to water, incubate with 3% H₂O₂ at room temperature for 10 min to eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity, and citrate antigen repair solution (P0081; Beyotime, China) at high temperature. After 30 min of closed at room temperature, INHBB primary antibody (ab128944; Abcam, UK) was added to incubate overnight at 4 °C, and the next day was incubated at room temperature for 20 min with drip reaction enhancer solution (PV-9001; Zsbio, China), and then incubated at room temperature for 30 min with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody, DAB chromogenic kit (PV-9019; Zsbio; China) color development, hematoxylin dye solution (G1076-500ML; Servicebio; China) counterstaining, gradient ethanol dehydration and transparent, neutral gum sealing.

Real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cell line using E.Z.N.A. ®Total RNA Kit II (R6934-01; Omega Biotek, USA). Use the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (K1622; Thermo Scientific, USA) for cDNA synthesis according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. qRT-PCR amplification was performed using SYBR™ Select Master Mix (4472908; Thermo Scientific, USA). Three different replicates of the experiment were performed, followed by qRT‒PCR and analysis using the 2−ΔΔCt method to calculate the final results. The primers used for qRT‒PCR are shown in Table 1.

Western blot

Logarithmic growth phase cells were collected, lysed using RIPA lysate (P0013B; Beyotime, China) supplemented with phosphatase inhibitor (04906837001; Roche, China) and protease inhibitor on ice for 30 min. The protein concentration was determined by BCA protein quantification kit (AR0146. BOSTER, China). 30 µg of protein samples were electrophoresis with a 10% SDS-PAGE gel preparation kit (PG122; Yase Biologicals, China), followed by 90 V transfer to PVDF membrane (IPVH00010;Merck Millipore, USA) for 1.5 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder (PS122; Yase Biologicals, China) at room temperature for 1 h, and the incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. ECL chemiluminescence kit (HY-K2006; MedChemExpress, USA) was used to quantitatively analyze the relative expression of the protein of interest.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

SW620 cells were inoculated in a laser confocal Petri dish, incubated for 24 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime, China) for 20 min, 0.1% Triton X-100 (ST1723; Beyotime, China) permeable at room temperature for 10 min. INHBB primary antibody was added to incubate overnight at 4 °C, and the next day FITC-labeled secondary antibody (SA00001-3;Proteintech, CHN) was added to incubate at room temperature for 1 h, and DAPI staining solution (C1002; Beyotime, China) was stained for 5 min. Add anti-fluorescence attenuation mountant (P0126; Beyotime, China).

Lentiviral transfection and stable cell line construction

According to the INHBB gene sequence, overexpression and knockdown primers were designed, lentiviral overexpression vector (LV-INHBB), knockdown vector (LV-shINHBB) and negative control vector (LV-NC) were constructed, and lentivirus was constructed and packaged by SHANGHAI GENECHEM CO., LTD. Cells were seeded into a 6-well plate (3 × 10⁵/mL), and when the confluency rate was 30–50% for 24 h of incubation, the 1/2 small volume infection method was used for infection, and after 48–72 h of incubation, the expression intensity of green fluorescence was observed by fluorescence microscopy to evaluate the infection efficiency. Subsequently, 2 µg/mL puromycin (S250J0; Source Accompanying Bio, China) was added for screening for 2 weeks, and stable transfected cell lines were obtained. The shRNA sequences used for INHBB knockdown in this study are as follows: shRNA INHBB: 5′-GGUGGAACAUGGUGGAGAATT-3′.

The stably transfected HT-29 cells were seeded into ultra-low adsorption 6-well plates (3471; Corning, USA), and after 24 h of suspension culture, the cells were collected.

CCK-8 experiment

The stably transfected cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 3000 cells/100 µL. Cell proliferation was assessed by adding 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution was added to each well (HY-K0301; MedChemExpress, USA) at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h and 72 h. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader.

Transwell

Migration experiment: The cells were centrifuged and resuspended in serum-free medium to a density of 5 × 104 cells/mL. Transwells were placed in a 24-well plate, 200 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper layer of each Transwell, and 600 µL of complete medium supplemented with 15% FBS was added to the lower layer of each Transwell to completely saturate the membrane and avoid air bubbles.

Invasion experiment: Matrigel matrigel (356234;Corning, USA) was pre-spread in the upper chamber, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify, and the rest of the steps were the same as the migration experiment.

After a 24-h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime, China) for 20 min and then stained with 0.1% crystalline violet (C0121; Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Subsequently, non-migrated/non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab.

Flow cytometry to detect cell cycle and apoptosis

Treated cells were harvested, washed, and divided for parallel assays. For cell cycle analysis, ethanol-fixed cells were treated with RNase A and stained with propidium iodide (C1052; Beyotime, China). For apoptosis detection, cells were stained with Annexin V-mCherry using a commercial kit (C1062; Beyotime, China) following the provided protocol. Cell cycle distribution was quantified using ModFit LT software, while apoptotic populations were distinguished and quantified using FlowJo software (Version 10.8).

Oxidative stress detection

HT-29 cells were collected and cultured in adherence culture and suspension culture for 24 h, washed twice with PBS buffer, and the cells were ultrasonically fragmented on ice to make a suspension. Follow the instructions of the SOD activity detection kit (A001-3-2; Nanjing Jiancheng, China) and the MDA content detection kit (A003-4-1; Nanjing Jiancheng, China), and the experiment was repeated independently 3 times. The reaction system was configured, and the reaction system was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min and then read at 450 nm/530nm with a microplate reader.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Transcriptome sequencing of LV-INHBB and LV-NC cells was performed by Shanghai Ouyi Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed by ClusterProfiler package.

Statistical analysis

RStudio 4.3.0, IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software were used for data analysis. The independent sample t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparison between multiple groups. Numerical data were expressed as frequencies (percentages), and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison between groups. Kaplan-Meier method and Log Rank test were used for survival analysis. The independent experiment was repeated three times, and the experimental results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±SD), ns means P > 0.05, * means P < 0.05, ** means P < 0.01, *** means P < 0.001, and when P < 0.05, the difference is considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Screening of differential genes related to oxidative stress and anoikis and identification of OARGs isotypes

Starting with a curated list of 997 genes linked to oxidative stress and anoikis, we interrogated the TCGA cohort to map their dysregulation in CRC. Differential expression analysis (|log₂Fold Change|≥1.0, P < 0.05) pinpointed 248 DEGs (154 upregulated and 94 downregulated) (Fig. 1A–B). Univariate Cox regression analysis retained 76 prognostic OARGs (Fig. S1). Protein-protein interaction mapping positioned OARGs within a densely connected subnetwork (Fig. 1C), with subsequent topological modeling linking specific interaction topologies to differential survival outcomes (Fig. 1D). At the genomic level, these 76 OARGs exhibited pervasive copy number variations (CNVs). Specifically, amplification was frequent in oncogenes such as MYC, SNAI1, and ROMO1, while deletions prevailed in genes like NAT1 and SLC4A11 (Fig. 1E–F), suggesting OARGs dysregulation might be driven by genomic instability.

To discern biological heterogeneity, we applied unsupervised consensus clustering, which robustly segregated the cohort into two clusters (Fig. 2A). This separation was corroborated by PCA (Fig. 2B). Cluster B emerged as the high-risk phenotype, associated with significantly inferior overall survival (Fig. 2C). The associations of OARGs subtypes with clinicopathological features and gene expression are visualized in the heatmap (Fig. 2D). Figure 6C directly compares the expression levels of OARGs between the two identified subtypes. Subtype B exhibited coordinated upregulation of oxidative stress and ubiquitination programs, coupled with selective enrichment in ECM-receptor interaction and MAPK signaling—hallmarks of aggressive tumor biology (Fig. S2).

Construction and validation of OARGs-related prognostic models

To refine the prognostic signature, we employed LASSO Cox regression analysis to eliminate multi-collinearity among redundant features, identifying 12 robust OARGs (Fig. 3A–C). Using the median risk score as a threshold, CRC patients were stratified into high- and low-risk cohorts. Kaplan–Meier analysis underscored a superior OS in the low-risk group—a trend consistently validated across the training, internal validation, and total cohorts (Fig. 3D–F). Furthermore, time-dependent ROC curves yielded AUC values ranging from 0.64 to 0.74 across 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals, indicating modest but significant discriminative ability(Fig. 3G–I).

Clinical efficacy verification of prognostic models

Univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis identified age, pathological T and N stages, and the risk score as significant correlates of OS (Fig. 4A). Subsequent multivariate adjustment confirmed that age, N stage, and the risk score remained robust independent prognostic indicators (Fig. 4B). To facilitate clinical utility, we engineered a prognostic nomogram integrating these independent variables (Fig. 4C). Calibration plots indicated strong alignment between predicted and observed outcomes (Fig. 4D). Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) showed that the nomogram achieved higher net benefit than any single clinical parameter across 1-, 3-, and 5-year thresholds (Fig. 4E–G).

Expression characteristics and clinical significance of INHBB in colorectal cancer

Pan-cancer interrogation of the TCGA dataset revealed a pervasive upregulation of INHBB across diverse malignancies, with CRC tissues exhibiting a strikingly higher expression profile compared to normal adjacent mucosa (Fig. 5A&Fig. S5A-B). Systematic correlation analysis linked elevated INHBB levels to advanced clinicopathological progression, specifically demonstrating a positive association with higher T, N, and M stages, as well as overall pathological grading (Fig. 5B). To validate these findings, we performed qRT-PCR and Western blotting, which confirmed that INHBB mRNA and protein levels were consistently enriched in CRC cell lines relative to the NCM460 control, with SW620 displaying the highest endogenous expression (Fig. 5C–D). Subcellular localization via immunofluorescence identified the cytoplasm as the primary niche of INHBB in SW620 cells (Fig. 5E).

Effect of INHBB on malignant phenotype of colorectal cancer cells

To explore its functional role, we established stable cell models; the potency of lentiviral-mediated overexpression in HT-29 cells and knockdown in SW620 cells was rigorously validated at at mRNA and protein levels. (Fig. 6A). To evaluate the oncogenic role of INHBB, we performed a series of gain- and loss-of-function experiments. Overexpression of INHBB promoted proliferation in HT-29 cells, whereas its knockdown suppressed growth in SW620 cells, as determined by CCK-8 assays (Fig. 6B). Migration and Matrigel invasion assays further quantified this effect, showing a dramatic surge in the number of traversed cells following INHBB overexpression, contrasted by a sharp decline in the sh-INHBB group (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, cell cycle analysis pinpointed an expansion of the S-phase population in INHBB-OE HT-29 cells, which coincided with a significant reduction in the basal apoptotic rate, confirming that INHBB facilitates survival signaling (Fig. 7A–B).

INHBB drives cell cycle transition and confers resistance to apoptosis. (A) Flow cytometric profiling of the cell cycle distribution, highlighting the effect on S-phase transition. (B) Assessment of apoptotic fraction via Annexin V/PI staining, showing the anti-apoptotic capacity of INHBB

Molecular mechanism of INHBB regulating malignant progression of colorectal cancer

We first evaluated the impact of INHBB on anoikis resistance, a hallmark of metastatic potential. Following 24 h of suspension culture, INHBB-OE HT-29 cells exhibited a significant reduction in apoptosis accompanied by the reciprocal modulation of Bcl-2 and Bax (Fig. 8A), confirming enhanced survival under detached conditions. Intriguingly, INHBB induction exacerbated oxidative stress, characterized by diminished SOD activity and elevated MDA levels (Fig. 8B-C). To decipher the underlying molecular landscape, transcriptomic profiling was performed, identifying a distinct signature of DEGs (Fig. S5A–B). GO and KEGG analyses pinpointed enrichment in extracellular matrix (ECM) organization and the PI3K-Akt/TGF-β signaling axes (Fig. 8D–E). Further validation via GSEA corroborated a robust positive correlation between INHBB and EMT-related gene sets (Fig. 8F). Consistently, Western blot analysis demonstrated a typical EMT switch—evidenced by E-cadherin loss and concomitant Vimentin/N-cadherin gain (Fig. 8G).

High expression of INHBB predicts poor prognosis of colorectal cancer and has potential diagnostic value

The clinical relevance of INHBB was further underscored by its profound impact on patient outcomes. High INHBB expression served as a potent predictor of poor prognosis, significantly correlating with curtailed overall survival (OS, P = 0.003), disease-specific survival (DSS, P = 0.005), and progression-free interval (PFI, P = 0.007) (Fig. 9A). A consolidated nomogram, integrating INHBB status with clinicopathological parameters, demonstrated remarkable calibration, with predicted survival trajectories closely aligning with observed outcomes (Fig. 9B–C& Fig. S4D). Furthermore, ROC analysis highlighted the diagnostic dexterity of INHBB in discriminating malignant from peritumoral tissues (Fig. 9D). This was empirically validated in our local cohort (n = 35); immunohistochemical profiling confirmed a significant elevation of the INHBB H-score in CRC specimens, with the protein predominantly sequestered within the cytoplasmic compartment (Fig. 9E).

Discussion

In this study, we integrated bioinformatics and functional assays to explore the potential relationship between oxidative stress and anoikis resistance in CRC. A 12‑gene prognostic signature was constructed and validated as an independent predictor of overall survival across multiple patient cohorts, providing a screening tool to identify key genes such as INHBB for further functional investigation [19–30].While INHBB had been previously associated with poor prognosis [31], its functional underpinnings in CRC remained elusive. We demonstrated that INHBB is significantly upregulated in CRC tissues, correlating with advanced clinical stages. In vitro experiments showed that INHBB overexpression not only accelerates proliferation and invasion but also blunts apoptosis.

INHBB overexpression confers resistance to anoikis under detached culture conditions, as evidenced by reduced apoptosis rates and reciprocal modulation of Bcl-2 and Bax expression. Intriguingly, INHBB-OE cells exhibit a pro-oxidant shift characterized by decreased SOD activity and increased MDA levels, which appears counterintuitive given the enhanced cell survival. To address this apparent paradox, we turned to the well-established concept of ROS signaling or ROS addiction in cancer biology.

A large body of literature supports that moderate, controlled ROS elevation acts as a second messenger to activate pro-survival and pro-metastatic pathways—a phenomenon often termed “redox signaling” or, in the context of cancer cells that thrive under such conditions, “ROS addiction” [32]. ROS promote proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis at controlled levels while inducing lethal damage when exceeding the cell’s buffering capacity [33].In our model, INHBB overexpression induces a pro oxidant shift (decreased SOD activity, increased MDA levels), but this shift remains below the cytotoxic threshold. The moderate elevation of ROS serves as a signaling cue that activates downstream pathways. In our study, GSEA confirmed significant enrichment of EMT gene sets, and Western blot analysis validated an INHBB-dependent EMT switch (E cadherin downregulation, N cadherin and vimentin upregulation). Thus, the observed increase in lipid peroxidation and reduction in SOD activity, rather than being purely detrimental, may represent a controlled redox shift that facilitates EMT and anoikis resistance.

And we state that while INHBB overexpression is associated with redox imbalance, the precise causal relationship remains to be determined, and the observed oxidative stress may reflect either a direct regulatory effect of INHBB on redox-related pathways or an indirect consequence of enhanced cellular proliferation and metabolic reprogramming. We acknowledge that our study did not measure other major antioxidant systems that may compensate for reduced SOD activity. It is plausible that INHBB engages these compensatory systems, either directly or indirectly, enabling CRC cells to survive under a pro-oxidant state while benefiting from ROS signaling. Future studies measuring GSH/GSSG ratios, GPx activity, TXNRD1 expression, and NRF2 activation status are warranted to fully resolve this redox paradox.

Despite these insights, several limitations warrant caution. Our findings, largely derived from public datasets and localized cell lines, require validation in large-scale, prospective clinical cohorts and diverse genetic backgrounds. Furthermore, the absence of in vivo data necessitates future verification using xenograft or PDX models to account for the complex tumor microenvironment. Mechanistically, the upstream regulators of INHBB and its downstream transducers-particularly the specific Smad-dependent or independent receptors involved in this EMT-redox axis-remain to be fully elucidated.

In summary, this study identifies INHBB as a critical malignant regulator in colorectal cancer, supporting its utility as a prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target. The proposed association between redox imbalance and malignant progression provides a foundation for future mechanistic investigations.

Conclusion

This study revealed the role of OARGs in the prognosis assessment of CRC from bioinformatics to experimental validation, and clarified the mechanism of INHBB as a pro-cancer factor to promote CRC progression through multiple pathways, providing new ideas for risk stratification and targeted therapy of CRC.
