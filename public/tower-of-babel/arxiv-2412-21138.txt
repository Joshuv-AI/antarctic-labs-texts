Optimal bound for survival time of the SIRS process on star graphs Thanks: Nguyen is supported by NSF Grant DMS–2246575 and the Salomon grant.
Phuc Lam, Oanh Nguyen, Iris Yang

Abstract
. We analyze the Susceptible-Infected-Recovered-Susceptible (SIRS) process, a continuous-time Markov chain frequently employed in epidemiology to model the spread of infections on networks. In this framework, infections spread as infected vertices recover at rate 1, infect susceptible neighbors independently at rate λ \lambda , and recovered vertices become susceptible again at rate α \alpha . This model presents a significantly greater analytical challenge compared to the SIS model, which has consequently inspired a much more extensive and rich body of mathematical literature for the latter. Understanding the survival time, the duration before the infection dies out completely, is a fundamental question in this context. On general graphs, survival time heavily depends on the infection’s persistence around high-degree vertices (known as hubs or stars), as long persistence enables transmission between hubs and prolongs the process. In contrast, short persistence leads to rapid extinction, making the dynamics on star graphs, which serve as key representatives of hubs, particularly important to study. In the 2016 paper by Ferreira, Sander, and Pastor-Satorras, it was conjectured, based on intuitive arguments, that the survival time for SIRS on a star graph with n n leaves is bounded above by ( λ 2 ​ n ) α (\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha} for large n n . Later, in one of a few mathematically rigorous results for SIRS, Friedrich, Göbel, Klodt, Krejca, and Pappik provided an upper bound of n α ​ log ⁡ n n^{\alpha}\log n , with contains an additional log ⁡ n \log n and no dependence on λ \lambda . We resolve this conjecture by proving that the survival time is indeed of order ( λ 2 ​ n ) α (\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha} , with matching upper and lower bounds. Additionally, we show that this holds even in the case where only the root undergoes immunization, while the leaves revert to susceptibility immediately after recovery.

1.1.
High-level proof sketch

Heuristically, we can explain the survival time on an
n
-star graph as follows. Each time that the root starts getting reinfected (namely, beginning of a “round") by its leaves, it stays infected for time
\xi\sim\text{Exp}(1)
. Typically,
\xi
is of order
\Theta(1)
and so, the root typically infects
N=\Theta(\lambda n)
leaves before it recovers and becomes immune. The probability that these leaves all recover before reinfecting the root can be written out explicitly (Lemma
3.4
) and shown to be of order
\Theta(\lambda N)^{-\alpha}
. Thus, as
N=\Theta(\lambda n)
, this probability of failing to reinfect the root (namely the round fails) becomes
\Theta(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}
. Therefore, the expected number of consecutive successful rounds would be of order
\Theta(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}
. Since the time between two consecutive reinfections of the roots would be typically of order 1, this translates to the order of the survival time.

Carrying out this strategy involves having a sharp control on the probability of the tail events.
For the upper bound, in
[
8
]
, an upper bound was achieved with an extra
\log n
term and no dependence on
\lambda
, namely it was shown that
\mathbb{E}\tau\leq Cn^{\alpha}\log n
. The
\log n
term there appeared naturally as an upper bound for length of each round because
\log N
is roughly the expected time that all
N
infected leaves recover. Here, we come up with an elegant argument to provide a tight upper bound which involves making use of the dominating process
(Y_{t})
and appropriately using Jensen’s inequality.

Moving to the lower bound, which turned out to be much more challenging, the first obstacle would be the unavailability of the Jensen’s inequality in the reverse direction. The second and more important issue is that for each round, the failing probability can be significantly larger than
(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}
, such as when
\xi
is too small, which leads to the number of infected vertices,
N
, being too small and failing probability being too large.
For instance, if
\alpha>1
, even in the first round, if we take any
1<\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
, then the probability that the first round fails is at least the probability that the root only stays infected for time
\xi\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha^{\prime}}
and no leaves were infected during that time. This happens with probability at least

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha^{\prime}}}\mathbb{P}(\text{Exp}(\lambda)>x)^{n}e^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-e^{(n\lambda+1)(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha^{\prime}}}}{n\lambda+1}\approx(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha^{\prime}},

much larger than the “typical"
(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}
.

In other words, the probability of surviving a round can be driven by rare but unfavorable scenarios. These bad rounds arise when the root is infected for only a very short time, or even worse, when such rounds occur consecutively, rapidly depleting the population of infected leaves. The key idea is that although bad rounds may follow one another, with high probability one can find a good starting point at which many vertices are infected. These infected vertices play a crucial role in sustaining the process over a chunk of consecutive bad rounds. Therefore, rather than focusing on individual bad rounds, we study such chunks. With this approach, we first obtain a preliminary lower bound of order
(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha-o(1)}
, as established in Lemma
5.2
. Building on this suboptimal estimate, we then carry out a more refined analysis to achieve the sharp bound.

Probabilistic tools

Throughout the paper, we will make repeated use of the following lemmas. The first lemma asserts that the maximum wait time of
n
clocks of rate
\lambda
has expectation roughly
\log n/\lambda
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
19
, Lemma 2.10]

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots,\xi_{n}
be i.i.d. exponentially distributed with rate
\lambda>0
. Let
H_{n}
be the
n
-th harmonic number, then

\mathbb{E}\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\xi_{i}\right)=\dfrac{H_{n}}{\lambda}<\dfrac{1+\log n}{\lambda}.

We also make use of the following standard lemma, which we state without proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\xi,\eta
be independent and exponentially distributed with rate
a,b>0
respectively. Then conditioned on
\{\xi<\eta\}
,
\xi
is exponentially distributed with rate
(a+b)
. In other words,

\xi\bigg|_{\xi<\eta}\sim\text{Exp}(a+b).

The next lemma gives the order of high moments of Binomial random variables.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
24
, Theorem 4]

Let
S\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)
. Then for any
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left((S-\mathbb{E}S)^{2d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2d}}=\Theta(1)\cdot\max_{2\leq k\leq d}\left(k^{1-\frac{k}{2d}}(np(1-p))^{\frac{k}{2d}}\right).

The sum of
n
independent exponential random variables with rate
\alpha
is the Gamma distribution
\text{Gam}(n,\alpha)
. We will use the following tail bound for this random variable.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\xi\sim\text{Gam}(n,\alpha)
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq(1+t)\dfrac{n}{\alpha}\right)\leq\left((1+t)e^{-t}\right)^{n}.

Proof.

This is a simple Chernoff bound. Let
\xi=Y_{1}+\dots+Y_{n}
, where
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d.
\sim\text{Exp}(\alpha)
random variables. Then for
s>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq(1+t)\dfrac{n}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\xi\geq(1+t)\mathbb{E}(\xi)\right)\leq e^{-(1+t)s\mathbb{E}(\xi)}\mathbb{E}\left(e^{s\xi}\right)=\left(e^{-(1+t)s\mathbb{E}(Y_{1})}\mathbb{E}(e^{sY_{1}})\right)^{n},

and setting
s:=\frac{\alpha t}{t+1}>0
gives the desired result.
∎

Finally, we recall a generalized version of Wald’s equation.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
3
, Generalized Wald’s equation, Theorem 5]

Let
c,c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\Psi
be a stopping time with respect to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
. Furthermore, let
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
be a random process over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq c}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}X_{i}
is integrable, and such that for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, it holds that
\mathbb{E}(X_{i+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i})\leq c^{\prime}
. Then

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}X_{i}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{0}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}\mathbb{E}(X_{i}\mid\mathcal{F}_{i-1})\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{0}\right).

and

3.1.
General strategy

Let
\tau
be the survival time for the process in consideration. Our starting point (for both processes
X
and
Y
) is the following.

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}\tau=\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})+\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}((\tau-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\{\tau_{i+1}=\infty\}}),

where the first and second summation is the total length of all successful rounds, and the length of the failed rounds, respectively. For the first summation, we will prove that

•

The probability of a round failing is at least of order
\Omega_{\alpha}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\right)
. This implies that the probability of the process surviving
i
rounds is at most
(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha})^{i}
, where
c^{\prime}>0
only depends on
\alpha
.

•

On the event that a round is successful, its expected length is at most
(2+1/\alpha)
, i.e. of constant order.

These two facts together that the total length of successful rounds is at most of order
O_{\alpha}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}\right)
.

For the second summation, we will prove that it is of order
O(\log n)
. The intuition that there are at most
n
infected leaves by the time the root becomes susceptible again, and on the event that the round fails, we can apply Lemma
2.2
.

3.2.
A coupling of two processes

We first couple the processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
. All the leaves in
(Y_{t})
does not gain immunity, so intuitively, they have a higher chance of being infected, which helps sustaining the infection of
Y
on the graph for longer than that of
X
. That being said, it is not necessarily true that
\tau_{Y}
stochastically dominates
\tau_{X}
because the "residual" times
\tau_{i+1}^{S}-\tau_{i}
would be shorter for
Y
than
X
. However, in a sense, the survival of
Y
still dominates
X
: the number of successful rounds of
Y
stochastically dominates that of
X
, via the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a coupling
((X^{\prime}_{t}),(Y^{\prime}_{t}))
of the processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
such that a.s.,

\Psi_{X^{\prime}}\leq\Psi_{Y^{\prime}},

where
\Psi_{X^{\prime}}
and
\Psi_{Y^{\prime}}
denotes the numer of successful rounds of the processes
(X^{\prime}_{t})
and
(Y^{\prime}_{t})
respectively. Moreover, in this coupling, we ensure that the number of infected leaves of
Y^{\prime}
at recovery times of the root of each round is always at least that of
X^{\prime}
. In other words, a.s., for all
1\leq i\leq\Psi_{X^{\prime}}
,

|\mathcal{I}_{X^{\prime},i}^{R}|\leq|\mathcal{I}_{Y^{\prime},i}^{R}|.

As a result,

\mathbb{E}\Psi_{X}\leq\mathbb{E}\Psi_{Y}.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathfrak{Q}_{\rho},\{\mathfrak{Q}_{v_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n},\mathfrak{D}_{\rho},\{\mathfrak{D}_{v_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\mathfrak{H}_{\rho v_{i}},\mathfrak{H}_{v_{i}\rho}\}_{i=1}^{n}\}

be the set of clocks for the SIRS process on a star graph (so
\mathfrak{Q}_{\rho},\mathfrak{Q}_{v_{i}}\sim\text{PPP}(1)
, and so on). Let
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},\dots
be independent copies of
\mathcal{C}
. We now define
(X^{\prime}_{t}),(Y^{\prime}_{t})
to be the coupled process of
(X_{t}),(Y_{t})
. For each process,

•

At round
i
, given that
\tau_{X^{\prime},i}<\infty
(resp.
\tau_{Y^{\prime},i}<\infty
), we use the clocks in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, where the starting time
t=0
of the clocks
\mathcal{C}_{i}
is lined up with time
\tau_{X^{\prime},i}
in the process
X^{\prime}
(resp.
\tau_{Y^{\prime},i}
in the process
Y^{\prime}
).

•

For process
Y^{\prime}
, we simply ignore the clocks
\mathfrak{D}_{v_{i}}
’s.

•

We let the processes run until
\tau_{X^{\prime},i+1}<\infty
(resp.
\tau_{Y^{\prime},i+1}<\infty
), at which point we replace the clocks in
\mathcal{C}_{i}
by those in
\mathcal{C}_{i+1}
and repeat the process as above.

Due to the memoryless property of PPP’s, we see that the processes
X^{\prime}
and
X
have the same distribution, and same goes for
Y^{\prime}
with
Y
. Thus,
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
as above is indeed a coupling for the processes
X
and
Y
. By a standard induction argument on
i
, one can argue that if
\tau_{X,i}<\infty
, then

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\tau_{Y^{\prime},i+1}-\tau_{Y^{\prime},i}\leq\tau_{X^{\prime},i+1}-\tau_{X^{\prime},i},\\
\tilde{I}_{X^{\prime},\tau_{X^{\prime},i}+t}\subseteq\tilde{I}_{Y^{\prime},\tau_{Y^{\prime},i}+t}\ \forall t\in[0,\tau_{Y^{\prime},i+1}-\tau_{Y^{\prime},i}].\end{cases}

Thus, this is the desired coupling.
∎

.

We note that in all the following subsections, the results apply to both processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
. The proofs are identical for both processes, except for those in Section
3.4
. Therefore, except for the aforementioned subsection, for the remaining subsections, we will omit the

and explain why it should also hold for the process

Lemma 3.3
.

For both processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
, the following holds: for any integer
b\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}|=b\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|=a\right)

\displaystyle=1_{\{a>0\}}\cdot\alpha\binom{a}{b}\cdot B(b+\alpha,a-b+1)+1_{\{a=0\}}\cdot 1_{\{b=0\}}

\displaystyle=1_{\{a>0\}}\cdot\alpha\dfrac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+a+1)}\cdot\dfrac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}{\Gamma(b+1)}+1_{\{a=0\}}\cdot 1_{\{b=0\}},

where
B(z_{1},z_{2})
denotes the Beta function and
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function.

Proof.

We omit the subscripts
X/Y
. The case
a=0
is straightforward, so we will consider
a>0
, which automatically implies that
\tau_{i}<\tau_{i}^{R}<\tau_{i}^{S}<\infty
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}
, let
H_{1},H_{2},\dots,H_{a}
be the time it takes, starting from
\tau_{i}^{R}<\infty
, for the leaves in
\mathcal{I}^{R}_{i}
to recover. Then
H_{1},\dots,H_{a}
are i.i.d.
\text{Exp}(1)
, so we have

\displaystyle 1_{\{a>0\}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{I}^{S}_{i}|=b\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|=a\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{a>0\}}\binom{a}{a-b}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-\alpha x}\mathbb{P}(H_{1},\dots,H_{a-b}<x)\cdot\mathbb{P}(x<H_{a-b+1},\dots,H_{a})\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{a>0\}}\binom{a}{a-b}\int_{0}^{\infty}\alpha e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-x})^{a-b}e^{-bx}\,dx=\ 1_{\{a>0\}}\alpha\binom{a}{a-b}\cdot B(b+\alpha,a-b+1),

giving the desired result.
∎

The next lemma controls the probability that a round fails, conditioned on the number of infected leaves by the time the root recovers in the given round.

Lemma 3.4
.

For both processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
, the following holds: for any positive integer
a
, conditioned on
\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|=a\}
, the probability that round
i
fails, i.e.
\tau_{i+1}=\infty
, is of order
(\lambda a)^{-\alpha}
. More precisely,

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|=a\right)=1_{\{a=0\}}+1_{\{a>0\}}\cdot\Theta_{\alpha}\left((\lambda a)^{-\alpha}\right).

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Again, we omit the subscripts
X/Y
. We have

\displaystyle 1_{\{a>0\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|=a\right)

\displaystyle=1_{\{a>0\}}\sum_{b=0}^{a}\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}=\infty,|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|=b\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}\right)

\displaystyle=1_{\{a>0\}}\sum_{b=0}^{a}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|=b\}}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i+1}=\infty\}}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{S}}\right)\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}\right).

Note that on
\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|=b\}
and conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{S}}
, the probability that round
i
fails is precisely when all
b
leaves recover before they can reinfect the root. This happens mutually independently with probability
1/(\lambda+1)
. Together with Lemma
3.3
, the above is

\displaystyle\sum_{b=0}^{a}\dfrac{1}{(\lambda+1)^{b}}\cdot\alpha\dfrac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+a+1)}\cdot\dfrac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}{\Gamma(b+1)}

\displaystyle=\alpha\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+a+1)}\sum_{b=0}^{a}\frac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}{\Gamma(b+1)}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\lambda)^{b}}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{a^{\alpha}}\cdot\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\right)

where in the last equality, we applied Stirling’s approximation to the first fraction and algebraic manipulation using properties of the Gamma function to the sum. A detailed calculation for the sum can be found in Appendix
A
.
∎

Moreover, in the proof above, if we take
\lambda\geq 1/2
, then we get the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
\lambda\geq 1/2
, then for both processes
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
, we have the following: for any positive integer
a
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}
, when

, the probability that round

fails, i.e.

3.4.
Infecting power of the root

For process
(X_{t})
, let
p_{X,S\mapsto I}(x)
be the probability that at any time
t
, conditioned on the fact that a leaf
v
is susceptible at time
t
, and the root
\rho
is infected throughout
[t,t+x]
, the aforementioned leaf is infected at time
t+x
. We define
p_{X,R\mapsto I}(x),p_{X,I\mapsto I}(x)
similarly.

For process
(Y_{t})
, we define
p_{Y,S\mapsto I}(x)
and
p_{Y,I\mapsto I}(x)
similarly (note that
p_{Y,R\mapsto I}
is not defined, since the leaves are never immune in the process
(Y_{t})
). We first prove the following lemma, which states that if
x
is large, then
p_{S\mapsto I}(x),p_{I\mapsto I}(x),p_{R\mapsto I}(x)
are also large.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C=C(\alpha,\varepsilon)\in(0,1)
such that the following holds: for
x\geq\varepsilon
,

p_{X,S\mapsto I}(x),\ p_{X,I\mapsto I}(x),\ p_{X,R\mapsto I}(x)\geq C\lambda.

Proof.

We omit the subscripts
X
for convenience, and first consider
\varepsilon\leq x\leq 1
. If
v\in\tilde{R}_{t}
, let
D_{v}\sim\text{Exp}(\alpha),H_{v}\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda),
and
Q_{v}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
respectively denotes the amount of time it takes (starting from
t
) for
v
to lose immunity, then infected, and then recover (assuming that these happen). Note that
D_{v},H_{v},Q_{v}
are independent, so for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(v\in I^{\prime}_{t+x}\ \bigg|\ v\in R^{\prime}_{t},\rho\in I^{\prime}_{t+y}\forall y\in[0,x]\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(D_{v}+H_{v}\leq x<D_{v}+H_{v}+Q_{v}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \lambda\dfrac{\alpha}{1-\lambda}\left(\dfrac{e^{-\lambda x}-e^{-\alpha x}}{\alpha-\lambda}-\dfrac{e^{-x}-e^{-\alpha x}}{\alpha-1}1_{\{\alpha\neq 1\}}-e^{-x}x1_{\{\alpha=1\}}\right)\geq C\lambda,

The proof for
p_{S\mapsto I}(x),p_{I\mapsto I}(x)
is completely analogous.
For
x>1
, we simply condition on the status of the leaf after
(x-1)
units of time and then apply these bounds for the next unit of time.
∎

We recall that
\xi_{i}
is the time it takes for the root to recover in round i. As an immediate corollary for Lemma
3.6
(and note that conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{X,i}}
and
\xi_{X,i}
, the states of the leaves at
\tau_{X,i}^{R}
are mutually independent), we have the following.

Corollary 3.7
.

On
\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}
and
\{\xi_{X,i}>\varepsilon\}
, conditioned on
\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{X,i}},\xi_{X,i})
, we have

|\mathcal{I}_{X,i}^{R}|\succeq\text{Bin}(n,C\lambda),

where
C=C(\alpha,\varepsilon)
is the constant in Lemma
3.6
.

As such, using a Chernoff bound, with probability
1-O\left(\exp(-\Theta_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\lambda^{2}n))\right)
,

|\mathcal{I}_{X,i}^{R}|\geq\dfrac{C\lambda n}{2}\approx_{\alpha,\varepsilon}\lambda n.

We note that the results in Lemma
3.6
and Corollary
3.7
also translate to the process
(Y_{t})
(without

We now control the number of infected leaves when the root recovers in each round.

Lemma 3.8
.

Consider the process
(Y_{t})
. For each round, conditioned on the previous round being successful and

Lemma 3.9
.

Consider the process

We omit the subscripts

r_{1}:=-\log\left(\dfrac{\alpha+7/8}{\alpha+1}\right)

\mathcal{A}:=\{\xi_{i}\in[r_{1},r_{2}]\}\in\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}},\xi_{i})

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\leq C\lambda n\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty,\mathcal{A}\right)

Proof of Lemma

.

Again, we omit the subscript

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha+1}{\alpha+1/2}\cdot\dfrac{\lambda n}{\lambda+1}+\dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+1/2}\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{I}_{i-1}^{R}|\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i-1}<\infty\right)\text{ by Lemma \ref{lm:UV}.}

.
∎

3.5.
Proof of the upper bounds

Combining these lemmas, we now prove the upper bound for Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
. Let
E_{i}:=\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}
be the event that the first
(i-1)
rounds are successful.

Probability of surviving to round
i
.
Note that conditioned on the first
(i-1)
rounds being successful, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(E_{i+1}^{c}\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq 1\}}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i+1}=\infty\}}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},E_{i}\right)\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right),

\displaystyle\approx_{\alpha}\lambda^{-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq 1\}}|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|^{-\alpha}\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right)\text{ by Lemma \ref{lm:orderfail}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\alpha}\lambda^{-\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq 1\}}|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right)\right)^{-\alpha}\text{ by Jensen's inequality}

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\text{ by Lemma \ref{lm:root:inf}}.

By induction on
i
, we obtain for some sufficiently small constant
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\alpha)>0
,

\Pr(E_{i+1})\leq(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})\Pr(E_{i})\leq(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})^{i}.

Length of a successful round.
The length of a successful round
i
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}^{S})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})+\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}}).

Note that the duration of time that the root recovers and becomes susceptible again is independent of the status of the leaves at the beginning of the round, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}\mathbb{E}\left((\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})\ \bigg|E_{i}\ \right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}\leq\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\right)^{i-1},

so it remains to upper bound
\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}^{S})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})
, which can be crudely bounded by the time it takes for all the infected leaves to recover, which is of order
\log(\lambda n)
. The following argument provides a better bound that avoids having the extra
\log(\lambda n)
term.
For
1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}}=1
, namely round
i
is successful, we need
|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|\geq 1
. Slightly abusing notations, let the (random) infected leaves at
\tau_{i}^{S}
be
v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|}
. Starting from
\tau_{i}^{S}
, for each
j
, let
\theta^{\prime}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)
be the time that
v_{j}
sends an infection to the root, and
\xi^{\prime}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
be the recovery time of
v_{j}
. We say that
v_{j}
is
good
if
\theta^{\prime}_{j}<\xi^{\prime}_{j}
, i.e. the leaf succeeds in infecting the root before it recovers, and
v_{j}
is
bad
otherwise. Let
V_{g}\leq|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|
be the number of good leaves, then for round
i
to succeed, there must be at least one good leaf, i.e.
V_{g}\geq 1
.

For a leaf
v_{j}
above, if we know that
v_{j}
is good, then we can condition on
\theta^{\prime}_{j}<\xi^{\prime}_{j}
; since
\theta^{\prime}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)
and
\xi^{\prime}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
are independent, we have that
\theta^{\prime}_{j}|_{\{\theta^{\prime}_{j}<\xi^{\prime}_{j}\}}\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda+1)
. On the event
\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{S}}
and
V_{g}
, we have

\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}^{S}=\min_{j:v_{j}\text{ is good}}\theta^{\prime}_{j}\sim\text{Exp}((\lambda+1)V_{g}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}^{S})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}\mathbb{E}\left((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i}^{S})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}}\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{1\leq V_{g}\leq|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{S}|\}}\dfrac{1}{(\lambda+1)V_{g}}\ \bigg|\ E_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda+1}\mathbb{E}1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}\leq(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})^{i-1}.

Combining all the above gives

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\mathbb{E}((\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{i})1_{\{\tau_{i+1}<\infty\}})

\displaystyle\leq\left(2+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\sum_{i\geq 1}(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})^{i-1}\lesssim(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}.

Length of a failed round.
For each term in the second summation of (
3.1
), corresponding to the failed round, by the same computations above, it is at most

(3.7)

\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\right)^{i-1}+\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\{\tau_{i+1}=\infty\}}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau_{i}^{S}<\tau\}}(\tau-\tau_{i}^{S})\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty,\tau_{i+1}=\infty\right)\right).

The second term now inevitably involves a
\log n
term.

Lemma 3.11
.

The length of survival after the root becomes susceptible again is of order
O(\log n)
, namely

\mathbb{E}\left(1_{\{\tau>\tau_{i}^{S}\}}(\tau-\tau_{i}^{S})\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty,\tau_{i+1}=\infty\right)\leq 2\log n.

A detailed proof, taking the conditional event into account, can be found in Appendix
B
.
Applying this lemma
3.11
to (
3.7
) and take the sum over
i
, the second sum in (
3.1
) is at most

(3.8)

\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left(1-c^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\right)^{i-1}+2(\log n)\mathbb{E}1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\{\tau_{i+1}=\infty\}}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}+\log n.

Combining (
3.1
), (
3.6
), and (
3.8
) gives the desired estimate, concluding the proof for the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
.

Remark 3.12

(Upper bound for
\lambda\geq 1/2
)
.

In this case, we can prove that the lower bound of the conclusion in Lemma
3.6
becomes
C=C(\alpha,\varepsilon)
instead of
C\lambda
. The proof involves routine but tedious calculations, so we will not include it here.
Similar comment applies for Corollary
3.7
and Lemma
3.8
. Finally, Lemma
3.11
make no assumption on the range of
\lambda
. Together with Lemma
3.5
, we can easily adapt the argument above to the upper bound of
\lambda\geq 1/2
.

Remark 3.13

(Bounds for
\lambda<C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
)
.

The upper bound above easily extends to
\lambda<C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
as well. Moreover, the lower bound follows directly from the above: the expected survival time is always at least
1
(recovery of the root), and we only need that for
\lambda\geq e/n
, with probability
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
,

|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\gtrsim_{\alpha}\lambda n.

We see that w.p.
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
,
\tau_{1}^{R}\geq 1/2
, and thus by Corollary
3.7
,
|\mathcal{I}_{1}^{R}|\succeq\text{Bin}(n,2C\lambda)=:Z
for some
C=C(\alpha)>0
. By Paley-Zygmund inequality,

\Pr(|\mathcal{I}_{1}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n)\geq\Pr(Z\geq\mathbb{E}Z/2)\geq\dfrac{1}{4}\cdot\dfrac{(\mathbb{E}Z)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}Z^{2}}=\dfrac{2C\lambda n}{4(1-2C\lambda+2C\lambda n)}=\Theta_{\alpha}(1),

completing the case
\lambda<C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
. Note that we can take care of the
\log n
term in the lower bound of
C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
in the same manner.

4.
Proof of the lower bounds: main ideas and useful lemmas

4.1.
General strategy

We will show that the expected number of successful rounds of the processes is of order at least
\approx(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}
, which is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Consider the SIRS process
(X_{t})
, let
\Psi_{X}
be the number of successful rounds of
(X_{t})
. Then there exist constants
C_{1},N,C_{0}>0
only dependent on
\alpha
such that for all
n\geq N
and
\lambda\geq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{n}}
,

\mathbb{E}\Psi_{X}\geq C_{1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}.

The same bound holds for the process
(Y_{t})
.

Lemma
2.6
, Lemma
4.1
, and Remark
3.13
together imply the needed lower bound.

Proof of lower bound for Theorems
1.3
and
1.5
.

Omitting the subscript
X
, then from the upper bound, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}(\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\tau<\infty,

i.e.
\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}(\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})
has finite expectation. Consider the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}=\{\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+1}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
(notice the shift of indices), then
\Psi
is a stopping time with respect to this filtration. Moreover, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left((\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\xi_{i}+\zeta_{i}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}}\right)=1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha},

regardless of whether
\tau_{i}<\infty
or not due to the way that we define
\xi_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
. Applying Lemma
2.6
,

\mathbb{E}\tau\geq\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}(\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i})\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\Psi}\mathbb{E}\left(\tau_{i}^{S}-\tau_{i}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i-1}\right)\right)=\left(1+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\mathbb{E}\Psi,

and the conclusion follows from Lemma
4.1
and Remark
3.13
by taking the maximum.
∎

As the main difficulty to obtain the desired bound for
\alpha>1
occurs when the root is only infected for a tiny amount of time, we divide the collection of rounds into "good" and "bad" rounds. We call the
i
-th round
\varepsilon
-good
if
\xi_{i}>\varepsilon
, and
\varepsilon
-bad
otherwise. Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
, we only need to prove it for
(X_{t})
. Therefore, in what follows, we will omit the subscript
X
.

Remark 4.2
.

Lemma
3.4
and Corollary
3.7
together directly implies that the probability of a good round failing is
\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}
.

In essence, our strategy is as follows. We will fix
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}(\alpha)>0
(to be chosen later), and choose parameters
\varepsilon\in(0,1),K\geq 1
accordingly (Lemma
5.1
). Let
1\leq m_{1}<m_{2}<\dots
be all the (random) indices such that the
m_{i}
-th round is
\varepsilon
-good, i.e.
\xi_{m_{i}}\geq\varepsilon
. Instead of attempting to work with
\Pr(\tau_{i+1}=\infty\mid\tau_{i}<\infty)

as previously, we will show that

\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{i+1}}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \tau_{m_{i}}<\infty\right)\lesssim_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha},

for all
i\leq\Psi_{0}=\Theta_{\alpha}((\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha})
. In other words, we look at
chunks
of bad rounds instead of each round individually. Since
m_{i}\geq i
for all
i
, this directly gives us Lemma
4.1
. All left is the analysis below.

Denote
M_{0}:=\left\lceil\frac{K\alpha\log(\lambda^{2}n)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right\rceil
and
M_{1}:=\left\lceil\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\alpha\log(\lambda^{2}n)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right\rceil
, then we consider the following cases for chunk length
(m_{i+1}-m_{i})
.

(i)

m_{i+1}-m_{i}>M_{0}
. This means we have at least
M_{0}
consecutive, i.i.d.
\text{Exp}(1)
clocks of length at most
\varepsilon
, which happens with probability

(1-e^{-\varepsilon})^{M_{0}}\leq\varepsilon^{M_{0}}\lesssim_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

(ii)

M_{1}<m_{i+1}-m_{i}\leq M_{0}
. In Section
5
, we will prove a suboptimal bound, which essentially states that condition on
M
consecutive bad rounds, where
M\leq M_{0}
, then the probability of failing is at most
\Theta_{\alpha}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}\right)
(Lemma
5.2
). Thus,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{i+1}}=\infty,M_{1}<m_{i+1}-m_{i}\leq M_{0}\ \bigg|\ \ \tau_{m_{i}}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \sum_{M=M_{1}+1}^{M_{0}}\Pr(m_{i+1}-m_{i}=M)\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{i+1}}=\infty\ \bigg|\ m_{i+1}-m_{i}=M,\tau_{m_{i}}<\infty\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\ \sum_{M=M_{1}+1}^{M_{0}}\ \varepsilon^{M}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}\lesssim_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

(iii)

m_{i+1}-m_{i}\leq M_{1}
. This is the most complicated case. A key ingredient is the
sustained SIRS process
, which is introduced in this section. More intuitions will be presented in Section
6
.

4.2.
Number of non-immune leaves cannot be too large

In the following lemma, we will show that with overwhelmingly high probability, for exponentially long time, the number of non-immune leaves in the SIRS process is always large enough. For the rest of the paper, we abuse notations and view
S_{t},I_{t},R_{t}
as the set of susceptible, infected, and immune
leaves
respectively (so those sets do not include the root
\rho
).

Proposition 4.3
.

With overwhelmingly high probability, at all times
t\geq 0
, the number of non-immune leaves of the SIRS process is of order
\Theta(n)
. More precisely, set
B:=\min\left(\frac{\alpha}{16(\alpha+1)^{2}},\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{8}\right)\in\left(0,\frac{1}{64}\right)
, then for some constant
C>0
only dependent on
\alpha
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(|S_{t}|+|\mathcal{I}_{t}|\geq Bn,\ \forall t\geq 0\right)\geq 1-Ce^{-4B^{2}n}.

Proposition
4.3
follows directly from a more technical statement which we will need later.

Lemma 4.4
.

Given any
b\in(0,1]
, for all stopping time
T
with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the following holds: conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T}
, on the event
\{|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\}
, with overwhelmingly high probability, at all times
t\leq\exp(\Theta_{\alpha,b}(n))
, the number of non-immune leaves of
(X_{t})
is of order
\Theta_{\alpha,b}(n)
. More precisely, setting
B:=\min\left(\frac{\alpha}{16(\alpha+1)^{2}},\frac{e^{-\alpha}}{8}\right)\in\left(0,\frac{1}{64}\right)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{T+t}|\leq(1-Bb)n,\ \forall 0\leq t\leq e^{4B^{2}bn}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T},|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{|\mathcal{R}_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\}}\left(1-2e^{-4B^{2}bn}\right).

Note that later, we will use this lemma with the process
(X_{t})
being replaced by the so-called sustained process
(\tilde{X}_{t})
. The lemma’s proof works for the latter as well.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

The idea is as follows.

•

We first "discretize" time, i.e. proving that with overwhelmingly high probability, at all times
T+i
, with
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
i\geq\exp(\Theta(n))
(to be determined later), we have
|R_{T+i}|\leq(1-2Bb)n
.

•

We then show that conditioned on
|R_{T+i}|\leq(1-2Bb)n
, with overwhelmingly high probability,
|R_{T+i+u}|\leq(1-Bb)n
for all
u\in(0,1)
.

We will execute the steps above. Obviously,
|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n<(1-Bb)n
. Now, for
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T+i}
, fixing a vertex
v
, and consider

\mathfrak{R}^{c}_{v}:=\mathfrak{Q}_{v}+\mathfrak{D}_{v}\sim\text{PPP}(1+\alpha).\quad

Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T+i}
, we see that
\{\mathfrak{R}^{c}_{v_{j}}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
are mutually independent. Let
E_{j}
be the event that
\mathfrak{R}^{c}_{v_{j}}
has at least two events on
[T+i,T+i+1]
, and that the last event originally belongs to
\mathfrak{D}
(losing immunity), the second-to-last event originally belongs to
\mathfrak{Q}
(recover). Observe that if
E_{j}
happens, then
R_{i+1}(v_{j})=0
, no matter how
\mathfrak{H}_{\rho v_{j}}
behaves. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{j}\ |\ \mathcal{F}_{T+i})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(1-e^{-(1+\alpha)}(2+\alpha)\right)}_{\geq 1/4}\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\right)\left(\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha}\right)\geq\dfrac{\alpha}{4(\alpha+1)^{2}}=4B.

Let
{R}^{*}_{T+i+1}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}1_{E^{c}_{i}}
, then conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T+i}
,

|R_{T+i+1}|\leq{R}^{*}_{T+i+1}\preceq\text{Bin}(n,1-4B).

By a Chernoff bound,

(4.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{T+i+1}|\geq(1-2Bb)n\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T+i}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{T+i+1}|\geq(1-2B)n\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T+i}\right)\leq\exp\left(-8B^{2}n\right).

We will next show that conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T+i}
, on the event
\{|R_{T+i}|\leq(1-2Bb)n\}
, with overwhelmingly high probabillity,
\sup_{u\in(0,1)}|R_{T+i+u}|\leq(1-Bb)n
. If
\sup_{u\in(0,1)}|R_{T+i+u}|>(1-Bb)n
, it means that there exists some
u_{*}\in(0,1)
such that
|R_{i+u_{*}}|>(1-Bb)n
. This means that conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{T+i}
, at least
Bbn
of the deimmunization clocks
\mathfrak{D}_{v}
, with
v\in R_{T+i}
, contain at least one event in
[i,i+1]
. These clocks are independent, therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{u\in(0,1)}|R_{T+i+u}|>(1-Bb)n\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T+i},R_{T+i}\leq(1-2Bb)n\right)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{|R_{T+i}|\leq(1-2Bb)n\}}(1-e^{-\alpha})^{Bbn}\leq 1_{\{|R_{T+i}|\leq(1-2Bb)n\}}\exp\left(-8B^{2}bn\right).

Combining (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) gives (and for clarity of presentation, we treat
L:=e^{4B^{2}bn}
as an integer)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|R_{T+t}|\geq(1-Bb)n,\ \forall 0\leq t\leq e^{4B^{2}bn}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T},|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ \mathbb{P}\left(|R_{T+t}|\geq(1-Bb)n,\ \forall 0\leq t\leq L,|S_{T+i}|\geq(1-2Bb)n,\ \forall i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,L]\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{T},|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{|R_{T}|\leq(1-b)n\}}\prod_{j=0}^{L}\left(1-2e^{-8B^{2}bn}\right),

which, by Bernoulli’s inequality, is at least
1-2e^{-4B^{2}bn}
, completing the proof.
∎

4.3.
The sustained SIRS process on star graphs

In this section, we will introduce a
sustained
SIRS process
\tilde{X}=(\tilde{X}_{t})
on star graphs as follows. We will equip the PPPs on the vertices and edges in the same time as in the SIRS process. The process evolves in the same way as in the SIRS process, with an additional rule: at the survival time
\tau
(when no vertices are infected),

•

if the root is immune, then we infect the root at the moment it becomes susceptible again;

•

otherwise, we infect the root immediately.

In this sustained SIRS process, the infection never dies out. Moreover, using a graphical representation, we can couple the original SIRS process
(X_{t})
and the sustained SIRS process
(\tilde{X}_{t})
by using the same clocks for both processes. Then
\tilde{X}
evolves in the exact same manner as
X
up until the survival time
\tau_{X}
of
X
.

Note that the notion of round still holds in this newly defined process. However, a successful/ failed round is defined differently from the original SIRS process. We say that a round of the sustained SIRS process is
successful
if the root is reinfected by a leaf, and
failed
if all leaves recover before the root is reinfected, i.e. the root is artificially reinfected.

We use similar notations as those for the SIRS process, but with
\verb|tilde|
on top of it. For example, we denote
\tilde{\tau}_{i}
the time that round
i
starts, and
\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R},\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{S},\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{i},\tilde{\mathcal{R}}_{i},\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
similarly. However, we still use the same notation
\xi_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
for the the time it takes for the root to recover and lose immunity in round
i
. This is because in the subsequent application of this sustained SIRS process, we will always view it as being coupled with the usual SIRS process by using the same clocks (on the graphical representation). For now, this newly defined process still seems mysterious; however, the purpose of using this process will be made clearer in the following section.

5.
Proof of the lower bounds: A suboptimal bound for runs of bad rounds

Here, we aim to provide a sub-optimal bound first, to show that a chunk of bad rounds, with a good starting point, fails with probability at most
(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha+o(1)}
. We start with a technical lemma, which aids in our choice of parameters. A proof for this lemma is deferred to Appendix
C
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all fixed
\varepsilon_{0}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{100(2\alpha+1)}\right)
, we can choose parameters
K\geq 1,\varepsilon\in(0,1/8),t>0
all dependent on
\varepsilon_{0},\alpha
such that setting
T:=t-\log(1+t)
, the following holds.

\dfrac{TK}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\geq 1-\varepsilon_{0},\qquad\dfrac{tK}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\leq 1-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{2},\qquad\dfrac{K\alpha(\varepsilon+1+1/\alpha)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\leq\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{4}.

We will prove that the probability of surviving at most
M_{0}
consecutive bad rounds is sufficiently large, which is captured in the main lemma below.

Lemma 5.2
.

For a fixed
\varepsilon_{0}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{100(2\alpha+1)}\right)
, let
K\geq 1,\varepsilon\in(0,1/8)
be the parameters introduced in Lemma
5.1
, and let
M_{0}:=\left\lceil\frac{K\alpha\log(\lambda^{2}n)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right\rceil
. Then there exist constants
C_{0},c>0
only dependent on
\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}
such that the following holds: for all
\lambda\geq C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
and
M\leq M_{0}
, starting at the
i
-th round, conditioned on the event that there are at least
C\lambda n
infected leaves at
\tau_{i}^{R}
(where
2C=2C(\alpha,\varepsilon)
is the constant in Lemma
3.6
), and that all the next
M
rounds are
\varepsilon
-bad, then the probability that the process survives all the next
\varepsilon
-bad rounds is at least
1-c(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}
. More precisely,

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+M+1}<\infty\ \bigg|\ |\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\xi_{i+j}\leq\varepsilon\ \forall 1\leq j\leq M\right)\geq 1-c(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}.

Together with Corollary
3.7
, as a direct consequence, there also exist constants
C_{0}^{\prime},c^{\prime}>0
only dependent on
\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
\lambda\geq C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
,
M\leq M_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{i+M+1}<\infty\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty,\xi_{i}>\varepsilon,\xi_{i+j}\leq\varepsilon\ \forall 1\leq j\leq M\right)\geq 1-C^{\prime}_{0}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

The condition
|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n
immediately implies that
\tau_{i}<\infty
. Let
\mathcal{E}:=\left\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\xi_{i+j}\leq\varepsilon\ \forall 1\leq j\leq M\right\}
. Let
t>0
be the parameter introduced in Lemma
5.1
. We introduce the following objects.

\displaystyle\eta_{i+j}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\tau_{i+(j+1)}-\tau_{i+j}^{S}&(\tau_{i+j+1}<\infty),\\
\infty&(\text{otherwise}),\end{cases}

\displaystyle\kappa

\displaystyle:=M_{0}\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{0}}\zeta_{i+j}+(M_{0}+1),

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}

\displaystyle:=M_{0}\varepsilon+\dfrac{t+1}{\alpha}(M_{0}+1)+(M_{0}+1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{*}

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathcal{I}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}:v\in\mathcal{I}_{t}\ \forall t\in[\tau_{i}^{R},\tau_{i}^{R}+\kappa]\}.

In words,
\eta_{i+j}
is the time it takes in round
(i+j)
for the susceptible root to be infected, and
|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{*}|
is the number of leaves that are infected at time
\tau_{i}^{R}
and stays infected all throughout
\kappa
units of time. We will explain the role of
\kappa,\kappa_{0}
shortly. The intuition for our approach is as follows.

•

We want to show that w.h.p.
\eta_{i+j}\leq 1
for all
0\leq j\leq M
, guaranteeing that the next
M
rounds (starting from round
i+1
) is successful. Then, starting from
\tau_{i}^{R}
, the remainder of the
i
-th round and the entirety of the next
M
rounds is covered within the next (random)
\kappa
units of time.

•

In order to do so, we will show that w.h.p.,
|\mathcal{I}_{*}|
is large, i.e. there is a substantial number of infected leaves that does not recover within
\kappa
units of time, therefore improving the chances of all
M
rounds being succesful and sufficiently fast.

•

However, we need to rule out the case when
\kappa
is too large (and we do not have enough leaves that are infected throughout all
\kappa
units of time). Thus, we show that w.h.p.
\kappa\leq\kappa_{0}
.

Rigorously,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\kappa>\kappa_{0}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right)+\Pr\left(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|\leq C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E},\kappa\leq\kappa_{0}\right)

(5.1)

\displaystyle\ +\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E},\kappa\leq\kappa_{0},|\mathcal{I}_{*}|>C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\right),

where
C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(\alpha,\varepsilon_{0})>0
is chosen appropriately during the course of our calculations below.

By Lemma
2.5
, note that
\sum_{j=0}^{M_{0}}\zeta_{i+j}\sim\text{Gam}(M_{0}+1,\alpha)
and
\kappa
is independent of
\mathcal{E}
, so setting
T=t-\log(1+t)
and by Lemma
5.1
,

(5.2)

\mathbb{P}(\kappa>\kappa_{0})\leq\left((t+1)e^{-t}\right)^{M_{0}+1}\leq\exp\left(-T\cdot\dfrac{K\alpha\log(\lambda^{2}n)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}.

For the second term in (
5.1
), we condition on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},\{\xi_{i+j}\}_{1\leq i\leq M}
and
\{\zeta_{i+j}\}_{0\leq j\leq M_{0}}
, and omit the conditions for clarity of presentation. On
\mathcal{E}\cap\{\kappa\leq\kappa_{0}\}
, we have

|\mathcal{I}_{*}|\succeq\text{Bin}(C\lambda n,e^{-\kappa_{0}}),

where we slightly abused the notation for the constant
C
in Lemma
3.6
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)

\displaystyle\geq C\lambda ne^{-\kappa_{0}}\gtrsim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}\lambda^{-1}\exp\left(\log(\lambda^{2}n)\left(\left(1-\dfrac{tK}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)-\dfrac{K\alpha(\varepsilon+1+1/\alpha)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\right)\right),

and by our choice of parameters in Lemma
5.1
, the above is

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{-1}\exp\left(\log(\lambda^{2}n)\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}}{4}\right)\right)=\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}.

Let
2C^{\prime}=2C^{\prime}(\alpha,\varepsilon_{0})
be the implicit constant above, i.e.
\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)\geq 2C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}
. Moreover, note that
1\geq 1-e^{-\kappa}\geq 1-e^{-M_{0}\varepsilon}=\Theta(1)
, so

\text{Var}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)=\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)(1-e^{-\kappa})\approx_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)\gg 1.

Thus, by Lemma
2.4
, setting
d^{\prime}=40\lceil\alpha/\varepsilon_{0}^{2}\rceil
, we have

(5.3)

\Pr\left(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|\leq C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E},\kappa\leq\kappa_{0}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}\dfrac{\text{Var}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)^{d^{\prime}}}{\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)^{2d^{\prime}}}\approx_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}\mathbb{E}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)^{-d^{\prime}}\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-10\alpha}.

For the last term in (
5.1
), we define the following event

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{J}:=\left\{\eta_{i+j}\leq 1\ \forall 0\leq j\leq J\right\},

then
\mathcal{B}_{0}\supseteq\mathcal{B}_{1}\supseteq\mathcal{B}_{2}\supseteq\dots\supseteq\mathcal{B}_{M}
. Intuitively,
\mathcal{B}_{J}
represents the fact that all the "residual" time from round
i
to round
(i+J)
behaves in accordance with the bound for them in
\kappa
. Condition on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{E}\cap\left\{\kappa\leq\kappa_{0},|\mathcal{I}_{*}|>C^{\prime}\lambda^{-1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\right\}
, then
\mathcal{B}_{M}
implies
\tau_{i+M+1}<\infty
. Thus, we have

(5.4)

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}_{M}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M}\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}_{j}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}_{j-1}\right),

where
\mathcal{B}_{-1}:=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.

Consider conditioning on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{B}_{j-1}
, where
j\geq 1
(the case where
j=0
is proven similarly). Slightly abusing notations, let
v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{I}_{*}|}
be all the leaves in
\mathcal{I}_{*}
. At time
\tau_{i+j-1}^{S}+1
, we have

(\tau_{i+j-1}^{S}+1)-\tau_{i}^{R}=\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}\xi_{i+k}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\zeta_{i+k}+\sum_{k=0}^{j-2}\eta_{i+k}\leq(j-1)\varepsilon+\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\zeta_{i+k}+(j-1)<\kappa,

so the recovery clocks for the vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{I}_{*}|}
do not ring before time
\tau_{i+j-1}^{S}+1
. Thus, if one of the infection clocks for
v_{1},\dots,v_{|\mathcal{I}_{*}|}
rings before
\tau_{i}^{S}+1
, then
\eta_{i}\leq 1
. Conditioned on
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i+j-1}^{S}}
, the earliest infection clock of
v_{i}
’s follows
\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)
. On
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
,
\lambda|\mathcal{I}_{*}|\geq C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}
, which means

(5.5)

\Pr\left(\mathcal{B}_{j}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}_{j-1}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\text{Exp}(\lambda|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)\geq 1\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{B}_{j-1}\right)\leq\exp\left(-C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\right).

Together, (
5.4
) and (
5.5
) implies that

(5.6)

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\leq(M+1)\exp\left(-C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}\log(\lambda^{2}n)\exp\left(-C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\varepsilon_{0}^{2}/4}\right).

Equations (
5.1
), (
5.2
), (
5.3
), and (
5.6
) together completes our proof.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

When
\lambda\geq 1/2
, we can replace
\lambda
by
1
in the definition of
M_{0},M_{1}
, and all of the analysis above. Together with Remark
3.12
, this ensures that
\text{Var}(|\mathcal{I}_{*}|)\gg 1
, so there are no issues in adapting the proof to larger
\lambda
.

6.
Proof of the lower bounds: a detailed analysis for short runs of bad rounds

6.1.
Intuition and main lemma

Our goal is to prove something of the form

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty,M\leq M_{1}\ \bigg|\ \tau_{i}<\infty,\xi_{i}>\varepsilon,\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\xi_{j}\leq\varepsilon\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

The main issue is that we have very little control over the events we are conditioning on; in particular, not much can be inferred from
\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}
. This calls for some additional events in the conditions. Luckily, there exist a few footholds allowing for reductions.

Our first reduction comes from Lemma
4.4
, which asserts that with overwhelmingly high probability, the number of non-immune leaves is of order
\Theta(n)
for exponentially long time. Define, for
t_{0}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t_{0}}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}|R_{t}|\leq(1-B)n\right\},\quad\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t_{0}}:=\left\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}|\tilde{R}_{t}|\geq(1-B)n\right\},\quad L:=\exp(4B^{3}n),

where
B
is the constant in Lemma
4.4
. If we take
\tau_{i}\lesssim\sqrt{L}
(which is a lot more wiggle room than needed), then we can already ensure that the number of non-immune leaves is always enough to work with.

Our second reduction comes from the fact that the chunk length is short. This means that we can guarantee that w.h.p. all of our bad rounds cannot be
that
bad! From the issues raised in the introduction, ideally, we want all of our
\xi
’s to be at least
(\lambda^{2}n)^{-1}
. Turns out, we can do much better: w.h.p., all
\xi
’s are at least of order
(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}
, where
0<\varepsilon_{0}<\varepsilon_{0}^{+}<\alpha/100
is chosen later.

To simplify our calculations, we would like to relate failure of a round to quantities of previous rounds
without
having to condition on their successes. It turns out that the
sustained
SIRS process is the right object to look at. Since we are viewing the SIRS and sustained SIRS as being coupled on the same graphical representation, we automatically have

\mathcal{U}_{t}=\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{t}\ \forall 0\leq t\leq\tau.

At this point, we are ready to state the central lemma of this section.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider the SIRS process
(X_{t})
. Then there exist constants
B,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
C_{0},C,C^{\prime}>0
only dependent on
\alpha
such that the following holds: For
\lambda\geq C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
and every
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
i\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}
, starting from
i
-th round, let
M
be the (random) number of consecutive
\varepsilon
-bad rounds that follows right after the
i
-th round. Condition on the event that there are at least
C\lambda n
infected leaves,
\mathcal{U}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}
, and
\{\tau_{i}^{R}\leq 2\sqrt{L}\}
, then the probability that the process survives
M
rounds after
i
happening is at least
1-C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}
. More precisely, let
M
be such that
\{\xi_{i+1},\dots,\xi_{i+M}\leq\varepsilon,\xi_{i+M+1}>\varepsilon\}
, then

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ |\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\tau_{i}^{R}<2\sqrt{L},\mathcal{U}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}\right)\leq C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

We have the following immediate corollary. In Section
6.2
, we will show how to complete the lower bound using this corollary.

Corollary 6.2
.

In the setting as in Lemma
6.1
, there exist constants
C_{0},C^{\prime},\varepsilon>0
only dependent on
\alpha
(that might differ from the constants
C_{0},C^{\prime}
in the Lemma
6.1
) such that for
\lambda\geq C_{0}/\sqrt{n}
,

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \xi_{i}\geq\varepsilon,\tau_{i}<\sqrt{L},\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{i}}|R_{t}|\leq(1-B)n\right)\leq C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

Proof of Corollary
6.2
.

Write
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}:=\left\{\xi_{i}\geq\varepsilon,\tau_{i}<\sqrt{L},\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau_{i}}|R_{t}|\leq(1-B)n\right\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\tau_{i+M+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ |\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\tau_{i}^{R}<2\sqrt{L},\mathcal{U}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}\right)

\displaystyle\ +\Pr\left(|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|<C\lambda n\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)+\Pr\left(\tau_{i}^{R}>2\sqrt{L}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)+\Pr\left(\mathcal{U}^{c}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right),

where in the first term, we can get rid of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
in the condition by using Law of Total Expectation over
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}
. Conditioning on
\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}},\xi_{i})
, we can see that (and increase our choice of
d
in each step if needed) in the summation above, the second term is of order
\exp\left(-\Theta_{\alpha}(\lambda^{2}n)\right)
(Corollary
3.7
), the third term is of order
O(L^{-1/2})
(Markov inequality and that
(\tau_{i}^{R}-\tau_{i})
has mean
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
), and the fourth term is of order
O(\exp(-4B^{3}n))
(Lemma
4.4
).
Together with Lemma
6.1
, the result follows directly.
∎

6.2.
Proof of the lower bound

Let
B,\varepsilon,C^{\prime}>0
be chosen according to Corollary
6.2
,
\Psi_{0}:=\frac{(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}}{3C^{\prime}}
, and
1\leq m_{1}<\dots
be the (random) indices of all
\varepsilon
-good rounds. We will show that

\tau_{m_{\Psi_{0}}}<\infty

with probability
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
. Then, since
m_{k}\geq k
for all
k\geq 1
, we have, with probability
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
,
\Psi_{X}\geq\Psi_{0}
, which completes the proof.

For all
1\leq j\leq\Psi_{0}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(\tau_{m_{j+1}}=\infty)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \Pr(\tau_{m_{j}}=\infty)+\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{j+1}}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \tau_{m_{j}}<\sqrt{L},\inf_{0\leq t\leq\sup_{m_{j}}}|R_{t}|\leq(1-B)n,m_{j}\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\ \Pr\left(m_{j}>(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)+\Pr\left(\sqrt{L}<\tau_{m_{j}}<\infty,m_{j}\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)+\Pr\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|R_{t}|>(1-B)n\right),

and by Corollary
6.2
and Proposition
4.3
, the above is, for some
C>0
dependent on
\alpha
,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \Pr(\tau_{m_{j}}=\infty)+C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}+\Pr\left(m_{j}>(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)

(6.1)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\ \Pr\left(\sqrt{L}<\tau_{m_{j}}<\infty,m_{j}\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)+Ce^{-4B^{2}n}.

Now,
m_{j}>(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}
means that within
\{\xi_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}}
, there are only
j<\Psi_{0}
rounds that are
\varepsilon
-good. Such number of
\varepsilon
-good rounds follows
\sim\text{Bin}((\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1},e^{-\varepsilon})
, so the probability that there are less than
\Psi_{0}
good rounds is, for sufficiently large
n
(and by a Chernoff bound)

(6.2)

\Pr\left(m_{j}>(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\dfrac{e^{-2\varepsilon}}{2}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right).

Finally, we see that together with the upper bound for
\mathbb{E}\tau
, by Markov’s inequality,

(6.3)

\Pr\left(\sqrt{L}<\tau_{m_{j}}<\infty,m_{j}\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha+1}\right)\leq\Pr\left(\tau>\sqrt{L}\right)\leq L^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\tau\leq C(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}e^{-2B^{2}n},

for some
C
only dependent on
\alpha
. Thus, (
6.1
), (
6.2
), and (
6.3
) together implies that for sufficiently large
n
, for all
1\leq j\leq\Psi_{0}-1
,

\Pr(\tau_{m_{j+1}}=\infty)\leq\Pr(\tau_{m_{j}}=\infty)+\dfrac{3C^{\prime}}{2}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}=\Pr(\tau_{m_{j}}=\infty)+\dfrac{1}{2\Psi_{0}}.

To complete the proof, we can use induction on
j
to see that

\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{\Psi_{0}}}=\infty\right)\leq\dfrac{\Psi_{0}-1}{2\Psi_{0}}+\Pr\left(\tau_{m_{1}}=\infty\right)\leq\dfrac{\Psi_{0}-1}{2\Psi_{0}}+\underbrace{\Pr(n_{1}>1)}_{\leq\varepsilon}+\underbrace{\Pr(m_{1}=1,\tau_{1}=\infty)}_{=0}\leq\dfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon<\dfrac{3}{4}.

The rest of this section is devoted to proving Lemma
6.1
, and is organized as follows. In Subsection
6.3
, we translate the problem to the sustained SIRS. Subsection
6.4
presents further intuition and reduces the problem to the key Lemma
6.4
, whose proof is presented in Subsection
6.5
.

Remark 6.3
.

For the rest of this section, we will chiefly work with conditioning on filtrations instead of events. We will trade off convenience of notations for freedom to enlarge/ shrink events we are conditioning on. Readers are encouraged to focus more on events than filtrations.

6.3.
Reductions and coupling

For
\varepsilon_{0}
to be chosen later, and
\varepsilon>0
to be chosen accordingly, pick
C=2C(\alpha,\varepsilon)\in(0,1)
according to Corollary
3.7
. We define the following events.

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle:=\left\{|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\tau_{i}^{R}<2\sqrt{L}\right\}\cap\mathcal{U}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},\qquad\qquad\tilde{\mathcal{E}}:=\left\{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}\leq 2\sqrt{L}\right\}\cap\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle:=\left\{\min_{1\leq j\leq M_{1}}\xi_{i+j}\geq(\lambda^{2}\alpha)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}\right\},\qquad E^{\prime}_{k}:=\left\{\xi_{i+j}\leq\varepsilon\ \forall 1\leq j\leq k\right\},\qquad E_{k}:=E\cap E^{\prime}_{k}.

We also let
A_{k}
denote the event that round
k
fails in the
sustained
SIRS process. Clearly,
E_{1}\supseteq E_{2}\supseteq\dots\supseteq E_{M_{1}}
and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
. Moreover, we leave it for readers to verify that
\{\tau_{i}<\infty\},\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}}
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{E}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}}
.

6.3.1.
Reductions

From Subsection
4.1
, our problem is readily reduced to showing that

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+m+1}=\infty,M\leq M_{1}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\tau_{i+m+1}=\infty,M\leq M_{1},E_{M}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \Pr\left(\tau_{i+m+1}=\infty\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E},E_{M}^{c},M\leq M_{1}\right)\Pr\left(M\leq M_{1},E_{M}^{c}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right),

and by Lemma
5.2
, and the fact that
\{\xi_{i+j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is independent of
\mathcal{E}
, the above is

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}

\displaystyle\ (\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha(1-\varepsilon_{0})}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}\Pr\left(\max_{1\leq j\leq m}\xi_{i+j}\leq\varepsilon,\min_{1\leq j\leq M_{1}}\xi_{i+j}\leq(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}1_{\mathcal{E}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

Our problem is thus further reduced to showing that

\Pr\left(\tau_{i+m+1}=\infty,M\leq M_{1},E_{M}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{E}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

6.3.2.
Coupling

Note that
|\mathcal{I}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n
automatically implies that
\tau_{i}<\infty
, and so
\tau_{i}^{R}=\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}
, and that
(X_{t})
and
(\tilde{X}_{t})
coincides up to at least time
\tau_{i}^{R}=\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\tau_{i+m+1}=\infty,M\leq M_{1},E_{M}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tau_{i}^{R}},\mathcal{E}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\mathcal{E}}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},M=m,E_{m}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right),

and since the calculations involving
k=0
is a routine application of Remark
4.2
, the above is

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\mathcal{E}}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}+\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},M=m,E_{m}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\mathcal{E}}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{m=k}^{M_{1}}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},M=m,E_{m}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 1_{\{\tau_{i}<\infty\}}1_{\mathcal{E}}\left((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}+\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E_{k}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)\right),

so our problem is now reduced to showing that

(6.4)

\sum_{k=1}^{M_{1}}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E_{k}\ \bigg|\ \mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}.

6.4.
Analysis of sustained SIRS: next intuitions

Having completely transferred the problem to the sustained SIRS process, we now introduce the following events and explain our motivations.

\displaystyle g_{i+k}

\displaystyle:=\{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+(k-1)}|>\lambda n\},\qquad G_{k}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k-1}g_{i+j},

\displaystyle G^{\prime}_{i+k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+k}|\geq\dfrac{B^{2}\lambda n}{2}\xi_{i+j}\ \forall 1\leq j\leq k\right\},

\displaystyle f_{i+k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+k}|\geq|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+k-1}|\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i+k}+2+\varepsilon)\right)\right\}\cup\left\{\xi_{i+k}>e^{-\zeta_{i+k-1}}\right\},\qquad F_{k}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}f_{i+j},

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n,\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}\leq\sqrt{L}\left(3-\frac{k-1}{M_{1}}\right)\right\}\cap\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}}\supseteq\tilde{\mathcal{E}}.

Probability of failing a round,
A_{i+(k+1)}
, is again directly related to
|\tilde{I}_{i+k}^{R}|
. Its main contributions are from (i)
|\tilde{I}_{i+(k-1)}^{R}|
and (ii) newly infected leaves at the beginning of round
(i+k)
.
f_{i+k}
is the event that contributions from either source is good enough, and we split it as above to compare contributions. Thus,
F_{k}^{c}
represents the
bad
event in which at some point, contributions from both sources are bad. Moreover,
g_{i+k}
is a
good
event in which we have enough infected leaves at
\tilde{\tau}_{i+(k-1)}^{R}
, at which point we can "restart" the procedure.

By virtue of Lemma
4.4
, we can make sure that starting from
\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}
, since
|\tilde{R}_{i}^{R}|\leq(1-B)n
, then with probability at least
1-2e^{-B^{3}n}
, the number of non-immune leaves is always going to be at least
B^{2}n
for at least
e^{4B^{3}n}
time unit, which is more than long enough to cover
M
rounds of
(\tilde{X}_{t})
with overwhelmingly high probability. Thus, when
\xi_{i+k}\leq\varepsilon
,
|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+k}^{R}|
is at least
\text{Bin}(B^{2}n,2\xi_{i+j}/3)
, and condition on
\xi_{i+j}\geq(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}
, we can use a routine Chernoff bound for
(G^{\prime}_{i+k})^{c}
to obtain

\Pr\left((G^{\prime}_{i+k})^{c}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{E}\right),E,\tilde{\mathcal{E}}\right)\leq 1_{E}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}}\exp\left(-\Theta_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0}}((\lambda^{2}n)^{1-2\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}})\right).

Together with the above, (
6.4
) follows directly from the following lemma.

Lemma 6.4
.

Keeping all the notations as above, there exists a constant
C^{\prime}>0
only dependent on
\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}
such that the following holds: for all
1\leq k\leq M_{1}
,

\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)\leq\ 1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}}C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\left(M_{1}^{-1}+2^{-k}\right).

We will prove Lemma
6.4
by induction on
k
. The role of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}
as a technical enlargement of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
is now clearer: at each step of induction, we decrease
\tilde{\tau}_{i+k}^{R}
by
\sqrt{L}/M_{1}
until we reach
k=M_{1}
, in which case it coincides with the original event
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
. To outline the induction step, we will split the above into the intersection with the following events:
G_{k}
,
G_{k}^{c}\cap F_{k}^{c}
, and
G_{k}^{c}\cap F_{k}
.

•

On
G_{k}
, within a run of
k
rounds, some
j
-th round has an abundance of infected vertices when the root recovers, and so we can use our induction hypothesis to bound the probability of surviving the first
j
rounds, and then the remaining
(k-j)
rounds (conditioned properly).

•

On
G_{k}^{c}\cap F_{k}^{c}
, note that
F_{k}^{c}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}f_{i+j}^{c}
, and each
f_{i+j}^{c}
can be dealt with by Chernoff bounds.

•

On
G_{k}^{c}\cap F_{k}
, the contribution to
|\tilde{I}^{R}_{i+k}|
by its previous rounds are moderate, allowing for some averaging over all previous rounds.

6.5.
Proof of Lemma
6.4

For clarity of presentation, when we refer to Chernoff bounds, the tail events are negligible (exponential decay), and we will not write it out explicitly. We will sometimes even use "w.e.h.p." ("with exponentially high probability") instead of referring to Chernoff bounds.

6.5.1.
Base case
k=1

We consider two cases, corresponding to two contributions to
|\tilde{I}_{i+1}^{R}|
.

Case 1:

\xi_{i+1}\leq e^{-\zeta_{i}}
. Then the main contribution comes from
|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\geq C\lambda n
. On
E
, since
e^{-\zeta_{i}}\geq\xi_{i+1}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}
, we have

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{S}|\geq\text{Bin}\left(|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|,e^{-\zeta_{i}}\right)\succeq\text{Bin}\left(C\lambda n,(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}\right),\qquad\text{w.e.h.p. }|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{S}|\geq\dfrac{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|e^{-\zeta_{i}}}{2}\geq\dfrac{C(\lambda^{2}n)^{1-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}}{2\lambda}.

When
|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{S}|
is large, then the probability that each infected vertex recovers before reinfecting the root is
1/(\lambda+1)
, so by another Chernoff bound, we have w.e.h.p.

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}|\geq\dfrac{|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{S}|}{2}.

Finally, on
|\xi_{i+1}|\leq\varepsilon
, when
|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}|
is large, we have

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+1}|\geq\text{Bin}\left(|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}|,e^{-\varepsilon}\right),\qquad\text{w.e.h.p. }|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+1}|\geq\dfrac{4}{e^{2}}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}|e^{-\varepsilon}.

Combining all the above, w.e.h.p.

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}^{R}|\geq\dfrac{4}{e^{2}}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}|e^{-\varepsilon}\geq|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i}+2+\varepsilon)\right)\geq C\lambda n\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i}+2+\varepsilon)\right).

Case 2:

\xi_{i+1}>e^{-\zeta_{i}}
. The main contribution then comes from newly infected leaves in the
(i+1)
-th round. On
G^{\prime}_{i+1}
,
|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}^{R}|\geq\frac{B^{3}\lambda n}{2}\xi_{i+1}.

From both cases, w.e.h.p. we have

\displaystyle|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+1}^{R}|

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(C\lambda n\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i}+2+\varepsilon)\right),\dfrac{B^{3}\lambda n}{2}\xi_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\left(C\lambda n\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i}+2+\varepsilon)\right)+\dfrac{B^{3}\lambda n}{2}\xi_{i+1}\right)

\displaystyle\gtrsim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}\lambda n\left(e^{-\zeta_{i}}+\xi_{i+1}\right).

Let
C^{\prime}>0
be the implicit constant in the above. Then

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,1}}O((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})+1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,1}}\Pr\left(A_{i+1},|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\geq C^{\prime}\lambda n\left(e^{-\zeta_{i}}+\xi_{i+1}\right)\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,1}}O((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha})+1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,1}}(C^{\prime}\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left(\left(e^{-\zeta_{i}}+\xi_{i+1}\right)^{-\alpha}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right)\right)}_{=C_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}<\infty,},\text{ by Lemma \ref{lm:fail}}

proving the base case. From the proof, it is also clear that we can use Chernoff bounds for the event

f_{i+j}^{c}:=\left\{\xi_{i+j}\leq e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}},|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+j}^{R}|<|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+j-1}^{R}|\exp\left(-(\zeta_{i+(j-1)}+2+\varepsilon)\right)\right\},

and so we will omit the details on bounding the event
G_{k}^{c}\cap F_{k}^{c}
.

6.5.2.
Induction step

For the induction step, keeping the notations as in Lemma
6.4
, and suppose it holds up to
(k-1)
. We are done if the following of inequalities holds.

(6.5)

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},G_{i+k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1_{E}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}}C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}\dfrac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\left(M_{1}^{-1}+2^{-k}\right),

(6.6)

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},G^{c}_{i+k},F_{k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1_{E}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}}\dfrac{C^{\prime}}{2}(\lambda^{2}n)^{\alpha}(2^{-k}).

To prove (
6.5
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},G_{i+k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \sum_{j=2}^{k}\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},g_{i+j},\tilde{\tau}^{R}_{i+(j-1)}-\tau_{i}^{R}\leq M_{1}^{-1}\sqrt{L}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)

(6.7)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=2}^{k}\Pr\left(\tilde{\tau}^{R}_{i+(j-1)}-\tau_{i}^{R}>M_{1}^{-1}\sqrt{L}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right).

The second summation in (
6.7
) is negligible: note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}-\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{j-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\zeta_{i+(j^{\prime}-1)}+(\tilde{\tau}_{i+j^{\prime}}-\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j^{\prime}-1)}^{S})+\xi_{i+j^{\prime}}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=(j-1)\left(\alpha^{-1}+2(\log n+1)+((\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}+1)\right)\leq C^{\prime\prime}(\log n)^{2},

for some
C^{\prime\prime}>0
dependent on
\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}
, so we can use Markov inequality accordingly. For the first summation, on
g_{i+j}
, we need to make sure that
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i+(j-1),k-1}
is satisfied when "restarting" the procedure at round
i+(j-1)
. For
2\leq j\leq k
,
\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}\leq\sqrt{L}(3-M_{1}^{-1}(k-1))
and
\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}-\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}\leq M_{1}^{-1}\sqrt{L}
implies that
\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}\leq\sqrt{L}(3-M_{1}^{-1}(k-2))
. Thus, by the induction hypothesis, starting at
\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},g_{i+j},\tilde{\tau}^{R}_{i+(j-1)}\leq\sqrt{L}(3-M_{1}^{-1}(k-2))\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \Pr\left(A_{i+(j-1)+(k-(j-1))},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},E^{\prime}_{j-1},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i+(j-1),k-1}\right),

(6.8)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ 1_{{E}}1_{E^{\prime}_{j-1}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i+(j-1),k-1}}C^{\prime}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\left(M_{1}^{-1}+2^{-(k-(j-1))}\right).

Since
E^{\prime}_{j-1}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}}
and that
\Pr\left(E^{\prime}_{j-1}\mid E\right)\leq\varepsilon^{j-1}
, taking
\mathbb{E}\left(\cdot\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)
on both sides of (
6.8
) and summing over all
2\leq j\leq k
yields (
6.5
).

For (
6.6
), the main idea is to observe that
G^{\prime}_{i+k}\cap G_{i+k}^{c}\cap F_{k}
implies, for all
1\leq j\leq k
,

(6.9)

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+j}^{R}|\geq\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}|\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right).

This means

\displaystyle|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+k}^{R}|\geq\left(\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}\right)^{k}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{R}|\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right)\geq\left(\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}\right)^{k}(C\lambda n)\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right),

and so by conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i+k}^{R}}
, applying Lemma
3.4
, and using Law of Total Expectation properly, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i+k},E^{\prime}_{k},G^{\prime}_{i+k},G_{i+k}^{c},F_{k}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right),{E},\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha,\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0}^{+}}

\displaystyle\ 1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\left(1_{E^{\prime}_{k}}\left(\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}\right)^{-\alpha k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right)^{-\alpha}\ \bigg|\ \sigma\left(\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}},1_{{E}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 1_{{E}}1_{\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i,k}}(\lambda^{2}n)^{-\alpha}\left(\left(\dfrac{4}{B^{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\alpha e^{-\alpha u}}{(e^{-u}+v)^{\alpha}}dudv\right)^{k},

the last inequality holds since
\{\xi_{i+j}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
,
\{\zeta_{i+(j-1)}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
are mutually independent and are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{\tilde{\tau}_{i}^{R}}
. All that remains for us is to (i) verify the implication (
6.9
), and (ii) prove that

\left(\dfrac{4}{B^{2}}\right)^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\alpha e^{-\alpha u}}{(e^{-u}+v)^{\alpha}}dudv\leq\dfrac{1}{2}.

The latter is a matter of simple computation:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\alpha e^{-\alpha u}}{(e^{-u}+v)^{\alpha}}dudv

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\alpha e^{-\alpha u}}{e^{-\alpha u}+v^{\alpha}}dudv=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left(\log(1+v^{\alpha})-\alpha\log v\right)dv

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\log(1+\varepsilon^{\alpha})-\alpha\varepsilon\log\varepsilon+\alpha\varepsilon,

which tends to
0
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, and we can choose
\varepsilon_{0}
such that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. The former is routine event analysis: for each
j
,

•

If
\xi_{i+j}\geq e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}
, then on
G^{\prime}_{i+k}
, we have

|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+j}|\geq\dfrac{B^{2}}{2}\lambda n\xi_{i+j}\geq\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}\lambda n\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right)\geq\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+(j-1)}|\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right),

the last inequality holds on
G_{i+k}^{c}
.

•

If
\xi_{i+j}\leq e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}
, then

\displaystyle|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+j}^{R}|

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}|\exp(-(\xi_{i+(j-1)}+2+\varepsilon)),\dfrac{B^{2}}{2}\lambda n\xi_{i+j}\right)\qquad\text{ on $f_{i+j}\cap G^{\prime}_{i+k}$}

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2}\left(|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}|\exp(-(\xi_{i+(j-1)}+2+\varepsilon))+\dfrac{B^{2}}{2}\lambda n\xi_{i+j}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{1}{2}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{i+(j-1)}^{R}|\left(\exp(-(\xi_{i+(j-1)}+2+\varepsilon))+\dfrac{B^{2}}{2}\xi_{i+j}\right)\qquad\text{ on $G^{c}_{i+k}$}

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{B^{2}}{4}|\tilde{\mathcal{I}}^{R}_{i+(j-1)}|\left(e^{-\zeta_{i+(j-1)}}+\xi_{i+j}\right).

Appendix A
Calculate the second sum in (
3.3
)

We set out to prove that for
0<\lambda\leq 1/2
,

(A.1)

\sum_{b=0}^{a}\dfrac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}{\Gamma(b+1)}\cdot\dfrac{1}{(\lambda+1)^{b}}\approx_{\alpha}\dfrac{1}{\lambda^{a}}\approx_{\alpha}\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^{\alpha}},

where
x:=\frac{1}{\lambda+1}\in\left[\frac{2}{3},1\right)
. Suppose
\alpha>1
. Denote

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{b\geq 0}\dfrac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}{\Gamma(b+1)}x^{b},

then we can show that

\displaystyle(1-x)S_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=(\alpha-1)S_{\alpha-1}(x),

and so we only need to prove (
A.1
) for all
\alpha\in(0,1]
and use induction. The claim is straightforward for
\alpha=1
, as
S_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{1-x}
. Recall Gautschi’s inequality: for
b>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we have

b^{1-\alpha}<\dfrac{\Gamma(b+1)}{\Gamma(b+\alpha)}<(b+1)^{1-\alpha}.

Then we have

S_{\alpha}(x)=\Gamma(\alpha)+\sum_{b\geq 1}\dfrac{\Gamma(b+\alpha)b^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(b+1)}\cdot\dfrac{x^{b}}{b^{1-\alpha}}=\Theta_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{b\geq 1}\dfrac{x^{b}}{b^{1-\alpha}}\right),

and since the function
b\mapsto\dfrac{x^{b}}{b^{1-\alpha}}
is decreasing on
[1,\infty)
, we can approximate the last sum using integrals, i.e.

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=\Theta\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{x^{y}}{y^{1-\alpha}}dy\right).

It remains to evaluate the asymptotics for the integral in the last display. Substituting
u=x^{-y}
gives

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{x^{y}}{y^{1-\alpha}}dy

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\left(\log\frac{1}{x}\right)^{\alpha}}\int_{1/x}^{\infty}\dfrac{du}{u^{2}(\log u)^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^{\alpha}}\left(\dfrac{1-x}{\log\frac{1}{x}}\right)^{\alpha}\int_{1/x}^{\infty}\dfrac{du}{u^{2}(\log u)^{1-\alpha}}.

Since
\frac{2}{3}\leq x<1
, we get that

\displaystyle\left(\dfrac{1-x}{\log\frac{1}{x}}\right)^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\Theta(1),

\displaystyle\int_{1/x}^{\infty}\dfrac{du}{u^{2}(\log u)^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\in\left(\int_{3/2}^{\infty}\dfrac{du}{u^{2}(\log u)^{1-\alpha}},\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{du}{u^{2}(\log u)^{1-\alpha}}\right)=\Theta(1),

and combining all the above gives the desired estimate. Note that this also means for
\lambda>1/2
, the LHS sum in (
A.1
) becomes
\Theta_{\alpha}(1)
(upper bound by replacing
\lambda=1/2
, lower bound by the term when
b=0
) instead of
\Theta_{\alpha}(\lambda^{-\alpha})
.

Proof of Lemma
