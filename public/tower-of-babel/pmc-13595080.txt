Digenome-Detect: Accurate, statistics-based analysis software for identifying off-target cleavage sites in genome editing
Authors: Takuma Yamashita, Yuki Naito, Takenori Yamamoto, Tokuyuki Yoshida, Yasunori Uchida, Eriko Uchida, Takao Inoue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOff-target mutations pose major safety concerns in genome-editing therapy owing to their potential for leading to serious adverse events such as carcinogenesis. Thus, accurate prediction and evaluation of off-target mutations are critical in ensuring the safety of genome-editing therapeutics. Among the methods available for predicting off-target mutations, cell-free assays, which detect cleavage sites in extracted genomic DNA treated with genome-editing tools in vitro, are valuable because of their genome-wide, unbiased, and sensitive detection capabilities. However, cell-free assays are prone to identifying false-positive off-target cleavage sites, limiting their practical utility. To address this issue, we developed “Digenome-Detect,” a highly accurate and sensitive data analysis software tool for Digenome-seq, which is the simplest cell-free assay. Digenome-Detect calculates a statistically derived score for genomic cleavage sites, followed by additional filters to reduce false positives. Compared with the current standard software, Digenome-toolkit, Digenome-Detect identified more off-target cleavage sites with fewer obvious false positives. Digenome-Detect enables accurate and sensitive prediction of off-target cleavage sites and can thereby contribute to ensuring the safety of genome-editing therapeutics.Keywords: genome editing, CRISPR-Cas, off-target, safety evaluation, software

Introduction

Genome editing is a method for introducing mutations at targeted sites in genomic DNA. For example, the most widely used genome-editing tool, the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas9 system, induces DNA double-strand breaks through base pairing between its guide RNA (gRNA) and genomic DNA, together with recognition of a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), resulting in mutations at the target site. Owing to its ability to disrupt, repair, or insert genes at a specific locus, this technology holds significant potential to expand the applications of gene therapy.1 Nevertheless, off-target mutations, which occur at unintended genomic sites, remain a major safety concern associated with genome-editing therapeutics.2,3 Off-target mutations can lead to serious adverse events, most notably carcinogenesis resulting from alterations in cancer-related genes. Therefore, accurate prediction and evaluation of potential off-target mutations are essential to ensure the safety of genome-editing therapeutics.

Off-target mutations can be predicted using (1) in silico assays, which involve sequence database searches to identify similar sequences in genomic DNA; (2) cell-free assays, which detect cleavage sites in extracted genomic DNA that has been treated with a genome-editing tool; and (3) cell-based assays, which identify mutation sites in cells treated with a genome-editing tool. Regulatory documents from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA) of Japan recommend using experimental analyses—cell-free and/or cell-based assays—in addition to in silico assays to predict off-target mutations.4,5,6

Among these experimental analyses, cell-free assays are generally more sensitive,7,8,9 as high concentrations of genome-editing tools can be used. In addition, the results obtained by cell-free assays are unaffected by cellular properties such as chromatin status10,11 and transcriptional activity,12,13 which are known to affect genome editing efficiency. Therefore, cell-free assays enable more comprehensive identification of potential off-target mutation sites. However, cell-free assays tend to identify more false-positive candidates for off-target mutation sites compared with cell-based assays,14 limiting their practical utility.

In this study, we improved the accuracy of Digenome-seq, the simplest cell-free assay (Figure 1),9,15 by developing analysis software, Digenome-Detect. By employing statistics-based cleavage detection algorithms, Digenome-Detect demonstrated higher accuracy and sensitivity for off-target cleavage detection than conventional software.

Results

Analysis of Digenome-seq data using Digenome-toolkit

First, we analyzed Digenome-seq data using the current standard software, Digenome-toolkit, to evaluate the accuracy and sensitivity of the conventional method. High-throughput sequencing data of genomic DNA treated with a model genome-editing tool, the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9)-gRNA complex (ribonucleoprotein [RNP]), were analyzed using Digenome-toolkit. This RNP recognizes a 20-base DNA sequence complementary to the gRNA followed by the canonical PAM, 5′-NGG-3′.

To examine whether Digenome-toolkit identified hybridization-dependent off-target cleavage sites, we focused on genomic loci with up to six total mismatches, insertions (RNA bulges), or deletions (DNA bulges) relative to the complementary gRNA and containing a canonical PAM. The total number of mismatches, insertions, or deletions between the genomic DNA and gRNA sequences is referred to as the distance (d) hereafter (Figure 2A).16 We searched d ≤ 6 sites with a canonical PAM of SpCas9 using GGGenome, as described in materials and methods. Digenome-toolkit identified 100% of d = 1 and d = 3 sites, 22.8% of d = 4 sites, 2.05% of d = 5 sites, and 0.05% of d = 6 sites as cleaved sites. No d = 2 site was present in the reference human genome (GRCh38). The proportion of cleaved sites was higher at sites with smaller distances, indicating that Digenome-toolkit is capable of detecting hybridization-dependent cleavage (Figure 2B).

Upon classifying all the sites detected using Digenome-toolkit based on distance, bimodal distribution was observed (Figure 2C). The peak at d ≥ 7, which accounted for 47% of the total, was hypothesized to represent false positives. To test this hypothesis, we generated 10,000 random sequences unrelated to the gRNA sequence and compared the distribution of their distance with that of d ≥ 7 sites (Figure 2D). We found that 99% of the random sequences had d ≥ 7, and the distance distribution of the random sequences was similar to that of the d ≥ 7 sites identified using Digenome-toolkit. Furthermore, the percentage of random sequences without canonical PAM was 85%, which was similar to that of the d ≥ 7 sites detected using Digenome-toolkit (84%). These results indicated that most of these d ≥ 7 sites were unrelated to the gRNA and PAM sequences and were therefore likely to be false positives.

We further examined these d ≥ 7 sites manually using integrative genomics viewer (IGV). In Digenome-seq, DNA cleavage by the genome-editing tool results in the generation of DNA fragments with the same 5′-ends, leading to the accumulation of forward reads with identical 5′-ends at the genomic cleavage site (Figure 1, red arrows). Similarly, reverse reads of DNA fragments with identical 3′-ends accumulate at the genomic cleavage site (Figure 1, blue arrows). Thus, at the actual cleaved sites, 5′ ends of the forward read (forward heads) and 3′ ends of the reverse read (reverse tails) accumulate, as observed in the on-target site (Figure 3A). Although a similar accumulation was observed at false-positive sites (Figures 3B and S1), most of these forward heads and reverse tails were ends of clipped reads, which included sequences that could not be mapped to the reference genome. For example, in the region shown in Figure 3B, compared with the reference genome, the genomic DNA used in the experiment had a sequence insertion. Because the read included an insertion sequence that could not be mapped to the reference genome, it was clipped (red sequences in Figure 3B), and the apparent ends of the clipped reads did not align with the actual ends of the DNA fragment. Therefore, the observed accumulation of the ends of the clipped reads did not indicate an actual cleavage. Similarly, 3′-ends of the forward read (forward tails) and 5′-ends of the reverse read (reverse heads) also accumulated at the false-positive sites as forward reads and reverse reads were clipped in the same way at the position where the insertion started. Thus, the accumulation of forward tails and reverse heads also indicated false positives.

Next, we examined whether there were false-negative sites that Digenome-toolkit failed to identify despite an apparent accumulation of forward heads and reverse tails. To this end, we manually checked the IGV images of d ≤ 4 sites containing canonical PAM. Usually, no accumulation of forward heads and reverse tails is observed at sites that are not cleaved by the genome-editing tools (Figure 3C). However, a certain number of forward heads and reverse tails accumulated at some of the sites that we examined, indicating cleavage at those positions (Figures 3D and S2). Because sequencing reads spanning across these accumulation sites were also observed, cleavage at these sites was evidently only partial. These data indicate that Digenome-toolkit is not capable of detecting this degree of forward head/reverse tail accumulation and may therefore overlook weak off-target cleavage.

Development of Digenome-Detect

To overcome the limitations of Digenome-toolkit described earlier, we developed Digenome-Detect, a software tool for highly accurate and sensitive detection of cleavage sites. The software calculates a statistically derived score for detecting cleavage sites and has additional features to filter out false positives.

Figure 4 shows the workflow of Digenome-Detect. First, the sequence data are preprocessed by mapping the sequence to a reference genome and removing duplicate reads. The preprocessed file is then transferred to Digenome-Detect. Digenome-Detect first extracts sites where either the number of forward heads or the number of reverse tails exceeds a user-defined threshold (default: 4) to reduce computational costs and then calculates the cleavage likelihood score (CL-score) for each site to determine whether the sites are cleaved correctly. These sites are subsequently filtered to reduce the number of false positives.

Workflow of the analysis using Digenome-Detect

After mapping the FASTQ files obtained from sequencing analysis to the reference genome, deduplication is performed. The obtained files are used to calculate the CL-score for each site with the number of forward heads ≥4 or reverse tails ≥4. The sites with CL-score >7 shared by cleaved and non-cleaved samples are removed as false positives. False-positive sites are further filtered by a count of reads with a mapping quality of zero and clipped reads. Thereafter, the CL-score threshold is determined by comparison with the non-cleaved sample. Sites with a CL-score above the threshold are deemed as cleaved sites. The right image shows that the score calculation is based on the likelihood of the number of forward heads and reverse tails accumulated by chance. The CL-score of the site with many forward heads or reverse tails is high, as it is not likely to occur by chance. Thus, the CL-score reflects the confidence that a site of interest is actually cleaved.

We developed the CL-score as a primary metric for identifying cleavage sites. It is based on the likelihood that the number of forward heads and reverse tails at a site of interest exists by chance, assuming random fragmentation of genomic DNA and Poisson-distributed read ends across the genome. For example, when there are 10 forward reads (each 150-nt long) covering a given position, the probability that 8 forward heads will accumulate at the same position by chance is 9 × 10−15, as calculated using the Poisson distribution (Figure 4, right). The same is true for the reverse tails. Thus, when there are 10 forward reads, of which 8 accumulate as forward heads, and 10 reverse reads, of which 8 accumulate as reverse tails, the likelihood of this happening by chance can be obtained by multiplying each probability (9 × 10−15 × 9 × 10−15). Given this extremely low likelihood, the accumulation is more likely caused by DNA cleavage induced by a genome-editing tool rather than by random events, indicating the site is most likely cleaved. The CL-score is defined as the negative logarithm of the likelihood that the forward heads and reverse tails will accumulate by chance; thus, the CL-score increases as the likelihood decreases. In other words, the CL-score reflects the confidence that a site of interest is a cleaved site (Figure 4). Further details of the CL-score calculation are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

To minimize false positives, we implemented two filters. The first filter compares cleaved and non-cleaved samples and removes the sites where the CL-score exceeds the user-defined threshold (default: 7) in both samples. The second filter excludes the sites with elevated CL-score due to improperly mapped sequence reads; this filters out the sites where the sum of clipped reads and reads with a mapping quality of zero exceeds the total count of forward heads or reverse tails.

To confirm that the calculated CL-scores reflect the degree of hybridization-dependent off-target cleavage, we evaluated the relationship between the CL-score and distance for the cleaved sample data. A smaller distance was found to be associated with a higher CL-score. Additionally, when comparing the CL-scores at the same distance, a higher CL-score was observed for sequences containing a canonical PAM (5′-NGG-3′) (Figures 5A and 5B). Compared with the non-cleaved samples, a higher CL-score was observed for the cleaved sample (Figure 5C). These results indicate that the CL-score reliably reflects hybridization-dependent cleavage.

Analysis of Digenome-seq data using Digenome-detect

First, we determined the CL-score threshold for identifying cleaved sites by comparing cleaved and non-cleaved samples. The maximum CL-score observed in the non-cleaved sample was 15.1 (blue line in Figures 6A, 6B, and S3A); however, even in the region below this value, a larger number of sites were detected in the cleaved sample compared with the non-cleaved sample (Figure 6B), likely representing true cleavage sites. Therefore, to detect such sites, the cumulative number of sites with a CL-score greater than or equal to a given value was calculated for each sample, and the threshold was defined as the minimum CL-score at which the ratio of these numbers (non-cleaved/cleaved) was ≤ 0.1 (10.37; red line in Figures 6A, 6B, and S3A). The number of sites exceeding the threshold was 960 and 92 for the cleaved and non-cleaved data, respectively. Although this non-cleaved/cleaved ratio used for threshold determination does not directly reflect the false-positive rate (Figure S3B), increasing the non-cleaved/cleaved ratio leads to higher sensitivity, and decreasing this ratio leads to higher accuracy (Figure S3A).

We analyzed the sites detected using Digenome-Detect based on the distance, similarly to those detected using Digenome-toolkit. We checked whether the d ≤ 6 sites containing a canonical PAM were detected as cleaved. We found that 100% of the d = 1 and d = 3 sites, 38.6% of the d = 4 sites, 5.5% of the d = 5 sites, and 0.36% of the d = 6 sites were detected as cleaved (no d = 2 sequences are present in GRCh38). Similar to the results obtained with Digenome-toolkit (Figure 2B), a smaller distance was associated with a higher percentage of detected cleaved sites (Figure 6C). In addition, when we further categorized detected cleaved sites by the presence of insertions or deletions (indels) relative to the gRNA sequence, sites without indels were more likely to be identified than sites with the same distance that contained indels (Figure S4). This result is consistent with the previous finding that mismatches are more tolerable than DNA/RNA bulges for Cas9.17 The percentage of detected sites with d ≥ 7 was 32%, which was less than that obtained with Digenome-toolkit (47%; Figure 2C). In addition, 78% of the d ≥ 7 sites detected using Digenome-Detect contained canonical PAM (Figure 6D), whereas only 16% of the d ≥ 7 sites detected using Digenome-toolkit contained a canonical PAM (Figure 2C). These results indicate that Digenome-Detect can reduce the number of false positives compared with the Digenome-toolkit.

To examine the impact of sequencing depth on this analysis, we analyzed further downsampled data (50×, 30×, and 10×) generated from our original data. The numbers of cleavage sites identified from the 50×, 30×, and 10× data were approximately 86%, 59%, and 25% of those identified in the original 70× data, respectively (Figures S5A–S7A). The sites identified from both the 70× and downsampled data mainly consisted of sites with d ≤ 6 or a canonical PAM, which are likely true positives (Figures S5B–S7B). The sites that became undetected when using downsampled data also mainly consisted of sites with d ≤ 6 or a canonical PAM (Figures S5C–S7C), indicating a loss of sensitivity at lower sequencing depths. Conversely, the sites newly detected using the downsampled data mainly consisted of d ≥ 7 sites without a canonical PAM, particularly for the 30× and 10× data (Figures S5D–S7D). This result suggests that lower sequencing depth also decreases the accuracy of the assay. When comparing three independently downsampled datasets generated at the same sequencing depth, the number and distance distribution of the identified sites were comparable across the datasets; however, concordance of identified sites across the datasets was greater at higher depth (Figure S8). This result further indicates that higher sequencing depth is preferable for obtaining stable results.

Comparison of Digenome-Detect and Digenome-toolkit

Next, we evaluated the differences in the detected sites between Digenome-toolkit and Digenome-Detect using 70× data (Figure 7A). Both tools commonly identified 223 sites as potential off-target cleavage sites. Digenome-toolkit uniquely identified 187 sites, whereas Digenome-Detect uniquely identified 737 sites.

For each group, we evaluated the number of detected sites and the presence or absence of canonical PAM at each site across various distances. We found that 95% of the sites commonly detected with both methods were d ≤ 6 (Figure 7B). In contrast, approximately 99% of the sites exclusively detected using Digenome-toolkit were d ≥ 7, suggesting that they were false positives (Figure 7C). One d ≤ 6 site, exclusively detected using Digenome-toolkit, was manually examined and considered false positive, probably due to clipping (Figure 7D). Additionally, other d ≤ 9 sites with canonical PAM detected exclusively using Digenome-toolkit were manually checked and deemed false positives (Figure S9). These results suggested that Digenome-Detect could detect the actual cleaved sites that were detected using Digenome-toolkit, with fewer obvious false positives.

Among the sites detected exclusively using Digenome-Detect, 60% had d ≤ 6. Among the sites with d ≥ 7, 78% had a canonical PAM, indicating a greater probability that most of these sites were actually cleaved (Figure 7E). Figures 7F and S2 present a representative IGV image of the sites exclusively detected using Digenome-Detect. At these loci, an accumulation of forward heads and reverse tails was observed, further supporting their cleavage. These results suggest that Digenome-Detect can detect actual cleaved sites with higher sensitivity compared with Digenome-toolkit.

Performance evaluation of Digenome-Detect with publicly available dataset

To evaluate the performance of Digenome-Detect on a different dataset, we analyzed off-target cleavage sites on a publicly available Digenome-seq dataset.

First, we analyzed a Digenome-seq dataset (NCBI: PRJEB20021) generated using a gRNA targeting the HBB locus. For this gRNA, off-target sites detected by orthogonal methods have been registered in the CRISPRoffT database,18 allowing us to compare these sites with those detected by Digenome-Detect. In this analysis, we excluded cleavage sites likely arising from contamination with other gRNAs, as described in the Supplemental Methods (Figures S10A–S10C). Digenome-Detect identified all 33 high-confidence off-target sites detected by two or more orthogonal methods, including all 4 sites identified by targeted deep sequencing (Figure S10D). These results indicate that Digenome-Detect reliably detects genuine off-target cleavage sites.

We next analyzed an additional Digenome-seq dataset (NCBI: SRX2487860). The dataset was obtained using ARPE-19 cells and a gRNA targeting the VEGFA locus, and 42 potential off-target sites were identified in the original study.19 The CL-score threshold (7.32) was determined by the same method applied to our dataset. Digenome-Detect identified 8,868 sites from the same dataset, including all 42 sites reported in the original article. The minimum CL-score among these 42 sites was 141.68, indicating that Digenome-Detect identified the previously reported off-target sites with high confidence. Even when applying the highest threshold that recovers all the reported sites (>141.67), Digenome-Detect identified 153 additional sites besides the reported sites. The number of identified sites was approximately 9 times greater than that in our dataset, potentially due to lower gRNA specificity and/or higher cleavage activity. Of the sites identified by Digenome-Detect, approximately 46% were d ≤ 6 sites, and 77% of the d ≥ 7 sites had a canonical PAM, suggesting that the majority of these sites were likely true positives (Figure 8).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a software tool, Digenome-Detect, for analyzing Digenome-seq data. The software introduces a more reliable score and mechanism for reducing false positives in the identification of cleaved sites. Our results showed that Digenome-Detect could detect cleaved sites with fewer false positives than Digenome-toolkit. In addition, the sensitivity of Digenome-Detect for detection of cleaved sites was higher than that of Digenome-toolkit.

We demonstrated the performance of Digenome-Detect using our own dataset and two publicly available datasets, each employing a different gRNA. Digenome-Detect is based on an algorithm that does not rely on information about the recognition sequence of genome-editing tools. Thus, it is potentially applicable to the detection of off-target cleavage induced by different gRNAs or other genome-editing tools, such as different Cas variants, TALEN, and ZFN. However, its applicability to each of these tools needs to be validated using experimental data.

The core algorithm for CL-score calculation relies on the likelihood of the number of forward heads or reverse tails accumulating at a specific position. This statistical model is based on a whole-genome sequencing (WGS) context, wherein the true signal (accumulated read ends at cleavage sites) is evaluated against background noise arising from the randomly distributed read ends of non-cleaved fragments. Thus, Digenome-Detect is specifically designed for WGS-based assays and cannot be directly applied to target-enrichment-based assays, such as SITE-seq, CIRCLE-seq, and GUIDE-seq.

For accuracy purposes, clipped reads are excluded from the CL-score calculation in Digenome-Detect. As a consequence, Digenome-Detect calculates a low CL-score for the false-positive sites detected using Digenome-toolkit, thereby removing these false positives. In addition, we introduced a filtering process to Digenome-Detect to further reduce false positives based on the clipped reads and reads that were not properly mapped to the reference genome. This filtering step is crucial for reducing false positives resulting from improper mapping, which often occurs in repeated sequences. Digenome-toolkit also removes this type of false positives using a filtering process. This process filters out sites when there are fewer than five forward heads and five reverse tails and when the number of forward heads and reverse tails is less than 20% of the depth at the position (default settings; parameters can be changed by users). However, this limits the sensitivity of the analysis, because it filters out sites where a small number of forward heads and reverse tails accumulate. Thus, we implemented an alternative algorithm in Digenome-Detect that uses the number of reads with a mapping quality of 0 and clipped reads at the site to remove these false positives while maintaining sensitivity.

To further reduce the false-positive rate, one possible approach is to select sites that are consistently detected across multiple replicates. Digenome-Detect was intentionally designed as a scoring engine for individual datasets, prioritizing flexibility for user-defined downstream analysis. Thus, depending on the purpose of the study, replicate-based analysis can be performed following independent analysis with Digenome-Detect. Another strategy used to reduce false positives in cell-free assays is sequence-based filtering, which excludes potential false-positive sites based on a gRNA and PAM sequence (e.g., filtering out d > 6 sites).7,8,20 However, this filtering restricts detection of sites that satisfy a prespecified sequence criterion as potential off-target cleavage sites, thereby preventing the assay from achieving true genome-wide coverage. Digenome-Detect reduces false positives without relying on sequence-based filtering, thereby enabling a genuinely genome-wide assay capable of detecting off-target cleavage sites across the entire genome.

The background noise reduction achieved by features implemented in Digenome-Detect contributes to not only higher accuracy but also higher sensitivity in the analysis. Although it is impossible to confirm whether all the detected sites were actual cleaved sites, many sites detected using Digenome-Detect had d ≤ 6 and contained canonical PAM, indicating that they are likely to be cleaved. Moreover, most of the d ≥ 7 sites detected using Digenome-Detect are likely to be true positive sites, as most of them had canonical PAM, and the accumulation of forward heads and reverse tails was observed at the selected sites that were manually confirmed. To distinguish true positives from false positives with greater confidence, comprehensive experimental validation using multiple orthogonal methods, such as targeted deep sequencing, GUIDE-seq, and CIRCLE-seq, would be beneficial.

The analysis using downsampled datasets generated from our original dataset showed that sequencing depth is an important factor for assay performance. Sensitivity, accuracy, and stability of the results appeared to decrease at lower sequencing depths (Figures S5–S8). Although the software sets no minimum sequencing-depth requirement, we recommend maximizing sequencing depth within practical constraints for sensitive and accurate identification of off-target cleavage sites, particularly for applications requiring rigorous off-target site prediction, such as safety evaluation of genome-editing therapeutics.

In this study, the CL-score threshold was determined by comparing the non-cleaved and cleaved samples (Figures 6A and S3). Although the threshold value itself varied across the datasets, this method consistently selected a threshold that retained likely true positives without an excessive number of obvious false positives. Nevertheless, sites detected using Digenome-Detect may contain some false positives. Even with the 70× data, Digenome-Detect did identify some d ≥ 10 sites lacking canonical PAM, which are potentially false positives. These potential false positives can be reduced by applying a higher CL-score threshold (e.g., blue line in Figures 6A and 6B). However, increasing the threshold value inevitably decreases sensitivity to detect true off-target cleavage sites. Given this trade-off between sensitivity and accuracy, the optimal threshold setting may vary depending on the objective of analysis. The setting used in this study was chosen with the safety evaluation of genome-editing therapeutics in mind, prioritizing sensitivity over accuracy to avoid overlooking genuine off-target mutation sites; however, users may freely apply alternative threshold settings.

In conclusion, our results suggest that Digenome-Detect enables highly sensitive, accurate detection of off-target cleavage sites in genome editing from Digenome-seq data. As mentioned in the introduction, cell-free assays are generally more sensitive and comprehensive than cell-based assays. Among cell-free assays, Digenome-seq is the most straightforward method utilizing standard PCR-free WGS, which is the least labor-intensive and, more importantly, intrinsically minimizes potential biases from processes such as PCR. Together, these attributes suggest that the Digenome-seq assay coupled with Digenome-Detect offers a sensitive, accurate, and methodologically less biased approach for off-target site prediction. Its sensitivity and accuracy are particularly advantageous for the safety evaluation of genome-editing therapeutics, where precise prediction and risk assessment of off-target mutations are essential to ensuring patient safety.

Materials and methods

Reagents

The following reagents were used in this study: Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) (#12440053; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), penicillin-streptomycin (#15140122; Thermo Fisher Scientific), Trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) and phenol red (#25200056; Thermo Fisher Scientific), Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (#14249-24; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), Fetal bovine serum (#172012-500ML; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES, Tokyo, Japan), NucleoBond HMW DNA (#740160.20; MACHEREY-NAGEL, Dueren, Germany), Guide-it sgRNA In Vitro Transcription Kit (#632635; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), EnGen Spy Cas9 NLS (#M0646M; New England Biolabs, MA, USA), NucleoSpin Tissue (#740952.50; MACHEREY-NAGEL), RNase A (#740505; MACHEREY-NAGEL), proteinase K (#740506; MACHEREY-NAGEL), and NucleoSpin gDNA clean-up (#740230.50; MACHEREY-NAGEL).

Biological resources

The human near-haploid cell line HAP1 (#C859; Horizon Discovery, Cambridge, UK) was used for in vitro experiments.21

Novel programs, software, and algorithms

Digenome-Detect and related software developed in this study are freely available on GitHub (https://github.com/nihsmtgt/Digenome-Detect) and Zenodo (Zenodo: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20637152).

Web sites/database referencing

The following resources were used in this study: GGGenome (https://GGGenome.dbcls.jp/),22 Digenome-toolkit,9 bwa 0.7.15,23 SAMtools,24 GATK 3.8,25 IGV 2.14.1,26 and R 4.6.0 (https://www.r-project.org/).

Cell culture and preparation of extracted genomic DNA

HAP1 cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere in IMDM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 50 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Genomic DNA was extracted from HAP1 cells suspended with trypsin using the NucleoBond HMW DNA kit. Briefly, the cells were lysed using a lysis buffer containing proteinase K, and genomic DNA was purified from the cell lysates using a NucleoBond HMW column after RNase A treatment. The DNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop 2000C (#ND-2000C; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Design and preparation of gRNA

A gRNA was designed for chr3:113566476–113566498 (GRCh38; 5′-CCTTGTTGTGGTTCGCCAGCAGA-3′) and prepared as a single gRNA using the Guide-it sgRNA In Vitro Transcription Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The transcription reaction (100 μL per tube) was performed at 37°C for 10 h to obtain substantial amounts of gRNA. Besides the sequence complementary to the target sequence, the gRNA was synthesized with a G added to the 5′-end because G is recommended as the base for transcription initiation by the T7 RNA polymerase.27,28

Digenome-seq experiment

Digenome-seq experiment was performed as previously described.15 First, 31.25 μL of gRNA (200 ng/μL) was mixed with 5.25 μL of EnGen Spy Cas9 NLS (20 μM) and incubated at 37°C for 15 min to obtain the Cas9-gRNA complex (RNP). To the RNPs, 21.5 μL of NEBuffer 3.1, 107 μL of water, and 50 μL of HAP1 genomic DNA (200 ng/μL) were added and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 5 h. Thereafter, RNase (50 μg/mL RNase A for 30 min at 37°C) and proteinase (2.3 mg/mL proteinase K for 10 min at 70°C) treatments were performed to complete the reaction. DNA was purified using NucleoSpin gDNA clean-up. In addition to the RNP-treated samples (cleaved samples), untreated genomic DNA samples (non-cleaved samples) were subjected to high-throughput sequencing by Rhelixa (Tokyo, Japan). Genomic DNA was fragmented via sonication, targeting a mean fragment size of 350 bp. Library was prepared following a PCR-free protocol using the NEBNext DNA Library Prep Kit, and sequencing analysis was performed with Novaseq 6000 to obtain 150 bp, paired-end reads.

Preprocessing of sequencing data

Sequencing data were mapped to a reference genome (GRCh38) together with the template DNA sequence using bwa-mem. Duplicate reads in the sequencing data were removed using Picard Tools. The sequencing data (approximately 100× depth) were resampled to 70× using SAMtools to equalize the depth of the data from the cleaved and non-cleaved samples.

Digenome-seq data analysis using Digenome-toolkit

Digenome-toolkit, the Digenome-toolkit2 software available on GitHub (https://github.com/chizksh/Digenome-toolkit2) was used for analyzing Digenome-seq data. Calculations were performed using the default threshold value (cleavage score >2.5). The GATKQueue and Sun Grid Engine were used for parallel analysis to reduce the calculation time. Detection sites located within 10 bases were merged into the site with the highest score to remove duplicate detections of the same cleavage site. In addition, the results of the cleaved sample were compared with those of the non-cleaved sample, and if a detection site was identified in the non-cleaved sample, it was considered an artifact and hence removed.

Digenome-seq data analysis using Digenome-detect

For Digenome-seq data analysis using Digenome-Detect, we used the same preprocessed sequencing data analyzed using Digenome-toolkit. Briefly, CL-scores were calculated for genomic positions with four or more 5′-ends of the forward read (forward heads) or 3′-ends of the reverse read (reverse tails). Details of the score calculation are described in the Supplemental Methods. Thereafter, sites with CL-score >7 were extracted. As in the Digenome-toolkit analysis, duplicate detections were removed by merging the detection sites located within 10 bases. Sites detected in the non-cleaved samples located within 10 bases were also removed. Each site was further filtered when the total number of clipped reads, which included sequences that could not be mapped to the reference genome, and reads with a mapping quality of zero was higher than the number of forward heads or reverse tails. The threshold value was determined as the minimum CL-score at which the ratio of the cumulative number of sites with a CL-score greater than or equal to this value in the non-cleaved sample to that in cleaved sample was ≤0.1. Sites with a CL-score greater than the threshold were identified as cleaved sites.

Evaluation of detection results obtained using the different analysis software

To evaluate the sequence similarity of the identified off-target cleavage sites, we used a metric referred to as distance (d), which is defined as the total number of mismatches, insertions (RNA bulges), or deletions (DNA bulges) between the genomic DNA and gRNA sequences (Figure 2A).16 The d ≤ 6 sites on GRCh38 were searched using the GGGenome software, which can comprehensively search for short sequences without missing any sites.16,21 The sites containing canonical PAM (5′-NGG-3′) were marked as canonical PAM (+). For d ≥ 7 sites, 30-nucleotide sequences upstream and downstream of each identified off-target cleavage site were obtained, and pairwise alignment with the gRNA sequence was performed to determine the distance and the presence of a canonical PAM. The distances between random sequences and the gRNA sequence were calculated in the same manner. Random sequences were generated computationally by combining 60 random bases (A, T, G, and C).

Analysis of downsampled data

The datasets were generated from our sequencing data by downsampling using SAMtools. The seed values used to generate each dataset were 3, 7, and 11 for the 50× datasets; 13, 17, and 19 for the 30× datasets; and 23, 27, and 33 for the 10× datasets. The data were analyzed using Digenome-Detect in the same manner as described earlier.

Re-analysis of publicly available data

The HBB dataset was obtained from the SRA (NCBI: PRJEB20021) and analyzed using Digenome-Detect as described earlier. Cleavage sites attributable to contamination with gRNAs were excluded, and the remaining sites were compared with off-target sites registered in the CRISPRoffT database, as described in the Supplemental Methods. The VEGFA dataset was also obtained from the SRA (NCBI: SRX2487860) and analyzed using Digenome-Detect as described earlier.
