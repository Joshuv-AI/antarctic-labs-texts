Imaging Strategies for Pediatric Neck Lesions: A Contemporary Review
Authors: Ji Eun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric neck lesions comprise a heterogeneous spectrum of congenital, inflammatory, vascular, and neoplastic conditions that often present overlapping clinical features. Because physical examination alone is frequently insufficient for an accurate diagnosis, imaging plays a crucial role in lesion characterization, differential diagnosis, treatment planning, and follow-up. US is generally the initial imaging modality owing to its accessibility and the absence of ionizing radiation, whereas CT and MRI provide complementary information regarding lesion extent, tissue composition, and anatomical relationships. This review summarizes current imaging strategies and characteristic imaging findings for common pediatric neck lesions.Keywords: Pediatric Neck Lesion, Pediatric Neck Mass

INTRODUCTION

Neck masses are commonly encountered in pediatric clinical practice and encompass a wide spectrum of pathological entities, including congenital anomalies, inflammatory conditions, vascular malformations, and neoplasms (1,2). The pediatric neck contains complex anatomical structures arising during embryological development that often contribute to diagnostic challenges. Clinical evaluation alone frequently cannot determine the exact origin or nature of a lesion, particularly when lesions involve deep cervical spaces or present with nonspecific symptoms.

Therefore, radiological imaging plays a central role in evaluating pediatric neck lesions. Imaging not only assists in distinguishing benign from malignant conditions, but also provides essential information regarding lesion location, internal characteristics, and relationships to adjacent vital structures (1,3). This information is critical for determining management strategies including observation, medical therapy, surgical intervention, and oncological treatment.

Ultrasonography is generally the initial imaging modality of choice after physical examination. High-resolution ultrasonography is particularly useful for characterizing superficial lesions, including internal septations and cystic or solid components. CT is often required in acute clinical settings, such as in cases of suspected infection, trauma, or compromised airway, and is useful for evaluating deep neck spaces. MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast for complex or deep neck masses and is essential for local staging of malignant tumors. In selected pediatric cases of branchial apparatus anomalies, fluoroscopy-guided esophagography or sinography may be used to detect sinuses or fistulous tracts. Radiography is also valuable when thyroid gland abnormalities, including ectopic thyroid tissue and thyroglossal duct cysts, are suspected (3,4,5,6).

CONGENITAL NECK LESIONS

Congenital neck lesions represent a substantial proportion of pediatric cervical masses and are usually associated with embryonic remnants or developmental anomalies (7).

Thyroglossal duct cysts are the most common congenital midline neck masses that arise from the incomplete involution of the thyroglossal duct during thyroid gland descent. Imaging typically reveals a well-defined cystic lesion located along the midline or paramedian neck within the strap muscle (Fig. 1). On ultrasonography, these cysts generally appear as anechoic or hypoechoic lesions with posterior acoustic enhancement. Internal debris or wall thickening may also be observed in cases of secondary infection (Fig. 2). The internal contents of cysts can vary depending on the presence of inflammation, associated ectopic thyroid tissue, or malignancy. CT and MRI provide additional anatomical delineation and demonstrate characteristic fluid signal intensities with variable protein contents (7).

Dermoid cysts are developmental inclusions observed on ultrasonography and CT (Fig. 3). Dermoid cysts may contain fat, calcifications, or sebaceous material (7,8).

C-E. CT images demonstrate a non-enhancing cystic mass in the midline neck (arrows) at the infrahyoid level.

Branchial apparatus anomalies constitute another major category of congenital neck lesions that result from incomplete obliteration of branchial apparatus components during embryological development (8). Secondary branchial cleft cysts are the most common and are typically located along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, lateral to the carotid space, and posterior to the submandibular gland. First branchial cleft cysts are less common and typically located near the parotid gland or external auditory canal (Fig. 4), whereas third and fourth branchial cleft cysts (Fig. 5) are rare and often located in the lower lateral neck (8).

Ranulas are mucous retention cysts that originate from the sublingual gland and can be classified as simple or plunging cysts. MRI is particularly useful in evaluating the extent of such lesions (Fig. 6) (1).

VASCULAR LESIONS

Vascular anomalies represent another important group of pediatric neck lesions, including vascular tumors and malformations (9).

Infantile hemangiomas are the most common vascular tumors in children and have a characteristic natural history consisting of a proliferative phase followed by gradual involution (9). Imaging during the proliferative phase typically reveals a hypervascular soft tissue mass, and ultrasonography with Doppler evaluation is often sufficient for diagnosis (Fig. 7) (3). Doppler US is particularly useful in the evaluation of pediatric vascular lesions because it enables the simultaneous assessment of lesion morphology and vascular hemodynamics. Infantile hemangiomas typically appear as well-defined hypervascular soft-tissue masses, demonstrating abundant internal blood flow on color Doppler imaging, particularly during the proliferative phase (3,9).

A. US image demonstrates a heterogeneous hypoechoic mass with increased vascularity in the left lateral neck.

B-D. Contrast-enhanced CT images demonstrate a well-defined lobulated hypervascular mass in the left lower lateral neck.

Venous malformations are slow-flow vascular anomalies resulting from dysplastic venous channels. MRI typically demonstrates lobulated masses with high T2 signal intensity and gradual contrast enhancement (Fig. 8), and the presence of phleboliths can serve as a helpful diagnostic feature (10).

D. T1-weighted MR image demonstrates low signal intensity.

Lymphatic malformations consist of abnormal lymphatic channels that form cystic spaces of varying sizes. Imaging often demonstrates multiloculated cystic masses with fluid-fluid levels and minimal internal enhancement, except along the septa (Fig. 9) (11). Large lesions may compromise the airway, highlighting the importance of imaging in treatment planning.

C, D. T1-weighted and contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MR images demonstrate a multiseptated cystic lesion with fluid–fluid levels and peripheral septal enhancement.

BENIGN CONDITIONS

Fibromatosis colli is a distinctive benign condition characterized by fibrous proliferation of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and is typically associated with birth trauma or difficult delivery. Ultrasonography typically demonstrates fusiform enlargement of the sternocleidomastoid muscle with a preserved fibrillar architecture (Fig. 10) (1). This condition typically resolves spontaneously with conservative treatment.

MALIGNANT PEDIATRIC NECK TUMORS

Although most pediatric neck masses are benign, malignant etiologies must always be considered in the differential diagnosis (2).

Lymphoma is the most common malignant neck mass in children and typically presents as a painless lymphadenopathy. Imaging often reveals multiple enlarged lymph nodes lacking a fatty hilum, sometimes forming nodal conglomerates (12). Cross-sectional imaging is essential for the staging and assessment of mediastinal involvement.

Neuroblastoma may present as a primary cervical mass or as a metastasis. Imaging findings typically include enhancing soft-tissue masses that may contain calcifications. Iodine-123 metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) scintigraphy and whole-body MRI may help to confirm the diagnosis and staging of tumors (13).

Rhabdomyosarcoma is the most common soft tissue sarcoma (and the second most common malignant tumor) of the pediatric head and neck region. MRI is generally preferred for evaluating tumor extent and involvement of adjacent structures (12).

DISCUSSION

A systematic imaging approach is essential for accurate evaluation of pediatric neck lesions. US is generally recommended as the initial imaging modality because of its safety, accessibility, and ability to differentiate cystic from solid lesions while assessing vascularity in real-time (3,5). However, when lesions are deep, complex, or suspicious for malignancy, cross-sectional imaging is required.

MRI is increasingly favored over CT in pediatric patients because it avoids ionizing radiation and provides superior soft tissue contrast (4,6). CT remains useful in emergency situations for the evaluation of calcifications or when rapid imaging is required.

Multidisciplinary collaboration between radiologists, pediatricians, surgeons, and oncologists is essential for optimal patient management. Advances in imaging technology may further improve the diagnostic accuracy of pediatric neck lesions (1).

Standardized imaging protocols remain an area of future research. Minimizing radiation exposure while maintaining diagnostic accuracy is particularly important in pediatric imaging (6).

CONCLUSION

Pediatric neck lesions include diverse pathological entities that often present diagnostic challenges. Imaging plays a central role in differentiating congenital, vascular, benign, and malignant conditions. Ultrasonography remains the primary imaging modality, while CT and MRI provide complementary information for complex cases. A systematic imaging approach improves diagnostic confidence and supports appropriate clinical management, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes.
