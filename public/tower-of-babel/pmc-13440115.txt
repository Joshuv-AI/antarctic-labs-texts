Age-stratified associations of glycemia, blood pressure, and cholesterol with mortality in diabetes: A prospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimization of HbA1c, blood pressure and cholesterol, referred to as the “ABCs”, is central to the management of diabetes. However, the age-specific associations of these factors with mortality in patients with diabetes remains unclear.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 43,732 Chinese adults aged ≥ 40 years with diabetes were included from the China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study. Participants were stratified by age (< 55, 55-<65, 65-<75, ≥ 75 years). Cox proportional hazards regression and Fine-Gray competing risk models were employed to estimate the associations of HbA1c, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) with all-cause, cardiovascular, and non-cardiovascular mortality across age groups. Relative importance and population attributable fractions (PAFs) were computed for each metabolic factor.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 10.1 years, 3,975 deaths were documented. Age significantly modified the associations of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C with all mortality outcomes (all P for interaction < 0.05). Among participants aged < 75 years, HbA1c showed graded positive associations with all-cause, cardiovascular, and non-cardiovascular mortality. The SBP thresholds associated with increased mortality risk were 140 mmHg in those aged < 65 years and 160 mmHg in those aged 65–<75 years. Among those aged ≥ 75 years, however, the patterns of these associations differed markedly. Elevated mortality risk was observed only at HbA1c ≥ 9%, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.51 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.19–1.91) for all-cause mortality and a subdistribution hazard ratio (SHR) of 1.70 (95% CI: 1.23–2.36) for cardiovascular mortality, while SBP showed no significant association with any mortality outcome in this age group. Moreover, LDL-C emerged as a significant risk factor for cardiovascular mortality. Compared with participants with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L, those with LDL-C of 1.8–<2.6 mmol/L exhibited a significantly higher risk (SHR: 1.86; 95% CI: 1.11–3.11). Additionally, LDL-C had the largest PAF for cardiovascular mortality (9.6%) within this age group.ConclusionsThe impacts of ABC factors on mortality risk vary substantially by age among adults with diabetes. In patients aged ≥ 75 years, less stringent glycemic and blood pressure targets may be appropriate, whereas lipid management remains critically important for reducing cardiovascular mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04946-1.Keywords: HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, elderly, mortality

Background

The global prevalence of diabetes continues to rise, posing a major public health challenge. It was estimated that over 828 million adults worldwide had diabetes in 2022, with the number projected to exceed 1.31 billion by 2050 [1]. To address the multifaceted burden of diabetes, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) introduced the concept of “Diabetes ABCs”, emphasizing integrated management of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), blood pressure (BP), and cholesterol [2]. Recent large-scale studies have demonstrated that comprehensive achievement of the ABC targets can substantially mitigate diabetes-related excess mortality risk [3, 4].

It is increasingly recognized that the optimal management for ABC parameters may differ across age groups, underscoring the need for age-specific targets. Age-specific HbA1c targets, for instance, have been incorporated into current guidelines. It is recommended that a less stringent goal for HbA1c (for example, 8%-9%) is more appropriate and safer for older adults [5, 6]. In contrast, evidence regarding age-tailored systolic blood pressure (SBP) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) targets in diabetes remains limited, especially for the oldest-old (≥ 75 years old) [7, 8]. Furthermore, the relative importance of individual ABC component may also shift with advancing age [9]. Despite these knowledge gaps, a systematic and age-stratified evaluation regarding the contributions of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C to mortality risk is lacking.

Therefore, leveraging the nationwide, prospective China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study, we aim to conduct an age-stratified analysis to investigate the associations of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C with all-cause, cardiovascular, and non-cardiovascular mortality among adults with diabetes. By including a substantial proportion of elderly participants, this study is intended to elucidate age-specific risk patterns and quantify the population-level contribution of each ABC factor. These findings help provide empirical evidence to inform more precise, age-appropriate ABC management strategies for adult with diabetes.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted based on China Cardiometabolic Disease and Cancer Cohort (4C) Study, a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort study covering 20 communities across China [10]. From 2011 to 2012, eligible participants aged ≥ 40 years old were identified from local registration systems. Trained community health workers then visited their homes and invited them to participate in the study. A total of 204,467 individuals agreed to participate and signed informed consent forms, and were scheduled for a personal interview and clinic visit within a week after the enrollment. After excluding 10,621 (5.2%) individuals who did not meet age eligibility criteria or withdrew their consents, 193,846 individuals were recruited at baseline. These participants were followed up till November 30, 2021.

The study was approved by the Committee on Human Research at Ruijin Hospital, affiliated to Shanghai JiaoTong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China. All participants have provided written informed consents.

Data collection

Baseline assessments were conducted at local community clinics in morning. Anthropometric measurements were taken by trained nurses according to standard procedures. Participants were required to avoid alcohol, cigarettes, coffee/tea or exercise for ≥ 30 min before the measurements. Blood pressure was measured using an automated monitor (model HEM-752 FUZZY; OMRON, Dalian, China) in seated positions after 5 min of quiet rest, with three consecutive readings obtained.

Blood samples were obtained for biomedical measurements after at least 8 h of fasting. All participants underwent an oral glucose tolerance test, and plasma was collected at 0 and 2 h during the test. Plasma glucose concentrations were analyzed locally using the glucose oxidase or hexokinase method, within 2 h after sample collection under a stringent quality control protocol. Finger capillary whole-blood samples were collected using the Hemoglobin Capillary Collection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and stored at 2 °C to 8 °C. HbA1c was measured within next 4 weeks by high-performance liquid chromatography (VARIANT II Hemoglobin Testing System, Bio-Rad Laboratories) at Shanghai Institute of Endocrine and Metabolic Diseases central laboratory, which is certified by the National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program and the College of American Pathologists Laboratory Accreditation Program. Blood lipids were analyzed at the same central laboratory (ARCHITECT ci16200 autoanalyzer, Abbott Laboratories, Abbott Park, IL).

Personal interviews were conducted by trained staff using structured questionnaires. Information on medical histories, lifestyle factors and socioeconomic status were captured through the questionnaires. Smoking and drinking status were dichotomized as current smokers or drinkers (yes/no). Educational attainment was classified as high school education or above (yes/no). Medication usage for diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia was verified based on medical records documenting relevant treatments in the past two weeks. Ideal physical activity was defined as moderate-intensity exercise of ≥ 150 min/week or vigorous-intensity exercise of ≥ 75 min/week, or an equivalent combination [11]. Dietary intake was recorded over the previous 12 months using a validated food frequency questionnaire, and evaluated according to the recommendation of the American Heart Association [12].

Definition of exposures and outcomes

Diabetes at baseline was diagnosed according to the ADA criteria [13], namely, fasting plasma glucose (FPG) concentration of 7.0 mmol/L or above, and/or 2-hour plasma glucose (2hPG) concentration of 11.1 mmol/L or above, and/or HbA1c of 6.5% or above, and/or a self-reported previous diagnosis by healthcare professionals or use of glucose-lowering medication. Previously-diagnosed diabetes was defined by self-report diagnosis or current use of glucose-lowering medication, whereas newly-diagnosed diabetes was defined solely by biochemical criteria (elevated FPG, 2hPG, or HbA1c) in participants without a prior diagnosis.

The primary outcome of the study was all-cause mortality during follow-up, and secondary outcomes were cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality. Deaths were ascertained by death certificates from the local death registries of the National Disease Surveillance Point System, based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes. Adjudications for cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular mortality were performed by the investigators from the adjudication committee of the 4C study. Each death was independently adjudicated by two members of the committee based on medical records and pathology reports. All discrepancies were resolved through committee discussion to reach a final determination. All committee members were blinded to the baseline characteristics of the study participants.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviations for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. One-way ANOVA was used to compare baseline variables across age groups.

Participants were stratified into four age groups: young (< 55 years), middle-aged (55–<65 years), old (65–<75 years), and elderly (≥ 75 years), in line with previous studies [9, 14], and all primary analyses were conducted separately within each group. Given the competing nature of cause-specific mortality, we used Fine-Gray competing risk models to estimate subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cause-specific mortalities, while Cox proportional hazards models were used for all-cause mortality. Time-to-event was calculated as the duration from baseline survey to the occurrence of death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study (2021), whichever came first. Covariates were selected a priori based on their established associations with both the exposures of interest (HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C) and mortality outcomes, as documented in previous literature [15, 16]. These relationships were mapped into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Additional File 1: Fig. S1). Specifically, models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, body mass index, current smoker (yes/no), current drinker (yes/no), high school education or above (yes/no), ideal physical activity (yes/no), healthy diet (yes/no), glucose-lowering medication use (yes/no), lipid-lowering medication use (yes/no), antihypertensive medication use (yes/no), and prevalent cardiovascular disease at baseline. In analyses examining individual ABC factors, the three components were mutually adjusted.

Associations between ABC factors and mortality were evaluated using categorical, continuous, and binary parameterizations. For categorical analyses, each factor was divided into four pre-defined strata in line with previous studies [17]: HbA1c (< 7%, 7–<8%, 8–<9% and ≥ 9%), SBP (< 120, 120–<140, 140–<160 and ≥ 160 mmHg), and LDL-C (< 1.8, 1.8–<2.6, 2.6–<3.4 and ≥ 3.4 mmol/L). To characterize the dose-response relationships, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were fitted with three knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, using guideline-recommended targets as reference values (7.0% for HbA1c, 130 mmHg for SBP, and 2.6 mmol/L for LDL-C) [6, 7]. Each ABC factor was further dichotomized as “target” or “non-target” based on the same thresholds. Cumulative metabolic risk was then assessed by summing the number of non-target factors per participant (range 0–3), and mortality risk was estimated for each level compared with those achieving all three targets.

To assess whether the associations of ABC factors with mortality were modified by age, multiplicative terms between predefined age groups and each categorized metabolic parameter were incorporated into the fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards and Fine-Gray competing risk models. The joint statistical significance of these interaction terms was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests, comparing models with and without the interaction components. P values < 0.05 were considered indicative of a statistically significant interaction [18].

The relative contributions of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C to mortality were further calculated using relative importance analysis, as proposed by Heller G [19]. This approach quantifies unique contribution of each predictor to the explained variance while accounting for other predictors. Analyses were conducted separately for each age group and mortality outcome.

We also used population attributable fraction (PAF) analysis to estimate the proportion of mortality cases that hypothetically would have been prevented, if all participants had achieved target ABC levels. Average PAFs for HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C were derived using the method introduced by Eide and Gefeller via the R package “averisk” [20]. Average PAFs for ABC factors were mutually adjusted using logistic regression models, with covariates adjusted as aforementioned. PAF analyses were performed separately for each age group and mortality outcome. According to prior studies, negative estimates were truncated to zero [21].

The robustness of the primary results was assessed through several sensitivity analyses. Specifically, the main analyses were repeated after excluding deaths occurring within the first two years of follow-up, restricting the sample to participants not receiving glucose-, blood pressure-, or lipid-lowering treatments at baseline, adjusting for baseline cancer history and further stratifying the cohort into newly and previously-diagnosed diabetes subgroups. As a supplementary analysis, cancer mortality was modeled as a specific outcome using a competing risk framework. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics according to age groups

For the current analysis, 46,571 participants with diagnosed diabetes were identified. After excluding those with missing information on HbA1c, SBP or LDL-C (n = 836), or lost to follow-up (n = 1,053), or having a history of cancer at baseline (n = 950), 43,372 eligible participants with diabetes were included in the final analysis. Flowchart of the study design is shown in Additional File 1: Fig. S2.

Baseline characteristics of the participants stratified by age groups are summarized in Table 1. With advancing age, the proportions of current smokers, current drinkers, individuals with high school education or above, those engaging in ideal physical activity, and adhering to a healthy diet all declined progressively. A similar downward trend was observed for BMI. Regarding glycemic parameters, older individuals presented with significantly lower FPG and HbA1c, but slightly higher 2hPG levels. Blood pressure patterns also shifted, marked by a substantial rise in SBP and a corresponding drop in diastolic blood pressure (DBP). Among lipid profiles, triglycerides decreased significantly, whereas total cholesterol, LDL-C, and HDL-C showed no consistently age-related changes. Furthermore, the use of antihypertensive medications scaled upward with aging, while lipid-lowering therapy remained consistently low across the cohort. Finally, baseline cardiovascular disease prevalence surged from 4.3% in the youngest group to 22.4% in the oldest group.

Distribution of ABC levels and control across age groups

The age-specific distributions of ABC parameters are presented in Additional File 1: Fig. S3. Across all age groups, the proportion of participants with HbA1c < 7% remained stable at approximately 60%, and LDL-C levels exhibited similarly modest age-related variation. Conversely, the SBP distribution progressively shifted toward higher values as age progressed; notably, the proportion of participants with SBP < 120 mmHg declined sharply from 24.3% in the youngest cohort (< 55 years) to merely 7.2% in the oldest-old (≥ 75 years).

Additional File 1: Fig. S4 further summarizes the proportion of participants achieving ABC targets in different age groups. While the achievement rates for HbA1c and LDL-C targets remained largely consistent across the four age categories, SBP control worsened substantially with age, falling from 45.6% in the youngest group to 18.4% in the oldest group.

Age-specific association between HbA1c, SBP, LDL-C and mortality risk

Mortality rates and person-years of follow-up by age group are presented in Additional File 1: Table S1, number of deaths and mortality rates stratified by HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C categories are shown in Additional File 1: Tables S2-S4. The mortality rate per 1,000 person-years within each age group was 3.22 for those < 55 years, 6.61 for those 55–<65 years, 16.6 for those 65–<75 years, and 38.3 for those ≥ 75 years.

In the total population, higher HbA1c were associated with progressively increased risk of all-cause mortality (Table 2). The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for HbA1c levels of 7–<8%, 8–<9%, and ≥ 9% in the total population were 1.17 (1.07–1.27), 1.38 (1.23–1.54) and 2.01 (1.83–2.20), respectively, compared to those with HbA1c < 7.0%. In age-stratified analyses, graded associations between HbA1c and mortality were observed in those younger than 75 years old. While for patients aged 75 or older, the risk of all-cause mortality increased significantly only at HbA1c ≥ 9% (HR:1.51, 95% CI: 1.19–1.91).

SBP demonstrated differential associations with mortality outcomes by age as well. In the overall population, mortality risk increased substantially at SBP levels > 140 mmHg for both all-cause (HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.01–1.25) and cardiovascular mortality (SHR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.08–1.57); while the risk of non-cardiovascular mortality increased when SBP exceeding 160 mmHg (SHR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.43). Age-stratified analyses revealed that these associations were most evident among young (< 55 years) and middle-aged (55–<65 years) participants, particularly for cardiovascular mortality. In old participants (65–<75 years), significant risk increases were observed only at SBP ≥ 160 mmHg (all-cause-mortality HR: 1.34, 95% CI: 1.11–1.61; cardiovascular-mortality SHR: 1.58, 95% CI: 1.17–2.14); while in elderly participants (≥ 75 years), SBP showed minimal impact on mortality risk.

Meanwhile, marked age heterogeneity was also observed in the associations between LDL-C and mortality. Among participants aged < 55 years, LDL-C > 3.4 mmol/L was associated with a 38% lower risk of all-cause mortality and potentially decreased risk of cardiovascular mortality. Conversely, within the elderly group (≥ 75 years), higher LDL-C levels showed a non-significant positive trend for all-cause mortality, but significantly and positively associated with cardiovascular mortality, with SHRs (95% CIs) of 1.86 (1.11–3.11), 1.91 (1.15–3.18), and 2.11 (1.28–3.50) for LDL-C levels of 1.8–<2.6, 2.6–<3.4, and ≥ 3.4 mmol/L, respectively. Moreover, higher LDL-C was consistently inversely associated with non-cardiovascular mortality across all age groups. Interaction analyses confirmed significant age-related heterogeneity in the associations between all three ABC factors and mortality risk (all P for interaction < 0.05).

Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings. The observed associations were not materially altered after excluding deaths within the first two years of follow-up, restricting analyses to participants not receiving glucose-lowering, antihypertensive or lipid-lowering therapy at baseline, or additionally adjusting for baseline cancer history (Additional File 1: Tables S5-S7). Stratified analyses separating newly-diagnosed and previously-diagnosed diabetes yielded generally consistent age-specific patterns, although hazard ratios were notably larger among those with newly-diagnosed diabetes (Additional File 1: Tables S8-S9). In the additional analysis, higher LDL-C was associated with a consistent trend toward lower risk of cancer mortality across all age groups (Additional File 1: Table S10).

The distinct nonlinear patterns of the relationships between HbA1c, SBP, LDL-C and mortality are further demonstrated by RCS analyses (Fig. 1). For HbA1c, pronounced J-shaped associations were observed among younger participants (< 55 years), with similar but attenuated patterns in other three age groups (Fig. 1A, B and C). For SBP, mortality risk increased substantially above 140 mmHg among young, middle-aged and old individuals, while the spline curves were approximately flat among elderly participants (≥ 75 years) (Fig. 1D, E and F). Discussion

In this nationwide prospective study of 43,732 Chinese adults with diabetes, we identified significant age-dependent heterogeneity in the association of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C with mortality. Notably, age 75 years emerged as a critical inflection point, beyond which the mortality-related patterns of ABC parameters shifted substantially. These findings highlight the importance of age-stratified risk assessment and underscore the need for age-tailored management of metabolic risk factors in patients with diabetes.

In our cohort, HbA1c and LDL-C target attainment remained relatively stable across age strata, whereas blood pressure control deteriorated progressively with age. These age-related differences in metabolic control have been observed in US population as well [22], and provide important context for understanding the heterogeneous associations between ABC factors and mortality.

Previous epidemiological studies have consistently shown that poor glycemic control, hypertension, and elevated LDL-C are major determinants of cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in adults with diabetes [22]. However, most previous studies enrolled relatively younger populations, without detailed discussion in older adults [15, 23]. As a result, evidence regarding the prognostic relevance of metabolic risk factors in advanced age remains relatively limited.

Several prior studies have reported attenuated or non-linear associations between HbA1c and mortality in older adults, with some suggesting a U-shaped relationship [24–26]. Our findings are broadly consistent with existing literature. Despite that HbA1c showed the highest PAFs to mortality risk, the associations in between were significantly weakened with age. Evidence regarding blood pressure has also been age-dependently heterogeneous. While elevated SBP is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular events in middle-aged adults [27], observational studies in older populations have reported weaker, null, or even inverse associations with mortality [28, 29]. Likewise, our study found that the association between SBP and mortality was no longer statistically significant among those ≥ 75 years.

Among the three metabolic risk factors examined, LDL-C exhibited the most complex age-dependent patterns. Several observational studies have reported the inverse associations between LDL-C and mortality in the general population [30, 31]. However, how LDL-C is related with mortality risk among diabetic patients is less studied [32]. In our study, higher LDL-C was paradoxically associated with lower all-cause and non-cardiovascular mortality among participants aged < 65 years. Conversely, among those aged ≥ 75 years, elevated LDL-C emerged as the dominant contributor to cardiovascular mortality.

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed attenuation of ABC-mortality associations with advancing age. First, individuals surviving to older ages likely represent a more resilient subgroup who have withstood prolonged exposure to metabolic risk factors, a phenomenon commonly referred to as survivor bias [9, 22]. Consequently, those who are most susceptible to the deleterious effects of elevated HbA1c or SBP may have already died before reaching the oldest age group, leading to an attenuation of the observed associations in the remaining cohort. Second, the clinical significance of individual ABC parameters may shift in the context of aging-related physiological changes. For instance, low HbA1c in older adults may reflect frailty and malnutrition rather than optimal glycemic control. Similarly, increased arterial stiffness in advanced age may necessitate relatively higher blood pressures to maintain adequate organ perfusion [33], whereas intensive blood pressure lowering may paradoxically increase the risk of falls and organ hypoperfusion [34].

The inverse association between LDL-C and mortality among younger participants has been reported before, and a plausible explanation is reverse causation [35, 36]. Subclinical malignancy or wasting conditions might precede clinical diagnosis, and the latent period could be up to 18 years according to the Framingham Study [37]. It is partly supported by our supplementary analysis taking cancer mortality as an additional outcome, in which LDL-C was consistently associated with cancer mortality in all age groups. However, we realize this mechanism alone is unlikely to fully explain our findings, as the inverse association persisted among young patients in sensitivity analyses excluding early deaths and adjusting for cancer history. Alternatively, residual confounding by unmeasured factors (e.g., frailty, subclinical infection) cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies with more comprehensive comorbidity data are warranted to further address this issue. In contrast, among those aged ≥ 75 years, elevated LDL-C was positively associated with cardiovascular mortality, consistent with its established atherogenic effects [38]. This finding likely reflects the dominant role of cumulative atherosclerotic burden in this age group.

Our findings are consistent with recent guidelines, emphasizing age-related heterogeneity in the target of metabolic control among patients with diabetes [39, 40]. Moreover, our findings indicate that 75 years of age may represent a clinically relevant threshold for metabolic target individualization. In this oldest group, the attenuated associations of HbA1c and SBP with mortality support consideration of relaxed targets, whereas the substantial cardiovascular mortality burden attributable to LDL-C supports continued active management.

This study has several strengths, including the large nationwide cohort with long-term follow-up, detailed age-stratified analyses, and comprehensive assessment of competing risks. However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. Firstly, as an observational study, residual confounding and reverse causality cannot be fully excluded. Secondly, metabolic parameters were assessed at baseline only, and changes in treatment or risk factor control over time were not captured. Thirdly, information on frailty status and functional measures was unavailable, which may further modify the associations observed. Fourthly, the limited number of mortality events among young participants aged < 55 years may reduce the statistical precision of estimates, and a longer follow-up would be warranted to more robustly characterize mortality risk in younger patients. Finally, participants were recruited through active health screening programs, which may not be fully representative of the broader diabetes population.

Conclusions

Among patients with diabetes, the associations of HbA1c, SBP, and LDL-C with mortality differed across age groups. These findings suggest that age should be considered when assessing mortality risk and individualizing metabolic management in diabetes care.
