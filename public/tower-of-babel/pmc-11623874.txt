Intramuscular Myxoma in the Thenar Eminence: Literature Review and a Rare Case Report
Authors: Salma I. Jarrar, Salim N. Al Lahham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Summary:Intramuscular myxoma (IM) is a rare benign soft-tissue neoplasm that most commonly involves the thigh muscles. It presents as a painless, solitary, slow-growing mass. The definitive diagnosis of IM can only be made by histopathological examination of the lesion. IM of the hand is extremely rare, with less than 5 cases being reported in the thenar eminence. Here we present a 54-year-old woman with a 10-year history of IM in the thenar eminence muscles.

Intramuscular myxoma (IM) is a rare benign soft-tissue neoplasm whose histological features closely resemble those of Wharton jelly found in the mature fetus.1,2 With a reported incidence of 0.1–0.13 per 100,000,3,4 onset is usually between the fourth and seventh decades of life, with the majority being in female patients. The thigh is the most common site of involvement, with approximately half of all incidences occurring in the thigh, followed by the upper arm (9%), calf (7 %), and buttock (7 %).3–6

Previous literature suggests that myxoma is extremely rare in the hand.6 Here we present a rare case of IM in the thenar eminence muscles in a 54-year-old woman of 10 years duration. Our patient first presented to the clinic on August 24, 2023, and her surgery was done on September 8, 2023. She had regular follow-ups postoperatively; the last one was on March 10, 2024.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 54-year-old woman who has type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia presented to the clinic with left thenar eminence swelling of 10 years duration, and left trigger thumb of 6 months duration, the latter was her main complaint which brought her to the clinic. The swelling was painless, asymptomatic, and gradually increasing in size over the last 10 years.

On examination, it was a firm, nontender, well-circumscribed mass fixed to the thenar muscles and occupying the whole thenar eminence with left trigger thumb-grade 3 based on Green classification (Fig. 1).

A preoperative hand photograph showing the thenar eminence swelling.

X-ray was done that revealed no bony involvement in the hand. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was done as well, which showed a cystic thenar mass with internal septations, intra- and intermuscular extension, displacement of the flexor pollicis longus tendon anteriorly, and edematous changes of the thumb suggesting venous and/or lymphatic obstruction caused by mass effect from the lesion. The differential diagnosis included myxomatous lesions (Fig. 2).

A preoperative MRI-T2 coronal view showing the thenar eminence mass abutting the thenar muscles.

Then, the patient underwent an urgent excisional biopsy of the whole mass to rule out malignancy. The incision was done based on the modified Bruner approach, then the neurovascular bundles were identified and preserved. Dissection of the mass was carried down to release it from surrounding structures, and it was removed as one piece. As a final step for completion of the procedure, the A1 pulley of the thumb was released.

The intraoperative gross appearance of the lesion was a white, well-circumscribed, firm, intramuscular, lobulated cystic mass, with gelatinous material. The mass was effacing the thenar eminence muscles, which appeared flat and atrophied, with compressed radial digital artery and nerve. Two stalks have been identified: the first one was attached to the first metacarpal bone and A1 pulley, and the second one was attached to the first carpometacarpal joint (Fig. 3).

An intraoperative picture of the mass after excision.

On the basis of the histopathology report, the gross description is a yellow-white, well-circumscribed mass measuring 4.5 × 3.5 × 3 cm, with a mucinous/gelatinous surface. The diagnosis is IM, 4.5 cm in greatest dimension. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays high magnification histopathologic slide view of the tumor, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D686.) (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the lower magnification histopathologic slide view of the tumor, which shows the outer encapsulation, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D687.)

At 6 months postoperatively, there was no recurrence of the myxoma, or trigger thumb, and the patient regained the normal bulk of thenar muscles, with a full range of motion and intrinsic muscle function (Fig. 4).

Six months postoperative photograph showing no recurrence and fully healed scar.

DISCUSSION

Myxoma is a soft-tissue tumor of undefined origin. One suggestion is that it is a fibroblast that has not differentiated sufficiently to lay down collagen but can produce mucin and hyaluronic acid. Another suggestion is that it is of primitive mesenchymal origin.1

Soft-tissue myxomas are divided into 5 groups: IM, juxta-articular myxoma, superficial angiomyxoma, aggressive angiomyxoma, and nerve sheath myxoma.3,6,7 The most common type of benign myxomas are IMs.3

IMs usually occur in the skeletal muscles of the thigh and gluteal regions8; however, fewer than 10 cases of IM of the hand have been reported.8,9 To the best of our knowledge, fewer than 5 cases of IM of the thenar eminence have been reported.8,9

The clinical presentation is a painless, slow-growing, solitary mass, as in our patient; however, multiple IMs have been associated with fibrous dysplasia as part of Mazabraud syndrome and McCure–Albright syndrome.5,6 Therefore, it has been recommended to routinely examine patients who are diagnosed with an IM, especially multiple lesions, for the presence of fibrous dysplasia.5

An accurate diagnosis before surgery is particularly challenging due to the clinical, radiological, and pathological overlap with malignant tumors, such as low-grade myxofibrosarcoma, or with other benign lesions, such as ganglion cyst and giant cell tumors of tendon sheath, and the small amount of material that is obtainable by preoperative needle biopsy.1

However, preoperative workup may include x-ray, to exclude bony involvement, as we did in our case. Ultrasound examination can show a hypoechoic cystic lesion with well-defined margins, surrounded by normal tissue. Examination with MRI can reveal homogeneous mass with a low-intensity signal on T1-weighted images, and a high-intensity signal on T2-weighted images, as shown in our case (Fig. 2), and sometimes a pseudocapsule.10

Although MRI offers a helpful description, the definitive diagnosis of IM can only be made by histopathological examination of the lesion.5,10 Macroscopically, as described in our case, IMs typically appear as gray-white, gelatinous masses with a firm consistency, extending between the muscle fibers and aponeurotic layers (Fig. 3).1,5,10

Microscopically, IMs appear as a mucoid matrix that is poorly vascularized, containing sparse cellular elements. Fibroblasts can rarely be found at the periphery, as parts of the pseudocapsule, or diffused, as part of fibrous septa. No signs of mitosis should be observed.1,9,10

The definite treatment is surgical excision with clear margins, which was done in our case (See Supplemental Digital Contents 1–2, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D686, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D687),5,10 though conservative treatment can be considered in small lesions, or when vital structures are in danger.10

There is no clear consensus on the proper timing of the surgical intervention. However, in our case, it was done on the next elective list, as malignancy could not be ruled out.

Local recurrence of IMs is rare and is associated with syndromes, such as Mazabraud syndrome; incomplete excision; or in cases of intramuscular low-grade myxoid neoplasms.5,10

CONCLUSIONS

IM is extremely rare in the hand, though it should be kept in the differential diagnosis of any swelling in the hand. It is usually painless; however, it can present with other symptoms, such as triggering, like in our case; rarely, neuropathy; or others. Complete surgical excision is mandatory to avoid recurrence. Definitive diagnosis can only be made by histopathological examination of the lesion.
