Popular digital sovereignty and digital real utopias: Lessons from the Homeless Workers’ Movement in Brazil
Authors: Rafael Grohmann, Alexandre Costa Barbosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article traces the ongoing construction of “popular digital sovereignty” within Brazil’s Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST), through its Tech Sector, arguing that the concept itself operates as a digital real utopia: an aspirational horizon made workable through imperfect and situated practice. Rather than treating digital sovereignty as a state-centric policy project, the movement frames popular digital sovereignty as a from-below definition forged through epistemic sovereignty, the collective control over how knowledge is produced, debated, and circulated inside and beyond the movement. Drawing on a workers’ inquiry approach conducted since 2021, including interviews, observations, workshops, and co-produced materials, the article analyzes how the MTST’s Tech Sector develops this concept through learning in struggle: iterative trial-and-error, learning by doing, and continuous revision in response to the material constraints of organizing. We show how popular digital sovereignty is assembled through territorial and class-based priorities, and how its meaning shifts as the movement grapples with the role of the state, infrastructural dependency, and the need to “meet workers where they are.” The article contributes to digital sovereignty studies and utopian media studies by conceptualizing popular digital sovereignty as an epistemic practice and a prefigurative, unfinished project, an effort to build tech otherwise from below under conditions of constraint.Keywords: digital sovereignty, digital real utopias, Homeless Workers’ movement, Brazil, popular digital sovereignty

Introduction

Founded in 1997, the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST) has emerged in Brazil as one of the world’s most significant urban social movements, particularly in the struggle for housing. In 2020, they 1 decided to create a Tech Sector in response to the emergence of the far right. The Tech Sector is composed of more than 100 tech workers from across Brazil who organize to carry out a range of activities within the social movement, who have more than 20,000 activists across the country. The collective is organizing tech workers and creating technologies for homeless workers. They are developing technologies for homeless workers through the initiative “Hire Those Who Struggle,” advocating for more equitable data protection policies, and producing games, among other activities. Through all of these activities, they advocate for and theorize popular digital sovereignty rooted in the movement’s needs and values, understood as community control over technologies and infrastructures.

The Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement is attempting to develop laboratories (Grohmann, 2025) for the future of the working class while simultaneously confronting the persistent challenges of the present. These laboratories function as worker-led experiments, without ready-made formulas, concerning both technology and organizing, as prefigurative projects (Jeffrey and Dyson 2021; Sandoval 2016), meaning an effort to build tomorrow’s world using the possibilities available in today’s world. However, far from serving as a normative model or a “best practices” case, prefigurative projects like this operate primarily through learning by doing and learning in struggle (Vieta 2014). This pedagogical process informs the movement’s practice.

Prefigurative projects such as the Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement can be understood as an important element of what this special issue refers to as “utopian media studies” because: (1) critical platform, data, and internet studies are currently a central component of media studies, and it is necessary to reflect on and design alternatives to dominant Big Tech and to capitalist realism (Fisher 2009); (2) however, the lessons of the Tech Sector indicate that utopia must be dialectically connected to concrete life, as “real utopias” in Wright’s (2010) sense, so as not to romanticize or crystallize working-class experiences (Grohmann, 2023, 2025a, 2025b), which would hinder their own development; and (3) the Tech Sector is not only developing technologies and organizing workers, which is already significant, but also theorizing its own projects from below, drawing on frameworks that address these real utopias, particularly through the notion of popular digital sovereignty.

This article examines how the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST) in Brazil theorizes, enacts and imagines “popular digital sovereignty,” embedding digital practices in its broader fight for housing, labor rights, and food sovereignty. The article does not address the specificities of the movement’s projects (for that, see MTST 2023, and other forthcoming publications), but focuses on how the movement itself has approached digital sovereignty in its own way, through a learning-by-doing process grounded in what this article calls digital real utopias.

This article joins ongoing conversations about communities controlling technologies and infrastructures through grassroots, bottom-up practices as a form of digital sovereignty, drawing on the singular experience of one of Latin America’s largest social movements. There is no single pathway for a social movement to articulate digital sovereignty in the context of “datafied movements” (Milan and Beraldo, 2024). The case of the Homeless Workers’ Movement adds at least two important contributions to the scholarship: (a) moving beyond normative visions of digital sovereignty, the movement engages with real utopias, balancing ideals of popular digital sovereignty while attempting, through trial and error, to address these issues in the everyday realities of communities; and (b) the very theorization of popular digital sovereignty emerges from the movement itself, also demonstrating epistemic sovereignty, that is, communities’ control over how knowledge is produced and circulated. The Homeless Workers’ Movement is theorizing and practicing technologies from below. The term “from below” refers to an approach that centers inquiry, mobilization, and organization on the perspectives of activists and organizers themselves, incorporating insights from the working class and the circulation of struggles (Mularoni and Dyer-Witheford, 2025). For the movement, this framework is further informed by Brazilian social thought, particularly regarding territory and technology, drawing on Santos (2021), and pedagogy and communication, influenced by Freire (1990).

The article develops a theoretical framework for understanding how the Homeless Workers’ Movement, through its Tech Sector, conceives the relationship between utopias and concrete life. It then presents methodological reflections, both concerning the research method itself and the ways in which this method informs utopian media studies. Methodologically, the paper employs a workers’ inquiry framework (Englert et al., 2020), combining workshops, interviews, and activist-produced materials, including a primer, a manifesto, and comics, developed in collaboration with movement members since 2021. The article then introduces the Movement and discusses the development of the notion of popular digital sovereignty. The article contributes to (utopian) media studies theoretically by centering the notion of popular digital sovereignty as something grounded in communities themselves; methodologically, through the co-creation of media in collaboration with the movement; and empirically, by demonstrating in a nuanced way how real utopias can be addressed by a social movement based in Brazil.

Digital real utopias: Imagining, learning and doing

The epistemological and ontological framework of the Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement is shaped by a permanent tension between, on the one hand, imagining the world of tomorrow by prefiguring alternative digital futures (Benjamin, 2024) and, on the other, the immediate needs of the working class and the temporality of political struggles. These demands impose constraints and urgencies that prevent such utopias from developing in a linear way. For this reason, the theoretical framework of this article is grounded in Erik Olin Wright’s notion of real utopias, combined with the concept of imagination drawn from the Brazilian educator Freire (1992).

Erik Olin Wright’s (2010) concept of real utopias embraces the tensions between dreams and practice as a dialectical way of engaging with technology and politics, as contradictions that persist without resolution (Harvey, 2014). Real utopias, according to Wright (2010: p. 6), are “utopian ideals that are grounded in the real potentials of humanity, utopian destinations that have accessible waystations, utopian designs of institutions that can inform our practical tasks of navigating a world of imperfect conditions for social change.” This means attempting to navigate worlds by embracing imperfection rather than striving for abstract principles.

In this sense, real utopias are prefigurative projects that, while oriented toward imagining futures and “tech otherwise” (Amrute and Murillo 2020), confront contradictions, challenges, and present-day necessities that make it impossible to fully realize their ideals. However, rather than abandoning the ideal in the name of normative “purity,” real utopias embrace these contradictions and do what is possible within the constraints of the current world to pursue the social (and technological) transformations they seek.

This real utopia framework can be complemented by a framework of imagination, especially drawing on Freire (1992). For Freire, imagining is inseparable from acting in and transforming the world, and concrete life conditions shape what we desire and imagine (Siles et al., 2023). As Freire argues (1992: p. 39), “imagination and conjecture about a different world than the one of oppression, are as necessary to the praxis of historical ‘subjects’ (agents) in the process of transforming reality.” This means that ways of transforming the world are informed both by imaginative capacity, in a world shaped by a crisis of imagination regarding other possibilities of being, existing, and developing technologies, and shaped by Silicon Valley ideology (Marwick 2017; Taşkale 2025), and by the concrete possibilities of the material world. This is complemented by the Kenyan decolonial scholar Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o (2013), for whom “it’s because we can imagine different futures that we can struggle against the present state of things” (Thiong’o, 2013: p. 41).

In response to the theme of this special issue on utopian media studies, this article argues that the Homeless Workers’ Movement in Brazil contributes to the development of real utopian approaches to technology, both in theory and in practice. It conceptualizes this as digital real utopias. This means prefigurative collective projects in relation to digital practices, from communities and workers’ perspectives. Following Raymond Williams (2005), this article understands technologies as both means of communication and means of production. This perspective entails recognizing the human labor embedded in technologies (Vieira Pinto, 2005), as well as approaching technologies themselves as media forms that have expanded and reshaped the field of media studies over recent decades.

Digital real utopias, then, articulate Wright’s (2010) notion of real utopias with Freire’s (1992) political perspective on imagination, and add to ongoing conversations around computing otherwise and tech otherwise (Amrute and Murillo 2020; Duarte et al., 2025; Domínguez Hernández et al., 2025; Ricaurte, 2021, among others), including debates around digital solidarity economies (Albornoz et al., 2026), that is, how to articulate other ways of designing technologies and building economies and worker arrangements.

Unlike other social movements for free technologies (Foletto, 2021; Murillo, 2025), the Homeless Workers’ Movement does not frame its practices around a normative vision of what technologies should or should not be. Instead, it operates within a constant tension shaped by the needs of working people and by what may concretely benefit the working class. This means that the Tech Sector experiments with other possible worlds through its projects, adopting a “trial and error” approach that avoids both technosolutionism and the essentialization of ready-made formulas about what constitutes “good” technologies for workers. The expression most often used by the movement is, in Portuguese, “pé no barro” (“feet in the mud”) (MTST, 2023), which signifies an understanding of frameworks from below, grounded in the concrete, material, and urgent experiences of the working class. This trial-and-error framework can be understood as a pedagogical process of learning in struggle (Vieta 2014), that is, learning by doing, collectively, within the social movement itself. This framework underpins how the movement understands and theorizes digital sovereignty.

Methods as/for digital real utopias

We are not mere observers of the digital real utopias pursued by the Tech Sector of the Homeless’ Workers’ Movement (MTST). On the contrary, we are active participants, in an engaged research framework. Methodologically, this paper adopts a workers’ inquiry framework (Englert et al. 2020; Data Workers Inquiry 2024), combining participatory action research with ethnographic methods conducted in collaboration with movement members since 2021. As a form of participatory action research, workers’ inquiry enables research not only to analyze phenomena but also to co-construct and collectively co-create both the research process and responses to community needs, with a focus on social justice. In this sense, the methodological process itself is part of these digital real utopias: it seeks to transform how academic knowledge is produced and circulated (Cowan and Rault 2024), while also constituting a learning process through which both researchers and movement activists come to understand digital concepts and practices via interaction and dialogue. Empirically, this involved a series of observations and interviews, while the authors themselves were part of the process of building the MTST’s Tech Sector, which requires heavy processing (Cowan and Rault 2024) in relation to ongoing reflexivity throughout the research process.

Both authors have strong engagement with the MTST’s Tech Sector, although in different roles. This matters here as a methodological perspective in which the personal is political, and positionality is as part of a digital real utopias framework, an acknowledgment that researchers’ positionalities shape the research process—as scholarship on feminist technopolitics (e.g., Shokooh Valle, 2023) has emphasized. In terms of positionality, Grohmann is a researcher who has maintained active collaboration with the MTST since 2021. This involvement includes the co-coordination of the projects Worker-Owned Intersectional Platforms and Digital Sovereignty Archives, funded by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada. He also supported a series of public engagement initiatives, such as a workshop on digital sovereignty (January 2025) and the production of a primer and a comic book. From the standpoint of an engaged researcher, Grohmann participated in a series of collective debates, both public-facing and internal, led by the MTST Tech Sector. He also interviewed activists affiliated with the collective. This long-term engagement has shaped both access to the field and the interpretive framework of the research.

Costa Barbosa’s primary affiliation is as an activist with the Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement since 2021, where they have become one of its key leaders, particularly in the theoretical and political development of notions such as popular digital sovereignty. As one of those responsible for coordinating the Tech Sector, Costa Barbosa engaged in a series of public debates, representing the collective at national and international events. He was also among those responsible for coordinating workshops on digital sovereignty and for coordinating the collective’s knowledge-production role. This article is therefore co-authored by one researcher institutionalized within a university and another who is rooted in the social movement.

The research process is not linear, as it follows the prefigurative framework of digital real utopias, caught between the desire to build alternative futures and the constraints and temporalities of material life, including the limited duration of funding. We follow what Cowan and Rault (2024) describe as heavy processing, both as friendship as method and as a messy approach to digital methods and research (Dadas, 2016; Postill and Pink, 2012). This reflects the non-linear character of the concrete life of a social movement. Within this ethnographic, reflexive, collaborative, and participatory perspective, we co-organized in-person workshops in São Paulo, and jointly took part, one speaking on behalf of the movement, the other as a university-based researcher, in events in Brazil and abroad between 2022 and 2025 on themes such as digital sovereignty, digital solidarity economies, and other topics related to the digital realm.

The co-production of materials between the co-authors and the movement was one of the methodological moves, and also an analytic one, that helps demonstrate the Tech Sector’s reflective process around digital sovereignty, and how this research process and co-production of materials is itself a mediated materiality of building digital real utopias. This can be understood as a process of critical pedagogies, as the methodological dimension of learning by doing, both within Freire’s framework (1990, 1992) and in scholarship that articulates critical pedagogies with digital media, such as Alvarez (2019) and McLaren and Jandrić (2013).

The context for these developments was the 2022 campaign for the election of Lula da Silva against the far right. Throughout that year, a series of events were organized with the participation of the MTST Tech Sector to discuss themes such as digital sovereignty and digital solidarity economies, held in cities including Porto Alegre, São Paulo, and Rio de Janeiro. One of these workshops, in Porto Alegre, led to the drafting of a manifesto on digital solidarity economies, which was personally delivered to President Lula during the electoral campaign. It was also debated publicly in events with other social movements, researchers, and unions. One of the central issues raised in this manifesto was the need to situate digital solidarity economies within the broader framework of digital sovereignty. This manifesto, signed by a range of Brazilian social movements, collectives, unions, and researchers, became a milestone in the movement’s reflection. One of the key lessons of the manifesto is that it is not possible to create worker-owned platforms (Grohmann 2023) without considering the broader context of dependency on the technological oligopoly, or without pursuing digital sovereignty from a worker-centered perspective.

Manifestos are historically political genres, and in recent decades the production of manifestos has gained traction in critical tech studies, as shown by Garcia et al. (2022), authors of the Feminist Data Manifest-No, 2 one of the most widely circulated manifestos in critical tech studies in recent years, alongside the AI Decolonial Manyfesto 3 and Towards a Digital Pluriverse. 4 Thus, manifestos can be understood as methods for digital real utopias, such as the one the Tech Sector co-led in 2022.

There are primarily three projects in which the authors are directly involved, and whose processes have inspired reflections and critical pedagogies within the MTST Tech Sector regarding its own notion of digital sovereignty. The first project is a booklet titled The Homeless Workers’ Movement in Brazil and the Struggle for Digital Sovereignty (MTST 2023), published in 2023 in both Portuguese and English. Between the second half of 2022 and the beginning of 2023, the Tech Sector convened to define the parameters for the booklet, which represented the first attempt to present and systematize the initiatives of this sector. One of the authors of this article led the writing and coordination of the booklet, while another contributed through review and supervision. This was the first time the social movement itself reflected on and wrote about popular digital sovereignty from the standpoint of social movements. The booklet attracted considerable academic attention in Brazil and internationally. This booklet can also be understood as a media and pedagogical artifact at once: it helped circulate the movement’s struggles (Dyer-Witheford, 2015) and has served as a springboard for more sustained reflection within the movement itself on digital sovereignty.

The second project is the Worker-Owned Intersectional Platforms (WOIP 2025) project, a participatory action research initiative involving technology and delivery collectives from Brazil and Argentina, among them the Tech Sector. One of the authors of this article served as the principal investigator of this project. Through this initiative, the authors presented on digital sovereignty at events in São Paulo and Buenos Aires in 2024 and jointly published the project report, which emphasized shared learnings. A key focus of the project was to imagine worker-owned platforms through intersectional perspectives, with digital sovereignty emerging as one of its central findings. One of the main lessons learned from this project, which also involved workshops and the production of videos, was the need to ground the notion of digital sovereignty in Latin American perspectives, and also to consider the role of the state within this conception of digital sovereignty.

The third project is a comic book titled Other Tech Worlds Are Possible, launched in September 2025. This comic book tells the story of the Tech Sector through the voices of grassroots activists in the movement, linking struggles for housing with the reappropriation of technologies from below. Both authors contributed to its development, conception, and realization. The process of producing the comic book involved discussions with activists from the Homeless Workers’ Movement about how they wanted to be represented. The comic format also played a simultaneously media and pedagogical role in circulating the movement’s struggles. One of the main lessons learned from this project was the power of images to circulate the movement’s struggles, both among activists themselves and in reaching broader society.

These three projects, processes, and outputs are means of activating and circulating the digital real utopias of the Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement. Alongside events and workshops, they function as media products that disseminate workers’ struggles (Mularoni and Dyer-Witheford 2025). They are also concrete examples of the practice and process of workers’ inquiry and of partnerships between universities and communities, as well as vehicles for circulating possibilities of real utopias. Produced in bilingual or trilingual formats, these media products raise awareness of the MTST around the world and, particularly in the case of comics, enable the movement to be represented through its own narrative choices. This is also crucial for audiences to recognize that prefigurative possibilities for technology, grounded in workers’ perspectives, are already being realized.

Introducing the Tech Sector of the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST)

This section aims to introduce the Homeless Workers’ Movement’s Tech Sector and provide an overview of some of these projects, solely as context for the article’s main discussion. The Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST) has emerged as one of the world’s most significant urban social movements. Over almost three decades, it has expanded its territorial and popular approach, fostering initiatives in food sovereignty, such as the “Solidarity Kitchens,” and popular education and science. The Tech Sector was founded in response to Jair Bolsonaro’s election in 2020, based on the movement’s assessment that the left needed to strengthen its presence in digital spaces. The collective comprises tech workers, including software engineers, designers, and systems analysts, who seek to “challenge the ideological hegemony of Silicon Valley in the field of Information Technology” (MTST, 2023) and move beyond its prevailing tech imaginaries (Marwick 2017).

Through its Tech Sector, the MTST has sought to combine the reappropriation of technology for working-class benefit with the creation of income opportunities, cross-sector worker organizing, and broader struggles for popular sovereignty. These initiatives are embedded in the movement’s militant trajectory and political education practices, which emphasize popular pedagogies and the co-production of knowledge (Freire and Guimarães, 1984; Simões et al. 2017). The MTST has explicitly situated itself within debates on digital solidarity economies and digital sovereignty, highlighting how the fight for housing is intrinsically linked to the struggle for more just digital economies (MTST 2023).

The MTST Tech Sector is a space for political construction and class articulation and organizing. It fosters the workers’ organizing across different class fractions (Rothstein, 2025). On the one hand, tech workers who had not previously found spaces for collective expression and action encounter in the Tech Sector a sphere of belonging and engagement. On the other hand, workers from sectors such as construction and domestic labor engage with technologies in an active way, learning, teaching, and appropriating these tools through the collective experiences of the MTST.

One of the most emblematic initiatives of the MTST Tech Sector is Hire Those Who Struggle (Contrate Quem Luta), a virtual assistant on WhatsApp that connects homeless workers with individuals seeking services such as domestic work, construction, painting, or trucking. The project represents the realization of worker-owned technology, designed not to follow the logic of constant novelty but rather to serve the concrete needs of communities. The choice to embed the tool within WhatsApp reflects an acute awareness of Brazil’s persistent digital inequalities: for many, internet access is effectively limited to WhatsApp, and there is often neither data nor storage available for installing new apps. By appropriating an everyday digital artefact, the MTST both “occupies WhatsApp” and challenges technosolutionist and techno-chauvinist imaginaries (Broussard, 2018), showing that not all problems require new platforms or sophisticated AI. Instead, the movement insists on conceiving technology from the standpoint of working-class realities (Vieira Pinto, 2005).

Through Hire Those Who Struggle, around 200 workers, primarily from construction, domestic, and care sectors, have accessed opportunities for income and decent work. However, the project is not only about economic survival, but it has become a space of collective formation. Workers meet regularly to debate issues such as fair remuneration and forms of internal governance, while cultivating a culture of mutual care that extends to client relationships, an ethos encapsulated by cooperative principles. This strengthens ties among homeless workers, facilitating the circulation of struggles and enabling dialogue with tech workers, who also begin to recognize themselves as part of the working class. In this sense, the Tech Sector itself operates as an organizing space for a sector often deemed “unorganizable.”

This project exemplifies the MTST Tech Sector’s messy approach to digital real utopias, and to its very concept of popular digital sovereignty. Hire Those Who Struggle was developed with a dual aim: to reduce the movement’s workers’ dependence on Big Tech platforms to carry out paid work (e.g., domestic and construction work), and to support worker organizing within the movement itself. However, the solution was conceived pragmatically. Rather than building a dedicated app, which would align more neatly with a normative model of digital sovereignty, the Tech Sector opted to develop a WhatsApp-based chatbot. Movement organizers describe this as a productive contradiction: they needed to meet workers where they already were. In Brazil, WhatsApp is widely used, in part because of “zero-rating” arrangements that allow people to use WhatsApp without mobile data charges. This makes proprietary platforms a practical organizing infrastructure, at least as an initial step. In this sense, Hire Those Who Struggle illustrates the challenges of acting with one foot in the future and one foot in the present.

Beyond Hire Those Who Struggle, the Tech Sector has experimented with a range of initiatives, alongside the media productions discussed in the previous section. This portfolio functions as a set of laboratories and experiments (Grohmann, 2025) oriented toward digital real utopias. These projects have included launching pedagogical initiatives in São Paulo’s peripheries, bringing digital infrastructures into MTST occupations, including an automated community garden using the Internet of Things, and pursuing inter-cooperative collaborations, such as co-developing platforms with other collectives and cooperatives. Taken together, these initiatives illustrate how the MTST combines technological reappropriation, worker organization, income generation, and popular sovereignty within the broader framework of a social movement.

Epistemic sovereignty and digital sovereignty

The Homeless Workers’ Movement’s digital real utopias framework centers epistemic sovereignty as a key way of imagining, theorizing, and acting across its different fronts, including developing, from below, its own conception of popular digital sovereignty. This section outlines the Tech Sector’s core epistemic values and their broader connections to debates on digital sovereignty.

A crucial feature of the movement is the centrality of epistemic sovereignty, understood as the capacity to produce and circulate knowledge within the movement itself. In other words, its praxis and grassroots organizing are grounded in knowledge and theory. According to Oliveira and Mendes Pinto (2024: p. 42), “epistemic sovereignty can be understood as the ability of a group or individual to exercise control and autonomy over their own epistemic processes, without being subject to external forms of domination or oppression.”

This principle is rooted in the very philosophy of the movement, insofar as it involves the movement itself reflecting upon and researching its own practices, not merely as a recipient of knowledge, but as an active producer of it. Epistemic sovereignty also becomes visible in the circulation of struggles and in practices of public engagement or knowledge mobilization, such as the production of campaigns, primers, comic books, and other materials. This ongoing act of reflection culminated in the publication of their own collectively authored book, launched in October 2025: “Homeless Technologies: Toward Sovereign Digital Territories” (MTST 2023). The decision to write a book collectively, by the movement’s own members, served multiple purposes: to document their trajectory, to imagine political and technological futures, to gain visibility, but above all, to show that the movement also produces knowledge.

Two core values, historically embedded in the MTST’s culture, have also been integrated into the Technology Sector. The first is the principle that territorial organization precedes all forms of struggle and organizing, including technological initiatives. Without a collectively built territorial base, nothing functions effectively. Consequently, the MTST prioritizes the relationship between technology and territorial needs, rooting its technological initiatives in community struggles. This approach aligns with the concept of situated sovereignty (Lange et al. 2023), which considers the social function of technology. The second core value is the movement’s commitment to a popular perspective that remains firmly anchored in class struggle. This entails recognizing the technological needs of the working-class members engaged in the movement and understanding how technological issues emerge “from below.” These principles serve as the foundation for the movement’s conceptualization of digital sovereignty.

The movement’s understandings of media and technology are deeply informed by two Brazilian thinkers: the pedagogue Paulo Freire and the geographer Milton Santos. For Freire (1990), education is inseparable from communication, in the sense that language must always be related to people’s concrete, everyday lives, and that considering life in its entirety is essential. Hence his well-known claim that “reading the world precedes reading the word” (Freire 1990: p. 139). In this framework, the context and lived experiences of people come before the word. In the case of the Tech Sector, the expression has been adapted to “reading the world precedes reading the code.” This paraphrase encapsulates the idea that, beyond simply programming and designing technologies, the MTST is concerned with doing so for a purpose, for a vision of the world grounded in social justice.

According to Santos (2021), technology is inseparable from territory. It does not occur in a vacuum but operates through specific materialities and territorialities, which can also be connected to Paulo Freire’s notion of the “world.” For the MTST, technologies must be meaningful: they should produce and circulate meanings within particular territories and respond to the needs and aspirations of those who inhabit them. Thus, there is no universal or “one-size-fits-all” solution, but rather situated responses to local problems shaped by community needs. The MTST has also drawn on Santos’s (2021) reflections on the social function of technology to emphasize that not all technologies need to exist, and that it is necessary to critically assess which technologies are genuinely required in relation to the realities of communities. It is worth noting, nonetheless, that according to Milton Santos, popular digital sovereignty is both the result of that process, hence a utopia, but also the ongoing technology resistance and adaptation. These two authors are helpful for understanding perspectives on technology as media that serve social change, while also demonstrating that the social movement grounds its practices in an epistemic sovereignty framework.

The synthesis of these experiences lies in how the movement has taken a leading role in debates on digital sovereignty from the standpoint of social movements (Couture and Toupin 2019). The concept of digital sovereignty, which has become central to media and internet studies in recent years, is multifaceted, polysemic, and the site of struggles over meaning among governments, corporations, and even Big Tech companies (Grohmann and Barbosa 2025). Despite being a topic that dates as early as the Internet itself, with the US as the default “digital sovereignty” country (Chander and Sun, 2023), the “new wave” of digital sovereignty in policy and scholarship has been mostly within the categories of state control over critical digital infrastructures; the potential of enabling industrial digital policies to foment national and regional companies; and the individual digital self-determination. Recent scholarship on the topic, including on technological sovereignty—which draws heavily on community technology appropriation (Haché, 2014)—has increasingly highlighted the role of social movements in shaping bottom-up experiences (Couture and Toupin, 2019; Couture et al., 2025; Grohmann et al., 2025; Costa Barbosa et al., 2024). Especially in Latin America, digital or technology sovereignty has largely been related to the struggle of pivoting autonomous grassroots initiatives (Lehuedé, 2024). While classical notions of sovereignty are tied to the role of the state in national sovereignty, a growing body of scholarship has shown the importance of also considering the role of diverse communities—from civil society to Indigenous peoples (Couture et al., 2025)—in shaping, from below, the policies and practices that define digital sovereignty in specific territories.

The struggle for digital sovereignty is not a given, but rather an ongoing process of struggle and organizing. Digital sovereignty has not been achieved. On the contrary, there remains a profound dependency on platforms owned by Big Tech companies (Valente and Grohmann, 2024; Grohmann 2025b) and their mechanisms (Poell et al., 2021). In this sense, the struggle for digital sovereignty, especially when placing the working class at the core of this struggle, embodies the digital real utopias framework, as it envisions other possible worlds through technology while remaining grounded in the everyday lives of communities. Thus, the Homeless Workers’ Movement’s way of theorizing digital sovereignty is both evidence of how it enacts epistemic sovereignty and part of its digital real utopias framework.

Popular digital sovereignty and the Homeless Workers’ Movement

One of the main examples of the Homeless Workers’ Movement’s epistemic sovereignty is its production of knowledge on popular digital sovereignty, from 2021 to the present. This section outlines, first, what popular sovereignty means in theoretical terms, and then the movement’s ongoing and messy process of defining, conceptualizing, and theorizing popular digital sovereignty from its own perspectives, values, and practices. The aim here is not to examine the activities in depth, but rather to focus on the conceptual construction of popular digital sovereignty as part of the digital real utopias framework.

MTST’s Tech Sector has developed a distinct framework of popular digital sovereignty, rooted in Latin American social thought and militant epistemologies (Freire, 1990; Martín-Barbero, 1993; Santos, 2021). Unlike state-centric or corporate-led visions of digital sovereignty, this model prioritizes territorial organization, collective digital infrastructures, and critical digital literacy as strategies to confront technological domination. By integrating digital sovereignty into everyday struggles, the movement challenges Silicon Valley’s imaginaries while reconfiguring digital technologies as instruments for working-class self-determination.

In Latin American studies, the term “popular” does not carry a negative connotation; rather, it denotes the agency of the popular classes within social struggles (Gago, 2017). Latin American media theorist Martín-Barbero (1993) highlights how the “popular” is intertwined with media, communication, and culture, conceptualizing it as “mediating the popular” (Martín-Barbero, 1993: p. 203). This perspective emphasizes the ways in which marginalized groups appropriate media and technologies, using them to navigate power relations from the standpoint of everyday life (Siles et al., 2023). From this viewpoint, technological practices “from below” reflect how people adapt and repurpose technologies in ways that diverge from dominant narratives (Martín-Barbero, 1993).

Discussions of sovereignty in the literature often reveal a conceptual misalignment—possibly due to translation gaps—between the notions of “people’s sovereignty” (as in Lenin’s [1917] classic formulation of “people’s power”) and popular sovereignty itself. Chatterjee (2004) characterizes popular sovereignty as a form of power that is pragmatic and relational rather than normative. While the concept of popular digital sovereignty has only been sporadically theorized, Rone (2024: p. 5) defines digital sovereignty from a popular democratic perspective as “a form in which power not only originates from the people but is also exercised by and for the people.” This implies greater participation and voice for citizens in key decisions concerning digital infrastructure. This article argues that the Homeless Workers’ Movement in Brazil has actively worked to integrate these dimensions of the “popular” within both digital technologies and broader social struggles.

The initial discussions of the Homeless Worker Movement around digital sovereignty began in 2022. According to one of the members, “We realize that what we have been doing is precisely the struggle for digital sovereignty. This involves advocating for technological appropriation to empower popular organizing, demanding meaningful access to the internet, critical digital education, and developing worker-owned platforms.” In 2023, they published a primer on digital sovereignty (MTST, 2023), emphasizing that digital sovereignty emerges from popular power, rooted in social movements and the working class. This perspective underscores the role of popular power in fostering technological development, organizing struggles, and pressuring the state for policies that secure rights and dignity. Consequently, this “from below” approach to digital sovereignty entails both the de-commodification of technology and the collectivization of digital relations, reducing dependence on oligopolistic platforms while increasing autonomy for the working class and mitigating digital inequalities.

MTST’s Tech Sector has conducted internal workshops to improve and develop a unified definition of popular digital sovereignty and introduced it in their book, entitled Homeless Technologies: Toward Sovereign Digital Territories. This is their current definition, based on group discussions according to the workshops: “Popular digital sovereignty is the process and result of collective organizing aimed at self-determination, creation, development, subversion, democratization, and control of infrastructures and technologies to serve the people and address needs identified in struggles for housing, land, labor, and food. It is shaped by territorial and temporal contexts, operating with, beyond, or in opposition to the State.” As a process of learning in struggle, the state did not appear in the earliest definitions of popular digital sovereignty. It entered the movement’s formulations gradually, as activists came to understand that addressing sovereignty, unlike related notions such as autonomy (Grohmann et al., 2025), necessarily entails grappling with the relationship to the state. The aim here is not to specify what this means in practical terms, but rather to highlight how the movement progressively took ownership of the definition at an epistemic level.

This emerging emphasis on the state also resonates with the movement’s longer political trajectory. Although the MTST positions itself as an autonomous organization, it has always maintained links to institutional politics, particularly because its central goal—securing affordable and adequate housing—necessarily involves pressing the state to fulfill its obligations. In this sense, MTST’s tactic of occupying unused land in urban peripheries can be read not only as direct action from below, but also as a claim on the Brazilian constitutional framework that limits absolute private property rights and establishes social duties for the state. The movement follows the logic of occupation, that is, occupying the territories. Keeping the state explicitly within the definition of popular digital sovereignty thus signals that the movement does not reject the state a priori, a stance consistent with its socialist roots and with the intrinsic relationship between sovereignty and statehood. This orientation is also visible in the movement’s more recent engagement with data governance. Since 2024, the MTST has held a seat on Brazil’s National Data Protection Council, representing the labor sector.

Gradually, the movement has developed a multidimensional approach to popular digital sovereignty, moving beyond addressing isolated issues such as digital literacy or digital solidarity economies. As the conceptual understanding of popular sovereignty, in terms of epistemic sovereignty, has deepened, it has become increasingly intertwined with concrete actions within the social movement. But there are certainly challenges in how this understanding can translate meaningfully into both an agenda for social movements and the circulation of struggles.

Therefore, the concept of popular digital sovereignty, as formulated by the Homeless Workers’ Movement, advances, as an ongoing process, both an epistemological and strategic shift: it seeks to place the knowledge, needs, and practices of the working class at the center of the production and circulation of technologies of all kinds, from ancestral knowledges to data, platforms, and artificial intelligence. These evolving definitions mean a long learning process on how the movement is understanding, theorizing and reappropriating the notion of digital sovereignty, updating popular frameworks from Latin America. By integrating territorial imperatives with a distinctly popular and class-oriented perspective, the Homeless Workers’ Movement contributes to broader debates on digital sovereignty, emphasizing grassroots agency and the lived experiences of the working class in relation to digital technologies on a massive scale.

The movement’s approach seeks to advance epistemic sovereignty while fostering a shared political understanding of how digital technologies both shape and are shaped by social struggles. As they put it, their goal is to promote “the use and development of technologies by and for those who struggle” (MTST, 2023). Both the internal reflection on the concept of popular digital sovereignty and the ways in which it materializes in concrete projects are part of this process of epistemic sovereignty.

The projects developed by the MTST that can be regarded as attempts and prototypes of popular digital sovereignty are multiple and diverse. MTST’s practical approach to popular digital sovereignty is organized around a set of pillars, each articulated through specific projects and political initiatives. These pillars include: (i) the development of technologies with a social function, oriented both internally and externally to the movement; (ii) critical and peripheral technological education; (iii) meaningful and community-based connectivity; (iv) decent work; (v) an integrated approach to data; (vi) a game laboratory for active social change; and (vii) user-friendly interfaces for political participation.

Concrete initiatives illustrate how these pillars are materialized in practice. One such project, beyond the already mentioned Hire Those Who Struggle, is the App da Vitória, designed to support the internal organization of the movement by facilitating the management of engagement among participants in occupied spaces. Other projects have been developed on demand, such as SUBA, an application for mapping rap battles, contesting consciousness through hip-hop, valuing peripheral voices and affirming technology in the service of the people, and a tool to manage sales flows for the Rede Mulheres do Maranhão, a cooperative with over two hundred women in the Amazonian region.

The Tech Sector has further developed technologies with accessible and user-friendly interfaces designed to strengthen political participation. These were notably employed during the 2024 São Paulo mayoral campaigns, including a mobilization platform, featuring campaign materials and events mapped with georeferenced data. Regarding popular sovereignty, these initiatives directly relate to the massive adoption of digital participation tools in the country. This effort culminated in the appointment of a Tech Sector member to lead the agenda within the Federal Government.

These projects remain small-scale experiments in technologies developed and controlled by the movement to pursue its own goals. They illustrate how the epistemic sovereignty embedded in popular digital sovereignty, however incomplete and messy, can serve the purposes of a form of digital sovereignty rooted in the popular power of social movements.

Moreover, the MTST is not reflecting on popular digital sovereignty only within the movement itself, but has also been practicing policy from below (De Peuter 2020), that is, pressing for policies emerging from social movements, in articulation with different sectors of civil society. This includes alliances and coalitions for digital sovereignty, which are crucial both for building digital real utopias and for advancing digital sovereignty from below (Couture et al., 2025). Their definition of popular digital sovereignty functions as a mobile framework: a theoretical and political unit appropriated, reinterpreted, and implemented by a network of allied actors, each contributing a distinct strategic layer to the same struggle. The circulation and adaptation of this concept do not indicate a dilution of its origins but rather demonstrate its strength and applicability across multiple fronts of contestation. As the Sector itself states, the objective is to “challenge, with or without state support, the very axis of the debate,” engaging in “other types of disputes to achieve other types of victories.” Its diffusion across movements demonstrates not a weakening of its original meaning, but the strength and adaptability of a concept that, as a digital real utopia, remains rooted in the working-class struggles that produced it.

From this point on, the notion of popular digital sovereignty, as formulated by the MTST’s Tech Sector, began to reach other social movements and policymakers, offering an example of the circulation of struggles. One important step in this process has been the appropriation and elaboration of the concept by the Socialism and Liberty Party in Brazil (Partido Socialismo e Liberdade, PSOL), of which the MTST is a member and which currently represents the dominant current within the party. While the MTST Tech Sector originally defined popular digital sovereignty in terms of the collective capacity to self-determine, create, subvert, democratize, and control digital infrastructures in service of the people, PSOL emphasizes not only the creation of popular and “free” tools, infrastructures, and platforms but also the material and subjective conditions that enable ordinary people to appropriate and shape the information society. This demonstrates how MTST’s framing has laid a foundation that can be extended into institutional and policy arenas, connecting grassroots technological experiments to broader debates on state policy and democracy. It also shows how a concept born within the tech sector of a social movement can come to influence party-level discussions.

In turn, the Civil BRICS Forum, an initiative that brings together civil society actors from the BRICS countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa), has taken up the term through the work of the ALBA Movements, a transnational coalition of grassroots organizations linked to the Bolivarian Alliance for the Peoples of Our America (ALBA-TCP). The MTST’s Tech Sector itself has participated in these discussions, bringing its experiences of popular organizing into dialogue with other movements across the Global Majority. In this context, popular digital sovereignty is tied to the autonomy and integration of the peoples of the Global Majority. It is defined as both a process and a product of popular organizing rooted in core struggles—housing, land, work, bread, and self-determination—but now extended into the digital sphere. This framing situates the concept within South–South geopolitical articulations, positioning digital sovereignty as a matter of international solidarity and collective resistance to the dominance of the Global North.

Finally, NetPopular, a campaign connected to activist networks around the MTST within the Brazilian Internet Governance Forum, the major digital policy event in the country, operationalizes the concept at the level of infrastructures. Its focus is on creating free networks and tools that guarantee autonomy, integration, and respect for local territories and communities. NetPopular promotes a vision of digital sovereignty as the work of popular power itself, directed toward building a more plural, free, and locally connected internet. By rooting tech design in popular organizing, it seeks to translate sovereignty into tangible infrastructures that are both accessible and politically grounded.

Taken together, these strands form a multifaceted ecosystem of popular digital sovereignty rooted in the original formulation of the MTST. The MTST Tech Sector drives grassroots experimentation and prefiguration, PSOL brings the struggle into institutional and policy arenas, the Civil BRICS Forum and ALBA Movements extend it into a transnational South–South frame, and NetPopular implements it through popular infrastructures within the traditional Internet Governance fora. Each dimension reinforces the others, showing how a concept born within the Brazilian housing movement can circulate, adapt, and be amplified across different scales, local, national, transnational, and technical—while remaining anchored in the struggles of the working class. This circulation highlights the vitality and versatility of popular digital sovereignty as both a political and epistemic project.

But this is neither a linear nor a perfect process. On the contrary, the path toward this popular digital sovereignty is marked by the challenges that Wright’s (2010) concept of real utopias anticipates. The Tech Sector must continually navigate scarce resources, the fact that volunteer activists must balance demanding jobs, and the immense technical and political pressures of Big Tech’s dominance. Recognizing these struggles is crucial, as they highlight that digital real utopias are not seamless accomplishments but rather ongoing, contingent processes shaped as much by long and arduous trajectories as by their moments of success. It is precisely within these tensions that the “feet in the mud” approach demonstrates its value as both a guiding principle and a practical necessity.

Conclusion

This article has argued that the Homeless Workers’ Movement (MTST) in Brazil offers a powerful case for understanding how digital real utopias can be conceived and practiced from below, even in incomplete and messy ways. In particular, the article argues that the epistemic sovereignty practiced by the movement is crucial for advancing digital real utopias, that is, controlling how knowledge is produced and circulated is central to reflecting on and shaping technologies and infrastructures. In this sense, the concept of popular digital sovereignty is central.

By situating technology within the everyday struggles for housing, labor, and food, the MTST demonstrates that digital sovereignty is a matter of working-class self-determination and ongoing practice of the movement around the struggles over the meanings of digital sovereignty. Its Tech Sector embodies the dialectic of utopia and the concrete here and now, as digital real utopias, engaging in prefigurative experiments that are at work-in-progress, imperfect, and deeply radical. These initiatives affirm that digital real utopias emerge from the trial-and-error practices of organized communities responding to urgent material needs.

At the theoretical level, the article has sought to situate the practices of the MTST within the framework of utopian media studies. By drawing on Erik Olin Wright’s notion of real utopias, the analysis highlights how contradictions, incompleteness, and imperfection are not obstacles but constitutive features of these digital experiments. This orientation resists both technosolutionism and the romanticization of grassroots struggles, while affirming the importance of Paulo Freire’s pedagogy and Milton Santos’s territorial reflections as resources used by MTST for thinking about media and technology from Latin America. In doing so, the article demonstrates how Latin American thought enriches global debates on utopian media studies (Grohmann 2025b).

The MTST’s theorization of popular digital sovereignty contributes to ongoing debates on digital real utopias and digital sovereignty by advancing a distinctly popular and class-based perspective. Unlike state-centric or corporate-led framings, popular digital sovereignty, as developed by the movement, prioritizes territorial organization, collective infrastructures, and epistemic sovereignty. In this sense, it expands the horizon of digital sovereignty beyond the state, while also highlighting how sovereignty itself can be reimagined through grassroots struggles. The fact that this framework has traveled into allied political, social, and transnational spaces, demonstrates its adaptability and resonance, while also affirming its anchoring in the experiences of workers and activists.

Methodologically, the research has highlighted the value of workers’ inquiry as both an analytic and prefigurative practice. By engaging in participatory action research and producing materials such as primers, manifestos, and comics alongside the movement, the study exemplifies how knowledge can be co-created and mobilized as part of broader struggles. This reflexive, collaborative, and multilingual process reflects the principles of epistemic sovereignty advanced by the MTST. The methodological contribution, then, lies in enacting and circulating digital real utopias.

This article contributes to digital sovereignty studies by showing that the knowledge and practices generated by working-class movements in the Global Majority must be taken seriously. While often imperfect and provisional, these experiments are already reshaping the horizon of technological emancipation. They are simultaneously acts of imagination and processes of production and circulation of both knowledge and technologies. More specifically, the article contributes to this special issue on Utopian Media Studies by demonstrating how social movements in Latin America are envisioning and practicing alternative digital futures from below. The case of the MTST reveals an approach to digital sovereignty that is grounded in collective ownership, territorial struggles, and the everyday needs of the working class. The MTST’s initiatives, ranging from digital solidarity economies to campaigns for digital rights and critical digital literacy, illustrate how utopian media practices emerge from material struggles for justice.
