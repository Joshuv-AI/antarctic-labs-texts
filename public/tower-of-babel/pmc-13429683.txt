Water-soluble triazolylpurine derivatives as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in 1 M HCl solution
Authors: Armands Sebris, Aleksejs Burcevs, Una Ušacka, Mairis Iesalnieks, Reinis Drunka, Kristaps Valkovskis, Irina Novosjolova, Māris Turks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Six water-soluble 2-triazolylpurine derivatives and one 2,6-bistriazolylpurine derivative were identified as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in aqueous 1 mol/L HCl solution. Among them highest inhibitory activity exhibited 2,6-bistriazolylpurine derivative reaching up to 93% according to electric impedance spectroscopy and 98% according to gravimetry at 10−3 mol/L. Whereas, in the presence of 6-methylamino-2-triazolypurine derivative an inhibition efficiency as high as 95% at 10−3 mol/L was achieved, according to Tafel polarization. In most cases inhibition efficiency increased at higher inhibitor concentrations. Molecular orbital densities and relevant parameters from quantum chemical calculations showed little difference among target compounds. It is supported by experimental results of high inhibitory efficiency of approximately 90% or higher for all triazolylpurine derivatives at the highest tested concentration (10−3 mol/L) with varying rates of efficiency reduction at lower concentrations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51582-z.

Introduction

Corrosion is a chemical process that occurs when a material interacts destructively with its surrounding environment, leading to surface deterioration, equipment failure, decreased productivity, and costly maintenance1. It can be managed by removing promoting factors, adjusting temperature and pH, reducing moisture and oxygen levels, and avoiding connections between different metals2. In real world conditions the aforementioned factors often cannot be changed, so alternative prevention methods such as alloying, surface coating, cathodic protection, and corrosion inhibitors are used3.

Mild steel (MS) is extensively used in industrial and construction applications due to its favorable mechanical and structural properties4. Nevertheless, MS is highly susceptible to corrosion in acidic environments commonly encountered in industrial processes, including acid pickling and oil well acidification5. Therefore, hydrochloric acid solutions, particularly 1 mol/L HCl, are frequently employed in corrosion studies as standardized media that provide reproducible and moderately aggressive conditions, enabling reliable comparison of corrosion rates and inhibitor performance6. Chloride ions coming from HCl further promote localized corrosion by destabilizing passive films on metal surfaces6.

When evaluating methods for preventing corrosion, the use of corrosion inhibitors is considered a promising approach. Corrosion inhibitors are chemicals, which reduce the rate of metal degradation caused by environmental factors such as moisture, oxygen, and acids. This technique can be used in various industries, including oil and gas production, metal manufacture and processing, marine operations and construction. Effective corrosion inhibition extends the lifespan of metal structures and equipment, reducing maintenance costs and preventing potential failures. Most corrosion inhibitors form a thin protective layer on the metal surface through adsorption or by interacting with metal atoms on the surface. Lewis basic groups such as heteroatoms (N, O, P and S) or π electron systems enable such an interaction7,8. Amines may reduce corrosive processes due to their inherent basicity, yet may become corrosion inducing after absorbing CO29,10. Many currently used corrosion inhibitors, such as phosphates, chromates molybdates and others are harmful to environment due to their toxicity. Those should be replaced with alternatives, which are safer for nature11,12.

In recent years, the use of heterocyclic organic corrosion inhibitors has emerged as a promising approach to mitigate corrosion, since heterocycles combine π systems with heteroatoms. Recent reports have shown corrosion inhibiting properties of various nitrogen heterocycles – thiazole13, imidazole14, benzimidazole15, pyridine16, pyrimidine17, pyridazine18,19, dihydroquinoxaline20, quinoxalinone21, quinazoline22 and triazole23. Several naturally occurring purine derivatives have been studied in this context24–30, yet reports on functionalized derivatives are still quite scarce31–33 and triazolylpurines have not been examined for this purpose at all. Purines are among the most common nitrogen heterocycles in nature and, over several decades, various functionalization methods for purine derivatives have been extensively studied because of their use in medicinal chemistry and pharmacy34. Purine derivatives that have been studied as corrosion inhibitors, exhibit high corrosion inhibition efficiency—up to 97%25. Purines were proven to be effective corrosion inhibitors due to their strong adsorption onto metal surfaces, which forms a protective barrier that minimizes metal oxidation. Their molecular structure, rich in nitrogen and aromatic rings, enhances their ability to bond with metal atoms, thus increasing inhibition efficiency. Purine derivatives are environmentally friendly, as they are common in nature35, and specifically adenine derivatives have shown low toxicity36. They offer a cost-effective solution for prolonging the lifespan of industrial metal components.

Despite these promising characteristics, research on functionalized purine derivatives as corrosion inhibitors remains limited, with most studies focusing on naturally occurring or minimally substituted compounds24–33. In particular, triazole-functionalized purines have not been systematically investigated in this context. Given the known effectiveness of both purine and triazole moieties in corrosion inhibition, their combination represents a potentially advantageous strategy. Until today, only naturally occurring or minimally substituted purine derivatives have been explored as corrosion inhibitors24–33.

In this work, we investigate a series of rationally designed, functionalized adenine derivatives as corrosion inhibitors, introducing a molecular design strategy (Fig. 1) that extends beyond the use of naturally occurring or minimally modified compounds. The novelty of this study lies in the purposeful modification of the purine scaffold, integrating multiple functional elements to modulate adsorption behavior and inhibition efficiency. In particular, incorporation of a triazole moiety at the C2 position extends the conjugated π-system and increases the density of nitrogen donor sites, thereby enhancing the potential for interaction with metal surfaces. Simultaneously, introduction of a tetraethylene glycol fragment was employed to improve aqueous solubility while potentially contributing to corrosion inhibition efficiency37. Modifications at the exocyclic amino group were designed to probe the influence of hydrogen-bonding capability and electronic effects on inhibitor performance. Furthermore, the synthesis of bistriazolyl derivatives enables evaluation of the impact of increased π-system size, whereas a triazolyladenosine analogue provides insight into hydrophilic substituent effects at the N9 position. Accordingly, this study applies a rational functionalization strategy to the purine scaffold, introducing selected moieties to modulate properties relevant to corrosion inhibition, such as adsorption behavior, solubility, and electronic characteristics, in analogy to molecular design approaches commonly employed in medicinal chemistry. A series of target compounds was synthesized via concise and scalable synthetic routes requiring no more than five steps from commercially available starting materials, and their inhibitory performance was evaluated to elucidate how specific structural modifications influence corrosion protection efficiency.Fig. 1Molecular design strategy of 2-triazolylpurine derivatives as corrosion inhibitors.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of triazolylpurine derivative corrosion inhibitors

Target compounds 1–6 were prepared over multiple steps using principles of divergent synthesis (Fig. 2). First, we introduced tetraethylene glycol moiety protected with the tetrahydropyranyl group (THP) at the N9 position via the Mitsunobu reaction to give compound 9, which has been previously reported38. THP protected tetraethylene glycol was prepared according to literature methods39. Diazido substituted purine derivative 10 was prepared in nearly quantitative yield in a SNAr reaction with conditions previously developed by our group40. Azido group at purine C6 position was selectively reduced using CuSO4 and sodium ascorbate to give compound 1141. Triazole rings in compounds 12 and 13 were formed in a copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) with THP protected propargyl alcohol42, using previously reported conditions43. Compounds 13a-d were prepared in a SNAr reaction with nitrogen nucleophiles, in which triazole at C6 position acts as a leaving group44. Finally, THP deprotection under acidic conditions provided water soluble corrosion inhibitors 1–6.Fig. 2Synthesis of triazolylpurine corrosion inhibitors 1–6.

Synthesis of triazolylpurine corrosion inhibitors 1–6.

Compound 7 was prepared as an analogue with a different water solubilizing moiety – a monosaccharide ring (Fig. 3). Compounds 1545 16 and 1741 were prepared according to literature methods. We used Vorbrüggen glycosylation followed by SNAr reaction and selective reduction to achieve compound 17. CuAAC reaction to form triazole and acetyl group deprotection to prepare 7 were done using known conditions41. Further details on synthetic procedures are given in section Methods and Electronic Supplementary Information (ESI).Fig. 3Synthesis of adenosine derivative 7.

Synthesis of adenosine derivative 7.

Importantly, the synthetic routes described here are based on robust, high-yielding transformations widely recognized for their scalability, including Mitsunobu reactions46, SNAr substitutions47, and copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC)48. These methodologies are known for their reliability, functional group tolerance, and compatibility with large-scale synthesis. Combined with the short reaction sequences (≤ 5 steps from commercially available precursors), this synthetic design provides a practical and scalable platform for the production of structurally diverse corrosion inhibitors, facilitating further development and potential industrial application.

From the results IE increases at higher purine derivative inhibitor concentration, which is the expected relation between inhibitor concentration and IE49. At different inhibitor concentrations we observed that logarithmic increases in concentration provided less than logarithmic increases in IE, except for compound 4, for which the relation between concentration and IE appears nearly linear on a logarithmic chart (Fig. 4). Compound 6 exhibited the highest IE—97.9% at 10−3 mol/L concentration alongside maintaining high efficiency—95.7% IE even at 10−5 mol/L. For other inhibitors a pattern was observed—compounds with a NH functionality (1, 2 and 7) exhibited slightly higher IE at higher concentration than compounds with fully substituted amines (3–5). At lower inhibitor concentrations, however, compounds 1, 2 and 7 with NH functionality showed a greater decrease in IE than 3–5, especially, compound 7. This trend indicates that the formation of a stable and protective adsorption layer is strongly dependent on the inhibitor concentration. As the concentration decreases, the amount of inhibitor molecules becomes insufficient to ensure continuous surface coverage, resulting in the formation of an inhomogeneous or defective layer. Consequently, part of the surface remains unprotected and is easily accessible to Cl- ions, which causes a significant decrease in inhibition efficiency. This indicates the existence of a critical concentration threshold below which the inhibitor is no longer able to provide effective protection. In most cases, a sharp decrease in efficiency is observed at concentrations below 10−4 mol/L, which should also be considered a limit.

Electrochemical analysis

All synthesized purine derivatives were tested at various concentrations for their inhibition efficiency by electrochemical analysis. Experiments were performed in triplicate in aqueous 1 mol/L HCl solution.

Before LVS and EIS measurements, open-circuit potential (VOCP) was measured for 60 min, which was sufficient to reach stable values. VOCP values are very similar among the tested substrates, but a general trend can be observed (Fig. 5). VOCP values increase with increasing inhibitor concentration. An increase in the VOCP value is often associated with the formation of a protective oxide layer on the metal surface50 or, in the case of inhibitors, with the adsorption of inhibitor molecules onto the metal surface51. At higher concentrations, VOCP tends to decrease over a 60-min measurement period. This is connected to the slow oxidation of the sample’s surface and the formation of a thicker oxidant layer52.Fig. 5Open circuit potential (VOCP) vs Ag/AgCl reference electrode during the initial 60 min for different concentrations of compounds 1–7 in 1 mol/L HCl.

Open circuit potential (VOCP) vs Ag/AgCl reference electrode during the initial 60 min for different concentrations of compounds 1–7 in 1 mol/L HCl.

Tafel polarisation curves were used to study kinetics of the cathodic and anodic reactions. Based on Tafel polarization analysis, the majority of the tested organic inhibitors exhibited significant corrosion protection for steel in 1 mol/L HCl solution (Fig. 6). It can be observed that the addition of an inhibitor suppresses the cathodic reaction and hydrogen evolution reaction. Tafel polarization analysis showed that all tested inhibitors significantly reduce the corrosion current density (icorr) and consequently the corrosion rate (mm/year). For the most effective inhibitors, icorr decreased severalfold, even up to 20–30 times.Fig. 6Tafel plot for all inhibitors at a molar concentration of 10–3 mol/L (a) and LSV dependence on inhibitor concentration for compound 6 (b) in 1 mol/L HCl.

This sample also demonstrated a high polarization resistance (319.5 Ω) in EIS measurements, indicating the formation of a stable protective layer on the steel surface. In contrast, sample 2.5 showed inversed effect, with a corrosion rate higher than that of the uninhibited control, likely due to adverse interactions at higher inhibitor concentrations that may promote corrosion or disrupt passive barrier layer formation. The corrosion current (icorr) decreased in experiments due to the adsorption of organic inhibitor molecules onto the steel surface, forming a protective layer. This layer physically and chemically blocked active surface sites, thereby limiting both the metal dissolution (anodic) and hydrogen reduction (cathodic) reactions. The corrosion potential (Ecorr) shifted toward a more positive direction because the inhibitors predominantly affected the anodic process, reducing the release of iron ions from the surface and thus lowering surface energy and enhancing its stability. These changes were concentration-dependent, with higher inhibitor concentrations strengthening the adsorption effect and leading to a greater shift in Ecorr and a more significant decrease in icorr. Such electrochemical behaviour indicates that the studied purine-based inhibitors function primarily as anodic or mixed-type inhibitors, controlling both the anodic and cathodic reaction rates.

Based on Tafel analysis (Fig. 7), the inhibitor concentration exhibits a pronounced impact on the corrosion rate. The uninhibited control displays a corrosion rate of 25.9 ± 1.7 mm/year, serving as the baseline. Among all tested inhibitors, sample 2.3 achieves the lowest corrosion rate of 1.23 mm/year at an optimal concentration of 10–3 mol/L. In contrast, sample 2.5 shows the worst performance, with a corrosion rate of 35.4 mm/year, which even exceeds that of the uninhibited control, which seems to contradict the function of the inhibitor. However, such behaviour can be explained by the nature of the interaction of this compound with the metal surface. Under the given conditions, sample 2.5 most likely forms an unstable and heterogeneous adsorption layer, which does not provide an effective surface coverage. Instead of acting as a protective barrier, such an incomplete layer may promote the transport of aggressive ions to the metal surface or more rapidly degrade the thin natural oxide layer on the metal surface, thus accelerating the corrosion process. Such behaviour has been described in the literature in cases where inhibitor molecules are characterized by weak adsorption, competitive desorption or unfavourable orientation on the metal surface, as a result of which a stable and continuous protective layer is not formed.53Fig. 7Corrosion rate and molar concentration dependency based on Tafel analysis in 1 mol/L HCl.

A general trend is observed where most inhibitors from the 2.x, 3.x, and 4.x series exhibit rapid changes in CR at intermediate concentrations and an inflection point on the graph, whereas inhibitors from the 1.x and 7.x series show more moderate changes in corrosion rate in this concentration range. It should be noted that these concentration dependencies were measured under conditions with insufficient thermostatting of the electrochemical cell; therefore, minor temperature fluctuations may have contributed to the scatter in the data. Nevertheless, the results clearly demonstrate that each inhibitor has a distinct optimal concentration and that exceeding it can be detrimental to corrosion protection.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results confirmed that the presence of inhibitors significantly increases the polarization resistance (Rp)—even more than 10–15 times compared to the pure acid electrolyte. High Rp values directly correspond to the effective formation of the protective layer. The EIS data were successfully modelled with an equivalent circuit (demonstrated in Fig. 8) that included the solution resistance (Rs), polarization resistance (Rp), and constant phase element (CPE). The proposed scheme describes both samples with inhibitor protection and the blank sample case. EIS measurements demonstrated high data reproducibility, as evidenced by a low error margin expressed through small standard deviations in the obtained equivalent circuit parameters. This precision allowed reliable determination of the significant increase in polarization resistance (Rp) in the presence of inhibitors, which directly corresponds to the effect of protective layer formation. It was found that the relative errors in solution resistance (Rs) and the CPE dispersion parameter (n) did not exceed 5%, confirming the stability of the experimental system and the consistency of the measurements. The CPE parameter n values close to 0.9–0.94 indicated an almost ideal capacitive surface behaviour, characteristic of a homogenous and dense adsorption layer54.Fig. 8Electrochemical impedance spectra and equivalent circuit model for different inhibitors based on their molar concentration in the electrolyte in 1 mol/L HCl.

Electrochemical impedance spectra and equivalent circuit model for different inhibitors based on their molar concentration in the electrolyte in 1 mol/L HCl.

The corrosion behaviour, as illustrated by the Bode impedance and phase spectra (Fig. 9), demonstrates a clear dependence on inhibitor concentration in the electrolyte. Samples containing the inhibitor exhibit significantly higher impedance values across the frequency range compared to the blank sample, indicating enhanced corrosion resistance and the formation of a protective surface layer. Additionally, the phase angle maxima for inhibited systems shift toward higher values and broader frequency regions, suggesting improved capacitive behaviour and more effective surface coverage, while the blank sample shows lower impedance and less pronounced phase angles, characteristic of an unprotected and actively corroding surface. Overall, the results confirm that increasing inhibitor concentration leads to more efficient corrosion protection due to stronger adsorption and barrier formation on the metal surface.Fig. 9Bode impedance and phase spectral for different inhibitors based on their molar concentration in 1 mol/L HCl.

Quantum chemical calculations

Chemical calculations were performed on target compound structures in forms predicted at pH 0. Protic equilibria for compounds 1–7 is provided in ESI. Optimized inhibitor structures and electron distribution in HOMO and LUMO energy are shown in Figs. 12 and 13. Calculated quantum chemical parameters are summarized in Table 6. Energy gap (ΔE) is the minimal energy needed to bring a molecule to an excited state. Lower ΔE is associated with higher IE15, as it increases reactivity of organic substance towards the metal surface57. Softness (σ), which is an inverse value to hardness, indicates ability of inhibitor molecules to bind to metal surfaces, with higher values correlated to higher IE58,59. A high global electronegativity (χ) value could suggest that the inhibitor is less able to donate its electron to the metal surface and form an adsorption layer60, yet in other sources high electronegativity is described as beneficial towards accepting electrons from the metal surface and maintaining a strong interaction15. Number of electrons transferred (ΔN) is the electron-donating ability of an inhibitor molecule, if ΔN < 3.6, then IE increases with higher ΔN values due to increased interaction between metal and inhibitor61. Also ΔN value should be positive to achieve the highest possible IE62. Low electrophilicity (ω) has been described as beneficial towards reducing corrosion rate63. From the optimized compound geometry and HOMO and LUMO electron densities, we can draw some conclusions about the mode of adsorption of the triazolylpurines on mild steel surface. Triazoles in purine C2 and C6 position do not sterically interfere with the purine ring, resulting in a large completely planar π system, while aliphatic components of inhibitors bend out of plane (Figs. 12 and 13). Large planar π systems are ideal for parallel adsorption on the mild steel surface, which potentially leads to improved coverage of the surface and decreased corrosion rate. Analysis of HOMO and LUMO distribution reveals little difference between 1(H+), 2(H+), 3(H+), 5(H+), 6, 6(H+) and 7(H+), where both HOMO and LUMO levels are distributed across the entire π system, except for 6(H+), where only one triazole is involved in LUMO. This suggests that the inhibitors have multiple sites for nucleophilic and electrophilic attack67. We conclude that the entire triazolylpurine π systems will take part in adsorption on the mild steel surface. For compound 4, the protonation sequence is different, starting with protonation on the methylpiperazine moiety, followed by protonation of purine N7, as opposed to typical protonation at N1. This leads to HOMO and LUMO distribution being localized on either purine, triazole or piperazine, except for 4(H+) HOMO, where it is spread across purine and triazole. Conclusions

In this study, a series of novel water-soluble triazolylpurine derivatives was synthesized and evaluated as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in aqueous HCl (1 mol/L). The compounds were designed by functionalization of the purine scaffold with triazole, tetraethylene glycol, ribose, and amino-substituent motifs to improve solubility and promote adsorption on the steel surface. All target compounds showed sufficient solubility for corrosion testing and demonstrated pronounced inhibition activity, particularly at concentrations 10–3 mol/L. Gravimetric measurements revealed high inhibition efficiencies of 95.09–97.86% at this concentration, with 2,6-bis(4-(hydroxymethyl)-1H-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)-9H-purine derivative 6 showing the highest performance. Electrochemical measurements confirmed the protective effect of the inhibitors: compound 6 reached 93.48% inhibition efficiency in electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, while 2-(4-(hydroxymethyl)-1H-1,2,3-triazol-1-yl)-6-(methylamino)-9H-purine derivative 2 showed the highest efficiency by Tafel polarization, reaching 95.25%. These results demonstrate that triazolylpurine derivatives can form effective protective layers on mild steel under strongly acidic conditions.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy indicated a substantial increase in polarization resistance in the presence of inhibitors, together with CPE exponent values close to ideal capacitive behavior, supporting the formation of dense and relatively homogeneous adsorption layers. The electrochemical data suggest that the compounds act mainly through adsorption-controlled protection and behave as anodic or mixed-type inhibitors. Langmuir adsorption analysis further confirmed spontaneous adsorption on the steel surface, with Gibbs free energy values consistent with a mixed physisorption–chemisorption mechanism. The near-unity slopes and high correlation coefficients obtained from the Langmuir plots indicate that the adsorption process is well described by monolayer surface coverage.

Quantum chemical calculations supported the experimental findings by showing broadly similar electronic parameters across the inhibitor series, consistent with the high inhibition efficiencies observed at the highest tested concentration. Optimized geometries and frontier orbital distributions suggest that the planar triazolylpurine π-system can participate in adsorption parallel to the steel surface, thereby promoting effective surface coverage. Although the calculations did not allow reliable prediction of the best-performing inhibitor before experimental testing, they were consistent with the proposed adsorption-based inhibition mechanism.

Overall, the results establish triazolylpurines as a promising class of corrosion inhibitors for mild steel in acidic media. The rational functionalization of the purine scaffold provides a useful platform for further inhibitor development. The rich chemistry of purine substitution at positions C2, C6, C8, and N9 enables systematic tuning of adsorption strength, solubility, and hydrophilic–lipophilic balance. For example, the introduction of lipophilic substituent at N9 may yield compounds suitable for hydrophobic coatings. In addition, the use of copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition and nucleophilic aromatic substitution reactions offers robust and scalable synthetic routes for the introduction of structurally diverse functional groups, facilitating the exploration of their influence on corrosion inhibition performance. This approach establishes a rational framework for the development of efficient and environmentally benign corrosion inhibitors. Further studies along these lines are currently in progress and will be reported in due course.

Methods

Synthesis and characterization

Commercially available reagents were used without further purification. The reactions were monitored by HPLC and TLC analysis. Silica gel 60 F254 aluminum plates (Merck) were used for TLC, achieving visualization with UV light. Column chromatography was carried out on 60 Å, 40−63 μm silica gel. Infrared spectra were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet™ iSTM50 spectrometer in the Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) mode over the range from 400 to 4000 cm−1 (co-adding 64 scans at 4 cm−1 resolution and deducting background spectrum from the sample spectrum before every measurement). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded with a Bruker Avance 500 spectrometer in chloroform-d (CDCl3), metahnol-d4 (CD3OD), dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d6) or deuterium oxide (D2O) at 500 MHz for 1H NMR spectra and 126 MHz for 13C NMR spectra. Chemical shifts (δ) are reported in ppm and coupling constants (J) in Hz. The proton (CDCl3 δ = 7.26 ppm; CD3OD δ = 3.31 ppm; DMSO-d6 δ = 2.50 ppm; D2O δ = 4.79 ppm) and carbon signals (CDCl3 δ = 77.16 ppm; CD3OD δ = 49.00 ppm; DMSO-d6 δ = 39.52 ppm) for residual non-deuterated solvents were used as an internal reference for 1H and 13C NMR spectra, respectively. The multiplicity is assigned using standard abbreviations. The Agilent Technologies 1200 Series system was used to perform HPLC analyses, using an XBridge C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, particle size 3.5 μm). Eluent A (0.1% TFA, 5% MeCN in H2O) and eluent B (100% MeCN) were used as the mobile phase with a flow rate of 1 mL/min, using following gradient (30 to 95% of eluent B at 0–5 min; 95% of eluent B at 5–10 min; from 95 to 30% of eluent B at 10–12 min). Wavelength of detection – 260 nm. High-resolution mass spectra (ESI) were recorded with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer operating in the Full Scan mode at the 120,000 resolutions38.

Gravimetric analysis

2.0 mm mild steel sheets were employed in the testing. The elemental composition is as follows: Fe 98.95% 0.50% Bi; 0.20% Mn; 0.19% Cr; 0.09% Co; 0.04% C; 0.011% Si; 0.014% P; 0.006% S. Each sample was weighed three times and the average value was taken to ensure the reliability of the data. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the average result was used for calculations. Measurements were performed at room temperature in corrosion studies to establish a stable, reproducible baseline that allows for accurate comparison of material performance and inhibitor efficiency, as higher temperatures introduce uncontrolled acceleration that does not reliably follow the Arrhenius equation for electrochemical corrosion processes. The purine derivative inhibitors (Fig. 1) exhibit good solubility in acid solution. The concentrated hydrochloric acid (37%) was diluted into 1 mol/L HCl solution. Different concentrations (10–3, 10–4, 10–5, 10–6 mol/L) of purine derivative inhibitors were added to 1 mol/L HCl solutions, respectively. Prior to each test, the carbon steel samples were polished step by step with different grades of emery paper from 400 to 2000 grit, then washed with deionized water, degreased with ethanol and acetone, and dried for later use.

The weight loss method was used to test the static corrosion rate of carbon steel in 1 mol/L HCl solution at four concentrations of corrosion inhibitors. The carbon steel sheets were cut into specimens in sizes of 50 mm × 20 mm × 2 mm with a 4 mm diameter hole 2 mm from the top of the sample for hanging for all weight loss experiments. The total surface area A (cm2) of a specimen was measured with a vernier calliper, and the initial weight W0 (g) was weighed with an electronic balance.

The test samples were immersed in different sealed beakers filled with blank 1 mol/L HCl and 1 mol/L HCl with different concentrations of purine derivative inhibitors, respectively. The beakers were kept at a constant temperature of 25 ± 1 ◦C in a water bath, and the immersion time of the samples was 120 h. After the immersion, the test samples were taken out and the loose corrosion product on the surface was removed with a soft brush, then put into a 1 L pickling solution containing 5 wt.% HCl and 10 g hexamethylenetetramine for ultrasonic cleaning for 5 min, followed by a rinse with deionized water, ethanol and acetone for 1 min then dried. Electrochemical analysis

Metrohm Autolab Potentiostat PGSTAT302N (The Netherlands) was used to characterize electrochemical corrosion processes in different modes. Electrochemical measurements were performed in a standard three-electrode system, with a cylindrical Pt mesh electrode (d-5 cm, h-6 cm, 50-mesh) as a counter electrode, Ag/AgCl (Metrohm, 3 M KCl) as a reference electrode and a steel plate (20 × 20 × 2 mm) of the same metal content as used in gravimetric analysis as a working electrode. The electrochemical cell was lab-made, with a bottom-mounted design. The PTFE cell was designed so that the working electrode mounts from the bottom, with an exposed area of 1 cm2. The reference electrode was mounted in close proximity to the working electrode’s surface. 20 mL of electrolyte (1 M HCl with an inhibitor) was used in all cases. Before all electrochemical measurements, the open circuit potential (VOCP) was measured for 60 min. Linear polarization curves (LSV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) were measured against the VOCP. LSV was measured at 1 mV/s in a potential interval of -0.7 to 0.4 V vs VOCP. A scan rate of 1 mV/s was used in corrosion measurements to ensure steady-state conditions at the electrode surface, which allows for accurate Tafel extrapolation and reproducible determination of corrosion current density. Higher scan rates can distort polarization curves, preventing the extraction of precise kinetic parameters68. All experiments were performed in triplicate and the average result was used for calculations.
