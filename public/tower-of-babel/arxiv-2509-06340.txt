A flat-mode perspective on the boson peak in amorphous solids
Shivam Mahajan 1, Long-Zhou Huang 2,3, Cunyuan Jiang 4,5, Yun-Jiang Wang 2,3, Massimo Pica Ciamarra 1 , 6 {}^{1},^{6}, Jie Zhang 4,7, Matteo Baggioli 4,5

Abstract
The boson peak is a characteristic anomaly of amorphous solids broadly defined as a low-energy excess in the density of states and heat capacity compared to the textbook predictions of Debye theory. The origin of this anomaly has long been the subject of ongoing debate and remains a topic of active controversy. While remaining agnostic about the microscopic origin of the phenomenon, we propose that the boson peak (BP) may universally originate from a dispersionless, optic-like excitation, which we refer to as the ‘ flat mode ’. We revisit both experimental and simulation data from the literature through this lens and conduct further simulations in 2D and 3D amorphous systems. These analyses collectively provide supporting evidence for this interpretation. Notably, if this is indeed the case, a striking analogy emerges with similar anomalies observed in crystalline materials, where the nonphononic flat mode is effectively replaced by anomalously low-energy optical phonons.



INTRODUCTION

Our understanding of the vibrational density of states (VDOS) and heat capacity of ideal crystalline materials is rooted in a century-old theory: the Debye model
Debye
. This well-established paradigm is based on the premise that the only relevant low-energy excitations in crystals are propagating collective plane waves, also named acoustic phonons. Neglecting dissipation, which would otherwise lead to attenuation, and ignoring different polarizations and possible anisotropies, these sound waves follow a linear dispersion relation
\omega=vq
(see blue solid line in Fig.
1
), where
\omega
,
q
, and
v
denote the frequency, wavevector, and propagation speed, respectively.

The properties of these acoustic phonons can be derived either through a microscopic normal mode analysis
Moon 2023
, or more abstractly, from the spontaneous breaking of translational symmetry
Leutwyler 1997
, that is, the formation of a periodic, ordered lattice. Leaveraging on these concepts, the vibrational density of states of ideal crystals takes the well-known form:

g(\omega)\propto\omega^{D-1},

(1)

where
D
is the number of spatial dimensions and numerical prefactors are omitted for simplicity. Equation (
1
), referred to hereafter as the Debye law, holds in the low-frequency regime where the phonon dispersion remains linear. In the Debye approximation, this linearity is assumed to extend up to a maximum cutoff frequency, the Debye frequency
\omega_{D}
, beyond which
g(\omega)
vanishes.

Dispersion relation
\omega(q)
of an acoustic phonon (blue), a low-lying optical mode (red) and a dispersionless flat mode (black).

By further recognizing that phonons are quantum mechanical in nature, specifically, bosonic excitations, Debye also derived an expression for the heat capacity of crystals, which in the low-temperature limit takes the universal form:

C(T)\propto T^{D}.

(2)

Both of Debye’s predictions have been experimentally confirmed in crystalline materials and stand as one of the most successful paradigms in solid-state physics
Kittel and McEuen 2018
.

However, in 1971, Zeller and Pohl
Zeller and Pohl 1971
reported that noncrystalline solids present characteristic anomalies in their heat capacity that evade the Debye paradigm. More precisely, they observed an anomalous peak in the normalized heat capacity
C(T)/T^{3}
around
\approx 10
K. This feature is now known as the “
boson peak
” (BP). Moreover, they speculate that this departure from the Debye specific heat may be characteristic of the glassy state, or of amorphous solids in general. Later, in the mid-1980s, Buchenau and colleagues
Buchenau et al. 1984
;
Buchenau et al. 1986
convincingly demonstrated that this anomaly was not related to the Bose-Einstein distribution, from where it takes its name, but rather stemmed from an excess of vibrational modes visible in the density of states, exceeding the prediction of the Debye law. This finding was subsequently confirmed by inelastic neutron scattering and various other experimental techniques (e.g.,
Hehlen et al. 2000
;
Sette et al. 1998
;
Chumakov et al. 2004
;
Kabeya et al. 2016
), and it is now widely accepted as an established fact.

Despite more than half a century of experimental investigations and theoretical modeling, the origin of the boson peak in amorphous solids remains highly controversial and often the subject of debate and disagreement. We refer to the excellent book by Ramos
Ramos 2022
for an extensive review of the
status quo
.

In this work, we combine a re-analysis of existing data with new measurements to revisit the nature of the boson peak in amorphous solids.
Except for a brief discussion at the end, we intentionally refrain from speculating about its microscopic origin, and corresponding theoretical models to rationalize it.

Our starting point is the recent observation in simulated glassy systems
Hu and Tanaka 2022
that the boson peak corresponds to a flat, dispersionless mode, clearly visible in both the dynamical structure factor
S(q,\omega)
and the vibrational spectrum.
More precisely, we refer to an optic-like collective excitation with a dispersion of the form
\omega\approx\omega_{0}
, independent of the wavevector
q
.
This mode is depicted in black in Fig.
1
.
The key finding in
Hu and Tanaka 2022
;
Hu and Tanaka 2023
is that the characteristic energy
\omega_{0}
matches the boson peak frequency
\omega_{\text{BP}}
in both 2D and 3D systems.

This dispersionless mode naturally produces a pronounced peak in the vibrational density of states at
\omega=\omega_{0}\approx\omega_{\text{BP}}
, potentially broadened by disorder or thermal effects. Intuitively, this arises because the density of states
g(\omega)
is inversely proportional to the slope of the dispersion relation
\omega(q)
, namely
g(\omega)\propto(d\omega/dq)^{-1}
. Any flat region in
\omega(q)
inevitably yields a peak in
g(\omega)
, irrespective of its microscopic origin. This is precisely the same mechanism underlying van Hove singularities in crystals.

Hence, if the observation of a flat nonphononic band at the BP frequency were confirmed across all amorphous solids, the central question would then shift to understanding the microscopic origin of such a dispersionless mode. It is, therefore, essential to determine whether this phenomenon is universal and, indeed, has already been observed or considered in other systems, including experimental results.

Interestingly, as noted in the
Introduction
of
Ramos 2022
, as early as 1959, prior to the seminal work of Zeller and Pohl, Flubacher et al.
Flubacher et al. 1959
suggested that the excess heat capacity in vitreous silica was due to low-frequency optical modes detected via Raman scattering. This idea was later revisited in depth by Nakayama; see
Nakayama 2002
for a comprehensive review.

In this work, we investigate the possibility that the boson peak is universally associated with the emergence of an optic-like, dispersionless mode.
We support this scenario with substantial evidence, combining new numerical analyses with a re-examination of existing experimental data and prior results on the dynamical structure factor.

Specifically, (i) we show that this phenomenon was present, but previously overlooked, in experimental data on a two-dimensional (2D) jammed packing of photoelastic disks, (ii) we show that this feature was already evident in the experimental dynamical structure factor of several disordered systems ranging from granular packings and silicate glasses, to metallic glasses and polymers, and (iii) we provide additional simulation evidence for this scenario in systems of polydisperse particles interacting via a Lennard-Jones-like potential in both 2D and 3D, along with a simulated 3D Cu-Zr metallic glass.

Despite similar ideas having surfaced in the past, the most compelling simulation evidence for the connection between the boson peak and a dispersionless flat mode has emerged only recently, in a series of works by Hu and Tanaka
Hu and Tanaka 2022
;
Hu and Tanaka 2023
, which have strongly inspired the explorations presented in this Perspective.

In
Hu and Tanaka 2022
, extensive molecular dynamics simulations were carried out for a two-dimensional bidisperse system in which particles interact via a power-law potential,
V(r)\propto r^{-10}
. This analysis was later extended in
Hu and Tanaka 2023
to three-dimensional (3D) model glasses with isotropic repulsive and attractive interactions.

A key observation from both studies is that the dynamic structure factor for transverse fluctuations,
S_{T}(q,\omega)
, with
q
and
\omega
denoting the wavevector and frequency, respectively, cannot be described solely by acoustic phonons modeled via a damped harmonic oscillator (see Fig.
2
(a)). Instead, a distinct contribution clearly emerges above a certain wavevector. Remarkably, this contribution was captured by an asymmetric peak structure, well-fitted using a log-normal function without any evident wavevector dependence. By incorporating both phononic and optic-like terms, the numerical dynamic structure factor could be accurately reconstructed.

The central result of this analysis is that the energy associated with the optic-like mode is independent of
q
and aligns almost exactly with the boson peak frequency, as independently extracted from the Debye-reduced density of states (see Fig.
2
(b)).
Setting aside the microscopic interpretation proposed in those studies, these empirical findings strongly suggest that the BP stems from a dispersionless mode and has, therefore, an optic-like nature.

Very recently, Ref.
Mizuno et al. 2025
performed extended numerical simulations for Silica (SiO
2
) glass, harmonic sphere (HS) glasses and Lennard-Jones (LJ) glass. For all these systems, a wavenumber-independent nonphononic band was observed in the dynamical structure factor at the BP energy (see Fig.
3
for a sample of their results).

In the following, we present simulation and experimental evidence suggesting that the relation between BP and a dispersionless mode is universal, i.e., independent of specific interactions, compositions, or system details.

(ii)

Polydisperse systems interacting via a family of LJ-like potentials whose attractive tail extends up to a distance modulated by a parameter

;
Chattoraj and Ciamarra 2020

Previous works have demonstrated that decreasing

Chattoraj and Ciamarra 2020
;
González-López et al. 2021a
;
González-López et al. 2021b

Zheng et al. 2021

In the following, we consider

and

, focusing on

The density depends on the dimensionality so that the interparticle spacing is fixed,
a_{0}=\rho^{-1/D}\simeq 0.977
.
The systems are equilibrated at high temperatures, well above their respective onset temperature, and the energy is then minimized via CJ.
Results are averaged over
50

(iii)

A model three-dimensional
\mathrm{Cu_{50}Zr_{50}}

EXPERIMENTAL EVIDENCE

After presenting substantial evidence for the universality of the flat BP mode in simulated glasses, we now turn to the question of whether experimental evidence supporting its existence can be found. Since many of the presented figures are taken from the existing literature, we will use the symbols
q
, and
k
interchangeably to indicate the wavevector.

Granular packing

We begin by revisiting the experimental data and theoretical interpretation presented by some of us in Refs.
Wang et al. 2018
and
Jiang et al. 2024a
.

The experimental setup of Ref.
Wang et al. 2018
consists of a two-dimensional jammed packing of bidisperse photoelastic disks (see Fig.
5
(a)), in which interparticle forces can be measured with high precision. By employing advanced image processing and force measurement techniques (see also Refs.
Majmudar and Behringer 2005
;
Zhang et al. 2017
), the harmonic Hessian matrix was reconstructed. Using standard diagonalization methods, the authors were then able to extract the vibrational density of states, as well as the transverse and longitudinal current correlation functions, which are directly related to the dynamical structure factor
Binder and Kob 2011
.

In Ref.
Wang et al. 2018
, the current correlation functions were modeled using a single phononic contribution, corresponding to longitudinal and transverse acoustic phonons.
While this approximation appears adequate for the longitudinal component (see the blue curves in Fig. 3 of Ref.
Wang et al. 2018
), the interpretation becomes more delicate for the transverse part, which we reproduce in
Fig.
5
(b).
Indeed, only at low wavevectors, specifically
k=0.85
and
1.54

C_{T}(k,\omega)
exhibits a peak which can be accurately fitted by a damped harmonic oscillator (DHO) form,

C^{\text{DHO}}_{T}(k,\omega)\sim\frac{1}{\left(\omega^{2}-\Omega_{T}^{2}(k)\right)^{2}+\Gamma_{T}^{2}\omega^{2}},

(3)

where
\Omega_{T}(k)
and
\Gamma_{T}(k)
denote the frequency and decay rate of the acoustic phonon mode.

For large values of the wavevector
k
, a pronounced low-frequency shoulder clearly emerges, indicating the presence of an additional contribution that was not accounted for in Ref.
Wang et al. 2018
.
This shoulder becomes increasingly pronounced with growing

Following Ref.
Hu and Tanaka 2022
, we model this optic-like contribution using a
k
-independent log-normal function, without attributing to it any particular physical significance:

C^{\text{nph}}_{T}(k,\omega)\sim\frac{1}{\omega}\,\exp\left(-\frac{(\ln\omega-\mu)^{2}}{\alpha}\right),

(4)

As shown in Fig.
5
(b), modeling the total correlation function as the sum of a DHO term [Eq. (
3

4

Importantly, the characteristic energy scale of the non-dispersive mode described by Eq. (
4

5

CHALLENGING OBSERVATIONS

Motivated by the well-known principle that falsifying a scientific hypothesis is often easier than confirming it, we examined the existing literature in search of counterexamples.
The only relevant study we identified is Ref.
Monaco and Mossa 2009
, which reports a molecular dynamics investigation of a monodisperse Lennard-Jones glass.
In that work, no flat, dispersionless mode at the BP frequency was observed (see Fig. 1 therein).
However, the range of wavevectors
q
explored may have been too limited to resolve the BP mode, particularly since its dispersion could significantly overlap with that of acoustic phonons.
Furthermore, the transverse structure factor was obtained from the velocity autocorrelation function, which is more delicate than the direct diagonalization of the Hessian used in our analysis of numerical systems, even though the two approaches are formally equivalent.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we have compiled all known evidence, both supporting and opposing, the hypothesis that the boson peak anomaly in amorphous solids is consistently associated with the emergence of a flat, dispersionless, optic-like mode. Alongside this review of existing literature, we presented new simulation results for two- and three-dimensional model glasses, as well as fresh analyses of experimental data from two-dimensional granular packings.

Taken together, these results strongly support the validity of our hypothesis across a broad range of systems, including granular matter, polymers, silica, and metallic glasses. While our findings do not resolve the microscopic origin of the boson peak, they provide a unified and intuitive perspective with potentially far-reaching implications for theoretical models and future research.

Although our primary aim was to present the available facts, we close with brief remarks on their broader significance. We also encourage the community to further probe the presence of this flat boson peak mode in their preferred models and materials, to establish a consensus on its universality.

Implications for theories of the BP

Our observations suggest that a valid theory of the boson peak should:

1.

include at least one low-energy excitation that is optic-like and clearly distinct from acoustic phonons;

2.

predict the emergence of this additional low-energy mode whose dispersion relation is approximately flat in wavevector;

3.

demonstrate that the energy of this dispersionless mode coincides with the excess observed in the vibrational density of states.

If accepted, these constraints challenge various proposed theories for the boson peak in glasses (see
Ramos 2022
for a review of the most famous approaches), as the only ones that predict, allow, or naturally coexist with a flat, non-dispersive, optic-like mode appear to be the soft-potential model (quasi-localized modes)

Determining which existing or new theoretical framework most accurately describes the boson peak is closely connected to understanding the microscopic nature of the flat BP mode. Once a model successfully reproduces the criteria (1)-(3) outlined above, several fundamental questions remain: What is the microscopic origin of this mode? Specifically, which microscopic dynamics give rise to it? Is its nature harmonic or anharmonic? Is the hybridization of such mode with acoustic phonons relevant or just tangential to the BP problem? What sets its characteristic energy, and how does it evolve as the temperature increases toward the liquid state? Is this mode related to the widely discussed low-energy quasilocalised excitations in structural glasses

While some preliminary progress has been made using specific models of the boson peak, much remains to be understood.
