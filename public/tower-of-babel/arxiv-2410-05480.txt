Self-Similar Singular Solutions to the Nonlinear Schrödinger and the Complex Ginzburg-Landau Equations
Joel Dahne, Jordi-Lluís Figueras

Abstract
We prove the existence of several branches of radial self-similar singular solutions for the mass supercritical complex Ginzburg–Landau equation, including the nonlinear Schrödinger equation as a special case. We are also able to control the number of monotone intervals of the profiles associated with these self-similar solutions. In particular, we prove the existence of monotone radial self-similar singular solutions for the three-dimensional cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation. The paper combines sharp analytic bounds on the self-similar profile at infinity with computer-assisted bounds around zero and their matching at an intermediate point.

1
Introduction

We address the problem of self-similar blowup for the complex
Ginzburg–Landau (CGL) equation,

i\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+(1-i\epsilon)\Delta u+(1+i\delta)|u|^{2\sigma}u=0\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

(1)

Here the parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
are nonnegative real
numbers and
\sigma
is positive. Our interest lies in the existence
of branches of such self-similar solutions when varying the parameter

\epsilon
(fixing
\delta=0
). These branches include the point

\epsilon=\delta=0
, corresponding to the focusing nonlinear
Schrödinger (NLS) equation. As a result, we also prove self-similar
blowup for the NLS equation.

1.1
The NLS case

The study of self-similar blowup for the NLS equation has a long
history; let us therefore start by describing our result in the
context of this special case. The NLS equation is one of the
fundamental equations in the study of nonlinear waves and the
questions of well-posedness and finite-time blowup are classical
problems. The existence of blowup solutions and sufficient conditions
for their occurrence were established by Glassey in
1977
[
36
]
. Since then, many researchers have sought to
characterize the behavior of these solutions.

The nature and existence of the blowup depend on the value of the
criticality constant
s_{c}:=\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{\sigma}
. In
the energy-subcritical case,
s_{c}<1
, the Cauchy problem is
locally well-posed in the energy space
H^{1}
. In the
mass-subcritical regime,
s_{c}<0
, there is global existence for

H^{1}
initial data, and hence no blowup occurs,
see
[
13
,
11
]
. For both the mass-critical
(
s_{c}=0
) and mass-supercritical but energy-subcritical
(
0<s_{c}<1
) cases, a large number of numerical studies in the
1980s and 1990s indicated the possibility of radial self-similar
blowup. An excellent reference is the comprehensive monograph by Sulem
and Sulem
[
72
]
.

For the critical case, the numerics indicated that these solutions
were only asymptotically self-similar. The questions of existence,
rate of convergence and stability for these solutions were finally
settled in a remarkable series of papers by Merle and
Raphaël
[
54
,
52
,
51
,
53
]
(see
also
[
62
]
), which provide a detailed description of the
phenomena.

Our main interest in this paper is the supercritical case. For
slightly supercritical parameters,
0<s_{c}\ll 1
, the existence
of self-similar blowup has been established,
see
[
67
,
68
,
56
,
55
,
3
]
. For larger values of
s_{c}
, which in particular
include the three-dimensional cubic NLS equation
(
s_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
), the question has remained unsolved for a
long time and the conjectured behavior is primarily based on numerical
simulations. However, while this manuscript was in preparation,
Donninger and Schörkhuber
[
24
]
gave a remarkable proof
of self-similar blowup for the three-dimensional cubic NLS equation,
which we discuss further below.

Before stating our results, let us describe the existing numerics for
the supercritical case in more detail. For the radial
self-similar solutions that we are interested in,
Zakharov
[
79
]
proposes the profile

u(x,t)=\dfrac{1}{(2\kappa(T-t))^{\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}+i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}\right)}}Q\left(\dfrac{|x|}{(2\kappa(T-t))^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right),

(2)

where
\kappa,\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and

Q\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
is a profile with asymptotic behavior

Q(\xi)\sim\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}\text{ as }\xi\to\infty.

Inserting the ansatz (
2
) into
Equation (
1
) gives the singular ODE

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q-\omega Q+(1+i\delta)|Q|^{2\sigma}Q,

(3)

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle Q(\xi)

\displaystyle\sim\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}\text{ as }\xi\to\infty.

For arbitrary values of
\kappa
and
\omega
, the equation does
not have a solution; the unknowns are hence

(Q(\xi),\kappa,\omega)
. For
\lambda>0
, there is a scaling
symmetry taking a solution
(Q(\xi),\kappa,\omega)
to another
solution

(\lambda^{1/\sigma}Q(\lambda\xi),\lambda^{2}\kappa,\lambda^{2}\omega)
. This allows us to fix
\omega=1
and look
for solutions
(Q(\xi),\kappa)
. Numerical simulations by Budd,
Chen and Russell
[

indicate the existence of a countable
set of

form of “multi-bump” solutions, which (in some parameter regimes) seem
to be characterized by the number of monotone intervals (the monotone
index) of
|Q|
. Of these, only the first solution, the monotone
one, appears to be (nonlinearly) stable. Sulem and
Sulem
[
72
]
refer to solutions of (
3
) for which

|Q|
is monotonically decreasing as “admissible solutions”. Based
on these numerical results, the following conjecture can be
formulated:

Conjecture 1.1

(NLS)
.

The mass-supercritical, but energy-subcritical, NLS equation has a
countable number of nontrivial, radial, self-similar singular
solutions associated with solutions of Equation (
3

first of these solutions has a monotone profile

Our first contribution in this paper is to provide a partial answer to

the choices considered in the work by Plecháč and
Šverák
[
63

, which we discuss further below, corresponding
to the following two cases:

Case I

\sigma=2.3
, corresponding to

.

Case II

d=3
and
\sigma=1
, corresponding to

s_{c}=\frac{1}{2}
. This is the three-dimensional cubic case.

More precisely, we have the following two results:

(

There exist 8 nontrivial radial self-similar singular solutions

Remark 1.4
.

In the two theorems above, the solutions are characterized by their
monotone index, similar to the results of Budd, Chen and
Russell
[
9
]
. In general, however, this does not appear to
be the case. For example, the third solution for the

e.g.
[

.

1.2
The CGL case

Next, we investigate these solutions as one moves into the regime of
the CGL equation. The CGL equation has a long history in physics,
serving as a model equation for a vast variety of phenomena, from
nonlinear waves and second-order phase transitions, to
superconductivity and superfluidity. We refer the reader to the review
paper
[
1
]
for many more details. Our interest in this
problem comes from the work by Plecháč and Šverák
[
63
]
.
One of their motivations for studying the CGL equation is the analogy
between the three-dimensional cubic CGL equation and the
Navier–Stokes equation. The two equations share the same scaling
properties and energy identity. The existence and partial regularity
theory for the two equations are also similar
[
78
]
. In fact,
a number of works in the 1990s studied the CGL equation as a model for
the Navier–Stokes equation, see
e.g.
[
4
,
23
]
.

Using a combination of asymptotic analysis and numerical simulations,
Plecháč and Šverák provided strong evidence that the self-similar
solutions of the NLS equation persist for small values of

\epsilon
, forming branches of solutions as
\epsilon
is varied
(see Figure
1
). Some of these solutions
were further studied numerically by Budd, Rottschäfer and
Williams
[
8
]
. In the case of asymptotically small

\kappa
, self-similar solutions of this type were proved to exist
by Rottschäfer
[
66
,
65
]
. The CGL equation
is also known to support other, non-self-similar, types of
blowup
[
50
,
12
,
27
,
25
,
26
]
. For global existence results,
see
[
71
,
17
,
16
]
.

Our second contribution in this paper is to prove the existence of
some of the branches of self-similar singular solutions studied by
Plecháč and Šverák. For the one-dimensional case, a summary of the
results is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5

(
Case I
: CGL equation)
.

The solutions in Theorem
1.2
extend into branches of
solutions for the CGL equation. Along these branches, the number of
monotone intervals of the profile
|Q|
remains constant.

We refer to Section
4
and
Theorem
4.1
for a more precise statement.
In
Case II
, the problem is computationally more challenging;
see Theorem
4.4
for the precise results we
prove in this case. We also give some extended results for

Case II
in Section
9
, where we
prove pointwise existence for a larger parameter range, but do not
prove that they necessarily connect into branches.

Remark 1.6
.

Previous numerical studies of these branches show that when plotted
in the
\epsilon
-
\kappa
space, they have a single turning
point in
\epsilon
, but otherwise appear monotone in these
parameters. Our results indicate that this does not, in fact,
capture the full behavior for all branches. In particular, for the
three-dimensional cubic case, our numerical results indicate that
the third branch has two turning points in
\kappa
, and the fifth
branch has multiple turning points in both
\epsilon
and

\kappa
(see Figure
1(b)
). This
behavior is not seen in
[
63
]
because the continuation of
the branches stops right before reaching the first turning point in

\kappa
(a likely explanation being that they did not expect this
behavior, and their numerical scheme was therefore not adapted to
handle it). In
[
8
]
, only the first two branches are
studied, which do not show this behavior. Our rigorous results
presented here only cover the first turning point in
\kappa
for
the third branch. Further numerical exploration of these phenomena
would be desirable. For example, it is at this point not clear if
the fifth branch eventually turns back to start approaching the
origin.

Remark 1.7
.

Both Plecháč and Šverák
[
63
]
as well as Budd,
Rottschäfer and Williams
[
8
]
study the stability of the
solutions along the branches. For the three-dimensional cubic case,
their results indicate that the top half of the first branch and the
bottom half of the second branch are stable, and the other parts of
the branches are unstable. The parallels between the CGL equation
and the Navier–Stokes equation make the existence of unstable
self-similar blowup particularly interesting, as this is one of the
possible scenarios regarding the question of well-posedness for
Navier–Stokes. This is, for example, mentioned as a possibility
in
[
76
]
, where they numerically isolate unstable
self-similar solutions for the Euler equation. However, there may be
constraints on the extent to which sufficiently unstable solutions
can exist
[
15
]
.

1.3
Proof strategy

Our strategy, discussed in more detail in
Section
2
, is based on a shooting method
inspired by the work of Plecháč and Šverák. The main idea is to
construct two solutions,
Q_{0}
and
Q_{\infty}
, to
Equation (
3
), with
Q_{0}
satisfying the boundary
condition at zero and
Q_{\infty}
the condition at infinity. We
then need to show that with the right choice of parameters, these two
solutions match at an intermediate point, forming a global solution
satisfying both boundary conditions. The approach shares similarities
with that used by Bahri, Martel and Raphaël
[
3
]
and
Rottschäfer
[
65
]
, an important difference being that
we cannot rely on smallness of any of the parameters to prove the
required matching condition. Instead, we make use of computer-assisted
methods to establish the necessary conditions. This requires obtaining
effective, not only asymptotic, bounds for
Q_{0}
and

Q_{\infty}
.

One of the main challenges is that the equation has an irregular
singular point at
\infty
. To handle this, we take inspiration from
the asymptotic analysis done by Plecháč and Šverák, computing
asymptotic expansions for the solution
Q_{\infty}
. However,
obtaining effective bounds from these expansions requires bounds on
all the remainder terms involved. Acquiring these effective bounds is
one of the most challenging aspects of the proof. This difficulty is
amplified by the fact that we need bounds that are uniform near

\epsilon=0
. Away from zero, one can use simpler bounds, but they
deteriorate as
\epsilon
approaches zero. Treating only the case

\epsilon=0
would also simplify the work, since several
parameters become exactly zero.

For
Q_{0}
, the approach is split into two parts. Near the origin,
the singularity at
0
is handled using a Taylor expansion with
explicit bounds for the remainder. Away from the origin, the solution
is then continued to sufficiently large
\xi
values using a
rigorous numerical ODE solver. At this stage, we are dealing with an
ODE on a finite interval bounded away from
0
, meaning that there
are no singularities in the path. For this setting, there is a large
amount of work available on the rigorous computation of solutions. We
make use of a solver from the CAPD library
[
45
]
based on
taking successive steps using Taylor expansions with rigorous error
bounds. Another common approach is the use of spectral methods, see
e.g.
[
47
,
41
,
5
,
7
]
.

The primary need for computer assistance arises within the
intermediate regime that bridges the local Taylor expansion at the
origin with the asymptotic series at infinity. This part is inherently
nonperturbative, and obtaining effective bounds without the help of
the computer seems difficult. To connect the intermediate regime with
the two endpoints, we must also explicitly evaluate the effective
bounds coming from the respective expansions. This evaluation is,
however, fairly standard, requiring only the evaluation of a couple of
special functions and a (large) number of explicit constants.

While most of the mathematical difficulty lies in the derivation of
the effective bounds for the asymptotic expansion of
Q_{\infty}
,
the technical difficulties for a complete implementation of the
computer-assisted proof are also substantial. In particular, tracking
the branches for the CGL equation requires a significant amount of
work. For example, the highly non-uniform computational difficulty
along the branches requires the use of adaptive bisection approaches
that scale across hundreds of cores.

In a recent independent work, Donninger and
Schörkhuber
[
24
]
proved the existence of a
self-similar singular solution to the three-dimensional cubic NLS
equation, corresponding to the first profile of our second theorem.
Their approach similarly relies on a combination of quantitative
analysis and computer assistance, but operates under a different
paradigm. They compactify the domain and formulate a fixed-point
problem centered around an explicit polynomial approximation. The
fixed-point setup is complicated by the fact that the associated
linear operator itself depends on the unknown
\kappa
. To handle
this, they introduce a specialized functional that vanishes at the
correct parameter value. The existence of the fixed point is then
proved by computing several bounds related to the linear operator and
the functional. The explicit polynomial provides the global control
required to connect the two endpoints, serving a role analogous to the
rigorous ODE solver used for the intermediate regime in our approach.

For an introduction to the type of computer assistance and rigorous
interval arithmetic that is used in this paper, see, for example, the
book
[
74
]
for a general introduction and
[
59
,
37
]
for more PDE-oriented discussions. These
techniques have yielded significant results; see, for example,
classical results such as the existence of universality in the
quadratic map (period-doubling cascade)
[
46
]
, the
double-bubble conjecture
[
39
]
, the existence of strange
attractors in the Lorenz system
[
73
]
or the solution to
the Kepler conjecture
[
38
]
. Moreover, computer-assisted
proofs have been successful in proving the existence of homoclinic and
heteroclinic orbits in different systems
[
35
,
77
,
40
,
58
]
. Other results for PDEs appear
in
[
28
,
29
,
2
]
, and in Hamiltonian
systems in
[
49
,
14
,
30
,
21
,
10
]
.

2
A rigorous shooting method

The shooting method is a classical numerical method for solving
boundary value problems by reducing them to a combination of an
initial value problem and a zero finding problem: one searches for
initial conditions on one of the boundaries for which the solution
also satisfies the boundary condition at the other boundary. We give a
rigorous version of this approach that, under the right conditions,
allows us to prove the existence of a solution to the boundary value
problem. Our version mirrors the approach used by Plecháč and
Šverák
[
63
]
when numerically computing the branches for
the CGL equation, the main difference being the use of rigorous error
bounds and a slight variation in how the solution at infinity is
handled.

Our starting point is to construct two partial solutions to
Equation (
3
), the first one satisfying the boundary condition
at zero and the second one satisfying the boundary condition at
infinity. Both of these functions depend on several parameters and
the goal is to find parameters such that these solutions agree at an
intermediate point, forming a global solution satisfying all required
boundary conditions.

We denote the solution starting from infinity by
Q_{\infty}(\xi)
. It satisfies

\begin{split}(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q_{\infty}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q_{\infty}^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q_{\infty}^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q_{\infty}-\omega Q_{\infty}+(1+i\delta)|Q_{\infty}|^{2\sigma}Q_{\infty}&=0,\\
Q_{\infty}(\xi)&\sim\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}\quad\text{as }\xi\to\infty.\end{split}

(4)

The boundary condition at infinity is, however, not enough to
determine the solution uniquely. As discussed by Plecháč and
Šverák
[
63
]
, one expects a one dimensional (complex)
manifold of solutions. We will (locally) parameterize this manifold by

\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
(the details of this parameterization are
discussed in Section
7
). For our purposes,
the dependence on the parameters
\kappa
and
\epsilon
is also
important; to emphasize this dependence, we use the notation

Q_{\infty}(\xi)=Q_{\infty}(\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi)
.

We denote the solution starting from zero by
Q_{0}(\xi)
. It is the
unique solution to the following initial value problem

\begin{split}(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q_{0}^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q_{0}^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q_{0}-\omega Q_{0}+(1+i\delta)|Q_{0}|^{2\sigma}Q_{0}&=0,\\
Q_{0}(0)&=\mu,\\
Q_{0}^{\prime}(0)&=0.\end{split}

(5)

Again, we emphasize the dependence on the parameters
\mu
,

\kappa
and
\epsilon
with the notation

Q_{0}(\xi)=Q_{0}(\mu,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi)
. Solutions could be
defined on
[0,\infty)
, however, generically they have large
oscillations as
\xi\to\infty
, and hence do not have finite
energy.

Since our ODE is of second order, the required matching condition is
that the values and derivatives of
Q_{0}
and

Q_{\infty}
should match at some point
0<\xi_{1}<\infty
. If
indeed we have parameters
\mu
,
\gamma
,
\kappa
and

\epsilon
such that

Q_{0}(\mu,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})=Q_{\infty}(\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})\text{ and }Q_{0}^{\prime}(\mu,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})=Q_{\infty}^{\prime}(\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1}),

(6)

then the two functions can be glued together to form a global function
satisfying both boundary conditions. With this condition, it is
natural to define the following map

G(\mu,\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon)=\left(Q_{0}(\mu,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})-Q_{\infty}(\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1}),Q_{0}^{\prime}(\mu,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})-Q_{\infty}^{\prime}(\gamma,\kappa,\epsilon;\xi_{1})\right)\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}.

(7)

By identifying
\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we can
interpret
G
as a map from
\mathbb{R}^{5}
to

\mathbb{R}^{4}
. A zero of the function
G
then corresponds to a
matching as in (
6
), and ultimately implies the
existence of a self-similar solution to the CGL equation.

In the NLS case, which fixes
\epsilon=0
,
G
is a function
from
\mathbb{R}^{4}
to
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and the zero set is
expected to consist of isolated points. The existence of such zeros is
asserted in Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
, and
discussed more in depth in Section
5
. In the general
case, which leaves
\epsilon
free, the zero set is expected to be
given by curves in
\mathbb{R}^{5}
. These curves form the branches
of self-similar singular solutions that are the topic of
Sections
4
and
6
.

(

5.1

Recall that the proof of existence is based on proving the existence
of a zero of the map

, defined in

since we are
considering the NLS case). By splitting

as a map from

\midint(X)-YG(\midint(X))+(I-YJ_{G}(X))(X-\midint(X))\subseteq\operatorname{int}(X),

denotes the interior of

denotes a box enclosing the Jacobian of

and

is a

of the midpoint of

satisfying this
condition, then the function
G
has a unique zero in
X
, and
this zero is contained in the set given by the left-hand side of the
above expression.

The first step in finding the set

is to find a good numerical
(noninterval) approximation of the zero. This is done by starting with
a rough approximation, which is refined using a few standard Newton
iterations. The resulting approximation, rounded to 16 digits, is

(\mu_{0},\gamma_{0},\kappa_{0})=(1.885656963035410,1.713600956210395-1.491793873532361i,0.9173561176773725).

(9)

Using interval arithmetic to enclose

G(\mu_{0},\gamma_{0},\kappa_{0})
, we obtain the interval box

G(\mu_{0},\gamma_{0},\kappa_{0})\in\big([-6.0\cdot 10^{-12},9.2\cdot 10^{-12}],[-1.8\cdot 10^{-12},1.4\cdot 10^{-11}],\\

we can take the curve to be parameterized by
\epsilon
. We are then
looking for a curve of the form

(\epsilon,\mu(\epsilon),\kappa(\epsilon))
, with

\epsilon\in[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]
. In practice, the curve we
compute will also contain the value of
\gamma

given by

(\epsilon,\mu(\epsilon),\gamma(\epsilon),\kappa(\epsilon))
, but
in the statement of Theorem
4.1
the

\gamma

To prove the existence, the interval
[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]

is split into
N
consecutive subintervals

[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\bm{\epsilon}_{i}.

For each subinterval
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
the proof of existence
and uniqueness follows exactly the same approach as in the example for
the NLS equation in Section
5.1
. The only
difference being that for the NLS equation we fixed
\epsilon=0

whereas in this case we do the computations with
\epsilon

represented by the interval
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
. The properties of
interval arithmetic then ensures that any results we get out of
this procedure, in this case enclosures of existence and uniqueness,
are valid for any
\epsilon\in\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
. For the
computations to succeed the subintervals
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}

cannot be too wide, otherwise we cannot compute tight enough
enclosures to verify the interval Newton
condition (
8

here, it is enough to split
[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]
uniformly
into
N=32
subintervals.

The procedure produces, for each
\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
, two boxes,

\bm{\mu}_{i}^{(e)}\times\bm{\gamma}_{i}^{(e)}\times\bm{\kappa}_{i}^{(e)}\subsetneq\bm{\mu}_{i}^{(u)}\times\bm{\gamma}_{i}^{(u)}\times\bm{\kappa}_{i}^{(u)},

where for all
\epsilon\in\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
the function

is proved to have a zero in the inner box, and this zero is unique in
the outer box. The boxes

\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\times\bm{\kappa}_{i}^{(e)}

(\epsilon,\mu_{i}(\epsilon),\gamma_{i}(\epsilon),\kappa_{i}(\epsilon))
defined for

\epsilon\in\bm{\epsilon}_{i}
.

This gives us
N
segments of curves. To prove that we have a
continuous curve on the whole interval
[\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}]

for the preceding interval. Note that in

satisfied for the middle parts. In our case, this condition is however
easily verified, and the

In some cases it will however not be possible to directly verify this
condition, this happens further down on some of the branches. In that

\bm{\epsilon}_{i}

7
Solution at infinity

In this section, we study solutions to the equation

(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q-\omega Q+(1+i\delta)|Q|^{2\sigma}Q=0,

(10)

satisfying the condition

Q(\xi)\sim\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}
as

\xi\to\infty
. We will see that there exists a manifold of
solutions, which can be parameterized by
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}
.

To perform the shooting method discussed in
Section
2
, for a fixed
\xi_{1}>1
, we
need to be able to compute enclosures of
Q(\xi_{1})
and

Q^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
, as well as the derivatives with respect to the
parameters
\gamma
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
. Since the
non-linearity
|Q|^{2\sigma}Q
is not holomorphic, we need to
separate derivatives in the real and imaginary directions. For the
derivatives with respect to
\gamma
we use the notation

Q_{\real\gamma}
and
Q_{\imag\gamma}
for the derivatives in the
real and imaginary directions respectively. Since
\kappa
and

\epsilon
are real, there is only one direction to consider, and we
write
Q_{\kappa}
and
Q_{\epsilon}
for the corresponding
derivatives. To count the number of critical points of
|Q|
, we
also need to prove that the solution is monotone for sufficiently
large
\xi
.

To compute the solution, we follow a similar approach to that
in
[
63
]
. The main idea is to use the variation of
parameters applied to the linearized equation to construct an operator
whose fixed points are solutions to the equation. All the necessary
bounds are then derived from this fixed point equation.

The associated linear equation is given by

(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q-\omega Q=0.

(11)

Making the change of variables

a=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma}+i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}\right),\quad b=\frac{d}{2},\quad c=\frac{-i\kappa}{2(1-i\epsilon)},\quad z=c\xi^{2},

(12)

this can be transformed into the well-studied Kummer’s equation,

z\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}+(b-z)\frac{dw}{dz}-aw=0.

One solution of Kummer’s equation is the confluent hypergeometric
function
U(a,b,z)
, with another, linearly independent, solution
given by
V(a,b,z)=e^{z}U(b-a,b,-z)
. This gives us two
linearly independent solutions
P(\xi)
and
E(\xi)

of (
11
), given by

P(\xi)=U(a,b,c\xi^{2})\quad\text{and}\quad E(\xi)=e^{c\xi^{2}}U(b-a,b,-c\xi^{2}).

We also note that the associated Wronskian is given by

W(\xi)=P(\xi)E^{\prime}(\xi)-P^{\prime}(\xi)E(\xi)=2ce^{\sign(\imag(c))\pi i(b-a)}\xi\left(c\xi^{2}\right)^{-b}e^{c\xi^{2}}.

Of the functions
P(\xi)
and
E(\xi)
, only
P(\xi)
has the
right asymptotic behavior at infinity. More precisely,
P(\xi)

asymptotically behaves like

P(\xi)\sim\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}
(see
Lemma
7.1
), whereas
E(\xi)
behaves like

E(\xi)\sim e^{c\xi^{2}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-d+i\frac{\omega}{\kappa}}
. For
\epsilon>0
we have

\real c>0
, so
E(\xi)
is exponentially increasing. However,
even for
\epsilon=0
, the large oscillations coming from the
exponential factor stop
E(\xi)
from having finite energy.

To go from solutions to the linear equation to solutions
to (
10
), we follow the method of variation of
parameters and look for solutions of the form

Q(\xi)=c_{1}(\xi)P(\xi)+c_{2}(\xi)E(\xi)
, where

c_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)=\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}E(\xi)W(\xi)^{-1}|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\quad\text{and}\quad c_{2}^{\prime}(\xi)=-\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}P(\xi)W(\xi)^{-1}|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi).

To get the right asymptotic behavior at infinity, we require that

c_{2}(\xi)
goes to zero rapidly as
\xi\to\infty
, to
counteract the exponential factor in
E(\xi)
. In contrast,

c_{1}
should have a nonzero limit at infinity. For this reason, it
is natural to use the representations

\displaystyle c_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma+\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}E(\eta)W(\eta)^{-1}|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta,

\displaystyle c_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}P(\eta)W(\eta)^{-1}|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta.

The vanishing interval of integration for
c_{2}
ensures that it
goes to zero in the limit. Using the notation

J_{P}(\xi)=\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}P(\xi)W(\xi)^{-1},\quad J_{E}(\xi)=\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}E(\xi)W(\xi)^{-1}

and

I_{P}(Q,\xi)=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P}(\eta)|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta,\quad I_{E}(Q,\xi)=\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}J_{E}(\eta)|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta,

this gives us the equation

Q(\xi)=\gamma P(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E}(Q,\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}(Q,\xi)

(13)

for
Q
. Specifically, if we let
T
be the operator

T(Q)(\xi)=\gamma P(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E}(Q,\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}(Q,\xi),

(14)

then fixed points of this operator correspond to solutions
of (
10
).

Our strategy for computing enclosures of
Q
is to study the
operator
T
and the fixed point
equation (
13
). We split this into
two parts:

1.

First, we prove the existence of a (locally unique) fixed point
of
T
by proving that it is a contraction in a ball in a weighted
function space. This gives us both the existence of a solution, as
well as initial bounds for its norm.

2.

Next, we compute refined enclosures of the fixed point using a
bootstrapping approach applied to the fixed point
equation (
13
). For the shooting
argument to be successful, these enclosures need to be sufficiently
tight.

Both of these steps require precise control over the solutions
P

and
E
to the linear equation. It is then relatively
straightforward to prove the existence of a fixed point. Getting
sufficiently refined enclosures for the shooting argument to succeed
does, however, require significantly more work. The main culprit is
getting good control over the integral
I_{P}(Q,\xi)
, whose
integrand has exponentially fast oscillations coming from
J_{P}
.
To handle this, we integrate by parts multiple times, giving us
progressively better control. This does, however, require the use of
higher order derivatives of
P
,
E
and
Q
. A significant
amount of the work is therefore dedicated to also handling these
higher order derivatives.

To control the derivatives with respect to
\real\gamma
,

\imag\gamma
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
, we directly
differentiate the fixed-point equation. To get tight enclosures, we
then follow a similar approach to that for enclosing
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
.
In particular, this requires precise control over the derivatives of

P
and
E
with respect to the parameters
\kappa
and

\epsilon
.

We proceed as follows:

1.

In Section
7.1
, we give asymptotic bounds
for functions related to
P
and
E
. This includes both higher
order derivatives with respect to
\xi
(required for the
integration by parts of
I_{P}
) and derivatives with respect to

\kappa
and
\epsilon
.

2.

Section
7.2
is devoted to studying the
operator
T
, and we give explicit conditions for the existence of
a fixed point.

3.

In Section
7.3
, we discuss how to
use the fixed point equation (
13
)
to compute tight enclosures of
Q
and its derivatives.

4.

Finally, Section
7.4
goes through the
extra details required to prove monotonicity of
Q
for large
values of
\xi
.

Lastly, let us comment on some of the notation and other conventions
used in this section. In general, we use
′
to denote
differentiation with respect to the argument, e.g.
P^{\prime}(\xi)
,
and subscripts to denote differentiation with respect to parameters,
e.g.
J_{P,\kappa}(\xi)
. For the most part, we do not make explicit
the functions’ dependence on parameters such as
\kappa
and

\epsilon
. In the same way, we generally drop the
Q
argument
for
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
defined above, i.e. we prefer to write
just
I_{P}(\xi)
instead of
I_{P}(Q,\xi)
. The sections contain
a large number of asymptotic bounds with explicitly given constants.
For example, from Lemma
7.3
we have the bound

|P(\xi)|\leq C_{P}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}},

valid for
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
and where
C_{P}
is an explicitly
given constant. In general, these explicit constants will depend on
parameters such as
\kappa
and
\epsilon
, as well as the value
of
\xi_{1}
. However, to keep the notation brief, we do not make
this dependence explicit. We use the variable
C
for all of these
types of constants, with a subscript to denote which function it is
associated with. For constants associated with derivatives with
respect to parameters, we generally prefer to put the parameter in the
top level of the subscript. For example, we use the notation

C_{P,\epsilon}
, rather than
C_{P_{\epsilon}}
, for a constant
associated with
P_{\epsilon}
. In some cases, there are multiple
constants appearing in the bound for a function; in this case, we opt
to use an integer subscript to enumerate them. An example is the bound
for
Q^{\prime\prime}
, which depends on the two constants
C_{Q^{\prime\prime},1}
and

C_{Q^{\prime\prime},2}
.

7.1
Asymptotic bounds for
P
and
E

In this subsection we give asymptotic bounds for
P
and
E
as
well as several related functions.

We start by giving bounds for the confluent hypergeometric function

U
, all other bounds are then derived from these bounds. Note that
differentiation of
U
with respect to the argument gives us another
confluent hypergeometric function, given by

U^{(n)}(a,b,z)=(-1)^{n}U(a+n,b+n,z)(a)_{n},

(15)

where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)(a+2)\cdots(a+n-1)
is the rising
factorial. As a consequence, having bounds for
U
also allows us to
get bounds for derivatives with respect to
z
. The following lemma
gives the asymptotic expansion of
U
as well as an explicit bound
for the remainder, both of which are available in the literature.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
a,b,z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\imag z|>|b-2a|
or

|\real z|>|b-2a|
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we have

U(a,b,z)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-z)^{k}}+R_{U}(a,b,n,z)z^{-n}\right)z^{-a},

where

\left|R_{U}\!\left(a,b,n,z\right)\right|\leq C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z)=\left|\frac{\left(a\right)_{n}\left(a-b+1\right)_{n}}{n!}\right|\frac{2\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{2}\pi n}}{1-\sigma(a,b,z)}\exp\!\left(\frac{\pi\rho(a,b,z)}{\left(1-\sigma(a,b,z)\right)\left|z\right|}\right)

with

\sigma(a,b,z)=\frac{\left|b-2a\right|}{\left|z\right|},\quad\rho(a,b,z)=\left|{a}^{2}-ab+\frac{b}{2}\right|+\frac{\sigma(a,b,z)\left(1+\frac{\sigma(a,b,z)}{4}\right)}{{\left(1-\sigma(a,b,z)\right)}^{2}}.

In particular, if
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies

|\imag z_{1}|>|b-2a|
or
|\real z_{1}|>|b-2a|
, then for
all
z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\arg(z)=\arg(z_{1})
and

|z|\geq|z_{1}|
, we have

C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z)\leq C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z_{1})

and, moreover,

|U(a,b,z)|\leq C_{U}(a,b,n,z_{1})|z^{-a}|,

with
C_{U}(a,b,n,z_{1})
given by

C_{U}(a,b,n,z_{1})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!z_{1}^{k}}\right|+C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z_{1})|z_{1}^{-n}|.

Proof.

The first statement follows from
[
22
, Sec. 13.7]
(see
also entries
[
33
,
34
,
32
]

from Fungrim
[
44
]
).

For the second statement, the bound

C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z)\leq C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,z_{1})
follows
immediately from the fact that
C_{R_{U}}
is decreasing in

|z|
. Since the condition
\arg(z)=\arg(z_{1})
implies that

z
also satisfies
|\imag z|>|b-2a|
or

|\real z|>|b-2a|
, the expression for the constant
C_{U}

then follows from bounding the asymptotic expansion for

U(a,b,z)
termwise and applying the monotonicity of the error
bound.
∎

For our purposes we also need bounds for derivatives with respect to

a
, i.e.
U_{a}
. In this case there are no bounds for the
remainder terms directly available in the literature. Instead, we
adapt the approach in
[
60
]
to cover also this case.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
a,b,z\in\mathbb{C}
, satisfying
|\imag z|>|b-2a|

or
|\real z|>|b-2a|
as well as
0<\real(a)<\real(b)

and
|\arg(z)|<\pi
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

\real(a-b+n+1)>0
we have

\displaystyle U_{a}(a,b,z)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-z)^{k}}+\log(z)^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left(\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-z)^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\left(1-\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(a)}{\Gamma(a)}\log(z)^{-1}\right)R_{U}(a,b,n,z)+R_{U,1}(a,b,z)+R_{U,2}(a,b,z)\right)z^{-n}\Bigg)\log(z)z^{-a}.

Here
R_{U}
is as in Lemma
7.1
,

\left|R_{U,1}\!\left(a,b,n,z\right)\right|\leq C_{R_{U,1}}(a,b,n,z)=\frac{\rho_{\gamma}C_{\chi}(a,b,n)e^{-\gamma\imag(a)}}{|\Gamma(a)|}\\
\left(C_{1}(a,n)+(|\gamma|C_{1}(a,n)+C_{2}(a,n))|\log(|z|)|^{-1}\right)

and

\left|R_{U,2}\!\left(a,b,n,z\right)\right|\leq C_{R_{U,2}}(a,b,n,z)=\frac{\rho_{\gamma}C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)C_{1}(a,n)e^{-\gamma\imag(a)}}{|\Gamma(a)|}|\log(|z|)|^{-1},

with
\gamma=\arg(z)
,

\displaystyle C_{\chi}(a,b,n)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(-\real(a-b))\Gamma(\real(a-b+n+1))}{n!},

\displaystyle C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)

\displaystyle=|\cos(\pi(a-b+1))|\frac{\Gamma(-\real(a-b))\Gamma(\real(a-b+n+1))}{n!}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{\real(a-b)^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma(-\real(a-b))\Gamma(\real(a-b+n))}{(n-1)!}\right),

\displaystyle C_{1}(a,n)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\real(a+n)),

\displaystyle C_{2}(a,n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\real(a+n)^{2}}+\Gamma(\real(a+n+1))

and

\rho_{\gamma}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|\gamma|\leq\frac{\pi}{2},\\
\sin(\pi-|\gamma|)^{-1}&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}<|\gamma|<\pi.\end{cases}

In particular, if
z_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, with
|z_{1}|>1
,
satisfies the requirements on
z
above, then for all

z\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\arg(z)=\arg(z_{1})
and

|z|\geq|z_{1}|
, we have

|U_{a}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{U,a}(a,b,n,z_{1})|\log(z)z^{-a}|

with
C_{U,a}(a,b,n,z_{1})
given by

\displaystyle C_{U,a}(a,b,n,z_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-z_{1})^{k}}\right|+|\log(z_{1})|^{-1}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left(\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-z_{1})^{k}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\left(1+\left|\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(a)}{\Gamma(a)}\log(z_{1})^{-1}\right|\right)R_{U}(a,b,n,z_{1})+R_{U,1}(a,b,z_{1})+R_{U,2}(a,b,z_{1})\right)|z_{1}|^{-n}.

Proof.

We follow the methodology established by Olver
[
60
]
,
specifically the techniques detailed in Sections 1 and 2. Note that
they prove stronger results than we intend to do here; we therefore
only need part of the paper.

The idea is to take the asymptotic expansion of
U(a,b,z)
from
Lemma
7.1
above and differentiate it termwise. However, to
get a bound for the remainder term we first need an explicit formula
for it. The explicit formula we use is
[
60
, Equation
2.5]
(note that their parameter
b
corresponds to

a-b+1
in our case). For completeness, we include a brief
derivation.

The starting point is the integral representation

U(a,b,z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{-(a-b+1)}\,dt.

However, this integral only converges when
\real(a)>0
and

\real(z)>0
. The requirement on
a
is satisfied by
assumption, but not the one on
z
. However, by analytic
continuation one can, for an arbitrary angle

\gamma\in(-\pi,\pi)
, get the representation

U(a,b,z)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}(1+t)^{-(a-b+1)}\,dt,

which is valid for all
z
such that
\real(ze^{-i\gamma})>0
,
still requiring
\real(a)>0
.

The next step is to use the Taylor expansion

(1+t)^{-(a-b+1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{k}\frac{(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!}t^{k}+\chi_{n}(a,b,t).

Inserting this into the integral gives us

U(a,b,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{k+a-1}\,dt+\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\,dt.

The identity

\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{k+a-1}\,dt=\frac{(a)_{k}}{(-z)^{k}}z^{-a}

gives us the asymptotic expansion in Lemma
7.1
. The
remainder term
R_{U}(a,b,z)
is then given by

R_{U}(a,b,z)=\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\,dt.

We are interested in bounding

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}R_{U}(a,b,z)
. Differentiating the
integral representation with respect to
a
gives us three terms:
one arising from the
z^{n+a}/\Gamma(a)
factor, and two from
the derivatives of
t^{a-1}
and
\chi_{n}(a,b,t)
in the
integrand. If we let

R_{U,1}(a,b,z)=\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}\log(t)t^{a-1}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\,dt

and

R_{U,2}(a,b,z)=\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}e^{-zt}t^{a-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\,dt

denote the two derivatives associated with
t^{a-1}
and

\chi_{n}(a,b,t)
respectively, then we can write the full
derivative as

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}R_{U}(a,b,z)=\left(\log(z)-\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(a)}{\Gamma(a)}\right)R_{U}(a,b,z)+R_{U,1}(a,b,z)+R_{U,2}(a,b,z).

Before bounding
R_{U,1}
and
R_{U,2}
, let us take a closer
look at
\chi_{n}(a,b,t)
. We start by noting that

\chi_{n}(a,b,t)=\frac{t^{n}}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{L}}\frac{(1+w)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}}(w-t)\,dw,

where
\mathcal{L}
is a simple closed contour enclosing
w=0

and
w=t
, but not
w=-1
. This loop can be deformed into
the one depicted in
[
60
, Figure 1]
. If

\real(a)<\real(b)
, which holds by assumption, then it can be
collapsed on to the interval
(-\infty,-1]
. For this we need to
take into account the branch cut along
(-\infty,-1]
, which adds
a factor

e^{-i(a-b+1)\pi}-e^{-i(a-b+1)(-\pi)}=-2i\sin(\pi(a-b+1)).

This gives us

\chi_{n}(a,b,t)=-\frac{\sin(\pi(a-b+1))}{\pi}t^{n}\int_{-1}^{-\infty}\frac{(1+w)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}(w-t)}\,dw,

which after a change of variables from
w
to
-w
gives us

\chi_{n}(a,b,t)=(-1)^{n}\frac{\sin(\pi(a-b+1))}{\pi}t^{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}(w+t)}\,dw.

We have

|\chi_{n}(a,b,t)|\leq\frac{|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}|t|^{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-\real(a-b+1)}}{w^{n+1}|1+t/w|}\,dw.

Some trigonometry gives us that the factor
|1+t/w|^{-1}
is
uniformly bounded by

\rho_{\gamma}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }|\gamma|\leq\frac{\pi}{2},\\
\sin(\pi-|\gamma|)^{-1}&\text{if }\frac{\pi}{2}<|\gamma|<\pi.\end{cases}

This gives us
|\chi_{n}(a,b,t)|\leq C_{\chi}(a,b,n)|t|^{n}
,
with

\displaystyle C_{\chi}(a,b,n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\gamma}|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-\real(a-b+1)}}{w^{n+1}}\,dw

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\gamma}|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(-\real(a-b))\Gamma(\real(a-b+n+1))}{n!}.

Furthermore

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)=(-1)^{n}\cos(\pi(a-b+1))t^{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}(w+t)}\,dw\\
-(-1)^{n}\frac{\sin(\pi(a-b+1))}{\pi}t^{n}\int_{1}^{\infty}\log(w-1)\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}(w+t)}\,dw,

giving us

\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\right|\leq C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)|t|^{n}

with

C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)=\rho_{\gamma}|\cos(\pi(a-b+1))|\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n+1}}\,dw\\
+\frac{\rho_{\gamma}|\sin(\pi(a-b+1))|}{\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}|\log(w-1)|\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n+1}}\,dw.

The last integral can be bounded as

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}|\log(w-1)|\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n+1}}\,dw

\displaystyle\leq\int_{1}^{2}(-\log(w-1))(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}\,dw+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{(w-1)^{-(a-b+1)}}{w^{n}}\,dw

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\real(a-b)^{2}}+\frac{\Gamma(-\real(a-b))\Gamma(\real(a-b+n))}{(n-1)!}.

We now give a bound for
R_{U,1}
. We have

|R_{U,1}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}\left|e^{-zt}\log(t)t^{n+a-1}\right|\,dt.

If we replace
t
by
\tau e^{-i\gamma}
we get

|R_{U,1}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\real(ze^{-i\gamma})\tau}|\log(\tau e^{-i\gamma})|\tau^{\real(n+a-1)}\,d\tau.

By taking
\gamma=\arg(z)
we get
\real(ze^{-i\gamma})=|z|
, giving us

|R_{U,1}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-|z|\tau}|\log(\tau e^{-i\gamma})|\tau^{\real(n+a-1)}\,d\tau.

The change of variables
s=|z|\tau
gives us

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-|z|\tau}|\log(\tau e^{-i\gamma})|\tau^{\real(n+a-1)}\,d\tau=|z|^{-\real(n+a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}|\log(s|z|^{-1}e^{-i\gamma})|s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds.

We have

|\log(s|z|^{-1}e^{-i\gamma})|\leq|\log(|z|)|+|\log(s)|+|\gamma|,

giving us

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}|\log(s|z|^{-1}e^{-i\gamma})|s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds

\displaystyle=|\log(|z|)|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}|\log(s)|s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+|\gamma|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds.

Let

C_{1}(a,n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds,\quad C_{2}(a,n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}|\log(s)|s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds.

We get
C_{1}(a,n)=\Gamma(\real(a+n))
and

\displaystyle C_{2}(a,n)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-s}(-\log(s))s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-s}\log(s)s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}(-\log(s))s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\real(n+a)}\,ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\real(a+n)^{2}}+\Gamma(\real(a+n+1))

Combining all of this gives us

|R_{U,1}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right||\log(|z|)||z|^{-\real(n+a)}\\
\left(C_{1}(a,n)+(|\gamma|C_{1}(a,n)+C_{2}(a,n))|\log(|z|)|^{-1}\right).

Using that
\left|\frac{\log(|z|)}{\log(z)}\right|\leq 1
and

\frac{|z^{n+a}|}{|z|^{\real(n+a)}}=\frac{|z|^{\real(n+a)}e^{-\arg(z)\imag(n+a)}}{|z|^{\real(n+a)}}=e^{-\arg(z)\imag(a)},

we get

|R_{U,1}(a,b,z)|\leq\frac{C_{\chi}(a,b,n)}{|\Gamma(a)|}e^{-\arg(z)\imag(a)}\left(C_{1}(a,n)+(|\gamma|C_{1}(a,n)+C_{2}(a,n))|\log(|z|)|^{-1}\right).

Next we give bounds for
R_{U,2}
. We have

|R_{U,2}(a,b,z)|\leq\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}\left|e^{-zt}t^{a-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)\right|\,dt.

Adding the bound for
\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\chi_{n}(a,b,t)

gives us

|R_{U,2}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty e^{-i\gamma}}\left|e^{-zt}t^{n+a-1}\right|\,dt.

As for
R_{U,1}
, we replace
t
by
\tau e^{-i\gamma}

to get

|R_{U,2}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\real(ze^{-i\gamma})\tau}\tau^{\real(n+a-1)}\,d\tau.

Taking
\gamma=\arg(z)
and making the change of variables
s=|z|\tau
then gives

|z|^{-\real(n+a)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\real(n+a-1)}\,ds.

This gives us

|R_{U,2}(a,b,z)|\leq C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)C_{1}(a,n)\left|\log(z)^{-1}\frac{z^{n+a}}{\Gamma(a)}\right||z|^{-\real(n+a)}\leq\frac{C_{\chi,a}(a,b,n)C_{1}(a,n)}{|\Gamma(a)|}e^{-\gamma\imag(a)}|\log(z)|^{-1}.

The final statement of the lemma follows in the same way as for
Lemma
7.1
.
∎

These two lemmas will be the basis for the asymptotic bounds for
P

and
E
and their derivatives. Before giving these bounds, let us
however introduce a bit more notation that will be helpful for the
handling of derivatives. To begin with, if we let

B_{W}=-\frac{1+i\delta}{i\kappa}e^{-\sign(\imag(c))\pi i(b-a)}c^{b},

then
J_{P}
and
J_{E}
can be written as

\displaystyle J_{P}(\xi)=B_{W}P(\xi)e^{-c\xi^{2}}\xi^{d-1}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{E}(\xi)=B_{W}E(\xi)e^{-c\xi^{2}}\xi^{d-1}.

When enclosing
I_{P,\kappa}
and
I_{P,\epsilon}
we will have to
work with the functions
J_{P,\kappa}
and
J_{P,\epsilon}
. For
this, it is convenient to write them in the form

J_{P,\kappa}(\xi)=D(\xi)e^{-c\xi^{2}}\xi^{d+1}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{P,\epsilon}(\xi)=H(\xi)e^{-c\xi^{2}}\xi^{d+1},

with
D
and
H
given by

\displaystyle D(\xi)

\displaystyle=P(\xi)(-c_{\kappa}B_{W}+B_{W,\kappa}\xi^{-2})+P_{\kappa}(\xi)B_{W}\xi^{-2},

(16)

\displaystyle H(\xi)

\displaystyle=P(\xi)(-c_{\epsilon}B_{W}+B_{W,\epsilon}\xi^{-2})+P_{\epsilon}(\xi)B_{W}\xi^{-2}.

(17)

We are now ready to give the asymptotic bounds. These include bounds
for
P
and
E
as well as their derivatives with respect to

\xi
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
. For derivatives with respect to

\xi
we go up to order three, which is related to how many
iterations of integration by parts are performed when enclosing

I_{P}
. We also give separate bounds for
J_{P}
,
J_{E}
,

D
, and
H
as well as some of their derivatives, since this
simplifies many of the later computations.

All of these bounds are relatively straightforward consequences of the
bounds for
U
and
U_{a}
from the two above lemmas. However,
assembling all of the explicit expressions is a rather tedious effort;
we therefore leave most of the details to
Appendix
A
. It is worth pointing out
that the constants, of course, depend on the parameters
\kappa
,

\omega
,
\epsilon
and
\delta
as well as the value of

\xi_{1}
, even if this dependence is not explicit in our notation.
Furthermore, the requirements of Lemmas
7.1
and

7.2
give us conditions that need to be satisfied for
these bounds to be valid. We do not explicitly specify these conditions
here, instead, they are verified when these constants are actually

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\xi_{1}>1
. Under suitable conditions on the parameters

\kappa
,
\omega
,
\epsilon
and
\delta
, we have, for

\xi\geq\xi_{1}
, the bounds

\displaystyle|P^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{(m)}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 3,

\displaystyle|P_{\kappa}^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{(m)},\kappa}\log(\xi)\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 2,

\displaystyle|P_{\epsilon}^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{(m)},\epsilon}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 2,

\displaystyle|E^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{(m)}}e^{\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-d+m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 3,

\displaystyle|E_{\kappa}^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{(m)},\kappa}e^{\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-d+2+m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 1,

\displaystyle|E_{\epsilon}^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{(m)},\epsilon}e^{\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-d+2+m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 1.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle|J_{P}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{P}}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}+d-1},

\displaystyle|J_{P,\kappa}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{P},\kappa}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}+d+1},

\displaystyle|J_{P,\epsilon}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{P},\epsilon}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}+d+1},

\displaystyle|J_{E}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{E}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1},

\displaystyle|J_{E,\kappa}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{E},\kappa}\log(\xi)\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1},

\displaystyle|J_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{J_{E},\epsilon}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1},

as well as

\displaystyle|D^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{D^{(m)}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 3,

\displaystyle|H^{(m)}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{H^{(m)}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m}

\displaystyle\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq m\leq 3.

Proof.

The bounds for
P
and
E
and their derivatives with respect to

\xi
are given in Lemma

, the derivatives with
respect to
\kappa
in Lemma
A.2
and the

in

. The bounds for
J_{P}
and

J_{E}
are given in Lemma
A.4
and their

\kappa
and
\epsilon
in

A.5
. Finally

and

are

.

Note that in all cases, the constants have explicit expressions

and

. For the derivatives with

∎

7.2
Existence of fixed point

In this subsection we give explicit conditions for the existence of a
fixed point of the operator

T(Q)(\xi)=\gamma P(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)

from (
14
). The fixed-point argument is performed in the Banach
space
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{v}}
, with
\mathrm{v}\geq 0
, of continuous
functions
Q:[\xi_{1},\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
for which the norm

\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}=\sup_{\xi\geq\xi_{1}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|Q(\xi)|

is finite. While the inclusion of
\mathrm{v}
in the norm is not
strictly necessary for the fixed-point argument itself, it is required
to bound the derivatives with respect to
\kappa
and
\epsilon
,
where
\log
terms appear in the leading order.

To begin, we provide the following lemma that gives us bounds for

I_{P}
and
I_{E}
in terms of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{v}}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Assume that
\xi_{1}>1
,

(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2<0
and

(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2<0
. Then, for
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
, we
have the following bounds

\displaystyle|I_{P}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{I_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2},

\displaystyle|I_{E}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

with

\displaystyle C_{I_{P}}

\displaystyle=\min\left(\frac{C_{J_{P}}}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2|},\frac{C_{J_{P}}}{2\real(c)}\xi_{1}^{-2}\right),

\displaystyle C_{I_{E}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{J_{E}}}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}.

The second term in the minimum for
C_{I_{P}}
, is understood to
be finite only for
\real(c)>0
.

Proof.

Let us start by noting that

|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma+1}\leq\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\left(\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)}=\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2}.

Combining this with the bound

|J_{P}(\eta)|\leq C_{J_{P}}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\eta^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}+d-1}

from Lemma
7.3
gives us

|I_{P}(\xi)|\leq\int_{\xi}^{\infty}|J_{P}(\eta)||Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma+1}\,d\eta\leq C_{J_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\,d\eta.

The bound
|J_{E}(\xi)|\leq C_{J_{E}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1}

similarly gives us

|I_{E}(\xi)|\leq\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}|J_{E}(\eta)||Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma+1}\,d\eta\leq C_{J_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-3}\,d\eta.

For
\real(c)\geq 0
, the first integral can be bounded as

\begin{split}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\,d\eta&\leq e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\,d\eta\\
&=\frac{1}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2|}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2}.\end{split}

In case
\real(c)>0
we can get a better estimate by first
integrating by parts. In this case we have

\begin{split}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\,d\eta&=\frac{1}{2\real(c)}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-4}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{2\real(c)}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\frac{d}{d\eta}\left(\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\right)\,d\eta.\end{split}

Since

\frac{d}{d\eta}\left(\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\right)<0
, the last integral is negative and we get
an upper bound by removing it. This gives us the upper bound

\begin{split}\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-\real(c)\eta^{2}}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-3}\,d\eta&\leq\frac{1}{2\real(c)}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-4}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2\real(c)}\xi_{1}^{-2}e^{-\real(c)\xi^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2}\end{split}

To avoid a bound which degenerates as
\real(c)
approaches zero,
we take the minimum of this bound and the above one.

For the second integral we immediately get the bound

\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-3}\,d\eta\leq\frac{1}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}.

∎

Consequently we obtain the following version of
[
63
, Lemma
A.2]
with explicit constants.

Lemma 7.5
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
7.4
, the operator

T
defines a continuous mapping

T:\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{v}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{v}}
. Moreover

\|T(Q)\|_{\mathrm{v}}\leq C_{P}|\gamma|\xi_{1}^{-\mathrm{v}}+C_{T}\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}

(18)

and

\|T(u)-T(v)\|_{\mathrm{v}}\leq M_{\sigma}C_{T}\xi_{1}^{-2+2\sigma\mathrm{v}}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{v}}(\|u\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}),

(19)

for all
u,v\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{v}}
. Here

C_{T}=C_{P}C_{I_{E}}+C_{E}C_{I_{P}}

and
M_{\sigma}
is as in Lemma
A.7
.

Proof.

To prove (
18
), we need to bound

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|T(Q)(\xi)|
for

\xi\geq\xi_{1}
. Bounding it termwise we get

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|T(Q)(\xi)|\leq\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|\gamma||P(\xi)|+\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P(\xi)||I_{E}(\xi)|+\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E(\xi)||I_{P}(\xi)|.

From Lemma
7.3
we get for the first term

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|\gamma||P(\xi)|\leq C_{P}|\gamma|\xi^{-\mathrm{v}}\leq C_{P}|\gamma|\xi_{1}^{-\mathrm{v}}.

Combining Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
we
have for the second and third term

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P(\xi)||I_{E}(\xi)|\leq C_{P}C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi^{-\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}\leq C_{P}C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}

and

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E(\xi)||I_{P}(\xi)|\leq C_{E}C_{I_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}\leq C_{E}C_{I_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}

respectively. Combining these three bounds gives us the result.

For (
19
) we get

\begin{split}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|T(u)(\xi)-T(v)(\xi)|&\leq\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P(\xi)|\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}|J_{E}(\eta)|\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\,d\eta\\
&\quad+\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E(\xi)|\int_{\xi}^{\infty}|J_{P}(\eta)|\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\,d\eta.\end{split}

By Lemma
A.7
we have the bound

\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\leq M_{\sigma}|u(\eta)-v(\eta)|(|u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}+|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}),

giving us

\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\leq M_{\sigma}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{v}}(\|u\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma})\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2}.

From which we get

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P(\xi)|\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}|J_{E}(\eta)|\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\,d\eta\\
\leq M_{\sigma}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{v}}(\|u\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma})\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P(\xi)|\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}|J_{E}(\eta)|\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2}\,d\eta

and

\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E(\xi)|\int_{\xi}^{\infty}|J_{P}(\eta)|\left||u(\eta)|^{2\sigma}u(\eta)-|v(\eta)|^{2\sigma}v(\eta)\right|\,d\eta\\
\leq M_{\sigma}\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{v}}(\|u\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}+\|v\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma})\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E(\xi)|\int_{\xi}^{\infty}|J_{P}(\eta)|\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2}\,d\eta

These integrals can be bounded in the same way as in
Lemma
7.4
, giving us the result.

The continuity follows in the same way as in
[
63
, Proposition
A.2]
.
∎

The estimates (
18
) and (
19
) show that
T
is a
contraction of the ball

B_{\rho}=\{Q\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{v}}:\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\leq\rho\}
into
itself if
\rho
is such that

\displaystyle C_{P}|\gamma|\xi_{1}^{-\mathrm{v}}+C_{T}\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}\rho^{2\sigma+1}

\displaystyle\leq\rho,

(20)

\displaystyle 2M_{\sigma}C_{T}\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2}\rho^{2\sigma}

\displaystyle<1.

(21)

From the Banach fixed point theorem we then get the existence of a
fixed point. More precisely we have the following result,
see
[
63
, Proposition A.3]
.

Proposition 7.6
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
7.5
and if
the two inequalities (
20
) and (
21
) are
satisfied, then the operator
T
has a unique fixed point
Q
in
the ball
B_{\rho}
.

7.3
Enclosures for fixed point

The above proposition allows us to prove the existence of a solution

Q
satisfying
T(Q)=Q
and gives us bounds on its norm. It
does, however, not directly give us an enclosure for
Q(\xi_{1})
,
other than what can be deduced from the bound of the norm. The goal of
this section is to develop bounds that allow us to compute accurate
enclosures of
Q
, as well as its derivatives, at
\xi_{1}
.

The fixed-point equation satisfied by
Q
, combined with the bounds
for
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
from Lemma
7.4
, already
allows for the computation of an improved enclosure for
Q(\xi_{1})
.
From the fixed-point equation we have

Q(\xi_{1})=\gamma P(\xi_{1})+P(\xi_{1})I_{E}(\xi_{1})+E(\xi_{1})I_{P}(\xi_{1}).

In the specific case when
\xi=\xi_{1}
, we have

I_{E}(\xi_{1})=0
, hence giving us

Q(\xi_{1})=\gamma P(\xi_{1})+E(\xi_{1})I_{P}(\xi_{1}).

One can directly compute tight enclosures for
P(\xi_{1})
and

E(\xi_{1})
. What remains is enclosing
I_{P}(\xi_{1})
, which
can be done using Lemma
7.4
and the bounds for the
norm of
Q
from Proposition
7.6
. While this does
give a significantly improved enclosure of
Q(\xi_{1})
compared to
what can be deduced from the bound of the norm alone, it is still not
good enough to prove the existence of a zero for the function

G
(
7
). To get better enclosures we will refine the
estimates for
I_{P}
through integration by parts.

To compute enclosures of the various derivatives of
Q
we directly
differentiate the fixed-point equation, giving us equations for the
derivatives. These equations will consist of derivatives of
P
and

E
, as well as
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
. Enclosures are then
computed similarly to those for
Q
, by computing initial bounds for the
norms and combining this with refined enclosures for the derivatives
of
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
.

We split the work of this part into four subsections:

1.

In Section
7.3.1
we give various
bounds for
I_{E}
and
I_{P}
and their derivatives. The
approach is similar to that of Lemma
7.4
, and follows
from direct bounds of the involved functions.

2.

In Section
7.3.2
we give
refined bounds for
I_{P}
and its derivatives based on integration
by parts.

3.

In Section
7.3.3
we derive bounds for the
norms of the various derivatives of
Q
. These are based on the
bound for the norm of
Q
from the fixed-point argument and the
bounds for derivatives of
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
from the earlier
subsection.

4.

In Section
7.3.4
we describe how to compute
tight enclosures at
\xi=\xi_{1}
using the derived bounds.

Before continuing, let us start by specifying the various quantities
we will be bounding. Recall that for evaluating the function
G
and
its Jacobian we need to compute enclosures for
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
, as
well as their derivatives with respect to
\real\gamma
,

\imag\gamma
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
. To apply the refined
integration by parts bounds for
I_{P}
we also need bounds for
higher order derivatives in
\xi
, namely
Q^{\prime\prime}
and
Q^{\prime\prime\prime}
. In
the end, we are thus interested in the functions

Q,\ Q^{\prime},\ Q^{\prime\prime},\ Q^{\prime\prime\prime},\ Q_{\real\gamma},\ Q_{\real\gamma}^{\prime},\ Q_{\imag\gamma},\ Q_{\imag\gamma}^{\prime},\ Q_{\kappa},\ Q_{\kappa}^{\prime},\ Q_{\epsilon}\text{ and }Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}.

From the fixed-point equation for
Q
we can derive equations
satisfied by the derivatives. For derivatives with respect to
\xi

we note the following identities, which follow from

I_{E}^{\prime}(\xi)=J_{E}(\xi)|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)
and

I_{P}^{\prime}(\xi)=-J_{P}(\xi)|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)
, together with
the Wronskian
W=PE^{\prime}-P^{\prime}E
:

\displaystyle P(\xi)I_{E}^{\prime}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}^{\prime}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi),

\displaystyle P^{\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{E}^{\prime}(\xi)+E^{\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{P}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}\frac{W^{\prime}(\xi)}{W(\xi)}|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}((d-1)\xi^{-1}-2c\xi)|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi).

This gives us

\displaystyle Q(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{\prime}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+P^{\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+E^{\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)-\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi),

\displaystyle Q^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)+P^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+E^{\prime\prime\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}((d-1)\xi^{-1}-2c\xi)|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1+i\delta}{1-i\epsilon}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right).

For the derivatives with respect to the parameters we get

\displaystyle Q_{\real\gamma}(\xi)

\displaystyle=P(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E,\real\gamma}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P,\real\gamma}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\real\gamma}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=P^{\prime}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\real\gamma}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\real\gamma}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\imag\gamma}(\xi)

\displaystyle=iP(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E,\imag\gamma}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P,\imag\gamma}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\imag\gamma}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=iP^{\prime}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\imag\gamma}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\imag\gamma}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\kappa}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\kappa}(\xi)+P_{\kappa}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)+E_{\kappa}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P,\kappa}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)+P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)+E_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\kappa}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\epsilon}(\xi)+P_{\epsilon}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P(\xi)I_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)+E_{\epsilon}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P,\epsilon}(\xi),

\displaystyle Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\gamma P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)+P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)+E_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\epsilon}(\xi).

Note that all of the various derivatives of
P
and
E
appearing
in these expressions are covered by Lemma
7.3
. The
goal of Sections
7.3.1

and
7.3.2
is to derive bounds for
all of the involved derivatives of
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
.

7.3.1
Direct bounds for
I_{P}
and
I_{E}

In this section we give bounds for
I_{P}
and
I_{E}
, as well as
their derivatives with respect to the parameters, based on direct
bounds for the involved functions and integrands (as opposed to
through integration by parts).

The bounds we derive will depend on a priori bounds for the norms of

Q
and its derivatives. In particular, we will need bounds for
derivatives of
|Q|^{2\sigma}Q
in terms of bounds for the norms of
the derivatives of
Q
. For this reason, the following lemma is
useful.

Lemma 7.7
.

For
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
we have the bounds

\displaystyle\left||Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi^{2}}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)(2\sigma\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial\xi^{3}}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)(2\sigma(2\sigma-1)\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{3}+6\sigma\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}\|Q^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-2}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

as well as

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\real\gamma}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\|Q_{\real\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\real\gamma}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\left(|Q(\xi)|^{2\sigma}Q(\xi)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)(2\sigma\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q_{\real\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{v}}+\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q_{\real\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2},

with identical bounds for the derivatives with respect to

\imag\gamma
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
.

Proof.

The bounds for the first order derivatives follow from

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(|Q|^{2\sigma}Q\right)=(\sigma+1)|Q|^{2\sigma}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}+\sigma|Q|^{2\sigma-2}Q^{2}\overline{\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right)},

valid for any real direction
t
, in particular for

t\in\{\xi,\ \real\gamma,\ \imag\gamma,\ \kappa,\ \epsilon\}
.
Bounds for remaining derivatives follow by differentiating this
identity further.
∎

For
I_{E}
and its derivatives with respect to
\gamma
,

\kappa
and
\epsilon
there are no oscillations to take into
account, and direct bounds suffice to get good enough estimates. For

I_{E}
, this means that the bounds in Lemma
7.4

suffice. For the derivatives with respect to the parameters we have
the following lemma.

Lemma 7.8
.

If
(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2<0
, then for

\xi\geq\xi_{1}\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle|I_{E,\real\gamma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\|Q_{\real\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle|I_{E,\imag\gamma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\|Q_{\imag\gamma}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\left|I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(C_{I_{E},\kappa}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\left\|Q_{\kappa}\right\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\left|I_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(C_{I_{E},\epsilon}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\left\|Q_{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}.

Here
C_{I_{E}}
is as in Lemma
7.4
and

\displaystyle C_{I_{E},\kappa}

\displaystyle=C_{J_{E},\kappa}\left(\frac{1}{((2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2)^{2}}+\frac{\log(\xi_{1})}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}\right),

\displaystyle C_{I_{E},\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{J_{E},\epsilon}}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}.

Proof.

Let us start by bounding
I_{E,\kappa}
, for which we have

I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)=\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}J_{E,\kappa}(\eta)|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta+\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}J_{E}(\eta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}(|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta))\,d\eta.

From Lemma
7.3
we have

|J_{E}(\xi)|\leq C_{J_{E}}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1}
and

|J_{E,\kappa}(\xi)|\leq C_{J_{E},\kappa}\log(\xi)\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1}
, combined with
the bounds for
Q
from Lemma
7.7
this gives
us

|I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)|\leq C_{J_{E},\kappa}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\log(\eta)\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-3}\,d\eta+(2\sigma+1)C_{J_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\|Q_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\eta^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-3}\,d\eta.

The second integral is bounded by

\frac{\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}
. The first integral we bound as

\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\xi}\log(\eta)\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-3}\,d\eta\leq\int_{\xi_{1}}^{\infty}\log(\eta)\eta^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-3}\,d\eta=\left(\frac{1}{((2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2)^{2}}+\frac{\log(\xi_{1})}{|(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2|}\right)\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}.

Combining these two bounds gives us the result for
I_{E,\kappa}
.

The bounds for
I_{E,\real\gamma}
,
I_{E,\imag\gamma}
and

I_{E,\epsilon}
are similar. In the case of
I_{E,\real\gamma}

and
I_{E,\imag\gamma}
there is only one term to bound, since

J_{E}
does not depend on
\gamma
. For
I_{E,\epsilon}
, the
bound

|J_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)|\leq C_{J_{E},\epsilon}\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-1}
means that there is no logarithmic factor, but otherwise
the approach is the same.
∎

Finally, we get to the bounds for
I_{P}
and its derivatives with
respect to the parameters. For these derivatives we get

\displaystyle I_{P,\real\gamma}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P}(\eta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\real\gamma}(|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta))\,d\eta,

\displaystyle I_{P,\imag\gamma}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P}(\eta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\imag\gamma}(|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta))\,d\eta,

\displaystyle I_{P,\kappa}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P,\kappa}(\eta)|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta+\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P}(\eta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\kappa}(|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta))\,d\eta

\displaystyle=I_{P,\kappa,1}(\xi)+I_{P,\kappa,2}(\xi),

\displaystyle I_{P,\epsilon}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P,\epsilon}(\eta)|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta)\,d\eta+\int_{\xi}^{\infty}J_{P}(\eta)\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}(|Q(\eta)|^{2\sigma}Q(\eta))\,d\eta

\displaystyle=I_{P,\epsilon,1}(\xi)+I_{P,\epsilon,2}(\xi).

For
I_{P,\real\gamma}
,
I_{P,\imag\gamma}
,
I_{P,\kappa,2}

and
I_{P,\epsilon,2}
, it suffices to give bounds similar to those
for
I_{P}
in Lemma
7.4
.

Lemma 7.9
.

Assume that
\xi_{1}>1
and that

(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}+d-2<0
. Then, for

\xi\geq\xi_{1}
we have the bounds

\displaystyle|I_{P,\real\gamma}(\xi)|

\displaystyle=\Big(C_{P^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{E}}\log(\xi_{1})\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{P^{\prime}}C_{I_{E},\kappa}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{E^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{P},2,1}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,1}\Big)\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle=(2\sigma+1)(C_{P^{\prime}}C_{I_{E}}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P}})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\kappa,3}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{P},2,2}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,2})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\kappa,4}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{P},2,3}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,3})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\kappa,5}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{P},2,4}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,4})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1}.

Proof.

We have

Q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)=\gamma P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)+P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)+E_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\kappa}(\xi).

Similarly to Lemma
7.20
, we get the termwise
bounds

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|\gamma P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime},\kappa}|\gamma|\log(\xi)\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime},\kappa}C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\log(\xi)\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\kappa}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime}}(C_{I_{E},\kappa}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\left\|Q_{\kappa}\right\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{\prime},\kappa}\Big(C_{I_{P},2,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{I_{P},2,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\Big)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\kappa}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{\prime}}\Big(C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{-1}+C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{I_{P},\kappa,1,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q_{\kappa}\|_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\quad\Big)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1}.

For
\xi\geq e
,
\log(\xi)\xi^{-1}
is
decreasing; this is enough to show that all terms are decreasing in

\xi
and hence bounded above by their value at
\xi_{1}
.
Collecting the bounds gives us the result.
∎

Finally, for
Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}
we have the following lemma.

Lemma 7.24
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
7.4
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}\right\|_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}|\gamma|\xi_{1}^{-\mathrm{v}-1}+\Bigg(C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,3}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}+C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,4}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,5}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\Bigg)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}.

Here

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,1}

\displaystyle=\Big(C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{E}}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{P^{\prime}}C_{I_{E},\epsilon}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{E^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{P},2,1}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,1}\Big)\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,2}

\displaystyle=(2\sigma+1)(C_{P^{\prime}}C_{I_{E}}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P}})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,3}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{P},2,2}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,2})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,4}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{P},2,3}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,3})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle C_{Q^{\prime},\epsilon,5}

\displaystyle=(C_{E^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{P},2,4}+C_{E^{\prime}}C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,4})\xi_{1}^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1}.

Proof.

We have

Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)=\gamma P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)+P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)+P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)+E_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\epsilon}(\xi).

Similarly to Lemma
7.21
we get the termwise
bounds

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|\gamma P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}|\gamma|\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|P^{\prime}(\xi)I_{E,\epsilon}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{P^{\prime}}(C_{I_{E},\epsilon}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{E}}\left\|Q_{\epsilon}\right\|_{\mathrm{v}})\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma}\xi^{-\mathrm{v}-1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{\prime},\epsilon}\Big(C_{I_{P},2,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{I_{P},2,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\Big)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1},

\displaystyle\xi^{\frac{1}{\sigma}-\mathrm{v}}|E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P,\epsilon}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{E^{\prime}}\Big(C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi^{-1}+C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{I_{P},\epsilon,1,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}+(2\sigma+1)C_{I_{P}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q_{\epsilon}\|_{\mathrm{v}}

\displaystyle\quad\Big)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}\xi^{2\sigma\mathrm{v}-1}.

All terms are bounded by their value at
\xi_{1}
, and collecting
the terms gives us the bound.
∎

7.3.4
Enclosures at
\xi_{1}

In this section we describe the final procedure for computing
enclosures of
Q
and its derivatives at
\xi=\xi_{1}
. The idea
is to use the results from the preceding sections to get initial
bounds for the norms. These are then iteratively improved using the
fixed-point equation. To get accurate bounds for
I_{P}
and its
derivatives with respect to the parameters we make use of the
integration by parts formulas from Lemmas
7.10
,

7.11

and
7.12
.

Let us start by considering the problem of enclosing
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
.
From Proposition
7.6
we get

\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\leq\rho
and using this together with
Lemma
7.18
allows us to compute bounds for

\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}
,
\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}
and
\|Q^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}
.
The bounds for the higher derivatives will be important when improving
the bound for
Q
in the next step. Using that

|Q(\xi_{1})|\leq\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}}
gives us initial enclosures for

Q(\xi_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
. The next step is an iterative
improvement of the bounds. For
Q
we use the equation

Q(\xi_{1})=\gamma P(\xi_{1})+E(\xi_{1})I_{P}(\xi_{1}),

and for
Q^{\prime}
we use

Q^{\prime}(\xi_{1})=\gamma P^{\prime}(\xi_{1})+E^{\prime}(\xi_{1})I_{P}(\xi_{1}).

Note that
I_{E}
is not included since
I_{E}(\xi_{1})=0
. For

P
,
P^{\prime}
,
E
and
E^{\prime}
we can easily compute tight
enclosures. For
I_{P}
, we make use of the expansion

I_{P}(\xi_{1})=B_{W}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{3}\frac{1}{(2c)^{k}}I_{P,k}(\xi_{1})+\frac{1}{(2c)^{3}}\hat{I}_{P,4}(\xi_{1})\right)

from Lemma
7.10
(taking
n=3
). Here,

I_{P,1}(\xi_{1})
,
I_{P,2}(\xi_{1})
and
I_{P,3}(\xi_{1})

are explicit functions depending on
Q(\xi_{1})
,
Q^{\prime}(\xi_{1})

and
Q^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{1})
. We already have initial enclosures for
Q
and

Q^{\prime}
, and an enclosure for
Q^{\prime\prime}
can easily be computed from the
defining ODE. To enclose
\hat{I}_{P,4}
, we make use of
Lemma
7.13
. Combining all of these
enclosures gives us new, in general tighter, enclosures of
Q
and

Q^{\prime}
. These new enclosures can then be fed back into the same
process, iteratively giving us better and better enclosures. In
practice, three iterations are enough to saturate the improvement. The
remaining error is then dominated by the error in the enclosure of

\hat{I}_{P,4}
, which is not improved in the iterative scheme.

The enclosures for
Q_{\real\gamma}(\xi_{1})
,

Q_{\real\gamma}^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
,
Q_{\imag\gamma}(\xi_{1})
,

Q_{\imag\gamma}^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
,
Q_{\kappa}(\xi_{1})
,

Q_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
,
Q_{\epsilon}(\xi_{1})
, and

Q_{\epsilon}^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
are computed using the same approach. In
this case we use expansions from
Lemma
7.11
for

I_{P,\real\gamma}
,
I_{P,\imag\gamma}
,
I_{P,\kappa,2}
and

I_{P,\epsilon,2}
and Lemma
7.12

for
I_{P,\kappa,1}
and
I_{P,\epsilon,1}
. The remainder terms
are then bounded using Lemmas
7.14

and
7.15
.

7.4
Verifying monotonicity

As part of counting the number of critical points of
|Q|
, one step
is verifying that
|Q|
is monotone for
\xi>\xi_{1}
. In
practice, it is easier to verify that

|Q|^{2}=\real(Q)^{2}+\imag(Q)^{2}
is monotone, and we thus
work with

\frac{d}{d\xi}|Q|^{2}=2\real(Q\overline{Q^{\prime}}).

The first step is to split
Q
and
Q^{\prime}
into a leading part we can
control, and remainder terms we can bound.

Lemma 7.25
.

For
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
we have

Q(\xi)=p_{Q}(\xi)P(\xi)+R_{Q}(\xi)\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-4}

and

Q^{\prime}(\xi)=p_{Q}(\xi)P^{\prime}(\xi)+R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-3},

with coefficient

p_{Q}(\xi)=\gamma+I_{E}(\xi).

The coefficient
p_{Q}
satisfies

|p_{Q}(\xi)-\gamma|\leq C_{p_{Q}},

and the remainders are bounded as

\displaystyle|R_{Q}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{R_{Q}},

\displaystyle|R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}.

Here the constants are given by

\displaystyle C_{p_{Q}}

\displaystyle=C_{I_{E}}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma+1}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

\displaystyle C_{R_{Q}}

\displaystyle=C_{E}\left(C_{I_{P},2,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{-1}+C_{I_{P},2,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\right)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1},

\displaystyle C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle=C_{E^{\prime}}\left(C_{I_{P},2,1}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,2}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\xi_{1}^{-1}+C_{I_{P},2,3}\|Q^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2}+C_{I_{P},2,4}\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}\|Q^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{v}}\right)\|Q\|_{\mathrm{v}}^{2\sigma-1}.

Proof.

Defining
p_{Q}(\xi)=\gamma+I_{E}(\xi)
, we can write
Q

and
Q^{\prime}
as

Q(\xi)=p_{Q}(\xi)P(\xi)+E(\xi)I_{P}(\xi)\quad\text{and}\quad Q^{\prime}(\xi)=p_{Q}(\xi)P^{\prime}(\xi)+E^{\prime}(\xi)I_{P}(\xi).

The bound on
|p_{Q}(\xi)-\gamma|
follows from the bound for

I_{E}
from Lemma
7.4
. We get the bound for

R_{Q}
and
R_{Q^{\prime}}
using Lemma
7.3
to
bound
E
and
E^{\prime}
, and Lemma
7.16
to bound

I_{P}
.
∎

This allows us to similarly split
\frac{d}{d\xi}|Q(\xi)|^{2}
into
a leading term and a remainder.

Lemma 7.26
.

For
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
we have

\frac{d}{d\xi}|Q|^{2}=2|p_{Q}(\xi)|^{2}\real\left(P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}\right)+2R_{\mathrm{mon}}(\xi)\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}-3},

with

|R_{\mathrm{mon}}(\xi)|\leq C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}

for

C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}=(|\gamma|+C_{p_{Q}})C_{P}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}+(|\gamma|+C_{p_{Q}})C_{P^{\prime}}C_{R_{Q}}\xi_{1}^{-2}+C_{R_{Q}}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-4}.

Proof.

The formula

\frac{d}{d\xi}|Q|^{2}=2\real(Q\overline{Q^{\prime}}),

together with the expansions from Lemma
7.25

gives us

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\xi}|Q|^{2}&=2\real\left(\left(p_{Q}(\xi)P(\xi)+R_{Q}(\xi)\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-4}\right)\left(\overline{p_{Q}(\xi)}\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}+\overline{R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-3}\right)\right)\\
&=2|p_{Q}(\xi)|^{2}\real\left(P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}\right)+2\real\Big(p_{Q}(\xi)P(\xi)\overline{R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)}+\overline{p_{Q}(\xi)}\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}R_{Q}(\xi)\xi^{-1}\\
&\qquad+R_{Q}(\xi)\overline{R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-4}\Big)\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-3}.\end{split}

To bound the remainder term we note that
Lemma
7.3
combined with
Lemma
7.25
gives us

\left|\real\left(p_{Q}(\xi)P(\xi)\overline{R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)}+\overline{p_{Q}(\xi)}\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}R_{Q}(\xi)\xi^{-1}+R_{Q}(\xi)\overline{R_{Q^{\prime}}(\xi)}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-4}\right)\right|\\
\leq|p_{Q}(\xi)|C_{P}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}}+|p_{Q}(\xi)|C_{P^{\prime}}C_{R_{Q}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-2}+C_{R_{Q}}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{1}{\sigma}-4}\\
\leq\left((|\gamma|+C_{p_{Q}})C_{P}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}+(|\gamma|+C_{p_{Q}})C_{P^{\prime}}C_{R_{Q}}\xi^{-2}+C_{R_{Q}}C_{R_{Q^{\prime}}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-4}\right)\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}},

from which the result follows.
∎

As a consequence, to prove that
|Q|^{2}
is monotone for

\xi>\xi_{1}
, it suffices to verify that

\left||p_{Q}(\xi)|^{2}\real\left(P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}\right)\right|>C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-\frac{2}{\sigma}-3}.

Or equivalently, taking into account that
P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}

decays like
\xi^{-\frac{2}{\sigma}-1}
,

\left|\real\left(P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}\xi^{\frac{2}{\sigma}+1}\right)\right|>\frac{C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}}{|p_{Q}(\xi)|^{2}}\xi^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2}.

(24)

To verify that this condition holds for large values of
\xi
we
need to get an enclosure for right-hand side. For this we have the
following lemma.

Lemma 7.27
.

Let
\xi_{1}>1
. We have

P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}\xi^{\frac{2}{\sigma}+1}=-2a|c^{-a}|^{2}(S_{1}(\xi)S_{2}(\xi)+R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}(\xi)),

where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are the sums

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\overline{\left(\frac{(a+1)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\right)}\xi^{-2k}

and the remainder is bounded as

|R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}(\xi)|\leq C_{R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}}

with

\displaystyle C_{R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\right|\xi_{1}^{-2k}\right)C_{R_{U}}(a+1,b+1,n,c\xi_{1}^{2})|c^{-n}|\xi_{1}^{-2n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left|\frac{(a+1)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\right|\xi_{1}^{-2k}\right)C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,c\xi_{1}^{2})|c^{-n}|\xi_{1}^{-2n}

\displaystyle\qquad+C_{R_{U}}(a,b,n,c\xi_{1}^{2})C_{R_{U}}(a+1,b+1,n,c\xi_{1}^{2})|c^{-n}|^{2}\xi_{1}^{-4n}.

Proof.

Writing
P
and
P^{\prime}
in terms of
U
we have

\displaystyle P(\xi)

\displaystyle=U(a,b,c\xi^{2}),

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2c\xi U^{\prime}(a,b,c\xi^{2})=-2ac\xi U(a+1,b+1,c\xi^{2}).

The expansion from Lemma
7.1
gives us

P(\xi)=c^{-a}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}+R_{U}(a,b,n,c\xi^{2})c^{-n}\xi^{-2n}\right)\xi^{-2a}

and

P^{\prime}(\xi)=-2ac^{-a}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a+1)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}+R_{U}(a+1,b+1,n,c\xi^{2})c^{-n}\xi^{-2n}\right)\xi^{-2a-1}.

Expanding the product
P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}
, we have

\begin{split}P(\xi)\overline{P^{\prime}(\xi)}&=-2a|c^{-a}|^{2}\Bigg(\\
&\qquad\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}\right)\overline{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a+1)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}\right)}\\
&\qquad+\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}\right)\overline{R_{U}(a+1,b+1,n,c\xi^{2})}\overline{c}^{-n}\xi^{-2n}\\
&\qquad+\overline{\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a+1)_{k}(a-b+1)_{k}}{k!(-c)^{k}}\xi^{-2k}\right)}R_{U}(a,b,n,c\xi^{2})c^{-n}\xi^{-2n}\\
&\qquad+R_{U}(a,b,n,c\xi^{2})\overline{R_{U}(a+1,b+1,n,c\xi^{2})}|c^{-n}|^{2}\xi^{-4n}\Bigg)\xi^{-\frac{2}{\sigma}-1}.\end{split}

The last three terms in the inner parenthesis is exactly

R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}
, which we can bound as in Lemma
7.1
.
∎

From this lemma we can derive a sufficient condition for
|Q|^{2}

to be monotone.

Lemma 7.28
.

If
|\gamma|>C_{p_{Q}}
and

\left|\real\left(2\overline{a}|c^{-a}|^{2}(S_{1}(\xi)S_{2}(\xi)+R_{P\overline{P^{\prime}}}(\xi))\right)\right|>\frac{C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}}{|p_{Q}(\xi)|^{2}}\xi_{1}^{(2\sigma+1)\mathrm{v}-2},

(25)

then
|Q|^{2}
is monotone for
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the
condition (
24
) and
Lemma
7.27
.
∎

To verify this condition, we need to compute an enclosure of the
left-hand side of (
25
). For this, we note
that
S_{1}(\xi)S_{2}(\xi)
is a polynomial in
\xi^{-1}
, we can
hence directly compute an enclosure by evaluating this polynomial on
the interval
[0,\xi_{1}^{-1}]
using interval arithmetic.

8
Solution at zero

In this section we study solutions to the initial value problem

\begin{split}(1-i\epsilon)\left(Q^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}Q^{\prime}\right)+i\kappa\xi Q^{\prime}+i\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}Q-\omega Q+(1+i\delta)|Q|^{2\sigma}Q&=0,\\
Q(0)&=\mu,\\
Q^{\prime}(0)&=0.\end{split}

To compute
G
(
7
) and its partial derivatives we need to
compute
Q(\xi_{1})
and
Q^{\prime}(\xi_{1})
, as well as their derivatives
with respect to
\mu
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
, at a given

\xi_{1}>0
. For counting the number of critical points of
|Q|

we need enclosures of the curves
Q
,
Q^{\prime}
and
Q^{\prime\prime}
on an
interval
[0,\xi_{2}]
.

For the computations we split
Q
into its real and imaginary parts,

Q(\xi)=a(\xi)+ib(\xi)
. This gives us the system of equations

\displaystyle a^{\prime\prime}+\epsilon b^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}(a^{\prime}+\epsilon b^{\prime})-\kappa\xi b^{\prime}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}b-\omega a+(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}a-\delta(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}b

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}-\epsilon a^{\prime\prime}+\frac{d-1}{\xi}(b^{\prime}-\epsilon a^{\prime})+\kappa\xi a^{\prime}+\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}a-\omega b+(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}b+\delta(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}a

\displaystyle=0.

with initial conditions
a(0)=\mu
,
b(0)=0
and

a^{\prime}(0)=b^{\prime}(0)=0
.

The system is integrated using a rigorous forward numerical integrator
implemented by the CAPD library
[
45
]
. The library
supports computations of very tight enclosures of the solution, and
its partial derivatives with respect to the parameters.

For
d\neq 1
the numerical integrator cannot deal with the
removable singularity at
\xi=0
. To handle this we must perform a
first step using a Taylor expansion at
\xi=0
to compute the
solution up to
\xi=\xi_{0}
for some small
\xi_{0}>0
. This is
described in detail below. After that the numerical integrator is used
on the interval
[\xi_{0},\xi_{1}]
. Note that for
d=1
there is
no removable singularity and the numerical integrator can be used on
the full interval
[0,\xi_{1}]
.

8.1
Handling the removable singularity at zero

For
d\neq 1
, the equation has a removable singularity at

\xi=0
. Taylor expanding
a
and
b
at
\xi=0
as

a=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\xi^{n}
and

b=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}\xi^{n}
gives us

a_{n+2}=\frac{F_{1,n}-\epsilon F_{2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},\quad b_{n+2}=\frac{\epsilon F_{1,n}+F_{2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})}

with

\displaystyle F_{1,n}

\displaystyle=\kappa nb_{n}+\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}b_{n}+\omega a_{n}-u_{1,n}+\delta u_{2,n},

\displaystyle F_{2,n}

\displaystyle=-\kappa na_{n}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}a_{n}+\omega b_{n}-u_{2,n}-\delta u_{1,n}.

Here

u_{1,n}=\left((a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}a\right)_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{2,n}=\left((a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}b\right)_{n}.

Using the above recursion with
a_{0}=\mu
,
b_{0}=0
and

a_{1}=b_{1}=0
it is straightforward to compute Taylor
expansions of arbitrarily high order. What remains is to bound the
remainder term. We will show that for
n>N
we have

|a_{n}|,|b_{n}|\leq r^{n}

for some
r
. This allows us to bound the remainder term for
0<\xi<\frac{1}{r}
as

\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}\xi^{n}\right|,\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}b_{n}\xi^{n}\right|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}(r\xi)^{n}=\frac{(r\xi)^{N+1}}{1-r\xi}.

For the first and second derivatives, the bounds are

\left|\frac{d}{d\xi}\left(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}\xi^{n}\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}na_{n}\xi^{n-1}\right|\leq\frac{1}{\xi}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}n(r\xi)^{n}=\frac{r(r\xi)^{N}(N+1-Nr\xi)}{(1-r\xi)^{2}}

and

\left|\frac{d^{2}}{d\xi^{2}}\left(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}a_{n}\xi^{n}\right)\right|=\left|\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}n(n-1)a_{n}\xi^{n-2}\right|\\
\leq\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}n(n-1)(r\xi)^{n}=\frac{r^{2}(r\xi)^{N-1}(N+N^{2}+(2-2N^{2})r\xi-(N-N^{2})(r\xi)^{2})}{(1-r\xi)^{3}},

with the same bound for the
b_{n}
case.

To start with we have the following lemma, which is valid for

\sigma=1
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\sigma=1
. Let
M
,
N
,
C
and
r
be such that

N
is even,
3M<N
,

|a_{n}|,|b_{n}|\leq Cr^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad n<M

and

|a_{n}|,|b_{n}|\leq r^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad M\leq n\leq N.

If

\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)}{(1+\epsilon^{2})}\Bigg(\frac{|\kappa|}{N+d}+\frac{|\omega|}{(N+2)(N+d)}\\
+2(1+|\delta|)\left(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2N}+\frac{3mC(1+3/N)}{2(N+d)}+\frac{3m^{2}C^{2}}{(N+2)(N+d)}\right)\Bigg)\leq r^{2},

(26)

where
m=\lceil M/2\rceil
, then

|a_{n}|,|b_{n}|\leq r^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad n>N.

Proof.

It is enough to show that

|a_{N+1}|,|b_{N+1}|\leq r^{N+1},|a_{N+2}|,|b_{N+2}|\leq r^{N+2}

and use that the left-hand side in (
26
)
is decreasing in
N
. The result then follows for
n>N+2
by
induction.

Since
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are zero for odd
n
and
N
is
even it follows that
a_{N+1}
and
b_{N+1}
are zero. We
therefore only have to prove that

|a_{N+2}|,|b_{N+2}|\leq r^{N+2}
. We give the proof for

a_{N+2}
, the bound for
b_{N+2}
follows in exactly the
same way.

With
\sigma=1
we get
u_{1,N}=(a^{3})_{N}+(ab^{2})_{N}
and

u_{2,N}=(a^{2}b)_{N}+(b^{3})_{N}
. We have

(a^{3})_{N}=\sum_{i+j+k=N}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}.

However, since
a_{n}=0
for odd
n
and
N
is even we can
limit the sum to

(a^{3})_{N}=\sum_{i+j+k=N/2}a_{2i}a_{2j}a_{2k}.

Since the bound for
a_{n}
depends on if
n
is less than
M

or not, let us group the terms in the sum by how many of the indices

i,j,k
are less than
m=\lceil M/2\rceil
:

1.

The number of terms with no indices less than
m
is

T_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{N/2-3m}\sum_{j=0}^{N/2-3m-i}1=\frac{(N/2-3m+1)(N/2-3m+2)}{2}.

These are bounded by
r^{N}
.

2.

For the terms with exactly one index less than
m
there are

3
choices for which index is the small one. If we let
i
be
the small index, then there are
N/2-2m-i+1
choices for

j
and
k
. Summing over all choices for
i
and
multiplying by
3
we get

T_{1}=3\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(\frac{N}{2}-2m-i+1)=3\frac{m}{2}\left(N-5m+3\right).

These are bounded by
Cr^{N}
.

3.

The number of terms with exactly two indices less than
m

is
T_{2}=3m^{2}
. These are bounded by
C^{2}r^{N}
.

Note that since
3M<N
there are no terms with three indices
less than
m
. In total this gives us the bound

|(a^{3})_{N}|\leq(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})r^{N}.

Since the bounds for
|a_{n}|
and
|b_{n}|
are the same we get the
same bound for
|(ab^{2})_{N}|
,
|(a^{2}b)_{N}|
and
|(b^{3})_{N}|
. In
particular, this gives us

|u_{1,N}|,|u_{2,N}|\leq 2(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})r^{N}.

Combining the above bounds for
|u_{1,N}|
and
|u_{2,N}|
with
the bounds for
|a_{n}|
and
|b_{n}|
we get

|F_{1,N}|\leq(|\kappa|(N+1)+|\omega|)r^{N}+2(1+|\delta|)(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})r^{N},

and the same for
|F_{2,N}|
. This gives us

\begin{split}|a_{N+2}|&\leq\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)(|\kappa|(N+1)+|\omega|+2(1+|\delta|)(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2}))}{(N+2)(N+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})}r^{N}\\
&=\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)}{(1+\epsilon^{2})}\left(\frac{|\kappa|}{N+d}+\frac{|\omega|}{(N+2)(N+d)}+2(1+|\delta|)\frac{(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})}{(N+2)(N+d)}\right)r^{N}.\end{split}

We have

\begin{split}\frac{T_{0}}{(N+2)(N+d)}&=\frac{(N/2-3m+1)(N/2-3m+2)}{2(N+2)(N+d)}\\
&=\frac{1}{8}\frac{(N-6m+2)(N-6m+4)}{(N+2)(N+d)}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{8}\frac{N+4}{N+d}\leq\frac{1}{8}\left(1+\frac{4}{N}\right)=\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2N},\end{split}

as well as

\frac{T_{1}}{(N+2)(N+d)}=3\frac{m}{2}\frac{N-5m+3}{(N+2)(N+d)}\leq 3\frac{m}{2}\frac{N+3}{(N+2)(N+d)}\leq\frac{3m(1+3/N)}{2(N+d)}.

This gives us

|a_{N+2}|\leq\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)}{(1+\epsilon^{2})}\Bigg(\frac{|\kappa|}{N+d}+\frac{|\omega|}{(N+2)(N+d)}\\
+2(1+|\delta|)\left(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2N}+\frac{3mC(1+3/N)}{2(N+d)}+\frac{3m^{2}C^{2}}{(N+2)(N+d)}\right)\Bigg)r^{N}.

By (
26
), the factor in front of

r^{N}
is bounded by
r^{2}
, giving us our required bound

|a_{N+2}|\leq r^{N+2}
.
∎

8.2
Derivatives with respect to
\mu
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon

Next, we handle the derivatives with respect to
\mu
,
\kappa

and
\epsilon
. We let
a_{\mu}
and
b_{\mu}
denote the
derivatives of
a
and
b
with respect to the initial value

\mu
, similarly with the derivatives with respect to
\kappa
and

\epsilon
.

Since the CAPD library automatically supports computing derivatives
with respect to the parameters we do not directly need the ODEs
satisfied by these functions. However, for the removable singularity
at zero we do need Taylor expansions of these derivatives. Since the
series expansions for
a
and
b
are absolutely convergent we can
directly differentiate the expressions for
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
to
get recurrence relations for the coefficients of the derivatives.

Differentiating the recurrence relations for
a_{n}
and
b_{n}

with respect to
\mu
,
\kappa
and
\epsilon
respectively, we get

\displaystyle a_{\mu,n+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{\mu,1,n}-\epsilon F_{\mu,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},\quad b_{\mu,n+2}=\frac{\epsilon F_{\mu,1,n}+F_{\mu,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},

\displaystyle a_{\kappa,n+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{\kappa,1,n}-\epsilon F_{\kappa,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},\quad b_{\kappa,n+2}=\frac{\epsilon F_{\kappa,1,n}+F_{\kappa,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},

\displaystyle a_{\epsilon,n+2}

\displaystyle=\frac{F_{\epsilon,1,n}-\epsilon F_{\epsilon,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},\quad b_{\epsilon,n+2}=\frac{\epsilon F_{\epsilon,1,n}+F_{\epsilon,2,n}}{(n+2)(n+d)(1+\epsilon^{2})},

with

\displaystyle F_{\mu,1,n}

\displaystyle=\kappa nb_{\mu,n}+\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}b_{\mu,n}+\omega a_{\mu,n}-u_{\mu,1,n}+\delta u_{\mu,2,n},

\displaystyle F_{\mu,2,n}

\displaystyle=-\kappa na_{\mu,n}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}a_{\mu,n}+\omega b_{\mu,n}-u_{\mu,2,n}-\delta u_{\mu,1,n},

\displaystyle F_{\kappa,1,n}

\displaystyle=\kappa nb_{\kappa,n}+nb_{n}+\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}b_{\kappa,n}+\frac{1}{\sigma}b_{n}+\omega a_{\kappa,n}-u_{\kappa,1,n}+\delta u_{\kappa,2,n},

\displaystyle F_{\kappa,2,n}

\displaystyle=-\kappa na_{\kappa,n}-na_{n}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}a_{\kappa,n}-\frac{1}{\sigma}a_{n}+\omega b_{\kappa,n}-u_{\kappa,2,n}-\delta u_{\kappa,1,n},

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon,1,n}

\displaystyle=-(n+2)(n+d)b_{n+2}+\kappa nb_{\epsilon,n}+\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}b_{\epsilon,n}+\omega a_{\epsilon,n}-u_{\epsilon,1,n}+\delta u_{\epsilon,2,n},

\displaystyle F_{\epsilon,2,n}

\displaystyle=(n+2)(n+d)a_{n+2}-\kappa na_{\epsilon,n}-\frac{\kappa}{\sigma}a_{\epsilon,n}+\omega b_{\epsilon,n}-u_{\epsilon,2,n}-\delta u_{\epsilon,1,n}.

Here

\displaystyle u_{\mu,1,n}

\displaystyle=\left((a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}a_{\mu}+2\sigma(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma-1}a^{2}a_{\mu}+2\sigma(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma-1}abb_{\mu}\right)_{n},

\displaystyle u_{\mu,2,n}

\displaystyle=\left((a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma}b_{\mu}+2\sigma(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma-1}aba_{\mu}+2\sigma(a^{2}+b^{2})^{\sigma-1}b^{2}b_{\mu}\right)_{n},

and similarly for
\kappa
and
\epsilon
. For the initial
conditions we get
a_{\mu,0}=1
and

a_{\mu,1}=b_{\mu,0}=b_{\mu,1}=0
for the derivatives with
respect to
\mu
. For the derivatives with respect to
\kappa
and

\epsilon
the initial conditions are all zero.

To bound the remainder term we follow the same approach as above,
showing that for
n>N
we have

|a_{\mu,n}|,|b_{\mu,n}|\leq r^{n}
for some
r
, and similarly
for the derivatives with respect to
\kappa
and
\epsilon
.

For
a_{\mu,n}
and
b_{\mu,n}
, the only difference compared to

a_{n}
and
b_{n}
is in the form of the non-linearity. For

a_{\kappa,n}
,
b_{\kappa,n}
,
a_{\epsilon,n}
and

b_{\epsilon,n}
we have slightly modified recurrence relations. We
therefore get slightly modified versions of
Lemma
8.1
in all three cases.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\sigma=1
. Let
M
,
N
,
C
,
r
satisfy the
conditions of Lemma
8.1
, as well as

|a_{\mu,n}|,|b_{\mu,n}|\leq Cr^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad n<M

and

|a_{\mu,n}|,|b_{\mu,n}|\leq r^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad M\leq n\leq N.

If the inequality (
26
) holds and we also
have

\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)}{(1+\epsilon^{2})}\Bigg(\frac{|\kappa|}{N+d}+\frac{|\omega|}{(N+2)(N+d)}\\
+6(1+|\delta|)\left(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2N}+\frac{3mC(1+3/N)}{2(N+d)}+\frac{3m^{2}C^{2}}{(N+2)(N+d)}\right)\Bigg)\leq r^{2},

(27)

then

|a_{\mu,n}|,|b_{\mu,n}|\leq r^{n}\quad\text{for}\quad n>N.

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as Lemma
8.1
.
The only required adjustment is in the bounds of
u_{\mu,1,N}
and

u_{\mu,2,N}
. In this case we have (for
\sigma=1
),

\displaystyle u_{\mu,1,N}

\displaystyle=(a^{2}a_{\mu}+b^{2}a_{\mu}+2a^{2}a_{\mu}+2abb_{\mu})_{N},

\displaystyle u_{\mu,2,N}

\displaystyle=(a^{2}b_{\mu}+b^{2}b_{\mu}+2aba_{\mu}+2b^{2}b_{\mu})_{N}.

The triple products can be bounded in exactly the same way as
before, giving us

|u_{\mu,1,N}|,|u_{\mu,2,N}|\leq 6(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})r^{N}.

The rest of the proof is the same as in
Lemma
8.1
. We get the bound

|a_{\mu,N+2}|\leq\frac{(1+|\epsilon|)}{(1+\epsilon^{2})}\left(\frac{|\kappa|}{N+d}+\frac{|\omega|}{(N+2)(N+d)}+6(1+|\delta|)\frac{(T_{0}+T_{1}C+T_{2}C^{2})}{(N+2)(N+d)}\right)r^{N},

This can then be shown to be bounded by the left-hand side of
inequality (
27
) times
r^{N}
,
which gives us the required bound.
∎

The versions for the derivatives with respect to
\kappa
and

\epsilon
follow in a similar way, and we leave out the proofs.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let

. Let

,

,

Implementation details

The full code on which the computer-assisted parts of the proofs are
based, as well as notebooks presenting the results and figures, is
available in the repository
[
19
]
. Here we give an overview of
the methods used and their implementations. More details about the
structure of the code and exactly how the results are generated can be
found in that repository. The majority of the code is implemented in
Julia
[
6
]
, except for the parts related to
CAPD
[
45
]

For the NLS equation, the computations can, at a high level, be split
into five parts:

1.

Computing the (non-rigorous) numerical approximations;

2.

Enclosing
Q_{\infty}
and its derivatives;

3.

Enclosing
Q_{0}
and its derivatives;

4.

Enclosing roots of

;

5.

Counting critical points of
|Q|
.

For the CGL equation, each individual segment of the branch is handled
using the same approach as for the NLS equation, but we also need to
compute numerical approximations of the branches and join all the
individual segments together.

For the non-rigorous parts, namely the numerical approximations, we
make use of classical numerical methods. For this, there are many
high-quality scientific libraries available in Julia. The most
important ones for our purposes are state-of-the-art methods for
solving ODEs with

DifferentialEquations.jl

[
64
]
, nonlinear
solvers in
NonlinearSolve.jl

[
61
]
, and methods for
automatic continuation of branches implemented in

BifurcationKit.jl

[
75
]
(this package
automatically handles bifurcation scenarios such as the saddle nodes
appearing in our problem).

The rigorous parts, on which the computer-assisted results are based,
make use of two different libraries, the CAPD
[
45
]

library and the Arb
[
42
]
/FLINT
[
31
]

library
3
3

3

In 2023 Arb was merged with the FLINT library.
. The
CAPD library is used for the rigorous numerical integration of the

5
), thus obtaining rigorous enclosures of

.

The enclosure of

(as well as its Jacobian) is computed
following these steps:

1.

Enclosures of the involved special functions are computed at

,

, as well as the expansion given in

2.

Bounds for all the coefficients given in

7.1
and

3.

An initial bound for

is computed by bounding

from Lemma

and (

from the fixed-point argument in

4.

The bound for

is then used to compute bounds
for the norms of derivatives of

with respect to
\xi

\real\gamma
,
\imag\gamma
,
\kappa
and

is done using the lemmas in Section

in turn rely on bounds for

and

from lemmas in
Section
7.3.1
. Since the bounds for higher
derivatives generally make use of bounds for the lower derivatives,
this has to be done in an iterative fashion.

5.

The enclosures for
Q(\xi_{1})
and associated derivatives are
computed following the approach in
Section
7.3.4
.

For the computation of
Q_{0}
, the first step is to compute an
enclosure of
Q_{0}(\xi_{0})
for some small value
\xi_{0}
using
a Taylor expansion at zero. The code starts by trying

\xi_{0}=0.01
, but tries successively smaller values
in case that fails. The Taylor expansion is computed using the
recurrence relation for the coefficients given in
Section
8.1
, which, combined
with the bounds for the remainder from Lemma
8.1
,
allows us to enclose
Q_{0}(\xi_{0})
. The solution is then
integrated along the interval
[\xi_{0},\xi_{1}]
using the CAPD
library: this makes use of high-degree Taylor expansions (degree
20 in our case) together with accurate representations of
the enclosures that are necessary to efficiently evolve the enclosures
in time. For the case
d=1
, there is no removable singularity at

\xi=0
, and
\xi_{0}
is taken to be zero.

As discussed in Section
5.1
, the enclosures
of the roots of
G
are computed using the
Krawczyk interval
Newton method
. This involves computing, for a given set
X
, an
enclosure of the set

\midint(X)-YG(\midint(X))+(I-YJ_{G}(X))(X-\midint(X)),

where enclosures of
G
and
J_{G}
are computed through

Q_{\infty}
and
Q_{0}
as described above. The matrix
Y
is a
preconditioner and is computed by taking the midpoint of the
enclosure for
J_{G}(X)
and inverting the resulting matrix using
regular non-rigorous floating-point arithmetic. To find a set
X

for which the Newton condition (
8
) is
satisfied, we start with an initial approximation,
X_{0}
, and then
compute

\tilde{X}_{1}=\midint(X_{0})-YG(\midint(X_{0}))+(I-YJ_{G}(X_{0}))(X_{0}-\midint(X_{0})).

The set
\tilde{X}_{1}
is then expanded slightly by increasing its
diameter by a small factor, and we call the expanded set
X_{1}
.
This process is then iterated, and we stop if at any step we have

\tilde{X}_{i+1}\subseteq\operatorname{int}(X_{i})
, in which
case we set
X=X_{i}
. Initially,
X_{0}
represents a thin
interval (a point), but due to the nonzero error bounds introduced in
the computations, the later iterations will always be non-thin
intervals. If the verification has not succeeded after
10 steps, the process stops and reports failure. The
process also stops early if at any point the enclosure of either

G(\midint(X_{i}))
or
J_{G}(X_{i})
is non-finite, or if the
computation of
Y
fails. On success, the set
X
returned by this
approach generally just barely satisfies the Newton condition. This
means that the existence and uniqueness sets are about the same size.
To expand the set of uniqueness, we can successively widen
X
until

J_{G}
stops being invertible; this generally gives us a much
larger set for which we have uniqueness.

The computation of critical points closely follows the approach
detailed in Section
5.1
, and there is not
much more to add. The bounds on
C_{p_{\mathrm{mon}}}
and

C_{R_{\mathrm{mon}}}
are computed in the same way as those used
for
Q_{\infty}
. The enclosures on the interval
[0,\xi_{2}]

are computed using a combination of a Taylor expansion at zero and
CAPD, as described for
Q_{0}
above.

\displaystyle=U(b-a,b,-c\xi^{2})B_{W,\epsilon}\xi^{d-1}-U^{\prime}(b-a,b,-c\xi^{2})c_{\epsilon}B_{W}\xi^{d+1}.

They can then be bounded termwise.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\xi_{1}>1
. For
0\leq m\leq 2
,

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
\xi\geq\xi_{1}
we have

|D^{(m)}(\xi)|\leq C_{D^{(m)}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m},\quad|H^{(m)}(\xi)|\leq C_{H^{(m)}}\xi^{-\frac{1}{\sigma}-m},

with the constants given by

\displaystyle C_{D}

\displaystyle=|c_{\kappa}B_{W}|C_{P}+(|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P}+|B_{W}|C_{P,\kappa}\log(\xi_{1}))\xi_{1}^{-2},

\displaystyle C_{D^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=|c_{\kappa}B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime}}+\Big(|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P^{\prime}}+2|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P}

\displaystyle\qquad+\big(|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime},\kappa}+2|B_{W}|C_{P,\kappa}\big)\log(\xi_{1})\Big)\xi_{1}^{-2},

\displaystyle C_{D^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=|c_{\kappa}B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P^{\prime\prime}}+4|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P^{\prime}}+6|B_{W,\kappa}|C_{P}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\big(|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime\prime},\kappa}+4|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime},\kappa}+6|B_{W}|C_{P,\kappa}\big)\log(\xi_{1})\Big)\xi_{1}^{-2},

\displaystyle C_{H}

\displaystyle=|c_{\epsilon}B_{W}|C_{P}+(|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P}+|B_{W}|C_{P,\epsilon})\xi_{1}^{-2},

\displaystyle C_{H^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=|c_{\epsilon}B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime}}+\left(|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P^{\prime}}+2|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P}+|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}+2|B_{W}|C_{P,\epsilon}\right)\xi_{1}^{-2},

\displaystyle C_{H^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=|c_{\epsilon}B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\Big(|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P^{\prime\prime}}+4|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P^{\prime}}+6|B_{W,\epsilon}|C_{P}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime\prime},\epsilon}+4|B_{W}|C_{P^{\prime},\epsilon}+6|B_{W}|C_{P,\epsilon}\Big)\xi_{1}^{-2}.

Proof.

For
D
we have

\displaystyle D^{\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-c_{\kappa}B_{W}P^{\prime}(\xi)+B_{W,\kappa}P^{\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-2}-2B_{W,\kappa}P(\xi)\xi^{-3}+B_{W}P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-2}-2B_{W}P_{\kappa}(\xi)\xi^{-3},

\displaystyle D^{\prime\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-c_{\kappa}B_{W}P^{\prime\prime}(\xi)+B_{W,\kappa}P^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-2}-4B_{W,\kappa}P^{\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-3}+6B_{W,\kappa}P(\xi)\xi^{-4}

\displaystyle\qquad+B_{W}P_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-2}-4B_{W}P_{\kappa}^{\prime}(\xi)\xi^{-3}+6B_{W}P_{\kappa}(\xi)\xi^{-4}.

The expressions for
H
are similar, with differentiation with
respect to
\kappa
replaced with differentiation with respect to

\epsilon
. All the bounds are then computed termwise.
∎

Finally, we have a bound that is used in the proof of
Lemma
7.5
.

Lemma A.7
.

For all
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sigma\geq 1
we have

\left||z_{1}|^{2\sigma}z_{1}-|z_{2}|^{2\sigma}z_{2}\right|\leq M_{\sigma}|z_{1}-z_{2}|(|z_{1}|^{2\sigma}+|z_{2}|^{2\sigma}).

For
\sigma=1
we can take

M_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{4-2\sqrt{2}}+1.

In general we can take

M_{\sigma}=\max\left(\max_{0\leq t\leq t_{0}}g(t),g_{t_{0}}(t_{1}),\max_{t_{1}\leq t\leq 2}g(t),2\right),

for any
0\leq t_{0}\leq 1\leq t_{1}\leq 2
. Here

g(t)=\frac{1-t^{2\sigma}}{(1-t)(1+t^{2\sigma})}+1\quad\text{and}\quad g_{t_{0}}(t)=\frac{1-t^{2\sigma}}{(1-t)(1+t_{0}^{2\sigma})}+1.

Proof.

Let

f(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\left||z_{1}|^{2\sigma}z_{1}-|z_{2}|^{2\sigma}z_{2}\right|}{|z_{1}-z_{2}|(|z_{1}|^{2\sigma}+|z_{2}|^{2\sigma})}.

We want
M_{\sigma}
to be an upper bound for
|f(z_{1},z_{2})|
. Writing

z_{2}=sz_{1}
, with
s\in\mathbb{C}
gives us

f(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{|1-|s|^{2\sigma}s|}{|1-s|(1+|s|^{2\sigma})}.

An upper bound is given by

\frac{|1-|s|^{2\sigma}|}{|1-s|(1+|s|^{2\sigma})}+\frac{|s|^{2\sigma}}{1+|s|^{2\sigma}}\leq\frac{|1-|s|^{2\sigma}|}{|1-s|(1+|s|^{2\sigma})}+1.

If we let
t=|s|
and use that
|1-s|\geq|1-|s||
we get
that a uniform upper bound for
|f(z_{1},z_{2})|
is given by

\sup_{t\geq 0}g(t)
, with
g(t)
as in the statement of the
lemma. For
\sigma=1
this simplifies to

g(t)=\frac{1+t}{1+t^{2\sigma}}+1,

for which the upper bound is attained at
t=\sqrt{2}-1
and
given by
\sqrt{2}/(4-2\sqrt{2})+1
. This gives us
M_{1}
.
For
\sigma\neq 1
we start by noting that

g(t)\leq\frac{1+t^{2\sigma}}{|1-t|(1+t^{2\sigma})}+1=\frac{1}{|1-t|}+1.

In particular, we have
g(2)\leq 2
, and hence

\sup_{t\geq 0}g(t)\leq\max\left(\max_{0\leq t\leq 2}g(t),2\right).

To handle the removable singularity at
t=1
we let

g_{t_{0}}(t)
be as in the statement of the lemma and note that

g(t)\leq g_{t_{0}}(t)
for any
t\geq t_{0}
. Furthermore, we
have that
g_{t_{0}}
is increasing in
t
. In particular, this
gives us

\max_{t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}}g(t)\leq g_{t_{0}}(t_{1})

for any
0\leq t_{0}\leq 1\leq t_{1}\leq 2
. Combining the bounds
for
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
,
t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{1}
,
t_{1}\leq t\leq 2
and
t\geq 2
gives us the result.

What is left is to prove that
g_{t_{0}}
is increasing in
t
. For
doing so, observe that its derivative is

g^{\prime}_{t_{0}}(t)=\dfrac{(2\sigma-1)t^{2\sigma}-2\sigma t^{2\sigma-1}+1}{(1-t)^{2}(1+t_{0}^{2\sigma})}.

Hence,
g_{t_{0}}
is monotone increasing if its derivative’s
numerator is non-negative (it is equal to
1
for
t=0
, equal to
zero at
t=1
and positive for large
t
). Finally, this numerator’s
derivative is equal to
2\sigma(2\sigma-1)t^{2\sigma-2}(t-1)
,
which is negative for
t\in[0,1)
, zero at
t=1
and positive for

t\in(1,\infty)
.
∎
