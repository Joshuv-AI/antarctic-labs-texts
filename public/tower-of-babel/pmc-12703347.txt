Identifying azithromycin responders with an individual treatment effect model in COPD
Authors: Kenneth Verstraete, Iwein Gyselinck, Helene Huts, Remco Stuart Djamin, Michaël Staes, Sander Talman, Sarah Lindberg, Menno van der Eerden, Maarten De Vos, Wim Janssens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveLong-term azithromycin treatment effectively prevents acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, patients would benefit from better identification of responders and non-responders to minimise unnecessary exposure. We aimed to assess treatment effect heterogeneity and estimate individual treatment effects (ITEs) to distinguish patients most likely to benefit from prophylactic treatment.MethodsWe used data from 1025 patients of the MACRO trial to assess the ITE of azithromycin on annual exacerbation rate. A Causal Forest was used as a causal machine learning model. We independently validated our findings using data from 83 patients of the COLUMBUS trial.ResultsThe tertile of patients with the best predicted ITE within MACRO and within the COLUMBUS independent validation cohort showed significant and substantially greater reductions in annual exacerbation rates (in MACRO −0.50, rate ratio 0.70, p=0.01, in COLUMBUS: −2.28, rate ratio 0.43, p<0.001) compared with the average treatment effect across the entire cohort (MACRO −0.35, rate ratio 0.83, p=0.01 and COLUMBUS −1.28, rate ratio 0.58, p=0.001). Conversely, no significant treatment effect was observed in the remaining two-thirds of patients. Primary determinants of ITE included respiratory symptoms, white blood cell count, haemoglobin, C-reactive protein and forced vital capacity. Smoking status did not emerge as a significant predictor.ConclusionBased on five easily obtainable parameters to predict ITE, we identified treatment effect heterogeneity in COPD subjects treated with azithromycin maintenance therapy and found a small subgroup of responders driving the average reduction in exacerbations reported in previous trials.

Introduction

Although long-term prophylaxis with azithromycin is effective to prevent acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD),14 its widespread use is limited due to concerns regarding cardiovascular adverse effects, ototoxicity, drug–drug interactions and, most importantly, the development of bacterial resistance.1 It would therefore be advantageous to reserve this treatment for patients most likely to derive significant benefit.

Identifying consistent subgroups with heterogeneous treatment effects of azithromycin has been challenging. Traits such as airflow limitation, age and smoking status have been associated with better or worse treatment responses in some post hoc analyses of existing data.5 Yet, the latter two have lacked independent replication, limiting their reliability.6 Alternatively, it has been suggested to model baseline exacerbation risk and only target the highest-risk patients with prophylactic treatment.7 However, the underlying assumption that the treatment effect of azithromycin is directly proportional to exacerbation risk lacks supporting data.

The traditional statistical methods to detect treatment effect heterogeneity that were employed in previous analyses have some inherent limitations. They are designed to detect differences between sufficiently large prespecified groups, yet may fail to incorporate multiple and often non-linear interactions between treatment and patient traits. In this post hoc analysis of the MACRO dataset,8 we explored a machine learning approach as a complementary method to address these limitations. Specifically, we used the Causal Forest algorithm,9 designed to identify treatment effect heterogeneity attributable to a causal effect between the treatment and a set of predetermined variables. Our aim was to estimate the individual treatment effect (ITE) of azithromycin on annual exacerbation rate, providing guidance for personalised patient care in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Subsequently, we used the data from the COLUMBUS trial to independently validate our findings.10 Additionally, we developed a freely accessible online tool for clinical use.

Materials and methods

A flow chart detailing the study population, model development, variable selection and external validation is presented in online supplemental figure E1.

Study design and participants

We used data of two double-blinded, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing azithromycin to placebo in patients with moderate to severe COPD. In the MACRO trial, 1142 patients with previous exacerbations or on long-term oxygen were recruited from 17 sites in the USA and randomised 1:1 to placebo or 250 mg azithromycin daily for 12 months.8 In the COLUMBUS trial, patients from a single Dutch centre having had at least three exacerbations in the year before inclusion were randomised 1:1 to placebo or 500 mg azithromycin three times a week for 12 months.10 Both trials showed significant benefit of azithromycin on their primary and secondary outcomes. Annual exacerbation rate was 1.48 versus 1.83 exacerbations per year (rate ratio (RR) 0.83, p=0.01) in MACRO, and 1.94 versus 3.22 exacerbations per year (RR 0.58, p=0.001) in COLUMBUS for azithromycin and placebo, respectively. The data of both trials are available through request to the study investigators.

ITE on exacerbation rate

We aimed to estimate the ITE of azithromycin on annual exacerbation rate, defined as the absolute difference in an individual patient’s annual exacerbation rate with and without azithromycin. A negative ITE indicated a benefit from azithromycin; for example, an ITE of −0.5 could reflect a reduction from 1 to 0.5 exacerbations per year or, equivalently, from 3 to 2.5 exacerbations per year.

Variable selection

Candidate variables for the model were baseline variables selected from the MACRO dataset. Highly correlated variables, zero-variance variables and variables with >20% missingness were omitted. Experienced physicians (IG, WJ) further refined the selection based on clinical relevance, focussing on removing highly correlated and clinically redundant variables.

To reduce overfitting and improve clinical usability, the number of variables was further reduced with a data-driven approach using complete case analysis. We performed a repeated procedure during which the MACRO dataset was randomly split (70:30) 100 times, with stratification based on treatment assignment, outcome and time on treatment. We trained a Causal Forest with the same hyperparameters in every split.9 Feature importances were directly derived from each Causal Forest, were averaged over all splits and ranked according to their mean importance.

To decide the final number of variables, model performance was evaluated as a function of the number of variables. The Q-score was used as an evaluation metric; this score ranges from −∞ to 1 with a strictly positive score indicating treatment effect heterogeneity identifiable by the model. Our approach and the Q-score have previously been described in detail.11 Variables were iteratively added in descending order of importance, and performance was measured over 100 additional random train-test splits per iteration. The number of variables was decided as the point where average model performance had reached a maximum and began to decline significantly (online supplemental figure E2). The full list of candidate variables eligible to build our model is provided in the online supplement (online supplemental table E1).

Model development and internal validation in the MACRO trial

After deciding the optimal number of variables, the same 70:30 stratified splitting procedure was now repeated 1000 times. During each iteration, we used the Causal Forest to develop a model with the selected predictor variables to estimate ITE. Grid search and threefold cross-validation were used on the 70% training data to tune model hyperparameters with the Q-score as the evaluation metric. For each random split, the optimised Causal Forest predicted ITEs for every subject in the test set. The internal validation Q-score was calculated as the average Q-score across all test sets. Since each subject appeared in multiple test sets, their mean ITE was calculated across the 1000 splits and used for the calibration plot. On these aggregated results, the Q-score was also calculated.

To illustrate treatment effect heterogeneity graphically, we ranked all subjects by their mean predicted ITE (averaged across all test set appearances) and discretised into quantiles. For consistency across the MACRO and COLUMBUS datasets and due to the limited number of subjects in the COLUMBUS trial, 3-quantiles or tertiles were used. Each tertile included subjects from both the placebo and azithromycin arms. Within and across tertiles, we compared mean annual exacerbation rates, rate ratios and relevant baseline characteristics for treated versus untreated subjects along the ITE spectrum.

Finally, we trained a Causal Forest model on the entire MACRO dataset using the optimal hyperparameters and used this for external validation.

External validation

We used the subjects from the COLUMBUS trial to externally validate the model that was trained on the entire MACRO dataset. The input variables of the model were extracted for these subjects and converted to the same units when necessary. The same tertile-based discretisation as before was used to illustrate treatment effect heterogeneity, and model performance was measured using the Q-score.

Relationship between ITE and baseline exacerbation risk

To assess a potential relationship between untreated exacerbation rate and variables that weighed the strongest on the treatment effect according to our own model, we performed regression analysis on the COLUMBUS data. Additionally, we contrasted our causal approach with traditional risk-based methods that assume the treatment effect of azithromycin is proportional to exacerbation rate under control conditions. For this comparison, we discretised the validation cohort according to each subject’s predicted baseline exacerbation rate, as estimated by the well-established Acute COPD Exacerbation Prediction Tool (ACCEPT).7 12 In this non-causal approach, ITE is indirectly estimated by assuming a constant relative rate reduction across the entire population, making the absolute treatment effect proportional to the predicted baseline rate. We then again compared exacerbation rates of treated and untreated patients within the corresponding risk-based tertiles. We also compared the treatment effect estimates by the ACCEPT 2.0 tool, which also estimates the ITE of azithromycin on exacerbation rate in COPD.12

Online ITE calculator

For the development of the online calculator, the externally validated model was retrained by pooling all available datapoints of both MACRO and COLUMBUS and retraining the model on the combined dataset. Because we expected inflated predictions at the ends of the ITE spectrum due to inherent limitations of the model, we chose not to provide direct ITE estimates as predicted by the Causal Forest. Rather, based on the distribution of the predicted ITE in the combined dataset, the calculator provides the ITE ranking of an individual patient (the percentile) and the observed treatment effect range of the corresponding tertile.

Data description and statistical analyses

Characteristics of the included population, including demographics, smoking status, spirometry data, exacerbation history, concomitant COPD medications and the variables that contributed to the causal model, were summarised. Data are presented as mean±SD, median (Q1–Q3 IQR) or counts and percentages. Complete case analysis was used for both MACRO and COLUMBUS. Exacerbation rates were compared with generalised linear models using the natural log of time on treatment as the offset variable. In accordance with the original analyses from both studies, the negative binomial distribution and the Poisson distribution were used for MACRO and COLUMBUS, respectively. The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) methodology was used on the MACRO dataset to assess how individual variables contributed to the ITE and to provide a personalised analysis per subject.13 14 Python 3.8 was used for the entire analysis. EconML15 by Microsoft Research and Scikit-learn16 were used for the machine learning models and statsmodels17 for the statistical analyses. All p values were two-sided, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Negative SHAP values are indicative of a beneficial influence on individual treatment responses. In general, one can observe that lower symptom scores, high white blood cell counts, low haemoglobin levels, high C-reactive protein and high forced vital capacity favour treatment. The ITE calculator is freely available online (https://azithromycin-ite-tool.streamlit.app/).

Discussion

Using a machine learning approach with data drawn from two RCTs, we developed and externally validated a model capable of providing ITE estimates of maintenance treatment with azithromycin to prevent AECOPD. The ITE estimates are derived from a concise set of parameters readily available in routine clinical practice. With this model, we observed significant treatment effect heterogeneity among patients with COPD in both trials, identifying a limited subset of patients experiencing notably beneficial treatment effects. To the best of our knowledge, our causal modelling approach is the first to pin down traits that have a causal relationship to the treatment response to azithromycin, integrate them in a clinically useful model, and successfully validate them in an external cohort.

COPD treatment guidelines emphasise careful patient selection and individualised decisions for prophylactic macrolide treatment. However, identifying patient traits that consistently determine better or worse treatment responses has proven challenging. Moreover, individual patients often accumulate multiple positive and negative traits, complicating assessments of predetermined subgroups and reducing the clinical relevance of such an approach. By contrast, our model offers an estimate of the absolute reduction in annual AECOPD occurrences with azithromycin treatment compared to without, based on individualised predictions. This straightforward and easily communicable measure can serve as a reference point for discussions with patients about their personalised treatment preferences.

The model is freely accessible online for clinician use by means of an ITE calculator that, based on five input variables, provides the ITE ranking (percentile of the predicted ITE distribution) and its corresponding observed treatment effect on exacerbations. Negative ITE values naturally carry greater weight for treatment decisions. Evidently, routine precautions (eg, exclusion of prolonged QT, drug–drug interactions and nontuberculous mycobacterial infection) as well as adherence to local antibiotic stewardship practices are always to be considered.

Some guidelines recommend reserving azithromycin exclusively for patients with the highest exacerbation rates, based on the assumption that the relative risk reduction remains constant and the absolute treatment effect scales proportionally with the untreated exacerbation rate.18 However, when we applied the ACCEPT score—an established tool for predicting individual future COPD exacerbation rates and severity7 12—to our validation cohort, we found no increased treatment effect in patients with the highest predicted exacerbation rates. This demonstrates that in this population, selected for having recent exacerbations in the previous year, further predictions based on baseline exacerbation rates are ineffective for identifying treatment effect heterogeneity, emphasising the superiority of our ITE model for this purpose.

SHAP analysis indicated that (super)responders have better preserved lung function and less symptom-driven disease but exhibit a clear inflammatory endotype characterised by a higher CRP, higher white blood cell count and lower haemoglobin. The inverse relationship between treatment effect and disease severity—measured by FEV1 and symptom burden—suggests that earlier intervention may yield greater benefits in this high-risk population. It aligns with our previous observation on exacerbation risk predicted by ACCEPT, of which FEV1 and SGRQ are major determinants. It is noteworthy that younger age and active smoking have previously been associated with a reduced treatment effect, and azithromycin use in active smokers has even been discouraged due to concerns about its alleged lack of efficacy. 5 19 While younger patients and smokers indeed seem to be overrepresented in tertiles with worse treatment response, neither trait has emerged as a significant independent predictor of treatment effect in our model. Although smoking cessation is of course strongly recommended, we caution against withholding prophylactic azithromycin solely based on smoking status.

Our feature selection method aimed to improve both model stability and clinical utility by constraining the number of predictor variables. Despite finding that the model’s performance did not benefit from the inclusion of extra variables beyond the initial five with the highest feature importance, it is worth noting that several other variables still retained substantial feature importance scores. Notably, tumour necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFR2) and two immunoglobulin G subclasses (IgG1 and IgG4) exhibited feature importance scores only slightly lower than those of the top five predictors. TNFR2 acts as an upstream regulator within the pro-inflammatory nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signalling cascade, associated with macrophage-induced inflammasome activation and bacterial dysbiosis.20 21 In the context of IgG, previous studies have shown an inverse correlation between serum IgG levels and the risk of AECOPD, likely attributed to an increased susceptibility to infections.22 As azithromycin is an antibiotic as well as a modulator of macrophage activation and a known inhibitor of NF-κB signalling, a plausible causal relationship exists for both variables.23 24 This hints towards the potential of our modelling approach in biomarker discovery and identification of endotypes.

Several limitations must be noted. First, the availability of biological biomarkers in the MACRO data was limited and restricted mainly to monocyte–macrophage related inflammatory signalling. Consequently, other relevant biomarkers, such as blood eosinophil counts, were not available for the training set, and their potential causal relationship with the treatment effect of azithromycin could not be assessed. No significant difference was observed, however, when these parameters were compared in the different quantiles of the COLUMBUS validation data. Second, the predictions of our model are not generalisable beyond the population included in the original data, which primarily involved patients of white ancestry with smoking-related COPD, and without concomitant asthma or bronchiectasis. Also, both studies focused exclusively on patients with a history of exacerbations, and the model should therefore not be applied for primary prophylaxis to prevent a first exacerbation event. Third, while the model trained on MACRO maintained good discrimination, its calibration in the COLUMBUS dataset was suboptimal due to significant disparities in the inclusion criteria (at least three treated exacerbations in the previous year for subjects in COLUMBUS) and, therefore, the exacerbation rate distribution (3.22 exacerbations/year in COLUMBUS vs 1.83 exacerbations/year in MACRO in the placebo groups). These disparities were potentially reflected in the Q-score. To address this, we retrained the model on the pooled data for the online calculator and provided observed differences in exacerbation rates based on ITE ranking rather than raw ITE predictions. Lastly, although our Causal Forest approach demonstrates robustness in an external validation cohort and is inherently less susceptible to the risks of multiple hypothesis testing compared with traditional subgroup analyses, it is not entirely immune to such risks. It should not withhold anyone from azithromycin in case this is empirically decided. Prospective validation should be conducted before making broad recommendations for adoption of this model in clinical practice. This may include an RCT in which patients are assigned to either a control arm (current standard clinical practice) or model-guided arm (treatment based on ITE predictions) to demonstrate superior clinical outcomes in the model-guided arm. Broad validation may be necessary with extended inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Conclusion

We identified treatment effect heterogeneity of long-term azithromycin therapy for preventing AECOPD, revealing that the average treatment effect reported in previous trials is driven by a small subgroup of responders. We successfully predicted ITE based on a limited number of parameters readily available in routine clinical practice. Our ITE estimation may serve as a reference to weigh the potential treatment benefit against potential adverse effects and guide patients’ personalised treatment preferences. Pending prospective validation, it holds promise for informing a more rational prescription of azithromycin. Moreover, our approach can be easily generalised and its applicability reaches far beyond the reassessment of pharmacological management of COPD.
