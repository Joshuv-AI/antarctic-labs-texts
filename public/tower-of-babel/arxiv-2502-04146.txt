On the E E -base of Finite Lattices: Semidistributive, Modular, and Geometric Lattices
Kira Adaricheva, Simon Vilmin

Abstract
Implicational bases are a well-known representation of closure spaces and their closure lattices. This representation is not unique, though, and a closure space usually admits multiple bases. Among these, the canonical base, the canonical direct base as well as the D D -base aroused significant attention due to their structural and algorithmic properties. Recently, a new base has emerged from the study of free lattices: the E E -base. It is a refinement of the D D -base that, unlike the aforementioned implicational bases, does not always accurately represent its associated closure space. This leads to an intriguing question: for which classes of (closure) lattices do closure spaces have valid E E -base? Lower-bounded lattices are known to form such a class. In this paper, we prove that for semidistributive lattices, the E E -base is both valid and minimum. We also characterize those modular and geometric lattices that have valid E E -base. Finally, we prove that any lattice is a sublattice of a lattice with valid E E -base. Keywords: Implicational bases, closure spaces, E E -base, semidistributive lattices, modular lattices, geometric lattices

Introduction

Implicational bases (IBs) are a well-known representation of finite closure spaces and their associated closure lattices.
Implications go by many names in a broad range of fields, e.g., attribute implications in Formal Concept Analysis

Since a closure space admits several IBs in general, a rich development of implications, summarized in two surveys

Moreover, recent contributions

Among possible IBs, two have attracted interest due to their structural and algorithmical properties.
First, the canonical (or Duquenne-Guigues) base

,

Second, the canonical direct base, surveyed in

These bases find their origin in lattice theory where special relations between join-irreducible elements were developed to tackle notoriously hard problems.

Question 1

For what classes of (closure) lattices do closure spaces have valid

Since all three classes do present

Theorem 1

The (aggregated)

The (aggregated)

As a byproduct, we obtain that geometric modular lattices do have valid

Then, looking at Question

is a diamond if it consists in a bottom, atoms, and a top.
Notice that the first part of the statement was partially stated without proof in
[
HW96
]
and later proved in
[
Wil00
]
.

Proposition 2
.

[
Wil00
, Proposition 4]

A non-ji closed set
C
is essential iff
[C_{*},C]
is isomorphic to a diamond with at least
3
ji elements.
Moreover, if
C
has predecessors
C_{1},\dots,C_{m}
, then the quasi-closed sets spanning
C
are precisely the sets

\bigcup_{i\in I}C_{i}\quad\text{ with $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}$ and $1<|I|<m$}.

The lattice
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is
geometric
if it is atomistic—i.e., every join-irreducible is an atom—and upper-semimodular.
If
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is geometric, the associated closure space
(S,\phi)
has the
exchange property
: for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
and every
x,y\notin C
,
x\in\phi(C\cup y)
implies
y\in\phi(C\cup x)
.
There is a well-known one-to-one correspondence between (finite) geometric lattices and matroids.
We briefly recall the link between the two structure, and refer to
[
Oxl06
]
for a detailed introduction to the topic.
If
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is a geometric lattice over
S
, then,
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{I}=\{I:I\subseteq S\text{ is a minimal spanning set of }\phi(I)\}
is the matroid associated to
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
.
The bases of
\mathcal{M}
are the inclusion-wise maximal sets of
\mathcal{I}
, i.e., the minimal spanning sets of
S
in
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
.
We denote by
\mathcal{B}
the family of bases of
\mathcal{M}
.
It is well-known that all bases of
\mathcal{M}
have the same size and that they are subject to the base exchange axiom: for every distinct
B_{1},B_{2}\in\mathcal{B}
and any
x\in B_{1}\setminus B_{2}
, there exists
y\in B_{2}\setminus B_{1}
such that
(B_{1}\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}\in\mathcal{B}
.
A circuit is an inclusion-wise minimal set
K\subseteq S
such that
K\nsubseteq B
for any base
B
.
Let us call
\mathcal{K}
the family of circuits of
\mathcal{M}
.
Circuits are closely connected to minimal generators.
Namely, for
x\in S
, the minimal generators of
x
are precisely the sets
K\setminus x
where
K
is a circuit containing
x
, that is,
\mathrm{gen}(x)=\{K\setminus x:K\in\mathcal{K},x\in K\}
.
Note that since
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is atomistic,
\mathrm{gen}(x)=\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)
for any
x\in S
.

Finally,
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is
meet-distributive
if it is lower-semimodular and join-semidistributive, and
join-distributive
if it enjoys the dual laws.
Meet-distributive lattices are also known as convex geometries.
In this case the corresponding closure operator
\phi
has the
anti-exchange property
, dual to the exchange property: for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
and every distinct
x,y\notin C
,
x\in\phi(C\cup y)
implies
y\notin\phi(C\cup x)
.

Implicational bases (IBs).

We refer to
[
BDVG18
,
Wil17
]
for a more detailed introduction to the topic.
An
implication
over
S
is a statement
A\rightarrow X
, with
A,X\subseteq S
.
In
A\rightarrow X
,
A
is the
premise
and
X
the
\emph{conclusion}
.
An implication
A\rightarrow x
,
x\in S
, is a
(right) unit implication
.
If both
A
and
X
are singletons, the implication is
binary
.
An
implicational base
(IB) is a pair
(S,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is a set of implications over
S
.
An IB is unit (resp. binary) if all its implications are unit (resp. binary).
The
aggregation
or
aggregated form
of a unit IB
(S,\Sigma)
is obtained by replacing all implications with the same premise by a single implication with unified conclusions.

An IB
(S,\Sigma)
induces a closure operator
\Sigma(\cdot)
, hence a closure space, where a subset
C
of
S
is closed if
A\subseteq C
implies
X\subseteq C
for all
A\rightarrow X\in\Sigma
.
Usually, each closure space
(S,\phi)
—each lattice
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
—can be represented by several
equivalent
IBs.
An implication
A\rightarrow X

holds
in
(S,\phi)
if for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
A\subseteq C
implies
X\subseteq C
.
An implication
A\rightarrow X
holds in
(S,\phi)
if and only if
X\subseteq\phi(A)
.
If
(S,\Sigma)
is an IB with closure space
(S,\phi)
, we say that an implication
A\rightarrow X

holds
in, or
follows from

(S,\Sigma)
if it holds in
(S,\phi)
.
Thus, two IBs
(S,\Sigma_{1})
,
(S,\Sigma_{2})
are equivalent if every implication of
\Sigma_{1}
follows from
(S,\Sigma_{2})
and vice versa.
The binary part of a closure space
(S,\phi)
can be represented by the binary IB
(S,\Sigma^{b})
gathering all valid (non-trivial) binary implications, that is
\Sigma^{b}=\{a\rightarrow x:x\in\phi(a),x\neq a\}
.
An IB
(S,\Sigma)
is
valid
for
(S,\phi)
if the closure space associated to
(S,\Sigma)
is indeed
(S,\phi)
.
Among the possible IBs of
(S,\phi)
, and besides the
E
-base, we will mention three:

•

the
canonical
or
Duquenne-Guigues base

(S,\Sigma_{DG})

[
GD86
]
.
It is defined from pseudo-closed sets as follows:

\Sigma_{DG}=\{P\rightarrow\phi(P)\setminus P:P\text{ is pseudo-closed}\}.

This IB is
minimum
, it has the least number of implications among all other IBs.
The following theorem is well-known and will be useful in the next sections.

Theorem 8

(e.g. Theorem 5 in
[
Wil94
]
)
.

Let
(S,\phi)
be a closure space and let
(S,\Sigma)
be an IB for
(S,\phi)
.
Then, for any pseudo-closed set
P
of
(S,\phi)
,
\Sigma
contains an implication
A\rightarrow B
such that
A\subseteq P
and
\phi(A)=\phi(P)
.

•

the
canonical direct base

(S,\Sigma_{cd})

[
BM10
]
.
It relies on minimal generators of each element of
S
.
More precisely,

\Sigma_{cd}=\{A\rightarrow x:x\in S,A\in\mathrm{gen}(x)\}.

•

the
D
-base

(S,\Sigma_{D})

[
ANR13
]
.
It is defined from
D
-generators and binary implications representing the binary part of
(S,\phi)
:

\Sigma_{D}=\Sigma^{b}\cup\{A\rightarrow x:x\in S,A\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)\}.

Remark that
\Sigma_{D}\subseteq\Sigma_{cd}
.

Note that for any
C\in\mathcal{C}
, the restriction of
\Sigma_{DG}
(resp.​
\Sigma_{cd}
,
\Sigma_{D}
) to the implications whose premise and conclusion are subsets of
C
constitutes the canonical base (resp. canonical direct,
D
-base) of the closure system
(C,\idl C)
.

3
The
E
-base and some of its properties

In this section, we first introduce the
E
-base in the line of seminal works on these objects
[
ANR13
,
FJN95
]
.
Then, we exhibit further properties of the
E
-base and connect it with other IBs, especially the canonical base.

Let us fix a (standard) closure space
(S,\phi)
with lattice
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
.
We start with the definitions of
E
-generators and
E
-base.
These definitions translate those of
[
ANR13
,
FJN95
]
within our terms.

Definition 1

(
E
-generator)
.

Let
(S,\phi)
be a closure space and let
x\in S
.
A subset
A
of

is a
E
-generator
of
x
if
A
is a
D
-generator of
x
and
\phi(A)
is inclusion-wise minimal in
\{\phi(B):B\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)\}
.
We denote by
\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)
the family of
E
-generators of
x
. We have

\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)=\{A:A\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)\text{ and }\phi(A)\in\min_{\subseteq}\{\phi(B):B\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)\}\}.

In general
\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)
may be empty, much as
\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)
.
In fact, this will happen precisely if
x
is prime in
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
.
Moreover, as pointed out in
[
Wil17
]
,
E
-generators share common points with critical generators of convex geometries
[
KLS12
]
.
Convex geometries form a well-known class of closure spaces.
For a convex geometry, a critical generator of some
x
is a minimal generator
A
of
x
whose closure is minimal among the closures of other minimal generators of
x
.
Thus,
E
-generators and critical circuits both asks for minimality in the closure lattice.
Yet, the two objects differ in that
E
-generators rely on
\phi^{b}
while critical generators do not.

Definition 2

(
E
-base)
.

The
E
-base
of a closure space
(S,\phi)
is the IB
(S,\Sigma_{E})
where

\Sigma_{E}=\Sigma^{b}\cup\{A\rightarrow x:x\in S,A\in\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)\}.

By definition, the
E
-base is a subset of the
D
-base.
Henceforth, it is also a subset of the canonical direct base.
Intuitively, the
E
-base is obtained from the
D
-base by ruling out implications spanning closed sets “too high” in the closure lattice with respect to a fixed element
x
.
More formally, one obtains the
E
-base from the
D
-base by repeatedly applying the following operation: if
A_{1}\rightarrow x,A_{2}\rightarrow x
lie in the non-binary part of
\Sigma_{D}
and
\phi(A_{1})\subset\phi(A_{2})
, then remove
A_{2}\rightarrow x
.

Understanding what closure spaces have valid

-base is an intriguing problem, and constitutes the main motivation for our contribution.
Before investigating this question, we give a characterization of

-generators in Lemma
1
.
It comes from the observation that if
A\in\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)
, any closed set
C\subset\phi(A)
generating

can only contain singleton minimal generators of
x
.
This makes
x
prime in the lattice
(\idl C,\subseteq)
.

Lemma 1
.

A subset
A
of
S
is a
E
-generator of
x
if and only if the following conditions hold:

(1)

A
is a
\phi^{b}
-minimal spanning set of
\phi(A)

(2)

A
is a non-trivial
\phi^{b}
-generator of
x

(3)

for every
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
C\subset\phi(A)
,
x\in C
implies that
x
is prime in
(\idl C,\subseteq)
.

Proof.

We start with the only if part.
Let
A\in\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)
.
By definition,
A\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)
so that conditions (1) and (2) readily holds.
We prove that condition (3) is satisfied.
Let
C\in\mathcal{C}
be such that
C\subset\phi(A)
and
x\in C
.
Assume for contradiction
x
is not prime in
(\idl C,\subseteq)
.
Then, there exists two closed sets
C_{1},C_{2}\subset C
such that
x\notin C_{1},C_{2}
but
x\in C_{1}\jn C_{2}=\phi(C_{1}\cup C_{2})
.
It follows that
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
contains a minimal generator
B
of
x
.
As
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
,
C_{1}\cup C_{2}
is
\phi^{b}
-closed.
Henceforth,
|B|\geq 2
must hold.
Moreover
B
can be chosen
\phi^{b}
-minimal, in which case
B
will be a
D
-generator of
x
.
But then,
\phi(B)\subseteq C\subset\phi(A)
, which contradicts
A
being a
E
-generator of
x
.
Therefore, all conditions (1), (2) and (3) are satisfied.

We move to the if part.
Assume all three conditions hold.
We prove that
A\in\mathrm{gen}_{E}(x)
.
First, let us show that
A\in\mathrm{gen}_{D}(x)
.
Let
B
be any proper
\phi^{b}
-refinement of
A
.
By condition (1),
\phi(B)\subset\phi(A)
must hold.
From condition (2),
x\in\phi(A)
but
x\notin\phi^{b}(A)
.
As
B

\phi^{b}
-refines
A
, we deduce
x\notin\phi^{b}(B)
.
On the other hand,

We move to the only if part.

Because

Without loss of generality, assume that

Embedding into lattices with valid
E
-base

In this section, we prove that any lattice can be embedded as a sublattice of a lattice with valid
E
-base.
This implies that lattices with valid
E
-base cannot be characterized by forbidden sublattices or universal sentences.

Beforehand, we give an informal presentation of the construction we shall use and the intuition on which it relies.
Let
(S,\phi)
be a closure space and assume that its
E
-base is not valid.
Then, following Section
3
, there are a number of faulty essential sets, with their associated faulty pseudo-closed sets, in
(S,\phi)
.
Our strategy consists in fixing these essential sets iteratively.
In order to avoid all ways
C
can be faulty, a number of which have been exhibited in Examples
2
to
4
, it is sufficient to guarantee that the
E
-base is as exhaustive as possible with respect to
C
: every
\phi^{b}
-minimal spanning set of
C
is a
E
-generator of a number of almost prime elements that generate all remaining elements (of
C
) via binary implications.

To obtain this situation, we will
lift

C
by introducing, for each predecessor
C^{\prime}
of
C
a new closed set
F
in between
C
and
C^{\prime}
so that
F
is almost prime w.r.t.
C
in the new closure system.
This operation of inserting a single closed in between a closed set and one of its predecessor corresponds to a particular case of one-point extension
[
Grä11
, Chapter IV]
in the lattice theoretic set-up.
Equivalently, and more visually, it consists in subdividing each edge
(C^{\prime},C)
of the Hasse diagram of
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
as shown in Figure
12
.
It is worth mentioning that this construction is not equivalent to doubling closed sets.
Indeed, if one wants to double a closed set
C
, then the new closed set
C^{\prime}
will lie below all successors of
C
, while here each closed set
C^{\prime}
being inserted lies in between
C
and only one of its successors.

C_{5}
,
C_{7}
and
C_{8}
(in black) are to be lifted. Lifting consists in inserting a new closed set in between these closed sets and each of their predecessors, or equivalently, in subdividing the (dashed) edges
(C_{2},C_{5})
,
(C_{3},C_{5})
,
(C_{5},C_{7})
,
(C_{6},C_{7})
and
(C_{6},C_{8})
of the diagram.
The resulting lattice is pictured on the right. Observe that the lattice on the left is isomorphic to the sublattice consisting only of the
F_{i}
’s on the right.

We proceed to formally describe the lifting operation for arbitrary closed sets in our language of closure systems.
We fix a standard (non-trivial) closure space
(S,\phi)
with closure system
(S,\mathcal{C})
.
We consider an indexing
\emptyset=C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{m}=S
of the closed sets of
\mathcal{C}
such that for all
1\leq i,j\leq m
,
C_{i}\subseteq C_{j}
entails
i\leq j
.
Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
be the set of closed sets to lift.
For each
C_{j}\in\mathcal{L}
and each predecessor
C_{i}
of
C_{j}
, we create a new element
y_{i,j}
.
It will be used as an almost prime element whose closure will lie in between
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
.
Let

Y=\{y_{i,j}:\text{$C_{i}$ is a predecessor of $C_{j}$ in $\mathcal{C}$ and $C_{j}\in\mathcal{L}$}\}

and let
T=S\cup Y
be a new ground set.

For convenience, we introduce an intermediate mapping
f
defined for all
Z\subseteq S
by
f(Z)=\{y_{i,j}:\text{$C_{j}\subseteq Z$ and $C_{j}\in\mathcal{L}$}\}
.
Observe that if
Z
does not include any closed set of
\mathcal{L}
, then

.
We also define three disjoint families of subsets of
T
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\{C_{i}\cup f(C_{i}):C_{i}\in\mathcal{L}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\{C_{i}\cup f(C_{i}):C_{i}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\{C_{i}\cup f(C_{i})\cup\{y_{i,j}\}:C_{i}\in\mathcal{C},y_{i,j}\in Y\}

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{L}}\cup\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{L}}
.
We will use
F_{i}
to denote the set
C_{i}\cup f(C_{i})
in
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}
and
F_{i,j}
to denote the set

\mathcal{F}_{Y}

It follows that
F_{i,j}=F_{i}\cup\{y_{i,j}\}
.
Finally, we put

Intuitively,

models the update of

according to

This concludes the construction of the set system

In what follows, we first need to show that

is indeed a standard closure system whose closure lattice

and corresponds to the construction we hinted, pictured in Figure

This constitutes Lemmas

The proofs of these intermediate claims being mostly technical, they are given in Appendix

This allows us to focus on our second step, namely, that the closed sets that have been lifted are now essential in

but not faulty.

Lemma 10

(restate=LEMclosuresystem, label=lem:F-closure-system)
.

The pair
(T,\mathcal{F})
is a closure system.

Since
(T,\mathcal{F})
is a closure system, it has a corresponding closure operator which we call
\psi
.
While the full description of

will not be necessary, we still need some of its properties that we give in the next proposition.

Proposition 3

(restate=PROPproppsi, label=prop:prop-psi)
.

The following statements hold:

(1)

\psi(Z)=\phi(Z)\cup f(\phi(Z))

(2)

for any

\psi(y_{i,j})=F_{i,j}

As a first consequence of Proposition

Corollary 8

(restate=CORpsistandard, label=cor:psi-standard)

The closure space

is standard.

Our next step is to show that the closure system

In the closure lattice

F_{i}

C_{j}
in

From Lemma

As a by product, for any

.

The lattice

In the next lemma, we show that the predecessors of such closed sets are all associated with almost prime elements.

Lemma 12

For any nonempty, non-ji

Proof.

We first show that

First, we show that it is prime in

for every predecessor
F

However, by Lemma

Given that

Now,

Corollary 11

Discussion

The study of the
E
-base and the classes of lattices where it is valid was initiated by Adaricheva et al.
[
ANR13
]
, who showed that, even though it is not valid in general, it is for closure spaces without
D
-cycles, i.e., lower bounded lattices.
In this contribution, we continued this line of work using the canonical base and its properties as our main weapon.
We investigated three classical generalizations of Boolean and/or distributive lattices being modular, geometric, and semidistributive lattices, as well as the class of lattices with valid
E
-base.
To conclude, we discuss possible directions for future research, and propose a couple of open questions in this regard.

In Section
3
we gave examples exhibiting different degrees to which the
E
-base can fail to be valid.
An essential set can be faulty because of (at least) the following three increasingly demanding reasons: it is not spanned by any
E
-generator, one of its pseudo-closed set is not subsumed by an
E
-generator, or the
E
-generators included in the pseudo-closed sets do not allow to recover the essential set properly.
This distinction suggests that even though a class of lattices might fail to have valid
E
-base in general, it can still obey some degree of validity.
This leads to our first two questions:

Question 2
.

Are there classes of lattices where the
E
-base is not valid in general, but where essential sets are always spanned by some
E
-generator?

Question 3
.

Are there classes of lattices where the
E
-base is not valid in general, but the right-closure (replacing implications
A\rightarrow B
by
A\rightarrow\phi(A)
) of the
E
-base is valid?

Notice that join-distributive, upper-bounded, geometric, and modular lattices are not such classes as proved by Examples
6
,
9
and
8
respectively.
An interesting way to tackle this question would be to craft maps that, contrarily to Section
7
, embed a lattice with invalid
E
-base in a lattice of the same class that has an essential set not spanned by
E
-generator.

In Sections
4
,
6
, and
5
, we proved that the
E
-base of semidistributive lattices is valid and characterized the modular and geometric lattices with valid
E
-base.
This study paves the way for further explorations of other classes of lattices such as join-distributive lattices or convex geometries for instance.
Within the class of semidistributive lattices, those without
D
-cycles are also covered by the result established in
[
ANR13
]
.
Therefore, the new cases for which
E
-base is valid are closure spaces that are semidistributive and possess
D
-cycles.
For instance in Example
1
, we have
D
-cycle
cDfDgDdDc
.
The non-binary implications in the
E
-base have exactly these elements and their
D
-generators
A_{k}
.
Other examples confirm this observation, which brings us to the following question.

Question 4
.

Does the
E
-base of a closure space with semidistributive lattice and
D
-cycles always include implications
A_{k}\rightarrow x_{k}
, where some
x_{k-1}\in A_{k}
and
x_{k-1},x_{k}
are consecutive members of some
D
-cycle?

Besides, geometric lattices seem to be an interesting class to study further.
Even though we characterized those geometric lattices that have valid
E
-base, it would be interesting to identify more classes of matroid, on top of binary ones, that fall into the scope of Theorem
.

Question 5
.

What are the matroids for which
E
-base is valid?

Given Theorem
, answering Question
5
leads to the question of characterizing essential sets of geometric lattices from the perspective of matroids.

Question 6
.

Is there a characterization of essential sets of geometric lattices in terms of bases or circuits of matroid?

While there exists some preliminary results on the question
[
Duq91
,
Wil94
]
, there is no full characterization to our knowledge.

More generally, upper-semimodular lattices are a natural generalization of both geometric and modular lattices.
Hence, it is also a natural candidate for future investigations.
For the moment though we observe that none of the characterization we used for geometric or modular lattices does actually characterize upper-semimodular lattices with valid
E
-base, as shown in the next example.

Example 11
.

The closure space associated whose associated lattice is given in Figure
15
is upper-semimodular and has valid
E
-base.
Its canonical base and
E
-base are given by:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{DG}

\displaystyle=\{f\rightarrow a,d\rightarrow c,e\rightarrow c\}

\displaystyle(\text{binary})

\displaystyle\cup\{\mathit{ab\rightarrow c},\mathit{ac\rightarrow b},\mathit{bc\rightarrow a}\}

\displaystyle(\mathit{abc})

\displaystyle\cup\{\mathit{abcf\rightarrow de},\mathit{abcd\rightarrow ef},\mathit{abce\rightarrow df},\mathit{cde\rightarrow bcf}\}

\displaystyle(\mathit{abcdef})

\displaystyle=\{f\rightarrow a,d\rightarrow c,e\rightarrow c\}

\displaystyle(\text{binary})

\displaystyle\cup\{\mathit{ab\rightarrow c},\mathit{ac\rightarrow b},\mathit{bc\rightarrow a}\}

\displaystyle(\mathit{abc})

\displaystyle\cup\{\mathit{ad\rightarrow ef},\mathit{ae\rightarrow df},\mathit{bd\rightarrow ef},\mathit{be\rightarrow df},\mathit{bf\rightarrow de},\mathit{cf\rightarrow de},\mathit{de\rightarrow f}\}

\displaystyle(\mathit{abcdef})

Observe that
\mathit{abc}
and
\mathit{abcdef}
are comparable essential sets.
Moreover, for any predecessor
C
of
\mathit{abcdef}
, we have
|\mathit{abcdef}\setminus C|>1
.

-base that has comparable essentials, and where the (set-)difference between an essential set and one of its predecessors can be greater than one.

Finally, we proposed in Section
7
a construction that embeds any lattice into a lattice with valid
E
-base by iteratively lifting faulty essential sets.
The lifting operation consists in inserting a new closed set between the essential set and each of its predecessor.
However, if one selects in a more surgical way the predecessors to which apply the operation, possibly according to the pseudo-closed sets that are not mapped to their closure in the
E
-base, then one might end up with a lattice with valid
E
-base that has fewer closed sets.
This idea is illustrated in Figure
16
where we improve the lifting of the lattice in Figure
14
.
We are thus led to the following question:

Question 7
.

Given a lattice
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
, what is the minimal possible size of a lattice
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
such that
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
is a sublattice of
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
and
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
has valid
E
-base?

Figure 16:
Lifting only some predecessors of a faulty essential set can lead to a smaller extension with valid
E
-base.

As a second observation, our construction does not, in general, preserve semidistributive or semimodular laws.
We give in Figure
17
an example where all four laws are lost during lifting.
This leads to wonder about embeddings that would preserve the class of lattice at hand:

Question 8
.

If a class of lattices does not have valid
E
-base in general, does it admit a lattice embedding that embeds any lattice with non-valid
E
-base in a lattice with valid one?

Figure 17:
On the left a distributive lattice with two elements to lift (in black).
The resulting lattice, on the right, fails upper- and lower-semimodularity as well as join- and meet-semidistributivity.

Proof.

We have
F_{m}=C_{m}\cup f(C_{m})=T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}

T\in\mathcal{F}

Now we prove that the intersection of any two incomparable closed sets of
\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}

We have three cases to consider depending on whether the closed sets belong to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}

\mathcal{F}_{Y}

We show that it is in fact sufficient to consider only closed sets in

F_{i,j}\cap F_{k,\ell}=F_{i,j}\cap F_{k}=F_{i}\cap F_{k,\ell}=F_{i}\cap F_{k}

F_{i,j}=F_{i}\cup\{y_{i,j}\}
and
F_{k,\ell}=F_{k}\cup\{y_{k,\ell}\}
, we have:

\displaystyle F_{i,j}\cap F_{k,\ell}

\displaystyle=(F_{i}\cup\{y_{i,j}\})\cap(F_{k}\cup\{y_{k,\ell}\})

\displaystyle=(F_{i}\cap F_{k})\cup(F_{i}\cap\{y_{k,\ell}\})\cup(F_{k}\cap\{y_{i,j}\})\cup(\{y_{i,j}\}\cap\{y_{k,\ell}\})

Since
F_{i,j}
and
F_{k,\ell}
are distinct,
y_{i,j}
and
y_{k,\ell}
are also distinct, and
\{y_{i,j}\}\cap\{y_{k,\ell}\}=\emptyset
holds.
Then, suppose for contradiction that
(F_{i}\cap\{y_{k,\ell}\})\neq\emptyset
, i.e., that
y_{k,\ell}\in f(C_{i})

We deduce that
C_{\ell}\subseteq C_{i}
and hence
F_{k,\ell}\subseteq F_{i}\subseteq F_{i,j}
, a contradiction with
F_{i,j}
and
F_{k,\ell}
being incomparable.
It follows that
F_{i}\cap\{y_{k,\ell}\}=\emptyset
and similarly
(F_{k}\cap\{y_{i,j}\})=\emptyset

We derive

\displaystyle F_{i}\cap F_{k}

\displaystyle=(C_{i}\cap C_{k})\cup(f(C_{i})\cap f(C_{k}))

\PROPproppsi

*

Proof.

We prove the items in order.

Item 1.
Let
Z\subseteq S
.
Observe that
\phi(Z)\cup f(\phi(Z))\in\mathcal{F}
holds by definition of
\mathcal{F}
.
Now, let
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
Z\subseteq F
.
We prove that
\phi(Z)\cup f(\phi(Z))\subseteq F
.
We can assume
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}
as
F\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
entails
Z\subseteq F\setminus\{y_{i,k}\}
where
F=F_{i,k}=F_{i}\cup\{y_{i,k}\}
.
By definition of
F
, there exists
C\in\mathcal{C}
such that
F=C\cup f(C)
.
Therefore,
\phi(Z)\subseteq C
holds as
\phi(Z)\subseteq F\cap S
.
This completes the proof that
\psi(Z)=\phi(Z)\cup f(\phi(Z))
.

Item 2.
Let
y_{i,j}\in Y
.
We show that
\psi(y_{i,j})=F_{i,j}
using double-inclusion.
The
\subseteq
part follows from the definition of
\mathcal{F}
.
To prove the
\supseteq
part, we show that
F_{i,j}\subseteq F
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
y_{i,j}\in F
.
Consider such a
F
.
Either
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}
or
F\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
.
In the case
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}
,
F=F_{k}=C_{k}\cup f(C_{k})
for some
C_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
.
Then,
y_{i,j}\in F
implies
C_{j}\subseteq C_{k}
by definition of
f
.
Therefore,
F_{i,j}\subseteq C_{j}\cup f(C_{j})\subseteq C_{k}\cup f(C_{k})=F_{k}
holds.
As for the case where
F\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
, there exists
y_{k,\ell}
such that
F=F_{k}\cup f(C_{k})\cup\{y_{k,\ell}\}
.
If
y_{k,\ell}=y_{i,j}
then
F=F_{i,j}
readily holds, otherwise
y_{i,j}\in F_{k}
and the previous reasoning applies.
We deduce that
\psi(y_{i,j})=F_{i,j}
as required.
∎

\CORpsistandard

Proof.

We show that for any
z\in T
,
\psi(z)\setminus\{z\}\in\mathcal{F}
.
We have two cases:
z\in Y
or
z\in S
.
We start with the case
z\in Y
.
Let
z=y_{i,j}
for some
y_{i,j}\in Y
.
By Proposition
,
\psi(y_{i,j})=F_{i}\cup\{y_{i,j}\}
.
As
F_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
, we directly obtain
\psi(y_{i,j})\setminus\{y_{i,j}\}\in\mathcal{F}
.

We consider now the case when
z\in S
.
We have
\psi(z)=\phi(z)\cup f(\phi(z))
by Proposition
.
Let us put
C_{j}=\phi(z)
.
Given that
(S,\mathcal{C})
is standard,
C_{j}
is ji in
(\mathcal{C},\subseteq)
, and hence it has a unique predecessor
C_{i}=C_{j}\setminus\{z\}
.
We have two cases,
C_{j}\in\mathcal{L}
or
C_{j}\notin\mathcal{L}
.
In the first case, we obtain
\psi(z)=C_{j}\cup f(C_{j})=C_{j}\cup f(C_{i})\cup\{y_{i,j}\}
.
Then, we deduce
(C_{j}\cup f(C_{j}))\setminus\{z\}=(C_{j}\setminus\{z\})\cup f(C_{i})\cup\{y_{i,j}\}=F_{i,j}

F_{i,j}\in\mathcal{F}

\psi(z)=C_{j}\cup f(C_{i})=F_{i}\cup\{z\}

Proof.

We prove the items in order.

Item 1.

The fact that
F_{i}
is the unique predecessor of
F_{i,j}
follows from the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
as well as from Proposition
.
Consider
F_{j}
.
We have
F_{i,j}\subseteq F_{j}
by definition.
We show that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
F_{i,j}\subset F
implies
F_{j}\subseteq F
.
If
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}
then
F=C_{k}\cup f(C_{k})
for some
C_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
.
By definition of
f
,
y_{i,j}\in f(C_{k})
entails
F_{j}\subseteq F
.
If
F\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}
and
F_{i,j}\subset F
, then
F=F_{k}\cup\{y_{k,\ell}\}
for some
y_{k,\ell}\neq y_{i,j}
.
We obtain
F_{j}\subseteq F
again.
This concludes the proof that
F_{j}
is the unique successor of
F_{i,j}
in
(\mathcal{F},\subseteq)
.

Item 2.

The fact that the
F_{i,j}
’s are predecessors of
F_{j}
is a consequence of the first case.
We need to argue that there are no other predecessors.
Assume for contradiction there is another predecessor
F
of
F_{j}
.
By Item 1 and by definition of
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
,
F
cannot belong to
\mathcal{F}_{Y}
as otherwise it would be equal to one of the
F_{i,j}
’s such that
C_{i}\prec C_{j}
.
Hence,
F=C_{k}\cup f(C_{k})
for some
C_{k}\subseteq C_{j}
in
\mathcal{C}
.
As
F\subset F_{j}
, we moreover has
C_{k}\subset C_{j}
.
Hence, there exists
C_{i}\subseteq C_{j}
such that
C_{k}\subseteq C_{i}\subset C_{j}
and we obtain
F_{k}\subseteq F_{i,j}\subset F_{j}
.
This contradicts
F
being a predecessor of
F_{j}
.

Item 3.

Since
f
is monotone, we readily have that
F_{i}\subseteq F_{j}
for any
C_{i}\prec C_{j}
in
\mathcal{C}
.
Moreover, all the
F_{i}

C_{i}
’s are.
We show that any
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
F\subset F_{j}
satisfies
F\subseteq F_{i}
for some
F_{i}
such that
C_{i}\prec C_{j}

If
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{C}}

F=C_{k}\cup f(C_{k})
for some
C_{k}\in\mathcal{C}

As for any

C_{k}\subset C_{j}
.
Therefore, there exists
C_{i}\prec C_{j}

\mathcal{C}

C_{k}\subseteq C_{i}\subset C_{j}

Assume now that
F\in\mathcal{F}_{Y}

Then,

C_{i}
