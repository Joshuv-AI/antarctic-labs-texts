Islamic lifesaving human milk donation model for small and sick preterm: Policy development
Authors: Dua' Y Al-Maharma, Hanan Sabah, Waddah Demeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPremature birth predisposes preterm infants to life-threatening complications. Donor human milk is as effective as a mother's own milk when mothers cannot produce enough milk. In Islam, receiving donor human milk creates a milk kinship that imposes religious restrictions. Therefore, Muslim countries have made limited efforts to develop human milk donation policies that meet international standards and Islamic regulations.PurposeTo develop a health policy model about human milk donation for small and sick preterm infants in neonatal intensive care units in Jordan based on the CDC policy analysis framework and by adhering to Islamic rules.MethodsSelf-reporting questionnaires assessed the perceptions of 94 postpartum mothers and 96 health care providers regarding human milk donation. In addition, four structured interviews with religious scholars were conducted to assess policy religious legitimacy.Results67% of mothers supported the establishment of a human milk donation model that adheres to religious and legal guidelines. Additionally, around 51.1% were willing to donate milk, and 47.9% would use donor human milk for their infants. Similarly, 59.4% of health care providers supported such a model, and 59.4% considered drafting health policies as a key facilitator, whereas 71.9% considered religious restrictions the main barrier. Finally, religious scholars value the existence of human milk donation policies, emphasizing the importance of documentation to ensure compliance with Islamic rules.ConclusionThe “Islamic Lifesaving Human Milk Donation Model” aims to improve milk donation based on Islamic rules, serving as a benchmark for Muslim communities.Keywords: Human milk donation, Muslims, Policy, Premature

Problem identification

Globally, preterm births, before 37 weeks of gestation, ranged from 4% to 16% of all births in 2020 [1]. The neonatal mortality rate (NMR) was 17 deaths per 1000 live births worldwide in 2022 [2], with preterm birth being a major contributor to infant mortality [1]. In Jordan, the preterm birth incidence is 5.8%, with 15% of these occurring before 32 weeks of gestation [3]. The NMR in Jordan is 14.1 per 1000 live births, and preterm infants (PTIs) face a mortality risk 13 times higher than that of term infants [4]. With a lower gestational age and birth weight, prematurity complications such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), feeding intolerance, and infections increase the risk of mortality and morbidity in PTIs [5]. Formula feeding is associated with NEC, with a mortality rate of 20% to 30%. In contrast, human breast milk contains many bioactive components that promote intestinal growth, improve intestinal barrier function, support microvascular development, and improve immune maturation, showing potential for treating NEC [6].

The prevalence of delayed lactogenesis II, the onset of copious milk production, is higher in premature mothers compared to those of term infants. This delayed onset is associated with insufficient milk volumes during the 14-day postpartum period [7]. When efforts to produce enough expressed milk fall short, or in cases where the mother's milk is contraindicated, donor human milk (DHM) is a preferable option over formula [8]. DHM is as effective as a mother's own milk in preventing BPD, NEC, feeding intolerance, and infections [9], [10], [11]. Even if more than 50% of the total volume is provided as human milk, it still has a significant effect on the protection of infants against complications such as NEC and BPD [8], [12].

Few efforts have been made in Muslim countries to establish a human milk donation (HMD) model that aligns with international milk bank standards and adheres to Islamic regulations. The main ethical reason for Islamic countries' reluctance to establish human milk banks is the protocol of donor anonymity. In Islam, receiving breast milk from a woman other than the infant's own mother creates a milk kinship, or ‘mahram,’ which is similar to the blood relationship. This kinship prohibits marriage and imposes other religious restrictions between the recipient and the donor's immediate family [13], [14].

The requirements for establishing milk kinship are interpreted differently among the four major Sunni schools of Islamic jurisprudence. This kinship, known as mahram, is analogous to a biological familial relationship in Islamic law, creating permanent prohibitions on marriage and defining specific religious rights and obligations between the recipient child and the immediate family of the milk donor [14]. In Jordan, the Shafi'i school is predominantly followed [15]. According to the Shafi'i, Hanafi, Maliki, and Hanbali schools, breastfeeding that establishes milk kinship must occur within the first two years of the infant's life [14], [16]. According to the Shafi'i and Hanbali schools, infants become milk siblings or foster siblings when they are breastfed by the same woman on at least five separate occasions until satiety, with the infant naturally ceasing breastfeeding each time without returning, regardless of the amount of milk consumed [14], [16]. In contrast, the Hanafi and Maliki schools maintain that a mahram relationship is established once breastfeeding occurs, even if only a small amount of milk is consumed [14], [16]. Adherents to the Shafi'i and Hanbali opinions benefit from this Shari'ah concession (rukhsah), whereas those who follow the Hanafi and Maliki opinions adopt a more precautionary approach regarding their religious obligations [16].

The initiation of feeding for small and sick PTIs can be affected by many health conditions such as gastrointestinal and neurological immaturity, as well as respiratory distress. These conditions can affect the amount of milk required to meet their nutritional needs [8]. The feeding process begins with minimal enteral feeding and is adjusted based on the infant's health and feeding tolerance [17]. The early and gradual advancement of enteral feeding before achieving full breastfeeding further complicates the determination of satiety during feeding, particularly for establishing milk kinship. This makes adherence to religious rules that require infants to be satiated on five separate occasions in order to establish milk kinship difficult. This paper proposes a policy of HMD and utilization for small and sick PTIs in Jordanian NICUs that incorporates Islamic perspectives to ensure alignment with religious values, rules, and cultural context.

Policy framework

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Policy Analytical Framework served as a guideline for this paper. This framework includes three major domains: problem identification, policy analysis, and strategy and development of policy. Understanding a problem's characteristics is important to determine the best potential policy solutions. Evidence for policy options is gathered through literature review, surveying best practices, and conducting an environmental scan. Each policy option is rated independently based on CDC criteria, including health impact, feasibility, and economic and budgetary impacts. In addition, other criteria added by the authors include alignment to Islamic rules and safety to suit the nature of the problem. The chosen policy is prioritized based on the weights assigned to these criteria and the overall analysis. Finally, to facilitate the implementation of a policy solution, a plan is developed to address operational issues, identify stakeholders, share relevant information, and conduct analyses for policy solution implementation, adoption, and evaluation [18].

Stakeholder consultation

Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach from the study settings. As this study aimed to inform policy development through stakeholder consultation rather than to estimate population prevalence or test hypotheses, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample was considered adequate to capture the perspectives of key stakeholder groups, including postpartum mothers and healthcare providers, to support the policy analysis process.

Mothers’ perceptions and attitudes

To evaluate mothers' perceptions and attitudes toward HMD and usage, a total of 94 postpartum mothers, predominantly Muslim (98.9%), participated in a survey after giving consent. The majority of mothers (94.7%) lacked previous experience with HMD and usage concepts. Only 11.7% of mothers had engaged in wet nursing (breastfeeding another person's child). Among them, 63.6% nursed infants of relatives. More than half of the mothers (56.4%) expressed unwillingness to wet nurse if the need arose. One-third of mothers (34%) held a positive perception regarding the use of donated human milk as the best nutrition for preterm and sick neonates when the mother's milk is insufficient or unavailable.

Only 24.5% believed formula feeding could expose preterm and sick neonates to various health problems. Most mothers declined to feed their infants from either non-relative (91.5%) or relative (71.3%) donated human milk if their own milk was inadequate or unavailable. Among the 75 mothers who provided reasons for this declination, the most common factors for refusal were complete rejection of the idea (28%), genetic mixing concerns (25.3%), fear of disease transmission (20%), and religious concerns (9.3%).

Most of the mothers were unwilling to donate their milk to non-relative infants (71.3%) and to relatives' infants (59.6%) if their own infants did not require it. Among the 39 mothers who provided reasons for willingness to accept donation, the most frequently mentioned reasons were the desire to help other newborns (41%) and the recognition of the benefits of breast milk (35.9%). Conversely, among the 50 mothers who provided reasons for rejection of donation, it was primarily attributed to a complete rejection of the idea (52%), followed by religious considerations (34%) and concerns about inadequate milk supply (10%), respectively.

About two-thirds of the mothers (67%) expressed support for implementing the HMD model with adherence to religious, legal, and health guidelines. Nearly half of the mothers (51.1%) indicated a willingness to consider donating milk to sick and premature babies under a religious and legal framework. Similarly, 47.9% stated that they would consider using donated milk for their own child in the NICUs under proper processing and a religious/legal system; see Table 1.

Healthcare providers' perceptions and attitudes

After obtaining consent, HCPs' perceptions and attitudes toward the initiation of HMD policy for small and sick infants in the NICUs were assessed using a self-reported questionnaire. The survey included 96 HCPs, predominantly female (89.6%); the mean age was 33.16 years (SD = 7.29). Most of the participants were Muslims (96.9%). Approximately half were nurses in neonatal, pediatric, and obstetric units (55.2%), followed by physicians who were neonatologists, pediatricians, or obstetricians (33.3%). The average years of experience for HCPs were 8.87 years (SD = 6.52). Most of the participants (62.5%) worked in a baby-friendly hospital.

Most participants had not encountered human milk donations in their healthcare settings (77.1%). In cases where PTIs received insufficient milk from their mothers, the majority resorted to formula milk feeding (78.1%). More than half of participants (59.4%) supported creating an HMD system because they believed in the benefits of donated human milk for the health of PTIs (53.1%). Finally, more than half of participants (57%) viewed their role in supporting human milk donation as strong advocacy or supportive involvement; see Table 2.

Facilitators and barriers

Based on HCPs' perspective, more than half of participants considered the existence of a health policy to regulate the HMD system (59.4%), and increased public awareness of the importance of HMD (52.1%) would facilitate the establishment of the HMD system. However, most HCPs identified several barriers that could hinder the implementation of the HMD system. These barriers include religious restrictions (71.9%), the availability of formula milk (54.2%), a lack of precise procedures to ensure the safety and quality of DHM (52.1%), and the absence of legal legislation to regulate the process (50.0%). About two-thirds of them found that certain requirements would encourage the use of HMD in healthcare settings, such as the existence of supportive policies that take into account religious and legal controls (67.7%) and the availability of religious Fatwa (official statement from an Islamic religious leader) (65.6%); see Table 3.

Religious scholars' perspectives

A structured interview was conducted with four religious scholars, two from the Jordanian General Fatwa Department and two Muslim scholars (professors in Islamic jurisprudence [fiqh]), to seek religious perspectives and rules regarding certain policy conditions. There was a consensus among them about the importance and encouragement of the existence of HMD policy. They emphasized that such a policy aligns with one of the objectives of Islamic law, “Maqasid Al-Shariah,” which emphasizes preserving life and strengthening social relationships between families through such kinship. A professor of Islamic jurisprudence stated,

It is a very important issue, and I encourage the presence of such a policy. It has many benefits for the health of PTIs, and it brings social benefits. This policy will strengthen social ties and enhance family development, as it allows families of breastfeeding mothers to include new children in their family who are not biologically related to them.

The scholars emphasized the importance of documenting milk kinship to prevent prohibited marriages in the future. Another Islamic scholar who has a brother through milk kinship said, “My mother documented my milk sibling's name on the back of my birth certificate to ensure that everyone knows this kinship in the future.” Considering the difficulty in determining the amount of milk needed to satisfy the feeding requirements of small and sick PTIs, one of the religious scholars suggested adopting a more conservative religious rule that aligns with the ‘Hanafi and Maliki’ schools.

Conventional human milk banks (CHMB)

This system can provide safe and high-quality DHM for PTIs, with donor mothers undergoing health screening and serological analysis. They are instructed about the pumping principle, storing, labeling, and transportation of milk. After that, the expressed milk is pasteurized, chilled, and microbiologically tested [19], [20]. The problem with milk banking is the process during which the milk from individual donors is pooled and de-identified, making it impossible to know its origins and acknowledge the newly formed kinship relationship [21]. In Islamic jurisprudence, consuming DHM establishes a kinship bond similar to that of biological siblings, and eventually prohibits marriage between milk siblings. Therefore, knowing the milk source is mandatory to prevent prohibited marriages [13].

Informal milk sharing (IMS)

It is defined as a personal, non-institutional arrangement between a donor and recipient mother, with or without a profitable basis. Mothers share breast milk directly through online forums or social media groups. IMS that adhere to Islamic principles implement specific measures, such as requiring donor and recipient infants to be of the same gender to prevent future marriage, limiting participation to three donors or recipients, and requiring both parties to know each other's identities. However, no measures were taken to prevent disease transmission. The recipients only questioned the donor's detailed health status and compliance with hygiene rules during milk expression [22].

The ambulatory human milk donors (AHMD) policy

It was implemented at Al-Adan Hospital in Al-Kuwait to facilitate the provision of donor milk for sick or small PTIs, with a focus on those born before 30 weeks gestation, and following Islamic guidelines. It operates administratively and regulatory within the hospital to increase access to DHM without mandatory participation. This policy is a revision of an earlier policy implemented in the year 2000, which was replaced due to a low rate of preterm infant recipients of DHM. This was due to a number of reasons, including a lack of immediate donors, reluctance to form milk kinship, and delays in finding a donor and serological screening, which led some families to opt for infant formula instead [13].

The AHMD policy secures potential donors during antenatal visits to ensure that DHM is timely available for PTIs when needed. This policy addresses kinship concerns through acquaintance between donor and recipient families. It ensures milk safety by screening potential donors for specific diseases and the milk pasteurizing process. The cost-effectiveness of the process was not investigated [13]. This approach is based on wet nursing, which is widely accepted in the Muslim world but is too small to address the significant need for donated milk [21].

The conditional identified milk banking system (CIMBS)

It is designed to meet the objections to milk banking raised by Islamic scholars while creating a donation system that meets the need for Muslim children to be able to access human milk on a larger scale. In CIMBS, both the donor's and recipient's identities are made accessible to each other through a voluntary registry. To limit the number of kinships, only three to five donors were allowed to pool their milk in one container. The safety of DM is ensured by screening the potential donors for health history and pumping practices. Serological testing for HIV and hepatitis is conducted on expressed milk. The fit donors are trained to maintain milk safety and quality. The expressed milk is then treated and pasteurized. However, there is no information provided regarding the feasibility, applicability, or cost-effectiveness of the proposed idea [21].

Shariah-compliant milk bank model (SCMBM)

This model offers an Islamic approach to human milk banking, based on the ‘Shafi'i’ school of thought. It includes essential steps to ensure the safety of DHM, including donor screenings, proper handling procedures, pasteurization, nutritional analyses, storage protocols, and distribution methods. DHM is used only to save the lives of PTIs in emergency situations. Muslim HCPs must verify donor milk collection to adhere to Islamic guidelines. Consent from both the donor and her husband is mandatory for milk donations. The donors have the right to decide whether the donation establishes milk kinship with the recipient or not. The quantity of DHM distributed to a recipient from a single donor must not exceed the amount required to establish milk kinship, which is five or more full feedings, if the donor chooses not to establish milk kinship with the recipient. If a donor wishes to provide equal to or more than five full feedings to a single recipient, the infant becomes the donor's ‘mahram.’ Both parties must acquaint themselves with each other, and a certificate issued by the Ministry of Health, certified by the State Islamic Religious Council, is also required [14].

Proposed policy

The proposed model for small and sick preterm infants in NICUs will be called the “Islamic Lifesaving Human Milk Donation Model” (ILHMDM). The ILHMDM was informed by the policy options identified in the literature review, stakeholder perspectives, and Islamic jurisprudence relevant to the Jordanian context, rather than representing a direct adoption of any single existing model. Consistent with Daud et al. [14], the proposed models will limit DHM to small and sick PTIs with insufficient milk supply from their mothers, aligning with the “Maqasid Al-Shariah” goal of preserving lives. The proposed model will follow the Hanafi and Maliki schools, which are more conservative regarding religious obligations [16], to address the difficulties of determining five full separate feedings among small and sick PTIs [8], [17], as required by the Shafi'i school of thought [14]. In the Hanafi and Maliki schools, mahram relationships begin with the initiation of breastfeeding, regardless of the amount of milk consumed. The proposed ILHMDM aligns with WHO recommendations promoting breastfeeding and the use of donor human milk for small, sick, and preterm newborns when a mother's own milk is unavailable, thereby supporting efforts to improve neonatal health outcomes and reduce preventable newborn mortality [8].

Limitations

Although this study focused on Sunni Islamic jurisprudence, Shia Islamic traditions may have distinct interpretations and practices regarding milk kinship and human milk donation. Exploring these perspectives would enhance the applicability of the findings across diverse Muslim communities. In addition, participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach, and no formal sample size calculation was performed because the survey was intended to inform stakeholder consultation within the policy analysis framework rather than to provide population-level estimates. The relatively small sample size and inclusion of participants from a limited number of healthcare settings may affect the representativeness and generalizability of the findings, and some observed patterns may differ in larger populations. Future studies involving larger and more diverse samples are needed to validate and refine the proposed model and to prospectively evaluate its implementation, acceptability, sustainability, and long-term impact in clinical practice. As the proposed model has not yet been implemented or evaluated in practice, its applicability and transferability beyond the Jordanian context remain uncertain and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

Donor human milk plays an important role in improving the health of PTIs, especially those who are small and sick, when their own mother's milk is inadequate or unavailable. The ILHMDM aims to improve the milk donation based on Islamic rules, serving as a benchmark for Muslim communities.
