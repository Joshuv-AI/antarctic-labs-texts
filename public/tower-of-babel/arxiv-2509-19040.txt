Nonparametric efficient estimation of the longitudinal front-door functional
Marie S. Breum, Helene C. W. Rytgaard, Torben Martinussen, Erin E. Gabriel

Abstract
The front-door criterion is an identification strategy for the intervention-specific mean outcome in settings where the standard back-door criterion fails due to unmeasured exposure-outcome confounders, but an intermediate variable exists that completely mediates the effect of exposure on the outcome and is not affected by unmeasured confounding. The front-door criterion has been extended to the longitudinal setting, where exposure and mediator are measured repeatedly over time. However, to the best of our knowledge, applications of the longitudinal front-door criterion remain unexplored. This may reflect both limited awareness of the method and the absence of suitable estimation techniques. In this report, we propose nonparametric efficient estimators of the longitudinal front-door functional. The estimators are multiply robust and allow for the use of data-adaptive (machine learning) methods for nuisance estimation while providing valid inference. The theoretical properties of the estimators are showcased in a simulation study.



Keywords:
front-door adjustment; longitudinal data; one-step estimation; TMLE; unmeasured confounders

Introduction

The seminal work of
11
showed that if a variable exists that completely mediates the effect of exposure on the outcome and is not affected by unmeasured confounding, then the intervention-specific mean outcome is identified by the front-door formula, even if there are unmeasured exposure-outcome confounders.
This is a valuable form of identification, specifically in observational settings with unmeasurable and complex confounders of exposure and outcome. In
11
, Pearl considered a hypothetical trial investigating the effect of smoking on lung-cancer, where smoking and lung-cancer are likely confounded by unmeasured genetic predispositions. If we can measure the amount of tar deposited in a subject’s lungs, and we believe that smoking does not have any direct effect on lung cancer except that mediated by tar, then the front-door criterion allows for identification of the average causal effect of smoking on lung cancer. Another possible example is an observational study of seasonal influenza vaccination, where many unmeasurable behavioral differences may lead to different probabilities of being vaccinated and infected. However, there are also numerous biological variables, many of which will be known and often used in Phase II trials of the vaccine, that are unlikely to be confounded with these behaviors. In this case, the front-door criterion would allow us to identify the average causal effect of vaccination on the infection rate, provided that we believe that vaccination does not have a direct effect on infection except that mediated by the biological variables measured in the trial.
Some recent real-data applications of the front-door criterion are given in
6
;
9
;
12
;
21
and
7
.

Often, the exposure and mediator will vary over time. For example, the vaccination scheme described above may require more than one dose.
13
extended the front-door criterion to the longitudinal setting to identify the population average difference in the outcome comparing two different exposure histories. To our knowledge, applications of the longitudinal front-door criterion remain an open area. This may reflect that, with the exception of a simple plug-in estimator proposed by
13
, no suitable estimating techniques exist. By introducing new estimators, this work provides the tools needed to bring such applications within reach.

Even in the point treatment setting, relatively few estimators have been suggested in the literature, compared to the rich literature on back-door estimation. In
5
the authors proposed a multiply robust one-step estimator of the front-door formula for a dichotomous mediator.
21
and
7
independently of each other proposed targeted minimum loss-based estimators (TMLE) of the front-door formula, which can handle continuous and multivariate mediators.
3
proposed various inverse probability weighted (IPW) estimators, including one which is applicable to continuous exposures.
We build on this work to derive flexible nonparametric estimators of the longitudinal front-door functional, allowing for continuous and multivariate mediators and binary exposures. In particular, we derive the efficient influence function and propose two types of nonparametric efficient estimators: a one-step estimator
(
20
)
and a targeted minimum loss based estimator (TMLE)
(
18
;
16
;
17
)
. We study the double robustness properties of the estimators, and we show that the method allows for the use of data-adaptive (machine learning) methods when estimating the nuisance models. Moreover, both of the proposed estimators avoid direct estimation of the conditional mediator density, which is an advantage when, as will typically be the case, the mediator is high-dimensional.

In the point treatment setting, the front-door functional was shown to still have a meaningful interpretation as a population intervention indirect effect (PIIE) when the assumption that there is no direct effect of treatment on the outcome is replaced by a cross-world independence assumption
(
5
)
. Also in the point treatment setting,
21
provided an interventionist interpretation of the front-door functional under a dismissible component assumption. In the
Supplementary Materials
, we generalize these alternative interpretations to the longitudinal setting, which further enhances the practical utility of our proposed estimators.

Some final remarks are provided in Section

4.1

\displaystyle W_{t}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t-1})\left\{Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t+1}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t})-Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right\},

(13)

\displaystyle D^{*}_{A_{t}}(P)(O)\equiv

\displaystyle H_{t-1}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\left\{R_{M_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t-1})-R_{A_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right\},

(14)

for

\displaystyle H_{t}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t})

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{k=0}^{t}\frac{g_{k}(M_{k}\mid L_{0},\bar{a}_{k},\bar{M}_{k-1})}{g_{k}(M_{k}\mid L_{0},\bar{A}_{k},\bar{M}_{k-1})},

(15)

\displaystyle W_{t}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mathbbm{1}(\bar{A}_{t}=\bar{a}_{t})}{\prod_{k=0}^{t}\pi_{k}(a_{k}\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{k-1},\bar{a}_{k-1})}.

(16)

Proof.

See Appendix

.

\times\left\{A_{t}-\pi_{t}(1\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1})\right\}.

4.3
Targeted minimum loss estimators

Targeted minimum loss based estimation (TMLE)
(
18
;
16
;
17
)
is a general method for constructing substitution-based estimators of parameters in non- and semiparametric models that solve the efficient influence function.

To construct our targeting algorithm, we will use the representation of the efficient influence function in Corollary
1
.

Define the loss functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Y}(Q_{Y})(O)

\displaystyle=-\left\{Y\log Q_{Y}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})+(1-Y)\log\left(1-Q_{Y}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{A_{t}}(\pi_{t})(O)

\displaystyle=-\left\{A_{t}\log\pi_{t}(A_{t}\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1})+\left(1-A_{t}\right)\log\left(1-\pi_{t}(A_{t}\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1})\right)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M_{t}}(Q_{M_{t}})(O)

\displaystyle=-\left\{Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t+1}}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t})\log Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t}}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})+\left(1-Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t+1}}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t})\right)\log\left(1-Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t}}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right)\right\},

and the least favorable submodels

\displaystyle Q_{Y,\varepsilon}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})=

\displaystyle\text{expit}\left(\text{logit}(Q_{Y}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T}))+\varepsilon H_{T}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})\right),

\displaystyle\pi_{t,\varepsilon}(A_{t}\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1})=

\displaystyle\text{expit}\big(\text{logit}(\pi_{t}(A_{t}\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1}))

\displaystyle+\varepsilon H_{t-1}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\left\{R_{M_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},1,\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})-R_{M_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},0,\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right\}\big),

\displaystyle Q_{M_{t},\varepsilon}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})=

\displaystyle\text{expit}\left(\text{logit}\left(Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t}}(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right)+\varepsilon W_{t}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right).

Together, these satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{L}_{Y}(Q_{Y,\varepsilon})(O)=

\displaystyle H_{T}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})\left\{Y-Q_{Y}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T})\right\},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{L}_{A_{t}}(\pi_{t,\varepsilon})(O)=

\displaystyle H_{t-1}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\left\{R_{M_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(P)(L_{0},1,\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})-R_{M_{t}}^{\bar{a}}(P)(L_{0},0,\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right\}

\displaystyle\times\left\{A_{t}-\pi_{t}(1\mid L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1},\bar{A}_{t-1})\right\},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathcal{L}_{M_{t}}(Q_{M_{t},\varepsilon})(O)=

\displaystyle W_{t}^{\bar{a}}(L_{0},\bar{A}_{t},\bar{M}_{t-1})\left\{Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t+1}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t})-Q^{\bar{a}}_{M_{t}}(P)(L_{0},\bar{M}_{t-1})\right\}.

6
Concluding remarks

In this report, we proposed various estimators of the longitudinal front-door functional, which identifies the intervention specific mean outcome without relying on the assumption of no unmeasured confounders between exposure and outcome.
We developed simple estimators based on inverse probability weighting and sequential regression, as well as more flexible non-parametric estimators based on semiparametric efficiency theory. We showed that the efficient influence function based estimators have many appealing properties, including being robust to misspecification of certain nuisance models, and allowing for the use of data-adaptive (machine learning) methods for model fitting. We verified the theoretical properties of the estimators in a simulation study.

S2.1
Identification the PIIE

Let
Y(\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T}))
be the potential outcome under an intervention that for

M_{t}

The longitudinal version of the population intervention indirect effect (PIIE) of

Theorem S1
.

Suppose that the following assumptions hold for
t=0,...,T
.

(i)

Sequential exchangeability:

\underline{M}_{t}(\bar{a}_{T})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}A_{t}\mid L_{0},\bar{A}_{t-1},\bar{M}_{t-1},

(ii)

Cross-world exchangeability:
Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T})\mid L_{0}
,

(iii)

Consistency:

\text{If }\bar{A}_{t}=\bar{a}_{t}\text{ then }M_{t}(\bar{a}_{t})=M_{t}\text{ with probability 1},

Proof.

By the law of total probability, we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{\bar{a}_{T}}(P)

\displaystyle=\iint\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T}))\mid\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T})=\bar{m}_{T},L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle p(M_{t}(\bar{a}_{t})=m_{t}\mid\bar{M}_{t-1}(\bar{a}_{t-1})=\bar{m}_{t-1},\ell_{0})=p(M_{t}=m_{t}\mid\bar{A}_{t}=\bar{a}_{T},\bar{M}_{t-1}=\bar{m}_{t-1},\ell_{0}).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{A}_{T},\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T}))\mid\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T})=\bar{m}_{T},L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mid\bar{M}_{T}(\bar{a}_{T})=\bar{m}_{T},L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mid L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{0}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mid A_{0}=a_{0}^{\prime},L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}p(a_{0}^{\prime}\mid\ell_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a_{0}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mid M_{0}=m_{0},A_{0}=a_{0}^{\prime},L_{0}=\ell_{0}\right\}p(a_{0}^{\prime}\mid\ell_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{a}_{T}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left\{Y(\bar{m}_{T})\mid\bar{m}_{T},\bar{a}_{T}^{\prime},\ell_{0}\right\}\prod_{t=0}^{T}p(a_{t}^{\prime}\mid\bar{m}_{t},\bar{a}_{t-1}^{\prime},\ell_{0})

We repeat the arguments in steps three and four until we arrive at the expression on the right-hand side of the sixth equality. The final equality follows by the consistency assumption.
∎
