Dynamics of conductive nonmagnetic objects in presence of the Lenz effect
Alessandro Arduino, Oriano Bottauscio, Michael Steckner, Umberto Zanovello, Luca Zilberti

Abstract
Purpose To model and predict the dynamics of conductive nonmagnetic objects within the MRI room under the influence of Lenz effect. Methods The dynamics are described by an ordinary differential equation and the Lenz effect approximated by recognizing that the skin effect is negligible. This separated Lenz effect dependency on the object position and velocity, leading to a simple numerical procedure for objects of any shape. Results The model and numerical procedure were validated with experimental data recording the rotation of an aluminum plate falling inside a 1.5 T 1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T} MRI scanner. The model was also applied for studying the translation of an aluminum plate pushed with constant force towards the MRI bore through the fringe field. Conclusion The collected results showed that it is possible to obtain accurate predictions of motion in the presence of Lenz effect by neglecting the skin effect while determining the electric currents induced in the metallic object during each infinitesimal motion step. Keywords: Dynamics, implant, Lenz effects, Lorentz force, static field



1
Introduction

The static field
{\bf B}_{0}
of a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner introduces numerous safety concerns and is considered by the guidelines regulating the safety of both patients and operators
[
1
]
.
Besides physiological temporary effects like vertigo, metallic taste and magnetophosphenes
[
2
]
, the introduction of metallic objects that would experience strong forces in the MRI room is a significant hazard
[
3
]
.
Nonetheless, conductive nonmagnetic objects are also susceptible to mechanical forces when moved within the static field, due to the Lorentz forces associated with the motion-induced eddy currents. This phenomenon is often called the Lenz effect
[
4
,
5
]
.

Lorentz forces arise every time an electric current flows through an object immersed in a magnetic field, so, in addition to being the origin of the Lenz effect, they cause vibrations in conductive nonmagnetic objects exposed to the switching gradient field of the MRI scanner
[
5
]
.
However, a recent mathematical investigation of the phenomenon suggested that no mechanical effects of concern are induced in passive orthopaedic implants, like hip, knee or shoulder replacements, by either Lenz effect, or gradient-induced vibrations
[
6
]
.

In the past years, some efforts were devoted to the safety assessment of heart valves with moving metallic parts
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.
In these passive implants, valve opening and closing are induced by variations in blood pressure. However, the damping forces associated with the Lenz effect, acting as an additional fluid friction, slow down the valve motion, with the risk of increased regurgitation and decreased cardiac output.

Condon and Hadley
[
4
]
performed a first, highly conservative evaluation of the damping forces due to motion in the static field
{\bf B}_{0}
of realistic valves with metallic strengthening rings.
Robertson
et al.

[
7
]
improved the assessment, by including the Lenz effect and blood viscosity in Newton’s equation of motion, to predict the actual delay in valve opening and closing. With respect to the opening time under normal conditions, there was a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
delay at
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
, and a
3.5\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
delay at
3\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
. Despite these figures were not alarming, significant delays were predicted in higher field scanners.
Other authors
[
8
]
questioned these results, suggesting that the delays that would occur in a real heart valve with a metallic strengthening ring were overestimated because of the neglected skin effect.
Actually, the velocities involved in the motion of heart valves make the skin effect negligible. A valve immersed in a uniform static field and performing a rotation of
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
in
10\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}

[
8
]
is experiencing, in the frame of the rotating valve, a rotating magnetic field at the extremely low frequency of
25\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
. Assuming a conductivity of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{MS}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}
, at this frequency the eddy currents have a penetration depth of about
10\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
and no skin effect is visible in valves of realistic size. This still holds true at higher frequencies, corresponding to higher velocities of motion
[
10
]
. The skin effect claimed above is due to a numerical artifact that may affect the implementation of the finite element method (FEM) for t
he eddy-current problem with
T-\Omega
formulation in non-simply connected domains. To avoid such an artifact and obtain the physically consistent solution, it is required to include in the finite element model the basis function associated to a non-contractible loop enclosing the ring hole
[
11
,
12
]
.

None of the previous papers
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
6
]
provide an experimental validation of their results. The only exception is a comparison between the computed and measured torques experienced by a nonmagnetic metallic frame with
5\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}
sides moving at
20\text{\,}\mathrm{cm}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
in the fringe field of the scanner
[
5
]
.
On the other hand, Edwards
et al.

[
9
]
collected measurements of the pressure experienced by different commercially available heart valves when moved through the static field of a
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
scanner, but their results cannot be directly compared with other numerical evaluations.

In this paper, a procedure to model the dynamics of conductive nonmagnetic objects in the static field of an MRI scanner, inspired by the analysis of Robertson
et al.

[
7
]
, is proposed.
The proposed procedure can be applied to objects of any shape and, in principle, can follow any kind of dynamics, allowing the investigation of the Lenz effect for any kind of implantable medical device.
In particular, it is presented here in specialized form to problems of rotational or translational motion of a rigid body with one degree of freedom, like a rotating plate or a plate translating along a straight line.
The proposed procedure was experimentally validated in the homogeneous static field of a
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
MRI scanner.

2
Theory

Consider a conductive nonmagnetic body moving in a (possibly non uniform) static magnetic field
{\bf B}_{0}
.
According to the Lenz effect, the body will experience velocity dependent Lorentz forces due to the induced eddy currents.

Either a rotation or a linear translation of a rigid body, described by one degree of freedom
q
, induced by an external forcing term
F_{\rm ext}(t,q)
, possibly depending on time
t
and system state
q
, is governed by the second order ordinary differential equation (ODE)

I\ddot{q}=F_{\rm ext}(t,q)+f_{\rm lenz}(q)\dot{q}\,,

(1)

where
I
is the inertia coefficient (mass for translations, moment of inertia for rotations) and
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
is the Lorentz force (or torque for rotations) the body would experience if moving with a unitary velocity in
q
.
This multiplicative separation between
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
and
\dot{q}
relies on the assumption that the skin effect is negligible at any reasonable velocity.

Equation (
1
) can be further enriched to take into account additional effects, like damping due to blood viscosity in the modeling of heart valves
[
7
]
, or more degrees of freedom.
The approximate solution can be computed by well-known numerical methods.

Difficulties in simulating the dynamics in the presence of the Lenz effect arise in the determination of the function
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
, that can be expressed analytically in closed form for only a few simple cases, like the rotating circular plate (see section
2.1
).
In the case of generic objects and motions, the computation of
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
has to rely on numerical simulations as shown in section
2.2
.

2.1
Rotating circular plate

One of the few cases where an analytical expression of
f_{\rm lenz}
can be computed in closed form is the circular plate of negligible thickness
d
rotating in a spatially uniform magnetic field
{\bf B}_{0}
.

Figure 1
:
Rotating circular plate. (a) Diagram of the falling plate in the
\hat{y}\otimes\hat{z}
plane with one degree of freedom
\vartheta
; the force of gravity
m{\bf g}
and the constraint force
{\bf V}
are reported. (b) Diagram of the falling plate in a view perpendicular to the plate itself. In blue, the circle
\mathcal{C}(r)
of radius
r
concentric with the plate. The light circular arrows inside the plate depict the currents induced by the plate rotation in a homogeneous magnetic field
{\bf B}_{0}=B_{0}\hat{z}
.

With reference to the notation introduced in Fig.
1
a, consider a plate with radius
R
rotating around an horizontal axis parallel to
\hat{x}
, while the magnetic field is directed along the horizontal axis
\hat{z}
(i.e.,
{\bf B}_{0}=B_{0}\hat{z}
).
The rotation is described by the evolution of the angle
\vartheta
of the plate with respect to the vertical axis
\hat{y}
and the plate is perpendicular to the field when the angle is null.

Since we are assuming that the plate thickness
d
is considerably smaller than the radius
R
, only the magnetic field component normal to the plate plays a role in the induction of eddy currents that develop on the plane containing the plate. Such a normal component is defined as

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle={\bf B}_{0}\cdot\hat{n}(\vartheta)=B_{0}\hat{z}\cdot\left(-\sin(\vartheta)\hat{y}+\cos(\vartheta)\hat{z}\right)

(2)

\displaystyle=B_{0}\cos(\vartheta)\,.

Therefore, independent of the origin of motion, if the plate rotates with angular velocity
\dot{\vartheta}
, it will experience a uniform variation in the normal component of magnetic flux density

\frac{\partial B_{n}}{\partial t}=-B_{0}\sin(\vartheta)\dot{\vartheta}\,.

(3)

To preserve the symmetry of the problem, such a uniform variation induces circumferential currents whose intensity depends exclusively on their distance from the circular plate centre, namely

{\bf J}(r,\varphi)=J(r)\hat{\varphi}\,,

(4)

being
r
and
\varphi
the polar coordinates having origin in the circular plate centre, as depicted in Fig.
1
b.
By neglecting the skin effect,
i.e.
, by neglecting the magnetic field produced by the eddy currents themselves, the current intensity can be evaluated through a direct application of the integral Faraday’s law,

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\mathcal{C}(r)}{\bf J}\cdot\hat{\varphi}\ \textrm{d}l

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathcal{C}(r)}\sigma\frac{\partial B_{n}}{\partial t}\ \textrm{d}s

(5)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow J(r)=\frac{r\sigma B_{0}}{2}\sin(\vartheta)\dot{\vartheta}\,,

where
\mathcal{C}(r)
and
\partial\mathcal{C}(r)
denote, respectively, the circle of radius
r
concentric with the plate and its boundary (see Fig.
1
b).

The induced eddy currents lead to the density of Lorentz forces
{\bf f}={\bf J}\times{\bf B}_{0}
. A null net force corresponds to
{\bf f}
, to which, however, a non-null torque around
\hat{x}
is associated. Such a torque can be quantified as

\displaystyle T(\vartheta,\dot{\vartheta})=d\int_{\mathcal{C}(R)}{\bf f}\cdot\hat{n}\underbrace{r\sin(\varphi)}_{\textrm{lever arm}}\ \textrm{d}s

(6)

\displaystyle=d\frac{\sigma B_{0}^{2}}{2}\sin(\vartheta)\dot{\vartheta}\int_{\mathcal{C}(R)}r^{2}\sin(\varphi)(\hat{\varphi}\times\hat{z})\cdot\hat{n}\ \textrm{d}s\,.

Since

(\hat{\varphi}\times\hat{z})\cdot\hat{n}=(\hat{n}\times\hat{\varphi})\cdot\hat{z}=-\hat{r}\cdot\hat{z}=-\sin(\vartheta)\sin(\varphi),

(7)

by substituting the latter in (
6
) and solving the integral, we get

T(\vartheta,\dot{\vartheta})=\underbrace{-d\frac{\sigma B_{0}^{2}R^{4}\pi}{8}\sin(\vartheta)^{2}}_{f_{\textrm{lenz}}(\vartheta)}\dot{\vartheta}\,,

(8)

from which the analytical expression of
f_{\textrm{lenz}}
is obtained.

This derivation can be extended to thin rings
[
7
]
as well.

2.2
Numerical procedure

In general,
f_{\textrm{lenz}}(q)
,
i.e.
the Lorentz force (or torque for rotations) the body would experience if moving with a unitary velocity in
q
, can be approximated numerically.

The interval of admissible values for the degree of freedom
q
is discretized in small subintervals. In each subinterval, denoted by
[q_{i},q_{i+1}]
, the currents induced in the moving object are computed numerically with FEM solving magneto quasi-static equations with forcing term
{\bf v}\times{\bf B}_{0}

[
13
]
, being
{\bf v}
the velocity of each material point of the object.
The velocity
{\bf v}
is computed as the difference between the position of each material point at
q_{i+1}
and
q_{i}
divided by the time needed to move from
q_{i}
to
q_{i+1}
with a unitary velocity in
q
.
To each material point, the average value of
{\bf B}_{0}
experienced in the subinterval is assigned.

In a way analogous to the previous section, the vector product between the resulting currents and
{\bf B}_{0}
provides the density of Lorentz forces from which the value of
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
is deduced in the midpoint of the interval.
The function
f_{\rm lenz}(q)
is then approximated for any value of
q
by linear interpolation of the computed values.

3
Methods

Two possible motions of a thin aluminum (6061T6,
\rho=$2700\text{\,}\mathrm{kg}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-3}$
,
\sigma=$37.7\text{\,}\mathrm{MS}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
) plate of thickness
d=$2.1\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
in a
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
MRI room were simulated: the rotation inside the scanner under the action of gravity (see Fig.
1
a); and the translation in the fringe field when pushed towards the scanner bore.
In both the cases, the dynamics with and without the Lenz effect were simulated to quantify its impact on motion.

The rotation under the action of gravity was also reproduced experimentally and recorded with a camera to validate the proposed procedure.

3.1
Simulations

A circular plate of radius
R=$150\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
and a square plate with side
L=$295\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
were considered. The former only for the rotation, the latter for both motions.

The rotation evolved as illustrated in Fig.
1
a: the plate was placed almost vertically on the bed inside the scanner bore and perpendicular to
{\bf B}_{0}
, so the torque due to the force of gravity and the constraint force induced the rotation.
In this case, the degree of freedom was the angle
\vartheta
of the plate with the vertical direction
\hat{y}
.
Being
m
the plate mass, the moment of inertia was
I=5mR^{2}/4
for the circular plate, and
I=4mR^{2}/3
for the square plate with
R=L/2
.
The forcing term was the torque
F_{\textrm{ext}}(\vartheta)=mgR\sin(\vartheta)
with gravitational acceleration
g
.
The Lenz effect
f_{\textrm{lenz}}(\vartheta)
was provided by (
8
) or by a numerical simulation for the circular and the square plate, respectively.

In the second test, the plate, perpendicular to the direction
\hat{z}
of the static field, was translated towards the scanner bore with a constant force
F_{\textrm{ext}}\equiv$20\text{\,}\mathrm{N}$
.
In this case, the degree of freedom was the coordinate
z
of the plate barycentre and the inertia its mass
m
.
The Lenz effect
f_{\textrm{lenz}}(z)
was provided by a numerical simulation with the fringe field of the realistic actively shielded magnet already used by Zilberti
et al.

[
6
]
.
The plate was assumed centred horizontally (
x=0
), whereas two heights were considered,
y=0
and
y=$300\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}$
.

An implicit Adams method
[
14
]
was used to solve (
1
).

3.2
Experiment

A square aluminum plate was placed in a
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
scanner to identically reproduce the rotation simulation.
The starting plate angle of about
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
was held with a wood stick.
The fall was recorded five times with the camera positioned in front of the plate, just outside the scanner bore at the same height as the upper edge of the plate.

Figure 2
:
Experimental setup and data processing. (a) Diagram of the experiment illustrating the dependence of the apparent plate height
s(\vartheta)
on the plate angle with respect to the vertical direction,
\vartheta

As depicted in the diagram of Fig.
2
a, the apparent plate height, denoted by
s(\vartheta)
, changes in each frame of the video as described by

s(\vartheta)=L\left(\frac{\cos(\vartheta)-1}{D-L\sin(\vartheta)}D+1\right)\,,

(9)

being
D
the distance from the camera to the plate.

The apparent plate height
s(\vartheta)
was deduced in each frame of the video according to the following algorithm, sketched in Fig.
2
b:

1.

The plate fulcrum was determined graphically (green line in Fig.
2
b) and the entire frame rotated to correct for the camera angle by imposing a horizontal fulcrum.

2.

The difference between sequential frames was computed and the upper and largest connected region identified.

3.

The upper side of the plate is determined as the upper boundary of the identified region (red line in Fig.
2
b) and, if necessary, the result is corrected manually.

The difference between the top edge of the plate and the fulcrum provides
s(\vartheta)
expressed in pixels.
Since
L=s(0)
and
D=L(s($90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$)-L)/s($90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}$)
, they were derived in pixels from the first and last video frames.
Finally, equation (
9
) was inverted according to a look-up table to retrieve the value of
\vartheta
.

5
Discussion

Making a nonmagnetic metallic plate rotate under the effect of gravity is a common way to demonstrate the Lenz effect
[
4
]
.
The simulations reported in Fig.
3
of a circular and a square plate estimate fall durations of the same order of magnitude as observed by Condon and Hadley
[
4
]
, both with and without
{\bf B}_{0}
.
In particular, the simulation results show that the fall in the
1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
magnetic field could last even more than
100
times the duration of the fall without the magnetic field.
It is worth noting that the dynamics at the very start of fall is almost independent of
{\bf B}_{0}
, because the moment developed by the Lenz effect is very low relative to the force of gravity. After about
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
the Lenz effect develops a moment slightly larger than the forcing term, leading to a deceleration of motion.
In the plots of Fig.
3
, the two moments seem perfectly overlapped, but the greater intensity of the Lenz effect is revealed by the decreasing trend of the angular velocity after approximately
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
.

The comparison between the experimental and the simulated data reported in Fig.
4
shows that not only the duration of the fall is predicted correctly, but the whole trend of
\vartheta
during motion is also estimated accurately.
The data processing of the recorded videos reported extremely repeatable measurements, leading to a near perfect agreement between the individual experiments and the simulation result, with a discrepancy lower than
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
for almost the entire motion.
Besides validating the proposed model of dynamics of metallic objects in presence of the Lenz effect, this comparison validates also the results obtained by computing the motion-induced electric field neglecting the skin effect
[
6
,
15
]
.

Another common and simple way to observe the Lenz effect is pushing a nonmagnetic metallic plate towards the scanner bore.
Without the magnet, this operation would be very simple, but the movement in the magnet fringe field discovers the opposing Lenz effect. The results reported in Fig.
5
show this dynamics by assuming that the plate is pushed with a constant force of
20\text{\,}\mathrm{N}
.
It can be seen that, at a certain point during motion (almost at the entrance of the scanner), a sudden large opposing force due to the Lenz effect appears with a magnitude two- or three-fold greater than the pushing force, depending on the plate
y
-coordinate. This leads to a sudden strong reduction of the plate velocity, which is reduced in less than
0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
of more than one order of magnitude, giving the operator the feeling of having hit a wall.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, a model describing the dynamics of conductive nonmagnetic objects in the MRI room taking into account the Lenz effect is presented. A method is proposed to numerically evaluate the Lenz effect for objects of any shape.
The model has been experimentally validated and applied to predict the motion of aluminum plates in two contexts with one degree of freedom.
The code developed to process the experimental data and perform the dynamics simulations is provided as Python Jupyter notebooks at:
https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17151932
.
