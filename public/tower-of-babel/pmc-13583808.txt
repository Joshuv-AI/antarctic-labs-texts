Triggering modalities to synchronise non-invasive respiratory support in preterm infants: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fabio A Blom, Ruud W van Leuteren, Frans H de Jongh, Nerissa P Denswil, Ilaria Girimonte, Raffaele Dellaca, Anton H van Kaam, G Jeroen Hutten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncreasing evidence suggests that synchronised nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation (sNIPPV) may be the optimal mode of non-invasive respiratory support. However, no comprehensive review of sNIPPV modes is available. This review aims to describe different synchronisation methods for sNIPPV and compare their effectiveness in clinical, physiological and technical outcomes with other modes of non-invasive respiratory support.MethodsThis review identified all clinical studies in preterm infants that compared sNIPPV to other non-invasive respiratory support modes or compared different trigger modalities between 1990 and 2026. A search was carried out in MEDLINE, Embase and the Cochrane Library. Main outcomes were categorised as clinical (eg, extubation failure (EF)), physiological (eg, breathing effort) and technical (eg, synchronisation rate).Results49 studies (2864 infants) were included, with a low to moderate risk of bias. Meta-analysis showed a reduction in EF (risk ratio=0.38; 95% CI 0.17 to 0.85; p=0.03) in favour of sNIPPV compared with nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP). Physiological outcomes were significantly improved during sNIPPV compared with nCPAP and nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), especially breathing effort. When reviewing technical outcomes, non-invasive neurally adjusted ventilatory assist showed lower patient-ventilator asynchrony (PVA) (index ranging from 7% to 50%), a higher synchronisation rate (80%–99%) and a shorter trigger delay (35 ms) compared with other sNIPPV modes.ConclusionsThis review shows that synchronising NIPPV results in consistent physiological (reduced patient effort) and technical benefits (reduced PVA). However, evidence on positive effects on (long-term) major clinical outcomes remains limited and requires further studies.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251022479

Introduction

Very preterm infants have an increased risk for morbidity and mortality. Respiratory failure due to immature respiratory control and underdeveloped lung function is a major cause of adverse outcomes. Infants demonstrate apnoea of prematurity and increased work of breathing (WOB) on the short-term and frequently develop long-term pulmonary complications like bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD).1–3

During their admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), preterm infants will receive respiratory support to optimise their lung function. Over the last few decades, efforts have been made to keep infants on non-invasive respiratory support because invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) is associated with long-term morbidities like BPD and neurodevelopmental impairment (NDI). Historically, nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP) has been the first-line non-invasive respiratory support mode in preterm infants. Studies have shown that nCPAP improves control of breathing and lung function. Furthermore, it lowers the incidence of BPD compared with IMV. However, some patients fail nCPAP and require more advanced non-invasive support to avoid IMV.4 5

One of the most advanced non-invasive respiratory support modes available is nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV). During NIPPV, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) is provided, which has effects similar to nCPAP. In addition, a peak inflation pressure (PIP) is applied at a set rate to provide mechanical inflations on top of PEEP. During conventional NIPPV, these inflations are not synchronised with the infant’s spontaneous breathing efforts, leading to patient-ventilator asynchrony (PVA). de Waal et al demonstrated that PVA can reach up to 70% during NIPPV.6

Over the last decade, growing evidence has indicated that NIPPV should preferably be synchronised (sNIPPV (synchronised nasal intermittent positive pressure ventilation)) with the infant’s spontaneous breathing efforts. Synchronisation of the inflations reduces the level of PVA, lowering the WOB and improving respiratory outcomes. In line with this, sNIPPV reduces the incidence of desaturations, bradycardias and central apnoea compared with both NIPPV and nCPAP and is superior in reducing extubation failure (EF).7–12

To provide sNIPPV, accurate detection of inspiratory onset is essential. Over the years, several synchronisation modes for sNIPPV have been described to detect inspiration and trigger the ventilator to provide synchronised support. Described sNIPPV modes include airway sensors (ie, pressure and flow sensors), pneumatic abdominal capsules (PCAPs), electromyography of the diaphragm (dEMG) and respiratory inductance plethysmography (RIP). Although the performance of these sNIPPV modes has been described, a comprehensive overview is lacking. This systematic review aims to provide an overview of clinical, physiological and technical outcomes of the various synchronisation modes used for sNIPPV.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies involved preterm infants admitted to the NICU and receiving non-invasive respiratory support. We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs), crossover studies, observational studies and retrospective studies comparing different trigger modalities (flow, pressure, RIP and dEMG) for sNIPPV or comparing sNIPPV with other non-invasive respiratory support modalities, such as high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC), nCPAP, NIPPV or nasal high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (nHFOV). Furthermore, we included (comparative and non-comparative) clinical studies investigating technical outcomes of an sNIPPV mode. Only available full-text English studies (including access with subscription) were considered. Studies were excluded if they involved animals or investigated IMV as intervention. Case reports, study protocols, reviews and letters were also excluded. Finally, studies without prespecified outcomes were excluded. Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Search strategy

Online databases MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Ovid) and the Cochrane Library were searched by NPD. We developed a search strategy for each database using controlled terms and title, abstract and keywords for the following search concepts: “Infants”, “Synchronized Noninvasive Respiratory Support” and “Trigger Modalities”. Search terms were combined using the Boolean operators AND and OR (see PROSPERO registration CRD420251022479 and the detailed search strategy in online supplemental materials 1). Based on an estimation of when the first sNIPPV modes were applied in clinical practice, the selected timespan was from 1990 to March 2026. Duplicates were removed using DedupEndNote (V.1.1.4).

Study selection

Two reviewers (FAB and RWvL) independently screened the articles retrieved from the search strategy based on title, abstract and full text, using Rayyan. Studies were included or excluded according to the eligibility criteria, with any disagreements resolved by consensus. Additionally, eligible articles were included by manually searching reference lists.

Data extraction

Extracted data included the first author, year of publication, study design, population, interventions used in the study and control group and the reported outcomes.

Main outcome measures

Three different outcome measure categories were assessed in this review: clinical, physiological and technical outcomes. Clinical outcomes included EF, duration of respiratory support, BPD, NDI and death. For EF, all reported definitions were accepted. Physiological outcomes contained breathing effort, respiratory support settings, gas exchange and cardiorespiratory events. Breathing effort metrics included WOB measured by oesophageal pressure or diaphragmatic electrical activity, diaphragmatic function measured by ultrasound and clinical signs, such as dyspnoea and/or chest wall distortion. Respiratory support setting metrics involved PIP, mean airway pressure (MAwP), tidal volume (VT) and minute ventilation (VE). Gas exchange metrics included peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), partial pressure of oxygen, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2), respiratory rate (RR) and acidity (pH). Cardiorespiratory events included apnoea, desaturation and bradycardia. Technical outcomes included trigger accuracy and trigger delay. Trigger accuracy metrics were synchronisation rate, asynchrony index and the proportion of time spent in backup mode. Trigger delay metrics involved the trigger time difference (the time between breath detection and the reference detected breath) and synchronisation delay (the time difference between trigger and mechanical insufflation).

Risk of bias assessment

Quality of evidence was evaluated by assessing the risk of bias. This was done using Version 2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB-2)13 for both RCTs and crossover trials and Risk Of Bias In Non-Randomised Studies - of Interventions (ROBINS-I)14 for non-randomised comparative studies. Risk of bias scores were low, some concerns and high risk. For non-randomised non-comparative studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale15 was used to assess the quality of the evidence.

Treatment effects

Treatment effects were expressed as risk ratio with 95% CIs and combined in a random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood. Forest plots were used to display study-specific estimates alongside the pooled effect and measures of heterogeneity (I², τ²).

Randomised controlled trials

The 12 RCTs16–27 included 698 infants. The study sample size ranged from 16 to 124 subjects. sNIPPV triggering modes were flow-based (flow-sNIPPV, n=2), PCAP-based (PCAP-sNIPPV, n=4) and dEMG-based (dEMG-sNIPPV, n=6; all using neurally adjusted ventilatory assist in non-invasive ventilation (NIV-NAVA)). They were compared with nCPAP (n=9) and NIPPV (n=3). The RoB-2 scores were low risk or some concerns, mainly caused by the selection of the reported results because study protocols were not available (table 2).

6 studies reported the duration of respiratory support16 22–25 27 and found no difference between sNIPPV and nCPAP or NIPPV.

8 studies16 20 22–27 comparing sNIPPV to nCPAP or NIPPV reported the incidence of BPD or death. Only Louie et al22 demonstrated a significant reduction in BPD incidence with sNIPPV compared with nCPAP, using NIV-NAVA.

Physiological outcomes

2 studies21 26 reported on WOB in NIV-NAVA versus nCPAP. Shin et al26 reported a significantly lower WOB during NIV-NAVA, while Lee et al21 showed no difference in WOB. With respect to respiratory support parameters, Makker et al23 reported a significantly reduced PIP during NIV-NAVA compared with NIPPV, and Lee et al21 reported an increase of MAwP in the NIV-NAVA group compared with nCPAP. In addition, four studies18–21 reported no difference in gas exchange when comparing sNIPPV with nCPAP or NIPPV. Lastly, 5 studies16 18 20 21 27 reported on cardiorespiratory events during sNIPPV compared with nCPAP, and two of these showed a significantly reduced event rate in favour of sNIPPV.16 21 Barrington et al16 showed a significantly lower apnoea event rate during PCAP-sNIPPV, while Lee et al21 showed reduced apnoea events in NIV-NAVA.

Technical outcomes

No technical outcomes were reported in these RCTs.

Clinical crossover trials

18 studies were clinical crossover trials, including 294 infants.7–11 28–40 The study sample size ranged from 8 to 51 subjects. The sNIPPV trigger modes were RIP-based (RIP-sNIPPV, n=1), pressure-based (pressure-sNIPPV, n=1), flow-sNIPPV (n=3), PCAP-sNIPPV (n=4), and dEMG-sNIPPV (n=8; including 6 NIV-NAVA and 2 continuous neurally adjusted positive airway pressure (NeuroPAP)). The control groups used HFNC (n=1), nCPAP (n=8), nHFOV (n=2), and NIPPV (n=7). Additionally, some studies used an alternative sNIPPV mode as the control group, including pressure-sNIPPV (n=1), flow-sNIPPV (n=1), PCAP-sNIPPV (n=1), and NIV-NAVA (n=1). When evaluating RoB-2, 8 studies scored low risk, 6 scored some concerns and 4 scored high risk, mainly caused by potential carryover effects and selection of reported results (table 3).

Clinical outcomes

No clinical outcomes were described in the crossover studies.

Physiological outcomes

15 studies7–10 30–40 reported on breathing effort during sNIPPV versus HFNC, nCPAP, NIPPV or another sNIPPV mode. 12 studies7–10 30–35 37 40 showed a significantly reduced WOB in sNIPPV. For PCAP-sNIPPV, all studies reported a significantly lower WOB compared with both nCPAP and NIPPV.7 8 10 For RIP-sNIPPV, Ali et al9 observed reduced WOB compared with nCPAP. For flow-sNIPPV, Charles et al31 described decreased WOB compared with HFNC, and Moretti et al37 showed decreased WOB compared with nCPAP. For dEMG-sNIPPV, four studies30 32–34 showed that NIV-NAVA significantly reduced WOB compared with nCPAP and NIPPV. When comparing NIV-NAVA with other sNIPPV modes, Lee et al35 and Treussart et al,40 respectively, showed a significantly lower WOB compared with pressure-sNIPPV and PCAP-sNIPPV, while Latremouille et al34 reported no difference in WOB between NIV-NAVA and flow-sNIPPV.

12 studies7–10 28 32 34–39 assessed respiratory support settings during sNIPPV versus nCPAP, nHFOV, NIPPV or another sNIPPV mode. For PCAP-sNIPPV, no difference in VT or VE was found compared with nCPAP, nHFOV or NIPPV, but Huang et al did report significantly lower MAwP compared with NIPPV.10 No difference in VT or VE was found when comparing RIP-sNIPPV to nCPAP.9 For flow-sNIPPV, Moretti et al37 showed significantly reduced VT and VE compared with nCPAP. For dEMG-sNIPPV, two studies showed significantly lower PIP compared with NIPPV,32 39 and only Rochon et al39 found significantly increased MAwP compared with NIPPV and nCPAP. When directly comparing different sNIPPV modalities, two studies showed that PIP was significantly reduced during NIV-NAVA compared with pressure-sNIPPV and flow-sNIPPV,34 35 and only Latremouille et al34 showed that MAwP was significantly reduced during NIV-NAVA compared with flow-sNIPPV. One study showed that NeuroPAP significantly increased dynamic PEEP compared with NIV-NAVA.38

18 studies7–11 28–40 evaluated gas exchange during sNIPPV versus HFNC, nCPAP, NIPPV or other sNIPPV modes. 7 studies showed significant improvements in favour of sNIPPV.9 10 28 29 32 36 39 For PCAP-sNIPPV, two studies10 28 reported improved gas exchange in favour of sNIPPV: Atanasov et al28 observed better oxygenation (lower FiO2 and higher SpO2) compared with nHFOV, and Huang et al10 found improved ventilation (reduced pCO2 and RR) compared with NIPPV. In the only RIP-sNIPPV study, Ali et al showed decreased FiO2 compared with nCPAP. For flow-sNIPPV, only Moretti et al37 showed improved gas exchange (lower pCO2 and lower RR) compared with nCPAP.9 4 dEMG-sNIPPV (both NIV-NAVA and NeuroPAP) studies showed significantly improved oxygenation (FiO2) or ventilation metrics (RR/pCO2) compared with nCPAP or NIPPV.30 32 36 39

10 studies9–11 28 30–32 34 38 40 examined cardiorespiratory events during sNIPPV versus HFNC, nCPAP, NIPPV or another sNIPPV mode. 3 studies found a significant decrease in events during sNIPPV. Atanasov et al28 found a significantly reduced incidence of desaturations in PCAP-sNIPPV compared with nHFOV; Gizzi et al11 found fewer apnoea events during flow-sNIPPV compared with nCPAP and NIPPV and Gibu et al32 observed fewer desaturations in NIV-NAVA compared with NIPPV. Three additional studies that directly compared different sNIPPV modes showed no difference in cardiorespiratory events.34 38 40

Technical outcomes

7 studies10 34–37 39 40 evaluated trigger accuracy during sNIPPV versus NIPPV or another sNIPPV modality. Overall, sNIPPV showed superior trigger accuracy compared with NIPPV.10 36 39 Among the different sNIPPV modes, NIV-NAVA demonstrated superior synchronisation compared with both pressure-triggered and flow-triggered sNIPPV.34 35 40 For PCAP-sNIPPV, Huang et al10 reported a median synchronisation rate of 82% (range 59%–97%). For flow-sNIPPV, Moretti et al24 found a synchronisation rate of 91% (±6) and an asynchrony index of 0.7% (±0.3). Across 5 dEMG-sNIPPV studies (4 NIV-NAVA and 1 NeuroPAP), synchronisation rates ranged from 51% to 99% compared with 9%–27% in NIPPV and 14%–51% in other sNIPPV modes.34–36 39 40

3 studies32 35 37 assessed trigger delay in sNIPPV. During flow-sNIPPV, Moretti et al37 observed a synchronisation delay of 65 ms (±12). For NIV-NAVA, Gibu et al32 showed a synchronisation delay of 124 ms (±21). When directly comparing sNIPPV modes, Lee et al35 found a shorter trigger time difference in NIV-NAVA (35±8 ms) compared with pressure-sNIPPV (295±102 ms).

Observational studies

7 observational studies33 41–47 were identified, including 122 infants. The study sample size ranged from 6 to 40 subjects. The studied sNIPPV modes were pressure-sNIPPV (n=1), PCAP-sNIPPV (n=5) and dEMG-sNIPPV (n=2; both NIV-NAVA and transcutaneous dEMG). When evaluating ROBINS-I, most comparative observational studies showed some concerns, mainly caused by confounding (table 4). When evaluating studies with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, 3 studies scored moderate due to the absence of a comparison group (table 5).

Clinical outcomes

2 studies41 42 reported on EF during sNIPPV versus nCPAP. Bai et al41 and Ding et al42 both showed significantly lower EF during pressure-sNIPPV and PCAP-sNIPPV, respectively, compared with nCPAP. Only Ding et al42 reported on time on respiratory support, showing no difference between PCAP-sNIPPV and nCPAP. This study also reported no difference in incidence of BPD or death between groups.

Physiological outcomes

Only Elkhouli et al43 reported on breathing effort, showing improved diaphragmatic excursions during NIV-NAVA compared with NIPPV. 2 studies43 44 investigated respiratory settings, and only Owen et al44 described a significantly higher MAwP during PCAP-sNIPPV compared with NIPPV, while there were no differences in VT and PIP. 2 studies41 43 investigated gas exchange parameters, and only Bai et al41 found a reduced pCO2 level in the pressure-sNIPPV group compared with nCPAP. This study also showed a significantly reduced incidence of apnoea and bradycardia events during pressure-sNIPPV compared with nCPAP.

Technical outcomes

4 studies44–47 reported on trigger accuracy during PCAP-sNIPPV. Owen et al44 showed a synchronisation rate of 82±17%, Stern et al45 reported a synchronisation rate of 72%–74% and Warth et al47 showed a synchronisation rate of 68% (IQR 56%–76%) for PCAP-sNIPPV. de Waal et al directly compared PCAP triggering with transcutaneous dEMG triggering and found a synchronisation rate of 67.8% compared with 62.6%, respectively.46

2 studies44 46 reported on trigger delay in PCAP-sNIPPV. Owen et al44 found a synchronisation delay of 28 ms±20 during PCAP-sNIPPV. de Waal et al46 directly compared PCAP triggering with transcutaneous dEMG triggering and found a shorter trigger time difference with dEMG triggering (−50 ms (range −90 to −22)) compared with PCAP triggering (+154 ms (range +118 to +164)).

Retrospective cohort studies

There were 12 retrospective cohort studies,48–59 including 1750 infants. The study sample size ranged from 32 to 469 subjects. The sNIPPV trigger modes were PCAP-sNIPPV (n=3), flow-sNIPPV (n=4) and dEMG-sNIPPV (n=5; all NIV-NAVA). When evaluating ROBINS-I, 4 studies scored some concerns and 8 studies showed high risk, mainly caused by confounding (table 6).

Clinical outcomes

7 studies50 52–55 57 58 evaluated EF during sNIPPV versus nCPAP or NIPPV. 4 studies reported significantly reduced EF with sNIPPV. Among flow-sNIPPV studies, two50 55 showed reduced EF compared with nCPAP and NIPPV. Among NIV-NAVA studies (n=3), two52 57 demonstrated significantly lower EF compared with nCPAP.

9 studies48–51 53–55 57 59 evaluated time on respiratory support during sNIPPV versus nCPAP or NIPPV. When comparing PCAP-sNIPPV to nCPAP, Bhandari et al48 reported longer nCPAP time, while Kulkarni et al51 found a shorter nCPAP time during PCAP-sNIPPV. For flow-sNIPPV53 55 59 and NIV-NAVA,54 57 no significant differences in duration of non-invasive support or time until EF were observed compared with nCPAP and NIPPV.

9 studies48–53 55 57 59 evaluated death, BPD and/or NDI during sNIPPV versus nCPAP or NIPPV. Kulkarni et al51 found a lower incidence of BPD or death during PCAP-sNIPPV compared with nCPAP, while Bhandari et al48 and Dumpa et al49 observed a higher incidence of BPD and BPD/death in PCAP-sNIPPV compared with nCPAP and NIPPV. For flow-sNIPPV, Zhang and Zhang found lower incidence of BPD compared with nCPAP, while Ricotti et al and Salvo et al found no significant difference compared with nCPAP and NIPPV.53 55 59 For NIV-NAVA,57 no significant differences were found in BPD or death compared with NIPPV. When evaluating the incidence of NDI and NDI/death, Bhandari et al48 found no difference between PCAP-sNIPPV and nCPAP.

Physiological outcomes

Only Lee et al52 reported on respiratory support settings and found no significant differences between NIV-NAVA and nCPAP. 3 studies reported on gas exchange, of which only Bhandari et al48 found significantly lower pCO2 levels in PCAP-sNIPPV compared with nCPAP. 3 studies reported on cardiorespiratory events, of which Lee et al52 and Tabacaru et al56 reported decreased bradycardia during NIV-NAVA compared with nCPAP and NIPPV.

Technical outcomes

None of the retrospective cohort studies reported on technical outcomes.

Discussion

This systematic review summarised all available evidence regarding the effects of different sNIPPV modalities on clinical, physiological, and technical outcomes. Overall, sNIPPV demonstrated clear advantages over other non-invasive respiratory support modes regarding technical performance and short-term physiological outcomes. Evidence for improved clinical outcomes remains limited, with reduced EF observed only compared with nCPAP and no differences in long-term outcomes such as BPD, NDI or NDI. Among triggering modalities, NIV-NAVA showed the most consistent superiority.

Regarding clinical outcomes, our review aligns with previous literature demonstrating the superiority of sNIPPV over nCPAP in reducing EF.12 60–62 Although this was expected, these studies do not isolate the effect of synchronisation, as the comparator was nCPAP and not NIPPV. In the studies that did compare sNIPPV with NIPPV, sNIPPV did not appear to be superior to NIPPV in reducing EF. However, it should be kept in mind that the definition of EF varied, which could be a confounding factor. When comparing sNIPPV modes regarding clinical outcomes, we could not identify a superior trigger mode, as they all performed superior to nCPAP, and no study compared different sNIPPV modes directly. As different EF definitions have been used in the pooled study results, our data are insufficient to make strong claims on the superiority of sNIPPV and the preferred synchronisation mode. For other clinical outcomes such as death or BPD, there is a lack of evidence showing the superiority of sNIPPV to other modes of non-invasive support, which is in line with previous literature.60 63 64

Concerning physiological outcomes, reduced WOB, less chest-wall distortions and lower PIP were found during sNIPPV compared with both nCPAP and NIPPV, which has also been shown by Tomé et al.65 However, the improved patient-ventilator interaction did not necessarily result in improved gas exchange, and only low-level evidence was found for decreased cardiorespiratory events, which has also been reported by De Poali et al.66 It is possible that, for these physiological outcomes, the follow-up duration in the included studies was insufficient to observe beneficial effects. In studies that directly compared sNIPPV modes, NIV-NAVA was associated with lower WOB than both PCAP-sNIPPV and flow-sNIPPV. However, whether this benefit of NIV-NAVA is caused solely by the synchronisation method or can also be attributed to NIV-NAVA’s unique proportional assist remains to be determined.

Regarding technical outcomes, sNIPPV demonstrated better synchronisation than NIPPV, which was expected, as the aim of sNIPPV is to improve synchronisation. In the studies that directly compared different sNIPPV modes, NIV-NAVA achieved superior trigger accuracy compared with pressure-sNIPPV and flow-sNIPPV, possibly because the latter two are sensitive to air leakage around the interface at the airway opening, which is not a concern for NIV-NAVA. Furthermore, transcutaneous dEMG and NIV-NAVA showed shorter trigger delays compared with pressure-sNIPPV, flow-sNIPPV and PCAP-sNIPPV modalities. This can be explained by the fact that diaphragm-based triggering detects the onset of inspiration earlier in the respiratory cycle compared with other synchronisation techniques. The superior trigger accuracy could also explain why NIV-NAVA shows reduced WOB compared with other sNIPPV modes. Although final conclusions cannot be drawn as technical outcome metrics varied significantly between studies, cautious interpretations can be made from the studies that directly compared triggering modalities.

Different study designs resulted in varying quality of evidence. RCTs generally had acceptable quality, with only low or some concerns for bias. In contrast, other study designs more often showed a high risk of bias, especially retrospective cohort studies. This makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions based on the results of these studies.

Although several studies suggest benefits of using sNIPPV, the implications for clinical practice are not entirely clear yet. This is mainly caused by the high heterogeneity of evidence, making comparison between sNIPPV modes difficult. First, outcome measures (eg, EF, WOB and synchronisation rate) lack consistent definitions and sometimes show conflicting results. Second, there was a high variety in patient characteristics between studies, especially regarding the cause of needing respiratory support. As a result, it is hard to speculate which population would benefit from synchronisation the most and which mode should be used. Third, results from pressure-trigger and flow-trigger modalities from different devices were pooled. Because ventilator/device performance (eg, leak compensation and trigger detection algorithms) may vary substantially, this pooling could mask relevant differences between devices. Fourth, most studies were small and/or showed concerns in their quality of evidence. Larger clinical trials are needed to resolve these limitations of the current evidence. These studies should be sufficiently powered and compare the impact of sNIPPV and NIPPV on meaningful clinical outcomes. Finally, more studies should directly compare different triggering modalities for sNIPPV to determine which method should be incorporated in different neonatal ventilators.

Conclusions

This systematic review summarised the evidence on the impact of sNIPPV on clinical, physiological and technical outcomes in preterm infants. sNIPPV shows better physiological outcomes and technical performance compared with non-synchronised non-invasive respiratory support modes, but evidence of clinical benefits is limited. Of all sNIPPV modes, triggering based on the diaphragm’s activity tends to show favourable technical and physiological outcomes. However, the heterogeneity in outcome metrics across studies shows that this field is still in development and that more research is needed to compare the different synchronisation modalities and evaluate the long-term clinical impact of sNIPPV.
