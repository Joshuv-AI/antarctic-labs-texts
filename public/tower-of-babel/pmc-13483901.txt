The impact of traumatic injuries on work productivity and economic burden among post-hospitalisation patients in Vietnam
Authors: Tung Hoang Tran, Hai Minh Vu, Trung Thanh Nguyen, Huyen Do, Duy Cao Nguyen, Cyrus S H Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic injuries are a major contributor to global morbidity and mortality, with profound socioeconomic impacts, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. However, evidence regarding post-discharge work productivity loss among injury survivors remains scarce in Vietnam. This secondary analysis was based on data collected between September 2020 and January 2021 at two hospitals in Hung Yen province, Vietnam. A total of 253 injury patients were included. Work productivity loss was assessed using the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire: General Health (WPAI:GH). Multivariate Tobit and logistic regression models were employed to identify factors associated with absenteeism and presenteeism. Among participants, 52.6% reported absenteeism and 85.8% reported presenteeism within the week prior to the survey. Higher age, greater monthly income, lower psychosocial resilience scores, poorer health status (EQ-5D-5L), and higher PTSD symptom severity were significantly associated with reduced work productivity (p < 0.05). The mean weekly costs of absenteeism and presenteeism were 28.3 USD and 12.0 USD, respectively. Compared to international studies, the prevalence of work impairment in this cohort was notably higher, likely reflecting limited access to rehabilitation services and sociocultural pressures to resume work prematurely in Vietnam. Traumatic injuries substantially impair work productivity among survivors after hospital discharge in Vietnam, imposing significant personal and economic burdens. These findings highlight the urgent need for comprehensive rehabilitation programs that address both physical and psychosocial dimensions to support sustainable workforce reintegration.Keywords: Injury, Post hospitalisation, Work ability, General health, Determinants, VietnamSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Traumatic injuries remain one of the leading causes of mortality and disability worldwide, exerting profound health and socioeconomic consequences, particularly among the working-age population1,2. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 4.4 million deaths each year are attributable to injuries, accounting for nearly 8% of global mortality. Among these, unintentional injuries are responsible for about 3.16 million deaths annually3,4. Beyond mortality, millions of survivors endure long-term physical and psychological sequelae, often resulting in persistent functional impairments that extend well beyond hospital discharge5.

Although the acute physical impacts of traumatic injuries have been extensively documented, the long-term consequences for work ability and daily functioning after hospital discharge remain inadequately explored, especially in low and middle-income countries such as Vietnam. International evidence has consistently demonstrated that diminished work productivity and inability to resume previous occupational roles are common and enduring outcomes following injury6,7. Studies from high-income countries have reported substantial challenges in returning to work after injury. For example, a study conducted in the United States reported that approximately 21% of individuals who were employed at the time of injury were unable to return to work within one to two years post-injury, closely aligning with a 20% reduction in employment rates observed four years after injury8. Another study found that among trauma patients, only 58.4% returned to full-time employment, while 21.5% secured part-time jobs and 20.1% remained unemployed9. In contrast, studies from low-income settings report more limited recovery of work capacity; for example, in Ethiopia, only 59% of injured patients returned to work within one year after injury10. These findings suggest that the socioeconomic context, availability of rehabilitation services, and labor market structures may substantially influence post-injury work outcomes. However, empirical evidence on long-term work productivity and functional recovery following injury in LMIC settings, remains scarce.

These indicators largely capture employment outcomes, such as return-to-work status and job participation. However, traumatic injuries may also affect labor productivity more broadly, including reduced work capacity, absenteeism, and decreased work performance among those who return to work11,12. In health economics, productivity loss represents a key component of the economic burden of injuries, which includes direct medical costs (such as hospitalization, medication, and rehabilitation), indirect costs related to income and productivity loss, and intangible costs associated with pain, psychological distress, and reduced quality of life13,14.

In Vietnam, although the age-standardized mortality rate for all injuries has shown an 11.6% reduction between 2007 and 2017, it remains high at 68.2 per 100,000 population, with traffic-related injuries constituting the predominant cause of injury-related deaths15. In comparison, neighboring countries such as Thailand and Malaysia reported lower age-standardized injury mortality rates of 29.7 and 23.7 per 100,000 population, respectively16. Despite this substantial burden, research on the long-term occupational outcomes and functional recovery of injury survivors in Vietnam remains limited. Most existing studies have primarily focused on mortality patterns and acute hospital outcomes, leaving a significant gap in understanding the broader socioeconomic consequences of injury. Persistent loss of work ability may reduce individual income, increase financial pressure on households, and contribute to the overall economic burden associated with traumatic injuries17,18.

Traumatic injuries may influence work productivity through multiple pathways. Physical impairments resulting from injury can limit mobility and functional capacity, thereby reducing individuals’ ability to perform occupational tasks19,20. In addition, psychological consequences such as anxiety, depression, or trauma-related stress may hinder recovery and reduce the likelihood of returning to work21. Furthermore, high medical expenditures associated with injury treatment may create financial pressure for households, potentially affecting labor supply decisions and long-term economic stability22.

Despite the considerable burden of traumatic injuries, empirical evidence on their long-term effects on work productivity and economic burden among injury survivors in Vietnam remains scarce. By examining work productivity loss and its associated factors among post-hospitalization injury patients, this study provides new evidence on the socioeconomic consequences of traumatic injuries in the Vietnamese context. In response to this gap, the present study aimed to assess the impact of traumatic injuries on work ability and daily functioning among patients following hospital discharge in Vietnam and to identify factors associated with work productivity loss. The findings may help inform health policies, rehabilitation strategies, and workforce reintegration programs for injury survivors.

Materials and methods

Study design, sampling method, and data collection

This study is a secondary analysis of an existing dataset from September 2020 to January 2021 at two hospitals in Hung Yen province, namely Thai Binh General Hospital and Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy Hospital. In the original study, trauma patients were identified from hospital admission records of individuals hospitalized due to traumatic injuries during the study period. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who had been hospitalized with traumatic injuries of any cause, were able to communicate with data collectors, and provided written informed consent. Patients with severe injuries that prevented them from completing the original survey were excluded.

The primary study applied a convenience sampling approach during the data collection period. Participants were interviewed 7 days after hospital discharge. In total, 278 trauma patients were enrolled, and after data cleaning, 253 with complete information were included in this secondary analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 91.0%.

Measurement and instrument

Data used in the present study were derived from a structured questionnaire that had been developed and administered in the original study. The questionnaire included five main components: (1) Socio-demographic status and health behaviors, (2) Injuries characteristics, (3) Health status and stress disorder, (4) Health-related work productivity loss, and (5) Personal and social relationship.

Variables

Outcome variable

Health-related work productivity loss: Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire: General Health (WPAI:GH) consist of 6 items assessing employment status and the impact of health problems on work and regular activities during the past 7 days. Q1 identifies whether the respondent is currently employed. Q2 measures the number of work hours missed due to injury, Q3 measures hours missed for other reasons, and Q4 measures hours actually worked during the past 7 days. Q5 assesses the degree to which health problems affected productivity while working, and Q6 assesses the extent to which health problems affected regular daily activities. Q5 and Q6 are scored on a scale from 0 to 10, where 0 indicates no effect and 10 indicates complete impairment23. The tool was evaluated by some previous study24,25.

Absenteeism and presenteeism were calculated only for participants who reported being currently employed. In this study, employed referred to individuals engaged in any income-generating work, including both formal and informal employment. Participants who were unemployed, retired, or students were not included in the analyses of absenteeism and presenteeism.

According to the regional minimum wage regulations in Vietnam28, Hung Yen province belonged to Region IV with income per month was about 134.5$. Additionally, the Labor Code 45/2019/QH14 of Vietnam regulates that the maximum number of working hours per week does not exceed 48 h29,30(equivalent to approximately 192 working hours per month). Based on these regulations, the minimum hourly wage was calculated by dividing the monthly minimum wage by the number of working hours per month, resulting in an estimated minimum hourly wage of USD 0.70 in Hung Yen province. In this study, the regional minimum wage was used as a proxy for annual wages in order to estimate the cost of absenteeism and presenteeism across participants31.

After calculating, the 0.70$ per hour was the minimum rate in Hung Yen province and was used to calculate the cost of absenteeism and cost of presenteeism per week by a formula:

Covariates

Socio-demographic

The questionnaire was designed including questions related to demographic factors such as age, gender (male / female), occupation (famers / worker / freelance / others), marital status (married / other), living location (rural / urban), monthly household income (USD) and living arrangements (family / caregivers / others). In addition, patients were also asked about recent tobacco and alcohol use.

Health status and stress disorder

The EQ-5D-5 L included 5 questions on 5 aspects: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain / discomfort, and anxiety / depression. The available responses were 1 - no problems, 2- slight problems, 3 - moderate problems, 4 - severe problems, and 5 - extreme problems32. A total of 3125 possible health states could be produced from the combination of options of five dimensions. After that, each health state can be converted to a utility index (from -0.512 to 1) by using the cross-walk value set for Vietnamese33,34. The tool was evaluated by a previous study in Vietnam35. In this study, The Cronbach’s alpha of this scale was excellent at 0.920.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared. BMI then classified into: Underweight (BMI < 18.5), Normal (BMI: 18.5-22.9), Overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 23) using the Asian BMI classification scale36.

Short Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Rating Interview (SPRINT) was an eight-item self-report measure that assesses the core symptoms of PTSD (intrusion, avoidance, numbing, arousal), somatic malaise, stress vulnerability, and role and social functional impairment. Symptoms are rates on five point scales from 0 (not at all) to 4 (very much)37. The SPRINT demonstrated solid psychometric properties and can serve as a reliable, valid, and homogeneous measure of PTSD illness severity and of global improvement. The total score of this scale was summed, with higher score indicating a higher Stress Disorder. The Cronbach’s alpha of this scale was excellent at 0.929.

Injuries characteristics

Information about history of injury (No/Yes), causes of injury (traffic accident / activity accident / fall / occupational accident / other), type of main injury (soft tissue / hand / upper limb / lower limb / fracture / other), and accompanied injuries of patients (soft tissue / hand / upper limb / lower limb / fracture / other) was collected. Soft tissue generally described muscle lesions, tendons, ligaments and skin, grease, and other affiliate organizations. Hand injury includes injuries related to wrists, hands, fingers while upper injury includes injury to the forearms, elbows and arms. The lower injury is the injury of the tendon, muscle and kneecap of the lower limb. Fractures are fractures of arms, forearms, femur bones, kneecaps, tibia, fibula and feet. Multiple injuries was two and more injuries.

Furthermore, date of hospital admission, date of hospital discharge and injury severity score (ISS) were looked up the information on the medical record. ISS is an anatomical scoring system that provides an overall measure of injury severity in patients with multiple injuries. Each injury is assigned an Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score ranging from 1 to 6 and allocated to one of six body regions. The highest AIS score in each body region is retained, and the ISS is calculated as the sum of the squares of the AIS scores for the three most severely injured body regions. ISS ranges from 0 to 75, and any injury assigned an AIS score of 6 is automatically given an ISS of 7538,39.

Protective factors for resilience scale and Lubben social network scale

Protective factors for resilience scale (PFRS) includes 15 items40 and divides into 3 domains: Personal Resource, Social Resources - Peers, and Social Resources - Family, with 5 items per domain. A 7-Likert scale was used to respond for each question (from 1-strongly disagree to 7-strongly agree). The total score of each domain was calculated with higher score indicating the perception that one had greater personal and psycho-social (from family and peers) protective factors available in order to be resourceful in the context of adversity40. The Cronbach’s alpha was excellent at 0.923.

Data analysis

Data management and analysis were performed using Stata 16.0. With missing data, we used the Listwise Deletion method to clean data before analyzing it. Listwise deletion means that any individual in a data set was deleted from an analysis if they were missing data on any variable in the analysis. Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentage. Comparison of Health-related work productivity loss between subgroups were using Chi-square test, Rank-sum test, and Kruskal-Wallis test. Via fractional polynomial, we described the health-related work productivity loss of injury patients based on the time after hospital discharge to interview date with a non-linear relationship. Multivariate logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with the presence of absenteeism and presenteeism (binary outcomes). Multivariate Tobit regression models were applied to analyze the continuous percentage outcomes of absenteeism and presenteeism (0-100%), which are censored at zero. Potential covariates for full models included socio-economic status, status and health behaviors, injuries characteristics, and Personal and social relationships. Covariates were initially selected based on theoretical relevance and previous literature. Stepwise forward selection (p < 0.20) was then applied to obtain more parsimonious models. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all VIF values were below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

For the study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Thai Binh University of Medicine and Pharmacy (Approval No. 1183/HDDD, issued on 31 December 2019). All participants in the primary survey received detailed information about the study aims and procedures and provided written informed consent before enrolment. Their participation was voluntary, with no financial or material compensation offered. For the current secondary analysis, only anonymized data were used, and no new information was collected from participants. The dataset was securely stored, with access restricted to the research team, and all procedures complied with international ethical principles, including those outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Discussion

Injuries continue to pose a significant public health and economic burden worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to post-discharge rehabilitation services remains limited. Understanding the impact of injuries on long-term functional outcomes, including work productivity, is essential for designing effective recovery and reintegration strategies. This study provides compelling evidence that injuries substantially impair work productivity among patients following hospital discharge in Vietnam. We found that 52.6% of injury patients experienced absenteeism and 85.8% reported presenteeism after hospital discharge, rates notably higher than those observed in previous studies. For example, absenteeism among lumbar spine surgery patients remained around 16.0% at 12 months postoperatively, while in ankylosing spondylitis patients, absenteeism and presenteeism were reported at 10.2% and 43.9%, respectively41,42. Similarly, among migraine patients, presenteeism ranged from 41.7% to 67.5%, with absenteeism between 12.4% and 22.5%43. The substantially higher rates observed in our cohort may partly reflect differences in study populations and healthcare settings. Unlike previous studies that focused on specific clinical conditions such as migraine or spinal disorders, our study included a broader group of trauma patients with varying injury types and severities. In addition, the study was conducted in a provincial setting where access to rehabilitation and post-discharge support services may be more limited compared with specialized centers in high-income countries.

Several key factors were associated with reduced work productivity among injury patients in our study. Interestingly, older age was associated with a lower likelihood of both absenteeism and presenteeism, as indicated by the regression results. This finding suggests that younger patients may experience greater work disruption following injury, possibly because they are more likely to engage in physically demanding occupations or have higher work-related expectations44. A similar pattern has been reported in some studies where younger workers experienced greater productivity loss after injury due to occupational demands and limited work flexibility45,46. Notably, higher monthly income was also associated with greater work impairment, which may reflect differences in occupational roles or job responsibilities rather than income itself. Lower psychosocial resilience, as measured by PFRS scores, was strongly correlated with diminished work ability, reinforcing prior findings that robust social support networks are critical for successful return-to-work outcomes. Furthermore, higher PTSD symptomatology and poorer overall health status (EQ-5D-5 L scores) were significantly related to increased work impairment, aligning with literature that emphasizes the impact of psychological distress and health decline on workforce reintegration47. These findings collectively highlight the necessity of adopting a comprehensive rehabilitation approach that addresses both physical and psychosocial dimensions of recovery.

The economic analysis from this study demonstrates that patients experiencing absenteeism and presenteeism incurred substantial indirect productivity losses. The mean weekly cost of absenteeism was estimated at 28.3 USD, while the mean weekly cost of presenteeism was 12.0 USD. Although these figures may appear modest compared to those reported in high-income countries, they represent a considerable financial burden in the context of Vietnam’s Region IV, where the average monthly income is approximately 134.5 USD. When extrapolated to the national level, given the high annual incidence of traumatic injuries, the overall productivity loss associated with injuries could represent a considerable economic burden. Previous global estimates have suggested that indirect costs account for up to 70% of the total economic burden of injuries, with lost productivity being the major contributor48. These findings underscore the urgent need to develop comprehensive rehabilitation programs aimed at facilitating workforce reintegration and mitigating long-term economic consequences for injury survivors in Vietnam.

This study represents one of the first systematic investigations in Vietnam to assess the impact of traumatic injuries on work productivity after hospital discharge, utilizing an internationally validated instrument (WPAI). By evaluating absenteeism and presenteeism alongside physical health status, psychological resilience, and injury characteristics, the study provides insight into the multifactorial nature of post-injury work impairment. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as this study was based on a secondary analysis of an existing dataset, the scope of the analysis was restricted to the variables collected in the original survey. Consequently, potentially relevant factors such as rehabilitation utilization, occupational characteristics, or workplace conditions could not be examined. In addition, patients with severe injuries who were unable to complete the baseline survey were excluded, which may introduce selection bias and potentially underestimate the true impact of injuries on work productivity. Second, the use of a convenience sampling strategy and the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of injury survivors in Vietnam49. Third, the productivity outcomes of absenteeism and presenteeism showed substantial overlap, reflecting the complex nature of work impairment following injury. Fourth, missing data were handled using listwise deletion, which may introduce bias if the missing data were not completely at random. In addition, stepwise variable selection was applied to identify parsimonious models, and the results should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Finally, productivity costs were estimated using the regional minimum wage as a proxy for individual earnings. While this approach provides a standardized estimate across participants, it may not fully capture variation in actual income levels and could lead to either underestimation or overestimation of the true productivity loss.

The findings highlight the urgent need for the development of comprehensive rehabilitation programs that address not only physical restoration but also psychological and social reintegration, aiming to optimize work re-entry and long-term functional outcomes. Future research should expand to nationally representative samples, adopt longitudinal designs, and incorporate objective assessments of work productivity to generate more robust evidence to inform health and social policy development.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that traumatic injuries exert a profound and sustained impact on work productivity among patients after hospital discharge in Vietnam. The elevated prevalence of both absenteeism and presenteeism, along with the substantial associated economic costs, reflects not only the clinical burden of injury but also broader systemic gaps in post-acute care. These results point to the critical need for a comprehensive rehabilitation strategy that addresses physical recovery, psychological resilience, and structured pathways to occupational reintegration. Strengthening such integrative post-injury care is essential not only to enhance individual outcomes but also to protect long-term economic and social stability in low- and middle-income settings.
