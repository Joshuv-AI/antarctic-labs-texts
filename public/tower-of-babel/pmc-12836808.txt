Evaluation of clinicians’ use of fast-curing and budget light-curing units in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Eman H Ismail, Afnan O Al-Zain, Awwadh Alzahrani, Omar Alghamdi, Faisal Martaa, Salem Baghdadi, Yousef Eshmawi, Abdulrahman A Balhaddad, Richard B Price
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLight-curing units (LCUs) are essential for polymerizing resin-based composites, and a lack of understanding will likely compromise the quality of the final restoration. The understanding and clinical application of LCU features—including the use of fast-curing modes and budget LCUs—require investigation. This study assesses the knowledge and practice patterns regarding LCU features among dental professionals.MethodsA self-administered questionnaire was distributed among specialized dentists in restorative dentistry, other dental specialists, general practitioners, and students/interns. The survey assessed participants’ knowledge and practices regarding LCU ergonomics, curing modes, use of budget LCUs, and their potential impact on restoration outcomes. It consisted of 23 main questions, in addition to 7 demographic and 4 eligibility questions. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Scheffé tests to compare knowledge and practice scores across groups (α = 0.05).ResultsA total of 338 questionnaires were completed (80.5% response rate). Specialized restorative dentists had significantly higher LCU knowledge scores (mean=64.31) compared to other specialists (48.84) and students/interns (53.27) (p<0.001). Clinical practice scores followed a similar trend (p=0.007). Notably, 33.4% of respondents were unsure if they were using a budget LCU, and 59.6% did not check the LCU irradiance of the lights used in their clinic. Only 4.2% measured the irradiance from the LCU daily.ConclusionSignificant gaps exist in the knowledge and practice about LCUs, particularly among non-restorative specialists and students. The widespread lack of awareness regarding budget LCUs and irradiance verification highlights a need for standardized education on LCU selection, curing modes, and routine output testing in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08469-w.Keywords: Light-curing unit features, Curing modes, Budget light cure, Dental devices, Curing features, Fast curing mode, High irradiance mode

Introduction

The light-curing unit (LCU) has become an indispensable tool in modern dentistry. It is widely utilized across various specialties to photo-activate resin-based composites (RBC), bonding agents, and resin-modified glass-ionomer cements [1–3]. The quality of light curing can significantly affect the properties of light-activated restorations, and insufficient polymerization can lead to increased water sorption and reduced mechanical properties [1–4]. This ultimately results in more microleakage, secondary caries, and postoperative sensitivity, which all eventually lead to the failure of the RBC restoration [1–3, 5]. Despite the LCUs’ routine use and the information provided to dentists on this topic [1–3, 5, 6], significant disparities exist in clinicians’ knowledge and understanding of its features. These deficiencies may compromise treatment outcomes [7–13].

The selection and operation of an LCU require the dentist to consider several factors [1–3, 5, 6, 14] including whether the LCU has been certified for use [6, 14–16]. Additionally, understanding the emission spectrum, ergonomics, light-guide tip diameter, power output, irradiance, and curing modes is crucial [1–3, 6, 17]. Factors such as the effect of distance on light output, battery life, and the unit’s durability also play significant roles in its overall functionality [1–3, 6, 17–20]. Variations in ergonomics, (i.e. gun-style and pen-style), which offer different capabilities and limitations that clinicians need to recognize; for instance, gun-style units might restrict access in difficult-to-reach areas due to the larger angle of the light-guide tip, whereas pen-style units can allow for more precise positioning [2, 3].

Three main light-curing modes are commonly used in dental practice. Continuous mode delivers a constant light intensity throughout the curing process, ensuring consistent polymerization but increasing heat generation and polymerization shrinkage stress [2, 3]. Soft-start mode begins with a lower light intensity that gradually increases, allowing initial stress relaxation in the resin and reducing polymerization shrinkage stress [2, 3]. Fast high-irradiance (1–3–5 s) mode delivers extremely high light output for very short exposure times, offering time efficiency but potentially compromising depth of cure and mechanical properties if the irradiance or exposure time is not properly optimized [21, 22]. Different curing modes are claimed to influence the polymerization kinetics and shrinkage stress. However, there is lack of sufficient evidence that the soft-start curing modes show any improvement in clinical outcomes when compared to continuous exposure modes [23, 24]. The fast high-irradiance exposure modes—such as 1, 3, and 5 s—may deliver insufficient radiant exposure, potentially compromising the physicochemical properties of some resin-based composites when compared to conventional exposure times of 10 to 20 s [25, 26]. Additionally, these effects are RBC-dependent, and only a few RBC manufacturers recommend a 3-second exposure, and none recommend a 1-second exposure [22, 27–33]. Consequently, while fast curing offers time efficiency, it may compromise the integrity and performance of the RBCs. Therefore, it is recommended that clinicians not use fast high-irradiance curing modes off-label and instead use RBCs designed for this protocol, following the RBC manufacturer’s instructions to avoid undesirable clinical outcomes [27].

While premium LCUs undergo rigorous testing, budget LCUs are increasingly being used by dentists due to cost advantages. However, there are concerns about inconsistent light output, premature LED degradation, and a lack of standardized testing, all of which can lead to issues such as inadequate thermal dissipation and non-uniform beam profiles [6, 34, 35]. Additionally, studies have highlighted significant gaps in clinicians’ knowledge regarding the use, maintenance, and safety of budget LCUs [8, 9, 11, 36], indicating that many are unaware of the potential risks associated with these devices and lack familiarity with the features provided by LCU manufacturers.

In Saudi Arabia, restorative dentistry is a recognized specialty by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties. Currently, LCU training is largely concentrated within restorative and operative dentistry programs, as if the topic pertains solely to these fields. However, LCUs are routinely used across all dental specialties including prosthodontics, endodontics, orthodontics, and pediatric dentistry. While previous studies have examined general practitioners’ knowledge of LCUs [7–13, 37, 38], none, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, have compared the understanding and use of budget LCUs, fast-curing modes, and other LCU features across various dental specialties, general dentists, interns, and students. Identifying knowledge gaps among these groups is critical from both clinical and educational perspectives.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate knowledge and practice patterns related to LCU features—specifically fast-curing modes and budget LCUs—by comparing restorative specialists with other dental professionals and trainee. The null hypothesis is that there will be no significant differences in knowledge or practice regarding LCU features among specialized restorative dentists compared to other dental specialties, general practitioners, and students/interns.

Methodology

Ethical approval was obtained by King Abdulaziz University (IRB #135-07-23). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants in the study. Participants were fully informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. This was stated in the consent form provided before participation. The survey language was in English and survey questions were adapted and modified from previous studies [8, 11–13, 37].

Content validation was performed by consulting four experts in the field, leading to further modifications. Face validation was conducted by pretesting the questionnaire with 10 volunteers from the target population to ensure clarity, comprehension, usability of questions and readiness. This stage was not intended to statistically test reliability but to refine the questionnaire’s wording and format based on participant feedback before large-scale data collection.

The inclusion criteria for the survey were dentists who use light cure units in their practices, focusing mainly on specialized restorative dentists, endodontists, orthodontists, pedodontists, prosthodontists, general practitioners, dental interns, and dental students in Saudi Arabia. Exclusion criteria included those who were not members of the above group. The questionnaire included a total of 35 questions in the following domains: consent and eligibility questions (4 questions), demographic information (7 questions), and questions regarding dental practice (23 questions) covering ergonomic preferences, using curing mode including soft-start and fast curing mode, LCU selection, budget LCU use and awareness, and light output measurement practices, and an open-ended question (one question) for any additional comments (Supplementary Material 1). The survey was administered online using a snowball sampling approach via SurveyMonkey (www.momentive.ai, Momentive Inc., San Mateo, California, USA).

Sample size calculation

Sample size calculation was carried out using STATA software, version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Assuming a 5% level of significance (α = 0.05), 80% power (1- β), number of groups = 3, and a small effect size (f = 0.01) according to Cohen’s convention [39], the estimated sample size for one-way ANOVA for group effect was 70 per group and a total of 210 for all three study groups. To compensate for non-response and non-completion rate, we doubled the sample size to 420.

An a priori sample size calculation for a one-way ANOVA (group effect) was performed using Stata software, version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Assuming a 5% significance level (α = 0.05), 80% power (1 − β = 0.80), three groups, a standardized within-group variance of 1, and a between-group variance of 0.047, the corresponding effect size was Cohen’s f = 0.22 (small-to-medium). Under these assumptions, the required sample size was estimated to be 70 participants per group (total N = 210). To account for potential non-response and incomplete data, the target sample size was increased to N = 420.

Statistical analyses

The sample characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics to describe the categorical and quantitative variables. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for the categorical variables (gender, current place of employment, specialty, and dental ranking). The data was not normally distributed (p > 0.05) for the continuous variable (age, years of experience); therefore, the medians and interquartile range (IRQ) were calculated. The study groups were categorized by specialty and divided into three groups: restorative dentistry, other specialties (encompassing all other specialties, including general dentists and recently graduated dentists who were not yet employed), and students/interns (comprising undergraduate students and interns).

The knowledge and clinical practice domains were analyzed. Regarding knowledge questions (8 questions), the correct answer for each question was given a score of 1; otherwise, a score of 0 was given. The total score for LCU knowledge was calculated by summing the scores for the knowledge questions. This score is divided by the theoretical maximum point [8] and multiplied by 100. The total knowledge score ranged from 0 (the minimum score) to 100 (the maximum score). Regarding the scoring of the practice questions (7 questions), the total score for LCU practice was calculated by summing the scores for each question. This score is divided by the theoretical maximum point [7] and multiplied by 100. The total practice score ranged from 0 (the minimum score) to 100 (the maximum score). The data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk W test for the LCU knowledge and practice total score. Statistical comparisons between groups (specialized restorative dentists, other specialties, students/interns) were performed using one-way ANOVA. This was followed up with a post hoc Scheffé test for multiple comparisons.

Discussion

This study shows that there are significant disparities in the knowledge and current practices regarding LCUs among dental professionals belonging to different specialties, students, and interns compared to specialized restorative dentist. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected. This highlights the need for more effective educational initiatives.

Specialized restorative dentists demonstrated higher levels of knowledge and more proficient practices compared to practitioners in other specialties and students/interns. Although the use of LCUs is common across dental disciplines, understanding their optimal use is not uniformly distributed among all specialists. The comparable levels of knowledge observed between students and non-restorative specialists suggest that these practitioners may not have received additional training beyond their undergraduate education. It has been reported that undergraduate students typically receive only one to two lectures on this topic during their dental education [40, 41], which is insufficient to support the advanced knowledge and clinical proficiency expected at the postgraduate level.

Furthermore, specialty training significantly enhances dentists’ clinical judgment, diagnostic skills, and technical proficiency. Compared with undergraduates and general practitioners, specialists demonstrate superior decision-making in caries management, endodontic and prosthodontic procedures, and complex diagnostics through the use of structured assessment methods, advanced techniques, and specialized interventions [42–45]. Therefore, postgraduate students’ knowledge LCUs should be more advanced, reflecting a higher level of understanding consistent with the depth of expertise expected within other domains of postgraduate dental disciplines.

Some LCU features, such as ergonomics, light-guide tip diameter, various curing modes, and the use of budget LCUs, can affect the quality of restoration polymerization, highlighting the complexity involved in effectively selecting and utilizing these devices [2, 3]. The significantly higher scores in knowledge and current practice of specialized restorative dentists compared to other specialties and students/interns can be explained by the additional information and lectures that specialized restorative dentists receive about photocuring as part of their graduate programs. Notably, 78% of specialized dentists recognized the influence of LCU ergonomics on treatment outcomes, aligning with previous studies that emphasize its importance as a core element of clinical competence [1]. However, awareness of the effect of protective barriers on irradiance was considerably lower, only 50% of restorative specialists identified this factor correctly, showing a statistically significant gap compared with other groups. Previous studies have shown that infection control barriers can affect the light output of LCUs and should be wrinkle-free to minimize the reduction in irradiance [2, 3, 46, 47]. This finding underscores a broader gap in clinicians’ understanding of equipment functionality and its direct impact on restorative outcomes [1–3].

Previous studies have highlighted a significant educational gap in understanding LCUs, underscoring the need for better instructional resources in the undergraduate dental curricula [13, 40, 41]. One study found that general practitioners and specialists lacked sufficient knowledge of basic LCU concepts, though it primarily focused on terminologies and parameters [8]. Notably, specialized restorative dentists comprised 34% of the specialist group, which is higher than the percentage of specialized restorative dentists in the present study (24.56%) [8]. Another study focusing solely on general practitioners revealed a lack of standardized protocols for light-curing [37]. These findings reinforce the importance of integrating comprehensive LCU education into dental training to better equip graduates for the job market and ensure they provide the highest standard of patient care.

Regarding LCU curing modes, an interesting finding is that 38.9% of participants use the soft-start mode, while 10.1% are unaware of the mode they used on their patients. Additionally, 81.9% reported using the continuous curing mode, primarily because it is the recommended setting for the specific restoration they are working with. Another common reason cited was that the continuous mode is the default setting on their LCUs. Notably, 16.3% of participants were unsure why they chose this particular mode. The absence of significant differences among groups in their reasons for selecting a curing mode suggests a limited understanding of how these modes influence polymerization outcomes. This aligns with previous evidence showing that continuous mode provides consistent polymerization but may increase heat generation and shrinkage stress, while soft-start and fast high-irradiance modes balance efficiency and stress reduction differently [2, 3, 21, 22]. These findings indicate that many practitioners may rely on default settings rather than informed decision-making.

A positive finding is that 73.6% of respondents adhere to the RBC manufacturer’s recommended exposure time rather than relying on the time suggested by the manufacturer of the LCU. This reflects a cautious and informed approach and indicates a preference for following material-specific guidelines. Additionally, there may be scepticism about fast-curing modes, especially among clinicians who have experienced issues with inadequate curing. A study emphasized that ultra-short curing times, such as 3 s, are only effective for certain materials, and using them “off-label” can compromise the material’s properties [48].

Despite the growing popularity of budget LCUs, they present significant challenges, such as inconsistent light output and reduced durability, as highlighted in previous studies [34, 35]. It is concerning that 30% of participants were unsure if they were using budget LCUs, while 16.7% cited lower costs as the reason for their use. Clinicians should be able to distinguish between budget and premium LCUs by reviewing the manuals and understanding the differences between manufacturers [14, 16]. Specialized restorative dentists are less likely to use budget LCUs compared to students, interns, and other specialties, likely due to their better understanding of LCU features and the importance of consistent curing for optimal outcomes. In contrast, students and interns often budget for LCUs, possibly because they lack exposure to high-quality devices, which limits their mastery of advanced curing techniques. Additionally, around 30% of respondents were unaware they were using budget LCUs or of the potential limitations, reflecting a knowledge gap that could reduce the success of their restorations.

Thus, the observed lack of awareness underscores broader gaps in knowledge and clinical practices related to budget LCUs, which is a critical concern given their unstable irradiance output with battery discharge, a factor that can significantly compromise polymerization and restoration longevity [6, 17]. Unlike premium devices, budget LCUs can lack built-in output stabilization, which makes their performance unpredictable and clinically unreliable [6, 17]. Therefore, routine irradiance checks using radiometers should be emphasized as a standard clinical practice to detect output decline, adjust curing protocols, and ensure adequate polymerization for durable restorative outcomes.

The higher knowledge scores among specialized restorative dentists can be attributed to their frequent use of LCUs in daily practice, where precise photocuring is essential for optimal restorations. Restorative dentists likely review more literature on LCUs than other disciplines, as reflected in their superior performance on questions related to exposure time and irradiance levels.

To enhance clinical outcomes, the findings underscore the need for standardized and evidence-based education on LCU selection, curing modes, and irradiance verification in both undergraduate and postgraduate curricula. Structured training, simulation-based learning, and routine irradiance checks should be emphasized to ensure consistent polymerization and minimize restoration failures. Incorporating such educational strategies will strengthen clinicians’ technical competence and promote reliable, long-lasting restorative outcomes.

Incorporating hands-on educational sessions using advanced training tools like the Managing Accurate Resin Curing-patient simulator (MARC-PS) (BlueLight Analytics, Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada) improves LCU skills and knowledge. The MARC system provides real-time feedback on light-curing techniques, helping clinicians understand the effects of curing distance, angulation, and exposure time on polymerization quality. Additionally, using the MARC simulator has been shown to increase the confidence of both undergraduate and graduate students in performing light-curing procedures [40, 41, 49–52]. Integrating such simulation-based training into dental curricula can standardize LCU education, enhance proficiency, and boost clinician confidence, ultimately leading to better restorative outcomes.

Several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design captures data only from Saudi Arabia at a single point in time, limiting the ability to assess temporal changes and generalizability. The use of self-reported data may introduce response and recall biases, and the high response rate could reflect sampling bias, as more knowledgeable clinicians may have been overrepresented. Additionally, combining restorative and non-restorative specialties into broad comparison groups may have increased heterogeneity among respondents. Furthermore, reliance on self-administered questionnaires rather than direct clinical evaluations may not fully capture real-world practices. Future studies should include longitudinal follow-up, direct clinical assessments, and multi-site sampling to enhance the validity and generalizability of the findings.

The questionnaire was designed to assess factual knowledge and standard practices related to light curing rather than to measure a latent construct such as attitudes or perceptions. Consequently, internal consistency measures (e.g., Cronbach’s alpha) were not applicable, as individual items were intentionally heterogeneous and not expected to correlate. The summed scores were used as a practical approach to quantify overall knowledge and practice for statistical comparison.

Conclusion

This study revealed notable gaps in knowledge and practice related to LCUs across dental specialties. Restorative specialists demonstrated superior understanding of curing modes, maintenance, and the risks of using budget LCUs compared with other practitioners. These findings underscore the need for standardized, evidence-based educational initiatives across all levels of dental training to improve LCU proficiency. Strengthening clinical education on light-curing principles will enhance restoration quality and promote best practices in patient care.
