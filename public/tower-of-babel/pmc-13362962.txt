Adipose-derived stem cell peptide 5 alleviates hypertrophic scarring through targeting pyruvate carboxylase or p50 to coordinate PI3K/AKT/mTOR-autophagy and NF-κB/IL-6 signaling
Authors: Ling Chen, Enyuan Zhang, Siqi Zeng, Qiyue Yan, Jingyun Li, Qian Li, Jun Li, Zezhang Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertrophic scars are a major clinical challenge with limited treatments. Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) play an important role in inhibiting pathological scar formation. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, we aimed to investigate the function, mechanism, and therapeutic potential of adipose-derived stem cell peptide 5 (ADSCP5), a novel peptide from adipose-derived stem cell-conditioned medium.MethodsWe used in vitro hypertrophic scar fibroblast, macrophage–fibroblast coculture and human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) assays, RNA sequencing, pathway analysis, peptide pull-down, molecular docking, cellular thermal shift assays, surface plasmon resonance, immunofluorescence colocalization analysis, western blotting, rescue experiments, reactive oxygen species (ROS), autophagy tests, and mitochondrial membrane potential assays and untargeted metabolomics. The efficacy of ADSCP5 was validated in rabbit and porcine scar models.ResultsIn hypertrophic scar fibroblasts, ADSCP5 significantly downregulated the expression of collagen genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A1) and actin alpha 2, smooth muscle, without affecting cell proliferation, apoptosis, or migration. Transcriptomic, enrichment, and western blot analyses confirmed that ADSCP5 reduced the protein levels of phosphorylated p65 (p-p65, NF-κB subunit), p-PI3K, p-AKT, and p-mTOR. Moreover, ADSCP5 decreased IL-6 transcription. Mechanistically, ADSCP5 bound directly to pyruvate carboxylase or the NF-κB subunit p50. This interaction resulted in the downregulation of PC or the upregulation of p50, ultimately inhibiting collagen expression, a finding confirmed by rescue assays. Furthermore, ADSCP5 induced ROS generation and autophagy, altered global metabolism, and modulated macrophage–fibroblast crosstalk to suppress fibrosis. It also exhibited antiangiogenic effects in HUVECs. In both the rabbit and porcine scar models, ADSCP5 treatment effectively attenuated collagen deposition and scar hyperplasia, increased the number of macrophages (increased CD68), reduced angiogenesis (decreased VEGFA and CD34), and promoted autophagy (reduced p62).ConclusionsOverall, this study demonstrate that ADSCP5 alleviates hypertrophic scarring by directly binding to PC and p50, suppressing the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and NF-κB pathways, reducing IL-6 and collagen production, promoting ROS and autophagy, and modulating metabolic, macrophage and angiogenic responses. These findings position ADSCP5 as a promising therapeutic agent for hypertrophic scar prevention and treatment.Keywords: Hypertrophic scar, ADSCP5, Fibroblasts, pyruvate carboxylase, p50, Scar treatment, Adipose-derived stem cells

Background

Hypertrophic scarring is a common pathological phenomenon during wound healing that significantly impairs functional recovery and quality of life [1]. Although various therapeutic approaches exist, their efficacy remains unsatisfactory [1]. Hypertrophic scarring is characterized primarily by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix in the dermis and is closely associated with postinjury tissue repair mechanisms involving inflammatory responses, fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling [2]. Enhanced expression of collagen type I (COL1A1 and COL1A2), collagen type III (COL3A1), and the myofibroblast marker gene ACTA2 (actin alpha 2 and smooth muscle) are hallmark features of hypertrophic scars [3]. Various signaling pathways, including TGF-β/SMAD, NF-κB/IL-6, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR, have been reported to be involved in the regulation of hypertrophic scars [1, 4]. Despite substantial progress in elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of hypertrophic scars, the pursuit of an ideal therapy continues to represent a critical and unmet challenge.

In recent years, adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs) have garnered considerable attention due to their paracrine functions in tissue repair and regeneration [5–7]. The ADSC secretome facilitates wound healing through several mechanisms: modulating macrophage phenotypic switching to influence the inflammatory phase; promoting angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell differentiation and migration; and mediating granulation tissue formation, skin cell regeneration, and extracellular matrix synthesis to advance the proliferative and remodeling phases [8]. However, the specific mechanisms underlying their role in suppressing hypertrophic scarring remain incompletely elucidated.

Peptide-based therapeutics hold great promise in disease treatment owing to their low molecular weight, high specificity, and low toxicity [9]. Multiple peptides inhibit scarring through diverse pathways [10]. Pro-healing peptide- and peptide-based interventions against skin wound healing have been extensively studied [11–15]. Our previous work demonstrated that the ADSC-derived peptide ADSCP2 inhibits hypertrophic scarring [16]. In the current study, we screened another functional peptide named adipose-derived stem cell peptide 5 (ADSCP5). Cellular functional assays demonstrated that ADSCP5 significantly suppressed the protein expression of type I collagen (COL1A1 and COL1A2) and the myofibroblast marker ACTA2 without significantly affecting cell proliferation, apoptosis, or migration. In rabbit and porcine scar models, ADSCP5 alleviated collagen deposition and hypertrophic scarring. Mechanistic analysis revealed that ADSCP5 alleviated hypertrophic scarring through targeting pyruvate carboxylase (PC) or p50 to coordinate PI3K/AKT/mTOR-autophagy and NF-κB/IL-6 signaling. Overall, this study provides new theoretical insights and potential therapeutic agents for the prevention and treatment of pathological scarring.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Women’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing Women and Children’s Healthcare Hospital) (2022KY-162). Human hypertrophic scar tissues were obtained with written informed consent from patients undergoing surgery.

Identification, synthesis, and preparation of peptides

Our previous work identified a total of 1108 peptides in ADSC-CM obtained at 48 h [16]. Based on the mass spectrometry results, we selected ADSCP5 because of its conservation and potential functional roles. Peptides (purity >98%), including ADSCP5 (sequence: ADKPDMGEIASFDKAKLKKTETQEKN) and its scrambled control (Sc, sequence: AEKAKDQKFDKEDNGITTLKMSKPEA, which shares the same amino acid composition as ADSCP5 but in a random order, controlling for nonsequence-specific effects), were synthesized by Shanghai Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd and dissolved in sterile distilled water (15230162, Thermo Fisher Scientific). A biotin-labeled version was used for pull-down assays. FITC-labeled ADSCP5 was used for location analysis.

1.1. Cell culture and treatment

Primary human hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HSFs) [16] at passages 3–7 were maintained in Fibroblast Medium (2301, ScienCell). Cells were treated with ADSCP5 or Sc (25 μM) for 24–48 h. For rescue experiments, an NF-κB inhibitor (10 nM; BAY 11–7082, MCE, USA) or an AKT activator (SC79, 40 μM, prepared in DMSO; T2274, TargetMOI, USA) was applied with or without ADSCP5 for 36 h. The cells were pretreated with the ROS scavenger NAC (N-acetylcysteine; HY-B0215, MedChemExpress) at 1 mM (dissolved in DMSO) for 2 h with or without ADSCP5 for another 36 h.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was extracted (RC112–1, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA was synthesized for qPCR (Q711–02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on an ABI Vii7 system. The gene expression levels of human COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, and ACTA2 were normalized to that of GAPDH using primers from previous work [16]. To analyze macrophage activation-related markers, quantitative PCR was carried out to assess the expression levels of mouse CD86, TNF-α, IL-6, CD204, Arg-1, iNOS, CD80, CD206, IL-1, IL-4, and IL-10. The sequences of primers used are listed in Table 1, with mouse Hprt employed as the endogenous control for normalization.

Cell viability analysis

Cell viability was measured by a cell counting kit-8 assay (CCK-8; A311–01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Fibroblasts (2000 cells/well) were treated with 25 μM ADSCP5, Sc, or vehicle control in sextuplicate. After treatment, the cells were incubated with CCK-8 for 1 h at 37°C, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, California, USA).

Cell apoptosis analysis

After Annexin V-FITC/PI staining, apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry (A211–01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Following a 24-h treatment with 25 μM Sc, ADSCP5, or the control (n = 3), the cells were processed by flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA), and 10 000 events per sample were analyzed using Kaluza software.

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was evaluated using a scratch assay. A wound was introduced into a confluent fibroblast monolayer, followed by treatment with 25 μM Sc, ADSCP5, or the control (n = 3) in serum-free medium. The wound area was imaged at the indicated time points and quantified by measuring the gap width with ImageJ.

Transwell migration assay

A Transwell migration assay was performed by seeding treated, serum-starved cells (Control, 25 μM Sc, or ADSCP5) in the upper chamber, with 10% FBS medium as a chemoattractant below. Following incubation (24/48 h), the migrated cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and quantified by imaging and Optical density (OD) measurement at 590 nm after 33% acetic acid lysis.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Transcriptome profiling was performed on hypertrophic scar fibroblasts treated in triplicate with 25 μM Sc or ADSCP5. Total RNA was extracted and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150) by Shanghai Bio Corporation. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a threshold of fold change ≥1.5 or ≤ 0.67 and P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway assessments.

RNA from porcine scar tissues harvested from control or ADSCP5-treated wounds on Day 42 was similarly extracted and sequenced. DEGs were determined using the same criteria, followed by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. The top 30 enriched KEGG pathways were ranked by Q value (Fisher’s exact test). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out using BROAD GSEA software with default parameters (1000 permutations).

Pulldown assay and silver staining

Biotin-labeled ADSCP5 (50 μg) was bound to Dynabeads™ M-280 Streptavidin (11205D, Invitrogen) and incubated with 2 mg of protein lysate from hypertrophic scar fibroblasts. A biotin-labeled scrambled peptide served as the negative control. After being washed, the bound proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and visualized using a Pierce™ silver stain kit (24600, Thermo Fisher). Differential bands were excised for mass spectrometry identification.

Surface plasmon resonance

The binding affinity of ADSCP5 to PC or p50 was assessed using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on an OpenSPR system (Nicoya Lifesciences Inc.). Recombinant human PC or p50 protein was initially expressed in a prokaryotic system (Escherichia coli) by Genecreate. The PC or p50 protein (20 μg/ml) was then immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva). Subsequently, graded concentrations of ADSCP5 or p50 were sequentially delivered across the chip surface. The interaction analysis was carried out under the following conditions: an association phase of 120 s, a dissociation phase of 300 s, and a maintained flow rate of 30 βl/min throughout the run..

Immunofluorescence staining

Hypertrophic scar fibroblasts were incubated with FITC-conjugated ADSCP5. Following fixation with formaldehyde, the expression of PC or p50 was detected via immunofluorescence staining. Cells were probed with a primary antibody against PC (16588–1-AP, Proteintech) or p50 (13586, CST) and subsequently with a goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (mp02764, Invitrogen). Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (mp01306, Invitrogen), and images were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS STED microscope.

Cellular thermal shift assay

After being incubated with ADSCP5 or the control for 2 h, cells were heated to various temperatures (37°C, 41°C, 45°C, 48°C, and 51°C). Cell lysates were then prepared by three freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen, followed by centrifugation. The pellets were boiled in loading buffer and subjected to western blot analysis.

Preparation of conditioned medium from ADSCP5-treated macrophages and coculture with hypertrophic scar fibroblasts

RAW264.7 macrophages (Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) at passages 3–7 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. At 60% confluence, the cells were treated with 25 μM ADSCP5 or Sc or control for 24 h. Conditioned medium (CM) was collected, centrifuged at 5000 × g (10 min, 4°C), and applied to HSFs to evaluate its effects on collagen expression.

1.2. Tube formation assay

human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) at passages 3–7 were plated at 1 × 104 cells/well on Matrigel (356234, Corning)-coated plates. Following incubation, capillary-like structures were imaged (20×) between 6 and 18 h. Tube formation was quantified (length, area, loops, and nodes) in three random fields per well using ImageJ.

ROS detection

ROS levels were assessed in treated cells using a specific fluorescent probe. Cells were stained with 10 μM probe for 30 min at 37°C in the dark, and fluorescence was measured microscopically and quantified with ImageJ. The mean fluorescence intensity per cell from 5 random fields and three independent experiments is shown.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assay

The mitochondrial membrane potential in hypertrophic scar fibroblasts treated with 25 μM Sc, ADSCP5, or control for 36 h was evaluated using a JC-1 assay kit (C2006, Beyotime, China). Cells were stained according to the manufacturers’ instructions and analyzed by confocal microscopy (Leica STELLARIS STED) and flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter). JC-1 aggregates (red, normal) and monomers (green, depolarized) were detected. The mean fluorescence intensity per cell from five random fields and three independent experiments is shown.

Autophagic flux analysis

Autophagic flux was analyzed in fibroblasts transfected with the mCherry-EGFP-LC3B reporter plasmid. Following 24 h of treatment with the control, Sc, or ADSCP5, cells were imaged by confocal microscopy, with yellow (mCherry+/EGFP+) and red (mCherry+/EGFP−) puncta representing autophagosomes and autolysosomes, respectively. Autophagic activity was evaluated by quantifying its ratio. The mean fluorescence intensity per cell from 5 random fields and three independent experiments is shown.

Plasmid and siRNA transfections

As previously reported [16], we utilized the same PC and vehicle control plasmids. Transfected complexes containing ~1 μg of plasmid and 2 μl of Lipo3000 were introduced into each well of 6-well plates in medium supplemented with either 25 μM scrambled control peptide or ADSCP5. Protein was collected 48 h later for evaluation.

The p50-targeting siRNA (si-p50) and its negative control were designed by RiboBio, with the si-p50 sequence being 5’-GTC ACT CTA ACG TAT GCA A-3′. siRNA transfection was performed using a riboFECT CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Untargeted metabolomics

Hypertrophic scar fibroblasts treated with 25 μM Sc or ADSCP5 for 36 h were subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling (BGI, China) using LC–MS/MS (Q Exactive, Thermo Fisher) in both positive and negative ion modes. The data were processed with Compound Discoverer 3.1 and analyzed using metaX. Differentially abundant metabolites were filtered by fold change (fold change >1.2 or <0.83), VIP (variable importance in the projection) ≥1, and P < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed based on the KEGG results, with P < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Establishment of a hypertrophic scar model in rabbit ears

All procedures were approved by the Experimental Animal Care and Use Committee (FJABR2022062201) and complied with NIH guidelines. Six male New Zealand white rabbits weighing 2.5 kg underwent creation of six 1-cm diameter full-thickness skin defects per ear under anesthesia with isoflurane gas. From Day 14, the wounds were subcutaneously injected with Sc, ADSCP5 (200 μM, 100 μl), or the blank control every 7 days (n = 4 total). On Day 42, scar tissues were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining after euthanasia.

Establishment of a dorsal scar model in Bama mini-pigs

Following ethical approval (FJABR2022122901), a dorsal scar model was established in five female Bama mini-pigs weighing 14 kg by creating six 3 × 3 cm full-thickness skin wounds on each side under anesthesia with intramuscular injection of tiletamine–zolazepam (Telazol) at a dose of 4 mg/kg into the gluteal muscle. Beginning on Day 14, the wounds were subcutaneously injected with ADSCP5 (200 μM) or the control treatment every 7 days. Scar tissues harvested on Day 42 were analyzed by HE, Masson’s trichrome, and Sirius red staining.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) via microwave irradiation. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity and nonspecific sites, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against p62 (1:500, Servicebio, GB11531), VEGFA (1:500, Servicebio, GB15165), CD34 (1:500, Servicebio, GB15013), CD68 (1:400, Servicebio, GB113109), or CD206 (1:500, Servicebio, GB153497). Detection was performed with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and diaminobenzidine (DAB) development, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, and mounting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent biological replicates for in vitro experiments, while the sample sizes for in vivo studies are indicated in the respective figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t test was employed. For experiments involving a single variable with more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. For experiments involving two independent factors (treatment and time/temperature), two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied to evaluate the main effects and their interactions. A P value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

As we previously reported [16], 25 μM ADSCP5 was added to the culture medium of human primary hypertrophic scar fibroblasts. For consistency and comparability, we directly adopted the same concentration of 25 μM in the present study. The results of the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that ADSCP5 did not significantly affect the proliferative activity of human primary hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (Figure 1d). The qPCR and western blot analyses revealed that ADSCP5 significantly inhibited the mRNA and protein expression levels of type I and III collagen, as well as the myofibroblast marker ACTA2, in human primary hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (Figure 1e, 1f). The results of apoptosis assays demonstrated that ADSCP5 had a minimal effect on apoptosis (Figure 1g). Scratch wound and Transwell assays were performed to evaluate the effect of ADSCP5 on cellular migration. The results demonstrated that ADSCP5 treatment did not significantly affect the migratory capacity of hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (Figure 1h, Supplemental Figure 1).

ADSCP5 regulated NF-κB and PI3K/AKT signaling

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms underlying the effects of ADSCP5, we performed RNA sequencing on hypertrophic scar fibroblasts treated with either the control Sc peptide or ADSCP5 (Figure 2a). Transcriptomic analysis revealed differential gene expression patterns, as shown in the volcano plot (Figure 2b). Compared with the control peptide, ADSCP5 treatment significantly upregulated the expression of 240 genes (fold change ≥1.50, P < 0.05) and downregulated the expression of 243 genes (fold change ≤0.67, P < 0.05) (Figure 2b). KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of these 483 differentially expressed genes revealed significant associations with the NF-κB signaling pathway (Figure 2c). Further GSEA revealed that interferon-alpha, inflammatory response, cholesterol homeostasis, and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling were enriched (Figure 2d). We then investigated the effects of ADSCP5 on the levels of p-p65, p65, p-PI3K, PI3K, p-AKT, AKT, p-mTOR, and mTOR in hypertrophic scar fibroblasts using western blotting. The results indicated that ADSCP5 decreased the protein levels of p-p65, p-PI3K, p-AKT and p-mTOR (Figure 2e, 2f). As a multifunctional cytokine, IL-6 is involved in various physiological processes, including inflammatory responses, immune regulation, and tissue repair. IL-6/p-STAT3 signaling has been shown to increase fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix production, underscoring its critical role in scar formation [18]. IL-6 is also downstream of NF-κB and promotes scar hyperplasia [19]. We further examined changes in IL-6 expression in hypertrophic scar fibroblasts following ADSCP5 treatment. Treatment with 25 μM ADSCP5 for 48 h significantly reduced the transcription of IL-6 (Figure 2g). These findings suggest that the mechanisms of ADSCP5 involve multiple signaling pathways, including the NF-κB/IL-6 and PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways.

ADSCP5 bound to the PC and p50 proteins, downregulating AKT and upregulating p50 protein expression

To identify potential protein interactors of ADSCP5, we performed peptide pull-down assays followed by silver staining (Supplemental Figure 2a). The differentially stained protein bands were excised and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis. Mass spectrometry identified 73 differentially enriched proteins in the ADSCP5 group (the top 20 proteins are listed in Supplemental Figure 2b). Enrichment analysis of the mass spectrometry results revealed that the identified ADSCP5-binding proteins were primarily involved in biological processes, including the endocrine system, immune system, and signal transduction (Supplemental Figure 2c).

Among these identified proteins, the PC protein was previously recognized as an ADSCP2-binding partner in our earlier studies [16] and thus was selected as our primary target for further analysis. Concurrently, the p50 protein was also prioritized for investigation due to its established association with the NF-κB pathway and its molecular weight corresponding to one of the differentially stained bands. Molecular docking analysis was subsequently performed for both proteins (Figure 3a). Using the HDOCK SERVER (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/), we obtained docking scores of −167.897 for the ADSCP5–PC complex and − 146.863 for the ADSCP5–p50 complex (Figure 3a), suggesting potential binding interactions between ADSCP5 and both the PC and p50 proteins.

We then performed western blot analysis to examine changes in PC and p50 protein expression following ADSCP5 treatment. The results demonstrated that compared with the control treatment, ADSCP5 treatment significantly downregulated PC but markedly upregulated p50 expression (Figure 3b). Furthermore, CETSA and SPR confirmed direct binding interactions between ADSCP5 and both the PC and p50 proteins (Figure 3c, 3d). After treatment with ADSCP5, the ratio of red/green fluorescence decreased, indicating that the mitochondrial membrane potential had partially decreased (Figure 5b, 5c).

Untargeted metabolomics to reveal the functions of ADSCP5

Given the crucial role of PC in anabolic processes, including gluconeogenesis, lipogenesis, insulin secretion, and glutamate neurotransmitter synthesis [23], decreased PC gene expression significantly disrupts multiple metabolic pathways, inhibiting the biosynthesis of key intermediates such as pyruvate, citrate, lactate, malate, aspartate, acetyl-CoA, and serine [24]. Therefore, hypertrophic scar fibroblasts were treated with 25 μM ADSCP5, and untargeted metabolomics analysis (Figure 6a) revealed 851 upregulated and 1012 downregulated metabolites (Figure 6b). A heatmap was constructed to visualize the expression profiles of differentially abundant metabolites across samples (Figure 6c). Z-score analysis revealed significant differences in metabolite levels between the control and ADSCP5-treated groups (Figure 6d). Enrichment analysis indicated predominant associations with purine and nucleotide metabolism (Figure 6e). The TCA cycle associated with PC protein is shown in Figure 6f. Several directly or indirectly related metabolites of PC protein exhibited upregulated or downregulated expression patterns (Figure 6f). The concurrent elevations in oxaloacetic acid and ADP levels suggest that following the ADSCP5-induced downregulation of PC protein expression, TCA cycle flux is impaired, triggering an energy crisis and the activation of compensatory anabolic pathways.

After RAW264.7 macrophages were treated with ADSCP5, qPCR was performed to measure the mRNA expression levels of M1 (classically activated macrophages, proinflammatory)-related genes (CD86, CD80, iNOS, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1) and M2 (alternatively activated macrophages, anti-inflammatory)-related genes (IL-10, CD206, CD204, Arg-1, and IL-4). This functional antifibrotic reprogramming represents a more therapeutically relevant outcome than a mere shift in M1/M2 classification.

Angiogenesis is a critical pathophysiological process in both wound healing and the development of hypertrophic scarring; although early angiogenesis supports effective wound repair, excessive vascular proliferation is closely associated with scar hyperplasia [26]. In our study, treatment of HUVECs with 25 μM ADSCP5 resulted in a significant reduction in tube formation under Matrigel conditions when compared with Sc treatment (Figure 7d). These findings highlight the potential role of this peptide in inhibiting angiogenesis, suggesting that ADSCP5 may suppress hypertrophic scar formation.

To evaluate the effect of ADSCP5 on scar hyperplasia, we used a rabbit ear ventral hypertrophic scar model. The Sc peptide has been confirmed to be functionally inert and was used as the control. Both Sc and ADSCP5 (200 μM, 100 μl) were administered via local intradermal injection into the wound sites on rabbit ears. Wounds were created on Day 0 (D0). Peptide injections began 14 days later and were administered once weekly for a total of four injections. A blank control group was also included. Scar conditions were documented photographically on D14, D21, D28, and D35. Scar tissues were collected after imaging on D42 (Figure 8a). The results demonstrated that ADSCP5 treatment significantly inhibited scar hyperplasia (Figure 8b). Histological examination using H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that ADSCP5 reduced collagen deposition (Figure 8c). Statistical analysis further confirmed that ADSCP5 significantly suppressed the scar areas on D28, D35, and D42 (Figure 8d).

In contrast, the porcine dorsal scar model closely mimics human skin in terms of epidermal thickness, collagen arrangement, and appendage distribution, enabling the formation of human-like scar structures.

Discussion

Hypertrophic scarring is an abnormal tissue remodeling phenomenon that occurs during the wound healing process following injury to the dermal layer of the skin [2]. Recently, several studies have demonstrated that ADSCs play significant roles in suppressing pathological scar formation [5, 6]. Adipose-derived stem cells can effectively improve pathological scarring by mediating inflammation and regulating fibroblast proliferation and activation, and vascular reconstruction [28]. However, the safety, separation, and extraction standards of ADSCs need to be considered in clinical use. Peptide-based therapeutics hold great promise in disease treatment owing to their low molecular weight, high specificity, and low toxicity [9, 29]. Here, we demonstrate that the binding of ADSCP5 to PC inhibits PC enzymatic activity, leading to disruption of the TCA cycle and metabolic imbalance and reducing AKT/mTOR activity, which promotes autophagy. Simultaneously, metabolic imbalance leads to increased ROS levels. ROS can suppress PI3K/AKT signaling (which is consistent with reduced p-AKT levels). Decreased phosphorylation of AKT was accompanied by decreased phosphorylation of p65. Independently, the binding of ADSCP5 to p50 stabilized or increased p50 protein expression. The increase in p50 results in the formation of homodimers that translocate to the nucleus and repress the expression of NF-κB-driven proinflammatory/profibrotic genes (including IL-6 and collagens). Importantly, ROS also promotes autophagy, which is further potentiated by AKT/mTOR inhibition, and autophagy has been shown to degrade collagen precursors. Thus, ADSCP5 alleviates hypertrophic scarring through targeting PC or p50 to coordinate PI3K/AKT/mTOR-autophagy and NF-κB/IL-6 signaling. In addition, ADSCP5 may be considered a potential therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of pathological scarring.

PC, located in the mitochondria, catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate to oxaloacetate and thus supplies substrates for the TCA cycle. Pyruvate is a key intermediate in glucose metabolism and serves as a metabolic hub linking carbohydrate, lipid, and amino acid metabolism. Macrophage PC promotes inflammatory responses and exacerbates atherosclerosis via the HIF-1 signaling pathway [30]. A derivative of pulchinenoside B4 targets PC to treat ulcerative colitis [31]. Fibroblast PC in the tumor microenvironment is necessary for collagen production [32]. Our previous research revealed that the ADSC-derived ADSCP2 inhibits scarring by binding to PC and altering metabolite composition, and knockdown of PC decreased COL1A2, COL3A1, and ACTA2 protein levels [16]. Here, through peptide pull-down assays, mass spectrometry, molecular docking, cellular thermal shift assays (CETSAs), surface plasmon resonance, immunofluorescence colocalization analysis, and western blot and rescue experiments, we demonstrate that ADSCP5 binds to PC, inhibits its expression, and alters the metabolite profile of hypertrophic scar-derived fibroblasts via untargeted metabolomics.

Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) is a transcription factor widely expressed in eukaryotic cells that regulates genes involved in proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and fibrosis. The NF-κB family includes RelA (p65), RelB, c-Rel, NF-κB1 (p50), and NF-κB2 (p52), which form homo- or heterodimers. The Rel homology domain (RHD) mediates dimerization, nuclear localization, DNA binding, and interaction with IκB inhibitors [33]. Studies have shown that p50 homodimers act as key anti-inflammatory transcription factors by suppressing the expression of proinflammatory genes and increasing the expression of anti-inflammatory genes [34]. RNA-seq analysis revealed 483 genes whose expression differed after ADSCP5 treatment (Figure 2b). KEGG and GSEA analyses revealed associations with NF-κB, interferon-alpha, the inflammatory response, cholesterol homeostasis, and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. We further confirmed that ADSCP5 binds p50 and promotes its expression. These findings suggest that ADSCP5 may facilitate p50 homodimer binding to the promoters of inflammatory genes such as IL-6 to suppress their transcription. Rescue experiments with a knockdown of p50 or an NF-κB inhibitor attenuated ADSCP5-mediated suppression of COL1A1, indicating that ADSCP5 inhibits collagen via p50.

Protein kinase B (AKT) is a serine/threonine kinase. FAK activates the PI3K/AKT pathway to prevent fibroblast apoptosis, leading to fibroblast accumulation [35]. miR-203a-3p reduces hypertrophic scarring by inhibiting PI3Kα via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [36]. Melatonin enhances autophagy through MT2 receptor-mediated PI3K/AKT/mTOR suppression, inhibiting fibroblast function and scarring [22]. Inhibiting ANGPT2 activates autophagy via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to regulate scarring [37]. We found that ADSCP5 inhibits AKT and mTOR expression, reduces p62 levels, and promotes autophagic flux. AKT activator treatment reversed ADSCP5-mediated suppression of collagen expression, suggesting that ADSCP5 acts through AKT/mTOR inhibition and autophagy induction.

Autophagy is a highly regulated intracellular degradation process that maintains homeostasis through the recycling of damaged organelles and proteins via autophagosome–lysosome fusion [38]. It plays crucial roles in all phases of wound healing, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [39]. Autophagy impairment in hypertrophic scars hinders regression, limiting autophagy-mediated apoptosis and increasing ECM deposition [40]. Compared with normal skin, scar tissue has reduced levels of autophagy-related proteins (e.g. LC3-II and Beclin1) [41]. Enhancing autophagy may induce apoptosis and promote matrix degradation, offering a potential therapeutic strategy. A self-driving microneedle system (TA-Cur MNs) combined with autophagy modulation promotes scar repair [42].

During hypertrophic scarring, fibroblast overproliferation and microvascular occlusion create a hypoxic microenvironment, increasing ROS levels. Drug-loaded MNs can ameliorate the pathological microenvironment by scavenging ROS and reducing the MMP2/MMP9 ratio [43]. Surprisingly, ADSCP5 increased ROS production and partially reduced the mitochondrial membrane potential in human scar fibroblasts. Whereas autophagy typically reduces ROS accumulation by clearing ROS-related substrates, decreased PC expression may cause energetic stress, reducing AKT/mTOR activity and promoting autophagy. Thus, ADSCP5 may suppress PC, leading to metabolic dysregulation, partial mitochondrial depolarization, increased ROS levels, and increased autophagy.

Peptides often exert biological effects by binding target proteins and modulating their activity or conformation [44]. ADSCP5 may reduce PC levels by inducing conformational changes that promote ubiquitin–proteasomal or autophagic–lysosomal degradation. Conversely, its binding to p50 may stabilize the protein and inhibit its degradation. The exact mechanisms require further validation. Overall, ADSCP5 appears to target PC or p50, although additional binding partners may exist.

Macrophages play dual roles in scarring, participating in both the proinflammatory and fibrotic phases via polarization, cytokine secretion, and fibroblast interactions [25]. Transdermal nanogels inhibit scarring by modulating macrophage polarization and exerting antifibrotic effects [45]. ADSCP5 treatment significantly reduced IL-6 mRNA expression in macrophages without affecting CD80, TNF-α, CD204, or IL-4 expression. CM from ADSCP5-treated macrophages suppressed collagen and ACTA2 expression in fibroblasts. Because IL-6 overexpression promotes scarring, ADSCP5 may inhibit collagen and ACTA2 expression by reducing IL-6 secretion from macrophages.

Vascular endothelial cells contribute to scarring via angiogenesis, inflammation, transdifferentiation, and paracrine signaling [46]. Angiogenesis supplies oxygen and nutrients during healing; however, excessive angiogenesis can lead to scarring. ADSCP5 inhibited angiogenesis in HUVECs, suggesting a potential mechanism for its antiscarring effect, although further validation is needed.

Animal models for hypertrophic scarring include rabbit ear, mouse, rat, hamster, guinea pig, and pig models, each with advantages and limitations [27]. We demonstrated that ADSCP5 effectively suppressed collagen synthesis and scarring in both the rabbit ear and porcine back models. The rabbit ear model is simple and exhibits human-like scarring without wound contraction, but has a long formation period (>28 days) and lacks skin appendages and tension. Therefore, our assessments focused on histological and immunohistochemical markers of hypertrophy rather than biomechanical contraction metrics. The porcine model closely mimics human skin in structure and scarring but is costly and typically produces flat rather than raised scars. The porcine scars in this study were flat rather than raised, which may limit direct morphological comparison to elevated human hypertrophic scars. However, the model robustly captured the essential increase in collagen pathology, making it suitable for evaluating antifibrotic therapeutics targeting collagen. In our study, the consistent results across the models underscore the conserved action of ADSCP5 and support its translational potential.

Conclusions

Overall, this study demonstrates that ADSCP5 alleviates hypertrophic scarring by directly binding PC or p50, suppressing the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and NF-κB pathways, and promoting ROS-mediated autophagy. By modulating metabolic, macrophage, and angiogenic responses, ADSCP5 effectively reduces collagen production and scar hyperplasia. These findings position ADSCP5 as a promising therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of pathological scarring, providing a theoretical basis for developing novel secretome-based anti-fibrotic strategies.
