Dihydrotestosterone Induces Keratinocyte Apoptosis by Activating ERK Signaling
Authors: JUNG-IL KANG, YOUN KYUNG CHOI, SANG-CHUL HAN, CHANG-GU HYUN, HYE-JIN BOO, JIN WON HYUN, EUN-SOOK YOO, HEE-KYOUNG KANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimDihydrotestosterone (DHT) plays a critical role in hair loss. However, research on DHT has mostly focused on its effects on dermal papilla cells expressing androgen receptor (AR). Accordingly, studies on the roles of DHT in keratinocytes, which undergo active proliferation and cell death during hair cycle progression, are lacking. This study investigated the effects of DHT on extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which are involved in cell proliferation, cell death, and hair cycle regulation.Materials and MethodsChanges in human immortalized keratinocyte (HaCaT) cell proliferation were investigated by WST assay after treatment with DHT without fetal bovine serum. Apoptosis induction was investigated using Hoechst33342 staining, immunoblotting, and Annexin V/7AAD staining. The involvement of specific signaling pathways was analyzed using WST assay with AR antagonist, Ras/Raf/mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) inhibitor, or glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) inhibitors. Changes in mRNA levels were analyzed using quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction.ResultsDHT inhibited HaCaT cell proliferation via apoptosis induction. The AR antagonist bicalutamide had no effect on DHT-induced proliferation inhibition. DHT treatment increased phospho-ERK levels, which was attenuated by pretreatment with the MEK inhibitor U0126. Pretreatment with U0126 suppressed DHT-induced increase in cleaved caspase-3 and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) levels. U0126 restored DHT-induced decrease in HaCaT cell proliferation. Further, DHT increased dickkopf-1 and CXXC-type zinc finger protein 5 levels but decreased total and cytoplasmic β-catenin levels. Moreover, GSK3β inhibitors (LiCl and CHIR99021) did not prevent DHT-induced proliferation inhibition.ConclusionDHT induces apoptosis in HaCaT cells through ERK activation, independently of AR, and Wnt/β-catenin pathways. These findings provide a basis for understanding the effects of DHT on hair follicle keratinocytes.

Introduction

Hair loss is a condition characterized by hair thinning and falling. It shows no gender or age bias and is caused by various factors, including hormonal imbalances, immune system changes, and chemotherapy (1-4). Androgenetic alopecia (AGA), the most common type of hair loss, affects more than 50% of white males over the age of 50 years (5). Interestingly, hair loss does not occur in individuals with a deficiency of 5α-reductase, which converts testosterone to dihydrotestosterone (DHT) (6, 7). This suggests that DHT is essential for AGA. In patients with AGA, androgen receptor (AR) is expressed at higher levels in dermal papilla cells than in normal individuals (8). Finasteride, a 5α-reductase inhibitor, inhibits AGA progression (9). These results indicate that DHT can bind to AR in dermal papilla cells of the hair follicle, thereby causing AGA. Most studies on AGA have used dermal papilla cells as the primary target of androgens (8, 10-12), and although several studies have shown that androgen-mediated changes in dermal papilla cell-derived factors affect keratinocytes, the direct effects of androgens on keratinocytes are less well-understood (10, 13).

The hair follicle is a small organ composed of various types of keratinocytes, including inner root sheath cells, outer root sheath cells, stem cells, and matrix cells, as well as dermal papilla cells (14). Cell proliferation, differentiation, and death in various cells within the hair follicle occur during hair cycle changes characterized by the anagen, catagen, and telogen phases (15).

The Ras/Raf/mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathways are essential for cell differentiation, proliferation, and survival (16). In dermal papilla cells of hair follicles, ERK activation induced by minoxidil increases cell proliferation (17). In contrast, studies using Raf deficiency, MEK antisense cDNA, and MEK inhibitors have revealed that Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK (also known as MAPK/ERK) signaling contributes to the inhibition of cell proliferation (18). ERK activation has been reported to induce senescence or apoptosis in various cancer cells and to promote apoptosis in keratinocytes (18, 19). Except for studies showing that DHT increases lipid synthesis in human immortalized keratinocyte (HaCaT) cells independently of ERK, there has been little research on the relationship between DHT and ERK signaling (20). Therefore, it is necessary to clarify the DHT-induced changes in the ERK pathway and their functional roles in keratinocytes.

Wnt/β-catenin signaling is essential for hair follicle development, hair growth, and hair regeneration, and dickkopf-1 (DKK-1) acts as a negative regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling (21-24). DHT suppresses total and nuclear β-catenin levels in dermal papilla cells (25). It is also known that DKK-1 levels are higher in the scalp of patients with AGA than in that of normal individuals (23). DHT treatment of dermal papilla cells derived from patients with AGA increases DKK-1 levels and induces apoptosis of co-cultured outer root sheath cells (23). CXXC-type zinc finger protein 5 (CXXC5), another negative regulator of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, is significantly increased in the hair follicle of bald scalps than in hairy scalps of patients with AGA (26).

A key characteristic of AGA is the shortened anagen phase and the rapid transition from the anagen to catagen phases (27). The transition from the anagen to the catagen phase results in a decrease in the number of dermal papilla cells and death of keratinocytes within the hair follicle (28, 29). Despite some reports that outer root sheath cells within hair follicle express AR, the direct effects and mechanisms of androgen action on keratinocytes are still unclear (30). Therefore, this study investigated the effects of DHT on the growth of HaCaT cells, a human keratinocyte as well as its effects on the ERK and Wnt/β-catenin pathways.

Materials and Methods

Reagents. DHT was purchased from TCI (Tokyo, Japan). Bicalutamide, cycloheximide, CHIR99012, and LiCl were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). U0126 was purchased from Calbiochem (Cambridge, MA, USA). Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) was purchased from Welgene (Daegu, Republic of Korea). Trypsin-EDTA was purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA). EZ-Cytox was purchased from Daile Lab Service (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Hoechst33342 solution, Annexin V/7AAD kit, and NE-PER reagent were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). TRIzol reagent was purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). MG cDNA Synthesis Kit II was purchased from MGmed (Seoul, Republic of Korea). KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR kit was purchased from Kapa Biosystems (Wilmington, MA, USA). PRO-PREP protein extraction solution was purchased from iNtRON Biotechnology (Seoul, Republic of Korea). Westar Nova 2.0 ECL solution was purchased from Cyanagen (Bologna, Italy). X-ray film was purchased from Agfa-Gevaert (Mortsel, Belgium).

Cell culture. HaCaT cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay. HaCaT cells (2.5×10⁴ cells/ml) were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h, then cultured in serum-free DMEM for an additional 24 h. The cells were treated with 0.1, 1, 10, 25, 50, or 100 μM DHT or vehicle (ethanol diluted 1:1,000 in serum-free medium). For inhibitor (bicalutamide, U0126, CHIR99021, or LiCl) treatment, HaCaT cells were pretreated with inhibitors for 2 h before DHT treatment. After 72 h of culture, EZ-Cytox (10 μl/well), a water-soluble tetrazolium salt, was added to cells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. Changes in cell proliferation were determined by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a Versamax microplate reader. The experiments were performed independently at least three times.

Hoechst33342 staining. HaCaT cells (2.5×10⁴ cells/ml) were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight, followed by an additional 24 h of incubation in serum-free DMEM. The cells were treated with DHT (50 and 100 μM) for 24 h and then stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 μg/ml), a DNA-specific dye, for 30 min. The cells were washed three times with DPBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope (magnification ×200).

Immunoblotting. HaCaT cells (5.0×10⁴ cells/ml) were seeded in 60 mm dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 h, followed by an additional 24 h of incubation in serum-free DMEM. The cells were then treated with 100 μM DHT for 0, 15, 30 and 60 min. In some cases, cells were treated with DHT (50 and 100 μM) for 24 h. To assess changes in ERK phosphorylation, cells were pre-cultured with serum-free DMEM for 24 h after attachment and then cultured for 0, 24, and 48 h, or treated with 100 μM DHT for 0, 15, 30, 60, and 180 min. In other cases, cells were pretreated with or without 250 nM U0126 for 2 h and then treated with 100 μM DHT for 30 min or 24 h. To assess changes in GSK3β phosphorylation, cells were treated with 100 μM DHT for 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min.

For immunoblotting, whole cells were lysed with PRO-PREP protein extraction solution. The nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separated using NE-PER reagent. Proteins were separated on 8-12% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated with PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20) containing 5% skimmed skin milk for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. The membranes were exposed to X-ray film using Westar Nova 2.0 ECL solution. Annexin V/7AAD staining. HaCaT cells (5.0×10⁴ cells/ml) were cultured in 60 mm culture dishes for 24 h in 10% FBS or FBS-free DMEM after cell attachment. Cells cultured in FBS-free DMEM were treated with or without 100 μM DHT for 24 h. Thereafter, cells were harvested, resuspended in binding buffer, stained with Annexin V-FITC and 7-AAD, and incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 15 min. Live and dead cells were measured using LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction(qRT-PCR). HaCaT (2.5×10⁴ cells/ml) were seeded in 60 mm culture dishes and allowed to adhere for 24 h, then cultured in serum-free DMEM for an additional 24 h. The cells were treated with DHT for 6 or 24 h. RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and reverse transcription was performed using the MG cDNA Synthesis Kit I. qRT-PCR was performed using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR kit on a CFX Duet Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The levels of DKK-1, CXXC5, WNT3A, WNT4, WNT5A, WNT10A, and WNT10B were normalized to those of the housekeeping gene (GAPDH). The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table II.

Immunofluorescence staining. HaCaT cells (3.0×104 cells/ml) were seeded in 4-well chamber slides (Lab-Tek Nalge Nunc, Rochester, NY, USA) for 24 h in FBS-free DMEM after cell attachment, treated with or without DHT for 24 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and washed with PBST. Thereafter, cells were permeabilized in PBST containing 0.1% TritonX-100 for 15 min, followed by incubation with 1% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with a specific primary antibody against β-catenin or α-tubulin at 4℃ overnight. The cells were incubated with a corresponding AlexaFluor 488 or AlexaFluor 594-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h and mounted in Vectastain (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) containing 4,6-diamido-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired using a FV1200 confocal laser-scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Bar graphs are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Differences between experimental groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA, with significance considered at p<0.05.

Results

DHT induces apoptotic cell death in keratinocytes. To determine whether DHT affects keratinocyte proliferation, HaCaT cells were treated with various concentrations of DHT (0.1, 1, 10, 25, 50, and 100 μM) for 72 h, and changes in cell proliferation were evaluated using the WST assay. DHT significantly inhibited HaCaT cell proliferation at concentrations >1 μM (Figure 1B). Compared to that of the control group (100%), the 50 μM and 100 μM DHT treatment groups showed cell proliferation rates of 66.7% and 70.7%, respectively (Figure 1B). Therefore, subsequent experiments were performed using 50 and 100 μM DHT. We next determined whether DHT inhibits HaCaT cell proliferation via AR. However, pretreatment with the AR antagonist bicalutamide (1 μM) did not interfere with DHT-induced proliferation inhibition (Figure 1C). These results suggest that DHT inhibits HaCaT cell proliferation independently of AR.

DHT inhibits the proliferation of HaCaT cells. (A) Structure of DHT. (B) Bar graph shows changes in cell proliferation after 72 h of DHT (0.1, 1, 10, 25, 50, or 100 μM) treatment in HaCaT cells. (C) Bar graph shows changes in cell proliferation of HaCaT cells treated with DHT for 72 h after bicalutamide (1 μM) pretreatment. Data are presented as mean±SD from three independent experiments. n=5-6 for each group. *p<0.05, ***p<0.001 vs. vehicle-treated control. NS, Not significant; DHT: dihydrotestosterone; HaCaT: human immortalized keratinocyte.

We also assessed whether DHT-induced apoptosis could affect the reduction in cell proliferation. Hoechst 33342 staining revealed induction of chromatin condensation in HaCaT cells after 24 h of DHT (50 μM and 100 μM) treatment (Figure 2A). In addition, the protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), markers of apoptosis, increased in HaCaT cells stimulated with 50 and 100 μM DHT (Figure 2B). We next examined changes in the number of apoptotic cells via Annexin V/7-AAD staining. Analysis of the dot blot results, which are shown as quadrants (Q1: necrosis, Q2: late apoptotic, Q3: viable, Q4: early apoptotic), revealed that the DHT-treated group showed a significant increase in the number of apoptotic cells and decrease in the number of viable cells compared to those in the control group (cultured in FBS-free medium) (Figure 2C and D). Moreover, an increase in the number of apoptotic cells and a decrease in the number of viable cells were observed in the FBS-free medium culture group compared to those in the 10% FBS medium culture group (Figure 2C and D). These results suggest that DHT, a hormone that causes hair loss, induces apoptosis of actively proliferating keratinocytes, leading to a decrease in cell proliferation.

Activation of ERK mediates DHT-induced keratinocyte cell death. MAPK/ERK signaling reportedly plays a variety of roles, including in cell apoptosis, differentiation, and proliferation (16, 18, 31). As the experiments were conducted under serum-starvation conditions, we investigated changes in ERK phosphorylation with the passage of incubation time in FBS-free medium. As shown in Figure 3A, phosphorylated ERK levels increased after 24 h of incubation in FBS-free medium. To determine whether DHT affects the phosphorylation of c-Raf, MEK1/2, ERK1/2, and p90RSK, HaCaT cells were treated with DHT for 0-120 min. The levels of phosphorylated c-Raf, MEK1/2, ERK1/2, and p90RSK increased between 15 min and 60 min following treatment (Figure 3B). To assess whether ERK activation plays a role in DHT-induced HaCaT cell death, additional experiments were conducted using the MEK inhibitor U0126. Pretreatment with U0126 suppressed the DHT-induced increase in phospho-ERK and -p90RSK levels (Figure 3C). Further, U0126 pretreatment inhibited the DHT-induced increase in the levels of apoptosis-related proteins (cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP) (Figure 3D). To determine whether inhibition of ERK phosphorylation could inhibit DHT-induced cell death, HaCaT cells were pretreated with U0126 for 2 h and then treated with DHT for 72 h to investigate changes in cell proliferation. U0126 inhibited the DHT-induced decrease in HaCaT cell proliferation and subsequently restored HaCaT cell proliferation (Figure 3E). Treatment with U0126 alone increased HaCaT cell proliferation, and this effect was inhibited by post-treatment with DHT (Figure 3E). These results suggest that DHT induces apoptosis in HaCaT cells through ERK activation.

Inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling by DHT in keratinocytes does not induce cell death. In the epidermis, Wnt/β-catenin activation reportedly induces a transition from the telogen phase to the anagen phase (32). Phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 inhibits proteasomal degradation of β-catenin, resulting in increased cytoplasmic levels (22). β-catenin then translocates to the nucleus and regulates target gene expression (22). Under treatment with 100 μM DHT for 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min, GSK3β phosphorylation levels in HaCaT cells decreased for 5 to 30 min (Figure 4A). Moreover, β-catenin protein levels were reduced by DHT treatment for 24 h (Figure 4B). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed that β-catenin levels in HaCaT cells decreased 24 h after DHT treatment (Figure 4C). We next determined whether DHT affects nuclear and cytoplasmic levels of β-catenin. DHT reduced cytoplasmic β-catenin levels but had no effect on nuclear levels in HaCaT cells (Figure 4D). Changes in DKK-1 and CXXC5 levels were investigated in HaCaT cells treated with 100 μM DHT for 0, 6, and 24 h. DHT significantly increased the mRNA levels of DKK-1 and CXXC5 (Figure 4E). We also evaluated whether DHT affects the mRNA levels of Wnt ligands in HaCaT cells stimulated for 6 and 24 h with DHT. DHT significantly downregulated WNT4 mRNA levels but had no effects on WNT3A, WNT5A, WNT10A, and WNT10B mRNA expression (Figure 4F). This result suggests that DHT inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling in HaCaT cells. To determine whether inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling induces cell death by DHT, HaCaT cells were pretreated with the GSK3β inhibitor CHIR (100 and 200 nM) or LiCl (0.5 and 1 mM) and then treated with DHT to observe changes in cell proliferation. The inhibitory effect of DHT on HaCaT cell proliferation was not restored by CHIR pretreatment (Figure 4G). CHIR alone inhibited cell proliferation compared to the control (Figure 4G). As shown in Figure 4H, LiCl failed to restore DHT-induced inhibition of HaCaT cell proliferation. These results suggest that DHT induces cell death independently of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in keratinocytes.

Discussion

An increase in DHT production via 5α-reductase is a major cause of AGA (6, 7). 5α-reductase is mainly expressed in dermal papilla cells of hair follicle, but it is known to be expressed to some extent in other cells, such as the connective tissue sheath and outer root sheath cells (33, 34). Androgen exerts its effect primarily by binding to AR in cells of hair follicles. AR are expressed at higher levels in the dermal papilla cells of hair follicles in patients with AGA compared to non-AGA individuals (8). Therefore, this suggests that DHT may primarily affect dermal papilla cells, which are targets of androgens, thereby inducing the catagen phase of hair cycle and AGA (27). Cells within the hair follicle interact with each other during the hair cycle, resulting changes in the shape and size of the follicle. During telogen phase, dermal papilla cells derived from the mesoderm are adjacent to hair germ cells and stem cells (35). In the anagen phase, matrix cells at the hair bulb surround the dermal papilla cells (15, 35). During the transition from anagen to catagen, cell death occurs in keratinocytes, including matrix and outer root sheath cells, resulting in contraction of the hair follicles (15, 35). Therefore, this suggests that keratinocytes, which undergo active cell proliferation and cell death during the hair cycle, can respond to DHT produced by 5α-reductase. However, research on direct effects of androgens on keratinocytes is limited. In this study, we discovered that DHT can induce apoptosis in keratinocytes by activating ERK signaling independently of AR, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

In this study, we observed that DHT at concentrations of 50 and 100 μM inhibited the proliferation of HaCaT. Chromatin condensation, caspase-3 activation, and PARP cleavage are representative indicators of apoptosis (36, 37). Herein, DHT at concentrations of 50 and 100 μM induced chromatin condensation, caspase-3 activation, and an increase in cleaved PARP, suggesting that DHT at concentrations of 50 and 100 μM induced apoptosis in HaCaT cells. DHT at concentrations of 10-200 μM, has been reported to induce apoptosis of dermal papilla cells, a central regulator of the hair cycle (38). The serum DHT concentration in healthy adult males is known to be approximately 0.47-2.65 nmol/l (39). There is no difference in serum DHT concentration between AGA patients and healthy individuals (40). Previous reports suggest that AGA occurs depending on the 5α-reductase activity and androgen receptors in the hair loss area rather than serum DHT concentration (41). DHT concentrations close to physiological standards do not inhibit the proliferation of dermal papilla cells (25). In the study, we also found that 0.1 μM DHT rarely affect the proliferation of HaCaT cells. DHT, unlike in HaCaT cells, induces the transcription of androgen target genes via AR in dermal papilla cells (42). HaCaT cells have been shown to lack AR transcriptional activity (43). Herein, the DHT-induced inhibition of HaCaT cell proliferation was not reversed by bicalutamide, an AR antagonist (Figure 1). These results suggest that DHT possibly induced HaCaT cell death via a mechanism independent of AR. Meanwhile, DHT-induced DNA damage and senescence in dermal papilla cells with overexpressed AR is restored by AR knockdown, suggesting that they are affected by AR (44).

MAPK/ERK signaling plays a crucial role in both cell proliferation and apoptosis (16, 18, 31). ERK activation by various apoptosis-inducing agents, such as etoposide, doxorubicin, cisplatin, taxol, tamoxifen, and H₂O₂, induces apoptosis in cancer cells, fibroblasts, and keratinocytes (18). Herein, we observed an increase in basal phospho-ERK levels under serum-starvation conditions (Figure 3A). Notably, serum starvation induced an increase in apoptotic cells compared with 10% FBS treatment (Figure 2C and D). Therefore, the DHT-induced increase in phospho-ERK levels observed in Figure 3B is noteworthy. Specifically, these results suggest that serum starvation induces ERK phosphorylation and that DHT further enhances ERK phosphorylation, thereby promoting apoptosis. Previous studies using the anticancer drug etoposide in HaCaT cells have shown that U0126 inhibits etoposide-induced apoptosis (19). In addition, it was demonstrated using a myocardial ischemia model that an increase in TUNEL-positive cells induced by ERK activation was attenuated by U0126 treatment, suggesting that ERK activation can promote apoptosis (45). Therefore, we determined whether DHT-induced ERK phosphorylation directly contributes to apoptosis and reduces cell proliferation using U0126. Pretreatment with U0126 suppressed DHT-induced caspase-3 cleavage and PARP cleavage levels and decreased cell proliferation, indicating that ERK activation is directly involved in DHT-induced apoptosis.

Inhibition of WNT/β-catenin signaling is known to induce catagen progression and inhibit hair growth (32, 46). DKK-1, a Wnt antagonist, is upregulated in dermal papilla cells of patients with AGA in response to DHT (23). DKK-1 inhibits WNT signaling by binding to LRP6 and preventing the binding of Wnt ligands to their receptor, Fz/LRP6 (47, 48). The high levels of DKK-1 expression in the balding scalp of patients with AGA suggest that follicular keratinocyte death is mediated by DKK-1 produced in human dermal papilla cells in response to DHT (23). CXXC5, another negative regulator of WNT/β-catenin signaling, is overexpressed in the scalp of patients with AGA (41). Herein, we found that not only were total and cytoplasmic β-catenin levels suppressed in DHT-treated HaCaT cells, but also that DHT induced an increase in DKK-1 and CXXC5 levels (Figure 4). WNT ligands are upregulated more in stem cells of anagen hair follicle than in those of telogen hair follicle, suggesting that Wnt ligands are important for stem cell activation (49). The canonical Wnt pathway primarily affects cell proliferation, while the non-canonical Wnt pathway influences cell migration and polarity (50, 51).

In this study, the levels of canonical ligands, such as Wnt3a, Wnt10a, and Wnt10b, were not altered by DHT (Figure 4F). Among non-canonical ligands (Wnt4 and Wnt5a) that act independently of β-catenin through the planar cell polarity pathway and Wnt/Ca2+ pathway, Wnt4 was inhibited by DHT (Figure 4F). Overexpression of Wnt4 in burn wounds leads to increased epidermal thickness, and knockdown of Wnt4 in HaCaT cells results in an increased number of apoptotic cells (52). However, DHT-induced HaCaT cell death could not be reversed by treatment with Wnt activators, such as LiCl or CHIR (Figure 4G and H). These results suggest that inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by DHT has little effect on cell proliferation but may affect other responses, including migration. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanism by which DHT specifically inhibits Wnt4 in HaCaT cells and to determine why Wnt activators, such as DKK-1 and CXXC5, did not affect cell death despite the significant increase in their levels.

Study limitations. HaCaT cells differ from hair follicle keratinocytes in that they are immortalized cells. A two-dimension culture model was used and thus we did not consider the hair follicle composed of multiple cells and the surrounding skin environment. Therefore, further studies on ERK activation by DHT in primary hair follicle cells, hair follicle organoids, ex vivo culture of hair follicle and an in vivo model are needed.

Conclusion

DHT, a hormone associated with hair loss, inhibits cell proliferation by inducing apoptosis in HaCaT cells through ERK phosphorylation. Moreover, DHT-induced keratinocyte apoptosis occurred independently of AR and Wnt/β-catenin signaling. This study provides evidence that DHT induces ERK-mediated apoptosis in keratinocytes and offers a foundation for understanding the effects of DHT on hair follicle keratinocytes.
