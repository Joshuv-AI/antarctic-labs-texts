Associations between estimated glucose disposal rate and peripheral artery disease: evidence from the UK Biobank and NHANES
Authors: Jilang Zeng, Xinjie Zeng, Weihong Lin, Xinjun Lin, Feng Hu, Lin Fan, Cheng Yu, Lianglong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInsulin resistance (IR) is considered a key pathogenic mechanism of peripheral artery disease (PAD). The estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) serves as a practical surrogate marker of IR. The study aimed to investigate the association between eGDR and incident PAD risk in the general population.MethodsA total of 456,743 participants free of PAD at baseline were included from the prospective UK Biobank cohort. In addition, we examined the cross-sectional association between eGDR and prevalent PAD among 7,208 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) as a supplementary analysis.ResultsMultivariable Cox regression analysis showed that participants in the highest eGDR quartile (Q4) experienced the greatest reduction in the risk of developing PAD compared with the reference group [Q4 vs. Q1; fully adjusted hazard ratio (HR) = 0.67, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.63–0.72, P < 0.001]. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve and threshold effect analysis also suggested a significant nonlinear inverse relationship between eGDR and PAD risk, with an inflection point at 7.829 (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity < 0.001). Two-piecewise Cox regression further indicated that below the threshold, an increase in eGDR was associated with a greater reduction in PAD risk (HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.84–0.87, P < 0.001). A similar inverse association between eGDR and prevalent PAD was observed in the NHANES analysis.ConclusionseGDR was inversely associated with PAD. Further studies are needed to explore the clinical utility of this association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-026-01149-6.Keywords: Estimated glucose disposal rate, Insulin resistance, Peripheral artery disease, UK Biobank, NHANES

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is an atherosclerotic vascular disorder characterized by symptoms such as intermittent claudication and ischemic pain. It is estimated that more than 113 million individuals worldwide are currently affected by PAD, and this number is projected to rise to 363 million by 2050 as the global population continues to age [1, 2]. Furthermore, as the disease progresses, patients face a markedly increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular (MACE) and limb events (MALE). Unfortunately, because most patients with PAD have mild or atypical leg symptoms, and public awareness of the disease remains limited, diagnosis and treatment are frequently delayed [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying accessible and modifiable risk factors in the general population is crucial for effective prevention and improved outcomes.

Insulin resistance (IR), characterized by reduced sensitivity of target tissues and organs to the effects of insulin, is widely recognized as a key pathophysiological mechanism underlying various metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [5]. Several methods have been developed to evaluate IR, with the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic (HIEG) clamp and the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) being among the most commonly employed [6]. Although the HIEG clamp is considered the gold standard for assessing IR, its complexity and invasiveness, together with the reliance of HOMA-IR on fasting insulin measurements that are not routinely performed, restrict their widespread use in large-scale population studies [6, 7]. Recently, the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) has been proposed as a practical surrogate index for insulin sensitivity. As a non-invasive measure, eGDR is derived from readily available parameters, including waist circumference (WC), hypertension status, and glycated haemoglobin, and was initially developed in the context of type 1 diabetes [8]. Besides, subsequent studies further demonstrated that eGDR correlated well with glucose utilization measured by the HIEG clamp and was associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, such as frailty, stroke, and heart failure [9–13]. Nevertheless, despite these advances, the association between eGDR and PAD remains unclear. To address this gap, we conducted a large prospective cohort study using data from the UK Biobank to examine the relationship between eGDR and PAD in the general population. Furthermore, we performed a supplementary cross-sectional analysis using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) dataset.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the UK Biobank (Application Number 216062; RRID: SCR_012815), a substantial population-based database encompassing over 500,000 individuals aged 37 to 73 years. Participants were recruited between 2006 and 2010 from 22 assessment centers located across England, Scotland, and Wales. At enrollment, extensive baseline information was gathered through questionnaires, interviews, physical measurements, and biological samples. Further details regarding the study design and data collection protocols have been described elsewhere [14]. Among the 502,131 initial participants, individuals with incomplete eGDR data (N = 39,140) and those with PAD at baseline (N = 6,248) were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 456,743. The study flow chart was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

The NHANES (RRID: SCR_013201) is a nationally representative program in the United States that employs a complex, multistage sampling design to collect comprehensive data on health and nutrition. Following NHANES Analytic and Reporting Guidelines, all analyses incorporated sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata [15]. Consistent with previous NHANES-based studies on PAD, our analysis included participants aged ≥ 40 years from three cycles between 1999 and 2004 [16]. Among 31,126 total participants, 7,571 individuals had valid ankle-brachial index (ABI) measurements. After further excluding participants without complete eGDR data (N = 363), 7,208 participants were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Assessment of estimated glucose disposal rate

Based on previous studies, eGDR (mg/kg/min) was calculated using the following formula: eGDR = 21.158 - (0.09 × WC) - (3.407 × HT) - (0.551 × HbA1c), where WC refers to waist circumference (cm), HT indicates hypertension (yes = 1/no = 0), and HbA1c is glycated haemoglobin (%). Hypertension was defined as any of the following criteria being met: (1) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; (2) a documented history of hypertension; or (3) the use of antihypertensive drugs. Covariates

To account for potential confounding, we considered a range of covariates covering demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Specifically, demographic factors consisted of age, sex (male/female), ethnicity (White/non-White), and body mass index (BMI). Socioeconomic and lifestyle variables comprised the Townsend deprivation index (TDI, a postcode-linked indicator of socioeconomic status with higher scores indicating greater deprivation) [18], average total household income (<£18,000, £18,000–£30,999, £31,000–£51,999, £52,000–£100,000, and >£100,000), education level (above or below college/university degree), smoking status (never/previous/current), alcohol drinker status (never/previous/current), and physical activity (< 150 or ≥ 150 min/week of moderate activity, with vigorous activity counted as twice the duration of moderate activity). Clinically relevant covariates encompassed statuses of diabetes, coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, and hyperlipidemia, as well as the use of antihypertensive drugs and aspirin (all yes or no). Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, a self-reported history of diabetes, or the use of glucose-lowering drugs (including insulin).

To minimize potential bias from missing data, multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package in R (random forest method). Five imputed datasets were generated, and estimates were combined using Rubin rules. The proportions of missing data for each covariate are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1, and comparisons of covariate distributions before and after imputation are presented in Supplementary Table 2, showing that the distributions remained largely consistent (all P > 0.05).

Study outcome

The main outcome was PAD. In the UK Biobank cohort, incident PAD was identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), Office of Population Censuses and Surveys’ Classification of Surgical Operations, Fourth Version (OPCS-4), or self-reported codes, as listed in Supplementary Table 3 [19]. The duration of follow-up was defined as the time from baseline assessment to the first occurrence of PAD, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (19 December 2022), whichever came first. A total of 1,142 participants (0.25%) were lost to follow-up. In the NHANES study, PAD is diagnosed based on the ABI. Specifically, participants are defined as having PAD if the ABI in either leg is ≤ 0.90.1

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to quartiles of eGDR. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages (%). Comparisons between groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PAD: Model 1 adjusted for demographic variables, Model 2 additionally incorporated socioeconomic and lifestyle factors, and Model 3 built on Model 2 by including comorbidities and medication use. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant multicollinearity was observed among covariates (all variance inflation factors < 2) (Supplementary Table 4). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of eGDR were applied to examine potential nonlinear associations between eGDR and PAD risk. If a nonlinear relationship was observed, the threshold value was determined using maximum likelihood estimation, and two-piecewise Cox models were fitted on either side of the threshold. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were then plotted, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival differences among the four eGDR groups. Besides, subgroup analyses were performed to investigate potential effect modification by age, sex, BMI, and smoking status, with multiplicative interaction terms included to assess interactions.

To explore whether systemic inflammation may partly explain the association between eGDR and PAD risk, we performed an exploratory mediation analysis using the product-of-coefficients approach with bootstrap resampling [20]. Biomarkers significantly associated with eGDR were first identified using multivariable linear regression and then included in a three-variable mediation model. In this model, linear regression assessed the association between eGDR and the mediator, while Cox regression evaluated the effects of eGDR and the mediator on PAD risk. The proportion mediated was defined as the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) was used to derive 95% CIs, and multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

To test the robustness of the main findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses. First, to assess the potential influence of unmeasured confounding, we calculated E-values. The E-values represent the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to completely account for the observed association [21]. The formulas used for calculating E-values were provided in Supplementary Table 5. Second, given the relatively high missing rate for physical activity, we performed an additional safety analysis after excluding participants with missing physical activity data. Third, excluding participants with missing key covariates. Fourth, excluding participants who developed PAD within the first two years of follow-up to minimize potential reverse causation. Fifth, treating all-cause mortality as a competing risk using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models. Sixth, excluding covariates closely related to the eGDR formula (including BMI, diabetes, and antihypertensive drugs) to address potential overadjustment issues.

Finally, we conducted a supplementary cross-sectional analysis in the NHANES population to examine whether the inverse association observed in UK Biobank was also present for prevalent PAD. Baseline characteristics were summarized using weighted means ± standard errors (SE) for continuous variables and unweighted counts (weighted proportions) for categorical variables. Weighted logistic regression models, including weighted RCS analysis, were used to examine the cross-sectional association between eGDR and PAD. Covariates included in the main model were generally similar to those in the UK Biobank. The proportions of missing data and the comparisons before and after imputation were provided in Supplementary Tables 6 and 7.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1; RRID: SCR_001905; https://www.r-project.org/), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Association between eGDR and PAD risk

During a median follow-up of 13.80 years, 14,704 (2.33 per 1,000 person-years; 3.22%) incident PAD cases were recorded. The incidence of PAD declined progressively with increasing eGDR quartiles (P for trend < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the fully adjusted Cox model (Model 3) indicated that participants with higher eGDR had a lower risk of PAD compared with Q1 (all P < 0.001; P for trend < 0.001), with HRs of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.86, P < 0.001) for Q2, 0.73 (95% CI: 0.69–0.78, P < 0.001) for Q3, and 0.67 (95% CI: 0.63–0.72, P < 0.001) for Q4 (Table 2). When eGDR was treated as a continuous variable, an inverse association with PAD risk was also observed (P for overall < 0.001) (Fig. 1B), with each SD increase associated with a 21% (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.77–0.81, P < 0.001) lower risk (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed similar patterns, with substantial differences in survival rates among the four groups (log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). In addition, similar results were obtained in the analysis of diabetic and non-diabetic populations (Supplementary Table 9; Fig. 2B and C).

Subgroup analysis

In all prespecified subgroups, eGDR levels were consistently inversely associated with PAD risk (Supplementary Fig. 2). Interaction tests further indicated that sex, BMI, and smoking status significantly modified the association (P for interaction < 0.05). The inverse association appeared stronger among males, participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m², and former or current smokers, as shown by lower HRs for Q4 versus Q1 compared with the corresponding reference subgroups. In contrast, no significant interaction was observed in the age subgroup (P for interaction > 0.05).

Exploratory mediation analysis

Multivariable linear regression indicated that eGDR was significantly negatively associated with multiple inflammatory markers (all PFDR <0.001) (Supplementary Table 10). In exploratory mediation analysis, CRP and WBC counts partially mediated the association between eGDR and PAD risk, with mediation proportions of 3.48% (95% CI: 3.01%-3.91%, P < 0.001) and 2.50% (95% CI: 2.08%-3.16%, P < 0.001), respectively (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

We first assessed the potential impact of unmeasured confounders. For the association between eGDR and PAD risk (per SD increase, fully adjusted HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.77–0.81), the E-value was 1.85 for the point estimate and 1.77 for the upper bound of the 95% CI (HR = 0.81). The E-values suggested that an unmeasured confounder would need to be moderately associated with both eGDR and PAD to fully explain the observed association. Additional sensitivity analyses included: (1) excluding participants with missing physical activity data (Supplementary Table 11); (2) excluding participants with missing key covariates (Supplementary Table 12); (3) excluding those who developed PAD within the first 2 years of follow-up (Supplementary Table 13); (4) applying Fine-Gray models to account for the competing risk of death (Supplementary Table 14); (5) excluding covariates closely related to eGDR formula (Supplementary Table 15). The results from all sensitivity analyses were similar to the primary findings.

Supplementary cross-sectional analysis in the NHANES population

In the supplementary cross-sectional analysis based on the NHANES population, participants with higher eGDR levels exhibited demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical features similar to those observed in the UK Biobank cohort (Supplementary Table 16). Additionally, weighted logistic regression analysis indicated that those in Q4 had lower odds of prevalent PAD compared with those in Q1 [fully adjusted odds ratio (OR) = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.12–0.82, P = 0.019] (Supplementary Table 17). A similar inverse trend was observed in the weighted RCS analysis (P for overall = 0.041; P for nonlinearity = 0.186) (Supplementary Fig. 3). In addition, subgroup analyses and interaction tests indicated that the inverse association was generally consistent among subgroups, and no significant interactions were observed (all P for interaction > 0.05; Supplementary Table 18).

Discussion

Primary findings

Based on data from the UK Biobank, the study systematically analyzed the relationship between eGDR and PAD in the general population. We found that higher eGDR was independently associated with a lower risk of PAD, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors. The association remained consistent among participants with and without diabetes. Moreover, a series of sensitivity analyses, along with supplementary analysis in NHANES, supported the consistency of the observed associations.

Comparison with previous studies

eGDR was originally proposed by Williams and colleagues in studies of type 1 diabetes and is calculated from routine anthropometric and laboratory measurements [8]. It was designed as a simple and practical alternative for assessing IR, replacing invasive methods such as the HIEG clamp and insulin suppression test (IST), which are generally impractical for large-scale population studies or clinical care settings. Following its initial proposal, numerous subsequent studies have observed variations in eGDR in different populations and explored its associations with metabolic disorders and vascular diseases. For example, Huang et al. [22] and Huo et al. [23] investigated the association between eGDR and stroke risk in general populations from the United Kingdom and China, respectively. Both studies reported a nonlinear inverse relationship between eGDR levels and stroke risk. These findings have also been reported in both diabetic [24] and non-diabetic populations [25]. Similarly, a study incorporating two national cohorts [26] also found that elevated eGDR was associated with a decreased risk of hyperlipidemia, with the RCS curve showing a significant threshold effect. Furthermore, several cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have indicated that eGDR was an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality [27, 28].

Building on these findings, our study revealed a significant negative correlation between eGDR and PAD risk, consistent with its reported association with various adverse outcomes. As a major manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis, PAD carries disability and mortality risks comparable to those of CHD [29]. However, its clinical importance has long been underestimated due to nonspecific symptoms, low diagnostic rates, and limited public awareness [30]. Besides, few previous studies have incorporated IR markers like eGDR into risk assessments for PAD. To address this gap, our study extended previous work by evaluating the association between eGDR and incident PAD in the UK Biobank and by conducting a supplementary cross-sectional analysis of prevalent PAD in NHANES. The generally consistent direction of association across two independent datasets supports the potential relevance of eGDR as an accessible marker associated with PAD risk.

Potential mechanisms

Although the precise mechanisms linking eGDR to the risk of PAD remain incompletely understood, several biologically plausible pathways may help explain the association. First, IR (as reflected by low eGDR) often coexists and interacts with other traditional risk factors, such as smoking and obesity. These factors may impair endothelial function by reducing nitric oxide (NO) production and increasing endothelin-1 (ET-1) activity, thereby facilitating the development and progression of peripheral atherosclerosis and elevating the risk of PAD [31]. Our subgroup analyses were also consistent with this possibility, showing that the eGDR-PAD relationship was more pronounced among obese individuals and those with a history of current or former smoking. These significant interactions may reflect greater cardiometabolic and vascular risk burden in these groups, including obesity-related IR and smoking-related endothelial injury. Second, inflammatory responses and oxidative stress may play a bridging role in this process. Basic research has shown that IR and hyperglycemia can activate various signaling pathways, initiating an inflammatory cascade and disrupting the oxidative/antioxidant balance. These processes lead to increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn further aggravate IR, promote vascular inflammation and remodeling, impair plaque stability, and ultimately contribute to arterial stiffness and a higher risk of PAD [32–34]. Consistently, our exploratory mediation analysis suggested that systemic inflammation, particularly as reflected by CRP, may partly contribute to the association between lower eGDR and higher PAD risk. Meanwhile, IR is often accompanied by lipid metabolism disturbances and a prothrombotic state, further promoting plaque formation and thrombosis, thereby increasing susceptibility to PAD [35, 36].

Moreover, we observed a threshold effect in the inverse association between eGDR and PAD risk. Below the inflection point, the relationship was steeper, whereas the association persisted but weakened above this level. This pattern suggested that individuals in the lower eGDR range may have a more sensitive PAD risk profile. From a biological perspective, severe IR may coexist with PAD-related processes, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and abnormal lipid metabolism, collectively amplifying PAD susceptibility. Therefore, the lower eGDR range may represent a metabolically vulnerable state in which small differences in IR are associated with more pronounced differences in PAD risk, suggesting that individuals with lower eGDR may warrant closer metabolic risk assessment and lifestyle risk-factor modification. However, this threshold should be interpreted as an exploratory statistical inflection point rather than a validated clinical cutoff or intervention target. Whether individuals with lower eGDR derive greater benefit from specific interventions requires confirmation in future prospective and interventional studies.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies in the use of two large population-based datasets, which not only provides substantial statistical power but also reduces the likelihood that the observed associations are driven by characteristics unique to a single cohort. Furthermore, by comprehensively adjusting for potential confounders and applying E-value analysis, we show that our findings are unlikely to be explained by unmeasured confounding, further reinforcing the robustness of our findings. In summary, this study may have potential significant clinical and public health implications. As a cost-effective and practical surrogate marker for IR, eGDR may provide additional information for PAD risk assessment beyond traditional risk factors.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be mentioned. First, like other observational studies, we cannot establish a causal relationship, and the possibility of residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, eGDR serves as an indirect estimate of glucose utilization, lacking the precision of gold-standard methods like the HIEG clamp to fully capture true insulin sensitivity, which may introduce a degree of measurement error. Third, eGDR was assessed from a single baseline measurement, limiting our ability to account for changes in IR over time. Fourth, differences in study design and PAD definition between NHANES and the UK Biobank limited the direct comparability of the two analyses, and the NHANES findings should therefore be interpreted as supplementary evidence rather than direct validation of the UK Biobank results. Finally, although we outlined biologically plausible mechanisms linking eGDR to PAD, these interpretations are derived from observational data and should be further verified in basic and translational research.

Conclusions

eGDR was inversely associated with incident PAD in the prospective UK Biobank cohort and with prevalent PAD in the cross-sectional NHANES analysis. As a readily accessible surrogate marker of IR, eGDR may help identify individuals at increased PAD risk. Further studies are needed to determine whether incorporating eGDR into PAD risk prediction models improves clinical risk stratification and preventive strategies.
