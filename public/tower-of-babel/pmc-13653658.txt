Focusing on VaIN2+: Development and validation of a prediction nomogram based on a large colposcopy population
Authors: Quanlong Lu, Yao Liu, Jing Li, Chenyao Guo, Ruifang Li, Bo Feng, Xun Gao, Lifang Gao, Zhilian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVaginal intraepithelial neoplasia 2 or worse (VaIN2+) has long remained in a largely passive diagnostic pattern due to the lack of specific clinical manifestations and routine screening strategies. This study aimed to explore its clinical characteristics and develop a prediction model for individualized risk assessment.Material and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics and human papillomavirus (HPV) genotype distribution in 3259 women undergoing colposcopy at a single center between September 2012 and December 2024. A nomogram was developed based on predictors initially screened by least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression and finalized through multivariable logistic regression. The model's discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed in both training and validation sets.ResultsAmong 1192 women with VaIN2+, older age, HPV infection, cytology worse than atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS), and concurrent cervical/vulvar lesions were more prevalent than in 2067 women without vaginal lesions. HPV16, HPV58, HPV52, HPV33, and HPV18 were the most common genotypes. A nomogram was developed based on 10 independent predictors identified by multivariable analysis, including age, hysterectomy history, specific HPV genotypes (16, 58, 56, 35, 39), cytological results (ASCUS and worse), concurrent cervical lesions (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia 1, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia 2 or 3, cervical squamous cell carcinoma), and concurrent vulvar lesions. The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.771 in the training set and 0.791 in the validation set. Calibration was satisfactory, and decision curve analysis confirmed superior clinical net benefit across a wide threshold probability range.ConclusionsBy profiling VaIN2+ characteristics, this study developed the first prediction model integrating specific HPV genotypes with multiple clinical factors. This model enables clinicians to proactively identify high‐risk individuals, thereby facilitating earlier diagnosis and intervention for VaIN2+.

INTRODUCTION

Vaginal squamous intraepithelial lesion is a pre‐invasive lesion confined to the vaginal epithelium, categorized into low‐grade and high‐grade lesions. 1 Low‐grade lesions (vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia 1, VaIN1) are mostly transient and typically regress spontaneously. 1 , 2 In contrast, high‐grade lesions, that is, VaIN2 and VaIN3, are widely recognized as direct precursors to invasive vaginal carcinoma. 1 , 3 Similar to cervical lesions, most VaIN2 or worse (VaIN2+) cases, which include VaIN2, VaIN3, and vaginal squamous cell carcinoma (VaSCC), are linked to persistent high‐risk human papillomavirus (HR‐HPV) infection. 4 , 5 Early detection and intervention are crucial for inhibiting disease progression and improving clinical outcomes. However, due to the lack of specific clinical symptoms, the detection of VaIN2+ relies almost exclusively on colposcopy and histological examination. 1 , 6 Consequently, VaIN2+ has long been underestimated, with most cases being detected incidentally during cervical cancer screening or follow‐up for other indications. 1

Although early studies reported a low incidence of VaIN2+ (approximately 0.2–2.0 per 100 000 women), accounting for only 0.4%–1.0% of lower genital tract intraepithelial neoplasias, its potential to progress to invasive cancer in approximately 12% of cases cannot be overlooked. 2 , 3 , 7 , 8 Recent years have witnessed a marked increase in VaIN2+ detection, with widespread cervical cancer screening and a consequent surge in colposcopy referrals. 1 , 8 , 9 Despite this, clinical attention remains focused on the cervix, with limited understanding of the prevention and management of vaginal lesions. Currently, there are no targeted routine screening strategies. This makes it difficult for clinicians to identify women at high risk for VaIN2+ among the numerous outpatients prior to colposcopy, leaving a substantial number of potential cases without further diagnostic evaluation. Moreover, although there is growing emphasis on the necessity of a comprehensive vaginal assessment during colposcopic examination, the unique anatomical features of the vagina (large surface area and numerous rugae) and the multifocal nature of the lesions significantly complicate thorough visualization and accurate biopsy. 1 , 10 , 11 These make the detection of VaIN2+ heavily dependent on the clinician's experience and vigilance, leading to a high risk of missed diagnosis and lost opportunities for early intervention. 10

Therefore, developing a practical, objective, and reasonably accurate method for predicting the probability of VaIN2+ occurrence becomes a pressing priority. Unfortunately, this area remains almost unexplored. For outpatients, how to rapidly identify those at high risk for VaIN2+ has become a pressing clinical challenge essential for guiding timely evaluation and intervention.

Based on a large‐scale colposcopy center dataset spanning 12 years, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics and HPV genotype distribution in women with VaIN2+ and identified their associated risk factors. Using these factors, we developed and validated a nomogram for the individualized assessment of VaIN2+ risk. We expect this model to serve as an objective and efficient decision‐support tool for clinicians, facilitating the early identification and precision management of high‐risk VaIN2+ patients.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This study enrolled 3630 women who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and underwent initial colposcopy and targeted biopsy at the Second Hospital of Shanxi Medical University between September 2012 and December 2024. After excluding those with incomplete clinical data, 3259 women were eligible for inclusion. Among them, 2067 women were confirmed to have no vaginal lesions by histological examination (normal group), and 1192 women were diagnosed with VaIN2+ (VaIN2+ group, including 570 VaIN2, 297 VaIN3, and 325 VaSCC).

Inclusion criteria: (1) Initial colposcopic examination at our hospital, with all diagnoses confirmed by histological examination; (2) Histologically confirmed to have either no vaginal lesions or VaIN2+; (3) No prior history of vaginal lesions.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Initial diagnosis of vaginal lesion at another institution; (2) Non‐lower genital tract squamous lesions; (3) Histological diagnosis of VaIN1; (4) History of vaginal procedures (e.g., ablation, cryotherapy) or radiotherapy involving the vagina; (5) History of non‐lower reproductive tract malignancies; (6) Previous hysterectomy for indications other than cervical precancerous lesions or cancer.

The detailed procedures for cytology, HPV genotyping, colposcopy, targeted biopsies, and histological examination were consistent with those in our previous study. 12 Briefly, cytological samples were obtained from cervical cells; for women with a history of hysterectomy, vaginal cuff cell samples were collected instead. All samples underwent ThinPrep liquid‐based cytology (Bethesda criteria 13 ). HPV genotyping covered 15 high‐risk (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68) and 6 low‐risk (6, 11, 41, 42, 44, CP8304) types.

Colposcopy was performed within 12 weeks after the cytology and HPV test results were obtained, and when there were indications for colposcopy referral: positive for HPV16 or HPV18, or persistent positivity for other high‐risk HPV subtypes; cytological abnormalities (atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance (ASCUS) with a positive HPV test; atypical squamous cells, cannot exclude high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (ASC‐H); low‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL); high‐grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL); atypical glandular cells (AGC) and cancer); large or palpable ulcers, masses or exophytic lesions; findings suspicious for cancer on visual inspection or spectroscopy; unexplained lower genital tract bleeding or discharge.

Women were instructed to abstain from sexual intercourse, vaginal douching, and vaginal medications for 48 h prior to colposcopy. During the colposcopic examination, the entire lower genital tract—including the cervix, vagina, and vulva—was complete colposcopical visit. The application of 3%–5% acetic acid and Lugol's iodine solution was used to identify potential lesions. For the cervix, targeted biopsies were taken from suspicious areas; when no abnormality was detected under colposcopy, random biopsies were performed if the cytology result was ASC‐H, AGC, or HSIL. Endocervical curettage was carried out according to clinical indications. For the vagina and vulva, targeted biopsies were taken from suspicious areas. Multi‐site biopsies were performed as needed based on the extent of the lesions.

All histological slides were independently assessed by two gynecologic pathologists blinded to HPV and cytology findings, with disagreements resolved by consultation with a senior pathologist to reach a final consensus.

Data collected from outpatient medical records included: age, symptoms or signs (bleeding, discharge, or vaginal neoplasm), hysterectomy history, cytological and HPV test results, and pathological diagnoses of the cervix, vagina, and vulva.

Dataset partition

To ensure a balanced distribution of key clinical characteristics for model development and validation, the study population was stratified by HPV infection status and randomly split into a training set (70%, n = 2283) and a validation set (30%, n = 976). The two sets were then compared across key variables to confirm their comparability.

Predictor screening and model development

In the training set, chi‐square tests were performed on all candidate predictors to evaluate their distributions between the normal and VaIN2+ groups. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was then employed for variable selection. The optimal regularization parameter (lambda.min) was determined through 10‐fold cross‐validation, and variables with non‐zero coefficients at this parameter were retained. These variables were then entered into logistic regression analysis to construct the prediction model, which was visualized as a nomogram.

Model validation and performance assessment

Model performance was evaluated in both the training and validation sets. First, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was utilized to assess the model's capability in differentiating women with VaIN2+ from general outpatients. Subsequently, calibration was evaluated using calibration curves, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, and the Brier score to measure the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Finally, to further assess the model's clinical utility, decision curve analysis was utilized to quantify the net benefit across threshold probabilities.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0) and R (version 4.4.2) software. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was set at a two‐sided p‐value <0.05 for all analyses. Specifically, the Chi‐square test, Welch t‐test, logistic regression (with interaction term test for effect modification), and Hosmer–Lemeshow test were conducted using SPSS. R was utilized for advanced modeling and visualization: the “glmnet” package for LASSO regression, the “rms” package for nomogram construction, Brier score calculation and calibration curve plotting, the “pROC” package for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and the “rmda” package for decision curve analysis.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics and HPV genotype distribution of the study population

A total of 3259 women were included in this study. According to pathological diagnoses, 2067 women (63.4%) without vaginal lesions were categorized as the normal group, and 1192 women (36.6%) with VaIN2+ were categorized as the VaIN2+ group. As summarized in Table 1, the VaIN2+ group exhibited significantly distinct clinical characteristics compared to the normal group. Specifically, the mean age of women in the VaIN2+ group (55.6 ± 11.9) was higher than that in the normal group (51.9 ± 10.5). When stratified by age, the proportions of women aged <30 years and 30–39 years did not differ significantly between the two groups. Of note, the VaIN2+ group had lower proportions of women aged 40–49 years (17.2% vs 23.2%) and 50–59 years (33.1% vs 41.0%), but higher proportions of those aged 60–64 years (17.1% vs 12.9%) and ≥65 years (22.0% vs 9.7%) compared to the normal group. In addition, women with VaIN2+ were more likely to report vaginal symptoms or signs (33.7% vs 20.6%), whereas a history of hysterectomy was less frequently reported in this group (12.5% vs 21.5%). Furthermore, both the detection rate of HPV infection (92.4% vs 79.9%) and the prevalence of multiple HPV infections (26.5% vs 20.0%) were higher in the VaIN2+ group. Although the proportion of women with ASCUS cytology was similar between the two groups (27.0% vs 25.5%), a higher percentage of women with worse than ASCUS cytology was observed in the VaIN2+ group (45.5% vs 20.3%). Meanwhile, concurrent cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 2/3 (21.5% vs 7.9%) and concurrent cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) (36.3% vs 18.1%) were more prevalent in the VaIN2+ group, whereas concurrent CIN1 was less common (17.4% vs 21.3%). The proportion of women with concurrent vulvar lesions (including vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia and vulvar squamous cell carcinoma) was also slightly higher in the VaIN2+ group (6.2% vs 4.6%).

Comparison of the training and validation sets

After stratified random sampling, the study population was split into a training set (70%, n = 2283) and a validation set (30%, n = 976). The proportion of VaIN2+ was highly consistent between the two sets; no significant differences were detected in the distribution of age, symptoms or signs, history of hysterectomy, HPV infection, cytological results, or cervical and vulvar pathological results (Table 2). This balanced distribution of key characteristics ensures that the nomogram developed from the training set can be reliably validated in the validation set.

Selection and analysis of predictors

In the training set, HPV infections were reclassified to enhance the granularity of predictor selection. To ensure statistical reliability, HPV genotypes with more than 10 positive cases in the VaIN2+ group were retained as individual variables, while those with fewer cases were categorized as other HPV infections. The baseline characteristics of the training set were similar to those of the total population (Table S1), showing consistent trends across age, symptoms and signs, hysterectomy history, HPV infection, cytology, and concurrent lesions.

All candidate variables were subjected to LASSO regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation, which selected 21 variables associated with VaIN2+ (Figure S1) at the optimal penalty parameter (lambda.min = 0.002358015). These variables were then entered into logistic regression analysis, which further identified 10 predictors for VaIN2+ (Table 3).

Compared with women aged <30 years, those aged 60–64 years and ≥65 years had a 2.125‐fold (95% CI, 1.041–4.341) and 3.906‐fold (95% CI, 1.901–8.029) increased risk of VaIN2+, respectively. In terms of HPV infections, women infected with HPV16, HPV58, HPV56, and HPV35 had a 2.997‐fold (95% CI, 2.352–3.819), 1.454‐fold (95% CI, 1.013–2.086), 1.860‐fold (95% CI, 1.138–3.039), and 2.438‐fold (95% CI, 1.145–5.190) increased risk of VaIN2+, respectively, compared to those not infected with these specific genotypes. Conversely, HPV39 infection was inversely associated with VaIN2+ (0.438‐fold; 95% CI, 0.237–0.810). Multiple HPV infections did not emerge as a statistically significant risk factor.

Notably, although univariate analysis (Table S1) revealed no significant difference in the proportion of women with ASCUS cytology or concurrent vulvar lesions between the normal and VaIN2+ groups, and even lower proportions of women with concurrent CIN1 and hysterectomy history in the VaIN2+ group, the logistic regression identified all these variables as independent risk factors. ASCUS cytology and worse than ASCUS cytology were associated with a 1.579‐fold (95% CI, 1.235–2.018) and 2.498‐fold (95% CI, 1.945–3.210) increased risk of VaIN2+, respectively. Concurrent CIN1, CIN2/3, or CSCC were associated with a 2.123‐fold (95% CI, 1.544–2.920), 3.676‐fold (95% CI, 2.570–5.258), and 2.764‐fold (95% CI, 1.966–3.886) higher risk, respectively. Concurrent vulvar lesions were also associated with a 1.569‐fold higher risk (95% CI, 1.014–2.428), and a history of hysterectomy was associated with a 1.587‐fold higher risk (95% CI, 1.139–2.212).

In addition, we performed two sensitivity analyses to verify the robustness of the predictive effect of cytology for VaIN2+. As shown in Table S2, after adding interaction terms between hysterectomy history and cytology, both “Hysterectomy × ASCUS” (p = 0.017) and “Hysterectomy × Worse than ASCUS” (p < 0.001) were statistically significant, indicating that hysterectomy history has an effect‐modifying influence on the strength of the association between cytology and VaIN2+. Given this effect modification, we further performed a subgroup analysis using the same set of covariates as in Table 3 to assess whether cytology remained predictive of VaIN2+ in women with an intact cervix. As shown in Table S3, the association between cytology and VaIN2+ was attenuated but remained statistically significant in this subgroup. Specifically, the OR for ASCUS cytology decreased from 1.579 to 1.315 (p = 0.047; 95% CI, 1.003–1.723), and the OR for worse than ASCUS cytology decreased from 2.498 to 1.977 (p < 0.001; 95% CI, 1.505–2.596).

Development and validation of the prediction model

Based on the 10 predictors, we developed a nomogram for estimating the individual risk of VaIN2+ in outpatients (Figure 2), where each predictor is assigned a specific point value (Table S4). The total point score is derived by adding the scores of all variables, and this score is then projected downward to the bottom axis of the nomogram to obtain the corresponding predicted probability of VaIN2+. A higher total score indicates a greater risk of VaIN2+.

We evaluated this 10‐predictor model's performance in both the training and validation sets. As shown in Figure 3A,B, the model demonstrated good discriminatory ability for identifying women with VaIN2+, with AUCs of 0.771 (95% CI, 0.751–0.790) in the training set and 0.791 (95% CI, 0.763–0.820) in the validation set. For calibration, the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was not significant in either set (training: χ 2 = 15.438, p = 0.051; validation: χ 2 = 4.187, p = 0.840), and the Brier scores were 0.184 and 0.175, respectively, confirming good calibration. The calibration curves also closely approximated the diagonal line in both sets (Figure 3C,D), further supporting the consistency between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

Next, decision curve analysis was used to assess the model's clinical utility. As depicted in Figure 3E,F, the horizontal dashed line represents the net benefit of assuming no woman has VaIN2+ and avoiding colposcopic examinations altogether, whereas the solid light orange line represents the net benefit of presuming all women have VaIN2+ and performing comprehensive vaginal assessments. Across a wide range of threshold probabilities from 10% to 70%, the model provided a greater net clinical benefit than both reference strategies in the training and validation sets. These results confirm that our prediction model possesses promising clinical application value and can assist clinicians in making better diagnostic decisions.

DISCUSSION

As a relatively uncommon disease of the lower genital tract, VaIN2+ has long received less attention than cervical lesions. 3 , 9 The low population incidence of VaIN2+ renders targeted mass screening cost‐ineffective, precluding its implementation in clinical practice. Together with the absence of specific clinical presentation, this leads to nearly all VaIN2+ cases being discovered incidentally during colposcopic and histological examinations. 1 , 9 , 14 This passive detection pattern highlights the urgent need for developing risk assessment tools. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical characteristics of women with VaIN2+ from a large colposcopy clinic population and identified 10 independent risk factors with predictive value. Based on these, we developed a nomogram prediction model to individually assess the risk of VaIN2+, thereby supporting early identification of high‐risk individuals and facilitating more precise clinical interventions.

In most prior studies, older age has been recognized as a risk factor for VaIN2+. 5 , 8 , 15 , 16 VaIN2+ is most commonly diagnosed in women in their 40s to 50s. 5 , 8 , 16 In this study, the mean age of women with VaIN2+ was 55.6 years, significantly higher than that of women without vaginal lesions. Notably, on further age stratification, we found that the proportion of women with VaIN2+ spanning the 40–49 and 50–59 age ranges was lower than that in women without vaginal lesions. The increased risk of VaIN2+ actually emerged only after the age of 60. We found that ages 60–64 and, especially, ≥65 were significantly associated with an elevated risk of VaIN2+. This finding warrants clinical attention, as the current detection of VaIN2+ relies heavily on cervical cancer screening, while routine screening is recommended to discontinue for women aged ≥65 years with prior negative results. 17 , 18 This means that women aged ≥65 years, who are at the highest risk of VaIN2+, may lose the opportunity to be detected upon exiting such screening. Therefore, continued clinical attention should be paid to the possibility of VaIN2+ in women aged ≥65 years to avoid missed diagnoses.

Given the critical role of HPV infection in driving VaIN2+ has long been established, research has recently shifted toward understanding the impact of specific genotypes. 19 , 20 In this study, 92.4% of women with VaIN2+ had HPV infection, and 26.5% had multiple infections. Among these, HPV16 was the most common genotype, followed by HPV58, HPV52, HPV33, HPV18, and HPV56. Consistent with previous studies, 4 , 6 , 8 , 20 , 21 we observed a remarkably high detection rate of HPV16 (64.7%) in women with VaIN2+, further confirming its extreme oncogenic risk in the lower genital tract. Unlike the expectedly high prevalence of HPV16, the second‐highest detection rate of HPV58 (9.6%) drew our particular attention. Although HPV58 is seldom emphasized in studies from North America and Europe, research from China has identified it as a major genotype in VaIN2+. 5 , 8 , 9 , 14 , 22 Multiple cervical studies have also reported HPV58 as the second most common genotype after HPV16 in high‐incidence areas of cervical cancer in China, 23 , 24 , 25 suggesting that HPV58 may have distinct regional characteristics. Its potential risk for vaginal lesions in the Chinese population therefore warrants greater attention. Consistent with previous reports, HPV52, HPV33, HPV18, and HPV56 were also identified as major infecting genotypes in this study. 5 , 20 , 21

However, it is crucial to recognize that the highly prevalent HPV genotypes in women with VaIN2+ do not necessarily serve as effective predictors, as these genotypes also show high detection rates among outpatients without vaginal lesions. Our findings revealed that although HPV52 and HPV18 ranked among the most common types, they were not independent risk factors for VaIN2+. In contrast, HPV16, HPV58, HPV56, and HPV35 were identified as independent predictors, even though some of these genotypes did not exhibit high overall prevalence. Of note, the observed inverse association between HPV39 infection and VaIN2+ must be interpreted with caution in light of the real‐world clinical setting. It may arise from the specific characteristics of the colposcopy referral population rather than representing a true biological protective effect. Besides, aligning with findings from Ao et al., 14 we observed that multiple HPV infection was not an independent risk factor for VaIN2+, despite its higher proportion in women with VaIN2+. These findings suggest that empirical risk assessment of vaginal lesions based solely on HPV genotype prevalence or the number of infections is unreliable. Instead, the specific predictive value of certain genotypes deserves greater clinical attention.

Because the entire lower genital tract is lined by continuous squamous epithelium and shares a common local microenvironment, the detection of VaIN2+ benefits considerably from the widespread implementation of cervical cancer screening. 14 Although current cytological tests primarily target cervical cells (with vaginal cuff epithelial cells sampled only in women who have undergone hysterectomy), previous studies have suggested that the sensitivity of cytology for detecting VaIN2+ appears comparable to that for cervical lesions. 5 , 22 , 26 In this study, the prevalence of ASCUS cytology was comparable between the VaIN2+ and normal groups, whereas a higher proportion of women with worse than ASCUS cytology was observed in women with VaIN2+. Notably, even ASCUS cytology, similar to higher‐grade cytological abnormalities, was identified as an independent risk factor for VaIN2+ in multivariable analysis. Additionally, sensitivity analyses further supported the predictive value of cytology for VaIN2+. Although sampling site differences modified the strength of the association between cytology and VaIN2+, the predictive value of ASCUS and higher‐grade cytology remained robust even among women with an intact cervix.

As previously mentioned, the vagina is anatomically continuous with the cervix and vulva. This structural continuity often leads to VaIN2+ coexisting with cervical lesions in most cases, which may even result from the spread of more prevalent cervical lesions. 14 Previous studies have consistently identified cervical lesions as a significant risk factor for VaIN2+, with the grade of cervical lesions showing a positive correlation with both the grade and incidence of VaIN. 8 , 14 , 22 This prompted us to investigate the predictive value of cervical lesions of varying severity for VaIN2+. Interestingly, we observed a lower proportion of concurrent CIN1, but higher proportions of concurrent CIN2/3 or CSCC, within women with VaIN2+. Nonetheless, in the final model, concurrent CIN1, CIN2/3, or CSCC all emerged as significant predictors for VaIN2+. This change in the direction of the effect for CIN1 may be attributable to the specific characteristics of the colposcopy referral population and to the unavoidable structural confounding between hysterectomy history and concurrent cervical lesions.

Shifting the focus to vulvar lesions, their association with VaIN2+ has rarely been reported, likely due to the low incidence of vulvar lesions themselves and the predominant localization of VaIN2+ in the upper third of the vagina. However, in this study, we noted a slightly elevated proportion of vulvar lesions in women with VaIN2+, which unexpectedly served as a predictor for VaIN2+.

A history of hysterectomy was also included in our model. Although hysterectomy effectively treats cervical cancer and its precursors, it does not eliminate persistent HPV infection in the lower genital tract, 27 , 28 , 29 and HPV may continue to induce vaginal lesions. Multiple prospective studies have confirmed that women who underwent hysterectomy for cervical precancerous lesions or cervical cancer face a higher risk of developing VaIN2+. 27 , 28 , 30 In this study, the proportion of women reporting a history of hysterectomy was lower in the VaIN2+ group than in those without vaginal lesions, but this does not mean that hysterectomy history can be overlooked. Similar to that of CIN1, the direction of the effect of hysterectomy history on VaIN2+ may also be influenced by structural confounding; however, after multivariable adjustment, hysterectomy history remained an independent predictor of VaIN2+. These findings further underscore the importance of comprehensive management for HPV‐related lower genital tract diseases, with particular attention to women with cervical lesions. Even low‐grade lesions such as CIN1, often regarded as self‐limiting and managed conservatively, should not be overlooked, as they also elevate the risk of VaIN2+. For women with CIN2/3 or CSCC, who face a substantially higher risk of VaIN2+, long‐term vaginal surveillance remains essential even after the primary cervical lesion has been treated.

To our knowledge, this is the first large‐scale VaIN2+ clinical study to incorporate specific HPV genotypes as predictors into a predictive model. Using a large colposcopy clinic cohort, we identified several independent risk factors of VaIN2+, including age, history of hysterectomy, specific HPV genotypes infection, cytological results, cervical lesions of different severity grades, and vulvar lesions. Based on these, we developed a 10‐predictor nomogram for VaIN2+ and evaluated its discrimination and calibration. The model demonstrated good and stable discriminative performance in both training and validation sets (AUCs >0.75), with predictions for VaIN2+ closely aligning with the actual observed outcomes. More importantly, decision curve analysis demonstrated that our model holds promising clinical utility. In current practice, due to the lack of targeted screening methods, guidelines recommend a comprehensive vaginal assessment during colposcopic examination of the cervix to detect potential vaginal lesions. 1 However, this approach faces two major challenges. First, it is a passive diagnostic strategy heavily reliant on the indication for cervical examination, making it difficult to promptly identify and manage VaIN2+ that occurs independently. Second, comprehensive vaginal assessment for all women requires substantial healthcare resources and demands a high level of expertise from colposcopists, which may be particularly challenging in resource‐limited settings or regions with few highly‐trained colposcopists. 10 Our model offers a promising approach to addressing these challenges. Given the widespread implementation of cervical cancer screening and the three‐tier prevention and treatment system, the information required for the model can be readily obtained in most cases and converted into a simple risk score. For women visiting gynecologic outpatient departments at tertiary care hospitals, even if they seek care for other conditions, clinicians can quickly assess their risk of VaIN2+ during the consultation as long as cervical cancer screening and relevant results are available, and can accordingly make more appropriate clinical decisions. Our model shows potential for initial risk stratification in gynecologic outpatient settings, enabling the proactive identification of high‐risk individuals from large outpatient populations without additional resource burden. The decision curve analysis demonstrated net benefit across a wide threshold probability range (10%–70%), suggesting that the model can accommodate different clinical preferences and decision‐making tendencies. Clinicians may choose a risk threshold appropriate to the clinical context for individualized management: patients with a lower predicted risk could continue routine follow‐up, whereas those with a higher predicted risk might warrant more proactive examination. Furthermore, the model can assist in colposcopy by identifying women with a high likelihood of VaIN2+ lesions, raising clinician vigilance, and guiding targeted examinations to reduce missed diagnoses.

Despite its strengths, this study has certain limitations. First, our model was developed and internally validated using data from a tertiary care hospital in China, still lacking external validation with datasets from other institutions. Second, women with a history of hysterectomy for indications other than cervical precancerous lesions or cancer were excluded; therefore, the applicability of the model in this population remains to be evaluated. Third, in this study, cytology samples were obtained from the cervix, except in women with a history of hysterectomy, for whom vaginal cuff samples were collected. This should be kept in mind when interpreting our findings. Fourth, given the wide diversity of HPV genotypes, this study could not cover all genotypes, and the potential risk associated with certain rare genotypes may not have been fully captured. Furthermore, because this study was based on real‐world data, although the model incorporated all clinical characteristics consistently available from outpatient records, the long study timeframe resulted in insufficient data quality for some potential influencing factors (e.g., smoking, immunosuppression), precluding their inclusion in the analysis. It should also be emphasized that the model is intended for women attending gynecologic outpatient departments who already have relevant results, and that it should not be generalized to routine health check‐up or primary care settings. We look forward to establishing multi‐center collaborations in the future, incorporating data from multiple institutions, and conducting prospective studies to further refine and validate the model, as well as to explore its applicability across diverse populations and clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

This study analyzed the clinical characteristics and HPV genotype distribution of women with VaIN2+ and developed a nomogram incorporating 10 predictors for individualized risk assessment. The model may serve as a preliminary screening tool in outpatient settings for identifying high‐risk individuals and as an adjunctive decision‐support tool during colposcopy, thereby promoting proactive detection and reducing missed diagnoses of VaIN2+.
