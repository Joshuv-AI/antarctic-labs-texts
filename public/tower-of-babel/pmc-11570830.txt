Tubular glycogen accumulation in acute kidney injury associated with red yeast rice supplement
Authors: Reina Miyazaki, Yasuhito Takahashi, Yuri Katayama, Tetsuya Kawamura, Nobuo Tsuboi, Takashi Yokoo
Published: 2024

To the Editor,

By 13 October 2024, 2549 cases of health hazards, including severe kidney dysfunction, related to the ingestion of red yeast rice supplements, had been identified in Japan. The Ministry of Health, Labour, and Welfare in Japan has identified that Penicillium adametzioides contaminates the cultivation of red yeast rice and produces puberulic acid, which causes kidney injury. Puberulic acid was first chemically recognized in 1931 [S1] and has only been studied as an antimalarial troponoid, with no mention of its effects on the human body, including renal toxicity [S2]. Therefore, the mechanisms involved in the acute tubular necrosis (ATN) associated with red yeast rice supplementation remain largely unknown. We discuss the mechanism of ATN through electron microscopy in two cases.

We recently reported two biopsy-proven cases of ATN associated with ingestion of a red yeast rice supplement manufactured by Kobayashi Pharmaceutical Co. [1, 2]. Details of the clinical course of the cases have been described previously [1, 2]. Figure 1 shows the electron microscopic findings of these two cases. Degeneration and necrosis, represented by loss of brush border, desquamation, or balloon formation, were prominent in the proximal tubules. In the cytoplasm of these cells, glycogen granules, which are normally barely present, were markedly aggregated, taken up into vacuoles, and shed into the ureteral lumen. Neither case showed evidence of abnormal mitochondrial or crista morphology.

The aetiology of Fanconi syndrome consists of the following six patterns: mitochondrial dysfunction, deposition of foreign substances, direct cell damage, impaired adherence junctions, and transport defects [3]. The electron microscopic findings identified in our cases showed no evidence of mitochondrial deformation, foreign bodies, or impaired adhesive junctions, suggesting the possibility of direct cellular damage and impaired glucose transport as the aetiology of Fanconi syndrome.

Glycogen accumulation in tubular cells may both cause injury and play a protective role [4]. Regarding the latter, when renal ischaemia occurs, activation of hypoxia-inducible factors causes glycogen to accumulate in tubular cells and switch to being used as a substrate for emergency adenosine triphosphate [5]. This suggests that accumulated glycogen may have a protective effect against the direct cellular damage to the tubules caused by puberulic acid. By contrast, the accumulation of glycogen granules is observed in tubular cells in patients with diabetes mellitus [S3], which may represent hypoxia and tubular injury [S4].

The pathogenesis in our cases is similar to that of Fanconi–Bickel syndrome, a glycogen storage disorder caused by glucose transporter type 2 (GLUT2) deficiency. This condition causes Fanconi syndrome, but sodium glucose transporter (SGLT)2 inhibitors are known to reduce renal cortical glycogen levels and improve renal proximal tubulopathy [S5][S6]. This suggests that puberulic acid may damage tubular cells due to glycogen accumulation, for example, by dysfunction of the tubular GLUT2 transporter. Accordingly, the reason why the proximal tubule S1/2 segment was selectively damaged in our cases may be explained by the differences in SGLT distribution and the metabolism of nephron segments. S1/2 segments are abundant in SGLT2, which reabsorbs 90% of glucose, whereas the S3 segment contains only SGLT1 [S7]. In addition, S1/2 segments are vulnerable to adenosine triphosphate depletion and stress because the potential abilities of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis on S1/2 are weaker than on S3 [S8].

In conclusion, we discuss the mechanisms potentially involved in tubular cell injury induced by puberulic acid based on electron microscopic findings. At present, it remains unclear whether the accumulation of glycogen granules in proximal tubules is injurious or protective. Further accumulation of cases and validation with experimental models are required to elucidate the mechanism of tubular damage specific to puberulic acid.
