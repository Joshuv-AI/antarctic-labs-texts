Ferns, marsupials, and Darwin's Wedge: Does competitive displacement really explain macroevolutionary patterns in ferns?
Authors: Jacob S Suissa
Published: 2026

Darwin's metaphors are palpable. The tangled bank, struggle for existence, and tree of life command comprehension of his arguments. One of his most profound metaphors is the likening of competition to a “yielding surface, with ten thousand sharp wedges” (Darwin, 1859, p. 67). In this metaphor, nature (the surface) is overly crowded with species (the wedges). As one wedge is struck, others are affected to varying degrees; and one will survive only if another is directly outcompeted. In this scenario, natural selection is the hammer that strikes the wedge—a driver of new adaptations that allow some species to conquer others. While competition between individuals of the same species is ubiquitous, this metaphor for competitive displacement between species is often invoked to describe long‐term evolutionary trends between lineages (Strotz and Lieberman, 2023).

In the narrative of evolutionary history, there are certain lineages to which we hand the short end of the proverbial stick—or the loose wedge on the surface. American marsupials are among the examples that come to mind (Marshall et al., 1982). Their story follows that during the Great American Biotic Interchange (GABI) northern placental mammals outcompeted the South American marsupials due to their superior reproduction and a slew of other adaptations. It is a tale of conquest through competition (Gould, 1983). The time period of the GABI was surely impactful on South America, as roughly half of its extant fauna is North American in origin and, many southern forms went extinct around this time (Carrillo et al., 2020). But does the decline of South American marsupials inherently imply that they were directly outcompeted and displaced?

Not necessarily.

Macroevolutionary trends showing the decline of one lineage and rise of another are often used as evidence for competitive displacement—a lineage replacing another in an environment, through direct competition (Sepkoski, 1996). With a reference to Darwin's “wedge,” Sepkoski (earlier [Benton, 1987]) describes this macroevolutionary pattern as a “double wedge” since it looks like two tip‐to‐tip wedges in a lineage through time plot. However, these patterns do not intrinsically imply competition occurred (Gould and Calloway, 1980; Simberloff, 1984; Lidgard et al., 2021); indeed in 1987 Benton explains that even if “a ‘double‐wedge’ pattern is demonstrated in the fossil record, it could be a chance association of two taxa that never interacted.” Even between living taxa, confirming competitive processes remains quite difficult (Harper, 1977; Vorburger and Ribi, 1999; Harpole and Tilman, 2007; Sakarchi and Germain, 2023); a difficulty that is compounded when trying to attribute causal ecological processes to correlative time series data (Strotz and Lieberman, 2023). Even if we restrict the data to correlational macroevolutionary patterns, some hypotheses receive stronger support than others.

In the case of South American marsupials, more recent evidence suggests that their decline was diachronous, rather than synchronous, with the timing of placental migration (López‐Aguirre et al., 2017; Tarquini et al., 2022). Instead of competitive displacement, the extinction of South American marsupials was more correlated with major ecological upheaval, like climatic changes (Freitas‐Oliveira et al., 2024) or even potential asteroid impacts (Carrillo et al., 2020). These patterns suggest that, while there was likely a plurality of causes for marsupial decline during the GABI, the macroevolutionary patterns do not favor exclusive competitive inferiority and displacement as a direct and ultimate cause.

I am not a zoologist, and I am perhaps ill‐fitted to discuss matters of mammalian evolution. As a botanist I can speak more to plants and their history. Among vascular land plants, the ferns (here loosely defined as free sporing euphyllophytes) are often likened to the marsupials, designated as loose wedges on the ecological surface because they lack seeds, flowers, and other physiological traits relative to seed plants. Based on dated phylogenies and fossils, ferns originated during the Devonian period >400 million years ago (Rothwell, 1987; Testo and Sundue, 2016; Noetinger et al., 2021; Nitta et al., 2022; Doran and Tomescu, 2025). Their story rings a familiar tone: ferns had their heyday in the Carboniferous period (~360 mya [Rothwell et al., 2008]) and then—like the South American marsupials with the northern placentals—were outcompeted by the superior seed plants. For instance, in an exploration of water use efficiency (WUE) in ferns and seed plants, Yang et al. (2021) concluded that “WUE in seed plants was superior to that in ferns," which provided “them with a competitive advantage under water deficit absent in ferns”, and that “fern species compete weakly.” Likewise, Su et al. (2023) examined root anatomy of vascular plants and hypothesized that the suberin layer (a protective layer around the vascular tissues), provides seed plants with “a decisive selective advantage over ferns in arid environments, resulting in the decline of ferns and the rise of gymnosperms.” And Meade et al. (2021) touch on the grandest argument of all, the supposed inferiority of fern reproduction in relation to seed plants, “The origin of the seed… in the Devonian period, c. 360 million years ago, was a key event in land plant evolution leading to a fundamentally new kind of reproductive strategy that overcame the evolutionary bottleneck of pteridophytic ‘free sporing’ reproduction.”

I highlight these quotes only to articulate a broader point about our collective view of evolutionary trends and the role of direct competition. I do not believe that the argument of the inferior fern is an antiquated strawman, but a narrative that persists into recent literature. It is part of a theme that leans into our biases about progress in evolution (Condamine et al., 2020; McDaniel, 2021), and how the new can only flourish at the expense of the old. These ideas of inferiority and competition through conquest permeate textbooks (e.g., Willis and McElwain, 2014), introductory classrooms (my own included, although sparingly I hope), and our general thinking about the role of interspecific competition in extinction (Simberloff, 1982, 1984; Raup, 1992). While cumulative selection, new adaptive forms, and competitive displacement are forces that can shape local communities, they do not always explain long term trends, given shifting selection pressures and idiosyncratic historical events. To be clear, I am not arguing that the seed, hydraulic innovations, and anatomical adaptations of seed plants do not have value, or that they did not contribute to seed plant diversification and ability to grow in new ecosystems. Rather, I ask whether this came at the expense of the ferns and free‐sporing vascular plants through direct competition? Here, I argue that fern evolutionary history is often misrepresented—despite numerous studies suggesting the contrary. Their evolutionary history is not marred by limits, lacks, or “less thans.” The fern story during the origin of seed plants is not one of wholesale destruction through direct competitive displacement, but reflects dynamic fluxes during ecological change. I end by argueing that ferns are an evolutionary success story, persisting and diversifying into one of the most species rich and diverse groups of vascular plants. My general goal in arguing these points is to question the interpretations of competitive displacement as the primary explanation for these macroevolutionary patterns in hopes to broaden the discussion for further inquiry.

Narratives of evolutionary hierarchy are not simply pedantic, but have tangible effects on how we teach, conduct research, and how we rank organisms in the polity of nature. When we teach from the design stance of progressive evolution and interspecific competitive displacement as the primary factor in the double wedge pattern, we rob students of a deep understanding of evolution, extinction, and natural selection (similar to teaching teleological adaptation [Kampourakis, 2020; Ginnobili et al., 2022]). This mindset can also reflect our research agendas, designing projects aimed to “find the traits” that led to the superior lineage, or affecting our interpretation of results (e.g., this trait allowed lineage A to outcompete lineage B, without direct study of competition). Of course, competition occurs, we don't have to look beyond our garden beds to see this (and many of the hypotheses of invasion biology deal with competition [Enders et al., 2020]). But it is not the only cause of extinction and the double wedge pattern (Lidgard et al., 2021) or ecological overlap (Chesson, 2000; Stuart and Losos, 2013). Appreciating the plurality of processes that could explain macroevolutionary patterns and extinction allows us to ask more relevant and insightful questions about the biology of organisms, their evolutionary history, and their potential fate.

THE FALL FROM GRACE, OR SO WE THOUGHT

The argument of evolutionary competitive displacement rests on the double wedge pattern, where declines in one lineage correspond with the rise of another (Sepkoski, 1996). The claim that ferns were outcompeted during the rise of seed plants is based on the apparent pattern of the decline of fern diversity when seed plants evolved. What does the fossil record actually show during this time?

The most cited paper for this narrative is Niklas et al. (1983). In an exceptional approach, they tallied up fossil plants and showed that extinct fern diversity peaked ~360 million years ago. Fern diversity seems to dip around 250 million years ago and, based on the same figure that is when seed plants were on the rise. Since this pattern was observed, subsequent papers used this to suggest that the Paleozoic ferns went extinct due to direct competition with seed plants.

There are several issues with this perpetuated interpretation. Niklas et al. (1983) do make the claim that the “appearance of a new plant grade was associated with a levelling off and subsequent decline in the number of species in the preceding dominant group,” suggesting evidence of competitive displacement. However, while this process was hypothesized for other lineages, they never explicitly stated that competitive displacement occurred between ferns and seed plants. Rather, they claim that the supposed decline of fern floras and rise of seed plant floras did not show the exact same double wedge patterns as other lineages, suggesting the fern to seed plant “transition” does not match macroevolutionary expectations of competitive displacement. A careful look at Figure 1 in Niklas et al. (1983), shows that in the Mesozoic era (before flowering plants evolved) ferns were just as diverse as the seed plants and, as a whole, were more diverse than any one singular seed plant lineage. Even if we assume competitive displacement as the cause of this pattern—which we know is difficult to explain through macroevolutionary data (Strotz and Lieberman, 2023)—its foundational correlative assumption (proper timing) does not even seem to hold in the initial description of Paleozoic fern evolution. But it was perpetuated nonetheless.

Of course, some fern lineages went extinct during this time, but Niklas et al. (1983) go on to explain that the decrease in fern diversity “coincides with environmental changes of the late Palaeozoic and earliest Mesozoic.” This suggests that larger transitions from fern to seed‐plant dominated ecosystems may be “a function of the partial extinction of [ferns and lycophytes] followed by the radiation of members of [seed plants] into the vacated ecological space” (Niklas et al., 1983). While Niklas et al. (1983) do not highlight specific environmental changes, the Permian period saw a shift in the global landscape. By 300 mya the supercontinent Pangea was formed and moving northwards (Vázquez et al., 2010; Willis and McElwain, 2014). The early Permian was a time of rapid, and spatially heterogeneous, climatic change. Large equatorial regions experienced general drying with greater temperature fluctuations and seasonality (Tabor and Poulsen, 2008). This was all occurring in, and contributing to, the Permian mass extinction (Nowak et al., 2019). Like the fall of southern marsupials, the patterns of ancient fern decline may track more with climatic shifts than to direct clade displacement through competition.

Fine‐scale analyses of free‐sporing vascular plant diversity during the Carboniferous and Permian further reveal more nuanced patterns that may not support the claim of competitive displacement. DiMichele et al. (2001a) highlighted several interesting patterns about plants in the Paleozoic period. Across different biomes and land masses, ferns did not simply experience a wholesale disappearance as seed plants evolved but instead co‐occurred with them in different regions. For instance, during the Pennsylvanian subperiod of the Carboniferous (323–298 Mya) in the Euramerican supercontinent, swampland ecosystems were dominated by free‐sporing towering tree‐sized ferns, lycopsid trees, and seed plants, co‐existing side‐by‐side for 30 million years. Similar patterns occurred in Angara (northeast of Euramerica) and Gondwana (the southernmost supercontinent), which had distinct floras but showed many sites of co‐occurring seed‐plants, ferns, and other spore‐bearing plants. In some cases, ecosystems showed a flipped pattern, transitioning from seed‐plant dominated ecosystems to fern and lycophyte dominated ones. Indeed, even between the early and late Carboniferous, free‐sporing plant and seed plant communities interspersed, with a dramatic turn over in community assemblages (Willis and McElwain, 2014).

Carboniferous ferns, of course, do not comprise a monophyletic entity (Rothwell, 1999). This brings two important points. First, the lineages leading to extant ferns (Polypodiopsida) may represent a small fraction of the distinct Carboniferous lineages that we could refer to as ferns. This may be analogous to the subset of animal body forms that survived through the Cambrian period; although some of these animals may be assigned to stem offshoots of modern lineages (Briggs, 2015). Many of the Paleozoic Era lineages that we could call ferns did go extinct (many seed plant lineages did also). This interpretation is bolstered by more recent studies integrating fossils and molecular phylogenetics, which found that competitive displacement does not explain extinction rates in ferns (Lehtonen et al., 2017). Rather, they predict that fern extinction patterns most likely followed the “Court Jester” hypothesis, which suggests that trends of speciation and extinction are more strongly correlated with random and extrinsic abiotic environmental factors, not biotic interactions (Barnosky, 2001). Second, many modern fern lineages were already radiating during the Carboniferous (Rothwell, 1987). Of course, these are the lineages that did not go extinct; rather they persisted through seed plant evolution and the Permian drying events, later giving rise to our modern fern flora. A question worth exploring is, why?

THE RISE OF SEED PLANTS: A STORY OF DIVERSIFICATION BY DISPERSAL?

Of course, seed plants have increased in diversity over time, leading to their dominance today (in terms of biomass, the boreal conifers pack a powerful punch, and the flowering plants take the crown for species number). How do we explain the success of seed plants if not through conquest of the previous lineages?

The interpretation has been that the aforementioned seed‐plant innovations led to their superiority in direct ecological contest with ferns (Condamine et al., 2020; Meade et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2021; Su et al., 2023). Again, I do not contest that the hydraulic, anatomical, and reproductive traits that evolved in seed plants were important in their evolutionary history, they likely facilitated their survival in new landscapes. However, the evolution of previously adapted traits that predispose a lineage to later success does not inherently imply that they diversified in direct competition with other co‐occurring lineages.

A pillar of competitive displacement is that the ecosystem is crowded, and the two competing lineages occupy the same niche. But the Carboniferous landscape may not have been that crowded. The evolution of new plant forms during the Paleozoic is hypothesized to be made possible by “what was initially an ecologically undersaturated terrestrial world” (DiMichele et al., 2001b); dryer environments were relatively open ecosystems. Seed plants co‐occurred with free‐sporing plants in tropical wet biomes of the Carboniferous, and then expanded in the Permian (Willis and McElwain, 2014). During this time, seed plants diversified into open and drier sites, against the backdrop of global drying (Montañez et al., 2007; Willis and McElwain, 2014). These drier communities may have populated a relatively bare surface, which allowed seed plants to evolve away from, and in parallel with, the fern and lycophyte dominated communities. If seed plants diversified in new drier environments, they were not directly competing with ferns (i.e., limiting their access to resources).

Putting the pieces together, macroevolutionary patterns, phylogenetic evidence, and the fine‐scale fossil record do not show expected evolutionary trends of clade replacement and competitive displacement (Niklas et al., 1983; DiMichele et al., 2001a; Lehtonen et al., 2017; Nowak et al., 2019). Although innovations of the seed plants such as increased water use efficiency and the seed, facilitated ecological expansion, these traits likely did not directly drive the decline of ferns through competition. Instead, some fern lineages may have gone extinct in particular ecosystems because they were unable to persist through environmental perturbations, like global drying in the Permian. Thus, the decline of certain lineages may reflect reduced fitness under changing local environments, rather than direct competition with seed plants. To draw a parallel, no one would argue that the rodent‐sized mammals of the Cretaceous directly outcompeted the dinosaurs. They had previously evolved traits (size, burrowing, thermal tolerance, etc. [Slater, 2013; Lovegrove et al., 2014; Pinkert et al., 2025]) that facilitated their survival during the KPg mass extinction. Perhaps this is what is meant in the papers explaining the rise of seed plants and the decline of ferns, but this is often not articulated clearly. In this alternate interpretation of Paleozoic plants, the rise of seed plants may not have been a story of conquest through competition of ferns in situ, but rather a story of diversification by dispersal to, or exploration of, changing environments.

HOW CAN WE KNOW WHAT HAPPENED?

There are several points we must consider in understanding the causes underlying the macroevolutionary patterns of ferns. First, extinction from climatic changes is not mutually exclusive from extinction through competition, both could interact (Urban et al., 2012; Alexander et al., 2015). Likewise, the proximate and ultimate causes of extinction may vary, and are often difficult to tease apart (Simberloff, 1993). In a refined explanation of this point, Simberloff (1993) states “it is exactly like assigning death in some human diseases. The cause of many cancer deaths is listed on death certificates as ‘congestive heart failure’, but the patient was doomed by the cancer.”

To understand these processes, we need more robust and fine scale information. Since Niklas et al. (1983) originally state that the double wedge does not seem to pertain to fern–seed plant macroevolutionary patterns, it may be prudent to reinvestigate the patterns of Paleozoic fern diversity (Cleal and Cascales‐Miñana, 2014). Assessing paleontological data is inherently difficult due to temporal and preservation biases as well as taxonomic limitations in the fossil record. Global drying during the Permian may have also limited the probability of favorable fossilization in wetter sites (Preto et al., 2010), with the apparent effect of lowering the presence of ferns in the fossil record. Interestingly, recent reassessment of the fossil record shows that many plants may not have experienced such a dramatic loss during the Permian mass extinction; and ferns may have even seen an increase into the Triassic period (figure 6 in Nowak et al., 2019). Moreover, paleoecological analyses are revealing different physiological perspectives on Carboniferous pteridophytes, which are hypothesized to function much like “extant gymnospermous seed plants” (Wilson et al., 2017). With new analyses and conceptual advances, competitive clade displacement can be tested more robustly—albeit still with correlational data. Strotz and Lieberman (2023) lay out possible ways to do so, which include examining: (1) functional trait data through time; (2) fine‐scale spatial overlap of putative competitors; and (3) complete “birth to death” lineage dynamics including time of first appearance of competitors. They also call for a pluralistic view and the need to examine “non‐competitive factors” including shifts in the abiotic environment. Even if a double wedge pattern is observed, we must not simply assume that competitive displacement is the cause. We ought to critical interrogate these patterns before assigning cause.

THE SECOND ACT: RADIATION OF MODERN FERN LINEAGES

I have one more point to raise for why competitive displacement may not be the best supported hypothesis for the macroevolutionary trends of ferns. If we still consider the hypothesis that ferns were outcompeted by “dominant” seed plants through the wedge of competition, would we not expect this to continue through time? Ferns still lack the traits that were attributed to their supposed competitive displacement. If superior traits of seed plants facilitated the fall of ferns, then the rise of angiosperms could have surely spelled their complete extinction. Relative to gymnosperms, hydraulic and reproductive traits are greater in the flowering plants. They can have faster reproductive cycles, more maternal investment in progeny, and higher rates of photosynthesis and water movement. What was the fate of ferns during the rise of flowering plants?

Time calibrated phylogenetic analyses of ferns and flowering plants suggest that the divergences between most lineages that gave rise to the over 11,000 extant fern species are younger than many flowering plant divergences (Schneider et al., 2004). This analysis is in accord with the observation that ferns were not wholly displaced as flowering plants evolved and diversified in the Cretaceous period (~145 mya). Rather, during this time, ferns showed patterns of diversification in the new angiosperm forests. Ferns then diversified again (perhaps as epiphytes) in the Cenozoic era (Schuettpelz and Pryer, 2009). While epiphytic ferns are not necessarily speciating at faster rates than terrestrial lineages (Lehtonen et al., 2017; Testo and Sundue, 2018), it is hypothesized that the new niche of the angiosperm canopy opened up even broader opportunities for the lineage (Watkins and Cardelús, 2012). This is reflected in the observation that roughly 30% of all living ferns are epiphytes, and some of the most species‐rich groups of ferns occupy this habit. While inferences about the direct causes of their speciation from macroevolutionary data should also be cautioned, their decline can surely be excluded as an observed pattern in more recent geologic time (Lehtonen et al., 2017; Azevedo‐Schmidt et al., 2024). These works help shift our views of ferns as a simply outcompeted lineage, to one that highlights the dynamism of their history and their apparent evolutionary success.

Ferns are now the second most species‐rich lineage of vascular plants, well behind the flowering plants, but far greater in number than the ~1000 gymnosperm species. They grow in varied ecosystems from deserts to rainforests (Holttum, 1938; Weigand et al., 2020). While modern ferns include a subset of their Carboniferous relatives, they have persisted for 400 million years, and the lineages giving rise to extant groups survived every major mass extinction over this time period (Azevedo‐Schmidt et al., 2024). Some ferns form complex cross‐kingdom interactions (Li et al., 2018), survive extreme drought (Holmlund et al., 2020), develop novel chemical defenses against herbivores (Wei et al., 2023), and have evolved unique chemistry of their cell walls (Ali et al., 2025). Ecologically, some ferns are also disturbance colonizers (Thomas and Cleal, 2022). When changes in the landscape occur—from small scale disturbances, like tree falls and wildfires, to large scale cataclysmic destructions like asteroid impacts—ferns are some of the first vascular plants to colonize the landscape (Azevedo‐Schmidt et al., 2024). This ecological phenomenon manifests itself in the fossil record as the “fern spike,” a stark increase in fern spore diversity after mass extinctions, especially the Cretaceous‐Paleogene period (Tschudy and Tschudy, 1985). In a modern ecological context, fern colonizers act as “ecological filters”, helping determine which tree seedlings will establish as well as their density and distribution—thus having implications for the types of forests that will establish there (George and Bazzaz, 1999a, 1999b). Azevedo‐Schmidt et al. (2024) argued that a “competition‐based view of ferns is outdated.” We ought to heed their advice.

Fern reproduction has perhaps been the most criticized aspect of their biology. Simply because ferns lack seeds and fruits does not mean they are inferior. Yes, the seed, fruit, and flower have helped some angiosperms reach impressive levels of diversity and species richness, but the free‐sporing fern life cycle is an impressive evolutionary strategy in its own right. The separation of gametophyte and sporophyte generations boasts many potential ecological advantages (Watkins and Mack, 2007; Watkins et al., 2007; Pittermann et al., 2013). Since gametophytes of homosporous ferns are potentially bisexual, the free‐sporing fern life cycle is often interpreted as simple, and a route for severe inbreeding (reviewed in Haufler et al., 2016). Bateman and DiMichele (1994) explain “homospory is generally treated as a simple life history.” Despite the title of their article and their argument for the importance of heterospory, they go on to say that “this view underestimates the collective diversity of homosporous systems. Both sporophytes and gametophytes can exhibit complex arrays of developmental and biochemical controls of sex ratio and gamete production” (Bateman and DiMichele, 1994). While ferns may be able to self‐fertilize under severe situations (Sessa et al., 2016), most do not. Rather they have sophisticated mechanisms to chemically castrate their neighbors to ensure outcrossing (Hornych et al., 2021). Fern reproduction is more dynamic than often assumed. The research on fern ecology, physiology, and reproduction is making it increasingly difficult to reconcile the notion of the inferior fern. Watkins et al. (2007) have suggested that “modern, and indeed ancient, thinking would suggest that fern gametophytes are ephemeral, small, delicate plants that are relegated to permanently wet habitats. Such a nonsensical view has placed a considerable limitation on our understanding of the organismal biology of the ferns.”

REEVALUATING OUR METRICS OF “SUCCESS”

How do we qualify a lineage's success? We could measure this based on the number of species. But this is only one metric, and perhaps not really a good one for competitive success; Lidgard et al. (2021) explain “proportional species richness is… only a proxy for relative abundance, and probably even less so for dominance” (Lidgard et al., 2021). So then, what about number of individuals? Total biomass? Persistence and longevity of a lineage? Are humans a success story when our lineage is currently only composed of one species? I would argue that all of these, and perhaps more, seem like reasonable ways to quantify evolutionary success. We could flip the fern narrative on its head and say that ferns are an evolutionary success story because they have persisted and diversified without the improved anatomical, hydraulic, and reproductive traits of seed plants.

Marsupials and ferns, linked by little but our stamp of evolutionary dismissal, remind us of our biases in viewing evolution. We often equate the loaded term “complex” with better, more diversity with success, and try to find ways to explain how new evolutionary innovations lead to competitive displacement and the decline of previous more “primitive” forms. Yet evolution is not a ladder or a march toward perfection (McDaniel, 2021). Darwin's wedge is a powerful metaphor, especially for individuals within a species, but our modern view of evolution should caution us from invoking it wholesale for macroevolutionary patterns between clades. We ought to appreciate the plurality of causes for potential macroevolutionary patterns.

Maybe some light can be shed on the narrative by expanding the wedge metaphor. Darwin's ecological surface is not static or fixed, but rather dynamic and shifting. The surface itself could be viewed as an uneven tarp under tension and lineages are weighted balls upon it. They surely collide and compete, but the tarp itself can deform. The evolutionary fate of a lineage may be determined by undulations of the surface, rather than direct collision.
