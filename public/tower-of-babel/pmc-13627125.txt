Risk Factor Evaluation for Loose Body Following First-Time Patellar Dislocation in Pediatric Patients
Authors: David M. Kell, Kevin J. Orellana, Sarah Wetzel, Katherine L. Ashe, Sulagna Sarkar, J. Todd Lawrence, Jie C. Nguyen, Brendan A. Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe reported rate of osteochondral loose body (OCLB) formation following lateral patellar dislocation (LPD) and risk factors for occurrence vary widely in the literature. The purpose of this study is to identify demographic and anatomic risk factors for loose body and chondral injury within a consecutive series of patients following first-time LPD.MethodsA retrospective review was performed identifying a consecutive series of pediatric patients (≤18 ​years old) treated at a single tertiary care hospital from 2011 to 2021 after first-time LPD with postinjury magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Patients with recurrent dislocations; delayed imaging (>3 ​months after injury); subluxation without dislocation; or those with syndromic, congenital or neuromuscular etiologies of injury were excluded. Patient demographics, injury characteristics, and patellofemoral imaging measures were recorded. Descriptive and bivariate statistics were applied. A multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors for OCLB after first-time LPD.ResultsStudy criteria identified 360 patients (n ​= ​209, 59% female) with a total of 376 knees/cases analyzed, including 67 cases (17.8%) with loose body. In 88.1% of cases, the donor site of the loose body was identifiable with 56.7% (n ​= ​38) originating from the patella, 31.3% (n ​= ​21) originating from the femur, and 11.9% (n ​= ​8) originating from an unknown donor site. Of the OCLBs with an identifiable donor site, 96.6% (n ​= ​57) were full-thickness chondral injuries. A relatively decreased patellar height (mean Caton-Deschamps Index [CDI]: 1.19 for OCLB cases vs ​1.28 for non-OCLB cases, P ​< ​.001), spontaneous reduction method (P ​< ​.001), and older age (P ​= ​.03) were associated with a higher rate of loose body formation. A multivariable logistic regression identified that a 1-standard-deviation (SD) increase (0.18) in CDI led to a 33.1% decrease in the risk of loose body formation (odds ratio ​= ​0.669, P ​= ​.011).ConclusionsEach additional year of age was associated with a 30% increase in the odds of a loose body, and each 0.18 (1 SD) decrease in CDI with a 33% increase in odds for an OCLB after first-time dislocation. These findings serve to enhance risk-counseling for patients with patellar instability and provide guidance for expedited imaging in certain patients where findings may immediately impact clinical care.Key Concepts(1)Osteochondral loose bodies were present in 17.8% of pediatric first-time lateral patellar dislocations.(2)Osteochondral loose bodies originated from the patella in 56.7% of cases.(3)A relatively decreased patellar height, spontaneous reduction method, and older age were associated with a higher rate of loose body formation.Level of EvidenceIII, Retrospective Cohort Study

Introduction

Traumatic lateral patellar dislocation (LPD) is a common injury in pediatric patients and can have short- and long-term impacts on the quality of life and joint health [[1], [2], [3]]. Enhancing our understanding of risk factors for occurrence, particularly those impacting treatment decision-making, is a critical aspect of managing and counseling patients with first-time LPD. Currently, the presence of osteochondral loose body (OCLB) remains the only consensus indication for acute surgical intervention following first-time dislocation. This is because patients with OCLBs treated nonoperatively likely demonstrate inferior subjective and functional outcomes and a higher risk for osteoarthritis [[3], [4], [5], [6]].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the optimal imaging modality for assessing articular anatomy and the presence of OCLBs [7]. MRI demonstrates high sensitivity in detecting not only loose bodies but also other capsular and ligamentous injuries following acute dislocation [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Additionally, many of the anatomical risk factors for patellar instability (including extensor mechanism lateralization, patellar height, and trochlear dysplasia), which guide risk counseling and surgical decision-making, are more reliably assessed with advanced imaging over standard radiographs. However, despite general consensus regarding the value of MRI following LPD, timely access to orthopaedic care and advanced imaging and its high cost may require more judicious use of advanced imaging in some resource-limited areas [12,13]. Therefore, improved understanding of factors associated with loose body formation in pediatric patients may aid in expediting timely imaging in patients at greatest risk for occurrence.

While prior work has identified multiple factors that may contribute to risk for OCLB formation following LPD, many have been completed in small and/or nonconsecutive cohorts or within heterogeneous populations of both pediatric and adult patients or those with both first-time and recurrent patellar instability. The purpose of this study is to explore the demographic and anatomic risk factors for OCLB after first-time dislocation within a consecutive series of pediatric patients following first-time LPD. We hypothesized that patients whose knees had more neutral patellar alignment would be more likely to sustain LPDs with associated loose body formation [14].

Methods

Cohort identification

After institutional review board approval, a retrospective review of a consecutive series of pediatric patients (aged 0-18 years) was performed at a single institution from 2011 to 2021. During the study period, MRI after first-time dislocation was the standard of care at our institution. Patients with MRI completed within 3 ​months of injury who did not sustain a subsequent dislocation were included. Patients were excluded if they had a clinical evaluation and/or imaging that was more consistent with maltracking/subluxation rather than dislocation; if they had a prior history of LPD (recurrent instability); or if they had an underlying syndromic, congenital, or neuromuscular diagnosis. Additionally, patients with concomitant osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) lesions of the knee ​or other associated complex intra-articular injuries (eg, Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) or meniscal tears) were excluded. A total of 3837 patients were identified at our institution with an associated ICD-9 (718.36, 718.86, 836.3, 836.4, and 836.50) and ICD-10 codes (M22.0∗, M22.1X∗, M22.2X∗, M25.36∗, S83.0∗∗, and S83.1∗∗), of which 771 had an MRI within our database (Fig. 1). Based on the retrospective chart review, 360 of these MRIs followed a first-time frank dislocation within 3 ​months of the injury. Sixteen patients had nonsimultaneous bilateral first-time dislocations that met the inclusion criteria, for a total of 376 MRIs.Figure 1Patient inclusion and exclusion flow chart. Demographic and injury data collection

Variables recorded from chart review included age at the time of dislocation, race/ethnicity, sex, and insurance type (private/public). Injury characteristics examined included laterality, injury mechanism, sports participation at the time of injury (yes/no), method of patellar reduction, and the time elapsed from injury to orthopaedic consult and to MRI. Injury mechanism was categorized as “twisting,” “fall from standing,” “fall from height,” “collision,” “spontaneous,” or other/unavailable. Reduction method was recorded as spontaneous (no reduction performed), self-reduction by the patient, reduction by a medical provider (eg, athletic trainer or physician), other (eg, reduction by a parent), and unknown.

MRI assessment

Imaging evaluations were completed by two experienced reviewers (an orthopaedic resident and physician assistant with several years of experience in pediatric orthopaedics practice) after extensive clinical training by the senior author. Injury characteristics were compared with those in an attending radiologist's report. Conflicts were resolved by secondary evaluation by the study principal investigator. The presence of an OCLB was noted based on the presence of free-floating chondral or osteochondral fragments within the knee joint. OCLBs were recorded as originating from the femur (trochlea or condyle), patella (medial facet, median ridge, or lateral facet), or both. If the donor site of the osteochondral fragment was unclear, it was recorded as unknown. The Caton-Deschamps Index (CDI) was used to assess patellar height, while extensor mechanism malalignment was quantified using the tibial tubercle-to-trochlear groove (TT-TG) and the tibial tubercle-to-lateral trochlear ridge (TT-LTR) distances [[15], [16], [17]]. Trochlear morphology was characterized using the Dejour classification and Oswestry-Bristol Classification (OBC) systems [18,19]. Skeletal maturity was classified as open, closing, or closed based on the intensity of the signal at the distal femoral physis.

For each injury, a mid-sagittal slice was used to define the longitudinal axis of the femur and the center of curvature of the femoral condyle. The anterior-most and posterior-most points of the injury were measured as angular degrees from this axis. Points anterior to the longitudinal axis were recorded as positive values, while those posterior to the axis were recorded as negative values.

Statistical analysis

A three-dimensional reference model of a human femur was used as the anatomical template for lesion mapping [20]. The longitudinal axis of the femur was defined using the center of the model's femoral shaft, and a circle of best fit was measured around the femoral condyle to estimate its center of curvature. Lesion boundaries from all patients were projected onto this curvature, generating an angular interval for each OCLB along the condylar arc. The overlapping angular intervals across all patients were then aggregated to generate a heat map representing the relative frequency of lesion involvement at each location along the distal femoral condyle. The sulcus terminalis was identified based on an estimated intersection of the articular cartilage and Blumensaat's line along a sagittal slice of the femur to better contextualize the weight-bearing surface of the distal femur.

Descriptive statistics and bivariate analysis of each demographic, injury-related, and imaging variable were performed to compare the loose body and chondral injury cohorts as appropriate. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to assess risk factors for loose body formation with patient age at the time of injury, standardized CDI, TT-TG distance, reduction method (spontaneous reduction vs ​other), OBC (normal/shallow vs ​flat/severe), and skeletal maturity (open vs ​closing/closed physis) as predictors [21]. CDI was standardized via z-score standardization for the logistic regression to ensure a more intuitive interpretation of the odds ratio (OR). Analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Python version 3.9.24 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, USA).

Loose bodies following first-time LPD were identified in 17.8% (67/376) of cases. In 88.1% (n ​= ​59) of cases, the donor site of the loose body was identifiable, with 56.7% (n ​= ​38) originating from the patella, 31.3% (n ​= ​21) originating from the femur, and 11.9% (n ​= ​8) originating from an unknown donor site. All femoral loose bodies were full-thickness chondral injuries originating from the femoral condyle (21/21). The majority of patellar loose bodies were full-thickness injuries (36/38, 95%) originating from the medial facet (24/38, 63%) or median ridge (11/38, 29%). No risk factors were identified that indicated an increased risk of femoral vs patellar loose bodies. Bivariate analysis identified older age (P ​= ​.03), spontaneous reduction method (P ​< ​.001), and shorter elapsed time from injury to MRI (P ​< ​.05) as factors associated with the presence of a loose body. Our logistic regression identified that a 1-standard-deviation (SD) increase (0.18) in CDI led to a 33.1% decrease in the risk of loose body formation (OR ​= ​0.669, P ​= ​.011). A spontaneous reduction increased the odds of loose bodies (OR ​= ​5.9, P ​< ​.001). Additionally, the odds of OCLB increased by a factor of 1.3 for each increasing year of age (OR ​= ​1.30, P ​< ​.001). Articular lesions of the patella most commonly involved the medial patellar facet (128/165, 78%, Fig. 2). Articular injuries of the femur resulting in loose bodies were distributed across the arc of the distal femur, with a median angle 3.5° (interquartile range [IQR]: −6.0° to 12.5°) ​anterior to the femoral shaft axis (see Fig. 3). Loose bodies formed most commonly just anterior (8.0° [95% confidence interval [CI]: −12.7° to 11.2°], Fig. 3) to the longitudinal axis of the femoral shaft, near the sulcus terminalis, estimated at 3.7° anterior to the longitudinal axis of the femoral shaft, representing a potentially weight-bearing zone of injury with 62.3% of injuries spanning this point. Discussion

The presence of a loose body remains one of the few majority consensus indications for time-sensitive surgical intervention after LPD [12]. While prevalence and risk factors for occurrence have been proposed in the existing literature, the evidence is varied and limited by a lack of consistency in inclusion criteria in this heterogeneous patient population. This study sought to address this knowledge gap using a large, well-defined cohort of pediatric patients following clinical ​and imaging-confirmed first-time dislocation. We identified that loose bodies occur in nearly one-fifth of pediatric first-time dislocators and found that each additional year of age was associated with a 30% increase in the odds of a loose body, each 0.18 (1 SD) decrease in CDI was associated with a 33% increase in the odds of a loose body, and spontaneous reduction increased the risk of a loose body in first-time dislocations nearly 6-fold compared with all nonspontaneous reduction types (provider, self, or unreported). Additionally, we found that the majority of femoral loose bodies originated from an articular region slightly anterior to the longitudinal axis of the femur, while patellar lesions most commonly involved the medial facet. These findings serve to bolster the limited evidence base on this topic and enhance risk-counseling for these patients and families. Together, these factors support expedited imaging in high-risk patients to facilitate timely surgical intervention when necessary and may help to guide advanced imaging utilization in more resource-limited settings.

The prevalence of osteochondral fracture or loose body formation in the existing literature ranges from 11.5% to 35.2% [[22], [23], [24]]. Variation within the literature is likely related to differences in cohort inclusion criteria (acute vs recurrent dislocation) and the method of detection (MRI vs arthroscopic findings). Within our cohort of pediatric first-time dislocators, we identified a prevalence of 17.8% falling on the lower end of this range and close to the values observed in a systematic review ​[22,23]. Interestingly, the highest published prevalence of loose bodies following first-time dislocation has been observed in a similarly large adolescent cohort by İğrek et al. [24]. Their observed frequency (35.2%) was nearly twice that of other published data. However, an evaluation of their methodology revealed that their multicenter cohort only included patients seen in orthopaedic clinics, which may bias their cohort towards more clinically concerning cases of patellar dislocation, as imaging and referral practices may not be consistent across all involved institutions. Our study screened all patients with a patellar instability diagnosis at our institution, while the local standard of care dictated imaging in all cases of primary dislocation. Due to the size of our cohort and the consistency of a single center's referral patterns and use of advanced imaging after first-time dislocation, we believe our findings may be more generalizable to the entire population of pediatric first-time dislocators.

Our study findings identified older age at the time of injury and spontaneous patellar reduction as independent risk factors related to the risk of loose body formation following acute patellar dislocation. Regarding age, Yi et al. [25] identified via systematic review multiple studies indicating older age and skeletal maturity as risk factors for osteochondral injury following patellar dislocation. Our study builds on their analysis by confirming the association within a single, homogenous cohort defined by uniform diagnostic and inclusion criteria. The higher likelihood of osteochondral injury or loose body formation among older patients may be related to those patients having a lower prevalence of abnormal anatomic risk factors or due to increased strength, a higher participation rate in high-impact activities and exposure to greater forces during patellar dislocation events [26]. Spontaneous patellar reduction was also found to increase the risk of loose body formation in this pediatric cohort. Review of the literature did not identify prior work examining reduction method as a predictor of osteochondral injury for comparison; however, the uncontrolled nature of a spontaneous reduction is likely more forceful than a controlled reduction that gradually brings the knee into extension.

The relationship between patellar height and osteochondral injury or loose body has been investigated in multiple prior studies, but the findings regarding the nature of this risk have been variable [24,[27], [28], [29]]. In this study, multivariable analysis identified a relatively decreased CDI as a significant risk factor for loose body formation. We found that a 1-SD increase (0.18) in CDI led to a 33.1% decrease in the risk of loose body formation (OR ​= ​0.669, P ​= ​.011) following acute patellar dislocation. Patients with patella alta may be more easily reducible, while those with normalized patellar height require greater force or full knee extension to achieve successful patellar reduction. These factors may increase the likelihood of chondral injury and loose body formation during the reduction process.

Despite being a well-known risk factor for recurrent LPD, trochlear dysplasia, categorized using both Dejour classification and OBC, was not observed to be a significant risk factor for loose body. Prior work evaluating this as a factor ​has identified conflicting results among adolescent patellar instability cohorts. While Fones et al. [27] found that a higher sulcus angle increased the odds of osteochondral damage and fracture, İğrek et al. [24] observed no relationship between loose body occurrence and numerous continuous and categorical measures of trochlear dysplasia. Prior studies have demonstrated that Dejour classification lacks reliability in the setting of skeletally immature populations [30]. To our knowledge, our study is the first to evaluate OBC, which was developed in part to address the reliability concerns of Dejour classification. Nevertheless, neither classification appeared to relate to the occurrence of loose bodies in this cohort. It should be noted that Fones et al. [27] included patients with recurrent instability and therefore likely contained a high proportion of patients with anatomic risk factors for recurrence and cumulative injury that may not have occurred solely from a first-time dislocation. Clinical observations suggest that patients with anatomically normal trochlear morphology require more force to dislocate, which increases the relative risk of osteochondral injury and loose body formation from a single dislocation. Conversely, patients with anatomic factors that increase the risk of recurrent instability have abnormal anatomy that requires less force to dislocate the patella and therefore decreases the risk for osteochondral injury during dislocation.

Our evaluation of the articular region of injury suggests that the majority of patellar OCLBs originate from the medial facet (63%). A more detailed analysis of the femoral zone of injury relative to the longitudinal axis of the femur identified that femoral loose bodies originated from a location most commonly centered slightly anterior (∼9°) to the axis in a potentially weight-bearing zone of articular cartilage. Wang et al. [31] recently evaluated osteochondral fractures in patients with patellar instability and found that most occurred within 10 ​mm of the anterior margin of the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus and were most commonly 10 ​mm in the anterior-posterior dimension. We opted to utilize angular rather than length measurements due to the curved surface of the femoral condyle and utilized a static reference axis, given that the lateral meniscus is mobile and its position is affected by the knee flexion angle when the MRI is obtained. Despite using different reference points and metrics, these findings suggest similar sizes and positions of these osteochondral lesions of the femur.

This present study is not without limitations that are typical for retrospective, single-center studies. First, although we included a robust array of demographic and imaging characteristics in our evaluation, some factors previously explored in the literature (eg, ligamentous laxity [24,32]) could not be included due to inconsistent documentation of these factors across different providers. Second, the restriction of our cohort to first-time dislocators relied in part on the completeness of the medical record for reporting ​prior instability events. However, given our institutional protocol for timely MRI after the first dislocation, we felt this standardized the cohort and permitted a more accurate representation of rates and risk factors for loose bodies in the acute dislocation setting. This, in turn, minimizes confounding variables from cumulative or attritional injury from recurrent instability for which the value of repeated MRI after each occurrence is less clear [25,33,34]. Additionally, our MRI findings, including the thickness of chondral injury, were not confirmed arthroscopically. However, MRI is a well-validated imaging modality to detect and characterize chondral injury and allows us to capture a consecutive cohort of first-time dislocators regardless of surgical intervention and helps reduce selection bias toward ​patients that require surgery. While we acknowledge there were patients who experienced a first-time patellar dislocation who were not captured by our study criteria, it remains the standard of care at our institution to obtain an MRI following first-time dislocation over the study period and represents a small proportion of patients. This may lead us to overestimate the true prevalence of loose body formation after patellar dislocations, but this remains a limitation across the entire literature base on this topic. Finally, other factors exist that may be related to patellar dislocation and injury severity that were not captured routinely in our database such as femoral version, coronal alignment, and ligamentous laxity. However, we were able to identify and measure the risk factors that are routinely captured on standard first-dislocation imaging which enhances our paper's clinical applicability. Sagittal TT-TG distance, which has been explored in regard ​to its association with degenerative chondral changes and contact pressures in adult populations, may warrant future exploration in relation ​to loose body and chondral injury risk in patellofemoral instability (PFI), but validation and normative assessment of this measure in pediatric populations should be a prerequisite to its inclusion in risk factor evaluation.

The findings of this study enhance our understanding of factors associated with loose body formation following primary dislocation in a well-defined, consecutive series of pediatric patients with this condition. We believe these findings will enhance the ability of providers to counsel pediatric patients and their families in this setting regarding the likelihood of severe articular injury that may require surgical intervention. Although the authors believe the value of information derived from advanced imaging after patellar dislocation extends beyond just loose body identification and will continue to obtain MRIs in all first-time dislocators, we understand that this is not the standard of care at all institutions. In those settings, we hope to offer providers evidence-based criteria for timely advanced imaging utilization following first-time dislocation. Spontaneous patellar reduction, older age, and a relatively decreased CDI were independent risk factors for loose body formation. The presence of these risk factors should prompt providers to strongly consider timely ordering of advanced imaging and allow for improved risk counseling with patients.
