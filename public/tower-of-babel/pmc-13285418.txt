Fate of proximal anastomosis after aortic repair for acute aortic dissection
Authors: Kayo Sugiyama, Hirotaka Watanuki, Masato Tochii, Katsuhiko Matsuyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate events related to proximal anastomosis after acute aortic dissection surgery.MethodsWe analyzed the data of 119 consecutive patients with Stanford type A aortic dissection who underwent emergency surgery at our hospital between October 2015 and March 2023. All patients underwent proximal anastomosis with felt strips and biologic glue reinforcement. Among these, 18 patients (15%) experienced events related to the proximal anastomosis: 14 (12%) had residual dissection in the aortic root, and 4 (3%) had pseudoaneurysms in the aortic root after surgery. We compared the clinical course between the 18 patients with proximal anastomotic events and the other 101 patients. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality, and the secondary endpoint was redo open-heart surgery.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in preoperative and procedural characteristics. The incidence of redo open-heart surgery showed a significant difference between the two patient groups (p = 0.0015); however, all-cause mortality showed no significant difference (p = 0.51). The clinical course was different between patients with residual aortic root dissections and those with proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms. Residual aortic root dissections were detected via postoperative computed tomography within 1 month and followed-up conservatively owing to the absence of worsening signs. All proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms were detected suddenly in the late postoperative phase and presented prompt worsening. All such cases underwent successful redo surgery with patch repair. At reopening, the anastomosis appeared completely detached and nearly ruptured in all cases.ConclusionsPatients with proximal anastomotic events required redo open-heart surgery significantly more frequently. Patients with residual aortic root dissections and those with proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms showed a different clinical course. Residual aortic root dissection was associated with insufficient proximal anastomotic repair because it manifested just after surgery. In contrast, proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysm occurs suddenly in the late phase and shows prompt worsening, thereby requiring acute repair.Keywords: Acute type A aortic dissection, Proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, residual aortic root dissection

Introduction

The optimal strategy of proximal repair in patients with acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) remains controversial. Cardiac surgeons have struggled for years with the procedure of proximal anastomosis in ATAAD. Despite numerous reports, no definitive method exists, and each institution and surgeon perform the procedure uniquely. Accordingly, postoperative complications of proximal anastomosis, such as residual aortic root dissection, aortic valve regurgitation due to aortic root enlargement, pseudoaneurysm from anastomotic disruption, and occasionally, hemolysis from reversed felt strips or graft warping, sometimes require redo open surgery [1–5].

BioGlue™ (Cryolife International, Inc, Kennesaw, GA) is used to secure graft anastomoses in fragile aortas due to dissection [6]. However, biologic glues for reapproximating the layers of the dissected aorta pose a risk of aortic wall necrosis [6, 7, 8, 9, 10], necessitating careful application [7].

While these complications present with similar imaging findings, the prognosis of patients with residual dissection in the aortic root or those who develop proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms has not been consistently reported [11, 12]. Various solutions, including endovascular treatment, exist for distal anastomotic events, whereas reintervention options for proximal anastomotic events are still limited to redo open-heart surgery.

Some suggest that aortic root replacement should be performed initially when the aortic root is broadly involved or the aortic root is relatively large [1, 2, 5, 13–15], however, surgeons may hesitate to choose aortic root replacement initially because of its high invasiveness.

In this context, we evaluated events related to proximal anastomosis in patients with ATAAD and assessed the impact of proximal anastomotic events of residual dissection and proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms on the postoperative course.

Materials and methods

This retrospective single-center study included a total of 134 consecutive patients diagnosed with ATAAD at our institution between August 2015 and March 2023 (Fig. 1). Patients who died either prior to hospital arrival or in the operating room were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, two patients who had undergone aortic root replacement during the initial surgery and 13 patients whose postoperative assessment via contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was unfeasible due to changes in their clinical condition were excluded. Aortic root replacement was considered in cases of near-complete circumferential detachment of the aortic root or when the aortic root diameter was ≥ 4.5 cm during the initial surgical intervention. Ultimately, 119 patients with ATAAD who underwent emergency surgery were included in the study. Among these, 14 patients exhibited residual dissection of the aortic root (Fig. 2a-d), and 4 developed pseudoaneurysms at the aortic root following surgery (Fig. 3a-d).

The diagnosis of ATAAD was confirmed by contrast-enhanced CT performed either at our institution or at previous hospitals. Patient data, including demographic details and comorbidities (such as hypertension, smoking history, chronic respiratory disease, chronic renal disease requiring hemodialysis, history of coronary artery disease, history of cerebrovascular disease, familial background, and family history of aortic dissection) were recorded. Coronary artery disease was defined as a history of coronary revascularization, while cerebrovascular disease was defined by the presence of permanent neurological deficits. Perioperative parameters, including operation duration, cardiopulmonary bypass time, aortic cross-clamp time, cerebral perfusion time, circulatory arrest duration, and lowest recorded body temperature, were assessed. Early outcomes, such as early mortality, cause of death, and postoperative complications, were also recorded during the hospitalization period. In preoperative CT, whether the aortic root was dissected or not and the number of detached commissures were evaluated.

Imaging evaluations are routinely conducted prior to patient discharge, with follow-up imaging scheduled at 6 months, 1 year, and annually thereafter, in accordance with guideline recommendations [16]. Wherever feasible, we conduct follow-up CT imaging at our institution, even in the absence of overt complications. Postoperative CT follow-up allowed for the identification of events in the aortic root or other complications, which were subsequently monitored with increased frequency.

The primary endpoint of this study was all-cause mortality, while the secondary endpoint was the incidence of needed redo open-heart surgery. Redo open-heart surgery was considered for patients with a maximum short diameter of the aortic root ≥ 55–60 mm or with evidence of rapid enlargement (≥ 5 mm per semester), consistent with a true aneurysm [16, 17]. Additional considerations for redo open-heart surgery included the presence of symptoms and other clinical factors. Indications for redo open-heart surgery also encompassed aortic root enlargement, moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation, and the development of a pseudoaneurysm at the anastomotic site.

Operative technique

Primary central aortic repair was performed to exclude the entry tear and restore patency to the true lumen. The extent of graft replacement was determined based on the location of the primary entry: hemiarch replacement was performed for entries in the ascending aorta, while total or partial arch replacement was indicated for entries within the aortic arch. Cardiopulmonary bypass was established via bicaval drainage with femoral artery cannulation, or, alternatively, through the axillary artery, brachiocephalic artery, or ascending aorta. Antegrade selective cerebral perfusion was employed for brain protection, targeting a deep hypothermic temperature of 25 °C, measured either at an intra-bladder or pharyngeal level.

While the surgical technique evolved during the course of the study, proximal aortic reconstruction typically involved transection of the ascending aorta approximately 1 cm above the aortic commissure. Aortic regurgitation resulting from detached commissures during dissection was addressed by resuspending the aortic commissures using primarily 4 − 0 polypropylene sutures, with felt pledgets placed on both the inner and outer surfaces of the aorta. Following this, BioGlue™ (Cryolife International, Inc., Kennesaw, GA) was injected into the proximal false lumen after the removal of blood and thrombi. BioGlue™ was utilized in every case of aortic dissection at our institution. For use in the false lumen, BioGlue™ was injected to fill the cavity of the lumen, and for use at the anastomotic line, BioGlue™ was spread thinly to make coated anastomotic line. Teflon felt strips were placed on both the inner and outer surfaces of the aorta to provide reinforcement, with 4 − 0 polypropylene running sutures securing the graft. After completion of the distal anastomosis, lower-body circulation was restored. Sequential reconstructions of the left subclavian artery, left common carotid artery, and brachiocephalic artery were performed as needed. The patient was then rewarmed, and the graft was anastomosed to the proximal aortic stump. Once the aortic graft was secured, blood flow to the heart was restored.

In cases requiring redo surgery for aortic root complications, minimal dissection was performed, and partial cardiopulmonary bypass was instituted via the right femoral artery and vein to facilitate safe resternotomy by decompressing the cardiac volume. Resternotomy was performed using a circular electric sternum saw. In cases where dehiscence extended deep into the aortic root, aortic root replacement was considered. If detachment was confined to a localized area, repair was performed using a bovine pericardial patch to address the detached proximal anastomosis. Four experienced cardiac surgeons performed open surgical repair with the same methods.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or median (range), and categorical variables were expressed as the number (%) of patients. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test, whereas non-parametric variables were analyzed using Mann–Whitney U test. All data analyses were performed using JMP 16.1 software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Survival curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier method; curves were compared using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The median age of the patients was 69 years (range, 39–88 years) and 70 were male (59%). None of these patients had connective tissue disease, including Marfan syndrome. Four (3%) patients had previous cardiac surgery. The preoperative backgrounds of 18 patients with proximal anastomotic events, including 14 patients with residual dissection of the aortic root and 4 patients with proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms, and 101 other patients were compared. The patient characteristics and preoperative data are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed regarding sex, previous cardiac surgery, resuscitation before surgery, intubation at referral, catecholamines at referral, aortic valve regurgitation (moderate-to-severe), preoperative organ malperfusion, preoperative hemiparesis, extension of dissection, location of the primary entry tear within the aortic arch, tamponade, history of hypertension, history of smoking, chronic respiratory disease, hemodialysis, coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, or family history between the two groups. In preoperative CT, 74 patients (62%) showed dissection in the aortic root and 71 patients showed a detached commissure in the non-coronary cusp, 43 in the right-coronary cusp, and 7 in the left-coronary cusp. Two or more commissures were detached in 46 patients. There was no clinical difference between the aortic root dissection before surgery and the root dissection after surgical treatment (p = 0.34), or between two or more detached commissures before surgery and the root dissection after surgical treatment (p = 0.58).

The median time to detect residual aortic root dissection was 8 (6–22) days, and for pseudoaneurysm due to central anastomotic disruption, it was 380 (45–792) days. All cases with residual aortic root dissection were followed conservatively because they did not show significant aortic root dilation despite identification on the postoperative imaging. Conversely, pseudoaneurysms caused by anastomotic disruption were not observed on CT immediately after surgery; however, they were detected later, when fluid accumulation around the anastomosis and disruption appeared suddenly on follow-up CT imaging. Since it appeared abruptly and expanded immediately, it needed urgent surgical repair. At reopening, the anastomosis appeared completely detached and almost ruptured in all cases. Histopathology of the inner wall of the re-dissected aortic root at the BioGlue™ application site showed almost complete disappearance of the nuclei of the medial smooth muscle cells on hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 4a and b). Redo surgical repair using a bovine pericardial patch was performed safely in all patients.

Discussion

Aortic root events were associated with proximal anastomosis and exhibited distinct clinical outcomes. Residual aortic root dissection was identified early in the postoperative period, which may be attributed to insufficient fixation of the false lumen by BioGlue™. In contrast, proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms resulting from anastomotic disruption were not immediately detectable on postoperative imaging, becoming apparent shortly thereafter with perianastomotic fluid accumulation and structural disruption. Histopathological analysis revealed necrosis of the aortic wall with complete detachment of proximal anastomosis. These findings suggest that pseudoaneurysm formation may be associated with the use of BioGlue™. These cases necessitated urgent surgical intervention because structural disruption showed prompt worsening; however, redo open-heart surgery was performed safely.

BioGlue™ is particularly effective in managing minor bleeding between the fragile aortic wall and the vascular prosthesis [6]. In cases of ATAAD, the benefits of BioGlue™ may outweigh its potential risks. Therefore, its use should not be discouraged solely due to the potential risk of late anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation. Furthermore, some previous studies suggested that the use of BioGlue™ in thoracic aortic surgery was not associated with excess incidence of anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation following surgical repair of thoracic aortic disease [10]. However, its application may contribute to aortic wall necrosis in the late postoperative phase [7–10]. Although glutaraldehyde in BioGlue™ is less cytotoxic to the aortic wall compared to formalin in gelatin-resorcinol-formaldehyde glue, excessive application may still lead to aortic wall necrosis and pseudoaneurysm development [6]. In several large single-center studies [18–20], the pseudoaneurysm rates were within the expected range [21]. In the present study, this proportion was 3%, which seems acceptable [6, 19, 21].

While it remains uncertain whether the histopathological findings observed in the present study are directly attributable to BioGlue™, its careful and judicious use considering the appropriate amount, location, and timing is imperative. In particular, its use within the false lumen should be minimized to the extent necessary to secure adhesion of the aortic wall. Erasmi et al. reported severe active inflammation and a foreign body reaction surrounding BioGlue™ at 3 months post-application, highlighting the potential risk of late-phase aortic wall necrosis [22]. Consequently, even patients who initially exhibit uneventful postoperative recovery require vigilant long-term surveillance for the possibility of abrupt dehiscence.

In our institution, we have adopted a policy of observation for residual dissection of the aortic root. While this may be controversial, all patients can be followed-up conservatively, and we observed no cases of apparent deterioration. Given that no cases of aortic root rupture despite late root dilation were also found in another study,13 further research is warranted to determine the optimal surgical indications for remaining aortic root dissection after ATAAD repair. The present study underscores that, despite their similar radiological appearance, residual dissection and proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms have distinct underlying mechanisms and prognostic implications.

Pedroza et al. suggest reoperative root replacement after prior ATAAD repair can be performed safely at experienced aortic centers [3]. In the present study, redo open heart surgery was achieved safely. The procedure with bovine pericardial patch was feasible and favorable if the detached area was small. Pugliese et al. reported that reoperations carry a low operative risk with good long-term survival. Conversely, Antoniou et al. described that reoperations on the proximal thoracic aorta represent a challenge, and the mortality rate is at least three times higher than that for the initial surgery [12]. According to the 2016 annual report compiled based on the database from the Japanese Society of Thoracic Surgeons, the in-hospital mortality rate for reoperated cases related to the aortic root is 20.6% [11]. Because this mortality rate is not negligible, we should be careful regarding aortic root replacement in redo open-heart surgery.

Estrera et al. stated that the primary goal of surgical repair of ATAAD is to save a life [23]. However, Yang et al. proposed that aortic root repair and aortic root replacement are feasible with favorable short- and long-term outcomes. They recommended that aortic root replacement should be performed for patients with ATAAD presenting with an intimal tear at the aortic root, root aneurysm 4.5 cm or greater, connective tissue disease, or unrepairable aortic valvulopathy [14]. Ikeno et al. demonstrated greater sinus of Valsalva diameter and number of detached commissures to be significant risk factors for a composite outcome consisting of aortic-related death or aortic root–related reoperation [13]. As highlighted by Ikeno and Yang et al., it might be necessary to initially consider root replacement in cases where the aortic root is enlarged or two or more of the three sinuses of Valsalva are dissected [13, 14]. In the present study, however, we could not detect clinical relevance between two or more detached commissures and postoperative root dissection. Furthermore, in the present study, whether the aortic root is dissected or not was not associated with postoperative aortic root dissection. Further investigations are warranted to clarify the preoperative root situation and the prognosis in the aortic root.

Study limitations

Even with a larger patient cohort and longer mean follow-up, this study was limited by its retrospective in nature and lack of a concurrent control group. We also did not compare BioGlue™ with other surgical adhesives. However, the incidence of pseudoaneurysm in this series is comparatively low (3%), making a lower rate in any control group improbable.

Second, relatively few patients were included owing to the rarity of the condition. Third, this was a retrospective single-center study without randomization, necessitating a multi- institutional study.

Fourth, despite our best efforts, we did not achieve 100% radiologic follow-up. In addition, cases where contrast-enhanced CT could not be performed postoperatively were omitted, which does not reflect the exact postoperative results.

Conclusion

To prevent proximal anastomotic events, appropriate strategies are essential. In our study, residual aortic root dissection was detected immediately after surgery, appearing to result from insufficient proximal anastomotic repair during the initial aortic repair. However, no progression was observed during follow-up, allowing for conservative management. In contrast, proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms were characterized by abrupt effusion and detachment at the proximal anastomosis, suggesting a potential association with the aortic wall necrosis and necessitating urgent redo open-heart surgery. Although redo open-heart surgery was frequently required in patients with proximal anastomotic events, which all developed proximal anastomotic pseudoaneurysms, no significant increase in all-cause mortality was observed. Aortic root repair using a bovine pericardial patch was effective. No correlation was found between aortic root dissection or the number of detached commissures before surgery and postoperative aortic root dissection. Given the potential for unpredictable postoperative changes, meticulous imaging follow-up is essential in all cases to enable timely detection and appropriate intervention.
