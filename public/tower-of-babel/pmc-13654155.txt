Interobserver agreement of non-parallel transmission technology zoomed-diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) for ordinal biparametric Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS)-based scoring in bladder cancer lesions ≥1 cm
Authors: Dianyu Wang, Zhenming Zhang, Yanjing Wang, Zhijia Wang, Hanyun Zhang, Cancan Cui, Zhao Zhang, Naiming Xue, Mengchao Zhang, Lin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative assessment of muscle invasion in bladder cancer using the Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) relies heavily on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), but conventional single-shot echo-planar imaging diffusion-weighted imaging (ss-EPI-DWI) may be limited by image distortion and artifacts, potentially affecting interobserver agreement. Zoomed diffusion-weighted imaging without parallel transmission technology (non-pTx zoomed-DWI) may improve image quality. This study aimed to compare the interobserver agreement of non-pTx zoomed-DWI versus conventional ss-EPI-DWI for ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring in bladder cancer lesions ≥1 cm across different morphological types among radiologists with varying levels of experience.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included patients with pathologically confirmed bladder cancer who underwent preoperative biparametric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), conventional ss-EPI-DWI, and non-pTx zoomed-DWI, before transurethral resection of bladder tumor or radical cystectomy. Three radiologists independently assigned ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based F-scores from 1 to 5 for each eligible lesion using ss-EPI-DWI and non-pTx zoomed-DWI datasets separately. T2WI was used for lesion localization and morphological classification, whereas the corresponding DWI sequence, including high-b-value DWI images and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, was used to assign ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based F-scores according to the standard VI-RADS scoring criteria. Subjective image quality was rated on a 4-point scale. Interobserver agreement was assessed using linear weighted kappa statistics with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsA total of 162 patients (138 males, 24 females; median age 67 years) with 214 eligible bladder cancer lesions were included. Non-pTx zoomed-DWI provided significantly higher subjective image quality scores than ss-EPI-DWI for both readers (P<0.001). Based on the original ordinal VI-RADS-based F-scores, overall pairwise interobserver agreement was good with ss-EPI-DWI (linear weighted κ=0.708–0.761) and excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (linear weighted κ=0.851–0.870). In morphology-specific analyses, agreement also improved with non-pTx zoomed-DWI across lesion subgroups. The improvement was particularly evident in broad-based nodules, for which agreement increased from good with ss-EPI-DWI (κ=0.700–0.752) to excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (κ=0.892–0.919), and in nodules with pedicles, for which agreement improved from moderate-to-good (κ=0.535–0.640) to good-to-excellent (κ=0.786–0.847).ConclusionsNon-pTx zoomed-DWI improved subjective image quality and interobserver agreement for ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring in bladder cancer lesions ≥1 cm compared with conventional ss-EPI-DWI. These findings suggest that non-pTx zoomed-DWI may support more consistent MRI-based assessment of muscle invasion risk, particularly in morphologically complex lesions. Further prospective studies with lesion-level pathological correlation are needed to determine whether improved agreement translates into improved diagnostic accuracy.

Introduction

Bladder cancer is a prevalent malignancy of the urinary system, ranking as the 9th most common cancer worldwide, and is characterized by high incidence and recurrence rates (1). Accurate determination of muscularis propria invasion is critical for clinical staging and directly informs treatment decisions, such as whether a patient is suitable for bladder-sparing therapy or requires radical cystectomy (2,3). Non-muscle-invasive and muscle-invasive bladder cancers differ substantially in prognosis, management strategies, and therapeutic aims, making reliable preoperative evaluation essential (2,3).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has become an important noninvasive tool for local staging and risk stratification of bladder cancer. The Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System (VI-RADS) was developed to standardize bladder MRI acquisition, interpretation, and reporting, and to estimate the risk of muscularis propria invasion (4,5). The original VI-RADS framework integrates T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE)-MRI to stratify the risk of muscle invasion (5). Several studies have investigated the diagnostic performance and interobserver agreement of VI-RADS in bladder cancer (6-12). In clinical practice, DWI plays a particularly important role in evaluating the tumor-muscle interface, especially when there are discrepancies between T2WI and DCE findings or when DCE-MRI is unavailable. Recent studies have suggested that a biparametric MRI approach combining T2WI and DWI may provide clinically useful information for evaluating muscle invasion risk, underscoring the importance of high-quality DWI (7,9).

Conventional single-shot echo-planar imaging DWI (ss-EPI-DWI) is widely used for bladder cancer evaluation, but it is prone to magnetic susceptibility artifacts, motion artifacts, and limited spatial resolution, especially at high b-values (13,14). These limitations can obscure lesion margins and the continuity of the bladder wall muscle layer, thereby reducing reader confidence and interobserver consistency. To address these challenges, reduced field-of-view DWI techniques have been developed to improve spatial resolution and reduce image distortion in pelvic imaging (15-18). Zoomed diffusion-weighted imaging without parallel transmission technology (non-pTx zoomed-DWI) is one such technique. Compared with conventional full-field-of-view DWI, reduced field-of-view or targeted DWI techniques can provide improved lesion conspicuity and clearer visualization of the tumor-muscle interface (19-22).

Despite these technical advances, few studies have evaluated whether non-pTx zoomed-DWI improves interobserver agreement among radiologists with different levels of experience in bladder cancer MRI. This issue is clinically relevant because consistent image interpretation is essential for standardized reporting and multidisciplinary decision-making. In addition, bladder cancer lesions show heterogeneous morphological appearances, including non-nodular lesions, nodules without pedicles, broad-based nodules, and nodules with pedicles. These morphological differences may influence the visibility of the tumor base, pedicle, and adjacent muscularis propria, potentially affecting the reproducibility of VI-RADS-based scoring (23).

Therefore, this study aimed to compare interobserver agreement between non-pTx zoomed-DWI and conventional ss-EPI-DWI for ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring of bladder cancer lesions ≥1 cm, with a particular focus on the influence of lesion morphology and reader experience. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (24) (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0512/rc).

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective single-center study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University (2024 Clinical Review Approval No. 2024111412). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee because of the retrospective nature of the study. This study used MRI, surgical, and pathological data that had already been obtained during routine clinical care. No additional MRI examinations or interventions were performed specifically for this study.

From August 2020 to October 2022, consecutive patients with suspected bladder cancer who underwent preoperative bladder MRI at China-Japan Union Hospital of Jilin University were retrospectively screened. All patients underwent preoperative biparametric MRI, including T2WI, conventional ss-EPI-DWI, and non-pTx zoomed-DWI, all performed during the same MRI examination. For patients who subsequently underwent surgery, transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) or radical cystectomy was performed within 2 weeks after MRI. None of the included patients had received neoadjuvant treatment before MRI.

The study used a paired within-lesion image interpretation design. For each eligible lesion, both ss-EPI-DWI and non-pTx zoomed-DWI datasets were available from the same MRI examination. The same three radiologists independently assigned ordinal biparametric VI-RADS F-scores (1-5) to the same group of eligible lesions using each DWI sequence, according to the VI-RADS scoring framework (5). Therefore, the interobserver agreement between ss-EPI-DWI and non-pTx zoomed-DWI was compared under paired conditions.

Lesion-level eligibility was assessed first. Lesions were included if they were MRI-visible, had a maximum diameter of at least 1 cm, had classifiable morphology, were interpretable on MRI, and were not affected by active bladder bleeding. Lesions with a maximum diameter <1 cm were excluded because, according to the VI-RADS assessment principle, very small lesions are generally considered highly unlikely to invade the muscularis propria and are assigned VI-RADS category 1 (5); therefore, they were not suitable for evaluating interobserver agreement in the assessment of muscle invasion risk. Patients were retained in the final cohort if at least one lesion met all lesion-level eligibility criteria. Patients were excluded from the final analysis only when none of their lesions fulfilled the eligibility criteria.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) lesions with a maximum diameter <1 cm; (II) lesions affected by active bladder bleeding, which could interfere with lesion margin delineation, morphological classification, and VI-RADS-based scoring; (III) absence of postoperative pathological confirmation of bladder cancer; and (IV) lesions with unclassifiable morphology.

Postoperative pathological data obtained from TURBT or radical cystectomy were reviewed to confirm the diagnosis of bladder cancer and the availability of postoperative pathological confirmation. For patients with multiple lesions, lesion-level radiologic-pathologic matching was not required because the primary endpoint of this study was interobserver agreement in biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring rather than lesion-level diagnostic accuracy. Therefore, all MRI-visible lesions that met the lesion-level eligibility criteria were included in the agreement analysis, and pathological findings were not used as a lesion-by-lesion reference standard for diagnostic performance analysis.

The primary outcome of this study was interobserver agreement in ordinal biparametric VI‑RADS‑based scoring and subjective image quality assessment, rather than lesion‑by‑lesion diagnostic accuracy for detrusor muscle invasion. Agreement analyses were performed on the raw VI‑RADS‑based F-scores (1-5), which were treated as ordinal categorical variables, and were not converted into a binary muscle‑invasive/non-muscle‑invasive classification.

MRI protocol

All MRI examinations were performed using a 3.0T scanner (MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a phased-array body coil. Each patient underwent a single preoperative MRI session in the supine position. MRI examinations were performed according to routine clinical scheduling, without a fixed time-of-day restriction. Patients were instructed to empty their bladder 2 hours before the MRI examination and to drink 500–1,000 mL of water 30 minutes before imaging to achieve moderate bladder distension. No additional special preparation, such as fasting, bowel preparation, enema, or routine administration of antispasmodic agents, was performed before MRI. The scanning sequences were performed in a fixed order: T2WI first, followed by conventional ss-EPI-DWI and then non-pTx zoomed-DWI. All three sequences were completed during the same MRI examination. The available imaging parameters are summarized in Table 1.

Image dataset preparation and blinding

All images were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format and reviewed on a Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) workstation. Before interpretation, all image datasets were anonymized, and sequence labels and other protocol identifiers were removed. To reduce potential recognition bias, the two DWI datasets per patient were exported as separate image sets. The ss-EPI-DWI and non-pTx zoomed-DWI datasets were randomly assigned to separate reading sessions and were not presented in the same session. A washout interval of at least 2 weeks was used between sessions. Readers were blinded to clinical information, ultrasound findings, cystoscopic findings, surgical information, pathological results, and DWI sequence type during interpretation.

Information from other diagnostic techniques, such as ultrasound or cystoscopy, was not used during image interpretation in this study. Although these examinations may have been performed as part of routine clinical care, these data were not provided to the readers during the study interpretation sessions. Therefore, the interobserver agreement analysis was based only on the predefined MRI datasets.

Image interpretation and ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring

Three radiologists independently reviewed the MRI datasets: Reader 1, a senior radiologist with more than 8 years of experience in abdominal imaging and 3 years of experience in bladder cancer imaging; and Readers 2 and 3, two junior radiologists, each with 1 year of experience. Before image interpretation, all readers completed a half-day bladder MRI interpretation course, including standardized instruction on VI-RADS and review of representative training cases not included in the study.

Because DCE-MRI was not included in the present biparametric MRI protocol, the assessment was performed as an ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring approach rather than a full multiparametric VI-RADS assessment. T2WI was used for lesion localization and morphological classification. The corresponding DWI sequence, including high-b-value DWI images and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, was used to assign each eligible lesion an ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based F-score from 1 to 5 according to the standard VI-RADS scoring criteria (5). These F-scores were used for interobserver agreement analysis and were not dichotomized to directly determine the presence or absence of muscularis propria invasion.

Subjective image quality assessment

Subjective image quality was evaluated per patient in 162 patients, with the largest eligible lesion selected for scoring in each patient. Two of the three radiologists, Reader 1 (senior) and Reader 2 (junior), independently evaluated the display quality of the bladder lesion margin and the continuity of the bladder wall muscle layer using a 4-point scale (Figure 1). A score of 1 indicates poor display with severe blurring and disruption of the muscle layer, 2 indicates fair quality with moderately blurred margins and poor muscle continuity, 3 indicates good quality with clear lesion edges and slight blurring of the muscle layer, and 4 indicates excellent display with sharp lesion edges and clear muscle layer continuity.

The 4-point scoring system for evaluating the relationship between the bladder lesion margin and the normal bladder wall muscle layer. Representative images illustrate scores 1 (poor) (A), 2 (fair) (B), 3 (good) (C), and 4 (excellent) (D) based on lesion margin clarity and muscle layer continuity.

Morphological classification

Lesion morphology was primarily determined on T2WI because T2WI provides anatomical information regarding lesion configuration, tumor base, pedicle, and relationship with the bladder wall. Lesions were classified as non-nodular lesions, nodules without pedicles, broad-based nodules, or nodules with pedicles (Figure 2). The three radiologists first independently assessed lesion morphology. If discrepancies occurred, the final morphological category was determined by consensus among the senior radiologist and the other radiologists.

Morphological classification of bladder cancer lesions. (A) Non-nodular lesion: a flat lesion growing along the bladder mucosa. (B) Nodule without pedicle: a nodular lesion without a visible pedicle. (C) Broad-based nodule: a lesion with a broad tumor base and thickening of the inner layer of the bladder wall beneath the lesion. (D) Nodule with pedicle: a papillary lesion with a visible pedicle.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and were expressed as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed or as median and interquartile range if not normally distributed. Image quality scores were treated as ordinal variables and compared between ss-EPI-DWI and non-pTx zoomed-DWI using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Interobserver agreement was assessed using the original ordinal VI-RADS-based F-scores ranging from 1 to 5 rather than dichotomized muscle-invasion status. Pairwise agreement between readers was evaluated using linear weighted kappa statistics with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The strength of agreement was interpreted as follows: ≤0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, good; and >0.80, excellent. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and lesion selection

A total of 232 patients with 357 MRI-visible bladder lesions entered the formal screening process. The exclusion categories were mutually exclusive and were applied sequentially. Nine patients with nine lesions were excluded because bladder cancer was not confirmed by postoperative pathology. Two patients with five lesions were excluded because active bladder bleeding affected lesion evaluation. Four patients with seven lesions were excluded because no subsequent surgical treatment was performed, and postoperative pathological confirmation was unavailable. Ninety-nine lesions measuring <1 cm were excluded, resulting in the exclusion of 35 patients with no remaining eligible lesions. Twenty-three lesions with unclassifiable morphology were excluded, leaving 20 patients with no remaining eligible lesions. Finally, 162 patients (138 males, 24 females; median age 67 years) with 214 eligible lesions were included in the analysis (Figure 3).

Interobserver agreement for ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring

Interobserver agreement for ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based F-scores was assessed using linear weighted kappa statistics. For all 214 eligible lesions, agreement was good with ss-EPI-DWI and excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI. The weighted kappa values for ss-EPI-DWI were 0.718 (95% CI: 0.620–0.795) between Reader 1 and Reader 2, 0.708 (95% CI: 0.609–0.790) between Reader 1 and Reader 3, and 0.761 (95% CI: 0.669–0.830) between Reader 2 and Reader 3. With non-pTx zoomed-DWI, the corresponding values increased to 0.865 (95% CI: 0.795–0.918), 0.851 (95% CI: 0.772–0.911), and 0.870 (95% CI: 0.795–0.925), respectively (Table 3).

In a representative case, the three readers assigned discrepant biparametric VI-RADS-based F-scores of 3, 2, and 4 on ss-EPI-DWI, whereas all three readers assigned an F-score of 2 on non-pTx zoomed-DWI because of clearer delineation of the tumor–muscle interface (Figure S3).

A similar trend was observed in the morphology-specific analyses. For non-nodular lesions, agreement improved from moderate-to-good with ss-EPI-DWI (κ=0.569–0.743) to excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (κ=0.815–0.835). For nodules without pedicles, agreement was good with ss-EPI-DWI (κ=0.733–0.788) and good-to-excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (κ=0.779–0.831). For broad-based nodules, agreement increased from good with ss-EPI-DWI (κ=0.700–0.752) to excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (κ=0.892–0.919). For nodules with pedicles, agreement improved from moderate-to-good with ss-EPI-DWI (κ=0.535–0.640) to good-to-excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI (κ=0.786–0.847) (Table 3). These results indicate that non-pTx zoomed-DWI improved the consistency of ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based scoring among readers, particularly for broad-based and pedunculated nodular lesions.

Discussion

In this study, interobserver agreement was assessed using the original ordinal biparametric VI-RADS-based F-scores rather than dichotomized muscle-invasion status. Using linear weighted kappa statistics, overall agreement was good with conventional ss-EPI-DWI and improved to excellent with non-pTx zoomed-DWI. These findings suggest that non-pTx zoomed-DWI may further standardize VI-RADS-based interpretation by providing clearer visualization of the tumor–muscle interface.

It is important to distinguish interobserver agreement from diagnostic accuracy. Non-pTx zoomed-DWI improved reader agreement and subjective image quality compared with ss-EPI-DWI, but these findings do not establish superior diagnostic accuracy for muscularis propria invasion. Postoperative pathological data were used to confirm the diagnosis of bladder cancer, but were not used as a lesion-level reference standard for diagnostic performance analysis. Future prospective studies with standardized lesion-level radiologic-pathologic correlation are needed to determine whether improved agreement translates into improved diagnostic performance.

The agreement benefit of non-pTx zoomed-DWI was observed across different lesion morphologies, particularly in broad-based nodules and nodules with pedicles, for which clear visualization of the tumor base, pedicle, and adjacent muscularis propria is important for VI-RADS-based assessment (23). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the reproducibility of VI-RADS (11,12) and with studies showing that reduced-field-of-view or targeted DWI improves image quality and lesion conspicuity (15-22). The present study extends these observations by demonstrating improved agreement among readers with different levels of experience and across multiple lesion morphologies. Clearer delineation of lesion margins and bladder wall muscle continuity may partly explain the greater improvements observed in morphologically complex nodules.

Greater consistency may be particularly relevant for less experienced readers, for whom clearer visualization of the tumor-muscle interface may support more standardized VI-RADS-based reporting and reduce interpretive variability. However, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of improved scoring reproducibility rather than evidence of improved clinical outcomes.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Multicenter prospective studies using standardized acquisition and interpretation protocols are needed for external validation.

Second, only three radiologists participated in image interpretation. Although they represented different levels of experience, a larger multireader study would provide a more comprehensive assessment of interobserver variability across different training backgrounds and practice settings.

Third, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. In addition, the distribution of lesion morphologies was uneven, with a predominance of nodules with pedicles and relatively small numbers of non-nodular and broad-based lesions. Together with the wide confidence intervals observed in some subgroup analyses, this imbalance may limit the precision and generalizability of the morphology-specific findings.

Fourth, lesion-level radiologic-pathologic matching was not performed in patients with multiple lesions, precluding reliable lesion-level diagnostic performance analysis. Future studies using standardized lesion mapping among MRI, surgery, and pathology are needed to determine whether improved agreement translates into improved diagnostic accuracy.

Fifth, because of the approximately spherical anatomy of the bladder, single-plane DWI may not fully depict the depth of tumor extension in all locations. Future studies should evaluate whether standardized biplanar reduced-field-of-view DWI provides additional value for lesion assessment (20).

Finally, despite anonymization, removal of sequence identifiers, separate reading sessions, and an adequate washout interval, intrinsic differences between the two DWI techniques may have allowed readers to infer the sequence type.

Conclusions

In summary, non‑pTx zoomed‑DWI improved subjective image quality and interobserver agreement in VI‑RADS‑based assessment of bladder cancer compared with conventional ss‑EPI‑DWI. These findings suggest that zoomed‑DWI may enhance the consistency of MRI‑based evaluation of muscle invasion risk, particularly across readers with different levels of experience. Whether these improvements in agreement lead to better diagnostic accuracy requires further investigation in appropriately designed studies.
