On Dimension Varying Control Systems: A Universal State Space Approach
Daizhan Cheng

Abstract
A cross-dimensional Euclidian space ( Ω \Omega ) is proposed for the state space of dimension varying (control) systems. It is shown that the topological structure of Ω \Omega is consistent with all ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} , which are the state spaces of each component modes of a dimension varying system. Using the universal metric from Ω \Omega , the switching laws are assumed to be Lipschitz. Some reasonable conventional switching laws are proposed. Under the topology deduced by the metric on Ω \Omega some fundamental properties of dimension varying dynamic systems, such as stability and robustness, are investigated. Then some control problems of dimension varying control systems, including controllability, observability, stabilization, disturbance decoupling, etc. are investigated. Finally, an aggregation approach for large scale hierarchical dimension varying networks is proposed.

Traditional opinion considers Euclidian spaces of different dimensions as completely separated or completely disconnected spaces. If we consider

It will be seen that the

The paper considers the controllability and observability, stabilization, disturbance decoupling of DVDCSs. Finally, the large scale hierarchical dimension varying networks are considered. Certain corresponding solvability conditions are obtained.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides some mathematical preliminaries, including (i) cross-dimensional Euclidian space; (ii) dimension-keeping semi-tensor product (DKSTP) of matrices. Section 3 formulates the dimension varying systems. First, the universal state space

Section 5 considers the synthesis of dimension varying control systems. The controllability, observability, stabilization, and disturbance decoupling problems are investigated. Section 6 is devoted to merge a dimension-varying control system into a common state space, which turns a dimension-varying control system to a conventional switching system in a fixed dimensional state space, which makes the results for switching control systems applicable to dimension-varying control systems. Section 7 considers the hierarchical networks, which have time-varying nodes (i.e., users). The nodes are aggregated into several groups, and each group uses a nominated dimension to approximate all members in this group. It is proposed to deal with large scale networks. Section 8 is a brief concluding remarks.

II-A

Cross-Dimensional Euclidian Space

Define a cross-dimensional Euclidian space as

{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

It provides a beaning state space for dimension varying dynamic systems.

First, we need to turn it into a path-connected topological space, which allows the trajectories of a dimension varying system to evolve continuously over such state space.

We define semi-tensor addition on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
.

Definition II.1

Let
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, and
t=\lcm(m,n)
. Define

\displaystyle x\pm y:=\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/m}\right)\pm\left(y\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/n}\right).

(1)

Note that under this addition and the conventional scalar product of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
with
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
becomes a pseudo vector space, which is almost a vector space except that the zero is not unique.

Definition II.2

Consider
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, assume
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
, and
t=\lcm(m,n)
.

(i)

The inner product of
x
and
y
is defined as

\displaystyle\langle x,y\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\frac{1}{t}\langle x\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/m},y\otimes{\bf 1}_{t/n}\rangle,

(2)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the conventional inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{t}
.

(ii)

The norm of
x
is defined as

\displaystyle\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}}}.

(3)

(iii)

The distance between
x
and
y
is defined as

\displaystyle d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y):=\|x-y\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}.

(4)

Remark II.3

(i)

Let
x,y\in{\mathbb{F}}^{\infty}
.
x
and
y
are said to be equivalent, denoted by
x\leftrightarrow y
, if

.

(ii)

It is easy to see that

, if and only if, there exist
{\bf 1}_{p}
and
{\bf 1}_{q}
such that

x\otimes{\bf 1}_{p}=y\otimes{\bf 1}_{q}.

(iii)

Under the distance deduced topology, we have a quotient space

\Omega:={\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}/\leftrightarrow.

The elements in
\Omega
are denoted by

\bar{x}=\{y\;|\;y\leftrightarrow x\}.

(iv)

Define

\begin{array}[]{l}\bar{x}\pm\bar{y}:=\overline{x\pm y},\\
\langle\bar{x},\bar{y}\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\langle x,y\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}},\\
\|\bar{x}\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}},\\
d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y).\end{array}

They are all properly defined. Hence

is a well defined topological vector space

. Meanwhile, it is also an inner product space and hence a metric space. But it is not Hilbert space, because it is not complete.

(We refer to

Denote by

III-A

State Space:
\Omega

Definition III.1

Let
\Sigma_{i}
,
i\in[1,s]
be a finite set of dynamic systems with state spaces
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{i}}

Assume there is a switching mapping
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow[1,s]

\displaystyle\Sigma(t)=\Sigma_{\sigma(t)},\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(15)

Denote the dimension varying system briefly by
\Sigma=\{\Sigma_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{i}}\;|\;i\in[1,s]\}

To begin with, we need to find a proper state space for dimension varying systems. A fundamental requirement is the trajectory of such a system could be continuous. A natural candidate is
{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}
with its topology deduced by the distance

, which is
\Omega
. Can we merge
\Sigma
into

Proposition III.2

\Omega^{n}:=\Omega\bigcap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Proof.

Let

. Then
\bar{x}\bigcap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=x
, which is unique. Since any two norms on a finite dimensional normed space are topologically equivalent

27
]
, the claim is obvious. This fact can also be seen from the fact that the two distances are proportional.

\Box

Proposition

Theorem III.4

(i)

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with its classical Euclidian topology is homeomorphic to
\Omega^{n}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cap\Omega

.

(ii)

Consider a sequence

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\bar{x}_{k}=\bar{x}_{0},

and
x_{0}\in\bar{x}_{0}\bigcap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Let

\displaystyle\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}x_{k_{i}}=x_{0}.

(16)

Proof.

(i)

Let

Note that over

(ii)

Since

\displaystyle d(x_{k_{i}},x_{0})=\sqrt{n}d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(\bar{x}_{k_{i}},\bar{x})\xrightarrow{i\rightarrow\infty}0,

(17)

the conclusion is obvious.

\Box

Remark III.5

(i)

Fact (i) of Theorem
III.4
is surprising. In fact,
\Omega
is a quotient space of

with its subspace topology seems could also be coarser than

with its subspact topology is homeomorphic to

, which is the classical Euclidian topology. This is unbelievably nice, which makes all switching modes can be merged into

automatically without any model modification.
This fact makes it convincing for taking

(ii)

Fact (ii) shows cross-dimensional convergence of a sequence

ensures the convergence of

for any

Summarizing the above arguments, one sees easily that a dimension varying system

Definition III.7

[

,

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\neq\emptyset
be a partially ordered set with the order
\prec

\sup(a,b)\in{\mathcal{L}}
, and a greatest common lower boundary, denoted by
\inf(a,b)\in{\mathcal{L}}

{\mathcal{L}}
is called a lattice.

Example III.8

Consider the set of positive integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Define an order

\displaystyle a\prec b,\quad\mbox{iff}~a|b,\quad a,b\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

(18)

It is easy to verify that

\sup(a,b)=\lcm(a,b),\quad\inf(a,b)=\gcd(a,b).

Hence
({\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\prec)
is a lattice, called the multiplication-division (MD-1) lattice.

Consider

\Omega=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\Omega^{n}.

As aforementioned that
\Omega^{n}
is homeomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\Omega^{m}

is a lattice, where

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}\sup(\Omega^{m},\Omega^{n})=\Omega^{m\vee n},\\
\inf(\Omega^{m},\Omega^{n})=\Omega^{m\wedge n}.\end{array}

Proof.
Assume

and
\Omega^{t}\prec\Omega^{n}
. By definition,

and

. That is,

Definition III.14

Example III.15

(i)

Constant Linear Mapping:

Assume there exist a set of constant matrices

such that the switching
\sigma(t):x(t^{-})\in\Sigma_{p}\rightarrow x(t^{+})\in\Sigma_{q}

Let

on

\|A\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}:=\sup_{0\neq x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}}\frac{\|A\,\rotatebox[]{-90.0}{$\ltimes$}\,x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}}{\|x\|_{{\mathcal{x}}}}.

It has been proved in

[
9
]
Proposition 13.4.2)

\displaystyle\|A\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}=\sqrt{\frac{n}{m}\sigma_{\max}(A^{T}A)}.

(25)

Using (

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}L^{p}_{q}=\|W^{p}_{q}\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}=\sup_{0\neq x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\infty}}\frac{\|W^{p}_{q}x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}}{\|x\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}}\\
\\
=\sqrt{\frac{n_{p}}{n_{q}}\sigma_{\max}((W^{p}_{q})^{\mathrm{T}}W^{p}_{q})}.\end{array}

(ii)

Nearest Jump:

Assume the trajectory jumps at

from
p

q
-th mode. That is, from

to

(29)

(iii)

Differentiable Jump:

In general, assume the jump functions

are differentiable functions. Then

W^{p}_{q}(t)=\frac{\partial(x^{q}_{1},\cdots,x^{q}_{n_{q}})}{\partial(x^{p}_{1},\cdots,x^{p}_{n_{p}})}(t).

The Lipschitz condition ensures the uniform boundary exists.

Remark III.16

(i)

Nearest jump is physically reasonable. For a physical system with transitions, the transitions generally occur at the closest distance to minimize the required energy. For instance, for any two charged bodies, the discharge consistently occurs at their nearest location.

(ii)

Even if the transition is assumed to occur in a small neighborhood of of the closest point, which can be treated as an error. The subsequent discussion remains valid.

(iii)

A common situation for the switches of a network is: some new recruits join the network, or some members leave the network.
If a switch is caused by some members leaving the network, it is obvious that the Lipschitz condition is satisfied. As some new recruits join the network, the Lipschitz condition meas the new recruits must meet certain required condition. Otherwise, they may destroy the network structures.

(iv)

In practice, the jumping functions are mostly the experimentally derived. For instance, in vehicle clutch transient process
[
28
]
provides such a jumping function.

Lemma IV.3

\displaystyle\Pi^{km}_{t}(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{k})=\Pi^{m}_{t}(x),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

(42)

Proof.
Denote

p=\lcm(t,km),\quad q=\lcm(t,m),\quad p=rq.

Then we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\Pi^{km}_{t}(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{k})=\frac{t}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{p/t}\right)\left(I_{km}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p/(km)}\right)(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{k})\\
=\frac{t}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{p/t}\right)\left(I_{k}\otimes I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{p/(km)}\right)((x\otimes{\bf 1}_{k})\otimes 1)\\
=\frac{t}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{p/t}\right)\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{k})\otimes{\bf 1}_{p/km}\right)\\
=\frac{t}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{p/t}\right)\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{p/m}\right)\\
=\frac{t}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{q/t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{r}\right)\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{q/m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{r}\right)\\
=\frac{tr}{p}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{q/t}\right)\left(x\otimes{\bf 1}_{q/m}\right)\\
=\frac{t}{q}\left(I_{t}\otimes{\bf 1}^{\mathrm{T}}_{q/t}\right)\left(I_{m}\otimes{\bf 1}_{q/m}\right)x=\Pi^{m}_{t}x.\end{array}

VI
Equivalent Switching System

VI-A

Embedding System

By embedding each mode into the common state space, a dimension varying system can be converted into a classical switching system
[
32
]
.

Denote by
\delta(t)
the Dirac function
[
27
]
. Assume at a switching moment
t
we have a jump from
x(t^{-})
to
x(t^{+})
. Denote

\|x(t^{+})-x(t^{-})\|_{{\mathcal{V}}}=r\neq 0.

Set the jump direction as a unit vector by

\varphi(t)=\frac{1}{r}(x(t^{+})-x(t^{-})).

Denote by
n(t)=\lcm(n_{t^{-}},n_{t^{+}})
,
then the extended switching mode is defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}:=\delta(t)\varphi(t)(x(t^{+})-x(t^{-}))+\Sigma_{k}\subset\Omega^{n(t)}.

(60)

Denote
n=\lcm(n_{i}\;|\;i\in[1,s])
. Then
\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}
can be merged into
\Omega^{n}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{k}:=\left(\delta(t)\varphi(t)(x(t^{+})-x(t^{-}))+\Sigma_{k}\right)\otimes{\bf 1}_{n/n(t)}.

(61)

In this way, a dimension varying control system becomes a switched control systems. The only difference is each mode has possible impulse.

Principally, the techniques for classical switching control systems remain available for the switching system in
\Omega^{n}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with modes described by (
61
).

f\in V({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

VIII
Concluding Remarks

The main contribution of this paper is to provide a universal state space for dimension varying (control) systems. As we mentioned in Introduction, to synthesize dimension varying systems, the “distance” between Euclidian spaces of different dimensions is necessary for comparing trajectories in different spaces. The “distance” is diverse. We proposed a unified “distance”, and provided some evidence to support it.

Under the unified distance space
\Omega
the dimension varying (control) systems have been well modelled and all the classical problems for fixed dimension (control) systems can be properly treated. The controllability, observability, stabilizability, and the DDP have been discussed in this paper. All other problems seem also to be formulated and discussed within this state space.

The only questionable point remains is: Is the distance physically meaningful? As aforementioned that
d_{{\mathcal{V}}}(x,y)=0
(i.e.,
x\leftrightarrow y
) is equivalent to existing
{\bf 1}_{\alpha}
and
{\bf 1}_{\beta}
such that

x\otimes{\bf 1}_{\alpha}=y\otimes{\bf 1}_{\beta}.

From information theory, if
x\leftrightarrow y
then it is obvious that each component
x_{i}\in x
has the same entropy as
x_{i}\in y
. Hence
x
and
y
have the same “information”. If the system comes from signal processing, this distance is very convincing. But for mechanical systems or systems from some other fields, practical verification is still necessary.
