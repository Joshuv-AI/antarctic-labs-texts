A single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of cardiac aging in African Turquoise Killifish
Authors: Baul Yoon, Yifeng Xu, Ping Zhu, Yuji Zhang, Xiaolei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging is a major contributor to functional decline of the heart and various cardiovascular diseases. Alterations across different cardiac cell types must be tightly orchestrated during the normative aging process that has begun to be mapped at the transcriptional level through single-cell RNA-sequencing. However, current rodent models are limited in their capacity to experimentally test large numbers of candidate differentially expressed genes (DEGs). As an attractive alternative, the African Turquoise Killifish (ATK) promises more efficient genetic studies of cardiac aging because it has the shortest lifespan among vertebrates. Despite its experimental advantages, single-cell transcriptomic studies on cardiac aging in ATK have not yet been conducted. Here, we generated scRNA-seq profiles of hearts from young and old GRZ strain ATK and demonstrated changes in cellular composition, gene expression, functional pathways, and intercellular communication during cardiac aging. This dataset not only recapitulates previously characterized cardiac aging hallmarks but also highlights the contribution of underappreciated cardiovascular cell types, such as epicardium and immune cell types, to cardiac aging.

Background & Summary

Aging is one of the major factors inducing physiological and functional changes in the cardiovascular system, leading to cardiac function decline and various cardiovascular diseases. Cardiac aging in humans is mainly characterized by left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, increased cardiac fibrosis, and a reduction in maximal exercise capacity1,2. Recent studies of systemic aging have proposed twelve aging hallmarks3, among which eight are applicable to cardiac aging, including primary (e.g., genomic instability), antagonistic (e.g., cell senescence), and integrative hallmarks (e.g., inflammation)4. Given that the heart consists of different cell types including cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, epicardial cells and immune cells, it is plausible that cardiac aging is an intricate process that consists of distinct molecular alterations across different cardiac cell types, which must also be interconnected.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) is a recently developed innovative technique that enables the characterization of gene expression changes in individual cell types simultaneously. To date, several scRNA-seq studies have been conducted primarily in mice and primates focusing on investigating age-dependent changes in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, and their relationships with cardiac aging hallmarks5–9. Recently, scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq were integrated to profile cellular and genetic changes in non-cardiomyocyte populations, including fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cell types, in mouse hearts10. Consequently, a large number of candidate DEGs are becoming available, which need to be tested experimentally to uncover molecular mechanisms of cardiovascular aging in a cellular context and characterize how each cell type contributes to and connects with known cardiac aging hallmarks. However, the throughput of such experimental studies in rodents remains a serious limiting factor. Therefore, a vertebrate model with a shorter lifespan and lower colony management cost is highly desirable.

Zebrafish is a vertebrate model with significantly improved throughput for both genetic and chemical genetic studies of cardiovascular diseases11. Previous studies of cardiomyopathies in our lab demonstrated that forward mutagenesis screening can be carried out in adult zebrafish for systematically discovering genetic modifiers of cardiomyopathy12; a large number of DEGs can be experimentally tested using a genome editing-based approach in F0 animals13,14; and a larger number of compounds can be rapidly assessed in adult zebrafish for their cardioprotective effects15. Despite these advantageous genetic tools, zebrafish, with a lifespan of 2–4 years, is not ideal for studying normative aging. Thus, we turned our attention to the African Turquoise Killifish (ATK), the shortest-lived vertebrate that can be bred in captivity, with a lifespan of up to six months16. After establishing the baseline of the cardiac aging process, we reported that aging hallmarks, such as cellular senescence, are conserved in this lower vertebrate model17. To date, scRNA-seq studies have been conducted in ATK to investigate renal aging18, age-related retinal dysfunction19, or sex-dimorphism across various tissues20. Despite the availability of a ribosomal-RNA depleted bulk RNA-seq dataset in its heart tissues21, cell type-specific transcriptomic analyses of cardiac aging in ATK have not yet been conducted.

To further develop ATK as a new vertebrate model for cardiac aging, in this study, we report the first scRNA-seq dataset generated from young (8 weeks-old; week 8 hereafter) and old (16 weeks-old; week 16 hereafter) female GRZ strain ATK hearts (equivalent to 30 and 60 years of age in humans, respectively). We found ten different cell clusters in this dataset and age-associated cell composition changes particularly in macrophages. We performed differential gene expression analysis and identified several DEGs in immune cells, endothelial cells, ventricular cardiomyocytes, and epicardium. We also conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis and identified changes in cellular transitions in ventricular cardiomyocytes during cardiac aging. In summary, this dataset captures numerous cell-type specific genetic factors associated with cardiac aging and lays a solid foundation for ATK to serve as an emerging experimental model for interrogating normative cardiac aging.

Methods

African Turquoise Killifish management and ventricles dissection

GRZ strains of the African turquoise killifish were maintained under a 14-hour light and 10-hour dark cycle at 28.5 °C. All animal experiments were approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Fifteen 8 weeks- and fifteen 16 weeks-old female killifish were used in this study. Killifish were anesthetized in 2.5 × 0.03% buffered tricaine, moved to a petri dish filled with 1x PBS, and monitored for unresponsiveness to ensure their deaths before dissection. Hearts were removed from the body and dissected further to remove atrium and outflow tract while leaving the ventricle. Dissected ventricles were quickly transferred to a RNase-free tube containing tissue dissociation solution (1 mL HBSS + 7.5 mg Collagenase/Dispase + 7.5 mg Collagenase type II).

Single-cell isolation and counting

Within the tube, dissected ventricles were minced with a plastic pestle for 2 minutes. The tubes were then placed on a rotator at room temperature for 45 minutes and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 minutes. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with 400 uL HBSS/10% FBS followed by centrifugation at 1,000 rpm for 2 minutes. The supernatant was removed again, and the pellet was mixed with 400 uL HBSS/10% FBS. Cells were filtered through a 70 μm sterile cell strainer to enrich the population of rarely detected cell types, such as immune cells and epicardium, and about 250 μL of the filtrate was collected in an Eppendorf Protein LoBind tube to assess cell number, morphology, and viability using a Beckman Coulter Vi-Cell XR Cell Viability Analyzer. To ensure sufficient cells for cell fixation followed by cDNA library preparation, samples with 1 × 106 cells /mL and a viability greater than 80% and minimal doublet formation were used.

Cell fixation

Fixation was completed following the Parse Biosciences Evercode cell fixation v3 kit protocol. Post-fixed cells were counted using a Beckman Coulter Vi-Cell XR Cell Viability Analyzer and stored at −80 °C in a Nalgene 1 °C Cryogenic Freezing container.

scRNA-seq library preparation and sequencing

scRNA-seq library preparation was performed using the Parse Biosciences Evercode Whole Transcriptome (WT) kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Fixed single cells were loaded across three rounds of combinatorial barcoding plates to generate eight sublibrary pools, each containing an equal number of cells with unique barcode information. For each sublibrary, a template-switch reaction was applied to biotinylated cDNA followed by cDNA amplification using template switch adapter primers (8 cycles for 6,000–12,500 cells in sublibrary). Amplified cDNA was purified, and its concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assy kit and cDNA fragment size was assessed using the High Sensitivity DNA kit on the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer. For sequencing library preparation, all purified cDNA was used for fragmentation and end repair, Illumina Truseq adapter ligation, and i5/i7 unique dual indexing (11 cycles for 50–99 ng cDNA input). Final sequencing libraries were size-selected, quantified using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assy kit, and assessed the fragment size distribution using the High Sensitivity DNA kit on the Agilent 2100 BioAnalyzer. Sequencing libraries were sequenced across two lanes of a 10B flowcell on an Illumina NovaSeq X instrument at the Genomic Analysis Core at Mayo Clinic following the recommended cycle configuration for Parse WT v3 (64 cycles for cDNA insert, 8 cycles for i5 and i7 index, and 58 cycles for barcodes 1–3) with the recommended sequencing depth (>20,000 read pairs/cell) and a 10% PhiX spike-in. A total of ~1.28 billion reads were generated with 54.2% sequencing saturation.

Data processing and alignment

Raw sequencing data were processed by the Genomic Analysis Core at Mayo Clinic, where Illumina bcl files were converted and a primary Parse Evercode analysis pipeline was performed for barcode reconstruction and alignment with removal of contaminants and mitochondrial reads. The resulting aligned data were archived as CRAM files. Because each sublibrary was sequenced across two lanes (L1 and L2) of a 10B flowcell, the corresponding CRAM files were merged and then converted to paired-end FASTQ files using samtools22. The African turquoise killifish genome assembly and annotations (GCA_043380555.1 and GCF_043380555.1-RS_2024_12) were obtained from the NCBI23. For each sublibrary, paired-end FASTQ files were aligned to the killifish reference genome using Parse BioSciences split-pipe v1.6.1 in ‘mode all’, with slight adjustments to the underlying STAR alignment parameters to increase multimapping and relax alignment stringency (–outFilterMultimapNmax 10,–winAnchorMultimapNmax 10,–outFilterScoreMinOverLread 0.3, outFilterMatchNminOverLread 0.3)24. After alignment, outputs from all sublibraries were merged using split-pipe in ‘mode comb’. This pipeline generated filtered and unfiltered digital gene expression (DGE) matrices, summary reports, and quality control figures, such as cell numbers and gene and transcript read distributions across barcode wells. The filtered DGE matrix was used for downstream analyses.

Quality control

The filtered DGE matrices for both week 8 and week 16 hearts were imported to Rstudio (version 4.3.2) and processed with Seurat (version 5.3.0)25. For each condition, the filtered count matrix was annotated with gene names and barcode well identifiers, and duplicated genes were removed before creating a Seurat object. Genes expressed in fewer than 3 cells and cells with fewer than 200 genes were initially filtered. Cells were further filtered to remove low-quality cells or potential doublets, retaining cells with 100 to 5,000 genes. Mitochondrial content was calculated using genes annotated with the prefix ‘-mt,’ but mitochondrial reads were not detected as they had been removed during initial raw data processing.

Data integration and clustering

Gene expression data were normalized using Seurat’s LogNormalize method, and the top 3,000 highly variable genes were identified from each dataset using the variance-stabilizing transformation (vst) method. Quality-controlled week 8 and week 16 heart datasets were merged into a single Seurat object. The merged dataset was scaled and used for principal component analysis (PCA), retaining the top 50 principal components. Batch correction was performed on the PCA embeddings using Harmony algorithm (version 1.2.3), grouping cells by condition with a conservative correction strength (θ = 10)26. UMAP was applied to the Harmony-adjusted embeddings using the first 25 dimensions with a neighborhood size of 30 cells. A SNN graph was constructed using the same Harmony dimensions followed by cell clustering using the Louvain algorithm at a resolution parameter of 0.6.

Cluster annotation

Cell type-specific marker genes were identified using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function. For each cluster, the top 50 genes differentially expressed relative to all other clusters were sorted, considering only positively enriched genes expressed in at least 25% of cells within a cluster and exhibiting a minimum log2 fold change of 0.25. Clusters were annotated based on known markers curated from the literature and clusters containing heterogeneous and ambiguous gene expression profiles were excluded from downstream analyses.

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis was performed to identify aging-associated transcriptional changes. The top differentially expressed genes per cluster were selected based on log2 fold change. Volcano plots were used to visualize the distribution of differentially expressed genes per cluster.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

The ventricular cardiomyocyte cluster was further subclustered into three groups (VCM1, VCM2, and VCM3) based on marker genes strongly expressed in each subcluster. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using Monocle3 (version 1.3.4) to identify cellular transitions among ventricular cardiomyocyte subclusters (VCM1, VCM2, and VCM3)27. Trajectory graphs were learned with parameters ncenter = 65, minimal_branch_len = 15 and pseudotime ordering was performed using a root node defined based on the VCM3 cluster.

Data Records

The dataset is available at GEO (GSE316983) (Title: A single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of cardiac aging in African Turquoise Killifish)28. This database includes gene annotation, cell-level metadata, and gene expression count matrix files for week 8 (young) and week 16 (old) hearts as well as an R data serialization file for downstream assays.

For each heart condition, we removed the low-quality cells with less than 200 genes and genes expressed in less than 3 cells. After initial filtration, cells with fewer than 100 detected genes were excluded as likely damaged cells while cells with more than 5,000 detected genes were removed to reduce potential doublets. Raw data underwent an initial QC process including mitochondrial DNA and contaminants removal and thus the proportion of mitochondrial genes in all cells was 0% in both conditions (Fig. 2B,C). The number of RNA and genes per condition averaged 1,347 and 1,519 genes, respectively (Fig. 2B–E). Following quality control, we integrated the data and conducted batch correction for clustering using Harmony algorithm26 (Fig. 2F).

After quality control, we obtained a total of 14,118 and 10,993 cells for week 8 and week 16 hearts respectively and used these cells for cluster annotation. We performed Harmony reduction and Louvain algorithm for dimensionality reduction and unsupervised cell clustering of the Seurat object. We sorted top 50 genes specifically expressed in each cluster, removed highly ambiguous clusters, and merged clusters that share similar gene expression profiles. We identified a total of 10 clusters in this dataset – macrophages, B cells, endothelial cells, ventricular cardiomyocytes, epicardium, T cells, epicardium-derived cells, granulocytes, smooth muscle cells, and neuronal cells. We observed a relatively high proportion of immune cells, likely reflecting dissociation and filtration biases that favor smaller cells and underrepresent larger cells, such as ventricular cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3A). We used several known markers to annotate each cluster. For example, macrophage cluster has both M-1 (marked by tnfa) and M-2 macrophage (marked by mrc1b) expression29 (Fig. 3B,C). To identify the molecular changes during cardiac aging, we selected immune cell types (macrophages, B, T cells), endothelial cells, epicardium, and ventricular cardiomyocytes to perform differential gene expression analysis between week 8 and week 16 hearts. We noticed strong and asymmetric DEG variations in macrophage, B cells, and endothelial cells, while weak and quiescent DEG variations in ventricular cardiomyocytes, epicardium and T cells (Fig. 4A). To identify cellular transitions in ventricular cardiomyocytes during cardiac aging, we further subclustered the ventricular cardiomyocyte population and conducted pseudotime analysis among subclusters. Ventricular cardiomyocyte consists of three subclusters (VCM1, VCM2, and VCM3) (Fig. 4B), each with distinct characteristics. VCM1 represents stress-responsive cardiomyocyte characterized by activation of ECM remodeling genes, such as col19a130, VCM2 represents transitioning mature cardiomyocytes, and VCM3 represents cardiomyocyte progenitors characterized by activation of growth factors, such as igfbp331 (Fig. 4C).
