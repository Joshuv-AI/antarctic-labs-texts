The effects of polyphenols on gut microbial metabolites and composition in neurodegenerative diseases: a systematic review
Authors: Rasa Zafari, Nooshin Goudarzi, Amirhossein Kamroo, Mahdi Falah Tafti, Atousa Ghorbani, Niki Talebian, Saeid Najafi, Shadi Shahbazian, Mohammad Rahmanian, Siavash Parvardeh, Leila Dargahi, Marjan Nassiri-Asl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) are growing global health concerns characterized by progressive cognitive and motor impairments. Recent studies have reported the role of gut microbiota and its interaction with the microbiome-gut-brain axis (MGBA) in these disorders. Dietary polyphenols possess antioxidant and antiinflammatory properties, which might modulate gut microbiota and attenuate the progression of these NDDs by acting on MGBA. This systematic review evaluates the effects of polyphenols on gut microbiota, their metabolites, and gut microbiota-related mechanisms in NDDs.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive literature search was conducted across Embase, PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. Studies investigating the effects of polyphenols on gut microbial metabolites and composition in NDDs were reviewed. Data were extracted on study design, interventions, and outcomes, and quality was assessed using SYRCLE’s risk of bias tool.ResultsTwenty-six animal studies met the inclusion criteria. Polyphenols favored microbial beneficial changes, increased short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), and reduced neuroinflammation by modulating inflammatory cytokine profiles (IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 reduction). In NDD animal models, memory and motor functions were observed to have improved, likely due to reduced oxidative stress, as well as decreased amyloid accumulation and improved gut and brain integrity. The interventions enriched beneficial genera (Bacteroidetes, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Rikenellaceae, and Alloprevotella) and reduced detrimental groups (Helicobacter, Bacteroidaceae, Rikenella, and Prevotella).ConclusionThe evidence suggests polyphenols may influence the gut microbiota and, in turn, alleviate NDD symptoms. However, we need to confirm this in more studies, especially in human studies.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01022-y.Keywords: Polyphenols, Neurodegenerative diseases, Gut microbiota, Microbiome-gut-brain axis, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease

Introduction

The prevalence of neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), including Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), has been increasing rapidly due to the aging global population, which has placed a significant burden on healthcare systems [1–3]. AD is the most common cause of dementia in elderly people, associated with memory deficits and cognitive impairments [4]. AD is characterized by the deposition of amyloid β (Aβ) plaques and tau protein tangles in the brain [5]. On the other hand, PD ranks as the second most prevalent NDD and is associated with the accumulation of Lewy bodies within neurons, especially in the brain’s substantia nigra [6]. In general, NDDs are complex, progressive, and multifactorial diseases with sporadic and familial forms influenced by lifestyle, environmental, and genetic factors [3, 7]. Polyphenols are secondary compounds found in fruits and vegetables [8]. Polyphenol metabolites produced by gut microbiota reflect antiinflammatory, antioxidant, and gut microbiota-modulating effects. These effects can help prevent metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, chronic inflammatory conditions, and malignancies. Polyphenol metabolites also promote gut function [8]. Recent studies have shown that polyphenols can prevent NDDs by affecting cerebral blood flow, neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, protein aggregation, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress in the central nervous system (CNS) [9–11]. For instance, it is reported that polyphenolic extracts from grapes and blueberries can improve memory in older individuals with initially lower memory performance [12]. Free polyphenols, constituting about 5–10% of total phenols, can be readily absorbed in the small intestine and reflect their functions [13]. However, the remaining 90–95% are bound polyphenols that reach the colon, where intestinal microbes break them down into small-molecule metabolites prior to entering the gut and liver blood circulation [13]. Polyphenols may also undergo conjugation reactions within the gut or enterocytes, affecting their bioavailability and bioactivity [14]. Consequently, the efficiency of polyphenols in human health depends highly on the balance of the gut microbiome and its functions.

Some studies suggest that aging can disrupt the microbial balance and increase the presence of pathogenic bacteria in the gut of older individuals. Additionally, dysregulation of the gut microbiota has also been reported in patients with NDDs [15]. Furthermore, polyphenols can significantly enhance gut bacterial growth, leading to the production of microbiota-derived metabolites such as bile acids and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). These metabolites can promote the secretion of neurotransmitters and hormones, such as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and serotonin, in brain tissue, thereby helping to prevent and treat NDDs by influencing the microbiome-gut-brain axis (MGBA) [13, 16, 17]. Based on this rationale, polyphenols may profoundly affect brain function by modulating the gut microbiome and could help prevent NDDs in the elderly.

Given the significance of polyphenols and their metabolites in disease prevention and treatment, there has been no systematic review of their impact on gut microbiota. This research aims to conduct a comprehensive review of prior studies that employ animal models to investigate how polyphenol metabolites affect gut microbiota patterns and help delay the progression of NDDs through the regulation of MGBA, with a focus on gut microbiota-related mechanisms.

Methods

Literature search

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across four databases, Embase, Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus, from inception to April 2023. Moreover, we conducted a search update until March 1, 2025. The search strategy combined keywords related to neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., “Neurologic Diseases, Degenerative”, “Alzheimer’s disease”, “Dementia, Presenile”, “Parkinson’s disease”, “multiple sclerosis”, “Huntington’s disease”) and polyphenols (e.g., “Polyphenol”, “Flavonoid”) and the microbiome (e.g., “Microbiome”, “Microbiota”, “Gut bacteria”, “Microbes”). The full list of search terms is provided in Supplementary Table 1. This systematic review used the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines to ensure complete and transparent reporting [18].

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened the retrieved studies based on titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full-text articles were then obtained and assessed for inclusion based on the following criteria: studies that evaluated the effect of polyphenols on the gut microbiome and metabolites in animal models of NDDs, reporting relevant outcomes such as cognitive function, disease severity, microbial metabolites, and the populations of various gut bacteria. Only studies published in English were included. Moreover, the exclusion criteria included in silico and in vitro studies, review articles, editorials, and conference abstracts—studies without full-text availability. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Data extraction and quality assessment

A standardized data extraction form was used to collect information from the included animal studies, including study design, animal model characteristics, details of the polyphenol intervention, outcome measures, and key findings. The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimentation (SYRCLE) tool for assessing risk of bias [19]. This tool evaluates ten domains that are particularly relevant for animal studies: (1) sequence generation, (2) baseline characteristics, (3) allocation concealment, (4) random housing, (5) blinding of caregivers and/or investigators, (6) random outcome assessment, (7) blinding outcome, (8) incomplete outcome data, (9) selective outcome reporting, (10) other sources of bias. Each domain was rated as having a low, high, or unclear risk of bias. The overall methodological quality of each animal study was assessed based on the number of domains identified as having a low risk of bias.

Results

Summary of reviewed studies

6324 articles were identified from scientific databases, including Embase, Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus. After removing duplicates, 5134 articles remained. Following evaluation of the title and abstract, 4986 studies were excluded, leaving 148 articles for retrieval. These remaining articles were assessed for eligibility, and finally, 23 publications were included in this review for qualitative analysis. After an update to the search, three additional studies were also included (Fig. 1).

Overview

This review study included 26 animal studies. These studies examined the role of various polyphenols on MGBA by altering gut microbiota, their metabolites, and barrier integrity in two neurodegenerative disorders, including AD and cognitive impairment in animals [20–41], and PD in animals [42–45]. The animals’ ages ranged from four weeks [37] to 18 months [40] in AD studies and from eight weeks to 12 weeks in PD studies [42, 43]. All AD studies involved male mice, except for one that used only female mice [28] and two that used rats [20, 41]. Two studies included both male and female animals [21, 25]. All PD studies involved male mice. The effect of polyphenols on animals with AD or other cognitive impairments

Out of the included studies, 22 focused on examining the effects of polyphenols affect factors, such as inflammation, brain oxidative stress, amyloidogenic pathways, tau phosphorylation, microbial metabolites, and the integrity of the intestinal and blood-brain barrier (BBB) as well as gut microbiome composition in animals with AD or other cognitive impairments (Table 1).

Polyphenols’ effect on Proinflammatory and antiinflammatory factors in animals with AD or cognitive impairments

Neuroinflammation increasingly links to AD and related cognitive impairments, with dysregulated proinflammatory cytokines worsening neuronal damage and cognitive decline [46, 47]. This section highlights the effects of specific polyphenols on these inflammatory components, showing reductions in cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and IL-6, and increases in antiinflammatory agents like IL-10. One study used baicalein as a flavonoid (200 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks), which decreased levels of IL-1β and TNF-α as two main proinflammatory cytokines in the brain cortex [22]. Similar reductions in IL-1β and TNF-α were also observed after administering other polyphenols, including gallic acid (GA) at 100 mg/kg, for 112 days [20], chlorogenic acid (CGA) (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks) [23], sesamol (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks and 0.05% w/v, in drinking water, for 10 weeks) [24, 36], Nano-Honokiol (20 mg/kg, p.o., for 17 weeks) [27], quercetin-3-O-glucuronide (Q3G) (50 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) [33], and blueberry–mulberry extract (BME) (300 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) [40]. Similar decreases were observed after administering ginger (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) and Triphala (extract 250 mg/kg, and powder 500 mg/kg, p.o., for 60 days) [38, 41]. Pterostilbene (20 and 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 10 weeks) also reduced IL-1β levels in the brains and colons of streptozotocin (STZ)- treated 4-week-old mice [37].

Additionally, several studies reported significant reductions in IL-6 levels following polyphenol treatment in AD mice. These included GA (100 mg/kg, for 112 days) [20], CGA (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks) [23], Nano-Honokiol (20 mg/kg, p.o., for 17 weeks) [27], Q3G (50 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) [33], and BME (300 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) [40], pterostilbene (20, 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 10 weeks) [37], and baicalein (20 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) [22]. Moreover, one study noted a significant decrease in IL-2, another proinflammatory cytokine, after BME administration (300 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) in 18-month-old AD mice [40]. These studies also showed increased levels of antiinflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, IL-4, and IL-5 following treatment with GA, CGA, and Q3G [20, 23, 33] (Table 2).

The effects of polyphenols on brain oxidative stress, amyloidogenic pathways, and Tau phosphorylation in AD models

This section examines the therapeutic effects of various polyphenolic compounds on brain oxidative stress markers, amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic pathways, and tau phosphorylation in established animal models of AD and related cognitive disorders. These findings provide insights into the mechanistic roles of polyphenols in combating oxidative and proteinopathic aspects of neurodegeneration, paving the way for targeted therapeutic strategies.

These studies reported preventive impacts of polyphenols in brain oxidative stress of AD mice after the administration of different polyphenols, such as GA (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 112 days), CGA (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks), which increase the level of glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase as three antioxidative stress enzymes in the brain of in the high-fat diet (HFD)-fed rat and transgenic mice respectively [20, 23], and resveratrol (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks) in obesity-related AD mice at the age of 6-weeks-old [34]. Similar results reflecting reduced oxidative stress were reported in 4-week-old STD mice with diabetic cognitive impairment (DCI) after receiving pterostilbene (20 and 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 10 weeks) [37].

Additionally, some of the included studies assessed changes in amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic pathways after giving polyphenols to these animals. One study using a polyphenolic extract from blueberries and grapes (PEBG) (500 or 2500 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 months) found that these compounds do not significantly affect proteins involved in amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic pathways, such as beta-secretase 1 and metalloproteinase domain 10 [21]. Another study reported that the level of cathepsin B, which activates amyloidogenic pathways, decreases after administering quercetin (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 21 days) in 14-month-old mice [35]. A different study with quercetin (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks) showed similar results, including reduced tau phosphorylation in aged mice fed advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) [39]. Another study used Radix Rehmanniae Praeparata (400 mg, p.o., for 21 days) in AD mice aged 6–8 weeks, which resulted in decreased expression of pSer404-tau protein but increased expression of insulin receptor (INSR) and IRS-1 proteins in the hippocampus and cortex of RRP-treated animals. Seven main components of RRP were identified via mass spectrometry [30] (More details in Table 2). Moreover, administering ginger (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) reduced Aβ plaque deposition in the hippocampus of female rats subjected to D‑galactose/ovariectomy [41] (see Table 2 for details).

The studies reviewed highlight the diverse neuroprotective effects of polyphenols in animal models of AD and cognitive disorders. These benefits are mainly achieved by reducing oxidative stress in the brain, influencing amyloidogenic and non-amyloidogenic pathways, and decreasing tau protein phosphorylation. Interventions such as GA (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 112 days), CGA (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks), resveratrol (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks), and pterostilbene (20 and 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 10 weeks) consistently enhanced antioxidant enzymes (e.g., glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, catalase) and alleviated oxidative stress in models like high-fat diet-fed rats, transgenic mice, and diabetic cognitive impairment [20, 23, 34, 37]. Concerning amyloid and tau, the results were mixed: polyphenolic extract PEBG (500 or 2500 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 months) showed no significant effects on proteins like beta-secretase 1 and metalloproteinase domain 10 [21], whereas quercetin (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 21 days or 16 weeks) reduced cathepsin B and tau phosphorylation [35, 39]; RRP (400 mg/kg, p.o., for 21 days) reduced pSer404-tau while increasing INSR and IRS-1 expression [30]; and ginger (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) lowered hippocampal Aβ plaque deposition [41] (details in Table 2). These oral treatments, lasting 3 to 24 weeks across different models and age groups, demonstrate polyphenols’ potential to target key AD pathologies, possibly through antioxidant and anti-amyloid effects. Still, the mixed results underscore the need to consider compound specificity, dosage, and model differences when evaluating outcomes.

The effect of polyphenols on gut microbial metabolites in AD models

The gut microbiome has a profound impact on brain health, and more in AD and other cognitive-related diseases, since dietary polyphenols, which are metabolized by the gut into SCFAs and phenolic metabolites, are potential neuroprotectors. These metabolites, obtained by the activity of the microbial populations in the intestine, contribute to the health maintenance by crossing the BBB to exert antiinflammatory, antioxidant, and cognitive effects, which can help to mitigate the AD main hallmarks such as Aβ aggregation, neuroinflammation, and synaptic loss [21, 24]. This section emphasizes the impact of the variety of polyphenols in the animal models of AD on phenolic metabolites and SCFAs.

Giving PEBG (2500 mg/kg, p.o., over 4 months) resulted in higher levels of 2-hydroxybenzoic acid, hydroxyphenylpropionic acid, and dihydroxyphenyl valerolactone in 12-month-old triple transgenic mice, and these increases were positively associated with better memory performance [21]. Liu et al. reported increased levels of different SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, and valerate in mice with AD fed by sesamol (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks), which in turn can improve the cognitive performance in 6-month-old male APPswe/PS1dE9 mice [24].

Similar results on the enhancement of acetate and propionate levels and improvement of cognitive functions in AD and DCI mice were shown in other studies using Q3G (50 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) [33], sesamol (0.05% w/v, in drinking water, for 10 weeks) [36], and Triphala (extract 250 mg/kg, and powder 500 mg/kg, p.o., for 60 days), which is full of polyphenols [38]. Furthermore, another study reported increased levels of SCFAs in fecal samples of AD mice using ginger (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) in a D-galactose/ovariectomy-induced AD-like disease model [41]. However, one included study revealed no significant changes in the acetate and propionate levels after giving dietary AGEs to cognitively impaired mice [39]. Moreover, administering sesamol and pterostilbene can increase the levels of butyric acid, isobutyric acid, and valeric acid in DCI mice [36, 37]. Moreover, administering sesamol and pterostilbene can increase the levels of butyric acid, isobutyric acid, and valeric acid in DCI mice.

Interventions with compounds like PEBG, sesamol, Q3G, Triphala, ginger, and pterostilbene consistently demonstrated elevations in key metabolites—including acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, and others—which correlated with enhanced memory performance, reduced neuroinflammation, and improved gut-brain axis functionality [21, 24, 33, 36–38, 41]. These observations imply that polyphenols might deliver neuroprotective benefits by addressing microbial dysbiosis, stimulating the synthesis of antiinflammatory and antioxidant molecules that can traverse the BBB, and consequently diminishing prominent AD characteristics like Aβ plaque formation and synaptic deterioration. Nonetheless, the data exhibit some discrepancies; for example, one study detected no meaningful variations in acetate or propionate levels after the intake of dietary AGEs [39], demonstrating how elements such as polyphenol category, dosage amounts, therapy timelines, and differences in models can shape the outcomes. This lack of uniformity underscores the need for standardized approaches in future research.

The effect of polyphenols on intestinal and BBB integrity in AD animals

The gut-brain axis relies on an intact gut and BBB to prevent neurodegenerative progression in conditions like AD and DCI. Barrier disruptions increase permeability, enabling lipopolysaccharides (LPS) to trigger neuroinflammation, Aβ buildup, and cognitive deficits [24, 36, 37, 40]. This section examines the effects of polyphenol-rich treatments on tight junctions and barrier function in AD animal models.

BME treatment (300 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) enhanced the expression of tight junction-associated proteins (occludin and ZO-1) in the intestine and the brain. In the intestine, it restored barrier function and reduced systemic inflammation, while in the brain, it strengthened the BBB and protected against neuroinflammation in 18-month-old mice [40].

Similarly, pterostilbene (20, 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 10 weeks) reduced intestinal permeability and neuroinflammation by enhancing ZO-1 and occludin, thereby protecting against systemic inflammation and neuronal damage in DCI 4-week-old mice [37].

Ginger extract (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) positively affected the expression of occludin and ZO-1 in D-galactose/ovariectomy mice. It suggests that ginger enhances intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins, thereby reducing permeability and improving gut barrier function [41]. Sesamol (0.05% w/v, in drinking water, for 10 weeks) significantly increased the mRNA expression of claudin-1, an essential tight junction protein, in the colon tissues of WT mice. This improvement helped maintain gut barrier integrity and decreased gut permeability. However, in ApoE-deficient mice, sesamol did not produce a statistically significant increase in claudin-1 expression, suggesting that the protective effects of sesamol on gut barrier function are partially dependent on the presence of ApoE. Furthermore, sesamol effectively reduced LPS levels, demonstrating its ability to enhance gut barrier function and decrease systemic inflammation in the presence of ApoE [36]. Sesamol (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks) and TG-Res-CS/TPP-NPs (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks) significantly decreased serum LPS concentrations in mice with AD, demonstrating their capacity to strengthen gut barrier function, restrain widespread inflammation, and thereby aid in alleviating the progression of AD [24, 34].

Polyphenol-rich treatments (e.g., BME, pterostilbene, ginger, sesamol, TG-Res-CS/TPP-NPs) effectively restore gut and BBB integrity in AD/DCI animal models by upregulating tight junction proteins (occludin, ZO-1, claudin-1), reducing permeability, LPS levels, and inflammation [24, 34, 36, 37, 40, 41]. They target the gut-brain axis to prevent neuroinflammation, Aβ buildup, and cognitive decline, with some model-specific variations (e.g., ApoE dependency). These findings position polyphenols as promising, non-invasive AD interventions, though personalized strategies and human trials are needed for clinical translation.

The effect of polyphenols on the gut Microbiome in AD animals

This section reviews polyphenol interventions (e.g., GA, curcumin, resveratrol, and sesamol) in AD animal models, emphasizing shifts in bacterial populations such as increased Bacteroidetes, Bifidobacterium, and Lactobacillus, and decreased Helicobacter, to mitigate inflammation and cognitive deficits. Chen et al. (2023) found that using GA (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 112 days) increased Bacteroidetes, Gammaproteobacteria, and Parasutterella, which may reduce AD-related protein expression and cognitive decline in HFD-fed rats at 5 weeks old [20]. Sun et al. (2020) reported that curcumin (50 and 200 mg/kg, p.o., for 3 months) decreased Bacteroidaceae in 24-week-old male APP/PS1 double transgenic mice [31]. Other studies have observed a reduction in Helicobacter abundance after administering Nano-Honokiol (20 mg/kg, p.o., for 17 weeks) and resveratrol (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks) to 12-week-old transgenic mice and obesity-related AD mice at 6 weeks old, respectively [27, 34].

Liu et al. (2021) and Yang et al. (2023) observed an increase in Bifidobacterium with sesamol (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks) and resveratrol (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks), but this increase was inhibited by epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) (45 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks) [24, 28, 34]. Sesamol (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 8 weeks) and curcumin (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 7 weeks) boosted Rikenellaceae [24, 31], while xanthohumol (5 mg/kg, every other day, p.o., for 3 months) decreased Rikenella [25]. Li et al. (2023) reported an increase in Lactobacillus with BME (300 mg/kg, p.o., 6 weeks), but Q3G (50 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) restored normal levels [33]. Prevotella decreased with baicalein (200 mg/kg, p.o., for 6 weeks) and curcumin (50 and 200 mg/kg, p.o., 3 months) [22, 31], while Alloprevotella increased with CGA (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 16 weeks) and resveratrol (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 24 weeks) [23, 34]. Triphala treatment (extract, 250 mg/kg, and powder, 500 mg/kg, p.o., for 60 days) increased beneficial bacteria, including Bacteroides, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia, in 6-month-old transgenic mice [38]. The effect of polyphenols in animals with PD

Out of the included studies, four examined the influence of polyphenols in modulating gut microbiota, metabolite production, inflammation, and their potential neuroprotective roles in PD models. Research has explored the impacts of several polyphenols, such as fisetin [43], polymannuronic acid [44], curcumin, and chicoric acid (CA) [42] (refer to Table 1).

Effects of polyphenols on dopaminergic neurons and motor impairments in 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced PD mice

Polyphenols have garnered attention for their antioxidant, antiinflammatory, and neuroprotective properties, which may preserve dopaminergic neurons and alleviate motor symptoms in PD. This section reviews key studies on polyphenols, as mentioned earlier, highlighting their effects on tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons and the resulting behavioral improvements in mice treated with MPTP.

Polyphenols such as CA (30 and 60 mg/kg, p.o., 12 days), fisetin (100 ng/kg, p.o., for 30 days), and polymannuronic acid (30 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) have been found to increase the number of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive dopaminergic fibers in the substantia nigra (SN) and striatum in 8–12 week old male mice [42–44]. The administration of fisetin (100 ng/kg, p.o., for 30 days) resulted in enhancements in mice’s locomotor activity impairments induced by MPTP. Fisetin treatment mitigated bradykinesia and coordination deficits induced by MPTP in 12-week-old mice [43]. Furthermore, treatment with polymannuronic acid (30 mg/kg., p.o., for 4 weeks) improved motor performance in PD-affected mice, as evidenced by a shorter total time to climb down in the pole test [44]. Similar results on the improvement of motor deficits, such as total movement distance and mean speed, were reported among PD mice after the administration of curcumin (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 7 weeks) in 8-week-old male mice [45].

These compounds consistently enhanced TH-positive fibers in the SN and striatum, while improving locomotor activity, reducing bradykinesia, and enhancing coordination and motor function metrics, such as descent time in pole tests and overall movement distance [42–45]. Administered orally at varying doses and durations in young adult male mice (8–12 weeks old), these interventions highlight the antioxidant and antiinflammatory mechanisms of polyphenols, which counteract MPTP-induced neurotoxicity, aligning with their role in preserving nigrostriatal integrity and behavioral performance.

The effect of polyphenols on Proinflammatory and antiinflammatory factors in PD animal models

Polyphenols, known for their potent antiinflammatory effects, may help mitigate these responses by modulating immune pathways and reducing oxidative stress. This segment reviews a significant study on polymannuronic acid (30 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks), a derivative of polyphenols, emphasizing its varied impacts on inflammatory indicators in the colon and substantia nigra pars compacta (SNpc) of 8-week-old mice treated with MPTP. The intervention significantly lowered TNF-α and IL-6 levels in both the colon and SNpc, while specifically inhibiting the inflammatory markers (e.g., COX-2 and iNOS) in the colon [44]. These observations highlight its role in regulating inflammation through the gut-brain axis, suggesting opportunities for targeted therapies based on polyphenols.

The effect of polyphenols on gut microbial metabolites in PD models

It has been demonstrated that polyphenols modulate the gut microbiota and restore beneficial metabolite profiles, potentially offering neuroprotective effects through the gut-brain axis. Some included studies also evaluated SCFAs alterations after polyphenol administration in PD mice (Table 2). A study using curcumin (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 7 weeks) in 8-week-old mice with a PD-like phenotype reflected a decreased butyric acid/acetic acid ratio and restored levels of total SCFAs compared to the PD mice [45]. Additionally, polymannuronic acid (30 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) can elevate the fecal concentrations of total SCFAs and the three primary SCFAs - acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid. The study by Wang and colleagues, which compared two doses of CA (CA30, CA60) in combination with MPTP, showed that the CA30 + MPTP group could restore normal SCFA concentration by increasing the levels of acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric, isovaleric, and valeric acid. Simultaneously, mice in the CA60 group exhibited a higher degree of acetic and butyric acid than control mice, resulting in a more significant increase in SCFA level [42].

Curcumin, polymannuronic acid, and CA modulate gut microbiota and restore beneficial SCFA profiles in PD mouse models, thereby providing neuroprotective benefits through the gut-brain axis. Studies demonstrated that curcumin reduced the butyric acid/acetic acid ratio while restoring total SCFAs [45], polymannuronic acid elevated key SCFAs like acetic, propionic, and butyric acids, and CA (at doses of 30 and 60 mg/kg) normalized or enhanced levels of multiple SCFAs, counteracting PD-related dysbiosis and pathology [42]. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of polyphenols in mitigating neurotoxicity in PD.

The effect of polyphenols on intestinal and BBB integrity in PD animal models

This section reviews evidence from MPTP-induced PD mouse models (all in 8-week-old mice), demonstrating how CA (30 and 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 12 days) effectively restored the expression of ZO-1 and occludin, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity. Furthermore, CA demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect on the excessive activation of glial cells, as well as reduced levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) [42]. Administration of polymannuronic acid (30 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) significantly increased the expressions of ZO-1 and occludin in both the colon and SNpc, indicating that the integrity of the intestinal barrier and BBB improved in response to polymannuronic acid intake in 8-week-old MPPT mice [44]. Similarly, curcumin (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 7 weeks) restored ZO-1 and occludin and intestinal barrier integrity [45] (Table 2). The possible mechanism of some polyphenols in PD is summarized in Fig. 2. These findings suggest that polyphenols may be promising agents for preserving barrier integrity in PD.

The effect of polyphenols on the gut Microbiome in PD animal models

This section summarizes the findings from MPTP-induced PD mouse studies, illustrating how various polyphenols affect the gut microbiota. CA (30 or 60 mg/kg, p.o., for 12 days) increased Firmicutes and decreased Bacteroidetes in 8-week-old MPPT mice [42]. Fisetin (100 ng/kg, p.o., for 30 days) significantly increased the abundance of Lachnospiraceae and decreased significantly the abundance of uncultured_bacterium_g_Escherichia-Shigella and uncultured_bacterium_g_Bacillus in 12-week-old MPTP mice [43]. Polymannuronic acid at a dose of 30 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks significantly decreased the abundance of Turicibacter and Lactobacillus in 8-week-old MPPT mice [44]. Curcumin (60 mg/kg, p.o., for 7 weeks) reversed gut microbial dysbiosis, restoring the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio and increasing beneficial genera, such as Turicimonas and Culturomica, in 8-week-old MPPT mice [45].

Polyphenols, such as CA, fisetin, polymannuronic acid, and curcumin, effectively modulate gut microbiota composition, often by enhancing beneficial bacteria, reducing pathogenic ones, and restoring microbial balance, including the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio [42–45]. These interventions highlight the prebiotic potential of polyphenols in alleviating dysbiosis associated with PD progression via the gut-brain axis.

Discussion

In this research, we systematically reviewed existing literature on the impact of polyphenols on gut microbiome changes and their metabolites in animal models of two specific NDDs. The analysis focused on related processes, including inflammation indicators, oxidative stress markers, and the integrity of gut and brain barriers. Polyphenols that we examined, including CA, Fisetin, polymannuronic acid, GA, BME, sesamol, quercetin, Q3G, PEBG, curcumin, and EGCG. These compounds were found to enhance SCFA levels by modulating gut microbiota, resulting in improvements in cognitive and motor functions in animals with AD and PD-like models. Additionally, they significantly reduced proinflammatory factors and oxidative stress in the brain tissue of NDD-affected animals, while promoting beneficial microbial taxa and suppressing harmful ones.

Recent studies have demonstrated that MGBA can affect the CNS by affecting the vagus nerve, neuroendocrine system, and enteric nervous system (ENS) [48, 49]. The interconnection between the gut and the CNS is established by the ENS [50]. The vagus nerve regulates several functions in the gut, including motility, intestinal permeability, and inflammation in the gut through the ENS [51, 52]. Moreover, the vagus nerve can receive messages from the gut microbiome through the produced metabolites by the microbiota [53]. Bravo et al. reported that some bacterial phyla can regulate the level of brain neurotransmitters, including GABA, glutamate, and 5-HT, by stimulating the vagus nerve, which can decrease anxiety-related behavior in mice [54]. Additionally, it is shown that enteroendocrine cells lining the gut epithelium can release particular hormones such as glucagon-like peptide-1, leptin, and ghrelin, which modify energy production and affect various functions in the CNS, including our feelings [55]. Disruptions in the MGBA may play a critical role in both neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental conditions [56–58]. Such disruptions can contribute to NDDs through multiple pathways, including immune system dysfunction, increased permeability of the BBB, and neuroinflammation triggered by neurotoxin release [59, 60]. It has also been noted that impairments of MGBA can result in deficits in microglia function, which represent one of the main pathological factors underlying NDDs [61–63]. The overactivation of microglia can cause unnecessary damage to neurons, thereby contributing to the development of NDDs [64]. Certain influences of polyphenols on the gut-brain axis in the context of NDDs are outlined in Fig. 3.

Summary of the effects of polyphenols on the gut-brain axis in neurodegenerative diseases

It has been demonstrated that inflammatory markers, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, which are produced through the gut microbiome, can significantly influence the development of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [65]. Research has shown that chronic neuroinflammation can worsen neuronal damage [66]. This review highlights compounds such as baicalein [22], GA [20], CGA [23], sesamol [24, 36], Nano-Honokiol [27], Q3G [33], BME [40], ginger [38], Triphala [41], and pterostilbene [37] which consistently reduced levels of IL-1β and TNF-α in the brain, with similar decreases observed for IL-6 across multiple interventions [20, 22, 23, 27, 33, 37, 40] and IL-2 specifically with BME [40]. Conversely, GA, CGA, and Q3G increased antiinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10, IL-4, and IL-5 [20, 23, 33], indicating a balanced modulation of the inflammatory response. Additionally, a study involving polymannuronic acid treatment in MPTP-induced PD models showed significant reductions in proinflammatory markers TNF-α and IL-6 in both the colon and SNpc. The intervention specifically suppressed the expression of COX-2 and iNOS in the colon [44]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of polyphenols in reducing neuroinflammation through the MGBA, offering promising opportunities for addressing the pathology of NDD and warranting further clinical research.

It is demonstrated that dysbiosis is correlated to a decreased abundance of bacterial phyla such as Faecalibacterium, Bifidobacterium, and Bacteroides, which produce SCFAs in the gut. Additionally, dysbiosis weakens the integrity of the BBB by reducing the secretion of SCFAs. It accelerates the entry of microbial metabolites into the brain tissue, thereby stimulating inflammatory responses in the brain [67]. SCFAs have been shown to play a protective role against NDDs through several mechanisms. They help reduce neuroinflammation, act as signaling molecules to facilitate communication between the gut and the brain, and enhance the integrity of the BBB [68]. Overall, interventions with various polyphenols—including sesamol, quercetin, Q3G, Triphala, ginger, pterostilbene, curcumin, polymannuronic acid, and CA—consistently elevated key metabolites such as SCFAs, which were associated with improved cognitive function, reduced neuroinflammation, and enhanced gut-brain axis activity in both AD and PD models [32, 33, 36–38, 41, 42, 44, 45]. These results suggest that polyphenols may exert neuroprotective effects by correcting microbial imbalances and boosting antiinflammatory and antioxidant metabolites capable of crossing the BBB, thereby alleviating hallmark features of neurodegeneration. However, some variability in outcomes highlights the influence of compound type, dosage, and experimental design, emphasizing the need for standardized protocols in future research to better define the therapeutic potential of polyphenols in NDDs.

Gut dysbiosis, characterized by an imbalance in the gut microbiome, results in the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which triggers inflammation by compromising gut barrier integrity, activating the immune response, and altering metabolic pathways [69]. This dysbiosis is further aggravated by ROS and inflammation, weakening intestinal wall integrity and enabling bacterial antigens and toxins to infiltrate gut cells and enter the bloodstream, thereby amplifying ROS generation [70]. Dietary polyphenols, such as grape polyphenol extract, have shown potential in mitigating gut dysbiosis by reducing ROS levels and promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria like Akkermansia muciniphila. These bacteria, often diminished in obese mice, play a crucial role in reducing extracellular ROS levels in the gut, thereby contributing to enhanced gut health [71]. This review highlights that oral interventions such as GA (100 mg/kg, for 112 days), CGA (80 mg/kg, for 16 weeks), resveratrol (60 mg/kg, for 24 weeks), and pterostilbene (20 and 60 mg/kg, for 10 weeks) effectively boosted antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase, reducing oxidative stress in HFD-fed rats, transgenic mice, and diabetic models [20, 23, 34, 37].

Recent studies have reported that changes in the gut microbiome in PD animals can cause the abnormal release of α-synuclein in the gut, which can then enter the brain tissue, possibly through the vagus nerve [72, 73]. Additionally, an increased accumulation of α-synuclein is reported as a consequence of gut dysbiosis and inflammation, resulting from impairments in TLR signaling pathways [74]. Some bacteria in the gut microbiome can secrete amyloids. It is shown that Pseudomonas, Streptomyces, and Bacillus subtilis can release FapC, chaplins, and TasA fibres, respectively, which are all particular types of amyloids and reflect similar structures to those that are responsible for NDDs [75, 76]. Recent animal studies have demonstrated that the beneficial effects of certain polyphenolic compounds in combating AD are primarily due to their ability to inhibit the aggregation of Aβ in the brain [77]. In this review, for amyloid and tau pathologies, outcomes varied: polyphenolic extract from PEBG (500 or 2500 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 months) had no impact on proteins such as beta-secretase 1 and metalloproteinase domain 10 [21], while quercetin (80 mg/kg, p.o., for 21 days or 16 weeks) lowered cathepsin B and tau phosphorylation [35, 39]; RRP (400 mg/kg, p.o., for 21 days) reduced pSer404-tau and enhanced INSR and IRS-1 expression [30]; and ginger (100 mg/kg, p.o., for 4 weeks) decreased hippocampal Aβ plaque deposition [41]. These findings suggest polyphenols have the potential to alleviate AD hallmarks through antioxidant and anti-proteinopathic mechanisms. However, variabilities underscore the need for standardized research and human trials to develop effective therapies.

Additionally, it is suggested that polyphenols can improve the function of MGBA in other ways. Some studies reported that various polyphenols, including genistein, resveratrol, and quercetin, can fortify the intestinal tight junction, which regulates inflammatory responses throughout the gut [78–83]. Several in vitro studies have discussed the biotransformation of polyphenols such as anthocyanins, rutin, and quercetin by gut microbiota [84, 85]. It has been demonstrated that flavonoids and anthocyanins can be converted into new metabolites after exposure to gut microbiota [86]. This review highlights that polyphenol-rich treatments, such as BME, pterostilbene, ginger, sesamol, and TG-Res-CS/TPP-NPs, are significantly effective in restoring gut and BBB integrity in animal models of AD and related conditions like DCI. These compounds work by upregulating the expression of key tight junction proteins, including occludin, ZO-1, and claudin-1, which help reduce barrier permeability, lower LPS levels, and decrease inflammation, as demonstrated by studies [24, 34, 36, 37, 40, 41]. By targeting the gut-brain axis, polyphenols can prevent neuroinflammation, inhibit the accumulation of Aβ, and alleviate cognitive decline. However, outcomes may vary depending on model-specific factors, such as ApoE dependency. Overall, these findings emphasize polyphenols as promising, non-invasive therapeutic options for AD treatment. Despite their potential, further research is necessary, including the development of personalized strategies, approaches, and rigorous human clinical trials, to ensure the safe and effective translation of findings from animal models to clinical practice.

Furthermore, polyphenols have shown promising effects in preserving intestinal and BBB integrity in MPTP-induced PD mouse models. CA effectively restored the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin, thereby enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, while also inhibiting excessive glial cell activation and improving levels of neurotrophic factors, such as BDNF and GDNF [42]. Polymannuronic acid similarly increased ZO-1 and occludin expression in both the colon and SNpc, improving the integrity of both the intestinal barrier and BBB [44]. Curcumin restored the expression of ZO-1 and occludin, further reinforcing intestinal barrier integrity [45]. These findings highlight polyphenols as potential therapeutic agents for maintaining barrier integrity in PD, with mechanisms summarized in Fig. 2.

Moreover, some studies have reported that polyphenols can significantly modulate the gut microflora [20, 22–25, 27, 28, 31, 33, 34, 38, 87]. One of the most studied gut microflora is the Firmicutes bacteria. Changes in the abundance of Firmicutes bacteria may contribute to the development of NDDs through several pathways, including the modulation of neuroinflammatory processes and the influence on the accumulation of pathogenic proteins such as Aβ and α-synuclein [88]. Most animal studies have shown that polyphenols can decrease the abundance of Firmicutes. However, one study found opposite results, highlighting the complexity of this relationship and the need for further research [42]. Polyphenol-based treatments, including GA, curcumin, resveratrol, sesamol, CA, fisetin, and polymannuronic acid, consistently demonstrated their ability to modulate gut microbiota composition across various studies. These interventions increased beneficial taxa, such as Bacteroidetes, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Rikenellaceae, and Alloprevotella, while reducing harmful groups, including Helicobacter, Bacteroidaceae, Rikenella, and Prevotella. Additionally, polyphenols restored microbial balance, including the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, in diverse models such as transgenic APP/PS1 mice, HFD-fed rats, and obesity-related AD rodents. These dose-dependent and context-specific microbial shifts were associated with reduced inflammation, improved cognitive outcomes, and alleviation of dysbiosis linked to PD progression, emphasizing the prebiotic potential of polyphenols via the gut-brain axis.

To summarize, this study aimed to assess how polyphenols alter the gut microbiome and how their metabolites affect the MGBA in animal models representing NDDs. This involved analyzing related mechanisms in the gut microbiota, including markers of inflammation, indicators of oxidative stress, and the stability of gut and brain barriers. The results showed that polyphenols and their metabolites can enhance the gut microbiome, potentially alleviating brain inflammation in animals with neurodegenerative conditions and improving their overall symptoms.

Future research should focus on translational studies, including randomized controlled trials in AD patients, to confirm these microbial modulations and their effects on cognition. Investigating synergistic combinations of polyphenols, precise dosing regimens, and underlying molecular pathways (e.g., via metagenomics and metabolomics) could improve therapeutic strategies. Ultimately, adding polyphenol-rich diets or supplements into AD prevention and treatment offers great potential, possibly transforming non-pharmacological approaches to combat this debilitating disease and highlighting the gut microbiota as a modifiable target in neurodegenerative health.

Finally, inclusion of polyphenol-rich interventions in PD therapy may offer novel preventive strategies that enhance the quality of life of PD patients.

Limitations

All studies included are preclinical and animal only, with no human participants, resulting in a lack of applicability to humans. Results may not be generalizable due to interspecies variations in metabolic rate, gut microbiota, and disease origin. Furthermore, most of these studies did not match animals based on age, disease duration, or sex, which may introduce selection bias. There is also a lack of head-to-head comparisons between different polyphenols in terms of their unique impact on the gut microbiome, which also complicates understanding of their disparate contributions. Variations in formulations, routes of administration, and durations of polyphenol use make direct comparison among studies difficult. Some compounds (e.g., EGCG) exhibited inconsistent results, such as inhibiting beneficial bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium. Long-term safety, bioavailability, and efficacy in humans remain underexplored, emphasizing the need for clinical trials to confirm these preliminary results.
