Introduction

Peritoneal inclusion cysts (PICs) are relatively rare cystic formations lined with mesothelial cells, primarily found in perimenopausal women. They can manifest as pelvic masses, pelvic discomfort, or pain, often leading to potential misdiagnosis as ovarian masses [1]. In this report, we present a case of PIC where clinical and radiological assessments initially indicated a benign ovarian lesion. However, upon closer examination during laparoscopy and subsequent histopathological analysis, it was conclusively identified as a peritoneal inclusion cyst. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges associated with PICs and highlights the critical role of laparoscopy and pathology in achieving an accurate diagnosis. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [2].

Case presentation

A 30-year-old female patient, with no significant past medical or surgical history, presented to our hospital due to mild left lower abdominal discomfort persisting for two days. Physical evaluation was unremarkable. Laboratory assessments, including a negative b-hcG test, ruled out pregnancy. She had no history of bleeding and no known drug allergies. In her obstetric history, she had one previous pregnancy resulting in a normal vaginal delivery of a 3.3 kg baby ten years ago, and her last menstrual period occurred on February 23, 2023. Pelvic ultrasound revealed a 4.5 cm cystic mass in her left ovary, while her right ovary and uterus appeared normal (Fig. 1). Further investigation with CA 125 and CA 19.9 blood tests showed no abnormalities. Operative course

Under general anesthesia in the Lloyd-Davies position, the patient's skin was thoroughly cleaned and drapes were applied. The bladder had been previously catheterized to ensure a clear surgical field. The laparoscopic procedure began with the insertion of three laparoscopic punctures through the Verres needle. This allowed for the creation of a pneumo-peritoneum, which is crucial for laparoscopic surgeries.

Upon exploration, omental adhesions were observed connecting to the pelvic sidewalls and the pod (pelvic organ or Douglas pouch). Adhesiolysis, the surgical process of separating adhesions, was performed to release these connections. During the procedure, a left-sided hydrosalpinx (fluid-filled fallopian tube) was identified, with a more pronounced presence on the left side. Additionally, a left inclusion cyst measuring approximately 4 × 5 cm was identified and carefully excised (Fig. 2). The excised cyst was sent for pathological examination to determine its nature.Fig. 2Selected intraoperative images showing the inclusion cyst and the normal left ovary.Fig. 2

While the left ovary was found to be embedded within the epiploic covering the bowel, it appeared to be normal in size. Hemostasis was meticulously ensured throughout the procedure, with repeated suction and irrigation to maintain a clear surgical field. After the successful completion of the procedure, the abdomen was gently deflated, and the skin incisions were closed using 2–0 proline sutures. Subsequently, the Foley catheter, which had been inserted to manage urinary output during the surgery, was removed. Histopathological report confirmed the inclusion cyst diagnosis and showed no malignant findings.

Discussion

Peritoneal inclusion cysts (PICs), also known as benign (multi) cystic peritoneal mesothelioma, inflammatory cysts of the peritoneum, postoperative peritoneal cysts, or benign papillary peritoneal cystosis, represent a diagnostic challenge for gynecologists and radiologists alike. These mesothelium-lined cysts typically make their appearance in perimenopausal women, presenting a spectrum of clinical symptoms. Commonly, patients with PICs experience progressive abdominal or pelvic pain, often accompanied by the presence of a palpable mass [[3], [4], [5]]. However, in our reported case, the patient's primary complaint was mild abdominal discomfort rather than severe pain. PICs can also, albeit rarely, lead to symptoms such as backache, dyspareunia, constipation, tenesmus, urinary frequency or incontinence, anorexia, dysfunctional uterine bleeding, or infertility. In some instances, PICs may even contribute to pulmonary embolism and venous stasis due to compressive effects [6,7].

Several risk factors have been associated with the development of PICs. These include a history of previous intraperitoneal surgeries performed within a timeframe ranging from 6 months to 20 years, intraperitoneal inflammation, pelvic inflammatory disease, peritoneal tuberculosis, leiomyoma, tubo-ovarian abscess, among others [8]. Notably, our patient did not have a history of surgical procedures or any of the aforementioned predisposing conditions, making this case particularly intriguing in the context of PIC diagnosis.

PICs can sometimes be wrongly identified as mucinous cystadenomas of the ovaries due to their similar clinical presentations and slight elevation of CA125, which is produced by the coelomic epithelium in both conditions. Ultrasound (USG) findings are not specific and typically show smooth, thin-walled multiseptated cysts containing fluid with varying densities [4]. Similarly, CT scans may reveal a cobweb-like appearance with fluid-filled compartments and septations, often involving the peritoneal space and either containing or closely associated with the adjacent ovary. During surgery, PICs often appear as a continuous mass or clusters of interconnected cysts [5].

Unfamiliar with this condition, our initial differential diagnosis considered the possibility of an ovarian cyst. The suggested pathology for PICs involves the inability of the affected, inflamed, or fibrosed peritoneum to absorb the natural secretions of active ovaries, leading to the formation of cysts within peritoneal adhesions. The diagnosis of this uncommon condition was initially missed, likely due to the patient's similar age, symptoms, clinical signs, and USG findings, all of which resembled those of a benign condition of the ovary.

The management of PICs encompasses a range of options, including watchful waiting with regular imaging, hormonal therapy involving medications like oral contraceptives, tamoxifen, or leuprolide, image-guided aspiration with or without sclerotherapy, and surgical removal, which can be done through laparoscopy or laparotomy for complete resection [5].

Hormonal treatment involving oral contraceptive combinations has shown favorable outcomes. Additionally, ultrasound (USG) or fluoroscopy-guided sclerotherapy using substances like 10 % iodine or absolute ethanol has been reported to achieve a success rate of around 90 %. While the gold standard treatment is complete surgical removal, either laparoscopically or through laparotomy, it's important to note that recurrence can occur in up to 50 % of cases [5].

Conclusion

Raising awareness about peritoneal inclusion cysts (PICs), especially in patients with predisposing conditions or even in cases where there are no known predisposing factors, can be instrumental in suspecting and diagnosing this uncommon condition. Increased awareness may enable healthcare providers to consider conservative management in select cases while closely monitoring for potential recurrence.
