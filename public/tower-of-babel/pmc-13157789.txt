Congo’s conflicts, minerals and microbes: planetary health implications
Authors: Shahul H Ebrahim, John Ditekemena, Barrak Alahmad, Ann-Christine Duhaime, Dieudonné Mwamba, Ziad A Memish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe abrupt re-escalation of the three-decade conflict in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) in January 2025, the July peace deal and new mineral extraction agreements highlight the Congo Basin’s global significance. Known as the world’s largest rare mineral reserve, the ‘second lung of the planet’, and a hotspot for emerging infectious diseases, the region embodies critical planetary health challenges. The conflict intensifies sylvatic-to-human transmission of zoonoses (involving at least 15 known pathogens), undermines climate change mitigation and conservation, disrupts human development—including education—and inflicts intergenerational harm.In the Congo basin, conflict-driven displacement and close contact with forests create ideal conditions for sporadic disease spillover. These risks are magnified by ecosystem disruptions from logging, mining, agriculture, wildlife trade, firewood collection and tourism, all intersecting with demographic shifts that push communities deeper into forested areas.Global demand for DRC minerals is surging, but without parallel investments in human development, biodiversity protection and disease surveillance, exploitation could accelerate regional instability and global health threats. The June 2025 peace deal and the rise of a mineral-based economy present a critical opportunity to align extraction with peacebuilding, sustainable development and climate resilience. Investments in enhanced surveillance and preparedness systems are essential to prevent transboundary pathogen spread and preserve fragile ecosystems. As the world grows increasingly dependent on the Congo’s mineral wealth, realising its benefits requires renewed global commitment to ensure that resource extraction strengthens peace, resilience and planetary health rather than deepening conflict and instability.Keywords: Communicable Diseases, Emerging; Africa; Hemorrhagic Fever, Ebola

Background

The Congo Basin holds three global distinctions: it has the largest rare mineral reserves such as coltan (used in electronics and communications manufacturing); is the ‘second lung of the planet’ as it contains the second largest rainforest; and is a hotspot for disease emergence.1 The abrupt re-escalation of the 30-year-old conflict in the east of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) in January 2025 has significance beyond the region as it cross-cuts planetary health and global development. Adding to the cumulative 7.1 million people displaced and close to 6 million killed, the 2025 escalation of the conflict has claimed over 7000 lives, displaced approximately 700 000 individuals, destroyed 90 displacement camps, forced closure of over 2500 schools and increased verified grave violations against children by 100% compared with the first quarter of 2024.2 3 Although peace is on the horizon, reports of fighting continue.

In addition to the direct suffering endured by the people of Congo during the 30-year conflict, three silent, interconnected and compounding impacts often go unnoticed: (1) infectious diseases, (2) climate setbacks and (3) intergenerational allostatic effects. DRC’s 16th documented Ebola outbreak, which began in September 2025, underscores these complexities. In this article, we advocate for a renewed postconflict development approach that integrates these less examined planetary health and human development perspectives.

Impact of conflict on human and animal disease transmission

Conflicts compound the stressors on the Congo basin, a hotspot for disease emergence (table 1), by exacerbating factors that enable the sylvatic to human transmission dynamics of zoonosis. Conflict-related land destruction alters biological interactions between living organisms promoting the alignment of events required for infectious emergence in humans.4 Conflict-related displacement increases human–animal contact. Deforestation affects habitat use by frugivorous bats, and there is a link between the destruction of natural bat habitats and the transmission of their viruses to other hosts. When forest fragmentation disrupts the ecology of non-human primates, it influences bidirectional spillover of bacteria and viruses within those fragments.5 These modifications/adaptations of species to their environment will directly or indirectly (eg, via interspecies competition) contribute to the modification of wildlife communities. Continued mineral extraction that increases contact of the outside world with the population of the Congo basin sustains sporadic disease transmission potential.

Conflict in the DRC overwhelms limited disease detection and control capacities amid a compromised public health infrastructure, as evidenced by cholera outbreaks, exacerbation of endemic diseases such as malaria, and increased incidence and severity of infections due to reduced access to vaccines, healthcare and nutrition,5 as reflected in outbreaks originating in or prevalent within the DRC (table 1). The 2024 DRC outbreak of an initially undiagnosed illness causing 406 cases and 31 deaths raised fears of ‘disease X’ (a placeholder for a pathogen of pandemic potential), later confirmed as malaria complicated by malnutrition. Mpox, first recorded in 1970 in the DRC, had been reported by 121 countries by 2024, with ongoing transmission in 23 countries.6 Although Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever virus was not detected in the Congo Basin until 1956, recent evidence indicates active circulation among livestock and ticks.7 The 2014–2016 West Africa Ebola crisis renewed attention on the Congo Basin’s zoonotic potential and strengthened the Global Health Security Agenda.

Tropical low-latitude forests such as the Congo Basin have high mammalian biomass diversity and harbour microbes highly pathogenic to humans relative to their sylvatic hosts (table 1). Forest disturbance, pathogen richness, increasing human–animal contact and conflict-related displacement have facilitated zoonotic spillover. The rise of HIV illustrates the long-term envirodevelopmental dynamics of zoonosis.1 8 Early HIV genetic diversity reflected multiple independent spillover events from chimpanzees, gorillas and sooty mangabeys in Central and West Africa in the 1920s. Rail and river transport in Congo accelerated dissemination, with transatlantic spread via returning Haitian teachers; cases were first recognised in 1981.

Impact of conflict on climate and environment

The Congo Basin, site of the world’s second largest tropical rainforest and also known as the ‘second lung of the world’, is a crucial asset for meliorating the impact of climate change. The Congo basin also holds over two-thirds of the global reserves of rare minerals, leading to the legal and illegal mining that is believed to underscore the three-decade-long conflict. Wars, the region’s stagnated development status over the past three decades, and the implementation challenges of conservation (table 1) are all multiple stressors that are expected to result in the disappearance of at least 27% of undisturbed rainforests in the Congo Basin by 2050.1

Wars have serious negative effects on the total environment. Deforestation (34%), soil erosion (23%) and loss of wildlife (and biodiversity) (18%) are the most cited environmental effects of wars worldwide.9 In the Congo Basin, conflict has derailed the conservation plans of the Central African Forestry Commission Subregional Convergence Plan. These issues were outlined in the Joint Donor Statement at the 2021 Conference of the Parties climate summit that pledged US$1.5 billion of financing between 2021 and 2025 for the Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and forest Degradation, and enhancement of forest carbon stocks (REDD + process) and the strategic options outlined under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change.1 Conflict has deprived the Congo Basin of benefitting from the global financing pledge.

Impact of conflict on intergenerational human development and socioeconomic determinants

Advanced sustainable mineral extraction practices require trainable manpower. Congo’s conflict has stalled human development in the Congo Basin. Over 1.6 million children in eastern DRC are now out of school. Adding to the direct physical effects of conflicts, indirect effects include the losses of loved ones, educational opportunities, traditional family structure and safe housing. Displacement and experiencing or witnessing violence is associated with post-traumatic stress, and dependents living with distressed caregivers further increase insecurity and the allostatic load.10,12

When people avoid farming and gathering food because they fear gender-based violence, it leads to greater food insecurity and financial hardship. Distrust in the medical establishment and other development efforts was experienced by Ebola responders, including the first author (SHE) during the 2012 Ebola outbreak. Direct as well as epigenetic effects of trauma and malnutrition in prenatal and early life may also affect subsequent generations. For example, during 2022–2023, in Kivu and Ituri provinces, about 75 000 children were displaced and at least 2100 schools were forced to stop operating due to the conflict.13

The collective effects of experiences during conflicts that extend to future generations as adverse events (table 1) are likely to create additional risk for increased morbidity and mortality. Sporadic outbreak-related global interventions without a sustained development plan and peace efforts have led to distrust in global assistance among the people of the Congo basin. Such distrust has proved detrimental to public health interventions such as the Ebola outbreak response.

The way forward

The ever-increasing global demand for the DRC’s minerals14 along with the global benefits of the Congo Basin remaining as the ‘second lung of the planet’ and the challenges from perturbations of its biomass concentration and diversity—all are powerful arguments for human development in the Congo Basin as a shared global responsibility.

First, interventions to reduce the intergenerational effects of conflicts on society, which are often sidelined amid competing priorities, are required to uplift the population to meet the growing workforce needs of just and sustainable mineral extraction. Decades of war, distrust and underdevelopment have deprived the Congo Basin population’s ability to evolve into a society competitive within the current global economic framework. Conflict-specific rehabilitation efforts across childhood (teaching socioemotional competencies, improving conflict resolution, promoting democratic coexistence in schools), and for youth (technical and vocational education and training aligned with emerging post-conflict needs) and adults (agricultural and economic empowerment coupled with trauma healing) are needed along with the establishment of cohorts to monitor adverse outcomes.

Second, there is a need to mainstream the concepts of environmental preservation and climate impact reduction among affected populations, parties to conflict and international actors. Global demand-suppressing interventions on trade in wildlife, wildlife products and forest products can reduce economic motivation incentives for deforestation. Science that incorporates society-participatory and citizen-participatory approach is a useful additional tool.15

Third, from a broader health sector reform perspective, the newly proposed humanitarian-development-peace nexus (HDPN) framework aligns well with the needs of DRC. HDPN is a structured framework that integrates health across the HDPN, emphasising policy alignment, sustainable financing, strong monitoring systems and adaptable governance.16 HDPN fills the gaps in piecemeal approaches during postconflict reconstruction and can collectively promote health security, achieving universal health coverage and the right of access to health for all.

Finally, positioning health security reform as part of overall health systems reforms is essential as outlined in the 10 post-COVID-19 proposals. Strengthening and operationalising a One Health approach within the bushmeat value chain to map both emerging pathogens and climate risks is critical. Use of citizen science and satellite spatial technologies advocated by the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration, shotgun pan-biodiversity monitoring (non-invasive eDNA sampling) using current portable and cloud-based approaches, and genomic monitoring of pathogens of global relevance using high-performance diagnostic systems can inform the natural history of future disease outbreaks. Use of aerial drones for transfer of biological specimens, medical equipment and medicine in hard-to-reach areas would be a helpful step. These efforts would enable the establishment and strengthening of targeted public health surveillance programmes.

The emerging DRC mineral wealth extraction deals should help chart a new course and fund development in the Congo Basin. In 2024, DRC attracted the largest volume of mineral exploration investments among all countries, and ranked 20th globally in exploration investment.14 Partnership with and involvement of the Pandemic Fund, the Africa Epidemic Fund, the International Monetary Fund’s Resilience and Sustainability Trust, and the Green Climate Fund can help propel this effort.

The public health, climate and intergenerational consequences of the conflict in the Congo basin reflect just a thin edge of its broader development issues. The need to contain zoonotic spillover presents a powerful economic argument to help the population of the Congo basin achieve the basic standard of ‘freedom from want’ while aligning with global principles of planetary health now and for future generations.
