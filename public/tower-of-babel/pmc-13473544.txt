Influence of hybrid fiber reinforcement on strength and durability properties of fly ash blended PSC concrete
Authors: J. Amirtharaj, S. Natarajan, J. Rajprasad, G. K. Arunvivek, Pramod Kumar, Regasa Yadeta Sembeta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The increasing demand for eco-friendly construction materials has driven the adoption of supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash (FA) and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), as they offer a lower carbon footprint and improved durability. Portland Slag Cement (PSC) has been introduced as a blended cement, but its slower hydration and delayed pozzolanic reaction reduce early-age strength, limiting structural applications. This study aims to overcome this limitation by incorporating hybrid fibers such as basalt fibers (BF) and recycled steel fibers (RSF) at volume fractions of 0%-0.75% to enhance the early-age mechanical performance of PSC concrete. Around 15 different concrete mixes (with fiber) were formulated along with 1 control mix (without fiber) and tested for their properties at 7, 14, and 28 days. It was found that the control mix exhibited lower early strength due to slower PSC hydration and a delayed pozzolanic reaction in fly ash. However, the inclusion of fibers significantly compensated for this weakness. At 7 days, compressive strength increased from 26.2 MPa for Low-Carbon Slag Cement (LCSC), to a maximum of 32.3 MPa for the hybrid mix LCSC11 (0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF), representing an improvement of about 23%. Similar synergistic improvements were observed in split tensile and flexural strengths, with 28-day values reaching 5.4 MPa and 8.3 MPa, respectively, for the optimum hybrid mix. The inclusion of 0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF (LCSC11) into the fly ash-modified PSC concrete exhibited enhanced durability properties, with a 26% reduction in water absorption rate, a 30% reduction in sorptivity, a 41% reduction in chloride ion ingress, and a 34% reduction in weight loss under acid exposure. The findings demonstrate that hybrid BF-RSF reinforcement effectively offsets the early-age strength deficiency of fly ash-modified PSC concrete while significantly enhancing its tensile and flexural performance.

Introduction

The primary binder used in the concrete was Portland Cement (PC), which, due to its high clinker content, significantly contributes to carbon emissions and embodied energy. It was reported that the incorporation of mineral admixtures such as ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBS), fly ash (FA), silica fume, etc., minimizes the carbon and embodied energy from cement production, while also enhancing the durability of concrete structures1–3. Over the past two decades, the utilization of mineral admixtures either directly or as blended cement in concrete has increased. The primary concerns with using mineral admixtures, which have persisted among most OPC users, are delayed setting and strength development, variability in the quality of mineral admixtures, and inadequate standards. In most cases, mineral admixtures are used as partial replacements for OPC, especially in Ready-Mix Concrete (RMC) and site batching plants. It has been reported that the utilization of mineral admixtures as blended cement, achieved through inter-grinding or blending, will develop superior concrete properties due to consistent quality uniformity and better-quality control practices4. Blended cement is produced by inter-grinding OPC clinker with gypsum and mineral admixtures. Portland Slag Cement (PSC) is one such blended cement produced using Portland cement clinker, Gypsum (5%), and GGBFS (25% to 70%) as per the recommendations of IS: 455–20155. The lower strength development of GGBS concrete compared to OPC concrete results in slower construction activity. GGBS is a by-product of the iron ore processing industry, which is obtained by quenching the molten slag using water to obtain sand-like glassy particles. The reactivity of the particles is enhanced when this glassy granulate is ground to very fine particles (mostly less than 10 microns); hence ground particles are termed as GGBS.

Comprehensive studies on the use of GGBS as a partial replacement for OPC have reported that it reduces early strength due to its slower hydration rate6–11. Teng et al. reported that this low strength development can be overcome by utilizing a low water-cement ratio at a specified replacement level6. The reduction in early-age strength becomes more pronounced as the GGSB replacement level increases7. Wang et al. reported that the difference in early-age compressive strength decreases at lower w/c ratios8. This lower strength development was compensated by the extended curing periods6,7,11,12. Some studies13–17 proposed using GGBS as a partial replacement for OPC, along with other SCMs such as fly ash and silica fume, in ternary blends. Some studies18–22 proposed using GGBS as a partial replacement for OPC, along with various industrial wastes as aggregates. Arularasi et al. reported that a blended mortar incorporating 10% OPC, 30% fly ash, and 60% GGBS exhibited a maximum flexural strength of 3.667 MPa and a split tensile strength of 0.361 MPa at 56 days15. Radwan et al. reported that a ternary blend with 20% fly ash and 30% GGBS partially replacing OPC reduces early-age strength16. However, a significant improvement in strength at later ages was observed with continued curing. Shubbar et al. reported that a ternary blended binder consisting of 35 wt% OPC, 35 wt% GGBS, and 30 wt% high-calcium fly ash (HCFA) achieved a compressive strength of 30.8 MPa, which was approximately 96% of that of the OPC binder, indicating a slight reduction in strength compared to conventional OPC concrete23.

Fibers are commonly used as reinforcement to compensate for concrete’s inherent brittleness24–29. The current market offers a wide variety of short fibers, but their high cost and large carbon footprint pose significant economic and sustainability risks30,31. FRC composites are relatively recent and innovative, offering several benefits, including high flexural and tensile strengths, the ability to be shaped into various structural forms under a variety of loading conditions, and the ability to remain unaffected for long periods without repair. The addition of one fiber or a hybrid mixture of fibers to concrete has no significant effect on the compressive strength and elastic modulus but has a significant effect on the flexural and tensile strength, toughness, and ductility. These FRC concrete also have excellent bending strength, resistance to deformation, acceptable energy absorption, enhanced impact resistance, and resistance to cracking. One of the most effective methods to enhance the mechanical and physical properties of concrete and reinforcing materials is to strengthen short fibers and make them tougher32,33. Carbon, glass, or even inorganic fibers are often used in the concrete. The use of natural fiber as a reinforcement in concrete is one of the comparatively recent developments in concrete composites34,35. Natural fibers are light, strong, economical and can be easily produced; they can be incorporated into concrete without making any ecological problems. However, relative to other forms of fiber, Basalt Fibre (BF) was made from volcanic rocks in a less energy-intensive process, and is a sustainable alternative.

Previous studies36–38 have shown that incorporating BF into concrete can markedly enhance the mechanical and durability properties of fibre-reinforced concrete (FRC). Lv et al. reported that even a small addition of about 0.3% basalt fiber increased the flexural strength by 11.9%, while also improving impact resistance with higher fibre content39. In addition, BF exhibits good resistance to both low and high temperatures and is chemically compatible with silicate-based matrices40. Owing to these advantages, BF can serve as an effective alternative to glass fibre (GF), steel fibre (SF), and Carbon Fibre (CF) in various construction applications41. The contribution of BF to compressive strength is generally limited; however, its influence on tensile and flexural properties is significant42. The inclusion of BF also helps reduce brittleness while improving durability, deformation capacity, and fracture resistance of concrete43. Several studies have reported only marginal gains in compressive strength with the addition of BF, whereas notable improvements are observed in flexural strength, modulus of elasticity (MOE), splitting tensile strength (STS), and fracture energy44,45. Fang et al. observed a slight increase in MOE with a fibre content of 0.2% by volume46. Similarly, Khan et al. reported that shorter fibres tend to slightly improve the modulus of elasticity (MOE), whereas longer fibres may reduce it due to increased void formation47. Furthermore, numerous researchers have extensively investigated BF-reinforced concrete, examining the effects of fibre inclusion on early-age shrinkage, crack development, impact resistance, compressive strength, tensile and flexural performance, and durability aspects such as impermeability48–52.

On the other side, the utilization of Recycled Steel Fibers (RSF) recovered from waste has gained increasing interest as a sustainable solution for producing environmentally friendly structural concrete. Numerous experimental studies have evaluated the mechanical performance of RSF-incorporated concrete, with generally encouraging results. For example, investigations by Masmoudi & Bouaziz53 and Micelli et al.54 demonstrated that the inclusion of RSF significantly improved the mechanical characteristics of concrete, while exerting minimal influence on durability and environmental performance. Similarly, Ismail et al.55 observed that RSF effectively reduced crack width and crack depth, thereby limiting the penetration of aggressive chemical agents into the concrete matrix. Enhanced flexural and compressive strength due to the incorporation of waste steel fibers has also been reported by Suleman et al.56, Liew and Akbar57, and Tuma and Aziz58.

The hybrid combination of steel and BF offers complementary benefits within the concrete matrix, combining the high strength and ductility of steel fibres with the crack-bridging and durability characteristics of BF59–64. However, existing studies have paid limited attention to the combined use of BF and RSF, particularly regarding their influence on the mechanical performance of concrete. In addition, while the effect of GGBS as a partial replacement for cement has been widely investigated, a notable research gap remains in evaluating PSC as a direct alternative to OPC, the primary binder in concrete. Indeed, ASTM C595/C595M-20 is a widely recognized standard specification for hydraulic cements that are blended, including PSC65. This standard outlines the composition, physical properties, and performance criteria of PSC, and provides detailed guidance on its production and application in the United States. Similarly, the EN 197-1:2011 standard is highly used throughout Europe66. It offers comprehensive information on the composition, specifications, and compliance criteria for commonly used cements, including PSC. The low early-age strength development of PSC concrete, compared to OPC concrete, limits its wide-scale application. To overcome the inherent weakness of PSC, this study explores the modifications of PSC- based concrete using fly ash and hybrid fiber reinforcement. Specifically, the study aims to examine the impacts of combined BF and RSF on the mechanical and durability properties of fly ash-modified PSC concrete. The novelty of this work lies in the synergistic use of hybrid fibres in a PSC-fly ash binder system to simultaneously enhance early-age strength and durability performance while maintaining workability.

Methodology

The concrete mix proportion was formulated in accordance with IS 10262:201975, utilizing cement, aggregate, water, and SP to achieve the target strength. Subsequently, it was modified with fibers and fly ash. The mix proportions for the various concrete mixes are presented in Table 7. A total binder content of 480 kg/m³ was adopted, consisting of 360 kg/m³ of PSC and 120 kg/m³ of fly ash, where fly ash replaced 25% of the total binder content. Specimen preparation

The mixing was carried out in a tilting drum concrete mixer. Initially, coarse and fine aggregates were dry-mixed for 1 min, then PSC and fly ash were added and mixed for another 2 min to ensure uniform blending. Fibres (BF and RSF) were then added gradually to the dry mix to avoid balling and ensure proper dispersion, and mixing was continued for 2 min. Subsequently, approximately 70% of the mixing water was added and mixed for 2 min. The remaining water mixed with superplasticizer was then added, and the entire mix was further mixed for 3 min to achieve a homogeneous and workable concrete. Fresh concrete was poured into the cube, cylinder and prism mould for compressive, split tensile, and flexural strength tests, respectively. The specimens were compacted using a table vibrator to eliminate entrapped air and ensure proper consolidation. For each mix and test, a minimum of three specimens were prepared and tested, and the average value was reported. After 24 h of casting, the specimens were demoulded and cured in water at 23 ± 2 °C until the respective test ages of 7, 14, and 28 days.

Testing of concrete specimens

All the concrete specimens were tested in accordance with the respective Indian and ASTM standards. The workability of fresh concrete was evaluated using the slump cone test as per IS 1199:201876/ASTM C14377, in which the concrete was placed in a standard cone in three equal layers, each compacted with 25 strokes of a tamping rod, and the vertical reduction in height after lifting the cone was measured as slump. Subsequently, the fresh concrete was filled into the cube. cylinder and prism mold for testing the compressive strength, split tensile strength and flexural strength are illustrated in Fig. 6. After 24 h of moist curing, the specimen is demolded and kept in a curing tank for curing as shown in Fig. 7. Compressive strength was determined in accordance with IS 516–202178 by testing 150 × 150 × 150 mm cubes under axial compression at a uniform loading rate until failure, and the strength was calculated from the maximum load as illustrated in Fig. 8. Split tensile strength was evaluated in accordance with IS 5816:199979 by placing 150 × 300 mm cylindrical specimens horizontally between the compression platens and applying a diametral load at a constant rate until splitting failure occurred, as illustrated in Fig. 9. Flexural strength was assessed following IS 516–202178 using 100 × 100 × 500 mm prismatic beams tested under two-point loading, and the modulus of rupture was calculated based on the failure load and span length as illustrated in Fig. 10. The modulus of elasticity was determined in accordance with ASTM C46980 by mounting strain gauges or a compressometer on cylindrical specimens of 150 × 300 mm in size and recording stress–strain data under incremental loading up to 40% of the ultimate stress, with the elastic modulus obtained from the slope of the stress–strain curve. Stress-strain behaviour was analysed by subjecting concrete cylinders to axial compressive loading under displacement-controlled conditions, with strains continuously recorded using strain gauges. The resulting data were used to plot full stress–strain curves that capture the peak stress, corresponding strain, and post-peak softening behaviour, following the guidelines of ASTM C46980 for elastic modulus measurement. For durability tests, specimens were cured for 28 days prior to testing. Acid resistance was assessed as per ASTM C26781 by immersing cured specimens in a 5% H₂SO₄ solution for 28 days and determining the weight loss (%) as illustrated in Fig. 11. The chloride ingress resistance of concrete was evaluated using the rapid chloride penetration test (RCPT) in accordance with ASTM C120282, in which 100 mm-diameter, 50 mm-thick concrete discs were subjected to a 60 V potential difference for 6 h, and the total charge passed (in Coulombs) was measured as illustrated in Fig. 12. Water absorption and sorptivity tests were conducted after oven-drying at 105 °C until a constant mass was achieved. Sorptivity was measured following ASTM C158583 by partially immersing oven-dried specimens in water, recording cumulative water absorption per unit area at specified time intervals, and calculating the sorptivity coefficient from the slope of absorption versus the square root of time. Water absorption was determined in accordance with ASTM C64284/IS 308585 by oven-drying the specimens to a constant mass, immersing them in water until saturated, and calculating the percentage increase in mass, as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Results and discussions

Fresh properties of low-carbon slag cement (LCSC) mix

Figure 14 presents the slump value of the LCSC mix. The slump of the control mix (LCSC) was 110 mm, which was due to the lubricating property of fly ash and SP. With the addition of the BF, a progressive decline in slump was observed to 102 mm, which is around 7% lower, 95 mm, which is around 14% lesser, and 88 mm which is around 20% lesser as compared to control (LCSC) mix with addition of fiber dosage of 0.25, 0.5, and 0.75 was observed respectively because of high surface area and friction. RSF reinforced mixes experienced a sharper workability reduction, and 0.25%, 0.5%, and 0.75% RSF mixes had slump values of 98 mm, which is around 11% less, 90 mm, which is around 18% less, and 82 mm, which is around 25% less, as compared to the control mix. The cause of this decrease is the rigidity and distorted shape of RSF, which limit flow and increase inter-particle resistance. Synergistic effects were observed in hybrid mixtures. For hybrid fiber mixes, the slump value ranges from 94 mm (0.25% BF + 0.25% RSF) to 65 mm (0.75% BF + 0.75% RSF). The hybrid mix containing 0.25% BF mix with 0.25% RSF (LCSC7) exhibits the highest slump (94 mm) among other hybrid fiber mixes. Further, the hybrid mix containing 0.5% BF and 0.5% RSF (LCSC11) had the highest slump (80 mm), which was still reasonably compactable. However, when the total fiber content reached 1.25% and above, a drastic reduction in slump value was observed. The hybrid mix containing 0.75% BF and 0.75% RSF (LCSC15) achieved the lowest slump value of 65 mm, which is about 30.85% and 18.75% lower than the LCSC7 and LCSC11 mixes, respectively. Despite a reduction in slump across fiber-reinforced mixes, all concretes mixes achieved “medium” degree of workability as per IS: 1199:201876 classification.

Mechanical properties of the LCSC mix

Figure 15 presents the compressive strength results of fly ash-modified PSC concrete mix made from PSC, fly ash and containing BF and RSF. All mixtures were prepared with consistent binder content, aggregate ratio, water-to-binder ratio, and SP dosage. Therefore, the differences in compressive strength were mainly attributed to the fibres type and dosage used. At 7 days, the control mix (LCSC) showed a compressive strength of 26.2 MPa, typical of PSC-fly ash-based concrete systems because of their slower early-age hydration and delayed pozzolanic reactions. The addition of fibres led to a slight increase in early-age strength, ranging from 27.8 MPa to 32.3 MPa. This enhancement is justified by the ability of fibres to restrain early microcrack development, improve internal stress redistribution, and maintain the integrity of the cementitious matrix under initial loading. Hybrid fibre mixes, especially LCSC7-LCSC11, showed enhanced early-age strength due to the synergistic effects of BF controls microcracks86,87 and RSF provides localised confinement88. The compressive strength of LCSC11 was 23.28% higher than that of the control mix (LCSC). After 14 days, a notable increase in strength was observed in all mixes due to continued hydration of PSC and the onset of secondary pozzolanic reactions in fly ash89,90. The control mix (LCSC) mix achieved 39.2 MPa, whereas fiber-reinforced mixes attained higher strength between 41.3 MPa and 46.8 MPa. The improved performance of fiber-reinforced mixes is justified by enhanced crack-bridging mechanisms, which delay crack initiation and reduce crack propagation under compressive loading. The LCSC11 (0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF) exhibited the highest compressive strength, which may be attributed to matrix densification and improved load-transfer efficiency. After 28 days, the control mix (LCSC) achieved a compressive strength of 51 MPa, demonstrating that fly ash-modified concrete can attain sufficient structural strength while using less cement. The addition of fiber significantly improved the compressive strength from 53.4 MPa to 59.5 MPa. The highest improvement in compressive strength of about 16%−17% compared to the control mix was observed for LCSC11, which can be due to the synergistic effects of micro- and macrocrack management, enhanced fiber–matrix interfacial adhesion, and decreased stress concentration in the concrete matrix. BF effectively controlled micro-level cracking, while RSF bridged larger cracks, thus enhancing the overall integrity of the composite. However, mixes with high fiber dosages (≥ 0.75% BF or RSF), such as LCSC3, LCSC6, and LCSC15, demonstrated a minor decrease in compressive strength. This reduction in strength is due to the adverse effects of high fiber dosages, such as fiber clustering, diminished workability, increased trapped air, and uneven fiber distribution, even when SP. The LCSC11 (0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF) was found to be optimum fiber concentration. This finding was consistent with Gong et al.60, who reported that the optimum fiber dosage was 1%. The compressive strength of concrete steadily increases with higher dosage; however, when the total fiber dosage exceeds 1 vol%, the concrete loses fluidity and suffers more internal structural damage, which reduces the compressive strength. The findings concluded an optimum hybrid fiber dosage of 0.5% BF and 0.5% RSF as the most effective combination for enhancing the compressive strength of fly ash modified PSC concrete.

Figure 16 illustrates the cracking characteristics of the LCSC (control) and the LCSC11 mix under compression. The LCSC11 specimen, containing a 0.5% BF-0.5% RSF hybrid, demonstrates superior crack resistance compared to the LCSC control mix. While the control specimen exhibits wide vertical cracks and significant spalling, the LCSC11 mix maintains structural integrity through a dense network of micro-cracks and a bridging mechanism. This synergistic effect occurs as the BF arrests micro-crack initiation at the early stages of loading, while the RSF prevents macro-crack propagation by spanning across crack planes, effectively increasing the energy absorption and post-peak toughness of the concrete matrix.

Figure 17 presents the split tensile strength results of fly ash-modified PSC concrete containing BF and RSF. Split tensile is a critical parameter for measuring the cracking resistance and tensile load-carrying capacity of concrete, especially in fiber-reinforced concrete. The control mix (LCSC) also showed a relatively low split tensile strength of 1.8 MPa at 7 days, a characteristic of fly ash PSC concrete due to reduced hydration and the limited tensile strength of the plain matrix. It was observed that there was a significant improvement in split tensile strength with the inclusion of fibers, and the values rose steadily to 3.2 MPa, as observed for the LCSC15 mix. This improvement in split tensile strength at early ages is due to the ability of fibers to inhibit the formation of microcracks, enhance the redistribution of stress, and postpone the crack opening even before the complete development of the matrix strength. RSF-containing mixes showed slightly higher early-age strength development during early stages than BF-only mixes because steel fibers had greater stiffness and crack-bridging capability. As the mixes continued to hydrate, a significant increase in split tensile strength was observed at 14 days, due to high PSC hydration and increased pozzolanic activity of fly ash. The control mix (LCSC) exhibits a split tensile strength of 2.7 MPa, whereas fiber-reinforced mixes (LCSC1- LCSC15) exhibit an enhanced split tensile strength in the range of 2.9 to 4.5 MPa. Hybrid fiber mixes exhibit better tensile performance as compared to the mono-fiber concrete mixes. Mixes LCSC11, LCSC12, LCSC14, and LCSC15 demonstrated pronounced strength gains, attributed to a synergistic effect between the BF and the RSF. BF were effective in controlling microcrack formation91–93, whereas steel-fibrous bridging of larger cracks enhanced tensile stress transfer across crack planes94,95. At 28 days, the control mix attained a split tensile strength of 3.5 MPa, whereas fiber-reinforced mixes showed significant enhancement, with values ranging from 3.8 to 5.4 MPa. The LCSC15 (0.75% BF + 0.75% RSF) mix exhibits the highest tensile strength of 5.4 MPa, which is around 54.28% higher than that of the control mix. Such a significant improvement is caused by the dominant role of fibers during tensile loading, where crack-bridging, pull-out resistance, and enhanced fiber-matrix interfacial bonding govern performance and not matrix strength alone. Unlike compressive strength, no notable reduction was observed at higher fiber contents for split tensile strength. This is because tensile failure is highly sensitive to fiber content and distribution, and the presence of higher fiber volumes increases the number of effective crack-bridging fibers across the failure plane. Although high fiber dosage can decrease the workability, its positive influence on tensile resistance outweighs these effects in split tensile testing. The findings are sufficient to support the concept that the effect of fiber inclusion on split tensile strength is significant and overwhelming in comparison with that of compressive strength.

The stress–strain characteristics of the fly ash modified PSC concrete (LCSC1–LCSC15) mixes, as presented in Fig. 21, show a consistent improvement in stiffness, maximum stress, and post-cracking behaviour with the addition of BF, RSF, and their hybrid mixes. At low strain levels (approximately 0.00020), all mixtures exhibit nearly linear elastic behaviour. In the control mix (LCSC), the stress increases from roughly 0.71 MPa at a strain of 0.00002 to around 7.14 MPa at 0.00020, indicating a relatively low Young’s modulus. In comparison, hybrid combinations such as LCSC11 exhibit higher stresses at the same strain level, reaching approximately 7.72 MPa at a strain of 0.00020. The rise in stress at the same strain levels aligns with their elevated Young’s modulus values and suggests enhanced elastic stiffness and effective stress transfer attributed to fiber–matrix interaction. When the strain reaches 0.00020, the stress–strain curves increasingly diverge from linearity due to the initiation and growth of microcracks in the cement matrix. At a strain of 0.00028, the control mix reaches a stress of approximately 10.0 MPa, while the fiber-reinforced mixes exhibit much higher stress values. For example, LCSC11 attain stress of 10.81 MPa indicating enhanced load-carrying capacity. The peak stress for most concrete mixes occurs within the strain range of 0.00028–0.00032. The control mix reaches a maximum stress of approximately 10.71 MPa, while the hybrid mixes exhibit higher peak stresses. Notably, LCSC11 achieves peak stresses of about 11.58 MPa, aligning closely with their elevated Young’s modulus values of 38.6 GPa. This increase in peak stress is associated with their higher stiffness and improved crack-bridging capability provided by the hybrid fiber system. After the peak stress, all mixes show post-peak softening. However, the way stress decreases varies considerably. The control mix exhibits a rapid decline in stress, from approximately 10.71 MPa at 0.00030 strain to approximately 6.42 MPa at 0.00036 strain, indicating brittle failure. Conversely, fiber-reinforced mixtures maintain greater residual stresses across broader strain intervals. For example, LCSC11 exhibits a stress of about 11.1 MPa at 0.00036 strain and approximately 9.65 MPa at 0.00040 strain, demonstrating improved post-cracking resistance. This gradual stress decay is attributed to fiber pull-out and crack-bridging mechanisms, which enhance energy absorption and ductility. Further, the stress–strain response shows a strong correlation with Young’s modulus, where mixes with higher modulus values exhibit steeper initial slopes, higher stress at equivalent strain, delayed peak strain, and improved post-peak behavior. The hybrid fiber (LCSC11) combinations, particularly those with 0.5% BF and 0.5% RSF, provide the most balanced performance by enhancing elastic stiffness, peak stress, and post-cracking ductility.

Durability properties of the LCSC mix

The control mix (LCSC) showed a higher water absorption rate of 4.6% at 28 days due to slower hydration of PSC and delayed pozzolanic reaction of fly ash during the early ages, as shown in Fig. 22. These elements lead to a relatively porous microstructure in the early phases of curing. The addition of fibres led to a gradual decrease in water absorption, achieving a lowest value of 3.4% for the hybrid mix LCSC11 (0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF). BF are very efficient in controlling microcrack initiation caused by plastic and drying shrinkage, thereby reducing the development of interconnected capillary pores. In the meantime, RSF provides efficient crack bridging for larger cracks, thereby reducing their propagation under internal stresses. The combined action of these fibres results in a denser and less permeable structure. However, at higher fiber volume fractions, a slight increase in water absorption was observed, attributable to decreased workability, uneven fiber distribution, and the formation of entrapped air voids during compaction.

Sorptivity measurements exhibited a comparable pattern to water absorption, emphasizing the effect of fiber reinforcement on capillary suction properties as presented in Fig. 23. The control mix exhibited the greatest sorptivity because its persistent pore structure enabled swift capillary water ascent. The inclusion of BF significantly diminished sorptivity by limiting the formation of fine surface microcracks, which serve as the main channels for capillary entry. The hybrid mixes, such as LCSC11, exhibited the lowest sorptivity, indicating enhanced resistance to capillarity-driven moisture transport. This enhancement is due to the combined effect of BF, which lowers microcrack density, and RSF, which prevents macrocracks from widening, thereby interrupting capillary continuity.

RCPT results show a significant decrease in chloride-ion permeability when using fibres, especially in hybrid concrete mixes, as shown in Fig. 24. The control mix showed “low” chloride permeability as per ASTM C120282. With fiber addition, the charge passed decreased from 1850 C to 1350 C, maintaining a ‘low’ permeability classification according to ASTM C120282. However, it is important to note that RCPT is an electrical conductivity-based test, and the presence of conductive phases such as RSF may influence the measured charge passed. Therefore, the RCPT results in this study are interpreted primarily as indicators of relative changes in pore structure and ionic transport characteristics rather than as a direct measure of chloride permeability. The observed reduction in charge passed for LCSC11 (1350 C) may be associated with a fiber reinforcement that may help restrict crack width and connectivity, thereby limiting pathways for ion transport. RSF, owing to its higher stiffness and tensile strength, may assist in restraining load-induced cracking, while BF may contribute to improved resistance to microcracking and preservation of matrix integrity. The slight increase in RCPT values observed at elevated fiber dosages (%) correlates with decreased workability and potential fiber clumping, which could locally increase porosity and promote ionic transport. To support the interpretation of durability performance, complementary non-electrical indicators, such as water absorption and sorptivity, have also been considered and show consistent trends with the RCPT results.

The acid attack test results of the fly ash-modified PSC concrete mixes were compared in the form of weight loss percentage after 28 days of exposure to 5% sulfuric acid (H2SO4) solution, as illustrated in Fig. 25 The highest weight loss, about 4.2%, was observed in the control mix (LCSC), indicating greater vulnerability to acidic environments, likely due to interconnected pores and microcracks that facilitate acid ingress. With the introduction of fibers, there was significant weight loss reduction in all the fiber-reinforced mixes. The mass loss of mono-fiber mixes with BF gradually declined to 3.9% (LCSC1-0.25% BF) to 3.4% (LCSC3-0.75% BF). This is also because the BF can limit the formation and propagation of microcracks, thereby reduce the permeability of the concrete matrix and limiting the diffusion of acidic solutions. Similarly, mixes containing RSF showed greater acid resistance, with weight loss decreasing to 3.3% (LCSC6 −0.75% RSF) compared to 3.8% (LCSC4 −0.25% RSF). RSF effectively bridges larger cracks formed under chemical and internal stresses, thereby maintaining structural continuity and slowing down acid penetration. The hybrid fibre mixes exhibited superior performance compared to mono-fibre systems. Among them, the LCSC11 mix (0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF) recorded the lowest weight loss of 2.5%, corresponding to approximately a 34% reduction compared to the control mix. This enhanced resistance is attributed to a synergistic crack-control mechanism, in which BF limits microcrack formation while RSF restrains macrocrack widening, resulting in a dense, less permeable matrix96. The PSC-fly ash binder system may enhance acid resistance by reducing the calcium hydroxide content and promoting the formation of stable aluminosilicate and secondary C-S-H gels. These hydrates exhibit greater chemical stability under acidic conditions and reduce calcium leaching when exposed to sulfuric acid. However, beyond the optimum fiber dosage, a marginal increase in weight loss was observed. For instance, mixes such as LCSC15 (0.75% BF + 0.75% RSF) showed a slightly higher weight loss of 2.9%, which can be attributed to reduced workability and fiber clustering, leading to the formation of micro voids and localized porosity. These defects act as preferential pathways for acid ingress, slightly compromising chemical resistance despite the presence of fibres.

Environmental impact assessment of the LCSC mix

Since the objective of this study is to formulate the Low-Carbon Slag Cement (LCSC) concrete mix without compromising the mechanical and durability properties, the embodied energy (EE) and embodied carbon (EC) of the optimum mix LCSC11 were determined and compared with those of conventional OPC concrete. The investigation was conducted using experimentally measured mix proportions, and the embodied energy and carbon coefficients of the materials were taken from the Inventory of Carbon and Energy (ICE) V3 Database. The EE of OPC concrete was calculated to be 2317 MJ/m3, whereas the EE of LCSC11 was 2283 MJ/m3, resulting in a reduction of about 1.5%, as shown in Fig. 26. The addition of BF and RSF fibers marginally increases energy demand, but this is offset by the reduced clinker content achieved with fly ash- and slag-based cement, thereby lowering the EE of LCSC1197. In terms of embodied carbon, OPC concrete recorded 392 kg CO₂/m³, while LCSC11 recorded 376 kg CO₂/m³, representing a 16 kg CO₂/m³ reduction, equivalent to 4.1%. This reduction is primarily attributed to the partial replacement of OPC clinker with fly ash, treated as a low-burden by-product in the ICE V3 database, and to the use of RSF, which has a much lower emission factor than virgin steel. The results demonstrate that LCSC11 achieves measurable reductions in both embodied energy and carbon emissions without compromising mechanical performance or durability.

Conclusions

This study investigated the mechanical and durability properties of the fly ash-modified PSC concrete hybrid fibers, with an aim of mitigating the early-age strength deficiency of PSC-fly ash-modified concrete. The various conclusions as observed from the findings are as follows;

i.The inclusion of 0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF (LCSC11) into the fly ash-modified PSC concrete exhibited a maximum improvement of 23% and 17.64% compressive strength at 7 and 28 days due to the synergistic crack-bridging and stress redistribution.ii.At 28 days, the inclusion of 0.75% BF + 0.75% RSF (LCSC11) into the fly ash-modified PSC concrete showed 54.28% and 59.61% increases in split tensile strength and flexural strength, attributed to effective crack-bridging, stress redistribution, and confinement effects provided by fibers.iii.The inclusion of 0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF (LCSC11) into the fly ash-modified PSC concrete exhibited enhanced durability properties with 26% lower water absorption, 30% reduced sorptivity, 41% lower chloride ingress, and 34% less acid-induced weight loss.iv.Although the split tensile and flexural strengths increased for mixes with fiber contents beyond 0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF, these mixes exhibited slight reductions in workability and durability performance due to potential fiber clustering as observed for LCSC11 mix, indicating 0.5% BF + 0.5% RSF is the most (optimum) appropriate balance of fiber contents in the overall mechanical performance, durability, and workability.v.The proposed LCSC concrete mix (PSC + fly ash + BF + RSF) offers a sustainable low-carbon alternative to OPC concrete, reducing CO₂ emissions by 16 kg CO₂ per cubic meter of concrete.

The study concluded that hybrid fiber reinforcement is a viable and effective approach for producing durable, low-calcium, and environmentally sustainable concrete suitable for aggressive exposure conditions. This study primarily focused on studying the effect of Hybrid Fibre Reinforcement on the mechanical and durability properties of Fly Ash-modified PSC concrete 7, 14 and 28 days. However, this study did not examine the properties of hybrid fibre-reinforced fly ash-modified PSC concrete at 56 days and 90 days. Future study will focus on examining the long-term performance of these mixes at these ages.
