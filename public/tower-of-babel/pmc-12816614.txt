Modeling, simulation, and optimization behavior of pharmaceutical compound removal from water in SR-AOPs technique for wastewater treatment
Authors: Karim Kriaa, Ali B. M. Ali, Loghman Mostafa, Mohamed Shaban, Narinderjit Singh Sawaran Singh, Abdellatif M. Sadeq, Khalil Hajlaoui, Amir Hamzeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tetracycline hydrochloride (TC) is an antibiotic frequently detected in water sources globally. Developing effective strategies for the removal of this pharmaceutical contaminant from water and wastewater is crucial. The primary objective of this study is to develop computational models to accurately predict and optimize the TC removal process. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was used to model the relationship between the removal efficiency (RE) and three key variables: initial TC concentration (TC0), catalyst dosage, and peroxymonosulfate (PMS) dose over treatment time. Finally, Response surface methodology optimization was used to discover the best approach for removing Tetracycline hydrochloride. The findings demonstrated the great accuracy of response surface methodology and artificial neural networks in predicting the RE behavior of TC in an aqueous solution. The artificial neural network’s output MSE, MAE, and R2 accuracy metrics are 2.02E−02, 7.38E−03, and 9.99E−01, respectively. To solve the worldwide problem of water pollution, the findings of this work might be used to create optimal states and strategies for removing and degrading pharmaceutical contaminants in water using computational artificial intelligence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32064-0.

Introduction

With the increased use of chemicals and personal care products worldwide, environmental contamination is becoming an increasingly serious issue1. They are widespread in bodies of water and are dangerous to aquatic ecosystems and human health. As new diseases arise, antibiotics—which are often used to treat illnesses in humans and food animals—become more and more concerning2. Due to their hazards, durability, and toxicity, pesticides used in aquaculture, medicine, and animal husbandry that linger in the water have recently generated major environmental issues3.

Tetracycline hydrochloride is an antibiotic extensively employed in animal husbandry, human disease prevention, and various other industries due to its potent immunological properties, cost-effectiveness, and user-friendly nature4,5. However, because bacteria cannot metabolize TC fully, TC is discharged as parent chemicals or metabolites into soil and water, polluting the environment. Low levels of tetracycline in the environment have an impact on the microbial community structure of the ecosystem in addition to the development and metabolism of bacteria. Simultaneously, because animals and people do not entirely absorb it, it is discharged into the water, harming the aquatic ecosystem6,7. As a result, the issue of how to get rid of TC antibiotics from aquatic environments has to be resolved immediately. According to research, TC is often detected in marine environments. For instance, the concentration of antibiotics in groundwater and wastewater from pig farms ranges from 19.9 to 416.4 μg/L, but the drug’s content in hospital wastewater may reach up to 500 mg/L. TC is essentially non-biodegradable because of its intricate ring structure8,9.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOP) based on PMS successfully reduce wastewater’s persistent organic pollutants to tiny, non-toxic particles. TC removal strategies include biological activities, as well as physical and chemical applications10,11. However, Advanced Oxidation Processes (AOPs), due to their great selectivity and high mineralization capacity, have proven more successful than conventional chemical and physical treatments for degrading persistent organic pollutants. Peroxymonosulfate (PMS) is a crucial component in AOPs and can generally be activated in two ways to effectively mitigate pollution12,13.

Moreover, inorganic anions (such as Cl−, HCO3−, NO3−, etc.) and natural organic matter (NOM) significantly limit the quantity of free radicals involved in the catalytic degradation process. AOPs based on sulfate (SO4) have gained more interest lately. Compared to OH, which has an oxidation state of 1.8–2.7 eV, SO4 (i) has a higher state of 2.4–3.0 eV. Because of its asymmetric structure, PMS may be readily synthesized using a variety of materials and techniques, resulting in the production of SO4 and other reactive oxidative species (ROS)14. Tetracycline can be eliminated by chemical oxidation, biodegradation, and physical adsorption. AOPs based on persulfate have garnered interest because of their broad pH range, robust redox potential, and capacity to generate a variety of oxides15.

As previously said, it’s critical to assess several methods to determine the most effective way of eliminating TC antibiotics as pharmaceutical substances from wastewater and water. It should be mentioned that to advance universal science, scientists need to employ cutting-edge methods like computational artificial intelligence (AI). The selection of appropriate operational parameters for modeling and optimization is also of key importance16–19. Several studies suggest that Computational AI can simulate, predict, and enhance the performance of crucial variables in chemical and biological processes. Also, using the novel computational AI based methods has been used in wastewater treatment contents of pharmaceutical components such as TC, recently. They employed artificial neural networks (ANN), machine learning, and response surface methodology (RSM) for this aim20–29.

To maximize ibuprofen breakdown by the UV/percarbonate process, Xue et al.30 employed RSM in this respect. They said the response surface approach was used further to enhance the ideal experimental conditions for IBP deterioration. Under ideal testing circumstances of 5 μM of IBP, 40 μM of SPC, 7.60 pH, and UV irradiation for 20 min, the IBP degradation rate achieved 97.3%. RSM and an ANN-based genetic algorithm (GA) were utilized by Salari et al.31 in a different work to model and optimize the degradation of dexamethasone. Based on statistical linear and nonlinear metrics, they determined that both models demonstrated a considerable level of adequacy for modeling and process prediction (R2 = 0.9998 and 0.9996, and RMSE = 0.2128 and 0.1784 for ANN-GA and RSM-CCD, respectively). RSM was employed in a different investigation to maximize the adsorption process’s diuron removal from aqueous solutions. They stated that an efficiency of 82.32% could be attained and that, under ideal adsorption conditions, 0.07 g/L CNC/CTM absorbed 5.86 mg/L diuron in less than 318.68 min. According to the simulation findings, the Sips model was most closely followed by CNC/CTM’s adsorption capacity for diuron elimination. Ebrahimi et al.32 employed ANN and RSM to degrade dye using a peroxymonosulfate catalyst in a different work. The study utilized an adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) in combination with RSM with central composite design (RSM-CCD) to examine the factors that affect the removal of OG. The findings indicated that their investigation was quite accurate. In a different work, Yang et al.33 optimized removing erfluorooctanoic acid from an aqueous solution using a peroxydisulfate/layered double hydroxide system through RSM and ANN. According to their findings, there were notable variations in the prediction of PFOA removal between RSM and an ANN under non-CCD settings. RSM demonstrated stronger predictability (R2 = 0.7574) than ANN.

The main focus of the current study is to model, forecast, and optimize the behavior of TC removal (RE or C/C0) as a pharmaceutical pollutant in water. The current experimental data came from a study that Pan et al.34 presented. They employed SR-AOPs with sulfate radical-based on AOP to break down TC in aqueous solutions. To eliminate pharmaceutical chemicals from the water treatment process, modeling and optimization were carried out under various operating circumstances, such as the starting concentration of TC, catalyst dosage, and PMS dosage. For this reason, multi-layer perceptron (MLP)-ANN and RSM have been used to mimic the behavior of TC removal. In the modeling, TC0 (mg/L), PMS dose (mg), catalyst dosage (mg), and time (min) were the independent variables. The range of values for the catalyst dose is 0 to 40 mg. The range of the PMS dose is 0 to 80 mg. Additionally, the range of the TC0 is 10–80 mg/L. There are 60 min in a day. Additionally, by RSM modeling of TC removal for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment, the association between RE and TC0 (time), RE and catalyst dosage (time), and RE and PMS dose (time) has been determined. Ultimately, the optimal method of TC removal was determined by RSM optimization. The application of RSM optimization has achieved a better understanding and assessment of the behavior of TC removal in a pharmaceutical water treatment process. To address the global problem of water pollution, the present study’s findings may be utilized to design, via computational AI, the optimal states and methods for eliminating and degrading pharmaceutical pollutants in water. In other words, creating new algorithms and methodologies to apply artificial computational intelligence in wastewater and water treatment is essential. This is vital to achieve improved outcomes by saving time and costs.

In a pharmaceutical wastewater treatment process, Fig. 1 displays 3D and contour graphs of RSM modeling for the behavior of RE-TC0-time, RE-catalyst dosage-time, and RE-PMS dosage-time. Greater RE values were achieved at higher time points and lower initial TC concentration levels. Stated differently, maximum RE values are obtained for TC0 values less than 50 mg/L and maximum time values exceeding 40 min. When it comes to time in the high value range of TC0, RE is more sensitive to time than it is to TC0 values. Furthermore, RE is more sensitive to TC0 values than time within the TC0 low-value range. In another way, the impact of time on RE is significant with smaller values of TC0. Higher RE values were noted in two areas in the second case: higher time values over catalyst dosage values and lower time values over catalyst dosage values. Overall, it was determined that RE is more sensitive to time based on the results of the catalyst dose and time experiments. In the third case, higher RE levels were observed at both the maximum and minimum PMS doses over time. This suggests that RE is more sensitive to the PMS dose than the reaction time. This finding may be partially explained by the catalyst acting as an adsorbent for TC, particularly when the catalyst is in excess. After degradation reaches a threshold, desorption of adsorbed TC molecules may occur, potentially leading to a slight apparent increase in TC concentration. The decline in degradation behavior observed in the RE-TC0-time model is attributed to the competitive interaction among TC molecules. Modeling description

RSM modeling

RSM is an organized statistical technique that helps scientists’ study and improve intricate systems effectively. RSM, which was first created in the middle of the twentieth century, has since grown to be a fundamental technique in many scientific fields, providing a methodical framework for examining the connection between several factors and the intended outcome40.

This study employed RSM to examine the intricate relationships between the experimental configuration and the effectiveness of TC removal from pharmaceutical wastewater. The goal was to find out how changes in the initial TC concentration (TC0), catalyst dose, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) dosage, and treatment time affect the effectiveness of TC removal.

The significance of the independent variables to the pharmaceutical wastewater treatment process led to the cautious selection of TC0, catalyst dose, PMS dosage, and time. These four variables represent the most critical and universally recognized operational factors governing pollutant degradation kinetics and efficacy in SR-AOPs. TC0 represents the initial concentration of TC in the wastewater, and PMS and catalyst dose indicate the amounts of catalyst and peroxymonosulfate utilized in the treatment, respectively. Time symbolizes how long the therapy will take.

An array of tests covering the practical ranges of every parameter was devised to methodically examine the influence of these factors on the efficacy of TC removal. In particular, the ranges for catalyst dose were 0–40 mg, PMS dosage was 0–80 mg, TC0 was 10–80 mg/L, and time was 0–60 min. By manipulating these variables within their respective ranges, an extensive dataset for further examination was produced.

The fundamental component of the RSM approach involves developing a mathematical response function that precisely maps the complex, and often nonlinear, relationship between the independent factors and the dependent variable, TC removal efficiency (RE). This predictive model, typically a quadratic or higher-order polynomial, is crucial for estimating TC removal under varied experimental settings.

The RSM model was applied to experimental data using statistical techniques such as regression analysis and analysis of variance (ANOVA), to assess the significance of each variable and their interactions. As a result, trends could be spotted, important variables pinpointed, and we improved the pharmaceutical wastewater treatment procedure’s TC removal effectiveness.

In summary, RSM gave this research a methodical and exacting framework that enabled us to decipher the complex correlations between experimental factors and TC removal efficiency in treating pharmaceutical wastewater. By utilizing historical RSM methodologies, this work adds to the corpus of knowledge on optimization techniques for environmental remediation procedures.

F-value (Fisher’s F-test):

The ratio of the mean square of residuals to the mean square of regression is used to get the F-value.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F = \frac{{MS_{Regression} }}{{MS_{Residual} }}$$\end{document}

The F-value assesses the regression model’s overall significance by comparing the variance explained by the model to the variance unexplained by the residuals. A high F-value means that, compared to a model without any predictors, the model substantially fits the data.

P-value:

Assuming the null hypothesis is correct, the p-value is the likelihood of finding a test statistic (such the F-value) that is as severe as or more extreme than the one derived from the sample data. The reported findings are very unlikely if the null hypothesis, which holds that there is no link between the variables, is correct, as shown by a p-value less than 0.05. Consequently, a low p-value suggests that the model is statistically significant.

Sum of Squares (SS):

A mathematical equation that adds up the squared differences between the actual and anticipated numbers is called the sum of squares. It quantifies the variability of all the data. While sum of squares regression (SSR) quantifies the variability explained, sum of squares error (SSE) assesses the variability that the regression model is unable to explain.

R-squared (R2):

Mathematical Relationship:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{2} = \frac{{SS_{Regression} }}{{SS_{Total} }}$$\end{document}

R-squared is a measure of how much of the total variability of the response variable can be explained by the regression model. A number nearer 1 indicates that a larger proportion of the variability is explained by the model. Its range is 0 to 1.

Adjusted R-squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{adj}^{2}$$\end{document}):

Mathematical Relationship:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{adj}^{2} = 1 - \frac{{\left( {1 - R^{2} } \right)\left( {n - 1} \right)}}{{\left( {n - k - 1} \right)}}$$\end{document}

Adjusted R2 penalizes the addition of extra independent variables to the model and offers a more cautious assessment of its explanatory power. This metric was selected alongside R2 to explicitly address the limitations of R2 alone, especially in nonlinear models or when over-parameterization is a risk, ensuring model parsimony and robustness. It modifies R2 by the number of predictors in the model.

Standard Deviation (Std. Dev.):

The Std. Dev. represents the average degree of variability or dispersion of data points around the mean. It quantifies the data points’ dispersion around the fitted regression line. Since the data points are closer to the regression line, a lower standard deviation indicates a better fit between the model and the data.

Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC):

BIC is calculated as:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BIC = n{\mathrm{l}} \,n\left( {\hat{\sigma }^{2} } \right) + k\,\ln \left( n \right)$$\end{document}where n is the sample size, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\widehat{\sigma }}^{2}$$\end{document} is the estimated variance of the residuals, and k is the number of parameters in the model. By punishing models with additional parameters, BIC compromises model fit and complexity. A lower BIC score indicates a more favorable balance between the fit of the model and its complexity.

Akaike Information Criterion corrected (AICc):

AICc is calculated as:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AICc = - 2\,\ln \left( L \right) + 2k + \frac{{2k\left( {k + 1} \right)}}{n - k - 1}$$\end{document}

L represents the likelihood, k represents the number of parameters, and n is the sample size. A statistical model’s relative quality is gauged by its AICc. Similar to BIC, it penalizes complexity models, although it works better with lower sample sizes. Similar to BIC, a lower AICc value denotes a better trade-off between model fit and complexity.

These characteristics are critical criteria in this work that assess the suitability and consistency of the RSM model in forecasting the effectiveness of tetracycline removal under various experimental setups. The many areas of model performance that each parameter provides distinct insights into include overall significance (F-value, p-value), goodness of fit (R-squared, Adjusted R-squared), and model complexity (BIC, AICc)41.

RSM optimization description

RSM optimization was a key instrument in this study that let us better understand and assess the dynamics of TC removal in pharmaceutical wastewater treatment facilities. A particular focus was placed on the interactions between TC concentration (TC0), catalyst dosage, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) dosage, and treatment duration (time), and the removal efficiency (RE) of TC under various operating circumstances was methodically investigated and optimized by utilizing RSM.

The main goal of the RSM optimization procedure was to improve the behavior of TC removal efficiency concerning the interactions between PMS dosage-time, catalyst dosage-time, and TC0-time. This methodological approach was used to investigate how changes in time, TC0, PMS dose, and catalyst dosage affect the effectiveness of TC removal.

A sequence of controlled trials was planned to carry out the optimization, with every independent variable methodically adjusted within a predefined range. An extensive dataset was produced, capturing the complex relationships between the experimental factors and the effectiveness of TC removal.

Finding the ideal parameters to maximize TC removal efficiency in three scenarios, TC0-time changes, catalyst dosage-time changes, and PMS dosage-time changes, was the key to successful RSM optimization. The exact operational parameter combinations that produced the best removal efficiency (RE) for TC were identified through careful examination of the response surface and contour plots generated from experimental data.

Due to the RSM optimization, three different optimal modes for TC removal efficiency were observed; these modes corresponded to other sets of adjustments in TC0-time, catalyst dosage-time, or PMS dosage-time. These ideal modes represent the pharmaceutical wastewater treatment process’s most efficient configurations for reaching maximum TC removal efficiency.

ANN modeling

ANNs are becoming more and more potent computer models that can represent detailed correlations in data. As a result, they are a good fit for forecasting and evaluating complex systems, including pharmaceutical wastewater treatment procedures. The ANN architecture was meticulously designed to capture the nonlinear interactions observed in the experimental data. It utilizes Tan-sigmoid and Log-sigmoid activation functions with two hidden layers of a maximum of five neurons each42.

The ANN architecture consists of an input layer, two hidden layers, and an output layer. The input layer consists of neurons that symbolize the independent variables, namely the initial concentration of TC (TC0), the catalyst dosage, the peroxymonosulfate dosage (PMS), and the treatment duration (time). These variables are scaled appropriately within the experimental parameters. These neurons send the input data to the first hidden layer, which uses the Tan-sigmoid activation function and weighted connections to perform complicated transformations.

As an intermediary processing unit, the first hidden layer extracts features and representations in a hierarchical format from the incoming data. Up to five neurons make up this layer, which is chosen through optimization and testing to capture the key features of the data without adding undue complexity.

The second hidden layer receives the output from the first and applies further nonlinear modifications using the Log-sigmoid activation function. Like the first one, the second hidden layer may hold up to five neurons and helps extract patterns and higher-order correlations from the data.

The ANN model learns from the input–output relationships in the experimental data, acting as a nonlinear regression framework. The model can effectively handle high-dimensional datasets and reveal hidden patterns that may defy conventional statistical approaches by utilizing the innate parallel processing capabilities of ANNs43.

During the training phase, the ANN iteratively modifies the weights of connections between neurons to minimize the disparity between the anticipated and actual values of TC removal efficiency. This optimization process aims to maximize the model’s forecast accuracy while preventing overfitting to the training set. It is frequently carried out using techniques like backpropagation.

The dataset was partitioned into training, testing, and validation sets, with 70%, 15%, and 15% of the data allocated to each set. This division was done to evaluate the performance of the ANN model. By employing this method, the model’s capacity to generalize to new data can be assessed, and biases created during training can be prevented44.

Several metrics, such as mean squared error (MSE), coefficient of determination (R-squared), and mean absolute error (MAE), are employed to assess the ANN model’s predictive capability. These measurements show how well the model predicts TC removal efficiency in various experimental scenarios and its accuracy, precision, and dependability.

Model limitations and applicability in real-world systems

Despite the high predictive accuracy demonstrated by the ANN and RSM models, particularly for the single-contaminant system studied, it is essential to discuss the limitations inherent to these models when considering their transition to real-world pharmaceutical wastewater treatment. Existence of confounding factors is an important parameter that affected to the ANN based calculations in present study. The factors such as false signals extracted, biases, relationship between outputs and inputs, and etc. In this regard confounding factors can adversely affect the predictive performance of ANN outputs.

First, the training data is derived from a simplified, single-pollutant system (TC only). Real wastewater is a complex matrix containing natural organic matter (NOM), various inorganic anions (e.g., chlorides, carbonates), and multiple other pharmaceutical co-contaminants. These background species can significantly scavenge the sulfate radicals (SO4-) or engage in competitive degradation pathways, effects that are not incorporated into the current model framework. Consequently, the predictive performance and optimal modes identified here would require recalibration and validation in genuine wastewater matrices.

Second, the models are based on ideal bench-scale experimental conditions. They do not account for real-time operational uncertainties, fluctuations in matrix composition, or complex factors related to scaling up the SR-AOPs process, such as imperfect mixing, non-ideal flow patterns, and mass transfer limitations in a larger reactor design. Addressing and quantifying these environmental and operational uncertainties is critical for successful implementation, as highlighted by work in related environmental analysis fields13.

Finally, the optimal ANN structure, while highly accurate, operates as a 'black-box’ model. This superior predictive capability comes at the cost of clear mechanistic interpretation, which may be a hurdle in industrial settings where understanding the cause of performance changes is as vital as the prediction itself for process control and troubleshooting. Future work will focus on integrating mechanistic insight with the predictive power of AI models to bridge this gap.

Conclusions

As new diseases emerge, antibiotics frequently used to treat infections in people and food animals become increasingly worrisome. Tetracycline hydrochloride (TC) is an antibiotic extensively used worldwide as a parent chemical or metabolite in soil and water, contaminating the environment. Evaluating many strategies to discover the most effective approach to removing TC antibiotics as pharmaceuticals from wastewater and water is necessary. The primary objective of this study was to assess, predict, and enhance the elimination performance of TC as a pharmaceutical contaminant in water (RE or C/C0). Furthermore, using RSM modeling of TC removal for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment, the relationship between RE and TC0, RE and catalyst dosage, and RE and PMS dose was found. Finally, RSM optimization was used to discover the best approach for removing TC. The findings demonstrated the great accuracy of RSM and ANN in predicting the RE behavior of TC in an aqueous solution. The R2 values for RSM modeling of TC RE behavior using RE-TC0-time, RE-catalyst dosage-time, and RE-PMS dosage-time are 0.9618, 0.9873, and 0.9699, respectively. The ANN’s output MSE, MAE, and R2 accuracy metrics are 2.02E−02, 7.38E−03, and 9.99E−01, respectively. Furthermore, the accuracy parameter of ANN training for RE of TC data based on catalyst dosage is 9.30E−06, MAE is 1.03E−03, and R2 is 1.00E + 00. The accuracy parameters of the ANN test are MSE = 5.25E−04, MAE = 2.13E−02, and R2 = 1.00E + 00. The accuracy parameters for ANN validation are MSE = 9.08E−04, MAE = 2.55E−02, and R2 = 9.97E−01. For a pharmaceutical wastewater treatment process, the optimal mode for RE of TC was identified in three scenarios based on TC0—time changes, catalyst dosage—time changes, and PMS dosage—time changes. With a TC0 of 10 mg/L, the first example had an ideal RE mode of 0.643 in 60 min. For the first case (TC0 vs. time), the optimal condition resulted in a minimum residual concentration (C/C0) of 0.643 at TC0 = 10 mg/L after 60 min. For the second case (catalyst dosage vs. time), the minimum C/C0 was 1.005 using a catalyst dosage of 0.5 mg after 10.375 min. For the third case (PMS dosage vs. time), the minimum C/C0 was 1.029 using a PMS dosage of 0.063 mg after 9.477 min. To solve the worldwide problem of water pollution, the findings of this work might be used to create optimal states and strategies for removing and degrading pharmaceutical contaminants in water using computational AI.
