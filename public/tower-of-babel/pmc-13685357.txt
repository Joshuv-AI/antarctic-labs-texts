Whole-transcriptome sequencing reveals Alzheimer’s disease–associated ceRNA regulatory networks and downstream pathways
Authors: KeFei Duan, Fangfang Zhan, Yaodan Zhang, Xinghang Wang, Gaoteng Lin, Xiaoyi Zhao, Dan Yu, Shida Duan, Yuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAlzheimer’s disease (AD) molecular mechanisms remain unclear, and effective therapies are lacking. This study employed whole-transcriptome sequencing to investigate the expression profiles of circRNAs, lncRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 transgenic mice, aiming to identify ceRNA regulatory networks and clarify their roles in AD pathogenesis.MethodsDifferentially expressed RNAs were integrated into ceRNA networks, and programmed cell death-related pathways were analyzed using GO and KEGG. The computationally predicted circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis was preliminarily evaluated in vitro using siRNA-mediated CD14 knockdown in microglial and neuronal AD models. Expression of CD14, NF-κB components, and NLRP3 markers was measured by qRT-PCR, while secreted IL-1β and IL-18 protein levels were quantified via ELISA. Neuronal survival was assessed via CCK-8 assays.ResultsTranscriptome analysis revealed 376 differentially expressed mRNAs, 491 lncRNAs, 5,253 circRNAs, and 21 miRNAs, which formed extensive ceRNA interaction networks. Functional enrichment highlighted programmed cell death pathways, including NF-κB and inflammasome signaling. The circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis emerged as a key regulatory node. qRT-PCR confirmed elevated CD14 expression in AD models. CD14 knockdown significantly attenuated Aβ1–42–induced NF-κB activation, NLRP3 inflammasome assembly, and proinflammatory cytokine release. Conditioned medium transfer experiments further demonstrated that CD14 silencing alleviated microglia-mediated neuronal damage and improved HT-22 cell survival.ConclusionWhole-transcriptome sequencing uncovered extensive ceRNA regulatory networks in AD hippocampus, with the circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis potentially implicated in neuroinflammation and programmed cell death through NF-κB signaling. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia in the elderly. Its clinical features include cognitive decline and memory impairment, while pathological hallmarks are extracellular amyloid-β (Aβ) deposits and intracellular tau neurofibrillary tangles, long regarded as diagnostic standards [1]. Although diagnostic frameworks such as the International Working Group-2 (IWG-2) and the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) criteria have incorporated biomarkers and neuroimaging [2], the molecular mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis remain poorly understood, and no effective therapies are available. Moreover, genetic and environmental factors are believed to jointly contribute to disease susceptibility [3].

Increasing evidence implicates dysregulated RNA processing in neurodegeneration. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), play key roles in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation [4]. MiRNAs, of which approximately 70% are brain-enriched, regulate neuronal development and disease processes. LncRNAs and circRNAs act as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), modulating miRNA activity and forming complex regulatory networks. Emerging evidence links ncRNA dysregulation to tumors, aging, and neurodegenerative diseases [5–7], but their roles in AD remain incompletely defined.

Neuroinflammation has emerged as a key contributor to AD pathogenesis. The NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) —comprising NLRP3, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and caspase-1—can be activated by Aβ, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18). Sustained inflammasome activation amplifies neuroinflammatory signaling, ultimately exacerbating neuronal damage and accelerating disease progression [8]. CD14, a pattern recognition receptor, has been implicated in innate immune signaling. Previous studies suggest CD14 modulates Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) / nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)–mediated inflammation and participates in Aβ phagocytosis in microglia [9, 10]. Genetic association studies reported links between TLR4 polymorphisms and AD risk, although the role of CD14 variants remains inconclusive.

In this study, we performed whole-transcriptome sequencing of hippocampal tissues from APP/PS1 transgenic mice and wild-type controls to construct ceRNA networks and identify programmed cell death–related pathways. We focused on the circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis and validated its role in NF-κB signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and microglia–neuron interactions. Our findings provide mechanistic insights into ncRNA-mediated regulation in AD and highlight CD14 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Animals and tissue collection

Nine-month-old male APP/PS1 double-transgenic mice and age-matched C57BL/6J wild-type controls (n = 6 per group) were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The hippocampus was selected as the target region for this study due to its early and severe involvement in AD pathology, particularly in mediating cognitive and memory deficits in the APP/PS1 model. At 9 months of age, these mice exhibit well-established amyloid-β (Aβ) plaque deposition and neuroinflammatory responses, representing a critical symptomatic stage of AD. Animals were maintained under standard conditions (25 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle, 50 ± 10% relative humidity) with free access to food and water. For whole-transcriptome sequencing, three APP/PS1 and three WT mice were selected at random. An additional independent set of three APP/PS1 and three WT mice was used for qRT-PCR validation to avoid repeated measurements on the same samples. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation before hippocampal dissection. Tissues were rapidly collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until further use. All procedures complied with the Guiding Opinions on the Care of Laboratory Animals (Ministry of Science and Technology of China, 2006) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (Ethics Approval No. GENINK-20230059).

RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampal tissue using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity was evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were used for library preparation.

For long RNA (mRNA/lncRNA) analysis, libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA with Ribo-Zero Gold kit (Illumina, Cat. No. RS-122-2301) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 1 µg of total RNA was used per sample. Ribosomal RNA was removed, and the remaining RNA was fragmented, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, end-repaired, adapter-ligated, and PCR-amplified to generate strand-specific libraries. For circRNA analysis, rRNA-depleted RNA was additionally treated with RNase R to enrich circular transcripts. For small RNA analysis, 3’-adapter ligation and reverse transcription were performed to construct miRNA libraries.

Library quality and concentration were confirmed by Qubit fluorometry and Bioanalyzer 2100. Qualified libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 110 million raw reads per sample. Detailed library construction steps and the complete bioinformatics analysis pipeline (including preprocessing, alignment, transcriptome assembly, and identification of novel lncRNAs and circRNAs) are described in Supplementary Material 1.

Sequencing data processing and quality control

Raw reads were processed using fastp v0.20.1 for adapter trimming and quality filtering, and overall quality was assessed with fastqc v0.11.9. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38.p6, NCBI) with HISAT2 v2.1.0 under strand-specific mode. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed using StringTie v2.1.7, and gene-level counts were generated with HTSeq-count v0.9.1. Normalized expression values (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads, FPKM) were calculated with Cufflinks v2.2.1.

circRNAs were identified from back-spliced junction reads using CIRI v2.0.3 and find_circ v1.2, and annotated with circBase, CIRCpedia v2, and circAtlas 2.0. Expression levels were reported as spliced reads per million mapped reads (RPM). Novel lncRNAs were predicted using CPC2, CNCI, PLEK, and Pfam v30, and integrated with known lncRNAs from Ensembl and RefSeq databases. miRNAs were identified and quantified against miRBase v22 using miRanda v3.3a and TargetFinder.

Quality control metrics (raw/clean reads, valid bases, Q30 scores, and GC content) as well as detailed command-line parameters for RNA-seq, circRNA, and miRNA analyses are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Differential expression analysis

Count matrices were imported into R v4.x. Differential expression (DE) of mRNAs, lncRNAs, and miRNAs was performed using DESeq2 v1.22.2 [11], while circRNAs were analyzed with DESeq v1.34.1. Prior to statistical testing, raw read counts were normalized using the size-factor normalization method implemented in DESeq2. This method calculates a geometric mean for each gene across all samples to correct for differences in sequencing depth and library composition. For miRNAs, expression levels were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM). To identify significantly differentially expressed transcripts, we applied a threshold of (|log2 (Fold Change)| ≥ 0.58, which is mathematically equivalent to a linear Fold Change (FC) ≥ 1.5, combined with an false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. No additional batch-effect correction was applied. Visualization of DE results, including volcano plots, hierarchical clustering, and heatmaps, was performed with ggplot2 and pheatmap in R.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes was performed using clusterProfiler v4.x with the org.Mm.eg.db annotation package. Both Gene Ontology (GO: biological process, cellular component, molecular function; http://geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG; http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) pathway analyses were conducted. The background gene set was defined as all expressed genes passing quality control in the corresponding assay. Enrichment significance was determined using the Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-value (q < 0.05). Results were visualized as dot plots and bar plots using clusterProfiler and ggplot2 in R.

Construction of ceRNA networks

According to the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) hypothesis, lncRNAs and circRNAs may act as molecular sponges to sequester miRNAs and thereby regulate mRNA expression. Putative lncRNA–miRNA and circRNA–miRNA interactions were predicted using miRanda v3.3a with default parameters. For each transcript, predicted binding sites, interaction scores, and free energy values were obtained. Differentially expressed miRNAs and their predicted lncRNA/circRNA targets were then integrated with corresponding mRNAs to construct the ceRNA network. For network visualization, to avoid severe visual overplotting and to highlight the most high-confidence core regulatory hubs, the top 300 most significant lncRNA/circRNA–miRNA pairs (ranked by the lowest p-value) and the top 100 most highly connected mRNA-lncRNA pairs were retained. Interaction networks were visualized using the R network package.

To evaluate the regulatory strength and reliability of the predicted ceRNA interactions, we calculated the coexpression coefficient and the ceRNA score for each pair. The coexpression coefficient was determined using Pearson correlation analysis to assess whether the expression levels of the ceRNA (lncRNA or circRNA) and the target mRNA were positively correlated across samples. A threshold of Pearson r ≥ 0.80 and p ≤ 0.05 was applied to define a strong coexpression relationship. Concurrently, the ceRNA score was calculated using a hypergeometric test to quantify the significance of shared miRNA binding sites between the ncRNA and the mRNA. A higher ceRNA score indicates a higher statistical probability that the two transcripts competitively bind to the same pool of miRNAs.

Enrichment was assessed by evaluating overlaps between differentially expressed mRNAs in the ceRNA network and the curated PCD gene sets using hypergeometric tests with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction (q < 0.05).

qRT-PCR validation

Total RNA from hippocampal tissue was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA was reverse-transcribed with the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA), and quantitative PCR was performed using the FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master (ROX; Roche, USA). β-actin was used as the internal reference for mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA, while U6 was used for miRNAs. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. To provide a standard baseline for relative comparison, the mean expression level of the control group (C57BL/6J) was strictly used as the calibrator and normalized to 1.0. Each reaction was run in triplicate, and three independent biological replicates were included per group. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Cell culture, Aβ treatment, siRNA transfection, and indirect conditioned medium transfer experiment

BV2 microglia and HT-22 hippocampal neuronal cells (Tianjin Institute of Neurology, Tianjin, China) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For AD model induction, cells were treated with Aβ1–42 peptide. For RNA interference, BV2 cells seeded in 6-well plates were transfected with 100 pmol of CD14-specific siRNA or negative control siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were exposed to the siRNA for 24 h before harvesting. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR. The expression of Cd14, Nfkb1, Rela, Nfkbia (IκBα), Nlrp3, Gsdmd, IL-1β, and IL-18 was quantified by qRT-PCR as described above. The protein concentrations of secreted IL-1β and IL-18 in the BV-2 cell culture supernatants were quantified using specific Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits (Mouse IL-1β Quantikine ELISA Kit, Cat# MLB00C, R&D Systems; Mouse IL-18 ELISA Kit, Cat# ab216165, Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To evaluate the effect of microglial neuroinflammation on neuronal cells, an indirect conditioned medium transfer experiment was employed. Briefly, the supernatants from treated BV-2 cells were collected and designated as CM. HT-22 cells were then cultured in this BV-2-derived CM for 48 h. Cell viability of HT-22 neurons was subsequently assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-group comparisons were conducted using two-tailed Student’s t-tests, and multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.

Results

Differential expression profiles of mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs in APP/PS1 hippocampus

Using a cutoff of |log₂FC| ≥ 0.58 and FDR < 0.05, we identified extensive transcriptomic alterations in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice compared with wild-type controls. A total of 376 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) were detected, including 276 upregulated and 100 downregulated genes. The volcano plot (Fig. 1A) illustrates the distribution of DEmRNAs, with red and green points indicating significantly up- and downregulated genes, respectively, while hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1B) reveals a clear separation between APP/PS1 and control samples, suggesting consistent mRNA expression patterns within each genotype.

(B) Heatmap of mRNA expression clustering. (C) Volcano plot of differentially expressed lncRNAs. (D) Heatmap of lncRNA expression clustering. (E) Volcano plot of differentially expressed circRNAs. (F) Heatmap of circRNA expression clustering. (G) Volcano plot of differentially expressed miRNAs. (H) Heatmap of miRNA expression clustering. Differentially expressed transcripts were defined based on the thresholds of |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 0.58 and FDR < 0.05

Similarly, 491 differentially expressed lncRNAs (281 upregulated, 212 downregulated) were identified (Fig. 1C–D). The volcano plot shows balanced distributions of up- and downregulated transcripts (Fig. 1C), and the heatmap (Fig. 1D) demonstrates distinct lncRNA expression profiles between groups. For circRNAs, 5,253 transcripts were differentially expressed (2,457 upregulated, 2,796 downregulated), as shown in the volcano and clustering plots (Fig. 1E–F), indicating broad circular RNA dysregulation. Finally, 21 miRNAs (3 upregulated, 18 downregulated) were significantly altered (Fig. 1G–H), and their clustering patterns also distinguished APP/PS1 from wild-type samples. Collectively, these findings demonstrate robust, genotype-specific transcriptomic differences across all four RNA classes. The full lists of differentially expressed transcripts are provided in Supplementary Material 3.

Construction of ceRNA interaction networks

To explore potential post-transcriptional regulatory interactions in AD, integrated ceRNA networks were constructed based on the differentially expressed mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs identified above. Correlation analysis was performed across all six hippocampal samples, incorporating 21 DEmiRNAs, 376 DEmRNAs, 493 DElncRNAs, and 5,253 DEcircRNAs.

For lncRNA-associated interactions, the top 300 lncRNA–miRNA pairs with the lowest p-values were selected to construct the interaction network (Supplementary Material 4, Supplementary Figure S1A). In this network, lncRNAs are represented as circles and miRNAs as triangles, with larger nodes indicating higher connectivity. The ceRNAScore and coexpression coefficients were further calculated to assess regulatory strength (Supplementary Figure S1B). The top 100 most connected mRNA–lncRNA pairs were visualized (Fig. 2A), and a representative ternary ceRNA relationship diagram illustrates the integrated lncRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory module (Fig. 2B). Similarly, circRNA-related regulatory relationships were identified and visualized. The top 300 circRNA–miRNA pairs were extracted to generate the circRNA–miRNA interaction network (Supplementary Material 4, Supplementary Figure S1C), with node size again reflecting network centrality. Coexpression analysis and ceRNAScore ranking confirmed the robustness of these interactions (Supplementary Material 4, Supplementary Figure S1D). The top 100 circRNA–mRNA associations were mapped (Fig. 2C), and a representative ternary circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory module is shown in Fig. 2D.

(C) Network visualization of the top 100 circRNA–mRNA associations based on interaction strength. In total, 143 lncRNA–miRNA pairs linked to 23,029 mRNA–lncRNA interactions and 505 circRNA–miRNA pairs linked to 193,907 mRNA–circRNA interactions were identified, forming the integrated ceRNA regulatory network in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice. Collectively, these analyses revealed multiple putative ceRNA regulatory relationships in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, suggesting that both lncRNAs and circRNAs may modulate gene expression through shared miRNA-mediated mechanisms.

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis of ceRNA networks

To elucidate the biological significance of the ceRNA networks, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted for the mRNAs participating in each network. For the lncRNA-associated ceRNA network, enriched GO terms were mainly related to biological processes such as immune regulation, inflammatory response, apoptosis, and synaptic signaling (Fig. 3A). The bubble plot further illustrates that the most significant enrichments were in pathways governing neuronal death and cytokine activity (Fig. 3B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed notable enrichment in NF-κB signaling, MAPK signaling, and apoptosis pathways (Fig. 3C), suggesting that lncRNA-mediated ceRNA interactions may contribute to neuroinflammatory and cell-death–related mechanisms in AD.

Identification of potential circRNA-mediated regulatory pathways

Based on the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the circRNA-associated ceRNA network, several mRNAs were found to be significantly enriched in signaling pathways related to programmed cell death (PCD), including the NOD-like receptor, MAPK, TLR, and NF-κB pathways. To further clarify the potential mechanisms underlying these findings, representative circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory axes were extracted from the network according to ceRNA scores and biological relevance.

Three circRNA-mediated ceRNA pathways were identified as potentially linked to PCD regulation. In the circRNA_02411/miR-205-5p/Naip2 axis, Naip2 is a PCD-associated mRNA participating in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, suggesting that this network may modulate pyroptotic processes. In the circRNA_13083/miR-205-5p/Trem2 axis, Trem2 is involved in osteoclast differentiation and has been previously implicated in neurodegenerative and inflammatory responses, indicating that this module may contribute to microglial activation in AD. In the circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis, CD14 is a pyroptosis-related gene enriched in several inflammation-associated pathways, including MAPK, NF-κB, and TLR signaling.

Among these pathways, the circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 network was prioritized for subsequent experimental validation. This axis may regulate microglial pyroptosis and neuroinflammation through NF-κB activation, thereby contributing to neuronal dysfunction in AD. Further molecular experiments were conducted to verify this hypothesis in the following sections.

qRT-PCR validation of selected ceRNA components and PCD-related genes

To validate the reliability of the sequencing data and confirm the differential expression of key ceRNA components, representative lncRNAs, circRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs associated with PCD pathways were examined by qRT-PCR. Specifically, fourteen mRNAs, three lncRNAs (XR_373236.2, XR_875865.1, XR_001783993.1), five circRNAs (circRNA_19046| Chr9:45683558_45685392_+, circRNA_502|Chr12:73926557_73932476_+, circRNA_05599|Chr13:93790039_93807703_+, circRNA_02411|Chr10: 108627253_108691063_−, and circRNA_13083| Chr4:28817445_28871996_+) and four miRNAs (mmu-miR-135b-5p, mmu-miR-216b-5p, mmu-miR-298-5p, and mmu-miR-205-5p) were selected for validation. To directly assess the trends between the two approaches, qRT-PCR results (Fig. 4A-D) were plotted alongside the normalized RNA-Seq data (Fig. 4E-H).

Comparison of the two platforms revealed that several programmed cell death-related genes showed consistent expression trends. Specifically, the apoptosis-related genes Gfap and Ccl4, the pyroptosis-related markers Mpeg1, Trem2, Cd14, and Naip2, and the cuproptosis-related gene Ccl3 were all significantly upregulated in the APP/PS1 group in both qRT-PCR and RNA-Seq assays (Fig. 4A and E). Interestingly, some genes exhibited discordant trends between the two methodologies, which is a recognized phenomenon due to differences in technical sensitivity and transcript variants. For instance, while the ferroptosis-associated protein Ngb and apoptosis-related Ret were significantly increased in the qRT-PCR validation, their expression levels were found to be reduced in the RNA-Seq dataset. Conversely, App expression was significantly reduced in qRT-PCR despite showing upregulation in sequencing. Casp1 and Bub1b showed no significant differences in the qRT-PCR assay.

Regarding lncRNAs, RNA-Seq data (Fig. 4F) demonstrated a robust upregulation for Lnc_Gm36359, Lnc_Gm28836, and Lnc_Gm38650; however, qRT-PCR validation (Fig. 4B) showed that they did not reach statistical significance. For circRNAs, circ_0004889 (also identified as circRNA_05599) and circ_0011550 (also identified as circRNA_13083 in our network analysis) were significantly upregulated in the APP/PS1 group across both platforms (Fig. 4C and G). Notably, while circ_0001939 (also identified as circRNA_02411) was significantly downregulated in qRT-PCR, it was elevated in RNA-Seq. circ_0004175 and circ_0015612 showed no significant changes in the qRT-PCR validation. Finally, miRNA analysis confirmed that miR-298-5p and miR-205-5p were significantly downregulated in APP/PS1 mice consistently in both qRT-PCR (Fig. 4D) and RNA-Seq (Fig. 4H) platforms. Interestingly, while RNA-Seq indicated no significant differential expression for miR-135b-5p, subsequent qRT-PCR validation revealed it to be significantly upregulated. Furthermore, miR-216b-5p did not show statistical significance in either the sequencing or the qRT-PCR analyses.

Importantly, the expression trends of these validated transcripts perfectly aligned with the theoretical ceRNA regulatory logic, reflecting their functional interacting effects. For instance, within the prioritized circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis, the significant upregulation of the competitive sponge circRNA_13083 coincides with the marked downregulation of its target inhibitor miR-298-5p. Consequently, the downstream functional target, CD14, is significantly upregulated due to the release of miRNA-mediated suppression. This clear directional consistency in expression levels across the network strongly supports the functional validity of the identified interactome axes in promoting AD-related neuroinflammation.

CD14 mediates Aβ1-42-induced neuroinflammation via NF-κB and inflammasome signaling

To validate the predicted regulatory role of CD14 in AD–related neuroinflammation, functional assays were performed in BV2 microglial cells. Given the central role of microglia in mediating Aβ-induced neuroinflammation [16], we utilized the murine BV-2 microglial cell line to evaluate this cascade in vitro. Furthermore, because CD14 emerged as a core target in our ceRNA network and is a critical mediator of microglial activation [9], we focused on knocking down CD14 to determine its functional impact. Cells were divided into six groups: control, Aβ1-42 exposure, si-NC, si-NC + Aβ1-42, si-CD14, and si-CD14 + Aβ1-42. Knockdown of CD14 significantly reduced its mRNA level compared with negative control cells (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S2A).

Following Aβ1-42 treatment, the mRNA levels of Nfkb1 and Ikbα were markedly increased, indicating activation of the NF-κB pathway. However, CD14 silencing suppressed Aβ1-42–induced Nfkb1 and Ikbα upregulation (Fig. 5A–B), suggesting that CD14 positively regulates NF-κB activation in microglia.

(A–F) Changes in mRNA levels of Nfkb1, Ikbα, Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Il-1β, and Il-18 in BV2 microglia after CD14 knockdown and/or Aβ1-42 treatment. (G–H) Protein concentrations of secreted IL-1β and IL-18 in BV2 microglial supernatants measured by ELISA. To assess the downstream inflammasome response, expression of Nlrp3 and Gsdmd was examined. Both genes were significantly upregulated in Aβ1-42–treated cells, whereas CD14 knockdown attenuated this induction (Fig. 5C–D). These findings imply that CD14 contributes to inflammasome assembly and pyroptotic signaling in the microglial AD model.

Consistently, pro-inflammatory cytokines Il-1β and Il-18 were markedly elevated after Aβ1-42 exposure but significantly reduced in CD14-silenced cells (Fig. 5E–F). To confirm this functional inhibition at the protein level, ELISA was performed on the cell culture supernatants. As shown in Fig. 5G–H, CD14 silencing significantly attenuated the Aβ1-42-induced secretion of mature IL-1β and IL-18 proteins. Together, these results indicate that CD14 facilitates the transcriptional upregulation of markers associated with Aβ1–42–triggered NF-κB activation, inflammasome formation, and cytokine release in microglia. Knockdown of CD14 alleviated these transcriptional changes, suggesting its potential modulatory role in inflammatory and pyroptotic responses at the mRNA level. While further protein-level validations are required to confirm the downstream functional pathways, these transcriptional alterations are consistent with the observed alleviation of AD-relevant microglial toxicity.

Furthermore, the viability of HT-22 neurons was significantly reduced when cultured with CM from Aβ1–42–treated microglia. Notably, CD14 silencing in microglia effectively alleviated this CM-mediated neuronal damage and improved HT-22 cell survival (Supplementary Figure S2B).

Discussion

In this study, whole-transcriptome sequencing of APP/PS1 mouse hippocampus revealed widespread dysregulation of non-coding RNAs and enabled the construction of ceRNA regulatory networks. Under the threshold of |log2FC| ≥ 0.58 and FDR < 0.05, we identified 143 lncRNA–miRNA pairs, 23,029 lncRNA–mRNA pairs, 505 circRNA–miRNA pairs, and 193,907 circRNA–mRNA pairs. This extensive network further supports the well-established consensus that complex post-transcriptional regulation is deeply involved in the pathogenesis of AD [7, 17]. Functional enrichment highlighted apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis, together with immune and inflammatory signaling, consistent with the multifactorial nature of neuronal loss in neurodegeneration. By integrating computational predictions with expression data, the circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis was prioritized and validated, providing mechanistic insight into the link between ceRNA regulation and microglia-mediated neuroinflammation.

Non-coding RNAs are increasingly recognized as key regulators of neuronal function and degeneration. LncRNAs can modulate transcriptional and post-transcriptional programs, and several have been reported to influence amyloid and tau pathology in AD, such as BACE1-AS/BACE1 [17]. CircRNAs, which form covalently closed loops and act as efficient miRNA sponges, are abundant in the brain and accumulate across development. Recent evidence has linked circRNAs to cognitive disorders including Parkinson’s disease, stroke, and traumatic brain injury [18]. The networks identified here support the notion that aberrant lncRNA/circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulation contributes to AD progression through disruption of neuronal and inflammatory homeostasis.

Importantly, the convergence of both lncRNA- and circRNA-associated networks on prominent inflammatory and immune-related pathways perfectly aligns with the disease progression context of our animal models. In this study, mice were sacrificed for sequence analysis at 9 months of age, a critical symptomatic stage in the APP/PS1 model characterized by robust Aβ plaque accumulation and peak microglial activation. The extensive neuroinflammation captured in our canonical pathways reflects this severe pathological reality. The enrichment of programmed cell death (PCD) pathways is consistent with current views that neuronal loss in AD is not limited to apoptosis but involves pyroptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis [19, 20]. The NLRP3 inflammasome represents a critical hub, mediating pyroptosis and intersecting with other PCD modalities [21, 22]. Our data indicated that ceRNA-linked genes overlap significantly with curated PCD gene sets, suggesting potential transcriptional enrichment of these processes.

Previous research has highlighted the critical involvement of miR-298-5p in mediating memory regulation, neurotoxicity, and neuronal apoptosis [23, 24]. More importantly, miR-298-5p is directly implicated in AD pathology, with elevated transcript levels observed in the temporal lobes of AD patients [25]. Furthermore, studies have demonstrated that extracellular miR-298-5p released from apoptotic neurons can act as an endogenous TLR ligand, triggering microglial accumulation and contributing to widespread neurodegeneration in vivo [26]. While the multifaceted roles of miR-298-5p in neural injury and neuroinflammation are becoming clearer, its specific upstream circular RNA regulators in AD have remained largely unexplored. By identifying this novel circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p upstream axis, our findings offer new mechanistic insights into how dysregulated post-transcriptional networks may contribute to microglial activation and AD pathogenesis.

Another important observation was the convergence of ceRNA networks on innate immune signaling. CD14, a pattern recognition co-receptor, emerged as a central mediator [27]. While the general role of CD14 in AD-related neuroinflammation is established, our study introduces a novel perspective by positioning CD14 as a downstream target regulated within a larger, upstream ceRNA network. Previous studies have shown that CD14 modulates TLR4/NF-κB signaling and contributes to amyloid uptake by microglia [28]. In our in vitro experiments, CD14 was upregulated in microglia exposed to Aβ1–42, accompanied by NF-κB activation and induction of NLRP3 inflammasome components. Silencing CD14 suppressed NF-κB signaling, reduced Il1b and Il18 expression, and improved neuronal survival in indirect conditioned medium transfer experiments, supporting its role as a key node in Aβ-driven neuroinflammation.

There are limitations that should be noted. The sequencing cohort was relatively small, which restricts the power of correlation-based network inference. Predictions of miRNA–target interactions require experimental confirmation, and our validations were performed primarily at the RNA level. Additionally, our qRT-PCR validation was performed on a separate, independent mouse cohort. While this confirms the biological reproducibility of our findings, it does not directly validate the technical fidelity of the initial RNA-Seq dataset. While we have validated the downstream functional cytokine release at the protein level, further investigation of intermediate signaling molecules via Western blot, as well as in vivo confirmation in APP/PS1 mice or clinical samples, is needed to strengthen the conclusions.

In summary, this work expands the understanding of ceRNA regulation in AD by highlighting how ncRNA–miRNA–mRNA interactions converge on microglial CD14 and NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling, thereby linking post-transcriptional regulation to programmed cell death and neuroinflammation. The circRNA_13083/miR-298-5p/CD14 axis represents a promising candidate for mechanistic exploration. Given the stability and detectability of circRNAs in biofluids, and the tractability of CD14-related pathways for pharmacological modulation, this regulatory axis may have translational potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in AD. Future work integrating in vivo validation, clinical cohorts, and functional rescue will be essential to advance these findings toward clinical application.
