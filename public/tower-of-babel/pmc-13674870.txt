Occupational needle stick injuries in a tertiary ophthalmic care setup: An eastern India perspective
Authors: Prerana Vijay, Sujata Das, Srikant Kumar Sahu, Bibhusita Samal, Kirteemayee Sarangi, Smruti Rekha Priyadarshini
Published: 2026

Needlestick injuries (NSIs) are a significant health hazard among healthcare workers exposing them to serious risks, like transmission of blood-borne viruses (BBVs), resulting in long-term health consequences. They are the second common cause and contribute to 17% of work-related accidents.[1] The adoption of advanced microsurgical techniques in ophthalmology has increased the risk of sharp injuries, especially when instrument handling occurs under high magnification and low lighting conditions.[23] This primarily occurs while operating, disposal and recapping of sharp instruments, improper handling, cleaning, and administering injections.[4]

We conducted a retrospective, observational analysis of all NSI/sharp injuries between 2021 and 2024. Details were obtained from the quality control departmental records and included location, occupation, instrument, mode of prick, time, and type of surgery. Following each incident, blood samples were collected within one hour and screened for BBV (HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (2025-230-BHR-I5).

Out of 1177 staff employed at our eye institute during the study period, a total of 51 professionals sustained NSI. During the study period, 673,952 patients attended the center and 87,307 underwent surgery, yielding a prevalence of 4.33% compared to a global estimate of 9.1–44.5%.[5] Overall, ophthalmic surgeons sustained maximum injuries (56.9%) followed by nurses (35.3%), housekeeping staff (5.9%), and outpatient staff (2%). Among the ophthalmologists, doctors in training were more vulnerable (31.37%) than senior doctors (25.5%). Most incidents occurred in the operating rooms (ORs) (56.86%), followed by waste disposal area (21.56%), preoperative area while instilling anesthesia (9.8%), inpatient wards (9.8%), and outpatient departments (2%). Needles constituted the predominant source of sharp injuries (61.5%), followed by blades (26.9%), and unspecified instruments (9.8%). Maximum cases happened in the cornea OR (48.3%), followed by the glaucoma (20.7%), retina (17.2%), oculoplasty (10.3%), and pediatric ophthalmology (3.4%). Most incidents occurred in the afternoon shift (67.7%). The seroprevalence of BBV has been reported to be 1.87%[6] in ophthalmology setups. However, the risk of transmission has been reported to be 0.3% (HIV), 6% to 30% (Hepatitis B), and 1.8%. (Hepatitis C) among health care workers.[7] Nevertheless, BBV screening at our institute reported negative in all cases. These injuries impact not only physical health but also psychological wellbeing, potentially impairing healthcare workers’ performance.

Operating room procedures require close team coordination, while distractions and time constraints may increase the risk of staff injuries.[8] The high rates of NSI suggest reinforcing the need for safety-engineered needles, needle-less systems, and safer disposal practices. Fatigue and reduced alertness are well-documented factors that contribute to workplace accidents, particularly in healthcare settings where staff often work long hours. It is thus important to implement fatigue management strategies, such as rotating staff or providing adequate breaks, ensuring that healthcare professionals remain alert and focused throughout their shifts.

NSIs remain an unfortunate occupational hazard. The key preventive measures to reduce the risk are adequate training and awareness, focusing on procedural safety protocols, introducing double gloving, use of nonreplaceable engineering safety devices, and providing psychosocial support and counseling to the affected individuals. This study highlights the role of a safety and accountability culture in driving sustainable change.
