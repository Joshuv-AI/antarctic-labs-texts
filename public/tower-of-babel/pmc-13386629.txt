Dynamic immune profiling enables early distinction between gastrointestinal GVHD and viral diarrhea after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation
Authors: Man Chen, Xue-qiao Wang, Jing Long, Min-Jing Fu, Hui Wang, Wei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAllogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is curative for hematologic malignancies, yet diarrhea after allo-HSCT—mainly due to gastrointestinal graft-versus-host disease (GI-GVHD) or viral infection—remains a major clinical challenge. Early etiological differentiation is essential for appropriate management.AimsThis study aimed to develop an immune-based predictive model by dynamically monitoring lymphocyte subsets and cytokines after allo-HSCT.ResultsAmong 232 patients who underwent allo-HSCT at Beijing Lu Daopei Hospital between 2021 and 2024, 58 developed biopsy-confirmed post-transplant (post-HSCT) diarrhea, comprising 34 cases of GI-GVHD and 24 cases of viral enteritis. Immune profiles were measured one week prior to diarrhea onset, as well as at weeks 4 and 5 post-HSCT. Predictive features were selected using LASSO and logistic regression to construct single- and multi–time–point models, which were validated using nomogram, calibration, and decision curve analyses. At the pre-diarrhea point, patients with aGVHD had higher Reg3α, CD3⁺T%, CD8⁺T%, naive CD4⁺/CD4⁺T%, and Th2/CD4⁺T%, but lower NK count and NK% (all P < 0.05). The single-time-point model achieved good discrimination (AUC = 0.799), while the multi–time-point model integrating nine dynamic parameters showed superior accuracy (AUC = 0.908). Conclusions: Dynamic immune profiling may aid in the early distinction between GI-GVHD and viral enteritis, highlighting its potential as a tool for immunological surveillance to guide intervention after allo-HSCT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12865-026-00850-8.Keywords: Viral diarrhea, allo-HSCT, GI-GVHD, Cytokines, Dynamic immune monitoring

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a practical curative approach for patients with high-risk hematologic malignancies and selected nonmalignant and congenital disorders [1]. Although significant advances have been made, non-relapse mortality (NRM) after transplantation remains substantial [2]. Diarrhea after allo-HSCT, with an incidence as high as 40%–91%, is the most common complication [3, 4] Early post-transplant (post-HSCT) can arise from diverse etiologies, including infectious pathogens, chemotherapy-induced mucositis, drug-related toxicity, and acute graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD). Among these, gastrointestinal GVHD (GI-GVHD) and viral infections are significant causes of morbidity and mortality in allo-HSCT recipients [5, 6]. The clinical manifestations of diarrhea caused by aGVHD and infection often overlap, yet their treatment strategies differ markedly. Thus early and accurate differential diagnosis is essential. Endoscopy with pathological biopsy is the gold standard, but some patients cannot undergo this procedure due to thrombocytopenia, coagulation disorders, or poor general condition. Hence non invasive methods are critically needed. Based on the distinct immune mechanisms of aGVHD and viral infection, this study aims to explore novel immunological approaches for differential diagnosis.

Patients and methods

Study population and eligibility criteria

This retrospective study included patients who underwent anti-thymocyte globulin/granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (ATG/G-CSF)-based allo-HSCT at Beijing Lu Daopei Hospital between April 2021 and December 2024 and subsequently developed diarrhea. Inclusion criteria required participation in the standardized post-transplant immune monitoring protocol, availability of complete lymphocyte subset and cytokine data collected within 7 days before diarrhea onset (± 3 days), and no contraindications to colonoscopy. Patients were excluded if they had missing pre-onset immune data, contraindications to colonoscopy or refusal of endoscopic evaluation, documented active bloodstream infection (bacteremia or fungemia confirmed by positive blood culture) or pulmonary infection (radiologically confirmed new pulmonary infiltrates with compatible clinical symptoms) at the time of diarrhea onset, or presence of identifiable enteric pathogens other than viruses. Comprehensive stool testing (including bacterial culture, fungal culture, Clostridioides difficile testing, as well as rotavirus, adenovirus, and norovirus assays) was performed in all enrolled patients to exclude patients with intestinal infection. All patients underwent endoscopy with intestinal biopsy and received uniform GVHD prophylaxis with cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and short-course methotrexate. Final etiological differentiation of either GI-GVHD or viral enteritis was assigned based on histopathological evaluation and comprehensive stool testing, according to the criteria detailed in the Diagnostic Criteria section. Furthermore, to ensure the etiological purity of the GI-GVHD group, we prospectively excluded patients with histologically confirmed or clinically suspected concurrent CMV colitis at enrollment. Follow-up data were collected via telephone interviews and medical record review, with survival data censored as of May 31, 2025.

Diagnostic criteria

In this section, we first describe the diagnostic criteria for underlying hematologic malignancies, followed by the criteria for etiological differentiation of post-HSCT diarrhea. Diarrhea was defined as > 3 bowel movements/day with stool weight > 200 g and > 85% water content; hematochezia was grossly bloody stool. Hematologic malignancies, acute myeloid leukemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS), and aplastic anemia (AA) were diagnosed per 1995 EGIL criteria [7] and 2016 WHO classification [8]. The severity of GI-GVHD was graded globally as mild (grade I-II) or severe (grade III-IV) according to the 2020 National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines [9], which operationalize the consensus criteria established by the Mount Sinai Acute GVHD International Consortium (MAGIC). GI-GVHD pathology was assessed per the 2015 National Institutes of Health (NIH) consensus criteria [10, 11], graded by the Lerner criteria [12] (0 = normal, 1 = epithelial apoptosis, 2 = crypt destruction, 3 = focal erosion, 4 = diffuse ulceration). Infectious enteritis was diagnosed following the 2021 Chinese consensus [13]; cytomegalovirus (CMV) enteritis required characteristic viral inclusion bodies; Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) enteritis required the presence of ≥ 10 EBV-encoded small RNA (EBER)⁺ cells per high-power field (HPF) with compatible histopathological features; intestinal quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was used only as an adjunct to confirm mixed infections in histologically proven cases. Human herpesvirus−6 (HHV−6) and adenovirus enteritides each required concordant histopathological and virological evidence for primary disease diagnosis.

Laboratory methods

Flow cytometric analysis of lymphocyte subsets

Immunophenotyping was performed longitudinally for all patients starting from engraftment (typically around week 3 post-HSCT). Samples were collected and analyzed at weekly intervals for the first 3 months, biweekly for months 3–6, and monthly thereafter using a BD Canto Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) with Diva 8.0.2 software.

Sample preparation and staining: For each immunophenotyping tube, approximately 1 × 10⁶ cells were aliquoted from whole blood anticoagulated with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). Then, 3–20µL of fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies were added, followed by thorough mixing and incubation for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, 3 mL of diluted 1× hemolysin was added to lyse erythrocytes. The mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min, then centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed once with 3 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After a final centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of PBS to obtain a single‑cell suspension for acquisition. Lymphocytes were identified during analysis by gating on CD45 + cells with low side-scatter properties. Antibody panels included: Tube 1 (absolute counts): CD3-FITC/CD56 + CD16-PE/CD45-PerCP-Cy5.5/CD4-PE-Cy7/CD19-APC/CD8-APC-Cy7; Tube 2: CD45RA-FITC/CD127-PE/CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5/CD4-PE-Cy7/CD25-APC/CD8-APC-Cy7/CD197-BV421; Tube 3: CD183-PE/CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5/CD4-PE-Cy7/CD196-APC/CD8-APC-Cy7; Tube 4: CD8-FITC/CD38-PE/CD3-PerCP-Cy5.5/CD4-PE-Cy7/CD28-APC/HLA-DR-APC-Cy7.

Percentage calculations: Percentages of major lymphocyte subsets—including CD3 + T cells (CD3 + T%), CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and NK cells (NK%, defined as CD3-CD56 + cells)—are reported as a proportion of total lymphocytes. Subset frequencies (e.g., naive CD4+/CD4 + T%) are expressed as a percentage of the parental population.

Cytokine and chemokine profiling

Serum samples collected concurrently with lymphocyte subsets were analyzed using multiplex bead-based flow cytometry (QBPlex, BDTC) with 14-plex and 10-plex reagent kits (Kuangbo Biotech). Analytes included interleukins (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-15, IL-17 A, IL-17 F, IL-22), TNF-α, TNF-β, IFN-γ, sCD25, MIP-1α, MCP-1, GM-CSF, Reg3α, Elafin, ST2, granzyme B, and TNFR1.

The assay procedure involved: standard curve preparation using serial dilutions; duplicate sample analysis; sequential addition of capture microspheres, detection antibodies, and PE-streptavidin; with appropriate incubation and washing steps before acquisition.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Lu Daopei Hospital (ethics No. DPEC-M-202108) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Clinical trial number: not applicable). Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants and from parents of participants under 18 years of age prior to enrollment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using a t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables using a χ² or Fisher’s exact test, with P < 0.05 considered significant. Overall survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Effect sizes were reported using Cliff’s Delta (non-parametric) and Cohen’s d (parametric) to quantify the magnitude of group differences. Effect sizes were interpreted as |δ| < 0.11 (negligible), 0.11–0.28 (small), 0.28–0.43 (medium), |δ| ≥ 0.43 (large) for Cliff’s Delta [Cliff, 1996], and |d| = 0.2 (small), 0.5 (medium), 0.8 (large) for Cohen’s d[1988]. Key predictors were selected via LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation (optimal λ = 0.06), and non-zero coefficient variables were included in logistic regression. To minimize overfitting, we (1) applied LASSO for high-dimensional screening, (2) retained only statistically significant and biologically relevant variables, and (3) performed internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples, reporting calibrated C-statistics and calibration curves. Nomograms were constructed with the rms package, and model performance was assessed via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Figures were generated using ggplot2 and ggpubr in R (v4.4.3).

Results

Baseline data and clinical characteristics

Between April 2021 and December 2024, a total of 232 patients underwent allo-HSCT at Beijing Lu Daopei Hospital. Of these, 58 patients who developed post-transplant diarrhea and met all eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1; CONSORT 2010 flow diagram). Histopathological evaluation and comprehensive stool testing identified 34 patients with GI-GVHD and 24 patients with viral enteritis. The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of these two groups are summarized and compared in Table 1.The cohort comprised 32 males and 26 females with a median age of 33 (16–49) years, including cases of aplastic anemia (n = 7), AML (n = 31), ALL (n = 12), and MDS (n = 8). GI-GVHD histology showed grade I–IV in 5, 10, 14, and 5 cases, respectively, while infections involved CMV (n = 8), EBV (n = 9), CMV + EBV (n = 5), HHV-6 (n = 1), and adenovirus (n = 1). No significant differences were found between the two groups in time from primary disease diagnosis to HSCT, timing, or duration of diarrhea, hematochezia, hyperbilirubinemia, or baseline clinical characteristics (all P > 0.05). Age was comparable (median 33 years, P = 0.75) and not correlated with disease duration (r=–0.13, P = 0.34). Conditioning regimen, graft source, ATG type, third-party donor use, graft cell dose, and cGVHD incidence were also similar. After a median follow-up of 383 days (range, 68–1313), a descriptive Kaplan-Meier analysis showed no statistically significant difference in overall survival between the two groups (Supplementary Figure S2). This preliminary observation suggests comparable survival outcomes in this cohort, with an estimated 2-year overall survival of 50.1% in the GI-GVHD group and 65.8% in the infectious enteritis group (P = 0.365, log-rank test). Due to the limited number of events, a multivariable survival analysis was not performed.

Absolute counts for these parameters were also analyzed and are presented in Supplementary Table S1. None of the absolute counts showed significant differences between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Predictive model construction

Lasso and logistic regression screening of multi-factor predictive variables

LASSO regression identified five potential predictors, including IL-4, IL-22, CD3⁺T%, NK cell count, and CD4⁺CD28⁺/CD4⁺T% (Supplementary Table S4, Fig. 3). In the initial multivariable logistic model—logit(P[infection]) = 0.6162 + 0.0141 × (NK cell count) − 0.0270 × (CD4⁺CD28⁺/CD4⁺T%)—overall performance was strong (P < 0.0001, AUC = 0.897, Brier = 0.135). CD4⁺CD28⁺/CD4⁺T% was a significant protective factor (P = 0.0043, 95% CI: 0.950–0.991), with each 1-unit increase reducing infection risk by ~ 3%, while NK cell count showed a positive trend (P < 0.1). A simplified two-variable model incorporating these predictors remained robust (χ²=19.98, df = 2, P < 0.001; C-statistic = 0.799, 95% CI: 0.66–0.98; Brier = 0.162). Regression coefficients, ORs, and 95% CIs are summarized in Table 2.

Logistic regression model with single-time-point predictors

Construct and validate the nomogram of a single time point bivariate diagnostic model

A diagnostic nomogram based on the two-variable model was developed to predict infection/GVHD risk by summing the corresponding scores of each index. The model showed good calibration (Fig. 4A) and clinical net benefit in decision curve analysis (DCA) (Fig. 4C). Calibration analysis demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 4B). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis yielded an AUC of 0.799, with an optimal cut-off of 0.408 (Youden index), indicating good discrimination. At this threshold, sensitivity and specificity were 0.708 and 0.882, respectively, suggesting high specificity with moderate sensitivity. The positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) at this cutoff were 0.800 and 0.789, respectively. Patients with a predicted infection risk > 40.8% were classified as high risk and warranting clinical attention (Fig. 4D).

Construct multi-time-point prediction models at one week pre-diarrhea, and at weeks 4 and 5 post-HSCT

For the multi-time-point analysis, seven patients who developed diarrhea before week 5th were excluded. Consequently, the longitudinal model was developed and validated using data from 54 patients (33 GI-GVHD, 21 infectious enteritis). For multi–time-point prediction (pre-diarrhea, 4th and 5th week), nine key predictors were retained (Supplementary Table S5, Supplementary Figure S3) and used to construct a weighted Predicted Score: Predicted Score = 2.6714–0.0116×(CD8⁺T% pre-diarrhea) + 0.0049×(NK pre-diarrhea) – 0.0217×(CD4⁺CD28⁺/CD4⁺T% pre-diarrhea) – 0.0256×(Th2/CD4⁺T% pre-diarrhea) – 0.0005×(TNFR1 4th week) + 0.0006×(NK 4th week) – 1.6570×(GM-CSF 5th week) + 0.0038×(NK 5th week) + 0.0243×(CD3⁺CD38⁺T% 5th week). Multi-factor logistic regression showed that each unit increase in Predicted Score was strongly associated with infection (OR = 5.11, 95% CI 2.47–14.71, P < 0.001). ROC analysis indicated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.908, cutoff = 0.379, sensitivity = 0.875, specificity = 0.794). Bootstrap internal validation (1000 resamples) confirmed good calibration and discrimination (C-statistic = 0.884 [0.880–0.889]). Calibration curves demonstrated agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and decision curve analysis confirmed the clinical net benefit of the nomogram-guided intervention (Fig. 5). We also assessed whether incorporating routine clinical variables (diarrhea onset, duration, frequency, presence of hematochezia or hyperbilirubinemia) could improve the predictive models. The addition of these clinical features did not yield a meaningful increase in discriminatory performance (Supplementary Table S6). For the multi-time-point model, the PPV and NPV at the optimal cutoff of 0.379 were 0.698 and 0.879, respectively.

Discussion

Given the critical need to differentiate GI‑GVHD from viral enteritis—two leading etiologies of post‑transplant diarrhea with opposing treatment strategies [5, 6, 14–18]—we aimed to develop an immune‑based tool for early discrimination. Our study demonstrates that dynamic immune profiling captures distinct lymphocyte and cytokine trajectories preceding symptom onset, providing a novel, non‑invasive approach to this persistent clinical challenge.

Anthony D. Sung et al. [19] reported similar diarrhea incidence in GVHD and non-GVHD groups. Although the triad of diarrhea, abdominal pain, and weight loss favors GVHD, it lacks specificity (60% vs. 42%). Histopathology remains the most reliable diagnostic tool, with apoptosis, crypt destruction, and gland/crypt dropout as key diagnostic features. In our cohort, diarrhea onset, duration, frequency, or volume did not differentiate aGVHD from viral infection, and hyperbilirubinemia, though more frequent in aGVHD, was not significant in univariate analysis. Since the addition of these clinical variables did not significantly improve model performance, we conclude that dynamic immune monitoring may offer discriminative value independent of routine clinical parameters. Despite being the gold standard, pathology has limitations: biopsy sampling is challenging, early GVHD changes may be subtle, etiological differentiation depends on the pathologist’s expertise, and CMV colitis can mimic GVHD [19, 20]. Moreover, rectosigmoid biopsy may miss severe GVHD in the terminal ileum, leading to 20–30% underdiagnosis [21]. Therefore, we investigated peripheral blood immune profiles to distinguish GVHD from viral infection and identify reliable biomarkers.

Bernardi C et al. [22] First used molecular imaging to non-invasively monitor GVHD, showing highly proliferative and activated T cells (CD3, CD4, CD8) with elevated co-stimulatory molecules (HLA-DR, OX40, 4-1BB, CD28), consistent with our findings. During the GVHD effector phase, activated T cells infiltrate target organs (skin, liver, gut) and mediate tissue injury via cytotoxic molecules (perforin, granzyme) and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, GM-CSF). CD8⁺ T cells are primary effectors in aGVHD [23, 24], while Tregs lose suppressive function [25]. In our cohort, GVHD patients had reduced circulating Tregs, elevated naive CD4⁺/CD4⁺ ratios, higher Th2 proportions, increased GVHD- and inflammation-associated cytokines (GM-CSF, TNFR1), and elevated gut injury marker Reg3α, indicating an effector T cell–driven process. Naive CD4⁺/CD4⁺% was a risk factor, whereas memory CD4⁺ T cells were not [26]. Upon initial alloantigen encounter, naive CD4⁺ T cells activate via TCR-MHC-II, release IL-2 and IFN-γ, promote Th1/Th17 differentiation, and sustain CD8⁺ effector responses, directly damaging host tissues. Th1/Th17 cells further drive M1 macrophage polarization via IFN-γ/IL-17, exacerbating aGVHD, while Th2 cells secrete IL-4/IL-13, promoting M2-mediated fibrosis in chronic GVHD [27, 28]. Both naive and Treg cells are therapeutic targets; selectively removing naive T cells while preserving memory T cells and Tregs reduces GVHD without impairing graft-versus-leukemia effects [29]. Notably, the lack of significant differences in absolute counts likely reflects high inter-patient variability and limited sample size, and does not diminish the biological relevance of our percentage-based findings, which directly capture immune composition and functional balance.

In GI complications, intestinal injury is a key feature. GVHD damages crypts and disrupts mucosal integrity, leading to the release of Paneth cell–derived Reg3α, whose serum levels correlate with GVHD severity [30, 31]. Persistent GVHD sustains macrophage activation through the GM-CSF/CSF-1R pathway, amplifying inflammatory responses [32]. GM-CSF, primarily derived from CD4 + T cells, has been identified as a central regulator of intestinal GVHD by linking adaptive and innate immunity through enhanced alloantigen presentation, making it a potential therapeutic target [33]. Elevated TNFR1 levels, strongly associated with TNF-α, are among the most robust biomarkers of aGVHD; notably, the TNFR1 ratio at day + 7 post-HSCT is an independent predictor [34, 35]. These findings align with our observations. Of note, ST2 levels did not differ significantly between the two groups, consistent with its established role as a marker of GVHD severity rather than early etiological distinction.

Reg3α is a well-established biomarker for distinguishing gastrointestinal GVHD from non-GVHD diarrhea [36]. The MAGIC algorithm, which incorporates ST2 and Reg3α, has been validated for predicting GVHD outcomes at multiple time points, including days 7, 14, 21, 28, and at GVHD diagnosis [37, 38]. In our cohort, Reg3α was significantly elevated in GI-GVHD patients prior to diarrhea onset, consistent with these findings. However, ST2 levels did not differ significantly between the GI-GVHD and viral enteritis groups. This does not contradict previous studies. Rather, it suggests that the utility of these biomarkers for etiological differentiation at first-onset diarrhea may differ from their established prognostic role in patients with confirmed GVHD. Thus, biomarker performance is context-dependent and may vary with clinical setting, comparator population, and transplant platform.

In contrast, viral infections after HSCT reflect states of immunosuppression or impaired immune reconstitution. It is noteworthy that while molecular diagnostics such as quantitative PCR for herpesviruses are central to managing post-HSCT viral infections [39], and metrics like CMV peak viral load provide valuable surrogates for disease risk [40], peripheral immune profiling, as employed in our study, offers a complementary approach by capturing the functional host response to these pathogens and to alloreactivity. Risk factors include ATG/ATLG-based conditioning, grade II–IV aGVHD, high-dose corticosteroids, and prolonged immunosuppressive prophylaxis, all of which delay immune recovery. Consistent with previous studies [41–44], we found that poor immune reconstitution, reduced CD8 + and CD4 + T cells, and increased immunosuppressive Tregs were associated with higher infection risk. However, NK cells emerged as key protective factors: sustained high percentages and absolute counts were strongly associated with lower infection risk. As critical components of innate immunity, NK cells play pivotal roles in early immune reconstitution and antiviral defense after HSCT [45]. Viral infections such as CMV are generally accompanied by NK cell maturation in the BM and/or donor graft, promoting NK maturation and expansion and leading to adaptive-like NK subsets with enhanced activity and improved disease-free survival [46]. Active viral infections (e.g., CMV, EBV) also trigger infected cells to release IL-15, IL-12, and type I interferons, which directly drive NK proliferation and activation [47].

CD38 is a well-recognized marker of T cell activation, and its expression on T lymphocytes has been linked to T cell activation during CMV and EBV infections [48]. CD38 + memory T cells can secrete IFN-γ to mediate antiviral responses upon CMV stimulation [49], which is consistent with our results. In our cohort, CD28 + CD8+ T cells showed slightly higher levels of CD38 expression in the infection group, potentially reflecting virus-driven metabolic activation. Conversely, absolute counts of CD8 + CD38+ T cells were higher in GVHD patients, which may be related to lower T cell counts or to stronger T cell activation in the infection group. Therefore, further validation in larger cohorts is warranted.

Based on clinical and univariate immune analyses, we developed predictive scoring models using immune parameters from both single and multiple time points. The multi–time-point model demonstrated greater stability in our cohort, with NK cells emerging as a consistent key discriminator between viral infection and GVHD [50], suggesting the potential value of dynamic immune monitoring for the early differentiation of GI‑GVHD from infection‑related diarrhea. These findings indicate that the single-time-point model could be considered for the differential diagnosis of diarrhea at onset, while the multi-time-point analysis serves as a proof-of-concept, highlighting how dynamic immune monitoring might provide added value for earlier risk assessment in the future. The high negative predictive value of the multi-timepoint model is particularly suitable for identifying patients at low risk of viral infection. We envision that this immune-based approach will serve as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, endoscopic biopsy, functioning as an initial risk stratification tool to guide the urgency of invasive assessment. Nevertheless, we acknowledge a selection bias inherent to our cohort as we included a higher proportion of patients with diarrhea occurring after week 5 post-HSCT to ensure sufficient data for the multi-time-point model. Hence, this model serves as an auxiliary tool for late-onset diarrhea (after week 5) and should be applied with caution to patients with earlier onset.

Our study has several limitations. The models were derived and internally validated using a retrospective, single‑center cohort of limited size. Excluding patients with concurrent infections to ensure etiological purity limits generalizability to real-world patients with mixed complications. The relatively low events‑per‑variable ratio remains a constraint, despite our use of LASSO regression, composite score construction, and 1000‑fold bootstrap resampling to mitigate overfitting. Consequently, the high AUC reported here reflects robust internal validation, whereas external generalizability requires confirmation. Furthermore, the sample size and event rate limited our ability to perform a multivariable survival analysis to identify independent prognostic factors. Additionally, as all patients received ATG-based conditioning, our findings may not be directly generalizable to HSCT settings without ATG exposure. Furthermore, the haplo-cord HSCT platform used in this study represents a regional practice that may further limit generalizability to centers employing different conditioning regimens. In addition, the observed incidence of EBV enteritis in our cohort may reflect the influence of ATG combined with letermovir prophylaxis, and future studies incorporating cohorts with different conditioning or GVHD prevention regimens are warranted to validate these findings. Furthermore, while all GVHD patients received systemic corticosteroids and some received second-line agents including basiliximab and ruxolitinib which may influence immune parameters, the balanced ATG exposure between groups and the consistent immune differences observed suggest our findings at least partially reflect intrinsic disease biology; future studies with treatment-stratified analyses are warranted to further disentangle disease effects from treatment effects. Moreover, our study is limited to first-onset diarrhea post-transplant; the model does not apply to new-onset diarrhea in GVHD patients already on immunosuppressive therapy. Lastly, our findings are based on a comparative analysis within a symptomatic cohort and are designed for etiological differentiation after diarrhea onset; they have not been benchmarked against a control population of transplant recipients without diarrhea. Future studies employing more sensitive detection methods in larger, multi‑center cohorts are warranted to further elucidate the complex interplay between GVHD and viral infections, to validate the generalizability of our predictive models, and to evaluate these immune biomarkers against a non‑diarrheal baseline.
