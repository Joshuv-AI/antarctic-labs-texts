Prevalence and correlates of post-traumatic stress disorder and depression among welfare- and justice-involved adolescents in Nigeria
Authors: Wasiu Olorunlambe, Sherifat Adeniyi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Childhood maltreatment (CM) is higher among welfare- and justice-involved youth than in those not involved in these systems, which increases the risk of depression and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). However, the mechanisms underlying the link between CM and these two psychiatric conditions are less well understood among at-risk populations in low- and middle-income countries.Objective: This study attempts to fill this gap by examining the prevalence of and risk factors for depression and PTSD among at-risk groups in Nigeria.Method: A cross-sectional research design using multistage sampling was adopted. The sample comprised 205 adolescents: justice-involved [102 (49.8%)] and welfare-involved [103 (50.2%)]. In total, 151 (73.7%) were males, while 54 (26.3%) were females. Multivariate logistic regression analysis and multivariate analysis of variance were applied.Results: The results showed that 66.1% of welfare-involved adolescents and 69.6% of justice-involved adolescents reported PTSD, while 68.9% of welfare-involved adolescents and 75.5% of justice-detained adolescents reported depression. Neglect (OR = 0.253; 95% CI 0.146–0.571; p < .001) and witnessing violence (OR = 0.230; 95% CI 0.114–0.597; p < .004) predicted depression. Emotional abuse (OR = 0.186; 95% CI 0.090–0.80; p < .015), witnessing violence (OR = 0.147; 95% CI 0.014–0.876; p < .043), neglect (OR = 0.187; 95% CI 0.14–0.90; p < .008), and physical abuse (OR = 0.27; 95% CI 0.254–0.937; p < .001) predicted PTSD. Also, PTSD significantly differed based on type of placement (F = 6.08, p < .014, η2 = .029), but depression did not differ based on type of placement (F = 2.46, p > .118, η2 = .012).Conclusions: CM profiles are risk factors in PTSD and depression among at-risk groups. CM screening should be included in mental health services of out-of-home placements to prevent the cycle of mental health problems and reoffending. Trauma-focused and cognitive–behavioural therapies have the potential to alleviate the suffering of traumatized adolescents.KEYWORDS: Childhood maltreatment, post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, justice- and welfare-involved, adolescents, Nigeria

Introduction

Adolescent psychopathology is a public health burden worldwide (World Health Organization [WHO], 2012). It remains a top policy priority of the World Health Organization (WHO, 2020) and policy makers across the world (Eckshtain et al., 2020). Closely connected to this is depression, which is the second major cause of childhood disability across the world (Korczak et al., 2023; WHO, 2022a). Depression has substantial long-term effects on children, and increases the risk of self-injury, suicide, mortality (Fitzpatrick et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2021), and criminal activities (Ozkan et al., 2019). A global estimate indicated that one in five adolescents is at risk of depression (Lu et al., 2024). It often emerges in adolescence and continues across the lifespan (Merritt et al., 2022; Morken et al., 2021). The diagnostic criteria of depression include ‘feelings of sadness, hopelessness, worthlessness, and excessive guilt’ (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013).

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a common problem among at-risk populations and is 12 times higher among justice-involved youth than community samples (Waserman et al., 2002). The symptoms of PTSD include ‘re-experiencing of traumatic incidents (e.g. nightmares, flashbacks); staying away from the events; changes in neural and behavioural responses; and being extremely vigilant’ [Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) (APA, 2013). Both PTSD and depression tend to have similar aetiology and consequences, including changes in cognition and mood (Price & van Stolk-Cooke, 2015; Watson, 2009), and they are often comorbid with substance use disorder and suicidal deaths (Nooner et al., 2012). Thus, understanding the underlying causes of depression and PTSD during adolescence is important, given that the mental health disorders developed at this sensitive stage tend to be severe (Dunn et al., 2017), are likely to disrupt brain functioning (M. Zhao et al., 2020), and are likely to continue until adulthood (Hankin & Griffith, 2023; Zisook et al., 2007).

A global epidemiological survey reported lifetime prevalence rates of mental ill-health among justice-involved adolescents of 66.8% for boys and 81% for girls (Borschmann et al., 2020). Similarly, a meta-analysis comprising 28,033 detained males and 4754 detained females indicated that one in 10 of the male juvenile offenders had higher odds of depression and one in four of the female offenders had higher odds of depression. Besides, one in five of them had higher odds of PTSD (Beaudry et al., 2020). Conversely, meta-analytic studies (Cummings & Shelton, 2024; Dubois-Comtois et al., 2021; Engler et al., 2022) reported rates between 1% and 82% of PTSD and depression among adolescents in residential care homes.

Child maltreatment (CM) is a public health burden, and one in four adolescents is at risk worldwide (Carr et al., 2020; Stoltenborgh et al., 2015; WHO, 2020). CM profiles often overlap and are broadly related to virtually all types of mental health problems (McLaughlin et al., 2020). CM includes physical, sexual, and emotional abuse, as well as neglect, to which a child has been exposed prior to 18 years of age (WHO, 2022b). CM which usually involves multiple incidents (i.e. poly-victimization) (Finkelhor et al., 2007a), and is highly prevalent among justice-involved (Baglivio et al., 2014; Baidawi, 2019; Catia et al., 2022; Ford et al., 2013) and welfare-involved adolescents (Greeno et al., 2019). Furthermore, CM and mental health disorders remain the underlying reasons why children are placed in residential care (Jäggi et al., 2021). They can lead to poor functional outcomes and have the potential to undermine adolescents’ normal development across the life course (Cicchetti & Toth, 2016; Gonzalez-Garcia et al., 2023). Different CM patterns are associated with unique behavioural health outcomes. For example, children who have been exposed to parental neglect, sexual abuse, and emotional abuse are at a higher risk of depression (Cohen et al., 2019). In contrast, physical abuse is linked to both internalizing and externalizing symptoms (Yoon et al., 2016). However, the mechanisms underlying CM-induced psychopathology are less understood, particularly among high-risk adolescents in low- and middle-income countries (Uppendahl et al., 2020), since not all CM patterns are associated with poor clinical outcomes (Finkelhor et al., 2015). This study, therefore, filled this gap by examining the association between CM profiles and mental health problems among two at-risk groups in Nigeria.

Some models can be used to explain the link between CM and psychopathology. One of these is the developmental psychopathology framework (Cicchetti & Rogosch, 2002). This model argues that exposure to CM increases the likelihood of depression and anxiety (Duprey et al., 2021). In addition, children who have been exposed to CM at the early stage of life are likely to face discrimination in the domains of education, job prospects, housing, and income later in life (Gypen et al., 2017). They are twice as likely to experience comorbid PTSD and depression, leading to more severe clinical outcomes (Flory & Yehuda, 2015; Gardner et al., 2019). This is why ending CM is now at the top of the agenda of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target 16.2 (United Nations [UN], 2015).

Residential placement represents a significant event in a child’s life because the child is moved to a strange environment with new caregivers and friends (Engler et al., 2022; Ferreira et al., 2020; Magalhães et al., 2018). In this condition, children’s vulnerability increases substantially owing to the combination of prior and recurrent maltreatment (Magalhães et al., 2018). In addition, children living in residential placements are at heightened risk of suicide attempts and premature mortality (Briere, Madni & Dietrich, 2016; Briere, Madni & Godbout, 2016; Okpych & Courtney, 2018), bipolar disorders (von Borczyskowski et al., 2013), adjustment problems (Correira-Santos et al., 2023), and developmental disorders (Baidawi, 2020) (Briere, Madni & Dietrich, 2016; Briere, Madni & Godbout, 2016). They also present complex symptoms outside the diagnostic scope of DSM-IV and International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision ICD-10, such as insecure relationships, attachment problems, defiant behaviour, trauma-induced anxiety, and hyperactivity (Gonzalez-Garcia et al., 2023).

Adolescents in contact with the juvenile justice system are generally regarded as a high-risk group. In Nigeria, such children are placed under the custody of the Nigerian Correctional Institution, otherwise known as the Borstal Institution. Borstal Institutions are saddled with the statutory responsibility to reform, rehabilitate, and reintegrate justice-involved adolescents. However, the institutions are bedevilled by structural problems, including inadequate funding, understaffing, and overcrowding, which undermine their abilities to provide effective mental health services (Atilola et al., 2019). In such facilities, the quality of food intake, conditions of the facilities, poor sleeping patterns, separation from friends and romantic partners, prolonged detention, and delayed court processes may increase the risk of PTSD and depression, and also undermine children’s developmental trajectories (Abram et al., 2017). Being arrested by the police, handcuffed, prosecuted, transported in a police vehicle, and regularly monitored by warders of the Juvenile Correctional Institution will be mentally damaging to children and can potentially increase their risk of PTSD and depression (Jackson et al., 2019; Jahn et al., 2021). Similarly, in Nigeria, justice-involved adolescents are subjected to regular beatings and will have been detained and tortured in police cells before being transferred to correctional institutions. These experiences can heighten the risk of depression and PTSD.

Family adversities have been shown to be potent risk factors in poor mental health and developmental outcomes among children (Leijdesdorff et al., 2017; Schroeder et al., 2020). Children are typically placed in residential care as a result of parental neglect and family dysfunction (Font & Gershoff, 2020; Schoenwald et al., 2022). Among family factors, domestic violence seems to exert greater impacts on the children, particularly if the violence is persistent (Lin et al., 2021). This is rooted in the family stress perspective, which posits that family stressors increase mental symptoms (Duncan et al., 2017). Moreover, emerging evidence has pointed out that children with a family history of depression have higher odds of exhibiting psychiatric symptoms (Weissman et al., 2016). In addition, children who have seen their parents being arrested and jailed are twice as likely to be traumatized (Arditt & Savla, 2015) and more likely to experience poor parental care, insecure attachment, and stigmatization (Murray & Murray, 2010). Similarly, the loss of a parent or caregiver is a traumatic event in a child’s life and is associated with poor mental health, including depression, PTSD, and anxiety (Atwine et al., 2004; Hughes et al., 2017). Overall, adolescents raised in violent neighbourhoods, who have seen their parents argue, shout, and/or fight, or who have seen someone being shot or killed are at higher risk of being traumatized (Bomysoad & Francis, 2020).

In view of the foregoing, the objectives of this study were: (1) to investigate the prevalence rates of depression and PTSD among two at-risk groups; (2) to explore the association between CM and depression and PTSD; and (3) to examine differences in mental health disorders based on types of placement (juvenile detention and residential placement).

Method

Sample

A cross-sectional approach was adopted, and welfare-involved (n = 103) and justice-involved (n = 102) adolescents were randomly selected. The overall sample size was 205, which was sufficient to ensure a power of 0.80, and to detect effect sizes in multivariate logistic regression analysis and multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), based on power analysis in G* Power software and Monte Carlo simulation (Tekle et al., 2016).

Multistage sampling was applied. Two samples of at-risk adolescents aged 10–17 years were selected in Southwest Nigeria – a regional representative sample. In the first stage, the Southwest geopolitical zone was selected. In the second stage, three states were randomly selected. In the third stage, local government areas were randomly selected. In the fourth stage, 10 registered residential care homes were randomly selected, and 10 or 11 adolescents were randomly selected from each residential care home.

In Nigeria, adolescents involved in the child protection services (abandoned, rescued, homeless) are placed under the care of registered civil society organizations, and they are registered with the Ministry of Women Affairs and must abide by the provisions of Child Rights Acts of the state.

The inclusion criteria for this study were: (1) adolescents 10–17 years old; this conforms with the World Health Organization (1986); (2) being currently in the custody of the Nigerian Juvenile Justice System; (3) living in residential homes for at least 6 months; (4) fluency in either English or an indigenous language; and (5) being in juvenile detention for not less than 24–72 hours before the survey was conducted.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) age > 18 years; and (2) adolescents with mental retardation or (3) psychotic conditions that could prevent them from understanding the contents of the survey materials; and/or (4) refusal to sign the assent form.

Procedures

The study protocols were approved by the ethics committee of the University College Hospital (UCH) (UI/EC/23/0677) Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria. The study’s protocols adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1964. The privacy and confidentiality of the respondents were guaranteed. The interviews were conducted in private sessions by enumerators who were experienced in the field of trauma and victimization. Copies of the questionnaires were anonymized, and the assessment was gender matched. Prior to the fieldwork, a one-week intensive training course was organized for the enumerators on scientific techniques of data collection. It took approximately 75 minutes to complete a battery of questionnaires. Each participant was offered one Euro (700 Naira) for completing the survey questionnaire. This study is part of a larger survey, and the other variables measured have been reported elsewhere (Olorunlambe & Adeniyi, 2025).

Regarding justice-involved adolescents, all respondents signed an assent form, and their legal guardians signed an informed consent form prior to the survey. This is consistent with previous studies (Baglivio et al., 2014; Teplin et al., 2002). The time that an adolescent spends in juvenile detention is determined by the type of offence committed and the court processes. Those who are convicted are aware of the time at which they will be released, whereas those awaiting trial are not, and those who are convicted of murder will stay in detention for a longer period. The participants in this study had spent a minimum of 6 months in detention. Based on the Children and Young Persons Act II, ‘children in conflict with the law’ refers to justice-involved children who have committed crimes; ‘children in need of care and protection’ refers to welfare-involved children, i.e. those who have been neglected by their parents/guardians; and ‘children beyond parental control’ refers to children taken to the juvenile justice system by their parents or guardians (Bella et al., 2010). For the adolescents in residential care, written informed consent was obtained from founders or coordinators of each residential care home before the survey.

A total of 17 participants showed marked physical and emotional trauma. They were referred to counselling units within the Juvenile Justice System and residential homes. The survey was conducted between December 2023 and April 2024, and a 94% response rate was achieved.

Measures

Covariates such as age, gender, religion, ethnicity, parental status, parents’ education, and parents’ occupation were self-reported. ‘Parental incarceration’ was measured using a single item adapted from Hiolski et al. (2019): ‘Have any of your parents or guardians ever been in jail or prison?’

Child and Adolescent Trauma Screen (CATS)

CATS (Sachser et al., 2017) is a 20-item self-report scale that assesses the symptom prevalence of traumatic experiences and PTSD in adolescents, based on DSM-5 and ICD-11 guidelines. A cut-off point of ≥ 15 was used, which is consistent with Suliman et al. (2005). The dimensions of CATS include re-experiencing (five questions), avoidance (two questions), negative alteration in cognition and mood (seven questions), and hyperarousal (six questions). Some of the items are ‘bad dreams reminding you of what happened’ and ‘Upsetting thoughts or pictures about what happened that pop into your head’. The scale is rated thus: 0 ‘(never)’, 1 ‘(once in a while)’, 2 ‘(half the time)’, and 3 ‘(almost always)’. Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was α = 0.86.

ISPCAN Child Abuse Screening Tool Child version (ICAST-C)

Child maltreatment was measured using ICAST-C (Zolotor et al., 2009). This is a self-administered instrument comprising 35 indicators. The scoring pattern ranges from ‘never’, ‘many times’, ‘not in the past year but this has happened’, to ‘sometimes’. Its dimensions include emotional abuse, physical abuse, witnessing violence, parental neglect, and sexual abuse. The items include: ‘locked you out of the home for a long time’ and ‘pushed, grabbed, or kicked you’. Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was α = 0.88.

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

Depression was assessed using PHQ-9 (Kroenke et al., 2001). This is a nine-item self-report scale which assesses depression as well as symptoms and severity. Responses range from 0 ‘(not at all)’ to 4 ‘(nearly every day)’. A cut-off point of ≥ 13 was adopted in this study. PHQ-9 is based on DSM-IV diagnostic criteria. The items include: ‘feeling tired or having little energy’ and ‘trouble falling or staying asleep, or sleeping too much’. Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was α = 0.78.

Statistical analyses

The data were analysed using SPSS IBM version 29.0. Frequencies and percentages were computed, and multivariate logistic regression analysis and multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) were performed.

Results

The data showed that 66.1% of the welfare-involved youth reported PTSD, while 69.6% of the justice-detained adolescents reported PTSD. Also, 68.9% of the welfare-involved adolescents reported depression, while 75.5% of the justice-detained adolescents reported depression. Neglect (OR = 0.253; 95% CI 0.146–0.571; p < .001) and witnessing violence (OR = 0.230; 95% CI 0.114–0.597; p < .004) both predicted depression. Emotional abuse (OR = 0.186; 95% CI 0.090–0.80; p < .015), witnessing violence (OR = 0.147; 95% CI 0.014–0.876; p < .043), neglect (OR = 0.187; 95% CI 0.14–0.90; p < .008), and physical abuse (OR = 0.27; 95% CI 0.254–0.937; p < .001) predicted PTSD. The MANOVA tests showed a significant difference in the dependent variables between the two types of placement (Wilk’s lambda F = 3.07, p < .001, η2 = .030). In addition, PTSD differed significantly based on type of placement (F = 6.08, p < .014, η2 = .029), whereas depression did not differ based on type of placement (F = 2.46, p > .118, η2 = .012).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the risk factors for PTSD and depression among welfare-involved and justice-involved adolescents in Nigeria. The results showed that 66.1% of the welfare-involved adolescents reported PTSD, while 69.6% of the justice-detained adolescents reported PTSD. Also, 68.9% of the welfare-involved adolescents reported depression, while 75.5% of the justice-involved adolescents reported depression. These rates are considerably higher than in a previous study in Nigeria (Atilola, 2012). These rates are also substantially higher than those found in meta-analyses (Beaudry et al., 2020; Carr et al., 2020; Fazel et al., 2008) among justice-involved adolescents (Rosenberg et al., 2014) and general populations (Barican et al., 2022). The study is comparable with previous studies conducted in high-income countries, notably Spain (Gonzalez-Garcia et al., 2023), the USA (Bronsard et al., 2011), and Germany (Jozefiak et al., 2016), which reported prevalence level of 40–88% for PTSD among welfare-involved adolescents.

Moreover, the results showed that physical abuse, witnessing violence, emotional abuse, and neglect were associated with PTSD. This claim is supported by a large body of studies (Dorahy et al., 2009; Fazel et al., 2008; LeMoult et al., 2020; Lewis et al., 2021; McLaughlin et al., 2021; Malvaso et al., 2021; Mozley et al., 2018; Vizard et al., 2022; Yohros, 2022), which confirmed that adolescents exposed to childhood adversities were at higher risk of developing many psychiatric disorders. Neglect has also been found to be associated with impaired regulation of emotions in children (Kim & Cicchetti, 2010; Konijn et al., 2019). These findings are supported by a meta-analysis (da Silva et al., 2024), which reaffirmed the relationship between CM profiles and PTSD. They also align with previous cross-sectional studies (Carrion & Steiner, 2000), which affirmed that PTSD is highly prevalent among at-risk populations. There is also evidence to suggest that CM increases the risk of transdiagnostic psychopathology (Dalgleish et al., 2020; McLaughlin et al., 2020). From the foregoing, it is apparent that CM is a risk factor in mental health disorders, which could persist into adulthood (Giarrantano et al., 2020).

Moreover, the results revealed that CM subtypes (witnessing violence and neglect) were associated with depression. This claim is consistent with reports by Cohen et al. (2019), Lippard and Nemeroff (2020), and Wang et al. (2022), who found that detained adolescents who had been exposed to parental neglect were three times more likely to develop depression. It is also in agreement with previous meta-analyses (Li et al., 2016; Van Vugt et al., 2014), which reported that neglect and witnessing violence increased the risk of mental health disorder among at-risk populations. This finding is supported by past in high-income countries (Humphreys et al., 2020; Mayer et al., 2021; Wrobel et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021), which found an association between CM and depression. It also aligns with previous studies in the Nigerian context (Atilola et al., 2014; Kuranga, 2013), which found between prevalences of PTSD of 82.5−90% among justice-involved adolescents. It is well supported by a Kenyan study (Maru et al., 2003), which reported depressive symptoms in 44.4% of justice-involved adolescents. It has been noted that adolescents with a long history of parental neglect are at greater risk of poor psychosocial outcomes (Goodman et al., 2011). Adolescents who had been exposed to parental neglect were found to be less likely to recognize and separate various types of emotions (Pollak et al., 2000). If left untreated, CM can have a substantial detrimental impact on adolescents’ well-being across the entire lifespan (Briggs et al., 2012; Widom et al., 2008).

Maltreated children tend to have negative views about themselves and surrounding events, which could trigger depressive symptoms (Zhang et al., 2022). They tend to have problems with self-concept, are pessimistic in nature (Yang & Lu, 2022), and, compared with children who have not been maltreated, are twice as likely to experience sleep problems (Laskemoen et al., 2021), which are a precursor to depression (Jiang et al., 2020). Depression and PTSD are often comorbid, particularly among children who have been maltreated (Gardner et al., 2019).

The above results are not surprising in view of the fact that adolescents in detention endure long periods of confinement and facilities-specific characteristics. The common sources of worry are the solitary lifestyle, prolonged incarceration, and delayed court verdicts. The cultural and social stigma associated with living in out-of-home facilities makes the experience more devastating for the children even after they are reunited with their families. More importantly, their experiences with the juvenile justice system and the child protection welfare system usually disrupt their educational trajectory and career prospects. A large proportion of justice-involved youth often relocates to other communities after being released, to avoid social and cultural stigma. Although vocation-tailored rehabilitation is currently in place, it is unclear how efficacious this programme has been in alleviating the conditions of victimized adolescents.

Furthermore, the adolescents in juvenile detention had significantly higher prevalences of both depression and PTSD than their counterparts in residential care homes. The reasons for this difference are not hard to find. Justice-detained adolescents may present clinical symptoms of depression owing to their precarious conditions. They are usually worried about their prolonged detention and delays in judgement, and they are extremely anxious and worried about sentencing, particularly for murder and defilement cases. Besides, they do not enjoy the same level of freedom as their counterparts in residential care.

Given the fact that depression has become a major cause of health-related disability among children around the world (World Health Organization, 2017) and a risk factor in global disease burden and suicide, addressing depression among at-risk populations is crucial for the smooth transition to adulthood (Korczak et al., 2023). Having contact with a juvenile justice system has been shown to be a factor in impaired development and quality of life (Dmitrieva et al., 2012). Compared with adolescents in residential homes, a larger proportion of justice-involved adolescents have been completely separated from their friends and social functions. They seldom talk to their parents on the phone, and they are not allowed to have money in hand, so they cannot buy things at will. These factors increase their vulnerability to poor behavioural health outcomes.

Untreated depression increases the risk of substance use, suicidal thoughts, and suicide attempts. Since suicidal ideation is more common among at-risk populations and depression is a risk factor, addressing the latter is critical for reducing the former (Teplin et al., 2002, 2021). Untreated PTSD is also associated with sleep problems, attention deficits, poor learning outcomes (Lehmann et al., 2020), suicidal thoughts (Tarrier & Gregg, 2004), and increased alcohol consumption (McCauley et al., 2012). Interventions aimed at preventing PTSD and depression among at-risk populations should focus on CM because of the likelihood of revictimization later in life (Walker et al., 2019). In the light of the foregoing, this study has made novel and original contributions to the existing literature on the impacts of CM profiles on PTSD and depression among vulnerable groups in low- and middle-income countries.

Limitations of the study

This study provides important insights into the risk factors for depression and PTSD among vulnerable populations in sub-Saharan Africa. However, there are several limitations. First, the study was not allowed to be conducted in Lagos State owing to stringent administrative protocols. This challenge limited the sample size and thus affected sampling validity. Secondly, the study relied on self-report, which is prone to recall bias. Therefore, future studies should consider using structured clinical interviews. Thirdly, the study was cross-sectional. As a result, it was not possible to determine cause-and-effect relationships among the variables of the study. Fourthly, the sample sizes for both groups were relatively small, which could affect the effect size. Finally, females were underrepresented in the study, especially in the justice-involved sample. In view of this, future studies should ensure more female representation.

Conclusion

Despite the high prevalence of mental disorders among at-risk populations, and this being one of the core policy objectives of the UN SDGs, the phenomenon is underinvestigated in low- and middle-income countries. The current study addressed this knowledge gap. The justice-involved adolescents reported significantly higher prevalences of PTSD and depression than their peers in residential care homes. Neglect and witnessing violence were associated with depression. The findings underscore the influence of physical abuse, emotional abuse, witnessing violence, and neglect on PTSD. The implication of these findings is that the experience of a wide array of mental health disorders during adolescence may disrupt the transition into adulthood, if left untreated. Since CM undermines normative development and adaptation, intervention during adolescence will enhance a healthy transition into adulthood.

The study highlights the need to prioritize the mental health of adolescents in out-of-home placements by relevant authorities. This is crucial because mental health services are grossly inadequate in Nigerian Correctional Services and residential care homes. Trauma-focused and cognitive–behavioural therapies have the potential to alleviate the suffering of traumatized adolescents.
