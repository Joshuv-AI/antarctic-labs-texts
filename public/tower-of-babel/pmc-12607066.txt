The impact of digital stress on suicide ideation among medical postgraduates: the chain mediating role of emotion regulation and meaning in life
Authors: RanRan Diao, JianHong Yao, Jian Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuicide is a serious social problem. This study aimed to explore the mediating effects of emotion regulation and sense of meaning in life on stress perception and suicidal ideation of medical graduate students. A survey was conducted among 546 medical postgraduates using the digital stress scale, the emotion regulation scale, the sense of meaning in life scale and the suicidal ideation scale. The results showed that: (1) digital stress was significantly positively correlated with emotion regulation, sense of meaning in life and suicidal ideation; (2) The mediating effect of sense of meaning in life between digital stress and suicidal ideation is significant; (3) Emotion regulation and sense of meaning in life have chain mediating effects between digital stress and suicidal ideation in medical graduate students. This study proposed Implications for the underlying mechanisms by which digital stress affects suicidal ideation.Keywords: Digital stress, Suicide ideation, Emotional regulation, Meaning in life, Chain mediating role

Introduction

The mental health problems of medical postgraduates, who are considered the backbone of the future medical system, have garnered a lot of attention due to long-standing pressures of high-intensity academic study, clinical practice, and career development uncertainties. According to a survey conducted in recent years, the detection rate of suicide ideation in this group has increased to 17.3%−24.6% [11]. There is a significant correlation between the occupation-specific psychological crisis and the stress ecology unique to the medical education stage. On the one hand, medical postgraduates need to undertake an average of 58 h of high-intensity clinical training every week and the frequent contact with doctor-patient conflicts [44].On the other hand, with the acceleration of the digital transformation of medical education, the widespread application of digital technologies such as electronic medical record systems [2], virtual simulation surgical platforms, and remote consultation tools is giving rise to new digital stressors, posing new challenges to the mental health of medical students. Medical postgraduates generally face technical dependence brought about by the “always online” state, typically manifested as the need to continuously process electronic medical record systems 24 h a day [45]. The invisible stress generated by this continuous exposure may ultimately induce suicide risk by destroying work-life boundaries, aggravating emotional disorders and lack of meaning [22].

Emotion regulation may play a significant mediating role in the stress-suicide pathway. Individual psychological adaptation results are greatly impacted by the distinct regulation of unpleasant emotions by two types of methods, such as expressive suppression and cognitive re-evaluation, according to Gross’s emotion regulation process model [15]. The emotion regulation strategy they adopt determines how much individuals convert threatening environmental stimuli into actual stress perception [12]. Additionally, the expressive suppression of emotion regulation plays an important role in the overall difference in stress perception [25]. Value theory believes that people develop their life significance through value realization, experience, and how to react to suffering [39]. Medical postgraduates often encounter life-and-death situations in their clinical work, providing a unique opportunity for the systematic reconstruction of their meaning in life [26]. However, there are currently no explanations of how emotion regulation strategies can regulate suicide risk by influencing meaning systems and no empirical tests on the chain conduction pathway of “stress perception → emotion regulation strategy → meaning in life → suicide ideation.”

In the context of resource conservation theory, individuals possess a fundamental motivation to acquire, retain, and protect their valuable resources (e.g. time, energy, positive emotions, and a sense of meaning). When confronted with stressors that threaten potential or actual resource depletion, individuals experience intense psychological stress [19]. Digital stress is a classic example of a chronic stressor that is known to deplete individuals' cognitive and emotional resources through information overload and technological intrusion [1]. As a primary coping strategy against digital stress, emotion regulation directly influences subsequent resource chains through its selection of either depletive expression suppression or gainful cognitive reappraisal. When facing threats to their resources, individuals first employ emotion regulation strategies to manage their emotional resources. Differences in these strategies lead to gains or losses in the core psychological resource of a sense of meaning in life [43]. The loss or gain of meaning is a key factor in the development of psychological distress (such as suicidal ideation) [7]. This chained pathway “stressor → resource management strategy → core resource → psychological outcome”provides a profound theoretical perspective for elucidating the mechanisms underlying digital stress.

Digital stress refers to a multifaceted psychological burden that people experience as a result of their overuse or dependence on digital technology, covering core dimensions such as information overload, online social comparison anxiety, and technological invasion [47].Digital stress has been linked to a number of emotional disorders [38], social isolation, and self-evaluation systems [1]. It has also been proven to have a strong correlation with suicide ideation [52]. In clinical training and scientific research, medical postgraduates, due to their professional characteristics, need to frequently use electronic medical record systems, medical databases, and remote consultation platforms. They also need to face the unique stressors of techno-invasion and indistinct work-life boundaries that arise from constantly connecting to work-related information technologies [16, 30, 33]. Empirical research confirms that in professional groups such as medical staff and teachers who require frequent use of digital technology, technology invasion is significantly positively correlated with suicide ideation [48]. The degree of technology invasion during the baseline period can predict the growth of suicide ideation after six months, according to a longitudinal study of medical students [36]. Therefore, we put forth hypothesis 1: suicide ideation can be positively predicted by digital stress.

The process model of emotion regulation highlights the differentiated effects of cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression on psychological adaptation [13]. Gross pointed out that digital overload, including information overload and multitasking, consumes a lot of cognitive resources. In this case, individuals may rely more on expressive suppression strategies rather than more efficient adaptive emotion regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal [15].Digital stress indirectly affects suicide ideation through the mediation of inhibitory strategies, while cognitive reappraisal boasts notable buffering benefits [27]. Numerous studies have shown that the selection of emotion regulation strategies is significantly impacted by digital stress. On the one hand, digital overload depletes cognitive resources and impairs the capacity for active reappraisal [37]. On the other hand, the “professional calm” culture emphasized in medical education encourages students to suppress negative emotions caused by clinical operational errors or patient death [18, 53]. Therefore, it makes them inhibit their emotional expression habitually [21]. Therefore, we propose hypothesis 2: suicide ideation and digital stress are mediated by emotion regulation.

The sense of meaning in life refers to the individual’s perception of the purpose and significance of his own existence [40]. For medical postgraduates, the sense of meaning in life is frequently closely linked to their sense of professional mission—curing diseases and saving lives [6]. However, there is a double threat under the digital stress. Firstly, electronic medical record systems and automated diagnostic tools reduce direct dialogue between doctors and patients, weakening the immediate sense of accomplishment from “treating diseases and saving lives” [35, 46]. Secondly, technical mistakes increase the workload of medical personnel and may cause self-questioning ethical dilemmas that cause them to lose the sense of meaning and, consequently, trigger existential reflection [11]. Research shows high-sense people often view stressful events as “coordinated challenges” and use goal-oriented behaviors to lessen their feelings of hopelessness [24]. As a result, hypothesis 3 is proposed: Meaning in life acts as a mediator between digital stress and suicide ideation.

According to the resource preservation theory proposed by Hobfoll [19], the choice of emotion regulation strategies will affect the consumption and accumulation of psychological resources. When an individual uses expressive suppression in non-adaptive regulation, his negative emotions may not be effectively relieved. This may result in the continuous consumption of psychological resources and weaken the sense of meaning in life. If cognitive reappraisal in adaptive regulation is employed, the individual may enhance the sense of meaning in life by reconstructing the meaning of stress. Individuals with a high sense of life are less likely to suicide because they see stress as a controllable challenge rather than a threat [54]. Based on this, we propose hypothesis 4: Emotional regulation and meaning in life have a chain mediating role in the relationship between digital stress and suicide ideation.

Method

Participants

Convenience sampling was used to conduct a questionnaire survey among 622 clinical medical graduate students in three universities in Jiangxi Province, China, from October to December 2024. The cohort is currently engaged in a three-year programme of standardised residency training, complemented by a master's degree programme. As a group of graduate students dedicated to clinical work within China's medical education system, they exhibit a highly concentrated age distribution (typically between 24 and 26 years old) and face unique clinical digital stressors. After removing the invalid questionnaires, a total of 546 valid questionnaires were obtained, with an effective response rate of 87.8%. In the sample, the participants’ average age was 25.00 ± 0.85, with 56.2% males and 43.8% females.

Measures

Digital stress

This study employs the Chinese adaptation of the Digital Stress Scale originally developed by Hall et al. [17] and subsequently revised by Jiang et al. [22]. With 31 items on a 5-point scale (1 being never and 5 being always), the scale includes six dimensions: availability stress, approval anxiety, social comparison, fear of missing out, connection overload, and online vigilance. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale is 0.87, X2/df = 2.098, RMSEA = 0.070, GFI = 0.911, IFI = 0.954, NFI = 0.916, TLI = 0.945, and CFI = 0.954. It shows that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Suicide ideation

The Positive and Negative Suicide Ideation Scale (PANSI), developed by Osman et al. [29], has been tested to be applicable in the Chinese context [49]. The active suicide ideation part of the 14-item scale is scored in reverse. Each item adopts a five-point scoring system, ranging from 1 for “never been like this” to 5 for “always like this,” for a total score of 0–70 points. The higher the total score, the stronger the suicide ideation. In the study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.87, X2/df = 2.098, RMSEA = 0.070, GFI = 0.911, IFI = 0.954, NFI = 0.916, TLI = 0.945, and CFI = 0.954. It shows that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Emotion regulation

The Chinese version of the Emotion Regulation Scale, originally developed by Gross [14] and subsequently revised by Chen et al. [5], was used to measure individuals’ emotion regulation. The 10-item scale includes two dimensions: cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression. A sample item is “ I won’t reveal my emotions.” It is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Very inconsistent, 7 = Very suitable). The higher the score, the more strongly the individual tends to adopt the corresponding strategy. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.758, X2/df = 2.496, RMSEA = 0.082, GFI = 0.93, IFI = 0.941, NFI = 0.906, TLI = 0.922, and CFI = 0.941. It shows that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Meaning in life

The Sense of Meaning in Life Scale developed by Steger et al. [41] and revised by Wang et al. [50] was used to measure the level of meaning in life. It consists of 10 items on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = completely inconsistent, 7 = completely consistent). The higher the score, the stronger the individual’s sense of life. In this study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.85, X2/df = 2.697, RMSEA = 0.088, GFI = 0.922, IFI = 0.926, NFI = 0.887, TLI = 0.900, and CFI = 0.925. It shows that the scale has good reliability and validity.

Study sample size

Boomsma & Hoogland [3]recommend a minimum sample size of 200 for simple models and at least 400 for more complex models. For the majority of models, a sample size of N ≥ 200 is adequate [32]. In general, larger samples are favoured when considering estimation methods, model complexity, and data characteristics. In the present study, a sample size of 546 was deemed appropriate and sufficient. In order to ascertain the study's adequate sample size for the detection of meaningful effects, a preliminary power analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 software at α = 0.05 and statistical power of 0.8. The findings of the study indicated that the current sample size achieved a statistical test power of 0.875, thereby exceeding the established benchmark value of 0.80. This finding serves to substantiate the adequacy of the sample size, thereby confirming its sufficiency to discern the effects embedded within the model.

Data analysis

The IBM SPSS 26.0 was utilized to conduct a comprehensive series of data analyses. Initially, this research utilized descriptive statistics to overview the fundamental characteristics of the data. Subsequently, it performed correlation analyses to explore potential relationships among variables. Then, the regression analyses in this study aimed at assessing the direct influence of independent variables, such as digital stress, on suicide ideation. Finally, it employed Model 6 from Hayes’s PROCESS macro to analyze the chain mediation effects.

Common method variance

This study used Harman’s single-factor test to assess common method variance. The findings revealed that the eight principal components have eigenvalues greater than 1, and the cumulative variance contribution rate reached 61.23%. Notably, the first principal component accounted for 24.52% of the variance, which is below the critical threshold of 40% [55],suggesting that the common method variance’s effect was within a reasonable range.

Discussion

Digital stress is directly associated with the level of suicide ideation among medical postgraduates

The extant research findings confirm that digital stress among medical graduate students has a significant and positive effect on suicidal ideation, Hypothesis 1 was fully supported, which is consistent with previous research [31]. Digital stress is defined as a chronic stressor which operates through two mechanisms. Firstly, it`s technological intrusion. When resource depletion exceeds an individual's compensatory threshold, it triggers a vicious cycle of “resource depletion-psychological distress” [34]. Secondly, it`s information overload. The forced multitasking mode has been shown to significantly intensify cognitive load, resulting in the cumulative effect of "perceived burden" [28]. These mechanisms continuously deplete cognitive resources. This dual-action mechanism has been shown to have a pronounced amplification effect among medical students. The high degree of technological dependency inherent in their profession serves to compound the impact of digital stress, thereby engendering a heightened risk of mental health impairment when compared with traditional academic pressures [10].

The statistical mediation through cognitive reappraisal, and expressive suppression

The results of this study show that emotional regulation is the intermediary variable between digital stress and suicidal ideation. This association was characterized by partially a positive pathway through expressive suppression and a partially negative pathway through cognitive reappraisal, supporting hypothesis 2. The selection of different emotion regulation strategies plays distinctly divergent roles in suicidal ideation within the context of digital stress, and serves as a key mediating factor influencing stress outcomes. In circumstances involving numerical stress, individuals who habitually engage in expressive suppression expend sustained mental effort to maintain this suppression, which in turn accelerates resource depletion [20]. This phenomenon has been shown to directly intensify feelings of distress and to undermine higher-order resources, such as the meaning in life, which require cognitive and emotional resources for their maintenance. The significant negative mediating effect of cognitive reappraisal indicates that by actively reappraising stressful events, individuals can transform threats into challenges. It′s an effective resource investment strategy and assists individuals in viewing stressful events in a more positive manner, thereby reducing negative emotions [42]. This approach not only prevents the unnecessary depletion of resources but also has the potential to achieve resource gain by creating new meaning (for example, by viewing digital stress as the cost of skill enhancement) [51], thereby reducing suicide ideation.

The statistical mediation through meaning in life

The association between digital stress and suicide ideation was mediated by meaning in life, consistent with hypothesis 3. Digital stress can trigger goal-oriented behaviors when medical postgraduates view academic stress and clinical challenges as essential to their professional development rather than as dangers [4]. For example, when medical students complete difficult surgeries or research projects, their sense of accomplishment may strengthen their professional significance in “treating diseases and saving lives” [42], forming a virtuous transformation path of “pressure-growth.” The sense of meaning in life is strengthened during this process and serves as a valuable psychological resource to resist suicide ideation.

Chain mediating effect

The findings of this study indicate that emotional regulation plays a crucial mediating role between digital stress and suicidal ideation, and this effect is further extended through a chain mediation mechanism involving life meaning. Hypotheses 4 have been proven to be valid. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that expressive suppression and cognitive reappraisal mediate the path from digital stress to suicidal ideation in positive and negative directions, respectively. Furthermore, it has been shown that these phenomena jointly form two distinct chain-mediated pathways with life meaning. The following sequence of relationships has been identified: "digital stress → expressive suppression → life meaning → suicidal ideation" and "digital stress → cognitive reappraisal → life meaning → suicidal ideation."

The study shows that digital stress predicts suicide ideation through cognitive reappraisal and meaning in life, and there is a chain mediation. When it comes to digital stress, people who are highly capable of cognitive reappraisal often view stress as a growth opportunity (such as considering heavy online learning tasks a way to improve skills), thereby enhancing the sense of meaning in life and suppressing suicide ideation. The sense construction theory believes that individuals can activate internal psychological resources such as hope and purpose by giving positive meaning to stressful events, thereby buffering the negative impact of stress [9]. Medical students under digital stress may have a much better sense of meaning in life and be less likely to commit suicide if they view rigorous scientific research training as part of their career mission.

Research has found that digital stress prompts individuals to suppress emotions. This aggravates suicide idea directly and amplifies risks indirectly by weakening the sense of meaning in life, aligning with the loss path of “stress → emotional depression → loss of sense of meaning → increased risk” [8]. Medical students may continuously lose psychological resources during online assessment if they suppress their anxiety about the assessment for a long time [23]. This loss will make them feel life is meaningless, thus aggravating their suicide tendencies.

Limitations

There are a number of limitations in the current study that might encourage innovative research directions. Firstly, the dynamic causal chains cannot be captured by a cross-sectional design, but the correlation between variables can. It is impossible to distinguish whether “digital stress leads to suicide ideation” or “those who already have suicide tendencies are more susceptible to digital stress.” Although all data were collected via self-report questionnaires, the influence of common method variance may still exist. Secondly, this study failed to control for several important potential confounding variables, such as depression, anxiety and occupational burnout levels. These factors are closely associated with perceived stress, emotional dysregulation, and suicidal thoughts. Their omission may mean that the identified mediating effects could be subject to bias due to confounding influences. Future research should incorporate these clinical variables as covariates in analytical models to elucidate the theoretical relationships between the variables more clearly. Finally, the study sample was limited to medical graduate students in Jiangxi Province, China, which restricts the generalizability of its conclusions. It is imperative to exercise caution when generalising the findings of this study to medical students nationwide or in other cultural contexts, given the observed variations in medical education environments and socio-cultural factors across the diverse regions of China. It is recommended that future research employ nationwide multi-center sampling and conduct cross-cultural comparative studies to validate the effective boundaries of this model.

Conclusion

This study examines the relationship between digital stress and suicide ideation among medical postgraduates. The results show that digital stress can directly or indirectly influence suicide ideation through the mediating effects of cognitive reappraisal, expressive suppression, and the sense of meaning in life. In short, digital stress will affect suicide ideation, where the emotional regulation and meaning in life play an important role.

The findings offer actionable insights for educators and policymakers. Firstly, the integration of mindfulness meditation and cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) into graduate orientation programs or mandatory curricula has the potential to systematically train students to reframe digital stress as "challenges for professional growth". Secondly, mentors are required to guide students in reflecting on the social value and existential meaning of their work, thereby reinforcing their professional mission to "heal and save lives." This is achieved through both clinical training and theoretical studies. This process serves to fortify their core psychological resources, thereby enhancing their resilience to stress. Thirdly, conduct regular, anonymous psychological resource assessments for medical students to enable early, targeted interventions, while providing personalized support for students identified as high-risk during these evaluations.
