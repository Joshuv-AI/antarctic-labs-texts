Effect of the double-application of one-step universal adhesives on bonding strength to bur-cut enamel
Authors: Fatih Bedir, Muhammet Karadas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne-step universal adhesives form a thinner adhesive layer, which is more susceptible to oxygen-induced polymerization inhibition, thereby weakening the bond strength. Some studies have suggested strengthening the adhesive layer by applying multiple layers of adhesive. Thus, this study aimed to examine the effect of double application on the bond strength of universal adhesives to bur-cut enamel.MethodsThe enamel surfaces were prepared using a medium-grit diamond bur. The specimens were randomly distributed into eighteen groups based on three factors: the adhesive used (Optibond Universal, G-Premio Bond, Clearfil S3 Bond Universal), the application method of adhesives (single application and double-application with or without light activation between layers), and the type of composite resin (Clearfil Majesty Flow and Clearfil Majesty Esthetic). The adhesive agents were applied in self-etch mode, and composite blocks were fabricated. After 24 h, the composite blocks were subjected to a shear bond strength test, and the fractured surfaces were examined using a stereomicroscope. Additionally, the thickness of the adhesive layers was assessed using scanning electron microscopy. The data were evaluated by a three-way ANOVA, Weibull analysis, and Kruskal-Wallis tests (α = 0.05).ResultsThe double-application of the adhesives did not improve the bonding potential to enamel (p > 0.05). The use of flowable composite resin did not enhance the bond strength. The adhesives demonstrated similar reliability. Optibond Universal exhibited higher bond strength to bur-cut enamel compared to G-Premio Bond and Clearfil S3 Bond Universal, regardless of application method and composite type (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe double-application of the adhesives and composite type did not affect the bonding strength to bur-cut enamel, regardless of whether light activation was applied between the applications.

Background

Research in dental adhesive systems focuses on enhancing the clinical techniques used to bond resin-based materials to tooth structures as part of various minimally invasive dental restoration treatments. The latest generation of so-called universal adhesives (UAs) has simplified the traditional approach to dental bonding, allowing for quicker and less technique-sensitive clinical application methods. Depending on the clinician’s preferences and the specific clinical situation, these multi-mode adhesives can be applied with self-etching, selective etching, and etch-and-rinse adhesion approaches [1]. UAs contain complex mixtures of hydrophilic and hydrophobic components, which include functional monomers that enhance chemical adhesion to tooth structures [2, 3]. However, these combined adhesive and primer solutions in a single bottle typically contain a higher amount of solvent, which means their adhesive thickness is generally quite thin. The film thickness of one-step UAs is often less than 10 μm, which may limit the strength of the bond they create [2]. This thin layer allows oxygen to interfere with the polymerization process throughout a significant portion of its depth. The presence of oxygen inhibits free-radical polymerization, resulting in a soft and adhesive oxygen-inhibition layer at the adhesive interface. Insufficient polymerization can lead to increased water absorption and may initiate hydrolytic degradation [4, 5]. Furthermore, a thin adhesive layer can decrease its ability to absorb stress at the adhesive layer, such as the polymerization shrinkage stress originating from the overlaid composite restorative material [4].

Several studies have suggested methods to enhance the bonding stability of single-step self-etching adhesive systems. One approach is to strengthen the vulnerable adhesive layer by reinforcing it with a hydrophobic adhesive or by applying multiple layers of adhesive [6–12]. Alternatively, applying a flowable composite over the adhesive, which is typically applied in a thin layer, can achieve similar results and help stabilize the adhesive interfaces [13]. Extra adhesive application increases the uniformity and thickness of the adhesive layer, which is expected to decrease fluid flow on the bonded surface [10, 12, 14]. Some studies have reported that the bonding performance improved with the thickness of the adhesive layer [10, 15]. The bonding strengths of single-step self-etching adhesives to dentin were enhanced by applying multiple coats of adhesive, each cured separately with light [15]. However, there is no consensus among studies regarding the effectiveness of multiple layers. In some research, a bonding agent served as the primer, while in other studies, multiple coats were applied and light-cured individually. Several studies have examined the bond strength of UAs with multiple coats, but the results have been inconsistent [9, 16–18].

The bonding strength and interfacial adaptation of composite materials to tooth hard structures can be enhanced by using an adhesive agent with low-viscosity resin [19, 20]. To maintain the integrity of structures, low-viscosity resin with a low elastic modulus can also function as a stress-absorbing layer [20]. It has been reported that the use of a flowable composite material provided the highest bond strengths to dentin regardless of the etching mode of the UA before and after aging [21]. Also, previous studies have shown that the use of a flowable composite results in fewer voids and less microleakage at the interface compared to the incremental layering technique [22, 23].

In laboratory settings, 600-grit carbide papers are used to create standardized smear layers for bond strength tests. In contrast, smear layers from regular or medium-grit diamond burs are rougher and denser, which can significantly compromise bonding to tooth-hard structures, especially with mild self-etch adhesives [24, 25]. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy demonstrated significant variations in the smear layer of enamel, showing differences in thickness, crystal density, and size depending on the specific surface preparation technique [24]. It has been found that the compositions of the smear layer on enamel and dentin significantly affected the bond strength of UAs [26]. A thicker smear layer hinders the penetration of resin monomers, which decreases micromechanical and chemical adhesion. Considering that UAs have a heterogeneous composition, it is important to examine the effects of the double-application of different UAs on bonding to enamel, especially in the presence of clinically relevant smear layers.

The aim of this study was to assess the effect of the double-layer applications of UAs on the shear bond strength (SBS) to bur-cut enamel. The null hypotheses were as follows: (1) the double application of UAs would not affect the enamel SBS, (2) there would be no differences in SBS among the adhesives, and (3) the type of composite would not affect the enamel SBS.

Methods

Tooth preparation

Human teeth were selected from the teeth extracted for reasons (e.g., orthodontic) unrelated to this study, following approval from the University’s Non-invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (document No. 02/2022). Caries-free, fully erupted human molars were stored in Chloramine-T solution (0.5%) for one week and then immersed in distilled water until use within one month. The crowns were detached from their roots and divided in the buccal-lingual direction using a diamond disk under irrigation. The crown segments were placed into auto-polymerizing acrylic resin to expose the enamel surfaces. Enamel surfaces were prepared using a high-speed handpiece (Kavo, Biberach, Germany) equipped with a medium-grit fissure diamond bur (WilofaDiamant, Willi Lohmann, Germany) under water cooling [27, 28]. The bur was moved across the enamel surface five times with light pressure and then replaced with a new one after every five teeth. The exposed enamel surface was inspected using a stereomicroscope (Zeiss Stemi 305, Göttingen, Germany) to ensure that there was adequate microporosity for enamel bonding and that no dentin was exposed.

Specimen randomization and bonding procedure

One hundred eighty specimens were randomly allocated into 18 groups (n = 10) by a combination of the following factors: three adhesive agents (OptiBond Universal (OBU), Clearfil S3 Bond Universal (CBU), and G-premio Bond (GPB), two composite materials (Clearfil Majesty Flow (CMF) and Clearfil Majesty Esthetic (CME)), and three application methods. The sample size of each group was determined based on previous studies [29] and checked using G*Power software (Heinrich Heine University, Germany). The sample size (n = 10) was determined to be sufficient for the present study (power (1-β) = 0.83, effect size = 0.30). In the first adhesive application method (SL), UAs were applied based on the manufacturer’s instructions in self-etch mode and then photo-cured for 10 s by a light-emitting diode (VALO, Ultradent, Utah, USA) with 1000 mW/cm2 intensity (Table 1). In the second method (DL), the adhesives were applied twice, each time in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by photo-curing [16]. In the third method (DLC), the first adhesive layer was photo-cured after application. Then, the second layer was distributed over the first layer surface without agitation, air-dried for 5 s, and then photo-cured. A polytetrafluoroethylene tube (3 mm high and 2.5 mm internal diameter) was fixed onto the bonded enamel surface before final photo-curing of the adhesive. Composite materials were applied into the tube in 2 layers up to 3 mm, and each layer was polymerized for 20 s. All processes were implemented by the same operator. Shear bond strength (SBS) testing

Specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h, and tubes were removed with a sharp scalpel. The bonding agents around the instructed composite were carefully cleaned using a sharp-scalpel. The specimens were fixed on a universal testing device (Model 3344, Instron, Canton, USA). Then, a sharp stainless steel blade was carefully positioned directly at the interface between the enamel and composite cylinder, moving at a precise cross-head speed of 0.5 mm/min until the bond failed. The maximum load (in Newtons) was converted to Megapascals (MPa) by dividing by the bonded area (mm2). The testing was performed by an independent person blinded to the groups. After the SBS test, the fractured surfaces were observed under the stereomicroscope at 40× magnification. Failure modes were classified as mixed, adhesive, cohesive in composite resin, or cohesive in enamel [30]. Failure mode data were evaluated using Fisher’s Exact test. The representative composite cylinders were selected. Then, they were coated with gold using a sputter-coater under vacuum conditions and then examined the fractured surfaces using a Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) (JEOL Brand JSM-6610 model, MA, USA) at 25x magnification.

Normality and homogeneity of SBS data were tested using Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests. Results were analyzed with three-way ANOVA and Tukey post hoc tests at p < 0.05. The reliability and failure probability of the adhesives were assessed using Weibull analysis. The parameters of the Weibull distribution, specifically the shape parameter (m) and the scale parameter (σθ), were calculated by the maximum likelihood estimation system. The shape parameter indicates the reliability of the material, while the scale parameter represents bond strength, with failure probabilities of 63.2%.

Adhesive thickness assessment

Five enamel specimens were ready for each subgroup, as detailed above. The enamel surfaces were bonded using each adhesive according to the prior instructions. The composite material (Clearfil Majesty Esthetic) was then placed on the bonded enamel surfaces, and the restored specimens were subsequently inserted in an epoxy resin. Each specimen was divided perpendicular to the adhesive interfaces using a low-speed cutting device. The cutting surfaces were polished in sequence using carbide papers of 600, 1200, and 2000 grit, followed by an ultrasonic cleaning process.

The specimens were desiccated and coated with gold using a sputter-coater under vacuum conditions. The adhesive interfaces were observed using SEM at 1500x magnification. Three images were taken from each specimen: one from the center and the other two from the left and right sides of the center. SEM images were processed into ImageJ (National Institute of Health, USA) to measure the thickness of the adhesive layer. Three measurements were taken from different areas within each image, specifically from the middle, left, and right lateral regions (Fig. 1). The average of these measurements for each image was recorded for statistical analysis. The data were not distributed homogeneously (p = 0.01). Therefore, the results were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with Bonferroni adjustments applied for the multiple comparisons.

Results

Three-way ANOVA analysis showed that adhesive type significantly affected SBS (p < 0.001, F = 9.002), but adhesive layer and composite type did not affect SBS (p > 0.05). Discussion

UAs offer versatility in clinical application and can be used with self-etch, selective-etch, or etch-and-rinse approaches, depending on the clinician’s preferences and the specific clinical context. Previous studies have shown that UAs produced lower bond strengths to enamel when used in the self-etch technique compared to etch-and-rinse [29, 31, 32]. However, UAs are easier to apply in clinical settings, require less technique-sensitive, and may reduce postoperative sensitivity when applied in the self-etch approach [33, 34]. Building on this background, the current study evaluated the SBS of UAs in self-etching mode with varying application layers and composite materials. A three-way ANOVA analysis indicated that applying the adhesives in a double layer did not impact the SBS, whether or not light activation occurred between the layers. Thus, the first null hypothesis that double-application of the UAs would not affect enamel SBS was accepted. A previous study found that applying UAs in double layers without light activation did not affect microtensile bond strength to enamel compared to a single-layer application [17]. In contrast, several other studies indicated that double-application with light activation between layers enhanced both shear bonding potential for UAs (CBU and GPB) in self-etching mode on enamel [9, 16], which conflicts with our results. The differences in the methodology may explain these conflicting findings. Factors such as the elastic modulus of the composites used, the testing methods (microtensile and shear), bonding surface area, and the thickness of the smear layers have all been found to influence bond strength results [26, 35].

In most previous studies, the enamel surface for bonding tests was ground by silicon carbide paper when cutting [26, 31, 32]. However, this method may not match what happens in real clinical settings. The size of the abrasives affects the density and thickness of the smear layer. It has been found that the smear layer thickness is between 0.9 and 2.6 μm for silicon carbide paper and between 1.0 and 2.8 μm for diamond burs [36]. Also, roughening the enamel with a diamond bur to eliminate the aprismatic layer is clinically mandatory to enhance the bonding ability to enamel when self-etch agents are used [27]. Therefore, in this study, a high-speed diamond bur was used to cut the enamel surface, producing a smear layer that better reflects clinical conditions.

In this study, the thickness of the adhesives increased with double-application. The greatest thickness was observed in CBU specimens, which may be related to the size of the filler particles, the type and amount of solvent used, and the filler load [37, 38]. Increasing the adhesive layer thickness through multiple applications could improve the mechanical properties of the adhesive layer, leading to a larger plastic zone that helps dissipate stress concentration [39]. However, it is important to note that each adhesive has an optimal thickness for its layer, although the precise value remains unclear. The application of multiple adhesive layers may result in a thickness that exceeds this optimal level. In bond strength testing, applied load stress may induce crack formation at the bonded interface, resulting in plastic deformation zones next to the crack ends. These zones can affect crack propagation and fracture behavior. If the adhesive layer is significantly thicker than the plastic deformation zone, the stress distribution at the interface may become more spread, which can reduce bond strength [40].

Additionally, it may be challenging to remove solvents from the added adhesive layer after polymerization of the first layer. On the other hand, with a thicker adhesive layer, volatilizing the solvent may be more challenging before light curing, which negatively affects bond strength [41]. Some ingredients, such as remaining water, solvents, and acidic monomers, might inhibit the polymerization reaction [16, 42]. The excess water and solvents hinder the formation of the cross-linked polymer chain, reducing mechanical properties. Solvent evaporation is typically achieved by agitating the adhesives and utilizing compressed air. Air-drying is necessary to remove solvents from the adhesive and ensure a uniform adhesive layer [31, 43]. Most manufacturers suggest an air-drying time of 5 s, which may not completely remove water and solvents from the adhesive interface [31].

Variations in the adherent substrate and the application method of adhesives can significantly impact the bonding potential achieved through multiple-layer adhesive applications [9]. In this study, adhesive agents were applied to bur-cut enamel surfaces, while previous studies utilized enamel surfaces ground with 240–4000 grit SiC papers [9, 16, 44]. A medium diamond bur produces a thicker smear layer compared to these SiC papers [24]. Some studies have suggested that thicker smear layers may negatively affect bonding strength to enamel [24, 26]. Consequently, poor bonding of the first adhesive layer to the enamel may hinder the bond strength of multiple-layer applications. This is supported by the predominant adhesive failure for the adhesives. However, the use of a double-application photo-cured OBU between layers demonstrated higher bond strength with the flowable composite material. While it is difficult to provide a definitive explanation for this result, potential factors include differences in adhesive content and the greater resilience of flowable composite material, which may lead to a more uniform stress distribution at the adhesive layer.

In this study, OBU presented the highest bond strength, regardless of the application layer or composite type used. Thus, the second null hypothesis was rejected. The functional monomer in OBU is glycerol phosphate dimethacrylate (GPDM), while CBU and GPB utilize 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP). A recent study found that the GPDM-based adhesive (OBU) formed stronger bonding to enamel than other 10-MDP-based adhesives [45], which supports our findings. OBU adhesive demonstrated a high etching capability, penetrating deeper portions of enamel, and similar to that achieved with phosphoric-acid etching [45]. Furthermore, GPDM is more hydrophilic than MDP, leading to higher wettability and the ability to penetrate deeper areas, resulting in strong micromechanical interlocking. Moreover, the molecular structure of GPDM features two polymerizable groups, whereas MDP contains only one [46, 47]. This difference suggests that GPDM can create a stronger resin matrix. In a network-meta analysis, GPDM has been stated to create stable monomer-Ca salts when applied to enamel and dentin, allowing for acceptable hybridization and bonding performance compared to 10-MDP [48].

OBU contains a ternary solvent system comprised of water, ethanol, and acetone, which effectively etches enamel. OBU contains a higher ratio of acetone (30–60%) than ethanol (5–10%), according to the manufacturer’s information [49]. While CBU is based on ethanol, GPB is comprised of acetone. Ethanol is more polar than acetone and may form hydrogen bonds; hence, adhesives based on acetone are less hydrophilic [31, 50]. Considering enamel as a moderately hydrophobic substance, it is expected that GPB and OBU can exhibit the best wetting ability, resulting in higher bond strength. However, GPB and CBU showed similar bonding strength to enamel, and both had lower bond strength than OBU. These findings indicate that the type of solvent, while important, does not solely determine wetting ability or bonding strength. Instead, the combined influence of solvent properties and the composition of monomers in the organic matrix must be recognized as jointly critical to adhesive performance. Each solvent or mixture of solvents has a distinct dipole moment, vapor pressure, and dielectric constant [51]. The etching efficacy of functional monomers can vary according to the solvent with which they are mixed. Also, it is noted that applying OBU to the enamel for an extended period (20 s) can enhance bond strength due to increased wettability. Longer application times result in greater evaporation of solvents, further contributing to improved bond strength [52].

The pH levels of adhesives are crucial for effectively dissolving the smear layer and optimizing the etching of the substrate. GPB is classified as having an intermediate pH level, specifically a value of 1.5. Additionally, it contains acetone as a solvent, which can enhance the permeability of resin monomers due to its high volatility and hygroscopic properties [53]. However, GPB demonstrated lower bonding strength to bur-cut enamel compared to OBU. The ability of resin monomers in self-etch adhesives to penetrate and form strong chemical bonds may be more critical than the effects of demineralization [54]. GPB has a relatively high solvent content, consisting of 25–50% acetone and 24% water, and it is also a HEMA-free adhesive. The fact that acetone does not form an azeotrope with water limits its capacity to support evaporation, which can lead to inadequate water removal and reduced polymerization. This, combined with the absence of HEMA, can result in cracks within the adhesive layer and contribute to premature bond failure [55].

In the present study, the composite type did not affect the shear bonding strength. Therefore, the third null hypothesis was accepted. It has been reported that the composite material used had a significant effect on microtensile bond strength on dentin [56]. The composite material with the highest hardness and elastic modulus provided higher dentin bonding strength, which could be attributed to uniform stress distribution in the adhesive layer [56]. This finding conflicts with our findings, which may be explained by methodological differences. Enamel is more brittle than dentin, making it prone to earlier fractures. Minor changes in the specimen or stress distribution during load application can significantly affect the results [57]. However, no studies have evaluated different composite materials in terms of shear bond strength to enamel.

This study has some limitations. It examined the effects of double-layer application on the bond strength of only three UAs in the self-etch approach. Thus, the findings may have some limited applicability to other application procedures and adhesive types. Also, specimens were not exposed to chemical challenges like saliva, unlike clinical scenarios. Further research should assess various UAs and include additional clinical conditions, such as thermocycling and saliva storage.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, it was concluded that the double-application of the UAs tested did not impact enamel bond strength, regardless of whether light-curing occurred between the layers. The use of flowable composite resin did not enhance bond strength. OBU demonstrated a higher bond strength to bur-cut enamel compared with CBU and GPB, while no significant difference was found between GPB and CBU. CBU presented the highest adhesive thickness, regardless of the adhesive application layer.
