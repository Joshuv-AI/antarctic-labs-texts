Alteration of Subchondral Bone Density Distribution Across the Knee Joint Before and After Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction
Authors: Taku Ebata, Koji Iwasaki, Dai Sato, Masatake Matsuoka, Tomohiro Onodera, Eiji Kondo, Norimasa Iwasaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury alters knee kinematics and contact mechanics, increasing posteromedial (PM) loading and the risk of knee osteoarthritis. Because subchondral bone density reflects long-term joint stress, its distribution may indicate chronic loading changes. Previous studies have shown higher proportions of high-density area (HDA%) in the PM tibial subchondral bone of ACL-injured knees than in healthy ones. However, it remains unclear whether ACL reconstruction restores normal tibial subchondral bone density distribution.Purpose/Hypothesis:The purpose of this study was to evaluate changes in tibial subchondral bone density after ACL reconstruction. It was hypothesized that reconstruction would reduce the elevated PM HDA% observed after injury.Study Design:Case series; Level of evidence, 4.Methods:Seventeen patients (7 men, 10 women) who underwent unilateral double-bundle ACL reconstruction were included. Computed tomography–osteoabsorptiometry (CT-OAM) was used to quantify the proportion of the proximal tibial surface occupied by HDA (top 20% of Hounsfield units) within 6 sagittal subregions: anteromedial (AM), centromedial (CM), PM, anterolateral, centrolateral, and posterolateral. The medial ratio represented the percentage of total tibial HDA in the medial compartment. Clinical assessments included side-to-side laxity, pivot-shift test, International Knee Documentation Committee score, Lysholm score, and Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS).Results:Anterior laxity improved from a mean of 3.3 mm (95% CI, 2.5 to 4.0 mm) to 0.4 mm (95% CI, −0.8 to 1.6 mm) postoperatively, and all pivot-shift tests were negative (P < .05). Clinical scores improved significantly. CT-OAM revealed decreased PM HDA% and increased CM HDA% (Holm-adjusted P = .003 and P = .011, respectively). The AM increase was nonsignificant after correction (P = .055). No significant lateral changes were noted. The medial ratio rose from 59.2% to 63.7% (P = .032) and correlated negatively with KOOS Sports (r = −0.561; P = .037).Conclusion:ACL reconstruction reduces PM overloading by decreasing PM HDA%, but the increased medial ratio suggests a shift of loading toward the medial compartment, which may relate to poorer sport-specific function.

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) serves as a primary restraint against anterior tibial translation relative to the femur and contributes to rotatory knee stability. An ACL injury disrupts normal knee kinematics, shifting contact location and pressure distribution, and thereby imposes abnormal stresses on the articular cartilage and subchondral bone.7,12,29,43 Over time, these stresses promote degeneration of joint components, leading to the development of knee osteoarthritis (OA).1,39

The distribution of subchondral bone density reflects bone remodeling in response to chronic mechanical loading, consistent with the law of Wolff. 16 Thus, it serves as an indicator of long-term, cumulative intra-articular loading and the relative distribution within the joint. Computed tomography–osteoabsorptiometry (CT-OAM) enables in vivo estimation of chronic stress distribution across joint surfaces by quantifying subchondral bone density.36,37,46 Previous CT-OAM studies have shown that the posteromedial (PM) region of the tibial plateau exhibited a larger high-density area (HDA) in ACL-deficient knees than in healthy controls. 33 This finding indicates that ACL deficiency increases loading in the PM region of the knee joint, potentially contributing to osteoarthritic change.

Several studies have reported favorable clinical outcomes and improved knee stability after ACL reconstruction.9,23,35,40 This surgical technique aims to restore both anterior and rotatory stability, potentially shifting tibiofemoral contact anteriorly and redistributing load within the medial compartment. However, the extent to which double-bundle ACL reconstruction normalizes the abnormal distribution of subchondral bone density remains unclear.

This study aimed to elucidate changes in tibial subchondral bone density distribution after ACL reconstruction. We hypothesized that reconstruction would reduce the elevated subchondral bone density in the PM region of the tibial plateau observed after ACL injury. These findings may enhance understanding of the biomechanical effects of ACL reconstruction and support the development of objective indicators for assessing the risk of secondary OA progression.

Methods

Study Design

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our hospital (approval ID: 022-0111), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. A retrospective analysis was conducted of the records of 50 patients who underwent unilateral ACL reconstruction between April 2019 and March 2023. The inclusion criteria were unilateral ACL injury and the availability of both CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans obtained preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meniscal injury identified on preoperative MRI (grade ≥2 according to the Lotysch and Mink MRI evaluation system 10 ) or confirmed during arthroscopic examination at the time of ACL reconstruction, (2) cartilage injury of grade ≥2 based on the International Cartilage Regeneration & Joint Preservation Society classification, 4 (3) OA of grade ≥2 according to the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system, 22 (4) concomitant chondral injury or ligamentous injury detected on preoperative MRI or during arthroscopy, and (5) previous surgery on the affected knee. Of the 50 identified patients, 33 were excluded based on these criteria, leaving 17 patients (17 knees; 7 men, 10 women) for analysis (Figure 1). Each knee underwent both clinical and radiological evaluation preoperatively and at 2 years postoperatively.

Surgical Procedure of Double-Bundle ACL Reconstruction

The details of the double-bundle ACL reconstruction technique have been described previously.23,47,48 Briefly, the harvested semitendinosus and gracilis tendons were prepared as 2 grafts using the previously reported “hybrid” method. 25 For the anteromedial (AM) bundle graft, one-half of the semitendinosus and/or gracilis tendon was used. Both free ends were firmly sutured and connected in series with a commercially available polyester tape (Leeds-Keio artificial ligament, Neoligament). The EndoButton-CL-BTB (Smith & Nephew Endoscopy) was then passed through the looped end of the hamstring tendon graft. The posterolateral (PL) bundle graft was fashioned similarly using the remaining one-half of the semitendinosus tendon, which was doubled and sutured in the same manner.

First, tibial tunnels for the PL and AM bundles were created using a Wire-Navigator device (Smith & Nephew Endoscopy Japan). After resecting the remnant ACL tissue, guide wires were inserted into the centers of the native PL and AM bundle attachments with the Wire-Navigator. The guide wire positions were confirmed using a C-arm fluoroscope (BV Endura; Philips Healthcare), and tibial tunnels for both bundles were then created using a cannulated drill. Subsequently, AM and PL femoral tunnels were made in the lateral femoral condyle using the transtibial technique. For the AM femoral tunnel, a guide wire was inserted into the center of the femoral footprint of the AM bundle with a 5-mm offset guide (Transtibial Femoral ACL Drill Guide; Arthrex) introduced through the AM tibial tunnel. The PL femoral tunnel was created by drilling a guide wire through the PL tibial tunnel to the center of the femoral footprint of the PL bundle. Each graft was then passed through its corresponding tibial tunnel into the femoral tunnel and secured with an EndoButton. The 2 tibial graft ends were simultaneously fixed with spiked staples (Smith & Nephew Endoscopy Japan) at 10° of knee flexion under a 30-N load applied to each graft. 24

All patients followed a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol as previously described. 26 A knee immobilizer was applied for 2 weeks after surgery, after which weightbearing was permitted using a hinged brace. Progressive athletic training was initiated at approximately 6 weeks postoperatively, and return to full sports participation was generally allowed between 9 and 12 months after surgery.

Clinical Outcomes Before and After ACL Reconstruction

The side-to-side difference in anterior laxity was measured using a KT-2000 arthrometer (MEDmetric) at 30° of knee flexion under an anterior drawer force of 133 N. One experienced orthopaedic surgeon (K.I.) performed the pivot-shift test in the outpatient clinic without anesthesia, and the results were evaluated subjectively. In the pivot-shift test, a 2+ + grade was assigned when the examiner perceived a sudden rotational slip between the tibia and femur (the “jog” phenomenon), indicating clear failure of ACL function. A + grade was recorded when subtle rotational movement differences were observed between the injured and uninjured knees without a distinct slip, suggesting partial insufficiency of the ACL function but not complete failure. Overall knee function was assessed using the Lysholm knee score (maximum, 100 points), 31 the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) form, 19 and the Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS). 8 Clinical assessments were performed preoperatively and 2 years after surgery in the outpatient clinic without anesthesia.

Radiographic Assessment

Bilateral standing full-length anteroposterior (AP) radiographs of the entire lower limb in full extension, along with lateral knee views, were obtained for evaluation. The following radiographic parameters were assessed preoperatively. The degree of OA in the tibiofemoral joint was graded according to the KL classification on weightbearing AP radiographs. 22 Additional measurements obtained from full-length AP radiographs (taken with a long cassette in the single-leg standing position) included the hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, percentage of mechanical axis, mechanical lateral distal femoral angle, and medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA). The posterior tibial slope (PTS) was measured on lateral radiographs.

Computed Tomography–Osteoabsorptiometry

High-resolution helical CT was performed using an Aquilion One/ViSION Edition scanner (Toshiba Medical Systems). Scans were obtained with patients in the supine position and the knee fully extended. The slice thickness and interval were both set at 0.5 mm. The CT data were transferred to a personal computer for processing. Sagittal and coronal slices were reconstructed at 1.0-mm intervals, and 3-dimensional (3D) bone models were generated from the axial data using commercial software (Ziocube; Ziosoft). The sagittal and coronal axes were determined with reference to the epicondylar axis of the distal femoral condyle on the axial slices.

Using sagittal and coronal CT images, along with a 3D CT model of the proximal tibial articular surface, we manually outlined the medial and lateral compartments of the proximal tibial subchondral surface to encompass the entire subchondral layer across all slices (Figure 2A). The subchondral bone density in each sagittal slice was analyzed using noncommercial software (OsteoDens 4.0), developed at our institution. The software automatically identified the maximum increment point in Hounsfield units (HU) from the joint surface as the starting point of the region of interest (ROI). Within a 2.5-mm ROI from this point, the maximum HU value was selected. The radiodensity of the identified subchondral bone region was measured at 1-mm intervals along each coordinate. Finally, a 2-dimensional density map representing the distribution of subchondral bone density was generated by stacking the sagittal slices.

The differences between the maximum and minimum HU values on the mapping images were categorized into 9 grades. A corresponding surface mapping image was then generated based on these grades, producing a color scale in which red represented the highest bone density and violet the lowest (Figure 2B). The selected regions of the medial and lateral plateaus included cortical bone or peripheral bony spurs at the margins of the articular surface, as these structures could not be excluded automatically by the software. However, these cortical and spur regions were meticulously removed manually during the subsequent quantitative analysis.

Quantitative analysis of the mapping data focused on determining the location of the HDA on the tibial articular surface, as previously described.13,21,33 The HDA was defined as the region composing the top 20% of HU values across the proximal tibial articular surface. Each of the medial and lateral compartments was divided into 3 equal subregions along the sagittal plane. The medial compartment was divided into AM, centromedial (CM), and PM regions, while the lateral compartment was divided into anterolateral (AL), centrolateral (CL), and PL regions (Figure 2C). The percentage of each subregion occupied by the HDA (HDA%) was then calculated. 33 The medial ratio was defined as the proportion of the HDA located within the medial compartment relative to the total HDA across both compartments.13,21 Because the HDA represents the top 20% of HU values across the entire tibial plateau, the medial ratio reflects the distribution of the highest-density bone fraction, rather than the absolute amount of dense bone.

The reproducibility of the data was evaluated using OsteoDens 4.0 software. Intra- and interobserver reliability were assessed using CT data from 3 randomly selected knees before and after surgery. The HDA% values were independently measured by 2 observers (T.E. and K.I.) in these 6 knees, yielding a total of 36 subregions, which were analyzed twice in a blinded manner at 4-week intervals. The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) for intraobserver reliability were 0.92 (T.E.) and 0.91 (K.I.), while the ICC for interobserver reproducibility was 0.89.

MRI Assessment of Bone Marrow Lesions

Preoperative MRI scans obtained for routine evaluation were assessed for the presence of bone marrow lesions (BMLs). BMLs were defined as areas of increased signal intensity within the subchondral bone on fat-suppressed proton density–weighted images. BML presence was recorded for the medial tibial plateau and the lateral tibial plateau.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis was conducted based on the expected improvement in HDA% in the PM region. Sample size/power estimation was informed by Miura et al, 33 who reported an approximate 6.9 difference in PM HDA% between normal and ACL-injured knees. Assuming a standard deviation of approximately 9 and a 2-sided α of .05, a sample size of 17 was calculated to provide approximately 85% power. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro Version 17.0 (SAS Institute). The paired t test and chi-square test were used to compare clinical outcomes and the subchondral bone density (HDA%) before and after surgery. Within-region paired comparisons were adjusted for multiplicity using the Holm method (familywise α = .05). Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were applied to examine the relationships between postoperative clinical outcomes and the postoperative medial ratio and the association between time from injury to surgery and changes in CT-OAM parameters. Statistical significance was set at a P value <.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of the 50 patients who underwent ACL reconstruction at our hospital between 2019 and 2023, 17 patients (7 men, 10 women) met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. The mean age at the time of surgery was 28.2 years (95% CI, 22.1 to 34.2 years). Radiographic evaluation showed a preoperative HKA angle of −0.7° (95% CI, −2.1° to 0.7°) and a PTS of 8.9° (95% CI, 7.7° to 10.1°) (Table 1).

Clinical Outcomes Before and After ACL Reconstruction

The preoperative side-to-side difference in anterior laxity was 3.3 mm (95% CI, 2.5 to 4.0 mm). Postoperatively, this difference decreased significantly to 0.4 mm (95% CI, −0.8 to 1.6 mm) (P < .001). A positive pivot-shift test was observed in 14 of 17 patients preoperatively, whereas all patients demonstrated a negative pivot-shift result after surgery, indicating a significant improvement (P < .001). Postoperative IKDC scores improved significantly compared with preoperative values (P < .001), although 1 patient received an IKDC grade D due to limited knee flexion. The Lysholm score and KOOS value were also significantly higher postoperatively in all domains except the KOOS Symptoms subscale. There were no significant differences in terminal knee motion before and after surgery (Table 2).

Alteration of Distribution Patterns of Subchondral Bone Density Across the Proximal Tibia Before and After ACL Reconstruction

The distribution pattern of subchondral bone density across the proximal tibia was compared before and after ACL reconstruction. The HDA% in the PM region significantly decreased after surgery, whereas that in the CM region significantly increased (Holm-adjusted P = .003 and P = .011, respectively). Although an increase in HDA% was also observed in the AM region, this change did not remain significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons (raw P = .0138; Holm-adjusted P = .055). No significant alterations were detected in any of the lateral subregions (Holm-adjusted P≥ .558) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study is the first to compare the distribution patterns of tibial subchondral bone density before and after ACL reconstruction using CT-OAM. Our findings demonstrated that double-bundle ACL reconstruction led to a significant decrease in HDA% in the PM region, accompanied by a compensatory increase in the CM region. Moreover, the postoperative medial ratio—reflecting the proportion of load borne by the medial compartment—was significantly higher than the preoperative value. Notably, this increased medial ratio negatively correlated with the KOOS Sports subscale, suggesting that greater medial compartment loading may be linked to diminished sport-specific functional outcomes.

The distribution of subchondral bone density based on CT-OAM is considered to reflect long-term, cumulative joint loading. In this study, ACL reconstruction was associated with a redistribution of chronic loading across the tibial plateau, with a postoperative decrease in PM HDA% and an increase in CM HDA%. ACL deficiency has been shown to increase stress in the PM tibia, contributing to the progressive development of OA over time. 41 A prior CT-OAM study reported that the HDA in the PM tibial region was significantly greater in ACL-deficient knees and correlated with the duration of ACL deficiency in meniscus-intact cases. 33 Similarly, Imhauser et al 20 demonstrated that ACL deficiency increased mean contact forces in the posterior region of the medial compartment under both anterior and rotational loading, whereas ACL reconstruction markedly reduced these forces at the same location. Cadaveric studies have likewise shown that ACL transection elevates posterior and PM tibial stresses and that reconstruction alleviates these stresses, with the extent of reduction influenced by the surgical technique and tunnel placement.30,32,34 In the present study, the significant postoperative decrease in PM HDA% aligns with these findings, suggesting that double-bundle ACL reconstruction effectively reduces excessive joint loading in the PM tibial region.

Our study demonstrated that the postoperative medial ratio was significantly higher than the preoperative value, indicating a relative shift of joint contact loading toward the medial compartment after ACL reconstruction. Kumar et al 28 reported an approximately 13% increase in peak knee adduction moment (KAM) in the reconstructed knee after surgery. Similarly, Butler et al 6 observed a 21% higher peak KAM in patients who underwent ACL reconstruction than in healthy controls. In addition, an electromyography-constrained musculoskeletal modeling study found that, although peak tibiofemoral contact forces in the involved limb did not differ from those in controls, lateral compartment contact forces were lower in reconstructed knees than in the contralateral knees during cutting and running, suggesting a relative increase in loading in the medial compartment. 27 Collectively, these findings suggest that joint loading within the medial compartment increases after ACL reconstruction compared with that in healthy or uninjured knees. Furthermore, previous studies have reported that patients exhibiting postoperative increases in peak KAM after ACL reconstruction experience poorer clinical outcomes and greater progression of cartilage degeneration in the medial compartment.14,28,45 In line with these reports, our study found that the postoperative medial ratio was negatively correlated with the KOOS Sports subscale, supporting the association between increased medial loading and less favorable functional outcomes.

Several studies have reported higher radiographic OA grades in ACL-reconstructed knees than in healthy or contralateral knees.2,18,49 Furthermore, meta-analyses comparing reconstructed and nonreconstructed ACL-injured knees within 5 to 10 years after injury have found no significant differences in OA incidence.11,15 The observed increase in the medial ratio after ACL reconstruction aligns with these clinical findings and suggests that a shift toward greater medial compartment loading may contribute to the persistent risk of OA progression after reconstruction.

Several mechanisms may explain postoperative changes in the medial ratio after ACL reconstruction. Greater medial joint contact force after reconstruction was associated with higher KAM during gait and periarticular muscle co-contraction around the knee. 45 A systematic review also reported weaker hip abductor and rotator strength in the reconstructed limb, suggesting that hip muscle deficits may contribute to increased dynamic medial knee loading. 5 These findings imply that altered neuromuscular control and gait adaptations could contribute to a relative shift toward greater medial compartment stresses and/or a relative reduction in lateral compartment loading. Furthermore, patient-related factors such as lower limb alignment may influence load sharing and subchondral bone remodeling. Additionally, progressive extrusion of the medial meniscus (MM) has been observed postoperatively,38,44 and such extrusion compromises load-distributing capacity, thereby elevating contact stress across the joint. 17 Therefore, postoperative MM extrusion and subtle meniscal changes such as internal derangement or changes in meniscal tissue quality may also contribute to increased medial compartment loading. Moreover, bone bruising, as reflected by BMLs on preoperative MRI, could potentially influence subchondral bone density patterns. In our supplementary analysis, the presence of medial or lateral tibial BMLs on preoperative MRI was not associated with the change in the medial ratio after ACL reconstruction. Nevertheless, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded because BMLs were assessed as present or absent, and their extent and temporal evolution were not quantitatively evaluated. Finally, surgical factors including tunnel position, initial graft tensioning, and technique-related differences (eg, single-bundle vs double-bundle reconstruction) have been reported to influence postoperative tibiofemoral kinematics and load distribution3,34,42 and may contribute to postoperative changes in the medial ratio.

Clinically, identifying factors associated with postoperative increases in the medial ratio could facilitate the development of targeted interventions to mitigate OA progression after ACL reconstruction. Potentially modifiable targets may include surgical approaches aiming for anatomic footprint tunnel placement, alignment-correcting strategies, and rehabilitation programs emphasizing restoration of hip abductor strength and neuromuscular control with gait retraining. Because we lacked direct biomechanical measures and longitudinal assessments of key determinants including meniscal status, these implications should be interpreted as hypothesis generating.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis with a small sample size and a high exclusion rate (33/50 eligible patients were excluded), which may introduce selection bias and result in a cohort that is not fully representative of the overall population with ACL injuries. In particular, the study cohort largely excluded knees with concomitant pathologies such as meniscal or chondral lesions, and all reconstructions were performed using a double-bundle, transtibial technique; therefore, the results may not be generalizable to other populations or surgical methods. Second, CT-OAM quantifies the distribution of subchondral radiodensity as an indirect measure of long-term cumulative loading and thus does not allow for the direct assessment of knee contact forces. Furthermore, HDA% and the medial ratio are relative metrics defined by the top 20% of subchondral bone density within each scan. Therefore, these parameters could change due to redistribution of the HDAs even without an absolute change in bone density, and our findings should be interpreted primarily as a shift in the spatial distribution of relative high-density regions. Third, the findings represent short-term outcomes approximately 2 years after surgery; whether the observed changes persist or evolve over longer follow-up periods remains unclear. Fourth, determinants that plausibly influence medial loading, including coronal leg alignment (eg, HKA angle and MPTA), PTS, activity level and return-to-sport status, longitudinal changes in weight, rehabilitation exposure, meniscal condition or extrusion, and graft or tunnel parameters, were not directly analyzed or adjusted for, precluding causal inference. We also explored whether time from injury to surgery was associated with changes in CT-OAM parameters (eg, regional HDA% and medial ratio); however, no significant associations were observed in this cohort, possibly due to the limited sample size and the relatively narrow range of intervals. Finally, because correlations were examined across multiple clinical outcome measures, the association with KOOS Sports should be interpreted as exploratory and requires confirmation in larger studies.

Conclusion

A significant decrease in HDA% was observed in the PM region after ACL reconstruction, while the postoperative medial ratio was significantly higher than the preoperative value. These results indicate that although ACL reconstruction alleviates the PM overloading associated with ACL deficiency, it may also induce a relative shift of joint loading toward the medial compartment overall. Such changes in loading distribution may underlie the residual risk and mechanisms of OA progression after ACL reconstruction.
