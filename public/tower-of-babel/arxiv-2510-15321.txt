Cantor’s Non-Equinumerosity Theorems, Inductively
Saeed Salehi

Abstract
We apply an inductive argument to three theorems of Cantor on (1) the uncountability of infinite binary sequences, (2) the uncountability of real numbers, and (3) the non-equinumerosity of sets with their powersets. This technique proves the powerset theorem by assuming the Axiom of Choice. \AMSclass 03E10 ⋅ \cdot 03E25

1
Binary Sequences

Cantor’s celebrated diagonal argument constructs a new infinite binary sequence
c
for a given infinite sequence
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of infinite binary sequences, such that
c
is different from each
s_{i}
(for
i=1,2,\cdots
). The sequence
c
differs from
s_{i}
at the
i
th place, as it is defined by
c|_{i}={\tt 1}-s_{i}|_{i}
, where
s|_{i}
denotes the
i
th element of the sequence
s
; so, we have
c|_{i}={\tt 0}
when
s_{i}|_{i}={\tt 1}
and
c|_{i}={\tt 1}
otherwise (when
s_{i}|_{i}={\tt 0}
). There are some other proofs for this result in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
. In this paper, we present a novel proof that inductively constructs a new sequence out of the given list. For an infinite sequence
s
and a positive integer
n
, let
s|_{\leqslant n}
denote the sequence
\langle s|_{i}\rangle_{i\leqslant n}=\langle s|_{1},s|_{2},\cdots,s|_{n}\rangle
, which is the initial segment of
s
with length
n
. Define the binary sequence
\sigma=\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle_{n>0}
by (strong) induction on
n>0
as follows:

\sigma_{n}=\begin{cases}{\tt 0},&\mbox{if }\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{i<n}\ast\langle{\tt 0}\rangle\neq s_{n}|_{\leqslant n};\\
{\tt 1},&\mbox{if }\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{i<n}\ast\langle{\tt 0}\rangle=s_{n}|_{\leqslant n};\end{cases}

where
\ast
denotes the concatenation of sequences. Let us notice that
\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{i<1}
is empty, and so

\sigma_{1}=\begin{cases}{\tt 0},&\mbox{if }s_{1}|_{\leqslant 1}\neq\langle{\tt 0}\rangle;\\
{\tt 1},&\mbox{if }s_{1}|_{\leqslant 1}=\langle{\tt 0}\rangle;\end{cases}\;\;={\tt 1}-s_{1}|_{1}.

Theorem
1.1

(Cantor, 1890)
.

The set of infinite binary sequences is not countable.

Proof
:

For a given infinite sequence
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
of infinite binary sequences, the binary sequence
\sigma=\langle\sigma_{n}\rangle_{n>0}
constructed above is different from each
s_{n}
since
\sigma|_{\leqslant n}=\langle\sigma_{i}\rangle_{i\leqslant n}\neq s_{n}|_{\leqslant n}
holds for every
n>0
.

❑

In other words,
\sigma
differs from
s_{n}
not necessarily at the
n
th place, but the initial segments of the two sequences (of length
n
) differ.

2
Real Numbers

Here, we apply this inductive argument to show the uncountability of the reals. As the history goes, the now-famous diagonal argument was not Cantor’s first proof for the uncountability of
\mathbb{R}
, not even his second; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
. There are multiple alternative proofs for this intriguing result, aside from Cantor’s; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
§​§​ 8.4–8.5 and Appendix A]
; see also
[
9
]
for a hypothetical route to the diagonal argument.

Fix a base
\mathfrak{b}>1
. Every real number
r
in the interval
(0,1]
has a unique infinite expansion in base
\mathfrak{b}
, which is of the form
r=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}
, where
d_{i}\in\{0,1,\cdots,\mathfrak{b}\!-\!1\}
is not ultimately
0
. The ultimately
0
, or finite, expansions are not unique; e.g., for
\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{b}\!-\!1
,
(0.1000\cdots)_{\mathfrak{b}}=(0.0\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\cdots)_{\mathfrak{b}}

holds since
\mathfrak{b}^{-1}=\mathfrak{b}^{\prime}\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}
.
The diagonal argument builds a real number
r
in
(0,1]
for a given infinite sequence
\{r_{i}\}_{i>0}
of reals in
(0,1]
, such that
r
is different from each
r_{i}
(
i>0
). To make sure that the new expansion will not be ultimately
0
, we take the base
\mathfrak{b}
to be greater than
2
.
The binary base,
\mathfrak{b}=2
, causes some problems that require more care; see
[
4
]
and the references therein.
The now-classical (anti-)diagonal construction goes as follows.
Let
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d_{n,i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}
be
the infinite expansion of each
r_{n}
in base
\mathfrak{b}
, where
d_{n,i}
’s belong to
\{0,1,\cdots,\mathfrak{b}\!-\!1\}
and are not ultimately
0
. Let

c_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }d_{i,i}\neq 1;\\
2,&\mbox{if }d_{i,i}=1;\end{cases}

and put
c=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}
. The real number
c
belongs to
(0,1]
and is different from each
r_{n}
since its infinite expansion in base
\mathfrak{b}
is different.

Theorem
2.1

(Cantor, 1874)
.

The real interval
(0,1]
is uncountable.

Proof
:

Let
\{r_{i}\}_{i>0}
be a given infinite sequence of reals in
(0,1]
. Fix a base
\mathfrak{b}>2
,
and write the infinite expansion of
r_{i}
in base
\mathfrak{b}
as
r_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}d_{i,j}\mathfrak{b}^{-j}
.
For each
n,i>0
, let
r_{i}|_{\leqslant n}
be the number
\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{i,j}\mathfrak{b}^{-j}
, the restriction of the infinite expansion of
r_{i}
in base
\mathfrak{b}
to the first
n
summands.
Define the sequence
\sigma_{n}
by (strong) induction on
n>0
as follows:

\sigma_{n}=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }\sum_{i<n}\sigma_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}+1\neq r_{n}|_{\leqslant n};\\
2,&\mbox{if }\sum_{i<n}\sigma_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}+1=r_{n}|_{\leqslant n}.\end{cases}

Put
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}\mathfrak{b}^{-i}
. This number is in
(0,1]
but does not belong to
\{r_{i}\}_{i>0}
since for each
n>0
, we have
\sigma|_{\leqslant n}\neq r_{n}|_{\leqslant n}
by the definition of
\sigma
.

❑

To see that this inductive argument is really different from Cantor’s diagonal argument, let us consider the following sequence of real numbers in
(0,1]
over a fixed base
\mathfrak{b}>2
:

r_{1}=0.1222222\cdots=0.1\overline{2}
,

r_{2}=0.1111111\cdots=0.\overline{1}
,

r_{3}=0.2222222\cdots=0.\overline{2}
,

r_{4}=0.2121212\cdots=0.\overline{21}
,

r_{5}=0.2111111\cdots=0.2\overline{1}
,

r_{6}=0.1212121\cdots=0.\overline{12}
,

\vdots

The (anti-)diagonal number is
c=0.221221\cdots
, while the inductive argument produces
\sigma=0.211121\cdots
.

Now, we consider the case of
\mathfrak{b}=2
.
Fix a sequence
\{r_{i}\}_{i>0}
of reals in
(0,1]
and their infinite binary expansions
r_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}b_{i,j}2^{-j}
, where
b_{i,j}\in\{0,1\}
are not ultimately
0
.
Neither the diagonal argument nor our inductive one can work for the binary base, since the new expansion could be ultimately
0
. To overcome this obstacle, an ingenious argument was presented in
[
4
]
, which can be rephrased as follows.

Put
h=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}h_{n}2^{-n}
, where

h_{2n-1}h_{2n}=\begin{cases}01,&\mbox{if }b_{n,2n}=0;\\
10,&\mbox{if }b_{n,2n}=1.\end{cases}

Note that the binary expansion of
h
cannot be ultimately
0
(or
1
).
Here are two more alternative arguments.

(I)

Let for every
n>0
,

s_{2n-1}s_{2n}=\begin{cases}01,&\mbox{if }b_{n,2n-1}b_{n,2n}\neq 01;\\
10,&\mbox{if }b_{n,2n-1}b_{n,2n}=01;\end{cases}

and
s=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}2^{-n}
.

(II)

Inductively, put for every
n>0
,

\sigma_{2n-1}\sigma_{2n}=\begin{cases}01,&\mbox{if }\sum_{i=1}^{2n-2}\sigma_{i}2^{-i}+2^{-2n}\neq r_{n}|_{\leqslant 2n};\\
10,&\mbox{if }\sum_{i=1}^{2n-2}\sigma_{i}2^{-i}+2^{-2n}=r_{n}|_{\leqslant 2n}.\end{cases}

The real number
\sigma=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{n}2^{-n}
belongs to the interval
[1\!/\!3,2\!/\!3]
and is different from each
r_{n}
, since
\sigma|_{\leqslant 2n}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\sigma_{i}2^{-i}\neq r_{n}|_{\leqslant 2n}
holds for each
n>0
.

3
The Powerset

This inductive argument can be generalized to the non-existence of a surjection
f\colon A\rightarrow\wp A
if
A
is well-orderable (here,
\wp A
denotes the powerset of the set
A
).
For a subset
S\subseteq A
of an ordered set
(A,\prec)
and an element
a\in A
, let
S|_{\prec\,a}
denote
\{x\in S\mid x\prec a\}
and
S|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}
denote

\{x\in S\mid x\preccurlyeq a\}
.

Theorem
3.1

(Powerset Theorem for Well-Ordered Sets)
.

If
A
is well-orderable, then no function
f\colon A\rightarrow\wp A
can be surjective.

Proof
:

Let
\prec
be a well-ordering on
A
, and let a function
f\colon A\rightarrow\wp A
be given. Define
B_{a}\subseteq A
for
a\in A
by (strong)
\prec
-induction as follows:

B_{a}=\begin{cases}\emptyset,&\mbox{if }\bigcup_{i\prec a}B_{i}\neq f(a)|_{\preccurlyeq\,a};\\
\{a\},&\mbox{if }\bigcup_{i\prec a}B_{i}=f(a)|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}.\end{cases}

Let
B=\bigcup_{a\in A}B_{a}
. The set
B
is a subset of
A
that is not in the range of
f
, since
B|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}\neq f(a)|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}
holds for every
a\in A
. It suffices to notice that

B|_{\prec\,a}=\bigcup_{i\prec a}B_{i}\subseteq A|_{\prec\,a}
and

B|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}=\bigcup_{i\preccurlyeq a}B_{i}\subseteq A|_{\preccurlyeq\,a}
.

❑

This is weaker than Cantor’s Powerset Theorem, since it requires the Axiom of Choice (AC) to well-order an arbitrary set. Let
V\sqsubseteq W
mean that the well-ordered set
V
is an initial segment (or equal to the whole) of the well-ordered set
W
.
The following inductive proof stems from
[
1
]
.

Theorem
3.2

(Cantor’s Powerset Theorem,
I
)
.

For a function
f:A\rightarrow\wp A
, any
\sqsubseteq
-maximal well-oderable subset
B\subseteq A
whose order
\prec
satisfies the following
(\bm{\ast})
, is not in the range of
f
.

(\bm{\ast})\;\forall b\in B:f(b)=B|_{\prec\,b}.

Proof
:

Assume for the sake of a contradiction that
f(a)=B
for some
a\in A
. Then
a\not\in B
, since otherwise, if
a\in B
, then
a\in f(a)
, and so by
\bm{\ast}
we would have
a\prec a
, a contradiction. Now, the set
B^{\prime}=B\cup\{a\}
is bigger than
B
, and
\prec^{\prime}
that extends
\prec
by
b\prec^{\prime}a
for every
b\in B
, well-orders
B^{\prime}
and satisfies
\bm{\ast}
as well.
This contradicts the
\sqsubseteq
-maximality of
B
.

❑

We notice that, in the above proof,
\bm{\ast}
could be relaxed to

(\bm{\star})\;\forall b\in B:f(b)\subseteq B|_{\prec\,b}.

It goes without saying that the existence of such a
\sqsubseteq
-maximal subset of
A
requires Zorn’s Lemma, or equivalently AC. The above proof is inductive since the set
B
can be defined as
B=\{b_{\gamma}\}_{\gamma\in\texttt{Ord}}
, where
Ord
is the class of all ordinals and
b_{\gamma}
’s are defined by (transfinite) induction as follows:
b_{\gamma}
is a member of
A
, if any, such that
f(b_{\gamma})=\{b_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\gamma\}
; if there is no such member, then
b_{\gamma}
is not defined for
\gamma
(neither for any bigger ordinal).

Another inductive proof goes with the same lines by taking
(I,\prec,B)
to be a
\sqsubseteq
-maximal well-ordered set
I
with a mapping
B:I\rightarrow\wp A
(denoted
i\mapsto B_{i}\subseteq A
, for each
i\in I
), in a way that

(\bm{\star}^{\prime})\;\forall j\in I\;\forall b\in B_{j}:f(b)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\prec j}B_{i}.

Here,
(I,\prec,B)\sqsubseteq(I^{\prime},\prec^{\prime},B^{\prime})
means that
(I,\prec)
is an initial segment of, or equal to,
(I^{\prime},\prec^{\prime})
and moreover
B_{i}^{\prime}=B_{i}
for each
i\in I
.
For such a
\sqsubseteq
-maximal
(I,\prec,B)
, the set
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
is not in the range of
f
, since otherwise the bigger set
I^{\prime}=I\cup\{\bot\}
(for a new
\bot\not\in I
) with
I\prec\bot
and
B_{\bot}=\{a\}
would satisfy
\bm{\star}^{\prime}
as well, contradicting the
\sqsubseteq
-maximality of
I
.
The main idea of the following proof is from
[
10
]
.

Theorem
3.3

(Cantor’s Powerset Theorem,
I​I
)
.

For a function
f:A\rightarrow\wp A
, the set
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\texttt{Ord}}B_{\gamma}
is not in the range of
f
, where
B_{\gamma}
’s are defined inductively by

(\bm{\ast}^{\prime})\;B_{\gamma}=\{x\in A\mid f(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\gamma}B_{i}\}.

Proof
:

If
\mathcal{B}=f(a)
for some
a\in A
, then
a\not\in\mathcal{B}
, since otherwise
a\in B_{\mu}
for the
\in
-minimum
\mu\in{\tt Ord}
would imply
f(a)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\mu}B_{i}
by
\bm{\ast}^{\prime}
, so
a\in f(a)
would imply
a\in B_{i}
for some
i\in\mu
, contradicting the
\in
-minimality of
\mu
; thus,
a\not\in B_{\gamma}
for each
\gamma\in{\tt Ord}
. In particular, for each ordinal
\gamma
, we have
a\not\in B_{\gamma+1}
, and so by
\bm{\ast}^{\prime}
, we have
f(a)\not\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\gamma+1}B_{i}
. Hence, for every
\gamma\in{\tt Ord}
there exists some
c_{\gamma}\in f(a)
such that
c_{\gamma}\not\in\bigcup_{i\in\gamma+1}B_{i}\;({\ddagger})
. By
f(a)=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\texttt{Ord}}B_{\alpha}
, there is some
\delta\in{\tt Ord}
such that
c_{\gamma}\in B_{\delta}
, and so by
{\ddagger}
we have
\gamma\in\delta
(since
\delta\not\in\gamma+1
). Therefore, for each ordinal
\gamma
we have
c_{\gamma}\in B_{\delta}-\bigcup_{i\in\gamma+1}B_{i}
for some bigger ordinal
\delta
(
\ni\gamma
). Thus, the set
\mathcal{B}
exhausts the class
{\tt Ord}
, which is impossible.

❑

We end this report with two observations about the set
\mathcal{B}
above. First, it can be defined as
\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
, where
(I,\prec,B)
is a
\sqsubseteq
-maximal set such that for some well-ordering
\prec
on
I
and some assignment
i\mapsto B_{i}\in\wp A
, the following holds:

(\bm{\ast}^{\prime\prime})\;\forall j\in I:B_{j}=\{x\in A\mid f(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\prec j}B_{i}\}.

Second, it is equal to the set

\mathcal{D}_{\infty}=\{a\in A\mid\bm{\not}\!\exists\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq A\!\!:x_{1}\in f(a)\wedge\bigwedge_{n\in\mathbb{N}}x_{n+1}\in f(x_{n})\},

which was directly shown in
[
6
]
to be out of the
f
’s range.

Proposition
3.4

(
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
)
.

The set
\mathcal{B}
, defined by
(\bm{\ast}^{\prime})
above, is equal to the set
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
(defined above also).

Proof
:

\bm{(}\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{\infty}\bm{)}
: If
x\in\mathcal{B}
but
x\not\in\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
, then for a sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq A
we have
a_{1}\in f(x)
and
a_{j+1}\in f(a_{j})
for each
j=1,2,\cdots
. By
x\in\mathcal{B}
we have
x\in B_{\alpha_{0}}
for some
\alpha_{0}\in{\tt Ord}
and so by
\bm{\ast}^{\prime}
we have
f(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\alpha_{0}}B_{i}
. Thus,
a_{1}\in B_{\alpha_{1}}
for some
\alpha_{1}\in\alpha_{0}
. Continuing this way, by
\bm{\ast}^{\prime}
we have

f(a_{1})\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\alpha_{1}}B_{i}

thus
a_{2}\in B_{\alpha_{2}}
for some
\alpha_{2}\in\alpha_{1}
, and so on and so forth. At the end, we get an infinite decreasing sequence of ordinals
\cdots\in\alpha_{2}\in\alpha_{1}\in\alpha_{0}
, which is a contradiction.

\bm{(}\mathcal{D}_{\infty}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\bm{)}
: If
x\not\in\mathcal{B}
, then
f(x)\not\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, since otherwise if for each
y\in f(x)
there existed some
\alpha_{y}\in{\tt Ord}
such that
y\in B_{\alpha_{y}}
, then for an ordinal
\gamma
which is bigger than all the ordinals in the set
\{\alpha_{y}\mid y\in f(x)\}
, we would have
f(x)\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in\gamma}B_{i}
, thus
x\in B_{\gamma}
by
\bm{\ast}^{\prime}
, so
x\in\mathcal{B}
, contradicting the assumption (of
x\not\in\mathcal{B}
). So, for some
x_{1}\in f(x)
we have
x_{1}\not\in\mathcal{B}
. Thus,
f(x_{1})\not\subseteq\mathcal{B}
again, and so there should exist some
x_{2}\in f(x_{1})
such that
x_{2}\not\in\mathcal{B}
. Repeating this will produce a sequence
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subseteq A
with
x_{1}\in f(x)
and
x_{j+1}\in f(x_{j})
for each
j=1,2,\cdots
. Thus,
x\not\in\mathcal{D}_{\infty}
.

❑

Let us note that the following set, for
n>0
, is also out of the range of
f:A\rightarrow\wp A
(cf.
[
5
,
§24]
):

\mathcal{D}_{n}=\{a\in A\mid\bm{\not}\!\exists\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq A\!\!:x_{1}\in f(a)\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n-1}x_{i+1}\in f(x_{i})\wedge a\in f(x_{n})\}.

Let us recall that Cantor’s (anti-)diagonal set is
\mathcal{D}_{0}=\{a\in A\mid a\not\in f(a)\}
; see
[
8
]
for another new proof of Cantor’s powerset theorem.
