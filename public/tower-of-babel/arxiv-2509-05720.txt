Time-domain sound field estimation using kernel ridge regression
Jesper Brunnström, Martin Bo Møller, Jan Østergaard, Shoichi Koyama, Toon van Waterschoot, Marc Moonen

Abstract
Sound field estimation methods based on kernel ridge regression have proven effective, allowing for strict enforcement of physical properties, in addition to the inclusion of prior knowledge such as directionality of the sound field. These methods have been formulated for single-frequency sound fields, restricting the types of data and prior knowledge that can be used. In this paper, the kernel ridge regression approach is generalized to consider discrete-time sound fields. The proposed method provides time-domain sound field estimates that can be computed in closed form, are guaranteed to be physically realizable, and for which time-domain properties of the sound fields can be exploited to improve estimation performance. Exploiting prior information on the time-domain behaviour of room impulse responses, the estimation performance of the proposed method is shown to be improved using a time-domain data weighting, demonstrating the usefulness of the proposed approach. It is further shown using both simulated and real data that the time-domain data weighting can be combined with a directional weighting, exploiting prior knowledge of both spatial and temporal properties of the room impulse responses. The theoretical framework of the proposed method enables solving a broader class of sound field estimation problems using kernel ridge regression where it would be required to consider the time-domain response rather than the frequency-domain response of each frequency separately.



I
INTRODUCTION

Sound field estimation is a fundamental audio signal processing problem, where the sound over a continuous spatial region is reconstructed from a limited set of measurements. Sound field estimation is crucially important for a number of sound field reproduction tasks, such as spatial active noise control, sound zone control, and spatial audio reproduction
[
1
,
2
]
. Sound field estimation is difficult in part due to the cost of equipment and measurement time, generally leading to too few spatial samples being collected for perfect reconstruction
[
3
]
. As a consequence of the undersampling, sound field estimation is an ill-posed inverse problem, requiring prior knowledge to achieve satisfactory estimation performance.

A sound field produced by a spatially localized source in a room emitting an impulse is referred to as a
room impulse response (RIR)
function, and such a function evaluated in a single point is referred to as a
RIR
.
RIR
functions are of particular interest due to their importance in sound field reproduction applications.
RIR
functions have a common structure that can be exploited
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Although such sound fields are mostly assumed to be stationary, the sound field is slowly time-varying
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. The time-variation means that the quality of previous estimates decreases as time passes, leading to a need to re-measure, which in turn requires the sound field estimation process to be practical.

A number of different approaches exist for sound field estimation, where different types of prior knowledge are exploited. If there exists a basis in which the sound field is sparse, the sound field can be reconstructed from fewer measurements, which is exploited in
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. A Bayesian approach allows for the inclusion of prior knowledge in the form of a probabilistic model
[
17
,
18
]
. Data-driven approaches have become more popular, which rely on prior knowledge extracted from data
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. The sound field can also be modelled using a set of equivalent sources, with which prior knowledge in the form of sparsity
[
24
]
or an optimal mass transport regularization
[
25
]
can be exploited.

The approach considered in this paper is based on
kernel ridge regression (KRR)

[
26
]
.
KRR
allows for optimization over a function space constrained to satisfy the Helmholtz equation, i.e. contains only physically realizable sound fields
[
27
]
. The
KRR
approach has been shown to be practical to apply to spatial active noise control
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
, sound zone control
[
31
]
and sound field reproduction
[
32
]
, in part due to the estimator being linear as a function of the data, which leads to practical algorithms when the estimators are integrated into the control methods. The
KRR
approach has been able to include more information about the sound field through a directional weighting
[
33
,
30
,
31
,
34
]
. The
KRR
approach has also been shown to be closely related to Bayesian inference of spherical harmonic wave functions
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
, as well as Gaussian process regression
[
40
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
]
. The strength of the approach lies in the representer theorems
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
, which characterize the optimal solutions of a large class of
KRR
problems, reducing infinite-dimensional optimization problems to finite-dimensional problems.

Existing sound field estimation methods based on
KRR

[
37
,
33
,
34
]
consider single-frequency sound fields. This restricts the set of problems that can be solved to those which can be formulated for each frequency individually, in addition to limiting the type of prior knowledge that can be used. Although a continuous-time formulation was proposed in
[
51
]
, in contrast to other kernel-based methods, no closed form solution is available, making the method costly to use. In addition, the obtained solution only satisfies the wave equation approximately by minimizing a physics-informed cost, rather than guaranteeing that such a constraint is exactly satisfied.

A generalization of the
KRR
approach is proposed in this paper, with which discrete-time sound fields in continuous space are modelled. The proposed method provides estimates which are guaranteed to satisfy the wave equation with a kernel that is obtained in closed form. Using the
discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
, a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
modelling discrete-time sound fields is derived. The model allows for the inclusion of prior knowledge both relating to the time-domain and frequency-domain properties of the sound field. This is shown to be useful for the identification of
RIRs
, which have a high degree of predictable time-domain behaviour.

The paper makes a theoretical contribution, by constructing sound field models and function spaces with which sound field-related optimization problems beyond those considered in the paper can be solved with
KRR
. Compared to previous methods, the generalization to time-domain sound fields allows for a broader class of problems to be considered using
KRR
. Such problems include those where the sound field at multiple frequencies must be considered jointly, or where the time-domain response is of crucial importance.

The structure of the paper is as follows. In section
II
the sound field estimation problem is introduced, with a model of the available data. In section
III
the sound field model is introduced, and a
RKHS
modelling time-domain sound fields is defined. In section
IV
the kernel ridge regression problem is defined and solved, producing a closed form expression for the optimal sound field estimate. In section
V
the inclusion of prior knowledge is considered, introducing a directionally weighted regularization, and a data-weighting using the time-domain properties of the
RIRs
. In section
VI
the proposed method is evaluated in a number of experiments. Finally the paper is concluded in section
VII
. To facilitate easy use of the results, code associated with the proposed method is made available at
github.com/sounds-research/aspcol
.

II
Problem statement

II-A

Data model

The objective is to estimate the sound field from the available data, as will be described in this section. Consider a simply connected region
\Omega\subset\real^{3}
in a room within which the
RIRs
from one or more sources should be estimated. Within the region
\Omega
there are
M
omnidirectional microphones placed at the positions
\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},\dots,\bm{r}_{M}\in\Omega
. The
RIR
associated with a single source and a single position
\bm{r}
can be modelled as an
L
-length sequence, representing a
finite impulse response (FIR)
filter. The set of all such sequences is denoted by
\mathfrak{t}=\real^{L}
, which is considered an inner product space with the standard Euclidean inner product
\langle\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}(\tilde{\bm{b}})_{n}(\tilde{\bm{a}})_{n}=\tilde{\bm{b}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{a}}
.

The
RIR
associated with a single source for all positions
\bm{r}\in\Omega
can then be represented by a vector-valued function
\tilde{\bm{u}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}
, which corresponds to the sound field that should be estimated. The signal recorded by a microphone placed at position
\bm{r}_{m}\in\Omega
at discrete-time index
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is

p(\bm{r}_{m},n)=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}_{m}),\tilde{\bm{\phi}}(n)\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}+s_{m}(n),

(1)

where
\tilde{\bm{\phi}}(n)\in\mathfrak{t}
denotes a vector containing the
L
most recent values of the source signal, and
s_{m}
represents the noise. It is assumed that there is one source of interest active at any given time for which the source signal
\phi
is known, and any other sound in the recording is considered part of the noise
s_{m}
. The assumption holds for instance when estimating the
RIR
of a loudspeaker under the user’s control.

If the signal in (
1
) is collected for a sufficient number of samples for each microphone, the measured signal can be deconvolved to obtain

\tilde{\bm{h}}_{m}=\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}_{m})+\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{m},

(2)

where
\tilde{\bm{\epsilon}}_{m}\in\mathfrak{t}
is an additive error that can depend on the measurement noise
s
, source signal
\phi
, and the deconvolution process. The available data associated with microphone
m
is therefore the vector
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{m}\in\mathfrak{t}
. The task considered in this paper is to estimate the
RIR
function
\tilde{\bm{u}}
from data of the form (
2
).

II-B

Frequency-domain representation

To relate the time-domain signals to their frequency-domain characteristics, the
DFT
is used. The conventional
DFT
contains redundancy when applied to real-valued signals, expressed as a conjugate symmetry in the frequency-domain signals. The redundancy can be eliminated by only considering the first
L_{f}=\lfloor\frac{L}{2}\rfloor+1
frequency-domain values. The set of frequency-domain signals is then defined as
\mathfrak{f}=\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}-2}\times\real^{2}
if
L
is even and
\mathfrak{f}=\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}-1}\times\real
if
L
is odd, due to the frequencies at
l=0
and
l=\frac{L}{2}
being strictly real-valued, where
l
is the discrete frequency index. The
DFT

\mathcal{F}:\mathfrak{t}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
is defined as
1
1

1

The time convention is chosen to be consistent with the acoustics literature
[
52
]
, but is opposite to some popular implementations
[
53
,
54
]
.

(\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}})_{l}=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}e^{2\pi jnl/L}(\tilde{\bm{a}})_{n}\quad 0\leq l<L_{f},

(3)

with the inverse
DFT

\mathcal{F}^{-1}:\mathfrak{f}\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}
defined as

(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{a})_{n}=\mathfrak{Re}\biggl[\sum_{l=0}^{L_{f}-1}c_{l}e^{-2\pi jnl/L}(\bm{a})_{l}\biggr]\quad 0\leq n<L,

(4)

where the real-part operator is defined as
\mathfrak{Re}[a]=\frac{1}{2}(a+\overline{a})
for any vector
a
, and

c_{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{L}&\quad\text{if }0<l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{1}{L}&\quad\text{if }l=0\text{ or }l=\frac{L}{2}.\end{cases}

(5)

The forward transform can be written in matrix form as
\bm{a}=\bm{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}
where
\bm{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}\times L}
defined by
(\bm{F})_{ln}=e^{2\pi jnl/L}
. The inverse transform is
\tilde{\bm{a}}=\mathfrak{Re}[\bm{B}\bm{C}\bm{a}]
, where
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L_{f}}
is defined by
(\bm{B})_{nl}=e^{-2\pi jnl/L}
and the diagonal matrix
\bm{C}\in\real^{L_{f}\times L_{f}}
is defined by
(\bm{C})_{ll}=c_{l}
.

It is desirable for the transform to be unitary, like the conventional
DFT
is up to a normalization constant when considered on spaces with the standard Euclidean inner product. This property leads to equivalent inner products and norms of the time and frequency-domain representations, specifically
\langle\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}=\langle\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}},\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}
. A unitary transform satisfies
\mathcal{F}^{*}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{F}^{*}:\mathfrak{f}\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}
is the Hilbert space adjoint defined by
\langle\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}},\bm{b}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{a}},\mathcal{F}^{*}\bm{b}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}
for all
\tilde{\bm{a}}\in\mathfrak{t},\bm{b}\in\mathfrak{f}
. The unitary property of
\mathcal{F}
can be used to determine the inner product of
\mathfrak{f}
as

\langle\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}=\sum_{l=0}^{L_{f}-1}c_{l}\mathfrak{Re}[(\bm{a})_{l}\overline{(\bm{b})}_{l}]=\mathfrak{Re}[\bm{b}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C}\bm{a}].

(6)

The transform
\mathcal{F}
is then linear, bijective, and unitary. Linear operators on
\mathfrak{t}
and
\mathfrak{f}
, the sets of which are referred to as
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
and
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{f})
respectively, are characterized in Appendix
A
.

III
Sound field model

In this section, the goal is to construct a function space containing physically realizable sound fields
\tilde{\bm{u}}
. The
RKHS
of
[
27
,
33
]
which is defined for a single frequency will be adapted to the
DFT
-based frequency-domain representation in Section
II-B
to define a
RKHS
representing time-domain sound fields in Section
III-D
.

III-A

Frequency-domain plane wave model

Consider a sound field in a source-free region
\Omega\subset\real^{3}
, represented by the sound pressure
u_{\omega}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, a function of the position
\bm{r}\in\Omega
for a specific angular frequency
\omega\in\real_{\geq 0}
. Such a sound field in a source-free region satisfies the homogenous Helmholtz equation

\Bigl(\Delta+\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\Bigr)u_{\omega}=0,

(7)

where
\Delta
is the Laplace operator, and
c\in\real_{>0}
is the speed of sound, assumed to be constant in
\Omega
.

A solution to (
7
) can be approximated arbitrarily well with the Herglotz integral
[
33
, Appendix A]
, defined as

u_{\omega}(\bm{r})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{-j\frac{\omega}{c}\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}\mathring{u}_{\omega}(\hat{\bm{d}})\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}}).

(8)

The directionality function
\mathring{u}_{\omega}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a square integrable function, the space of which is denoted by
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C})
, within which functions are considered equivalent if they only differ on a set of measure zero
[
55
, Section 2.4.6]
. The directionality function evaluated for a single direction
\hat{\bm{d}}
can be interpreted as the complex coefficient of a plane wave
[
56
,
33
,
34
,
39
]
. The integral is formulated in terms of the natural spherical measure
s
, and
\hat{\bm{d}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
corresponds to the incoming plane wave direction, which is the opposite of the propagation direction.

III-B

Time-domain plane wave model

The sound field model in (
8
) for continuous frequencies
\omega\in\real
should be adapted to the function
\bm{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
representing a sound field for the
L_{f}
sampled frequencies. The proposed sound field model is

\bm{u}(\bm{r})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}})\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}}),

(9)

where
\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{f})
contain the plane wave basis functions, and the directionality function
\mathring{\bm{u}}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
describes the plane wave coefficients at all sampled frequencies. The basis functions
\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})
can be represented by the diagonal matrix

(\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}}))_{ll}=\begin{cases}e^{-j\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}&0\leq l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\cos(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}})&\text{if }l=\frac{L}{2}.\end{cases}

(10)

The sampled angular frequency
\omega_{l}
corresponds to the continuous angular frequency
\omega_{l}=$\frac{2\pi f_{s}l}{L}\text{\,}\mathrm{rad}$
where
f_{s}
is the sampling rate in
\mathrm{Hz}
.

Due to the real-value constraint at
l=\frac{L}{2}
, the standard plane wave is exchanged for
\mathfrak{Re}[e^{-j\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}]=\cos(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}})
. The real-value constraint is satisfied without changes for
l=0
. This means that the physical correctness of the model is lost for
l=\frac{L}{2}
, in order to maintain the properties of
\mathfrak{f}
as sequences obtained from the
DFT
. However, this is not considered to be a significant drawback in practice. Whenever
L
is odd, there is no frequency with index
\frac{L}{2}
. Otherwise if
L
is even, given that any measurement is processed with an ideal anti-aliasing filter having a zero output at the Nyquist frequency, no error is incurred.

A time-domain sound field that is either
L
samples in length, or periodic with period
L
can be represented by a function
\tilde{\bm{u}}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}
. Any such sound field can be defined via the
DFT
as

\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{u}(\bm{r}).

(11)

Due to the invertibility of the
DFT
, both a frequency-domain and time-domain sound field can be represented by the same directionality function
\mathring{\bm{u}}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
.

III-C

Reproducing kernel Hilbert space for frequency-domain sound fields

Following
[
27
,
33
]
, the function space for sound fields
u_{\omega}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of a single frequency
\omega
can be represented by

\mathcal{H}_{\omega}=\{\mathcal{A}_{\omega}\mathring{u}_{\omega}|\mathring{u}_{\omega}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{C})\},

(12)

where
\mathcal{A}_{\omega}
is the operator transforming a function
\mathring{u}_{\omega}
to
u_{\omega}
as in (
8
). The associated inner product is

\displaystyle\langle u_{\omega},v_{\omega}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathring{u}_{\omega}(\hat{\bm{d}})\overline{\mathring{v}_{\omega}(\hat{\bm{d}})}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}}).

(13)

The Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\omega}
is a
RKHS
, and is characterized by the kernel function
\kappa_{\omega}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined as
\kappa_{\omega}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=j_{0}\bigl(\frac{\omega}{c}\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\bigr)
, where
j_{0}
is the zeroth order spherical Bessel function of the first kind
[
27
]
.

An analogous function space for sound fields
\bm{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
on the considered set of sampled frequencies can be defined as

\mathcal{H}=\{\mathcal{A}\mathring{\bm{u}}|\mathring{\bm{u}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f})\},

(14)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the operator transforming functions
\mathring{\bm{u}}
to
\bm{u}
corresponding to (
9
). The associated inner product analogous to (
13
) can be defined as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\mathring{\bm{v}}(\hat{\bm{d}})\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}}).

(15)

The kernel function of the
RKHS

\mathcal{H}
is a positive definite function
\Gamma:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{f})
, which can be represented by the diagonal matrix

\displaystyle(\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}))_{ll}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\kappa_{\omega_{l}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})&0\leq l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{\omega_{l}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})+\kappa_{\omega_{l}}(\bm{r},-\bm{r}^{\prime}))&l=\frac{L}{2},\end{cases}

(16)

as is shown in appendix
B
.

III-D

Reproducing kernel Hilbert space for time-domain sound fields

There is a unique time-domain sound field
\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
for each frequency-domain sound field
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{H}
, due to the invertibility of the
DFT
. The set of time-domain sound fields can therefore be defined in terms of the frequency-domain sound fields as

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\{\tilde{\bm{u}}\;|\;\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{u}(\bm{r}),\;\forall\bm{r}\in\Omega,\bm{u}\in\mathcal{H}\}.

(17)

Due to the equivalence of the time- and frequency-domain representations, the natural choice of inner product is the same as for
\mathcal{H}
in (
15
), specifically,
\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{v}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}=\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. The reproducing kernel
\tilde{\Gamma}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
is obtained in terms of (
16
) as

\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{F}=\mathfrak{Re}[\bm{B}\bm{C}\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\bm{F}],

(18)

as is shown in appendix
C
.

IV
Sound field estimation

IV-A

Kernel ridge regression

To estimate a sound field, the task is to find a function in
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
that corresponds to data in the form of (
2
). Since
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
is a
RKHS
, the task can be accomplished through
KRR
. An optimization problem can then be formulated as

\min_{\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lVert\tilde{\bm{h}}_{m}-\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}_{m})\rVert_{\mathfrak{t}}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\tilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}^{2},

(19)

where
\lambda\in\real_{\geq 0}
is a regularization parameter.

Due to the representer theorem
[
49
]
, the optimal solution of (
19
) evaluated at any
\bm{r}\in\Omega
has the form

\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\text{opt}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{m})\tilde{\bm{a}}_{m}

(20)

for a set of unknown parameters
\tilde{\bm{a}}_{m}\in\mathfrak{t}
for
m=1,2,\dots,M
. Inserting (
20
) into (
19
), an equivalent finite-dimensional optimization problem is obtained as

\min_{\tilde{\bm{a}}\in\mathfrak{t}^{M}}\;\langle(\tilde{\Gamma}^{2}+\lambda\tilde{\Gamma})\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}}-2\langle\tilde{\Gamma}\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}}+\langle\tilde{\bm{h}},\tilde{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}},

(21)

where the space
\mathfrak{t}^{M}
is the direct sum
\bigoplus_{m=1}^{M}\mathfrak{t}
with inner product
\langle\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\langle\tilde{\bm{a}}_{m},\tilde{\bm{b}}_{m}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}
, corresponding to
ML
. The stacked vectors in (
21
) are

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\bm{a}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\bm{a}}_{M})\in\mathfrak{t}^{M}

(22)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{h}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\bm{h}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\bm{h}}_{M})\in\mathfrak{t}^{M},

and the stacked kernel
\tilde{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t}^{M})
is represented by the matrix

\tilde{\Gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{1})&\dots&\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{M})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}_{M},\bm{r}_{1})&\dots&\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}_{M},\bm{r}_{M})\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(23)

Note that
\tilde{\Gamma}
is self-adjoint, due to
\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r})^{*}
.

By computing the gradient of (
21
) and setting it to zero, and by observing that the problem is convex, the unique optimum is obtained, which is

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\text{opt}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\Gamma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\tilde{\bm{h}}.

(24)

The optimal parameters (
24
) can be inserted in (
20
) to obtain an estimate of the sound field for any position
\bm{r}\in\Omega
. The solution in (
24
) is equivalent to the frequency-domain solution in
[
27
]
, as is shown in Appendix
D
.

IV-B

Kernel ridge regression with prior knowledge

Obtaining a sufficiently large number of spatial samples is difficult in practice, hence it is important to incorporate prior knowledge into the optimization problem, in order to maintain estimation performance despite undersampling. This can be done in the
KRR
framework by adding an appropriate regularization.

A more general
KRR
formulation than (
19
) is proposed here, defined as

\min_{\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lVert\tilde{\bm{h}}_{m}-\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}_{m})\rVert_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2},

(25)

where
\linreg:\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}
is a linear operator,
\mathcal{Z}
is an arbitrary Hilbert space, and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}}
is a weighted norm induced by the inner product
\langle\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{b}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}
for a self-adjoint positive definite linear operator
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
. The linear operator
\linreg
should penalize functions that are unlikely to represent the true sound field by increasing their norm. Functions that closely match the prior knowledge should be given a small norm, promoting them as optimal solutions. The data weighting defined by
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}
allows for e.g. different levels of importance to be given to different parts of the data. How to choose
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}
and
\linreg
will be investigated in Section
V
.

In order to solve (
25
) in a straightforward manner, the regularization operator
\linreg
is restricted to be invertible with domain
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. The regularization term can then be written as
\lVert\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}=\langle\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}},\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}}\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{u}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}=\lVert\tilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}^{2}
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}
is an inner product space over the same set of functions as
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
, but with the inner product
\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\bm{v}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}=\langle\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}},\linreg\tilde{\bm{v}}\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}}
. A necessary implication of this assumption on
\linreg
is that
\mathcal{Z}
has to be infinite-dimensional. The optimization problem in (
25
) is then equivalent to

\min_{\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lVert\tilde{\bm{h}}_{m}-\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}_{m})\rVert_{\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}}^{2}+\lambda\lVert\tilde{\bm{u}}\rVert_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}^{2},

(26)

The kernel function
\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
associated with
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}
is defined by

\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{c}}=\Bigl((\linreg^{*}\linreg)^{-1}\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{c}}\Bigl)(\bm{r})

(27)

for all
\tilde{\bm{c}}\in\mathfrak{t}
, as is shown in appendix
E
.

Due to the representer theorem
[
49
]
, the optimal solution of (
26
) evaluated at an arbitrary
\bm{r}\in\Omega
can be written in the form

\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\text{opt}}(\bm{r})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{m})\tilde{\bm{a}}_{m}

(28)

for the unknown parameters
\tilde{\bm{a}}_{m}\in\mathfrak{t}
for
m=1,\dots,M
.

Inserting (
28
) into (
26
) an equivalent finite-dimensional optimization problem is obtained as

\displaystyle\min_{\tilde{\bm{a}}\in\mathfrak{t}^{M}}

\displaystyle=\langle(\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}+\lambda\tilde{\Gamma}_{r})\tilde{\bm{a}},\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}}

(29)

\displaystyle-2\langle\tilde{\bm{Q}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}\tilde{\bm{h}},\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}}+\langle\tilde{\bm{Q}}\tilde{\bm{h}},\tilde{\bm{h}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}^{M}},

where
\tilde{\bm{Q}}=\diag\{\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t}^{M})
, with
\diag
defined such that
\tilde{\bm{Q}}\tilde{\bm{a}}=(\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{1}\tilde{\bm{a}}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{M}\tilde{\bm{a}}_{M})
. Both
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}
are self-adjoint, which was used to simplify the expression.

By computing the gradient of (
29
) and setting it to zero, the unique optimum is obtained, which is

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{a}}^{\text{opt}}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}+\lambda\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{-1})^{-1}\tilde{\bm{h}},

(30)

and can be inserted into (
28
) to obtain the sound field estimate for any
\bm{r}\in\Omega
.

V
Regularization and data weighting

V-A

Directional regularization

An informative choice of regularization operator
\linreg
can improve the estimate in (
30
). It has been shown in
[
33
,
30
,
31
,
34
]
that a directional weighting can significantly improve estimation performance by exploiting prior knowledge of the propagation direction of the sound field. An analogous but generalized directional weighting can be used in the proposed
RKHS

\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. The directional weighting operator
\mathcal{R}:\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
can be defined as

(\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}})(\bm{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}})ds(\hat{\bm{d}}),

(31)

where
\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}}):\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{f})
. The adjoint operator
\linreg^{*}:\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
is then

(\linreg^{*}\tilde{\bm{u}})(\bm{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})^{*}\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}})ds(\hat{\bm{d}}).

(32)

The kernel function
\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
of the associated space
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}
is then

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})

(33)

\displaystyle\Bigl(\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})^{*}\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})\Bigr)^{-1}\bm{E}(-\bm{r}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{d}})ds(\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}.

where it is used that
\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})^{*}=\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})
. The expression corresponds to (
27
) for the specific choice of
\linreg
defined in (
31
). The kernel function can be used in (
30
) to obtain a sound field estimate. If
\bm{W}
is chosen to be diagonal for all
\hat{\bm{d}}
, the frequency-wise weighting from previous works is recovered. The directional weighting defined here has more degrees of freedom as it also allows the relationship between frequencies to be taken into account. Finding general weighting functions
\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})
from data, and solving the integral in (
33
) numerically has been explored for the single-frequency case in
[
34
,
57
]
.

The integral in (
33
) can be simplified if the directional weighting function is diagonal and each element takes the form
(\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}}))_{ll}=e^{\frac{\beta_{l}}{2}\bm{\eta}_{l}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}
, where
\bm{\eta}_{l}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
represents the direction of the weighting and
\beta_{l}\in\real_{\geq 0}
represents the strength of the weighting
[
33
]
. This directional weighting is up to a scaling equivalent to the von Mises-Fisher distribution
[
58
, Section 3.5.4]
. With this weighting, estimates where the sound field is primarily propagating in the direction
\bm{\eta}_{l}
are preferred.

The weighting function
\bm{W}
is then self-adjoint, which leads to
(\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}})^{*}\bm{W}(\hat{\bm{d}}))_{ll}=e^{\beta_{l}\bm{\eta}_{l}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}
. The strength of the weighting is restricted to be
\beta_{l}=0
for
l=\frac{L}{2}
to ensure a closed-form solution of (
33
). The regularized kernel function then follows from the identity (
50
), and can be represented by a diagonal matrix defined as

(\Gamma_{r}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}))_{ll}=\begin{cases}\kappa_{l}^{\text{dir}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})&0\leq l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{\omega_{l}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})+\kappa_{\omega_{l}}(\bm{r},-\bm{r}^{\prime}))&l=\frac{L}{2},\end{cases}

(34)

where the function
\kappa_{l}^{\text{dir}}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is

\displaystyle\kappa_{l}^{\text{dir}}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=j_{0}\Bigl(\sqrt{\bm{\xi}_{l}^{\top}\bm{\xi}_{l}}\Bigr)

(35)

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}_{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})-j\beta_{l}\bm{\eta}_{l}.

V-B

Data weighting envelopes

Fig. 1:
Examples of envelopes used for the data weighting
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
.

The data weighting operator
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
should be chosen such that a higher weight is given to data of high quality or importance. The time-domain characteristics of
RIRs
are predictable, which can be used to construct such a weighting.
RIRs
generally exhibit an exponential decrease after an initial delay, while noise affecting
RIRs
is distributed uniformly in time. Therefore, the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
varies drastically over the duration of the
RIR
, and the data could be weighted accordingly.

With this motivation, the data weighting for each microphone
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}
is chosen to be diagonal, with diagonal elements
(\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m})_{ll}=(\tilde{\bm{q}}_{m})_{l}
for
l=0,\dots,L-1
, where the time-domain sequence
\tilde{\bm{q}}_{m}\in\mathfrak{t}
is referred to as an envelope. The envelope is intended to be an approximation of the magnitude of the
RIR
, which then also encodes the
SNR
over time. Commonly available prior knowledge that can be used to construct the envelope are acoustic parameters of the space, as well as the position of the loudspeaker and microphones.

An
individual
version of the envelope can be considered, where
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}
is created from information associated with microphone
m
. A
non-individual
version can be considered where aggregate information is used from all microphones, leading to
\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m}=\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m^{\prime}}
for all
m,m^{\prime}
. The former requires more information, but allows for more detailed control of the weighting.

The inverse data-weighting operator is used in (
30
), therefore it must be be invertible, implying that all elements of
\tilde{\bm{q}}
must be non-zero. It is sufficient to choose each element according to
(\tilde{\bm{Q}}_{m})_{ll}=\max(q_{\text{min}},(\tilde{\bm{q}}_{m})_{l})
for some small
q_{\text{min}}\in\real_{>0}
.

Four types of envelopes are considered here, which differ in how accurate they approximate the
RIR
, and which prior knowledge is required. Examples of the considered envelopes can be seen in Fig.
1
.

V-B
1
Uniform

The envelope is set to
(\tilde{\bm{q}})_{l}=1
for all
l
, corresponding to an identity
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
, whereby all data is weighted equally. The weighting is therefore equivalent to previous methods without data weighting. The
individual
version of this envelope equals the
non-individual
version.

V-B
2
Exponential

The propagation delay
l_{0}
in samples and the reverberation time
\text{RT}_{60}
is used to model the
RIR

[
59
]
. These two pieces of information define the envelope as

(\tilde{\bm{q}})_{l}=\begin{cases}10^{3(l-l_{0})/(\tau_{\text{init}}f_{s})}&l<l_{0}\\
10^{-3(l-l_{0})/(\tau_{\text{decay}}f_{s})}&l_{0}\leq l.\end{cases}

(36)

The initial time-constant
\tau_{\text{init}}
is meant to capture the fast but non-instantaneous onset of a sampled
RIR
, and can be viewed as a parameter to be chosen. The second time-constant
\tau_{\text{decay}}
is set according to the measured
\text{RT}_{60}
. Note that the delay induced by the recording equipment should be included in the propagation delay. The
non-individual
envelope can make use of the minimum propagation time of all microphones, and the median
\text{RT}_{60}
.

V-B
3
Linear

The same information as
exponential
is required, but a linear function is used instead of an exponential function, defined as

(\tilde{\bm{q}})_{l}=\begin{cases}\frac{l}{l_{0}}&l<l_{0}\\
1-\frac{l-l_{0}}{f_{s}\tau_{\text{decay}}}&l_{0}\geq l\leq l_{0}+f_{s}\tau_{\text{decay}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(37)

The envelope is expected to perform worse than
exponential
, as it does not capture the decay rate that can be expected of a
RIR
.

V-B
4
Oracle

The absolute value of the true
RIRs
at the microphone positions. The envelope has the correct decay rate like
exponential
while also capturing finer detail of the
RIR
. It can be expected to perform better than the other envelopes, while being infeasible to implement in practice. The
non-individual
envelope uses the mean of the absolute values of the
RIRs
at the microphones.

VI
Evaluation

To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, a set of numerical experiments are presented. The evaluation is made by comparing the estimated function
\tilde{\bm{u}}
with the true function
\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\text{true}}
on a set of
E
evaluation points, referred to as
\mathcal{E}=\{\bm{r}_{1},\dots,\bm{r}_{E}\}
. The primary evaluation metric is the
normalized mean square error (NMSE)
, defined as

\text{NMSE}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{E}}\lVert\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r})-\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\text{true}}(\bm{r})\rVert_{\mathfrak{t}}^{2}}{\sum_{\bm{r}\in\mathcal{E}}\lVert\tilde{\bm{u}}^{\text{true}}(\bm{r})\rVert_{\mathfrak{t}}^{2}}

(38)

Except where explicitly mentioned, all results shown are the mean results from 10 experiments, with random microphone positions and noise realizations. All experiments are performed at a sampling rate of
f_{s}=$1600\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
. The regularization parameter
\lambda
is chosen as
\frac{\sigma_{p}^{2}-\sigma_{s}^{2}}{10\sigma_{s}^{2}}
, where
\sigma_{p}^{2}
and
\sigma_{s}^{2}
are the measured powers of the microphone signal and the noise signal respectively, equivalent to
\frac{1}{10\text{SNR}}
. The minimum value for all envelopes is chosen as
q_{\text{min}}=10^{-6}
, and the initial time-constant for the
exponential
envelope is chosen as
\tau_{\text{init}}=$0.05\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.

In the following, the three considered types of sound field data will be described, followed by the four noise models, and finally the evaluation results will be presented.

VII
Conclusion

A sound field estimation method has been presented, with which time-domain sound fields can be estimated using kernel ridge regression. The method is a generalization of previous kernel ridge regression methods, allowing for the inclusion of prior knowledge relating to time-domain properties of the sound field. A data weighting has been proposed, which can improve estimation performance especially in noisy conditions, by considering the time-domain characteristics of the
RIR
as prior knowledge. The weighting has been shown to be effectively modelled by only the propagation delay and reverberation time, which is information that can be obtained in practice. In addition, the data weighting has been shown to be even more effective when combined with a directional weighting, which exploits spatial characteristics of the sound field.

Appendix A
Discrete Fourier transform

A linear operator
\bm{B}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{t})
can always be represented by a real matrix
\bm{B}\in\real^{L\times L}
. Its adjoint is then represented by
\bm{B}^{\top}
, which can be shown by

\displaystyle\langle\bm{B}\bm{a},\bm{a}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}

\displaystyle=\bm{a^{\prime}}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{a}=\bm{a^{\prime}}^{\top}(\bm{B}^{\top})^{\top}\bm{a}=\langle\bm{a},\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{a}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}.

(39)

Despite having complex-valued vectors,
\mathfrak{f}
is a real vector space, as can be seen from its inner product (
6
). Any
\bm{a}\in\mathfrak{f}
can be written in terms of its real and complex elements, which for even
L
looks like

\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1}+jb_{1},\dots,a_{\frac{L}{2}-1}+jb_{\frac{L}{2}-1},a_{\frac{L}{2}}).

(40)

The definition is the same for odd
L
, but then
a_{\frac{L}{2}}
is excluded. An isomorphism
\mathcal{S}:\mathfrak{f}\rightarrow\real^{L}
can be defined for even
L
as

\mathcal{S}\bm{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{\frac{L}{2}},b_{1},\dots,b_{\frac{L}{2}-1})\in\real^{L},

(41)

where the inverse
\mathcal{S}^{-1}:\real^{L}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
, and the analogous definitions for odd
L
follow naturally. A linear operator
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{f})
can always be represented by the matrix
\bm{D}\in\real^{L\times L}
, applied to
\bm{a}\in\mathfrak{f}
as

\bm{A}\bm{a}=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\bm{D}\mathcal{S}\bm{a}.

(42)

In contrast, all such linear operators cannot be represented by complex matrices
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}\times L_{f}}
. Some can be represented in that form, and because of its convenience it is the form primarily used in this paper. In order to guarantee that
(\bm{A}\bm{v})_{0}
and
(\bm{A}\bm{v})_{\frac{L}{2}}
are still real-valued, the associated matrix for even and odd
L
should respectively have the form

\bm{A}_{\text{even}}=\begin{bmatrix}a&0&b\\
0&\bm{G}_{\text{even}}&0\\
c&0&d\\
\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bm{A}_{\text{odd}}=\begin{bmatrix}a&0\\
0&\bm{G}_{\text{odd}}\\
\end{bmatrix},

(43)

for
a,b,c,d\in\real
and
\bm{G}_{\text{even}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(L_{f}-2)\times(L_{f}-2)}
,
\bm{G}_{\text{odd}}\in\mathbb{C}^{(L_{f}-1)\times(L_{f}-1)}
. The adjoint
\bm{A}^{*}
is then represented by the matrix
\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C}
, as shown by

\displaystyle\langle\bm{A}\bm{a},\bm{b}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Re}[\bm{b}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C}\bm{A}\bm{a}]

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{Re}[\bm{b}^{\mathsf{H}}(\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C})^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C}\bm{a}]

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{a},\bm{C}^{-1}\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{C}\bm{b}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}.

Appendix B
Frequency-domain reproducing kernel

The point evaluation operator on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{r}}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}
, and is defined by
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{r}}\bm{u}=\bm{u}(\bm{r})
. The reproducing kernel is characterized by the adjoint of the point evaluation operator
\evalop_{\bm{r}}^{*}:\mathfrak{f}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
as
[
49
, Example 1]

\evalop_{\bm{r}}^{*}=\Gamma(\cdot,\bm{r}).

(45)

A direct consequence of (
45
), following from the definition of the adjoint
\langle\evalop_{\bm{r}}\bm{u},\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}=\langle\bm{u},\evalop_{\bm{r}}^{*}\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

[
67
, Ch.2 Theorem 2.2]
, is the well known reproducing property

\displaystyle\langle\bm{u}(\bm{r}),\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}=\langle\bm{u},\Gamma(\cdot,\bm{r})\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}

(46)

for all
\bm{u}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\bm{a}\in\mathfrak{f}
.

The left-hand side of (
46
) can be expanded using (
9
) as

\displaystyle\langle\bm{u}(\bm{r}),\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})

(47)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{a}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}}).

From the inner product definition in (
15
), (
47
) can be identified as the inner product between
\bm{u}
and a function in
\mathcal{H}
defined by the directionality function
\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{a}
. Inserting the directionality function into (
9
) gives

\displaystyle\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\bm{a}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{E}(-\bm{r}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{d}})\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{a},

(48)

for which the integrand is

\displaystyle(\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{E}(-\bm{r}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{d}}))_{ll}

(49)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}e^{-j\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}&0\leq l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}(\cos(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}})\\
\quad+\cos(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}(\bm{r}+\bm{r}^{\prime})^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}))&l=\frac{L}{2}.\end{cases}

The expression can be simplified using the identity
[
33
, Eq.65]

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}e^{-j\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}}}\;ds(\hat{\bm{d}})

\displaystyle=j_{0}(\sqrt{\bm{r}^{\top}\bm{r}}),

(50)

which holds for both real and complex
\bm{r}
. Note that
\sqrt{\bm{r}^{\top}\bm{r}}
is identical to the Euclidian norm when
\bm{r}
is real, but not when it is complex. For real
\bm{r}
, also
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\cos(\bm{r}^{\top}\hat{\bm{d}})\;ds(\hat{\bm{d}})=j_{0}(\sqrt{\bm{r}^{\top}\bm{r}})
holds. The kernel function is then

\displaystyle(\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}))_{ll}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}j_{0}(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert_{2})&0\leq l<\frac{L}{2}\\
\frac{1}{2}(j_{0}(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\lVert\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert_{2})\\
\quad+j_{0}(\frac{\omega_{l}}{c}\lVert\bm{r}+\bm{r}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}))&l=\frac{L}{2}.\end{cases}

(51)

Appendix C
Time-domain reproducing kernel

The time-domain kernel can be derived similarly to the frequency-domain kernel, using the reproducing property
\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}),\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}
for all
\tilde{\bm{a}}\in\mathfrak{t}
and
\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
.

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}),\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})

(52)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}.

The last equality allows the directionality function of
\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}
to be identified as
\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}
, which can be inserted into (
11
) and (
8
) to obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\bm{E}(\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\bm{E}(-\bm{r}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{d}})\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Gamma(\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}},

from which the kernel function can be easily identified.

Appendix D
Equivalence between time- and frequency-domain kernel ridge regression

The proposed method with identity weighting-operators
\linreg
and
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
is equivalent to a previously proposed frequency-domain
KRR
approach
[
27
]
, which will be shown in this section. The equivalence holds except for the Nyquist frequency, which is not explicitly modelled in
[
27
]
as it is derived for continuous frequencies
\omega
. The two methods will therefore give different estimates for the Nyquist frequency. Hence, the following derivation will be made assuming odd
L
where there is no frequency bin at index
\frac{L}{2}
. In practice, the method in
[
27
]
is applied to data obtained using the
DFT
, which means that the data at the Nyquist frequency will also be real-valued, even though this is not explicitly modelled.

The frequency-domain estimate for frequency
\omega
as obtained by
KRR
in
[
27
, Eq. 24]
is given by

\displaystyle p(\bm{r},\omega)

\displaystyle=\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r},\omega)^{\top}(\bm{K}(\omega)+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{p}(\omega),

(54)

where
\bm{p}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
represents the data for all microphones for frequency
\omega
, the system matrix
\bm{K}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
is defined as
(\bm{K}(\omega))_{mm^{\prime}}=\kappa_{\omega}(\bm{r}_{m},\bm{r}_{m^{\prime}})
, and
\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r},\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
is defined as
(\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r},\omega))_{m}=\kappa_{\omega}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{m})
.

Concatenating the variables for each considered frequency, assumed to be the frequencies sampled by the
DFT
, the estimate
\bm{p}(\bm{r})\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}}
is

\displaystyle\bm{p}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r})^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{p},

(55)

where the constituent variables are

\displaystyle\bm{p}

\displaystyle=(\bm{p}(\omega_{0}),\dots,\bm{p}(\omega_{L_{f}-1})\in\mathbb{C}^{ML_{f}}

(56)

\displaystyle\bm{K}

\displaystyle=\diag\{\bm{K}(\omega_{l})\}_{l=0}^{L_{f}-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{ML_{f}\times ML_{f}}

\displaystyle\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\diag\{\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r},\omega_{l})\}_{l=0}^{L_{f}-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{L_{f}\times ML_{f}}.

For comparison, the time-domain estimate shown in (
20
) can be expressed as

\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r})=\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r})(\tilde{\Gamma}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\tilde{\bm{h}},

(57)

where
\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r}):\mathfrak{t}^{M}\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}
can be represented by the matrix

\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r})=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{1})&\dots&\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r},\bm{r}_{M})\end{bmatrix}.

(58)

The time-domain data vector and the frequency-domain data vector can be related by
\tilde{\bm{h}}=\mathcal{F}_{M}^{-1}\bm{P}\bm{p}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{M}^{-1}=\diag\{\mathcal{F}^{-1}\}_{m=1}^{M}:\mathfrak{f}^{M}\rightarrow\mathfrak{t}^{M}
, and
\bm{P}:\mathbb{C}^{ML_{f}}\rightarrow\mathfrak{f}^{M}
is a permutation that changes frequency-major order to microphone-major order, after which the vector can straightforwardly be identified with vectors from
\mathfrak{f}^{M}
. The same constructions can be used for the other quantities in (
55
) and (
57
), which gives the relationships
\tilde{\Gamma}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{M}\bm{P}\bm{K}\bm{P}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{M}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}(\bm{r})=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r})^{\top}\bm{P}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{M}
. Inserting the relationships into (
57
) leads to the expression

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r})^{\top}\bm{P}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{M}(\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{M}\bm{P}\bm{K}\bm{P}^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{M}

(59)

\displaystyle+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{M}\bm{P}\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{\kappa}(\bm{r})^{\top}(\bm{K}+\lambda\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{p}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\bm{p}(\bm{r}),

which is the expected result. This shows that the time-domain solution is the inverse
DFT
of the frequency-domain solution if the frequency-domain data is obtained from the
DFT
.

Appendix E
Regularized kernel function

The regularized
KRR
in (
25
) is an optimization problem over the space
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}
, which has a different inner product and kernel function compared to
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
. The reproducing property is
\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}),\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}
, where the right-hand side can be expanded as
\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\linreg}}=\langle\linreg\tilde{\bm{u}},\linreg\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathcal{Z}}=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\linreg^{*}\linreg\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}
. Expanding the left-hand side gives

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\bm{u}}(\bm{r}),\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\langle\mathring{\bm{u}}(\hat{\bm{d}}),\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\mathfrak{f}}\,ds(\hat{\bm{d}})

(60)

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\bm{u}},\linreg^{*}\linreg\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}.

The last equality allows the directionality function of
\linreg^{*}\linreg\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}
to be identified as
\bm{E}(-\bm{r},\hat{\bm{d}})\mathcal{F}\tilde{\bm{a}}
. This directionality function is identical to the one considered in (
52
), which was determined to correspond to
\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{c}}
, which leads to the equality
\linreg^{*}\linreg\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{c}}=\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{c}}
. With the assumption that the operator
\linreg
is invertible, the equality can be rewritten

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{r}(\cdot,\bm{r})\tilde{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=(\linreg^{*}\linreg)^{-1}\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot,\bm{r}^{\prime})\tilde{\bm{a}},

(61)

which sufficiently defines the regularized kernel.
