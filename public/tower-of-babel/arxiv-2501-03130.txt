SpinSVAR: Estimating Structural Vector Autoregression Assuming Sparse Input
Panagiotis Misiakos, Markus Püschel

Abstract
We introduce SpinSVAR, a novel method for estimating a structural vector autoregression (SVAR) from time-series data under sparse input assumption. Unlike prior approaches using Gaussian noise, we model the input as independent Laplacian variables, enforcing sparsity and yielding a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) based on least absolute error regression. We provide theoretical consistency guarantees for the MLE under mild assumptions. SpinSVAR is efficient: it can leverage GPU acceleration to scale to thousands of nodes. On synthetic data with Laplacian or Bernoulli-uniform inputs, SpinSVAR outperforms state-of-the-art methods in accuracy and runtime. When applied to S&P 500 data, it clusters stocks by sectors and identifies significant structural shocks linked to major price movements, demonstrating the viability of our sparse input assumption.

Introduction

Time series arise in numerous applications where multi-dimensional observations are recorded at regular intervals, such as meteorology
[
Yang et al., 2022
]
, finance
[
Kleinberg, 2013
,
Jiang and Shimizu, 2023
]
, and brain imaging
[
Smith et al., 2011
]
. A fundamental challenge in analyzing time series is causal discovery, which seeks to uncover causal dependencies over time
[
Assaad et al., 2022b
,
Hasan et al., 2023
]
.
If causal effects occur faster than the data’s temporal resolution, they appear instantaneous and can be modeled with a linear structural equation model (SEM)
[
Peters et al., 2017
]
. When the resolution is higher, they appear as lagged effects, typically captured by vector autoregression (VAR)
[
Kilian, 2013
]
. Regardless of the model, recovering true causal relationships requires additional assumptions, such as the absence of latent confounders, identifiability conditions, or access to interventions
[
Vowels et al., 2021
]
, which rarely hold in real-world settings.
For instance, in financial markets, it is nearly impossible to observe all hidden confounders or directly intervene in stock prices.
Instead of identifying true causal effects, we focus on learning instantaneous and lagged dependencies through a structural vector autoregression (SVAR), which unifies a linear SEM and a VAR
[
Hyvärinen et al., 2010
]
.

Structural vector autoregression

Originally introduced by
Sims [1980]
, SVAR has been widely applied in econometrics
[
Lütkepohl, 2005
,
Kilian, 2013
]
and serves as a foundation for causal discovery in time-series data
[
Pamfil et al., 2020
]
. SVAR models linear dependencies between variables, distinguishing between instantaneous effects (occurring within the same time step) and lagged effects (propagating over time). The model naturally associates time-series data with a directed acyclic graph (DAG), which encodes how each time step is generated from previous ones. These relationships collectively form the window graph, a DAG that uniquely determines the SVAR parameters. SVAR further assumes stationarity, meaning that the dependencies remain constant over time.

Challenges and Limitations

Even when abstracting away the need for true causal effects, DAG learning from time-series data remains computationally challenging due to the complexity of temporal dependencies and the high dimensionality of real-world datasets. Theoretically, it generalizes DAG learning from static data, which is already an NP-hard problem
[
Chickering et al., 2004
]
.
Several methods have been proposed to estimate the weighted window graph from time-series data, including approaches specifically tailored for SVAR
[
Hyvärinen et al., 2010
]
. However, many existing methods suffer from critical limitations. Some approaches, such as Granger causality-based methods, learn the summary graph that fails to incorporate time lags
[
Bussmann et al., 2021
]
, while others do not account for instantaneous dependencies
[
Khanna and Tan, 2019
]
.
Most methods face computational challenges when applied to large DAGs, making them impractical for graphs with thousands of nodes
[
Cheng et al., 2024
]
.
Structural shocks, i.e., the input variables of an SVAR
[
Lanne et al., 2017
]
at each node, are often interpreted merely as noise variables in prior work
[
Hyvärinen et al., 2010
,
Pamfil et al., 2020
]
, limiting their interpretability and potential insights into the underlying causal mechanisms.
To address these challenges, we introduce a novel, efficient method that enforces sparsity in the input of the SVAR.

SpinSVAR: Sparse Input SVAR

Hyvärinen et al. [2010]
model SVAR under a non-Gaussian noise assumption for the inputs. We extend this by enforcing sparsity in the input, following
Misiakos et al. [2023]
, and model it as independent Laplacian random variables. The Laplace distribution naturally promotes sparsity
[
Jing et al., 2015
]
due to its sharp peak at zero and heavy tails. Intuitively, this means a few significant independent events drive the observed data through the SVAR structure. This contrasts with prior work, which typically assumes zero-mean Gaussian input, either explicitly
[
Lachapelle et al., 2019
]
or implicitly via mean square error-based optimization objectives
[
Pamfil et al., 2020
,
Sun et al., 2023
,
Tank et al., 2021
]
. By incorporating this Laplacian input model, we derive a maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) based on least absolute error regression, leading to SpinSVAR, a new method for efficient SVAR estimation from time-series data. This framework provides both theoretical and empirical advantages.

Contributions

Our main contributions are:

•

We model sparse SVAR input as independent zero-mean Laplacian variables, yielding an MLE formulation for estimating SVAR parameters.

•

We prove the consistency of this MLE under mild assumptions on the window graph weights.

•

We introduce SpinSVAR, a regularized MLE framework enabling fast, and accurate SVAR estimation from time-series data.

•

In synthetic experiments with sparse SVAR input, generated via Laplacian or Bernoulli-uniform distribution as in
[
Misiakos et al., 2023
]
, SpinSVAR can learn an associated DAG with up to several thousands of nodes and outperforms various state-of-the-art methods.

•

On real-world financial data from the S&P 500 index, we show that the sparse input assumption allows to cluster stocks by sector and identify structural shocks reflecting significant changes in the stock prices.

SVAR with Sparse Input

We introduce notation, the needed background on SVARs, the motivation for sparsity assumption in the input and its statistical modeling using the Laplace distribution.

Time-series data

A multi-dimensional data vector
{\bm{x}}_{t}
, measured at time point
t\in{0,1,\dots,T-1}=[T]
, is written as
{\bm{x}}_{t}=(x_{t,1},x_{t,2},\dots,x_{t,d})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d}
. A time series consists of a sequence of such data vectors
{\bm{x}}_{0},\dots,{\bm{x}}_{T-1}
recorded at consecutive time points. We assume these vectors are stacked as rows in a matrix, representing the entire time series, denoted as
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
.
When multiple realizations of
{\bm{X}}
are available, they are collected as slices of a tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T\times d}
. These can obtained by dividing a long time series into smaller segments of length
T
.

Example: stock market

We consider an example of time-series data from the stock market. We collect daily stock values
{\bm{x}}_{t}
for a particular stock index (e.g., S&P
500
) for, say, 20 years. A time series for one year is denoted with the matrix
{\bm{X}}
and
20
years yield the data tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
.

Model Demonstration

We impose a graph-based model on the generation of time-series data and first illustrate it with a simple example. Suppose that the vector
{\bm{x}}_{t}
at time
t
is generated from the previous time step’s data

according to the equation:

{\bm{x}}_{t}={\bm{x}}_{t-1}{\bm{B}}+{\bm{s}}_{t},

(1)

where
{\bm{s}}_{t}
represents the input variables, commonly referred to as structural shocks
[

]
, though they have also been described as root causes
[

]
. Given
{\bm{s}}_{t}
, the data

is fully determined by the matrix

1

The model in (

[

]
. The
(i,j)

quantifies the influence of

, where

is the set of nodes enumerated as
{\mathbb{V}}={1,2,...,d}
. The primary objective is to learn
{\bm{B}}
from time-series data
\{{\bm{x}}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}
.
The model in (
1
) is stationary, meaning that
{\bm{B}}
remains constant across all time steps. Additionally, it has a time lag of one, as each observation
{\bm{x}}_{t}
depends only on the previous time step
{\bm{x}}_{t-1}
and the newly introduced inputs
{\bm{s}}_{t}
at time
t
.

Example

In the stock market example, the stocks
{1,2,\dots,500}
in the S&P 500 market index would represent the nodes of a graph and
{\bm{B}}
would encode the influences between these stocks. The model then would imply that the value
x_{t,i}
of stock
i
on day
t
is determined by the stock values
{\bm{x}}_{t-1}
from day
t-1
, combined with a structural shock

representing an event occurring on day
t
.

Sparse input

We denote with
{\bm{x}}_{t,\text{past}}=\left({\bm{x}}_{t},...,{\bm{x}}_{t-k}\right),

t\in[T]

{\bm{x}}_{t}

{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times d(k+1)}

{\bm{x}}_{t,\text{past}},\,t\in[T]

(3)

Example

In our stock market example, the structural shocks
{\bm{s}}_{t}
would represent significant events (big news) that trigger changes in the prices of the stocks at day

. Examples include unexpected quarterly results, administrative changes in the company, capital investment, lancing a new product, etc.
It is intuitive that significant events happen rarely and affect few stocks every day, and thus

is sparse. Later, we confirm the sparse input assumption in experiments with real-world financial time series.

Laplace Distribution

In practical applications, input sparsity can only be approximately satisfied. Therefore, we consider a distribution for
{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}
that encourages sparsity formation. A natural choice is the
\text{Laplace}(0,\beta)
distribution, which is characterized by a sharp peak at
0
and heavy tails
[
Jing et al., 2015
]
.

Tibshirani [1996]
introduced the classical LASSO regression by adopting the Laplace prior, leading to the well-known
L^{1}
regularizer that promotes sparsity. The Laplace prior has also been used in Bayesian linear regression
[
Castillo et al., 2015
]
, compressive sensing
[
Babacan et al., 2009
]
, sparse matrix factorization
[
Jing et al., 2015
]
, and sparse principal component analysis (PCA)
[
Guan and Dy, 2009
]
.
Based on this, we impose the following assumption on
{\bm{S}}
and derive its probability density function
f_{S}
:

{\bm{S}}_{t,j}\sim\text{Laplace}(0,\beta)\Leftrightarrow f_{S}({\bm{S}}_{t,j}|\beta)=\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{\left|{\bm{S}}_{t,j}\right|}{\beta}}.

(4)

We denote the unknown ground truth
\beta
parameter as
\beta^{*}
.

Learning the SVAR

In this section, we establish the identifiability of our setting, derive the Laplacian MLE, prove its consistency, and formulate the proposed optimization framework, SpinSVAR.

Identifiability

A fundamental question in causal discovery is whether the graph structure is identifiable from the data
[
Park, 2020
]
.
Let
{\bm{W}}^{*}
be the ground-truth window graph, and
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)
denote the probability density function of the data, parameterized by
({\bm{W}},\beta)
.
Identifiability means that if
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)=f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
, then necessarily
{\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}^{*}
.
This ensures that the window graph
{\bm{W}}^{*}
is uniquely determined by the data distribution.
Theorem
3.1
establishes the identifiability of
{\bm{W}}
and the parameter
\beta
, which is a necessary condition for our consistency result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the time-series model (
3
) with
{\bm{S}}
following a multivariate Laplace distribution (
4
) with
\beta^{*}>\frac{1}{NTd}
.
Then the adjacency matrices
{\bm{B}}_{0},{\bm{B}}_{1},...,{\bm{B}}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and
\beta
are identifiable from the time-series data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
.

Proof sketch.

We unroll
{\bm{W}}
over time into a DAG and rewrite (
3
) as a linear SEM, as explained in
[
Misiakos et al., 2024
]
. The identifiability then follows from LiNGAM
[
Shimizu et al., 2006
]
, since
{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}
follows a Laplacian distribution. The window graph is identified by extracting
{\bm{B}}_{0},{\bm{B}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{B}}_{k}
from the unrolled DAG. The parameter
\beta
is identified using the monotonicity of the probability density function. A full proof is provided in App.
A.2
.
∎

Laplacian MLE

The MLE is a fundamental statistical method for estimating model parameters by maximizing the likelihood function
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)
given the observed data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
. Under the Laplacian noise model (
4
), the probability density function of
{\bm{X}}
is given by (see App.
A.3
for details):

f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)=\frac{\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|^{NT}}{(2\beta)^{NdT}}e^{-\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}/\beta}.

(5)

The MLE seeks to find the optimal parameters by maximizing the likelihood function. Equivalently, we maximize the log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)=\log f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

\displaystyle=NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log(2\beta)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}.

(6)

Thus, the MLE estimate
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
is given by:

\widehat{{\bm{W}}}=\argmax_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right).

(7)

A desirable property of the MLE is that
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}={\bm{W}}^{*}
. Under the assumption of identifiability, this property holds for the population log-likelihood
[
Newey and McFadden, 1994
]
, defined as:

L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}}\left[\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)\right],

(8)

where
L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
represents the expected value of the log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
computed under the ground truth probability density
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
.
Intuitively, it corresponds to the log-likelihood if we had access to infinitely many samples.
The following lemma formalizes this property, with a proof provided in App.
A.4
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that the ground truth parameters
({\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
are identifiable from the data distribution
f_{X}\left({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)
.
Then, the population likelihood
L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
has a unique maximum at
({\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
.

Lemma
3.2
implies that with infinite data, the log-likelihood has a unique global maximizer at the ground truth
{\bm{W}}^{*}
.
However, since we only have a finite dataset, we require a stronger result for the empirical log-likelihood
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
.

Consistency of MLE

We prove the consistency of the MLE, which states that as the amount of data increases,
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
converges in probability to
{\bm{W}}^{*}
. Formally, we show the following result.

Theorem 3.3
.

The maximum log-likelihood estimator (
7
) satisfies the conditions of Theorem 2.5 of
Newey and McFadden [1994]
and thus is consistent under the following assumptions:

•

The space of window graphs is
\mathcal{W}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d(k+1)\times d}
and
{\bm{B}}_{0}
acyclic.

•

\beta\in[a,b]
is bounded, with a lower bound
a>1/NTd
.

Proof sketch.

The proof requires a compact search space for
{\bm{W}}
, which is satisfied by the given bounds on
{\bm{W}}
and
\beta
. Additionally, the set of acyclic matrices is closed, as it can be expressed as the pre-image
h^{-1}(\{0\})
, where
h
is a continuous function characterizing acyclicity
[
Zheng et al., 2018
]
. Identifiability of
{\bm{W}}
and
\beta
is ensured by Theorem
3.1
. Finally, the log-likelihood is continuous, and it can be shown that
\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|
has finite expectation. Under these requirements, Theorem 2.5 of
Newey and McFadden [1994]
then utilizes the uniform law of large numbers to show that
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
converges in probability to
{\bm{W}}^{*}
. A full proof is provided in App.
A.5
.
∎

Our method SpinSVAR

Theorem
3.3
implies that
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
in (
7
) converges in probability to
{\bm{W}}^{*}
as
N\to\infty
. Since the parameter
\beta
is fixed but unknown, we estimate it by maximizing the log-likelihood function (
6
). Following
Ng et al. [2020]
, we compute an estimate
\widehat{\beta}
by solving:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta}=0\Leftrightarrow\widehat{\beta}=\frac{1}{NTd}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}.

(9)

This estimate is consistent in expectation. Indeed, it is true that
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}\right]=NTd\beta^{*}
.
Thus, the log-likelihood maximization problem for approximating
{\bm{W}}
reduces to (see App.
A.6
for details):

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{W}}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\widehat{\beta}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\log\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{d}\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|.

However, directly minimizing (
10
) over the space of DAGs is computationally inefficient. This would require enforcing a hard DAG constraint to restrict
{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
, as in
[
Zheng et al., 2018
]
, where it is implemented via the augmented Lagrangian method. Such an approach demands careful fine-tuning and can lead to numerical instabilities, as demonstrated by
Ng et al. [2020]
. To overcome these challenges, following
Ng et al. [2020]
, we relax the hard acyclicity constraint and introduce a soft regularizer. This approach maintains strong performance while improving efficiency, as demonstrated in our experiments. The final optimization problem for SpinSVAR is formulated as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{W}}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d(k+1)\times d}}\log\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}

(11)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{d}\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|+\lambda_{1}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\cdot h\left({\bm{B}}_{0}\right).

The first term in (
11
) promotes sparsity in the structural shocks, while the remaining terms encourage sparsity in the window graph
{\bm{W}}
and enforce acyclicity in
{\bm{B}}_{0}
, respectively. The acyclicity regularizer
h\left({\bm{B}}_{0}\right)=e^{{\bm{A}}\odot{\bm{A}}}-d
, introduced by
Zheng et al. [2018]
, ensures that
{\bm{B}}_{0}
satisfies the DAG constraint. Notably, (
11
) is well-suited for GPU acceleration using tensor operations, making it highly efficient in practice.
In our implementation, we represent
{\bm{W}}
as the parameter matrix of a (PyTorch) linear layer with
(k+1)d
inputs and
d
outputs. The precomputed
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}
serves as input, and the linear layer’s output is subtracted from the observed data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
. The objective in (
11
) is then computed and optimized using the Adam optimizer
[
Kingma and Ba, 2014
]
. More implementation details can be found in App.
C
.

Since the proposed objective function is non-convex, it may have multiple local optima, and there is no guarantee of convergence to the global maximum. However, in practice, our method performs well and often even recovers the edges of
{\bm{W}}^{*}
without error. This phenomenon, also observed in GOLEM
[
Ng et al., 2020
]
, motivates further theoretical investigation.

Once
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
is obtained via (
11
), we approximate the input
\widehat{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}
:

\widehat{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}={{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}-{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}_{\text{past}}\widehat{{\bm{W}}}.

(12)

In recovering
{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}
from
\widehat{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}
, we are particularly interested in identifying significant structural shocks. To this end, we apply thresholding to filter out insignificant values in
\widehat{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}
. In our experiments, this threshold is selected based on the synthetic data generation process.

Related Work

Time-series causal discovery

Our work falls within the category of continuous optimization methods but differs in its assumption of sparsity in the input of the SVAR. Closely related approaches include functional causal model-based methods such as VAR-LiNGAM
[
Hyvärinen et al., 2010
]
, which estimates an SVAR, as well as TiMINO
[
Peters et al., 2013
]
and NBCB
[
Assaad et al., 2021
]
, which recover only the summary graph that disregards time delays
[
Gong et al., 2023
]

In our experiments, we compare against these methods, as well as others that learn the window graph from observational time-series data, selecting both methodologically relevant approaches and representative alternatives.

Different from our approach, constraint-based methods infer edges using conditional independence tests. Examples include PCMCI

Maximum Likelihood Estimator

By modeling sparsity with a Laplacian distribution, we derive an MLE objective based on least absolute error loss, unlike prior causal discovery methods
[
Ng et al., 2020
,
Pamfil et al., 2020
,
Nauta et al., 2019
]
, which use mean-square loss suited for Gaussian noise.
Peters and Bühlmann [2014]
provide consistency guarantees of the MLE for a linear SEM with equivariant Gaussian errors and GranDAG
[
Lachapelle et al., 2019
]
applies it to nonlinear additive noise models.
However, these methods neither support time-series data nor enforce input sparsity.
For SVAR estimation,
Hyvärinen et al. [2010]
propose a generic MLE for non-Gaussian noise but do not integrate it explicitly in the methodology.
Other MLE approaches for SVAR
[
Lanne et al., 2017
,
Fiorentini and Sentana, 2023
,
Maekawa and Nakanishi, 2023
]
remain generic and are not specific for Laplacian or sparse inputs.

Least Absolute Error and Sparsity

The least absolute error (LAE) loss arises as an MLE when assuming that the SVAR input follows a Laplacian distribution
Chai et al. [2019]
,
Li and Arce [2004]
, enforcing sparsity in the model. LAE has been widely used as a regression objective across various fields, including dynamical systems
[
Jiang et al., 2023
,
He and Sun, 2024
]
, due to its robustness against outliers compared to mean square error (MSE) loss
[
Pollard, 1991
,
Bassett Jr and Koenker, 1978
,
Kumar and Singh, 2015
,
Narula et al., 1999
]
.
Despite this, the only method that employs LAE regression to enforce sparsity in the input of a linear SEM is SparseRC, proposed by
Misiakos et al. [2023]
.

Misiakos et al. [2024]
extended SparseRC to time-series graph learning by unrolling the window graph into a DAG, requiring the estimation of
(dT)^{2}
parameters—rendering it computationally infeasible for our experiments.
Our method advances over SparseRC by formulating a Laplacian MLE to enforce sparse input, providing both consistency guarantees and improved computational efficiency in practice.

6
Limitations

SpinSVAR inherits limitations of structure learning based on SVAR, which implies a linear and stationary model. The directed edges found are not necessarily true causal relations; establishing those would require further assumptions. We implicitly assume no undersampling: the measurement frequency is at least as high as the causal effects frequency. This may affect the stock market experiment where we used daily measurements, but stock market effects happen within split seconds.
In addition, we assume that there are no missing values in the data and the measurements on each node are taken with the same frequency.
Also, while we can learn DAGs with up to thousands of nodes, very large graphs beyond that are still out of reach.
Finally, our work is designed specifically for sparse SVAR input.
In App.
E.6
,
E.7
we include experiments on a simulated financial dataset and the Dream3 gene expression dataset. While our method performs competitively it is not best, potentially because the sparse input assumption (or even linearity) is violated.

7
Conclusion

We proposed SpinSVAR, a novel method for estimating SVARs from time-series data under the assumption of sparse input. By modeling the input as independent Laplacian variables, SpinSVAR is formulated as a maximum likelihood estimator based on least absolute error regression. Our method is supported by theoretical consistency guarantees and demonstrates superior performance over the state-of-the-art in experiments with synthetic and real-world financial datasets. The results highlight the utility of the sparse input assumption in uncovering interpretable structures and identifying significant events in real-world time-series data. This work opens avenues for future research in leveraging sparse input SVARs for causal discovery in time series.

A.1
SVAR stability

Whenever a measurement can be taken in a system, stability in the measured data holds by definition.
For example, temperature measurements or stock price markets are never unbounded.
To ensure that the same happens for synthetic data, one needs to guarantee the stability of the data generation process.
A few prior works mention stability
[
Gong et al., 2015
,
Khanna and Tan, 2019
,
Bellot et al., 2022
,
Malinsky and Spirtes, 2018
]
, and here we want to acknowledge its importance.

Equation (
3
) can be viewed as a discrete-time multi-input multi-output (MIMO) system
[
Skogestad and Postlethwaite, 2005
]
, in which the input is the structural shocks
{\bm{S}}
and the output is the time-series data
{\bm{X}}
.
As the time-series length
T
in (
2
) increases, the values of
{\bm{X}}
can get arbitrarily large.
We desire to find a range of weights for the matrices
{\bm{B}}_{0},{\bm{B}}_{1},...,{\bm{B}}_{k}
that guarantees that our time-series data are bounded.
In particular, we require a condition for the bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO) stability of this system.
This has been already considered by
Lütkepohl [2005]
(linear case, for non-linear refer to
[
Saikkonen, 2001
]
).
The proposed condition requires the roots of the reverse characteristic polynomial to have a modulus less than
1
.
Here, we prove a practical and intuitive condition for stability as a derivation of the
[
Lütkepohl, 2005
]
result.

Transitive closure

To begin, we introduce the definition of the weighted transitive closure of the unrolled DAG (
42
).

\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}=\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}{\bm{A}}+\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}=\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1}=\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}+\overline{{\bm{A}}}\right),

(13)

On the right hand (
13
)
\overline{{\bm{A}}}={\bm{A}}+...+{\bm{A}}^{dT-1}
is the weighted transitive closure
[
Seifert et al., 2023
]
of the unrolled DAG
{\bm{A}}
.

that we are interested in.
This provides a sufficient condition under which the model (

Consider our stock market example.
Then the condition in
A.1
means that for every stock that affects a set of other stocks, each with some factor
<1
, the total sum should be less than
1
.
Of course, this is only a sufficient condition for the data to be bounded, but we believe that it is meaningful to consider that the influences between stocks are of this form in reality.
To understand better why the condition in
A.1
provides bounded data, we can think about it in the following way.
When the
L^{\infty}-
norm is bounded, the total effect of a stock is divided into individual fractions that affect other stocks and doesn’t get iteratively increased (which could be the case with sum
L^{\infty}-
norm
>1
). Bounding the sum of outcoming weights to
1
has also been considered in
[
Seifert et al., 2023
,
Misiakos et al., 2023
]
in the scenario of pollution propagation in a river network.

We further include another submultiplicative property, that we later use on our proofs.

Lemma A.3
.

The
L^{1}-
norm, defined as sum of absolut values of the entries of a matrix is submultiplicative.

Proof.

Consider any two square matrices
{\bm{A}},{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. We want to show that
\left\|{{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|{{\bm{A}}}\right\|_{1}\left\|{{\bm{B}}}\right\|_{1}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\left\|{{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{d}a_{ik}b_{kj}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|a_{ik}b_{kj}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|a_{ik}\right|\left|b_{kj}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left|a_{ik}\right|\mathbbm{1}_{k=l}\left|b_{lj}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left|a_{ik}\right|\left|b_{lj}\right|

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|a_{ik}\right|\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\left|b_{lj}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{{\bm{A}}}\right\|_{1}\left\|{{\bm{B}}}\right\|_{1}

∎

A.2
Identifiability

Theorem A.4
.

Consider the time-series model (
3
) with
{\bm{S}}
following a multivariate Laplace distribution as in (
4
) with
\beta\in[a,b]
and
a>\frac{1}{NTd}
. Then the matrices
{\bm{B}}_{0},{\bm{B}}_{1},...,{\bm{B}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\beta
are identifiable from the time-series data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
.

Proof.

As we explain later in Appendix
B
we can rewrite the SVAR (
3
) as a linear SEM:

{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}={\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}+{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\Leftrightarrow\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}=\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}{\bm{A}}+\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}.

(14)

where
\widetilde{{\bm{X}}},\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times dT}
and
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dT\times dT}
.
The structural shocks follow a Laplacian distribution which implies that
\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}
is non-Gaussian.
Moreover, based on the acyclicity assumption on
{\bm{B}}_{0}
, the unrolled matrix
{\bm{A}}
represents a DAG and therefore (
14
) describes an SEM with non-Gaussian noise variables as in
[
Shimizu et al., 2006
]
.
The identifiability of
{\bm{A}}
then follows from LiNGAM
[
Shimizu et al., 2006
]
.
Moreover, identifiability on
{\bm{A}}
implies identifiability for the parameters
{\bm{B}}_{0},{\bm{B}}_{1},...,{\bm{B}}_{k}
of the window graph
{\bm{W}}
, as desired.

We will now establish identifiability of
\beta
using the monotonicity of the Laplacian probability distribution. Notice, identifiability on
{\bm{W}}
means, that for any
{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
and any
\beta\in[a,b]
, the equation
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)=f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
gives
{\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}^{*}
. This in turn implies that the parameter
\beta
is identifiable. Indeed:

f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta)=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|^{NT}\frac{1}{(2\beta)^{NTd}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta}}

(15)

The derivative with respect to
\beta
is:

\frac{\partial f_{X}}{\partial\beta}=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|^{NT}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}-NTd\right)\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}}{2^{NTd}\beta^{NTd+1}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta}}<0

(16)

Therefore,
f_{X}
is monotonically decreasing and thus bijective for
\beta>\frac{1}{NTd}
.
Therefore:

f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)=f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})\xRightarrow{\text{LiNGAM}}{\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}^{*}\text{ and }f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta)=f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})\xRightarrow{\text{monotonicity}}\beta=\beta^{*}

(17)

Thus,
\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
are identifiable from the data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
.

∎

A.3
MLE computation

Estimator computation

Here we compute the MLE assuming that each entry of the structural shocks
{\bm{S}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times T}
follows independently a Laplace distribution
\text{Laplace}\left(0,\beta\right)
. The multivariate probability density function of
{\bm{S}}
is:

f_{C}({\bm{S}})=\prod_{\tau,j}\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{\left|{\bm{S}}_{\tau,j}\right|}{\beta}}

(18)

Solving with respect to
{\bm{X}}
equation (
3
) gives
{\bm{X}}={\bm{S}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}})^{-1}
where
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{dT\times dT}
is the unrolled DAG matrix of
{\bm{W}}
according to (
42
). Here, we didn’t change our notation, but
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{S}}
are supposed to represent
1\times dT
dimensional vectors. For simplicity we will do this interchange in the following computations as it doesn’t affect the probability distribution. Using this linear transformation the probability density function (pdf) of
{\bm{X}}
, or likelihood of the data, becomes

\displaystyle f_{X}({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{C}\left({\bm{X}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}\right)\right)}{\left|\text{det}\left(\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1}\right)\right|}

\displaystyle=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}\right)\right|\prod_{\tau,j}\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}{\bm{W}}_{:,j}\right|}{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)^{T}\right|\prod_{\tau,j}\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}{\bm{W}}_{:,j}\right|}{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|^{T}\frac{1}{(2\beta)^{dT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{X}}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta}}

Therefore, for
N
realizations of
{\bm{X}}
in the tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
we have that:

\displaystyle f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)

\displaystyle=\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|^{NT}\frac{1}{(2\beta)^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{\mathsfit{W}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta}},

(19)

which in turn gives the log-likelihood for the data:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\log f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

\displaystyle=NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log(2\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}.

(20)

In what follows, for simplicity of notation we will skip the parameter
\beta
and will use
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
and
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)

interchangeably.

A.4
MLE consistency background

We proceed by analyzing the prior theorems that we will use to prove our results.
First, denote with
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
the population log-likelihood
[
Lachapelle et al., 2019
,
Newey and McFadden, 1994
]
, defined as:

L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}}\left[\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)\right].

(21)

Note that we use
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
and
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)

interchangeably, as well as
L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
and
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
.
In essence, the population log-likelihood is the expected value of the log-likelihood function computed with the probability density
f_{X}({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*})
with parameters the ground truth window graph
{\bm{W}}^{*}
and parameter
\beta^{*}
.

Lemma A.5
.

Assume that the ground truth window graph
{\bm{W}}^{*}
and parameter
\beta^{*}
are identifiable from the data distribution. This means, that for
\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)\neq\left({\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)
it is true that
f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)\neq f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)
. Then, the population likelihood
L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
has unique maximum at the true window graph
{\bm{W}}^{*}
and true
\beta^{*}
.

Proof.

We show that
L\left({\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)>L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
for every
\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)\neq\left({\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)
. By simplifying our notation we have:

\displaystyle L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-L\left({\bm{W}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[-\log\frac{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)}{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle>-\log\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[\frac{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)}{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=-\log\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\frac{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)}{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)}f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle=-\log\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle=-\log 1=0

On the second line we used that
\frac{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)}{f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*}\right)}
is non-constant, so we can apply the strict Jensen inequality
[
Newey and McFadden, 1994
]

\mathbb{E}\left[a({\bm{Y}})\right]>\mathbb{E}\left[a({\bm{Y}})\right]
for a convex function
a
and non-constant random variable
{\bm{Y}}
.
∎

As a next result for our toolset to prove the MLE consistency, we include the uniform law of large numbers as stated by
Newey and McFadden [1994]
.

Lemma A.6

(Uniform Law of Large Numbers)
.

Consider that the log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right),\,{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
satisfy the following conditions.

•

The data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}
are independent and identically distributed.

•

\mathcal{W}
is a compact space.

•

\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}|{\bm{W}}\right),\,{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
is continuous at each
{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
with probability
1
.

•

There exists dominating function
D({\bm{W}})
such that
\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|\leq D\left({\bm{W}}\right)
and
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[D({\bm{W}})\right]<\infty
.

Then the population likelihood
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
and the empirical average log-likelihood converges uniformly in probability to it:

\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}|{\bm{W}}\right)-L\left({\bm{W}}\right)\right|\xrightarrow{p}0

(22)

We now present Theorem
A.7
, which establishes the consistency of the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE). This theorem is based on a set of sufficient assumptions for ensuring MLE consistency. For completeness, we include a detailed proof of Theorem
A.7
, leveraging the uniform law of large numbers.

Theorem A.7
.

Consider that the average log-likelihood function
L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)
and population
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
satisfy the following conditions for
{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
:

•

{\bm{W}}^{*}=\argmax_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
is identifiable from the data.

•

\mathcal{W}
is a compact space.

•

\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}|{\bm{W}}\right)
is continuous at each
{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}
with probability
1
.

•

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|\right]<\infty
.

Then, if the maximum of
L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}|{\bm{W}}\right)
is achieved at
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}
then
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}
converges uniformly to
{\bm{W}}^{*}
.

Proof.

We repeat the proof of
Newey and McFadden [1994]
for our scenario.
From the identifiability assumption, Lemma
A.5
implies that
{\bm{W}}^{*}
is the unique and global maximizer of
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
.
Also, if we set
D({\bm{W}})=\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|
, then the conditions of Lemma
22
are satisfied and therefore
L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)
converges uniformly in probability to
L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
.
We will leverage the compactness of the space
\mathcal{W}
to show that their maxima satisfy

\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\xrightarrow{p}{\bm{W}}^{*}

(23)

From the uniform convergence it follows that with probability approaching
1
for any
\epsilon
(or
\epsilon/3
as we use next):

\left|L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)-L\left({\bm{W}}\right)\right|<\epsilon\Leftrightarrow L\left({\bm{W}}\right)-\epsilon<L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)<L\left({\bm{W}}\right)+\epsilon,\,\forall{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}.

(24)

Since by definition
L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}\right)
is a continuous function and
\mathcal{W}
is compact it takes a maximum value at point
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}
. Since
L_{n}\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)\geq L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)
the maximum would satisfy for any
\epsilon>0

L_{n}\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)>L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-\epsilon/3.

(25)

This in combination with (
24
) would imply

L\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)>L_{n}\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)-\epsilon/3>L_{n}\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-2\epsilon/3>L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-\epsilon.

(26)

In essence we have proved that
L\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)
can get arbitrarily close to
L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)
. This in turn gives that
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}
approaches
{\bm{W}}^{*}
with probability
1
as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, if we consider any open interval
\mathcal{I}
containing
{\bm{W}}^{*}
, then
\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{I}^{c}
is compact and we can compute

M=\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{I}^{c}}L\left({\bm{W}}\right)<L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)

(27)

Note that by Lemma
22

L\left({\bm{W}}\right)
is continuous, so the supremum is a finite value.
If we choose
\epsilon=L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-M
then:

L\left(\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\right)>L\left({\bm{W}}^{*}\right)-\epsilon=M

(28)

Thus
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
which concludes the proof.
∎

A.5
MLE consistency for DAGs

We will now show that the MLE computed at (
20
) satisfies the requirements of Theorem
A.7
for consistency. Practically, this result implies that as the amount of available data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
increases, the maximizer
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
of the log-likelihood function
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
converges to the maximizer
{\bm{W}}^{*}
of the population likelihood
L\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
. To begin with we introduce the following useful lemma. In essence, using the continuous characterization of acyclicity
[
Zheng et al., 2018
]
we show that the space of bounded DAGs is also closed and thus compact.

Lemma A.8
.

The set of acyclic matrices
\mathcal{A}=\left\{{\bm{A}}\in[-1,1]^{d\times d}|{\bm{A}}\text{ is acyclic}\right\}
is compact.

Proof.

Note that
Zheng et al. [2018]
proved that

{\bm{A}}\text{ is acyclic}\Leftrightarrow h\left({\bm{A}}\right)=0,

(29)

where
h\left({\bm{A}}\right)=e^{{\bm{A}}\odot{\bm{A}}}-d
is a continuous function. We proceed by showing that
\mathcal{A}
is closed and bounded.

•

Closed:

[-1,1]^{d\times d}
is closed and since
h\left({\bm{A}}\right)
is continuous and
\left\{{\bm{A}}\text{ is acyclic}\right\}=h^{-1}(\{0\})
implies that
\mathcal{A}
is closed
[
Sutherland, 2009
]
.

•

Bounded:

\mathcal{A}
is bounded because
\mathcal{A}\subset[-1,1]^{d\times d}
which is bounded.

Therefore, since
\mathcal{A}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is closed and bounded,
\mathcal{A}
is compact
[
Sutherland, 2009
]
.
∎

Now using this Lemma we are ready to prove our consistency result.

Theorem A.9
.

The maximum log-likelihood estimator of (
20
) satisfies the conditions of Theorem
A.7
and thus is consistent under the following assumptions:

•

The space of window graphs
\mathcal{W}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d(k+1)\times d}
is bounded and
{\bm{B}}_{0}
is acyclic.

•

The Laplacian parameter
\beta\in[a,b]
is bounded with lower bound
a>\frac{1}{dT}
3
3

3

This value in our experiment is at most
\frac{1}{2\cdot 10^{4}}
, so this is a mild assumption.
.

Proof.

We check one-by-one the requirements of Theorem
A.7
.

First, the identifiability of the ground truth
{\bm{W}}^{*}
and
\beta^{*}
follows from Theorem
A.2
.

Also,
({\bm{W}},\beta)\in\mathcal{W}\times[a,b]=\mathcal{A}\times[-1,1]^{dk\times d}\times[a,b]
which is compact because the space
\mathcal{A}
of acyclic graphs
{\bm{B}}_{0}
is compact from Lemma

and

and

are both closed and bounded and thus compact according to

.

Moreover, the log-likelihood

\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)=NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log(2\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}

(30)

is continuous at
\left({\bm{W}},\beta\right)
.

Finally, we need to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|\right]<\infty
. For this we compute:

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\log f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log(2\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|-NTd\log(2\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|NTd\log(2b)\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+\frac{1}{a}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}+C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}

Here we used that
{\bm{B}}_{0}
is acyclic and thus

.
We assumed that
\beta\in[a,b]
is bounded. Also we used that the
(\texttau,j)
entry of
{\bm{X}}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}
is
{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}{\bm{W}}_{:,j}
and

\displaystyle\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}{\bm{W}}_{:,j}\right|

\displaystyle<\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}\right|+\left\|{{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}}\right\|_{1}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau,j}\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}-{\bm{X}}_{\text{past}\tau,:}{\bm{W}}_{:,j}\right|

\displaystyle<\sum_{\tau,j}((k+1)d+1)\left|{\bm{X}}_{\tau,j}\right|=((k+1)d+1)\left\|{{\bm{X}}}\right\|_{1},

which furthermore implies

\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{i}\left|{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i,\text{past}}{\bm{W}}\right|<\sum_{i}((k+1)d+1)\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{i}}\right\|_{1}=((k+1)d+1)\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}=C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1},

(31)

for some constant

. Therefore:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{T\times d}}\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle<\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}\right)f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta^{*}\right)d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}\right)\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}^{*}\right)\right|^{NT}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}\right)\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{*}\right)\right|d{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}\right)\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}\right\|_{1}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{*}\right)^{-1}}\right\|_{1}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

Note that,
\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{*}\right)^{-1}}\right\|_{1}=\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}+{\bm{A}}^{*}+...+({\bm{A}}^{*})^{dT}\right)}\right\|_{1}
. From Lemma
A.3
we have that

\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{*}\right)^{-1}}\right\|_{1}=\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}+{\bm{A}}^{*}+...+({\bm{A}}^{*})^{dT}\right)}\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}\left(dT+\left\|{{\bm{A}}^{*}}\right\|_{1}+...+\left\|{({\bm{A}}^{*})}\right\|_{1}^{dT}\right)\leq\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}\cdot C_{3}

(32)

Thus:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{W}}^{*}}\left[\left|\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}}\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle<C_{1}+C_{2}\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}^{*}\right)^{-1}}\right\|_{1}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

\displaystyle<C_{1}+C_{2}C_{3}\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}C_{3}\sum_{i,\tau,j}\int_{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}}|{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}|\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})^{NdT}}e^{-\frac{\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+C_{2}C_{3}\sum_{i,\tau,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}|\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})}e^{-\frac{|{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}|}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+2C_{2}C_{3}\sum_{i,\tau,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}|{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}|\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})}e^{-\frac{|{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}|}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+2C_{2}C_{3}\sum_{i,\tau,j}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}\frac{1}{(2\beta^{*})}e^{-\frac{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}

\displaystyle=C_{1}+2C_{2}C_{3}\sum_{i,\tau,j}\left\{-\frac{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}}{2}e^{-\frac{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}}{\beta^{*}}}\Big|_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-\frac{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}}{\beta^{*}}}d{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}\right\}

\displaystyle=const<\infty.

∎

Remark A.10
.

Note that LiNGAM identifiability is true in the entire space of real matrices
{\mathbb{R}}^{d(k+1)\times d}

[
Shimizu et al., 2006
,
Ng et al., 2020
]
. In other words for any
{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d(k+1)\times d}
different from the ground truth DAG
{\bm{W}}^{*}
the distribution
f_{X}({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}},\beta)
induced by
{\bm{W}}
is different from that of
{\bm{W}}^{*}
, namely
f_{X}({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}}^{*},\beta)
. The reason we restrict our search space to be a DAG is to constrain the magnitude of the terms of the MLE that contain
{\bm{B}}_{0}
.

A.6
SpinSVAR optimization derivation

Here we derive the optimization objective of SpinSVAR for approximating the ground truth window graph parameters of the SVAR of (
3
), given that the SVAR input
{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}
entries are distributed independently according to
\text{Laplace}(0,\beta^{*})
. We consider
N
realization of time series
{\bm{X}}
collected in a tensor
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times T\times d}
. According to (
20
) the log-likelihood of the data
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\log f_{X}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)=\log\prod f_{X}\left({\bm{X}}_{i}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log f_{X}\left({\bm{X}}_{i}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)

(34)

\displaystyle=NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log(2\beta)-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}

(35)

To maximize the log-likelihood with respect to
\beta
we solve:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta}=0\Leftrightarrow-\frac{NTd}{\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}=0\Leftrightarrow\beta=\frac{1}{NTd}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}.

(36)

Note that if
{\bm{W}}={\bm{W}}^{*}
this is a reasonable value for
\beta
as on expectation

\mathbb{E}_{\beta^{*}}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\beta^{*}}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}\right\|_{1}\right]=\sum_{i,\tau,j}\mathbb{E}_{\beta^{*}}\left[\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}_{i,\tau,j}}\right\|_{1}\right]=NTd\beta^{*}.

(37)

Moreover,

\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{L}}{\partial\beta^{2}}=0\Leftrightarrow\frac{NTd}{\beta^{2}}-\frac{2}{\beta^{3}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{NTd}{\beta^{3}}\left(\beta-\frac{2}{NTd}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right)<0.

(38)

So,
\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\beta\right)
is locally concave at
\beta=\frac{1}{NTd}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}
, which gives a local maximum. Similarly to
Ng et al. [2020]
, we profile out the parameter
\beta
using its approximation
\widehat{\beta}=\frac{1}{NTd}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}
to formulate a log-likelihood maximization problem for approximating
{\bm{W}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}|{\bm{W}},\widehat{\beta}\right)=NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log\left(\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right)+\text{const}.

The window graph
{\bm{W}}^{*}
is then be approximated as:

\displaystyle\widehat{{\bm{W}}}=\argmax_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathcal{L}\left({\bm{X}}|{\bm{W}}\right)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left\{NT\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|-NTd\log\left(\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right)+\text{const}\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\left\{d\log\left(\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}\right)-\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|\right\}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathcal{W}}\log\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{d}\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|

(39)

In practice, searching for the minimum of (
39
) over the space of DAGs is computationally inefficient. Following
Ng et al. [2020]
we use the acyclicity as a soft constraint, i.e. a regularizer. This simplifies the optimization algorithm without compromising performance, as will be shown in our experiments. The final optimization of SpinSVAR is the following.

\widetilde{{\bm{W}}}=\argmin_{{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d(k+1)\times d}}\log\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{1}-\frac{1}{d}\log\left|\text{det}\left({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}\right)\right|+\lambda_{1}\cdot\|{\bm{W}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\cdot h\left({\bm{B}}_{0}\right).

(40)

Appendix B
Applying SparseRC to time-series data

SparseRC
[
Misiakos et al., 2023
]
is designed to learn a DAG from static data.

Misiakos et al. [2024]
applied SparseRC to learn graphs from time-series data by exploiting the structure of the unrolled DAG corresponding to the time series.
For long time series, such a formulation creates a huge DAG to be learned - ranging from 20 thousand to 1 million nodes in our experiments.
However, SparseRC can only be executed for
\approx 5000
nodes at maximum to terminate in a reasonable time
[
Misiakos et al., 2023
]
.
Thus it is impossible to be applied in our scenario in its prior form.
For this reason, we propose an alternative way to apply SparseRC, which however, comes with a cost in approximation performance.

SVAR as a Linear SEM

To start with we show how an SVAR can be written as a linear structural equation model (SEM), which is the analogous model for generating linear static DAG data.
We consider a time series
{\bm{X}}
generated with the SVAR in (
3
) (noiseless for simplicity).
Consider the single-row vector
{\bm{x}}=\left({\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1},...,{\bm{x}}_{T-1}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times dT}
consisting of the concatenation of the time-series vectors
{\bm{x}}_{0},{\bm{x}}_{1},...,{\bm{x}}_{T-1}
along the first dimension.
Then (
3
) can also be encoded as:

{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}{\bm{A}}+{\bm{s}},

(41)

where the structural shocks
{\bm{s}}
here also have dimension
1\times dT
.
The matrix
{\bm{A}}
is the adjacency matrix of a DAG with a special structure called the
unrolled DAG

[
Kim and Anderson, 2012
]
, which occurs by repeating the window graph corresponding to (
2
) for every time step
t\in[T]
:

{\bm{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{B}}_{0}&{\bm{B}}_{1}&\ldots&{\bm{B}}_{k}&\ldots&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&{\bm{B}}_{0}&{\bm{B}}_{1}&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&{\bm{B}}_{k}\\
&&&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&&\ldots&\bm{0}&{\bm{B}}_{0}&{\bm{B}}_{1}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}&&\ldots&\bm{0}&{\bm{B}}_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

This allows us to rewrite (
3
) as a linear structural equation model (SEM)
[
Shimizu et al., 2006
]
:

\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}=\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}{\bm{A}}+\widetilde{{\bm{S}}},

(43)

where
\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times dT}
consists of the
N
time series as rows and
\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}
is defined similarly for the structural shocks.
Since
{\bm{A}}
is a DAG, (
43
) represents a linear SEM.

Original SparseRC

We now explain how SparseRC can be applied to learn the window graph from time series according to
Misiakos et al. [2024]
.
SparseRC can be used to learn
{\bm{A}}
from (many samples of)
{\bm{x}}
stacked as a matrix
\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times dT}
, generated from a linear SEM (
43
).
Its optimization objective aims to minimize the number of approximated non-zero structural shocks
\widetilde{{\bm{S}}}
in (
43
).
This is expressed with the following discrete optimization problem

\widehat{{\bm{A}}}=\argmin_{{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{dT\times dT}}\left\|{\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}-\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}{\bm{A}}}\right\|_{0},\quad\text{s.t. }{\bm{A}}\text{ is acyclic}.

(44)

The window graph
\widehat{{\bm{W}}}
can be then extracted from the first row of the approximated
\widehat{{\bm{A}}}
.
SparseRC in practice uses a continuous relaxation to solve optimization problem (
44
), but here we keep the discrete formulation for simplicity.

It can be seen that the DAG
{\bm{A}}
consists of
dT
nodes. In our smallest experiment this equals to
20\times 1000=20000
nodes, which is already out of reach for SparseRC. In contrast, SpinSVAR requires to learn only
(k+1)\times
DAGs with
d
nodes each.
Thus, we necessarily need to formulate SparseRC differently to be able to compare against it.

Modified SparseRC

The idea is to reduce the size of
{\bm{A}}
by getting rid of the
\bm{0}^{\prime}
s in (
42
). Specifically, instead of feeding SparseRC
\widetilde{{\bm{X}}}
we feed as input
{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}
.
The resulting algorithm aims to find an
\widehat{{\bm{A}}}
according to:

\widehat{{\bm{A}}}=\argmin_{{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)d\times(k+1)d}}\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{A}}}\right\|_{0},\quad\text{s.t. }{\bm{A}}\text{ is acyclic}.

(45)

To be compatible with the data-generating process, the following structure is assumed for
{\bm{A}}
:

{\bm{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{B}}_{0}&\bm{0}&...&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
{\bm{B}}_{1}&{\bm{B}}_{0}&\ddots&&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&{\bm{B}}_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bm{B}}_{k-1}&&\ddots&{\bm{B}}_{0}&\bm{0}\\
{\bm{B}}_{k}&{\bm{B}}_{k-1}&\ldots&{\bm{B}}_{1}&{\bm{B}}_{0},\\
\end{pmatrix}

(46)

The optimization objective (
45
) is different from
\left\|{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}}\right\|_{0}
used from SpinSVAR and promotes a different convention in the data generating process.
In particular by setting
\widetilde{{\bm{\mathsfit{S}}}}={\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{A}}
the structural shock
\widetilde{{\bm{s}}}_{t-j}
corresponding to the position
j
of row
t
of
{\bm{x}}_{t,\text{past}}=\left({\bm{x}}_{t},{\bm{x}}_{t-1},...,{\bm{x}}_{t-j},...,{\bm{x}}_{t-k}\right)
of a sample
i
of
{{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}}_{\text{past}}
would be:

\widetilde{{\bm{s}}}_{t-j}={\bm{x}}_{t-j}-{\bm{x}}_{t-j}{\bm{B}}_{0}+{\bm{x}}_{t-j-1}{\bm{B}}_{1}+...+{\bm{x}}_{t-k}{\bm{B}}_{k-j}\neq{\bm{s}}_{t-j}.

(47)

This implies that the approximation of the structural shocks is not consistent with the data generation in (
3
), except when
j=0
.
Thus, only the first column of
{\bm{A}}
promotes the correct equations and the rest undermine the performance of SparseRC.
Resolving this discrepancy and keeping only the first column as trainable parameters is among the technical contributions of our paper.

SpinSVAR

Our implementation in PyTorch is outlined in Algorithm

.
It parametrizes the window graph matrix
{\bm{W}}
using a single PyTorch linear layer
and optimizes the objective function (
11
) with the Adam optimizer.
The overall computational complexity of the algorithm is:

\mathcal{O}\left(M\cdot(NTd^{2}k+d^{3})\right),

where
M
is the total number of epochs (up to
10^{4}
).

The primary term in our objective,

represents a fundamental difference from prior work on causal discovery in time series.
Methods such as VAR-based optimization approaches
[
Pamfil et al., 2020

Sun et al., 2023
]

L^{1}
penalty

L^{1}
norms,
promoting sparsity not only in the DAG but also in the SVAR input.
This design aligns with the assumption of sparse SVAR input. Potentially,
L^{2}
leads to longer convergence times, which makes our algorithm terminate faster in the experiments.

C.1
Comparison with baselines

SparseRC

As we explained in the main text, the method from
Misiakos et al. [2023]

is infeasible to execute for long time series data.
In its original form, SparseRC has complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(M\cdot(Nd^{2}T^{2}+d^{3}T^{3})\right)
, where
M
is the total number of iterations. SparseRC learns a
dT\times dT
unrolled DAG, which for our smaller scenario, results in a DAG with
d\times T=20\times 1000=20000
nodes that goes beyond its computational reach
[
Misiakos et al., 2023
]
.

In Appendix
B
, we design a modified version of SparseRC
that learns a
(k+1)d\times(k+1)d
adjacency matrix, which ultimately leads to a complexity of
\mathcal{O}\left(M\cdot(NTd^{2}k^{2}+d^{3}k^{3})\right)
.
This adaptation can be executed in most scenarios but comes at the cost of reduced model performance.

VAR-LiNGAM

First, the method fits a VAR model to the data:

{\bm{x}}_{t}=\widetilde{{\bm{B}}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{t-1}+...+\widetilde{{\bm{B}}}_{k}{\bm{x}}_{t-k}+{\bm{n}}_{t},

(49)

and then performs Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to compute the self-dependencies matrix
{\bm{B}}_{0}
:

{\bm{n}}_{t}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0}){\bm{n}}_{t}+{\bm{s}}_{t}.

(50)

The resulting matrices are calculated as

{\bm{B}}_{\tau}=({\bm{I}}-{\bm{B}}_{0})\widetilde{{\bm{B}}}_{\tau}.

The ICA step can be replaced with Direct LiNGAM
[
Shimizu et al., 2011
]
,
which guarantees convergence in a finite number of steps (under certain assumptions).
This variation leads to the method Directed VARLiNGAM. However, both approaches have worse complexity compared to ours:

•

For Direct LiNGAM:
\mathcal{O}\left(NTd^{2}k+NTd^{3}M^{2}+d^{4}M^{3}\right)
, where
M
is the number of iterations of Direct LiNGAM.

•

For ICA LiNGAM:
\mathcal{O}\left(NTd^{2}k+NTd^{3}+d^{4}\right)
, which lacks convergence guarantees.

In the large-DAG regime, these algorithms are inevitably slower than ours.

cuLiNGAM

Akinwande and Kolter [2024]
accelerate Directed VARLiNGAM by implementing a parallelized version on GPUs. While this method is faster than Directed VARLiNGAM, our experiments show that it still times out, likely due to high convergence times.

DYNOTEARS

Here, the mean-square error (MSE) is used, transforming the optimization into a quadratic problem:

\frac{1}{2NT}\left\|{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}-{\bm{\mathsfit{X}}}_{\text{past}}{\bm{W}}\right\|_{2}+\lambda_{w}\|{\bm{W}}\|_{1}+\frac{\rho}{2}h({\bm{B}}_{0})^{2}+ah({\bm{B}}_{0}),

(51)

where the
L^{2}
norm in the first term doesn’t enforce sparsity on the structural shocks.
As a result, this method experiences longer convergence times and produces a poor approximation of the ground truth window graph.

TCDF

This method fits convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to predict the time series at each node,
based on the time-series values of other nodes in previous time steps.
The approximation is optimized using the MSE loss.
However, both the non-linearity of CNNs and the MSE loss do not align with our data generation process,
which limits the method’s effectiveness for our specific task.

NTS-NOTEARS

Similar to TCDF, this method also uses CNNs and MSE loss to approximate the window graph.
In addition, the acyclicity regularizer from NOTEARS is applied.
For similar reasons, we anticipate low performance in our experiments with this method as well,
due to the mismatch between the assumptions of the method and the characteristics of our data.

tsFCI, PCMCI

For the constraint-based baselines, there is no clear comparison in terms of optimization.
These methods rely on statistical independence tests to infer causal dependencies between nodes at different time points.
Empirically, however, these methods perform poorly, likely due to their inability to determine the causal direction for every edge they discover.

Appendix D
Sparsity properties of Laplace distribution

A random variable
X
follows a Laplace distribution
[
Eltoft et al., 2006
]
, denoted as
\text{Laplace}(\mu,\beta)
, if its probability density function is given by:

f_{X}(x|\mu,\beta)=\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{|x-\mu|}{\beta}}.

(52)

We now analyze why the Laplace distribution is better suited for modeling sparse vectors compared to the Gaussian distribution. Specifically, we consider Laplacian noise variables centered at zero, setting
\mu=0
. Our motivation is that Laplace-distributed variables are more likely to produce large outliers, whereas Gaussian-distributed variables tend to be concentrated around zero.

To investigate sparsity, we consider three approaches for achieving approximately
5\%
sparsity. The first follows our experimental procedure described in Section
5.1
, which combines Bernoulli and uniform distributions. The second uses a Gaussian distribution, and the third uses a Laplace distribution. Since strict sparsity cannot be achieved,
95\%
of the values will be approximately zero.

We compare these distributions in terms of their sparsity-inducing properties by addressing the following question:
How much more significant are the nonzero values compared to the approximately zero ones?
To do so, we define a threshold
\omega
that classifies values above
\omega
as significant and those below
\omega
as approximately zero.

For each scenario, we generate a random vector
{\bm{s}}
with
d
entries
(s_{1},\dots,s_{d})
and consider a threshold of
\omega=0.1
.

Bernoulli & Uniform

Each
c_{i}
is generated independently, and with probability
1-p=0.95
, it is set to zero.
Otherwise, with probability
p=0.05
, it takes a uniform random value from the range
[-0.4,-0.1]\cup[0.1,0.4]
.
The upper bound of
0.4
ensures that the maximum absolute value is comparable to that of the Laplace distribution, as described later.
To each
c_{i}
, we then add Gaussian noise with a standard deviation of
0.03
. Since
99\%
of the Gaussian noise values lie within
[-0.09,0.09]
, this noise does not significantly affect the sparsity structure.
Thus, given
\omega=0.1
, approximately
95\%
of the entries in
{\bm{s}}
will have absolute values below
\omega
, effectively maintaining sparsity.

Gaussian

For a Gaussian-distributed variable, it is known that approximately
95\%
of values lie within
[-2\sigma,2\sigma]
. To achieve the required sparsity threshold
\omega=0.1
, we set the standard deviation to
\sigma=0.05
.

Laplace

For a Laplace-distributed variable
X
, the probability that its absolute value does not exceed
\omega
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|X|\leq\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\omega}^{\omega}\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{|x|}{\beta}}dx

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{1}{2\beta}e^{-\frac{x}{\beta}}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\omega}\frac{1}{\beta}e^{-\frac{x}{\beta}}dx

\displaystyle=-e^{-\frac{x}{\beta}}\Big|_{0}^{\omega}=1-e^{-\frac{\omega}{\beta}}.

Setting
\beta=\omega/3
ensures that
\mathbb{P}\left(|X|\leq\omega\right)\approx 0.95
, thereby achieving the desired sparsity.

Empirical Evaluation

With the distribution parameters set, we empirically evaluate the sparsity patterns of the generated vectors. Our goal is to demonstrate that the Laplace distribution is better suited for generating sparse vectors compared to the Gaussian. For each distribution listed in Table
2
, we generate a vector
{\bm{s}}
with
d=10^{6}
entries and compute the following evaluation metrics:

•

Sparsity fraction
: The percentage of values with absolute values greater than
\omega
.

•

Maximum absolute value
:
\max_{i}|c_{i}|
.

•

Contrast ratio
:

\text{Contrast ratio}=\frac{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{|c_{i}|>\omega}|c_{i}|}{\omega},

(53)

where
M
is the number of entries satisfying
|c_{i}|>\omega
.

•

Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
:

\text{SNR}=\frac{\sum_{|c_{i}|>\omega}|c_{i}|^{2}}{\sum_{|c_{i}|<\omega}|c_{i}|^{2}}.

(54)

The computational results are shown in Table
2
. Given the
5\%
sparsity constraint, the best performance is achieved by the Bernoulli-Uniform method, which produces higher values with a maximum magnitude of
0.51
and exhibits a superior contrast ratio and SNR, indicating better sparsity characteristics. The Laplace distribution achieves the second-best performance.

E.10
Compute resources

Our experiments were run on a single laptop machine (Dell Alienware x17 R2) with 8 core CPU with 32GB RAM and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3080 GPU.
The execution of the synthetic experiments for the
5
repetitions amounts to approximately 1 week of full run.
Of course, initially there were some failed experiments, and after debugging the experiments were executed for only
1
repetition to determine where each method has a time-out.
We thus chose the time-out to
10000
to try to make our experiments with as little cost as possible.
