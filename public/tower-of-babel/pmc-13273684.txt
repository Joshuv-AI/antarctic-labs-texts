Twenty-two years of Aeromonas septicemia: Clinical characteristics, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and mortality predictors in a tertiary care center
Authors: Abdullah Awadh, Adel Sheerah, Mohanned Alsaeed, Abdulrahman Abbas, Wadee Filimban, Abdullah Bazayd, Abdulfattah Alamri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAeromonas species are opportunistic pathogens causing severe bloodstream infections primarily in immunocompromised individuals. Regional epidemiologic and resistance data are scarce.MethodsRetrospective chart review of all culture-confirmed Aeromonas septicemia cases (January 2003–September 2025) at a tertiary care center. Clinical and demographic data were extracted from electronic records. Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) were performed according to CLSI guidelines using VITEK systems. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to ≥1 agent across ≥3 antimicrobial classes [24]. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified mortality predictors, adjusting for age, gender, cancer type, diabetes, neutropenia, and appropriateness of empirical therapy.ResultsSixty-six patients (mean age 53.2 years; 53% male) were analyzed. Cancer (51.5%)—predominantly gastrointestinal—and diabetes (43.7%) were primary comorbidities. Fever (58%) and septic shock (18%) were common presentations. Thirty-day all-cause and sepsis-related mortality were 15% and 12%, respectively. Empirical therapy selection critically influences outcomes. Introduction

Aeromonas species are gram-negative, oxidase- and catalase-positive bacilli first isolated by Sanarelli in 1891. These aquatic organisms are ubiquitously distributed in environmental water systems and survive diverse conditions, including high salinity and temperature extremes, and have been isolated from fish, vegetables, drinking water, and hospital water systems [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

Currently, 36 Aeromonas species are recognized. Human infections are primarily caused by A. hydrophila, A. caviae, A. veronii, and A. dhakensis. Initially characterized as enteric pathogens associated with gastroenteritis, Aeromonas species are increasingly recognized as opportunistic pathogens capable of causing invasive infections, including bacteremia, wound infections, and severe septicemia in immunocompromised hosts [5].

Virulence is attributed to multiple pathogenicity mechanisms, including aerolysin production, serine protease secretion, heat-labile enterotoxins, and acquired antimicrobial resistance traits [6]. Published mortality rates for Aeromonas septicemia range from 15% to 68%, depending on patient population characteristics and infection severity [7,8]. Early organism identification and appropriate therapy are critical for reducing morbidity and mortality.

Regional epidemiologic data on Aeromonas septicemia are limited, particularly from the Middle East [9,10]. This study characterizes the clinical epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and predictors of mortality in Aeromonas septicemia over a 22-year period at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective chart review of all patients diagnosed with Aeromonas species septicemia at King Abdullah Medical City (KAMC), a 1500-bed tertiary referral and teaching hospital located in the Holy City of Makkah (Mecca), Western Saudi Arabia. KAMC serves as the primary referral center for the Makkah Al-Mukarramah region (estimated population >2 million residents plus seasonal pilgrimage populations) and operates comprehensive oncology, hematology, transplantation, nephrology, and critical care services. The study period spanned January 2003 to September 2025. The study was approved by the institutional review board at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC; protocol SP24J-024-03), with patient consent waived due to the retrospective design.

Geographic and environmental context

Makkah is situated in a hot arid climate (Köppen classification BWh) with mean annual temperatures of 30–32 °C and minimal annual rainfall (<100 mm). The city lacks natural freshwater bodies such as rivers or lakes; however, the municipal water supply is derived from desalination plants and distributed via a network that includes rooftop storage tanks, which may serve as potential environmental reservoirs for Aeromonas species. Additionally, Makkah hosts millions of international pilgrims annually during the Hajj and Umrah seasons, creating unique population dynamics; however, none of the patients in our cohort were identified as pilgrims. The hospital's proximity to the Al-Haram area and the hot climate conditions may influence local water microbiology and Aeromonas ecology.

Microbiological methods

Blood cultures were processed using the BACTEC blood culture system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) with incubation per the manufacturer's protocol. Bacterial identification was performed using VITEK MS (bioMérieux, Durham, NC, USA) matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry. Antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) was conducted using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux) and interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [11] current for the year of testing.

Definitions

Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility (resistant or intermediate) to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more distinct antimicrobial classes [12]. Classes evaluated included β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems), aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.

Empirical therapy appropriateness is defined as the initiation of antimicrobial treatment before identification of the infecting microorganism and its antibiotic susceptibility. Therapy was classified as "appropriate" if the empirical regimen included at least one agent to which the isolate was subsequently shown to be susceptible; it was labeled "inappropriate/mismatch" if the isolate was resistant to all empirical agents used.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or the Fisher exact test (when cell frequency <5). Continuous variables were compared using an independent samples t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Univariate logistic regression identified associations between patient characteristics/comorbidities and sepsis-related mortality.

Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of mortality while controlling for potential confounders: age (≥59 years vs. <59 years, using the cohort median), gender, cancer status (any malignancy vs. none), diabetes status, neutropenia status, and empirical therapy appropriateness. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p-values were calculated. Significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Multiple comparisons correction (Bonferroni) was applied where appropriate. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing results for all 66 isolates are presented in Table 4, with subgroup analysis comparing MDR and non-MDR isolates. Overall, high rates of resistance were observed among β-lactam antibiotics: only 25.0% of isolates were susceptible to amoxicillin–clavulanate, and carbapenem susceptibility was 67.0% for imipenem and 75.0% for meropenem. In contrast, aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones retained excellent activity, with susceptibility rates of 96.0% for amikacin and 95.0% for ciprofloxacin. Carbapenem resistance was significantly more prevalent among MDR isolates compared with non-MDR isolates (imipenem: 50.0% vs. 21.1%, p = 0.005; meropenem: 35.7% vs. 15.8%, p = 0.04).

Mortality analysis and outcome predictors

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of sepsis-related mortality (Table 6). The 30-day all-cause mortality rate was 15% (10 of 66 patients), and the sepsis-related mortality rate was 12% (8 of 66 patients). On univariate analysis, cancer diagnosis (OR 5.67, 95% CI 1.08–29.73, p = 0.04), MDR isolate status (OR 7.43, 95% CI 1.42–38.82, p = 0.018), APACHE II score (OR 1.14 per point, p = 0.02), and empirical therapy mismatch (OR 15.28, 95% CI 3.58–65.14, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with sepsis-related mortality. On multivariate analysis adjusting for age, gender, cancer status, diabetes, neutropenia, and clinical severity, empirical therapy mismatch was the only variable that retained independent significance (adjusted OR 12.4, 95% CI 2.1–73.8, p = 0.005). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated adequate model fit (p = 0.73).

Discussion

Clinical epidemiology and patient populations

Aeromonas septicemia remains an understudied opportunistic infection in tertiary care settings. Our 22-year retrospective analysis provides a regional perspective on epidemiology, resistance patterns, and clinical outcomes.

Consistent with prior literature, our cohort demonstrated marked enrichment for immunocompromised populations. Cancer (51.5%) was the most prevalent comorbidity, with gastrointestinal malignancies representing 50% of cancer cases (17 of 34 patients). This pattern aligns with the multicenter prospective study by Sakurai et al. (2023) [13], which documented a similar predominance of gastrointestinal malignancies among Aeromonas bloodstream infections in immunocompromised hosts. Gastrointestinal malignancies and their chemotherapy induce mucosal barrier dysfunction, facilitating bacterial translocation through damaged intestinal epithelium into the hepatobiliary and portal circulation. Notably, 100% of cancer patients in our cohort were neutropenic (mean ANC 287 ± 156 cells/μL) at the time of infection, undergoing active chemotherapy. This contrasts with some published case reports where Aeromonas septicemia occurred in patients with remote cancer history but stable, non-immunosuppressive disease states.

Diabetes mellitus (43.7%) was the second most common comorbidity, representing a 2–2.5 fold enrichment compared to the general Saudi Arabian population prevalence (∼18-20%). This enrichment reflects genuine immunocompromised physiology rather than ascertainment bias, as diabetes induces [1]: impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and phagocytic function [2]; reduced complement activation [3]; dysregulated inflammatory cytokine response [9]; hyperglycemia-induced endothelial dysfunction promoting bacterial adhesion [14,15]. Our findings parallel the systematic review by Pessoa et al. (2022) [10], which documented similar diabetes enrichment across the Aeromonas series. The endemic nature of diabetes in Saudi Arabia (among the highest globally) may contribute to the regional reservoir of susceptible individuals.

Additional risk factors, including chronic renal failure (6.1%), Crohn's disease (3%), and renal transplantation (1.5%), reflect broader patterns of Aeromonas tropism for severely immunosuppressed populations. These findings are consistent with reports from Campbell et al. [16] (2024) and Stanbaugh et al. (2022) [17] documenting Aeromonas bacteremia among end-stage renal disease and cirrhotic populations.

Clinical presentation and non-specific symptomatology

Most patients presented with non-specific symptoms. Fever (57.6%) and hypotension (22.7%) were the most common findings, while septic shock occurred in 18.2%. Notably, 13.6% presented with diarrhea, reflecting the enteric origin of the organism, though most patients lacked gastrointestinal symptoms. Analysis of presumed infection sources revealed that primary bacteremia without identifiable focus was the most common presentation (57.6%), followed by hepatobiliary or intra-abdominal sources (21.2%). Skin and soft tissue infections accounted for only 9.1% of cases, which is lower than might be expected given the environmental ubiquity of Aeromonas in aquatic systems. This finding reflects the composition of our cohort, which is dominated by immunocompromised cancer patients in whom mucosal barrier disruption and bacterial translocation from the gastrointestinal tract represent the primary pathogenesis pathway, rather than traumatic or environmental wound exposure. In contrast, community-acquired Aeromonas infections in immunocompetent hosts are more commonly associated with wound infections and waterborne exposures. These findings confirm prior reports that Aeromonas septicemia often lacks distinguishing clinical features, complicating diagnosis and delaying targeted therapy. No temporal trends in clinical presentation were detected across the three study decades, suggesting stable phenotypic characteristics of Aeromonas within our region.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns: regional epidemiology and mechanistic insights

Our analysis reveals a substantial regional burden of antimicrobial resistance in Aeromonas isolates, with critical implications for empirical therapy selection.

High β-lactam resistance

Only 25% of isolates were susceptible to amoxicillin–clavulanate, and 33% were resistant to imipenem—substantially higher than reported European cohorts (12–18% carbapenem resistance per Schwartz et al., 2024 [18]) but aligned with emerging Southwest Asian data (Moradi et al., 2025 [19] meta-analysis: Iran 25–40% carbapenem resistance) and with large contemporary cohorts that first highlighted substantial carbapenem resistance in this genus [20]. This suggests distinct geographic resistance gene pools., a pattern increasingly reported in regional surveillance and genotypic analyses [21,22].

Proposed resistance mechanisms.

Empirical therapy appropriateness and mortality association

A striking finding emerged upon detailed mortality analysis: empirical therapy mismatch was the dominant independent predictor of sepsis-related mortality (aOR 12.4, 95% CI 2.1–73.8, p = 0.005), remaining significant after multivariate adjustment for age, comorbidities, and clinical severity indicators.

Among patients receiving inappropriate empirical therapy (n = 14), sepsis-related mortality reached 28.6% compared to 6.3% in those receiving appropriate therapy (p = 0.008). Mean time to appropriate therapy switch was 28.4 ± 12.1 h—a substantial window of inadequate antimicrobial coverage.

This finding underscores the clinical imperative articulated by Kumar et al. and quantified in systematic reviews (Al-Kader et al., 2022 [23]): each hour of delay in appropriate therapy increases sepsis mortality by 4–9%. For Aeromonas specifically, our data suggest similar kinetics of prognostic deterioration in the presence of antimicrobial mismatches.

Clinical interpretation: MDR status itself does not independently predict poor outcomes when therapy is subsequently appropriately de-escalated. Rather, MDR increases the likelihood of empirical therapy mismatch, which is the proximate driver of mortality. This distinction suggests intervention opportunities.

1.Rapid diagnostics: Accelerate organism identification and susceptibility testing (e.g., MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry performs Aeromonas identification rapidly; real-time PCR for resistance genes could further accelerate therapy optimization).

Rapid diagnostics: Accelerate organism identification and susceptibility testing (e.g., MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry performs Aeromonas identification rapidly; real-time PCR for resistance genes could further accelerate therapy optimization).

2.Empirical therapy optimization: In high-risk immunocompromised populations (particularly cancer patients on chemotherapy, renal failure patients), empirical regimens should include agents with known excellent Aeromonas activity (e.g., aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, carbapenems where carbapenem resistance rates are documented).

Clinical and public health implications

Our findings suggest several actionable strategies to reduce Aeromonas septicemia-associated mortality in tertiary care settings.

1.Rapid diagnostic approaches: Prioritize MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry for Aeromonas identification (achievable within 3–4 h of positive blood culture), enabling faster antimicrobial stewardship decisions vs. traditional biochemical identification (24–48 h).

Rapid diagnostic approaches: Prioritize MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry for Aeromonas identification (achievable within 3–4 h of positive blood culture), enabling faster antimicrobial stewardship decisions vs. traditional biochemical identification (24–48 h).

2.Empirical therapy optimization: In immunocompromised populations (particularly cancer patients on active chemotherapy, renal failure patients), initial empirical sepsis coverage should include aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin) or fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) with known excellent Aeromonas activity. Carbapenems remain acceptable, but regional resistance rates (33% imipenem) mandate rapid susceptibility testing.

Carbapenems remain acceptable, but regional resistance rates (33% imipenem) mandate rapid susceptibility testing.

3.Antimicrobial surveillance: Institutions should establish local resistance surveillance tracking Aeromonas susceptibility patterns to guide institutional antibiotic stewardship and empirical therapy algorithms. Conclusions

Aeromonas septicemia primarily affects immunocompromised tertiary care patients, with cancer and diabetes as predominant risk factors. Regional antimicrobial resistance patterns are substantial, particularly for β-lactams and carbapenems. Critically, empirical therapy mismatch independently predicts sepsis-related mortality, emphasizing the clinical imperative for rapid organism identification and susceptibility-guided therapy. Future prospective, multicenter studies incorporating molecular characterization and real-time diagnostics are warranted to further elucidate resistance epidemiology and optimize clinical outcomes.
