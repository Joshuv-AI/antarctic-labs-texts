Intestinal health of broiler chickens fed red seaweed (Chondracanthus chamissoi) under dexamethasone-induced stress
Authors: Olumide S Olowe, Ajuwon M Kolapo, Olayiwola Adeola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional feed additives such as seaweed are increasingly studied as alternatives to antibiotics to mitigate intestinal health challenges in poultry. In this study, Cobb500 broilers received in-feed dexamethasone (DEX) to induce stress and gut integrity impairment from day 14 to 21 post hatching. Seaweed (Chondracanthus chamissoi) was supplemented to evaluate its potential to mitigate DEX-induced changes in performance, intestinal permeability, and jejunal gene expression. In phase 1, a total of 384 broiler chicks were assigned to three dietary treatments (0%, 1%, or 2% seaweed) with 16 replicate cages of eight birds each from day 0 to 14. During phase 2, from d 14 to 21, 336 birds on the same diets from Phase 1 were reallocated to a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement with or without DEX challenge. Intestinal permeability was assessed using fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FITC-D). Data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure in SAS, and factorial analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted for Phase 2. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts analysis was done to examine linear and quadratic trends associated with seaweed inclusion. Seaweed tended to linearly increase feed intake (P = 0.097) and improved the gain-to-feed ratio (P < 0.05) in phase 1. In phase 2, DEX increased (P < 0.0001) serum FITC-D concentration and reduced most of the physiological response variables. Seaweed tended to reduce serum FITC-D (P = 0.08), but did not influence intestinal morphology and growth performance variables. Seaweed reduced nutrient digestibility and elevated the mRNA expression of Zona occludens 1, Superoxide Dismutase 1, Glutathione Peroxidase-1 and glucose transporters. In conclusion, seaweed can potentially reduce oxidative stress in broiler chickens, but its effect is dose dependent.Keywords: Broiler chicken, Glucocorticoids, Intestinal health, Macroalgae, Oxidative stress

Introduction

Broiler chickens are exposed to stressors during production cycles. These stressors, including transportation, feed and water deprivation, stocking density, and disease challenges, such as those from Eimeria species and Clostridium perfringens, can negatively affect poultry health and performance by compromising the intestinal epithelial barrier system (Mench, 1991; Bowling et al., 2018; Madlala et al., 2021; Dal Pont et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2021; Zainool et al., 2022). Additionally, environmental stress and physical characteristics of feed ingredients can undermine gut integrity by promoting oxidative stress and mechanical irritation. These changes may increase intestinal permeability, reduce nutrient absorption and immune function (Oke et al., 2024), and contribute to mucosal damage that further weakens immune responses. Associated physiological response is the disruption of the intestinal mucosa, characterized by decreased villus height, reduced epithelial cell proliferation, and altered tight junction proteins, which can increase bacterial translocation. Subsequently, this leads to slower growth as nutrients become less available, compromises animal health, and ultimately reduces productivity and profitability.

Given the importance of these challenges, multiple studies have explored the use of feed additives and antibiotics as nutritional strategies to mitigate intestinal dysfunction in broiler chickens. Due to the potential development of antibiotic resistance, non-antibiotic feed additives continue to be developed and evaluated. To contribute to this antibiotic-free strategy, the current study focuses on the use of a red seaweed (Chondracanthus chamissoi) as a nutritional strategy for improving broiler chickens’ gut health and functioning. Seaweeds (otherwise called macroalgae) serve as potential prebiotics and antioxidants due to their polysaccharide and bioactive content (Liu et al., 2021). Macroalgae have been reported to improve tight junction proteins (TJPs), antioxidant capacity, and innate immune responses in stressed chickens (Liu et al., 2021; O’Sullivan et al., 2010). Additionally, seaweed supplementation has been reported to attenuate oxidative stress and inflammation and to mitigate muscle atrophy (Dutra et al., 2021; Yoshikawa et al., 2021). We have also recently established that Chondracanthus chamissoi can positively influence the intestinal morphology, alter cecal microbiota, and potentially increase butyrate production in broiler chickens (Olowe et al., 2025).

Given that beneficial microbiota and their short-chain fatty acid metabolites can support intestinal barrier function and attenuate compromised intestinal integrity (Yosi et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022; Khongthong et al., 2025), we hypothesized that dietary Chondracanthus chamissoi can mitigate increases in intestinal permeability and oxidative stress. Dexamethasone (DEX) was used as a stress model because it has been reported to disrupt tight junction proteins, increase paracellular permeability, and compromise gut immune functions (Vicuña et al., 2015; Osho and Adeola, 2020; Barekatain et al., 2021). In addition, DEX can elevate oxidative stress and inflammation in broiler chickens (Fathi et al., 2023). Accordingly, this study evaluated the effects of dietary Chondracanthus chamissoi supplementation on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, intestinal morphology, intestinal permeability, and health in Cobb 500 broiler chickens subjected to DEX-induced stress.

Materials and methods

The methods used in this study were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Purdue University, West Lafayette, Indiana.

Experimental design and diets

The experiment was conducted over 21 days in two phases using Cobb500 broiler chickens. Birds were kept in battery cages (model SB 4 T; Alternative Design Manufacturing and Supply, Siloam Springs, AR, USA). Phase 1 of the study lasted 14 days, from day 0 to 14, during which 384 birds were tagged, weighed, and allocated in a randomized complete block design (RCBD; body weight as the blocking factor) into one of three dietary treatment groups. Each dietary treatment was allotted to 16 replicate cages, with 8 birds per cage. Dietary treatments (Table 1) consisted of a basal corn-soybean meal mash diet (CON), CON supplemented with 1% Chondracanthus chamissoi, and CON supplemented with 2% Chondracanthus chamissoi. At the conclusion of Phase 1, the same birds were carried forward into Phase 2, retaining their respective dietary treatment assignments. Prior to Phase 2, birds were reweighed and re-randomized within each dietary treatment group to equalize mean body weight across cages before DEX administration. This resulted in a 3 × 2 factorial arrangement, comprising three levels of Chondracanthus chamissoi (0%, 1%, or 2%) and two DEX (0 or 1 mg/kg diet) levels (Osho and Adeola, 2020). Thus, in phase 2, there were six dietary treatments, with 8 replicate cages (7 birds per cage). The concentration gradient for Chondracanthus chamissoi was selected to assess a dose-dependent response in a conservative and incremental manner, informed by our previous study (Olowe et al., 2025). Given the novelty of this seaweed, a moderate range was deemed appropriate before wider inclusion levels are explored in future studies. Diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutritional requirements of Cobb500, with birds having ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the study. All diets in phase 2 contained titanium dioxide (5 g/kg) as an indigestible marker. Chondracanthus chamissoi powder was procured from Nutricargo (222 Getty Ave, Clifton, New Jersey).

Sampling procedure

On day 21 post hatching, all birds and unconsumed feed were weighed to estimate the body weight gain (BWG), feed intake (FI), and gain-to-feed ratio (G: F). Additionally, all seven birds in each cage were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation, followed by excision of ileum from all birds, and jejunal tissue samples from the second-heaviest bird per cage. Fresh excreta samples were carefully collected from clean white papers underneath each cage. Ileal digesta was flushed into labeled containers using distilled water, and both ileal digesta and excreta were stored at −20 °C for subsequent determination of apparent ileal digestibility (AID) and apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) of nutrients, respectively. Excised jejunal segments were rinsed with chilled 10% phosphate-buffered saline, immersed in 10% buffered formalin, and retained for intestinal morphological analysis. Jejunal mucosa was carefully scraped, placed in 1.5 mL tubes prefilled with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for later RNA extraction and PCR analysis.

Fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran permeability assay

Intestinal permeability was evaluated using a fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FITC-D) assay, following protocols modified from previously published studies (Kuttappan et al., 2015; Barekatain et al., 2019). On day 21, the heaviest bird in each cage was orally gavaged with 1 mL of 40 KDa FITC-D (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO) solution at a concentration of 2.2 mg/mL. After 3 h, blood was collected, allowed to clot at room temperature, and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 15 min. Fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran concentration in the aliquoted serum was assessed immediately in triplicate using a Tecan microplate reader (excitation 485 nm, emission 530 nm).

Chemical analysis and calculations

Dry matter of ground samples from diets and excreta (VEVOR 2000 g; VEVOR, Hong Kong), ileal digesta (KRUPS coffee grinder, F203; Solingen, Germany) was determined by subjecting samples to forced air drying at 105 ° C (Precision Scientific Co., Chicago, IL, USA). Gross energy of all samples was determined using an isoperibol bomb calorimeter (Model 6400, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA). Nitrogen content was analyzed via combustion method with a TruMac® N analyzer (LECO Corp., St. Joseph, MI, USA). Water-holding capacity (WHC) was determined using a previous method (Baig et al., 2023) with modifications. Two grams of sample was hydrated in 25 mL of deionized water in a preweighed 50 ml tube. Hydrated sample was shaken and incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. Samples were centrifuged (5000 × g for 20 mins), and the supernatant was carefully discarded. The tube containing hydrated residue was weighed, and WHC was calculated and expressed as grams of water retained per gram of sample (g/g). Total antioxidant capacity of seaweed was determined using the phosphomolybdenum assay with L-ascorbic acid as the standard and results expressed as ascorbic acid equivalents, AAE (Prieto et al., 1999), while total polyphenol content was assessed using a modified Prussian Blue assay with phloroglucinol as the standard and results expressed as phloroglucinol equivalents, PGE (Price and Butler, 1977; Hagerman, 2025).

Intestinal morphology

Excised jejunal segments were sectioned into three separate pieces, which were then processed and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining at Purdue University’s Histology Research Laboratory. Morphometric assessment, including image capturing of the villi and crypt, was conducted with a microscope (National Optical and Scientific Instruments, Inc., Schertz, TX, USA) fitted with an MC500W-G1camera. Subsequent measurements of Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were performed using ImageJ software (version 1.8). Eleven intact villi per bird were randomly selected for measurements, and the villus height to crypt depth ratio (VH: CD) was subsequently estimated.

RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qPCR analysis

Jejunal mucosa was homogenized in TRIzol using a QIAGEN TissueLyser II, followed by phase separation with bromochloropropane and centrifugation. After separating and collecting the aqueous layer, RNA was precipitated using isopropanol, rinsed with ethanol, and then quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (A260/280; Fisher Scientific, St. Louis, MO, USA). Complementary DNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) immediately and stored at −80 °C. Quantitative PCR was performed on a Bio-Rad CFX thermocycler using SYBR Green Master Mix (Biotool, Houston, TX, USA) under standard cycling conditions, with primer sequences listed in Table 2. The target genes were Claudin 1 (CLDN1), Occludin (OCLN), Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter 1 (SGLT1), Glucose Transporter 2 (GLUT2), Mucin 2 (MUC2), Superoxide Dismutase 1 (SOD1), Glutathione Peroxidase 1 (GPX1), Zonula Occludens 1 (ZO1), Interleukin-10 (IL-10), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), and Interleukin-1β (IL-1β). All samples were analyzed in duplicate, with coefficients of variation below 5%. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) and normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). Birds in the CON group were used as calibrator.

Statistical analysis and calculations

Phase 1 data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure in SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), whereas phase 2 data were analyzed as a factorial arrangement with day 14 body weight included as a covariate (ANCOVA). The model (Phase 2) tested the main and interaction effects of dietary seaweed and DEX. Orthogonal polynomial contrasts analysis was conducted to examine the linear and quadratic effects associated with seaweed inclusion. In the model, dietary treatment was treated as a fixed effect, block (weight) was treated as a random effect, and cage was designated as the experimental unit. A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical differences, and results with 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10 were interpreted as a tendency.

Results

Across all response variables, there was no interaction between seaweed and DEX. Therefore, the results in this section are presented as main effects.

Growth performance and nutrient digestibility

Growth performance of broiler chickens fed seaweed diets from day 0 to 14 post hatching is presented in Table 3. Seaweed did not influence the BWG or FI. A tendency for a linear increase in FI was observed with increasing seaweed levels (P = 0.097), and G: F linearly decreased (P = 0.025) with dietary seaweed inclusion, such that the lowest feed efficiency was observed at the highest inclusion level. There was a tendency for quadratic effects of seaweed on BWG (P = 0.086), and no influence of seaweed on the FI and G: F (Table 4). Dexamethasone decreased (P < 0.0001) BWG, FI, and G: F of broiler chickens during d 14 to 21. Main effects of AID and ATTD from d 14 to 21 are shown in Table 5. Dexamethasone reduced (P < 0.0001) the AID and ATTD of DM, GE, N, ileal digestible energy (IDE), and apparent metabolizable energy (AME). Seaweed inclusion influenced the AID of DM (P = 0.032), AID of GE (P = 0.052), AID of N (P = 0.001), and ATTD of DM, GE, N, and AME (P < 0.001), with a tendency for a decrease in AID of GE and IDE (P = 0.052). Response to seaweed was predominantly linear for AID of DM, GE, N, and IDE (P < 0.05) and ATTD of DM, GE, N, and AME (P < 0.01), whereas a quadratic effect was observed only for ATTD of DM (P = 0.035).

Intestinal morphology, permeability, and mRNA expression

Jejunal morphology responses to dietary seaweed and DEX are presented in Fig. 1. Seaweed supplementation did not influence the jejunal VH, CD, and VH: CD of broiler chickens. Dexamethasone reduced the VH and CD of broiler chickens (P < 0.001). As shown in Table 6 and Fig. 2, seaweed tended to influence serum FITC-D, with a quadratic decrease observed across graded seaweed levels (P = 0.068), whereas DEX significantly increased FITC-D (P < 0.0001). For tight junction proteins (Table 6), seaweed influenced the mRNA abundance of ZO1 (P = 0.041), which showed a quadratic response to graded seaweed inclusion (P = 0.024). A quadratic response to graded seaweed inclusion was observed for Claudin 1 (P = 0.054). Dexamethasone increased the mRNA expression of Occludin (P = 0.039) and ZO1 (P < 0.001). Among interleukins, seaweed increased IL-10 mRNA abundance (P = 0.041) with a significant (P = 0.014) quadratic response to graded seaweed inclusion levels, whereas DEX increased IL-1β (P < 0.01). Seaweed increased the mRNA abundance of the glucose transporters SGLT1 (P = 0.024) and GLUT2 (P = 0.025). The mRNA expression of SGLT1 was also affected by DEX (P = 0.023) and showed a tendency for a linear response to seaweed inclusion (P = 0.050), whereas GLUT2 exhibited a quadratic response to graded seaweed inclusion (P < 0.01). Mucin2 expression was affected by DEX (P < 0.0001). Main effect of seaweed on MUC2 was not different, but MUC2 tended to exhibit a quadratic response to graded seaweed inclusion (P = 0.057). Seaweed increased mRNA abundance of the antioxidant genes SOD1 (P < 0.01) and GPX1 (P < 0.0001), and both genes showed quadratic responses to graded seaweed inclusion (P < 0.01 and P < 0.0001, respectively). No effects of DEX were noted for these antioxidant genes.

Discussion

Alterations in feed efficiency, growth, antioxidant status, intestinal morphology, and intestinal permeability are widely associated with disease and oxidative stress in the poultry industry. In many conditions involving intestinal dysfunction, tight junction disruption, and impaired redox balance. In the current study, dexamethasone administration reduced performance and feed efficiency and was accompanied by impaired nutrient utilization, disrupted intestinal morphology, and decreased nutrient digestibility, consistent with previous reports (Vicuña et al., 2015; Mehaisen et al., 2017; Barekatain and Kluenemann, 2023). Dexamethasone can promote muscle atrophy via increased atrophy F-box expression and disruption of the IGF-1 pathway, thereby limiting PI3K/Akt phosphorylation and compromising mTORC1-mediated protein synthesis (Fappi et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Dexamethasone can impair feed efficiency by suppressing appetite-stimulating pathways (Liu et al., 2016). In addition to epithelial barrier disruption, dexamethasone may impair sIgA function, which can compromise microbiome activity and contribute to increased intestinal permeability (Spitz et al., 1994). Given these potential mechanisms of action, the increased serum FITC-D and associated physiological responses indicate that DEX increased intestinal permeability, supporting its use as a model to induce intestinal permeability and stress responses in broiler chickens. Although seaweed supplementation did not fully attenuate the physiological responses induced by DEX, due to the absence of interaction between seaweed and DEX, our findings indicate that it has the potential to reduce intestinal permeability, as reflected by a tendency for lower serum FITC-D, as well as improve anti-inflammatory cytokine expression, support antioxidant responses, and promote glucose uptake. The influence of seaweed on leaky gut may have occurred through the increased expression of ZO1 observed in this study and its potential to increase butyrate production (Olowe et al., 2025). Zona occludens 1 functions as a scaffold that stabilizes tight junction complexes and supports epithelial proliferation and mucosal repair (Kuo et al., 2021). It has been suggested that ZO proteins can act as temporally and spatially regulated scaffolds for other tight junction proteins, including occludin and claudins, and can contribute to adherens junction organization (Fanning and Anderson, 2009). Therefore, the upregulation of only ZO1 may help explain the observed tendency for reduced FITC-D.

Dexamethasone can impair jejunal glucose uptake by downregulating SGLT1 and GLUT2 and reducing brush-border glucose transport (Li et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2020), with SGLT1 being most sensitive to oxidative stress compared to the GLUT family (Andrade et al., 2018). The increase in glucose transporters observed in the seaweed group may therefore reflect seaweed effects on epithelial redox conditions that favor maintenance of glucose transport capacity. Our hypothesis is that seaweed bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides and bromophenols, activate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) and antioxidant response element pathway (Liu et al., 2011; Liu et al., 2021), thereby promoting antioxidant enzymes that protect cells by reducing oxidative stress (Liu et al., 2011; Dong et al., 2020). Superoxide dismutase converts superoxide radicals to hydrogen peroxide, and GPX1 reduces hydrogen peroxide to water, thereby limiting oxidative damage and supporting normal cellular function. Additionally, seaweed's potential for increasing butyrate production (Olowe et al., 2025) may have contributed to the protection against oxidative stress (Yang et al., 2021; Sarker et al., 2025). Furthermore, seaweed has the potential to alter the gut microbiome (Olowe et al., 2025), which may have increased IL-10 upregulation, because microbiome-derived short-chain fatty acids have been reported to promote IL-10 production to maintain intestinal homeostasis (Sun et al., 2018). However, despite these potential benefits, seaweed supplementation was associated with reduced nutrient digestibility, which may explain the lack of improvement in growth performance or intestinal histomorphology.

The lower nutrient digestibility observed with seaweed supplementation may be partly explained by its high water-holding capacity (4.16 g/g), which can increase digesta viscosity and reduce enzyme access to substrates. Viscous sulfated polysaccharides contribute to water binding and can impair feed utilization by limiting enzyme–substrate interactions and nutrient hydrolysis (Jha and Mishra, 2021). Carrageenan in Chondracanthus chamissoi contains β-1,4 linkages that are poorly hydrolyzed by monogastric enzymes, which may further depress digestibility (Bhattacharyya et al., 2010; Jha and Mishra, 2021). Additionally, seaweed can bioaccumulate minerals, resulting in high ash content that may further impair digestibility as inclusion level increases (Stokvis et al., 2021). Consistent with the digestibility responses, seaweed did not affect growth performance in this experiment. This growth response contrasts with reports of improved growth performance with seaweed supplementation (Azizi et al., 2024) and from our previous findings (Olowe et al., 2025). This inconsistency may reflect variation in the chemical composition or bioavailability of polyphenols in Chondracanthus chamissoi, which has been reported across different seaweed species (Min et al., 2021). Seaweed quality varies with season, location, water temperature, harvest timing, and storage conditions, which influence structural polysaccharides and other bioactive components (Huyghebaert et al., 2011; Schiener et al., 2015). For example, structural polysaccharide content can increase during summer and autumn and decline during winter as reserves are mobilized, whereas protein and ion profiles may also shift across seasons (Schiener et al., 2015). Therefore, seasonal variation should be considered when evaluating seaweed inclusion levels and efficacy. It is worth noting that the quadratic responses observed in this current study suggest that a 2% inclusion level of this seaweed may not be optimal for broiler chickens. This pattern may be partly attributable to seaweed physicochemical and compositional factors, including mineral accumulation.

Overall, the findings support dexamethasone as a model of intestinal dysfunction in broiler chickens. Seaweed appears to support intestinal function by increasing antioxidant genes, promoting glucose uptake, and regulating tight junction protein scaffolding. However, compared to our established study, its efficacy on growth and intestinal morphology is inconsistent. The balance between potential health benefits and reduced performance likely depends on the inclusion level and variation in seaweed composition. Future studies should evaluate the effects of pretreatments and extracts of Chondracanthus chamissoi to optimize its benefits. The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of certain limitations, particularly the absence of information on the seaweed harvest season and processing method, as well as the lack of quantification of individual bioactive compounds, which may affect the strength of the mechanistic inferences drawn.
