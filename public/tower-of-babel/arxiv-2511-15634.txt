Rényi Differential Privacy for Heavy-Tailed SDEs via Fractional Poincaré Inequalities
Benjamin Dupuis benjamin.dupuis@inria.fr, Mert Gürbüzbalaban mg1366@rutgers.edu, Umut Şimşekli umut.simsekli@inria.fr, Jian Wang jianwang@fjnu.edu.cn, Sinan Yıldırım sinanyildirim@sabanciuniv.edu, Lingjiong Zhu zhu@math.fsu.edu

Abstract
Characterizing the differential privacy (DP) of learning algorithms has become a major challenge in recent years. In parallel, many studies suggested investigating the behavior of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with heavy-tailed noise, both as a model for modern deep learning models and to improve their performance. However, most DP bounds focus on light-tailed noise, where satisfactory guarantees have been obtained but the proposed techniques do not directly extend to the heavy-tailed setting. Recently, the first DP guarantees for heavy-tailed SGD were obtained. These results provide ( 0 , δ ) (0,\delta) -DP guarantees without requiring gradient clipping. Despite casting new light on the link between DP and heavy-tailed algorithms, these results have a strong dependence on the number of parameters and cannot be extended to other DP notions like the well-established Rényi differential privacy (RDP). In this work, we propose to address these limitations by deriving the first RDP guarantees for heavy-tailed SDEs, as well as their discretized counterparts. Our framework is based on new Rényi flow computations and the use of well-established fractional Poincaré inequalities. Under the assumption that such inequalities are satisfied, we obtain DP guarantees that have a much weaker dependence on the dimension compared to prior art.

Introduction

Setup.

Let
\mathcal{Z}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
be a data space endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
and a probability distribution
\mu_{z}
, where
\mathcal{X}
denotes the space of input data and
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the space of output data.
In the context of supervised learning, stochastic optimization algorithms are used to solve the following
empirical risk minimization
(ERM) problem

\displaystyle\min\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(w):=\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w,z_{i}):~w\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}\right\},

(1)

where
\ell:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
is a loss function and
S:=(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\sim\mu_{z}^{\otimes n}
is a dataset sampled from the data distribution. With these notations, stochastic learning algorithms for solving the ERM problem can be seen as randomized mappings
\mathcal{A}:S\mapsto W_{S}\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
.

Due to the rapid development of machine learning, it has become essential to certify the
differential privacy
(DP) of learning algorithms.
Informally, this notion can be understood as the question: given two
neighbouring
datasets
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
(
i.e.
, differing by only one data point), how hard is it to distinguish the probability distributions of
W_{S}
and
W_{S^{\prime}}
,
i.e.
, can we identify whether one particular data point has been used during training.
A related question then is the creation of algorithms with provable DP guarantees.
Several formalizations of DP have been proposed
(
27
;
24
;
26
)
.
In our paper, we are in particular interested in the
Rényi differential privacy
(RDP)
(
42
)
, presented as a natural relaxation of classical DP conditions.
More precisely, a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}:S\mapsto W_{S}
is said to be
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP when
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(\mathrm{Law}(W_{S}),\mathrm{Law}(W_{S^{\prime}}))\leq\kappa
, for neighboring datasets
S
and
S^{\prime}
, where
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the Rényi divergence for
\beta>1
.
We provide in
Section

2.1
a detailed presentation of these notions.

In our study, we aim to prove RDP guarantees for heavy-tailed SDEs. More precisely, given
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and a time horizon
T>0
, the learning algorithm is given by
\mathcal{A}:S\mapsto W_{T}
, where
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a solution of the following stochastic differential equation (SDE) in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W_{t}=-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(W_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\alpha}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(2)

where
\sigma_{\alpha},\sigma_{2}\geq 0
are fixed,
(L_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geq 0}
is a rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with tail index
\alpha\in(0,2)
(see
Section

2.2
), and
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion.
We also consider Euler-Maruyama discretizations of
Equation

2
, which corresponds to heavy-tailed stochastic gradient descent (SGD), as studied by
51
in the case
\sigma_{2}=0
.

These heavy-tailed algorithms have recently gained a lot of attention in learning theory. First, it was shown in several studies, both empirically and theoretically, that SGD can produce heavy-tailed distributions under certain choices of hyperparameters
(
52
;
33
)
.
Following these findings, it was shown that the presence or injection of heavy-tailed noise can ensure good generalization properties
(
46
;
47
;
23
;
39
)
, and improve the compressibility of the model
(
7
;
57
)
. In addition to these properties, it has been shown that heavy-tailed noise can lead to satisfactory minimization of the empirical risk in convex and non-convex settings
(
60
;
52
)
, and in the context of one-hidden-layer neural networks
(
57
)
.
These observations support the interest in obtaining DP guarantees for heavy-tailed dynamics such as
Equation

2
.

Related works.

Many differentially private mechanisms have been studied with Gaussian
(

28
)

(

The main inspiration for our work is the well-established differentially private SGD (DP-SGD) mechanism

In our study, this model corresponds to a discretized version of

In particular,

3.1

(
4

2

(

(
62
;
18
;
29
;
48

Table

Unfortunately, these works cannot be directly extended to the heavy-tailed case (

\sigma_{\alpha}>0

Recent studies derived DP guarantees under heavy-tailed noise.
37

\alpha

In the case of SGD,

. However, this study does not directly contain the case of
\alpha
-stable noise, which is the focus of our paper.
More recently,
51

{\mathds{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle W_{k+1}=W_{k}-\eta\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(W_{k})+\sigma_{\alpha}\eta^{1/\alpha}\xi_{k},\quad\xi_{k}\overset{{\it i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{S}\alpha\mathcal{S}:={\mathrm{Law}\left(L_{1}^{\alpha}\right)},

-DP guarantees (see
Definition

\delta=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{(1+\alpha)/2}/n\right)

Their study is based on a Markov chain perturbation analysis and, most importantly, can handle unbounded gradients, while many works rely on bounded gradients or finite sensitivity assumptions
(
19
)
.
Despite successfully obtaining the first DP guarantees for heavy-tailed SGD, this work has three main disadvantages: (i)

1.1
Contributions

In our work, we propose to address the issues mentioned above by proving RDP guarantees for heavy-tailed SDEs and their discrete counterparts. Our framework is based on the extension of the entropy flow computations of
19
in the heavy-tailed setting.
In contrast with the relative entropy flow derivations obtained by
23
in the context of generalization bounds, we obtain
Renyi divergence flows
(
i.e.
, the time derivative of the Rényi divergence) for SDEs driven by general Lévy processes.
In order to circumvent the lack of LSI in this heavy-tailed case, we show that we can obtain time-uniform RDP guarantees based on the fractional versions of the celebrated Poincaré inequalities
(
59
;
43
)
.
Based on this method, we obtained the first RDP guarantees for heavy-tailed (
i.e.
,
\alpha
-stable) SGD.
Our detailed contributions are summarized below and in
Table

2
.

•

We provide a general framework to compute the flow of Rényi divergences along Lévy-driven SDEs and study the associated functional inequalities. Our proof technique applies to a very general class of Lévy processes, which makes it more general than existing works on heavy-tailed SGD.

•

In the multifractal case (
i.e.
, when both
\sigma_{\alpha}>0
and
\sigma_{2}>0
), we obtain dimension-independent
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP guarantees with
\kappa=\mathcal{O}\left(\beta^{2}/(n^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2})\right)
, under a fractional Poincaré inequality (FPI) assumption. We also obtain a guarantee in
\kappa=\mathcal{O}\left(\beta T/(n^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2})\right)
when this assumption is removed.

•

In the pure-jump
\alpha
-stable case (
i.e.
, when
\sigma_{2}=0
), we draw a new link with Bourgain-Brezis-Mironescu’s type formulas
(
11
)
and show that, under slightly stronger assumptions, we can obtain
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP guarantees with
\kappa=\mathcal{O}\left(\beta^{2}d^{1-\alpha/2}/(n^{2}\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha})\right)
under FPI and
\kappa=\mathcal{O}\left(\beta d^{1-\alpha/2}T/(n^{2}\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha})\right)
without FPI.
Our results imply
(0,\delta)
-DP guarantees which have much weaker dependence on the dimension
d
than in existing works in both the multifractal and the pure-jump case.

•

Finally, we extend our RDP to the discrete-time setting, which is used in practical application. Moreover, as a sanity check, we investigate the satisfiability of fractional Poincaré inequalities in this case, hence providing theoretical foundation for our main assumptions. As a by-product of our analysis, we derive new stability results for fractional Poincaré inequalities.

2.1
Background on differential privacy

Let
\mathcal{Z}
be a data space endowed with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
. Given two datasets
S,S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
for some
n\geq 0
, we say that they are
neighbors
, and we denote
S\simeq S^{\prime}
, if both datasets differ by only one element.
In this paper, we define a learning algorithm as a mapping

\mathcal{A}:\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
,
S\longmapsto\mathcal{A}(S)
,
where
\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
denotes the set of Borel probability measures on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
.
We will refer to the distribution
\mathcal{A}(S)
, for
S\in\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}^{n}
, as a
posterior distribution
.

The classical notion of
Differential Privacy
(DP), as introduced in
24
;
25
, regards the possibility of distinguishing the posterior distributions induced by two neighboring datasets.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP)
.

Let
\varepsilon\geq 0
and
\delta\in[0,1]
. The learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is said to satisfy
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP if, for any
S\simeq S^{\prime}
and any Borel set
B
in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
,

\mathcal{A}(S)(B)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})(B)+\delta.

In
42
, the author defined a notion of DP based on the Rényi divergence between neighboring posterior distributions.

Definition 2

(
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP)
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\beta>1
.
The learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is said to satisfy
(\beta,\kappa)
-Rényi differential privacy (RDP) if, for any
S\simeq S^{\prime}
,

\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(\mathcal{A}(S),\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime}))\leq\kappa
,
where, given two Borel probability measures
{\mathds{Q}}\ll\mathds{P}
, we define their Rényi divergence by

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P}):=\frac{1}{\beta-1}\log\mathrm{E}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P})\quad\text{with}\,\,\,\,\mathrm{E}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P}):=\int\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}}\right)^{\beta}\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}.

(5)

By convention, we set
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P}):=+\infty
when
{\mathds{Q}}\not\ll\mathds{P}
.
Note that we also define
\mathrm{R}_{1}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P}):=\text{{{KL}}}\left({\mathds{Q}}||\mathds{P}\right):=\int\log(\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}/\mathrm{d}\mathds{P})\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}
, which is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
{\mathds{Q}}
and
\mathds{P}
.
It can be seen that
\beta\mapsto\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(\cdot,\cdot)
is an increasing and continuous map for
\beta\geq 1

(
56
)
.
The particular interest of the above notion comes from the fact that RDP implies DP.

Lemma 3
.

For any
\kappa>0
,
\beta>1
, and
\delta\in(0,1]
,
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP implies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, with
\varepsilon=\kappa+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\beta-1}
.

The proof of this result is elementary and can be found in
1
;
42
. This result is also mentioned in
5
, who also prove tighter conversion results.
In the case where
\varepsilon=0
, we also have the following conversion lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
\beta>1
.
Then,
(\beta,\kappa)
-RDP implies
(0,\sqrt{\kappa/2})
-DP.

Proof.

Let
S\simeq S^{\prime}
in
\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}^{n}
. By
(
56
, Theorem 3)
, the Rényi divergence
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P})
is non-decreasing in
\beta
, and thus,

\displaystyle\text{{{KL}}}\left(\mathcal{A}(S)||\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\right)\leq\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(\mathcal{A}(S),\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime}))\leq\kappa.

Therefore, by Pinsker’s inequality
(
56
, Theorem 31)
,
we obtain that
\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{A}(S),\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime}))\leq\sqrt{\kappa/2}
.
∎

We also define the following Rényi information, which plays a crucial role in
19
, as well as in the proofs of our main results. We follow the convention of
19
for the normalization of this quantity.

Definition 5
.

Let
{\mathds{Q}}
and
\mathds{P}
be two probability measures on
\Omega
such that
{\mathds{Q}}\ll\mathds{P}
and
\beta>1
. Assume that the Radon-Nikodym derivative
\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}/\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}
is differentiable. Then, the Rényi information of
{\mathds{Q}}
with respect to
\mathds{P}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}_{\beta}({\mathds{Q}},\mathds{P}):=\int\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}}\right)^{\beta-2}\left\|\nabla\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathds{Q}}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}}\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}.

2.2
Lévy processes and infinitely divisible distributions

An interesting aspect of our approach based on Rényi flows is that the proof techniques extend to a very general class of Lévy processes. This fact is highlighted in
Section

B.1
in the appendix. In order to present it clearly, we give below some technical background on Lévy processes.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathds{P})
be a fixed probability space.
A stochastic process
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is called a Lévy process, if
L_{0}=0
almost surely, and it satisfies the following properties.

•

Stationary increments:
For all
s\leq t
,
{\mathrm{Law}\left(L_{t}-L_{s}\right)}={\mathrm{Law}\left(L_{t-s}\right)}
.

•

Independent increments:

L_{t}-L_{s}
is independent from the
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(L_{u},~u\leq s)
.

•

Stochastic continuity:
For all
\varepsilon>0
, we have
\lim_{s\to t}\mathds{P}\left(\left\|L_{t}-L_{s}\right\|>\varepsilon\right)=0
.

Under these conditions, the distribution of
L_{t}
is
infinitely divisible

(
50
)
, which means that its distribution can be written as an
m
-fold convolution for all
m\in{\mathds{N}}^{\star}:=\{1,2,\cdots\}
. There is a one-to-one correspondence between Lévy processes and infinitely divisible distributions.
Classically, this implies that the characteristic function of the Lévy process
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be expressed for all
\xi\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
as
\mathds{E}\left[\exp(i\xi^{\top}L_{t})\right]=\exp(-t\psi(\xi))
, where
i:=\sqrt{-1}
and
\psi
is called the characteristic exponent, which is given by the celebrated Lévy-Khintchine formula

10
)

\displaystyle\psi(\xi)=-ib^{\top}\xi+\frac{1}{2}\xi^{\top}\Sigma\xi+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(1-e^{i\xi^{\top}z}+i\xi^{\top}z\chi(\left\|z\right\|)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(z),\quad\xi\in\mathds{R}^{d},

(6)

where
b\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
,
\Sigma\in\mathds{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix,
\chi
satisfies
1
1

1

(
10
)
.

\chi(s):=(1+s^{2})^{-1}
, and
\nu
is a positive Radon measure on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
such that

\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\min(1,\left\|z\right\|^{2})\mathrm{d}\nu(z)<+\infty
.
The measure
\nu
is called the
Lévy measure
and
(b,\Sigma,\nu)
is the
Lévy triplet
of
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
For instance, the standard Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
is a Lévy process with triplet
(0,I_{d},0)
.
In our paper, we are in particular interested in the rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable Lévy process
(L_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geq 0}
, whose characteristic exponent is given by
\psi(\xi)=\left\|\xi\right\|^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha\in(0,2]
. When
\alpha<2
, its Lévy triplet is given by
(0,0,\nu)

(
10
, Example 2.4.d)
, with

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\nu(z):=C_{\alpha,d}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{\alpha+d}}\quad\text{and }\quad C_{\alpha,d}:=\alpha 2^{\alpha-1}\pi^{-d/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}.

(7)

Lévy processes are characterized by their infinitesimal generator. A complete understanding of this notion is not absolutely necessary to understand our paper; however, the reader may consult
50
;
10
for additional details on this topic.
For a Lévy process with triplet
(0,\Sigma,\nu)
, the infinitesimal generator is given by

\displaystyle Au(x):=\frac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\Sigma\nabla u(x)\right)+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(u(x+z)-u(x)-\nabla u(x)\cdot z\chi(\left\|z\right\|)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(z)

(8)

for any

In the case of the
\alpha

;
44

Definition 6

(Fractional Laplacian)
.

For
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the fractional Laplacian of

is given by

-\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u(x):=C_{\alpha,d}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\frac{u(x+z)-u(x)}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}z
.
We can show that this definition is equivalent to
Equation

in the case where

and

Remark 7
.

The fractional Laplacian is sometimes defined without the constant

2.3
Fractional Poincaré inequalities

The analysis of differential privacy in the presence of Gaussian noise makes crucial use of the
logarithmic Sobolev inequalities
(LSIs)

(
32
;
6
)
, which are instrumental in making the proposed bounds uniform in time
(
19
;
18
;
48
;
62
)
. In such cases, assuming that an LSI is satisfied is motivated by the fact that it is satisfied by the posterior distributions of the learning algorithms under reasonable assumptions. Unfortunately, as was noted by
23
in their study of the generalization error of heavy-tailed SDEs, such LSIs may not always be applicable in the presence of
\alpha
-stable noise.

In our paper, we show that, in the context of heavy-tailed dynamics, LSIs can be replaced by
fractional Poincaré inequalities
.
Such inequalities have been widely studied
(
59
;
14
;
43
)
and have been used in the context of machine learning for generalization bounds
(
23
)
and sampling
(
34
)
.
In particular, we have the following result
(
31
;
61
)
.

Theorem 8

(Fractional Poincaré inequalities)
.

Let
\mu
be an infinitely divisible distribution on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
with Lévy triplet
(0,\Sigma,\nu)
in the sense of
Equation

6
. Then, for any differentiable and
\mu
-square-integrable function
f:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu-\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left\|\nabla f\right\|^{2}_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\mu+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}(f(x)-f(x+z))^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(z),

where
\left\|u\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}:=u^{\top}\Sigma u
for any
u\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
. In particular, if
\mu
is the law of
L_{1}^{\alpha}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu-\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}\leq C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}(f(x)-f(x+z))^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}.

In particular, when
\nu=0
, the above result recovers the classical Poincaré inequalities for Gaussian distributions.
By analogy with the generalizations of classical Poincaré inequalities from the Gaussian measures to more general ones, we propose the following definition.

Definition 9

(
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequalities)
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure and
\alpha\in(0,2)
, we say that
\mu
satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality with constants
(a,b)
, if for any differentiable and
\mu
-square-integrable function
f:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu-\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{2}\leq aC_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\frac{(f(x)-f(x+z))^{2}}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z+b\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu.

We use the unusual denomination
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality instead of fractional Poincaré inequality because we allow the inequality to contain a Gaussian component, corresponding to the term
\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu
. This allows our analysis to apply to the case where the noise of the algorithm is a combination of Gaussian and
\alpha
-stable noises. We prove in
Section

4.2
several properties related to the stability of such inequalities under certain transformations, thus showing that these inequalities can be satisfied by a wide variety of probability distributions. For more general conditions regarding the validity of these inequalities, we refer to
59
;
43
.

In order to simplify the notation, we will use the following notation for Dirichlet forms.

Definition 10
.

For
\alpha\in(0,2)
, define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(f,f):=\frac{1}{2}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\frac{(f(x)-f(y))^{2}}{\left\|x-y\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}y,\quad f\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})};

and for
\alpha=2
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(f,f):=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad f\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}.

These quantities correspond, respectively, to the Dirichlet forms associated with
(L_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\sqrt{2}B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. While a complete knowledge of Dirichlet forms is not required for this paper, we invite the reader to consult
6
;
10
for more details.

3
Differential Privacy of Lévy-Driven SDEs

In this section, we present our main results, which include RDP guarantees for Lévy-driven SDEs. We first present our setup and key technical lemmas in
Sections

3.2
and
3.1
respectively, before presenting two cases. First, we derive RDP guarantees under multifractal noise (
i.e.
, a combination of Gaussian and
\alpha
-stable noise) in
Section

3.3
. We will then explain how our analysis can be extended to pure-jump
\alpha
-stable noise in
Section

3.4
.

3.1
Setup and assumptions

Given a dataset
S\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and a loss function
\ell:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
, we define the empirical risk as
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(w):=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(w,z_{i})
. We also denote by
\mu_{z}
the data distribution on
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{F})
.

Let us consider two neighboring datasets
S\simeq S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
. Given a fixed common initial distribution, we consider the two following stochastic differential equations (SDEs):

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}W_{t}=-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(W_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\alpha}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\prime}=-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}(W_{t}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{\alpha}\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\alpha}+\sigma_{2}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},\end{cases}

(9)

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion,
(L_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geq 0}
is a
d
-dimensional rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with
\alpha\in(1,2)
, and
(\sigma_{2},\sigma_{\alpha})
are positive constants.
Inspired by
19
, we define the gradient sensitivity as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{g}:=\esssup_{(z,z^{\prime})\sim\mu_{z}\otimes\mu_{z}}\sup_{w\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla\ell(w,z^{\prime})-\nabla\ell(w,z)\right\|.

(10)

In some of our results, we make the following finite sensitivity assumption.

Assumption 1

(Finite sensitivity)
.

The gradient sensitivity is finite, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{g}<+\infty
.

Remark 11
.

In some of our main results, we use
1
to uniformly control the difference between the two drifts in
Equation

9
. This assumption is typically satisfied when
\ell(w,z)
is a regularized loss of the form
\ell(w,z)=\ell_{0}(w,z)+\lambda\left\|w\right\|^{2}
, where
\ell_{0}(\cdot,z)
is Lipschitz-continuous, for all
z\in\mathcal{Z}
.
Alternatively, one can impose this condition by clipping all the individual gradients
\nabla\ell(\cdot,z)
outside a compact set.
Similar conditions appear in the literature related to Gaussian noise
(
19
;
18
;
48
;
62
)
, where it is often associated with projections on a compact set.
For Brownian motion–driven SDEs, reflection on convex sets is well-defined via the Skorokhod problem
(
40
)
, which constrains the dynamics to a convex domain by means of a boundary regulator. For Lévy processes, however, jumps can overshoot the boundary, so that the reflection is not canonical and typically induces non-local, state-dependent boundary conditions. Existing constructions of regulators are case specific and apply only in restricted settings
(
41
;
20
;
53
;
45
)
. For this reason, we consider projections only in the discrete-time case.

Remark 12
.

In our proofs, the quantities
\left\|\nabla\ell(w,z^{\prime})-\nabla\ell(w,z)\right\|
are integrated with respect to certain probability distributions (see
Appendix

B
). This suggests that
1
could be replaced by weaker but more intricate conditions. We leave this discussion for future work and focus our main results on the finite sensitivity case.

We denote by
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{\prime}
the probability density functions of
W_{t}
and
W_{t}^{\prime}
. Throughout the paper, we assume that both SDEs are initialized from the same distribution. It is known that, under mild regularity assumptions
(
22
;
55
)
,
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{\prime}
are solutions (at least in a weak sense) of the following fractional Fokker-Planck equations,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}p_{t}=-\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}p_{t}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\Delta p_{t}+\nabla\cdot\left(p_{t}\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}\right),\\
\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\prime}=-\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}p_{t}^{\prime}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\Delta p_{t}^{\prime}+\nabla\cdot\left(p_{t}^{\prime}\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}\right),\end{cases}

(11)

where
\left(-\Delta\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is the fractional Laplacian operator given in
Definition

6
.

Our analysis of the differential privacy of heavy-tailed SDEs relies on estimates of the so-called
Rényi flow
,
i.e.
, the time-derivative of the Rényi divergence between
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{\prime}
. In order to make these derivations perfectly rigorous, we need to impose enough regularity on the probability density functions of
W_{t}
and
W_{t}^{\prime}
, which is made precise as follows.

Assumption 2

(Regularity conditions)
.

Let
v_{t}:=p_{t}/p_{t}^{\prime}
. We assume that
p_{t}
,
p_{t}^{\prime}
and
v_{t}
are positive, differentiable in
t
, and belong to
{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}
. Moreover, we make the following
non-explosion
assumption, i.e., the maps
t\mapsto\left\|v_{t}\right\|_{\infty}
are locally bounded on
\mathds{R}_{+}
, and the functions
|\partial_{t}p_{t}|
and
|\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\prime}|
are locally uniformly integrable on
\mathds{R}_{+}
.

We say that a family of functions
\{f(t,\cdot)\}_{t\geq 0}
is locally uniformly integrable if for all
t_{0}\geq 0
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\sup_{t_{0}-\varepsilon<t<t_{0}+\varepsilon}|f(t,\cdot)|\in L^{1}(\mathrm{d}x)
.

We make
2
for two reasons. First, it ensures that we can rigorously compute the entropy flow by differentiating
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
under the integrals. Secondly, the uniform integrability near
t=0
ensures that the map
t\mapsto\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
is continuous at
t=0
, which simplifies the derivations.
Intuitively, the non-explosion condition ensures that the time-derivatives of the density functions of
W_{t}
and
W_{t}^{\prime}
do not explode too fast, so that the derivative of their relative Rényi divergence can be controlled.
Intuitively, we observe that the above condition suggests that the drifts
\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}
and
\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}
do not grow too fast at infinity.

These conditions are reasonable in the context of SDEs driven by rotationally invariant
\alpha
-stable Lévy processes. Indeed, some studies show that, under mild conditions, the distributions of
(L_{t}^{\alpha})_{t\geq 0}
and those generated by such SDEs have polynomial tails, typically in
\left\|x\right\|^{-d-\alpha}

(
49
;
9
;
17
)
.
This can be readily verified in the case of the
\alpha
-stable Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that
v_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}
.
We believe that this (reasonable) condition could be relaxed and we use this assumptions mainly to simplify some technical arguments, as checking such tail estimates is beyond the scope of this paper.

Finally, it should be noted that
2
could be replaced by other conditions. Indeed,
23
computed (different but) similar entropy flow along heavy-tailed SDEs, and their main theorems make use of intricate regularity and domination conditions of the functions
v_{t}(x)
. In our work, we simplify this approach by noting that
2
is enough for the Rényi flow computations to be rigorous.

3.2
Rényi flows computations

Our work is based on a new upper bound of the Rényi flow,
i.e.
, the time derivative of the Rényi divergence along the fractional Fokker-Planck equations. With a slight abuse of notation, in what follows, we denote by
p^{\prime}_{t}
the probability distribution whose density function is also denoted by
p^{\prime}_{t}
.

Theorem 13

(Rényi flow for heavy-tailed SDEs)
.

Assume that
2
holds.
Then, for any
\beta\geq 2
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq-\frac{2\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\beta-1}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}-\frac{4\sigma^{2}_{2}}{\beta}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}+\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{potential}},

where the following term accounts for the contribution of the potentials

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{potential}}:=\beta\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}^{\beta-1}\frac{\langle\nabla v_{t},\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}\rangle}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

See
Section

B.2
.
∎

Theorem

13
shows that the Rényi flow is controlled by three terms: (i) the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}

\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{potential}}

In the absence of pure-jump noise (
i.e.
,
\sigma_{\alpha}=0
),

13
extends existing computations obtained in the case of Gaussian noise
(
19
)
.
In the case
\beta=2

However, the extension to arbitrary

3.3
Differential privacy of multifractal SDEs

Based on the technical lemmas of the previous subsection, we can now derive our main results, which consists in RDP guarantees for

We first present the case where the noise has a non-trivial Gaussian component,

This case has the advantage of being technically simpler, due to the regularizing effect of the Gaussian noise.

The following theorem is a differential privacy bound for multifractal SDEs.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\beta\geq 2
.
Suppose
1
and
2
hold and
p_{t}^{\prime}
satisfies an

(\gamma\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha},\gamma\sigma_{2}^{2})

Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq\frac{\beta\mathcal{S}_{g}^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}n^{2}}t=:K_{n}t,\quad t>0.

(12)

If moreover
K_{n}<a:=\frac{1}{\gamma\beta}

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq-\log\left(1-\frac{\gamma\mathcal{S}_{g}^{2}\beta^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}n^{2}}\right),\quad t>0.

(13)

Proof.

By
Theorem

13
, for
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq-\frac{2\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\beta-1}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}-\frac{4\sigma^{2}_{2}}{\beta}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}+\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{potential}}.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities, we have that for all
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{potential}}

\displaystyle=\beta\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}^{\beta-1}\frac{\left\langle\nabla v_{t},\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}\right\rangle}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}^{\beta-2}\left\|\nabla v_{t}\right\|^{2}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}v_{t}^{\beta}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x\right)

In the equation above, we choose

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq-\frac{2\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\beta-1}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}_{2}}{\beta}\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}+\frac{\beta\mathcal{S}_{g}^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}n^{2}}.

Now we use

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\leq-\frac{1}{\gamma\beta}\left(1-e^{-\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}\right)+\frac{\beta\mathcal{S}_{g}^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}n^{2}}.

(14)

We solve this type of differential inequality in
Lemma

26
in Appendix
A
.
In order to apply this lemma, we observe that
2
also implies the continuity of

The claim immediately then follows by noting that both SDEs are initialized with the same probability distributions.
∎

The differential inequality (

).
A similar differential inequality appears in the derivations of

We distinguish two regimes: (i) it always holds that

grows at most linearly with time, corresponding to a regime where the Poincaré constant of the SDEs is too large, and (ii) if additionally
K_{n}<a
, then
\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
is bounded by a constant for all
t>0
. Note that, as long as
\gamma<\infty
, we have
K_{n}<a
as long as the sample size
n
is sufficiently large.

Theorem

15
has an interesting dependence on
\beta
(the order of the Rényi divergence). By
Equation

12
, we always have a RDP guarantee in
\rho\beta
, with
\rho=\mathcal{O}\left(t/n^{2}\right)
. This dependence in
\beta
corresponds to the zero-concentrated DP guarantee introduced by
12
but comes at a cost of a potential linear dependence on time. On the other hand, we observe that when
K_{n}<a
, we can remove the time-dependence at the cost of a slightly worst dependence on
\beta
, yielding a bound in
\mathcal{O}\left(\beta^{2}/n^{2}\right)
. It can be seen from our proofs (in
Lemma

14
) that the presence of
\beta^{2}
is inherently due to the non-local nature of the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}
, and cannot be avoided in our approach. We see this behavior as a
semi-concentrated
DP guarantee.

In the case
K_{n}<a
, we obtain
(\beta,\gamma)
-RDP with
\gamma=\mathcal{O}\left(\beta^{2}/n^{2}\right)
as
n\to\infty
. By

(0,\delta)
-DP with

, which is the same rate in

that was obtained by

for (S)GD under heavy-tailed noise.
Moreover, our results provide a differential privacy guarantee that is independent of the dimension.
This independence of the dimension is intrinsically related to the presence of Gaussian noise. In the next section, we will analyze in more detail the case of pure-jump noise and show that we still obtain a weaker dependence on the dimension compared to existing works.

3.4
Differential privacy of pure-jump SDEs

We now present the case of pure
\alpha
-stable noise. That is,
\sigma_{2}=0
, and we consider the following SDEs, where we denote
\sigma:=\sigma_{\alpha}
for simplicity,

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}W_{t}=-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(W_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\alpha},\\
\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{\prime}=-\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S^{\prime}}(W_{t}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}L_{t}^{\alpha}.\end{cases}

(15)

Before presenting the main result of this subsection, we first motivate the approach.
In the proof of
Theorem

15
, the Young inequality is used, leading to a term proportional to
{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)
, which is canceled out by the diffusive part of the Rényi flow. Unfortunately, this procedure cannot be applied when
\sigma_{2}=0
.

The intuition to address this issue comes from the celebrated Bourgain-Brezis-Mironescu’s theorem (also known as BBM formula)
(
11

, stating that for all

in the Sobolev space
H^{1}\left({\mathds{R}^{d}}\right):=\left\{u\in L^{2}\left({\mathds{R}^{d}}\right):\nabla u\in L^{2}\left({\mathds{R}^{d}}\right)\right\}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 2^{-}}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\iint_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{\left\|x-y\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{2\Gamma\left(1+\frac{d}{2}\right)}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla u(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x.

(16)

In our case, the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}(u,u)
corresponds to a weighted version of the left-hand side of
Equation

16
. In fact, we are even able to prove a weighted version of the BBM formula; namely, for any
u\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}
and any Borel probability measure

,

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to 2^{-}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(u,u)={\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(u,u).

(17)

The proof of this result is deferred to
Lemma

31
in
Section

B.3
. Note that a similar observation can be found in
(
34
)
.
Equation

17
shows that it is reasonable to approximate
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}
by
{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}
and, hence, makes the proof technique of
Theorem

15
applicable to our case.
In order to leverage this intuition more quantitatively and non-asymptotically, we require the following additional regularity assumption.

Assumption 3
.

We assume that, with
v_{t}:=p_{t}/p_{t}^{\prime}

and all

\displaystyle\sup_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\sup_{t\leq T}\left(\left\|\nabla v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\nabla^{2}v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right\|_{\infty}\right)<\infty\quad\text{and }\quad\inf_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\inf_{t\leq T}\left\|{\nabla v_{t}^{\beta/2}}\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t}^{\prime})}>0.

Equipped with this assumption, we can prove the following result, which is a RDP guarantee for SDEs driven by pure-jump

Differential privacy of heavy-tailed (stochastic) gradient descent

Setup.

Let

\begin{cases}X_{k+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\left(X_{k}-\eta\widehat{g}_{S}(X_{k},\Omega_{k})+\sigma\eta^{1/\alpha}\xi_{k}\right),\\

\displaystyle\Theta_{t}=\Theta_{t_{k}}-\eta F_{1}^{(k)}(\Theta_{t_{k}})-(t-t_{k})F_{2}^{(k)}(\Theta_{t_{k}})+\sigma(t-t_{k})^{1/\alpha}\xi_{k},~~t_{k}\leq t<t_{k+1},

4.2
Stability properties of the fractional Poincaré inequalities

In this section, we study the stability of fractional Poincaré inequalities through the noisy (S)GD iterations.
This section is a sanity check. It ensures that the
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequalities can be satisfied in practice.
In the case of pure Gaussian noise, similar stability properties have already been used in the context of differential privacy
(
19
;
18
;
48
)
.
These existing results are based on two properties of the classical Poincaré inequalities: (1) stability by Gaussian convolution and (2) stability under pushforward by Lipschitz mapping. It is also well known that Poincaré inequalities are stable by bounded perturbations
(
36
;
6
)
. In our paper, we show that fractional Poincaré inequalities also satisfy comparable properties, which might be of independent interest beyond this work.

The stability of fractional Poincaré inequalities under convolution with an
\alpha
-stable distribution was already observed by
34
. We extend this result in the following lemma to the
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequalities defined in

(Stability under convolution with stable distributions)

Let

Assume that

and

-stable Poincaré inequality with constants
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
and

, respectively.
Let us denote

.
Then,

satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré’s inequality with constants
\left(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\right)
.

Assume that

Estimation of fractional Poincaré constants.

Based on the stability properties derived above, we provide in the next proposition an upper bound of the fractional Poincaré constant of heavy-tailed (S)GD for smooth and strongly convex losses.

Proposition 23
.

Assume that
\ell(w,z)
is
\lambda
-strongly convex and
M
-smooth in
w
. We consider
Equation

19
with
\mathcal{C}={\mathds{R}^{d}}
. We assume that the initial distribution of
X_{0}
satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality with constants
(\gamma,0)
. We further assume that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{M}\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{d}\right)>1.

(20)

Let us define

c_{0}:=\frac{\eta\sigma^{\alpha}}{1-F(\eta_{0})}
with
F(\eta):=\frac{(1-\eta\lambda)^{\alpha+d}}{(1-\eta M)^{\alpha}}
.
Then
F(\eta_{0})<1
and if

, then the distribution of

satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality with constants
(c_{0},0)
for all

.

Proof.

See
Section

B.5
.
∎

In practice, this result imposes a relatively strong condition on the condition number
M/\lambda
, so that it would apply only for well-conditioned losses. This behavior is due to
Lemma

21
, which introduces dimension-dependent quantities in the estimation of the
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality constants.
To better understand this aspect, we observe that our
Lemma

21
does not recover the Gaussian limit as
\alpha\to 2^{-}
, which could however be expected by the weighted BBM formula,
Equation

17
. This shows that
Lemma

21
might be largely improvable, leading to much better estimates of the fractional Poincaré constants. Improving such stability lemmas for fractional Poincaré inequalities is beyond the scope of this paper, and we leave it for future works.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we provided the first RDP guarantees for heavy-tailed SDEs with
\alpha
-stable noise.
We explored both the multifractal (
i.e.
, with a non-trivial Gaussian component) and the pure-jump case.
In both cases, we obtain a semi-concentrated RDP guarantee with two regimes: (i) a time-uniform bound when under the assumption of an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality and (ii) a regime where the bound can grow at most linearly with time but has the same dependence on the order of the Rényi divergence than in concentrated DP.
In both cases, we can convert our results to
(0,\delta)
-DP, in which case we obtain a dependence on the dimension comparable to existing works.
Finally, we extended our results in the discrete time setting and proved stability lemmas for
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequalities, hence, providing theoretical foundations to satisfy these inequalities in practice.

Limitations & future works.
As already discussed above, some of our results rely on the existence of fractional Poincaré inequalities for the finite-time distributions generated by the heavy-tailed SDEs we consider.
We provided several stability lemmas for these inequalities, allowing to verify them in certain cases.
However, we believe that the estimate of the fractional Poincaré constants can be improved, and is an important (yet, difficult) direction for future works.
We observed in
Section

3
that our main results have two regimes, one that can grow at most linearly with time but with a concentrated DP guarantee (
i.e.
,
\mathcal{O}\left(\beta\right)
), and one that is uniform in time but grows as
\beta^{2}
, under the assumption of a fractional Poincaré inequality. While this dependence on
\beta
in the second case seems unavoidable with our approach, we believe that it is a relevant direction for future work, as it might require to strengthen certain functional inequalities existing in the literature.
Finally, relaxing or verifying the regularity conditions made in this work is another promising research direction, which might be related to recent fractional heat kernel estimates.

, presents a solution to the differential inequality that naturally appears in our Rényi flow computations in
Section

3.2
.

Definition 24

(Bregman divergence)
.

Let
\Phi:(0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathds{R}
be a convex function. Then we define the Bregman divergence, for
a,b\in(0,\infty)
, as

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}\left(a,b\right):=\Phi(a)-\Phi(b)-\Phi^{\prime}(b)(a-b).

By convexity, it is clear that the above quantity is always non-negative. When
\Phi(x)=\Phi_{\beta}(x):=x^{\beta}
for
\beta>1
, we will shorten the notation and denote

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{\beta}\left(a,b\right):=\mathrm{D}_{\Phi_{\beta}}\left(a,b\right)=a^{\beta}+(\beta-1)b^{\beta}-\beta ab^{\beta-1}.

(21)

Note in particular that
\mathrm{D}_{2}\left(a,b\right):=(a-b)^{2}
.

Lemma 25
.

Let
\beta\geq 2
. Then, for all
a,b\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{2}\left(a^{\beta/2},b^{\beta/2}\right)\leq\mathrm{D}_{\beta}\left(a,b\right).

Proof.

If
\beta=2
, the result is obvious; if
\beta>2
, we first note that when
a=0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{2}\left(a^{{\beta}/{2}},b^{{\beta}/{2}}\right)-\mathrm{D}_{\beta}\left(a,b\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a^{{\beta}/{2}}-b^{{\beta}/{2}}\right)^{2}-\left(a^{\beta}+(\beta-1)b^{\beta}-\beta ab^{\beta-1}\right)=(2-\beta)b^{\beta}\leq 0.

Let us now assume
a>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}_{2}\left(a^{{\beta}/{2}},b^{{\beta}/{2}}\right)-\mathrm{D}_{\beta}\left(a,b\right)

\displaystyle=\left(a^{\beta/2}-b^{\beta/2}\right)^{2}-\left(a^{\beta}+(\beta-1)b^{\beta}-\beta ab^{\beta-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-(\beta-2)b^{\beta}-2(ab)^{{\beta}/{2}}+\beta ab^{\beta-1}

\displaystyle=-(ab)^{{\beta}/{2}}\left((\beta-2)\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{{\beta}/{2}}-\beta\left(\frac{b}{a}\right)^{{\beta}/{2}-1}+2\right).

Therefore, it makes sense to study the polynomial function

\displaystyle P(x)=(\beta-2)x^{\frac{\beta}{2}}-\beta x^{\frac{\beta}{2}-1}+2,\quad x\geq 0.

Clearly
P(0)=2>0
. For any
x>0
,

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\beta(\beta-2)}{2}x^{\frac{\beta}{2}-2}(x-1).

Thus, the minimum of
P
is
P(1)=0
. The result follows.
∎

In the next lemma, we solve a class of differential inequalities appearing in our theory.

Lemma 26
.

Consider a non-negative function
f:(0,\infty)\longrightarrow\mathds{R}_{+}
that is continuous and differentiable, admits a right limit as
t\to 0^{+}
, and satisfies

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t)\leq K-a\left(1-e^{-f(t)}\right)\quad\text{for any $t>0$},

(22)

where
a,K>0
are two constants.
Then, we have the following results:

•

For any
t>0
,
f(t)\leq f(0^{+})+Kt
.

•

If
K<a
and
f(0^{+})\leq\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)
, then for any
t>0
,
f(t)\leq\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)
.

•

If
K<a
and
f(0^{+})>\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)
, then for any
t>0
,

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)+\log\left(1+e^{-(a-K)t}\left(e^{f(0^{+})}\frac{a-K}{a}-1\right)\right).

Proof.

Case
1
:
We note that for any
t>0
,
f^{\prime}(t)\leq K
. Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
t>0
,
f(t)\leq f(\varepsilon)+K(t-\varepsilon)
. By taking the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we conclude that
f(t)\leq Kt+f(0^{+})
for all
t\geq 0
.

In the following, we denote
f_{0}:=\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)
.

Case
2
:
Assume that
K<a
and
f(0^{+})\leq f_{0}
. Suppose that there exists
\tau>0
such that
f(\tau)>f_{0}
.
By
Equation

22
, this implies that
f^{\prime}(\tau)<0
.
By continuity of
f
, there exists
t_{0}\in(0,\tau]
such that
f(t_{0})=\sup_{s\in(0,\tau]}f(s)
. Necessarily,
t_{0}>0
, and thus
f^{\prime}(t_{0})=0
, as
f^{\prime}(\tau)<0
, which implies
f(t_{0})\leq f_{0}
by
Equation

22
; but that is absurd. The claim follows.

Case
3
:
Let
g(t):=f(t)-f_{0}
. Let
\tau:=\inf\{t>0:g(t)\leq 0\}
. By assumption, we know that
\tau>0
. For
t\in(0,\tau)
, a simple computation shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(e^{g(t)}-1\right)=g^{\prime}(t)e^{g(t)}\leq-(a-K)e^{g(t)}\left(1-e^{-g(t)}\right)=-(a-K)\left(e^{g(t)}-1\right).

By Grönwall’s inequality, we deduce that, for
t\in(0,\tau)
:

\displaystyle e^{g(t)}\leq 1+e^{-(a-K)t}\left(e^{g(0^{+})}-1\right)=1+e^{-(a-K)t}\left(e^{f(0^{+})}\frac{a-K}{a}-1\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle f(t)\leq\log\left(\frac{a}{a-K}\right)+\log\left(1+e^{-(a-K)t}\left(e^{f(0^{+})}\frac{a-K}{a}-1\right)\right).

For
t\geq\tau
, we can apply
Case
2
and observe that the above inequality is still true; hence, it is proven for all
t>0
.
∎

Lemma 28
.

We have the following results:

\displaystyle C_{\alpha,d}\underset{\alpha\to 2^{-}}{\sim}(2-\alpha)\pi^{-d/2}d\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right),\quad C_{\alpha,d}\underset{d\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{\alpha 2^{\alpha/2-1}\pi^{-d/2}}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)d^{\alpha/2}.

Proof.

Recall that

C_{\alpha,d}:=\alpha 2^{\alpha-1}\pi^{-d/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+d}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}
.
By the continuity of the
\Gamma

\Gamma\left(\frac{\alpha+d}{2}\right)\to\Gamma\left(1+\frac{d}{2}\right)=\frac{d}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)

as

B.1
Renyi flow along general Lévy processes

We first prove a more general version of
Theorem

13
we allow the driving noise in
Equation

9
to be an arbitrary Lévy process (with a symmetric Lévy measure).
This highlights one of the main advantages of our approach compared to the existing literature on differential privacy for heavy-tailed algorithms: our proof techniques are not restricted to
\alpha
-stable noise.

In this section, we therefore study general Lévy processes, as they have been introduced in
Section

2.2
.
Formally, it corresponds to replacing
Equation

2
with the following SDE,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W_{t}=\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(W_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}L_{t},

(23)

where
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Lévy process with triplet given by
(0,\sqrt{2}I_{d},\nu)
, with
\nu
a
symmetric
Lévy measure (
i.e.
,
\nu(\mathrm{d}z)=\nu(-\mathrm{d}z)
).

Moreover, in this section, we also allow the drift terms in the SDEs to be time-dependent, which will be particularly helpful to extend our theory to the discrete-time setting in
Section

4
.

More precisely, we consider two probability densities following fractional Fokker-Planck equations (FPEs)
(
22
;
55
)
driven by different force fields. We define two time-dependent vector fields

\displaystyle F_{t},F_{t}^{\prime}:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\longrightarrow{\mathds{R}^{d}},

and we consider
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{\prime}
two probability densities that are smooth solutions of the following fractional FPEs, for some tail-index
\alpha\in(0,2)
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}p_{t}=I[p_{t}]+\sigma_{2}^{2}\Delta p_{t}+\nabla\cdot(p_{t}F_{t}),\\
\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\prime}=I[p_{t}^{\prime}]+\sigma_{2}^{2}\Delta p_{t}^{\prime}+\nabla\cdot(p_{t}^{\prime}F_{t}^{\prime}),\end{cases}

(24)

where the pseudo-differential operator
A:=\sigma_{2}\Delta+\mathrm{I}
corresponds to the infinitesimal generator of the Lévy process
(L_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, according to
Equation

8
.
Therefore, with the notations of
Equation

8
, the non-local operator
\mathrm{I}
is given for
u\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}
by

\displaystyle I[u](x):=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(u(x+z)-u(x)-\nabla u(x)\cdot z\chi(\left\|z\right\|)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(z).

Note that the operator
A
is self-adjoint with respect to the Lebesgue measure
(
31
)
(for smooth enough functions). We will use this fact repeatedly in our proofs.

This leads us to the following lemma, which is a Rényi flow computation along SDEs driven by general Lévy processes.
This lemma fundamentally differs from the derivations of
23
for two main reasons: (1) we focus on the Rényi flow instead of the entropy flow and (2) in this lemma we allow both dynamics to by time-varying, instead of considering stationary distributions.

Lemma 29
.

Assume that
2
holds. Then, for any
\beta>1
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})=-\frac{\sigma_{\alpha}^{\alpha}}{\beta-1}\frac{\mathrm{B}_{\beta}^{\alpha}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}-\beta\sigma^{2}_{2}\frac{\mathrm{I}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}+\beta\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}^{\beta-1}\frac{\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}-F_{t}^{\prime}\rangle}{\mathrm{E}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right)}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x,

where the term
B_{\beta}^{\alpha}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
is called the Bregman integral and is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}^{\alpha}_{\beta}\left(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}\right):=C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\mathrm{D}_{\beta}\left(\frac{p_{t}}{p_{t}^{\prime}}(x),\frac{p_{t}}{p_{t}^{\prime}}(x+z)\right)p_{t}^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\nu(z).

Proof.

We consider the Fokker-Planck equations given by
Equation

24
, with the notations above. To simplify the notations, we use as above the operator

A[u]:=I[u]+\sigma_{2}\Delta u.

Let us denote
v_{t}:=p_{t}/p_{t}^{\prime}
and
\Phi(x):=x^{\beta}
.
2
ensures that we can differentiate under the integral, and write

\displaystyle(\beta-1)\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi(v_{t})p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})\left(\partial_{t}p_{t}-v_{t}\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\prime}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi(v_{t})\partial_{t}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})\left(A[p_{t}]+\nabla\cdot(p_{t}F_{t})-v_{t}A[p_{t}^{\prime}]-v_{t}\nabla\cdot\left(p_{t}^{\prime}F_{t}^{\prime}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi(v_{t})\left(A[p_{t}^{\prime}]+\nabla\cdot\left(p_{t}^{\prime}F_{t}^{\prime}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x.

Using the facts that
I
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{d};\mathrm{d}x)

(
31
)
and that
p_{t}
and
p_{t}^{\prime}
vanish at infinity (so that the boundary terms are equal to
0
) by
2
, we can integrate by parts to obtain that

\displaystyle(\beta-1)\mathrm{E}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{R}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{diffusion}}+C_{\mathrm{fractional}}+C_{\mathrm{potential}},

where

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{diffusion}}:

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}p_{t}^{\prime}\left(v_{t}\Delta\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})-\Delta(v_{t}\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}))+\Delta\Phi(v_{t}))\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}p_{t}^{\prime}\Big(v_{t}\nabla\cdot(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\nabla\cdot(\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t}+v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t})+\nabla\cdot(\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t}))\Big)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}p_{t}^{\prime}\left(v_{t}\nabla\cdot\left(\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t}\right)-\nabla\cdot\left(v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\nabla v_{t}\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\left\|\nabla v_{t}\right\|^{2}p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x=-\sigma_{2}^{2}\beta(\beta-1)\mathrm{I}_{\beta}(p_{t},p_{t}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{potential}}:

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}p_{t}^{\prime}\left(v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}\right\rangle+\left\langle\nabla(v_{t}\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}))),F_{t}^{\prime}\right\rangle-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}p_{t}^{\prime}\left(v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}\right\rangle+v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}^{\prime}\right\rangle\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(v_{t})\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}^{\prime}-F_{t}\right\rangle p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x=\beta(\beta-1)\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}v_{t}^{\beta-1}\left\langle\nabla v_{t},F_{t}^{\prime}-F_{t}\right\rangle p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x

and

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{fractional}}:=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left(I[\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})]v_{t}-I[v_{t}\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t})]+I[\Phi(v_{t})]\right)p_{t}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x.

Now we use the expression of the non-local operator as

\displaystyle I[\phi](x)=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(\phi(x+z)-\phi(x)-\langle\nabla\phi(x),z\rangle\chi(\left\|z\right\|)\right)\mathrm{d}\nu(z).

The terms containing
\chi(\left\|z\right\|)
cancel each other out, so that we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{fractional}}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\Big\{v_{t}(x)\left[\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}(x+z))-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}(x))\right]-v_{t}(x+z)\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}(x+z))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\qquad\qquad+v_{t}(x)\Phi^{\prime}(v_{t}(x))+\Phi(v_{t}(x+z))-\Phi(v_{t}(x))\Big\}p_{t}^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\mathrm{d}x.

After rearranging the terms, we obtain the following expression

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{fractional}}=-C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(v_{t}(x),v_{t}(x+z))p_{t}^{\prime}(x)\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\mathrm{d}x.

The proof is complete.
∎

The following lemma is a formal proof of

Lemma 31

(The weighted BBM formula)
.

Let
f\in{\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}({\mathds{R}^{d}})}
and
\mu
any Borel probability measure on
{\mathds{R}^{d}}
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(f,f)\underset{\alpha\to 2^{-}}{\longrightarrow}{\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(f,f).

Proof.

We write
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(f,f)=\frac{C_{d,\alpha}}{2}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}\right)
, where

\displaystyle I_{1}:=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{\left\|z\right\|\leq 1}\frac{(f(x+z)-f(x))^{2}}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z,

and

\displaystyle I_{2}:=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{\left\|z\right\|>1}\frac{(f(x+z)-f(x))^{2}}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z\leq 2\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}\int_{\left\|z\right\|>1}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}=\frac{C}{\alpha}.

Here,
C<\infty
is a constant independent of
\alpha
.
On the other hand, by the Taylor-Lagrange formula, for every
x,z\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}
,
f(x+z)-f(x)=\langle z,\nabla f(x)\rangle+\langle z,\nabla^{2}f(\xi_{x,z})z\rangle/2
, with
\xi_{x,z}\in\{x+az,~a\in[0,1]\}
. Therefore, we can further write
I_{1}=I_{3}+I_{4}
as

\displaystyle I_{3}:

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{\left\|z\right\|\leq 1}\frac{\frac{1}{4}\left\langle z,\nabla^{2}f(\xi_{x,z})z\right\rangle^{2}+\left\langle z,\nabla^{2}f(\xi_{x,z})z\right\rangle\left\langle z,\nabla f(x)\right\rangle}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2}}^{2}\int_{\left\|z\right\|\leq 1}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha-3}}=\frac{C^{\prime}}{3-\alpha}

with
C^{\prime}<\infty
being another constant independent of
\alpha
, and

\displaystyle I_{4}:=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{\left\|z\right\|\leq 1}\frac{\langle z,\nabla f(x)\rangle^{2}}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\mathrm{Vol}({\mathds{S}^{d-1}})}{d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}},

thanks to a spherical change of variable and the invariance by rotation.

Therefore, by
Lemma

28
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(f,f)\underset{\alpha\to 2^{-}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}(2-\alpha)\pi^{-d/2}d\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\,\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{d\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}\frac{1}{2-\alpha}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)={\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(f,f).

This completes the proof.

We can now discuss the proof of
Theorem

, which is based on two technical lemmas.
The next lemma is an integral representation of the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}
appearing in our Rényi flow computations.

Lemma 32

(Spherical parameterization of the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(f,f)
)
.

Consider
u:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\longrightarrow\mathds{R}
a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function with bounded gradient. Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathds{R}^{d}})
. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}(u,u)=\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{2d}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathscr{J}(r,u,\mu)\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}},

where
\sigma_{d-1}:=\mathrm{Vol}({\mathds{S}^{d-1}})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}
and
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u,\mu):\mathds{R}_{+}\to\mathds{R}_{+}
is a continuous function satisfying

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(0,u,\mu)={\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(u,u)=\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}.

Proof.

We apply a spherical change of coordinates to get

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}\left(u,u\right)

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}(u(x+z)-u(x))^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}

\displaystyle=C_{\alpha,d}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left(\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{1+\alpha}},

where
\sigma
is the Hausdorff measure on the sphere
{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}
. By Tonelli’s theorem, we can rewrite the above integral as

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,\mu}\left(u,u\right)=K_{\alpha,d}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathscr{J}(r,u,\mu)\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}},

where the function
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u,\mu)
defined for
r>0
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(r,u,\mu):=\frac{d}{\sigma_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\sigma)(x,\theta)

(25)

with
K_{\alpha,d}:=\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{d}.

We note that the integral above is clearly finite under our assumptions on
u
. Moreover, we note that for all
(x,\theta)\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}
, we have by continuity of
\nabla u
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\underset{r\to 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x)\rangle.

Moreover, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}\leq\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}_{\infty}<+\infty.

Therefore, by the dominated convergence theorem with respect to
\mu\otimes\sigma
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(r,u,\mu)\underset{r\to 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{d}{\sigma_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\times{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x)\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mu\otimes\sigma)(x,\theta).

By invariance by rotation and Tonelli’s theorem, we deduce that

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(r,u,\mu)\underset{r\to 0^{+}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{d}{\sigma_{d-1}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla u(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\int_{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}\langle\theta,e_{1}\rangle^{2}\mathrm{d}\sigma(\theta)=\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)},

where
e_{1}\in{\mathds{S}^{d-1}}
is arbitrary. Therefore, we can continuously extend the function
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u,\mu)
on
\mathds{R}_{+}
by setting
\mathscr{J}(0,u,\mu)=\left\|\nabla u\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}={\mathcal{E}}_{2,\mu}(u,u)
. By the dominated convergence, we also obtain that
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u,\mu)
is continuous on
\mathds{R}_{+}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 33

(Lower bound on the Dirichlet form)
.

Assume that
\beta\geq 2
, and that
v_{t}
is twice continuously differentiable for all
t\in(0,T]
so that

\displaystyle\sup_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\sup_{t\leq T}\left(\left\|\nabla v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|\nabla^{2}v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right\|_{\infty}\right)<+\infty,\quad\inf_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\inf_{t\leq T}\left\|\nabla v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right\|_{L^{2}(p_{t}^{\prime})}>0,

(26)

where
S\simeq S^{\prime}
ranges over all neighboring datasets in
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{Z}^{n}
. Then there exists a constant
R>0
(that may depend only on
d
and
\beta
as well as
T
) such that for all
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)\geq\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{2d(2-\alpha)}R^{2-\alpha}{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right).

Proof.

fix
\beta>0
.
Let us introduce
u_{t}:=v_{t}^{\beta/2}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. By our assumptions,

Lemma

32
gives us the spherical representation of the Dirichlet form
{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}(u_{t},u_{t})=\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{2d}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathscr{J}(r,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}}.

Let us denote the integrand in
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
(see (
25
)) by

\displaystyle F(\theta,x,r):=\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}.

We have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\left(\frac{-1}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+s\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\langle\theta,\nabla u(x+r\theta)\rangle\mathrm{d}s\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{\infty}\left|\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\int_{s}^{r}\left\langle\theta,\nabla^{2}u(x+l\theta)\theta\right\rangle\mathrm{d}l\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\nabla^{2}u\right\|_{\infty}<+\infty.

Therefore, we can differentiate under the sum and use the expression of
\mathscr{J}(\cdot,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
(given in
Lemma

32
) to obtain that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial\mathscr{J}}{\partial r}(r,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\right|\leq C_{1}:=d\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\nabla^{2}u\right\|_{\infty}<\infty.

In particular, for all
r>0
and
t\in[0,T]
,

|\mathscr{J}(r,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})-\mathscr{J}(0,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})|\leq C_{1}r.

On the other hand, by our assumptions, we have
C_{2}:=\inf_{S\simeq S^{\prime}}\inf_{t\leq T}\left\|\nabla u_{t}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(p_{t}^{\prime})}>0
. Thus, for all
t\in[0,T],

\mathscr{J}(0,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\geq C_{2}.

Therefore, there exists
R>0
(potentially depending only on
\beta
and
d
as well as
T
) such that for all
r\leq R
,
\mathscr{J}(r,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\geq(1/2)\mathscr{J}(0,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})
. Thus, by
Lemma

32
, for all
r\leq R
, we have

\displaystyle 2{\mathcal{E}}_{\alpha,p_{t}^{\prime}}(u_{t},u_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{d}\int_{0}^{R}\mathscr{J}(r,u_{t},p_{t}^{\prime})\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{2d}{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right)\int_{0}^{R}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{r^{\alpha-1}}=\frac{C_{\alpha,d}\sigma_{d-1}}{2d(2-\alpha)}R^{2-\alpha}{\mathcal{E}}_{2,p_{t}^{\prime}}\left(v_{t}^{\beta/2},v_{t}^{\beta/2}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\{\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(f(x+y)-f(x+y+z)\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right\}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\gamma_{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x+y)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(f(x)-f(x+z)\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}m(x)+\gamma_{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}m(x).

Now, we apply the Poincaré inequality for
\mu^{\prime}
on the function
y\mapsto\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f(x+y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
. This provides the following bound,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{\prime}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left\{\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f(x+y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f(x+z+y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right\}^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\{\left\|\nabla_{y}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f(x+y)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right\|^{2}\right\}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y).

Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{\prime}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left(f(x+y)-f(x+z+y)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x+y)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{\prime}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(f(x)-f(x+z)\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}m(x)+\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}m(x).

This gives the desired result.

\blacksquare

Proof of
Lemma

21
.

Let
f
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
function vanishing at infinity. Using the change of variable formula, the Poincaré inequality and Tonelli’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{T_{\#}\mu}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f^{2}\mathrm{d}(T_{\#}\mu)-\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f\mathrm{d}(T_{\#}\mu)\right)^{2}=\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}(f\circ T)^{2}d\mu-\left(\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}f\circ Td\mu\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(f(T(x))-f(T(x+z))\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\gamma_{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla(f\circ T)\right\|^{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{x\}}\left(f(T(x))-f(T(y))\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\left\|x-y\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\gamma_{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\mathrm{Jac}(T)(x)\right\|^{2}_{2}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{T_{\#}\mu}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{v\}}\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\frac{|\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(u)||\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(v)|}{\left\|T^{-1}(u)-T^{-1}(v)\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mu(T^{-1}(u))\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}L_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}L_{2}^{\alpha+d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{v\}}\left(f(u)-f(v)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\frac{|\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(u)||\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(v)|}{\left\|u-v\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mu(T^{-1}(u))\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}L_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}L_{2}^{\alpha+d}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{T(x)\}}\left(f(T(x))-f(v)\right)^{2}\frac{|\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(v)|}{\left\|T(x)-v\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}L_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

Now we note that
T^{-1}
is
(1/L_{1})
-Lipschitz continuous, so
that, by Hadamard’s inequality
(
35
)
, we have

\left|\det\mathrm{Jac}(T^{-1})(v)\right|\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\nabla(T^{-1})_{i}(v)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{L_{1}^{d}}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{T_{\#}\mu}(f)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\frac{L_{2}^{\alpha+d}}{L_{1}^{d}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\{T(x)\}}\left(f(T(x))-f(v)\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\left\|T(x)-v\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}L_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}C_{\alpha,d}\frac{L_{2}^{\alpha+d}}{L_{1}^{d}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}\backslash\left\{0\right\}}\left(f(T(x))-f(T(x)+z)\right)^{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{\left\|z\right\|^{d+\alpha}}\mu(x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma_{2}L_{2}^{2}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\left\|\nabla f(T(x))\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x),

which yields the desired result.

\blacksquare

Proof of
Lemma

22
.

Letting
\Phi(x):=x^{2}
, we have the classical variational formula
(
6
)
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\nu}(f)=\inf_{a\in\mathds{R}}\int_{{\mathds{R}^{d}}}\mathrm{D}_{\Phi}(f(x),a)\mathrm{d}\nu(x).

Proof of
Proposition

23
.

Let us denote
T(x):=x-\eta\nabla\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{S}(x)
. Using the strong convexity and smoothness assumptions, we classically prove that
T:{\mathds{R}^{d}}\to\mathds{R}
is bi-Lipschitz with constants given by

\displaystyle(1-\eta M)\left\|x-y\right\|\leq\left\|T(x)-T(y)\right\|\leq(1-\eta\lambda)\left\|x-y\right\|,\quad x,y\in{\mathds{R}^{d}}.

Now let
X
be a random variable whose probability distribution satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality with constant
(c,0)
and let
s\in(0,\eta)
. Then, by
Lemmas

21
and
19
, the distribution of
T(X)+s^{1/\alpha}\sigma L_{1}^{\alpha}
satisfies an
\alpha
-stable Poincaré inequality with constant
(c^{\prime},0)
, with

\displaystyle c^{\prime}:=c\frac{(1-\eta\lambda)^{\alpha+d}}{(1-\eta M)^{d}}+s\sigma^{\alpha}=:F(\eta)c+s\sigma^{\alpha}.

We easily see that
F:(0,M^{-1})\to\mathds{R}_{+}
satisfies
F(0^{+})=1
and
F(\eta)\to+\infty
as
\eta\to M^{-1}
. Moreover,

F^{\prime}(\eta_{0})=0
if and only if
\eta_{0}=\frac{(\alpha+d)\lambda-dM}{\alpha\lambda M}
.
As
\lambda\leq M
, we have
\eta_{0}<1/M
, and, on the other hand,
Equation

20
ensures that
\eta_{0}>0
. Therefore, we necessarily have
0<F(\eta_{0})<1
. Now we observe that

c\leq\frac{\eta\sigma^{\alpha}}{1-F(\eta_{0})}
implies
F(\eta)c+\eta\sigma^{\alpha}\leq\frac{\eta\sigma^{\alpha}}{1-F(\eta_{0})}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle c^{\prime}\leq F(\eta)c+\eta\sigma^{\alpha}\leq\frac{\eta\sigma^{\alpha}}{1-F(\eta_{0})}.

The result follows by an immediate recursion.

\blacksquare
