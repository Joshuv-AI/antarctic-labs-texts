Opportunistic Cardiovascular Risk Assessment in the Eye Clinic: Validation of a Retinal Deep Learning–Based 10-Year Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Estimator
Authors: Simon Nusinovici, Heesuk Kim, Jaewon Seo, Tyler Hyungtaek Rim, Qingyu Chen, Zhiyong Lu, Chan Joo Lee, Yih-Chung Tham, Dongjin Nam, Sahil Thakur, Qingsheng Peng, Marco Yu, Geunyoung Lee, Sungha Park, Sung Soo Kim, Tien Yin Wong, Wathudurage De Silva, Tiarnan DL Keenan, Ching-Yu Cheng, Emily Y Chew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo derive and validate a retinal 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk estimator (“Dr Noon cardiovascular disease [CVD]”) and compare its performance with the clinical gold-standard pooled cohort equation (PCE) in a US population.DesignProspective cohort study with up to 10 years of follow-up.ParticipantsA total of 3096 participants (aged 55–75 years) from the multicenter Age-related Eye Disease Study (AREDS) with gradable baseline fundus photographs, including 1013 with PCE.MethodsThe Dr Noon CVD estimator, which incorporates a deep learning–derived retinal coronary artery calcium surrogate along with age and sex, was derived using a Cox proportional hazards model and validated within the AREDS cohort. Its predictive performance was compared directly with the PCE.Main Outcome MeasuresIncident fatal and nonfatal ASCVD events over 10 years. Performance metrics included risk stratification (hazard ratios [HRs]), discrimination (concordance index [C-index]), calibration (plot with intercept/slope), and reclassification (net reclassification index [NRI]).ResultsAmong 3096 participants, 202 (6.5%) experienced an ASCVD event. Although the PCE was precluded for 67.3% of the cohort (n = 2083) due to missing laboratory data, the retinal estimator provided scores for all participants with available imaging. Dr Noon CVD significantly stratified participants into low, moderate (HR, 1.84; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.24–2.71), and high-risk groups (HR, 2.54; 95% CI, 1.64–3.93; P-trend < 00.001). Beyond traditional risk factors, the model significantly improved discrimination (ΔC-index, 3.4%) and reclassification (overall NRI, 0.194; 95% CI, 0.044–0.344). In direct comparison among participants with PCE (n = 1013), Dr Noon CVD demonstrated numerically higher discrimination (C-index, 0.665 vs. 0.647; P = 0.74) and significantly superior calibration, whereas the PCE overestimated risk at higher deciles (P-intercept = 0.004 vs. 0.141 for Dr Noon CVD). Finally, performance was enhanced in hypertensive participants.ConclusionsDeep learning analysis of routine retinal photographs provides cardiovascular risk stratification comparable to established clinical equations, offering a valid, noninvasive solution for opportunistic screening even when missing laboratory data precludes the standard risk calculators. These findings support the potential for opportunistic systemic risk assessment within the eye clinic, enhancing the role of the ophthalmologist in primary prevention.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Deep learning, Retinal photography, Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Risk stratification, Opportunistic screening

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality globally.1 While other risk assessment models are utilized globally (e.g., QRISK in the United Kingdom or SCORE in Europe), the pooled cohort equation (PCE) represents the current standard of care for primary CVD prevention in the United States.2 However, several studies have shown that PCE overestimate the risk of developing CVD, especially in higher risk populations,3 which has motivated research into additional markers that may improve CVD predictive performance. Among several proposed risk markers, coronary artery calcium (CAC) provides the greatest improvement in discrimination and risk reclassification, particularly among individuals at intermediate risk.4, 5, 6, 7

This evidence underpins the 2018 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline recommendation to consider CAC assessment for additional risk refinement in guiding statin therapy.2 However, computed tomography (CT) scans require a lot of resources, are time-consuming, are not available in primary or ophthalmic clinics, and present issues related to radiation risk. This calls for alternative solutions of risk-free, fast, simple, inexpensive, and noninvasive biomarkers that could be obtained easily in diverse clinical settings. The ophthalmologist is uniquely positioned to fill this gap through routine fundus imaging, which provides a noninvasive window into the microvasculature.

Retinal photography is a simple and effective imaging tool long established in ophthalmology that provides information on the human vasculature. Recent advances in artificial intelligence and deep learning have enabled the detection of subtle retinal features that may precede clinical manifestations and elude conventional human assessment.8,9 In our previous studies, we developed a deep leaning–based system (multitask convolutional neural network) called RetiCAC in participants from diverse Asian populations and the United Kingdom that predicts the presence of CAC directly from retinal images.10 We demonstrated that RetiCAC added incremental value beyond PCE,10 improved discrimination when added to QRISK3,11 was able to identify individuals with intermediate and high risk for CVD, in accordance with PCE, QRISK3, and modified Framingham Risk Score,12 and had better risk stratification performance than CT-measured CAC, carotid intima-media thickness, and brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity.13

Although the RetiCAC score demonstrated strong performance as a standalone biomarker for predicting CAC, clinical decision-making is typically guided by absolute, time-specific risk estimates (e.g., 10-year event probabilities). Accordingly, integrating the RetiCAC retinal biomarker with fundamental demographic variables such as age and sex in a Cox proportional hazards model enables the translation of retinal-derived information into a clinically actionable 10-year atherosclerotic CVD (ASCVD) risk estimate. A comprehensive summary of this model architecture and the training process is provided in Table S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). We hypothesized that this integrated retinal 10-year ASCVD risk estimator incorporating RetiCAC with age and sex would demonstrate robust risk stratification and predictive performance comparable to established clinical risk equations in a US population.

In the present study, we aim to (1) derive a retinal 10-year ASCVD risk estimator that incorporates a retinal CAC surrogate along with age and sex, called “Dr Noon CVD,” estimated using a Cox proportional hazard model, and (2) to validate this retinal risk estimator in a US population from the Age-related Eye Disease Study (AREDS) and compare its performance to the PCE.

Methods

Ethics Statement

This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institutes of Health. For the original AREDS cohort, the study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating clinical center, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study Population

We used clinical data including retinal photographs from the AREDS. The AREDS is a multicenter study that investigated the clinical course of cataract and age-related macular degeneration, including a randomized clinical trial that evaluated the effects of daily oral supplements on age-related macular degeneration and cataract progression.14 Data on age, gender, race, and smoking status (current, other) were available from an interviewer-administered questionnaire obtained at the baseline visit.

Among the eligible participants aged between 55 and 80 years at enrollment, we excluded (1) those with missing ASCVD status (n = 460), (2) those >75 years old at enrollment (n = 388), (3) those who did not have retinal photographs (n = 71), (4) those with pre-existing angina at baseline (n = 414), and (5) those who had ASCVD events or death before taking retinal photographs (n = 17). A total of 3096 AREDS participants were included for the main analysis (Fig S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). We then further excluded participants without relevant information to calculate PCE (n = 2083), which resulted in a population of 1013 individuals for the comparison of the performance of Dr Noon CVD and PCE.

Definition of ASCVD Events

In the AREDS cohort, we used the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision codes from hospitalization and mortality data. The main outcome of interest in the current study reflected the outcome used in the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline.2 Coronary heart disease was defined as International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision codes 410 to 411; stroke was defined as International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision codes 430 to 437 (excluding 432.1 and 435).15,16 Both nonfatal and fatal ASCVD events were considered.

Retinal Photographs

The ophthalmic eligibility criteria were primarily defined by the requirements of the age-related macular degeneration component of the AREDS study, which determined the availability and quality of retinal imaging used for biomarker derivation.14 The baseline stereoscopic color fundus photographs were used for this study. Fundus photographs were assessed for quality at a central retinal image center.

New Retinal-Based CVD Risk Stratification System

Details of our previous deep learning–based retinal risk estimators have been described elsewhere.10 Briefly, a deep learning algorithm (called RetiCAC) was developed using retinal photographs for predicting the presence of CAC using Korean health screening center data. The probability scores ranged from 0 to 1, with a higher value indicating increased probability of CAC presence. We further derived a retinal 10-year ASCVD risk estimator called “Dr Noon CVD” representing the predicted 10-year absolute risk of ASCVD expressed as a probability. To do so, we built a Cox proportional hazards model using UK Biobank data (n = 24 008) with incident ASCVD as outcome and RetiCAC, age, and sex as predictors, and used the corresponding baseline hazard and coefficients to predict Dr Noon CVD in AREDS. Finally, Dr Noon CVD was categorized into 3 risk groups based on quintiles: low risk (Q1), moderate risk (Q2–Q4), and high risk (Q5).

PCE

The PCE was calculated based on the following traditional risk factors: age, sex, ethnicity, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes status, and current smoking status. Because laboratory data were only available for a subset of AREDS participants, the PCE was calculated for this subpopulation (1013 of 3096). Consequently, applying guideline-based PCE thresholds (<5%, 5%–20%, ≥20%) produced highly imbalanced groups, with very few participants and events in the highest-risk category (≥20%: n = 58, 5 events). To ensure adequate sample size and event counts within each category and improve the stability of stratified risk estimates and reclassification analyses, we therefore categorized PCE risk into empirical tertiles.

Statistical Analysis

All the statistical analyses were done using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). First, we described the baseline characteristics of the included AREDS participants according to Dr Noon CVD risk groups.

Second, the risk stratification performance of Dr Noon CVD and PCE were determined using the Kaplan–Meier method to estimate the survival function over time across the risk groups, and Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), while adjusting for hypertension, diabetes status, dyslipidemia, and smoking status. Moreover, linear trend in HRs across ordered risk categories was assessed using the trend test. Finally, unadjusted Cox proportional hazards models were fitted using restricted cubic splines with 4 knots for both Dr Noon CVD and the PCE, and the resulting associations were plotted to visualize their potentially nonlinear relationships with incident ASCVD.

Third, discrimination was assessed using the Harell's concordance index (C-index). The comparison of Dr Noon CVD and PCE discriminations was done using the difference in C-index, with 95% CIs and 2-sided P values derived from 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Fourth, we assessed the incremental value of Dr Noon CVD beyond (1) traditional risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, and ethnicity) and (2) PCE, using the continuous net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement. Both event-NRI and nonevent-NRI were calculated.17

Fifth, we determined the calibration performance of Dr Noon CVD and PCE using (1) visual inspection of the observed risks across deciles of the predicted risks and (2) by calculating intercept and slopes and testing whether they differed significantly from 0 and 1, respectively.

Finally, 2 sensitivity analyses were performed. The first one was among participants not taking lipid-lowering therapy at baseline, and the second one was according to hypertension status.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Among the 3096 AREDS participants included in this study, 202 (6.5%) had an ASCVD event (fatal or nonfatal) during the 10-year follow-up (median = 10 year; interquartile range = 9.8–10.0 years). Dr Noon CVD showed a narrower, more symmetric distribution, whereas PCE predictions were more right-skewed with a longer upper tail (Fig S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The proportion of ASCVD events increased across Dr Noon CVD risk groups: 3.8%, 6.9%, and 9.3% in the low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups, respectively (Table 1).

Although Dr Noon CVD scores were successfully generated for all 3096 participants using only fundus photography, age, and sex, the PCE could only be calculated for a subset of 1013 (32.7%) individuals due to the absence of required laboratory lipid panels in the remaining 2083 participants. Consequently, the PCE was clinically unusable for 67.3% of this population-based cohort at baseline. However, there were no differences in the demographic (except slightly more White participants [94.9% vs. 96.8%]) and clinical characteristics between those participants and those for which PCE could not be calculated (Table S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Predictive Performance of Dr Noon CVD

The survival curves showed increased risks across Dr Noon CVD risk groups (log-rank test P < 0.001) (Fig 1). After adjusting for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, and ethnicity, the HRs were 1.84 [95% CI: 1.24–2.71] for the moderate-risk group and 2.54 [95% CI: 1.64–3.93] for the high-risk group, compared to the low-risk group (Table 2). The corresponding HR trend across risk groups was 1.56 [95% CI: 1.27–1.92]. Furthermore, the C-index for Dr Noon CVD, representing the discrimination performance, was 0.647 [95% CI: 0.614–0.688] (Table 2). Among participants for whom PCE could be calculated, the C-index increased to 0.665 [95% CI: 0.606–0.743]. Finally, the calibration plot showed a close agreement between observed and predicted risks across deciles, with intercept and slope not significantly different from 0 (P = 0.141) and 1 (P = 0.367), respectively (Fig 2).

The first sensitivity analysis, performed among participants not taking lipid-lowering therapy at baseline, showed similar risk stratification and discrimination performance (HR trend = 1.50 [95% CI: 1.21–1.86] and C-index = 0.643 [95% CI: 0.604–0.689]) (Table S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Predictive Performance of the PCE and Comparison with Dr Noon CVD

The survival curves showed increased risks across PCE risk groups (log-rank test P = 0.004), although the 95% CIs tended to overlap (Fig 1). Moreover, the HRs were 1.80 [95% CI: 0.85–3.77] for the moderate-risk group and 3.00 [95% CI: 1.50–6.01] for the high-risk group, compared to the low-risk group (Table 2). The corresponding HR trend across risk groups was 1.72 [95% CI: 1.24–2.40]. Furthermore, the C-index was 0.647 [95% CI: 0.583–0.711] (Table 2). Finally, calibration was good among participants at low-to-moderate ASCVD risk, but with increasing overestimation at higher risk levels (intercept significantly different from 0 [P = 0.004] and borderline significant slope [P = 0.059]) (Fig 2).

Dr Noon CVD demonstrated a monotonic increase in ASCVD risk across its distribution, while the PCE displayed a nonlinear relationship characterized by an early rise in risk followed by attenuation at higher predicted values, with wider uncertainty at the extremes (Fig S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Finally, among participants for whom PCE could be calculated, Dr Noon CVD showed a higher C-index, albeit nonsignificant (ΔC-index = 0.017; P value = 0.74) (Table 2).

Reclassification Performance of Dr Noon CVD Beyond Traditional Risk Factors and PCE

We evaluated the incremental predictive value of Dr Noon CVD when added to models including traditional risk factors and PCE. Addition of Dr Noon CVD to model including diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, and ethnicity significantly increased the discrimination (C-index) by 3.4% (95% CI: 1.5%–6.5%) and reclassification performance (overall NRI = 0.194 [95% CI: 0.044–0.344]) (Table 3). However, addition of Dr Noon CVD to model including PCE did not improve the discrimination (ΔC-index = 0.1% [95% CI: –2.0% to 3.6%]) or reclassification performance (overall NRI = –0.216 [95% CI: –0.305 to 0.505]) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we validated a deep learning algorithm using retinal photographs (Dr Noon CVD) in the AREDS cohort, a US population composed of 3096 participants followed over 10 years. First, Dr Noon CVD effectively stratified participants into 3 distinct risk categories—low, moderate, and high. Compared with individuals in the low-risk group, those in the moderate- and high-risk groups had 84% and 154% higher risks of ASCVD, respectively. This stratification performance was comparable to that of the PCE, as reflected by similar HR trends across risk categories (HR per category increase: 1.56 [95% CI 1.27–1.92] vs. 1.72 [95% CI 1.24–2.40]). Second, discrimination of Dr Noon CVD was similar to that of the PCE and numerically higher, although the difference was not statistically significant (ΔC-index = 0.017; P = 0.74). Finally, whereas the PCE tended to overestimate risk among participants at high predicted risk, Dr Noon CVD showed good agreement between observed and predicted risks, with no evidence of systematic overestimation or underestimation.

Digital health is poised to transform cardiovascular medicine, with advances in digital phenotyping—from electrocardiography and imaging to wearables and electronic health records—now extending to retinal photography.18 Prior studies have shown that retinal vascular abnormalities predict CVD risk,17 and deep learning enables automated detection of subtle retinal features beyond human perception. Retinal fundus–based models have demonstrated promise for cardiovascular risk assessment and related phenotypes,9 including biological age.19,20

This study demonstrated that Dr Noon CVD has the potential to serve as a simple, noninvasive general risk stratification tool. However, confirming its generalizability to the broader US population will require further evaluation in diverse US cohorts. In our previous studies, we showed that retinal-based ASCVD risk estimators can be used as a general screening tool for the UK and diverse Asian populations.10 The present study therefore further confirmed and validated that retinal-based ASCVD risk estimators offer a simple and effective initial assessment for asymptomatic patients that can be utilized not only by general health care professionals but also by eye-related professionals for opportunistic eye screening. Importantly, sensitivity analyses further suggested that the performance of the retinal risk estimator may be enhanced in individuals with hypertension, with stronger risk stratification and slightly higher discrimination observed in this subgroup compared with participants without hypertension. This finding indicates that retinal biomarkers may capture hypertension-related microvascular damage that is particularly informative for cardiovascular risk assessment. Moreover, consistent risk stratification and discrimination performance were observed among participants not receiving lipid-lowering therapy at baseline, supporting the robustness of the retinal risk estimator independently of statin treatment status.

Crucially, the clinical utility of any risk calculator is fundamentally limited by the availability of its inputs. In the present study, the clinical gold-standard PCE was precluded for over two-thirds of the cohort (67%) because necessary laboratory lipid data were missing from the clinical record. In stark contrast, the Dr Noon CVD estimator provided immediate, automated risk stratification for all the participants using routine imaging already performed in the eye clinic. This highlights the potential of retinal artificial intelligence to bridge the “screening gap” for millions of patients who undergo regular eye examinations but lack recent systemic blood work. Because retinal photographs are already routinely acquired in ophthalmology clinics and increasingly in primary care settings, Dr Noon CVD could be readily applied to existing imaging data to identify individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk who may otherwise remain unrecognized. Such opportunistic screening facilitates timely referral for cardiovascular evaluation and targeted preventive interventions—such as statin initiation—without requiring additional testing or patient burden. In clinical workflow, this positions the ophthalmologist as a vital triage point; upon receiving an automated high-risk alert from a routine eye examination, the ophthalmologist can directly refer the asymptomatic patient to a primary care provider or cardiologist for systemic management. Furthermore, these results support the feasibility of large-scale community screening initiatives, as retinal imaging can be performed using noncontact, relatively low-cost devices that are widely available and do not involve radiation exposure.

In our previous study, Dr Noon CVD not only demonstrated performance comparable to that of CT-based CAC scores for ASCVD prediction but also improved risk discrimination among individuals classified as borderline or intermediate risk according to the PCE.10 Unfortunately, in the present study, PCE estimates could be derived for only a subset of the participants owing to missing lipid panel data, precluding stratification of the full cohort according to PCE risk categories. Nevertheless, within this subset, we observed that the discrimination of Dr Noon CVD was numerically higher than that of the PCE (ΔC-index = 0.017), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. Furthermore, compared with the PCE, Dr Noon CVD showed a more gradual and approximately linear increase in hazard across predicted risk levels, whereas PCE estimates plateaued at higher risk values and were associated with greater uncertainty. These findings suggest that the retinal estimator performs at least as well as the PCE in ranking individuals according to future ASCVD risk.

Importantly, beyond discrimination, calibration analyses revealed meaningful differences between the 2 approaches. Whereas the PCE tended to overestimate risk among participants at the highest predicted risk levels, the retinal estimator demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed event rates, with no evidence of systematic over- or underestimation. This favorable calibration profile is particularly relevant for clinical decision-making, as risk overestimation may lead to overtreatment and unnecessary patient anxiety, while underestimation may delay preventive interventions. Together with its ability to stratify individuals into clinically distinct risk groups with HR gradients comparable to those of the PCE, these results support the robustness and clinical relevance of the retinal risk estimator as an alternative tool for ASCVD risk assessment.

Moreover, the superior discrimination of the retinal estimator compared with traditional cardiovascular risk factors alone, as well as its improvement in overall risk reclassification (NRI = 0.19), highlights its potential to capture additional prognostic information not fully reflected by conventional clinical variables. This suggests that retinal imaging–derived biomarkers may reflect cumulative microvascular and systemic vascular damage, thereby providing complementary insight into an individual's long-term cardiovascular risk.

The strength of this study is the use of a well-known and characterized US cohort that allowed us to further demonstrate the generalizability of the retinal estimator as an ASCVD risk stratification tool in a US-based cohort. Our study had some limitations. First, because the PCE could not be derived for the full cohort, we could not investigate the possible retinal risk discrimination improvement among individuals classified as borderline or intermediate risk according to the PCE. Finally, we were unable to perform direct comparison for CAC score prediction due to the lack of available CT data.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Dr Noon CVD can effectively stratify cardiovascular risk in a US-based cohort using standard color fundus photographs alone. By providing valid risk estimates for patients in whom the clinical gold-standard (PCE) could not be calculated due to missing labs, this tool offers a highly accessible, noninvasive, and scalable solution for opportunistic screening for ASCVD risk. This allows the ophthalmologist to identify high-risk individuals and facilitate primary prevention without the burden of additional laboratory testing.
