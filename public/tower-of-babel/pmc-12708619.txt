Psychosocial determinants of oral health-related quality of life in periodontitis patients
Authors: Junxiao Zhang, Chen Zhao, Li Xu, Yuqing Li, Zihao Zhang, Dan Wang, Shengkai Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to examine the association between oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) and oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices (OHRKAP) that reflect oral health knowledge alongside psychosocial factors including self-efficacy for oral care, perceived social support (PSS), and socio-economic status (SES) among periodontal patients using structural equation modeling (SEM). This cross-sectional study included 294 patients with periodontitis from October 2022 to May 2024 in East China. Demographic data, OHRQoL, self-efficacy for oral care, OHRKAP, SES, and PSS were collected using questionnaires. The periodontal status was assessed via a full-mouth examination and imaging. Structural equation modeling was used to analyze the relationships between variables and mediation effects. Among patients with periodontitis, self-efficacy for oral care, OHRKAP, SES, and OHRQoL were directly linked, with self-efficacy having the strongest effect on OHRQoL. PSS indirectly influenced OHRQoL through two pathways: self-efficacy for oral care and OHRKAP. SES indirectly affected OHRQoL through PSS, OHRKAP, and self-efficacy for oral care. Self-efficacy for oral care, OHRKAP, and SES were the primary factors impacting OHRQoL, with SES and PSS significantly moderating other variables. Targeted interventions addressing psychosocial factors in patients with periodontitis, particularly in vulnerable populations, may improve oral health outcomes.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic, multifactorial inflammatory disease associated with the accumulation of dental plaque. It is characterized by progressive destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone1. As of 2021, more than 1 billion people worldwide suffer from severe periodontal disease2. The estimated global annual economic loss due to severe periodontitis is approximately $54 billion3. As the population ages, the incidence of periodontitis is anticipated to increase, particularly impacting vulnerable groups, thereby contributing to socioeconomic disparities4.

Periodontal disease is the leading cause of tooth loss among adults worldwide potentially leading to multiple tooth loss, edentulism, and masticatory dysfunction. It results in significant socioeconomic consequences and healthcare costs5,6, as well as negative impacts on nutritional status, quality of life, and self-esteem7. Consequently, the influence of periodontal disease on oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) has emerged as a significant area of research. Numerous studies have shown that the progression and severity of periodontal disease are highly detrimental to OHRQoL8. It has been reported that successful management may improve patients’ OHRQoL9.

Oral OHRQoL is a complex construct. It includes subjective assessments of an individual’s oral health, functional status, emotional well-being, expectations and satisfaction with treatment, and self-perception10. OHRQoL provides information that supplements other clinical parameters. It reflects the influence of the oral system and the patient’s subjective assessment of oral health11. Numerous intricate factors, such as social, psychological, socioeconomic, and demographic factors, influence the OHRQoL12. According to the findings of a previous study, psychosocial factors such as perceived socioeconomic status (SES) and dental anxiety were substantially correlated with patients’ OHRQoL13. Previous research has established that age, number of missing teeth, interproximal cleaning habits, perceived stress8, and dental anxiety14 are factors associated with OHRQoL in patients with periodontal disease, according to Anderson’s behavioral model and structural equation modelling. However, there is a gap in the field regarding the impact of periodontitis patients’ oral health knowledge and psychosocial factors on their quality of life.

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) theory is a conceptual framework aimed at modifying health-related behaviors in individuals15. According to the theory, fostering healthy behaviors is the aim, attitudes are the catalysts for behavioral changes, and health knowledge serves as the foundation for developing positive attitudes and healthy behaviors. Over the past three decades, China has implemented annual campaigns focused on oral health education. Regrettably, the prevalence of periodontal disease continues to increase owing to the inadequacy of preventive measures. Thus, investigating more efficient methods to lower the prevalence of periodontal disease among Chinese individuals is crucial16. The prevention of periodontal disease is significantly reliant on an individual’s oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices17. Research indicates that oral health knowledge directly and favorably affects attitudes, while knowledge indirectly impacts practice through attitudes18. Additionally, both oral health attitudes and behaviors are directly linked to the OHRQoL. Oral health attitudes can also indirectly and significantly influence OHRQoL through actual practices19.

The enthusiasm for investigating the correlation between mental structure and OHRQoL has increased alongside the popularity of positive psychology11. Self-efficacy signifies a firm belief in one’s ability to manage health behaviors20. Enhanced self-efficacy fosters greater adherence to oral health care directives and reduces the probability of noncompliance with periodontal therapy21. Farley et al.22. conducted a study highlighted the relationship between self-efficacy, health knowledge, and self-management skills. The findings confirmed that implementing targeted interventions to enhance self-efficacy in patients with low health knowledge can lead to increased engagement in health promotion activities and improved adherence to treatment. Furthermore, self-efficacy originates from one’s self-identity, which is initially shaped by external resources. Social support, as an external resource, can strengthen self-identity and increase self-efficacy23.

Perceived social support pertains to individuals’ perceptions of the anticipated assistance from their relationships and social networks, as well as the quality of that support. This involves evaluating the adequacy of social support from a subjective standpoint24. Socioeconomic status is generally represented by indicators such as educational attainment, family income, and occupational status19. During clinical consultations, physicians may sometimes neglect the significance of social activities or interpersonal connections and instead prioritize pharmaceutical or surgical interventions. Research conducted by Wang et al.25 indicated a correlation between diminished social support and the extent of periodontal attachment loss in older adults. Additionally, Shi et al.26 noted that individuals with a lower socioeconomic status are more prone to cognitive impairment, negatively influencing treatment outcomes and health recovery. Furthermore, social support is essential for alleviating the effects of socioeconomic status and cognitive ability in the elderly population27.

The concept of mediating effects has been extended to multiple fields, with researchers focusing not only on the relationship between exposure and response variables, but also on the underlying mechanisms28. For example, low socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with poor health, but it is unclear whether the differences stem from individual behaviour or the living environments of different SES groups29. Therefore, it is necessary to distinguish and quantify the mediating effects of different risk factors to guide effective interventions under resource optimisation.

While the influence of clinical factors on periodontitis progression has been well documented, the impact of psychosocial determinants, such as self-efficacy, social support, and socioeconomic status, on patients’ oral health behaviors and overall well-being remains less explored. Examining how these factors interact with oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices (OHRKAP) can inform targeted interventions to enhance periodontal care and patient outcomes. Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the relationships among OHRQoL, OHRKAP, self-efficacy for oral care, perceived social support, and socioeconomic status in patients with periodontitis using structural equation modeling. The findings provide a theoretical foundation for designing effective interventions to improve periodontal health and enhance OHRQoL.

Materials and methods

Participants and sample size calculation

This cross-sectional study involved 294 patients with periodontitis admitted from October 2022 to May 2024 in eastern China, using purposive sampling method. For the diagnostic criteria of periodontitis patients, it is referred to the “2018 New International Classification of Periodontal and Peri-implant Diseases.“30. Specifically, it was characterized by the presence of interdental clinical attachment loss detectable at ≥ 2 non-adjacent teeth, or buccal/oral clinical attachment loss ≥ 3 mm with pocketing > 3 mm at ≥ 2 teeth.

The sample size was determined utilizing the population proportion formula31, N = Z2P (1 - P) /d2, (Z = critical value with 95% confidence = 1.96, P = proportion with a periodontal health rate of 9.1%32, d = 0.05). The minimum sample size of the study, accounting for a 20% non-response rate, was 152. In contrast, our study included 294 participants, which far exceeded the minimum required and ensured the reliability of the study. We acknowledge that our sample size, calculated based on prevalence estimation, may limit the statistical power for complex structural equation modeling and have listed this as a major limitation of the study.

Data collection

The study population comprised patients from the periodontal department of a major general hospital in East China. Subsequently, the objectives and substance of this study were comprehensively explained to patients with periodontitis. Following the acquisition of informed consent, clinical periodontal assessments and oral imaging evaluations were performed on the patients, and the findings were documented on a periodontal examination form.

Periodontal diagnosis involved measurements conducted by two qualified and calibrated examiners (J.Z., C.Z.). Periodontal indicators such as gingival recession (GR), probing depth (PD), bleeding on probing (BOP), furcation involvement (FI), clinical attachment loss (CAL), and tooth mobility (TM) were measured utilizing a ball-ended World Health Organization Community Periodontal Index (WHO-CPI) probe. Oral imaging to assess radiographic bone loss (RBL) and vertical bone resorption for the staging and grading of periodontitis.

Questionnaires were distributed to 302 patients diagnosed with periodontal disease, and independent completion was requested. The questionnaires administered to 302 patients included sections on demographics, oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices (OHRKAP), self-efficacy for oral care, perceived social support, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). Upon finalizing the collection of the questionnaires, the survey data were meticulously verified. Flawed surveys with logical problems and contradictory answers were eliminated, ultimately acquiring 294 complete and valid responses. The questionnaire was comprehensive and reliable, with an effective response rate of 96.5%. This study complied with ethical standards, prioritizing participant welfare and preventing any psychological distress. This study was conducted at Bengbu Medical University, and the study protocol was approved under reference n. 2023366.

Instruments

A preliminary questionnaire was developed for 50 periodontal patients following a review of relevant literature and the findings of the Fourth National Oral Health Survey in Mainland China32. This questionnaire was intended for use in a pre-survey prior to the formal study. The questionnaire was amended according to expert evaluations and preliminary survey results. The final questionnaire covered sociodemographic characteristics, OHRQoL, self-efficacy for oral care, socioeconomic status, perceived social support, and oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

Oral health-related quality of life

Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) was assessed using the validated Chinese Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14)33, which is a shortened version of the OHIP-49. It comprises 14 items across four domains (functional limitations, pain/discomfort, psychological distress, and physical disability), rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = often). Lower scores indicate a better OHRQoL. The Oral Health Impact Profile-14 Chinese version (OHIP-14 CHN) demonstrates strong reliability and validity and has been verified34. The OHIP-14 CHN in the research exhibits a Cronbach’s α of 0.907.

Self-efficacy for oral care

Self-efficacy for oral care was assessed using the Chinese Self-Efficacy Scale for Oral Care (SESS)35, which comprises 15 items across three domains: (1) oral counseling, (2) proper brushing, and (3) balanced diet. Higher scores indicate greater self-efficacy, with strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.915).

Perceived social support

Perceived social support was measured using the Chinese version of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS)36,11, which comprises 12 items across three dimensions (family, friends, and other support). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 7 = Strongly Agree), with higher total scores reflecting stronger support. Cronbach’s α was 0.901.

Oal health-related knowledge, attitudes and practices

Oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices (OHRKAP) were assessed using three scales: (1) the Oral Health Knowledge Scale (13 items, e.g., “Can plaque cause periodontal diseases/caries?“; scored 2/1/0 for “Yes/Unsure/No”), (2) the Oral Health Attitude Scale (8 items, e.g., “Oral diseases harm systemic health”; scored 0/1/2 for “No/Unsure/Yes,” with item A2 reverse-scored), and (3) the Oral Health Practice Scale (9 items, e.g., “Brush teeth ≥ 2 times daily?“; scored 1/2 for “Yes/No”). The total scores were summed up for all items, with higher scores indicating better levels. The scale was developed in accordance with the pertinent literature and China’s Fourth National Oral Health Survey, exhibiting a high level of reliability. Cronbach’s α for OHRKAP was 0.952.

Socioeconomic status

The Socioeconomic Status Index (SESI)25 combined educational attainment (categorized as low [< 6 years = 0], secondary [6–12 years = 1], or tertiary [> 12 years = 2]) and annual household income (≤¥50,000 = 0, ¥50,000–120,000 = 1, ≥¥120,000 = 2). The total SESI score ranged from 0 to 4 (sum of both scores).

Cronbach’s alpha was 0.875, KMO was 0.915, and the p-value of Bartlett’s test was less than 0.001 for the full questionnaire.

Hypotheses

H1: Self-efficacy for oral care, PSS, OHRKAP, and SES have a significant impact on OHRQoL.

H2: SES significantly impacts self-efficacy for oral care, PSS, and OHRKAP.

H3: PSS has a significant impact on Self-efficacy for oral care and OHRKAP.

Statistical analysis

The research data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0. Qualitative and quantitative data are expressed as frequencies (%) and mean ± SD, respectively. Normality was assessed using the skewness-kurtosis test, with data considered normally distributed (absolute skewness < 3, absolute kurtosis < 8)37. Pearson’s correlation was used to assess the correlations between the prospective variables. All differences were assessed using two-tailed tests with a significance level of P < 0.05. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure and Cronbach’s alpha were used to assess the validity and reliability of the questionnaires. Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) was employed to examine the intricate interactions among OHRQoL, self-efficacy for oral care, socioeconomic status, perceived social support, and oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices. The quality of the measurement model was evaluated using a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). To examine whether the knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) items could be represented as a single latent construct, a bifactor CFA was conducted. This analysis assessed the loadings of individual items onto a general KAP factor to justify combining them into a single score for the structural equation modeling. CFA for each scale demonstrated acceptable model fit, supporting the validity of the measurement models and justifying their use in subsequent structural equation modeling. Model fit was evaluated using chi-square/degrees of freedom (X2/df), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and more38. Specifically, the structural equation model demonstrated good fit, with the incremental fit index (IFI), comparative fit index (CFI), normed fit index (NFI), relative fit index (RFI), and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) all exceeding 0.9. The root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) was below 0.08, and the chi-square/degrees of freedom (χ2/df) was less than 3, indicating an overall acceptable model fit. The bootstrap method was employed39to test the strength and significance of the mediating effects between variables in the final model, and bias-corrected bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to test the importance of the direct and indirect effects. Parameter estimation was performed using the maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), and the test level was α = 0.05.

This study involved 294 participants, including 128 men and 166 women, aged between 18 and 80 years. Overall, most participants exhibited poor periodontal health. Specifically, 61.5% of the patients were categorized with Periodontitis Staging of 3 or above, while 51.0% were classified under Periodontitis Grading of C. The mean total OHIP-14 score for all participants was 14.20 ± 9.19.

The preliminary hypothesis model is shown in Fig. 2. Following the testing of this hypothetical model, analysis excluded the direct pathway from “perceived social support” to “OHRQoL” due to the lack of statistical significance in their coefficients (p > 0.05). However, in the bootstrap test, a fully mediated effect was identified for the two pathways. The final constructed SEM is shown in Fig. 3, and the standardized path coefficients for this model are all statistically significant (p < 0.05). The SEM model fitted the data well, and the fitness indicators all eventually reached acceptable standards40, with chi-square values > 0.5, χ2/df < 5, RMSEA < 0.05, and AGFI, GFI, NFI, TLI, and CFI all > 0.90 (Table 4).

The latent variables of the final model were analyzed for standardized direct, indirect, and total effects. Path analyses indicated that 9 paths were statistically significant. Figure 3; Table 5 demonstrate a significant direct relationship among self-efficacy for oral care (β = -0.534), OHRKAP (β = -0.183), SES (β = -0.176) and OHRQoL. Meanwhile, perceived social support (β = -0.433) and SES (β = -0.269) had an indirect effect on OHRQoL. SES exerted a significant direct effect on OHRKAP (β = 0.203), self-efficacy for oral care (β = 0.220) and perceived social support (β = 0.265). Additionally, it had an indirect effect on self-efficacy for oral care (β = 0.177) and OHRKAP (β = 0.112). Perceived social support significantly directly impacted Self-efficacy for oral care (β = 0.666) and OHRKAP (β = 0.421).

Mediating effects in the final model

Our analysis revealed seven statistically significant mediating pathways among the latent variables (Table 6). PSS showed indirect associations with OHRQoL through two mediating pathways: primarily via self-efficacy (β = -0.356, 82% of total indirect effect), and secondarily through OHRKAP (β = -0.077, 18%). SES exerted indirect effects through PSS mediation on both self-efficacy (β = 0.177, 100%) and OHRKAP (β = 0.112, 100%). Four mediated pathways linked SES to OHRQoL: (1) SES→PSS→self-efficacy→OHRQoL (β = -0.095, 35%), (2) SES→PSS→OHRKAP→OHRQoL (β = -0.020, 8%), (3) SES→OHRKAP→OHRQoL (β = -0.037, 14%), and (4) SES→self-efficacy→OHRQoL (β = -0.117, 43%).

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the intricate interactions among self-efficacy for oral care, OHRKAP, SES, perceived social support, and OHRQoL in patients with periodontitis. In this study, the mean OHIP-14 score for patients with periodontitis was 14.20 ± 9.19. A study conducted by Machado et al.8 revealed an OHIP-14 score of 9.5 ± 11.3 in Portuguese respondents. Additionally, research by Fuller et al.41 indicated that the OHIP-14 score for patients with chronic periodontitis was 12.9 in London, compared to 6.6 in the healthy population. In contrast, Chinese patients with periodontitis may experience diminished quality of life regarding dental health. Given the influence of varying national contexts, cultural backgrounds, and patient demographics, this study advocates for a comprehensive analysis of oral health-related quality of life and its direct and indirect effects in patients with periodontitis, depicting the intricate interconnections involved in this relationship.

The current findings underscore that oral health-related knowledge, attitude, self-efficacy, and socioeconomic status are strongly and negatively associated with OHRQoL. In other words, patients with periodontitis who exhibited superior knowledge, attitude, and practice levels, together with elevated self-efficacy and socioeconomic status, demonstrated reduced OHRQoL scores, indicating an enhanced oral health quality of life. The direct effect of self-efficacy was the most substantial, at 60%, aligning with the findings of several previous studies. A study of Chinese college students identified self-efficacy as a fundamental component affecting OHRQoL among this demographic42. Allen et al.43 indicated that those with diminished self-efficacy exhibited elevated disease levels, increased gingival bleeding scores, and reduced satisfaction regarding their oral health. Additionally, recent research suggests that mobile health applications designed for periodontal health, when incorporating features to enhance self-efficacy, can significantly influence how individuals manage their dental and gum health. The findings on self-efficacy should also serve as the foundation for the development of artificial intelligence-based digital monitoring systems and self-efficacy assessment tools44,45.

The cultivation of self-efficacy is a protracted process shaped by multiple factors, such as individual experiences, verbal feedback, and social support. Previous research indicates that social support indirectly impacts OHRQoL among Chinese college students by affecting self-efficacy and various multidimensional factors, including knowledge, attitudes, and practices42. This study reconfirms that subjective social support (PSS) significantly influences self-efficacy. Simultaneously, PSS can act as a mediator of the effects of socioeconomic status on self-efficacy. It is presumed that this has practical value in buffering the adverse effects of low socioeconomic status and enhancing mental health. This is in line with the research conducted by Huang et al. regarding college students during the COVID-19 closure46. Secondly, the mediation pathways identified suggest that subjective social support exerts an indirect influence on OHRQoL by impacting self-efficacy (82%); additionally, social support can also indirectly impact OHRQoL through OHRKAP (18%). Research indicates that social support can be improved through two main areas23. First, instrumental support, such as social and medical security systems, pensions, and financial assistance for child-rearing expenses, is provided. Second, emotional support can alleviate the emotional stress of patients with chronic diseases. For example, community health education activities and regular check-ups for patients with periodontal disease can reduce their concerns about their physical condition. As a dimension of family support, good emotional expression can reduce patients’ feelings of guilt regarding the economic burden of their illness and the burden of caring for their children. However, current policy guidance lacks recommendations for this crucial socioeconomic sector.

The Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) theoretical model has been thoroughly examined in relation to oral health-related quality of life. Oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices were incorporated as variables in the SEM, thereby decreasing the number of variables, streamlining the model structure, and introducing methodological innovation. The findings of this research indicated a substantial correlation between OHRKAP and OHRQoL, suggesting that those with elevated OHRKAP levels experience an improved oral health-related quality of life, similar to the results of Zheng et al.18. A study examining the current status of periodontal health knowledge, attitudes, and practices among Chinese adults16 indicated that such variables were correlated with gender, age, education, income, smoking habits, and history of periodontal disease. This study revealed that socioeconomic status and perceived social support were significantly and positively associated with the OHRKAP. Furthermore, perceived social support can mitigate the influence of socioeconomic status on OHRKAP through mediation. Specifically, patients from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds generally receive less financial assistance and are more likely to possess poor oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices. In this context, social support functions as a moderating buffer, mitigating the effects of socioeconomic disparities on OHRKAP among patients with periodontal disease. This is similar to prior research26, which found that social support is vital in buffering the impacts of SES and cognitive capacities associated with an aging population.

In addition, the total score for oral health care knowledge, attitudes, and practices among periodontal patients was 39.04 ± 10.33, categorizing it within the low to medium level. A cross-sectional study revealed that knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding periodontal health among Chinese individuals were predominantly insufficient, with less than 20% of participants exhibiting an understanding of periodontal disease16. Periodontal health initiatives should be advocated for the entire adult population, with particular emphasis on older adults, individuals with lower educational attainment, and those from lower-income backgrounds.

A positive association between socioeconomic status (SES) and health has been well documented47. This association has been established for socioeconomic status indicators at both the individual level48 and the societal level49 across nearly all health outcomes, including mortality, morbidity, and disability50. In this study, SES, while highly correlated with OHRQoL, also had a favorable impact on two psychosocial factors: self-efficacy and perceived social support. In other words, having a higher SES usually results in more economic assistance, more social support, greater self-confidence, and a higher quality of life. Similar findings were discovered in Ting Ge’s study, which revealed that individuals with a high socioeconomic status are typically thought to have more financial support, positive family, friends, and social support, and thus more life satisfaction and pleasant emotional states51.

Additionally, numerous mediating pathways between SES and OHRQoL were identified. Socioeconomic status (SES) exerted an indirectly influenced OHRQoL through multiple mediating effects of perceived PSS, oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices (OHRKAP), and self-efficacy. SES affected the quality of life and OHRKAP of patients with periodontitis sequentially through perceived social support and self-efficacy. This may be attributed to unequal access to or control over material, property, social, and intellectual resources among patients with varying socioeconomic statuses52. This results in disparities in personal views and behavioral outcomes when confronted with a disease. Overall, the main findings suggest that SES is an important moderator of the OHRQoL. This study aimed to contribute to the dialogue on socioeconomic inequalities by elucidating the complex relationship between SES and OHRQoL among patients with periodontitis.

Furthermore, this study revealed that more females than males had periodontitis, which is inconsistent with previous findings that more males than females had periodontitis13. It is intriguing that the majority of female patients experienced mild to moderate periodontitis. The number of male patients was comparatively low; however, the majority of them experienced moderate to severe periodontitis (Fig. 1a). It can be considered that the possibility may be associated with factors such as pregnancy and hormonal alterations in women53. A prior study [54] indicated that women display more favorable attitudes toward dental visits, possess greater oral health knowledge, and are more aggressive in pursuing disease prevention than men. In contrast, men are more predisposed to pursue oral treatment for acute problems than women. Inadequate oral hygiene practices and increased tobacco consumption are significant factors contributing to the progression of periodontitis to moderate or severe stages by the time men seek treatment. Recent studies have indicated that men and women are not equally effective in using electronic health tools and participating in digital intervention measures, which may be one reason for the differences in oral health outcomes between the sexes [55].

Regarding age structure, young people have a higher rate of periodontal disease visits, with mild to moderate severity, while older adults have a lower frequency of periodontal disease visits and higher severity. The infrequent periodontal visits among the elderly, coupled with the high severity of periodontitis. This can be attributed not only to direct factors such as immune and cellular senescence and impaired wound healing, but also to indirect factors, including physical and cognitive impairments and limited access to care [56]. Given the challenges posed by China’s aging population, additional efforts to promote periodontal health in the elderly are essential.

Our study highlights that self-efficacy, perceived social support, and socioeconomic status significantly impact OHRQoL in patients with periodontitis, consistent with findings from international research. Evidence from Europe, North America, and Southeast Asia similarly indicates that psychosocial determinants, such as confidence in oral care practices and the presence of supportive social networks, play key roles in shaping oral health behaviors and outcomes. Although the pathways and effect sizes may differ across populations due to variations in healthcare access, cultural norms, and socioeconomic conditions, the overarching influence of psychosocial factors on oral health appears globally consistent. Notably, our structural equation model demonstrated that self-efficacy has the strongest direct effect on OHRQoL, while perceived social support functions as an important moderator, reflecting multi-country evidence on the value of empowerment and supportive networks in oral health management. These international comparisons contextualize our findings and underscore the potential benefits of interventions that enhance self-efficacy, reinforce social support, and address socioeconomic disparities to improve periodontal health and quality of life. Moreover, recognizing population-specific differences can guide tailored strategies for vulnerable groups, including the elderly and individuals with lower socioeconomic status, to optimize intervention outcomes.

This study provides important insights into the relationships among oral health knowledge, attitudes, practices, psychosocial factors, and oral health-related quality of life in patients with periodontitis. Additionally, our findings have important implications for multiple stakeholders. Researchers can build on these results to design targeted interventions aimed at improving oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Public health practitioners and policymakers may use these insights to develop programs and strategies that enhance periodontal care and oral health literacy at the population level.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, our study included only patients diagnosed with periodontitis and did not incorporate a comparison group without the disease. Therefore, while the findings offer valuable information on factors influencing quality of life among affected individuals, they cannot directly assess determinants of disease occurrence. Third, the cross-sectional design of the study limits causal inference, and future research using longitudinal designs is warranted to clarify causal relationships. Fourth, more targeted questionnaires specifically addressing periodontal health knowledge, attitudes, and practices could enhance the precision of future studies. Finally, we employed a structural equation model to represent oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices collectively, which simplified the analysis but may have overlooked potential interactions among these components.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that self-efficacy for oral care, oral health-related knowledge, attitudes, practices, and socioeconomic status significantly influenced OHRQoL using a structural equation model. Self-efficacy was found to exert the most substantial effect on OHRQoL, while perceived social support significantly influenced both self-efficacy for oral care and OHRKAP, and socioeconomic status was positively correlated with perceived social support, self-efficacy, and OHRKAP. The model identified eight mediating pathways, highlighting SES and perceived social support as important moderators affecting various other studied factors. Examination of patient demographics revealed that gender and age were associated with variations in periodontal visits and were substantially correlated with the severity of periodontal disease. These findings underscore the importance of addressing psychosocial factors, such as enhancing self-efficacy and strengthening social support networks for periodontitis patients, particularly among vulnerable populations including the elderly and those with lower socioeconomic status. Based on these results, we recommend that researchers develop targeted interventions to improve oral health knowledge, attitudes, and practices; public health practitioners implement programs promoting positive oral health behaviors; and policymakers support strategies aimed at improving periodontal health and overall oral health literacy at the population level.
