Noise Monitoring and Health Assessment at a Hospital Near Tan Son Nhat Airport: Associations between Aircraft and Road Traffic Noise, Sleep Quality, and Health Outcomes
Authors: Tran Thi Hong Nhung Nguyen, Thu Lan Nguyen, Makoto Morinaga, Yasuhiro Hiraguri, Takashi Morihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Hospitals near airports are exposed to continuous aircraft and road traffic noise, potentially affecting sleep and physiological responses. This study assessed environmental noise conditions and related health outcomes at a hospital near Tan Son Nhat International Airport, Vietnam.Methods:From 2022 to 2024, noise monitoring was conducted at indoor (patient and staff rooms) and outdoor (balconies and rooftop) locations, measuring the equivalent continuous A-weighted sound pressure level (LAeq), the day–evening–night equivalent sound level (Lden), the nighttime equivalent sound level (Lnight), the maximum A-weighted sound pressure level (LAmax), and the minimum A-weighted sound pressure level (LAmin). Questionnaire surveys assessed perceived noise, annoyance, sleep disturbance, and health indicators. Sleep stages and heart rate variability were assessed using wearable devices in a subsample (28 staff members and 5 patients). Associations were analyzed using correlation and logistic regression analyses.Results:Outdoor noise levels were higher than indoor levels, with rooftop Lden reaching approximately 65 dB, while the indoor nighttime equivalent sound level ranged from about 47 to 54 dB. Objective noise indicators were not significantly associated with insomnia. In contrast, odor sensitivity (odds ratio [OR] = 24.57, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.99–303.85) and life satisfaction (OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.04–0.64) were significantly associated with insomnia. Noise annoyance was strongly associated with perceived quietness (OR = 31.47, 95% CI: 1.59–622.93).Conclusions:Hospital noise exposure near airports differs markedly between outdoor and indoor environments and cannot be explained by sound levels alone. Psychological and perceptual factors play a key role in sleep disturbance and annoyance, while patients appear more vulnerable to intermittent noise events than staff.

INTRODUCTION

Noise pollution from aircraft and road traffic is a growing public health issue, especially in densely populated urban regions with heavy transportation activity. Numerous studies have shown that nighttime noise exposure, particularly from aircraft and traffic, can significantly impair sleep quality, leading to long-term adverse health outcomes.[1] For example, a systematic review reported that transportation noise (aircraft, road, and rail) is associated with both self-reported sleep disturbance and objective measures (such as cortical awakenings), and that a 10 dB increase in Lnight increases odds of being highly sleep disturbed.[2] Similarly, Basner et al.[1] demonstrated both auditory and non-auditory health effects of noise exposure, including sleep disturbance, stress, and cardiovascular and metabolic consequences.[1]

In addition to nonauditory outcomes, environmental noise exposure is also associated with adverse auditory effects. Prolonged exposure to high sound levels can contribute to hearing fatigue, tinnitus, and impaired speech perception, particularly in environments characterized by high background noise and frequent impulsive events.[1] In hospital settings, excessive noise may interfere with verbal communication between patients and medical staff, increase listening effort, and exacerbate sensory stress, especially among vulnerable patients with pre-existing health conditions.[34] These auditory-related effects further highlight the importance of controlling noise in healthcare environments.

Aircraft noise has long been recognized as one of the dominant environmental stressors around airports, particularly in densely populated urban areas. Previous studies conducted around Tan Son Nhat (TSN) International Airport in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, have provided critical insights into community responses and health impacts associated with aviation noise. Our earlier work developed models of aviation noise and examined how variations in aircraft operation levels influenced annoyance and sleep disturbance among nearby residents.[5] Furthermore, an assessment of aircraft noise exposure and residents’ subjective responses revealed that both Lden and Lnight were significantly correlated with annoyance and self-reported sleep disturbance.[6] These studies emphasized the urgent need for more detailed investigations of noise effects in sensitive environments, such as hospitals located within urban flight corridors.

Hospitals are particularly sensitive environments in which excessive noise poses serious risks to both patients and staff. Previous research has demonstrated that since the 1960s, background noise levels in hospitals have increased markedly − average daytime levels rising from ∼57 dB(A) in 1960 to ∼72 dB(A), and nighttime levels from ∼42 dB(A) to ∼60 dB(A). These levels are often well above World Health Organization (WHO) guideline values (which recommend noise in patient rooms should not exceed ∼35 dB[A] during the day and ∼30 dB[A] at night) and are linked to impaired sleep, delayed recovery, increased stress, and reduced quality of care.[7]

Intensive care units (ICUs) represent one of the hospital settings where noise exposure is especially problematic. The ICU environment frequently causes severely fragmented sleep, reduced duration of deep (slow wave) and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, frequent arousals, and decreased sleep efficiency (SE). These disruptions are contributed by environmental noise, medical interventions, alarms, staff activity, and other hospital operational demands. The clinical consequences include delayed healing, impaired immune response, cognitive impairment (including delirium risk), prolonged hospitalization, and worse overall patient outcomes. Several studies have reviewed both the impacts and the efficacy of noise reduction strategies in ICUs.[4]

TSN International Airport in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam, represents a major source of environmental noise due to its high air traffic volume and central location within a densely populated area. As the country’s busiest airport, TSN generates substantial noise pollution that affects nearby residential and institutional areas, including hospitals. Military Hospital 175, located approximately 1km from the southeast runway end of TSN and surrounded by major roads, is one of the facilities most exposed to continuous aircraft and traffic noise. This raises concerns regarding the potential impacts on sleep quality, physiological responses, and overall health among both hospital patients and staff.

Building on previous exposure–response research, the present study aimed to provide a comprehensive assessment of environmental noise exposure and its health-related effects in a hospital located near a major international airport. The study was conducted at Military Hospital 175 from 2022 to 2024, with three main objectives: (1) to evaluate environmental noise levels in different hospital areas and compare them with national and international guideline values; (2) to examine sleep quality, perceived noise, annoyance, and health-related indicators among patients and medical staff; and (3) to explore associations between objective noise exposure, subjective perception, and short-term physiological responses using wearable devices.

In this study, the surveyed hospital was selected due to its proximity to the TSN International Airport flight path and its exposure to both aircraft and urban road traffic noise. A multi-method approach was employed, combining noise monitoring, questionnaire surveys, and physiological measurements. The monitored acoustic indicators included Lden, Lnight, LAeq, LAmax, and LAmin, and temporal noise variation. In addition, sleep-related parameters (SE, total sleep time [TST], and wake after sleep onset [WASO] and heart rate variability (HRV) indices were assessed. METHODS

Study Plan

This study was conducted across 3 years (September 2022, August 2023, and August 2024) to comprehensively assess environmental noise exposure and its potential effects on sleep quality, health-related outcomes, and short-term physiological responses in a hospital located near an international airport. The study design was aligned with the research objectives described in the Introduction section and employed a repeated observational approach across different stages of post-pandemic air traffic recovery. Data collection was carried out through a combination of environmental noise monitoring, questionnaire surveys, and wearable-based physiological measurements. In 2022, a preliminary survey was conducted to assess the baseline acoustic conditions of the hospital and to characterize the existing noise environment, serving as a reference for subsequent surveys conducted during periods of increased flight activity. In 2023, a comprehensive survey was conducted, integrating continuous noise monitoring, questionnaire administration, and physiological recordings to examine the associations between noise exposure, sleep quality, and health-related indicators during regular hospital operations. In 2024, additional noise measurements and updated questionnaire data were collected. Data obtained from the three survey periods were pooled for statistical analysis to evaluate relationships between objective noise indicators, subjective perception (e.g., annoyance and quietness), sleep-related outcomes, and short-term physiological responses. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shimane University, Japan (Approval No. 2022-1, approved on August 4, 2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki,[8] and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Noise Exposure Assessment

Environmental noise exposure was assessed at Military Hospital 175 across multiple departments and floors between 2022 and 2024. Measurement locations were selected to represent typical patient-care and staff-working environments with varying proximity to aircraft flight paths and major road traffic. Noise monitoring was conducted in patient rooms, staff rooms, balconies, and the rooftop, covering both indoor and outdoor settings. The selection of departments and floors was based on functional use and exposure characteristics. Patient rooms were selected to evaluate noise conditions directly affecting patient rest and recovery, while staff rooms represented occupational exposure for medical personnel. Balconies and the rooftop were included to capture outdoor noise levels and to characterize spatial variation in aircraft and traffic noise across different building heights. Measurements were repeated over multiple years to reflect changes in noise exposure associated with the gradual recovery of air traffic following the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic.

Noise levels were measured using a Class 1 sound level meter (NL-42, RION Co., Ltd., Japan), which complies with IEC 61672 standards.[9] The instrument was calibrated before and after each measurement campaign using an acoustic calibrator (94 dB at 1 kHz) to ensure measurement accuracy. At each location, the microphone was positioned at a height of approximately 1.2–1.5 m above the floor, corresponding to the typical human ear level.[10] To minimize reflection effects, measurement points were selected to maintain a minimum distance of approximately 1 m from walls, ceilings, or other large reflective surfaces whenever feasible. Continuous noise measurements were conducted for 1 week at each location to capture typical temporal variations in noise exposure. The recorded acoustic parameters included the daytime equivalent continuous A-weighted sound pressure level, LAeq(6:00–21:00), the nighttime equivalent continuous A-weighted sound pressure level, LAeq(21:00–6:00), as well as Lden. Measured data were averaged for each location to quantify representative noise exposure levels.

In addition to field measurements, aircraft noise exposure from 2022 to 2024 was estimated using the Integrated Noise Model (INM), version 7.0.[11] Aircraft operation data were derived from Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast records collected using a receiver installed in the inpatient ward of the surveyed hospital, enabling real-time tracking of aircraft movements. Typical aircraft types operating at TSN International Airport were classified according to standard INM aircraft categories. Default noise–power–distance relationships and engine power settings during takeoff and landing phases embedded in the INM database were applied due to the lack of airline-specific operational data.

Aircraft route data from TSN International Airport were integrated with flight tracking information obtained from Flightradar24[12] to generate aircraft noise contour maps. Based on the model outputs, Lden and Lnight were calculated to represent long-term aircraft noise exposure at the hospital site. During the 3-year monitoring period, a gradual increase in flight activity was observed, reflecting the recovery of aviation operations following the COVID-19 pandemic.

Self-Reported Health Via Questionnaire Survey

Questionnaire surveys were administered in three survey waves conducted in September 2022, August 2023, and August 2024 to assess self-reported sleep disturbance and insomnia symptoms among patients and hospital staff exposed to aircraft and road traffic noise. The surveys were conducted in patient rooms and staff rooms, representing various noise exposure conditions within the hospital. Face-to-face interviews were conducted, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation.

The questionnaire items were derived from established international instruments to ensure methodological consistency and validity. Two main outcome variables were examined: annoyance and insomnia symptoms.

(i) Annoyance

Noise annoyance was assessed in accordance with the Technical Specification ISO/TS 15666.[13] This standard provides a validated framework for assessing environmental noise annoyance using structured socio-acoustic survey methods.

Participants were asked:

“When you stay at the hospital/since you were admitted to the hospital, what number from 0 to 10 best shows how much you are bothered, disturbed, or annoyed by aircraft noise?”

Responses were recorded on an 11-point numerical rating scale ranging from 0 (not annoyed at all) to 10 (extremely annoyed). For analysis, annoyance was evaluated using the percentage of highly annoyed respondents (%HA), defined as participants selecting scores of 8, 9, or 10 on the scale.

(ii) Insomnia

Insomnia symptoms were evaluated using the Insomnia Symptom Questionnaire (ISQ).[14] The ISQ is a validated screening instrument with demonstrated reliability and diagnostic accuracy in identifying insomnia symptoms in population-based studies.

Participants were first asked:

(a) Do you have any trouble sleeping? (No/Yes)

Participants who answered “Yes” were further asked to indicate the frequency of specific sleep-related complaints using the following response options: rarely or not at all, once or twice a week, or more than three times a week. The assessed symptoms included:

(1)difficulty falling asleep;

difficulty falling asleep;

(2)difficulty resuming sleep after nighttime awakenings;

(3)early morning awakenings;

(5)daytime sleepiness affecting work performance; and

Participants were classified as having insomnia symptoms (%ISM) if they reported sleep problems, experienced daytime sleepiness affecting work performance more than three times per week, and reported at least one of the symptoms (1)–(4) occurring more than three times per week. Participants who did not meet these criteria were classified as not having insomnia symptoms.

Participants were recruited voluntarily during each survey wave. To minimize respondent burden and avoid duplicate responses, participation in each survey wave was independent, and individuals were not followed longitudinally across the 3 survey years. The relatively small number of patient participants reflects the limited availability of eligible patients, short hospital stays, and clinical constraints during the data collection periods.

Health Index Monitor

Sleep Stages

Sleep parameters were continuously recorded using a Fitbit Sense (Fitbit Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA), a wrist-worn photoplethysmography-based wearable device that automatically tracks sleep stages and physiological responses. The Fitbit Sense integrates a triaxial accelerometer, optical heart rate sensor, and electrodermal activity sensor to estimate sleep stages—wake, REM, light, and deep, based on proprietary algorithms validated against polysomnography in previous studies.[1516] Data on TST, SE, sleep onset latency, WASO, and the proportions of wake, REM, light, and deep sleep were extracted from the Fitbit application. To evaluate sleep quality, nightly averages of these parameters were calculated for each participant. Among them, SE (the ratio of TST to time in bed) and the percentage of deep sleep were used as the primary indicators of sleep quality. All data were exported and analyzed using Python (version 3.10; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA), and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Differences in noise exposure across sleep stages were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis test, a nonparametric method suitable for comparing more than two independent groups when normality assumptions are not met. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. The use of the Fitbit Sense enabled non-invasive, continuous, and practical monitoring of sleep among both recovering patients and healthcare staff under real-world hospital conditions. This approach aligns with previous research confirming the device’s reliability and validity for clinical and population-level sleep assessments.

HRV

HRV is a physiological indicator reflecting the variation in time intervals between consecutive heartbeats. It serves as a noninvasive marker of autonomic nervous system activity, providing insights into the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic regulation.[17] In this study, HRV was measured using the Bittium Faros 180 device (Bittium Corporation, Finland), a wearable electrocardiogram (ECG) recorder designed for continuous cardiac monitoring. Participants were instructed to wear the device during sleep, enabling continuous ECG signal recording throughout the night. This approach allowed for the assessment of nocturnal cardiac autonomic modulation and sleep-related physiological responses under different environmental noise exposure conditions. The recorded ECG data were analyzed using Kubios HRV Premium software (version 3.5, Kuopio, Finland), a validated and widely used tool for HRV.[18] The software automatically detected R-peaks and extracted RR intervals, followed by artifact correction and detrending using the smoothness prior’s method. HRV parameters were computed in the time domain, frequency domain, and nonlinear domain, as summarized in Table 2. These indices provided a comprehensive evaluation of autonomic activity, parasympathetic–sympathetic balance, and the physiological effects of environmental noise during sleep.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 13 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize demographic characteristics, noise exposure indicators, questionnaire responses, and physiological variables. Bivariate associations between insomnia symptoms and explanatory variables were examined using likelihood ratio chi-square tests. Differences in sleep stage parameters across noise exposure groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were not performed due to the small sample size and the exploratory nature of the analysis, in order to avoid overinterpretation of stage-specific differences. Outliers were identified using boxplot inspection and interquartile range (IQR) criteria and were retained in the analysis unless they were identified as clear measurement errors. Variables showing associations at P < 0.10 in bivariate analyses were considered for inclusion in multivariate logistic regression models, together with key noise exposure indicators, to account for potential confounding effects. Multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with insomnia symptoms and annoyance, and results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and explanatory power was described using the generalized R2. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the Gastroenterology Department, indoor noise measurements on the second floor remained relatively stable over the 3-year period. Daytime equivalent continuous sound levels, LAeq(6:00–21:00), ranged from 56.4 to 61.3 dB, while nighttime levels, LAeq(21:00–6:00), ranged from 50.6 to 53.2 dB. Lden varied slightly between 59.0 and 62.8 dB. Although interannual differences were modest, measurements in 2024 indicated a slight increase in indoor noise compared with previous years.

In the Inpatient Ward, measured noise levels consistently exceeded the thresholds for special areas defined by the Vietnam National Technical Regulation on Noise (QCVN 26:2010/BTNMT).[19] Indoor areas on the fourth floor recorded LAeq(6:00–21:00) values of 56.5–58.0 dB, whereas outdoor balcony and rooftop locations exhibited substantially higher Lden values, reaching 65.3 dB on the rooftop in both 2023 and 2024. Nighttime levels on the rooftop remained above 57 dB, indicating continuous exposure to high noise even during rest periods.

For the Orthopedic/Physiotherapy–Rehabilitation Department (C6) in 2024, indoor measurements showed LAeq(6:00–21:00) and Lden values comparable to those observed in the Gastroenterology Department, confirming that excessive noise extends beyond patient wards to rehabilitation facilities. Across all departments and measurement points, none of the recorded values complied with the QCVN 26:2010/BTNMT daytime (55 dB) or nighttime (45 dB) limits.

The number of daily flight operations at TSN Airport increased from 2022 to 2024. During daytime (06:00–18:00), arrivals increased from 175 in August 2022 to 206 in August 2024, and departures from 176 to 226, resulting in a total of 346 flights in 2022, 429 in 2023, and 432 in 2024. In the evening (18:00–22:00), arrivals increased from 55 to 75 and departures from 56 to 77, giving total evening operations of 111 in 2022, 150 in 2023, and 152 in 2024. Nighttime operations (22:00–06:00) rose from 51 arrivals and 53 departures (total 104) in 2022 to 87 arrivals and 71 departures (total 158) in 2024. Overall, total daily arrivals and departures increased from 566 in 2022 to 742 in 2024. This increase corresponded temporally with the observed upward trends in predicted aircraft noise exposure levels Lden and Lnight at the hospital, suggesting that the recovery of flight activity contributed to higher background aircraft noise exposure in the surrounding hospital environment.

Relationship between Noise Exposure and Self-Reported Health Outcomes

Questionnaire Survey Results

Table 5 presents the results of structured questionnaire surveys conducted in 2022, 2023, and 2024 among patients and staff at the surveyed hospital. The surveys assessed participants’ characteristics, perceptions of the hospital environment, noise sensitivity, sleep quality, and self-rated health status. The descriptive results indicate variations in noise sensitivity, annoyance, and perceived quietness across survey years. Among staff members, the proportion reporting dissatisfaction with quietness was higher in later survey waves, while the frequency of window opening showed a decreasing pattern across years. In 2024, 17.1% of staff reported annoyance related to aircraft noise exposure. Sleep- and health-related responses also varied between patients and staff. A relatively high proportion of patients reported poor sleep during periods of higher noise exposure, whereas insomnia symptoms and perceived stress were reported by a smaller proportion of staff members. Across all survey years, patients consistently reported poorer self-rated health compared with staff. Lifestyle-related responses among staff, such as alcohol consumption and physical activity, showed differences across survey waves; however, no formal statistical trend analysis was conducted for these variables.

Associations between Noise Exposure and Insomnia

Bivariate analyses indicated that among all examined variables, only odor sensitivity (L–R χ2 = 4.81, P = 0.028) and life satisfaction (L–R χ2 = 5.11, P = 0.024) were significantly associated with insomnia. Objective noise exposure indicators, including Lnight, Lden, and LAeq, did not show statistically significant relationships (P > 0.05), nor did subjective environmental factors such as perceived quietness and stress. Based on these results, odor sensitivity and life satisfaction were included in the multiple logistic regression model together with Lnight, duration of residence, perceived quietness, and stress to account for potential confounding effects. As shown in Table 6, odor sensitivity (OR = 24.57, 95% CI: 1.99–303.85, P = 0.013) remained significantly associated with insomnia, with participants who were more sensitive to odors showing a substantially higher likelihood of reporting insomnia. Life dissatisfaction was significantly associated with lower odds of insomnia (OR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.04–0.64, P = 0.009).

Associations between Sleep Stages and Noise Exposure

A total of five recovering patients and twenty-eight healthy hospital staff members participated in the study. Participants wore the device throughout their sleep period, from bedtime until waking, for one night of observation. In 2023, data were obtained from twenty-one participants, including nineteen staff members and two patients. Among the staff, eleven were male, and eight were female, predominantly aged between 20 and 39 years. The two patients were male and aged 50–59 years. In 2024, 14 participants were included in the analysis, comprising nine staff members and five patients. Among staff, six were male, and three were female, while among patients, three were male and two were female, with ages ranging from 20 to 59 years. These distributions allowed for assessment of sleep quality across different sexes and age groups over the 2 years.

Figure 4 presents the distributions of LAeq, LAmax, and LAmin across different sleep stages among patients after outlier removal. LAeq showed relatively small variation across stages, ranging approximately from 48 to 50 dB, indicating a stable background noise environment during sleep. DISCUSSION

Environmental Context and Acoustic Implications

This study provides new evidence that hospitals located near airports are continuously exposed to high background and intermittent noise levels that exceed both QCVN 26:2010/BTNMT standards[19] and WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines.[7] Rather than reiterating the specific values, it is important to note that these acoustic conditions reflect a combination of external sources (aircraft, road traffic) and internal contributors (medical equipment, human activity). The coexistence of these noise sources creates a complex soundscape that is difficult to control. Similar situations have been reported in hospital settings near major transport corridors, where indoor noise levels frequently remain above recommended thresholds despite structural insulation measures.[34] Previous research has also shown that hospital sound environments are often dominated by intermittent peak events and speech-related sounds, which are particularly disruptive during nighttime rest periods.[13] These findings suggest that acoustic mitigation in hospitals near airports cannot rely solely on passive building insulation but should consider both external exposure control and internal noise management strategies.

Role of Perception and Psychological Factors

The absence of a statistically significant association between objective noise indicators and insomnia symptoms does not imply that noise exposure has no health relevance. Rather, it underscores the mediating role of psychological and perceptual factors in shaping health outcomes. In this study, odor sensitivity and life satisfaction emerged as significant predictors of insomnia. Participants with higher odor sensitivity showed a markedly increased likelihood of reporting insomnia, supporting previous findings that individual sensitivity can amplify responses to environmental stressors. Interestingly, life dissatisfaction was associated with lower odds of insomnia, indicating that individuals reporting higher life satisfaction had a greater likelihood of experiencing insomnia. This finding is contrary to our initial hypothesis and suggests a complex relationship between subjective well-being and sleep outcomes. One possible explanation is that individuals with higher life satisfaction may engage in more demanding professional or social activities, which could contribute to shorter sleep duration or increased sleep disturbances. In contrast, individuals with lower life satisfaction may have less structured daily routines, potentially allowing for more flexible sleep schedules and greater sleep opportunity. In addition, given the cross-sectional design of this study, reverse causality cannot be ruled out; insomnia itself may influence perceived life satisfaction. Therefore, this unexpected finding should be interpreted with caution. Overall, these findings reinforce the notion that individual responses to environmental exposures are not determined solely by objective measures such as sound pressure levels, but are also shaped by cognitive appraisal, individual sensitivity, and broader psychological context.[12]

Previous research has demonstrated that noise sensitivity functions as a vulnerability factor, amplifying annoyance and sleep disturbance even under moderate exposure conditions. The observed association between perceived quietness and insomnia risk further supports this framework, suggesting that subjective evaluations of the acoustic environment may play an important role alongside objective noise indicators. Consequently, interventions that address perceptual and psychological dimensions, such as improving perceived environmental quality, spatial reorganization, and patient-centered design, may yield meaningful improvements in comfort and sleep quality, even when large reductions in sound levels are difficult to achieve.

Physiological Adaptation and Vulnerability

Physiological findings from HRV analysis provide further insight into the differential responses of patients and medical staff exposed to the same acoustic environment. Among staff members, correlations between HRV indices and noise indicators were weak and non-significant, suggesting relative physiological stability. This pattern may reflect habituation or long-term adaptation to chronic occupational noise exposure, as previously discussed in environmental noise research.[1]

In contrast, patients exhibited moderate trend-level correlations between noise indicators and specific HRV metrics, including increased RMSSD and decreased DFAα indices with higher noise exposure. Although these associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size and inter-individual variability, they suggest a tendency toward greater physiological reactivity among patients. This observation aligns with broader environmental health literature indicating that individuals with compromised health status or reduced coping capacity may demonstrate heightened autonomic sensitivity to environmental stressors, including noise exposure.[1]

Taken together, these findings support a conceptual framework in which objective noise exposure, subjective perception, and physiological susceptibility interact to influence health outcomes in hospital settings near airports. While medical staff may develop adaptive mechanisms over time, patients may remain potentially vulnerable to short-term acoustic fluctuations. Therefore, effective hospital noise management should combine conventional noise control with strategies that enhance perceived environmental control and promote a calm and restorative soundscape.

Implications for Hospital Noise Management Near Airports

The present findings indicate that hospital noise management near airports should extend beyond uniform sound level reduction strategies and explicitly account for differences in vulnerability between patients and medical staff. At the same time, staff may develop physiological adaptation to long-term occupational noise exposure; patients, particularly those in recovery, appear more susceptible to short-term acoustic fluctuations and intermittent high-level noise events. From a practical perspective, patient-centered acoustic strategies should prioritize the reduction of temporal variability and peak noise events during rest periods, especially at night. In addition to conventional building insulation, soundscape-oriented interventions, such as acoustic zoning, control of intrusive operational sounds, and the introduction of predictable and low-arousal background acoustic conditions, may enhance perceived environmental control and support physiological recovery.[13] These approaches are particularly relevant for hospitals located near airports, where complete elimination of external noise is often impractical.

Limitations

This study has limitations related to sample size. The small and uneven sample size, particularly among patients, may have limited the statistical power to detect significant associations, especially in subgroup and physiological analyses. Staff were unavailable for monitoring due to busy night shifts, and only a limited number of “healthy patients” could participate. In this study, “healthy patients” refer to patients in stable condition without severe cardiovascular diseases, diagnosed sleep disorders, or conditions requiring intensive care. Therefore, the generalizability of results is restricted. Future studies should include patients with various levels of illness severity and medical staff with varying years of professional experience to better clarify exposure–response relationships.

CONCLUSION

This study examined environmental noise conditions and health-related impacts in a hospital located near TSN International Airport. The results showed that hospital noise is a complex combination of external and internal sources and cannot be fully explained by sound pressure levels alone. Although measured acoustic indicators such as LAeq, Lden, and Lnight exceeded both QCVN 26:2010/BTNMT standards[19] and WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines,[7] these physical parameters did not directly correspond to insomnia symptoms. Instead, psychosocial and perceptual variables such as perceived quietness, life satisfaction, and odor sensitivity were more strongly related to annoyance and sleep disturbance. The physiological analysis also indicated that exposure patterns affect individuals differently: hospital staff appeared to adapt to long-term noise exposure, whereas patients showed greater sensitivity and autonomic responses to intermittent loud events. These findings suggest that hospital noise management should integrate objective acoustic control with interventions targeting psychological and behavioral aspects, such as improving perceived quietness, reducing intermittent noise events during rest periods, and applying soundscape-based design strategies to support patient recovery and staff well-being.
