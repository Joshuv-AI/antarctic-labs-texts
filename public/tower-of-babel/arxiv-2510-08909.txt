Gravity-Induced Modulation of Negative Differential Thermal Resistance in Fluids
Qiyuan Zhang 1, Juncheng Guo 1, Juchang Zou 1, Rongxiang Luo 1,2

Abstract
We investigate how gravity influences negative differential thermal resistance (NDTR) in fluids modeled by multiparticle collision dynamics. In the integrable case, we derive the heat flux formula for the system exhibiting the NDTR effect, and show that by introducing a gravity along the direction of the thermodynamic force, the temperature difference required for the occurrence of NDTR can be greatly reduced. Meanwhile, we also demonstrate that the heat-bath-induced NDTR mechanism—originally found to be applicable only to weakly interacting systems—can now operate in systems with stronger interactions due to the presence of gravity, and further remains robust even in mixed fluids. These results provide new insights into heat transport and establish a theoretical foundation for designing fluidic thermal devices that harness the NDTR effect under gravity.



Introduction.
The study of negative differential thermal resistance (NDTR)—a phenomenon in which the heat flux decreases with increasing temperature difference between the heat baths—is crucial for uncovering fundamental mechanisms of heat transport and enables novel functionalities in thermal management and energy conversion (see
Li2012
;
Landi2022
for reviews and references therein). First predicted in 2004 for nonlinear phononic lattices
Li2004
, NDTR has since enabled the theoretical design of novel thermal control devices (TCDs)
Li2006
;
Wang2007
;
Wang2008
;
Wu2012
, including the thermal transistor, logic gates, and memory. To date, extensive studies based on lattice models have revealed the fundamental characteristics of NDTR, as well as the conditions and mechanisms underlying its emergence
S2006
;
Yang2007
;
He2009
;
Shao2009
;
P2010
;
Zhong2011
;
M2015
. Furthermore, motivated by these pioneering studies, NDTR has been extensively explored in diverse quantum systems as a route toward quantum TCDs
Ben2014
;
Schuab2016
;
Joulain2016
;
Ren2013
;
Fornieri2016
;
Guo2018
;
Liu2019
;
Du2019
, notably the quantum thermal transistor.

Similarly, heat-bath-induced NDTR in fluids has recently attracted significant interest due to its fundamental importance and potential for enabling novel fluidic TCDs
Luo2019
;
Luo2022
;
Zou2025
. In 2019, it was demonstrated that NDTR can be induced in one-dimensional (1D) hard-point gas systems, representing 1D fluids, when coupled to heat baths at different temperatures
Luo2019
. In such systems, NDTR arises because lowering the temperature of the cold bath suppresses particle motion, thereby drastically reducing the frequency of collisions between the colder particles and the hot bath. Consequently, the heat flux remains small even for large temperature differences across the baths. Subsequently, studies of the mesoscopic fluid model based on multiparticle collision (MPC) dynamics
Luo2022
further showed that the heat-bath-induced NDTR mechanism also applies to high-dimensional fluidic systems with weak interactions and is very robust even in mixed fluids. These advances in NDTR have established new fundamental insights into a broad class of nonequilibrium fluid systems.

Under certain conditions, Earth’s gravitational field can significantly influence the thermophysical properties of a broad class of fluid systems
Thorsten2001
;
van1997
;
Scholz2017
;
van1985
;
Haussmann1999
, including granular gases, macromolecular gases, and simple fluids near gas-liquid critical points. In such systems, gravity induces an inhomogeneous density distribution, leading to height-dependent variations in all thermophysical properties. A clear understanding of gravitational effects on heat transport in these fluids is therefore essential. The present work addresses a central question: How does gravity modify the emergence and characteristics of NDTR in such systems?

To obtain a clear and minimal physical picture about the answer, we consider a two-dimensional (2D) system of particles confined in a rectangular box and governed by MPC dynamics—a stochastic method used to simulate simple fluids. The MPC approach replaces conventional deterministic molecular dynamics with stochastic velocity rotations
M1999
. A key advantage of this method is its ability to correctly reproduce hydrodynamic behavior with significant computational efficiency
Gompper2009
;
Padding2006
. MPC has been applied to study coupled particle and heat transport in nonequilibrium systems
Benenti2014
;
Luo2020
, and more recently, has offered fundamental insights into heat conduction mechanisms
Luo2025
.

Here, we investigate the validity of the heat-bath-induced NDTR mechanism in fluids under gravity. First, we derive an analytical expression for the heat flux that captures the emergence of NDTR in the presence of gravity, and we show that when the gravitational field is aligned with the thermodynamic force, the temperature difference required for the onset of NDTR is significantly reduced. Second, we demonstrate that gravity enables this mechanism to operate in systems with stronger interparticle interactions, thereby extending its applicability beyond the weakly interacting systems for which it was originally established. Finally, we show that the mechanism remains robust in mixed fluids. Additional results for the case in which the gravitational field opposes the thermodynamic force are provided in the Supplemental Material.

The dynamics in the bulk is governed by MPC dynamics
M1999
;
Gompper2009
. In essence, MPC simplifies the numerical simulation of interacting particle systems by coarse-graining both time and space, and replacing deterministic interaction forces with stochastic collision rules. In the MPC framework, the system evolves through discrete time steps, each consisting of a streaming phase during which particles move under the influence of the gravitational force
\emph{\textbf{g}}=(0,-g)
for a time interval
\tau
, followed by an instantaneous stochastic collision step. During the streaming phase, the evolution equations for this system are:

\displaystyle\dot{\emph{\textbf{r}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\emph{\textbf{v}}_{i}+\emph{\textbf{g}}\tau,

(2)

\displaystyle\dot{\emph{\textbf{p}}}_{i}

\displaystyle=m_{i}\dot{\emph{\textbf{v}}}_{i}=m_{i}\emph{\textbf{g}}.

In each collision step, the simulation domain is divided into identical square cells of size
a\times a
(see Fig.
1
). Within each cell, all particles have their velocities rotated relative to the cell’s center-of-mass velocity
\mathbf{V}_{\text{c.m.}}
by an angle of either
\theta
or
-\theta
, each chosen with equal probability. The updated velocity of a particle in a given cell is therefore given by:

\textbf{\emph{v}}_{i}\rightarrow\textbf{\emph{V}}_{\textmd{c.m.}}\\
+\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm\theta}\left(\textbf{\emph{v}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{V}}_{\textmd{c.m.}}\right),

(3)

where
\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{\pm\theta}
denotes the rotation operator corresponding to an angle of
\pm\theta
. This collision rule ensures the conservation of total momentum and kinetic energy within each cell. It is worth noting that a rotation angle of
\theta=\pi/2
leads to the most efficient mixing of particle momenta. Additionally, as the time step
\tau
decreases, the frequency of collisions increases, meaning that
\tau
can be used as a tunable parameter to control the effective interaction strength and, consequently, the transport properties of the system.

In our model, we set
T_{U}=T
and
T_{L}=T-\Delta T
, where
\Delta T
represents the temperature difference across the system. The main parameter settings are as follows:
m_{i}=k_{B}=T=a=1
,
\theta=\pi/2
,
L=2
,
H=10
, and the average particle number density
\rho=N/(LH)=6
. At the beginning of the simulation, all particles are uniformly distributed throughout the system and are assigned random initial velocities sampled from a Boltzmann distribution corresponding to the temperature
T
. After the system reaches a steady state, we compute the heat flux
J
that crosses the system according to its definition (i.e., the average energy exchanged in the unit time and unit area between particles and heat bath). Additionally, the distributions of particle number density
\rho(h)
and temperature
T(h)
, where
h
is the spatial coordinate in the
y
-direction, are similarly measured, as described in
Luo2019
;
2020Luo
. To guarantee Galilean invariance of the stochastic rotation dynamics, the collision grid is shifted randomly before each collision step

Theoretical analysis.
Here, we theoretically investigate the influence of gravity on the NDTR properties of fluids and infer its mechanism. If no particles interact (i.e., each particle is only subject to the force of gravity when it crosses the system from one heat bath to the other.), the model becomes integrable. In the integrable case, the heat flux flowing across the system can be derived explicitly (see Section A of Supplemental Material) as

J=N\frac{(1+d)}{2}\left|\frac{k_{B}\left(T_{U}-T_{L}\right)}{\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle+\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle}\right|,

(4)

where
d(=1,2,3)
is the spatial dimension (the theoretical results presented here apply also to one- and three-dimensional systems.). In the formula,
\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle
and
\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
represent the average times taken by a particle to travel from the upper to lower heat bath and vice versa, respectively. These quantities can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{U}}{2m}}\left\{e^{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{U}}}\left[1-\mathrm{Erf}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{U}}})\right]-1\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}},

(5)

\displaystyle\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{L}}{2m}}\left\{e^{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}}\left[1+\mathrm{Erf}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}})\right]-1\right\}-\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}.

Notice that in the limit
g\to 0
, by performing the Taylor expansion in the above expressions, we can recover the results for the case without gravity
Luo2019
, with
\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle=H\sqrt{\pi m/2k_{B}T_{U}}
and
\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle=H\sqrt{\pi m/2k_{B}T_{L}}
.

Next, we explain why a larger value of

Next, we explain why this mechanism breaks down in the absence of gravity under strong interactions, yet remains effective when gravity is present. As shown in Figs.

Discussions and conclusions.
In summary, we have investigated the role of gravity in the emergence and characteristics of NDTR in a fluid model simulated using MPC dynamics. We derive an analytical expression for the heat flux that agrees well with simulations, demonstrating that the temperature bias required for NDTR is significantly reduced when gravity is aligned with the thermodynamic force. Furthermore, gravity extends the applicability of the heat-bath-induced NDTR mechanism to systems with stronger interactions, beyond the weakly interacting regime in which it was originally established. Notably, this mechanism under gravity remains robust across a variety of fluid mixtures.

The case of gravity opposing the thermodynamic force is studied in Section B of the Supplemental Material. In this configuration, while the mechanism also works in fluid mixtures, the threshold temperature bias for NDTR is substantially increased, and gravity no longer extends the mechanism to stronger interactions. These findings highlight the directional sensitivity of gravitational control and underscore the generality and tunability of NDTR in nonequilibrium fluids.

The influence of gravity on heat transport is central to both fundamental nonequilibrium physics and potential thermal management applications. The MPC dynamics with gravity presented here provides a computationally efficient model for exploring such effects. Extensions to higher-dimensional systems could enable the study of Rayleigh-Bénard flow
Stefanov2022
;
2022Stefanov
, thermal rectification
2024Zou
, and dimension-dependent heat conduction
Lepri2016
. Furthermore, incorporating thermochemical baths—allowing both heat and particles exchange with the environment
Benenti2014
;
Luo2020
—would enable the study of coupled heat and particle transport, offering insights into thermoelectric-like effects and potential designs for fluidic thermal devices.

Fluidic TCDs—including thermal switches, diodes, and regulators—have been implemented across diverse length and temperature scales
Klinar2021
. However, unlike electronic systems, fluidic thermal engineering lacks a functional analogue to the transistor. The NDTR demonstrated here provides a mechanism for strong nonlinearity and signal gain, essential for realizing a fluidic thermal transistor. This paves the way for designing such a transistor and other more complex fluidic TCDs, as previously achieved in lattice systems and quantum systems
Li2012
;
Landi2022
. The present work will stimulate further theoretical and experimental investigation of NDTR in fluidic systems.

Appendix A
Derivation of heat flux and collision frequency

A.1
Derivation of heat flux

The fluid model shown in Fig.
1
belongs to the integrable case when the interactions among particles in the system are not considered. In such a case, the heat flux flowing across the system from the lower to the upper end can be written as

J=N\left|\frac{\langle E_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle-\left(\langle E_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle-mgH\right)}{\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle+\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle}\right|,

(7)

where
N
is the number of particles in the system,
mgH
represents the work done by a particle to overcome gravity, and
\langle E_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle
and
\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle
represent, respectively, the average energy a particle transfers and the average time it takes during the a journey from the upper to the lower end. The quantities
\langle E_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
and
\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
have similar meanings but for the journey from the lower to the upper end. Note that a particle that leaves from the upper end will reach the lower end straightforwardly, but when it leaves from the lower end to reach the upper end, it may collide with the lower end one or more times due to the presence of gravity.

In the following, we first compute
\langle E_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle
and
\langle E_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
. By definition, the average energy transferred by a particle during a journey from the upper to the lower bath is

\displaystyle\langle E_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}mv_{y}^{2}f_{U}\left(v_{y}\right)dv_{y}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}mv_{x}^{2}f_{U}\left(v_{x}\right)dv_{x}=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T_{U}.

(8)

Considering that for a particle to move from the lower to the upper bath, its velocity in the
y
-direction must satisfy the condition
v_{y}\geq\sqrt{2gH}
. Therefore, the average energy transferred by a particle during a journey from the lower to the upper bath is

\displaystyle\langle E_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle=\frac{\int_{\sqrt{2gH}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}mv_{y}^{2}f_{L}\left(v_{y}\right)dv_{y}}{\int_{\sqrt{2gH}}^{\infty}f_{L}\left(v_{y}\right)dv_{y}}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}mv_{x}^{2}f_{L}\left(v_{x}\right)dv_{x}=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T_{L}+mgH.

(9)

Next, we compute
\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle
. The time that a particle moves from the upper to the lower end can be written as

t_{U\rightarrow L}=\frac{\sqrt{v^{2}_{y,U}+2gH}-v_{y,U}}{g},

(10)

by considering the following two equations

\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}_{y,L}=\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}_{y,U}+mgH,

(11)

v_{y,L}=v_{y,U}+gt_{U\rightarrow L},

(12)

where
v_{y,U}
and
v_{y,L}
represent the velocities of the particle in the
y
-direction at the upper boundary and lower boundary, respectively. Then, the average time a particle spends during this journey is given by

\displaystyle\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}t_{U\rightarrow L}f_{U}(v_{y})dv_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{U}}{2m}}\left\{e^{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{U}}}\left[1-\mathrm{Erf}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{U}}})\right]-1\right\}+\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}.

(13)

Then, we compute
\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
. For the average time of the journeys from the lower to the upper bath it is not so straightforward, as the particle may collide with the lower bath multiple times before reaching the upper bath. To compute
\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle
, let us denote by
p_{n}
the probability for a particle to take a journey that starts from the lower end, collides with the lower bath
n-1
times, before reaching the upper bath, and denote by
t_{n}
the time the particle spends to go through such a journey. Then, we have

\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}t_{n},

(14)

where the summation runs over all the allowed journeys. For a particle starting from the lower end, suppose the probability it collides with the upper end or the lower end next is
p_{L\rightarrow U}
or
p_{L\rightarrow L}
, respectively, and the time it spends during such a motion is
t_{L\rightarrow U}
or
t_{L\rightarrow L}
, respectively, then
p_{n}
and
t_{n}
can be expressed as

p_{n}=p^{n-1}_{L\rightarrow L}p_{L\rightarrow U},

(15)

t_{n}=(n-1)t_{L\rightarrow L}+t_{L\rightarrow U}.

(16)

Additionally, according to the above definitions, we have

p_{L\rightarrow L}=\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2gH}}f_{L}(v_{y})dv_{y}=1-e^{-\frac{mgH}{kT_{L}}},

(17)

p_{L\rightarrow U}=\int_{\sqrt{2gH}}^{\infty}f_{L}(v_{y})dv_{y}=e^{-\frac{mgH}{kT_{L}}},

(18)

\displaystyle t_{L\rightarrow L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2gH}}t_{L\rightarrow L}f_{L}(v_{y})dv_{y}}{p_{L\rightarrow L}}=\frac{-2\sqrt{2gH}e^{-\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\pi k_{B}T_{L}}{m}}\mathrm{Erf}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}})}{g(1-e^{-\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}})},

(19)

\displaystyle t_{L\rightarrow U}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\sqrt{2gH}}^{\infty}t_{L\rightarrow U}f_{L}(v_{y})dv_{y}}{p_{L\rightarrow U}}=\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}-\frac{1}{g}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{L}}{2m}}\left[1-e^{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}}\mathrm{Erfc}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}})\right].

(20)

In Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
),
t_{L\rightarrow L}=\frac{2v_{y,L}}{g}
is obtained by considering the free-fall motion of a particle and
t_{L\rightarrow U}=\frac{v_{y,L}-\sqrt{v_{y,L}^{2}-2gH}}{g}
is derived in a similar manner to Eq. (
10
). Substituting Eqs. (
15
)-(
20
) into Eq. (
14
) gives

\displaystyle\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}t_{n}=\frac{p_{L\rightarrow L}}{p_{L\rightarrow U}}t_{L\rightarrow L}+t_{L\rightarrow U}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g}\sqrt{\frac{\pi k_{B}T_{L}}{2m}}\left\{e^{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}}\left[\mathrm{Erf}(\sqrt{\frac{mgH}{k_{B}T_{L}}})+1\right]-1\right\}-\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}}.

(21)

What Eq. (
A.1
) suggests is clear: For a particle starting from the lower end and reaching the upper end in the end, it will experience
p_{L\rightarrow L}/p_{L\rightarrow U}
collisions with the lower end on average. Therefore, the average time the particle spends can be divided into two parts as the r.h.s. of Eq. (
A.1
) indicates.

The above derivation results can be easily generalized to one-dimensional or three-dimensional systems. By summarizing these results, the heat flux formula, Eq. (
7
), applicable to three dimensions is finally expressed as

J=N\frac{(1+d)}{2}\left|\frac{k_{B}\left(T_{U}-T_{L}\right)}{\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle+\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle}\right|,

(22)

where
d(=1,2,3)
is the spatial dimension.

A.2
Derivation of collision frequency

The collision frequency of a particle colliding with the upper bath is defined as
f^{(1)}=1/(\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle+\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle)
. Therefore, if the system consists of
N
non-interacting particles, the collision frequency of particles colliding with the upper bath is given by

f=Nf^{(1)}=\frac{N}{\langle t_{L\rightarrow U}\rangle+\langle t_{U\rightarrow L}\rangle}.

(23)
