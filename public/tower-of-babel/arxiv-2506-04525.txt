User Altruism in Recommendation Systems
Ekaterina Fedorova, Madeline Kitch, Chara Podimata

Abstract
Users of social media platforms based on recommendation systems (RecSys) (e.g. TikTok, X, YouTube) strategically interact with platform content to influence future recommendations. On some such platforms, users have been documented to form large-scale grassroots movements encouraging others to purposefully interact with algorithmically suppressed content in order to “boost” its recommendation; we term this behavior user altruism . To capture this behavior, we study a game between users and a RecSys, where users provide the RecSys (potentially manipulated) preferences over the contents available to them, and the RecSys—limited by data and computation constraints—creates a low-rank approximation preference matrix, and ultimately provides each user her (approximately) most-preferred item. We compare the users’ social welfare under truthful preference reporting and under a class of strategies capturing user altruism. In our theoretical analysis, we provide sufficient conditions to ensure strict increases in user social welfare under user altruism, and provide an algorithm to find an effective altruistic strategy. Interestingly, we show that for commonly assumed recommender utility functions, effectively altruistic strategies also improve the utility of the RecSys! We show that our results are robust to several model misspecifications, thus strengthening our conclusions. Our theoretical analysis is complemented by empirical results of effective altruistic strategies on the GoodReads dataset, and an online survey on how real-world users behave altruistically in RecSys. Overall, our findings serve as a proof-of-concept of the reasons why traditional RecSys may incentivize users to form collectives and/or follow altruistic strategies when interacting with them.

Introduction

Social networking and media platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube learn about users by tracking their interactions with content via likes, comments, views, etc. Using these interactions, they can infer user preferences and provide new content recommendations. From the user perspective, while the technical algorithm is a black box, the basic idea of how it functions is well-known
[

Knowing that engaging with content of this type will likely cause it to be recommended again in the future, the user purposefully likes, comments, and watches this type of content
more often
than they personally would like in the moment. There are several game theoretic models of self-interested strategic behavior
[

However, existing modeling approaches do not acknowledge that recommendations are informed by both a user’s own interactions and the interactions of

users. Collaborative filtering, a widely-adopted recommendation methodology
[

, allows the recommender to infer the preferences of one user from another by embedding users into a shared latent space. When one user engages with a piece of content, the recommender may predict how much engagement other users would have with said item based on the similarity of user embeddings. Like with self-interested strategic engagement, users also have a heuristic understanding of how their engagements impact others’ recommendations, and may use this information to interact strategically to impact other users’ platform experiences
[

,

]
. This behavior has been particularly relevant for users who want to combat algorithmic suppression and injustice. Indeed, Karizat et al.
[

document that users believe certain types of content are suppressed by the algorithm and thus
altruistic

On an individual level, it is unlikely that

1.1
Our contributions

Model.
In Section
2
, we present a game-theoretic model of users’ correlated strategies in recommendation systems (RecSys) and we provide a proof-of-concept analysis of the reasons why
collaborative filtering or matrix factorization-based RecSys may incentivize users to collectivize and behave altruistically
as documented by HCI research and real-world events. To the best of our knowledge, ours is the
first
theoretical model capturing
strategic
behavior of users in RecSys that are
not self-interested
.

Altruistic behavior.
For ease of exposition, we focus on a large class of settings where the preferences of the population over the different items satisfy a
majority-minority
relationship, i.e., users are mostly clustered with regards to the items that they prefer (and the intensity with which they do), though this is generalized in Appendix
F
. In Section
3
, we compare the recommendations and social welfare that users receive under
truthful
content interactions (i.e., when they consume content, they do so following their “ground truth” preferences, and not caring about other people) to those they receive under a class of simple
correlated
interaction strategies; we call these strategies “altruistic”. We find that under reasonable conditions on these strategies, our class of correlated strategies is
effective
in improving social welfare and recommendations beyond the truthful interaction baseline. We construct an algorithm to find an effectively altruistic strategy, if one that satisfies the conditions exists. Our conclusions are robust to misspecifications in the information users share about their true preferences and level of altruism. These results provide the first theoretical groundwork for the documented collectivist behavior.

RecSys utility.
Interestingly, considering two commonly used utility functions for the RecSys (engagement-based and social-welfare-maximizing), we find that users’ altruistic engagement under our model is also good for the recommendation system’s welfare itself (Section
4
)! Intuitively, this is because the altruistic nature of these correlated strategies not only improves recommendations for minority users but it also increases the general users’ engagement with the platform content (which in turn is beneficial for the platform that can sell more ads).

Empirical results.
We conduct two empirical studies to supplement our theoretical findings. The first one (Section
5.1
) is an experiment on a dataset of Goodreads book reviews. Similarly to movements in the prominent “BookTok” community where TikTok users have organized to intentionally interact with content from marginalized groups in order to battle unfair algorithmic promotion
[
48
]
, we simulate collectives of readers of the most popular genre behaving altruistically by strategically increasing engagement with less popular book genres. The behavior
improves social welfare of the minority
groups in our dataset by as much as
15 times
! Our second empirical analysis (Section
5.2
) is an exploratory survey given to 100 Prolific participants. Our results (1) indicate that the proportion of users who intentionally attempt to impact other people’s recommended feeds is relatively large; and (2) provide more reliable (textual) descriptions of users’ underlying altruistic reasoning in addition to those already documented by HCI researchers.

Finally, Section
6
includes a discussion on our model assumptions and future research directions.

2.1.1
Low-rank approximation phase

For the
Low-rank approximation phase
, the recommender performs a singular value decomposition (SVD). Recall that for a matrix
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}

\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^{\top}

\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\mathbf{R}=\sum_{j\in[\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})]}\sigma_{j}\mathbf{u_{j}}\mathbf{v_{j}}^{\top}

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}=\sum_{j\in[k^{\star}]}\sigma_{j}\mathbf{u_{j}}\mathbf{v_{j}}^{\top},

k^{\star}<\mathrm{rank}(\mathbf{R})

\mathrm{rank}(\widehat{\mathbf{R}})=k^{\star}

2.2
Agent preferences and utility functions

For ease of exposition, we focus on a specific class of ground truth preference/ratings matrices
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
which we term the
“majority-minority matrix”

Definition 2.3

We say that
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m\times n}
is a
majority-minority matrix
if there exist two groups of users and items such that users only like the items in their respective group. Formally, there exists a partition of users
\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}=[m]
(where
\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}},\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\subseteq[m]
) and a partition of items
\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{maj}}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{min}}=[n]
(where
\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{maj}},\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{min}}\subseteq[m]
) such that if
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}
and
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{min}}
or
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}
and
i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{maj}}
, then
r_{u,i},r_{u^{\prime}i^{\prime}}=0
. Further, no user has ratings of all
0
s (i.e.,
\forall u\in[m]
,
\sum_{i\in[n]}r_{u,i}>0
).

Intuitively, these are matrices where there are two groups of users that have exclusive preferences.

Remark 2.1

The utility of each user is invariant under any re-ordering of users and items.

The proof of this remark can be found in Appendix
C.2
. As a result of Remark
2.1
, we will wlog refer to
\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}
as the partition of users such that
|\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}|\geq|\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}|
, and order the rows and columns of the preference matrices such that for some
\bar{m}\geq m/2
and
\bar{n}\in[n]
,
\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}=[\bar{m}]
and
\mathcal{I}_{\textsc{maj}}=[\bar{n}]
. Therefore, we can write a majority-minority matrix,
\mathbf{R}
, as

\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{maj}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{min}}\end{pmatrix}

where
\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{maj}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\bar{m}\times\bar{n}}
and
\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{min}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{(m-\bar{m})\times(n-\bar{n})}
are the ratings of majority and minority users.

We name these matrix blocks “majority” and “minority” to indicate that they represent user groups that are more/less dominant in the system, respectively. We represent this dominance of preference mathematically using a gap in the singular values of the two matrix blocks. For a matrix
\mathbf{R}

, and define:
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R}):=(\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{min}}),\sigma_{k_{\textsc{maj}}}(\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{maj}}))
.
If
\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R})\neq\emptyset
, then we say that
\mathbf{R}
has a

. The matrices that we focus on in this work have the following property.

5

Agent utility.
We compare two distinct behavioral models related to how agents choose the rankings that they report for the revealed preference matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
:
truthful
and
altruistic
. When agents are
truthful
, then
\tilde{r}_{u,i}=r^{\star}_{u,i}
. In the absence of any other information (e.g., information about other agents, or the learner’s algorithm) truthfulness is a best response for each agent; i.e., the agents maximize their utility by reporting
\tilde{r}_{u,i}=r^{\star}_{u,i}
. The central focus of our work is cases where truthfulness is
not
the agents’ best-response. Rather, agents will be
altruistic
; i.e., they will try to submit rankings that will help other, less well-represented agents in getting their top items recommended to them.

User Preferences and Social Welfare

In this section, we focus on the users, their behaviors, and the social welfare they obtain.
We calculate the social welfare of agents under two different rating strategies: (1) all agents honestly communicate their personal preferences, and (2) a subgroup of majority agents purposefully
uprate
an item preferred by minority agents.
We find sufficient conditions on uprating strategies to ensure social welfare gain, construct a correlated mechanism game to model the agent interaction needed to play an effective altruistic uprating strategy, and create a robust algorithm to find such an uprating strategy.

We outline the results in terms of formal statements and defer the proofs to Appendix
D
. Generalized rating strategies are studied in Appendix
F

3.1
Social welfare of majority-minority preference matrices under truthfulness

In a majority-minority matrix satisfying Assumption
2.1
, the majority is more “important” in the low-rank approximation because its higher singular values indicate that the majority matrix block is more informative. Additionally, because preferences are exclusive, each majority user’s preference vector is orthogonal to all minority users’ preference vectors meaning majority and minority users’ preferences live in different subspaces. Thus, it is natural that a dimensionally-constrained learner may be forced to reduce out the subspace containing minority user preferences. As a result, when
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}^{\star}
, some learners will only recommend accurately to the majority. The formal proof is deferred to Appendix
D.1
.

Theorem 3.1
(Truthfulness is good for majority, bad for minority)

Let the ground truth preference matrix
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
be a majority-minority matrix satisfying Assumption
2.1
and
\mathbf{R}^{\star}=\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
(i.e., truthful reporting). If
\alpha
is in the singular value gap (i.e.
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R}^{\star})
), then all majority users are accurately given their top item, while minority users are given popular items they do not like. Formally:

3.2
Improving social welfare via simple correlated rating strategies

If (some) users collaboratively distort ratings, can they force the learner to retain more information about minorities? We focus on distortions that help a group of “picky” minority users, whose preferences are hard for RecSys to learn otherwise.

Definition 3.1
(Picky Items)

We say that item
i^{\star}
is a picky item with picky user group
\mathcal{U}_{i^{\star}}
if

r_{u,i^{\star}}>0\iff u\in\mathcal{U}_{i^{\star}},\forall u\in[m]

and for all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{i^{\star}}
and
i\neq i^{\star}
,
r_{u,i}=0
. That is, if users like
i^{\star}
, it is the only item they like.

Appendix
C.1

We model the users’
altruistic

5.1.2
Limitations

While we are able to see welfare improvements, there are a some limitations to this empirical study. Firstly, there are very few minority users. It would be more interesting and meaningful if minority users constituted a greater fraction of total readers, as this would translate to greater social welfare improvements and hence greater incentives to participate in EA. A counter to this is that the benefits are essentially for free (no groups are meaningfully hurt by EA behavior), and that helping even a small number of users is important. Secondly, the singular value gap is rather small given the size of the singular values. Hence, it may be unrealistic to assume that the learner follows this truncation protocol. This is potentially a limitation of the particular dataset that we have chosen. Thirdly, while this altruistic behavior is similar to the real-world movement we model, the groupings we have defined are not the same. On BookTok, groups of users have been documented to intentionally increase engagement with books from with marginalized author groups such as black or indigenous authors
[
48
]
rather than genres. We did not have the data to test this behavior exactly.

6
Conclusion and future work

We model a recommender system in which users’ preferences influence each other’s recommendations. Constructing a class of simple correlated rating strategies, we find that users are able to strictly improve social welfare beyond truthful preference reporting. These strategies represent altruistic manipulations: users in the majority are able to improve the minority group’s recommendations. We provide a robust algorithm to find an effective strategy and prove that the learner’ utility is also improved under altruism. We supplement our theoretical results with empirics: (1) a simulation of altruism on the Goodreads dataset and (2) an online survey of real users. We are the first to lay groundwork for analysis of recommendations as multi-agent models in which users are
not exclusively self-interested
. We outline some directions for future work.

Learner’s perspective.

To focus on agents’ welfare, we imagine a learner follows a specific protocol: (1) Low-rank Approximation (2) Top-1 Recommendation. Thus, there are natural extensions to our model that consider a more complex learner. The most obvious complication, general top-
s
recommendation, is somewhat uninteresting because in our setting, the approximated matrix,
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
, has
0
values for all minority preferences. Therefore, even if the learner chooses to provide the top-
s
items, he still lacks the information necessary to make good minority recommendations and our SW results hold up to
s\leq\bar{n}
. More interestingly, rather than following this protocol, the learner may explicitly maximize his own utility. While we show user EA strategies yield the learner higher welfare when he follows the protocol, this learner strategy is not necessarily optimal. Further work could analyze this setting as a multi-agent Stackelberg game in which some agents have altruistic utilities and the principal has benevolent, engagement-based or an alternative utility. An alternative utility that penalizes the learner for incentivizing agents to increase data dimensionality would be particular relevant to the type of user strategies we study.

(More) HCI.

While we reference many cases of correlated user rating strategies, there are more from news sources than academic literature and the HCI works that do exist are primarily interview-based. There are no large-scale experiments of how users, given they have an understanding of the algorithm, intentionally interact with content when they want to improve the fairness, safety, accuracy, quality, etc of algorithmic recommendation. Further, it is still unclear how wide-spread this intention is across “regular” users. Our survey suggests that it may be fairly commonplace, but this is not yet a robust conclusion.

B.1
Extended related works

Our work is related to four major streams of literature: Human Computer Interaction, RecSys modeling, strategic classification, and collaborative filtering & matrix completion. We provide extensive details on each of these below.

Human computer interaction.
There is a breadth of human computer interaction literature that serves as the inspiration for our theoretical modeling of user strategic behavior. It has long been clear that even “normal” users are aware of social platforms’ recommendation algorithms
[
22
]
. HCI researchers study the complex mental models people develop to understand algorithms as folk theories
[
52
,
25
]
. How a user forms their algorithmic folk theories is heavily impacted by the way in which they present themselves to the platform (posts, statuses, etc) and interact with content (likes, comments, etc.)
[
21
]
. Thus, there are many works that conduct interview studies with users of different intersections about their experiences/theories related to curation/recommendation algorithms. These intersections include YouTube creators
[
65
]
, LGBTQ+ TikTok users
[
61
]
, transfeminine TikTok creators
[
20
]
, Queer people targeted by ads
[
59
]
, participants of online mental health communities
[
51
]
, and black content creators
[
29
]
. Across the intersections of these interview studies and in a recent large scale experiment
[
12
]
, results indicate that people use their folk theories to strategically interact (e.g. liking, commenting, watching, etc) with platform content in order to tailor their personal recommendation feeds when possible. However, a user’s folk theories (including those documented in the interview studies above) are not limited to how the algorithm impacts them personally. Many users of the online book discussion community known as “BookTok”, for example, theorize that the recommendation algorithm generally suppresses content from marginalized creators
[
49
]
. Karizat et al.
[
41
]
document the relationship between such folk theories and altruistic actions users take in order to ameliorate bad/harmful recommendation for other groups. While there are not yet large scale experiments on altruistically strategic interactions, grassroots movements among communities engaging in this to support BLM
[
16
]
and marginalized authors
[
48
]
have been reported. Our theoretical model serves as a proof-of-concept mathematically for altruistic interaction and our survey indicates that altruistic interaction may be fairly commonplace.

RecSys modeling.

There is a large body of work on the modeling of recommender systems through a game-theoretic or economic lens. Two are most relevant to this work as they model users themselves behaving strategically. First, Haupt et al.
[
31
]
model strategic users and a recommender system as a two-phase Stackelberg game in which the recommender commits to a policy that maps a user’s interactions to a content recommendation and then users strategically interact with content during a learning phase. In our model, we abstract away from the recommender learning preferences over rounds and allow the interactions of one user to impact the recommendations of another. Second, Cen et al.
[
13
]
also model the interaction as a repeated two-player (Stackelberg) game. At each round, the learner provides recommendations to the user based on an estimate of user strategies, and the user responds with a behavior generated by an [evolving] user strategy. Authors conclude that trustworthy algorithms are those that do not incentivize a user to be strategic and guarantee
\kappa
payoff to the naive agent. Our model differs from this one because we consider a multi-agent game, though their conception of trustworthiness could likely be made applicable to our model if we consider payoff guarantees across all users.

Other relevant game-theoretic models consider some sort of user or item social welfare (though not strategic users). Dean et al.
[
18
]
find that under certain conditions in top-
N
collaborative filtering methodologies, some items, despite being in the system, would never be recommended to any type of user. Guo et al.
[
27
]
expand upon this problem and find that it can be ameliorated when users are represented by multiple feature vectors. We take a more user-centered approach in our analysis of social welfare. Peng et al.
[
54
]
analyze the recommender’s accuracy/diversity trade-off when providing content and conclude that users’ consumption cost constraints should imply more diversity is utility-maximizing for users. Because our results focus on top-1 recommendation according to a fixed preference matrix, this is not an issue for the learner we study. Some game-theoretic recommender system models focus primarily on welfare or mechanisms for the learner. Hébert and Zhong
[
36
]
consider the recommender’s problem as an optimal information design over a sequence of rounds to keep a user engaged with the platform for as long as possible, Ben-Porat et al.
[
7
]
consider the learner as a multi-armed bandit wanting to avoid user attrition, and Keinan and Ben-Porat
[
42
]
create a model that maximizes engagement to avoid user churn. While not the primary focus of our work, we consider a version of engagement maximization for the learner’s utility function in section
4
. Additionally, there are a handful of modeling works that consider content creators as strategic. Some of these directions include characterizing/learning equilibria of the content creation marketplace
[
38
,
6
]
, modeling the incentives creators have to invest in creating quality or clickbait content
[
37
]
, designing learning algorithms for the recommender that will incentivize quality content generation
[
35
]
, designing fair and stable recommenders under content creator strategization
[
5
]
, and . While content creators are a type of user, creation of new content is not the type of rating interaction we consider with our preference matrices (see section
6
for discussion of ratings).

Finally, while not explicitly a model of content recommendation, there is also a connection between our modeling of altruistic agents and ideas of public spirit. Flanigan et al.
[
24
]
model a voting setting in which a public-spirited agent, who may otherwise suffer distortion in social welfare due to the nature of voting with cardinal preferences
[
56
]
, accounts for other agents’ utility functions in the submission of her cardinal preferences to the voting mechanism. Our conception of agents intentionally manipulating ratings to help others is similar, but because in our model every agent receives her own recommendation (rather than the election in which one candidate is chosen) and the strategy space is continuous the public-spirited voting regime is significantly different.

Strategic classification.
Given that we focus on strategic individuals in recommender systems, there is a breadth of relevant work on strategic agents in classification problems. Presented first by Hardt et al.
[
28
]
, in this setting there is a learner who publishes a classifier to agents whose utility depends on their classification. Top-
1
recommendation resembles strategic multi-class classification where the learner takes as input user features and outputs (a type of) content each user would like. There exists a large thread of learner-centric strategic classification literature, such as algorithms that are in some way robust to agent strategization such as incentive-awareness
[
28
,
46
,
14
]
, truthfulness
[
30
]
or strategyproofness
[
2

4
, more relevant to our work is strategic classification literature focusing on user-centric perspectives. Particularly relevant are works on fairness across user groups. Hu et al.
[
34
]
address unequal outcomes in terms of misclassification and Milli et al.
[
50
]
study unequal outcomes in terms of effort to manipulate one’s features. Both papers, including
[
23
]
examine interventions that the learner could take and how that impacts outcomes for different user groups. By contrast, we present a
user
intervention, show that altruistic users are incentivized to act in this manner, and that no user is made worse-off. We consider the setting where altruistic agents strategize collectively via a coordinating mechanism which may not know everything about the learner and participating agents. Hence a related thread of literature analyzes when agents have incomplete information about the classifier, e.g., because it is purposefully withheld or too complicated for everyday people to understand. Bechavod et al.
[

study the setting where information is inferred and shared within sub-populations, others assume agents have knowledge of a distributional prior
[

. In contrast to these papers, which study incomplete knowledge of the agents on the classifier, we consider incomplete information of a coordinating mechanism regarding both the participating strategic agents and the learner.

Collaborative filtering and matrix completion.

In this methodology, since users rarely rate or view all available items estimates of user’s preferences for unseen items depend on their past ratings and the ratings of other users
[
60
,
45
,
62
]
.
Formally, this can be viewed as a matrix completion problem where the matrix represents user-item ratings (e.g., stars or likes). Without any knowledge of the matrix properties, this problem is impossible: the remaining entries could be anything. Consequently, ratings matrices are commonly assumed to be low-rank.
A series of papers provide bounds on the number of random matrix entries required to perfectly recovery low-rank matrices that also satisfy some coherence conditions (which loosely tell us how informative ratings are about one another)
[
11
,
10
,
57
]
.
Other papers study extensions of this problem: when there is noise
[
9
,
43
]
and when recovery is online and sampling is active
[
1
,
39
]
.
Our learner protocol is a tractable abstraction of matrix completion-based recommendation by directly considering the problem of low-rank approximations of completed preferences matrices.

Matrix completion in recommendation systems is particularly relevant to our use of rank reduction for the learner. Matrix factorization
[
44
]
approximates ratings as dot products of low-dimensional user and item embeddings. When the objective is to minimize squared estimation error, it is equivalent to PCA. Nuclear norm minimization
[
10
]
, is a convex relaxation of the rank function and in some cases provably recovers the rank-minimizing solution subject to agreement with known entries
[
58
]
.

Many of the common techniques such as matrix factorization
[
44
]
and nuclear norm minimization
[
10
]
rely on the assumption that the matrix is low-rank. implicitly assuming that preference data may be represented in a low-dimensional latent space. If he does not receive ratings certain key user/item pairs (because of extremely disparate user preferences or bad luck, for example) that prove a user/item (i.e. row or column) is linearly independent from others, he will likely assume users/items are more similar than they really are. We point the reader to appendix section
C.1
for some technical results. Studying this problem through an explicitly matrix factorization lens, however, is difficult in generality. Thus our model represents this by first having a fully completed matrix and then requiring some level of rank reduction.

C.1
Theoretical connections to matrix completion

Realistically, the recommender learns (potentially non-deterministic) user preferences over rounds in which users are served items and provide ratings (likes, comments, watchtimes, etc). Over these rounds, he may fill in the preference matrix with estimates how much each user likes the shown item.
Because there are often a large number of missing entries in the full ratings matrix, assuming a simpler structure for the ratings matrix is necessary to create estimates of the remaining entries. A common method in collaborative filtering is matrix completion. While there are many methods and optimization problems used in practice such as matrix factorization
[
44
]
, here we discuss the connection of low-rank approximation to a specific method that has been extensively in the literature: nuclear norm minimization subject to agreement with observed entries
[
10
,
57
,
11
]
.

In particular, we assume that
\mathbf{R}
is some unknown low-rank matrix which we want to recover. Define
\Omega
to be the set of
(u,i)
pairs that have been observed so that
(u,i)\in\Omega
if user
u
has seen item
i
. Formally,

\Omega:=\{(u,i)\in[m]\times[n]:r_{u,i}\text{ has been observed}\}.

If we know that
\mathbf{R}
is the only rank-
d
matrix that agrees with observed entries then the following rank-minimization problem will return

\displaystyle\text{minimize}_{\bf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}(X)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle x_{u,i}=r_{u,i}\quad\forall(u,i)\in\Omega.

However, this problem is NP-hard. Consequently, the aforementioned papers consider the nuclear norm,
\|\cdot\|_{*}
which is the sum of the singular values and solve the following problem instead:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}_{\bf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}

\displaystyle\|X\|_{*}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle x_{u,i}=r_{u,i}\quad\forall(u,i)\in\Omega.

The nuclear norm is a proxy for rank in the same way that the
L_{1}
norm is a proxy for the
L_{0}
norm of a vector and can be minimized via semi-definite programming. And, when singular values are at most
1
, it is the best convex lower approximation of the rank function
[
58
]
.

In our paper, the
\alpha

\alpha
). The nuclear norm minimization problem makes this sum as small as possible. Therefore, it tends to “smooth” out potential discrepancies in observed (or unobserved) entries. To give an example of this, we will now discuss a similar result to Theorem

on the recovery of minority preferences.

Let

\cal{D}
with constant

Proof.
Consider two arbitrary points

. If
f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})\geq f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{1})

\displaystyle|f(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f(\mathbf{z}_{2})|

\displaystyle=|f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-\min\{f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{2}),f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{2})\}|

\displaystyle=|f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})+\max\{-f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{2}),-f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{2})\}|

\displaystyle=|\max\{f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{2}),f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{2})\}|

\displaystyle\leq|\max\{f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{2}),f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{2})\}|

\displaystyle\leq\max\{|f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{1}(\mathbf{z}_{2})|,|f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{1})-f_{2}(\mathbf{z}_{2})|\}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{L_{1}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|,L_{2}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{L_{1},L_{2}\}\|\mathbf{z}_{1}-\mathbf{z}_{2}\|_{2}

Appendix F

In this subsection, we will derive analogous social welfare results for a more complicated class of preference matrices and altruistic strategies than those which are in the main body of this manuscript. This preference matrix class will not be limited to majority minority groups with exclusive preferences and strategic altruism is not limited to uprating. Because of these complexities, the proof techniques necessary will be significantly different than in the main body, but the majority of results will have analogies. Additionally, we have not been able to provide a simple algorithm for the computation of an altruistic strategy as we do in the simpler case.

items.
Define
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
to be
\max_{i^{\prime}\in\{(\bar{n}+1),\dots,n\}}\|\mathbf{R}_{i^{\prime}}\|_{1}
, the greatest L-1 norm for any unpopular item. The
\mathbf{R}
matrix but with the preferences for unpopular items zeroed out will be important for the remainder of our analysis, so we define this as follows.

Definition F.1
(Popular Preferences Matrix,
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})
)

Let
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
be a matrix s.t.:

r^{\prime}_{u,i}=\begin{cases}r_{u,i},&\text{if $i\leq\bar{n}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Thus
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})
is a block matrix where
\mathbf{A}({\bar{n}})\in[0,1]^{m\times\bar{n}}
is the popular item block of
\bf{R}
.:

\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}({\bar{n}})&\mathbf{0}^{m\times(n-\bar{n})}\end{pmatrix}

(6)

Because the learner is interested in recovering a best item for each user, we shall define a set of top item(s) for a user
u
with respect to the matrix,
\mathbf{R}
:

Definition F.2
(User
u
’s Top Item(s))

Define
\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)
to be a set of top items for a user
u
according to preference matrix
\mathbf{R}
:

\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u):=\mathrm{arg}\max_{i\in[n]}r_{u,i}

Note that
|\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)|\geq 1
.

We shall define user groups based on whether a user’s top item(s) is(are) popular or unpopular:

Definition F.3
(Majority User)

A majority user for a particular
\bf{R}
preference matrix and
\bar{n}
is one who has top rated item
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)
, such that
i\leq\bar{n}
, meaning
i
is one of the popular items. Formally, we define the set of majority users for a particular
\bf{R}
preference matrix:

\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}:=\{u:\exists i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)\quad s.t.\quad i\in[\bar{n}]\}

Definition F.4
(Minority User)

A minority user for a particular
\bf{R}
preference matrix and
\bar{n}
is one who has item
i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)
, such that
i>\bar{n}
, meaning
i
is one of the unpopular items. Formally, we define the set of minority users for a particular
\bf{R}
preference matrix:

\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}:=\{u:\exists i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)\quad s.t.\quad i\in\{(\bar{n}+1),\dots,n\}\}

For some results, it will be useful to make assumptions that a tuple,
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})
is such that there exist nonempty user majority/minority groups and they are exclusive.

Assumption F.1
(Minority/Majority User assumptions)

Preference matrix
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{n}
is such that the following is true of majority and minority user groups:

1.

Majority and Minority user sets are exclusive:

\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}=\emptyset

2.

There is at least one minority user:

\forall m:|\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}|>0

Remark F.1

Note that the majority/minority exclusivity of assumption
F.1
is a weaker assumption than the exclusivity assumption in the main body of the paper as assumption
F.1
exclusivity does not imply that majority and minority users’ preferences are entirely exclusive, only that their
top items
are exclusive. Formally:

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}},\forall i\in\{\bar{n}+1,\dots,n\},

\displaystyle i\notin\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)

\displaystyle\forall u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}},\forall i^{\prime}\in[\bar{n}],

\displaystyle i^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u^{\prime})

We will now construct a class of preference matrix,
\mathbf{R}
and popular item index
\bar{n}
tuples. In order to do this, we define the assumptions that tuples belonging to this class must satisfy. These assumptions will be about the difference between particular ratings of the preference matrix, so before proceeding we define
\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})
. This will have a similar function to the singular value gaps of the class of matrices used in the main body in that we will use
\Delta
to impose a gap in popularity between items.

Definition F.5
(Sufficient Ratings Gap,
\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})
)

The sufficient ratings gap is a function of the ratings of
\mathbf{R}
and which items are popular,
\bar{n}

\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}):=\frac{2^{\frac{5}{2}}\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}n^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\left[\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))\right]^{2}}

(7)

Assumption F.2
(Majority users’ top item(s) are sufficiently highly rated)

Majority users don’t care about all items equally:
[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)\neq\emptyset
and there is a sufficient gap between a majority user’s top rating (which may appear on multiple items) and her other ratings:

\max_{i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R},u)}r_{u,i}<\max_{i\in[n]}r_{u,i}-\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}

(8)

Assumption F.3
(Minority users have sufficient preference for a popular item)

Each minority user likes at least one popular item by a sufficient amount:

\exists i\in\bar{n}\quad s.t.\quad r_{u,i}>\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}

(9)

Note that because assumption
F.3
states that each minority user likes at least one popular item by some small amount, the majority-minority matrices discussed in the main body cannot satisfy this assumption as they are block matrices that impose complete exclusivity in preference. Thus, while the class we will construct here does not technically include matrices analyzed in the main body, the appendix class can be viewed as “more general” because it handles the settings in which preferences matrices do not have the
\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{min}},\mathbf{R}_{\textsc{maj}}
block structure that creates exclusivity between all items a majority user likes and all items a minority user likes.

One may consider assumption
F.3
as reminiscent of a non-zero support assumption for minority users’ preference over popular items while the main body imposes zero-support over the same space.

We can now define the key class of preference matrix and popular item index tuples that we will use for the remainder of the results in this section.

Definition F.6
(Popularity Gap Class,
\mathcal{M}
)

The following is an important class of tuples where the popular items (whose indices lies in
[\bar{n}]
) are sufficiently more highly rated by a variety of users than the unpopular items:

\mathcal{M}:=\{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}):\text{Assumption \ref{maj_users_high}, \ref{min_users_high} hold.}\}

(10)

Remark F.2
(The Meaning of Popularity)

We note that for any valid
\mathbf{R}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
and
\bar{n}
where assumptions
F.2
and
F.3
are true, it must be the case that the following is true:

2^{\frac{5}{4}}n^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}<\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))

(11)

Intuitively, equation
11
means that popular items (those in
[\bar{n}]
) are sufficiently well-liked by enough users such that their associated singular values are big relative to the magnitude of minority items’ ratings (whose l1-norms are upper bounded by
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
).

We call
\mathcal{M}
the “Popularity Gap Class” following the intuition detailed in remark
F.2
. That is, in order for the assumptions to be potentially satisfied, it must be the case that the singular value of the associated Popular Preferences Matrix,
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})
, dominates over the a function of the unpopular ratings. Much like the singular value gap assumption of the main body, ensuring that equation
11
holds essentially insures that the items labeled as popular by
\bar{n}
are actually mathematically popular.

F.2
Learner’s selection of optimal truncation rank

In the section above, we present a class of
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})
.
However, the
\alpha
-loss tolerant learner must approximate the received preference matrix,
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
, to an optimal rank less than or equal to
\mathrm{rank}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})
.
Given that the tuple
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, in this section we will show that
k^{*}=\bar{n}
for learners whose
\alpha
total variance loss budget is within a particular range.

F.3
Preliminary: useful singular value bounds on
\mathbf{R}
if
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}

Recall that
\alpha
-loss tolerant learners are defined in terms of how large the next singular value after truncation would be. Thus, it will be useful to have bounds on the singular values of
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})
.

Corollary F.1
(Corollary of Lemma
D.2
)

Let matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times(n-j)}
where
j<n
. Define
\sigma_{r}(\hat{\mathbf{A}})=0
for singular values lost to column deletion. Then the following relation of singular values holds:

\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{A})\geq\sigma_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\geq\sigma_{i+j}(\mathbf{A})

Proof.
This is easily seen by induction on
j
using the Horn and Johnson lemma as a
j=1
base case.

\square

Proposition F.1
(
\bar{n}
and
\bar{n}+1
singular value bounds)

If a tuple
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
then the singular values of
\mathbf{R}
satisfy the following relations:

\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R})\geq 2^{\frac{5}{4}}n^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}

\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\mathbf{R})\leq\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}

Proof.
The first inequality comes from the fact that assumptions
F.2
and
F.3
hold. Clearly
\frac{2^{\frac{5}{2}}\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}n^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\left[\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))\right]^{2}}\leq 1
if assumption
F.2
is true because otherwise the minimum difference between a top items and next ratings is greater than what
\mathbf{R}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
allows. This yields
\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}}))\geq 2^{\frac{5}{4}}n^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}
Because
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})
is the same as
\mathbf{R}
with the unpopular columns removed (and replaced with zeros, which does not affect singular values) we can invoke Corollary
F.1
to get the desired inequality in terms of
\mathbf{R}
.

Now we will show the second inequality. Define matrix
\mathbf{B}\in[0,1]^{m\times(n-\bar{n})}
to be matrix
\mathbf{R}
but where popular item columns have been removed. We have the following:

\displaystyle\sigma_{1+\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{B})\quad\text{Corollary \ref{relations_singular_vals}}

\displaystyle=||\mathbf{B}||_{2}\quad\text{Def of spectral norm}

\displaystyle\leq||\mathbf{B}||_{F}\quad\text{Matrix Norm Equivalences}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}

To get the last inequality, recall that
||\mathbf{X}||_{F}=\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})}
and note that

(\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B})_{i,j}\leq\max_{i\in[n-\bar{n}]}\mathbf{B_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\max_{i\in[n-\bar{n}]}||\mathbf{B_{i}}||_{1}=\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}

where
\mathbf{B_{i}}
is the
i
th column vector of
\mathbf{B}
.
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})
must thus be upper bounded by
(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
because there are
n-\bar{n}
diagonal elements of
\mathbf{B}^{\top}\mathbf{B}
each upper bounded by
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
.

\square

F.3.1
A learner whose
k^{*}=\bar{n}

Intuitively, we have shown in the above preliminaries that if
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, then the
\bar{n}
-th singular value of
\mathbf{R}
must be relatively big while the next singular values must be quite small. This should mean that retaining singular values 1 through
\bar{n}
is “important” while the remainder of the singular values do not contribute very much.

Definition F.7
(
\bar{n},\mathbf{R}
-Singular Value Gap)

For any
\mathbf{R}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
and
\bar{n}\in[n]
s.t.
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, define the space

\mathcal{G}(\bar{n},\mathbf{R}):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}:y\in\left(\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}},2^{\frac{5}{4}}n^{\frac{3}{4}}\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}\right)\}

Much like in the main body of the paper, a learner whose
\alpha
parameter falls into this gap will select to truncate exactly to dimension
\bar{n}
. Formally:

Proposition F.2
(
k^{\star}=\bar{n}
for the
\alpha
-loss tolerant learner)

For all
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
such that
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
,
If the
\alpha
loss tolerant learner is such that
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\bar{n},\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})
,
then it must be the case that
k^{*}=\bar{n}
.

Proof.
By proposition
F.1
,
\sigma_{\bar{n}}>\alpha
while
\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}<\alpha
. By properties of singular values,
\sigma_{j}\geq\sigma_{\bar{n}}\forall j\leq\bar{n}
, thus
\bar{n}
is the minimum
k
such that
\sigma_{k+1}<\alpha

\square

Remark F.3
(
\mathcal{G}(\bar{n},\mathbf{R})
is nonempty)

Note that for any
\bar{n}\geq 1
,
2^{\frac{5}{4}}n^{\frac{3}{4}}>\sqrt{n-\bar{n}}
therefore the space
\mathcal{G}(\bar{n},\widetilde{\mathbf{R}})
is not empty for any reasonable tuple.

Obviously, this range will limit the type of learners we discuss, however it is important to note that it is always nonempty (see remark
F.3
) and for large
n
, this range for
\alpha
is also very big, therefore this is a non negligible space of general
\alpha
-loss tolerant learners.

F.4
Recommendations and top-1 social welfare when
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}
s.t.
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}

We will derive the recommendations made and resulting social welfare when the received preference matrix is such that
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
and the
\alpha
-loss tolerant learner is parametrized such that
k^{\star}=\bar{n}
.

F.4.1
Preliminary: SVD truncation error bounds

First we will remind the reader of an equivalent representation of truncated SVD and derive a useful lemma to upper bound the approximation error.

Recall that for a preference matrix,
\mathbf{R}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
, a
k^{\star}
-truncated SVD approximation is equivalent to solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\text{minimize}_{\mathbf{\Pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Pi}\|^{2}_{F}

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Pi}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k^{\star}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{k^{\star}}

Where
\mathbf{I}_{k^{\star}}
is a
k^{\star}
-dimensional identity and clearly
\mathbf{\Pi}
is a projection matrix.

We can define
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\star}
where
\mathbf{\Pi}^{\star}
is the minimizer and this
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is equivalent to the
k^{\star}
-truncated SVD. We can derive a bound on how close the optimal projection matrix,
\mathbf{\Pi}^{\star}
, is to
\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}
, a “partial” identity matrix where only the first
\bar{n}
diagonal elements are
1
s. Functionally, because
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\star}
, this will be an upper bound on how close
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}
is to just being the first
\bar{n}
columns of
\mathbf{R}
with the remaining columns zeroed out. We note that this bound (and its proof) is a version of Theorem 1 from
[
47
]
.

Proposition F.3

Let
\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}_{\bar{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the optimal projection operator of
\mathbf{R}
to its
\bar{n}
-truncated SVD. Assume that
\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))>0
. We have the following:

||\mathbf{\Pi}^{*}_{\bar{n}}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}||_{F}\leq\frac{\Delta(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}{2\sqrt{n}},

(13)

where
\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}
is a
n\times n
matrix where the first
\bar{n}
diagonal entries are
1
and all other entries are
0
.

In order to show this, we will invoke a well-known matrix theory result that we define as a lemma and prove for completeness below. As a preliminary, recall from matrix analysis that between two matrices, we may compare their subspaces using
principal angles
. In particular, between two matrices,
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\bar{n}}
made up of orthonormal columns, the vector of principal angles is
\mathbf{d}:=(\cos^{-1}\sigma_{1},\dots\cos^{-1}\sigma_{\bar{n}})
where
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
th singular value of
\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
. We will denote
\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}):=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{d})
.

Lemma F.1

Let
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times\bar{n}}
be matrices with orthonormal columns.

\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}\|_{F}.

Proof.
Let
\mathbf{\Pi}:=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}
and
\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime}:=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}
notice that these are projection matrices.

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{\Pi}-\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbf{\Pi}-\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})^{\top}(\mathbf{\Pi}-\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\text{def of Frobenius norm}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{\Pi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Pi}+\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime}-\mathbf{\Pi}^{\top}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime}-\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{\Pi}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{2})+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime 2})-\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})-\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Pi})

\displaystyle\quad\text{projection symmetric, trace linear}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{2})+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime 2})-2\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\text{trace cyclic}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi})+\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})-2\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\text{projection idempotent}

\displaystyle=2\bar{n}-2\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{\Pi}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad\text{trace of projection = rank}

\displaystyle=2\bar{n}-2\mathrm{Tr}\left((\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})^{\top}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=2\bar{n}-2\sum_{i\in[\bar{n}]}\cos^{2}(d_{i})

\displaystyle\quad\sigma_{i}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\mathbf{U}^{\prime})=\cos(d_{i})

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{i\in[\bar{n}]}1-\cos^{2}(d_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{i\in[\bar{n}]}\sin^{2}(d_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\text{trig identity}

\displaystyle=2\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}^{2}

Taking square root and dividing by
\sqrt{2}
on both sides gives the desired identity.

\square

Proof of Proposition
F.3
.

Let
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})
, thus
\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C}^{\prime}

\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Let
\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\bar{n}}
be matrices whose columns correspond to the
\bar{n}
normalized eigenvectors of the
\bar{n}
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{C},\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
.

We will complete this proof by going through the following claims:

1.

\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}=\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}

2.

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}=\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}

3.

\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}n\kappa}{[\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))]^{2}}

For some notational cleanliness, for this proof, we will refer to
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
as simply
\kappa
.

Claim F.1

\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}=\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}

where
\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}
is a
n\times n
matrix where the first
\bar{n}
diagonal entries are
1
and all other entries are
0
.

Proof of Claim
F.1
.

Notice that because

\mathbf{R}^{\prime}({\bar{n}})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}({\bar{n}})&\mathbf{0}^{m\times(n-\bar{n})}\end{pmatrix}

(14)

We have that:

\mathbf{C}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}&0&\ldots&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&0\end{pmatrix}

(15)

Where
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{n}\times\bar{n}}
.

Notice that
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
is a symmetric
\bar{n}\times\bar{n}
matrix, thus it has an orthonormal eigenbasis. Consider orthonormal matrix
\mathbf{V^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{n}\times\bar{n}}
s.t. that it’s columns are eigenvectors of
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
. By orthonormality,
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}\mathbf{V}^{\prime\top}=\mathbf{I}_{\bar{n}}
. Define
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\bar{n}}
such that it is
\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
padded with zeros. Clearly,
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}
is a normalized matrix of the
\bar{n}
eigenvectors corresponding to the
\bar{n}
largest eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}^{\prime}
, therefore
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}
is simply
\tilde{\mathbf{V}}^{\prime}
. Thus we can see that
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}=\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}
.

\square

Claim F.2

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}=\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}

Proof of Claim
F.2
.

By Claim
F.1
,

\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}.

Further, since
\bf{U},\bf{U}^{\prime}
are composed of normalized orthogonal columns, by Lemma
F.1
, we have that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}\|_{F}=\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}.

Put together, we have the desired equality:

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}^{\prime\top}\|_{F}=\|\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})\|_{F}.

\square

Claim F.3

\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}n\kappa}{(\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})))^{2}}

Proof of Claim
F.3
.

From Yu et al
[
66
]
Theorem 2 using
r=1
and
s=\bar{n}
, we have:

||\sin\Theta(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}^{\prime})||_{F}\leq\frac{2\min(\sqrt{\bar{n}}||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{op},||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{F})}{\min(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\lambda_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})-\lambda_{\bar{n}+1})}

where
\lambda_{0}=\infty
and
\lambda_{\bar{n}+1}=0
by construction. Note that by assumption,
\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}))>0
, so the denominator is well-defined.

Thus we have:

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{U}\mathbf{U}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}_{n,\bar{n}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\min(\sqrt{\bar{n}}||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{op},||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{F})}{\min(\lambda_{0}-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\lambda_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})-\lambda_{\bar{n}+1})}

\displaystyle\quad\text{Claim 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\min(\sqrt{\bar{n}}||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{op},||\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}||_{F})}{\min(\infty-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\lambda_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle\quad\text{by construction}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}}{\min(\infty-\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}),\lambda_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}}{\lambda_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{C}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}}{(\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})))^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\text{Def of singular value}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}n\kappa}{(\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})))^{2}}

Where the last inequality is as follows. Notice that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{R}^{\top}\mathbf{R}-\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})&\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})\\
\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})&\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})\\
\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})&\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})^{\top}\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{F}

Where
\mathbf{A}(\bar{n})\in[0,1]^{m\times\bar{n}}
are the popular columns of the preference matrix and
\mathbf{B}(\bar{n})\in[0,1]^{m\times(n-\bar{n})}
are the unpopular columns of the matrix.
Thus we can upper bound every element of
\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}

(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime})_{ij}\leq\max_{i>\bar{n}}\mathbf{R_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}=\max_{i>\bar{n}}\|\mathbf{R_{i}}\|_{1}=\kappa.

There are
n^{2}
elements in
(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime})

Therefore

\|\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n^{2}\kappa^{2}}\leq n\kappa.

\square

To reach the final statement of the theorem, notice that

creates the projection matrix,

recall that

\square

F.5

We are interested in whether and how agents who are in the majority defined by
(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})
could improve top-1 social welfare given the learner is
\alpha
-loss tolerant such that
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})

We consider altruistic misreporting on a truthful preference matrix,
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
. Much like altruism in the main body of the paper, this altruism transforms
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
into
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}

\bar{n}+1

\mathbf{r}^{\star}
is changed to any
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\in[0,1]^{m}

Remark F.4
(Minority item reordering)

Like the main body of the paper, we focus, WLOG, on item
\bar{n}+1
when it comes to altruism. Minority items can be reordered with no consequence.

Definition F.8
(General Altruistic Rating)

Consider a ground truth preference matrix
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
such that for some
\bar{n}
,
(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}

\bar{n}+1
th column vector,

\tilde{r}_{u}=r^{*}_{u,\bar{n}+1}\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}},\quad\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\in[0,1]^{m}

Such uprating will result in the learner receiving a strategically manipulated preference matrix,
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}\in[0,1]^{m\times n}
instead of the true matrix
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
.

Naturally, because the goal of manipulating ratings of item
\bar{n}+1
is to help minority users who like it, it will be important to establish that there exists enough [true] preference for item
\bar{n}+1
such that it is “worthwhile” manipulating.

Assumption F.5
(Manipulated item is sufficiently liked)

For a given
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\star},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, define
\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}:=\{u:u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}},(\bar{n}+1)\in\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)\}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}

\bar{n}+1
and assume
|\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}|\neq 0
.
Assume the following is true of ground truth preferences:

\exists\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that:

1.

For minority users whose top item is not
\bar{n}+1
, the variation of popular item ratings is not too large:

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}\max_{i\in[\bar{n}+1]}r^{\star}_{u,i}\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}\min_{i\in[\bar{n}+1]}r^{\star}_{u,i}+\delta

(20)

2.

The switch users like item
\bar{n}+1
sufficiently more than popular items:

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}r^{\star}_{u,(\bar{n}+1)}>\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}\max_{i\in[\bar{n}]}r^{\star}_{u,i}+\delta

(21)

Just like the main body of the paper, we shall now derive sufficient conditions on
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
in order to improve social welfare beyond the truthful baseline. Intuitively, these sufficient conditions will represent the following: altruistic rating increases the
\bar{n}+1

Because we want conditions for

\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}(\bar{n}+1))

\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}(\bar{n}+1)

Definition F.9

\bar{n}

\mathbf{R}^{\star}

Proposition F.4

\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})
.

The following are sufficient conditions on
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
(definition
F.8
) to ensure
\text{{SW}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\alpha)>\text{{SW}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\alpha)
:

1.

\alpha<\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{n}+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}(\bar{n}+1))

2.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}:\quad\tilde{r}_{u}<\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})

3.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}:\quad\max_{i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)}r^{\star}_{u,i}<\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})

4.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}:\quad\max_{i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)}r^{\star}_{u,i}<r^{\star}_{u,\bar{n}+1}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})

5.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}:\quad 0<\max_{i\in[\bar{n}+1]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})

Proof.
We shall break this proof into the following claims:

Claim F.7

\text{{SW}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\alpha)
is upper bounded by:

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}}\max_{i\in[\bar{n}]}r^{\star}_{u,i}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}}\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}

Claim F.8

The altruistically transformed matrix and
\bar{n}+1
index falls into the popularity gap class,
\mathcal{M}
. Formally:

(\tilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)\in\mathcal{M}

Claim F.9

\text{{SW}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\alpha)
is bounded from below by:

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}\min_{i\in[\bar{n}+1]}r^{*}_{u,i}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}}\max_{i\in[n]}r^{*}_{u,i}

and thus yields desired (strict) inequality.

Proof of Claim
F.7
.

By assumption,
(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})
thus
by proposition
F.2
, if the users were to submit preferences truthfully, the learner will reduce to rank
\bar{n}
. Thus, by proposition
F.4
:

\mathrm{arg}\max_{i\in[n]}\hat{r}_{u,i}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)\cap[\bar{n}]\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}

\mathrm{arg}\max_{i\in[n]}\hat{r}_{u,i}\subseteq[\bar{n}]\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}

Which directly yields the desired social welfare upper bound because majority users get their actual maximum value, while minority users cannot do any better than their maximum value amongst the popular items, which is strictly less than the actual maximum value over all items by assumption
F.1
.

\square

Proof of Claim
F.8
.

Recall that a preference matrix
\mathbf{R}
such that
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
looks like this:

\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{P}&\mathbf{U}\end{pmatrix}

(22)

Where
\mathbf{P}\in[0,1]^{m\times\bar{n}}
and
\mathbf{U}\in[0,1]^{m\times(n-\bar{n})}
are the matrices of popular and unpopular item ratings respectively.
Construct the following:

\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}&\mathbf{P}\end{pmatrix}

(23)

Where
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\in[0,1]^{m\times 1}
is the
\bar{n}+1
modified column vector of
\mathbf{R}^{\star}
(ie. the 1st column of
\mathbf{U}
) to represent majority users’ altruism. Thus
\mathbf{X}\in[0,1]^{m\times(\bar{n}+1)}
.
Let
\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}
. Thus we clearly have

\mathbf{A}=\begin{pmatrix}c&\mathbf{a}^{\top}\\
\mathbf{a}&\mathbf{M}\end{pmatrix}

Where:

1.

\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{P}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{n}\times\bar{n}}

2.

\mathbf{a}^{\top}:=\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}\in[0,1]^{1\times\bar{n}}

3.

c:=\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}

Note that the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}
would be the same as the squared nonzero singular values of
\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}({\bar{n}+1})
. Thus we can use the lower bound given by Lemma
D.1
to get a lower bound on
\bar{n}+1
th singular value of
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}({\bar{n}+1})
. From Lemma
D.1
:

\left[\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}({\bar{n}+1})\right]^{2}\geq\min(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{r}},\left[\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star^{\prime}}({\bar{n}}))\right]^{2})-||\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}||_{2}=\left[\hat{\sigma}_{\bar{n}+1}(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}(\bar{n}+1))\right]^{2}

Note that this means that our estimate of delta:

\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\geq\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)

So now that we’ve established that our estimate is an upper bound on the true
\Delta
our sufficient conditions clearly ensure that assumptions
F.2
,
F.3
would hold on
(\tilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)
using the real
\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)
. We write this out explicitly below:

Assumption
F.2
:
This holds because the users who will be the new majority under
(\tilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)
are now
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}
.

1.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}:\quad\tilde{r}_{u}<\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\leq\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)

2.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{maj}}:\quad\max_{i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)}r^{\star}_{u,i}<\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\leq\max_{i\in[n]}r^{\star}_{u,i}-\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)

3.

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}:\quad\max_{i\in[n]\setminus\mathcal{I}_{\textrm{top}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star},u)}r^{\star}_{u,i}<r^{\star}_{u,\bar{n}+1}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\leq r^{\star}_{u,\bar{n}+1}-\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)

Assumption
F.3
:
Minority users is slightly more subtle because on

the new minority group is
\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}
(recall that majority and minority groups are not necessarily exclusive unless stated). However, once again by construction, the properties
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}
satisfies ensure that assumption
F.3
is satisfied on all
u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{min}}\cup\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}

\forall u\in\mathcal{U}_{\textsc{switch}}:

Which automatically implies

0<r^{\star}_{u,\bar{n}+1}-\Delta(\tilde{\mathbf{r}};\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})\leq r^{\star}_{u,\bar{n}+1}-\Delta(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)

And then satisfaction of assumption

\tilde{\mathbf{r}}

Of course it is also the case that

Thus we have
(\widetilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)\in\mathcal{M}
as desired.

Proof of Claim
F.9
.

We shall now leverage the fact that

\alpha

\alpha\in(\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})}},\sqrt{\min(\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{r}},\left[\sigma_{\bar{n}}(\mathbf{R}^{\star^{\prime}}({\bar{n}}))\right]^{2})-||\tilde{\mathbf{r}}^{\top}\mathbf{P}||_{2}})

\alpha\in(\sigma_{\bar{n}+2}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}),\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}))

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}^{\prime}({\bar{n}+1}))

)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})

(Corollary

Now the RHS:

\displaystyle\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R}^{\star},\bar{n})}}

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\tilde{\mathbf{R}},\bar{n}+1)}}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\bar{n}+2}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}})

F.6
Extra results on
\mathcal{M}

While we have explained how the
\mathcal{M}
class of tuples represents matrices with a popularity gap, it is not intuitively clear exactly what combinations of
\mathbf{R}
and
\bar{n}
may work. Can one
\mathbf{R}
have multiple values of
\bar{n}
such that
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}),(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}
?
We can show conditions that, given
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
, for
\bar{n}^{\prime}>\bar{n}
,
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})\notin\mathcal{M}
. There are perhaps other interesting propositions about this class we leave to future work.

Proposition F.5
(Greater
\bar{n}
does not satisfy assumptions for
\mathcal{M}
)

Define

\underline{\kappa}_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}:=\min_{i^{\prime}\in\{(\bar{n}+1),\dots,n\}}||\mathbf{R_{i^{\prime}}}||_{1}

If a tuple
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})\in\mathcal{M}
and
\underline{\kappa}_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}>\frac{(n-\bar{n})\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}{4\sqrt{2}n\sqrt{n}}
then
\nexists\bar{n}^{\prime}\in\{\bar{n}+1,\dots,n\}
s.t.
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})
satisfies the assumptions of the previous subsection.

Proof.
Define
\bar{n}^{\prime}\in\{\bar{n}+1,\dots,n\}
,
\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{m\times\bar{n}^{\prime}}
to be the matrix
\mathbf{R}
, but with all columns
i>\bar{n}^{\prime}
set to be 0 vectors, and
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})}:=\max_{i^{\prime}\in\{(\bar{n}^{\prime}+1),\dots,n\}}||\mathbf{R_{i^{\prime}}}||_{1}
. We want to show that
\sigma_{\bar{n}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}(\bar{n}^{\prime}))<\sqrt{4\sqrt{2}n\sqrt{n}\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})}}
. If this is the case, it definitely cannot be true that
(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})
satisfies the assumptions of the previous subsection because it would require the difference between top and next rating to be greater than 1.

By corollary
F.1
and proposition
F.1
,
\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}^{\prime}})\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}(n-\bar{n})}
. Note that this is in terms of
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
and not in terms of
\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})}
. Using the asssumption, we have that:
\frac{\underline{\kappa}_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}(4\sqrt{2}n\sqrt{n})}{n-\bar{n}}>\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
. Thus we can write:

\sigma_{\bar{n}+1}(\mathbf{R}^{\prime}_{\bar{n}^{\prime}})\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}(n-\bar{n})}<\sqrt{4\sqrt{2}n\sqrt{n}\underline{\kappa}_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}}\leq\sqrt{4\sqrt{2}n\sqrt{n}\kappa_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n}^{\prime})}}

Where the last inequality follows because
\underline{\kappa}_{(\mathbf{R},\bar{n})}
is minimum.

\square
