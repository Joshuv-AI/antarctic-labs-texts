Assessing the toxicological effects of synthetic musk ketone using the Caenorhabditis elegans model
Authors: Jun-Tao Sun, Qi Zhao, Ying-Ying Ren, Tian-Yu Wang, Wen-Hua Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSynthetic musk ketone (MK) is extensively used in perfumes, cosmetics, and detergents. Due to its high lipophilicity, persistence, and limited removal efficiency in wastewater treatment, MK has been detected in various environmental matrices and human tissues. However, the ecotoxicity of MK remains poorly characterized. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the toxicological effects of MK and elucidate its underlying mechanisms using Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) as a model organism.MethodsWild-type C. elegans (strain N2) were exposed to MK at concentrations ranging from 0.4 to 20 mg/L for 24 h. Physiological endpoints including body size, locomotor activity, lifespan, and brood size were assessed. Biochemical markers comprising reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and lipofuscin accumulation were quantified. Acute heat-stress tolerance was evaluated to assess stress resistance. Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) were employed to investigate molecular mechanisms. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, and functional enrichment analyses were conducted for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways.ResultsMK exposure significantly and concentration-dependently reduced body length and width, locomotor activity (head thrash and body bend frequencies), brood size, and lifespan. Lipofuscin accumulation and intracellular ROS levels were markedly elevated. Acute heat-stress tolerance was significantly impaired. Transcriptomic analysis identified 159 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 2 mg/L MK (19 up-regulated, 140 down-regulated) and 136 DEGs at 20 mg/L MK (30 up-regulated, 106 down-regulated). Key DEGs included ugt-30 (detoxification), Y9C9A.16 (oxidation-reduction), msp-59 (spermatogenesis), and acdh-8 (lipid metabolism). GO enrichment revealed significant alterations in spermatogenesis, protein catabolism, and kinase activity. KEGG pathway analysis identified perturbations in sulfur metabolism, branched-chain amino acid metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and apoptosis. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified cyc-2.2 and acdh-8 as hub genes.ConclusionsMK exposure induces multi-faceted toxicity in C. elegans, including developmental impairment, neurotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, accelerated aging, and diminished stress resilience. These adverse outcomes are strongly associated with oxidative stress, germline impairment, and disrupted lipid metabolism, suggesting that these pathways play central roles in MK-induced toxicity. Our findings indicate that environmental exposure to MK may pose significant toxicological risks to living organisms and warrant further assessment of its ecological and health impacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40360-026-01220-1.Keywords: Musk ketone, Caenorhabditis elegans, Oxidative stress, Reproductive toxicity, RNA-seq

Introduction

The high cost of natural musk has led to its gradual replacement by synthetic musks, which are now extensively used in perfumes, cosmetics, and detergents. Over recent decades, global production of synthetic musks has increased annually and is projected to grow further. Structurally, synthetic musks are classified into four major classes: nitro musks, polycyclic musks, macrocyclic musks, and alicyclic musks [1]. Owing to their high lipophilicity and semi-volatility, these compounds adsorb strongly to sediments and soils, can bioaccumulate in organisms, and may volatilize into the atmosphere [2, 3]. As a result, synthetic musks have been detected at notable concentrations in various environmental matrices, including surface waters [4–6], soil [7], air [8], fish tissue [9], and even human tissues and breast milk [10].

Nitro musks, the first class of synthetic musks developed, are highly alkylated nitrobenzene derivatives and remain among the most widely used. The predominant nitro musks in current use are musk ketone (MK) and musk xylene (MX). Other members of this class include musk moskene (MM), musk tibetene (MT), and musk ambrette (MA). Studies have demonstrated that synthetic musks induce various toxic effects in organisms, including disruption of growth and development, endocrine disruption, neurotoxicity, genotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and phototoxicity [11]. In accordance with China’s Technical Specifications for Cosmetic Safety (2015 Edition), several nitro musks, such as MM, MT, and MA, have been prohibited due to their toxicity, while the use of MK and MX is also subject to specific regulatory restrictions.

As a stable synthetic musk, MK is resistant to natural photolysis and hydrolysis, leading to its persistence in the environment at relatively high concentrations. Approximately 77% of synthetic musks enter sewers and are transported to wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) [12]. Owing to their extensive use and the limited removal efficiency of conventional treatment processes (<90%), synthetic musks can reach concentrations of several tens of micrograms per liter in effluent, rendering WWTPs a major environmental emission source [13]. Environmental toxicants like MK can impair the growth and development of both humans and animals, leading to reduced physiological performance and anomalies. For instance, MK exhibits high toxicity to aquatic organisms; at sublethal concentrations with a 5-day EC₅₀ (half-maximal effective concentration) ranging from 0.03 to 0.16 mg/L, it strongly inhibits the development of Daphnia magna larvae [11]. In vitro studies have shown that MX, MK, or 6-acetyl-1,1,2,4,4,7-hexamethyltetralin (AHTN) can significantly enhance the proliferation of MCF-7 human breast cancer cells [14]. Furthermore, blood levels of nitro musks in mice have been negatively correlated with progesterone and associated with weak estrogenic activity, while chronic exposure significantly increased tumor incidence [15]. In Hep G2 cells, mutagenic and co-mutagenic effects were observed following exposure to MK in a micronucleus assay. Furthermore, MK was found to potentiate the genotoxicity of benzo[a]pyrene (BaP), a well-known environmental carcinogen widely present in cigarette smoke, automobile exhaust, and grilled foods [16]. This suggests that human exposure to MK may increase susceptibility to the health hazards posed by BaP and other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Consequently, detailed investigations into the toxicological effects of MK are urgently needed.

The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) serves as an excellent model organism in toxicological research. The free-living, non-parasitic nematode C. elegans is approximately 1 mm in length as an adult. Its adult hermaphrodite form comprises 959 somatic cells, and the male form comprises 1,031. Under standard laboratory conditions, C. elegans feeds on Escherichia coli and develops from egg to adult in 3–5 days. The biology of C. elegans is exceptionally well characterized, with its complete somatic cell lineage known and its entire complement of 302 neurons, including their positions and synaptic connections, fully mapped [17, 18]. Owing to its fully sequenced genome, well-described developmental biology, and high sensitivity to environmental stressors, C. elegans is widely established as a valuable alternative model for assessing toxicity in higher animals [19, 20].

To evaluate the toxicity of MK, we exposed C. elegans to a concentration range of 0.4–20 mg/L for 24 h. A suite of physiological, biochemical, and molecular analyses was then conducted. Key phenotypic endpoints quantified included: body size, lifespan, locomotor activity, brood size, and the biomarkers of oxidative stress (ROS) and aging (lipofuscin). These assessments were designed to determine the impact of environmentally relevant MK concentrations. To elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and quantitative real-time reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) were employed. These findings will provide a crucial scientific foundation for assessing the toxicological risks posed by MK.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and organisms

A stock solution of MK, (≥98% purity, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.) was prepared at 200 mg/L by dissolving the compound in 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; ≥99.8%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.). Working solutions were freshly diluted from this stock to the desired concentrations immediately prior to exposure.

The wild-type C. elegans strain N2 (Bristol) was acquired from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC). Nematodes were cultured and maintained on standard nematode growth medium (NGM) plates seeded with uracil-deficient Escherichia coli OP50, which was prepared by Southeast University.

Sample preparation

Synchronization of C. elegans

A synchronized population of C. elegans was obtained by standard hypochlorite treatment. Gravid adult hermaphrodites were collected and washed with M9 buffer (22 mM KH₂PO₄, 42 mM Na₂HPO₄, 86 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgSO₄). After settling for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the nematodes were washed two additional times with fresh M9 buffer. For egg isolation, the pellet of adult C. elegans was treated with 0.5 mL of 1 M NaOH and 0.5 mL of freshly prepared 10% (w/v) sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) solution and vortexed vigorously for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant containing the lysed adult tissue was discarded. The pelleted eggs were then washed twice with 1 mL of M9 buffer. The eggs were resuspended in 1 mL of M9 buffer, and transferred to a 35-mm Petri dish containing 2 mL of M9 buffer for hatching. The dish was incubated at 20 °C for 12 h to obtain a synchronized population of first-stage larvae (L1). The synchronized L1 larvae were transferred to NGM plates seeded with uracil-deficient E. coli OP50. Plates were then incubated at 20 °C for 48 h to allow development to the synchronized young adult (L4) stage.

Chemical exposure

Synchronized L4-stage nematodes were harvested from the plates using M9 buffer and transferred onto fresh NGM plates. The nematodes were briefly air-dried to remove excess M9 buffer. They were then incubated in solutions containing MK at specified concentrations (0.4, 0.8, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20 mg/L) at 20 °C for 24 h. The control group consisted of nematodes treated with 0.1% DMSO (the solvent used for MK dissolution) [21]. The final DMSO concentration in all treatment groups and the control was 0.1% (v/v). Published evidence has shown that DMSO up to 0.5% does not significantly affect C. elegans physiology under standard conditions. Thus, 0.1% DMSO served as an appropriate vehicle control, with any solvent effects uniformly present and neutralized across groups.

Image analysis for morphometry and lipofuscin quantification

After the 24 h exposure, nematodes from each treatment group (n = 15 per group, from three independent biological replicates) were harvested and transferred to 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes. The tubes contained M9 buffer to rinse the nematodes, and the nematodes were allowed to settle for 5–6 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was carefully aspirated. the nematodes were first immobilized by adding 20 µL of 5 mM levamisole hydrochloride, then transferred and mounted onto microscope slides containing agarose pads (2%, w/v). Imaging was performed using an inverted fluorescence microscope to document morphology and fluorescence signals. Body length and width were measured in bright-field images using ImageJ software (version 1.54f; National Institutes of Health, USA). Lipofuscin accumulation, detected as intestinal autofluorescence, was quantified by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity within the intestinal region using ImageJ software. For both morphometric and lipofuscin analyses, 15 nematodes per treatment group were analyzed across three independent biological replicates.

Mortality and longevity

After the 24 h exposure, nematodes were washed with M9 buffer (three times) and transferred to new NGM plates containing E. coli OP50. They were then cultured at 20 °C under standard conditions. After 24 h, the nematodes were transferred to fresh NGM plates to isolate them from their offspring. The number of dead nematodes was recorded daily. Nematodes were scored dead if they failed to respond to gentle tactile stimulation with a platinum wire. For the lifespan assay, 30 age-synchronized L4 nematodes per group were used, with three independent biological replicates (total n = 90 per group), and the assay continued until all nematodes died.

Head-thrash and Body-Bend Frequencies

After the 24 h exposure, nematodes were washed with M9 buffer (three times) to remove residual chemicals. They were then placed on unseeded NGM plates for subsequent observation or assay. Nematodes were briefly air-dried to remove excess M9 buffer. Subsequently, their locomotor activity was assessed by stereomicroscope (Stemi 508, ZEISS).

Body bend frequency was quantified by counting the number of complete sinusoidal waves along the body axis during a 20-s observation period. Head thrash frequency was measured as follows. First, 200 µL of M9 buffer was added to the culture plate. After a 1-min acclimation period, the number of complete lateral head oscillations (from one side to the other and back) was counted over a 20-s interval. For both head-thrash and body-bend frequency measurements, 15 nematodes per group were assayed, with three independent biological replicates (total n = 45 per group). Each individual was scored only once.

Quantification of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS levels were determined using the fluorescent probe 2‘,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime Biotechnology, S0033S). After the 24 h exposure, nematodes from each treatment group were collected into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and allowed to settle in M9 buffer. The supernatant was removed, and the nematodes were washed three times with fresh M9 buffer by gravity sedimentation. The washed nematodes were incubated in 100 µL of 10 µM DCFH-DA working solution at 37 °C for 3 h in the dark, with gentle hourly agitation to ensure uniform dye exposure. After the incubation period, the DCFH-DA staining solution was removed. The nematodes were subjected to five washes with M9 buffer to remove residual probe. Finally, the nematodes were resuspended in a small volume of M9 buffer and mounted on a 2% agarose pad for microscopic imaging. Fluorescence images were captured using a fixed exposure time under a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, BX51). The mean fluorescence intensity per nematode was determined using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). For ROS quantification, 15 nematodes per treatment group were analyzed across three independent biological replicates (total n ≥ 45 per group).

Determination of acute heat stress

The acute heat-stress assay was performed as previously described [22], with minor modifications. After chemical exposure, the nematodes were rinsed three times with M9 buffer. The nematodes were then transferred to NGM plates pre-equilibrated at 37 °C. The plates were maintained at 37 °C, and nematode survival was assessed every hour. Nematodes were scored as dead if they showed no response to a gentle mechanical stimulus applied with a platinum wire. The assay was terminated when all nematodes in a plate had died. For the heat-stress assay, 30 age-synchronized young adult nematodes per group were used, with three independent biological replicates (total n = 90 per group).

Assessment of reproductive toxicity

To assess chronic toxicity, age-synchronized L1 larvae were transferred to NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50. Plates were first air-dried briefly to allow excess moisture to evaporate. They were then treated with 200 µL of MK solution at the appropriate concentration (ranging from 0 to 20 mg/L) and incubated at 20 °C for 48 h. After exposure, nematodes were rinsed with M9 buffer and transferred to food-free NGM plates to allow for gut clearance. To measure daily fecundity, individual young adult nematodes were transferred each day to fresh NGM plates using a stereomicroscope. Plates containing the laid eggs were incubated at 20 °C for a further 24 h. Following this incubation, the total number of eggs per plate was counted under a stereomicroscope. The brood size per nematode was defined as the total number of eggs produced over its reproductive lifespan. Brood size was assessed using 10 individual nematodes per treatment group, with three independent biological replicates (total n = 30 per group).

The effects of MK on germline development were examined by chronic exposure. Nematodes were exposed from the L1 stage to the young adult stage using the protocol described for chronic toxicity testing. After exposure, nematodes from each group were rinsed with M9 buffer and allowed to recover on fresh NGM plates for 24 h. The nematodes were collected and transferred to 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes. They were then washed twice with M9 buffer and finally immobilized by adding 20 µL of 5 mM levamisole hydrochloride. The immobilized nematodes were mounted on microscope slides with 2% (w/v) agarose pads for imaging and observed using an integrated fluorescence microscope (Keyence BZ-X800, China). Oocyte morphology was assessed, and the length of the −2 oocyte (the second most proximal oocyte to the spermatheca) was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). For oocyte number and −2 oocyte length measurements, 10 nematodes per group were analyzed across three independent biological replicates (total n = 30 per group). Germline dissection and analysis were performed as described previously [23]. Representative differential interference contrast (DIC) images were captured from at least 5 nematodes per group to document germline morphology.

Transcriptome-based gene-expression Analysis

Based on the observed phenotypic alterations, three experimental groups were selected for transcriptomic profiling: the blank control (0.1% DMSO), 2 mg/L MK, and 20 mg/L MK. Total RNA was extracted from C. elegans samples, followed by mRNA enrichment using oligo(dT) magnetic beads. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using random primers and reverse transcriptase. The RNA template was digested with RNase H. Subsequently, the second cDNA strand was synthesized in a reaction containing DNA polymerase I, dNTPs, and reaction buffer. The double-stranded cDNA product was purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) and then fragmented to an average size of 250–300 bp. The cDNA libraries were constructed through sequential steps of end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification.

The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding 150-bp paired-end reads. The raw reads underwent quality control and adapter trimming. This preprocessing step produced high-quality clean data suitable for subsequent analysis. High-quality clean reads were then aligned to the C. elegans reference genome (Ensembl release 108, assembly WBcel235) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Gene expression levels were quantified as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) using StringTie (v2.1.4). We identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by applying the DESeq2 R package, setting thresholds at |log₂(fold change)| >1 and an adjusted *p*-value < 0.05.

We performed Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses on the set of DEGs. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed for the DEGs enriched in significant KEGG pathways, using the STRING database (version 11.0) [24]. The constructed networks were imported into Cytoscape software (version 3.7.1) for visualization and topological analysis Within Cytoscape [25], the cytoHubba plugin was employed to identify hub genes based on network connectivity metrics [26].

To validate the transcriptomic results, the expression of selected target genes was analyzed by qRT-PCR. Gene-specific primers (See Table S2) were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool. Their sequences are listed in Table S2. Total RNA isolation was performed with TRIzol Reagent. From 1 µg of the extracted total RNA, first-strand cDNA was then synthesized in accordance with the instructions of the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa). qRT-PCR was conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 4 µL of diluted cDNA template, and 5.2 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 seconds, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 seconds. All reactions were performed in triplicate. The act-1 gene was used as an internal reference for normalization, and Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

For RNA-seq analysis, three independent biological replicates per treatment group were used. This sample size represents the minimum requirement for DESeq2 differential expression analysis; accordingly, DEGs with marginal significance (adjusted p-value close to 0.05 or |log₂FC| close to 1.0) should be interpreted with caution.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent biological replicates. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated by Levene’s test. For data meeting parametric assumptions, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test for post hoc multiple comparisons. When variances were heterogeneous, Welch’s ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc test was applied. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used with Dunn’s test for pairwise comparisons.

Lifespan and heat-stress survival data were analyzed by the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

For transcriptomic analysis, DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log₂(fold change)| >1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler with hypergeometric testing and FDR correction.

qRT-PCR data were analyzed by the 2-ΔΔCt method with act-1 as the reference gene. Statistical significance was set at * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp.), GraphPad Prism 9.0, and R 4.2.0.

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent biological replicates. The specific sample sizes (n) for each assay are detailed in the corresponding Methods sections and figure legends as follows: morphometric and lipofuscin analyses (n = 15 per group), locomotor behavior assays (n = 15 per group), ROS quantification (n = 15 per group), lifespan and heatstress assays (n = 30 per group), and reproductive toxicity assays including brood size, oocyte number, and oocyte length measurements (n = 10 per group). All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Results

Toxic effects of MK on the physiology of C. elegans

Exposure to MK for 24 h caused a concentration-dependent suppression of growth in wild-type N2 nematodes compared to the control. MK exposure progressively decreased both body length and width, with effects intensifying as concentration increased. Body length was significantly reduced starting from 2 mg/L (* p < 0.05) and more markedly at ≥10 mg/L (** p < 0.01) (Fig. 1A); body width was significantly reduced starting from 5 mg/L (*p < 0.05) and most markedly at 20 mg/L (** p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). The highest concentration tested (20 mg/L) caused reductions of 17.1% in body length and 26.9% in body width. In addition to growth inhibition, MK exposure also led to a marked increase in lipofuscin (age-pigment) accumulation. Representative fluorescence images (Fig. 2) visually showed increased intestinal lipofuscin autofluorescence in MK-treated nematodes; this observation was confirmed by quantitative analysis (Fig. 3), which revealed a significant increase in lipofuscin levels at concentrations as low as 1 mg/L (** p < 0.01). Lipofuscin accumulation reached its peak at 1 mg/L and subsequently plateaued or showed a mild decline at higher concentrations (2–20 mg/L), exhibiting a non-monotonic dose-response pattern.

Lifespan defects caused by MK

Survival curves analysis showed that MK exposure affected nematode lifespan in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4). At low concentrations (0.8 mg/L), survival curves largely overlapped with the control group (Fig. 4A), indicating no significant lifespan alterations at these doses. At 1 mg/L, a modest but non-significant reduction was observed. In contrast, when the exposure concentration reached 2 mg/L and above, survival curves shifted substantially downward and leftward (Fig. 4B), with the 20 mg/L group showing the most pronounced reduction. Consistently, quantitative analysis of lifespan indicators (Fig. 4C) showed that mean, median, and maximum lifespans were significantly shortened at ≥2 mg/L MK, with the maximum lifespan declining from ~20 days in controls to ~ 15 days at 20 mg/L. These results indicate that MK exhibits a threshold effect at approximately 1–2 mg/L for lifespan reduction in C. elegans.

Locomotion behavior assay

A 24 h exposure to MK led to a significant, concentration-dependent reduction in locomotor activity in C. elegans (Fig. 5). Both head-thrash and body-bend frequencies were markedly reduced. Head-thrash frequency was significantly reduced at MK concentrations ≥ 2 mg/L compared to the control (** p < 0.01, Fig. 5A). Specifically, the decreases were 7.0%, 11.2%, and 13.8% at concentrations of 2, 10, and 20 mg/L, respectively. Likewise, body-bend frequency was even more severely suppressed in a concentration-dependent manner. This frequency was also significantly reduced at concentrations of 2 mg/L and above (** p < 0.01, Fig. 5B), exhibiting declines of 13.5%, 21.6%, and 33.8% at 2, 10, and 20 mg/L, respectively. A clear dose-dependent suppression of motor activity by MK was thus observed.

ROS assay

MK exposure concentration-dependently elevated ROS levels in C. elegans (Fig. 6). Quantitative analysis confirmed that ROS levels were significantly elevated across all tested MK concentrations compared to the control (** p < 0.01). The increase displayed a dose-response relationship, rising progressively across lower concentrations before reaching a plateau at higher MK levels. Representative fluorescence images confirmed this dose-dependent trend, displaying markedly intensified ROS-associated fluorescence in MK-exposed nematodes (Fig. 7).

Acute heat-stress assay

MK exposure impaired acute heat stress tolerance in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 8B, C). Low concentrations of MK (0.4 and 0.8 mg/L) did not significantly affect thermotolerance compared to the control, while 1 mg/L showed a modest but non-significant reduction (Fig. 8A). In contrast, exposure to ≥2 mg/L MK significantly and progressively reduced survival under heat stress (Fig. 8B), with mean survival time decreasing from approximately 48 h in the control group to less than 20 h at 20 mg/L (Fig. 8C). These results suggest that MK compromises the organism’s ability to mount an effective defense against environmental stressors, with a threshold effect observed between 1 and 2 mg/L.

Toxic effects of MK on the reproduction of C. elegans

Chronic exposure to MK significantly and concentration-dependently impaired reproductive function in C. elegans (Fig. 9). In the control group, the mean brood size was 182.0 ± 5.7 eggs per nematode (mean ± SEM, n = 8, from three independent biological replicates). At 1 mg/L MK, the brood size decreased to 132.9 ± 7.2 (a reduction of 27.0%); at 5 mg/L, it decreased to 118.7 ± 9.7 (a reduction of 34.8%); and at 20 mg/L, it decreased to 114.0 ± 18.8 (a reduction of 37.4%) (Fig. 9A). The number of oocytes in one gonadal arm was significantly decreased upon MK exposure (* p < 0.05), with a highly significant reduction at higher concentrations (** p < 0.01) (Fig. 9B). Additionally, the length of the −2 oocyte was significantly shortened in exposed nematodes (* p < 0.05) (Fig. 9C). Upon microscopic examination, severe morphological abnormalities were observed in the germline following MK exposure (Fig. 10). Observed defects included aberrant oocyte development, distortion of the gonadal structure, and the absence of a clear vulva. Collectively, these morphological alterations indicate a severe disruption of reproductive system integrity.

RNA-Seq data quality assessment

It should be noted that three biological replicates per group is the minimum threshold for DESeq2-based differential expression analysis. Therefore, DEGs with borderline significance should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, all reported DEGs met the stringent criteria of |log₂FC| >1 and adjusted *p < 0.05.

We obtained high-quality transcriptomic sequencing data from the three experimental groups: control, 2 mg/L MK, and 20 mg/L MK. The average numbers of clean reads obtained were 46,599,849, 45,667,817, and 55,288,099, respectively. All libraries were of high sequencing quality (Table S1). The specific metrics were as follows: Q30 ≥ 94.88%, Q20 ≥ 98.31%, and GC content ranging from 46.3% to 46.83%.

Differential gene expression analysis

Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct gene expression profiles in C. elegans following MK exposure. Compared to the control, 159 DEGs were identified in the 2 mg/L group, comprising 19 up-regulated and 140 down-regulated genes. Notable up-regulated genes included ugt-30 (UDP-glycosyltransferase, log₂FC = 1.077, p = 0.033) and Y9C9A.16 (oxidoreductase, log₂FC = 1.345, p = 0.166, not significant), while key down-regulated genes included msp-59 (spermatogenesis-associated, log₂FC = −1.324, p = 0.0002) and acdh-8 (involved in lipid metabolism, log₂FC = −2.373, p = 0.0005). Exposure to 20 mg/L MK yielded 136 DEGs, comprising 30 up-regulated and 106 down-regulated. Prominent among the down-regulated genes were C02F12.5 (implicated in nervous and reproductive system functions) and cyp-25A1 (involved in heme/iron binding and monooxygenase activity). GO enrichment analysis revealed the biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions associated with MK-induced transcriptional changes (Fig. 12A–C). Key biological processes enriched included the negative regulation of ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism, spermatogenesis, pseudopod assembly regulation, and glutamate secretion. Regarding cellular components, enriched terms were primarily associated with structures like cellular protrusions, the cytoskeleton, membrane-free organelles, and the cytoplasm. Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the toxic effects of MK on C. elegans at physiological, biochemical, and molecular levels, and comprehensively analyzed its toxicological mechanisms. Body length and body width are recognized biomarkers of biological development and are used to quantitatively assess the developmental toxicity induced by exogenous substances in C. elegans. Relative to control groups, both parameters were significantly reduced in a concentration-dependent manner, at the highest tested concentration (20 mg/L), MK caused reductions of 17.1% in body length and 26.9% in body width (Fig. 1). Consistent with prior studies showing that synthetic musks (HHCB, AHTN) reduce body length in C. elegans [27] and that MK inhibits copepod development [11], our results demonstrated that MK significantly decreased both body length and width in a concentration-dependent manner. These morphological changes indicate that MK interferes with fundamental growth and developmental processes, potentially through disruption of cell proliferation or metabolic pathways essential for somatic growth.

MK has significant endocrine-disrupting activity, which can disrupt luteinizing hormone homeostasis [28], and has been confirmed to have reproductive toxicity in a variety of aquatic organisms, such as Daphnia magna [29] and Danio rerio [30]. Using C. elegans, which is an established model for reproductive toxicology owing to its transparent body wall and well-characterized gametogenesis, we systematically evaluated MK-induced reproductive toxicity. Our results demonstrated that chronic MK exposure concentration-dependently reduced brood size by up to 37.3% at 20 mg/L (Fig. 9A), significantly decreased unilateral oocyte number (Fig. 9B), and shortened −2 oocyte length (Fig. 9C), accompanied by severe germline morphological abnormalities including gonadal distortion and aberrant oocyte development (Fig. 10). Consistent with previous reports [30–32], these findings confirm that MK exerts potent reproductive toxicity in C. elegans. Importantly, the transcriptional down-regulation of spermatogenesis-associated genes such as msp-59 in our RNA-seq data (Section “Differential gene expression analysis”) provides molecular evidence supporting the observed reproductive deficits, suggesting that MK disrupts germ cell development at the transcriptional level Mortality and lifespan are the core indicators for evaluating the long-term toxicity of C. elegans [33]. In our study, MK exposure significantly shortened the mean lifespan in a concentration-dependent manner, with a 2-day reduction observed at 1 mg/L (* p < 0.05) and more pronounced effects at higher concentrations. Survival curve analysis revealed a clear threshold effect: at low concentrations (0.4 and 0.8 mg/L), survival curves largely overlapped with the control group (Fig. 4A), indicating no significant lifespan alterations at these doses. By contrast, exposure to ≥2 mg/L MK caused a substantial downward and leftward shift in survival curves (Fig. 4B), with the 20 mg/L group showing the most pronounced reduction. This lifespan shortening is consistent with the observed accumulation of lipofuscin (Fig. 3), an established biomarker of aging, suggesting that MK accelerates age-related physiological decline. Notably, no significant mortality was observed even at 200 mg/L, consistent with previous findings that high concentrations of synthetic musks (AHTN and HHCB) do not cause acute lethality in nematodes [27]. This dissociation between lifespan shortening and acute mortality indicates that MK primarily affects the rate of aging and physiological vitality rather than inducing immediate lethality, a pattern commonly observed with chronic low-dose toxicant exposure.

Lipofuscin is an aging marker, and its cross-linked polymer structure is difficult to degrade and excrete and Lipofuscin accumulates in lysosomes and cytoplasm with age, which presenting a brownish-yellow autofluorescence [34]. Recent advances in aging research have elucidated the complex genetic and epigenetic alterations that accompany the aging process, providing deeper insights into cellular senescence and age-related physiological decline [35]. After MK treatment, lipofuscin content increased significantly, peaking at 1 mg/L and then plateauing at higher concentrations (Fig. 3). This non-monotonic pattern is consistent with a hormetic dose response, wherein moderate stress triggers adaptive antioxidative defenses that transiently counterbalance oxidative damage, while higher concentrations may saturate or induce compensatory adjustments in deposition kinetics [36]. Our results demonstrated that MK-induced lipofuscin accumulation was indicative of heightened oxidative stress, influencing lifespan, lipid metabolism, and redox homeostasis in C. elegans. MK neurotoxicity in aquatic organisms has been confirmed [37]. Consistent with this, our results demonstrated that MK significantly inhibited head-thrash and body-bend frequencies in a concentration-dependent manner, with reductions of 13.8% and 33.8% at 20 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 5), confirming that MK induces neurobehavioral impairment in C. elegans.

The generation and clearance of ROS are regulated by multiple mechanisms, and their imbalance can alter the redox status of cells [38]. In our study, ROS levels increased significantly and concentration-dependently in MK-treated C. elegans (Fig. 6), confirming MK-induced oxidative stress. Previous studies show that nuclear DAF-16 expression, a downstream target of insulin signaling, enhances immunity and stress resistance in C. elegans [39]. DAF-16 functions as a key transcription factor that translocates to the nucleus under stress conditions to activate antioxidative enzymes and heat-shock proteins [40, 41]. In our study, MK-induced excessive ROS accumulation may overwhelm this DAF-16-mediated adaptive response, leading to insufficient activation of downstream protective genes and consequent decline in stress resistance. MK exposure significantly impaired acute heat stress tolerance and reduced mean survival time in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 8B, C). Notably, low concentrations (0.4 and 0.8 mg/L) did not significantly affect thermotolerance (Fig. 8A), suggesting that a threshold concentration (~1–2 mg/L) is required to compromise the heat stress response. This threshold effect is consistent with the observation that only higher MK concentrations (≥2 mg/L) significantly increased ROS levels and down-regulated stress-responsive genes. The progressive decline in survival at ≥2 mg/L (Fig. 8B) supports the interpretation that MK disrupts the DAF-16-regulated stress response network, as DAF-16 is essential for coordinating cellular defenses against thermal stress. The mean survival time data (Fig. 8C) further quantify this effect, showing a reduction from approximately 48 h in the control group to less than 20 h at 20 mg/L.

High-throughput transcriptomics can rapidly and comprehensively characterize the transcriptional changes of cells or tissues under different time and processing conditions. MK can significantly increase the activity, protein and mRNA levels of CYP2B in rats, and increases CYP1A activity ~30-fold over controls [42]. MK strongly induces hepatic enzymes in rats [43], which also induced cytochrome P450 isoenzymes in Hep G2 cells, indicating mutagenic potential [16]. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying MK toxicity in C. elegans, we performed transcriptomic analysis and identified 159 DEGs at 2 mg/L (19 up-regulated, 140 down-regulated) and 136 DEGs at 20 mg/L (30 up-regulated, 106 down-regulated) (Section “Differential gene expression analysis”). GO and KEGG analyses revealed that MK significantly interferes with fatty acid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, apoptosis, cytoskeletal assembly, kinase activity, and NADPH oxidase-mediated superoxide generation (Figs. 12 and 13). The hierarchical clustering of DEGs (Fig. 11) further supported these functional annotations: genes with similar expression patterns formed distinct clusters corresponding to detoxification responses (ugt-30, cyp-34A10, ntr-2), reproductive dysfunction (msp-59), and metabolic disruption (acdh-8, cpt-3). The clear separation of treatment groups in the column dendrogram confirmed that MK induces a dose-dependent transcriptional reprogramming, with the 20 mg/L group showing the most pronounced deviation from the control. Protein-protein interaction network analysis identified cyc-2.2 and acdh-8 as hub genes (Fig. 14). cyc-2 encodes a cyclin involved in cell cycle regulation and sexual reproduction [44], and its down-regulation may contribute to the observed germline developmental defects. The acdh-8 gene encodes acyl-CoA dehydrogenase 8 [45], which is essential for fatty acid metabolism and energy production; its dysregulation disrupts butyryl-carnitine (C4) metabolism in neonates [46], and its reduced expression likely underlies the observed disruptions in lipid metabolism and energy homeostasis. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism has been increasingly recognized as a critical factor in various disease states, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease and cancer metastasis [47]. Importantly, the transcriptional changes we identified—particularly those related to oxidative stress response, lipid metabolism, and apoptosis—are highly consistent with the observed phenotypic alterations including ROS elevation (Fig. 6), lipofuscin accumulation (Fig. 3), and impaired heat-stress tolerance (Fig. 8), providing molecular-level validation of our physiological findings.

This study demonstrates that MK induces concentration-dependent damage at the physiological, biochemical and molecular levels. Its toxicity mainly stems from the excessive generation and accumulation of ROS, leading to oxidative damage, down-regulation of key genes, metabolic disorders and homeostasis imbalance. Thus, elucidating MK toxicity in C. elegans illuminates its potential human hazard and provides new insights into the underlying mechanisms. It should be noted that 0.1% DMSO was used as the vehicle control in this study. Published evidence has shown that DMSO up to 0.5% does not significantly affect C. elegans physiology under standard conditions [21]. Moreover, because all treatment groups contained the same final DMSO concentration (0.1%), any potential solvent effect, if present, would be uniformly distributed across all groups and thus neutralized in intergroup comparisons. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the absence of a dedicated DMSO-only control as a limitation of the present work, and we will incorporate a complete control setup in future studies. Most importantly, this study provides a solid scientific basis for the safe use of MK.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that MK induces multi-faceted toxicity in C. elegans, with these effects being strongly associated with oxidative stress and subsequent molecular dysregulation. Acute exposure inhibited growth (body length/width) and locomotor behavior in a concentration-dependent manner, indicating developmental and neurotoxicity. Chronic exposure accelerated aging (shortened lifespan and increased lipofuscin), impaired stress tolerance, and caused severe reproductive defects, including reduced brood size, oocyte depletion, and germline malformations. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that these phenotypic outcomes stem from large-scale transcriptional disturbances. Key DEGs were enriched in pathways involving sulfur and amino acid metabolism, fatty acid β-oxidation, and apoptosis. Network analysis identified cyc-2.2 (cell cycle) and acdh-8 (fatty acid metabolism) as dose-specific hub genes. Mechanistically, MK-triggered oxidative stress (elevated ROS) disrupts metabolic homeostasis, cell-cycle regulation, and redox balance, ultimately converging on the observed deficits in growth, neuromuscular function, reproduction, and stress resilience. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for understanding MK toxicity and highlight its potential health and ecological risks, though further studies are needed to establish causal relationships at the molecular level.
