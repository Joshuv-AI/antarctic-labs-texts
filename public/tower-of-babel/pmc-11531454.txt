Immune checkpoint inhibitors-related thyroid dysfunction: influencing factor analysis, prediction model development, and management strategy proposal
Authors: Xinya Li, Zaiwei Song, Yixuan Chen, Jingjing Wu, Dan Jiang, Zhen Zhang, Zeyuan Wang, Rongsheng Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the extensive utilization of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across various cancers, ICIs-related thyroid dysfunction (ICI-TD) has become a growing concern in clinical practice. This study aimed to devise an individualized management strategy for ICI-TD to enhance the early identification and proactive management in cancer patients.MethodsWe designed and conducted a three-phase study. Initially, we analyzed the influencing factors through a systematic review and meta-analysis, which adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Moreover, the study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42019131133). Subsequently, prediction models for ICI-TD were developed utilizing 11 algorithms based on the real-world cohort data from July 20, 2018 (the approval date of the first ICIs, Pembrolizumab in China), to October 31, 2022. Considering discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, we selected the model with the best performance for web calculator development. Finally, individualized management strategies for ICI-TD were proposed by combining evidence-based analysis with practical considerations.ResultsThe systematic review encompassed 21 observational studies involving 4,145 patients, revealing associations between ICI-TD and factors such as female gender, age, receipt of Pembrolizumab (versus other ICIs), and baseline levels of thyroid-stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, and antithyroid antibodies. In the prediction model development phase, 621 participants were enrolled, with 36 patients developing ICI-TD. The model based on the LightGBM algorithm demonstrated superior performance, leading to the development of a web calculator. Based on these findings and existing guidelines, individualized monitoring and treatment pathways for pharmacists were devised.ConclusionThis study offers comprehensive insights into managing ICI-TD, potentially enhancing tailored cancer immunotherapy management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-024-03816-0.

Introduction

In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a pivotal advancement in cancer immunotherapy, exerting antitumor effects by modulating the body’s immune response, targeting anti-programmed death receptor 1 (anti-PD-1), anti-programmed death ligand 1 (anti-PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte associate protein-4 (anti-CTLA-4) [1]. Since ipilimumab’s approval in 2011, ICIs have substantially revolutionized the treatment landscape across multiple cancer types, with approximately 40% of cancer patients eligible for ICI treatment [2, 3]. However, as ICIs activate T cells against malignant antigens, they may also prompt immune attacks on normal cells, leading to immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which have an increasingly troubled clinical practice, potentially resulting in treatment discontinuation, tissue damage, or even fatalities [4, 5].

Among the most prevalent irAEs are ICIs-related endocrinopathies, particularly ICIs-related thyroid dysfunction (ICI-TD), mainly encompassing hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, and thyroiditis, with a high incidence of 7.2%. Besides, ICI-TD can significantly disrupt treatment schedules, increase complications, prolong hospital stays, diminish life quality, and escalate medical costs, posing challenges for healthcare professionals and patients alike [6, 7]. Hence, early detection and effective management of ICI-TD are essential; however, its clinical manifestations are often nonspecific or asymptomatic, complicating timely identification, especially when overshadowed by cancer symptoms. Additionally, distinguishing primary or secondary thyroid dysfunction adds complexity. Despite guideline recommendations, the current approach to monitoring and managing ICI-TD remains passive, lacking proactive risk assessment [8–10]. Consequently, there’s an urgent need to understand the risk factors and predict ICI-TD to prevent and address adverse events adequately. Nevertheless, comprehensive evidence on ICI-TD risk factors is lacking, and while recent attention has focused on prediction models for adverse drug events (ADEs) of antineoplastic drugs, specific areas such as ICI-TD require further exploration [11].

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to consolidate existing evidence on ICI-TD influencing factors, and developed an online risk prediction tool accordingly. Ultimately, we established an evidence- and prediction model-based integrated management strategy for ICI-TD, aiming to enhance the early identification and proactive management.

Methods

We initiated and executed a three-phase study (Fig. 1). Firstly, we analyzed influencing factors through a systematic review and meta-analysis. Secondly, we developed prediction models for ICI-TD utilizing multiple algorithms based on real-world data. Finally, we proposed individualized management strategies for ICI-TD by amalgamating evidence-based and practical analyses.Fig. 1Overall flowchart of this three-phase study

Systematic review

The systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) guidelines [12, 13]. Apart from that, the study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42019131133).

Search strategy and selection criteria

We considered observational studies eligible if they enrolled adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with any type of solid or hematologic tumors, treated with ICIs, and reported potential risk factors of ICI-TD. Searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library from inception to October 20, 2022. Specific search strategies were developed for each database (Table S1), and manual searches of reference lists of previous systematic reviews and included literature were performed. Exclusions comprised duplicate publications, abstracts without available full texts, and studies lacking qualified or extractable data, with no language restrictions. Thereafter, all identified studies underwent review regardless of quality.

Data extraction and quality evaluation

Two reviewers (XL and ZS) independently screened potentially eligible studies from search results, initially by titles and abstracts, and then by full texts according to inclusion and exclusion criteria. Likewise, data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers (XL and ZS). Moreover, extracted data included study title, first author, publication year, country, study design, patient characteristics, assessment criteria for ICI-TD, key risk factors, and quality assessment. For potential influencing factors, we extracted age, gender, body mass index, cancer diagnosis, medication regimen, comorbidities, the level of thyroid hormones and antithyroid antibodies (ATA) at baseline (the initiation of receiving ICIs) for patients with and without ICI-TD in cancer patients receiving ICIs. Remarkably, we first all extracted the raw data of these factors, and if there were no raw data, the results of the univariate analysis were extracted. Furthermore, diagnosis criteria for ICI-TD varied among studies, categorized as any ICI-TD and overt ICI-TD. Analogously, data were extracted into Microsoft® Office Excel® 2019 software. For included studies, two authors (XL and ZS) independently assessed its quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), with scores above 6, between 4 and 6, and below 4 rated as high quality, moderate quality, and low quality, respectively [14]. Any discrepancies were resolved by consulting the corresponding author (RZ).

Data analysis

We analyzed the overall effects quantitatively using both categorical and continuous approaches to explore the risk factors of ICI-TD. We calculated numbers and proportions for qualitative variables and mean with standard deviations (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for quantitative variables as well as effect sizes including relative risks (RR), odds ratios (OR), hazard ratios (HR), and their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI). The meta-analysis applied random effect models. In order to assess the robustness of the findings by examining how different assumptions or analytical approaches affect the overall results, we conducted sensitivity analysis, which involved excluding each included study and utilizing both random effect and fixed effect models for each potential influencing factor to see if the main findings remain consistent. Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify sources of heterogeneity. A statistically significant difference was set at a p value of < 0.05. Data synthesis was performed using the RevMan 5.4 software.

Prediction model development

The development of prediction models adhered to standards outlined in the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement [15].

Data source

We conducted a retrospective observational study utilizing the real-world cohort data from Peking University Third Hospital (PUTH), covering data from July 20, 2018 (the approval date of the first ICIs, Pembrolizumab in China), to October 31, 2022. Eligible participants were adult inpatients (age ≥ 18 years) with a pathological diagnosis of malignant tumors who received ICI treatment, which included Atezolizumab, Camrelizumab, Durvalumab, Nivolumab, Pembrolizumab, Serplulimab, Sintilimab, Tislelizumab, Toripalimab, and Zimberelimab. Patients were excluded if they had a pre-existing diagnosis of thyroid dysfunction, were prescribed thyroid-related medications before ICI treatment, were pregnant or lactating, or had missing data regarding thyroid-related metrics. The study was conducted following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Board of Peking University Third Hospital (IRB00006761-M2023628).

Outcomes and candidate predictor variables

The outcome was any ICI-TD, defined as a composite outcome of overt and subclinical ICI-related hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and thyroiditis based on the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) Version 5.0. Therefore, patients with thyroid dysfunction were identified based on clinical diagnoses, and causality assessment between ICIs and thyroid dysfunction was conducted by two qualified pharmacists using the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) system. All possible variables for model development were collected at the initiation of immunotherapy, encompassing demographic characteristics, disease diagnosis, therapy regimen, vital signs, and laboratory test results, as determined from the results of the systematic review and meta-analysis (Table S2).

Model development

We utilized a de-identified dataset comprising medical orders for ICIs spanning from August 30, 2018, to March 14, 2022, for model development. To address the imbalance between ICI-TD positive and negative samples, we employed the Borderline Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) [16]. Subsequently, the dataset was randomly split into training and test sets at a 4:1 ratio. Machine learning algorithms were then fitted to the training set, with hyperparameters tuned via stratified fivefold cross-validation. This resampling technique iteratively divided the data into five subsets, with one serving as the test set in each iteration. Notably, this process was repeated until each fold of the five folds was used as the test set [17]. In addition, we adopted various algorithms, including logistic regression, linear discriminant analysis, naive Bayes, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), decision tree, random forest, support vector machines (SVM), gradient boosting, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM), and neural network. During the model parameter adjustment process, Grid Search technology was applied to all models except LightGBM to search for the best parameter configuration within the preset parameter range, and a fivefold cross-validation was implemented to ensure the model's generalization ability. Given that applying Grid Search on complex models like LightGBM would lead to extremely low efficiency, especially when dealing with high-dimensional parameter spaces, which is very time-consuming, we instead used a more efficient Random Search strategy to optimize the hyperparameters. All random processes used a unified random number seed set to the value 42 to ensure the reproducibility of the experiment. Also, we selected important variables for clinical use employing the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which identifies the significance of each variable in data interpretation [18]. Our strategy was to select variables based on their SHAP values, prioritizing those with the highest impact, until adding further variables didn’t significantly enhance the AUC metric. Seventeen features with the highest SHAP values were thus included in the models, and Shapley values were calculated to elucidate each parameter’s contribution to the prediction models.

Model evaluation and validation

We evaluated the models’ discrimination ability using metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and Brier score. The AUC was obtained by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate at various thresholds. Congruently, the true positive rate was also known as sensitivity, and the false positive rate was calculated as 1—specificity. In addition, calibration was assessed graphically to visualize agreement with calibration assumptions, which was mainly based on the idea of generating new plots which mimic the available data based on the calibration assumption [19]. To further evaluate the clinical utility, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted. DCA assists in judging the relative value of benefits and harms associated with prediction models [20, 21] across a range of clinically reasonable preferences. External validation utilized a de-identifying dataset containing medical orders for ICIs from March 15, 2022, to October 31, 2022. Based on comparison results from external validation, we selected the model with the best performance to develop a web calculator for clinical application. A gauge indicates various risk levels (low, medium, high, and very high) based on risk scores of 0.01, 0.10, and 0.50.

Statistical analysis

We tackled the issue of missing data by employing multiple imputation (MI), a robust statistical technique replacing each missing value with a set of plausible values to generate multiple complete datasets. Subsequent analysis was conducted on each of these datasets, and the results were aggregated to obtain unbiased estimates and correct standard errors. Leveraging the power of multiple imputations enabled us to mitigate the impact of missing data, thereby yielding more reliable and valid results [22].

Accordingly, baseline characteristics were presented as either the mean with SD for normally distributed continuous variables or the median with IQR for skewed continuous variables, while categorical variables were summarized by the number of subjects with percentages. To compare baseline characteristics between cohorts, we employed either Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, setting a statistically significant difference at a p value of < 0.05. Lastly, all statistical analyses were conducted using Python software version 3.6.7, R software version 4.2.0, and Microsoft Office 365.

Management strategy proposal

Our proposal integrates evidence-based review and practical analysis. First, based on the findings from influencing factor analysis and prediction model development, we identified patient’s characteristics requiring attention before ICI administration. Second, we comprehensively searched PubMed, Embase, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, and official websites of guideline-related associations to gather clinical practice guidelines for ICI-TD. These guidelines were then used to extract recommendations. Additionally, we analyzed and identified core elements of pharmaceutical care for patients undergoing ICI therapy. Ultimately, we formulated an integrated management strategy for ICI-TD.

Quality evaluation of included studies

Quality evaluation based on the NOS revealed that 20 studies were rated as high quality, while one was rated as moderate quality. In the selection domain, 19 studies scored 4, while 2 scored 3 due to inappropriate definitions of controls. Regarding comparability, four studies scored 2, while the remaining studies scored 1. In the exposure domain, 18 studies scored 3, while the rest scored 2. Detailed evaluation results for each study were presented (Table S3).

Association between ICI-TD and patient-related factors

Aggregate findings from all included studies revealed a significant association between female gender and an elevated risk of ICI-TD (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.20–1.78, P = 0.003). Subgroup analysis further highlighted a statistically significant association between female gender and overt ICI-TD (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.52–2.81, P < 0.00001). Association between ICI-TD and disease diagnosis or treatment regimen

The pooled results revealed a statistically nonsignificant association between cancer type and ICI-TD, except Hodgkin lymphoma, which displayed an increased risk. Furthermore, receiving Pembrolizumab was associated with a decreased risk of ICI-TD compared to other ICIs (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.28–0.99, P = 0.05) (Table 2, Figure S4 ~ S5).

Association between ICI-TD and thyroid function at baseline

Meta-analysis indicated a significant association between baseline TSH levels and the onset of ICI-TD (OR 0.44, 95% CI 0.08—0.80, P = 0.02). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant association between baseline TSH levels and any ICI-TD (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.57, P = 0.0006). Additionally, lower baseline free thyroxine (FT4) levels were significantly associated with ICI-TD (OR − 0.53, 95% CI − 0.91–− 0.14, P = 0.007). Furthermore, positive ATA at baseline exhibited a significant association with ICI-TD (OR 10.12, 95% CI 5.73–17.87, P < 0.00001), along with thyroglobulin antibody (TGAb) (OR 16.12, 95% CI 8.38–31.02, P < 0.00001) and thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) (OR 5.59, 95% CI 2.44–12.83, P < 0.00001) (Table 2, Figure S6 ~ S8).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analysis affirmed the robustness of the results (Figure S9 ~ S16), and funnel plots indicated no significant publication bias (Figure S17).

Risk stratification and web calculator

According to the risk stratification criteria, the low, moderate, high, and very high risks represent the risk score of less than 0.01, between 0.01 and 0.1, between 0.1 and 0.5, and greater than or equal to 0.5. The distribution of risk stratification in 621 patients included in the model development and validation was shown in Table S6, with a Pearson’s Chi-square value of 359.103 (P < 0.001).

In conjunction with the LightGBM-based prediction model, we generated a web calculator named “Individualized risk prediction tool for immune checkpoint inhibitors-related thyroid dysfunction in cancer patients” (http://39.106.85.129:11200), offering instructions in both English and Chinese. Users input BMI, number of diagnoses, and laboratory test results with assigned units. Immediate feedback prompts users if there is a null item. Management strategy of ICI-TD

Building upon the aforementioned findings and guidelines, we have devised individualized monitoring and treatment pathways. Patients scheduled for ICIs receive risk score assessment and ICI-TD stratification through the online tool. What’s more, pharmacists play a pivotal role in tailoring immunotherapy regimens. Throughout ICI administration, pharmacists offer comprehensive support, addressing various aspects. Meanwhile, a pharmaceutical care plan is crafted to monitor the onset and progression of ICI-TD. Individualized monitoring protocols are established based on risk levels determined by the online prediction tool. Clinical symptoms related to TD are monitored daily for patients across all risk levels. More importantly, thyroid function is assessed every 2 ~ 3 weeks for patients at very high risk and every 4 ~ 6 weeks for other risk levels. Discussion

Main findings and study strengths

Employing evidence-based medicine and artificial intelligence, this study facilitates individualized risk prediction of ICI-TD in cancer patients through an online tool based on prediction models and a systematic review of 21 observational studies. In light of these outcomes, individualized management strategies are proposed to enhance the safety of ICI therapy. Notably, this systematic review and meta-analysis delineated an evidence-based profile of ICI-TD and assessed its influencing factors. Additionally, we applied 11 algorithms to develop prediction models of ICI-TD and selected the model with the best performance through rigorous internal and external validation, subsequently utilized for creating a user-friendly web calculator. Moreover, by considering a wide array of potential influencing factors in both aspects, including demographic characteristics, lifestyles, disease diagnosis, therapy regimen, vital signs, and laboratory test results, our study offers a comprehensive approach. Also, the synergy between the influencing factor analysis and prediction model development bolsters research findings, enabling individualized risk prediction of ICI-TD based on easily obtainable parameters, thereby facilitating tailored monitoring and treatment by clinicians and pharmacists.

The performance of risk prediction models

As the clinical use of ICIs expands, the incidence of irAEs attributed to ICIs rises, necessitating proactive monitoring and prediction of associated risks. Presently, constrained by small sample sizes, incomplete variables, and limited algorithms, there remains room for enhancing the predictive performance and clinical application of models [11]. Focusing on ICI-TD, Kim et al. developed prediction models using 187 patients and four machine learning algorithms, with the random forest model demonstrating the best prediction with an AUC of 0.77 in internal validation [25]. Recognizing these limitations, our study encompassed a larger participant pool and employed 11 algorithms to develop prediction models. Consequently, the LightGBM model exhibited the highest AUC, followed by the random forest model and the XGBoost model, indicating superior predictive performance. In addition, we evaluated the calibration of models through calibration plots, which compares the predicted probabilities of ICI-TD occurring with the actual observed frequencies. While the calibration curve may not be ideal, we have found through risk stratification that a majority of positive patients (80.56%) can be predicted as being at very high risk, which is desirable and holds practical significance. Furthermore, an online risk calculation tool was devised for clinical utility, thus showcasing promising prospects for advancing the individualized management of ICI-TD.

Significant features in the ICI-TD

Regarding the variable selection process for prediction models, we have adopted the SHAP method, which is renowned for its ability to provide a game theoretic approach to explain the impact of each feature on the model's output. This approach ensures that we retain only the most informative and predictive variables in our prediction models for the risk of ICI-TD.

This study identified several clinical features significantly associated with ICI-TD, necessitating consideration for cancer patients undergoing ICIs. Demographic characteristics and lifestyle factors such as gender, smoking status, and BMI emerged as pertinent variables. Additionally, baseline thyroid function-related factors including free triiodothyronine (FT3), FT4, total thyroxine (TT4), TSH, and ATA warrant attention from clinicians. Furthermore, laboratory test-related factors such as the percentage of eosinophils (EOS%), uric acid (UA), urea, the percentage of lymphocytes (LYM%), total cholesterol (TC), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), lymphocyte counts (LYM), mean platelet volume (MPV), the percentage of monocytes (MONO%), and triglyceride (TG) may influence the risk of ICI-TD.

Demographic characteristics and lifestyles-related factors of ICI-TD

In this systematic review, the female gender was associated with an increased risk of overall ICI-TD and overt ICI-TD, aligning with several retrospective studies [44, 45]. Concerning the immune response, differences between genders and the greater prevalence of thyroid autoimmunity in women were noted [46–48]. Animal models exhibited sex-related susceptibility to autoimmune thyroiditis, reflecting human conditions, likely influenced by genes on sex chromosomes (X or Y) and sex hormones. Likewise, sex hormones may explain sex differences in the autoimmune response against the thyroid to some extent [46].

Regarding personal habits, the association between smoking status and ICI-TD garnered attention, with smoking linked to an elevated risk of overt ICI-TD [25]. Studies demonstrated a significant role of cigarette smoking in thyroid dysfunction, with smokers exhibiting altered thyroid hormone levels possibly mediated by sympathetic nervous system activation stimulating thyroid hormone synthesis and release [49]. The NHANES III survey that is conducted in the USA demonstrates that smokers have lower TSH levels compared with nonsmokers, and those of active smokers are higher than mild smokers. It has also been reported that smoking lowers serum TSH, which is accompanied by a slight rise of serum FT3 and FT4 [49]. Correspondingly, a cross-sectional study conducted across 10 cities found that cigarette smoking was associated with a significant decline in the concentrations of TSH, TPOAb, and TgAb, and daily smoking numbers, just as long-term smoking decreases serum TSH and TPOAb levels [50].

The association between BMI and ICI-TD

BMI featured prominently in the final prediction model. Pollack 2020 has reported that increasing BMI correlated with a higher likelihood of developing ICI-TD, particularly overt thyrotoxicosis post-PD-1/L1 inhibitor treatment [35]. Notably, overt thyrotoxicosis onset was earlier in obese individuals, underscoring the intricate interplay between obesity and immune response in ICI recipients [35]. Similarly, the meta-analysis conducted provided further evidence suggesting a relationship between higher BMI (overweight–obesity) and increased risk of irAEs [51]. Notably, the influence of thyroid function on body weight and obesity-related comorbidities underscores the need for further investigation [52, 53]. More so, the clinical implications of ICIs in populations with higher BMI may necessitate optimized dosing strategies for efficacy and safety considerations, warranting additional research efforts [54].

Baseline thyroid function-related factors of ICI-TD

Both the influencing factor analysis and prediction model development underscored the association of baseline TSH, FT4, FT3, and TT4 with the onset of ICI-TD. Additionally, the presence of ATAs at baseline emerged as a predictive biomarker for ICI-TD [55, 56]. Muir et al. demonstrated a particular association between ATA positivity and overt thyroid dysfunction with higher ICI-TD incidence observed in ATA-positive patients [41, 44, 56]. Moreover, an irregular echo pattern in the thyroid gland at baseline correlated with ICI-TD development [57]. These findings support the recommendation to stratify ICI-TD risk based on baseline thyroid hormone levels and ATA status, facilitating personalized safety management of ICIs [58, 59]. Furthermore, the exclusion of TT3 from the models is an interesting observation, which aligns with the findings of the systematic review. It suggests that, within the context of our dataset and modeling framework, TT3 may not have been as predictive as the other variables or its information might have been captured by the included variables to a sufficient extent. A combination of factors, including clinical relevance and sensitivity, model simplicity, and study design, may contribute to the exclusion of TT3.

Other laboratory tests-related factors of ICI-TD

The model, particularly the LightGBM-based one, highlighted the significance of EOS% as evidenced by its highest SHAP value. Eosinophils, classified as granulocytes, secondary to their large cytoplasmic secretory granules, influence T and B cell populations, granulocytic cell survival, and propagate Th1 and Th2 CD4 + immune response. Their cytokines secretion interacts with CD80/CD86 and CTLA- 4 surface antigens, potentially serving as a biomarker and therapy target in cancer treatment [60, 61]. Remarkably, in melanoma treatment, higher peripheral blood eosinophil counts correlated with improved ICI outcomes, suggesting their role in the tumor microenvironment and ICI response modulation. Further research should delve into eosinophils-specific mechanisms and their impact on ICI-TD [62–65].

In the LightGBM-based model, UA got the second-highest SHAP value. Interestingly, the results of a study involving 1,186 Chinese adults indicated that reduced UA contents may serve as a risk factor for predicting poor thyroid function in Chinese individuals [66]. It was also reported that patients within the low-range UA group displayed markedly lower FT3 than those in the high- and mid-range UA groups. Furthermore, it was predicted that moderate elevation in plasma UA content may affect thyroid hormones due to its potent anti-oxidation. Notably, the proper UA levels between men and women are different. Specifically, UA levels between 4.83 and 6.06 mg/dL may act to protect FT3 in women, while UA levels between 6.39 and 7.09 mg/dL may also protect FT3 in men [66]. Similarly, systematic reviews highlighted the association between subclinical thyroid dysfunction and hyperuricemia, emphasizing the interplay between UA levels and thyroid function [67].

Practical implications on the management of ICI-TD

The online calculator facilitates tailored screening for ICI-TD, aiding clinicians and pharmacists in distinguishing high-risk patients requiring a close follow-up from low-risk subjects needing less frequent testing. Some ICIs-related toxicity management guidelines recommend testing thyroid function every 4 to 6 weeks, which may make the adverse events overlooked in very high-risk patients [8–10]. Therefore, monitoring frequency should be customized based on risk level from the online tool’s results. Patients at very high risk should undergo routine thyroid function testing every 2–3 weeks, while every 4 ~ 6 weeks is proper for other levels of risk [9].

Pharmacists play a crucial role in medication therapy management, promoting safe ICI use by considering drug treatment history and potential combinations. Notably, certain medications, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors and iodized drugs, lithium, glucocorticoid, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and some drugs for the central nervous system may induce thyroid dysfunction, emphasizing the need for vigilant monitoring during cancer treatment [68]. HLA-DPA1*01:03 and DPB1*02:01 were shown to be associated with the development of ICI-TD [58]. Therefore, it is particularly important to correctly understand the effects of antitumor agents on thyroid function during cancer treatment.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. In the systematic review, retrospective observational studies’ pooled results might be affected by the limited number of studies reporting relevant data, potentially leading to overestimated or underestimated associations. Moreover, variations in ICI-TD diagnostic criteria among included studies introduce heterogeneity, despite efforts to classify ICI-TD as subclinical and overt. However, minor differences in diagnostic criteria within groups may still introduce a little bias. In prediction model development, the single-center retrospective data’s representativeness limits extrapolation, highlighting the need for multi-center data to enhance model performance. Additionally, small sample sizes leave room for model improvement, emphasizing the importance of optimizing prediction models in future studies, notwithstanding that we have adequately exploited as much data as we can from PUTH. Therefore, in subsequent studies, plans should be made to incorporate multi-center data to train and optimize prediction models.

Conclusion

The study offered evidence-based insights into ICI-TD influencing factors and developed 11 algorithms employing easy-to-obtain parameters. Leveraging clinical guidelines and experience, tailored monitoring and management strategies for ICI-TD in cancer patients were formulated. Overall, this comprehensive approach to ICI-TD management may enhance personalized cancer immunotherapy management. Moving forward, optimizing early warning systems and refining treatment protocols for ICI-TD remain critical areas for future research to further advance the safety and efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
