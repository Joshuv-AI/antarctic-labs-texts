Reversible Right-to-Left Shunt After Mitral TEER Resolved by Tricuspid TEER
Authors: Özlem Önder, Aysel Akhundova, Ekrem Güler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIatrogenic atrial septal defects are common after transseptal interventions and are usually clinically insignificant; however, under specific hemodynamic conditions, they may result in clinically relevant right-to-left shunting and hypoxemia.Case SummaryA 67-year-old woman with severe mitral and torrential tricuspid regurgitation underwent a combined transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) strategy. Shortly after successful mitral TEER, the patient developed acute hypoxemia (oxygen saturation decreased from 96% to 82%) during preparation for tricuspid intervention. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed a 0.7-cm iatrogenic atrial septal defect with predominant right-to-left shunting. Instead of defect closure, tricuspid TEER was performed, resulting in rapid improvement in oxygen saturation (to 95%) and reversal of shunt direction.DiscussionRight-to-left shunting after mitral TEER may be driven by right-sided pressure overload and altered flow dynamics rather than the septal defect itself.ConclusionsTreating tricuspid regurgitation can restore interatrial pressure balance, reverse shunt direction, and potentially obviate the need for atrial septal defect closure.

Transseptal access during transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) frequently results in the creation of iatrogenic atrial septal defects (iASDs). In most cases, these defects are small and clinically insignificant. However, their hemodynamic impact is highly dependent on the interplay between right and left atrial pressures.Take-Home Message•Right-to-left shunting after mitral TEER is not always a septal problem; addressing right-sided pressure overload may reverse shunt direction and resolve hypoxemia without ASD closure.

Right-to-left shunting after mitral TEER is not always a septal problem; addressing right-sided pressure overload may reverse shunt direction and resolve hypoxemia without ASD closure.

Right-to-left shunting across an iASD is uncommon, but it may lead to acute hypoxemia, particularly in patients with elevated right-sided pressures or right ventricular dysfunction. In such scenarios, the defect is often considered the primary cause, and closure is frequently pursued.

We present a case demonstrating that right-to-left shunting following mitral TEER may instead represent a reversible hemodynamic phenomenon driven by right-sided pressure overload, with immediate resolution following tricuspid TEER without the need for atrial septal defect (ASD) closure.

History of Presentation

A 67-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea and peripheral edema.

Past Medical History

The patient had chronic kidney disease requiring maintenance hemodialysis.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included acute pulmonary edema, anesthesia-related hypoventilation, pulmonary embolism, and right-to-left shunting through an iatrogenic atrial septal defect.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (30%), dilated left atrium and right heart chambers, severe mitral regurgitation, and torrential tricuspid regurgitation with a large coaptation gap (1.2 cm) (Figures 1A and 1B). Management

Given symptomatic severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, a combined TEER strategy was planned.

Following transseptal access, mitral TEER was successfully performed with implantation of 2 clips, resulting in mild residual mitral regurgitation (Figures 3A and 3B). This was accompanied by a marked reduction in left atrial pressure from 50 to 28 mm Hg, confirming effective mitral valve repair.Figure 3Transesophageal Echocardiography Demonstrating Successful Mitral TEER(A) Three-dimensional image showing 2 clips implanted on the mitral valve with adequate leaflet grasp. Subsequently, preparation for tricuspid TEER was initiated, and the same guiding catheter was repositioned into the right atrium. However, during this phase, the patient developed acute hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation abruptly decreasing from 96% to 82%, requiring supplemental oxygen. Right heart catheterization was not performed given the need for immediate intervention in the setting of acute hypoxemia. In this context, management decisions were guided by real-time TEE findings.

Rather than proceeding with defect closure, we opted to address the underlying cause of elevated right atrial pressure by performing tricuspid TEER. Given the torrential tricuspid regurgitation and large coaptation gap, 3 clips were implanted. Outcome and Follow-Up

Oxygen saturation rapidly improved to 95%, and repeat echocardiography demonstrated reversal of the interatrial shunt direction from right-to-left to left-to-right (Video 3).

This immediate hemodynamic improvement supports right-sided pressure overload as the primary mechanism of hypoxemia. In addition, clip implantation altered the direction of the tricuspid regurgitant jet away from the interatrial septum, which may have further contributed to resolution of the right-to-left shunt.

Discussion

The management of iASDs after mitral TEER remains controversial. While closure may be considered in cases of persistent right-to-left shunting with hypoxemia, the underlying hemodynamic mechanism should be carefully evaluated.1, 2, 3, 4

In our case, elevated right atrial pressure due to severe tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction resulted in a reversed interatrial pressure gradient. Instead of immediate closure of the iASD, we opted to address the underlying right-sided pathology. After tricuspid TEER, right atrial pressure decreased, restoring the left-to-right gradient and resolving hypoxemia.5, 6, 7

In addition to the pressure gradient, flow dynamics may have contributed to the severity of right-to-left shunting in this case. The regurgitant jet of severe tricuspid regurgitation was directed toward the interatrial septum and the iatrogenic atrial septal defect, potentially facilitating preferential right-to-left flow. This directional component may further amplify shunting beyond what would be expected based solely on pressure differences. After tricuspid TEER, both the reduction in right atrial pressure and the alteration of jet direction likely contributed to the resolution of the shunt.

Current guidelines recommend preprocedural right heart catheterization (RHC) before tricuspid valve interventions, as it provides important hemodynamic information including right atrial pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, and cardiac output that may assist procedural planning and risk stratification.8 Furthermore, previous studies have identified severe tricuspid regurgitation, elevated right atrial pressure, pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular dysfunction as factors associated with clinically significant right-to-left shunting following successful mitral TEER.2, 3, 4

We acknowledge that preprocedural RHC was not performed in this case, and in retrospect, invasive hemodynamic assessment might have provided additional information regarding the patient's risk of postprocedural right-to-left shunting. In this patient, however, the heart team considered a combined transcatheter strategy appropriate based on the severity of symptomatic heart failure, torrential tricuspid regurgitation with a large coaptation gap, severe right ventricular dysfunction, and comprehensive echocardiographic evaluation.

In retrospect, severe tricuspid regurgitation, right ventricular dysfunction, and likely elevated right atrial pressure were factors associated with an increased risk of clinically significant right-to-left shunting after mitral TEER. This case highlights the potential value of comprehensive hemodynamic assessment in selected high-risk patients and underscores the need for heightened awareness of this complication. In selected patients, preprocedural RHC may help identify individuals at increased risk and facilitate proactive procedural planning, including consideration of concomitant tricuspid intervention when appropriate.

The possibility of right-to-left shunting should be considered when planning intervention in high-risk patients. In selected cases, operators may prepare for concomitant tricuspid TEER or have an ASD occluder device readily available if clinically indicated. In this case, a combined treatment strategy had already been considered because of the coexistence of severe mitral and torrential tricuspid regurgitation. However, clinically significant right-to-left shunting was not anticipated before the procedure. Once desaturation occurred, TEE rapidly identified the underlying mechanism and allowed immediate physiology-guided treatment.

Importantly, the choice of guiding system may also be influenced by this anticipation: If a combined mitral and tricuspid TEER approach is planned from the outset, a steerable guiding catheter capable of accessing both the mitral and tricuspid valves without repositioning may facilitate a more efficient and safer procedure. Consistent with this strategy, the same steerable guiding catheter used for mitral TEER was subsequently repositioned into the right atrium for tricuspid TEER, enabling rapid transition to tricuspid intervention once desaturation was recognized. This seamless procedural pivot was central to the favorable outcome. When right-to-left shunting is considered likely preoperatively, operators may also choose a platform that allows simultaneous access to both valves, reducing procedural time and minimizing the window of hemodynamic vulnerability.

Both procedures were performed using the DragonFly transcatheter valve repair system (Valgen Medtech). Two clips (XW0612 and XN0412) were implanted during mitral TEER, and 3 clips (XW0612, SW0609, and SW0609) during tricuspid TEER.

Importantly, closure of the iASD without addressing the underlying right-sided pathology might not have resolved the hemodynamic substrate responsible for shunting in this patient.

This case demonstrates that in selected patients, treating right-sided disease may be more appropriate than defect closure alone.1,3,6,7 Importantly, this approach emphasizes the need for a physiology-guided rather than anatomy-driven management strategy in post-TEER iASDs. Future studies should prospectively evaluate whether preprocedural RHC-derived hemodynamic parameters can reliably identify patients at risk for hemodynamically significant right-to-left shunting after mitral TEER, and whether this information should routinely inform procedural planning in high-risk individuals.

Conclusions

Right-to-left shunting after mitral TEER may be driven by elevated right-sided pressures rather than the septal defect itself. Targeting tricuspid regurgitation can restore interatrial pressure balance, reverse shunt direction, and potentially eliminate the need for ASD closure.
