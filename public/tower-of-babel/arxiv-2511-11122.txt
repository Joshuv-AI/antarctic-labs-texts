Non-Convex Global Optimization as an Optimal Stabilization Problem: Convergence Rates Thanks: Y.H. has been supported by a Roth Scholarship from Imperial College London.
Yuyang Huang, Dante Kalise, Hicham Kouhkouh

Abstract
. We propose a discounted infinite-horizon optimal control formulation that generates trajectories converging to the set of global minimizers of a continuous, non-convex function. The analysis provides explicit convergence rates for both the variational behavior of the value function and the pathwise convergence of the optimal trajectories. This paper is a companion to our previous work [ 23 ] , where a more general framework was introduced; here, we focus on a specific setting in which sharper and more detailed results can be established.

1.
Introduction

We are concerned with the construction of algorithms for solving the optimization problem

(1.1)

\min\limits_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(z)

whose objective function
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is non-convex and merely continuous, with a non-trivial set of global minimizers. Building on the control-based framework introduced in
[
7
]
and further developed in our companion work
[
23
]
, we demonstrate that the minimization task can be effectively reformulated as an optimal stabilization problem

(1.2)

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x,t):=\inf_{\alpha(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)e^{-\lambda s}\,\mathrm{d}s\,,\qquad\lambda>0\,,

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,,

where
y_{x}^{\alpha}(\cdot)
denotes the trajectory starting from
x
with velocity
\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s)
, and
\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a measurable control. The value function
u_{\lambda}(x,t)
represents the optimal cost-to-go over all such admissible trajectories.

The central idea of this work is to overcome the challenges posed by non-convexity and the existence of multiple minimizers. These challenges naturally arise when directly minimizing the objective
f(\cdot)
. Therefore, instead of tackling this problem in its original finite-dimensional form (
1.1
), we recast it as a family of control problems (
1.2
), defined over an infinite-dimensional space. This formulation allows us to capture the optimization landscape through the lens of the value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
, which acts as a surrogate for the original objective
f(\cdot)
. The value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
not only encodes essential information about the optimization problem, but also serves as the driving mechanism for constructing trajectories that effectively converge to the set of global minimizers of
f(\cdot)
, typically through its associated gradient flow. Although the value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is not guaranteed to possess any form of convexity (its gradient need not be monotone), it nonetheless provides an effective means to navigate the objective landscape of
f(\cdot)
and reach global minimizers despite non-convexity.

In the present manuscript, we undertake a deeper analysis to ensure the convergence of trajectories generated by this control problem and to provide both qualitative and quantitative estimates of their convergence behavior. More precisely, we refine and extend the dynamical analysis carried out in
[
23
]
in relation to the optimization objective, and establish explicit convergence rates that further substantiate this control-based approach to tackling non-convex optimization problems where the set of global minimizers can be non-trivial.

1.1.
Outline of the Main Results.

Let us denote by
\underline{f}
the minimal value of a function
f(\cdot)
that we seek to minimize. Our main findings concern the rate of convergence from both viewpoints (variational and pathwise). Mainly, let us define
y^{*}_{x}(\cdot)
as an optimal trajectory for the problem (
1.2
), where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is arbitrarily fixed. Then the following results hold.

(1)

Variational convergence: There exists
K>\lambda>0
such that

0\leq u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))-\underline{f}/\lambda\leq e^{-(K-\lambda)t}\big(u_{\lambda}(x)-\underline{f}/\lambda\big)\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\forall\,t\geq 0.

(2)

Pathwise convergence: There exist
\tau,\delta>0
such that

\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{*}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\lesssim\;e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\quad\forall\,t\geq\tau\,,

and

|\dot{y}_{x}^{*}(t)|^{2}\;\lesssim\;\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau\,,

where
\mathfrak{M}
is the set of global minimizers of
f(\cdot)
. The notation “
\lesssim
” means “
\leq
” up to a multiplicative constant, and “
a.a.
” stands for “
almost all
”.

We will also show that these results hold for quasi-optimal trajectories, i.e. trajectories that are optimal up to some small error.

The variational convergence result (1) allow us to identify the minimal value, while the pathwise convergence (2) identifies a minimizer.
Note that the resulting trajectories and convergence guarantees are obtained through deterministic control principles, rather than through randomized search or sampling strategies commonly used in non-convex optimization. In this sense, the method remains fully analytical and its convergence properties can be rigorously established.

1.2.
Related results in literature

Over the last decades, there has been growing interest in the interplay between optimization and dynamical systems. Modeling classical iterative optimization algorithms as discretizations of suitable dynamical systems has led to valuable insights into convergence and stability, as demonstrated in
[
1
,
3
,
34
,
32
,
4
,
26
,

,
18
,
33
,
31
]
. However, these results are typically obtained under assumptions of smoothness and some form of convexity, including standard convexity, strong convexity or quasi-convexity, imposed on the objective function. Even in the non-convex setting, available results often rely on Łojasiewicz-type conditions, as in
[
2
,
5
]
.

In general non-convex regimes, global convergence results typically only guarantee to critical points, while methods specifically designed for global optimization are largely metaheuristic, such as Ant Colony Optimization
[
14
]
, Particle Swarm Optimization
[
37
]
, or Simulated Annealing
[
8
]
. A prominent example is Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO), a class of derivative-free, interacting particle methods for global minimization of non-convex functions. Assuming the existence of a unique global minimizer, a comprehensive global convergence result with an explicit convergence rate is established for the finite-particle, discrete-time dynamics
[
17
,
16
]
, whereas the case of multiple global minimizers is treated in
[
21
]
. Of particular relevance to the present work is the controlled-CBO dynamics introduced in
[
22
]
, where the standard CBO drift is modified by an optimal feedback term computed from a numerical approximation of an associated HJB value function.

Parallel to these stochastic approaches, a fully deterministic line of research formulates optimization as optimal control problems governed by Hamilton–Jacobi or eikonal-type equations. Unlike heuristic methods, this perspective avoids randomness entirely and is grounded in analytical control theory. In
[
7
]
, global asymptotic convergence to the set of global minimizers is obtained in the vanishing-discount limit
\lambda\to 0
by analyzing a stationary eikonal equation. A closely related and now active direction connects Moreau envelopes and proximal operators to viscous Hamilton–Jacobi equations via the Hopf–Lax formula and artificial viscosity
[
27
,
20
]
. In this context, exponential convergence rates for convex, lower semi-continuous functions under generalized
Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequalities has been established
[
2
]
. We refer to
[
23
]
for a more complete literature review on PDE-based optimization methods.

The rest of the manuscript is organized as follows.
Section

introduces the optimal control problem that forms the basis for both the design and the analysis of our method.
Section

establishes the variational convergence of the value function towards the global minimum. The results are formulated for both optimal and quasi-optimal trajectories, with explicit convergence rates and error estimates in the quasi-optimal case. In

In particular, we prove that (quasi-)optimal trajectories generated by the control problem converge exponentially fast to the set of global minimizers. As a byproduct, we obtain a Turnpike-type property and highlight connections with controllability.
Section
5
discusses several standard assumptions in optimization and demonstrate how our framework relates to the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition, local convexity, and metric regularity. The section concludes with several examples illustrating the applicability of our assumptions.
Finally,
Appendix

A
,
B
, and

collect auxiliary definitions and provide additional proofs for completeness and the reader’s convenience.

2.
The control problem

In the following, we present a global-optimization-as-optimal-control approach. Consider a control system where the control variable
\alpha(\cdot)
governs the state trajectory
y_{x}^{\alpha}(\cdot)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
through the dynamics

\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(t)=\alpha(t),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x,\quad t\geq 0

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a given initial condition and the control
\alpha(\cdot)
belongs to an admissible set
\mathcal{A}
, which is defined by

(2.1)

\mathcal{A}:=\{\,\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\text{measurable},\;|\alpha(s)|\leq M\;\forall\,s\in[0,\infty)\,\}.

The trajectory
y_{x}^{\alpha}(\cdot)
associated with an admissible control
\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}
is called an admissible trajectory.
For a given objective
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and discount factor
\lambda>0
, to quantify the performance of the control, we consider the running cost functional defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{A}
as

(2.2)

\mathscr{J}(x,\alpha(\cdot)):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,s}\,\mbox{\rm d}s.

The quadratic term
\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(\cdot)|^{2}
is a control penalization which regularizes the effort required to move through the landscape. The term
f(\cdot)
evaluates the objective along the trajectory and rewards reaching and staying in regions with low objective value.

Finally, the optimal control is obtained by solving the time-homogeneous, discounted infinite horizon optimal control problem (see
[
11
]
for background and general results)

(2.3)

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)=\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\mathscr{J}(x,\alpha(\cdot))

\displaystyle\text{such that }\;\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{and the controls }\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to B_{M}\text{ are measurable}

where
B_{M}
is the closed ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of radius
M
.
The optimal value function is also a unique viscosity solution
[
6
]
of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation:

(2.4)

\lambda\,u_{\lambda}(x)+\frac{1}{2}|Du_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}=f(x)\quad\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Our first main assumption is the following.

(A1)

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and bounded that is

\exists\;\underline{f},\,\overline{f}\;\text{ such that }\;\underline{f}\leq f(x)\leq\overline{f},\quad\forall\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Remark 2.1
.

Under Assumption
(A1)
, we have from
[
23
, Lemma 2.1(iii)]
the uniform bound
|Du_{\lambda}(x)|\leq\sqrt{6\|f\|_{\infty}}
, and one can drop the bound
\|\alpha\|_{\infty}\leq M
in the definition of the admissible control set in (
2.3
)-(
2.1
) as long as
M
is chosen larger than
\sqrt{6\|f\|_{\infty}}
. Thus (
2.3
) can be equivalently expressed by

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)=\;\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\text{d}s,

\displaystyle\text{such that }\;\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{and the controls }\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ are measurable. }

Indeed, the maximum in the Hamiltonian
H(x,u,Du)
is reached in an interior point, that is

\displaystyle H(x,r,p)

\displaystyle=\lambda r+\sup\limits_{\alpha\in B_{M}}\left\{-\alpha\cdot p-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}\right\}-f(x)=\lambda r+\sup\limits_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{-\alpha\cdot p-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}\right\}-f(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda r+\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}-f(x).

Therefore we can assume without loss of generality that the controls are bounded by some constant
M>\sqrt{6\|f\|_{\infty}}
which can be made arbitrarily large.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let Assumption
(A1)
hold. The value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
satisfies
\underline{f}\leq\lambda\,u_{\lambda}(x)
for all
x
. Moreover,
\lambda\,u_{\lambda}(x)=\underline{f}
if and only if
x\in\mathfrak{M}
.

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
satisfies the Dynamic Programming Principle (DPP) that is

(2.5)

u_{\lambda}(x)=\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\,\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\mbox{\rm d}s\,+\,u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t))\,e^{-\lambda t}\,\right\}.

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be fixed. For any
\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}
, define the function
h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by

h(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\mbox{\rm d}s\,+\,u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t))\,e^{-\lambda t}.

A direct consequence of the DPP (
2.5
) is the following.

Proposition 2.4
.

The function
t\mapsto h(t)
is non-decreasing for all
\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}
, and it is constant if and only if
\alpha(\cdot)
is optimal.

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

With the preliminary analysis in place, we proceed to synthesize a control law that achieves the desired system behavior.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let Assumption
(A1)
be satisfied.
The curve starting from
x
at
t=0
, and governed by
\dot{y}^{*}_{x}(t)=-Du_{\lambda}(y^{*}_{x}(t))
for almost every
t\geq 0
, is an optimal trajectory for the control problem (
2.3
).

The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

While optimal trajectories can be characterized analytically as in the previous theorem, their practical computation is frequently hindered by the limitations of numerical approximation schemes, which may suffer from instability, inefficiency, or sensitivity to problem data. Therefore, it becomes necessary to consider quasi-optimal trajectories, which we now proceed to define.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
be an admissible control. The corresponding trajectory
y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot)
is called quasi-optimal if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\text{d}s=\mathscr{J}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\cdot))\leq u_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon.

When
\varepsilon
is fixed, then we shall call it an
\varepsilon
-optimal trajectory. In particular,
optimal trajectories are those corresponding to
\varepsilon=0
. A slightly more general definition will be later introduced in Assumption
(C)
.

Lastly, we define the shifted problem which corresponds to
\tilde{f}(\cdot):=f(\cdot)-\underline{f}
. Thus the control problem is

(2.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)=\;\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\text{d}s,

\displaystyle\text{such that }\;\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{and the controls }\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to B_{M}\text{ are measurable. }

where the value function satisfies
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda
, and is the viscosity solution of

(2.7)

\lambda\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\frac{1}{2}|D\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)|^{2}=\tilde{f}(x),\quad\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

It is clear that (
2.6
) and (
2.3
) share the same solutions.
In particular, the result in Theorem
2.5
holds for (
2.6
) mutatis mutandis.

3.
The convergence rate

We have seen from Proposition
2.2
that the minimal value of the value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is
\underline{f}/\lambda
, and it is achieved on the set
\mathfrak{M}
of global minimizers of
f(\cdot)
. In the notation of (
2.6
)-(
2.7
), this means that
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is always non-negative, and
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)=0
if and only if
x\in\mathfrak{M}
. Our aim in this section is to provide qualitative and quantitative insights about the convergence of
s\mapsto u_{\lambda}(y_{x}(s))
towards its minimal value, along optimal or quasi-optimal trajectories
y_{x}(\cdot)
.

Our second main assumption is the following.

(B)

There exists
K>\lambda>0
such that
\;Ku_{\lambda}(x)\leq f(x)\quad\forall\,x.

It implies in particular
\;K\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)\leq\tilde{f}(x)\;\forall\ x.\;
This assumption is inspired from
[
19
, Assumption 2.3]
where it is used to ensure stability properties of the control problem.
Later in the next sections, we will provide further conditions which guarantee the validity of Assumption
(B)
; see Proposition
4.15
, and Section §
5.1
.

3.1.
Optimal trajectories

Thanks to Theorem
2.5
, an optimal trajectory for the control problem (
2.3
) or (
2.6
) is the following

(3.1)

\dot{y}^{*}_{x}(t)=-Du_{\lambda}(y^{*}_{x}(t))\quad\text{for a.a. }t\geq 0,\quad y_{x}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Let us recall some classical notions in dynamical system theory. We call
\mathcal{V}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
a
Lyapunov function
for a dynamical system
\dot{y}(t)=G(y(t))
with respect to the set
\mathfrak{M}
if

(i)

\mathcal{V}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathfrak{M}
, and
\mathcal{V}(x)>0
otherwise,

(ii)

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\mathcal{V}(y(t))=D\mathcal{V}(y(t))\cdot G(y(t))\leq 0
.

Such a function acts as an energy measuring how far the system is from the equilibrium set
\mathfrak{M}
. Its existence guarantees the stability of
\mathfrak{M}
, that is trajectories starting sufficiently close to
\mathfrak{M}
remain close for all time. If, in addition,
\mathcal{V}(\cdot)
satisfies

(iii)

\dot{\mathcal{V}}(x)<0
for all
x\notin\mathfrak{M}
,

then
\mathfrak{M}
is
asymptotically stable
, meaning that trajectories starting close to
\mathfrak{M}
, not only stay close but converge to it.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y^{*}_{x}(\cdot)
be an optimal trajectory governed by (
3.1
).
Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
holds.
Then the function
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a Lyapunov function for this dynamics with respect to the set
\mathfrak{M}
. Moreover,
\mathfrak{M}
is asymptotically stable.

Proof.

Recall
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda
.
It suffices to compute

(3.2)

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y^{*}_{x}(t))=Du_{\lambda}(y^{*}_{x}(t))\cdot\dot{y}^{*}_{x}(t)=-|Du_{\lambda}(y^{*}_{x}(t))|^{2}\leq 0.

Moreover, Proposition
2.2
ensures that
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)=0
if and only if
x\in\mathfrak{M}
, which proves that
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is indeed a Lyapunov function and that
\mathfrak{M}
is Lyapunov stable.

To prove that
\mathfrak{M}
is asymptotically stable, we show in addition that (
3.2
) is strict when outside of
\mathfrak{M}
. Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
-|Du_{\lambda}(z)|^{2}<0
. Using the HJB equation (
2.7
), it holds
\;\lambda\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(z)-\tilde{f}(z)=-\frac{1}{2}|D\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(z)|^{2}<0,\;
hence
0\leq\lambda\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(z)<\tilde{f}(z)
and
z\notin\mathfrak{M}
because otherwise
0=\tilde{f}(z)
. Therefore, the inequality (
3.2
) is strict whenever outside of
\mathfrak{M}
, which ultimately guarantees its asymptotic stability.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y_{x}^{*}(\cdot)
be an optimal trajectory for (
2.3
) (or (
2.6
)) governed by (
3.1
).
Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
and
(B)
hold. Then

0\leq u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))-\underline{f}/\lambda\leq e^{-(K-\lambda)t}\big(u_{\lambda}(x)-\underline{f}/\lambda\big)\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\forall\,t\geq 0.

In particular,
\lambda\,u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))\to\underline{f}
exponentially fast when
t\to\infty
, regardless of the initial position
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

As we will soon-after show, Theorem
3.2
can be seen in fact as a corollary of Theorem
3.4
in the next subsection. However the proof of Theorem
3.2
being simpler and instructive, we provide it next.

Proof.

Recall
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda
and
\tilde{f}(\cdot)=f(\cdot)-\underline{f}
.
Using the DPP as in Proposition
2.3
and Proposition
2.4
, we know that the function

\tilde{h}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha^{*}(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{x}^{*}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\mbox{\rm d}s\,+\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))\,e^{-\lambda t}

is constant for all
t\geq 0
, where we recall
y_{x}^{*}(\cdot)
is an optimal trajectory given by (
3.1
) and Theorem
2.5
, and
\alpha^{*}(\cdot)=\dot{y}_{x}^{*}(\cdot)
. In particular, noting that
\tilde{h}(0)=\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)
, we have

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))=e^{\lambda\,t}\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha^{*}(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{x}^{*}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\;\mbox{\rm d}s

and for almost every
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))-\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha^{*}(t)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{x}^{*}(t))\right)

\displaystyle\leq-(K-\lambda)\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))\quad\text{ using }\ref{main assumption}.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality yields

0\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))\leq e^{-(K-\lambda)t}\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)\quad\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

recalling the absolute continuity of
t\mapsto\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{*}(t))
, and the non-negativity bound from Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3.2.
Quasi-optimal trajectories

When an optimal solution to the control problem is not computationally accessible, or when it is available with some error, one needs to rather use quasi-optimal trajectories as in Definition
2.6
.

Recalling the cost functional (
2.2
), we write

\mathscr{J}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t),\alpha(t+\,\cdot\,))=\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,(s-t)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s

and define the shifted one as

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t),\alpha(t+\,\cdot\,))=\int_{t}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,(s-t)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s=\mathscr{J}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t),\alpha(t+\,\cdot\,))-\underline{f}/\lambda.

We need the following assumption on quasi-optimal trajectories.

(C)

There exist
\varepsilon_{\circ}\geq 0
and
\eta(\cdot)\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}_{+})

such that

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))+\varepsilon(t)\quad\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq 0

where
\varepsilon(t)=\eta(t)\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))+\varepsilon_{\circ}
.

Remark 3.3
.

This more general framework for quasi-optimal trajectories allows for better flexibility in a wide range of applications. The following two observations are worth noting.

(1)

Assumption
(C)
is simply

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))\leq\big(1+\eta(t)\big)\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

which coincides with Definition
2.6
in the particular case when
t=0
,
\eta(\cdot)\equiv 0
, and then
\varepsilon(\cdot)\equiv\varepsilon_{\circ}
. If moreover
\varepsilon=0
, then this is an optimal trajectory.

(2)

When
t=0
, it also implies

{\mathscr{J}}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,))\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\tilde{\varepsilon}

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}:=\|\eta\|_{\infty}(\overline{f}-\underline{f})/\lambda+\varepsilon_{\circ}\geq\eta(0)u_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon_{\circ}=\varepsilon(0)
. Hence, when Assumption
(C)
is satisfied, then the quasi-optimal trajectory is also quasi-optimal in the sense of Definition
2.6
, though with a different
\varepsilon
.

Before we state our next result, let us recall from Proposition
2.2
that
0\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
. The following is an analogue to Theorem
3.2
for quasi-optimal trajectories (instead of optimal ones).

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot)
be a quasi-optimal trajectory.
Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
,
(B)
, and
(C)
hold, and that
\eta(\cdot)
in
(C)
satisfies
\;\|\eta\|_{\infty}<1-\lambda/K\;
.
Then we have

0\leq\;\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\;\leq\;C\,e^{-\delta\,t}\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(1\,+\delta^{-1}\right)\quad\quad\forall\,t\geq 0

where

\delta=(1-\|\eta\|_{\infty})K-\lambda\,>0\quad\text{ and }\quad C=(1+\eta(0)).

In particular, we have

0\leq\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\;\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))=\left(1\,+\delta^{-1}\right)\,\varepsilon_{\circ}.

Recalling
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda
, this means that
\lambda u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
converges exponentially fast to a small neighborhood of
\underline{f}
.
As we have previously mentioned, this result generalizes Theorem
3.2
which is indeed obtained when
\varepsilon_{\circ}=0
, and
\eta(\cdot)\equiv 0
, then
\delta=K-\lambda
and
C=1
.

Proof.

Let us use the notation
\tilde{\ell}(\alpha,x):=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^{2}+\tilde{f}(x)
. By definition, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{\infty}\tilde{\ell}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s),y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s))\,e^{-\lambda\,(s-t)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{\ell}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s),y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s))\,e^{-\lambda\,(s-t)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\ell}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s),y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s))\,e^{-\lambda\,(s-t)}\,\mbox{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\,t}\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,))-e^{\lambda\,t}\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{\ell}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}(s),y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s))\,e^{-\lambda\,s}\,\mbox{\rm d}s.

This is absolutely continuous, hence for almost every
t\geq 0
, we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))

\displaystyle=\lambda\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))-\tilde{\ell}(\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t),y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))-K\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\quad\text{ using }\ref{main assumption}

\displaystyle\leq-(K-\lambda)\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))+K\,\varepsilon(t)\quad\text{ using }\ref{quasi opt cost}.

Recalling the definition of
\varepsilon(t)
in Assumption
(C)
, we have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(t)=\eta(t)\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

\displaystyle\leq\eta(t)\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

Hence, we have

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))

\displaystyle\leq-(K-\lambda-K\,\eta(t))\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

Let us define
c(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
such that

c(t):=(1-\eta(t))K-\lambda\quad\text{ and }\quad C(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mbox{\rm d}r=(K-\lambda)(t-s)-K\int_{s}^{t}\eta(r)\mbox{\rm d}r

Applying Grönwall’s inequality yields

(3.6)

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))\leq e^{-C(0,t)}\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,))+\varepsilon_{\circ}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

We have
\;\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))
,  and from Assumption
(C)
we also have

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(x,\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,))\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon(0)=(1+\eta(0))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon_{\circ}

The last two inequalities, when together with (
3.6
), yield

(3.7)

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq e^{-C(0,t)}\,(1+\eta(0))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(e^{-C(0,t)}\,+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s\right).

The assumption
\;\eta(t)\leq\|\eta\|_{\infty}<1-\lambda/K\;
for a.a.
t\geq 0
guarantees that

0<\delta\leq c(t)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad 0<\delta(t-s)\leq C(s,t).

where
\delta:=(1-\|\eta\|_{\infty})K-\lambda
.
Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\delta(t-s)}\mbox{\rm d}s=\frac{1-e^{-\delta\,t}}{\delta}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}.

The inequality (
3.7
) finally becomes

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq(1+\eta(0))\,e^{-\delta\,t}\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(e^{-\delta\,t}\,+\delta^{-1}\right)\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

∎

The difference with Assumption

Theorem 3.5

Let

Recalling

of the optimization problem.

Proof.

We start as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
. We denote
\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(t):=\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))
for simplicity. By modifying (

) according to
(

C
Δ

)
, one has for
t\in[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})

(3.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(t)

\displaystyle\leq-(K-\lambda)\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(t)+K\,\varepsilon(t)

\displaystyle\leq-\left[K-\lambda-K\,\frac{\sigma}{2}e^{-K\,\Delta_{\max}}\right]\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(t)

Define
\Gamma:=K-\lambda-K\,\frac{\sigma}{2}e^{-K\,\Delta_{\max}}.

Integrating over
[\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1})
yields

At each sampling time, by Assumption
(

C
Δ

)

Since

combining this with (

By induction starting from

∎

4.
Main Results in Optimization

This section is devoted to formalizing our findings in the context of optimization. Recall the problem we shall be concerned with

\min\limits_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(z).

Our analysis relies on the following additional assumptions
1
1

1

The assumptions
(A2)
and
(A3)
have appeared previously in
[
23
]
. To maintain consistency with that work, we shall refer to them by the same names.
regarding the function
f(\cdot)
.

(A2)

f
attains the minimum, i.e.,

\mathfrak{M}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,f(x)=\underline{f}:=\min\limits_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(z)\}\neq\emptyset.

(A3)

For all
\delta>0
, there exists
\gamma=\gamma(\delta)>0
such that

\inf\{f(x)-\underline{f}\,:\,\text{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\,>\delta\}\,>\,\gamma(\delta).

(D)

There exist
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
such that

\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq\;f(x)-\underline{f}\;\leq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2},\quad\forall\,x\in\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}.

Later, we shall assume
\mathfrak{M}
to be compact. In that case, Assumption
(A3)
is equivalent to

(4.1)

\liminf\limits_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)>\underline{f}

But in general, when
\mathfrak{M}
is unbounded, (
4.1
) is not true as it has been noted in
[
7
]
. From an optimization viewpoint, Assumption
(A3)
imposes a uniform energy gap between the global minimizers and all other points, in particular non-global minimizers, thereby quantifying how much higher the energy must be outside the optimal set. Such a separation guarantees the robustness of minimizers, supports asymptotic principles such as large deviations and Laplace approximations, and, in the context of Gibbs measures, leads to exponential concentration of probability mass near the set of global minimizers.

4.1.
A stability result

The following theorem is borrowed from
[

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
be satisfied. Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be given, and
y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot)
be an
\varepsilon

2.3

\varepsilon\geq 0
small.
Then for all
\mathbf{r}>0
, there exist
\lambda>0
small and
\tau=\tau(\mathbf{r},\varepsilon,\lambda)>0
such that

\operatorname{dist}\left(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s),\mathfrak{M}\right)\leq\mathbf{r}\quad\quad\forall\,s\in[\tau,\infty).

In other words,
y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(s)\in\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}
for all
s\geq\tau
.

Let us comment on the time interval
[0,\tau]
. By definition of
\tau
in Theorem
4.1
, this interval corresponds to the initial phase during which a (quasi-)optimal trajectory remains outside a neighborhood of the set of global minimizers
\mathfrak{M}
. As shown in
[

, the duration of this phase decreases as
\lambda\to 0
; more precisely, it is of order
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
. Hence, by tuning the parameter
\lambda

can be made arbitrarily short. Once the trajectory enters a neighborhood of the global minimizers, the results that we are about to establish in this manuscript will ensure exponential convergence to the set
\mathfrak{M}

4.2.
A Riccati-type structure in the value function

Let us first consider a closed and non-empty set
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and show that in the situation where we choose
x\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})
instead of
f(\cdot)
in the control problem (
2.3
), one obtains a closed-form expression for the corresponding value function. This is comparable with Riccati solution in the linear-quadratic case. To this aim, we define for some
\mathfrak{c}>0
constant

(4.2)

\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{c}}(x,\mathscr{O}):=\;\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{c}}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha}(s),\mathscr{O})^{2}\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\mbox{\rm d}s,

\displaystyle\text{such that }\;\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\text{and the controls }\alpha(\cdot):[0,\infty)\to B_{M}\text{ are measurable}.

The following result provides an explicit definition of the value function
R_{\mathfrak{c}}(x,\mathscr{O})
. Its proof is postponed to Appendix
C
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a non-empty and compact set, and let
\mathfrak{c}>0
. Then the value function corresponding to (
4.2
) enjoys the closed form expression

R_{\mathfrak{c}}(x,\mathscr{O})=C\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})^{2}\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
C=(-\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4\mathfrak{c}}\,)/4\,>\,0
.

Remark 4.3
.

To compare Lemma
4.2
with Riccati theory, let us consider the case where
\mathfrak{M}=\{0\}
, that is

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\alpha(\cdot)}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(s)|^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{c}}{2}\,|y_{x}^{\alpha}(s)|^{2}\right)\,e^{-\lambda s}\;\mbox{\rm d}s,

\displaystyle\text{such that }\;\dot{y}_{x}^{\alpha}(s)=\alpha(s),\quad y_{x}^{\alpha}(0)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

This is a simple example of a linear–quadratic regulator (LQR). Both the optimal control and the value function are given by the discounted algebraic Riccati equation

\lambda E=\mathfrak{c}\mathbb{I}_{n}-E^{2}

where the unknown is
E
, a symmetric matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{I}_{n}
is the identity matrix. To solve this matrix equation, one observes that: if
E
is a solution, then
U^{\top}EU
is also a solution for all
U
an orthogonal matrix. But the only symmetric matrices that are invariant under orthogonal transformations are those of the form
\varrho I_{n}
where
\varrho\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar. Thus, we search for a solution of the form
E=\varrho\mathbb{I}_{n}
. This leads to a second-order equation
\varrho^{2}+\lambda\varrho-\mathfrak{c}=0
with two algebraic roots, only the positive one (
\varrho=(-\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4\mathfrak{c}})/2
) yields a stabilizing feedback controller. Therefore
E=\varrho\mathbb{I}_{n}
, the optimal control is
\alpha^{*}(s)=-Ey(s)=-\varrho\,y(s)
, and the optimal trajectory is
y(s)=x\,e^{-\varrho s}
. Plugging this control and this trajectory in the cost functional, one recovers the value function
R_{\mathfrak{c}}(x,\{0\})=\frac{\varrho}{2}|x|^{2}
. This is the same solution as in Lemma
4.2
, where
C
therein is exactly
\varrho/2
here, and
\operatorname{dist}(x,\{0\})=|x|
. The proof of this lemma when
\mathfrak{M}
is not a trivial set requires a different strategy; see Appendix
C
.

Let us recall
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda

\int_{t}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{\xi}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(s),\mathfrak{M})\right)\,e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\;\mbox{\rm d}s\leq R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+\varepsilon_{\circ}.

Using now the second inequality in (
4.3
), one has

\int_{t}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{\xi}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\;\mbox{\rm d}s\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}+\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{\xi}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(s),\mathfrak{M})\right)\,e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\;\mbox{\rm d}s.

The control
\beta_{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
being admissible (without necessarily begin optimal) for the problem (
2.3
), one gets

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}\;\left(\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{\varepsilon}(s)|^{2}+\tilde{f}(y_{\xi}^{\beta_{\varepsilon}}(s))\right)\,e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\;\mbox{\rm d}s

and then

(4.5)

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)\leq R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+\varepsilon_{\circ}.

Finally, from (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) one obtains

\displaystyle R_{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon(t)=(1+\eta(t))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon_{\circ}

\displaystyle\leq R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+\varepsilon(t)+\varepsilon_{\circ}

\displaystyle=R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+\eta(t)\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+2\varepsilon_{\circ}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\eta(t))\,R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+(2+\eta(t))\varepsilon_{\circ}

that is

R_{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon(t)\leq R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+\varepsilon(t)+\varepsilon_{\circ}

or, recalling the definition of
\varepsilon(t)
in Assumption
(C)
,

R_{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})\leq(1+\eta(t))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon_{\circ}\leq(1+\eta(t))\,R_{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})+(2+\eta(t))\varepsilon_{\circ}.

It suffices now to observe, using Lemma
4.2
, that

R_{\mathfrak{c}_{i}}(\xi,\mathfrak{M})=C_{i}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\text{ where }C_{i}=(-\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4\mathfrak{c}_{i}}\,)/4,\text{ and }i=1,2,

which leads to

C_{1}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon(t)\leq C_{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}+\varepsilon(t)+\varepsilon_{\circ}

or equivalently

C_{1}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq(1+\eta(t))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\xi)+\varepsilon_{\circ}\leq(1+\eta(t))\,C_{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}+(2+\eta(t))\varepsilon_{\circ}

∎

Our next result provides a guarantee to a similar condition as in Assumption
(B)
which will be useful for our main result in the upcoming section.

Proposition 4.6
.

4.3.
Convergence towards global minimizers

We are now ready to state and prove our main convergence result. It shows that a (quasi-)optimal trajectory of the control problem (
2.3
)) converges exponentially fast to the set of global minimizers
\mathfrak{M}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\cdot)
be a quasi-optimal trajectory for problem (
2.3
). Suppose the following:

-

Assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
are satisfied, and
\;\mathfrak{M}
is compact.

-

Assumption
(C)
holds, with
\eta(\cdot)
s.t.
\;\|\eta\|_{\infty}<1-\lambda/K
,
\;K=\mathfrak{c}_{1}/(2C_{2})
, and
\varepsilon_{\circ}\geq 0
.

-

Assumption
(D)
holds with some constants
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
.

Then there exist
\lambda>0
small and
\tau=\tau(\mathbf{r},\varepsilon_{\circ},\lambda,\|\eta\|_{\infty})>0
such that
\;\forall\,t\geq\tau

\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\leq\;\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\circ})

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}=\frac{C_{2}(1+\|\eta\|_{\infty})}{C_{1}},\quad\delta=(1-\|\eta\|_{\infty})K-\lambda

\displaystyle\text{ and }

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\circ})=\frac{\varepsilon_{\circ}}{C_{1}}\left(1+\delta^{-1}+(2+\|\eta\|_{\infty})\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\right)

The constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are as defined in Proposition
4.4
.

It should be noted that one has
\;\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\,\leq\mathbf{r}\,\leq\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2},

hence the inequality in Theorem
4.7
can be equivalently expressed as

\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\leq\;\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon_{\circ})\quad\text{ for all }t\geq\tau,

and so is the case in the subsequent results.

A direct consequence of this theorem is next.

Corollary 4.8
.

For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(\cdot)
be an optimal trajectory for problem (
2.3
) as given by Theorem
2.5
. Suppose the following:

-

Assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
are satisfied, and
\;\mathfrak{M}
is compact.

-

Assumption
(D)
holds with some constants
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
.

Then there exist
\lambda>0
small and
\tau=\tau(\mathbf{r},\lambda)>0
such that

\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\leq\;\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\quad\forall\,t\geq\tau

and

|\dot{y}_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(t)|^{2}\;\leq\;\mathfrak{c}_{2}\,\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau

where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}=\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}},\quad\text{ and }\quad\delta=K-\lambda.

The constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are as defined in Proposition
4.4
.

Remark 4.9
.

About the “almost all
t\geq\tau
” requirement in Corollary
4.8
: If one assumes

to be in addition semi-concave, then the value function
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is also semi-concave
[
7
, Lemma 2]
. In this case, the value function is differentiable everywhere along an optimal trajectory

will be true “for all

. We should note that the semi-concavity is a curvature upper bound, which does not conflict with any type of convexity.

Proof of Corollary
4.8
.

The convergence of the trajectory is a consequence of Theorem
4.7
where we set
\eta(\cdot)\equiv 0
and
\varepsilon_{\circ}=0
. Only the convergence of the velocity
\dot{y}_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(\cdot)
is needed. To this aim, we shall use the HJB equation (
2.4
) together with the characterization of an optimal control in Theorem
2.5
that is
\dot{y}_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(t)=\alpha^{*}(t)=-Du_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t))
for almost every

. Then Using the HJB equation, one has

\lambda(u_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t))-\underline{f}/\lambda\,)+\frac{1}{2}|Du_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t))|^{2}=f(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t))-\underline{f}\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq 0.

Let
\mathbf{r}>0
be fixed, and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
be given as in Assumption
(D)
.
The existence of
\lambda>0
small and
\tau=\tau(\mathbf{r},\lambda)>0
such that the optimal trajectory satisfies for all

\;y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t)\in\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(z,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}
, is guaranteed by Theorem
4.1
together with Remark
3.3
.

On the other hand, from Proposition
2.2
, we know that
\lambda(u_{\lambda}(\cdot)-\underline{f}/\lambda\,)\geq 0
, and using the second inequality in Assumption
(D)
, one gets

|\alpha_{*}(t)|^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau.

Therefore, using the first statement of this corollary, one obtains

\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\leq\;\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\,\alpha_{*}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}

Therefore, one obtains

|\alpha_{*}(t)|^{2}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}\,\mathfrak{a}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau.

where
\mathfrak{a}=C_{2}/C_{1}
as in Theorem
4.7
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

We proceed as in the proof of Theorem
3.4
using the conclusion of Proposition
4.6
with
K:=\mathfrak{c}_{1}/(2C_{2})
.

Let
t\geq\tau
be arbitrarily fixed.
As in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, recalling the notation of Assumption
(C)
, let us define
c(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
such that

c(t):=(1-\eta(t))K-\lambda\quad\text{ and }\quad C(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}c(r)\mbox{\rm d}r=(K-\lambda)(t-s)-K\int_{s}^{t}\eta(r)\mbox{\rm d}r

Then the same computation leading to (
3.6
) still hold, the only difference being the range of integration when applying Grönwall’s inequality which is now between
\tau
and
t\geq\tau
(and not between
0
and
t
). This yields

(4.6)

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))\leq e^{-C(\tau,t)}\,\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\tau+\,\cdot\,))+\varepsilon_{\circ}\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

We have
\;\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t+\,\cdot\,))
,  and from Assumption
(C)
we also have

\widetilde{\mathscr{J}}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(\tau+\,\cdot\,))\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau))+\varepsilon(\tau)=(1+\eta(\tau))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

These last two inequalities, when together with (

), yield

(4.7)

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq e^{-C(\tau,t)}\,(1+\eta(\tau))\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau))+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(e^{-C(\tau,t)}\,+\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s\right).

The assumption
\;\eta(t)\leq\|\eta\|_{\infty}<1-\lambda/K\;
for a.a.

guarantees that

0<\delta\leq c(t)\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad 0<\delta(t-s)\leq C(s,t).

where
\delta:=(1-\|\eta\|_{\infty})K-\lambda
.
Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-C(s,t)}\mbox{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\delta(t-s)}\mbox{\rm d}s=\frac{1-e^{-\delta\,(t-\tau)}}{\delta}\leq\frac{1}{\delta}.

The inequality (
4.7
) finally becomes

\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))\leq(1+\eta(\tau))\,e^{-\delta\,(t-\tau)}\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau))+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(e^{-\delta\,(t-\tau)}\,+\delta^{-1}\right)\quad\forall\,t\geq 0.

We conclude using Proposition
4.4
which yields
\;\forall\,t\geq\tau

\displaystyle C_{1}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(t))+\varepsilon_{\circ}

\displaystyle\leq\,(1+\eta(\tau))\,e^{-\delta\,(t-\tau)}\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau))+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(1+e^{-\delta\,(t-\tau)}\,+\delta^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\;(1+\eta(\tau))\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\,\left[C_{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}+\,\varepsilon_{\circ}\right]+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(1+e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\,+\delta^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{2}\,(1+\eta(\tau))\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}+\varepsilon_{\circ}\left(1+\delta^{-1}+(2+\eta(\tau))\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\right).

∎

4.4.
A Turnpike-type result

An interpretation of Corollary
4.8
that deserves particular attention is its connection with the Turnpike phenomenon. Indeed, Corollary
4.8
shows that any optimal control–trajectory pair (as in Theorem
2.5
) converges exponentially fast toward its stationary counterpart. This interpretation is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.10
.

For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
(\alpha^{*}(\cdot),y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(\cdot))
be an optimal control-trajectory for problem (
2.3
) as in Theorem
2.5
. Suppose the following:

-

Assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
are satisfied, and
\;\mathfrak{M}
is compact.

-

Assumption
(D)
holds with some constants
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
.

Then there exist
\lambda>0
small and
\tau=\tau(\mathbf{r},\lambda)>0
such that

|\alpha_{*}(t)|^{2}\,+\,\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;\leq\;\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}\,e^{-\delta(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})^{2}\quad\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathfrak{a}}=(1+\mathfrak{c}_{2})\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}},\quad\quad\delta=K-\lambda\quad\text{ and }\quad K=\mathfrak{c}_{1}/(2C_{2}).

The constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are as defined in Proposition
4.4
.

We refer to Remark
4.9
for a discussion about the “
almost all

t\geq\tau
” requirement.

It is easy to note that the same proof also provides

|\alpha_{*}(t)|+\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(t),\mathfrak{M})\;\leq\;\hat{\mathfrak{a}}\,e^{-\frac{\delta}{2}(t-\tau)}\;\operatorname{dist}(y_{x}^{\alpha_{*}}(\tau),\mathfrak{M})\quad\quad\text{ for a.a. }t\geq\tau

where
\hat{\mathfrak{a}}=(1+\sqrt{\mathfrak{c}_{2}})\sqrt{C_{2}/C_{1}}
.

The connection with the Turnpike property is straightforward (e.g., see
[
29
]
, the books
[
38
,
39
]
, a link with HJB in
[
15
]
, and the recent survey
[
35
]
with the many references therein). Indeed, to the optimal control problem (
2.3
), is formally associated a stationary (finite dimensional) problem

\min\limits_{(a,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad\frac{1}{2}|a|^{2}+f(x),\quad\text{s.t.: }0=a

which turns our to be nothing but the optimization problem that we are seeking to solve

\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(x).

Therefore, the Turnpike theory tells us that both the optimal control and trajectory should converge (exponentially) towards the steady optimal control-trajectory pair, which are in the present case the null control
0
and the set
\mathfrak{M}
of global minimizers of
f(\cdot)
. This is the statement of Theorem
4.10
.

Finally, the behavior of the trajectory during the time interval
[0,\tau]
is discussed earlier in §
4.1
. This interval is also commonly referred to as the “first arc” in the terminology of the Turnpike theory.

4.5.
Controllability and linear growth

We provide next two assumptions which, we will show, guarantee the validity of Assumption
(B)
.

(E)

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a control
\hat{\alpha}(\cdot)
(not necessarily optimal) which drives
x
to some point
x_{\ast}\in\mathfrak{M}
in time
t(x)\leq\beta(f(x)-\underline{f})
, where
\beta>0
is a constant.

(F)

There exist
\mathbf{r}>0
and
C>0
such that

\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq C\,(f(x)-\underline{f}),\quad\forall\,x\in\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}.

Remark 4.11
.

When the set
\mathfrak{M}
is closed (or, when
f(\cdot)
is continuous) and non-empty,
Assumption
(E)
could be seen as a strong controllability assumption, as it implies small-time controllability defined in
[
6
, Definition IV.1.1]
. Indeed, let us choose the constant control

\hat{\alpha}(s)=\frac{x_{\ast}-x}{|x_{\ast}-x|}M\quad\quad\forall\,s\in[0,\,t(x)]

where
x_{\ast}
is the projection (not necessarily unique) of
x
onto
\mathfrak{M}
which exists thanks to closedness. Then, our dynamics is
\dot{y}_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(s)=\hat{\alpha}(s)
with
y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(0)=x
. Integrating over
[0,t(x)]
yields
y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(t(x))-y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(0)=\frac{x_{\ast}-x}{|x_{\ast}-x|}M\,(t(x)-0)
and by definition of
t(x)
we have
y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(t(x))=x_{*}
. Thus it holds
x_{\ast}-x=\frac{x_{\ast}-x}{|x_{\ast}-x|}M\,t(x)
. Taking the norm on both sides finally yields
t(x)=\frac{1}{M}|x-x_{\ast}|
.
Now recall from Remark
2.1
that the constant
M
can be chosen as large as we want, which then makes
t(x)
as small as we want, and thus the small-time controllability.

Proposition 4.12
.

If Assumptions
(A2)
and
(E)
hold, then Assumption
(B)
is satisfied.

Proof.

Let us choose the constant control

(4.8)

\hat{\alpha}(s)=\frac{x_{\ast}-x}{|x_{\ast}-x|}M\quad\quad\forall\,s\in[0,\,t(x)]

where
M
is fixed,
x_{\ast}\in\mathfrak{M}
exists thanks to
(A2)
, and
\hat{\alpha}(s)=0
whenever
s>t(x)
. Using the fact that it is admissible (without necessarily begin optimal), we have

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\hat{\alpha}(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(s))\right)e^{-\lambda s}\,\mbox{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t(x)}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\hat{\alpha}(s)|^{2}+f(y_{x}^{\hat{\alpha}}(s))\right)e^{-\lambda s}\,\mbox{\rm d}s+\underline{f}\,\frac{e^{-\lambda\,t(x)}}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+\overline{f}\right)\,t(x)+\underline{f}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq\beta\left(\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+\overline{f}\right)\,\big(f(x)-\underline{f}\big)+\underline{f}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}\quad\text{ thanks to \ref{Cont}.}

Therefore, it holds

\widetilde{K}\left(u_{\lambda}(x)-\underline{f}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)\leq f(x)-\underline{f}\quad\forall\,x\quad\text{ where }\;\widetilde{K}=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{2}M^{2}+\overline{f}\right)^{-1}.

It suffices now to choose
\lambda
small such that
\widetilde{K}>\lambda>0
.
∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold.
Then
Assumption
(F)
is equivalent to Assumption
(E)
.

Proof.

Observe first that continuity of
f(\cdot)
from Assumption
(A1)
guarantees that the set of its global minimizers
\mathfrak{M}
is closed, and Assumption
(A2)
ensures that
\mathfrak{M}
is non-empty. Hence, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can define its projection (possibly not unique) onto
\mathfrak{M}
.

Now let us define

t(x)=\frac{1}{M}|x-x_{\ast}|
as in Remark
4.11
where
x_{\ast}
is a projection of
x
onto
\mathfrak{M}
. We have
t(x)=\frac{1}{M}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})
. When put together with Assumption
(E)
, it implies

\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\beta\,M\,(f(x)-\underline{f}).

Conversely, if Assumption
(F)
is satisfied for some constant
C>0
, then one could choose as a control the projection of
x
onto the set
\mathfrak{M}
with any constant speed
M>0
, that is
\hat{\alpha}(s)=\frac{x_{\ast}-x}{|x_{\ast}-x|}M
which corresponds to straight lines. In this case, we have again

, and since
\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})=|x-x_{\ast}|
, it holds

M\,t(x)=\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq C\,(f(x)-\underline{f})

hence Assumption
(E)
is satisfied with
\beta=C/M
.
∎

.

Suppose Assumptions
(A1)
,
(A2)
, and

Proof.

This is a consequence of Proposition

.

Proposition 4.15
.

or
(F)
, instead of
(B)
.

5.
Comparison to standard conditions

5.1.
Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition

Recalling the HJB equation (
2.4
),
Assumption
(B)
implies

(5.1)

\frac{1}{2}\left|Du_{\lambda}(x)\right|^{2}\geq(K-\lambda)u_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{for a.a. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and

\frac{1}{2}\left|D\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)\right|^{2}\geq(K-\lambda)\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{for a.a. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

which are Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL)–type inequalities for
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
respectively. Conversely, if (
5.1
) holds, then using the HJB equation (
2.4
) one gets
K\,\tilde{u}_{\lambda}(x)\leq\tilde{f}(x)
for a.a.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
u_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a classical solution to (
2.4
), the PL-inequality (
5.1
) holds everywhere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and Assumption
(B)
becomes equivalent to (
5.1
).

5.2.
Smooth functions

Stronger conditions than Assumption
(D)
are the following.

(G1)

There exist
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
such that the function
f(\cdot)
is
L
-smooth
\forall\,x\in\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}
.
That is, it is differentiable, and its gradient is
L
-Lipschitz in a bounded region around the minimizers.

(G2)

There exist
\mathbf{r}>0
and
0<\mathfrak{c}_{1}\leq\mathfrak{c}_{2}<\infty
such that the function
f(\cdot)
is
\mu
-strongly convex in
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}
.
That is,

f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\leq\theta\,f(x)+(1-\theta)\,f(y)-\frac{\mu}{2}\theta(1-\theta)|x-y|^{2}\quad\forall\,\theta\in[0,1]

for all
x,y
in
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}
.

Assumption
(G1)
implies in particular the following inequality

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\,|y-x|^{2}.

If one assumes
f(\cdot)
to be differentiable, then Assumption
(G2)
becomes

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\,|y-x|^{2}.

If both Assumptions
(G1)
and
(G2)
hold, then
f(\cdot)
has a unique minimizer, i.e.
\mathfrak{M}=\{x_{*}\}
where
f(x_{*})=\underline{f}
, moreover

\frac{\mu}{2}\,|x-x_{*}|^{2}\;\leq\;f(x)-\underline{f}\;\leq\;\frac{L}{2}\ |x-x_{*}|^{2},\quad\forall\,x\in\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})\leq\mathbf{r}\right\}.

Indeed, the uniqueness of the minimizer is a consequence of the inequality in
(G2)
.

Assumption
(D)
is weaker in that it does not require differentiability of the function

f(\cdot)
, and it also accommodates the presence of multiple minimizers, possibly forming a non-trivial set.

5.3.
Metric regularity

We show the connection between
(D)
and the classical notion of metric regularity
[
9
, Definition 2.80, p. 58]
, which is related to openess
[
9
, Theorem 2.81, p. 58]
. See also
[
30
, Chapter 9, §G]
, in particular Theorem 9.43 therein where other equivalent properties are given.

More precisely, we shall verify that the lower-bound in Assumption
(D)
implies metric regularity of the subdifferential of
f(\cdot)
, meaning that subgradients cannot vanish faster than linearly with respect to the distance to minimizers. This measures the steepness of the function near
\mathfrak{M}
.

Recall that a set-valued map
\Psi:X\to 2^{Y}
is
metric regular
at a point
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\operatorname{Graph}(\Psi)
at a rate
\kappa
, if for all
(x,y)
in a neighborhood of
(x_{0},y_{0})
it holds

\operatorname{dist}(x,\Psi^{-1}(y))\leq\kappa\,\operatorname{dist}(y,\Psi(x)).

Recall the graph of a set-valued function is
\operatorname{Graph}(\Psi):=\{(x,y)\in X\times Y\,:\,x\in\Psi(y)\}
.

Suppose
f(\cdot)
is subdifferentiable in a neighborhood of
\mathfrak{M}
, and denote its subdifferential
\partial f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to 2^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
defined at some
x
by

\partial f(x):=\{\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,f(x)-f(y)\leq\langle v,x-y\rangle\;\forall\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,\}.

Let
(x_{*},0)\in\operatorname{Graph}(\partial f)
such that
x_{*}\in\mathfrak{M}
, and
0\in\partial f(x_{*})
. Then
f(x_{*})=\underline{f}
and it holds

f(x)-\underline{f}\leq\langle v,x-x_{*}\rangle\leq|v|\,|x-x_{*}|\quad\forall\,v\in\partial f(x).

Taking the infimum over
v\in\partial f(x)
and over
x_{*}\in\mathfrak{M}
yields

f(x)-\underline{f}\leq\operatorname{dist}(0,\partial f(x))\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})

where
\operatorname{dist}(0,\partial f(x))=\inf\{|v|\,:\,v\in\partial f(x)\}
is the so-called subgradient residual which precisely measures the steepness of the function near
\mathfrak{M}
. Using the lower-bound in Assumption
(D)
, and noting that
\partial^{-1}f(0)=\mathfrak{M}
, one gets

\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial^{-1}f(0))\leq\;\frac{2}{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}\;\operatorname{dist}(0,\partial f(x))

which holds for all
(x,0)
in a neighborhood of
(x_{*},0)
. This is the metric regularity of the set-valued map
\partial f(\cdot)
at all points
(x_{*},0)
where
x_{*}\in\mathfrak{M}
, with rate
2/\mathfrak{c}_{1}
.

5.4.
Examples

We now consider several well-known examples from the literature and verify that each one adheres to our framework, with particular attention to Assumption
(D)
.

Although the following examples involve functions
f(\cdot)
that are unbounded, in practical optimization it is customary to restrict the search to a sufficiently large but bounded region. Accordingly, one may truncate
f(\cdot)
outside this region without loss of generality.

Moreover, some examples admit a non-compact set of global minimizers
\mathfrak{M}
. This issue can likewise be handled by truncating the domain to the bounded search region used in practice. Under such truncation, Assumption
(A3)
is automatically satisfied, since we define
\;\liminf\limits_{|x|\to\infty}f(x)=:\overline{f}\;
the truncation level of the loss function, chosen so that
\overline{f}>\underline{f}
, see (
4.1
).

•
Example 1
. Strongly convex quadratic functions

Consider
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{\top}Qx+b^{\top}x+c
where
Q
is symmetric positive definite with smallest eigenvalue
\mu>0
. Then
\mathfrak{M}=\{-Q^{-1}b=:x^{*}\}
, and

f(x)-f(x^{*})=\frac{1}{2}(x-x^{*})^{\top}Q(x-x^{*}).

So

\frac{\mu}{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}\leq f(x)-f(x^{*})\leq\frac{L}{2}|x-x^{*}|

where
L
is the largest eigenvalue of
Q
.

Such functions appear for example in: least-squares regression, ridge regression, LQR cost functions in control, Kalman filter cost function.

•
Example 2
. Non-convex quadratic functions with a flat minimizer set

Consider
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}-x_{2})^{2}
. Here the set of minimizers if the line
\mathfrak{M}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,x_{1}=x_{2}\}
. Then

f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}

and the inequality holds with
\mathfrak{c}_{1}=\mathfrak{c}_{2}=1
.

Such functions appear for example in: Consensus optimization, distributed averaging, synchronisation in multi-agent systems, over-parametrized models in machine learning.

•
Example 3
. Regularized losses and penalized problems

Consider
f(x)=|Ax-b|^{2}+\lambda|x|^{2}
. Here
f(\cdot)
is strictly convex, and thus the quadratic growth holds globally with constants related to the smallest/largest eigenvalues of
A^{\top}A+\lambda\mathbb{I}_{n}
.

Such functions appear for example in: Machine learning regularization, statistical estimation, robust control.

•
Example 4
. Double-well potentials

Consider
f(x)=(x^{2}-1)^{2}=x^{4}-2x^{2}+1
. The set of global minimizers is
\mathfrak{M}=\{-1,1\}
with
\underline{f}=0
.

Around a minimizer, for example
1
, it holds
f(1+\delta)=4\delta^{2}+4\delta^{3}+\delta^{4}
. Hence for sufficiently small
|\delta|
, it holds
\;2\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq f(x)\leq 5\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\;
for all
x
such that
|x-1|\leq\delta
and
\delta=0.4
.

Such functions appear for example in: problems with phase transitions, bistable control systems, symmetry-breaking potentials in physics and machine learning (scalar neural activations, binary classification energy models).

•
Example 5
. Matrix factorization and low-rank learning

Consider
f(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\|XY^{\top}-M^{*}\|^{2}_{F}
, where
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. This is a non-convex problem but satisfies local quadratic growth around the set of global minimizers
\mathfrak{M}=\{(X,Y)\,:\,XY^{\top}=M^{*}\}
. The constants
\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathfrak{c}_{2}
depend on the singular values of
M^{*}
.
This is also called
Restricted Isometry Property
, see
[
36
, Definition 3.1]
and references therein.

Such functions appear for example in: Matrix completion, dictionary learning, phase retrieval, Principal component analysis

•
Example 6
. Neural Network Losses with symmetries

Consider
f(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}x_{2}-1)^{2}
. This is a nonconvex function with minimizer set a hyperbola
\mathfrak{M}=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\,:\,x_{1}x_{2}=1\}
. One could perform a Taylor expansion around any such points
(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\mathfrak{M}
and obtain
f(x_{1},x_{2})\approx\frac{1}{2}(\omega_{1}\delta_{2}+\omega_{2}\delta_{1})^{2}
, where
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})=(x_{1}-\omega_{1},x_{2}-\omega_{2})
. Hence
f(\cdot)
grows quadratically with the distance to
\mathfrak{M}
.

Such functions appear for example in: over-parameterized networks, deep linear networks, matrix sensing with factorized parametrizations.

•
Example 7
. Trigonometric/periodic landscapes

Consider
f(x)=1-\cos(x)
. The set of global minimizers is
\mathfrak{M}=2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and
\underline{f}=0
. Near any minimizer
x^{*}=2\pi k
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, on has
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-x^{*})+\mathcal{O}(|x-x^{*}|^{4})
, and the quadratic growth thus holds locally.

Such functions appear for example in: synchronization problems, phase-locked loops, periodic optimal control.

•
Example 8
.
k
-means objectives

Given data points
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
k
-means objective is

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq k}|a_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}.

Here the variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are the cluster centres.

We suppose that clusters are well-separated, that is: if
(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{k}^{*})
is a global minimizer, then there exists a positive margin
\gamma>0
such that every point
a_{i}
is strictly closer to its assigned centre
x_{p(i)}^{*}
than to any other centre

|a_{i}-x_{p(i)}^{*}|^{2}+\gamma\leq|a_{i}-x^{*}_{j}|\quad\forall\,i,\,\forall\,j\neq p(i).

Then, in a small neighborhood of
\mathfrak{M}
and around each optimal cluster, the function becomes quadratic. The set of minimizers is
\mathfrak{M}=\{(x_{\sigma(1)}^{*},\dots,x_{\sigma(k)}^{*})\,:\,\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}\}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
is the set of permutations of
\{1,\dots,k\}
.

This property (of separated clusters) is one of the reasons why Lloyd’s algorithm
[
24
]
exhibits linear convergence when initialized near a correct solution (as shown in e.g.
[
25
]
).

•
Example 9
. The Exponential/Soft
k
-Means Objective

Consider the smooth “soft”
k
-means objective

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}e^{-\beta\,|a_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}}\right)\right],\quad\beta>0.

For well-separated data and sufficiently large
\beta
, the global minimizers form a finite set
\mathfrak{M}=\{(x_{\sigma(1)}^{*},\dots,x_{\sigma(k)}^{*}):\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}\}
,
where
(x_{1}^{*},\dots,x_{k}^{*})
are the true cluster centres.
In a neighborhood of
\mathfrak{M}
,
f(\cdot)
satisfies the two-sided quadratic growth condition

\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}{2}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq f(x)-\underline{f}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}{2}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathfrak{M})^{2},

with constants
\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathfrak{c}_{2}>0
depending on
\beta
and the cluster covariance structure.

•
Example 10
. Gaussian mixtures and the EM objective

Let
f(\theta)
denote the negative log-likelihood of a Gaussian mixture model

f(\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}w_{j}\,\phi(a_{i};\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})\right),

where
\phi(\,\cdot\,;\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})
is the Gaussian density and

\theta=(w_{1},\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1},\dots,w_{k},\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})
.
If the mixture components are well-separated and the true parameters

\theta^{*}
are identifiable up to permutation, then the set of global
minimizers is

\mathfrak{M}=\{\theta_{\pi}^{*}\,:\,\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}\}
.
In a neighborhood of
\mathfrak{M}
, the function
f(\cdot)
satisfies the
two-sided quadratic growth condition

\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{1}}{2}\operatorname{dist}(\theta,\mathfrak{M})^{2}\leq f(\theta)-\underline{f}\leq\frac{\mathfrak{c}_{2}}{2}\operatorname{dist}(\theta,\mathfrak{M})^{2},

where the constants
\mathfrak{c}_{1},\mathfrak{c}_{2}>0
depend on the
Fisher information matrix at
\theta^{*}
.

5.5.
Conclusions

Our approach offers a deterministic and principled alternative to stochastic or heuristic methods commonly used in non-convex optimization. It is based on lifting the problem to an infinite dimensional setting by embedding it into an optimal control framework. It allows us to obtain trajectories whose convergence towards the set of global minimizers can be rigorously analyzed and quantified. This formulation relies solely on deterministic control principles (without invoking randomness or sampling) and thus provides both theoretical guarantees and a clear dynamical interpretation of the optimization process.

Appendix A
Elements from nonsmooth analysis

We first review several standard definitions and results from nonsmooth analysis, after which we present the key result in Lemma
A.7
. These definitions will also be used in Appendix
B
and in Appendix
C
.

Definition A.1
.

(See
[
6
, II.1, page 29]
)
Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open set. We call super-differential of
u(\cdot)
at the point
x
the set

D^{+}u(x):=\left\{\;p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\limsup\limits_{y\to x,y\in\Omega}\,\frac{u(y)-u(x)-p\cdot(y-x)}{|y-x|}\leq 0\;\right\}.

We call sub-differential (or semi-differential) of
u(\cdot)
at the point
x
the set

D^{-}u(x):=\left\{\;p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\liminf\limits_{y\to x,y\in\Omega}\,\frac{u(y)-u(x)-p\cdot(y-x)}{|y-x|}\geq 0\;\right\}.

Definition A.2

(Dini derivatives)
.

(See
[
6
, Definition III.2.36, page 125]
])
Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
an open set. the lower Dini derivative, or upper contingent derivative, of
u(\cdot)
at the point
x\in\Omega
in the direction
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

\displaystyle\partial^{-}u(x;q)

\displaystyle:=\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
p\rightarrow q\end{subarray}}\;\frac{u(x+t\,p)-u(x)}{t}

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{\delta>0}\,\inf\left\{\frac{u(x+tq+tz)-u(x)}{t}\,:\,0<t\leq\delta\,\;|z|\leq\delta\right\}\;\geq-\infty.

Similarly the upper Dini derivative, or lower contingent derivative of
u(\cdot)
at
x
in the direction
q
is

\displaystyle\partial^{+}u(x;q)

\displaystyle:=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
p\rightarrow q\end{subarray}}\;\frac{u(x+t\,p)-u(x)}{t}\;\leq+\infty.

One notes in particular

\displaystyle\partial^{-}u(x;q)\leq\liminf\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{u(x+t\,p)-u(x)}{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad\partial^{+}u(x;q)\geq\limsup\limits_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{u(x+t\,p)-u(x)}{t}

with equalities if
u(\cdot)
is Lipschitz.

Lemma A.3
.

(See
[
6
, Lemma III.2.37, page 126]
)
Let
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
and
\Omega
an open set. Then

\displaystyle D^{-}u(x)

\displaystyle=\big\{\,p\,:\;p\cdot q\leq\partial^{-}u(x;q)\quad\forall\,q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\big\},

\displaystyle D^{+}u(x)

\displaystyle=\big\{\,p\,:\;p\cdot q\geq\partial^{+}u(x;q)\quad\forall\,q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\big\}.

Definition A.4
.

[Generalized directional derivative](See
[
12
, Page 70]
)
Let
\phi(\cdot)
be Lipschitz near a given point
x
. The generalized directional derivative of
\phi(\cdot)
at
x
in the direction
v
, denoted by
\phi^{\circ}(x;v)
, is defined as

\phi^{\circ}(x;v):=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\rightarrow x\\
t\rightarrow 0^{+}\end{subarray}}\;\frac{\phi(y+t\,v)-\phi(y)}{t}.

Definition A.5
.

[Generalized gradient](See
[
13
, Page 27]
)
The generalized gradient of
\phi(\cdot)
at
x
, denoted by
\partial^{C}\phi(x)
, is given by

\partial^{C}\phi(x):=\left\{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;\phi^{\circ}(x;v)\geqslant\langle\zeta,v\rangle\quad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

Proposition A.6
.

(See
[
12
, Proposition 1.5, page 73]
)
Let
\phi(\cdot)
be Lipschitz near a given point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The following hold

(a)

\partial^{C}\phi(x)
is a non-empty, convex and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(b)

For every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has in particular

\phi^{\circ}(x;v)=\max\{\;\langle\zeta,v\rangle\;:\;\zeta\in\partial^{C}\phi(x)\;\}

(c)

\zeta\in\partial^{C}\phi(x)
if and only if
\langle\zeta,v\rangle\leq\phi^{\circ}(x;v)\quad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(d)

\partial^{C}\phi(x)
is upper-semicontinuous at

With the preceding results and definitions, we are now ready to state and prove a key result that will enable the proof of Lemma
4.2
in Appendix
C
. We recall that Lemma
4.2
is used at the end of the proof of Proposition
4.4
.

Lemma A.7

(Subdifferential of squared distance)
.

Let
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a non-empty and compact set. Define
\;\phi(x):=\frac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})^{2}.\;
Then

(i)

\phi(\cdot)
is locally Lipschitz.

(ii)

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
q\in\partial^{C}\phi(x)
, one has

|q|\leq\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})
.

(iii)

If
y(\cdot):[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is absolutely continuous, then
t\mapsto\phi(y(t))
is absolutely continuous and there exists
q(t)\in\partial^{C}\phi(y(t))
such that

\frac{\mbox{\rm d}}{\mbox{\rm d}t}\phi(y(t))=\langle q(t),\dot{y}(t)\rangle\quad\text{for a.a. }t\in[0,T].

Proof.

First we prove
(i)
.

Using the triangular inequality, the distance map
x\mapsto\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})
is
1
-Lipschitz. Hence for all
x,y\in K
a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leq\frac{1}{2}\big(\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})+\operatorname{dist}(y,\mathscr{O})\big)|\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})-\operatorname{dist}(y,\mathscr{O})|\leq 2L_{K}|x-y|.

with
L_{K}:=\sup_{x\in K}\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})<\infty
.

Next we prove
(ii)
.

For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since
\mathscr{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is non-empty and compact, the projection set

\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y):=\arg\min_{z\in\mathscr{O}}|y-z|\,=\{z\in\mathscr{O}\,:\,|y-z|=\min\limits_{\omega\in\mathscr{O}}|y-\omega|=\operatorname{dist}(y,\mathscr{O})\}

is non-empty and compact. Fix
z_{y}\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y)
.
Then for any
t>0
and
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\phi(y+th)\leq\frac{1}{2}|y+th-z_{y}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}|y-z_{y}|^{2}+t\langle y-z_{y},h\rangle+\frac{t^{2}}{2}|h|^{2},

hence

\frac{\phi(y+th)-\phi(y)}{t}\leq\langle y-z_{y},h\rangle+\frac{t}{2}|h|^{2}\;\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y)}\langle y-z,h\rangle+\frac{t}{2}|h|^{2}

The
\limsup
as
y\to x
,
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
yields the generalized directional derivative (Definition
A.4
)

(A.1)

\phi^{\circ}(x;h)\ \leq\ \limsup_{y\to x}\ \sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y)}\langle y-z,h\rangle

We claim that

(A.2)

\limsup_{y\to x}\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y)}\langle y-z,h\rangle\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)}\langle x-z,h\rangle.

Pick any converging sequence
y_{k}\to x
. Since
\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y_{k})
is compact, for each
k
, choose an element
z_{k}\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y_{k})
such that

\langle y_{k}-z_{k},h\rangle=\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y_{k})}\langle y_{k}-z,h\rangle.

Since
y_{k}\to x
and
|y_{k}-z_{k}|=\operatorname{dist}(y_{k},\mathscr{O})
is bounded, the sequence
\{z_{k}\}_{k}
is also bounded and admits a convergent subsequence (again denoted by
z_{k}
) such that
z_{k}\to z^{*}\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)
. Then,

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(y_{k})}\langle y_{k}-z,h\rangle=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\,\langle y_{k}-z_{k},h\rangle=\langle x-z^{*},h\rangle\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)}\langle x-z,h\rangle,

which proves (
A.2
). Combining (
A.1
)–(
A.2
) yields

\phi^{\circ}(x;h)\ \leq\ \sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)}\langle x-z,h\rangle.

Recalling Definition
A.5
and statement
(c)
in Proposition
A.6
,
for any
q\in\partial^{C}\phi(x)
and any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one has

\langle q,h\rangle\leq\phi^{\circ}(x;h)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)}\langle x-z,h\rangle.

Taking the supremum over
|h|\leq 1
gives

|q|=\sup_{|h|\leq 1}\langle q,h\rangle\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbf{P}_{\mathscr{O}}(x)}|x-z|=\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O}).

Now we prove

\displaystyle=e^{-\lambda\,t}|\phi(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t))|\;\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\lambda\,t}|y_{x}^{\alpha}(t)-z|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{3}+C_{4}\,t^{2})e^{-\lambda\,t}\quad\text{ where }C_{3}:=\frac{1}{2}|z|^{2}+|x|^{2},\;C_{4}:=M^{2}

and then
\int_{0}^{\infty}|\psi(t)|\mbox{\rm d}t\leq\frac{C_{3}}{\lambda}+2\frac{C_{4}}{\lambda^{3}}<\infty
. Together with the previous argument, this ensures that
\psi_{\infty}=0
. Going back to (
C.4
), after sending
T\to+\infty
, the term
e^{-\lambda\,T}\phi(y_{x}^{\alpha}(T))=\psi(T)\to 0
, and one gets

p\,\phi(x)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(t)|^{2}+\mathfrak{c}\,\phi(y_{x}^{\alpha}(t))\right)\,e^{-\lambda\,t}\,\mbox{\rm d}t.

This last inequality being true for any
\alpha(\cdot)\in\mathcal{A}
, in particular when taking the infimum, one recovers the desired result

C\,\operatorname{dist}(x,\mathscr{O})^{2}=p\,\phi(x)\leq R_{\mathfrak{c}}(x,\mathscr{O})

where
C=p/2
and
p=(-\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+4\mathfrak{c}}\,)/2
.
∎
