Local well-posedness of the minimum energy estimator for a defocusing cubic wave equation
Jesper Schröder †

Abstract
. This work is concerned with the minimum energy estimator for a nonlinear hyperbolic partial differential equation. The Mortensen observer – originally introduced for the energy-optimal reconstruction of the state of nonlinear finite-dimensional systems – is formulated for a disturbed cubic wave equation and the associated observer equation is derived. An in depth study of the associated optimal control problem and sensitivity analysis of the corresponding value function reveals that the energy optimal state estimator is well-defined. Deploying a classical fixed point argument we proceed to show that the observer equation is locally well-posed.



Keywords:
nonlinear observer design, minimum energy estimation, cubic wave equation, optimal control, Riccati equations

AMS subject classification:
35L71, 49J99, 93B53

1.
Introduction

We consider the task of reconstructing the state of the system that is modeled by a cubic wave equation subject to disturbances given as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}{u}+{u}^{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta{u}+v\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle{u}(0)

\displaystyle=u_{1}+\eta_{1}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{u}(0)

\displaystyle=u_{2}+\eta_{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the physical domain with sufficiently smooth boundary and the equation is complemented by homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The displacement of the wave at time
t\in[0,T]
and position
x\in\Omega
is denoted by
u(t,x)
. As usual
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian on the spatial variable. We work in the usual
energy space

\mathcal{E}\coloneqq H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and the modeled initial displacement and velocity are given by
u_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
u_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively. Finally,
v\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
denotes the
unknown
deterministic disturbance in the system dynamics while
\eta=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in\mathcal{E}
represents the
unknown
error in the initial state. The objective is the approximation of the displacement
u
and velocity
\partial_{t}u
based on a disturbed output that is available through measurements taken from the system. The output is modeled as

(1.2)

y_{\mathrm{abs}}=C\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\partial_{t}u\end{bmatrix}+\mu,

where
C
is a bounded, linear operator mapping from
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
to the Hilbert space
Y
. The
unknown
disturbance in the output is represented by
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T;Y)
.

1.1.
Motivation and available literature

Semilinear wave equations with monomial nonlinearities of the form
u\mapsto u^{2p+1}
frequently appear in mathematical physics
[
21
,
25
,
41
]
and are well-studied by the mathematical community. In particular, systems with physical dimension three have been of interest and authors differentiate the
subcritical
(
p<2
), the
critical
(
p=2
) and the
supercritical
(
p>2
) case. Proofs of well-posedness of the equations in various settings are given in
[
19
,
26
,
39
]
and
[
15
,
27
]
study the wave equations with respect to attractors for the subcritical and the critical case. Further
[
13
]
discusses stabilization and control of the subcritical case and
[
31
]
is concerned with optimal control of the critical case. We note that the positive sign of the nonlinearity is essential, as a negative sign is well-known to cause ill-posedness due to finite-time blow-ups, cf.,
[
33
]
.

The study of designs for the state-reconstruction for disturbed dynamical systems has a rich history going back at least to Wiener’s work
[
42
]
who interpreted the disturbances as random processes laying the foundation to the field of
filtering theory
. In their pioneering works tackling time-discrete systems
[
28
]
and time-continuous systems
[
29
]
Kalman and Bucy introduce a filter optimal in squared expectation for linear dynamics. The demand to generalize the widely popular
Kalman(-Bucy) filter
to nonlinear dynamics has inspired numerous designs such as the the
extended Kalman filter
obtained by linearization of the dynamics along the filter and the more elaborate
unscented Kalman filter

[
35
]
. While they are successfully applied in various settings, their derivation is purely heuristic and they lack the optimality property of the original Kalman filter.

Taking a deterministic viewpoint, in
[
37
]
Mortensen formulates and formally reviews the so-called
maximum-likelihood estimator
, today also known as the
Mortensen observer
, which is constructed as the trajectory minimizing the squared energy of the unknown disturbances. As mentioned in the original work, applied to linear systems the
Mortensen observer
reduces to the Kalman filter equations, see also
[
43
]
for the deterministic derivation of the Kalman filter. Therefore it can be interpreted as a truncation free generalization of the (deterministic interpretation of the) Kalman filter to nonlinear systems. Its dependence on the
value function
corresponding to the optimal control problem associated with the energy minimization, however, inflicts the
curse of dimensionality
making its numerical realization a challenging task.
Since its formal introduction the Mortensen observer has been studied for numerous classes of finite-dimensional systems, first in
[
17
]
under the assumption of smooth dynamics with globally bounded derivatives followed by
[
30
]
showing convergence of the observer assuming the dynamics to be globally Lipschitz. More recently a discrete-time version for affine dynamics was analyzed in
[
36
]
and
[
6
]
proposes an approximation of the observer based on neural networks. The authors of
[
9
]
introduce and analyze a version for non-smooth systems. In particular a lack of equivalence with the stochastic interpretation is shown. In
[
8
]
a proof of well-posedness is given that drops the assumption of global Lipschitz continuity and in turn yields local results and
[
7
]
proposes a numerical realization based on a polynomial approximation of the value function.

While the aforementioned literature exclusively discusses systems of finite dimension, generalizations to systems governed by partial differential equations are available for some of the concepts. We refer to the review article
[
11
]
on infinite-dimensional filtering that in particular discusses the Kalman filter and its well-posedness, see also
[
4
]
. Taking the deterministic perspective the authors of
[
1
]
were recently able to show well-posedness of the extended Kalman filter for a class of infinite-dimensional systems. While a wide array of observer designs for systems governed by nonlinear partial differential equations is available in the literature (e.g.,
[
2
,
24
,
38
,
45
]
),
to the best of the author’s knowledge no rigorous analysis of the Mortensen observer applied to infinite-dimensional systems is available yet.

1.2.
The Mortensen observer for a nonlinear wave equation

In the following we introduce the Mortensen observer for the system described by (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) and present the formal derivation of the associated observer equation. Due to its formal nature it is conducted entirely analogous to the finite-dimensional case, see e.g.
[
6
]
. For the sake of self-containedness we present a brief exposition of the concept.

In order to follow the structure found in
[
6
]
we state the first order form of the wave equation and its associated output, which reads

(1.3)

\displaystyle\dot{w}(t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathrm{Id}\\
\Delta&0\end{bmatrix}w(t)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(t)\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon F(w(t))}+Bv(t),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in(0,T),

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=w_{0}+\eta,

\displaystyle y_{\mathrm{abs}}(t)

\displaystyle=Cw(t)+\mu(t),

where

w(t)=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}(t)\\
w_{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u(t)\\
\partial_{t}u(t)\end{bmatrix}
,

Bv=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
v\end{bmatrix}
,

w_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}
,
and

\eta=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{1}\\
\eta_{2}\end{bmatrix}
.
The construction of the state estimate
\widehat{w}(t)
at time
t\in(0,T]
is based on the optimal control problem

(1.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{w,v}J(w,v;t)&=\frac{1}{2}\left\|w(0)-w_{0}\right\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|v(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\alpha\left\|y_{\mathrm{abs}}(s)-Cw(s)\right\|_{Y}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{s.t.}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \dot{w}(s)&=F(w(s))+Bv(s),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ s\in(0,t),\\
w(t)&=\xi,\end{split}

with
y_{\mathrm{abs}}
obtained via measurement and fixed final value
\xi
.
Intuitively speaking, (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) formalizes the task of finding energy minimal disturbances
v
,
\eta
, and
\mu
that fit the output
y
measured until time
t
and the fixed state
\xi
at time
t
. The associated value function

\mathcal{V}(t,\xi;y_{\mathrm{abs}})=\inf J(w,v;t)\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{subject to}\penalty\ \eqref{eq: StateIntro}

represents the
minimal amount of energy
required to enforce the state
\xi
at time
t
and is formally associated with the
Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation

(1.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)=-\left(D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi),F(\xi)\right)_{\mathcal{E}}-\frac{1}{2}\|B^{*}D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y_{\mathrm{abs}}(t)-C\xi\|_{Y}^{2}.

The Mortensen observer is defined pointwise in time as the energy minimizing state, i.e.,

(1.7)

\widehat{w}(t)\coloneqq\argmin_{\xi\in\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi;y_{\mathrm{abs}}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ t\in[0,T].

For a derivation of a governing equation first note that it immediately follows

D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall t\in[0,T].

Taking a time derivative and applying the chain rule yields

(1.8)

\partial_{t}D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))=-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))\,\dot{\widehat{w}}(t).

Taking a derivative with respect to
\xi
along
\widehat{w}(t)
of the HJB (
1.6
) shows

D_{\xi}\partial_{t}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))=-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))\,F(\widehat{w}(t))-\alpha C^{*}\left(y_{\mathrm{abs}}(t)-C\widehat{w}(t)\right).

Switching the order of differentiation on the left hand side and inserting (
1.8
) leads to

D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))\,\dot{\widehat{w}}(t)=D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))\,F(\widehat{w}(t))+\alpha C^{*}\left(y_{\mathrm{abs}}(t)-C\widehat{w}(t)\right).

Finally by assuming
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))
is an isomorphism and applying its inverse we arrive at the Mortensen observer equation

(1.9)

\dot{\widehat{w}}(t)=F(\widehat{w}(t))+\alpha D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\widehat{w}(t))^{-1}C^{*}\left(y_{\mathrm{abs}}(t)-C\widehat{w}(t)\right).

Remark 1.1
.

We comment on several critical points regarding the optimal control problem (
1.4
)-(
1.5
).

(i)

Even though (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) is formulated as an optimal control problem, we do not have any means to influence the system. Here, the optimal control formulation is to be understood as a tool to reconstruct energy minimal disturbances only. Nonetheless, since the technical analysis of the problem is tackled using strategies from optimal control, throughout this work
v
will be referred to as a control.

(ii)

The dynamical system (
1.3
) as well as the observer equation (
1.9
) are given an initial condition and evolve forward in time. The analysis, however, is in large parts concerned with the state equation (
1.5
) which evolves backward in time. While this poses a major challenge for parabolic dynamics such as diffusion, it does not pose any issue for the undamped hyperbolic dynamics under consideration, cf.,
Lemma

2.2
below.

1.3.
Contributions of this work

The foregoing formal derivation illustrates the most pressing questions regarding well-posedness of the concept. In the following we summarize our contributions:

(i)

We perform a rigorous analysis of the optimal control problem in order to show local
C^{\infty}
regularity of the associated value function
\mathcal{V}
with respect to space and measurement, cf.,
Corollary

4.4
. This in particular implies the existence of its second derivative
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}
.

(ii)

By analyzing the linearized optimal control we conduct the proof of
Proposition

5.4
stating that under assumptions on the data
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}
is a coercive bilinear form, implying that the associated linear mapping is an isomorphism and hence invertible and further that
\mathcal{V}
is locally strictly coercive.

(iii)

By an application of the implicit function theorem we show existence of a unique root of
D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot)
, ensuring the well-definedness of the Mortensen observer as a pointwise in time minimizer of the squared energy of disturbances, cf.,
Corollary

6.4
.

(iv)

Finally we utilize existence and boundedness of
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}^{-1}
to apply a fixed point argument and show existence of a locally unique mild solution to the observer equation, cf.,
Theorem

6.6
.

In large parts the paper follows the structure of
[
8
]
where we investigated these issues for finite-dimensional systems. In the setting considered in this work, however, solutions of the state equation are less regular and therefore alternative techniques are deployed for the analysis of the second derivative of the value function, cf.,
Section

5
and well-posedness of the observer equation, cf.,
Section

6.2
. In particular, the analytical arguments deployed here do not rely on a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation.

2.
Analytical framework

This section presents the basic notions required for our analysis. In particular appropriate solution concepts are presented and well-posedness of the wave equation is discussed.

We commence by stating a lemma essential for treating the cubic wave equation in three spatial dimensions.
Only because the spatial dimension is set to three we have the continuous embedding
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
, see
[
14
, P. II, Sec. 5.6, Thm. 2]
, and throughout this work denote the associated constant by
c_{\mathrm{em}}>0
.
An application of Hölders inequality yields the following estimate.

Lemma 2.1
.

The product of three functions
w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
lies in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and it holds

\|w_{1}\,w_{2}\,w_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq c_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}\,\|w_{1}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\|w_{2}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\|w_{3}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The assertion is a direct consequence of the embedding
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
and Hölder’s inequality.
∎

2.1.
The forward equation

In this subsection we discuss the solution theory concerned with the forward equation (
1.1
).
For the sake of readability for
t\in(0,T]
we introduce the notation
\mathcal{L}_{t}=L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
To simplify the presentation for the moment we set
\eta=0
and the equation reads

(FWE)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}u+u^{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta u+v\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle(u(0),\partial_{t}u(0))

\displaystyle=(u_{1},u_{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

As above we assume
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
.
In the following we briefly introduce the analytical tools required to formulate the concept of a
mild solution
to (
FWE
). The energy space
\mathcal{E}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
is equipped with the scalar product

\left(\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}\\
p_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)_{\mathcal{E}}\coloneqq(w_{1},p_{1})_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}+(w_{2},p_{2})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

and forms a Hilbert space. In order to formulate the wave equation as a first order problem in
\mathcal{E}
consider the linear, unbounded operator in
\mathcal{E}
defined via

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{D}(\Delta)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathcal{A}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
\Delta&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{2}\\
\Delta w_{1}\end{bmatrix},

where the Laplacian
\Delta
is understood as a linear unbounded operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)
defined via

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\left\{w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\colon\exists f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\colon(\nabla w,\nabla\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=-(f,\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\forall\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\right\}

\displaystyle\Delta w

\displaystyle=f.

We set
w_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}
and formally rewrite (
FWE
) as the
Cauchy problem

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}w(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\,w(t)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(t)+v(t)\end{bmatrix}\penalty\ \text{ for }t\in(0,T),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ w(0)=w_{0}.

Note that any sufficiently smooth classical solution
u
of (
FWE
) yields a tuple
w=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\partial_{t}u\end{bmatrix}
satisfying (
2.2
).
Before defining a proper solution concept associated with the Cauchy problem we state some properties of
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The linear unbounded operator
\mathcal{A}
defined in (
2.1
) is skew-adjoint, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A}^{*})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
and for all
w\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})
it holds
\mathcal{A}^{*}w=-\mathcal{A}w
. Further
\mathcal{A}
generates a unitary group on
\mathcal{E}
denoted by
t\mapsto e^{\mathcal{A}t}
, i.e., for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
w\in\mathcal{E}
we have

e^{\mathcal{A}t}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \|e^{\mathcal{A}t}w\|_{\mathcal{E}}=\|w\|_{\mathcal{E}}.

Proof.

An application of integration by parts shows the skew-adjointness. According to
[
23
, Rem. 6.2.6]
any skew-adjoint operator generates a unitary group and the assertion is shown.
∎

In particular
\mathcal{A}
generates a strongly continuous semi-group denoted by
e^{\mathcal{A}t}
allowing the following definition of
mild solutions
. For convenience we denote the space in which the solutions lie by
\mathcal{C}_{t}\coloneqq C([0,t];\mathcal{E})
.

Definition 2.3
.

A function
w
is called a mild solution to (
FWE
) on
[0,T]
if
w\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
and for
t\in[0,T]
it holds

(2.3)

w(t)=e^{\mathcal{A}t}w_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(s)+v(s)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Remark 2.4
.

(i)

We note that the integrand in (
2.3
) is not trivially well defined. Only due to
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
Lemma

2.1
we have
w_{1}^{3}(s)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. This does not need to hold for other exponents or dimensions of
\Omega
.

(ii)

As mentioned above a classical solution
u
of (
FWE
) is not a mild solution according to
Definition

2.3
. Other authors avoid this issue using a different notion of mild solution, see e.g.,
[
31
, Def. 2.1]
. The definition stated in this work instead is in line with the notion of mild solutions for general Cauchy problems, cf.,
[
12
, Def. 5.1.4]
and is better suited for our analysis.

(iii)

Any mild solution
w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}
satisfies
w_{2}=\partial_{t}w_{1}
. This is due to the structure of
\mathcal{A}
and can be seen by noting that
w
solves a linear problem with a state dependent inhomogeneity and considering
[
3
, Prop. 3.1.16]
.

We additionally introduce the notion of
weak solutions
.

Definition 2.5
.

A function
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
with
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
is called a weak solution to (
FWE
) if

u(0)=u_{1}
,
\partial_{t}u(0)=u_{2}

and additionally for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\Omega)
it holds

(2.4)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(u^{3},\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{0}^{T}(v,\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Note that we have
u^{3}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, and hence
\partial_{tt}u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
. Therefore
u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u\in C([0,T];H^{-1}(\Omega))
and the initial conditions are well-defined.

The concept of a weak solution already makes sense for initial values from
L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{-1}(\Omega)
and right hand sides
v\in L^{1}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
, leading to less regular solutions. For our purpose we assume more regular data leading to equivalence of mild and weak solutions. While the correspondence of mild and weak solutions is well-known in the general linear setting, see e.g.
[
12
, Thm. 5.1.10]
, in our case this issue requires some extra care.

Lemma 2.6
.

A function
w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
\partial_{t}w_{1}\end{bmatrix}
is a mild solution to (
FWE
) if and only if
w\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
and
w_{1}
is a weak solution.

The proof can be done analogous to the proof of
[
31
, Lem. 2.6]
and is omitted here. Note that at this point there might be discontinuous weak solutions that do not yield any mild solution. This possibility is ruled out by the following result on well-posedness of weak solutions.

Proposition 2.7
.

For any
u_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
u_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
there exists exactly one weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{E})
to (
FWE
). Further
u\in C([0,T];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
.

The proof can be conducted by a well-known
Galerkin approximation
. For a thorough presentation we refer to classical results by Lions. Existence and uniqueness of a weak solution
u
are presented as Théorème 1.1 and Théorème 1.2 in
[
32
]
, respectively. The asserted regularity follows by noting that
u
is the weak solution of a linear wave equation with inhomogeneity
f=v-u^{3}\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
and considering
[
34
, Ch. 3, Thm. 8.2]
.

Well-posedness of mild solutions follows immediately.

Corollary 2.8
.

For
u_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,
u_{2}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
there exists exactly one mild solution
w\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
to (
FWE
).

From here on the term solution will refer to a mild solution unless stated otherwise. We conclude the section by defining the
nominal trajectory
.
The model equation in its strong form is given by
(
1.1
) with
v=0
and
\eta=0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}\tilde{u}+\tilde{u}^{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta\tilde{u}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }(0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle(\tilde{u}(0),\partial_{t}\tilde{u}(0))

\displaystyle=(u_{1},u_{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

According to
Corollary

2.8
there exists a unique mild solution which throughout this chapter we denote by
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
and refer to it as the
nominal trajectory
or
model trajectory
.

2.2.
The backward state equation

This section introduces solution concepts for the backward state equation of the optimal control problem and states that results from the previous section transfer. Its strong form reads

(SWE)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}u+u^{3}

\displaystyle=\Delta u+v

\displaystyle\text{ in }(0,t)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t))

\displaystyle=(\tilde{w}_{1}(t),\partial_{t}\tilde{w}_{1}(t))+\xi\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

Unlike the model it is not considered on
[0,T]
but on
[0,t]
, where
t\in(0,T]
is fixed. The disturbances
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
is fixed and the final value consists of the model trajectory at time
t
and a fixed disturbance
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
. The equation is completed with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions. The following definition of mild solutions for the backward state equation relies on the fact that
\mathcal{A}
generates a group.

Definition 2.9
.

A function
w
is called a mild solution to (
SWE
) if
w\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
and for all
s\in(0,t]
it holds

(2.5)

w(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\left(\tilde{w}(t)+\xi\right)-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(\tau)+v(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Remark 2.10
.

To illustrate the relationship between the forward and the backward formulation and for later use we note that any solution
w
of (
FWE
) can be viewed as the solution of a backward problem on
(0,t)
with final value
w_{t}=w(t)
, i.e.,

(2.6)

w(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}w_{t}-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(\tau)+v(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t].

Weak solutions for the backward are defined entirely analogous to the forward case.

Definition 2.11
.

A function
u\in L^{\infty}(0,t;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
with
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))
is called a weak solution to (
SWE
) if

(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t))=\tilde{w}(t)+\xi

and additionally for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,t)\times\Omega)
it holds

(2.7)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}(u^{3},\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s=-\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla u,\nabla\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}(v,\varphi)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s.

The results presented in
Remark

2.4
(iii) and
Lemma

2.6
for the forward equation can be proven analogously for the backward case. Reversing the time in (
2.7
) via
\overleftarrow{u}(s)=u(t-s)
it is shown that
u
is a weak backward solution if and only if
\overleftarrow{u}
is a weak solution to a forward problem with initial condition
\overleftarrow{u}(0)=\tilde{w}_{1}+\xi_{1}
and
\partial_{t}\overleftarrow{u}(0)=-\tilde{w}_{1}-\xi_{1}
and the well-posedness transfers.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
t\in(0,T]
,
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
. Then there exists a unique weak solution
u
to (
SWE
). It holds
u\in C([0,t];H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u\in C([0,t];L^{2}(\Omega))
. Further (
SWE
) admits a unique mild solutions and it is given by
w=\begin{bmatrix}u\\
\partial_{t}u\end{bmatrix}
.

3.
Optimal control problem

In this section we give a rigorous formulation of the optimal control problem analyze it with respect to existence of solutions, and the first order optimality condition.
The cost functional of the optimal control problem for the construction of the Mortensen observer for a fixed
t\in(0,T]
is given via

(OCP)

\displaystyle J(\cdot,\cdot\,;t,y)\colon\mathcal{C}_{t}\times\mathcal{L}_{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\left(w,v\right)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\|w(0)-w_{0}\right\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|y-C\left(w-\tilde{w}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2},

subject to

e^{t}(w,v,\xi)=0,

where the constraint
e^{t}\colon\mathcal{C}_{t}\times\mathcal{L}_{t}\times\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{t}
is defined as

(3.1)

e^{t}(w,v,\xi)(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\left(\tilde{w}(t)+\xi\right)-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}+v\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\,-\,w(s).

For
t\in(0,T]
we denote
\mathcal{Y}_{t}\coloneqq L^{2}(0,t;Y)
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
is the measured output relative to the modeled output, i.e.,

y(s)=y_{\mathrm{abs}}(s)-C\tilde{w}(s),

where
y_{\mathrm{abs}}\in\mathcal{Y_{T}}
is the given absolute disturbed measurement. Finally
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
is a fixed disturbance in the final value. For convenience we denote
c_{C}=\|C\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E};Y)}=\|C^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(Y^{*};\mathcal{E}^{*})}
.

Note that (
OCP
) is trivial for any
t\in(0,T]
in case
(\xi,y)=(0,0)
. Here the minimizing pair is given by the model and
J(\tilde{w},0;t,0)=0
.

Remark 3.1
.

We stress that this formulation differs from the original exposition in (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) in two subtle aspects. The tracking term is transformed equivalently to depend on
y
instead of
y_{\mathrm{abs}}
. Further, in the original formulation
\xi
represents the final state of the system, while here it describes its difference from the nominal trajectory. Denoting the value function associated with (
OCP
) by
\mathcal{V}
and the one from the original formulation (
1.4
)-(
1.5
) by
\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{orig}}
we have

\mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{orig}}(t,\xi+\tilde{w}(t),y_{\mathrm{abs}})=\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y).

In a sense, the optimal control problem was shifted by
\tilde{w}
. As a result our local analysis is performed on more convenient neighborhoods of zero, rather than neighborhoods of the nominal trajectory and its associated output.

3.1.
The data-to-state operator

Based on
Corollary

2.12
we define the data-to-state operator and proceed to show smoothness and uniform boundedness of said operator.

Definition 3.2
.

The control-to-state operator

\mathcal{S}^{t}\colon\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{L}_{t}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{t}

maps
(v,\xi)
to the solution
w
of (
SWE
) corresponding to
\xi
and
v
.

In the following we discuss the differentiability of
\mathcal{S}^{t}
and characterize its derivatives using the implicit function theorem. For the benefit of readability here we abbreviate
w=\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)
. Note that even though we only give explicit formulas for the partial derivatives, formulas for the full derivative follow with identities of the form
D\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[(u,\eta)]=D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u]+D_{\xi}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\eta]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The data-to-state operator is of class
C^{\infty}
on its domain and the partial derivatives can be characterized as unique solutions of linear problems.
Let
\xi
,
\eta
,
\psi\in\mathcal{E}
and
v
,
u
,
h\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
.

Then
D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u]
solves

(3.2)

z(s)=-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-3w_{1}^{2}(\tau)z_{1}(\tau)+u(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t]

and
D_{\xi}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\eta]
solves

(3.3)

z(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\eta-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-3w_{1}^{2}(\tau)z_{1}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t].

For partial derivatives of second order consider the formula

(3.4)

z(s)=-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-3w_{1}^{2}(\tau)z_{1}(\tau)-6w_{1}(\tau)\,p_{1}(\tau)\,q_{1}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Then
D_{vv}^{2}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u,h]
solves (
3.4
) if we set
p=D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[h]
and
q=D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u]
,
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\eta,\psi]
solves (
3.4
) with
p=D_{\xi}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\psi]
and
q=D_{\xi}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\eta]
, and finally
D_{\xi v}^{2}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u,\eta]
solves (
3.4
) with
p=D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[u]
and
q=D_{\xi}\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)[\eta]
.

Proof.

We apply the implicit function theorem to the mapping
e^{t}
.
By an application of
[
20
, Thm. 7]
it can be shown that the Nemytskii operator induced by
w\mapsto w^{3}
mapping
L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{6}(\Omega))
to
L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega))
is of class
C^{\infty}
. All remaining terms are (affine) linear bounded operators. Therefore
e^{t}
as defined in (
3.1
) is of class
C^{\infty}
.
To show the required bijectivity of the partial derivative note that for
z,f\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
it holds

D_{w}e^{t}(\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi),v,\xi)[z]=f

if and only if for all
s\in[0,t]
it holds

(3.5)

z(s)=\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
3\mathcal{S}(v,\xi)_{1}^{2}z_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau-f(s).

We now show that for any given
f\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
there exists exactly one
z\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
satisfying (
3.5
). To that end we adjust the technique applied in
[
31
, Thm. 4.3]
and choose a partition
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=t
such that

3\,(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\,c_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}\,\penalty\ \|\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}^{2}<\tfrac{1}{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ i=0,\ldots,n-1,

where
c_{\mathrm{em}}
is the constant associated with
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{6}(\Omega)
. For
i=0,\ldots,n-1
let
\xi_{i}\in\mathcal{E}
be fixed and define the operators

\mathcal{T}_{i}\colon C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})\rightarrow C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})

via
\mathcal{T}_{i}\,h=z
, where for
s\in[t_{i},t_{i+1}]

z(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t_{i+1}-s)}\xi_{i}+\int_{s}^{t_{i+1}}e^{-\mathcal{A}(s-\tau)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
3\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)_{1}^{2}(\tau)\,h_{1}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau-f(s).

For
h_{1},h_{2}\in C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})
it holds

\|\mathcal{T}_{i}h_{1}-\mathcal{T}_{i}h_{2}\|_{C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})}\leq 3c_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}(t_{i+1}-t_{i})\|\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)\|_{C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})}^{2}\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})},

showing that
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is a contraction. Banach’s fixed point theorem implies existence of a unique fixed point
z_{i}\in C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})
depending on
\xi_{i}
. Iteratively setting
\xi_{n-1}=0
and
\xi_{n-1-i}=z_{n-i}(t_{n-i})
for
i=0,\ldots,n-2
yields functions
z_{i}\in C([t_{i},t_{i+1}];\mathcal{E})
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1
that can be glued together to obtain a solution of (
3.5
). The uniqueness of the fixed points implies uniqueness of the solution to (
3.5
).

The implicit function theorem now yields the asserted regularity of
\mathcal{S}^{t}
and equations (
3.2
) and (
3.3
). Taking appropriate derivatives of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) yields formulas for the second order partial derivatives. Due to the regularity of
w
unique solvability of the linear equations (
3.2
) - (
3.4
) follows by reversing time and considering
[
5
, II Prop. 3.4]
.
∎

In the following we show appropriate estimates for the solution
\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)
. Our local analysis, however, requires an estimate for the solution’s distance to the nominal trajectory. Such bounds are obtained by application of a fixed point argument and only hold for sufficiently small
\xi
and
v
. More specifically we show that the unique solution of the equation satisfied by
\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)-\tilde{w}
lies in a certain neighborhood of zero.
First we analyze the linear part of said equation using the definitions and techniques presented in
[
5
, II Sec. 3.4]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\tilde{w}
be the nominal trajectory and let
t\in(0,T]
,
f\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
. Then there exists exactly one solution
z\in L^{2}(0,t;\mathcal{E})
to

(3.6)

\displaystyle z(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\xi-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(\tau)z_{1}(\tau)+f(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ s\in[0,t].

It holds
z\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
and there exists
c_{w}>0
depending on
T
but independent of
f
,
\xi
and
t
such that

\left\|z\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|f\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right).

Proof.

A time transformation unveils that
z
being a solution to (
3.6
) is equivalent to
s\mapsto\overleftarrow{z}(s)\coloneqq z(t-s)
being a solution to

\overleftarrow{z}(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}s}\xi-\int_{0}^{s}e^{-\mathcal{A}(s-\tau)}\left(-F(\tau)\overleftarrow{z}(\tau)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
f(t-\tau)\end{bmatrix}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau,

where for
s\in[0,T]
the operator
F(s)\colon\mathcal{E}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
is defined via

(3.7)

F(s)\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(s)\,w_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
, we have
F(s)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
for every
s\in[0,T]
and further
s\mapsto F(s)x
is continuous from
[0,T]
to
\mathcal{E}
for every
x\in\mathcal{E}
. The existence of a unique solution then follows from
[
5
, II Prop. 3.4]
.
The regularity of
\tilde{w}
yields uniform boundedness of
\|F(s)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E};\mathcal{E})}
in
s\in[0,T]
. Standard estimates of (
3.6
) together with Gronwall’s inequality yield the estimate for the solution.
∎

With this tool at hand the fixed point argument can be realized.

Proposition 3.5
.

There exists
\delta_{1}>0
independent of
t
such that for any
\xi\in\mathcal{E}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
satisfying

\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}<\delta_{1}

it holds

\left\|\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)-\tilde{w}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right),

where
c_{w}
is the constant from
Lemma

3.4
.

Proof.

We define the set

\mathcal{M}=\left\{w\in\mathcal{C}_{t}\colon\left\|w\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)\right\}

and the operator

\mathcal{Z}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mapsto z,

where
z
is the unique solution of (
3.6
) with
f=-w_{1}^{3}-3\tilde{w}_{1}w_{1}^{2}+v
. Note that by
Lemma

2.1
it holds
f\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
. Now any fixed point
\bar{w}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{w}_{1}\\
\bar{w}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{Z}
satisfies for
s\in[0,t]

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{w}_{1}\\
\bar{w}_{2}\end{bmatrix}(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\xi-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\bar{w}_{1}^{3}-3\tilde{w}_{1}\bar{w}_{1}^{2}-3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}\bar{w}_{1}+v\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

The analogue of (
2.6
) for
\tilde{w}
implies

\tilde{w}(s)+\bar{w}(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\left(\tilde{w}(t)+\xi\right)-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-(\tilde{w}_{1}-\bar{w}_{1})^{3}+v\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

and hence
\tilde{w}+\bar{w}=\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)
.
The estimate holds due to the fact that
\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,\xi)-\tilde{w}=\bar{w}\in\mathcal{M}
.

It remains to show that
\mathcal{Z}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{M}
. Then Banach’s fixed point theorem yields existence of a fixed point.
We first show
\mathcal{Z}(\mathcal{M})\subset\mathcal{M}
. To that end note that for any
w=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{M}
it holds

\|w\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)<2c_{w}\delta_{1}.

With
Lemma

2.1
it follows

\displaystyle\|w_{1}^{3}+3\tilde{w}_{1}w_{1}^{2}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2Tc_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}}\left(\|w\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}^{2}+3\left\|\tilde{w}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\|w\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\right)\|w\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2Tc_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}}\left(4c_{w}^{2}\delta_{1}^{2}+6c_{w}\|\tilde{w}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\delta_{1}\right)2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)

and for a sufficiently small
\delta_{1}
one obtains
\sqrt{2Tc_{\mathrm{em}}^{3}}\left(4c_{w}^{2}\delta_{1}^{2}+6c_{w}\|\tilde{w}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\delta_{1}\right)2c_{w}<1
. Utilizing
Lemma

3.4
it follows

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{Z}(w)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|-w_{1}^{3}-3\tilde{w}_{1}w_{1}^{2}+v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}+\|w_{1}^{3}+3\tilde{w}_{1}w_{1}^{2}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right),

implying
\mathcal{Z}(w)\in\mathcal{M}
.

To show the Lipschitz continuity of
\mathcal{Z}
assume
w
,

. Then
z=\mathcal{Z}(q)-\mathcal{Z}(w)
satisfies (
3.6
) with
\xi=0
and
f=-(q_{1}^{3}-w_{1}^{3})-3\tilde{w}_{1}(q_{1}^{2}-w_{1}^{2})
. Hence

(3.8)

\|\mathcal{Z}(q)-\mathcal{Z}(w)\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq c_{w}\left(\|q_{1}^{3}-w_{1}^{3}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}+3\|\tilde{w}_{1}(q_{1}^{2}-w_{1}^{2})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right).

Using the identities

(3.9)

\displaystyle q_{1}^{3}-w_{1}^{3}

\displaystyle=(q_{1}-w_{1})^{3}+3w_{1}(q_{1}-w_{1})^{2}+3q_{1}^{2}(w_{1}-q_{1}),

\displaystyle q_{1}-w_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=(q_{1}+w_{1})(q-1-w_{1}),

and the estimate implied by

it is straightforward to show that for a sufficiently small
\delta_{1}
the operator
\mathcal{Z}
is a contraction.
∎

We are now in a position to estimate the operator norms of the derivatives of
\mathcal{S}^{t}
independently of
t
. The following lemma will be needed for the sensitivity analysis of the value function.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
t\in(0,T]
be fixed and
\|(\xi,v)\|_{\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{L}_{t}}<\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{1}.

For
i=1,...,5
let
u_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
and
\eta_{i}\in\mathcal{E}

Then there exists

Proof.

The first estimate follows directly from

Again denote

and for some fixed

-3w_{1}^{2}\zeta_{1}+u\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

∎

In this subsection existence of a minimizing pair is shown. For any

Proposition 3.7

There exists
\delta_{2}\in\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\delta_{1}\right]

3.5

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\leq J(w^{0},0;t,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|w^{0}(0)-w_{0}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|y-C\left(w^{0}-\tilde{w}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2}

\mathcal{L}_{t}

(w_{k})

\mathcal{C}_{t}

and (

) implies

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\bar{u}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,t;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{k}

\displaystyle\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\partial_{t}\bar{u}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

\displaystyle\text{ in }L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\Omega)).

is the weak solution associated with
\bar{v}
which then implies that
\bar{w}
is the mild solution.
For all
k
and

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{t}u_{k}(s),\partial_{t}\varphi(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}(u_{k}^{3}(s),\varphi(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

Passing to the limit in (

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}(\partial_{t}\bar{u}(s),\partial_{t}\varphi(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{u}^{3}(s),\varphi(s))_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s

\partial_{t}u_{k}\rightarrow\partial_{t}\bar{u}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }C([0,t];H^{-1}(\Omega)).

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{1}(t)+\xi_{1}

\displaystyle=u_{k}(t)\rightarrow\bar{u}(t)\penalty\ \text{ in }L^{3}(\Omega)

3.3.

In this subsection we introduce the

Definition 3.8

For any fixed

and

satisfying

Before we state the first order optimality condition the adjoint state is estimated from above.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(\xi,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{2}}^{t}

3.7

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\leq\|D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(\bar{v},\xi)^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}_{t}^{*};\mathcal{L}_{t})}\left(\|\delta_{0}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}^{*};\mathcal{C}_{t}^{*})}\|\bar{w}-\tilde{w}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}+\alpha\|\Psi[\bar{w}-\tilde{w},y]\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}^{*}}\right).

The first order necessary condition follows from the definition of

Proposition 3.10
.

For any
(\xi,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{2}}^{t}
and any minimizing pair
(\bar{w},\bar{v})
of the corresponding optimal control problem (

\bar{v}=-p\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ in }\mathcal{L}_{t},

We conclude the section by highlighting one of the major differences in the analysis of the finite-dimensional and the infinite-dimensional case.

Remark 3.11
.

A well-known implication of the optimality condition is that optimal controls may inherit higher regularity from the adjoint state. In the finite-dimensional case discussed in
[
8
]
this has tremendous consequences, namely that optimal trajectories are classical solutions of the state equation. This ultimately leads to
C^{1}
-regularity of the value function, implying that it is a classical solution of the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. In contrast, in the context analyzed in this work no such effect occurs.

4.
Regularity of the value function

This section discusses the regularity of the value function with respect to the spatial variable and the measurement.
For
t\in(0,T]
and

In time zero we set

In order to show smoothness we apply the inverse mapping theorem to a mapping representing the optimality condition. Define the two spaces

Lemma 4.1

\|D\mathcal{S}^{t}(0,0)[(u,\eta)]-D\mathcal{S}^{t}(\bar{v},\xi)[(u,\eta)]\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}

A

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|D(D_{v}\mathcal{S}^{t}(\bar{v},\xi)^{*})[(u,\eta)]\left(\delta_{0}^{*}(\bar{w}(0)-w_{0})-\alpha\Psi[\bar{w}-\zeta_{\tilde{w}},y]\right)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}.

5.
Properties of the second derivative

This section is dedicated to the analysis of the second order spatial derivative of the value function, i.e., the second derivative of the mapping

\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{E}\penalty\ \colon\penalty\ \|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{3}\right\}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \xi\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y),

where
t\in[0,T]
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
satisfying
\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}<\delta_{3}
are fixed. We first clarify some notation. By definition of the Fréchet derivative we have

and
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E};\mathcal{E}^{*})
. We apply Riesz to identify the dual of
\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{E}
and obtain
D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)\in\mathcal{E}
and
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
. Reminiscent of the finite-dimensional case
D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}
and
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}
will be referred to as the
gradient
and the

of the value function.

Remark 5.1
.

Since we have
\mathcal{E}=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
, we implicitly identify the dual of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
with the space itself. While this is contrary to the common identification
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)^{*}\simeq H^{-1}(\Omega)
, it exactly fits the scalar products appearing in the following arguments.

First we show that locally the second derivative is an isomorphism before deploying arguments based on Riccati equations to analyze the time regularity of its inverse.

5.1.
Local coercivity

We first show that for all
t\in[0,T]
the bilinear form associated with the Hessian evaluated along the model is coercive. For sufficiently small data this property is then carried over to
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)
using continuity arguments. Since all results are clear for
t=0
, we only discuss
t\in(0,T]
.
The following lemma characterizes the derivative along the model in terms of
\mathcal{W}_{t}
and
\mathcal{U}_{t}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
t\in(0,T]
and
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
satisfying

it holds

\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)\,\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}}=\|D_{\xi}\mathcal{W}_{t}(0,0)[\eta]\,(0)\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|D_{\xi}\mathcal{U}_{t}(0,0)[\eta]\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}^{2}+\|CD_{\xi}\mathcal{W}_{t}(0,0)[\eta]\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2}.

Proof.

The assertion follows directly from
Corollary

4.4
and the chain rule.
∎

This characterization enables the proof that the Hessian is in fact coercive.

Proposition 5.3
.

There exists a constant

such that for all

and

it holds

\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)\,\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}}\geq M_{0}\,\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}.

Proof.

In the proof of

4.5
it is shown that the tuple

solves a linear quadratic optimal control problem. Using

we specify that

is the unique minimizer of

\displaystyle\min J_{0}(w,v)

\displaystyle=\|w(0)\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}^{2}+\alpha\|Cw\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{subject to}

\displaystyle w(s)=

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle e^{-\mathcal{A}(t-s)}\eta-\int_{s}^{t}e^{-\mathcal{A}(\tau-s)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(\tau)w_{1}(\tau)+v(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t].

In the spirit of

.
∎

5.2.

In this subsection we characterize the Hessian along the model as the solution of an integral Riccati equation ensuring that it is a strongly continuous operator. This property is then transferred to the inverted Hessian.

-v\end{bmatrix}

-3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(\tau)w_{1}(\tau)+v(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,T-t].

In the following we equivalently transform (

For a given function

(5.6)

\overleftarrow{w}(s)=e^{-\mathcal{A}(s-t)}\eta+\int_{t}^{s}e^{-\mathcal{A}(s-\tau)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
3\overleftarrow{\tilde{w}}_{1}^{2}(\tau)\overleftarrow{w}_{1}(\tau)-\overleftarrow{v}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[t,T].

Note that due to the regularity of
\tilde{w}
the operator
F(s)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
3\overleftarrow{\tilde{w}}_{1}^{2}(s)\mathrm{Id}&0\end{bmatrix}
is strongly continuous. Hence (
5.6
) is equivalent to

(5.7)

\overleftarrow{w}(s)=U(s,t)\eta+\int_{t}^{s}U(s,\tau)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\overleftarrow{v}(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[t,T],

where

is the evolution operator associated with
-\mathcal{A}+F
.
Defining the cost functional

(5.8)

J_{2}(w,v)=\|w(T)\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\int_{t}^{T}\|v(s)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\alpha\|Cw(s)\|_{Y}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

we observe
J_{1}(w,v)=J_{2}(\overleftarrow{w},\overleftarrow{v})
and conclude that
(\overleftarrow{w^{*}},\overleftarrow{v^{*}})
minimizes
J_{2}
under the constraint (
5.7
). Further it holds

\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(T-t,0,0)\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}}=J_{2}(\overleftarrow{w^{*}},\overleftarrow{v^{*}}).

We apply the results from
[
16
, Sec. 3.2]
to the control problem given by
J_{2}
and (
5.7
). Due to the arguments made in
[
18
, Sec. 2]
and the separability of
\mathcal{E}
no strong continuity of
U^{*}
is required to do so. It follows

J_{2}(\overleftarrow{w^{*}},\overleftarrow{v^{*}})=\left(P(t)\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}},

where
P
is the unique strongly continuous, self-adjoint, non-negative solution of (
5.3
). Since
t
and
\eta
were chosen arbitrarily, it follows that for all
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
and
t\in(0,T]
it holds

\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(T-t,0,0)\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}}=\left(P(t)\eta,\eta\right)_{\mathcal{E}}.

With the self-adjointness of the two operators and the
polarization identity
it follows
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(T-t,0,0)=P(t)
and the assertion is shown.
∎

The section is concluded by a proof that the strong continuity and uniform coercivity of
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)
imply strong continuity of
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)^{-1}
.

Corollary 5.6
.

The mapping
[0,T]\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
,
t\mapsto D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)^{-1}
is strongly continuous.

Proof.

For convenience denote only within this proof
A(t)=D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)
and let
t\in[0,T]
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
such that
t+\tau\in[0,T]
. For any
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
it holds

(5.9)

\displaystyle\|A^{-1}(t+\tau)x-A^{-1}(t)x\|_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{-1}(t+\tau)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}\|x-A(t+\tau)A^{-1}(t)x\|_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\|x-A(t+\tau)A^{-1}(t)x\|_{\mathcal{E}},

where
Proposition

5.4
was used. Since
A^{-1}(t)x\in\mathcal{E}
is independent of
\tau
, the strong continuity of
A(\cdot)
implies

A(t+\tau)A^{-1}(t)x\rightarrow A(t)A^{-1}(t)x=x,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \tau\to 0

and the right hand side in (
5.9
) tends to zero for

. Then
A^{-1}(t+\tau)x\rightarrow A^{-1}(t)x,\penalty\ \tau\to 0
.
∎

6.
Well-posedness of the Mortensen observer

With the preparations from the previous sections we are finally in a position to show that the Mortensen observer is well-defined as the minimizer of the value function and that the observer equation admits a solution.

6.1.
Existence of a unique minimizer of the value function

In this subsection we show that for sufficiently small
y
and any fixed
t\in[0,T]
the mapping
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)
admits a unique minimizer. Again the result is clear for
t=0
and we only consider
t\in(0,T]
.
First we characterize any minimizer as a root of the gradient that satisfies an upper bound and show uniqueness of the minimizer. This requires a result analogous to
Proposition

3.5
for the corresponding forward problem.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
\delta_{1}
and
c_{w}
be the constants from
Proposition

3.5
. For
t\in(0,T]
and

\left\|\xi\right\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}<\delta_{1}

the equation

w(s)=e^{\mathcal{A}s}\left(\tilde{w}(0)+\xi\right)+\int_{0}^{s}e^{\mathcal{A}(s-\tau)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-w_{1}^{3}(\tau)+v(\tau)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t]

admits exactly one solution
w
. It satisfies

\left\|w-\tilde{w}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right).

Proof.

The assertion can be shown analogously to
Proposition

3.5
.
Since
\|e^{\mathcal{A}t}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}=\|e^{-\mathcal{A}t}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}
. The same constants
\delta_{1}
and
c_{w}
appear. ∎

We now show that the vanishing gradient together with an upper bound is a necessary and sufficient condition to be a minimizer.

Lemma 6.2
.

There exists
\delta_{5}\in(0,\delta_{4}]
such that for any
t\in(0,T]
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
satisfying
\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}<\delta_{5}
it holds: The mapping
\xi\mapsto\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)
admits at most one global minimizer and

\xi\in\mathcal{E}\text{ is a minimizer of }\mathcal{V}(t,\cdot,y)\iff D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)=0\text{ and }\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}.

Proof.

We start by establishing an upper bound for the norm of any minimizer. Set
\delta_{5}=\delta_{4}
and let
\xi^{*}\in\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{V}(t,0,y)
. Then

\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)\leq\inf_{v}J(\mathcal{S}^{t}(v,0),v;t,y)\leq J(\mathcal{S}^{t}(0,0),0;t,y)=J\left(\tilde{w},0;t,y\right)\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\delta_{5}^{2}.

Hence there exists
w^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{t}
and
v^{*}\in\mathcal{L}_{t}
satisfying

(6.1)

J(w^{*},v^{*};t,y)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|w^{*}(0)-\tilde{w}(0)\right\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|v^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|y-C\left(w^{*}-\tilde{w}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}^{2}<\alpha\delta_{5}^{2}

and
w^{*}
solves the state equation controlled by
v^{*}
which by the arguments made in
Remark

2.10
is equivalent to

w^{*}(s)=e^{\mathcal{A}s}\left(\tilde{w}(0)+\left(w^{*}(0)-\tilde{w}(0)\right)\right)+\int_{0}^{s}e^{\mathcal{A}(s-\tau)}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-{w^{*}}_{1}^{3}+v^{*}\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall s\in[0,t].

With (
6.1
) we get

\|w^{*}(0)-\tilde{w}(0)\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\|v^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}^{2}\leq 2\,J(w^{*},v^{*};t,y)\leq 2\alpha\,\delta_{5}^{2}.

A decrease of
\delta_{5}
ensures
\|w^{*}(0)-\tilde{w}(0)\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}<\delta_{1}
and
Corollary

6.1
yields

\|\xi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{E}}=\left\|w^{*}(t)-\tilde{w}(t)\right\|_{\mathcal{E}}\leq\left\|w^{*}-\tilde{w}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}_{t}}\leq 2c_{w}\left(\|w^{*}(0)-\tilde{w}(0)\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|v^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}_{t}}\right)<c\delta_{5}.

Yet another decrease of
\delta_{5}
yields that any
\xi^{*}
satisfying
\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{V}(t,0,y)
must satisfy
\|\xi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}

or equivalently

(6.2)

\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{E}}\geq\delta_{4}\implies\mathcal{V}(t,\eta,y)>\mathcal{V}(t,0,y).

Now let
\xi^{*}
be a minimizer. It follows
\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{V}(t,0,y)
and hence
\|\xi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}
. The fact that
D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)=0
follows immediately.

Assume now
\xi^{*}
satisfies
\|\xi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}
and
D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)=0
. Further let
\eta\in\mathcal{E}
be such that
\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}
and
\eta\neq\xi^{*}
. Applying Taylor’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(t,\eta,y)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)+\left(D_{\xi}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y),\eta-\xi^{*}\right)_{\mathcal{E}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}(1-\tau)\penalty\ \left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*}+\tau(\eta-\xi^{*}),y)(\eta-\xi^{*}),\eta-\xi^{*}\right)_{\mathcal{E}}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

Due to the vanishing gradient and
Proposition

5.4
it follows that for all
\eta\neq\xi^{*}
satisfying
\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{E}}<\delta_{4}
it holds

\mathcal{V}(t,\eta,y)\geq\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)+M^{-1}\|\eta-\xi^{*}\|_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}>\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y).

With (
6.2
) for all
\eta
with
\|\eta\|_{\mathcal{E}}\geq\delta_{4}
it holds

\mathcal{V}(t,\xi^{*},y)\leq\mathcal{V}(t,0,y)<\mathcal{V}(t,\eta,y).

It follows that
\xi^{*}
is the unique global minimizer.
∎

Applying the implicit function theorem shows existence of a minimizer.

Theorem 6.3
.

There exists
\delta_{6}\in(0,\delta_{5}]

Proof.

Let

22

Corollary

4.4

\Omega^{\prime}

(y_{0},\xi_{0})=(0,0)\in\Omega^{\prime}

(i)

(ii)

(iii)

According to

5.4

(iv)

We define

(v)

\displaystyle\|D_{\xi}P(y,\xi)-D_{\xi}P(y_{0},\xi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}=\|D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}\leq g_{1}(\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}},\|\xi\|_{\mathcal{E}}).

(vi)

\displaystyle\|P(y,\xi_{0})\|_{\mathcal{E}}

[

6.2.
Local well-posedness of the observer equation

In
[
8
]
we used similar strategies to show existence of an energy minimizing trajectory in the setting of ODEs. Utilizing regularity of the value function and optimal trajectories we proceeded to show that this minimizing trajectory also satisfies the observer equation.
Unfortunately this strategy can no be applied in the PDE setting discussed here, cf.,
Remark

3.11
. We can, however, show that the observer equation admits a locally unique solution.

The reader is reminded that the optimal control problem and the corresponding value function discussed in
Section

4
and
Section

5
are a shifted version of the one used for the derivation of the formal observer equation (
1.9
), cf.,
Remark

3.1
. Hence we consider a shifted version of the observer equation that is obtained by subtracting the model dynamics from (
1.9
) and in its strong form reads

(6.3)

\displaystyle\dot{\check{w}}(t)=\mathcal{A}\check{w}(t)

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\check{w}_{1}^{3}(t)-3\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\check{w}_{1}^{2}(t)-3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(t)\check{w}_{1}(t)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+\alpha D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\check{w}(t),y)^{-1}(y(t)-C\check{w}(t)),

\displaystyle\check{w}(0)

\displaystyle=0.

In order to apply a fixed point argument the equation needs to be rearranged by inserting a zero. The above equation for
\check{w}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\dot{\check{w}}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}\check{w}(t)+F(t)\check{w}(t)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\check{w}_{1}^{3}(t)-3\tilde{w}_{1}(t)\check{w}_{1}^{2}(t)\end{bmatrix}+\alpha D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\check{w}(t),y)^{-1}C^{*}y(t)

\displaystyle-\alpha\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\check{w}(t),y)^{-1}-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)^{-1}\right)C^{*}C\check{w}(t),

\displaystyle\check{w}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

where

F\colon[0,T]\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ F(t)=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
-3\tilde{w}_{1}^{2}(t)\,\mathrm{Id}&0\end{bmatrix}-\alpha D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,0,0)^{-1}C^{*}C.

The regularity of
\tilde{w}
and
Corollary

5.6
ensure that
F
is strongly continuous allowing a formulation of the equation in terms of
the evolution operator
U(t,s)
associated with
\mathcal{A}+F
, cf.,
[
5
, P. II, Ch. 3.5]
.
We call
\check{w}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}
a solution to the observer equation (
6.3
) if for all
t\in[0,T]
it holds

\displaystyle\check{w}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle U(t,s)\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-\check{w}_{1}^{3}(s)-3\tilde{w}_{1}(s)\check{w}_{1}^{2}(s)\end{bmatrix}+\alpha\,D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(s,\check{w}(s),y)^{-1}C^{*}y(s)\right)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle U(t,s)\left(\alpha\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(s,\check{w}(s),y)^{-1}-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(s,0,0)^{-1}\right)C^{*}C\check{w}(s)\right)\mathrm{d}s.

Note that due to
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}^{-1}
local results obtained in previous sections ensure that the right hand side is well defined only for
\check{w}(s)<\delta_{4}
.
By application of a fixed point argument on a suitable subset of
\mathcal{C}_{T}
we show that there exists such a
\check{w}
satisfying the mild formulation.
The following lemma is crucial in showing the contraction property of the fixed point operator.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
t\in[0,T]
be fixed. There exists a constant
L>0
independent of
t
such that for all
(\xi_{1},y_{1})
,
(\xi_{2},y_{2})\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{4}}^{t}
it holds

\|D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{2},y_{2})^{-1}-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{1},y_{1})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})}\leq L\left(\|\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}\|_{\mathcal{E}}+\|y_{2}-y_{1}\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{t}}\right).

Proof.

Using the definition of the Fréchet derivative and the Neumann series it can easily be confirmed that the operator

\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coerc}}(\mathcal{E})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coerc}}(\mathcal{E}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ A\mapsto A^{-1}

is continuously differentiable with derivative
B\mapsto A^{-1}BA^{-1}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coerc}}(\mathcal{E})
denotes the subset of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E})
containing all elements associated with coercive bilinear forms. Due to
Proposition

5.4
we have
D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coerc}}(\mathcal{E})
for all
(\xi,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{4}}^{t}
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coerc}}(\mathcal{E})
is open and convex, Taylor’s theorem can be applied. We denote
\xi_{\tau}=\xi_{1}+\tau(\xi_{2}-\xi_{1})
and
y_{\tau}=y_{1}+\tau(y_{2}-y_{1})
and get

\displaystyle D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{2},y_{2})^{-1}-D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{1},y_{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}D_{\xi}\left(D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\right)[\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}]+D_{y}\left[D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\right][y_{2}-y_{1}]\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\,D_{\xi^{3}}^{3}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})[\xi_{2}-\xi_{1}]\,D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\,D_{y\xi^{2}}^{3}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})[y_{2}-y_{1}]\,D_{\xi\xi}^{2}\mathcal{V}(t,\xi_{\tau},y_{\tau})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\tau.

The assertion follows with
Lemma

4.6
and
Proposition

5.4
.
∎

We are now prepared to show that the observer equation admits a solution.

Theorem 6.6
.

There exists
\delta_{7}\in(0,\delta_{4}]
and

such that for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{T}
satisfying

\check{w}\in\mathcal{C}_{T}

\|\check{w}\|_{\mathcal{C}_{T}}<2\gamma\|y\|_{Y_{T}}

Proof.

For now let
\gamma>0
be some constant and set
\delta_{7}=\min(\delta_{4},\tfrac{\delta_{4}}{4\gamma})
. Let
y\in\mathcal{Y}_{t}
be such that
\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{T}}<\delta_{7}
and define the set

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\left\{w\in\mathcal{C}_{T}\colon\|w\|_{\mathcal{C}_{T}}\leq 2\gamma\|y\|_{\mathcal{Y}_{T}}\right\}

and the operator
\mathcal{Z}\colon\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{T}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(w)(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}U(t,s)\left(\vphantom{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-{w}_{1}^{3}(s)-3\tilde{w}_{1}(s){w}_{1}^{2}(s)\end{bmatrix}}\right.\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
-{w}_{1}^{3}(s)-3\tilde{w}_{1}(s){w}_{1}^{2}(s)\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon\mathcal{G}_{1}[w](s)}\underbrace{\vphantom{\begin{bmatrix}0\\

7.
Conclusion

In this work we took a first step towards establishing well-posedness of the Mortensen observer applied to infinite-dimensional systems. For the nonlinear wave equation at hand it was shown that the state estimation via the energy minimizer is well-defined and further that the associated observer equation admits a locally unique solution. It still remains to show that the energy minimizer coincides with this trajectory, a task that could possibly be tackled analyzing the associated Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation and the regularity of its solution. Another strain of future research is the extension to more general systems. Since the arguments of the present work rely on the reversibility of the dynamics, hyperbolic systems are the natural first candidate. However, one might be able to also treat irreversible dynamics and parabolic behavior by limiting the search for a minimizer of the energy to a subset of states reachable by the dynamics, hence allowing for a backward solution.
