Using BDF schemes in the temporal integration of POD-ROM methods
Bosco García-Archilla, Alicia García-Mascaraque, Julia Novo

Abstract
In this paper we consider the numerical approximation of a semilinear reaction-diffusion model problem (PDEs) by means of reduced order methods (ROMs) based on proper orthogonal decomposition (POD). We focus on the time integration of the fully discrete reduced order model. Most of the analysis in the literature has been carried out for the implicit Euler method as time integrator. We integrate in time the reduced order model with the BDF-q time stepping ( 1 ≤ q ≤ 5 1\leq q\leq 5 ) and prove optimal rate of convergence of order q q in time. Our set of snapshots is obtained from finite element approximations to the original model problem computed at different times. These finite element approximations can be obtained with any time integrator. The POD method is based on first order difference quotients of the snapshots. The reason for doing this is twofold. On the one hand, the use of difference quotients allow us to provide pointwise-in-time error bounds. On the other, the use of difference quotients is essential to get the expected rate q q in time since we apply that the BDF-q time stepping, 1 ≤ q ≤ 5 1\leq q\leq 5 , can be written as a linear combination of first order difference quotients.



Key words
BDF schemes; POD-ROMs methods; Difference quotients; Pointwise error estimates in time; Semilinear reaction-diffusion problems

1
Introduction

It is well known that using reduced order models one can drastically reduce the computational cost of numerical simulations while
keeping enough accuracy in the desired approximations. In this paper we consider the so called proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) methods,
[
8
]
.

The literature concerning POD methods has increased considerably in the later years although few attention has been paid to the error coming from the temporal discretization. Most of the papers concerning error analysis apply the implicit Euler method as time integrator for the reduced order model or at most second-order methods in time. However, in practice, higher order time integrators, including higher order BDF schemes are used. The use of higher order methods in time allow to take smaller time steps and, as a consequence, can also reduce the computational effort to get the final approximation. Since the literature about POD methods is quite
extensive we will mention in this paper only those references that are related with the analysis we carry out in the present paper.

In the note
[
9
]
, the authors prove that second-order error bounds in time can be obtained for POD methods using the BDF2 scheme in time to approach the heat equation. The authors include difference quotients in the set of snapshots. The aim of the present paper, is to extend some of the ideas of
[
9
]
to get order
q
in case of using BDF-q schemes for
q\leq 5
for semilinear reaction-diffusion problems. Then, we extend the ideas in
[
9
]
to higher order methods and to more complicated model problems. As in
[
9
]
, the use of difference quotients in the POD method is necessary to get pointwise-in-time error estimates. Moreover, the use of difference quotients allow us to get optimal bounds in time since we prove that any BDF-q method can be written as a linear combination of first order difference quotients (this idea was applied in
[
9
]
for the BDF2 scheme).

For the error analysis of this paper, we consider
H_{0}^{1}
-projections for the POD method since, as shown in
[
7
]
, better bounds in terms of the tail of the eigenvalues can be proved than in the case of
L^{2}
-projections. By tail of the eigenvalues we mean the quantity

\Sigma_{r}^{2}\equiv\sum_{k>r}\lambda_{k}

(1)

where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{d_{r}}>0
are nonzero eigenvalues of the correlation matrix of the data set in the POD method. The reason is that
when projecting onto
L^{2}
they are
H_{0}^{1}
errors (concerning projections onto the reduced order space) that have to be estimated
and that are optimal in
L^{2}
, by definition, but not in
H_{0}^{1}
. In any case, the error analysis of the present paper also works for

L^{2}
projections. In that case, the analysis is simpler, not optimal in the sense of
[
7
]
, as any other
error analysis for
L^{2}
projections, and the idea of writing any BDF-q scheme as linear combination of first order finite differences is not needed.

Apart from the idea of writing BDF-q methods as linear combination of first order schemes the key point for the error analysis of this paper is the analysis appearing in the recent reference
[
2
]
. In
[
2
]
the authors analyze time-stepping methods for BDF schemes of order 1 to 6 for the approximation of the transient Stokes equations with both inf-sup stable and symmetric pressure stabilized mixed finite element methods. As explained in
[
2
]
, there are some results concerning
the
G
-stability of BDF schemes that allow the use of quite standard estimates that have made it easier to carry out the error analysis of
the POD method in this paper. The analysis of the BDF-6 method, presented in
[
2
]
, could also be adapted to get order
6
in time in the present paper. Nevertheless, this error analysis is harder and has to be done separately.

For the error analysis of the POD method we also follow the ideas in
[
10
]
where a continuous-in-time POD method is analyzed. As in
[
10
]
, we assume our set of snapshots is based on continuous-in-time finite element approximations to the model problem at different times. In practice, any time integrator can be applied to compute the snapshots, not necessarily the same used for the time integration of the POD method. Since the computation of snapshots is done in the offline stage, we can assume the snapshots are computed with enough accuracy so that the temporal errors coming from the time discretization are negligible.
Although the error analysis of the present paper could be seen, in some sense, as the fully discrete case of the error
analysis in
[
10
]
, the ideas of
[
10
]
go further. In particular, interpolation techniques are applied in
[
10
]
to prove error estimations that are valid for values of the time variable different that those in the set of snapshots.
As in
[
10
]
, the POD method is based on difference quotients of the snapshots both to get pointwise estimates and to be able to bound the errors coming from the temporal discretization.

Finally, we want to mention that one could also use the standard POD method based on the snapshots instead of its difference quotients and follow the error analysis in
[
12
]
. In that paper, it is shown that a rate of convergence
for a standard POD method not including difference quotients is as close as that of methods including difference quotients as the smoothness of the function providing the snapshots allow. In
[
12
]
error bounds for the projections onto the POD space of the difference quotients are also provided, which would be needed to get the temporal estimations corresponding to the BDF-q scheme.

The outline of the paper is as follows, in Section 2 we introduce some preliminaries and notations. In Section 3 we state
some of the properties of the BDF methods appearing in
[
2
]
that are needed to bound the time discretization error. In Section 4 we introduce some ideas concerning POD methods. The error analysis of the method is carried out in Section 5. Finally, some numerical experiments are shown in the last section to check in practice the theoretical results obtained.

2
Preliminaries and notation

As a model problem we consider the following reaction-diffusion equation

\begin{array}[]{rcll}u_{t}(t,x)-\nu\Delta u(t,x)+g(u(t,x))&=&f(t,x)&\quad(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\Omega,\\
u(t,x)&=&0,&\quad(t,x)\in(0,T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
u(0,x)&=&u^{0}(x),&\quad x\in\Omega,\end{array}

(2)

\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, where
g
is a nonlinear smooth function and
\nu
is a positive parameter.
For simplicity here and in the sequel we take

as initial time.

Let
C_{p}
be the constant in the Poincaré inequality

\|v\|_{0}\leq C_{p}\|\nabla v\|_{0},\quad v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(3)

Let us denote by
X_{h}^{k}
a finite element space based on piece-wise continuous polynomials of degree
k
defined
over a triangulation of
\Omega
of diameter
h

and by
V_{h}^{k}
the finite element space based on piece-wise continuous polynomials of degree
k
that satisfies also the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions at the boundary
\partial\Omega
.

In the sequel,
I_{h}u\in X_{h}^{k}
will denote
the Lagrange interpolant of a continuous function

. The following bound can be found in
[
1
, Theorem 4.4.4]

|u-I_{h}u|_{W^{m,p}(K)}\leq c_{\text{\rm int}}h^{n-m}|u|_{W^{n,p}(K)},\quad 0\leq m\leq n\leq k+1,

(4)

where
n>d/p
when
1<p\leq\infty
and

Let us assume we want to approximate (
2
) in a given time interval
[0,T]
. We consider the semi-discrete finite element approximation: Find
u_{h}\ :\ (0,T]\rightarrow V_{h}^{k}
such that

(u_{h,t},v_{h})+\nu(\nabla u_{h},\nabla v_{h})+(g(u_{h}),v_{h})=(f,v_{h}),\quad\forall\ v_{h}\in V_{h}^{k},

(5)

with
u_{h}(0)=I_{h}u^{0}\in V_{h}^{k}.

If the weak solution of (
2
) is sufficiently smooth, then the following error estimate is well known,
see for example
[
13
, Proof of Theorem 1]
,
[
16
, Theorem 14.1]
,
[
17
, Lemma 4.2]
,

\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\left(\|(u-u_{h})(t)\|_{0}+h\|(u-u_{h})(t)\|_{1}\right)\leq C(u)h^{k+1}.

(6)

3
BDF methods

In this section we follow the notation and theory presented in
[
2
]
. For the temporal integration of the reduced order method introduced in Section 4 we are going to use the
q
-step backward differentiation formulae (BDF). For
T>0
, let
us denote by
\Delta t=T/M
and by
t_{n}=n\Delta t
,
n=0,\ldots,M
a uniform partition of
[0,T]
. For
u^{n}=u(t_{n})
, the BDF approximation of order

q
to the time derivative
u_{t}
is given by

\overline{\partial}_{q}u^{n}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}\sum_{i=0}^{q}\delta_{i}u^{n-1},\ n\geq q,\quad{\rm for}\quad\delta(\zeta)=\sum_{i=0}^{q}\delta_{i}\zeta^{i}=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\frac{1}{l}(1-\zeta)^{l}.

(7)

We reproduce the results on
G
-stability for BDF schemes of
[
2
]
that allow to use energy estimates in the
error analysis of the methods. The following lemma is stated and proved in
[
2
, Lemma 3.6]
, see also
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
.

Lemma 1

Let
\delta(\zeta)=\sum_{i=0}^{q}\delta_{i}\zeta^{i}
and
\mu(\zeta)=\sum_{j=0}^{q}\mu_{j}\zeta^{j}
be polynomials that have no common divisor. Assume the following condition holds

{\rm Re}\frac{\delta(\zeta)}{\mu(\zeta)}>0,\quad\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}},|\zeta|<1.

(8)

Let
(\cdot,\cdot)

be a semi-inner product on a Hilbert space
H
with associated norm
|\cdot|
. Then, there exists a symmetric positive-definite
matrix
G=[g_{i,j}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times q}
such that for
v_{0},\ldots,v_{q}\in H
, the following bound holds

{\rm Re}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{q}\delta_{i}v^{q-i},\sum_{j=0}^{0}\mu_{j}v^{q-j}\right)\geq\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}g_{i,j}(v^{i},v^{j})-\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}g_{i,j}(v^{i-1},v^{j-1}).

(9)

The application of
G
-stability to BDF schemes is ensured by the following lemma (see
[
2
, Lemma 3.7]
) based on the multiplier technique of Nevanlinna and Odeh
[
14
]
.

Lemma 2

For
q=1,\ldots,5
there exits
0\leq\eta_{q}<1
such that for
\delta(\zeta)=\sum_{l=1}^{q}(1/l)(1-\zeta)^{l}
and
\mu(\zeta)=1-\eta_{q}\zeta
condition (
8
) holds. The values of
\eta_{q}
are found to be

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=0,\ \eta_{3}=0.0769,\ \eta_{4}=0.2878,\ \eta_{5}=0.8097.

(10)

As in
[
2
]
we define a
G
-norm associated with the semi-inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
on a Hilbert space
H
. Given

\boldsymbol{V}^{n}=[v^{n},\ldots,v^{n-q+1}]
with
v^{n-i+1}\in H
,
i=1,\ldots,q
, we define

\left|\boldsymbol{V}^{n}\right|_{G}^{2}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}g_{i,j}(v^{n-i+1},v^{n-j+1}),

(11)

where
G=[g_{i,j}]
is the matrix in Lemma
1
. Since
G
is symmetric positive definite the following inequality holds

\tilde{\lambda}_{m}|v^{n}|^{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{\rm min}\sum_{j=1}^{q}|v^{n-j+1}|^{2}\leq\left|\boldsymbol{V}^{n}\right|_{G}^{2}\leq\tilde{\lambda}_{M}\sum_{j=1}^{q}|v^{n-j+1}|^{2},

(12)

where
\tilde{\lambda}_{m}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{M}
denotes the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
G
, respectively, and
|\cdot|
is
the semi-norm induced by the semi-inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
.

4
Proper orthogonal decomposition

For
T>0
,
\Delta t=T/M
,
t_{j}=j\Delta t
,
j=0,\ldots,M
and
N=M+1
, we define the

\boldsymbol{U}={\rm span}\left\{\sqrt{N}w_{0},\tau\frac{u_{h}(t_{1})-u_{h}(t_{0})}{\Delta t},\tau\frac{u_{h}(t_{2})-u_{h}(t_{1})}{\Delta t},\ldots,\tau\frac{u_{h}(t_{M})-u_{h}(t_{M-1})}{\Delta t}\right\},\\

(13)

where

or

,

We denote

\displaystyle\|\nabla w_{0}\|_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{M+1}\frac{\tau^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\|\nabla Du_{h}(t_{j})\|_{0}^{2}=\sum_{k={r+1}}^{d_{r}}\lambda_{k},

The following lemma is taken from
[

Lemma 3

Let
\tilde{C}=1

Let us denote by

\displaystyle\left(2+4\tilde{C}\frac{T^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\right)C_{p}^{2}\sum_{k={r+1}}^{d_{r}}\lambda_{k},

(18)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq n\leq M}\|\nabla(u_{h}^{n}-P^{r}u_{h}^{n})\|_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(2+4\tilde{C}\frac{T^{2}}{\tau^{2}}\right)\sum_{k={r+1}}^{d_{r}}\lambda_{k}.

(19)

As a consequence, since

, it follows that

(20)

5
Error analysis of the POD method

We will consider the following fully-discrete POD-ROM approximation to approach (
2
): Find
u_{r}:(0,T]\rightarrow\boldsymbol{U}^{r}

such that

(\overline{\partial}_{q}u_{r}^{n},v_{r})+\nu(\nabla u_{r}^{n},\nabla v_{r})+(g(u_{r}^{n}),v_{r})=(f^{n},v_{r}),\quad\forall\ v_{r}\in\boldsymbol{U}^{r},\quad n\geq q,

(21)

with
u_{r}^{0},\ldots,u_{r}^{q-1}\in\boldsymbol{U}^{r}

For the error analysis of this section, we will assume that the nonlinear function

is Lipschitz continuous (globally), with

being a short form

Concerning the initial conditions

In practice one could also take

We observe that to get the POD approximation (

\begin{array}[]{rclcl}u_{t}&=&\nu\Delta u+1+u^{2}v-4u,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],\\
v_{t}&=&\nu\Delta v+3u-u^{2}v,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T],\\
&&u(x,t)=1,\quad v(x,t)=3,&&(x,t)\in\Gamma_{1}\times(0,T],\\
&&\partial_{n}u(x,t)=\partial_{n}v(x,t)=0,&&(x,t)\in\Gamma_{2}\times(0,T],\\
\end{array}

(56)

where
\nu
is a positive parameter,
\Omega=[0,1]\times[0,1]
,
\Gamma_{1}\subset\partial\Omega
is the union of of sides

\{x=1\}\bigcup\{y=1\}
, and
\Gamma_{2}
is the rest of
\partial\Omega
. This system has an unstable equilibrium
u=1
,
v=3
, and, for
\nu
, sufficiently small, a stable limit cycle (see e.g.,
[
5
, § I.16,§ 2.10]
). If the boundary conditions are
\partial_{n}u(x,t)=\partial_{n}v(x,t)=0
for
(x,t)\in(0,T]\times\partial\Omega
, then, the periodic orbit is flat in space,
\nabla u=\nabla v=0
, but with those in (
56
) it has more spatial complexity. This system was used in the numerical experiments of
[
10
, Section 6]
.

We choose the value
\nu=0.002
, which offered an adequate balance between spatial complexity and computability. In this case we consider a triangulation of
\Omega
based on a uniform mesh with 80 subdivision per side and with diagonals running southwest-northeast. We used a spatial discretization with quadratic finite elements, which resulted in a system with 50558 degrees of freedom. For this discretization, we computed the corresponding limit cycle (see details in
[
10
, Section 6]
), the period being
T=7.090636
(up to 7 significant digits).

In all the experiments reported below the value of
\tau
in (
13
) was set to 1. The mean is subtracted from the snapshots
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}(t_{j})
to obtain the fluctuations. As a consequence, we transform the problem into one with homogeneous boundary conditions on
\Gamma_{1}

and
w_{0}
in (
13
) is taken as 0 (the mean value).

We fix
r=18
in (
15
) and take different values of
\Delta t=T/M
. We are interested in checking the theoretical rate of convergence in time of the POD method based on the BDF-q formula. For this reason we consider the continuous-in-time problem:
find
u_{r}:(0,T]\rightarrow\boldsymbol{U}^{r}

such that

(u_{r,t},v_{r})+\nu(\nabla u_{r},\nabla v_{r})+(g(u_{r}),v_{r})=(f,v_{r}),\quad\forall\ v_{r}\in\boldsymbol{U}^{r},

(57)

with
u_{r}(0)=P^{r}u^{0}
. Then, we integrate in time (
57
) with the numerical differentiation formulae (NDF)
[
6
]
,
in the variable-step, variable-order implementation of
Matlab
’s command
ode15s

[
15
]
, with sufficiently small tolerances for the local errors (
10^{-12}
)
Then, in our numerical experiments we measure

u_{r}^{s}-u_{r}^{bdf_{q}}
, for
u_{r}^{bdf_{q}}
the solution of (
57
) with BDF-q. We observe that with this procedure the only component of the
error in (
4
) is the second term on the right-hand side:

Ce^{2\alpha T}(\Delta t)^{2q}\int_{0}^{T}\|\partial_{t}^{q+1}u_{h}(s)\|_{0}^{2}\ ds.

(58)

To compute the starting values in
u_{r}^{bdf_{q}}
we use the following procedure. For
q=1
the only starting value
u_{r}^{bdf_{1}}(t_{0})=P^{r}u^{0}
. For
q=2
,

u_{r}^{bdf_{2}}(t_{0})=P^{r}u^{0}
as before. To compute
u_{r}^{bdf_{2}}(t_{1})
we solve (
57
) from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
with the BDF-1 method.
For
q\geq 3
the BDF-q computes the starting values at times
t_{1}
to
t_{q-1}
with the
q-1
formula and fixed step
\Delta t^{\prime}=(\Delta t)^{q/(q-1)}

to guarantee that the starting values have order
q
in time. In tables
1
and
2
we show the maximum error of the starting values of the formulas of order
2
to
5
in the
L^{2}
and
H_{0}^{1}
norms respectively, for
M=64,128,256,512
and
1024
, i.e.
\max\limits_{1\leq n\leq q-1}\|u_{r}^{s}(t_{n})-u_{r}^{bdf_{q}}(t_{n})\|_{0}
and

\max\limits_{1\leq n\leq q-1}\|\nabla(u_{r}^{s}(t_{n})-u_{r}^{bdf_{q}}(t_{n}))\|_{0}

M

Since the BDF-q formulas are implicit we use Newton’s method with standard local extrapolation for the initial guess to solve the corresponding equations.
We take tolerance
(\Delta t)^{q}/100
to guaranty that the error coming from the Newton’s iteration does not affect the rate of convergence of the corresponding BDF formula. We have observed that the number of steps in the Newton’s iteration ranges between 2 and 4. The average number of steps in Newton’s method across one period considering all methods is
2.97
.
As stated before, in Figure
1
we represent the maximum error in time in one period both in
L^{2}
and
H^{1}
norms.

The expected rate of convergence
q
can be clearly observed for all the formulas, according to (
58
). We have measured the slopes
in the figure in the asymptotic regime. While for formulas 1 and 2 the asymptotic slope can be observed from the first value of
M
,
M=64
, for the last two formulas 4 and 5 this regime needs a larger value of
M
. This fact is in agreement with our theoretical results since condition (
23
)
is required to achieve the rate of convergence. This means that smaller
\Delta t
is required as
q
grows since
\eta_{q}
increases in the
right condition of (
23
) while
\tilde{\lambda}_{m}
decreases.
