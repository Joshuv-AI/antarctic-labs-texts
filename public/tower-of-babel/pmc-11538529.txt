The in vitro effect riboflavin combined with or without UVA in Acanthamoeba castellanii
Authors: Joohee Park, Choul Yong Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The anti-Acanthamoeba properties of riboflavin and its enhanced amoebicidal effects when combined with ultraviolet A (UVA) radiation were investigated in vitro. The viability of cultured Acanthamoeba castellanii was assessed by adding varying concentrations (0 ~ 0.2% w/v) of riboflavin to the culture medium or after combined riboflavin and UVA treatment (30 min, 3 mW/cm2) over 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were measured following a 30-minute exposure to riboflavin. Additionally, the cysticidal effects of riboflavin, UVA, and their combination were evaluated. Gene transcription in Acanthamoeba was analyzed using RNA-seq. Riboflavin demonstrated dose-dependent toxicity on Acanthamoeba, accompanied by an increase in intracellular ROS. Exposure to 0.2% riboflavin reduced Acanthamoeba viability by over 50% within one day. UVA treatment alone also reduced viability by over 50%. Combined treatment with 0.2% riboflavin decreased trophozoite survival by more than 80%, and approximately 60% of cysts were killed when 0.1% riboflavin was combined with UVA. RNA-seq analysis indicated significant changes in gene expression after exposure to riboflavin, UVA, and their combination, particularly affecting oxidoreductase activity, cystathionine β synthase, and serine-threonine kinase activity. These findings indicate that riboflavin exhibits dose-dependent toxicity in Acanthamoeba, primarily through increased ROS generation. Combining riboflavin and UVA did not fully eradicate trophozoites and cysts of Acanthamoeba, but was able to partially inactivate them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77787-8.

Introduction

Corneal crosslinking using ultraviolet-A (UVA) light and riboflavin (CXL) was initially developed to treat corneal ectasia, such as keratoconus1,2. CXL aims to enhance the biomechanical strength and stability of the cornea by inducing new covalent bonds between collagen fibers. Riboflavin serves as a photosensitizer in CXL and produces reactive oxygen species (ROS) when activated by UVA, which in turn initiate the cross-linking process3. Moreover, increasing evidence supports that both the mechanical strengthening of the corneal tissue and the antimicrobial effect of UVA in CXL are effective against bacterial or fungal keratitis4–7.

While evidence supporting the use of CXL for bacterial and fungal keratitis accumulates, studies addressing its effectiveness against Acanthamoeba keratitis remain limited and contentious. Berra et al. reported that post-inoculation CXL exacerbated keratitis and increased the number of Acanthamoeba in rabbit models8. Kashiwabuchi et al. found no anti-trophozoite effect of CXL in vitro and noted no improvement in clinical signs of Acanthamoeba keratitis in Chinese hamster models9. Additionally, del Buey et al. observed live Acanthamoeba trophozoites and cysts on culture plates post-CXL, indicating no amoebicidal effect of CXL10. Despite these negative laboratory findings, clinical reports indicate that CXL has either cured or halted the progression of Acanthamoeba keratitis in human cases11–13. The discrepancy could be attributed to the physiological differences between humans and animals or might suggest that CXL predominantly enhances corneal mechanical strength without exerting anti-Acanthamoeba effects. However, given the shared biology of eukaryotes, it is plausible to expect some detrimental impacts on Acanthamoeba viability with CXL using UVA14. Although the resilience of Acanthamoeba, which forms cysts in adverse environments, complicates eradication by a single CXL treatment, CXL may still reduce the burden of live Acanthamoeba in the cornea and serve as an effective adjunctive therapy.

In this investigation, we assessed whether riboflavin and UVA, employed in CXL, exhibit any anti-Acanthamoeba effects. The study revealed that riboflavin alone demonstrates a certain degree of anti- Acanthamoeba activity against both trophozoites and cysts. Furthermore, using RNA-seq, we explored the alterations in the expression of Acanthamoeba transcripts induced by riboflavin, UVA, and their combination.

Results

Riboflavin and UVA toxicity on A. castellanii

Riboflavin exhibited dose -dependent toxicity towards A. castellanii. The reduction in viability of Acanthamoeba was almost consistent from day 1 to day 7, and a significant decrease in viability was noted at a mere concentration of 0.005% on both day 3 and day 7. With 0.01% riboflavin, viability decreased notably within 3 or more days of culture. When exposed to 0.2% riboflavin, a reduction in viability exceeding 50% occurred within just 1 day (Fig. 1).

Discussion

Riboflavin, a water-soluble vitamin, plays a critical role in numerous metabolic processes within the human body. It is generally recognized as safe and non-toxic, even at elevated doses, as any excess is effectively excreted via the urine. Riboflavin significantly contributes to metabolic pathways and cellular processes by acting through its cofactors, such as flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)15. However, under specific conditions such as exposure to light or interaction with metal ions, riboflavin may adversely affect cellular functions16–18. For example, riboflavin is capable of forming complexes with tryptophan or tyrosine—amino acids found in cell culture media—and can produce hydrogen peroxide upon light exposure17,18. Hydrogen peroxide, a reactive oxygen species (ROS), may induce oxidative stress, leading to damage in cellular components like lipids, proteins, and DNA18. These findings suggest that riboflavin may not be as benign as previously believed, particularly in the context of CXL, where it is applied at high concentrations and exposed to UVA irradiation. The role of riboflavin in CXL is to serve as a photosensitizer3,19. Upon activation by light, such as UVA, it transitions to an excited singlet or reactive triplet state and interacts with triplet oxygen species in the atmosphere to eventually generate active singlet oxygen, a potent ROS. Additionally, riboflavin free radicals, such as 2,3-butanedione, can also induce cellular toxicity20,21.

In our study, we observed a dose -dependent anti-Acanthamoeba effect of riboflavin. Additionally, it was noted that riboflavin elevated intracellular ROS levels and upregulated the expression of genes related to oxidoreductase activity in A. castellanii. Our findings indicated that a single treatment consisting of 0.2% riboflavin combined with 30 min of UVA irradiation reduced the viability of Acanthamoeba to 22%, 8%, 14%, and 13% at days 1, 3, 5, and 7, respectively, compared to the control. Given the doubling time of Acanthamoeba, ranging from 7 to 23 h22, this result suggests that continuous culture in riboflavin-enriched media can inhibit Acanthamoeba proliferation for up to 7 days even without further UVA exposure. However, this inhibitory effect alone may be insufficient for the complete eradication of Acanthamoeba, which is desirable in clinical settings. Using chlorhexidine as a positive control, we confirmed that Acanthamoeba survival rates approached nearly zero. Chlorhexidine is indeed clinically employed to treat Acanthamoeba keratitis. Nonetheless, our experiment highlights the potential of using riboflavin in conjunction with UVA as an adjunctive treatment for Acanthamoeba keratitis.

Some of our findings conflict with prior reports from other researchers. The discrepancies may arise from variations in experimental conditions, the cells used, differences between animal experiments and cell culture experiments, and exposure times. Besaratinia et al.23 exposed mouse embryonic fibroblasts to concentrations of riboflavin ranging from 0.01 to 100 µM for 20 min and observed no toxicity. In contrast, Voicescu et al.24 reported that riboflavin exhibits a pro-oxidant effect in the presence of bovine serum albumin, noting significant toxicity when L929 cells, which are a mouse connective tissue cell line, were exposed to concentrations exceeding 0.0825 µM for 24 h. Edwards et al.25 demonstrated that visible light can generate a substantial amount of oxygen radicals when the culture medium is enriched with both riboflavin and tryptophan, although media enriched solely with riboflavin or tryptophan did not show cytotoxic effects. Silva et al.26 discovered that visible light irradiating a tyrosine solution in the presence of riboflavin led to cytotoxicity in a mouse tumor cell line. In our study, we measured the levels of illumination under experimental conditions. As anticipated, the luminance inside the incubator was nearly zero. Nonetheless, the cultured cells were briefly exposed to room light during transfer between the incubator and the clean bench. Despite the usual practice of turning off the light inside the clean bench during cell manipulation, turning on the laboratory light alone significantly increased the luminance to 112 lx. Consequently, it is plausible that such light exposure could generate oxygen radicals from riboflavin and tryptophan contained in the culture media.

To investigate the underlying mechanisms of riboflavin induced toxicity, we employed RNA-seq. The nature of RNA-seq allowed for the assessment of approximate changes in gene expression across numerous representative pathways rather than the expression of a single specific gene.

We observed significant changes in oxidoreductase activity, cystathionine β synthase, and serine-threonine kinase activity when A. castellanii was exposed to riboflavin. Oxidoreductase plays a crucial role in eliminating ROS27. Cystathionine β synthase is involved in converting homocysteine to cystathione, which is subsequently converted to glutathione, an important antioxidant molecule28. Additionally, serine-threonine kinase significantly contributes to cell proliferation and apoptosis29. These findings indicate that riboflavin significantly induced ROS in A. castellanii and impaired the proliferation mechanism, shifting it towards apoptosis. This may also elucidate why the viability of A. castellanii decreased more significantly when treated with a combination of riboflavin and UVA compared to treatments with either riboflavin or UVA alone.

It is noteworthy that UVA treatment caused extensive changes in gene expression of Acanthamoeba, particularly in genes related to DNA damage such as double strand break repair, DNA repair, and DNA lyase activity, which showed significant changes following UVA exposure. When combined with riboflavin treatment, genes related to ATP, including ABC transporters, ATPase activity, ATP binding, AAA + type ATPase, AAA- type ATPase, and protein kinase ATP binding sites, exhibited significant alterations. These findings may suggest that significant DNA damage in Acanthamoeba was induced by UVA, and intracellular energy metabolism pathways were activated to counteract this damage, though not entirely successfully.

No previous study has applied extensive RNA seq techniques to examine changes in gene expression following UVA treatment of Acanthamoeba. Therefore, this represents an advantage of our study, which also provides insights into changes in gene expression of Acanthamoeba following exposure to riboflavin. Studies exploring gene expression changes in Acanthamoeba when exposed to a combination of riboflavin and UVA treatment are also scarce. Again, due to the nature of RNA seq technology, it measures changes in the expression of a wide spectrum of genes, thus offering approximate information about changes occurring within the target cells. Additionally, since UVA treatment in our study induced severe cell death (exceeding 50%), gene expression could only be assessed in surviving cells, precluding the acquisition of gene expression data from the deceased cells. Consequently, there were inherent limitations in detecting instantaneous changes in gene expression within cells due to the applied stimuli (riboflavin or UVA treatment).

There are several limitations in our study. This experiment was conducted in an in vitro environment, so the results may differ in an in vivo setting. Additionally, since the study was conducted on a single species of Acanthamoeba, there may be variations when applied to other species of Acanthamoeba. The methods used in our study differ from those used clinically. Notably, Acanthamoeba was cultured in a medium containing riboflavin even after UVA exposure in our research, differing from clinical settings. Maintaining a constant riboflavin concentration of 0.2% in a patient’s cornea for several days is nearly impossible in practice. The role of the eye’s normal immune cells in eradicating Acanthamoeba was not considered under our experimental conditions. Therefore, while the results of this experiment cannot conclusively prove the efficacy of UVA plus riboflavin treatment in treating Acanthamoeba keratitis in clinical settings, they are significant in demonstrating that UVA plus riboflavin treatment can kill Acanthamoeba and even partially destroy cysts under laboratory conditions.

In conclusion, this study has demonstrated that riboflaviin induces a dose -dependent toxicity in Acanthamoeba by increasing ROS levels. Furthermore, it has been shown that a combination of riboflavin and UVA can effectively kill Acanthamoeba to some extent. These results suggest that riboflavin UVA treatment could be utilized as an adjunctive therapy in the management of Acanthamoeba keratitis.

Materials and methods

Acanthamoeba culture

A. castellanii (Cat. No. ATCC-30010) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Rockville, MD, USA) and cultured axenically in a T25 tissue culture flask. This flask contained 5 mL of ATCC Medium 712 (basal medium inclusive of 2% (w/v) proteose peptone, 0.1% yeast extract, and final concentrations of 50mM-CaCl2, 400mM-MgSO4, 250mM-Na2HPO4, 250mM-KH2PO4, 0.1%-Na Citrate, 5mM- Fe (NH4)2(SO4)2, and 2 M glucose), and was incubated at 25 °C. Upon reaching near peak density, 0.25 mL of the culture was transferred to a new tube containing 5 mL of fresh medium.

Acanthamoeba viability assay

Acanthamoeba viability assay was performed using AlamarBlue™ Cell Viability Reagent (Cat. No. DAL1100, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., CA, USA) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, A. Castellanii was cultured at 1.0, 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2 × 104 cells/well over periods of 1, 3, 5, and 7 days, respectively. Riboflavin (Cat. No.77623, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the culture media in a dose-dependent manner (0,0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.05, 0.08, 0.1, 0.2% w/v). The riboflavin stock solution was prepared at 1.0% w/v in distilled water, with the vehicle comprising 0.2% v/v of this solution. Following the Standard Dresden protocol, the cultures underwent ultraviolet A (UVA) irradiation with and without riboflavin treatment, at a setting of 30 min and 3mW/cm2. After sufficient incubation, 10% v/v AlamarBlue™ solution was added to each well, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm, with values normalized against a wavelength of 600 nm.

To investigate the cysticidal effect of riboflignite and/or UVA irradiation, cystic transformation in A. Castellanii was induced by culturing the organism in Neff’s encystment medium (NEM; 100 mM KCl, 0.4 mM CaCl2, 8 mM MgSO4, 1 mM NaHCO3, and 20 mM Tris-HCl), adjusting the pH to 8.9 ± 0.2. Subsequently, the induced cysts were transferred to an axenic culture medium and subjected to 0.1% riboflavin and/or UVA irradiation over a period of three days. Post incubation, a living -cell viability marker, NucGreen Dead 488 ReadyProbes Reagent (SYTOX Green ™; Cat. No. R37109, Thermo Fisher Scientific), was directly applied to the cystic Acanthamoeba (2 drops/mL, 10 min at room temperature [RT]) for cell death analysis. SYTOX Green™ permeates the compromised cell membranes of dead cells, thereby staining the nucleic acids. Images of the stained Acanthamoeba cysts were captured using a confocal live imaging system (Leica Microsystems CMS GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). The ratio of NucGreen positive (+) cysts to total cysts was quantified.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species

To measure intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), the DCFDA/H2DCFDA-Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Cat. No. ab113851: Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was employed. A. Castellanii were treated for 30 min with varying concentrations of riboflavin (0,0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.05, 0.08, 0.1, 0.2% w/v) and/or UVA irradiation. Following treatment, the media were removed and the Acanthamoeba were rinsed using 100 µL/well of 1× ROS buffer in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. In the dark at 37 °C, cells were stained for 45 min by adding 100 µL/well of a 20 µM diluted solution of 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA). Subsequently, the cells were removed from the staining solution, and 100 µL/well of 1× ROS buffer was added. Fluorescence was immediately quantified at 485 nm excitation/535 nm emission using an endpoint assay.

RNA extraction and RNA seq analysis

Total RNAs were isolated from the A. Castellanii control and experimental groups (treated with riboflavin and/or UVA irradiation) using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). These total RNAs were then fragmented and converted into cDNA libraries containing sequencing adaptors. The workflow of RNA sequencing was conducted in accordance with a previous typical transcriptome assembly experiment30. Each cDNA library sample was sequenced, and the short reads were analyzed for quality control (Tables 1 and 2). The pre-processed reads were mapped to the reference genome using the HISAT2 program (HISAT2 ver.2.1.0), and transcript assembly was performed through the StringTie program (StringTie version 2.1.3b) using information from aligned reads based on reference. Finally, functional annotation and gene-set enrichment analysis based on the GO and KEGG databases were conducted for differentially expressed genes31.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error, and statistical significance was established with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. GraphPad Prism Ver. 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) was utilized for analysis, and p values below 0.05 were considered significant.
