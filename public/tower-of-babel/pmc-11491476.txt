The broad-spectrum deubiquitinating enzyme inhibitor PR-619 protects retinal ganglion cell and augments parkin-mediated mitophagy in experimental glaucoma
Authors: Xinxin Hu, Juntao Zhang, Haixia Ma, Wei Lian, Wenqiu Song, Chao Du, Shengcan Chen, Dandan Wang, Jiaqi Wei, Qinkang Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible visual impairment worldwide, characterized by the progressive death of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). Deubiquitinating enzyme (DUB) inhibitors have shown promise as pharmacological interventions for neurodegenerative disorders. Our study focuses on the pan-DUB inhibitor PR-619 and its potential neuroprotective effects on RGCs through modulation of parkin-mediated mitophagy in experimental glaucoma models. The results show that impaired mitophagy exists in RGCs of our experimental glaucomatous model. In vivo, PR-619 increased RGCs survival in glaucomatous rats. In vitro, it protected RGCs against excitotoxicity and reduced ubiquitin-specific protease (USP) 15 expression. Additionally, PR-619 upregulated parkin expression, increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratios, and elevated LAMP1 levels, indicating enhanced mitophagy in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, numbers of mitophagosomes were increased in optic nerves of PR-619-treated ocular hypertensive rats in vivo. Furthermore, parkin knockdown negated the salutary effects of PR-619 and attenuated expression of parkin-dependent mitophagy effectors in RGCs subjected to glutamate excitotoxicity in vitro. Collectively, these findings implicate augmented parkin-mediated mitophagy as the mechanistic substrate underscoring the neuroprotective capacity of PR-619 in experimental glaucoma. Introduction

Glaucoma, which represents a principal etiology underpinning irrecoverable visual impairment globally, constitutes a progressive optic neuropathy consequent to apoptotic attrition of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs)1. Elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) stands as the principal precipitator for the initiation and advancement of glaucoma, currently representing the only therapeutic target2. Regrettably, the reduction of IOP in isolation does not invariably confer protection against the injurious effects exerted upon RGCs and their intricate axonal projections in individuals afflicted by this ocular malady3 The intricate molecular pathways that underlie the malfunction and demise of RGCs in glaucoma persist as an enigma, with substantial gaps existing in our understanding of these intricate mechanisms of pathogenesis. These gaps in knowledge underscore the urgent need to decipher the intricate molecular signatures that underpin the demise of RGCs and identify effective neuroprotective strategies for RGCs in glaucoma.

Parkin, an intricately multifunctional member of the E3 ubiquitin ligase family, mediates the selective elimination of impaired mitochondria through mitophagy4. When mitochondria lose membrane potential, indicating dysfunction or damage, parkin activation and accumulation facilitate mitophagy5. Parkin exerts its ubiquitin tagging prowess upon a myriad of proteins nestled within the mitochondrial outer membrane. These ubiquitinated proteins, bearing the molecular insignia of Parkin, serve as beacons of recognition for a host of autophagy receptors and adaptors, among which optineurin emerges as a prominent player6. LC3, essential for autophagosome formation, interacts with optineurin to engulf and digest mitochondria for autolysosomal degradation7. Evidence shows activated parkin-mediated mitophagy reduces neuronal apoptosis in Parkinson’s8 and Alzheimer’s diseases9. Multiple observations implicate dysfunctional mitophagy in glaucoma pathogenesis10,11. Our previous studies suggested that overexpression of parkin10 or OPA112 rescue the death of RGCs by partially alleviating mitophagy dysfunction in experimental glaucoma models in vitro and in vivo. Given these findings, investigating small molecule compounds to promote parkin-mediated mitophagy in RGCs and prevent neurodegeneration is worthwhile.

Balanced ubiquitination and deubiquitination regulate homeostasis of mitophagy. Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) oppose parkin-mediated mitophagy by reversing ubiquitination, maintaining the balance between ubiquitination and deubiquitination13. Inhibitors of deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are emerging as auspicious pharmacological entities for ameliorating neuronal deterioration in diverse neurodegenerative pathologies14. PR-619, a pan-DUB inhibitor, exerts various biological functions including inducing autophagy15, anti-apoptotic effects16, and antitumor properties17. PR-619 recruits LC3 and induces autophagy in oligodendroglia15. Furthermore, our prior investigations substantiated that PR-619 confers neuroprotection to RGCs challenged with glutamate excitotoxicity by augmenting parkin-dependent mitophagy18. Nevertheless, the putative pathophysiological interplay elicited by PR-619 within the milieu of the glaucomatous retina remains inadequately elucidated.

This study investigated whether PR-619 could confer neuroprotection to RGCs within the milieu of experimental glaucoma models.

Methods

Animals

In strict adherence to the ethical guidelines set forth by the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) for the ethical use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research, all animal-related procedures conducted within the purview of this study meticulously adhered to the stipulated protocols. Prior to their implementation, the proposed animal procedures were subjected to rigorous scrutiny and obtained the official approval of the esteemed Animal Ethics Committee of Ningbo University, China. All experiments were conducted in line with the ARRIVE guidelines (PLoS Bio 8(6), e1000412, 2010) and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). We have taken great care to ensure that our methods for anesthesia and euthanasia adhere to the commonly accepted norms of veterinary best practice, avoiding the use of substances such as chloral hydrate, ether, and chloroform. To ensure the validity and reproducibility of the experimental outcomes, male Sprague-Dawley rats, weighing between 240 and 300 g, were judiciously selected as the experimental subjects. Rats were housed under a meticulously regulated 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, which mirrors the diurnal rhythm observed in nature. Furthermore, with the paramount objective of preserving their optimal health, the experimental subjects were provided ad libitum access to a nutritionally balanced diet and free-flowing potable water.

Experimental glaucoma

Elevated IOP was induced by translimbal laser photocoagulation of the trabecular meshwork19,20. The rats were rendered insensible to pain and immobilized through the intraperitoneal injection of a precisely measured dosage of ketamine (80 mg/kg; Gutian Pharmaceutical, Fujian, China). To further enhance the anesthetic effect, xylazine (5 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was judiciously administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 5 mg/kg, thereby synergistically augmenting the depth of anesthesia. Topical proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcon-couvreur, Belgium) were applied as a local anesthetic. Once anesthetized, rats were immobilized under a slit-lamp biomicroscope and administered 80–120 argon laser photocoagulations circumscribing the limbus utilizing parameters of 532 nm wavelength, 300 mW power, 0.5 s pulse duration, and 50 mm spot diameter via a Visulas Trion laser photocoagulator (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany).

IOP quantification was conducted in each oculus following anesthetization utilizing a TonoLab rebound tonometer (Tiolat Oy, Helsinki, Finland). IOP measurements were obtained at baseline preceding initial laser photocoagulation and subsequently at 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days succeeding each laser treatment. Slit lamp ophthalmoscopy was performed during each IOP measurement to examine for anterior chamber inflammation or hemorrhage - rats with these findings were excluded. Following a lapse of precisely one week, a meticulously planned repetition of the laser treatment was executed for all experimental subjects.

Injection of PR-619

As mentioned previously, the rats were subjected to general anesthesia. Topical anesthesia was induced with proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops (Alcon). Pupils of the experimental subjects were meticulously dilated through the application of topical phenylephrine hydrochloride and tropicamide (Shenyang Xinqi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China). With the utmost precision and delicacy, a 33-gauge needle was meticulously inserted into the vitreous chamber and 5 µL of PR-619 (8.23 µM) was injected near the center of the retina. To minimize the potential diffusion of the injected substance along the injection tract, a calculated interval of 1 min of infusion was meticulously observed. Following this period, the needle was left in place for an additional 60 s, allowing for sufficient time for the drug to distribute within the targeted area while minimizing its spread along the injection pathway. Extra care was taken to avoid damaging the lens and retinas.

Slit-lamp exams were performed to assess lens opacities and inflammation. Indirect ophthalmoscopy under an operating microscope evaluated retinal damage. Rats with lens puncture, retinal damage, or severe inflammation were excluded.

Retrograde labeling of RGCs

As previously described, Fluorochrome [FluoroGold 3% diluted in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Sigma-Aldrich] was sterotactically microinjected bilaterally into the superior colliculi of anesthetized rats at a volume of 2 µl per injection site. This procedure was performed 4 days prior to induction of experimental ocular hypertension in order to retrogradely label retinal ganglion cell populations10,12. At 2 weeks following IOP elevation, the eyes of the deceased rats are harvested. A small incision is made 2 mm behind the limbus of the cornea, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1.5 h. Subsequently, dissected to generate flat-mounted retinal preparations. Fluorescence micrographs of these retinal specimens were obtained using a Leica epifluorescence microscopy system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). RGCs numbers were quantified by manual counting within three defined concentric zones centered on the optic nerve head: central (1–2 mm), middle (2–3 mm), and peripheral (3–4 mm). Cell counts from each retinal zone were averaged for each eye. Strict methodological consistency was maintained to enable valid statistical comparisons across treatment groups.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

At 2 weeks post-laser induction of experimental glaucoma, rats were subjected to intraperitoneal injection of anesthetic agents and then euthanized by cervical dislocation per approved protocols. Eyes were promptly enucleated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution overnight, followed by a two-step dehydration process in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions, respectively, until the eyes reached the bottom of the tubes. Eyes were then sectioned along the limbus and the anterior segments were discarded. The posterior segments were carefully dried with filter paper and embedded in OCT Compound (Tissue-Tek; Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) before being frozen at − 80 °C. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 7-µm-thick sections of full-thickness retina. The tissue sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 20 min at room temperature, washed thrice with PBS, and then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were then incubated with primary monoclonal mouse anti-GFAP antibody (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or polyclonal rabbit anti-LAMP1 (1:200, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After several washes with PBS at room temperature, the sections were incubated with an appropriate Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat IgG secondary antibody (1:200; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 1 h in a humidified, darkened chamber. During the final washes, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1 µg/mL; Life Technologies) in PBS. Images of the stained tissues were captured with a confocal microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Strict methodological consistency was maintained and representative sections were analyzed from each group (n = 3 retinas per group).

Transmission electron microscopy

At the subcellular level, transmission electron microscopy enabled ultrastructural elucidation of mitochondrial morphology as well as quantitation of autophagosomes and mitophagosomes within the unmyelinated axonal segments of the optic nerves proximal to the retina. For this analysis, immediately after euthanasia, optic nerves were rapidly dissected 2.5 mm posterior to the globe from 3 rats per experimental group on ice to preserve fine structural details. Optic nerves were immersion fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde buffered with 0.1 M phosphate at pH 7.4 for a minimum of 2 h at 4 °C. After buffer rinses, the tissue was postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at 4 °C to enhance membrane contrast. Following additional buffer washes, dehydration was performed through a graded ethanol series. Optic nerves were embedded in Epon epoxy resin to facilitate ultramicrotomy. Ultrathin sections were obtained from the unmyelinated intracranial segments and examined under a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7800; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Strict methodological consistency was maintained between groups throughout the tissue preparation and imaging workflow.

Western blot analysis

At 2 weeks following the initial laser treatment to induce ocular hypertension, the rats were administered deep anesthesia via intraperitoneal injection and then euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with approved protocols. The retinas and optic nerves were promptly extracted and either immediately processed for biochemical assays or frozen at -80 °C for storage until analysis.

The retinas, with a sample size of three per group, underwent lysis in Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay buffer (Weiao Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Subsequent to lysis, the retinas were subjected to an ultrasonic homogenization process, resulting in the formation of a homogenate. The resultant proteins (20 µg) were then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes after undergoing separation via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Membranes were blocked utilizing a 5% skim milk solution in Tris-buffered saline with Tween (TBST) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were exposed to a range of primary antibodies, including polyclonal rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:2000; Yesen, Guangdong, China), polyclonal rabbit anti-parkin (1:1000; Abcam), polyclonal rabbit anti-optineurin (1:200; Abcam), monoclonal rabbit anti-LC3 (1:2000; Abcam), polyclonal rabbit anti-LAMP1 (1:1000; Abcam) and polyclonal rabbit anti-GFAP (1:10000; Abcam). These primary antibodies were suitably diluted in a primary antibody dilution solution (Weiao Biotechnology), and the incubation took place overnight at a temperature of 4 °C. Following the incubation period, the membranes were subjected to four successive rinses, each lasting 5 min, utilizing a 1× TBST buffer (Worthington, Columbus, OH, USA). Subsequently, the membrane is incubated with a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody, particularly goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:2000; Jackson), at room temperature for 2 h. To visualize the proteins of interest, chemiluminescence detection was carried out using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Weiao Biotechnology), following the manufacturer’s instructions meticulously. The resulting chemiluminescent images were captured using a Tanon-4600 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon, Tokyo, Japan). To quantify the intensity of the protein bands observed, the captured images were subjected to analysis using ImageJ, a software tool developed by the National Institutes of Health situated in Bethesda, MD, USA (ImageJ 1.50i, Version number: Java 1.6.0_24 (64bit). URL link: https://imagej.nih.gov/ij). This comprehensive process ensured accurate assessment and evaluation of the band intensity during the experimental procedures.

During the steps prior to hybridization with antibodies, the membranes were not cut. We maintained the integrity of the membranes throughout the experimental process to avoid any potential artifacts. However, after blocking with BSA, the membranes were cut into different segments based on marker positions before incubating with different primary antibodies. Therefore, we are unable to provide full-length membrane images. To address this limitation, we have included images of all the blots as they are, with visible membrane edges, in the Supplementary Information file. We assure you that this does not impact the scientific validity and rigor of our experimental results.

Cultured retinal ganglion cells and their treatment with PR-619

Primary RGCs were isolated and purified utilizing a meticulous two-step immunopanning procedure, as previously elucidated21. To initiate this process, retinas derived from neonatal Sprague-Dawley rats aged between 2 and 3 days were subjected to dissociation through papain digestion, employing a concentration of 5 mg/mL of the enzyme procured from Worthington. Subsequently, the papain inhibitor was introduced to terminate the enzymatic activity, ensuring the preservation of cellular integrity. The resulting retinal suspensions were then subjected to an incubation period within petri dishes that had been pre-coated with a specific rabbit anti-macrophage antibody (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). This step served the purpose of effectively eliminating macrophages from the retinal cell population, thus ensuring the isolation of the desired RGCs. Following this initial immunopanning stage, the retinal suspensions were subsequently transferred to petri dishes coated with a mouse anti-Thy1.1 antibody (Abcam), in order to selectively enrich the RGC population. By employing this immunopanning methodology, the isolated RGCs were successfully collected via centrifugation at 200 × g for a duration of 5 min. To facilitate further experimentation and analysis, the purified RGCs were then carefully seeded onto glass coverslips that had been previously coated with a 0.01% poly-D-lysine solution (Sigma-Aldrich).

Three days after seeding, 8.23 µM PR-619 or a vehicle control consisting of cell culture medium supplemented with 45 mg/mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), was judiciously introduced into the cell culture medium. RGCs were exposed to one of two distinct conditions. The first condition entailed the maintenance of the RGCs in cell culture medium alone, serving as a control group, while the second condition involved the inclusion of 100 µM glutamate (Sigma-Aldrich) within the cell culture medium. This carefully orchestrated exposure lasted for a duration of three days, during which the RGCs were housed in a temperature-controlled incubator operating at 37 °C, complemented by a 5% CO2 environment, simulating the physiological conditions conducive to tissue culture.

Survival assays

Cytotoxicity and cell death quantification of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) were achieved by spectrophotometric measurement of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release utilizing a commercial colorimetric assay kit (Takara Biotechnology, Shiga, Japan)18 per manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance detection was conducted at 490 nm employing a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Apoptosis was assessed through Hoechst staining, where RGCs were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Hoechst 33,342, followed by confocal microscopy imaging to calculate the percentage of apoptotic cells with pyknotic nuclei. Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using the JC-1 assay, where RGCs were incubated with JC-1 dye, and fluorescence intensities of red aggregates (hyperpolarization) and green monomers (depolarization) were measured. Mitochondrial distribution was visualized by staining RGCs with MitoTracker Red, followed by fixation, permeabilization, and confocal microscopy imaging.

Statistical analyses

All experimental procedures were conducted in triplicate to permit rigorous statistical analysis. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Variance between experimental groups was assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc comparisons performed using Bonferroni’s t-test. A probability value of P < 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance in all analyses.

Results

Due to the limited water solubility inherent to PR-619, a meticulous dissolution process was undertaken, involving the incorporation of the compound into a medium consisting of 45 mg/mL of dimethyl sulfoxide, which functioned as the designated vehicle control. Subsequently, rats assigned to both the PR-619 group and the ocular hypertensive group were subjected to translimbal laser-induced elevation of IOP three days after the administration of PR-619 or the vehicle control. The induction of IOP elevation through the precise application of translimbal laser stimulation resulted in a significant rise in IOP levels across all eyes subjected to laser treatment, as indicated in Table 1. Notably, no statistically significant disparities were observed between the PR-619 group and the ocular hypertensive group in terms of mean or peak IOP values. These findings highlight the comparable responses exhibited by the two groups, suggesting that the administration of PR-619 did not exert a discernible impact on the overall magnitude or peak levels of IOP elevation.

Bax is a pro-apoptotic protein, and its upregulation in a glaucoma model leads to the apoptosis of retinal ganglion cells23. Bcl-2 is an anti-apoptotic protein that prevents apoptosis by inhibiting the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane. In a glaucoma model, the upregulation of Bcl-2 can counteract the effects of pro-apoptotic proteins like Bax, thereby protecting RGCs24. Ocular hypertensive retinas exhibited elevated levels of the pro-apoptotic effector Bax (P < 0.01; Fig. 2d and f) concomitant with attenuated expression of the anti-apoptotic factor Bcl-2 (P < 0.05; Fig. 2d and g) relative to normal rats. Furthermore, the administration of PR-619 has been observed to exert a discernible impact on the protein expression levels of Bax and Bcl-2. Specifically, a marked decrease in Bax protein levels was evident upon PR-619 treatment (P < 0.05; Fig. 2d and f). In contrast, an increase in the protein expression of Bcl-2 was observed following PR-619 administration (P < 0.05; Fig. 2d and g). The experimental results demonstrated that PR-619 decreases the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax, while simultaneously increasing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, in the retinas of glaucomatous rats.

PR-619 enhanced mitochondrial health and mitophagy in glaucomatous rats

Parkin-mediated mitophagy involves several key proteins. Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, is recruited to damaged mitochondria where it ubiquitinates outer membrane proteins. Optineurin then recognizes these ubiquitinated proteins and recruits LC3, which facilitates the formation of autophagosomes around the damaged mitochondria. Finally, LAMP1, a lysosomal-associated membrane protein, helps in the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, leading to the degradation of the damaged mitochondria7. Western blot analysis verified that there is no significant difference in the levels of parkin between ocular hypertensive retinas and the normal rats (P>0.05, Fig. 2h and i). PR-619 elicited a significant upregulation of parkin expression in ocular hypertensive retinas relative to controls at 2 weeks post-IOP elevation (P < 0.05; 2h, 2i). Compared with the normal rats, ocular hypertensive retinas exhibited a noteworthy elevation in optineurin protein level (P < 0.05; Fig. 2h and j) and the ratios of LC3-II to LC3-I after a duration of two weeks (P < 0.05; Fig. 2h and k). However, no statistically significant differences in optineurin expression were discerned between PR-619-treated and ocular hypertensive retina (P >0.05; Fig. 2h and j). Furthermore, the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I, a marker of autophagic flux, was markedly augmented in ocular hypertensive retinas (P < 0.05; Fig. 2h and k) treated with PR-619 compared to ocular hypertensive retinas at the 2 week time point. There was a discernible reduction in the protein level of LAMP1, a key marker associated with lysosomal function, within the ocular hypertensive retinas (P < 0.05, Fig. 2h and l). Conversely, PR-619 provoked a substantial enhancement of LAMP1in hypertensive retinas (P < 0.05; Fig. 2h and l). Additionally, PR-619 upregulated LAMP1 immunoreactivity in the ganglion cell layer (P < 0.01; Fig. 3a and f). The experimental results detailed herein indicated that PR-619 enhances the expression levels of proteins associated with Parkin-mediated mitophagy in the retinas of rats afflicted with glaucoma.

PR-619 protected RGCs and upregulated mitophagy against glutamate excitotoxicity

Apoptotic cell death was attenuated by PR-619 treatment under both glutamate excitotoxicity and normal condition (P < 0.05, Fig. 5b). Parallel assays of LDH release verified reduced cytotoxicity in PR-619-treated during excitotoxic challenge (P < 0.05, Fig. 5c) and normal condition P < 0.01, Fig. 5c). Additionally, western blot quantification demonstrated that PR-619 increase the expression of pro-survival protein Bcl-2 (P < 0.05; Fig. 5f and g) in RGCs under glutamate excitotoxicity and normal condition, whilst suppress the pro-apoptotic factor Bax under glutamate excitotoxicity (P < 0.05; Fig. 5f and h). Under normal condition, Bax level showed no significant difference between control and PR-619-treated RGCs. The experimental results presented herein suggested that PR-619 exerts a neuroprotective effect on RGCs subjected to glutamate-induced excitotoxicity.

Discussion

Our findings showed aberrant accumulation of degenerated mitochondria and incomplete mitophagosomes within hypertensive optic nerves at 2 weeks post-IOP elevation. PR-619 prevented RGCs from death, improved mitochondrial health, reduced Bax and USP15 expressions, enhanced parkin and Bcl-2 expressions, and restored mitophagy functions in experimental glaucoma rats and a glutamate-induced excitotoxicity model. Additionally, parkin knockdown abolished PR-619’s protective effects and mitophagy on RGCs under glutamate excitotoxicity.

A growing number of studies suggests that mitophagy dysfunction leads to accumulation of damaged mitochondria, impacting RGC activity in experimental glaucoma10–12,18. As a critical quality control apparatus, parkin-mediated mitophagy plays an integral role in the selective removal of damaged mitochondrial substrates26. Parkin, a ubiquitin E3 ligase enzyme recruited to dysfunctional mitochondria exhibiting membrane depolarization, elicits the initial signaling events driving mitophagic induction via ubiquitinylation of outer mitochondrial membrane proteins27. This promotes mitochondrial recruitment of autophagy regulators like optineurin. Parkin also has a neuroprotective effect against RGCs death10,12,21. During mitophagy, LC3 binds and encapsulates damaged mitochondria, forming double-membrane mitophagosomes28. The ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I serves as a widely utilized index of autophagic flux29. In the present study, both the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I and quantitative autophagosome/mitophagosome numbers were significantly elevated in hypertensive optic nerves, corroborating similar reports30,31 and our prior investigations10,12. Although heightened the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I can indicate enhanced autophagosome biogenesis, it may also portend defective autophagic completion resulting from impaired autophagolysosomal fusion and resultant accumulation of undegraded substrates32.Therefore, we evaluated expression of the lysosome membrane marker LAMP1, which is commonly used to evaluate lysosomal content. Our results showed that LAMP1 was decreased in ocular hypertensive retinas at 2 weeks. Indeed, concomitant reduction of the lysosomal membrane protein LAMP1 alongside elevation of the ratio of LC3-II to LC3-I strongly implies compromised autophagic flux and inefficient mitochondrial recycling in optic nerve axons11 following chronic ocular hypertension in our rat model.

Ubiquitin signaling intricately regulates mitophagy through modulating the stability of autophagic effector proteins and facilitating recruitment of selective autophagy adapters to the outer mitochondrial membrane33,34. DUBs, which counteract parkin activity, can reportedly act as an important negative regulator of mitophagy35,36. Our prior work substantiated that PR-619, a broad-spectrum inhibitor of DUBs, confers neuroprotection to cultured RGCs challenged with glutamatergic insult via amplification of parkin-mediated mitophagy18. Expanding on these initial findings, the current study newly provides both in vitro and in vivo evidence that PR-619 promotes RGCs survival in the setting of experimental glaucoma. Mechanistically, PR-619 enhanced parkin expression while targeted parkin knockdown abolished the cytoprotective benefits of PR-619 in cultured RGCs during excitotoxicity. We previously found that increased parkin expression plays a neuroprotective role in experimental glaucoma rats10 and a glutamate-induced excitotoxicity model21. In line with previous studies, the present findings reveal that PR-619 mediates its neuroprotection against experimental glaucoma by upregulating parkin expression.

Selective mitophagic removal of dysfunctional mitochondria plays a cytoprotective role in alleviating cellular stress. Indeed, interventions to enhance mitophagy restore mitochondrial quality control and improve RGCs survival in glaucoma models10,12,37. To elucidate the mechanistic underpinnings of PR-619-mediated mitophagy modulation and associated neuroprotection, we further investigated the downstream pathway of parkin-mediated mitophagy cascade in chronic ocular hypertensive glaucoma rats in vivo alongside an in vitro model of excitotoxicity. Our results demonstrate PR-619 enhanced levels of optineurin, a canonical autophagy receptor recruited downstream of parkin to ubiquitinated mitochondria, in treated groups under both conditions. We further observed increased ratios of LC3-II to LC3-I and LAMP1 expression, indicative of autophagosome formation and lysosomal turnover. Additionally, LAMP1 immunoreactivity and co-localization with mitochondria was pronounced in PR-619-treated RGCs during excitotoxic challenge. Concordantly, autophagosomes and mitophagosomes were dramatically increased in PR-619-treated ocular hypertensive optic nerves. Collectively, these data causally implicate amplified parkin-mediated mitophagy as the principal mechanism underlying PR-619 mediated survival advantage in RGCs facing injury due to elevated IOP or glutamate excitotoxicity.

A burgeoning body of research indicates intimate crosstalk between parkin-mediated mitophagy and Bcl-2 family proteins, including the pro-apoptotic factor Bax and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2. As a key effector of apoptosis, Bax translocates to the outer mitochondrial membrane, triggering cell death. Parkin can reportedly suppress Bax-mediated apoptosis, enabling effective mitochondrial engulfment via mitophagy without triggering apoptosis38. In the present study, we newly demonstrate that PR-619 elicits robust upregulation of parkin expression alongside suppression of Bax levels in retinal tissues and cultured RGCs. Bcl-2, an anti-apoptosis protein, stabilizes mitochondrial membrane potential and prevents cytochrome c release. Bcl-2 is reported to regulate mitophagy by modulating parkin recruitment to mitochondria through protein-protein interactions39. In the current study, PR-619 boosted Bcl-2 protein levels in the setting of chronic glaucoma in vivo and during cytotoxic glutamatergic signaling in vitro. Functionally, PR-619 stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential in cultured RGCs under excitotoxic duress. Mechanistically, these results implicate amplified parkin-mediated mitophagy and coordinated regulation of pro- and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family members as the key pathways underlying the anti-apoptotic benefits of PR-619 treatment in experimental glaucoma. Further research is warranted to fully elucidate the nuanced interplay governing selective mitophagy, apoptotic signaling, and cytoprotection in the context of glaucomatous neurodegeneration.

As a broad-spectrum DUB inhibitor, it is unclear which specific DUBs confer PR-619’s protective effects on RGCs in experimental glaucoma. Based on previous dose-response data, we selected 8.23 µM PR-619 to optimize protection of RGCs under glutamate excitotoxicity. This concentration is estimated to be the half-maximal effective concentration for inhibiting ubiquitin-specific protease (USP) 15 activity per the manufacturer’s datasheet. USP15 uniquely suppresses mitophagy by antagonizing parkin-mediated mitochondrial ubiquitination. Moreover, USP15 inhibition can promote mitophagy and alleviate mitochondrial defects in a Parkinson’s disease model25. Deubiquitinating enzymes of the USP family represent attractive and theoretically druggable targets for pharmacotherapy, as exemplified by a recently developed small-molecule inhibitor of USP30 demonstrating the ability to confer cytoprotection in cultured RGCs and enhance mitophagy during excitotoxic challenge36. In this study, PR-619 decreased USP15 expression on RGCs under glutamate excitotoxicity. Further research on specific USP15 or other USP inhibitors is warranted to explore their roles in regulating parkin-mediated mitophagy and protecting RGCs in experimental glaucoma.

Conclusions

In summary, our results indicate that impaired mitophagy exists in RGCs of our experimental glaucomatous model. PR-619 exhibited remarkable neuroprotection of RGCs by augmenting parkin-mediated mitophagy in experimental glaucoma. These revelations engender the prospect that pharmacological agents or biotherapeutics augmenting parkin-mediated mitophagy may proffer viable therapeutic modalities for glaucomatous neurodegeneration characterized by impaired mitophagy.
