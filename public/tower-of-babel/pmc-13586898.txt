A Critical Synthesis of Machine Learning in Autism Spectrum Disorder Genomic Research: From Transcriptomics to Microbiome
Authors: Zahra SADR, Bita FALLAHPOUR, Alireza Alireza DASTGHEIB, Reza BAHRAMI, Mohammad Golshan TAFTI, Amirmasoud SHIRI, Ali MASOUDI, Fatemeh NEMATZADEH, Hossein NEAMATZADEH
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAutism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent impairments in social communication, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors. This narrative review synthesizes advances in machine learning applications to ASD genomic research through May 2026, spanning gene expression analysis, whole-exome sequencing (WES), non-coding variant interpretation, multi-omics integration, single-cell transcriptomics, epigenetic profiling, and gut microbiome analysis. A purposive, thematic literature synthesis approach was employed, allowing broad coverage of emerging methodological innovations and biological insights. We critically evaluate state-of-the-art deep learning architectures—including the Separate Translated Autism Research Neural Network and SHapley Additive exPlanations-based explainable artificial intelligence frameworks. Reported discrimination across the field varies widely, from receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC) values near 0.66 to implausibly perfect values of 1.00; the best-validated specialized genomic architecture achieves only modest discrimination (ROC-AUC≈0.73). We emphasize that interpretability and predictive performance are orthogonal properties: specialized architectures yield biologically interpretable feature attributions despite modest discriminative power; and several extreme AUC values in the literature are, in our assessment, more consistent with overfitting or data leakage than with genuine signal, although the primary reports did not always provide the information needed to definitively attribute them. Key themes include: (1) identification of differentially expressed genes through meta-analysis of transcriptomic data; (2) validation of predictive gene features from large-scale WES; (3) detection of non-coding regulatory mutations affecting synaptic transmission pathways; (4) discovery of gut microbiome signatures associated with ASD classification; and (5) discovery of data-driven subtypes enabling precision medicine stratification. Critical challenges include population bias toward European ancestry, socioeconomic ascertainment bias, modest predictive effect sizes, conflation of association with causation, and gaps between computational prediction and clinical utility. Future directions emphasize multi-modal data integration, diverse cohort expansion, engagement with neurodiversity perspectives, and regulatory science development.

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent impairments in social communication, restricted interests, and repetitive behaviors, with onset typically in early childhood1. The most recent Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network surveillance report, published in April 2025, established that ASD prevalence among 8-year-old children in the United States in 2022 was 1 in 31 (3.2%), representing a 22.8% increase from the 2020 estimate of 1 in 361. The etiology of ASD involves intricate interactions between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. A widely cited meta-analysis of twin studies by Tick et al.2 reported heritability estimates ranging from 64% to 91%; however, these figures derive from cohorts ascertained under DSM-IV or earlier diagnostic criteria and should be interpreted as period- and framework-specific estimates rather than fixed constants.

Large-scale genomic studies have since defined this architecture more precisely and provided the quantitative backdrop for the present synthesis. Satterstrom et al.3, integrating de novo and case-control rare variation across 35,584 samples (11,986 with ASD) using the transmission and de novo association framework, identified 102 ASD risk genes at a false discovery rate ≤0.1. Fu et al.4 subsequently expanded rare-variant discovery in a cohort of 63,237 individuals, reporting 185 ASD-associated genes at a false discovery rate ≤0.05 (72 at ≤0.001) and, in a combined ASD/developmental-delay meta-analysis, 373 genes. On the common-variant side, Grove et al.5 conducted the largest ASD genome-wide association meta-analysis to date (18,381 cases; 27,969 controls), identifying five genome-wide significant loci and estimating common-variant single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based heritability at approximately 0.118 (roughly 11-12% of variance in liability). This low common-variant heritability is central to our argument: it directly indicates that genotyped common variation—and, by extension, most of the genomic features available to current machine learning (ML) models—captures only a minority of ASD liability, thereby bounding what genotype-based prediction can achieve.

Despite decades of intensive research, the genetic architecture of ASD remains incompletely understood; it is characterized by extreme locus heterogeneity, variable expressivity, and incomplete penetrance, which present substantial challenges for traditional genetic analysis approaches. The advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies has generated unprecedented volumes of genomic data, necessitating sophisticated computational approaches to extract meaningful biological insights6. ML, particularly deep learning methodologies, has emerged as a prominent paradigm for analyzing complex, high-dimensional genomic datasets. Unlike traditional statistical methods that rely on predefined hypotheses and linear relationships, ML algorithms can detect subtle, non-linear patterns across millions of genetic variants, in principle enabling identification of novel risk factors and biological pathways, as discussed in recent reviews by Al-Tamimi et al.6 and Agrawal and Agrawal7.

Recent applications of ML in ASD research span diverse molecular data types: identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from transcriptomic data8, predicting ASD status from whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS)9, interpreting the functional impact of non-coding regulatory variants10, discovering epigenetic modifications associated with ASD11, 12, analyzing gut microbiome compositions13, 14, 15, and integrating multi-omics data to reveal convergent biological mechanisms16, 17. Reilly et al.18 recently published a comprehensive narrative review synthesizing ML across ASD screening, phenotypic stratification, diagnostic biomarkers, neuroimaging, and personalized therapies. Del Casale et al.19 conducted a 2023 systematic review of ML and pharmacogenomics in psychiatry, providing additional methodological context for ML-based biomarker discovery in neurodevelopmental conditions.

This narrative review critically examines the application of ML methods across diverse genetic and molecular data modalities in ASD research up to May 2026. We employ a purposive literature selection strategy rather than a systematic search protocol, because this manuscript is a narrative review that synthesizes conceptual developments, methodological innovations, and emerging biological themes. The manuscript first presents methodological foundations, including explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) and deep learning architectures, followed by applications to specific data types: transcriptomics, non-coding variants, epigenetics, single-cell analysis, and the gut microbiome. Dataset characteristics, pathway analysis, clinical translation challenges, ethical considerations, and future directions are discussed.

Three overlapping 2025 reviews have recently addressed ML in ASD (Al-Tamimi et al.6; Agrawal and Agrawal7; Reilly et al.18). The present review is distinguished by an explicitly critical, artifact-aware lens applied across an unusually broad set of molecular modalities—transcriptomic data, coding and non-coding variants, epigenetic data, single-cell data, gut microbiome data, and multi-omics data. Rather than cataloguing reported performance, we interrogate whether reported discrimination is credible, separate interpretability from predictive validity, and foreground the population biases and reproducibility failures that temper translational claims.

MATERIALS and METHODS

This manuscript is a narrative review of ML applications in ASD genomic research. No protocol was registered in PROSPERO or any other registry because the work was intentionally designed as a narrative synthesis employing a purposive, thematic literature selection.

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and arXiv for peer-reviewed articles, preprints, and authoritative reports published through May 12, 2026. Search terms included combinations of “autism spectrum disorder,” “machine learning,” “deep learning,” “genomics,” “transcriptomics,” “multi-omics,” “gut microbiome,” “biomarkers,” “epigenetics,” and “precision medicine.” Additional studies were identified through citation tracking of seminal papers and manual screening of reference lists from recent reviews.

Study selection prioritized the following: (1) peer-reviewed original research and high-quality preprints employing ML or deep learning on ASD genetic or molecular data; (2) landmark cohort studies with sample sizes exceeding 1,000 participants; (3) methodologically innovative frameworks introducing novel architectures or integrative approaches; (4) studies published in 2024-2026, reflecting the current state of the field; and (5) authoritative reviews providing conceptual context. Studies were excluded if they used ML exclusively on non-biological data or methodological details were insufficient for critical appraisal. As an approximate account of selection intensity, rather than a systematic review flow, roughly 480 records were identified through database searching and citation tracking. After removal of duplicates and title/abstract screening against the above criteria, approximately 90 full-text sources were assessed for eligibility, of which 41 primary studies, reviews, and authoritative reports were retained for synthesis.

To improve transparency and mitigate confirmation bias, screening and source selection were performed by the authors and cross-checked against eligibility criteria; disagreements were resolved through discussion. We acknowledge that, as a narrative review, this process does not constitute dual independent screening. Data extraction focused on study design, sample characteristics, ML methodology, performance metrics, biological interpretability, validation strategy, and key findings. All cited references were verified for authenticity through direct database confirmation.

Foundational Methodologies in Autism Spectrum Disorder Genomic Research

Explainable Artificial Intelligence and Bioinformatics Integration

The integration of XAI frameworks with advanced bioinformatics pipelines represents a notable advancement in ASD genomic research6, 7. Traditional black-box ML models, while often achieving competitive predictive accuracy, provide limited insight into the biological mechanisms underlying their predictions. XAI methodologies, particularly SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations, partially address this limitation by quantifying the contribution of individual genetic features to model predictions6, 7. Feature attribution improves interpretability, not predictive performance; a model can be highly interpretable yet only modestly discriminative.

A study by Nahas et al.8 employed XAI approaches to analyze gene expression data from five independent Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets, comprising 351 neurotypical controls and 358 individuals with ASD. Using recursive feature elimination (RFE) combined with SHAP analysis, the investigators identified the MID2 gene as a candidate ASD biomarker. Because MID2’s status as an ASD biomarker rests on a single study8 and it is not a high-confidence SFARI gene, it should be regarded as a provisional candidate rather than an established biomarker. The XAI framework prioritized six additional candidate biomarker genes through text mining and statistical validation: HOXB3, NR2F2, MAPK8IP3, PIGT, SEMA4D, and SSH1 (Table 1). The analysis also highlighted RPS4Y1 and KDM5D, two Y-chromosome-linked genes; their apparent contribution may partly reflect the male predominance of the datasets rather than ASD-specific biology8. Deep Learning Architectures: The STAR-NN Model and Beyond

The Separate Translated Autism Research Neural Network (STAR-NN) exemplifies architecture-level innovation tailored to the biological reality of genetic variation, as described by Wu et al.9 Traditional ML approaches treat all genetic variants as equivalent inputs, ignoring the biological reality that different mutation types have distinct functional consequences.

STAR-NN addresses this limitation through a three-to-one sparse mapping architecture that separately processes distinct variant categories before gene-level integration: (1) protein truncating variants; (2) possibly damaging missense variants with missense badness, polyphen-2, constraint (MPC) scores greater than 1; and (3) mild-effect missense variants with MPC scores of 0-1. The model was trained using WES data from 43,203 individuals (16,809 ASD cases; 26,394 controls) in the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK) dataset. Feature selection using the tree-based pipeline optimization tool identified 1,487 optimal gene features, 115 of which overlapped the SFARI gene database (hypergeometric test p=3.057×10-5)9.

STAR-NN achieved a receiver operating characteristic-area under the curve (ROC-AUC) of 0.7319 on the held-out test set and 0.7302 on an independent validation cohort of 27,879 individuals, demonstrating robust generalizability and outperforming traditional ML models including logistic regression (ROC-AUC≈0.71), random forest, XGBoost, and support vector machines (SVM) (Table 2). We caution that the original study did not report 95% confidence intervals or DeLong tests for pairwise AUC comparisons. Formal calibration metrics (e.g., Brier score and calibration plots) are needed to substantiate claims of superiority9. The ≈0.73 figure should itself be read as an optimistic-leaning point estimate rather than a fixed benchmark: it derives from very few studies (principally STAR-NN9, with the non-coding framework10 addressing a different task—variant-burden comparison in simplex families), and SPARK’s cryptic relatedness and ascertainment structure tend to inflate apparent discrimination. We therefore treat it as an approximate ceiling for current specialized genomic models, not an established constant. Figure 2 illustrates the STAR-NN architecture, baseline model comparisons, and SHAP-based explainability framework.

Work from 2025-2026 has further explored the application of deep learning to ASD genomics. Investigators at Princeton University reported four candidate biologically distinct subtypes of autism identified using computational analysis of data from more than 5,000 children21, 22. As of this writing, the corresponding peer-reviewed primary publication was not available; accordingly, the specific quantitative claims attributed to this work should be regarded as provisional pending peer-reviewed publication.

Applications to Diverse Molecular Data Types

Transcriptomic Analysis and Differential Expression

Large-scale meta-analyses of gene expression data have provided statistical evidence for widespread transcriptional alterations in ASD. The integration of five independent GEO datasets (GSE37772, GSE111175, GSE42133, GSE64018, and GSE7329) comprising 709 total samples (358 ASD; 351 controls) enabled the identification of 3,339 DEGs with adjusted p-values ≤0.058. Stringent meta-analysis criteria (adjusted p≤0.001) refined this to 342 highly significant DEGs, including 19 consistently upregulated and 10 consistently downregulated genes across all datasets (Table 3). Because these datasets derive from heterogeneous microarray and RNA-sequencing platforms, between-study heterogeneity and the specific normalization and batch-correction procedures materially affect the robustness of the pooled estimates; therefore, we interpret the 342 high-confidence DEGs as candidates warranting independent replication.

Pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed overrepresentation of calcium ion transport, synaptic transmission, neuronal development, and immune response pathways, consistent with established ASD neurobiology. The calcium signaling enrichment is concordant with the STAR-NN Gene Ontology analysis9; cross-methodological concordance must be interpreted cautiously when studies share cohorts, pipelines, or annotation databases.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism and De Novo Variant Analysis

SNP analyses using ML approaches have catalogued substantial genetic variation associated with ASD risk. Advanced bioinformatics pipelines identified 1,286 ASD-linked SNPs using integrative analysis of genomic and transcriptomic data8. Among these, 14 high-risk SNPs were observed on chromosomes 10 and X. We emphasize that apparent chromosomal clustering requires formal statistical testing for non-random spatial enrichment before it can be interpreted as evidence of regional susceptibility hotspots.

De novo mutations provide powerful evidence for ASD risk genes by circumventing population stratification confounds. An analysis of 27,232 individuals identified 13 unrelated cases with de novo variants in MAPK8IP3 (encoding JIP3), representing a statistically significant enrichment (p=6.165×10-5)20. MAPK8IP3 is, therefore, more accurately described as a neurodevelopmental risk gene with variable ASD co-occurrence than as an ASD-specific gene (Table 1).

Non-Coding Variant Analysis Through Deep Learning

While protein-coding variants have historically received primary attention, non-coding regions comprise the large majority of the human genome and harbor substantial regulatory information. A foundational deep learning framework developed by Zhou et al.10 in 2019 addressed this challenge using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on biochemical profiling data to predict regulatory effects. When applied to 1,790 ASD simplex families with WGS, the framework indicated that ASD probands harbored higher burdens of mutations disrupting transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulation than unaffected siblings.

Because this review covers literature through May 2026, the Zhou et al.10 framework should be understood as a landmark but no longer state-of-the-art approach. Since 2019, sequence-to-function modeling has advanced substantially: attention-based and transformer-based architectures (e.g., Enformer) and large self-supervised genomic language models have begun to be applied to the interpretation of regulatory variants. Critical work in 2025 by Li and Ernst23 demonstrated that accounting for local guanine-cytosine content is essential to avoid spurious associations when interpreting non-coding variant effects.

Epigenetic Modifications and DNA Methylation

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, provide mechanistic links between genetic predisposition and environmental influences. ML approaches have identified candidate methylation biomarkers for ASD classification. A study of high-functioning adults with ASD identified cg20793532 within the PPP2R2C gene as a differentially methylated position using random forest and Coarse Approximation Linear Function algorithms11, with this CpG site showing AUC=0.79 for ASD classification. Recent analysis of peripheral blood from school-aged children identified 138 differentially methylated positions. SVM-RFE with cross-validation selected 11 optimal CpG sites, and random forest and XGBoost achieving roughly 75% classification accuracy12.

Placental DNA methylation analysis has been reported to achieve an AUC=1.00 for autism detection using differentially methylated loci identified at birth24. This perfect classification result warrants strong skepticism. An AUC of 1.00, with 100% sensitivity and specificity, is extraordinarily unusual in any biological prediction task; in our assessment, it is more consistent with overfitting, information leakage between training and validation partitions, or an inadequate validation design than with genuine signal, though the primary report does not provide the information needed to distinguish these possibilities definitively. This value is treated as an outlier in Tables 4 and 5. Broader reviews of DNA methylation as a pharmacological target and biomarker in ASD25 and multi-region brain methylation profiling26 provide important biological context for these ML-derived findings.

Gut Microbiome Analysis

The gut microbiome has been proposed as a contributing factor in ASD, and ML approaches have enabled identification of microbial signatures that are statistically associated with ASD. We stress at the outset that the cited studies are cross-sectional and therefore establish an association, not causation. A microbiome signature associated with ASD across studies was reported using the recursive ensemble feature selection (REFS) algorithm by Peralta-Marzal et al.13, analyzing 16S rRNA sequencing from 60 autistic American children aged 2-7 years and 57 of their unaffected siblings; REFS identified 26 amplicon sequence variants that distinguish children with ASD from controls; these were validated across independent datasets (Table 5).

A study by Su et al.14 performed metagenomic sequencing on fecal samples from 1,627 children with or without ASD aged 1-13 years across five cohorts in China. After controlling for confounders, the authors reported alterations in 14 archaea, 51 bacteria, 7 fungi, 18 viruses, 27 microbial genes, and 12 metabolic pathways also and constructed an ML model from a 31-feature multi-kingdom panel14. A predictive model by Dan et al.15 used 692 gut microbiota samples from public 16S rRNA datasets. Feature-selected random forest models achieved a training AUC of 1.00, a testing AUC of 0.86, and age-stratified testing AUCs of 0.82 (ages 2-4), 0.79 (ages 5-7), and 0.66 (ages 8-13) (Table 5). A training AUC of 1.00 is a hallmark of overfitting, not of model quality; the lower age-stratified testing AUCs better reflect generalizable performance.

The associational nature of these findings is underscored by Yap et al.28, who performed a large stool metagenomic study (n=247, drawn from the Australian Autism Biobank and a twin cohort) and found negligible direct associations between ASD diagnosis and the gut microbiome; instead, ASD-related restricted interests were associated with a less-diverse diet and, in turn, with reduced microbial taxonomic diversity and looser stool consistency. This result illustrates that microbiome differences reported in ASD may partly reflect a diet driven by autistic traits—a reverse-causation pathway—rather than a microbial contribution to ASD. It implies that ML classifiers trained on taxonomic features may be learning correlates of diet and stool consistency rather than of disease biology.

Multi-Omics Integration and Network-Based Approaches

Recent work has extended beyond single-data-type analysis to integrate multiple molecular layers. A 2025 multi-omics study by Liao et al.16 reported two variants associated with increased ASD risk—rs2735307 and rs989134—through cross-organ integration suggesting synergistic regulatory networks spanning brain, gut, and immune systems. In 2026, an integrative multi-omics analysis of the Roots of Autism and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder Twin Study in Sweden cohort applied the Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker Discovery using Latent Variable Approaches for Omics Studies framework to identify cross-omics features. This work is available as a medRxiv preprint (unreviewed)17. Statistical frameworks for high-dimensional multi-omics data integration in neurodevelopmental disorders have been reviewed separately31. Figure 3 illustrates the hypothesized gut microbiome-immune-brain axis regulatory network through which candidate ASD risk variants may exert cross-organ effects.

Further multi-omics frameworks include the NeuroPathX system, described by Wang et al.32 in a 2026 peer-reviewed conference publication which integrates structural neuroimaging with genetic pathway data using cross-attention mechanisms. Network-based neighborhood regression frameworks, introduced in 2025 by Zhen and Du33, model the strength of regulation between gene modules and have been applied conceptually to WES and RNA-sequencing ASD datasets.

Single-Cell Transcriptomics and Advanced Deep Learning

Single-cell RNA sequencing enables high-resolution analysis of cellular heterogeneity in ASD. The single-cell mixed effects deep autoencoder learning framework addresses batch-effect challenges by separately modeling batch-invariant and batch-specific effects, as described by Andrade et al.34 in a 2026 peer-reviewed publication. A study by Liu et al.35 used single-nucleus RNA sequencing to assess expression of 159 necroptosis-related genes in children with ASD. Unsupervised clustering categorized children into two necroptosis subtypes, and four characteristic necroptosis-related genes (TICAM1, CASP1, CAPN1, and CHMP4A) were identified using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, random forest, and SVM-RFE; these genes were reported to be predictive of ASD onset35. Additional advanced frameworks in this period include a generative adversarial network with deep Q-learning applied to neuroimaging data for ASD identification36 and a network-based approach exploiting non-invasive salivary biomarkers37. Dataset Characteristics, Quality Control, and Validation Frameworks

Cohort Demographics and Study Design

The statistical power of ML studies in ASD genomics has been enhanced through multi-cohort designs and large-scale biobank initiatives (Table 7). The SPARK dataset is the most comprehensive genetic resource for ASD, providing WES data for over 43,000 individuals with detailed phenotypic characterization9. Two ascertainment biases warrant emphasis. First, SPARK and comparable cohorts are skewed toward individuals of European ancestry (approximately 70% of SPARK participants). Second, families who enroll in biobanks such as SPARK tend to be wealthier and more educated than the broader ASD population, creating a socioeconomic ascertainment bias that genetic principal components cannot correct. These asymmetries are compounded in low- and middle-income settings, where genomic data-generation infrastructure, reference panels, and biobank participation remain sparse, further narrowing whom current models represent. The 2025 launch of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Autism Data Science Initiative (ADSI), a $50 million effort announced in September 202539, aims to apply advanced analytic methods—including ML, exposome-wide analyses, and organoid models—to study how gene-environment interactions contribute to ASD.

Quality Control and Validation Frameworks

Rigorous quality control is essential for valid ML inference in genomic studies. Standard pipelines include variant-level quality control (coverage depth >20×, call quality scores, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium testing), sample-level quality control (contamination assessment, sex concordance, relatedness filtering), population stratification analysis, and batch-effect correction. The STAR-NN study used DeepVariant for variant calling9.

A robust validation framework should extend well beyond a single discrimination metric. In addition to independent test sets, external validation cohorts, cross-ancestry validation, and phenotypic stratification, it should also incorporate temporal validation, site/batch validation, probabilistic calibration, and clinical-utility evaluation. Reporting ROC-AUC alone—as is common across this literature—is insufficient for judging clinical utility. Current specialized genomic models report ROC-AUC values clustering around 0.73 across independent validation sets (Table 4); we interpret this as evidence of a genuine but modest biological signal rather than of clinically deployable performance, while noting (as above) that even the ≈0.73 value is an optimistic-leaning estimate given SPARK’s relatedness and ascertainment structure. We treat reported AUC values at or near 1.00 (Tables 4 and 5) as methodologically suspect outliers.

Pathway Analysis and Functional Insights

Convergent Biological Pathways

ML-based gene prioritization has revealed recurrent enrichment of specific biological pathways across diverse analytical approaches (Table 8). The apparent convergence of coding variant studies9, non-coding regulatory analysis10, and transcriptomic meta-analysis8 on synaptic transmission pathways is frequently cited as evidence of biological coherence. Oxidative stress and NRF2-mediated antioxidant pathways represent additional recurrently implicated mechanisms, with pharmacogenomic ML studies identifying NFE2L2 variants and NRF2 dysregulation as candidate ASD-associated processes29, 30. We caution that this reasoning carries a real risk of circularity: when multiple studies draw on overlapping cohorts, apply similar ML pipelines, and rely on the same pathway annotation databases, apparent convergence may partly reflect shared methodological and resource dependencies rather than independent biological corroboration. Figure 4 presents a network diagram illustrating how ML approaches, when applied to diverse data types, implicate a recurring set of biological pathways in ASD.

Network-Based Gene Interactions and Subtype Identification

Beyond individual gene associations, ML approaches have characterized gene-interaction networks underlying ASD risk. Protein-protein interaction network analysis indicates that ASD-associated genes form interconnected clusters, or disease modules, with SHANK3 frequently appearing as a central hub9. Disease-agnostic gene prioritization frameworks, such as mantis-ml40, provide complementary approaches for ranking candidate ASD risk genes within such networks. A study by Yuwattana et al.38 applied ML to enhance ASD diagnosis and subgrouping using a sample of 2,480 ASD cases. Unsupervised k-means clustering identified three subgroups based on autism diagnostic interview-revised scores, and a clinical-phenotype-based approach outperformed a transcriptome-based approach in correlating molecular and clinical features (r=0.80 for the clinical-phenotype-based approach vs. r=0.61 for the transcriptome-based approach)38. A complementary approach employing topological data analysis and network measures to redefine ASD subtypes has also been reported in a preprint41.

DISCUSSION

Methodological Innovations in Context

Several recent reviews provide contextual benchmarks for our synthesis. Al-Tamimi et al.6 conducted a systematic review of ML for genetic variant prediction in ASD (2009-2024) and concluded that deep learning architectures can achieve superior accuracy relative to traditional ML. Our review extends this assessment to a broader array of modalities—non-coding variants, epigenetic marks, gut microbiome profiles, single-cell data, and multi-omics integration. We agree that deep learning offers advantages for high-dimensional genomic prediction, but we emphasize that even the largest cohorts yield only modest discrimination (ROC-AUC≈0.73 for STAR-NN9), consistent with the low common-variant heritability estimated by Grove et al.5 and implying that genetic data alone do not capture the majority of ASD liability.

Agrawal and Agrawal7 reviewed explainable AI in early autism detection across neuroimaging, genetic, and behavioral modalities, highlighting interpretability as a prerequisite for clinical adoption. We extend their diagnostic focus by examining how XAI can surface biological mechanisms (e.g., MID2 as a top discriminative feature8; convergence of coding and non-coding effects on synaptic pathways9, 10), while reiterating that interpretability and discrimination are distinct properties.

Prioritization, Not Prediction

Taken together, the observations above suggest that the principal near-term yield of ML in ASD genomics may be mechanistic gene prioritization rather than case/control prediction. When discrimination is modest and bounded by low common-variant heritability, the more durable contribution of specialized, interpretable architectures is that their feature attributions nominate biologically plausible genes and pathways—MID2, synaptic and calcium-signaling genes, MAPK8IP3-linked axonal transport—for experimental follow-up. When framed this way, a model’s value lies less in classifying individuals than in ranking candidate genes and in generating testable mechanistic hypotheses, an aim for which even an AUC near 0.73 can be scientifically useful while remaining clinically insufficient. This reframing also realigns evaluation: prioritization is better judged by enrichment for independently validated risk genes and by experimental confirmation, rather than by discrimination metrics alone.

Cross-Modal Biological Convergence and Discrepancies

A central theme is the recurrent—though not fully independent—appearance of a limited set of pathways across modalities: synaptic transmission, neuronal development, calcium signaling, immune regulation, and axonal transport recur across coding variant studies9, non-coding analyses10, transcriptomic meta-analyses8, epigenetic profiling11, 12, and multi-omics integration16, 17. We interpret this as strengthening, but not proving, the biological coherence of ML predictions, given shared cohorts and annotation resources.

Important discrepancies also exist. Gut microbiome studies implicate microbial taxa and metabolic pathways that do not directly overlap nuclear genetic pathways13, 14, 15. Single-cell analyses34, 35 reveal cell-type-specific disruptions—particularly in necroptosis-related immune pathways and glutamatergic neurons—that may be diluted in bulk assays.

Negative Results, Failed Replications, and Reproducibility

A critical review must engage with the “graveyard” of negative and non-replicated results, not only with successful ones. Microbiome ML is susceptible to overfitting and batch confounding; reported perfect or near-perfect training metrics (e.g., training AUC=1.00 in Dan et al.15) and small-cohort perfect classification (e.g., placental methylation AUC=1.0024) are, in our assessment, more consistent with overfitting than with genuine performance, though the primary reports do not always contain the information required to confirm the mechanism. Similarly, administrative-data models reporting extraordinary discrimination (e.g., AUC=0.997 for national-health-record prediction27) are frequently attributable to leakage of outcome-correlated codes rather than biological signal. Based on the evidence reviewed, genuine progress in the field is best characterized as modest and incremental. This interpretation is reinforced by Yap et al.28, whose controlled design attributed much of the ASD-microbiome association to diet rather than to a microbial contribution.

Population Bias, Socioeconomic Ascertainment, and the Generalizability Crisis

The European-ancestry skew in current ASD datasets—approximately 70% of SPARK participants9—is a critical equity issue. Biobank participants are also systematically wealthier and more educated than the broader ASD population, a socioeconomic bias, which genetic principal components cannot correct. These limitations are amplified in low- and middle-income settings, where sequencing infrastructure, ancestry-matched reference panels, and biobank participation are scarce, resulting in models trained on existing data transferring poorly to the majority of the world’s population. Strategies to address ancestry bias include diverse cohort recruitment, transfer learning, population-specific model development, and ancestry-aware architectures. We caution that transfer learning is not a panacea; the 2025 NIH ADSI39 represents a promising step toward more equitable data collection.

Translational Challenges: From Prediction to Clinical Actionability

Even granting modest but real discrimination (ROC-AUC≈0.73), translation to practice faces several obstacles. In a population with ASD prevalence near 3.2%, even 90% specificity yields a low positive predictive value owing to the base-rate effect; therefore, ensemble approaches combining genetic, neuroimaging, and behavioral data will likely be required to achieve clinically useful performance. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) frameworks for ML-based genetic tests require demonstrated analytical validity, clinical validity, and clinical utility; the latter of which is essentially undocumented for ASD genetic prediction.

Clinical and Research Consequence

The findings synthesized in this review have several specific implications for clinical practice and the design of future research. From a clinical perspective, current ML-based genomic models do not yet provide actionable diagnostic or predictive information beyond established clinical assessment tools. The best-validated architecture (STAR-NN) achieves ROC-AUC≈0.739—insufficient for stand-alone diagnostic deployment given the base-rate consequences of 3.2% ASD prevalence in the surveillance population1. Practitioners should therefore treat ML-derived genomic scores as hypothesis-generating adjuncts rather than decision-support instruments, and reported AUC values at or near 1.0024, 27 should be interpreted with extreme caution pending independent external validation.

Concretely, a genomic score with a modest AUC today would not change how autism is diagnosed, which remains a clinical determination based on standardized behavioral assessment. At most, such a score might flag a specific gene or pathway that warrants confirmatory clinical genetic testing (for example, targeted sequencing of a nominated high-confidence gene) or slightly closer developmental surveillance, always interpreted alongside and never in place of clinical evaluation. It would not, by itself, justify a prognostic statement to a family, a change in intervention, or any prenatal decision. Understood this way, the score functions as a research and triage signal rather than a clinical test.

The priorities set out below are the authors’ recommendations rather than conclusions established by the reviewed studies. For research, the highest-priority recommendations are: (1) mandatory external validation across ancestrally and socioeconomically diverse cohorts before any performance claim is published; (2) adoption of probabilistic calibration reporting (Brier score, calibration plots) alongside discrimination metrics; (3) pre-registration of analytic plans to curb post-hoc reporting bias; (4) longitudinal study designs and, where suitable genetic instruments exist, Mendelian randomization capable of distinguishing causal from associational microbiome and epigenetic findings; (5) integration of autistic self-advocate perspectives into research priority-setting; and (6) development of a regulatory science framework—in collaboration with FDA and EMA—for ML-based ASD biomarker tests. Initiatives such as the NIH ADSI39 and the Princeton subtype-discovery program21, 22 represent meaningful steps toward these goals, provided that their outputs are subjected to rigorous independent replication before clinical translation is pursued.

Ethical, Neurodiversity, and Regulatory Considerations

Aggregating large genetic datasets raises substantive ethical questions, including privacy and re-identification risks, implications for family members, and concerns about insurance and employment discrimination. A 2026 review must engage with the neurodiversity paradigm: many autistic self-advocates and disability scholars argue that framing ASD as a condition to be predicted and prevented is itself harmful. Prediction research—especially prenatal prediction using placental biomarkers24—raises the prospect of stigmatization and the privileging of a deficit model over a difference model of autism. We hold—as a normative position rather than as a finding of the reviewed studies—that ML-ASD research should involve autistic people in setting research priorities and determining acceptable uses.

Federated learning, which trains models across distributed datasets without centralizing sensitive information, offers a partial technical safeguard; reported approaches have achieved high accuracy in ASD detection while preserving privacy7. We advocate that federated learning and differential privacy become standard for multi-site ASD genetic ML and stress that these technical measures complement—rather than substitute for—community governance and ethical considerations.

CONCLUSION

The application of ML to ASD genomic research has produced incremental but meaningful advances rather than a wholesale transformation of clinical understanding. Concrete contributions include candidate biomarker discovery through XAI-based analysis of gene expression; specialized predictive architectures achieving modest, biologically interpretable discrimination (ROC-AUC≈0.73); deep learning frameworks for non-coding regulatory variants; blood-based and placental DNA-methylation signatures; gut microbiome signatures associated with—but not shown to cause—ASD; and provisional reports of biologically distinct autism subtypes. Significant challenges persist. Population and socioeconomic ascertainment biases limit generalizability; association is routinely conflated with causation in the microbiome and epigenetic literatures; implausible performance metrics recur and, in our assessment, are best treated as artifacts; the translation gap from prediction to clinical actionability remains wide. As our synthesis suggests, the most defensible near-term role for these methods is mechanistic gene prioritization and hypothesis generation rather than individual-level prediction. Future priorities should include expansion of diverse, socioeconomically representative cohorts; multi-modal data integration; longitudinal and causal-inference designs; transparent reporting; and regulatory science. Initiatives such as the NIH ADSI and emerging subtype frameworks represent a promising direction, but the responsible path forward is measured; ML is a powerful tool for hypothesis generation and incremental prediction in ASD genomics, not yet a transformative force in clinical care.
