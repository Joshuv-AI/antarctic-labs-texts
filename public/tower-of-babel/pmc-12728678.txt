Goalie Phenomenon: Unveiling Hidden Health Conditions in Young Athletes
Authors: Jaber H. Jaradat, Rawan Hamamreh, Abdulqadir J. Nashwan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Jaradat et al. (2025) first describe the goalie phenomenon in youth sports in which children with undiagnosed asthma gravitate toward less physically demanding positions, such as goalkeepers, to avoid exercise‐induced respiratory symptoms like breathlessness, wheezing, or coughing. This compensatory behavior may inadvertently mask underlying health conditions, underscoring the importance of clinical vigilance and early diagnostic assessment in pediatric athletes

Goalie Phenomenon

The goalie phenomenon describes a group of health issues that may become apparent in children who prefer playing as goalkeepers. This preference might not only be about the excitement of the role but also as a coping mechanism for underlying health issues that make continuous running and high physical exertion challenging. The term highlights how certain positions in sports can sometimes obscure or reveal hidden health conditions, particularly among young athletes [1, 2, 3]. To our knowledge, this is the first paper to formally introduce the term “Goalie phenomenon” in the literature exploring exercise‐induced asthma and sports role preferences in children. Unlike traditional research that focuses on physiological triggers, this paper presents a behavioral and observational screening hypothesis, which may prompt earlier clinical investigation in children whose symptoms may otherwise be overlooked.

Goalkeepers must react quickly and handle occasional high‐impact situations, but their continuous running is less than that of field players. Children with asthma often choose positions that involve less continuous physical exertion, such as a goalkeeper in soccer, a goalie in hockey, or a libero in volleyball, which may also provide opportunities for participation while minimizing the risk of exacerbating respiratory symptoms [4, 5].

Consider an imaginary case of a 6‐year‐old soccer player who prefers to play as a goalkeeper rather than in other physically demanding roles. Initially, this choice was considered a sign of skill, but it obscured an underlying medical condition. Once, when he was obliged to leave this role and play another role, he appeared severely exhausted and breathless compared to his teammates. After a medical examination, he was found to have undiagnosed asthma that undermined his ability to take on other roles. His role as a goalkeeper helped him manage his condition while avoiding physically demanding positions [6, 7]. Such scenarios are common in youth sports, where conditions such as asthma often lead children to choose positions that minimize physical exertion. Although this example is hypothetical, it reflects behavioral trends noted in the literature and may serve as a foundation for future clinical reporting. We hope this conceptual introduction encourages further research and documentation of real‐world cases that align with the goalie phenomenon.

The causal chain proposed by the goalie phenomenon follows this pathway: (1) children with exercise‐induced asthma experience discomfort during high‐exertion activities; (2) to mitigate symptoms, they gravitate toward positions involving less physical strain; (3) this positional preference is interpreted as personal interest or skill rather than a compensatory behavior; (4) as a result, underlying asthma remains undetected, leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment (Figure 1). This causal model underscores the importance of considering behavioral adaptations as early signs of chronic illness [8, 9].

Understanding the goalie phenomenon in youth sports.

Asthma is the most prevalent chronic disease among children globally, affecting approximately 9.1% of children and 11.0% of adolescents [8]. It is characterized by inflammation and narrowing of the airways, leading to symptoms such as shortness of breath, wheezing, and coughing [1]. Moreover, exercise‐induced bronchoconstriction (EIB), a common manifestation in asthmatic individuals, is reported in about 40%–90% of children with asthma. Despite its prevalence, asthma often remains underdiagnosed or undertreated in young athletes [2]. In contrast to soccer, sports like baseball, volleyball, and taekwondo provoke fewer symptoms, involving less continuous, high‐intensity breathing [3, 4]. This underdiagnosis can lead children to self‐select into less physically demanding roles in sports, such as goalkeepers in soccer, to manage their symptoms discreetly. Such behavioral adaptations underscore the plausibility of the “Goalie phenomenon,” where positional preferences may mask underlying health conditions, warranting systematic evaluation by the healthcare provider and requiring attention of the coaches and parents.

Patients with asthma can safely participate in various sports; however, choosing appropriate activities is essential to control symptoms. Low‐impact and intermittent activities like baseball, volleyball, and golfing are generally less likely to trigger asthma symptoms than more intense sports such as soccer (football) and basketball [5]. To avoid exercise‐induced asthma symptoms, it is essential to implement both nonpharmacological and pharmacological strategies. Nonpharmacological approaches include avoiding exercise when air quality is poor, taking precautions such as covering the nose and mouth in cold weather, and maintaining hydration [6]. Pharmacological approaches include using a quick‐relief inhaler and short‐acting beta‐agonists before exercise. For ongoing control, daily controller medications include inhaled corticosteroids, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and mast cell stabilizing agents [7]. It is essential to consult a doctor before starting a new sport or exercise program.

Athletes often compete in diverse environments, where factors such as cold air or low ceilings in the rink can impact the frequency and severity of asthma symptoms. This underscores the importance of considering local environmental conditions when assessing asthma triggers in youth sports, highlighting the necessity for individualized monitoring of children based on their playing settings' specific climate and environmental factors [4, 9].

Recommendations

Parents, coaches, and sports educators should be aware of signs that may indicate health issues in children. This awareness allows young athletes to participate in sports without unnecessary limitations. Additionally, it is crucial to recognize that a child's preference for certain positions may be a coping mechanism.

In addition, parents should be proactive in seeking medical evaluation if they notice signs of potential health issues in their children. Regular health checks can aid in the early detection and management of conditions. Likewise, coaches and sports programs are crucial for helping children with asthma by creating an inclusive environment and enhancing awareness. They should implement practices that allow children with health conditions to engage safely in activities, educate themselves and their teams about asthma, and implement effective management strategies.

A comprehensive analysis of the health impacts on athletes across various sports would provide a more nuanced understanding of how specific athletic roles affect health outcomes [9]. Limitations

This article introduces the “Goalie phenomenon” as a conceptual hypothesis based on behavioral patterns observed in youth sports, particularly among children with asthma. However, the absence of direct empirical data limits the generalizability of our conclusions. While helpful for conceptual clarity, the hypothetical case used for illustration may also introduce observer interpretation bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, it is essential to note that implementing comprehensive health screening and asthma management programs may be challenging in resource‐limited settings due to limited access to healthcare services or specialized personnel. Therefore, regionally adapted strategies such as community‐based awareness programs and school‐level screening should be considered alternative pathways to early detection and support for children with underlying health conditions. Parents, coaches, and sports programs should work together to create an inclusive environment supporting all children's health and safety in sports. Future research should aim to validate this phenomenon through clinical observations, case reporting, and population‐based studies.
