Psychological flexibility typologies and non-suicidal self-injury behavior among Chinese adolescents: a latent profile and multiple mediation analysis
Authors: Yuhan Ni, Shuanghu Fang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is a common concern among adolescents. While psychological flexibility (PF) has been established as a key protective factor that prevents NSSI in adolescents, its potential heterogeneity and the mechanisms by which PF influences NSSI remain unclear. The purpose of this study was to explore the heterogeneity of PF among Chinese adolescents and examine whether negative emotions and school interpersonal relationships mediate the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior.MethodsA convenience sampling method was employed. Participants included 1,562 Chinese adolescents (mean age 13.13 years, SD = 0.99; 54.42% males) from Anhui Province. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was performed to identify the heterogeneity in adolescent PF, and structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed to examine the multiple mediating roles of negative emotions and school interpersonal relationships in the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior.ResultsThe following five distinct PF profiles were identified: the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup (4.42%), the low-PF subgroup (15.43%), the medium-PF subgroup (33.80%), the high-PF subgroup (33.35%), and the extremely high-PF subgroup (13.00%). Both negative emotions and school interpersonal relationships significantly mediated the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior. Compared with adolescents in the extremely high PF subgroup, adolescents in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, low-PF subgroup, and medium-PF subgroup presented higher levels of negative emotions, poorer school interpersonal relationships, and greater degrees of NSSI behavior. In particular, among the five profile groups, adolescents in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup exhibited the greatest degree of NSSI behavior.ConclusionsPF profile affected NSSI behavior among adolescents indirectly through negative emotions and school interpersonal relationships, and stronger multiple mediating effects were observed among adolescents with lower levels of PF and openness. Our study highlights the importance of focusing on the heterogeneity of PF among adolescents, the critical role of openness, and the need for tailored interventions to improve PF as well as emotional, interpersonal, and behavioral issues.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03605-z.Keywords: Adolescent, Psychological flexibility, NSSI behavior, Latent profile analysis, Multiple mediation analysis

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is a prevalent and urgent concern among adolescents [1, 2]. NSSI refers to the direct and repetitive infliction of physical harm without suicidal intent, and it includes behaviors such as cutting, burning, hitting, and scratching [3]. Adolescence is a period of vulnerability to NSSI, potentially due to increased impulsivity and emotional reactivity associated with ongoing brain maturation [4]. Meta-analytic research has shown that the prevalence of NSSI among Chinese middle school students was 22.37% (95% CI: 18.84–25.70%) [5], and data suggest this trend has been increasing in recent years [6, 7]. Beyond direct physical harm, NSSI serves as a critical marker of poor mental health. Engagement in NSSI is associated with a variety of comorbid difficulties, including mental disorders, problem behaviors, and interpersonal dysfunction [8, 9]. Notably, some adolescents continue to engage in self-injurious behaviors into adulthood [10]. Numerous studies suggest that NSSI predicts an elevated risk of future suicidal behavior [11, 12]. Therefore, clarifying the mechanisms underlying adolescent NSSI is crucial for informing prevention and intervention efforts.

Several theoretical models have been proposed to better understand the mechanisms underlying NSSI. Among the earliest is the Experiential Avoidance Model (EAM), which posits that NSSI is primarily a mechanism for escaping distressing emotional experiences that is maintained by negative reinforcement, in which the behavior is followed by an immediate decrease or cessation of emotional distress [13]. For example, Laye-Gindhu and Schonert-Reichl [14] reported that adolescents experience reduced feelings of anger, depression, loneliness, and frustration during and immediately after self-injury, accompanied by a sense of relief. However, NSSI may provide only a temporary relief but ultimately perpetuates difficulties. Indeed, longitudinal studies have demonstrated that NSSI often leads to negative emotions in the long run [15]. Thus, while the EAM provides valuable insights into the short-term functions of NSSI, its explanatory scope remains limited.

Contemporary theoretical models that promote psychological flexibility (PF) [16, 17] have advanced this perspective by empirically developing alternative constructs for understanding how individuals can adaptively respond to internal experiences (e.g., thoughts and emotions). Unlike earlier models, PF theory does not regard reducing distress as the desired outcome of regulatory responses. Rather, reducing distress is considered functional only insofar as it facilitates the pursuit of self-endorsed, meaningful, and valued goals [18]. As Hayes et al. [17] described, PF refers to an individual’s ability to “contact the present moment more fully as a conscious human being, and to change or persist in behavior when doing so serves valued ends” (p.7). PF has been shown to promote adaptive changes in behavior and is considered a protective factor for maintaining healthy functioning [18]. Conceptually, PF is composed of six interrelated subprocesses that are further organized into three pillars: openness (defusion and acceptance), awareness (present moment awareness and self-as-context), and engagement (contact with values and committed action) [19]. The three-pillar model yields a concise yet theoretically robust structure, and recent empirical studies have supported its utility in capturing individual differences in psychological functioning and developmental outcomes [20, 21].

As an important protective factor for a range of positive psychological, interpersonal, and health-related outcomes, PF has been extensively studied in adult populations. Recently, emerging research has emphasized its developmental importance during adolescence. Adolescence is a developmental period that is characterized by rapid emotional, cognitive, and social transitions, including heightened emotional reactivity and identity formation, highlighting the importance of flexible coping strategies [22]. For example, Petersen et al. [23] highlighted the promise of PF as a core mechanism that aids the adolescent development of emotional and cognitive abilities. Kukkola et al. [24] found that PF was both concurrently and prospectively related to adolescent identity processes during educational transitions. These findings position PF as a developmentally sensitive factor that influences adolescent well-being. More importantly, an increasing number of studies on PF during adolescence have explored the relationships between PF and NSSI [25, 26]. Specifically, greater PF has been associated with fewer NSSI behaviors among adolescents, whereas a lack of PF (or psychological inflexibility, PI) has been positively related to several indicators of dysfunction and psychopathology and longitudinally predicts the perceived likelihood of future NSSI [27–29]. Therefore, improving our understanding of PF as a modifiable protective factor is essential for early intervention efforts on adolescent NSSI.

The Four-Function Model of NSSI offers a valuable framework for understanding the potential link between PF and NSSI. According to the model, individuals with intra- or interpersonal vulnerabilities may exhibit affective or social dysregulation in response to challenging or stressful events, creating a need to use NSSI to modulate their experience [3]. As such, both intrapersonal (e.g., internalizing symptoms) and interpersonal (e.g., perceived social support) factors are considered central to the initiation and maintenance of NSSI behaviors [30, 31]. This framework suggests that a comprehensive understanding of NSSI among adolescents requires the consideration of multiple pathways. From the perspective of intrapersonal processes, negative emotions (NEs) are particularly salient. Research has shown that many adolescents who engage in NSSI experience intense NEs prior to an episode (e.g., depression and stress) [32, 33]. Individuals who habitually use NSSI to escape from emotional distress may fail to develop distress tolerance or learn adaptive strategies for managing NEs [34]. PF enables individuals to cope with negative and distressing experiences by improving emotion regulation, facilitating cognitive reappraisal, and guiding value-driven behavior, thereby being closely associated with mental health [35]. These mechanisms may reduce reliance on NSSI [3, 29]. Conversely, lower levels of PF have been linked to increased emotional dysregulation and trigger the use of maladaptive emotion regulation strategies among adolescents [36]. As a result, adolescents with low PF may experience more intense NEs and resort to NSSI as a last means of regulating emotional distress. Collectively, these findings suggest that NEs may serve as a critical intrapersonal mediator in the relationship between PF and NSSI.

From the perspective of interpersonal processes, developmental contextualism posits that adolescent development cannot be examined in isolation from the social and environmental contexts that shape adolescents’ experiences [37]. Similarly, researchers argue that definitions of PF must include repeated transactions between people and their environmental contexts (i.e., the physical and social settings that can influence behavior and perceptions in various situations), rather than focusing on specific content (within a person) [28]. Integrating the contextual framework of PF with the Four-Function Model of NSSI, interpersonal relationships should be considered in relation to adolescent PF and its developmental outcomes, particularly NSSI. Flexible individuals are better equipped to navigate relational challenges by effectively regulating their emotions and actions, thereby fostering more harmonious interpersonal connections and promoting psychological functioning. For example, in a meta-analysis by Daks and Rogge [38], PF served as a critical intra- and interpersonal life skill and was associated with higher-quality family relationships. Those authors also reported that dimensions of PF can meaningfully contribute to our understanding of dynamics in other domains of interpersonal functioning [38]. School is a primary setting for students’ socialization and development. Adverse peer and teacher–student relationship experiences might increase the risk of NSSI behavior [39, 40]. However, previous research has focused predominantly on the associations between PF and interpersonal relationships within the family context, with comparatively little attention given to school-based interpersonal processes. Since PF is a critical intrapersonal trait and school interpersonal relationships (SIRs) play a significant role in predicting NSSI behavior among adolescents, further investigations are warranted to determine whether SIRs function as an interpersonal mediator linking PF to NSSI behavior.

As previously noted, PF is a multifaceted construct. Traditionally, PF has been studied using a variable-centered approach, which uses the total PF score to represent the level of PF [25, 35] or treats the PF subprocesses as the units of analysis [41, 42]. For example, Bramwell and Richardson [41] studied the role of defusion and value-based action in reducing distress and depression, and Wang et al. [42] examined the longitudinal reciprocal relationships among experiential avoidance, cognitive fusion, and suicidal ideation. These achievements have enriched related studies, but certain problems remain. The total score approach assumes that each subprocess of PF affects outcomes to the same degree; however, individuals may perform inconsistently on different PF subprocesses [43]. The single or units of subprocesses cannot fully represent PF [44], which hinders a comprehensive understanding of the PF model and the underlying complex mechanisms. Moreover, the variable-centered approach typically ignores heterogeneity among participants, which may lead to biased parameter estimates. In contrast, person-oriented approaches, such as latent profile analysis (LPA), can help capture heterogeneity by identifying distinct subgroups of individuals on the basis of response patterns [45]. The classification schemes derived from LPA modeling are both conceptually meaningful and methodologically useful for developing and incorporating typologies on the basis of data [46]. Moreover, LPA-based profiles can be incorporated into more complex multivariate analyses involving outcome variables, such as examining whether the associations or pathways among variables vary across subgroups (i.e., latent profiles) [47, 48]. To date, only a few studies have used the person-oriented approach to study PF, with a focus on college students and adults [49–53]. Little evidence has related to PF profiles among adolescents and the relationships between different PF profiles and related outcomes (e.g., NEs, SIRs, and NSSI behavior). Understanding the impact of profiles on these outcomes on the basis of different components of PF can help identify adolescent subgroups, thereby informing tailored interventions aimed at improving emotional, interpersonal, and behavioral disorders.

As such, the first aim of the current study was to investigate the heterogeneity of PF among Chinese adolescents from a person-centered approach. To this end, the present study conducted LPA utilizing six core subprocesses based on the PF model, including defusion, acceptance, present moment awareness, self-as-context, contact with values, and committed action. The second aim was to explore the relationships among PF profiles, NEs, SIRs, and NSSI behavior to further determine specific therapeutic target domains and tailor interventions for different adolescent subgroups. Overall, we propose the following hypotheses: Hypothesis 1 several distinct PF profiles among Chinese adolescents can be identified via LPA. Hypothesis 2 NEs and SIRs may mediate the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior (see Fig. 1).

The hypothesized model. PF = Psychological flexibility, NEs = Negative emotion, SIRs = School interpersonal relationships, NSSI = Non-suicidal self-injury

Methods

Participants and procedure

This study used a convenience sampling method in which data were collected through the online survey platform powered by www.wjx.cn (functionally similar to Amazon Mechanical Turk). We contacted middle schools in Anhui Province and provided head classroom teachers with professional training on questionnaire administration. Subsequently, the QR code for the online survey was distributed to the head classroom teachers, who then guided the students to complete the questionnaire at home under parental supervision. Participants were informed that all data collection was voluntary and that they had the right to decline participation in the study at any time. Data confidentiality was ensured.

A total of 1,600 questionnaires were collected in this study. Thirty-eight participants were excluded because they had an average response time of less than 2 s per item [54] (n = 20) or provided illogical responses (n = 18). Ultimately, 1,562 participants were included in the current analysis, with a retention rate of 97.6%. All 1,562 participants were middle school students (i.e., grades 7 to 9), among whom 54.42% were male (n = 850), and most participants had urban household registration (64.26%; n = 1,004). The mean age of the participants was 13.13 ± 0.99 years (range = 11–16). Table 1 provides a brief descriptive statistical summary of the sample.

Measures

Psychological flexibility

PF was assessed using the PF subscale of the Chinese version of the Multidimensional Psychological Flexibility Inventory (MPFI) [55], which has been demonstrated to have good reliability and validity in Chinese adolescent samples [56]. The PF subscale of the MPFI consists of 30 items for evaluating global PF and its six core subprocesses [57]. Participants were instructed to reflect on their experiences over the past two weeks and respond using a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 6 (always). Higher average scores on the overall PF scale and its subprocesses indicate greater levels of PF. In this study, the PF subscale and its six dimensions (i.e., defusion, acceptance, present moment awareness, self-as-context, contact with values, and committed action) demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.96, 0.87, 0.79, 0.91, 0.83, 1.00, and 1.00, respectively.

Negative emotions

NEs were assessed using the Chinese version of the Depression Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 (DASS-21) [58], which has been shown to have acceptable psychometric properties and has been extensively applied in Chinese adolescent samples [59, 60]. The DASS-21 consists of three subscales, namely depression, anxiety, and stress, which were developed to evaluate three negative emotional states over the past week [61]. Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (totally inconsistent) to 3 (completely consistent). Higher total scores indicate higher levels of negative emotional experience. Recent psychometric research has shown that a general psychological distress factor can be validly represented through the bifactor structure of the DASS-21 [62, 63]. Thus, the construct of NEs was modeled as a latent construct indicated by the three subscales in the present study, providing a more reliable and parsimonious representation of overall negative emotional states while minimizing measurement error associated with observed scores. In this study, the total scale and its subscales (depression, anxiety, and stress) demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.92, 0.81, 0.82, and 0.79, respectively.

School interpersonal relationships

SIRs included the teacher–student relationships (TSRs) and peer relationships (PRs), which were measured by the Chinese version of the Delaware School Climate Scale-Student (DSCS-S) [64]. The DSCS-S is a widely accepted self-report instrument for measuring school climate among young people [65], and it has been demonstrated to have acceptable psychometric properties in Chinese adolescent populations [64]. We adopted the TSR subscale (items 2, 7, 17, 22, and 26) and the PR subscale (items 11, 16, 21, 30, and 31) for use in the current study. Each item was scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Items within the subscales were averaged to create scores for TSRs and PRs, with a higher average score reflecting better quality of SIRs. In this study, the measure of SIRs, the TSR subscale, and the PR subscale demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.95, 0.94, and 0.83, respectively.

Non-suicidal self-injury behavior

NSSI behavior was assessed using the behavior subscale of the Adolescent Non-suicidal Self-injury Assessment Questionnaire (ANSSIA-Q) developed by Wan et al. [66], which has good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents. Participants were asked about the frequency of self-injury behaviors other than suicide, including deliberately hitting themselves, scratching themselves, and banging their heads against hard objects, among other forms, over the past year. The behavior subscale comprises 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (always), with higher scores corresponding to higher levels of NSSI behavior. In this study, the behavior subscale of the ANSSIA-Q demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus 8.0 [67]. First, we examined common method deviation tests and conducted a correlation analysis among the core variables. Second, we performed LPA to identify the subgroups distinguished by the six core subprocesses (i.e., dimensions) of PF. Previous research recommended that the selection of the best-fitting profile solution should rely not only on statistical fit values but also on theoretical and content-related considerations [45, 46, 68, 69]. For statistical fit indices, the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample size adjusted BIC (SABIC), Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR), adjusted Lo–Mendell–Rubin likelihood ratio test (aLMR), and Entropy were utilized as benchmarks [46]. Additionally, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the scores of related outcome variables among samples with different LPA-based profiles. Finally, a multiple mediation analysis in a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework was applied to estimate the mediating role of NEs and SIRs in the relationship between PF profiles and NSSI behavior. Compared with methods based on observed variables, SEM provides greater validity and generalizability by allowing for the correction of measurement error [70]. In this model, LPA-based profiles of PF were treated as a categorical independent variable. NEs and SIRs were considered latent variables with three observed variables (depression, anxiety, and stress) and two observed variables (TSRs and PRs), respectively. NSSI behavior was estimated as an observed variable. A bootstrapping procedure (1000 samples) was used to test the mediation effects of the model. Model fit was evaluated with several global fit indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker‒Lewis index (TLI), standardized root mean squared residual (SRMR), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). The models were considered well-fitted when the CFI and TLI values were approximately 0.95 or greater and when the values of the SRMR and RMSEA were less than 0.08 [71].

Results

Common method deviation test

Since all the data that were used in the current study were collected through self-report measures, the potential for common method bias (CMB) was considered. Therefore, we applied Harman’s single-factor test to examine CMB through an exploratory factor analysis on all 73 items from our study. The test results revealed that there were 11 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 and that the first factor accounted for 17.89% of the total variance (well below the 50% threshold); therefore, CMB was unlikely to pose a significant concern in this study.

Correlation analysis

Table 2 presents the standardized correlation coefficients among the study variables in the current study. The correlation results revealed that PF was negatively correlated with NEs and NSSI behavior (r = − 0.328, p < 0.001; r = − 0.154, p < 0.001; respectively), and positively correlated with SIRs (r = 0.430, p < 0.001). Additionally, NEs were positively correlated with NSSI behavior (r = 0.522, p < 0.001), whereas SIRs were negatively correlated with NSSI behavior (r = − 0.211, p < 0.001).

Latent profile analysis

LPA models with 1 to 6 classes were analyzed to find the optimal PF profile model. Table 3 describes the class solutions from 1- to 6-class solutions. Following methodological recommendations, we considered both statistical fit and theoretical interpretability when selecting the optimal model. From a statistical fit perspective, the LMR and aLMR reached a significant level in the 2- to 5-class solutions, while the p-value for the comparison between the 5- and 6-class solutions exceeded 0.05, suggesting that the latter did not offer a significantly better fit than the 5-class solution did. Although the AIC, BIC, and SABIC continued to decrease with the number of profiles, previous work has cautioned that the information criterion (IC) values may not necessarily reach a single minimum value at a specific number of classes [72]. Therefore, an “elbow” plot can be used to visually inspect the point of diminishing returns in model fit based on these indices. As shown in Supplementary Fig. A1, the elbow plot for the ICs revealed an inflection point at the 4-class solution. To further evaluate the theoretical utility of the competing models, we plotted and compared the conditional mean values of each latent profile in both the 4-class and 5-class solutions. As shown in Supplementary Fig. A2, the 4-class solution produced profiles with a largely ordinal structure (i.e., very high, high, medium, and low). In contrast, the 5-class solution yielded an additional profile (C1) characterized by a distinct configuration of PF indicators. Although this smallest class comprised less than 5% of the sample, it captured a theoretically meaningful subgroup with unique features that would have otherwise been masked. Therefore, the 5-class solution was selected as the optimal model.

Figure 2 and Supplementary Table A1 show that the conditional mean scores of the 5 latent profiles on the six dimensions of PF were significantly different. The first subgroup (C1, n = 69, accounting for 4.42%) presented lower scores on the defusion and acceptance dimensions and higher scores on the contact with values and committed action dimensions. The results revealed that while the adolescents in the C1 subgroup presented active engagement guided by personal values, they could not actively face and accept the internal experience or disentangle behaviors from verbal traps and rules. Therefore, based on the three-pillar model of PF, the C1 subgroup can be named as the “weakly open-highly engaged subgroup”. For the C2 to C5 subgroups, these four identified latent classes demonstrated clear levels of low (C2, n = 241, accounting for 15.43%), medium (C3, n = 528, accounting for 33.80%), high (C4, n = 521, accounting for 33.35%), and very high (C5, n = 203, accounting for 13.00%) values in all six dimensions, which can be named as the “low-PF subgroup”, “medium-PF subgroup”, “high-PF subgroup”, and “extremely high-PF subgroup”, respectively.

Multiple mediating analyses

Taking the extremely high-PF subgroup (C5) as the reference, the other four subgroups were compared by using multiple mediation analysis in an SEM framework, and several demographic variables (gender, age, and household registration) were assigned as covariates in the model. The structural model in the multiple mediation analyses exhibited good fit indices (CFI = 0.961, TLI = 0.923, RMSEA = 0.070, and SRMR = 0.040).

As expected, NEs and SIRs had multiple mediating effects on the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior. For example, when the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup (C1) was compared to the extremely high-PF subgroup (C5; reference group), significant relative mediation effects were observed. The total indirect effect was 0.145, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.097, 0.197]. Specifically, the independent mediating effect of NEs was 0.130, where the path coefficient from PF profile to NEs (a1) was 0.244 and the path coefficient from NEs to NSSI (b1) was 0.532. Similarly, the independent mediating effect of SIRs was 0.015, where the path coefficient from PF profile to SIRs (a2) was − 0.171 and the path coefficient from SIRs to NSSI (b2) was − 0.086. Thus, compared with the extremely high-PF subgroup, the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup presented higher levels of NEs and poorer SIRs, resulting in a greater degree of NSSI behavior. Compared with that of the extremely high-PF subgroup (C5), the confidence interval of the two specific paths of the relative mediation effect did not include 0 for the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup (C1), low-PF subgroup (C2), or medium-PF subgroup (C3). The specific paths were as follows: (1) PF profile→NEs→NSSI (effect size: 0.130 for C1, 0.159 for C2, and 0.068 for C3); (2) PF profile→SIRs→NSSI (effect size: 0.015 for C1, 0.034 for C2, and 0.036 for C3). Among the different indirect paths, NEs emerged as the strongest mediator. Notably, the mediating effect of the first path “PF profile→NEs→NSSI” in the low-PF subgroup (C2) accounted for the largest proportion of the total indirect effect (31.99%), followed by the same pathway in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup (C1), which contributed 26.16% of the total indirect effect. Discussion

On the basis of Hayes’ model of PF [16], the present study aimed to identify the heterogeneity of PF and explore the relationships between the heterogeneity of PF and NSSI behavior among adolescents. The current study utilized LPA to identify PF subgroups and employed a multiple mediation model to examine the internal mechanisms by which PF subgroups affect adolescent NSSI behavior.

Latent profiles of PF among Chinese adolescents

Using the six subprocesses (i.e., dimensions) of PF, the LPA results revealed five distinct latent profiles among adolescents: the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, which is characterized by low levels of openness (i.e., defusion and acceptance) and high levels of engagement (i.e., contacted with value and committed action); the low-PF subgroup, which is characterized by low levels of all PF dimension; the medium-PF subgroup, which is characterized by moderate levels of all dimensions; and the high-PF subgroup and extremely high-PF subgroup, which are characterized by uniformly higher levels across all dimensions. Follow-up analysis indicated significant differences among the groups in their scores for the dimensions of PF, which supports our first hypothesis.

The emergence of the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup among adolescents provided empirical support for Hayes’ three-pillar model of PF [16]. While models such as the unified flexibility and mindfulness (UFM) model [73] consider openness to be foundational to engagement, our findings suggest that openness and engagement appear to be interconnected yet function as partially independent mechanisms among certain individuals. A possible explanation may lie in sociocultural influences. In collectivist cultures such as China, individuals may engage in value-driven actions due to social expectations rather than personal openness. Behavioral adjustment is typically well established by adolescence [74], allowing adolescents to act in accordance with socially sanctioned goals even when they experience difficulty in emotional openness or cognitive flexibility. These results suggest the importance of using comprehensive measures to capture multidimensionality when assessing PF. In this regard, the three-pillar model offers a useful theoretical framework for interpreting the heterogeneity of PF, as it helps reveal individuals’ relative strengths and weaknesses across PF domains and informs more targeted interventions. Such a framework appears especially relevant in collectivist cultures where socially sanctioned behavioral norms may drive value and actions independently of internal openness.

Furthermore, we found that the five PF subgroups reported significantly different levels of NEs, SIRs, and NSSI behavior. Specifically, compared with adolescents in the high-PF subgroup, those in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup and the low-PF subgroup experienced more NEs, poorer SIRs, and more NSSI behavior. Consistent with the present findings, previous latent class studies have indicated that the low-PF subgroup often has high levels of emotional problems, whereas the high-PF subgroup has the opposite pattern [51, 52]. Notably, despite demonstrating relatively high levels of engagement, the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup presented the highest propensity for NSSI. This pattern suggests that active engagement in a single domain may not compensate for the risks associated with deficits in openness. Since PF is a malleable capacity, interventions such as Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) have been shown to enhance various processes of adolescent PF by promoting a more open, aware, and engaged approach to life [75, 76]. Therefore, we advocate for the use of targeted ACT interventions to increase the levels of PF in different subgroups according to their specific characteristics, thereby improving their psychological and behavioral health. In particular, adolescents in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup may not display overt deficits in PF at the level of action. Thus, it is essential to regularly assess and understand the implicit emotions and cognitions of this subgroup, as well as to provide interventions aimed at increasing their openness skills.

Multiple mediating effects on the association between PF profiles and NSSI behavior

Consistent with our second hypothesis, both NEs and SIRs mediated the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior. Specifically, multiple mediation analysis revealed that compared with the extremely high-PF subgroup, the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, low-PF subgroup, and medium-PF subgroup presented higher levels of NEs and poorer SIRs, which were correspondingly associated with increased engagement in NSSI behavior.

NEs were the strongest mediator in our model, followed by SIRs. PF is a central aspect of emotion and thought management [17]. Individuals with a high level of PF often experience less entanglement with thoughts and consequent avoidance or control of undesirable internal events and fewer experiences of negative emotional states [77]. In contrast, individuals with low levels of PF (i.e., PI) tend to manifest topographically as struggles with emotion regulation, avoidance behaviors, and maladaptive cognitions, often leading to cycles of negativity [78]. Given that the relationships among PF, NE, and NSSI behavior have been confirmed in several subgroups, interventions that aim to increase PF and directly address NEs may both yield significant benefits for reducing NE and ultimately mitigating NSSI. Emotion-focused interventions such as cognitive reappraisal [79] and mindfulness-based interventions [80] may help adolescents reappraise emotion-eliciting stimuli and develop adaptive awareness and acceptance of emotional states, thereby preventing NEs from escalating to the point at which an individual experiences an urge to engage in NSSI. Notably, the effectiveness of specific emotion regulation strategies depends on situational demands and individual differences [81]. Thus, interventions should be aimed at developing broad repertoires of strategies rather than emphasizing the use of a single, fixed approach.

Our findings also support the perspective of developmental contextualism and the Function Model of NSSI, highlighting the important role of interpersonal relationships within school settings in the association between PF and adolescent NSSI behavior. Adolescents who reported lower levels of PF and dimensions of openness tended to experience poor SIRs. This finding may be attributed to their difficulty in maintaining openness to others’ perspectives or their tendency to respond to interpersonal conflicts in a more reactive and aggressive manner [38]. These social vulnerabilities may elevate the risk of NSSI. Previous studies have shown that adolescents who are more satisfied with relationships at school tend to report lower levels of NSSI [30, 82, 83], suggesting that more positive SIRs may protect against adolescent NSSI behavior. These findings highlight the importance of fostering positive interpersonal relationships within school settings for adolescents with lower levels of PF. In this regard, schools should take an active role in establishing a well-structured and supportive educational environment. We recommend that schools and educators consider incorporating and integrating social-emotional learning (SEL) curricula that specifically target relationship skills, such as cooperative learning [84] and circle time training [85]. Simultaneously, strengthening teachers’ competencies in mental health education and social-emotional skills is likely to further ensure that students receive the necessary support and guidance.

With respect to the comparison of indirect effects between PF profiles, our study revealed that the mediating effects of NEs were pronounced in the low-PF subgroup and weakly open-highly engaged subgroup. Correspondingly, compared with adolescents with high levels of PF, adolescents in these subgroups presented higher levels of NEs, leading to increased NSSI behavior. These results indicate that the effects of PF on NSSI behavior in these two subgroups occur predominantly through NEs. Therefore, intervention efforts for these adolescents should prioritize addressing NEs, to not only reduce the impact of PF on NSSI behavior in a mediated way but also directly reduce the impact of NEs on NSSI behavior. Notably, among adolescents in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, high levels of engagement did not translate to lower levels of NEs. This finding suggests that insufficient openness may undermine the potential psychological benefits of behavioral engagement. Previous research in clinical samples has shown that enhancing contact with values is associated with better mental health outcomes in clinical populations [41], and depression is commonly treated through increasing value-driven behavior [86]. Building on this, the UFM model posits that value-driven behaviors are most strongly and directly (i.e., proximally) associated with individual well-being and psychological distress [73]. While value-driven behaviors provide a more proximal target, our findings suggest openness as a key flexibility process of intervention for mental health. In addition to focusing on adolescents’ outward behaviors, attention should be given to their experiential acceptance ability and ability to separate themselves from their thoughts or emotions. For adolescents with active engagement but weak openness, fostering internal openness may serve as a critical point for interventions targeting NEs.

Limitations and future directions

While this study contributes valuable insights into the heterogeneity of PF and the associated outcome variables among adolescents, it is necessary to acknowledge its limitations. The primary limitations of the present study include its cross-sectional design and reliance on a convenience sample consisting solely of middle school students, thereby likely limiting the ability to infer causal relationships between PF profiles and NSSI behavior and limiting the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Further research to explore longitudinal evidence in a national sample is essential to replicate and extend the current findings. For example, latent transition analysis and causal mediation analysis can be employed to examine the developmental patterns of PF and changes in the relationships between PF subgroups and NSSI behavior over time.

Another limitation of this study is that the results of the LPA revealed one subgroup comprising less than 5% of the total sample, which falls below the percentage of overall sample size recommended by the rule of thumb. Such solutions may yield the possibility of lower power and lower precision relative to larger profiles [87], and the generalizability of the findings may therefore be limited. This may be attributable to the relative insufficiency of heterogeneous samples and could be prevented by a prior planned larger sampling. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are warranted to confirm the robustness of these findings and to further validate the observed subgroup characteristics.

Furthermore, while the study focused on the multiple effects of a set of emotional and interpersonal variables, there may be other relevant traits that were not considered. For instance, recent theoretical research has identified the proximal and distal neurobiological traits associated with NSSI, including epigenetics, biological manifestations of childhood maltreatment, brain circuitry, peripheral stress response systems, and the pain system [88]. Interestingly, genetic variations (e.g., in the 5HTT gene) have also been linked to changes in PF levels during treatment [89], central activations are associated with cognitive flexibility, and heart rate variability (HRV) is associated with affective flexibility [90, 91]. Future studies could expand the scope of the factors examined to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between PF and NSSI behavior among adolescents.

Conclusions

This study investigated the heterogeneity of PF among Chinese adolescents and elucidated the mechanism underlying the relationship between PF heterogeneity and NSSI behavior. First, five distinct PF profiles were identified and labeled as the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, low-PF subgroup, medium-PF subgroup, high-PF subgroup, and extremely high-PF subgroup. Second, the present study provided empirical evidence to support multiple mediating effects of NEs and SIRs on the associations between PF profiles and NSSI behavior. Compared with the extremely high-PF subgroup, the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup, low-PF subgroup, and medium-PF subgroup presented greater levels of NEs, poorer SIRs, and more NSSI behavior. In particular, adolescents in the weakly open-highly engaged subgroup reported the highest levels of NSSI behavior among the five profiles. The findings of this study indicate that educational and clinical practitioners should prioritize the assessment of PF heterogeneity and implement targeted interventions for adolescents, rather than treating PF as a unidimensional construct (i.e., using the total PF score). Moreover, beyond value-driven behaviors, openness may serve as an equally critical process of flexibility for mental health intervention. Additionally, emphasis should be placed on emotion-focused interventions and the cultivation of positive interpersonal relationships within school settings.
