A nomogram combined substantia nigra hyperechogenicity with third ventricular width assessed by transcranial sonography in prediction Parkinson’s disease-related cognitive impairment
Authors: Qi-hui Yang, Chao Hou, Ming-xing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop a nomogram that incorporating clinical data and transcranial sonography (TCS) markers for predicting Parkinson’s disease patients with cognitive impairment (PD-CI).Methods149 PD with normal cognition (PD-NCI), 117 PD with mild CI (PD-MCI), and 79 PD with dementia (PDD) were included as the training set, 145 PD patients and 154 age- and gender-matched volunteers were enrolled as the test set and control group, respectively. Logistic regression was utilized to screen risk factors for predicting PD-CI, and a nomogram was generated.ResultsA predictive model was developed using age, education level, homocysteine, substantia nigra hyperechogenicity (SNH), and third ventricle (V3) width. Receiver operating characteristic curves indicated that V3 width, homocysteine, and the model effectively distinguished between PD-NCI and PD-CI, PDD and non-PDD, as well as PDD and PD-MCI, with the predictive model yielding the highest area under the curve. Calibration curves showed that the predictions of the final model in both the training and test sets closely matched the actual probabilities, while clinical decision curves suggested that the nomogram provided a substantial clinical net benefit.ConclusionA predictive model for PD-CI that incorporates age, education level, plasma homocysteine levels, SNH, and V3 width has been developed and validated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02407-y.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Transcranial sonography, Substantia nigra hyperechogenicity, The third ventricle, Cognitive impairment

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder [1]. Although primarily recognized as a movement disorder, nearly all patients with PD experience non-motor symptoms, with cognitive impairment (CI) being one of the most common manifestations [2]. PD-related CI (PD-CI) is characterized by a high incidence, poor treatment response, and significant rates of disability, severely affecting patients’ functional abilities and quality of life while increasing the burden on caregivers [3]. This situation presents major challenges for patients, families, and public health systems. Therefore, developing effective methods to assess and predict cognitive function levels in PD patients is a critical scientific issue that necessitates urgent clinical solutions. Neuroimaging and biofluid biomarkers play essential roles in evaluating cognitive status in PD. Transcranial sonography (TCS) examination is convenient and cost-effectiveness, both the substantia nigra hyperechogenicity (SNH) and third ventricle (V3) width identified by TCS are important imaging biomarkers in terms of neurodegenerative diseases. SNH serves as a neuroimaging marker for both the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of PD [4]. However, conflicting results exist regarding the intrinsic relationship between SNH and PD-CI [5, 6]. The width of V3 indirectly reflects the degree of brain atrophy, thereby helping to distinguish physiological aging from dementia [7]. A cross-sectional study further confirmed the diagnostic efficacy of V3 width for predicting PD dementia [8]. Additionally, levodopa treatment, along with deficiencies in vitamin B12 (VitB12) or folic acid, can result in elevated homocysteine (Hcy) levels, including oxidative stress and increasing the risk of brain atrophy in PD [9]. Cognitive decline in both community residents and PD patients is associated with hyper-Hcy and widening of V3 [10]. Research indicates that models combining neuroimaging and plasma protein for predicting PD-CI demonstrate good diagnostic performance [11]. Our preliminary study demonstrated a efficacy of the model that integrates SNH with clinical parameters in predicting PD-CI, and subsequently confirmed the correlation between V3 width and the transition of cognitive function in PD [12]. Nevertheless, whether the combination of SNH and V3 width exerts a synergistic effect in predicting PD-CI remains to be investigated further. Based on this, the present study aims to explore the correlation between SNH, V3 width, circulating Hcy levels, and cognitive function in PD, as well as construct a predictive model for PD-CI based on TCS parameters and clinical data.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and study design

A total of 345 PD patients (mean age 64.10 ± 9.75 years, 59.42% male) hospitalized in the Department of Movement Disorders at Beijing Tiantan Hospital from January 2019 to October 2022, who underwent TCS examination, were retrospectively included as the training set. Additionally, 145 PD patient (mean age 63.40 ± 9.53 years, 57.24% male) hospitalized at the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University form November 2019 to October 2022 were included as the test set. Furthermore, 154 age- and gender-matched volunteers (mean age 62.78 ± 8.94% years, 67.32% male) without neurological diseases and with normal cognition, who were hospitalized during the same period were included as the healthy control (HC) group. The ratio of the training set to the test set is approximately 7:3. According to the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) criteria [13], patients in the training set were categorized into three group: PD with normal cognitive group (PD-NCI, MoCA score ≥ 26), PD with mild CI group (PD-MCI, 20 ≤ MoCA < 26), and PD with dementia (PDD, MoCA score < 20).

Inclusion criteria for PD patients were defined as individuals aged ≥ 18 years who had completed TCS examination and had comprehensive medical history data. The diagnosis of PD was established based on the consensus statement issued by the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) in 2015 [14]. The exclusion criteria included PD secondary to cerebrovascular diseases, intracranial tumors, trauma, or drug therapy; hydrocephalus; CI prior to PD diagnosis or PD in conjunction with Alzheimer’s disease; ambiguous grading of SNH and V3 width due to inadequate temporal window acoustic penetration; deep brain stimulation history; pallidotomy; or patients who had undergone stem cell or gene therapy. For patients who were hospitalized multiple times during the inclusion period, only data from their first hospitalization were considered. The patient inclusion process is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Clinical data collection

Demographic and clinical data were obtained through a review of medical record. All PD patients underwent systematic assessment of motor and cognitive functions prior to imaging and laboratory examinations. Motor function assessments were conducted during the “off” state, while cognitive assessments were performed during the “on” state. A neurologist with over five years of experience assessed motor function using the MDS Unified PD Rating Scale Part III (MDS UPDRS-III). The severity of motor symptoms and disease staging were evaluated using the modified Hoehn-Yahr (H-Y) staging scale, while cognitive function was assessed using the MoCA scale.

TCS examination

The instruments and parameter setting utilized for the TCS examination were consistent with those employed in our previous studies [15]. The echogenicity of the substantia nigra (SN) was assessed, and the V3 width was measured (Fig. 1). SNH + was defined as the presence of patchy hyperechogenicity in the SN that was equal to or greater than the echogenicity of the surrounding basal cistern (Fig. 2) [16]. The built-in tracing method of the instrument was utilized to delineate the SNH area. The maximum plane of SNH was selected for area tracing and the total bilateral SNH areas were recorded as the SNH area. All measurements were independently conducted by two physicians with over five years of experience in neurosonography, who were blinded to the clinical data. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized to assess the consistency of measurements among different observers, with ICC value ≥ 0.75 indicates good interobserver consistency [17].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corporation, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). To ensure an adequate sample size, individual missing values were replaced with the series mean. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while skewed distribution measurement data were presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical data were reported as frequency (percentage). Intergroup differences were compared using independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance, chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and non-parametric tests. Post-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni or Dunn corrections. Spearman correlation analysis and partial correlation analysis were employed to examine correlations between variables. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were utilized to identify potential risk factors affecting CI. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate logistic regression were included in multivariate logistic regression to construct a predictive model. This predictive model was subsequently converted into a visual nomogram using RStudio (version 4.3.3). The receiver operating characteristics curve (ROC) was plotted to evaluate the predictive performance of the model for varying degrees of CI, using the area under the curve (AUC). Calibration curves and clinical decision curves were employed to assess the model’s calibration capability and clinical utility, respectively, with validation conducted through the test set. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In comparison to the HC group, both the training and testing sets demonstrating lower levels of body mass index (BMI), MoCA scores, and VitB12 levels. Conversely, these sets exhibited higher plasma Hcy levels, a lower prevalence of smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia history, and a higher prevalence of sleep disorders, dysphagia, constipation, anxiety, and depression history (P < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in all clinical parameters between the PD training set and test set (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

TCS results

The ICC values for V3 width, midbrain area, and SNH area were 0.87 (range 0.82 ~ 0.90), 0.85 (range 0.81 ~ 0.86), and 0.92 (range 0.88 ~ 0.95), respectively, indicating good inter-observer agreement. Compared to HC, both the training set and test set exhibited a wider V3 width, a higher proportion of SNH+, and a larger SNH area (P < 0.001). In the training set, the V3 width was greater in the PD-MCI and PDD groups compared to the PD-NCI group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the proportion of SNH + and SNH area were larger in the PDD group than in the PD-NCI group (P < 0.05), and the V3 width was greater in the PDD group than in the PD-MCI group (P < 0.001) (Tables 1 and 2).

Correlation analysis

Spearman correlation analysis indicated that in the training set, age was positively correlated with disease duration, H-Y stage, UPDRS-III score, Hcy level, and V3 width. Similarly, disease duration exhibited positive correlations with H-Y stage, UPDRS-III score, Hcy level, and SNH area. Notably, both age and disease duration were negatively correlated with MoCA. Consequently, age and disease duration were incorporated as confounding factors in the partial correlation analysis. The results demonstrated that H-Y stage (r = -0.24, P < 0.01), UPDRS-III score (r = -0.31, P < 0.01), Hcy level (r = -0.25, P < 0.01), and V3 width (r = -0.46, P < 0.01) all exhibited mild negative correlations with MoCA scores (Table 3).

Risk factor stratification

Based on the MoCA scores, the dataset was divided into two groups for further screening of factors affecting cognitive functions: PD-NCI group and PD-CI group. The within-group comparisons of clinical data and TCS results between the training set and test set are presented in Table 4. Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses indicated that age (odds ratio [OR] = 1.05, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.00 ~ 1.08, P < 0.001), education level (OR = 0.89, 95%CI = 0.84 ~ 0.94, P < 0.001), Hcy concentration (OR = 1.07, 95%CI = 1.01 ~ 1.14, P = 0.018), V3 width (OR = 2.16, 95%CI = 1.63 ~ 2.86, P < 0.001), and SNH+ (OR = 0.40, 95%CI = 0.22 ~ 0.74, P < 0.004) were identified as independent risk factors influencing cognitive function in PD.

Construction and assessment of prediction model

The prediction model was constructed using five risk factors identified through logistic regression. Given that age and education level are demographic variables, ROC curves were generated for the prediction model, V3 width, SNH+, and plasma Hcy to assess their ability to predict varying degrees of PD-CI. The results indicated that the prediction model, V3 width, and plasma Hcy could effectively distinguish between PD-NCI and PD-CI, as well as between PDD and non-PDD, and between PDD and PD-MCI. Notably, the prediction model demonstrated the highest AUC (Fig. 3; Table 5). Based on the maximum Youden index, the cutoff values for V3 width in diagnosing PD-CI and PDD were determined to be 5.75 mm and 6,55 mm, respectively, while the cutoff values for plasma Hcy in diagnosing PD-CI and PDD were found to be 17.33 µmol/L and 17.63 µmol/L, respectively.

Using R language, the prediction models for both the training and test sets were visualized as nomograms (Fig. 4). In the training set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.855 for predicting PD-CI (95%CI: 0.816 ~ 0.895, P < 0.001), with sensitivity and specificity values of 82.1% and 85.5%, respectively. In the test set, the model’s AUC for predicting PD-CI was 0.900 (95%CI: 0.847 ~ 0.952, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 2), with sensitivity and specificity values of 90.9% and 80.7%, respectively. The calibration of the nomogram was conducted using the 1000 bootstrap resampling method. The calibration curves indicated that the predicted probabilities of the model closely aligned with the actual probabilities in both the training and test sets, with mean deviations of 0.038 and 0.022, respectively, demonstrating good model consistency (Fig. 5). The clinical decision curve revealed that the model provides significant clinical net benefit when the threshold probabilities ranged from 6% to 98% (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The qualitative and quantitative assessment of SNH identified by TCS as an auxiliary indicator for clinical diagnosis of PD has been incorporated into the guideline [18]. Recent studies have drawn attention to the intrinsic relationship between SNH and CI: when employing a cutoff value of 22 mm2 for SNH area, PD patients with SNH+ demonstrated lower cognitive abilities compared to those without, particularly affecting tasks related to repetition and command execution [19]. Our previous longitudinal study indicated that as cognitive abilities decline in PD, the proportion of SNH+ cases increases [20]. This study identified SNH + as an independent influencing factor for PD-CI, with no significant correlation observed between SNH area and MoCA scores, corroborating findings from previous literature [6]. The formation mechanism of SNH is complex, current research suggests that iron deposition, microglial activation, and neuromelanin loss constitute the pathological basis for SNH [21], which leads to dopaminergic neuron damage and triggers motor symptoms. Although the mechanism by which SNH contributes to cognitive decline in PD remains unclear, the SN contains subregions that regulate cognitive function. Its anteromedial portion receives input form the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex via the striatum, thereby modulating cognitive decline [22]. In healthy individuals, cortical dopamine influences memory, visuospatial skills, attention, and other cognitive domains, thereby enhancing cognitive performance [23, 24], indicating the critical role of dopamine in cognitive function. In PD, dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway exhibit varying degrees of loss. Compared to cognitively normal PD patients, those with MCI demonstrate more pronounced dopaminergic denervation in the dorsal caudate nucleus. As cognitive function progressively declines, dopaminergic neuron degeneration gradually extends to the limbic system and neocortical regions, resulting in severe damage to the lateral dopaminergic system in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes in PDD [25]. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that the cognitive decline in PD patients with SNH may be associated with degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the SN par compacta, leading to impaired pathways for receiving afferent impulses and projecting feedback impulses. However, this hypothesis warrants further investigation in future studies.

Patients with PD-CI exhibit varying degrees of cerebral volume reduction, sulcal widening, and hippocampal atrophy, with the width of the V3 serving as an objective indicator of brain atrophy. TCS has proven to be a cost-effective and reliable method for identifying V3, achieving a detection rate as high as 96% even in patients with suboptimal temporal bone windows [8]. As physiological aging is associating with brain atrophy, the age- and disease duration-adjusted partial correlation analysis conducted in this study revealed a negative correlation between V3 width and MoCA scores. The cutoff values for V3 width as independent risk factors for diagnosing PD-CI and PDD were determined to be 5.75 mm and 6.55 mm, respectively, both exhibiting a sensitivity of 71.8% and specificities of 86.7% and 85.1%. Previous studies conducted on the German population identified TCS-derived V3 width cutoff values for diagnosing PDD at 6.0 mm for patients under 70 years old and 7.5 mm for those over 70 years old [8]. In a study based on a population in central China, the sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing PD-CI were found to be 62.7% and 75.6%, respectively, when the V3 width was 4.75 mm [26]. The variations in these thresholds can be partially attributed to differences in ethnic backgrounds and age distributions of the study populations. In this study, the mean age of the training set was 64.10 ± 9.75 years, which is slightly higher than that reported by Gao (median age 60, range 51 ~ 66) [26] but lower than that reported by Behnke (median age 69, range 62 ~ 74) [8]. Although the pathological morphological correlates of PD-CI are diverse, regardless of the underlying pathological basis of CI, the enlargement of V3 should be considered an independent risk factor for cognitive deterioration in PD [8].

Cerebrospinal fluid and plasma levels of Aβ and neurofilament light chain serve as biological markers for cognitive function in neurodegenerative diseases [27]. However, some patients experience difficulty tolerating lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid collection, and the plasma concentrations of these two markers are not routinely measured. In contrast, recent studies have shown that elevated plasma Hcy levels are associated with CI in various central nervous system disorders and neurodegenerative diseases. Hcy may impact cognition by promoting oxidative stress, increasing the release of inflammatory factors, depleting energy reserves, and mediating neuronal apoptosis through DNA damage, which can lead to brain atrophy [28]. The findings of this study indicate that plasma Hcy is an independent risk factor influencing PD-CI, with the width of V3 positively correlated with Hcy levels. V3 is associated with age and the degree of brain atrophy. Once brain atrophy occurs, it is difficult to reverse, and an enlarged V3 responds poorly to medication. However, drug therapy for PD, particularly oral levodopa, can result in elevated Hcy concentrations, while deficiencies in VitB12 and folic acid also influence Hcy levels [29]. Therefore, the impact of Hcy on cognitive function in PD should be considered within the contest of comprehensive factors.

Recent study has demonstrated that predictive models integrating clinical features and imaging data possess significant potential for the diagnosis and prognosis of diseases across various systems, with some models already implemented in clinical practice [30]. Chen et al. developed a model that incorporates the mean signal and asymmetry index of the putamen in DAT imaging, cerebrospinal fluid p-tau/α-Syn ratio, p-tau levels, and rs55785911 genotype to predict PD-MCI with REM sleep behavior disorder [31]. However, the parameters selected in this model are not routinely obtainable through standard clinical examinations, which limits its widespread clinical application. In this study, a predictive model was constructed using five independent risk indicators identified through logistic regression: age, education level, plasma Hcy, V3 width, and SNH+. Compared to individual indicators such as Hcy, V3 width, or SNH+, this predictive model exhibited superior performance in assessing varying degrees of cognitive function in PD patients. The model was transformed into a visual nomogram to effectively illustrate the weight of each risk factor, and its performance was validated through calibration curves and clinical decision curve analysis. Unlike the model developed by Chen et al., these indicators are more readily accessible in clinical settings, facilitating external validation and clinical application. Age and educational level are critical components of this model, as the degree of brain atrophy tends to worsen with advancing age, leading to cognitive decline, which is consistent with the physiological processes of human neurodegeneration. Additionally, higher educational attainment correlates with improved cognitive performance, and studies on elderly populations have confirmed the positive influence of education in preserving cognitive function under adverse conditions [32]. Furthermore, in this study, correlation analysis showed that both H-Y stage and UPDRS-III scores were negative correlated with MoCA scores, and univariate logistic regression identified both factors, along with certain affective disorders (anxiety, depression, and constipation), as predictors of CI. However, the results from multivariate logistic regression indicated that neither variable acted as independent predictor of cognitive function. On one hand, the H-Y stage and UPDRS-III scores in PD are intrinsically associated with various factors such as age and disease duration. On the other hand, these factors may interact with one another [33], which could explain why these measures do not serve as independent risk factors for predicting cognitive levels.

In this model, SNH+ yields an OR of 0.40 (95% CI 0.22–0.74), indicating a protective factor against PD-CI. This finding contradicts previous results, as the proportion of SNH+ progressively increased from PD-NCI to PD-MCI and then to PDD. This discrepancy may be attributed to potential confounding factors present in the multivariate analysis. Additionally, SNH + may exhibit collinearity with V3 width or age [34]. Notably, the AUC value of this model on the test set surpassed its performance on the training set. This difference could be partially explained by variations in patient populations, changes in the disease severity distribution, or certain random factors.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective cross-sectional study, the risk of bias cannot be entirely eliminated, particularly during data analysis. To ensure a sufficient sample size, missing individual data points were imputed using the series mean method. Secondly, since levodopa can influence plasma Hcy levels, the absence of levodopa dosage as a variable in the experimental design somewhat limits the capacity of Hcy to serve as an independent risk factor for predicting cognitive function. Thirdly, the study did not perform follow-up tracking measurements, which precludes a survival risk analysis. Future cohort studies targeting this patient population are necessary to evaluate the changes in risk factors throughout the progression of PD-CI. Finally, this study evaluated the cognitive function of PD patients exclusively using MoCA scores, neglecting the two-tiered assessment recommended by the MDS for diagnosing PD-MCI or PDD. And we did not further analyze the correlations between the seven MoCA sub-items and these risk factors, thereby limiting a comprehensive understanding of how specific factors influence particular cognitive domains.

In conclusion, the SNH and V3 width identified through transcranial ultrasound serve as ultrasonographic markers for PD-CI. The integration of clinical and ultrasonographic markers can significantly enhance the predictive performance for PD-CI. A predictive model was developed incorporating age, education level, plasma Hcy concentration, SNH, and V3 width, and its efficacy in predicting MoCA-derived cognitive category in PD was subsequently validated. In clinical practice, when treating PD patients who exhibit one or more of the following high-risk factors—advanced age, prolonged disease duration, SNH+, an enlarged V3 width, or elevated plasma Hcy levels—clinicians must remain vigilant for the potential onset of cognitive dysfunction.
