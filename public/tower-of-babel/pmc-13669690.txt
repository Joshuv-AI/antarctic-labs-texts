Artesunate ameliorates experimental colitis by suppressing intestinal inflammation and modulating the gut microbiota
Authors: Haiyang Sun, Rujiao Jiang, Jingjing Jing, Xinying Wang, Ning Wang, Meihua Guo, Ce Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtesunate (ART) is a semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin with well-established antimalarial activity. In this study, we demonstrate that ART treatment markedly attenuates disease severity in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)–induced colitis. ART significantly reduces the accumulation of inflammatory neutrophils, monocytes, Th17 cells, and IL-17-producing innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and γδT cells in the colon, accompanied by suppression of pro-inflammatory gene expression, including Tnf, Il6, and Il1b, and upregulation of anti-inflammatory mediators such as Tgfb and Il10. In parallel, ART restores the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and claudin-1, indicating improved epithelial barrier integrity. ART treatment inhibits the IL-17 production from both Th17 cells and other lymphocytes. ART also reshapes the gut microbiota, which contributes to protection against DSS-induced colitis. Notably, mesalazine co-treatment with ART augmented its protective efficacy against DSS-induced colitis. Collectively, these findings identify ART as a potent modulator of intestinal inflammation and barrier function, highlighting its therapeutic potential for mucosal inflammatory diseases.Keywords: artesunate, colitis, commensal microbiota, IL-17, mouse

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract characterized by continuous and diffuse inflammation of the colonic mucosa. Persistent intestinal inflammation leads to progressive epithelial damage, and patients with advanced or long-standing UC are at increased risk of serious complications, including intestinal perforation and colorectal cancer [1]. Despite substantial advances in disease management, current therapeutic strategies for UC primarily rely on non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressive agents, and biologics. However, the long-term use of these therapies is frequently associated with significant limitations, including steroid dependence or resistance, diminished therapeutic responsiveness, increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections, and substantial economic burden [2, 3]. These challenges underscore the urgent need to identify safer, more effective, and sustainable alternative or complementary therapeutic approaches for UC prevention and treatment.

Artesunate (ART) is a semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin isolated from Artemisia annua L. (Asteraceae) and is widely recognized as a highly effective first-line antimalarial drug in clinical practice [4]. The antimalarial efficacy of ART is largely attributed to its ability to generate reactive free radicals that disrupt pathogen protein synthesis and compromise cell membrane integrity, ultimately leading to parasite clearance [5]. Beyond its established antimalarial activity, accumulating evidence indicates that ART exerts a broad spectrum of pharmacological effects, including anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, and anti-fibrotic activities. ART has been shown to inhibit bladder cancer progression by regulating cell cycle progression, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and migration [6]. Similarly, ART inhibits the growth of oral tongue squamous cell carcinoma by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction–associated oxidative damage [7]. In inflammatory disease contexts, ART alleviates acute kidney injury by suppressing macrophage-mediated necroptosis and inflammatory responses in renal tubular epithelial cells [8]. In liver disease, ART attenuates hepatic fibrosis by inhibiting hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, inducing autophagy and apoptosis in HSCs, and reducing collagen production [9,10,11].

Importantly, ART has demonstrated protective effects in colorectal cancer models by inhibiting tumor cell growth, dampening oxidative stress, and promoting cancer cell apoptosis [12]. Notably, in a 1,2-dimethylhydrazine–induced colon cancer rat model, ART significantly reduced colon cancer risk through suppression of oxidative stress pathways and inflammation [13]. ART has also been reported to perform its protective effects against DSS–induced colitis in mice through maintaining the expression intestinal mucosal barrier-related proteins mucin-2 (Muc2) and claudin-1, and suppressing the apoptosis and NF-κB–mediated inflammatory response [14]. ART treatment can also reduce IL-17-producing CD4+ T (Th17) cells, restore the M2/M1 macrophage ratio and inhibits endoplasmic reticulum stress [15, 16]. In addition, a recent study also shows that ART suppresses Th17 cells and maintains regulatory T cell population, and promotes intestinal epithelial barrier by facilitating ketogenesis in DSS–colitis model [17]. However, since IL-17 is produced not only by CD4+ T cells, but also by other lymphocytes such as CD8+ T cells, γδ T cell and type 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) in the intestines [18], whether ART influences other IL-17-producing cells in the process of colitis development is still obscure.

Gut microbiota and the intestinal barrier maintain intestinal homeostasis, while the dysbiosis of pathogenic bacteria in the intestine may cause or accelerate the development of intestinal inflammation [19]. Metabolites derived from the probiotics promote mucus secretion and regulate immune cells to maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier [20]. Although ART has been reported to affect the composition of microbiota in rats with colitis [15], whether ART improves DSS–induced colitis through modifying gut microbiota also needs investigation.

In this study, we demonstrate that ART treatment significantly ameliorates DSS–induced colitis in mice, as evidenced by increased colon length and reduced disease activity and histopathological scores. ART markedly decreases the accumulation of inflammatory neutrophils, monocytes, and IL-17–producing not only CD4+ T but also other immune cells in the colon, accompanied by downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulation of anti-inflammatory mediators. ART treatment also restores the expression of intestinal tight junction proteins, indicating improved epithelial barrier integrity. In addition, ART inhibits the differentiation of IL-17–producing leukocytes in vitro. Furthermore, ART reshapes some specific species of gut microbiota to suppress colitis, and augments the efficacy of the conventional therapeutic approach. These findings identify ART as a promising therapeutic agent for the prevention and treatment of colitis.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks were used for all in vivo experiments. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a γ-ray-sterilized diet, acidified tap water (0.002 N HCl, pH 2.5), and autoclaved wooden chip bedding in environmentally controlled clean rooms at the Laboratory Animal Center of Dalian Medical University. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with institutional ethical regulations and guidelines and were approved by the Experimental Animal Management and Use Committee of Dalian Medical University (approval number: XL250815301).

DSS-induced colitis

Acute colitis was induced by administering 3% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS; Cat#9011-18-1, MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA, USA) in drinking water for 7 consecutive days. Body weight, stool consistency, and fecal bleeding were monitored daily. Disease severity was evaluated using a Disease Activity Index (DAI) based on the following criteria: diarrhea score, 0 (normal), 2 (loose stools), 4 (watery diarrhea); blood in stool score, 0 (normal), 2 (slight bleeding), 4 (gross bleeding); and weight loss score, 0 (none), 1 (1–5%), 2 (5–10%), 3 (10–15%), 4 (>15%). The DAI was calculated as the sum of the stool consistency, bleeding, and weight loss scores. Mice were sacrificed on day 7 after DSS administration for subsequent analyses.

ART treatment

For in vivo experiments, mice were intraperitoneally injected with artesunate (ART; 50 mg/kg; Cat#88495-63-0, Macklin, Shanghai, China) once daily either during or prior to DSS administration for 7 days. For in vitro experiments, ART was added at final concentrations of 0, 0.2, 2, or 10 µM during IL-17–producing cell differentiation.

Administration of 5-ASA and Lactobacillus johnsonii

Mice were orally administrated with 5-ASA (50 mg/kg; Cat# B1969-10000, APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) once daily either during or prior to DSS administration for 7 days. Lactobacillus (L.) johnsonii was originally isolated from the feces of mice, and mice were orally administrated with L. johnsonii (1 × 108/mouse per time) before DSS administration on day −6, −3, and 0.

Vancomycin treatment

Mice were treated with vancomycin (Cat#1404-93-9, Macklin) in water (45 mg/l) for 7 days, and were then intraperitoneally injected with ART (50 mg/kg) or saline daily during DSS administration for 7 days.

Histological analysis

Colon tissues were excised, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histological scoring was performed in a blinded manner using the following criteria: epithelial damage (E), 0 = normal morphology; 1 = loss of goblet cells; 2 = loss of goblet cells in large areas; 3 = loss of crypts; 4 = loss of crypts in large areas; inflammatory infiltration (I), 0 = no infiltrate; 1 = infiltrate around the crypt base; 2 = infiltrate reaching the lamina muscularis mucosae; 3 = extensive infiltration reaching the lamina muscularis mucosae with mucosal thickening and edema; 4 = infiltration extending into the submucosa. The total histological score was calculated as E + I.

Preparation of colonic lamina propria cells

Following euthanasia, colons were excised, washed with PBS, and cut into approximately 2-mm pieces. Tissue fragments were incubated in 10 ml Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 3 mM EDTA at 37°C for 30 min with agitation to remove epithelial cells. After discarding the EDTA supernatant, tissues were washed twice with HBSS and digested in 5 ml RPMI 1640 medium (Cat#61870036, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 400 U/ml collagenase (Cat#C5138-1, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 5 U/ml DNase inhibitor (Cat#D8070, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37°C for 120 min. Samples were vortexed for 15 s and filtered through sterile gauze to obtain single-cell suspensions.

Flow cytometry

Cells were washed twice with FACS buffer (HBSS containing 2% FCS) and incubated with anti-Fc receptor antibody (2.4G2, Cat#70-0161-U500, Tonbo Biosciences, San Diego, CA, USA) to block non-specific binding. Surface staining was performed using fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA): 7-AAD (Cat#420403), anti-mouse I-A/I-E (M5/114.15.2, Cat#107614/107605), CD11b (M1/70, Cat#101216/101205), CD45 (30-F11, Cat#103116), Ly6C (HK1.4, Cat#128007), Ly6G (1A8, Cat#127606), CD11c (N418, Cat#117322/117305), CD3ε (145-2C11, Cat#100305), CD4 (GK1.5, Cat#100411/100431), CD90.2 (53-2.1, Cat#140311), TCRγδ (GL3, Cat#118123), CD8α (53-6.7, Cat#100707).

For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; 50 ng/ml; Cat#P1585, Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA), ionomycin (500 ng/ml; Cat#56092–82-1, Merck Millipore), and monensin (1 mM; Cat#475895, Merck Millipore) for 4 h at 37°C, or left unstimulated. Cells were surface-stained for 30 min at 4°C, fixed and permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm solution (Cat#554722, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and stained intracellularly with anti-mouse IL-17A (TC11-18H10.1, Cat#506918) or IFN-γ (XMG1.2, Cat#505806) antibodies. All antibodies were used at 1:200 or 1:250 dilutions. Data were acquired on a Cytek® Aurora/Northern Lights™ flow cytometer (Cytek Biosciences, Fremont, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.1 software (BD Biosciences).

Real-time RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the Mammalian Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Cat#RTN350-1KT, Sigma-Aldrich). RNA (200 ng) was reverse-transcribed using Evo M-MLV RT Master Mix (Cat#AG11706, Agbio, Changsha, China). Quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Cat#AG11706, Agbio) on a QuantStudioTM 5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct method and normalized to Gapdh. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

In vitro Th17 cell differentiation

Cells were isolated from spleens and lymph nodes of wild-type mice and incubated with biotin-conjugated antibodies against CD11b (M1/70, Cat#101203), CD11c (N418, Cat#117303), I-A/I-E (M5/114.15.2, Cat#107603), CD19 (6D5, Cat#115504), and NK1.1 (PK136, Cat#108703) for 20 min at 4°C. Cells were incubated with anti-biotin microbeads (Cat#130-090-485, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) and negatively selected using an autoMACS Pro Separator. Naïve T cells were further purified by positive selection using anti-CD62L microbeads (MEL-14, Cat#130-049-701, Miltenyi Biotec). Negatively selected cells using an autoMACS Pro Separator or naïve T cells were stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (8 × 104 beads/well; Cat#11456D, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in the presence of recombinant IL-6 (20 ng/ml; Cat#216-16, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), TGF-β (5 ng/ml), IL-1β (10 ng/ml; Cat#211-11B, PeproTech), IL-23 (10 ng/ml; Cat#200-23, PeproTech), anti-IFN-γ antibody (XMG1.2, 5 µg/ml; Cat#505847, BioLegend), and anti-IL-4 antibody (5 µg/ml) for 6 days.

Immunofluorescence staining and analysis

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using citric acid-based buffer (Cat#G1201, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). Sections were blocked with 5% BSA (Cat#232100, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBST for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against TNF-α (Cat#AF7014), ZO-1 (Cat#AF5145), or claudin-1 (Cat#DF6919) (Affinity, Changzhou, China). After washing, sections were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and counterstained with DAPI (Cat#S2110, Solarbio, China). Images were acquired using a Leica DM6B fluorescence microscope. The mean of fluorescence intensity was calculated by ImageJ software, and presented as relative expression vs control.

16S rDNA sequencing analysis

Bacterial genomic DNA (>500 ng) was used for amplification of the V3–V4 region of the 16S rDNA gene using primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAA T-3′). PCR products were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using AMPure XT beads, and quantified using a Qubit fluorometer. Libraries were assessed using a 2100 Bioanalyzer and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp). Data processing included read merging (FLASH), quality filtering (Fqtrim), chimera removal (Vsearch v2.3.4), OTU clustering (97% similarity), and taxonomic assignment using the SILVA database. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were performed using QIIME 1.8.0.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Multiple-group comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Body weight and DAI curves were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with repeated measures or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction, as appropriate. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ART treatment attenuates DSS-induced colitis and suppresses proinflammatory immune responses

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of ART in experimental colitis, mice were administered 3%DSS to induce acute colitis and simultaneously treated with intraperitoneal ART (50 mg/kg) once daily (Fig. 1A). Compared with DSS-treated controls, ART-treated mice exhibited significantly attenuated clinical symptoms, including reduced body weight loss, decreased incidence of bloody stools, and lower disease activity index scores (Figs. 1B–D). Consistent with these clinical improvements, ART treatment markedly alleviated DSS-induced colon shortening (Figs. 1E and F) and reduced histopathological damage, as evidenced by lower histological scores reflecting diminished mucosal inflammation, epithelial injury, and immune cell infiltration (Figs. 1G and H). Immunofluorescence staining further showed elevated expression of the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α in the colonic tissue of DSS-treated mice, which was markedly reduced following ART treatment (Fig. 1I). At the transcriptional level, DSS administration up-regulated the mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokine genes such as Tnf, Il6, and Il1b while anti-inflammatory mediators such as Tgfb and Il10 were down-regulated, and ART treatment substantially reversed these DSS-induced alterations in cytokines (Fig. 1J). Together, these data indicate that ART effectively ameliorates DSS-induced mouse colitis.

We next examined whether ART modulates inflammatory immune cell infiltration in the colon. Flow cytometric analysis revealed that DSS treatment significantly increased the frequencies and numbers of Ly6C+CD11b+ monocytes and Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils (Figs. 2A and B), IL-17+CD4+ Th17 cells and IFN-γ+CD4+ Th1 cells (Figs. 2C and D), and IL-17+CD90.2+ ILCs and IL-17+ γδT cells (Figs. 2E and F) compared with untreated controls. Notably, ART treatment robustly suppressed the expansion of these proinflammatory cell populations in DSS-treated mice (Figs. 2A–F). Together, these findings suggest that ART mitigates acute colitis by suppressing mucosal inflammation immune responses.

ART treatment restores colonic epithelial tight junction integrity

Given the critical role of epithelial barrier integrity in colitis pathogenesis, we assessed the expression of tight junction proteins in colonic tissue following ART treatment. Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that DSS administration severely disrupted the expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 (Figs. 3A and B) and claudin-1 (Figs. 3C and D) compared with control mice. In contrast, ART-treated mice exhibited a marked restoration of ZO-1 and claudin-1 expression in the colonic epithelium (Figs. 3A–D). These results suggest that ART protects against DSS-induced colitis partially by preserving epithelial barrier integrity.

ART selectively suppresses homeostatic intestinal IL-17–producing cells

To determine whether ART affects the intestinal immune landscape under physiological conditions, mice were treated with intraperitoneal ART (50 mg/kg) daily for 7 days without DSS (Fig. 4A). ART administration did not alter colon length (Figs. 4B and C) or induce detectable histopathological abnormalities (Fig. 4D). Moreover, the frequencies of colonic Ly6C+CD11b+ monocytes and Ly6G+CD11b+ neutrophils remained unchanged following ART treatment (Fig. 4E). In contrast, ART treatment significantly reduced the proportion of Th17 cells (Fig. 4F), IL-17+ ILCs and IL-17+ γδT cells (Fig. 4G), while Th1 cells were unaffected (Fig. 4F), indicating a preferential inhibitory effect of ART on IL-17 production.

To directly test whether ART interferes with IL-17–producing cell differentiation, naïve CD4+ T cells isolated from mouse spleen were cultured under Th17-polarizing conditions in the presence or absence of ART. After 6 days of polarization, ART treatment significantly inhibited the differentiation of IL-17+CD4+ T cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, when sorted naïve whole T cells were treated with ART, not only IL-17+CD4+ but IL-17-producing other lymphocytes were also drastically suppressed (Fig. 5B). These IL-17-producing non-CD4+ leukocytes served as the major source of IL-17 (Fig. 5B). To identify ART-suppressed IL-17-producing cell types other than Th17 cells, we isolated CD11b−CD11c−NK1.1−CD19− IA/IE− cells from mouse spleens and lymph nodes and cultured them under Th17-polarizing conditions in the presence of ART for 6 days. In IL-17+CD45+ leukocytes, frequencies of CD3ε−CD90.2+ ILCs and γδT cells were much more than CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5C). In addition, ART treatment also significantly inhibited the population of IL-17-producing CD8+ T cells, ILCs and γδT cells (Figs. 5D–F). ART treatment greatly suppressed the mRNA expression of transcription factor STAT3 and RORγt, and also the proliferation-associated gene Mki67 (Fig. 5G). These findings suggest that ART suppresses IL-17 production in a cell type-independent manner.

ART reshapes the intestinal microbiota composition

To explore whether ART treatment modulates the gut microbiota contributes, fecal samples from ART-treated mice were subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing. While ART treatment did not significantly alter the α-diversity of the fecal microbiota compared with control mice (Fig. 6A), β-diversity analysis revealed a pronounced shift in microbial community composition following ART administration (Fig. 6B). At the phylum level, ART treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Bacillota (formerly named Firmicutes) (Fig. 6C) and elevated the Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio (Fig. 6D), a microbial feature associated with reduced colitis susceptibility [21]. Conversely, the abundance of Pseudomonadota (also named Proteobacteria), a phylum known to promote or exacerbate intestinal inflammation [22, 23], was significantly reduced in ART-treated mice (Fig. 6E). Interestingly, the abundance of Lactobacillus which belongs to Bacillota was significantly increased in ART-treated mice (Fig. 6F). In parallel, colonic expression of Reg3b, encoding the antibacterial peptide REG3β, was significantly decreased following ART treatment (Fig. 6G). These data indicate that ART reshapes the gut microbiota toward a less inflammatory configuration, potentially contributing to its protective effects against colitis.

To further investigate whether ART-mediated modulation of the gut microbiota contributes to protection against colitis, mice were orally administered culture medium, Gram-positive Lactobacillus johnsonii (L. johnsonii), or a Gram-positive–targeting antibiotic vancomycin in drinking water for 7 days, followed by daily administration of ART during 3% DSS treatment (Fig. 6H). Compared with medium-treated controls, both ART-treated and L. johnsonii–treated mice exhibited significantly attenuated clinical symptoms, including reduced body weight loss and lower DAI scores (Figs. 6I and J). Consistent with these clinical improvements, ART and L. johnsonii treatment markedly alleviated DSS-induced colon shortening (Figs. 6K and L) and reduced histopathological damage (Figs. 6M and N). In contrast, vancomycin pretreatment abrogated the protective effects of ART against colitis (Figs. 6I–M). These findings indicate that ART-mediated remodeling of the gut microbiota, particularly Gram-positive commensals, contributes to its protective effects in colitis.

ART pretreatment confers protection against DSS-induced colitis

Having established the therapeutic efficacy of ART during DSS administration, we next investigated whether ART pretreatment could prevent colitis onset. Mice were pretreated with intraperitoneal ART (50 mg/kg) daily for 7 days prior to DSS exposure (Fig. 7A). Compared with non-pretreated controls, ART-pretreated mice developed significantly milder colitis, as reflected by reduced body weight loss, diarrhea, and bloody stools (Figs. 7B and C). Consistently, ART pretreatment markedly attenuated DSS-induced colon shortening (Figs. 7D and E) and reduced histological scores indicative of mucosal inflammation, epithelial damage, and immune cell infiltration (Figs. 7F and G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ART pretreatment effectively prevents the development and severity of DSS-induced colitis in mice.

ART strengthens the therapeutic effect of mesalazine

Having established that ART administration both prevents and ameliorates DSS-induced colitis, we next assessed whether ART influences the therapeutic efficacy of the standard IBD drug mesalazine (5-aminosalicylic acid, 5-ASA) [24]. Mice were treated with ART or orally administered 5-ASA daily for 7 days during DSS exposure (Fig. 8A). Compared with PBS-treated controls, both 5-ASA– and ART-treated mice developed significantly milder colitis, as evidenced by reduced body weight loss, diarrhea, and rectal bleeding (Figs. 8B and C). Consistently, both treatments markedly attenuated DSS-induced colon shortening (Figs. 8D and E) and reduced histopathological scores reflecting decreased mucosal inflammation, epithelial damage, and immune cell infiltration (Figs. 8F and G). Notably, combined ART and 5-ASA treatment resulted in a further enhancement of therapeutic efficacy compared with either treatment alone (Figs. 8B–G). Together, these results demonstrate that ART not only exerts independent protective effects against colitis but also enhances the therapeutic efficacy of mesalazine in DSS-induced colitis.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that artesunate (ART) markedly alleviates DSS–induced colitis, as evidenced by reduced body weight loss, lower disease activity index scores, and attenuation of inflammation-associated colon shortening. Consistent with these clinical improvements, ART treatment suppressed colonic expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines while enhancing anti-inflammatory mediators. These findings are in line with previous reports showing that ART exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of IκBα and NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, resulting in reduced production of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α and increased IL-10 expression [14]. Together, these data support a central role for ART in dampening intestinal inflammatory signaling pathways.

Disruption of epithelial barrier integrity is a hallmark of UC and a key driver of sustained mucosal inflammation. Consistent with this concept, Wang et al. reported that ART reverses tight junction proteins of intestines, including claudin-3 and E-cadherin [17]. Extending these observations, our study demonstrates that ART restores the expression of ZO-1 and claudin-1 in the colonic mucosa, suggesting that preservation of epithelial barrier integrity represents an important mechanism underlying ART-mediated protection in colitis. Moreover, a previous study showed that ART mitigates intestinal inflammation by reducing the frequency of Th17 cells and restoring the M2/M1 macrophage ratio [16]. In agreement with these findings, we observed a significant reduction in both colonic IL17+CD4+ T cells and other IL-17 producing lymphocytes such as CD8+ T cells, γδ T cells and ILCs following ART treatment both in vivo and in vitro, further highlighting the anti-inflammation capacity of ART in the intestine and indicating the suppressive function of ART on IL-17 is cell type independent and physiologically ubiquitous.

Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiota plays a critical role in shaping intestinal immune responses and regulating susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease. In the present study, ART treatment alone, in the absence of DSS, was sufficient to markedly reshape the gut microbial community. Specifically, ART increased the abundance of the Bacillota phylum and elevated the Bacillota-to-Bacteroidota ratio with the increased Lactobacillus species, while reducing the abundance of Pseudomonadota. Previous analyses comparing healthy individuals with patients with UC have demonstrated a correlation between microbial composition and disease status, including altered Bacillota/Bacteroidota ratios [25]. In addition, Pseudomonadota abundance has been reported to positively correlate with colitis severity [26]. Furthermore, ART failed to protect DSS-colitis in mice pretreated with antibiotics against gram-positive bacteria, and oral colonization of L. johnsonii reproduced the effect of ART against DSS-induced colitis. These observations indicate that ART-mediated remodeling of the gut microbiota contributes to reduced susceptibility to colitis and enhanced intestinal homeostasis.

Mechanistically, ART treatment led to a marked reduction in IL-17–producing cells in the intestine. IL-17 induces antimicrobial peptides such as Reg3β from intestinal epithelial cells in a STAT3-dependent manner [18, 27, 28]. As ART is reported to function as a STAT3 inhibitor [29, 30], the observed suppression of Reg3b expression in the colon following ART treatment is likely mediated, at least in part, through inhibition of STAT3–IL-17 signaling. REG3β preferentially targets gram-positive bacteria, including members of the Bacillota phylum such as Lactobacillus and Enterococcus, while bacteria within the Pseudomonadota phylum are relatively resistant to REG3β-mediated killing [31]. We therefore speculate that ART-induced downregulation of Reg3β may reduce selective pressure against gram-positive commensals, thereby facilitating the expansion or stable colonization of Bacillota such as Lactobacillus, which in turn may competitively limit the abundance of Pseudomonadota. Further mechanistic studies will be required to directly test this hypothesis.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that ART protects against DSS-induced colitis by suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, limiting IL-17–driven immune responses, and preserving intestinal epithelial barrier integrity. Moreover, ART pretreatment confers resistance to colitis development through long-term remodeling of the gut microbiota. Together, these results identify ART as a promising therapeutic and preventive agent for UC and highlight its multifaceted immunomodulatory effects within the intestinal mucosa.
