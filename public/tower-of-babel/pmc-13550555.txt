Clinical utility of molecular residual disease detection in early-stage resected EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Fei Zhou, Chunxia Su, Wenhua Liang, Li-Di Xu, Wei Tao, Juanjuan Qian, Shidong Xu, Dan Liu, Rui Fan, Yaxi Zhang, Yiqian Liu, Lin Wu, Xiangning Fu, Xiaodong Zhang, Ning Shen, Di Ge, Qun Chen, Weimin Mao, Lin Xu, Chun Chen, Bing Hu, Guoguang Shao, Jian Hu, Jian Zhao, Xiaoqing Liu, Zhidong Liu, Zheng Wang, Zemin Xiao, Taiqian Gong, Wen Lin, Xingya Li, Feng Ye, Yang Liu, Haitao Ma, Yunchao Huang, Jianying Zhou, Zhonglin Wang, Junke Fu, Lieming Ding, Li Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular residual disease (MRD), detected through circulating tumor DNA, has emerged as a promising biomarker for surveillance in patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) following curative-intent resection. Here we report the MRD analysis from the phase 3 EVIDENCE trial, which includes patients with stage II–IIIA resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC who have received either adjuvant icotinib or chemotherapy. A total of 1,352 plasma samples from 175 patients are analyzed using the MinerVa Prime assay, a personalized tumor-informed MRD platform. At the landmark timepoint, MinerVa Prime detects MRD positivity in 35.8% (38/106) of patients with stage III disease and 14.7% (10/68) of patients with stage II disease, with a longitudinal positivity rate of 47.4%. MRD-positive status is significantly correlated with inferior disease-free survival (DFS), both at landmark (hazard ratio [HR]: 4.44, P < 0.001) and during longitudinal monitoring (HR: 7.82, P < 0.001). Icotinib confers DFS benefits over chemotherapy in both MRD subgroups, enhancing MRD clearance in MRD-positive patients while reducing molecular recurrence in landmark MRD-negative patients. Longitudinal MRD shows a 91.3% negative predictive value, with a median lead time of 169 days prior to clinical recurrence. Among serial monitoring timepoints, MRD status at 24 weeks post-randomization demonstrates the highest prognostic value.

Introduction

For patients with early-stage resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harboring epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) sensitizing mutations, the current standard of care involves surgical resection followed by adjuvant EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR TKIs) with or without chemotherapy1. Compared with adjuvant chemotherapy alone, several first-generation EGFR TKIs, including icotinib, gefitinib, and erlotinib, significantly improve disease-free survival (DFS)2–4. Notably, osimertinib, a third-generation EGFR TKI, has demonstrated both DFS and overall survival (OS) benefit over placebo with or without adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with resected EGFR-mutated stage IB-IIIA NSCLC5,6.

The EVIDENCE trial, a phase 3, multicenter, randomized study, compared adjuvant icotinib with chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutated stage II–IIIA NSCLC after complete surgical resection2. A preplanned interim analysis of EVIDENCE revealed that adjuvant icotinib significantly improved DFS compared with chemotherapy (47.0 vs. 22.1 months; hazard ratio [HR]: 0.36; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.24–0.55)2. Nevertheless, even with up to two years of adjuvant icotinib treatment, a considerable proportion of patients still experienced disease relapse or death2. Identifying and monitoring high-risk patients for cancer recurrence remains a critical challenge in managing completely resected, EGFR-mutated NSCLC during and after adjuvant EGFR TKI treatment.

Molecular residual disease (MRD), detected via circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), has emerged as a promising biomarker for surveillance in early-stage NSCLC following curative resection7. Extensive evidence indicates that patients with detectable MRD post-resection exhibit worse prognosis compared to those with undetectable MRD8–14. Longitudinal MRD monitoring can forecast radiological recurrence with a lead time of several months8–14. Furthermore, MRD detection has the potential to guide personalized adjuvant therapy, though current evidence is largely derived from subgroup analyses with limited sample sizes13,15.

In this post-hoc MRD analysis of the phase 3 EVIDENCE trial, we sought to address several unresolved questions. First, we evaluated the prognostic relevance of postoperative MRD and its capacity to predict recurrence via longitudinal monitoring in EGFR-mutated NSCLC. Second, by comparing MRD dynamics between the adjuvant targeted therapy and chemotherapy arms, we explored the clinical utility of dynamic MRD monitoring for assessing treatment efficacy. Finally, we aimed to identify optimal MRD monitoring timepoints during follow-up for early-stage NSCLC patients receiving postoperative adjuvant therapy, and to propose personalized, cost-effective MRD surveillance strategies.

Results

Study cohort and experimental design

A total of 184 patients from the EVIDENCE cohort were retrospectively included for MRD analysis, of whom 175 (95%) had successful MRD results (Fig. 1A, B). The cohort had a median age of 59 years, with 49% male patients, and was predominantly composed of adenocarcinoma cases (n = 168, 96%), including 39% with stage II disease at diagnosis (n = 68). Overall, 90 patients carried EGFR exon 21 L858R and 85 carried exon 19 deletion (19Del) mutations (Table 1). Among these patients,106 were from the icotinib arm, and 69 from the chemotherapy arm. The imbalance between the two arms was primarily attributable to logistical constraints and differences in clinical management. As noted in the full analysis set, treatment non-adherence in the chemotherapy arm had already created a numerical imbalance (icotinib: n = 151; chemotherapy: n = 132)2, which was further exacerbated in the current MRD analysis set. The rigorous plasma collection schedule (every 12 or 24 weeks) proved challenging for the chemotherapy group, as these patients transitioned to routine imaging surveillance without active systemic therapy. Nevertheless, baseline characteristics were balanced between treatment arms in both the MRD analysis set and the full analysis set. The median DFS was 47.6 months for icotinib versus 25.1 months for chemotherapy (HR: 0.37; 95% CI: 0.21–0.64; P = 0.0002), consistent with the full analysis set (47.0 vs. 22.1 months; HR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.24–0.55; P < 0.0001) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Personalized next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels for this cohort included 7452 single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs), along with 17 rearrangements. Tracked mutations were predominantly patient-specific (n = 7419, 99.6%), with only 0.4% (n = 33) shared across ≥ 2 patients. For landmark MRD assessment, 174 postoperative plasma samples were analyzed using the MinerVa Prime assay. Longitudinal MRD analysis included 1352 plasma samples, with a median of 8 samples per patient in the icotinib arm and 7 in the chemotherapy arm (Supplementary Data 2). MRD positivity was defined using a predefined assay threshold (see “Methods”). EGFR-denoted MRD positivity, defined by detection of tumor-derived EGFR mutations in plasma, was compared to MRD status determined by MinerVa Prime at landmark and longitudinal timepoints.

The analytical performance of the MinerVa Prime MRD assay

The analytical performance of the MinerVa Prime assay was evaluated using the Seraseq® ctDNA MRD Panel Mix commercial reference material. Seven ultra-low ctDNA concentration gradients were established through serial dilutions. Tumor-derived mutations were pre-identified using whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis of the reference sample, which consisted of a cell line DNA mix. A patient-specific panel was constructed by combining customized probes targeting 88 unique mutations with an off-the-shelf panel covering > 1000 hotspot mutations. To reflect clinical feasibility, detection thresholds were assessed at ultra-low (5 ng and 10 ng) and standard (30 ng) plasma input levels using MinerVa Prime. Probit analysis determined the limit of detection (LoD) values of 0.0075%, 0.0042%, and 0.0024% for 5 ng, 10 ng, and 30 ng inputs, respectively, when tracking 50 variants per sample. In contrast, EGFR-denoted MRD detection (reliant solely on EGFR L858R or 19Del) yielded an LoD of 0.06% at 30 ng input (Supplementary Fig. 2A). An independent set of reference material (ctDNA MRD Panel Mix, GeneWell, China) was used to further ascertain the sensitivity of MinerVa Prime. Positive detection rates of 97.2% (35/36) and 100% (9/9) were achieved at the dilutions of 0.0025% and 0.005%, respectively, by tracking 37 hotspots (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

To assess specificity, 14 plasma samples from healthy donors were tested using 40 personalized panels designed for cancer patients, achieving 100% specificity (560/560) (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Furthermore, 135 plasma samples from cancer patients were analyzed by tracking simulated variants absent from matched tumor tissues. Specificity remained consistently above 99.5% across a wide range of tracked variant numbers (6 to 60) and varying cfDNA input levels (Supplementary Fig. 2D). Moreover, enhanced quantitation accuracy was achieved with higher input amounts or increased variant tracking (Supplementary Fig. 2E, F).

Prognostic value of qualitative and quantitative MRD assessment at landmark timepoint

In this study, among 174 patients with postoperative plasma samples (median collection: 30 days post-operation; range: 9–62 days), MinerVa Prime detected landmark MRD positivity in 27.6% (48/174). Landmark MRD-positive patients demonstrated a significantly increased risk of relapse (DFS HR: 4.44; 95% CI: 2.57–7.67; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A) as well as an increased risk of death (OS HR: 6.70; 95% CI: 2.18-20.61, P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 3A). The median DFS was 20.1 months for MRD-positive patients versus not reached for MRD-negative patients (Fig. 2A). Notably, icotinib improved DFS over chemotherapy in both landmark MRD-negative (HR: 0.26; 95% CI: 0.11–0.62; P = 0.001) and MRD-positive subgroups (HR: 0.40; 95% CI: 0.18–0.89; P = 0.021) (Fig. 2B). Landmark MRD positivity was significantly associated with shorter DFS and OS in both the icotinib arm (DFS HR: 6.29; 95% CI: 2.61–15.18; P < 0.001; OS HR: 6.68; 95% CI: 1.63–27.36; P = 0.002) and chemotherapy arm (DFS HR: 3.75; 95% CI: 1.79–7.84; P < 0.001; OS HR: 13.46; 95% CI: 1.18–153.78; P = 0.011) (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 3B)Fig. 2Prognostic prediction of disease-free survival (DFS) by landmark MRD status.A Kaplan-Meier (K-M) curves were generated to analyze DFS based on postoperative landmark MRD determined by MinerVa Prime within a median of 30 days post-surgery (range: 9–62 days; n = 174). We further explored the distribution of MRD positivity across clinical subgroups. Stage III patients exhibited a higher MRD positivity (35.8% [38/106]) versus stage II (14.7% [10/68]; P = 0.003), and N2 patients had higher positivity than N0-N1 (38.1% vs. 11.6%; P < 0.001). No differences were observed by treatment regimen, T stage, histology, sex, age, ECOG status, EGFR mutation type, or smoking status (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed landmark MRD positivity as an independent DFS predictor (P < 0.001), contributing 53.7% of the predictive capacity for DFS versus 29.8% from adjuvant therapy (Fig. 2C, D).

Subgroup analysis revealed stronger MRD predictive utility in stage II (HR: 17.56) versus stage IIIA (HR: 2.94; interaction P = 0.015) (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 4B). This observation aligns with findings from previous studies10,14,16–18, which reported enhanced MRD stratification in early-stage diseases (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Additionally, enhanced stratification was also observed in females (interaction P = 0.004) and non-smokers (interaction P = 0.016) (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D).

To complement qualitative findings, we further explored the predictive value of landmark ctDNA concentrations for DFS. In the chemotherapy arm, higher ctDNA concentrations predicted shorter DFS (Pearson correlation coefficient, R2 = 0.39; P = 0.013). Meanwhile, a similar trend appeared in the icotinib arm, though it did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 2F).

Longitudinal MRD monitoring and recurrence prediction

We analyzed a total of 1352 plasma samples from 175 patients, all of whom had at least one sample available. Longitudinal MRD (assessments at all timepoints), on-therapy MRD (during icotinib treatment), and post-therapy MRD (after treatment completion) evaluations were conducted. Specifically, on-therapy MRD was assessed in 104 patients (all from the icotinib arm), while post-therapy MRD was assessed in 109 patients (icotinib arm: 40; chemotherapy arm: 69 patients). MRD positivity was defined as the presence of any positive result within the pre-assigned visit windows.

Among the 104 patients receiving TKI treatment (on-therapy samples), 27 (26.0%) had at least one MRD-positive test during therapy. These patients had a 14.66-fold higher risk of relapse compared with those with remained consistently MRD-negative (95% CI: 4.84-44.44; P < 0.001), with a negative predictive value (NPV) of 93.5% (72/77) for recurrence prediction (Fig. 3A). Notably, none of the MRD-negative patients died (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Longitudinal MRD positivity, observed in 83 patients (47.4%), was associated with significantly shorter DFS and OS (DFS HR: 7.82; 95% CI: 3.68–16.61, P < 0.001; OS HR: 7.95; 95% CI: 1.77–35.69; P = 0.001) (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Fig. 5B). The positive predictive value (PPV) of longitudinal MRD was 54.2% (45/83), while the NPV was 91.3% (84/92) (Fig. 3C).

Dynamic NPV and PPV of longitudinal MRD were calculated as the proportions of non-recurrent and relapsed patients among corresponding MRD-negative and MRD-positive patients, respectively, within projected intervals following each longitudinal test14. The NPV remained high at 96.8%, 90.6%, and 73.9% at 180, 360, and 720 days, respectively. In contrast, the PPV increased from 42.5% at 180 days to 63.9% at 360 days, and 90.3% at 720 days (Fig. 3D), suggesting that short-term PPV limitations may reflect the temporal window between MRD detection and radiological recurrence.

Subgroup analyses of longitudinal MRD in the icotinib arm and the chemotherapy arm revealed consistent trends (Supplementary Fig. 6A–D). In the icotinib arm, longitudinal MRD-positive patients (46/106, 43.4%) had a 13.77-fold higher relapse risk (median DFS: 36.4 months; 95% CI: 3.21–59.06; P < 0.001) compared to MRD-negative patients (median DFS: not reached), with an NPV of 96.7% (58/60) (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Meanwhile, post-therapy MRD positivity similarly predicted inferior survival (Supplementary Fig. 6B). In the chemotherapy arm, both longitudinally and post-therapy MRD positivity were associated with significantly reduced DFS (Supplementary Fig. 6C, D).

As illustrated by the swimmer plot (Supplementary Fig. 7), MRD positivity among recurrence-free patients frequently occurred near the data cutoff, suggesting that the initially observed PPV (54.2%) may have been constrained by insufficient lead time. To further evaluate the clinical significance of these presumptive ‘false positives’, we performed an extended follow-up analysis. Of the 38 patients who remained longitudinally MRD-positive but relapse-free at the initial cutoff, updated clinical records were obtained for 25 individuals (data cutoff: October 17, 2023). 18 of these 25 patients (72%) eventually manifested clinical recurrence (Supplementary Data 3). Consequently, the recalculated PPV for longitudinal MRD monitoring rose to 75.9% (63/83) in the overall cohort, reaching 86.5% (32/37) in the chemotherapy arm and 67.4% (31/46) in the icotinib arm. Notably, out of the 7 patients who remained unrelapsed by the end of the extended follow-up, 6 had only landmark MRD positive and/or with initial two positives and subsequently achieved long-term clearance during and after the treatment, suggesting that these patients may have derived substantial benefit from treatment (Supplementary Fig. 8). Therefore, when re-calculating longitudinal MRD PPV by excluding landmark timepoint, the overall PPV of longitudinal MRD increased to 83.3% (55/66) (icotinib arm: 75.8% [25/33]; chemotherapy arm: 90.9% [30/33]).

Quantitative ctDNA in recurrence prediction

Overall, median ctDNA concentrations were significantly higher in patients who experienced recurrence, both on-therapy (P = 0.035) and post-therapy samples (icotinib arm: P < 0.001; chemotherapy arm: P = 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 9A, B). Notably, on-therapy MRD-positive samples (n = 62) exhibited higher ctDNA concentrations than post-therapy MRD-positive samples (n = 27), suggesting effective tumor burden reduction by TKI treatment (P = 0.048) (Supplementary Fig. 9C).

Previous studies have reported variable MRD sensitivity in predicting relapse across metastatic sites13. In our cohort, among 53 patients with documented metastatic sites, 85% (45/53) had longitudinal MRD positivity. All patients with multiple metastatic sites (3/3) or pleural recurrence (6/6) were MRD-positive. Sensitivity for single-organ recurrence detection was 91% (10/11) for bone, 89% (8/9) for lung, 83% (10/12) for lymph nodes, 70% (7/10) for brain, and 50% (1/2) for liver metastases (Fig. 3E). Detection rates aligned with ctDNA concentrations in MRD-positive samples, which were highest in multi-metastatic cases, followed by lymph node and bone metastases. Notably, lung recurrences showed lower ctDNA concentrations despite high sensitivity (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the median lead time from MRD detection by MinerVa Prime to radiological recurrence was 169 days (Supplementary Fig. 9D).

To elucidate the relationship between longitudinal ctDNA fluctuations and disease recurrence, we analyzed temporal trends in ctDNA concentrations among patients with ≥ 2 MRD-positive samples, stratified by recurrence status (recurrent: n = 31; recurrence-free at follow-up closure: n = 12). MRD-negative samples, lacking quantitative ctDNA values, were excluded from this analysis. The results demonstrated that recurrent patients exhibited progressive ctDNA escalation across all treatment groups, with pronounced acceleration in both absolute concentration and growth kinetics proximal to relapse. Conversely, recurrence-free patients maintained stable, low-level ctDNA concentrations with minimal temporal variability, mirroring patterns observed in the recurrence individuals during pre-relapse intervals (1 year before relapse) (Fig. 3G).

We subsequently implemented a joint Bayesian (JB) model19 to assess time-dependent, patient-specific recurrence risk by synthesizing serial quantitative ctDNA trajectories with clinical covariates (e.g., stage, treatment modality). The model incorporated all longitudinal plasma samples collected prior to a defined post-randomization timepoint (t days post-randomization; see “Methods”). To illustrate dynamic risk profiles, we present two representative cases: Patient P25 (recurrence) and Patient P34 (recurrence-free). For P25, cumulative recurrence risk escalated concordantly with rising ctDNA concentrations at serial timepoints (2.8, 5.6, 8.6, and 11.2 months; Supplementary Fig. 10A). In contrast, P34 showed sustained low-risk estimates attributable to stable, low ctDNA concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 10B). These findings underscore the prognostic utility of quantitative ctDNA surveillance in refining clinical risk stratification.

Longitudinal MRD surveillance for disease monitoring and therapeutic efficacy evaluation

Adjuvant chemotherapy and targeted therapies are recommended for stage II-III EGFR-mutated NSCLC, with MRD assessment emerging as a critical tool for evaluating therapeutic efficacy. Analysis of longitudinal MRD positivity rates revealed that patients with landmark MRD positivity who received icotinib exhibited sustained reductions in subsequent MRD detection compared with the chemotherapy arm (Fig. 4A), reflecting improved ctDNA clearance efficacy with adjuvant TKI treatment. Week 24 postoperative MRD status as a pivotal biomarker of therapeutic efficacy

We next explored the optimal prognostic timepoint for longitudinal MRD monitoring. Prognostic analysis of standardized postoperative MRD timepoints identified CF24W as the most promising interval for outcome discrimination, with the highest HRs for predicting DFS, regardless of treatment regimen (Fig. 4B). In the icotinib arm, CF24W MRD positivity conferred a 60.99-fold elevated relapse risk (95% CI: 17.06–217.99; P < 0.001) compared with MRD-negative counterparts. The chemotherapy arm similarly showed peak prognostic performance at CF24W (HR, 9.18; 95% CI, 4.08–20.67; P < 0.001). Moreover, 8 of 9 (89%) MRD-positive patients in the icotinib arm and 14 out of 15 (93%) in the chemotherapy arm experienced tumor recurrence within the follow-up period (Supplementary Fig. 11A). These findings persisted after explicitly accounting for potential immortal survival bias by recalculating DFS starting from 24-week post-randomization (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Furthermore, a Cox proportional hazards model incorporating clinical stage, adjuvant therapy regimen, and CF24W MRD status showed the highest time-dependent AUC for predicting DFS across follow-up intervals, outperforming models using MRD status at landmark, CF12W, or CF36W (Fig. 4C).

MRD clearance at CF24W (defined as transition from landmark positivity to negativity at CF24W) can also stratify risk subgroups. Among 44 landmark MRD-positive patients, 29 achieved clearance, with baseline characteristics balanced between those who cleared and those who remained persistently positive (Supplementary Data 4). Notably, landmark ctDNA concentrations were significantly lower in patients who achieved clearance versus those with persistent positivity (P = 0.032) (Supplementary Fig. 11C), implying postoperative molecular tumor burden may influence ctDNA clearance potential. In the icotinib arm, 76% (19/25) cleared MRD, showing better DFS (47.6 months) than persistently or newly MRD-positive patients, though inferior to those negative at both timepoints (Fig. 4D). A similar trend appeared in the chemotherapy arm (52.6% clearance) (Fig. 4E). Notably, DFS for MRD-cleared patients was longer with icotinib comparing with chemotherapy (47.6 vs. 21.4 months), supporting CF24W MRD status as a key marker for evaluating therapy benefit (Fig. 4D). To mitigate the potential immortal survival bias, DFS was recalculated starting from 24 weeks post-randomization, yielding consistent findings (Supplementary Fig. 12A, B).

Although MinerVa Prime demonstrates enhanced sensitivity for MRD detection, EGFR-directed single-variant testing offers a pragmatic, cost-effective alternative for routine clinical use. Genomic profiling of 53 relapsed patients revealed that EGFR-denoted MRD performed well in EGFR copy number variation (CNV)-gain tumors (86% detection rate); however, in CNV-neutral cases, MinerVa Prime demonstrated higher sensitivity (85% [39/46] vs. 61% [28/46]; Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 13C).

Discussion

Previous investigations of MRD in NSCLC have predominantly enrolled genomically heterogeneous cohorts, revealing divergent ctDNA shedding patterns across molecular subtypes20. In particular, tumors harboring EGFR mutations are more likely to exhibit non-shedding or low-shedding characteristics, manifesting as lower preoperative ctDNA detection rates and concentrations20. Despite representing 7–10% of Western and up to 50% of East Asian NSCLC cases1, EGFR-mutated NSCLC remains understudied in the context of postoperative MRD dynamics21. The current study provides a comprehensive evaluation of MRD’s prognostic utility in patients with early-stage, resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC receiving adjuvant therapies, yielding several key insights: (1) postoperative landmark and longitudinal MRD status affirmed robust prognostic utility, with MRD-negative status associated with longer DFS compared with MRD-positive status across adjuvant therapy modalities (icotinib or chemotherapy); (2) adjuvant icotinib enhanced MRD clearance in landmark MRD-positive patients and reduced molecular recurrence in landmark MRD-negative patients, consistent with its established therapeutic superiority over chemotherapy; (3) CF24W postoperative MRD status demonstrated the highest prognostic value, warranting prospective validation as a potential surrogate endpoint for adjuvant therapy response. By establishing the clinical utility of MRD detection at specific timepoints, we provide a framework for future prospective trials aimed at integrating MRD into personalized care paradigms.

In alignment with prior investigations8–14, our study corroborated that both landmark and longitudinal MRD are robustly associated with DFS. Additionally, landmark MRD demonstrated significant prognostic value across all clinical subgroups analyzed. However, the magnitude of risk stratification appeared to be more pronounced in groups conventionally considered lower risk, particularly stage II patients, females, and non-smokers, than in their stage III, male, and smoker counterparts. One plausible explanation is that in lower‑risk populations, MRD status captures otherwise occult biological heterogeneity not fully reflected by conventional clinicopathological factors, thereby enabling clearer discrimination between patients with divergent recurrence risks. In contrast, in higher stage patients, the prognostic contribution of MRD may be partially attenuated by the presence of multiple coexisting adverse factors that independently drive recurrence risk. While the interaction between tumor stage and MRD has been sporadically reported10,14,16–18 and further discussed in a recent study22, the associations with sex and smoking status are notable. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating MRD status into comprehensive, multivariable risk models alongside established clinicopathological factors.

Analogous trends were observed across both the adjuvant icotinib and chemotherapy groups, suggesting that all patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC, irrespective of their postoperative MRD status, should receive adjuvant targeted therapy. Specifically, in the landmark MRD-positive subgroup, a higher proportion achieved MRD clearance following initiation of adjuvant icotinib therapy, while in the landmark MRD-negative subgroup, a lower proportion transitioned to MRD-positive under icotinib treatment. These observations are consistent with the findings of the ADAURA trial, which demonstrated that adjuvant osimertinib is more effective in maintaining MRD negativity23. This partly explains the mechanism by which adjuvant targeted therapy prolongs DFS compared with chemotherapy. Additionally, we observed that in the icotinib group, the proportion of MRD-positive patients remained below 10% throughout the 2-year treatment window. However, a marked increase in MRD positivity was observed after treatment discontinuation, particularly at 108 weeks (2 years) post-treatment. This suggests that the standard 2-year duration of adjuvant icotinib therapy may be inadequate to preserve MRD negativity and prevent disease recurrence. Collectively, these findings underscore the possible need for extended durations of adjuvant targeted therapy or the implementation of combination therapies for these patients, a hypothesis that requires prospective validation in future clinical studies.

The current clinical management framework for early-stage resected patients who have completed adjuvant therapies relies largely on routine imaging-based surveillance. Although extensive evidence has underscored the prognostic significance of postoperative MRD, the translational challenge of integrating MRD into personalized treatment strategies remains unresolved. In stage II colon cancer, the DYNAMIC trial utilized ctDNA status at 4 or 7 weeks post-surgery to guide adjuvant therapy decisions24; however, the applicability of this timepoint in lung cancer remains uncertain. Additionally, Dong and colleagues have initiated trials to explore the feasibility of a ctDNA-guided adaptive TKI treatment break for patients with advanced NSCLC and undetectable ctDNA. Their findings potentially support the de-escalation strategy for certain low-risk patients25,26. Beyond these efforts, our study further explored the correlation between MRD status at various postoperative timepoints and DFS. We found that MRD assessment at 24 weeks post-randomization exhibited maximal prognostic discrimination in both therapeutic arms, yielding HRs of 60.99 for icotinib and 9.18 for chemotherapy, respectively. This 24-week (6-month) timepoint aligns with a critical interval in routine clinical follow-up: for patients undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy, it typically marks the period following the completion of their treatment cycles, while for those receiving target therapy, it serves as a pivotal evaluation milestone. Using the 24-week MRD status as a cutoff provided important insights into treatment efficacy. Specifically, in patients with landmark MRD-positive status who subsequently became negative at 24 weeks, DFS was 47.6 months in the icotinib group and 21.4 months in the chemotherapy group. These findings are consistent with the overall cohort outcomes, suggesting that these patients are the primary beneficiaries of adjuvant treatment. In contrast, patients who remained MRD-positive at 24 weeks exhibited a median DFS of only 10 months, highlighting the urgent need for intensified treatment in this subgroup. This finding informs the development of MRD-guided adaptive strategies and warrants prospective validation of the 24-week timepoint as a rational intervention timepoint for tailoring therapeutic approaches.

Previous studies have suggested a potential non-shedding or low-shedding tendency in EGFR-mutated NSCLC16,17. A recent analysis of preoperative ctDNA within the TRACERx cohort reported significantly lower preoperative ctDNA concentrations in patients with EGFR-mutated tumors compared with those with EGFR wild-type tumors20. In contrast to comparable investigations such as the ADAURA trial, our cohort exhibited a substantially elevated landmark MRD positivity rate (27.6% vs. 8% in ADAURA23), with several methodological distinctions likely underlying this divergence. This difference may stem from cohort and design variations: ADAURA included stage I patients and administered chemotherapy before randomization, which may have contributed to a lower baseline MRD positivity rate. In contrast, by excluding stage I patients and limiting the cohort to patients who had not received adjuvant therapy prior to the landmark timepoint, our study naturally yielded a higher prevalence of MRD positivity at the landmark. Additionally, the biological shedding characteristics of ctDNA in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC may also differ, potentially influencing the detected MRD rates. Despite the potential low-shedding characteristics attributed to this genotypic subgroup, our findings revealed a robust NPV of 91.3% for predicting recurrence. Longitudinal MRD monitoring predicted relapse 169 days before radiological detection, thereby providing a critical window for clinical intervention and suggesting that MRD assessment should be performed no less frequently than every six months. Notably, whether early targeted therapy intervention based on MRD detection will improve outcomes in early-stage resected NSCLC remains unclear, and prospective trials will be required to validate this hypothesis.

To delineate context-specific utility, we conducted a head-to-head comparison of exome-informed MRD detection (MinerVa Prime) and single-variant EGFR-MRD, which serves as a pragmatic, cost-effective alternative for tumors harboring canonical driver mutations. This comparison is particularly clinically relevant in East Asian populations, where EGFR-mutated NSCLC constitutes nearly 50% of cases1. As expected, MinerVa Prime demonstrated superior sensitivity for DFS prediction and postoperative recurrence detection compared with EGFR-MRD. It further identified an intermediate-risk subgroup, with clinical outcomes intermediate between the super-positive and MRD-negative groups, underscoring its capacity for refined risk stratification. Notably, exomic profiling revealed comparable sensitivity for predicting recurrence between EGFR- denoted MRD and MinerVa Prime in EGFR CNV-gain tumors, despite the rarity of this alteration. These results support a precision-based strategy to MRD assay selection. Specifically, EGFR-MRD may be appropriate for EGFR CNV-gain tumors, offering diagnostic value at a lower cost. Conversely, MinerVa Prime is preferred in CNV-neutral settings, where its broader mutation coverage and superior sensitivity improve detection of low-burden disease (Fig. 5F). By tailoring assay selection to the tumor genomic characteristics, clinicians can optimize MRD monitoring approaches, balancing efficiency with diagnostic accuracy. Such exome-informed strategies exemplify the evolving paradigm of precision medicine in optimizing postoperative surveillance.

This study has several notable strengths, including the use of a large sample size from a phase 3 trial, a high-quality and molecularly homogeneous EGFR-mutated NSCLC population, and systematic longitudinal plasma sampling. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as the current study focused on icotinib, a first-generation EGFR TKI, further investigation is needed to determine whether these findings can be generalized to patients receiving third-generation EGFR TKIs, such as osimertinib, in the early-stage setting. Second, while the present study, along with other clinical trials17,23, employed WES-informed MRD assays, the field is rapidly transitioning toward whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and ultra-high-plex assays. This technological shift is exemplified by the TRACERx study, in which a WGS-based assay tracking up to 1800 variants achieved enhanced detection sensitivity while maintaining a high longitudinal PPV of 93.5%20. Future studies leveraging the broader coverage of WGS may further improve assay sensitivity and reliability, particularly during periods of deep therapeutic suppression. Third, the relatively short follow-up period in the present study limits the evaluation of long-term outcomes, and the association between MRD status and overall survival warrants validation with extended follow-up. The modest PPV observed in our study may partly reflect this constraint, as several apparent “false positives” emerged near the censoring timepoint. Given that ctDNA detection often precedes clinical relapse by many months, prolonged and systematic follow-up across the entire cohort remains critical to accurately define the temporal relationship between molecular and clinical relapse. Although renewed follow-up for previously discordant cases yielded a modest improvement in PPV, incomplete surveillance likely results in an underestimation of MRD’s true predictive performance.

In summary, our findings provide independent corroboration of the clinical utility of ctDNA-based MRD detection in early-stage resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC. While this work utilizes an earlier-generation TKI, it contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting the use of MRD as a potential tool for recurrence prediction and response evaluation. Future research should continue to refine longitudinal monitoring strategies to determine how MRD-guided adaptive paradigms can be safely integrated into the clinical management of EGFR-mutated lung cancer.

Methods

Study design and patients

This post-hoc MRD analysis of the EVIDENCE study was designed to evaluate the prognostic and monitoring utility of ctDNA-based MRD in patients with early-stage, resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC receiving adjuvant therapy. The EVIDENCE study was a phase 3, multicenter, randomized, open-label trial conducted in China (NCT02448797), with methodology details published previously2. Key eligibility criteria included confirmed EGFR 19Del or L858R mutations. The primary endpoint was DFS, defined as the time from randomization to recurrence, metastasis, or death from any cause. The secondary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. Plasma samples were collected every 12 weeks for the first 2 years and every 24 weeks during years 3–5, and additionally collected alongside RECIST assessments when clinically feasible. For further details, see the Supplementary Methods. The trial complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital and each participating center. All patients provided written informed consent.

Customized probe design

For each patient, somatic variants were identified via whole-exome sequencing (WES) of tumor tissue and matched peripheral blood lymphocytes. During probe design, variants located in multi-mapping regions or regions with abnormally high or low GC content were excluded. Up to 52 variant-specific probes with tumor variant allele frequencies (VAF) ≥ 4% were selected for synthesis. Patient-specific panels were generated by combining customized probes with a fixed panel covering > 1000 mutation hotspots.

Somatic mutations calling

Single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs) were detected in tumor tissue using GATK Mutect2 following best practices27. Variants absent in matched normal samples were classified as candidate somatic mutations and further filtered with a Panel of Normals (PON) model to remove recurrent technical artifacts. Additionally, mutant and reference allele counts were quantified using in-house software to correct for orientation bias arising from DNA damage and sequencing-related artifacts.

Structural variants (SVs) were identified using LUMPY28, which converts variant signals into breakpoint probability distributions and categorizes supporting reads as breakpoint-spanning paired reads or breakpoint-containing split reads. SVs were annotated via in-house software to map breakpoints to gene loci, thereby facilitating the identification of gene fusions and ensuring correct splicing orientation of upstream and downstream sequences.

Copy number variations (CNVs) in tumor samples were analyzed using CNVkit29. The “--reference” parameter was employed to specify a copy number baseline derived from the normal CNV files in the EVIDENCE cohort, serving as a negative control.

MinerVa Prime MRD calling based on personalized panel

MRD positivity for each plasma sample was determined through a multi-step error-suppression and statistical calling pipeline. First, plasma DNA libraries were tagged with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and subjected to ultra-deep sequencing (≥100,000×). Reads sharing the same UMI and mapping coordinates (based on the hg19 reference genome) were grouped into families, enabling the generation of consensus sequences that effectively corrected for amplification errors. Second, the intra-sample background noise in consensus alternate bases, which originated from pre-PCR DNA damage or early PCR errors, was analyzed using genomic regions in the plasma data that were known to be free of tissue variants. This noise was calculated based on tri-nucleotide context (TNC) and EDGE effectors, factors related to key fragment-level molecular features for each tracked variant. These effectors enabled the establishment of feature-refined noise profiles (see Supplementary Methods). Third, a multidimensional Poisson regression model was established for each tracked variant, accounting for both the fragment-level features and their corresponding refined noise profiles. Finally, an integrated statistical framework was employed to generate a sample-level p-value. Specifically, we assessed the probability that the aggregated signal from all tracked variants originated solely from background noise. A stringent p-value threshold of 0.01, corresponding to a false positive rate of ≤ 1%, was applied to define MRD-positive status.

ctDNA-based MRD was analyzed in four distinct ways: (1) landmark MRD: based on the first single plasma sample collected post-surgery (median: 30 days; range: 9–62 days post-surgery, 0–21 days before treatment); (2) on-therapy MRD: based on a series of plasma samples collected on-therapy, applicable only to the TKI treatment arm. (3) post-therapy MRD: based on a series of plasma samples collected post-therapy but before disease relapse. (4) longitudinal MRD: based on a series of plasma samples collected after surgery but before disease relapse. MRD was considered positive if any ctDNA status within the series was positive.

EGFR-denoted MRD calling

EGFR-denoted MRD positivity was defined as the presence of at least one duplex variant detected for tumor-derived EGFR L858R or 19Del. All EGFR-denoted MRD-positive samples were also positive by independent MinerVa Prime MRD calling.

Statistical analyses

Survival probabilities were estimated via the Kaplan–Meier (K-M) method, and between-group comparisons were assessed using log-rank tests. HRs, 95% CIs, and interaction P values were derived from Cox proportional hazards models. To mitigate potential immortal survival bias in analyses of ctDNA status at CF24W and ctDNA dynamics between landmark and CF24W, we included patients who were relapse-free within the 24-week period and those with available ctDNA status at the 24-week timepoint. K-M estimates were performed with DFS starting from 24 weeks post-randomization. The relative contribution of covariates to survival risk was quantified using chi-square statistics calculated with the rms package. To assess the discriminative ability of our prognostic models at pre-defined follow-up timepoints, we performed time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses. Risk scores for individual patients were derived from the Cox proportional hazards regression model. Subsequently, time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values were estimated using the timeROC R package30. To address potential bias introduced by censored data, a marginal weighting approach, the Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) method, was applied for the AUC estimation.

Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2).
