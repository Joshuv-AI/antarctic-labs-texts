( 2 ​ k + 1 ) (2k+1) -Neighborhood Balanced Coloring
Maurice Genevieva Almeida

Abstract
Let G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) be a simple graph and ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) (2k+1) be a prime integer. Let each vertex of G G be colored using one of the ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) (2k+1) colors, say R 1 , R 2 , … , R 2 ​ k + 1 R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{2k+1} . If every vertex has an equal number of neighbors of each color, then the coloring is a ( 2 ​ k + 1 ) (2k+1) -neighborhood balanced coloring. We establish a number of results for common families of graphs and present some families of graphs that have this property.

Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a simple graph. The
order
of
G
is
|V|
, and the
size
of
G
is
|E|
, and for any vertex

, define the
neighborhood
of

as

. The members of

are the

of

. For graph theoretic notations, we refer to Chartrand and Lesniak
[
1
]
.

Freyberg et al.
[
2
]
introduced the concept of
neighborhood balanced coloring
as follows: Let
G
be a graph with each vertex colored one of two colors (e.g. red and blue). If every vertex has an equal number of neighbors of each color, then the coloring is a neighborhood balanced coloring (NBC).

It is easy to see that if a graph admits a neighborhood balanced coloring, then the degree of every vertex is even. For such a coloring, let
\sigma(BB)
be the number of blue-blue edges,
\sigma(RR)
be the number of red-red edges, and let
\sigma(RB)
be the number of red-blue edges in a neighborhood balanced coloring
\sigma
of a graph. Similarly, let
\sigma(B)
and
\sigma(R)
denote the number of blue and red vertices, respectively, in a neighborhood balanced graph coloring
\sigma
. Freyberg et al.
[
2
]
proved the following results about graphs admitting neighborhood balanced coloring.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
2
]

Suppose a graph
G
admits a neighborhood balanced coloring
\sigma
, then
\sigma(RB)=\frac{|E(G)|}{2}
and
\sigma(RR)=\sigma(BB)=\frac{|E(G)|}{4}
.

Corollary 1.2
.

[
2
]

Suppose
G
is a regular graph on
n
vertices that admits a neighborhood balanced coloring
\sigma
. Then
\sigma(R)=\sigma(B)=\frac{n}{2}
.

Corollary 1.3
.

[
2
]

If
G
is a regular graph on
n
vertices that admits a neighborhood balanced coloring, then
n
is even and
|E(G)|\equiv 0\ (\bmod\ 4)
.

Freyberg et al.
[
2
]
studied the neighborhood balanced coloring of cycle

, the complete graph

, and the complete multipartite graph. They also studied the neighborhood balanced coloring of various graph products.

Theorem 1.4
.

[

If graphs

and

Theorem 1.5
.

[

Let

If

.

Freyberg et al.
[

further completely classified 4-regular circulants that possess neighborhood balanced colorings.

Theorem 1.7

[

A quartic circulant graph

Taking motivation from this, we define a new concept called
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring, for a prime integer
(2k+1)
, as follows:

Definition 1.8
.

Let
G
be a graph with each vertex colored with one of the
(2k+1)
colors, say
R_{1},R_{2},R_{3},\dots,R_{2k+1}
. If every vertex has an equal number of neighbors of each color, then the coloring is a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring.

In other words, a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring is a partition of
V
into
(2k+1)
sets such that each vertex has an equal number of neighbors from each set. Sometimes it is convenient to use
c\colon V\rightarrow\{-k,-(k-1),\dots,-1,0,1,\dots,,k-1,k\}
to denote a
(2k+1)
-coloring with each number representing a different color. Let
w(v)=\sum_{u\in N(v)}c(u)
. Thus, G admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring if and only if
w(v)=0
. Note that if
h^{1}
is a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of graph
G
using colors in the order
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{2k+1}
, then the colorings
h^{2},h^{3},\dots,h^{2k+1}
obtained by rotating the colors in a cyclic order are also
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of the graph
G
.

In this paper, we present some characteristics of graphs that admit
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring. We also study
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of various graph products.

2
Main Results

The following proposition gives a necessary condition for a graph to admit a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring.

Proposition 2.1
.

If a graph
G
admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring, then the degree of every vertex is a multiple of
(2k+1)
.

Let
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
,
\dots
,
R_{2k+1}
be the
(2k+1)
colors. Let
\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})
be the number of
R_{i}R_{j}
edges and
\sigma(R_{i}R_{i})
be the number of
R_{i}R_{i}
edges in a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of a graph
G
. Similarly, let
\sigma(R_{i})
be the number of
R_{i}
colored vertices.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
G
has
n
vertices and admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring. Then for any
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring
\sigma
,

\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})=\frac{2|E(G)|}{(2k+1)^{2}}\ \text{and}\ \sigma(R_{i}R_{i})=\frac{|E(G)|}{(2k+1)^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
stand for the set of
R_{i}
colored vertices, in a coloring of
V
. Consider the bipartite subgraph
H
of
G
induced by the set of
R_{i}R_{j}
edges. Then for any
v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}\cup\mathcal{R}_{j},

d_{H}(v)=\frac{1}{2k+1}d_{G}(v).

Therefore, counting
\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})
by summing degrees over each part of the bipartite graph,

\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\frac{1}{2k+1}d_{G}(v)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{j}}\frac{1}{2k+1}d_{G}(v)

(1)

which implies

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}d_{G}(v)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{j}}d_{G}(v).

Thus,

\displaystyle 2|E(G)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}d_{G}(v)+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}d_{G}(v)+\dots+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{2k+1}}d_{G}(v)

\displaystyle=(2k+1)\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}d_{G}(v)

\displaystyle=(2k+1)^{2}\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})

which proves the first claim of the theorem. As for the second assertion, let
G_{{R}_{i}}
be the subgraph induced by the
R_{i}R_{i}
edges. For all
v\in G_{R_{i}}
,
d_{G_{R_{i}}}(v)=\frac{1}{2k+1}d_{G}(v)
. Therefore, by (
1
) and the first claim of the theorem,

\displaystyle 2\sigma(R_{i}R_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in G_{R_{i}}}d_{G_{R_{i}}}(v)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}\frac{1}{2k+1}d_{G}(v)=\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{2|E(G)|}{(2k+1)^{2}}

Therefore

\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})=\frac{|E(G)|}{(2k+1)^{2}}.

∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose
G
is a regular graph on
n
vertices of degree
r>0
that admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring. Then
\sigma(R_{i})=\dfrac{n}{2k+1}.

Proof.

From (
1
) we have,

\displaystyle\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k+1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{i}}d_{G}(v)=\frac{r\sigma(R_{i})}{2k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2k+1}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{R}_{j}}d_{G}(v)=\frac{r\sigma(R_{j})}{2k+1}

which implies
\sigma(R_{i})=\sigma(R_{j}).

As
\sigma(R_{1})+\sigma(R_{2})+\dots+\sigma(R_{2k+1})=n
, we have
\sigma(R_{i})=\dfrac{n}{2k+1}.

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

If
G
is a regular graph on
n
vertices of degree

that admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring, then
n\equiv 0\bmod(2k+1)
and
|E(G)|=0\bmod(2k+1)^{2}
.

Proof.

Corollary
2.3
gives
n
is a multiple of
(2k+1)
and
|E(G)|=(2k+1)^{2}\sigma(R_{i}R_{j})
from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

The complete graph

admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring if and only if

.

Proof.

If

, any coloring is
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring. So assume

. Suppose a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring exists. Since

is a regular graph of degree

, by Preposition

,

is a multiple of

, but by Corollary

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
p\geq 2
. The complete multipartite graph
G\cong K_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{p}}
admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring if and only if
n_{i}\equiv 0\bmod(2k+1)
for
i=1,2,\dots,p.

Proof.

Since
n_{i}\equiv 0\bmod(2k+1)
, we may color
\frac{n_{i}}{2k+1}
vertices using color
R_{i}
for
i=1,2,\dots,2k+1
. It is easy to see that this is a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of
G
.

On the other hand, suppose
G
admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring and let
R_{j}(i)
be the number of vertices colored
R_{j}
in the
i^{th}
partite set of
G
. Then the equation

\sigma(R_{i})-R_{i}(l)=\sigma(R_{j})-R_{j}(l)

(2)

must be satisfied for every
l=1,2,\dots,p
. Adding these
p
equations gives

(p-1)\sigma(R_{i})=(p-1)\sigma(R_{j}).

Thus
\sigma(R_{i})=\sigma(R_{j})
. It follows from Equation
2
that
R_{i}(l)=R_{j}(l)
for
l=1,2,\dots,p
. Hence, each
n_{i}
must be a multiple of

, and we have completed the proof.
∎

Next, we recall the definitions of graph products. Let graphs
G
and
H
be given. The
cartesian product

G\ \square\ H
is a graph with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and two vertices
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if
u=u^{\prime}
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
or
v=v^{\prime}
and
uu^{\prime}\in E(G).

The
lexicographic product

G[H]
is a graph with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and two vertices
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if either
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
or
u=u^{\prime}
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.

It may be instructive to instead construct
G[H]
by replacing every vertex of
G
with a copy of
H
and then replacing each edge of
G
with a complete bipartite graph between the corresponding copies of
H
.
The
direct product

G\times H
is a graph with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and two vertices
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.

The
strong product

G\ \boxed{\times}\ H
is a graph with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and two vertices
(u,v)
and
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are adjacent if and only if
u=u^{\prime}
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
or
v=v^{\prime}
and
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
or
uu^{\prime}\in E(G)
and
vv^{\prime}\in E(H)
.

One can clearly see that the strong product is the union of the direct product and the cartesian product.

For either of the above products and a fixed vertex
u
of
G
, the set of vertices
\{(u,v):\ v\in V(H)\}
is called an
H
-layer. Similarly, if
v\in V(H)
is fixed, then the set of vertices
\{(u,v):\ u\in V(G)\}
is called a
g
layer. If one constructs
V(G)\times V(H)
in the natural way, the
H
-layers are represented by rows and the
G
-layers are represented by columns.

We further recall the definition of the join of graphs. The
join of graphs

G+H
is a graph having vertex set
V(G)\cup V(H)
and edge set
E(G)\cup E(H)\cup\{xy:x\in V(G)\ \text{and}\ y\in V(H)\}
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
G
and
H
be graphs. If both

and
H
admit
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring, then the lexicographic product

admits a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring.

Proof.

Let
g
and
h^{1}
be the
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of
G
and
H
respectively. Let
h^{1}
be the coloring of
H
that uses colors in the order
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{2k+1}
on the vertices of
H
. Let
h^{2}
be the coloring of
H
that uses colors in the order
R_{2},R_{3},\dots,R_{2k+1},R_{1}
on the vertices of
H
. Similarly, we define colorings
h^{3},h^{4},\dots,h^{2k+1}.
Let
(u,v)\in V(G[H])
. Apply
h^{i}
to the
H
-layer containing
(u,v)
in
G[H]
whenever
g(u)
is
R_{i}
. We may assume that
u
has
p
neighbors of each color in
G
for some integer
p
. Since
h^{1},h^{2},\dots,h^{2k+1}
are
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced coloring of
H
, we know
(u,v)
has an equal number of neighbors with colors
R_{1},R_{2},\dots,R_{2k+1}
within the
H
-layer in which it sits. Therefore, it remains to show that
(u,v)
has an equal number of neighbors of each color outside this
H
-layer.

The number of neighbors of
(u,v)
with color
C_{i}
is
p(\sigma_{h^{1}}(C_{i})+\sigma_{h^{2}}(C_{i})+\dots+\sigma_{h^{2k+1}}(C_{i})
. But

\displaystyle l_{1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{h_{1}}(C_{1})=\sigma_{h_{2}}(C_{2})=\dots=\sigma_{h_{2k+1}}(C_{2k+1})

\displaystyle l_{2}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{h_{1}}(C_{2})=\sigma_{h_{2}}(C_{3})=\dots=\sigma_{h_{2k+1}}(C_{1})

\displaystyle l_{2k+1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{h_{1}}(C_{2k+1})=\sigma_{h_{2}}(C_{1})=\dots=\sigma_{h_{2k+1}}(C_{2k})

So the number of neighbors of
(u,v)
with colors
C_{i}
is equal to the number of neighbors of
(u,v)
with color
C_{j}
for

and is equal to

, and the result follows.
∎

(2k+1)

(2k+1)

A family
\mathcal{F}
of graphs is hereditary if
G\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
H
an induced subgraph of
G
together imply that
H\subseteq\mathcal{F}
. Hereditary classes can be characterized by providing a list of forbidden induced subgraphs. Indeed, a family of graphs is hereditary if and only if it has a forbidden induced subgraph characterization. Next, we show that the class of
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not a hereditary class of graphs. Indeed, there is no graph that is a forbidden induced subgraph for the class of
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graphs.

Theorem 2.16
.

Every graph is an induced subgraph of a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graph.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph and write
V(G)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
. We form the graph
H
as follows. Define the vertex set as
V(H)=\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{2k+1}\{v_{i}^{j}\ :\ 1\leq i\leq n\}
and the edge set as

E(H)=\bigg(\displaystyle\bigcup_{p=1}^{2k+1}\{v_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{p}\ :\ v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\}\bigg)\cup\bigg(\bigcup_{p,q=1,p\not=q}^{2k+1}\{v_{i}^{p}v_{j}^{q}\ :\ v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\}\bigg)
.
Note that
G
is an induced subgraph of
H
. In graph
H
, color each vertex
v_{i}^{j}
with color
R_{j}
, where
1\leq j\leq 2k+1
. Then each vertex of
H
has an equal number of neighbors of every color. So
H
is a
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graph.
∎

Corollary 2.17
.

The class of
(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not hereditary.

-neighborhood balanced colored graph. By Theorem

, there exists a

-neighborhood balanced colored graph

(2k+1)
-neighborhood balanced colored graphs is not hereditary.
∎
