Tcf4 CTG Repeat Expansion Induces RNA Toxicity and MBNL-Associated Splicing Dysregulation in a Mouse Model of Fuchs Endothelial Corneal Dystrophy
Authors: Taichi Yuasa, Hayato Tsutsumi, Naoyuki Ueda, Itsuki Matsuoka, Daiya Nemoto, Suguru Ito, Ayaka Izumi, Yoshinori Oie, Motokazu Tsujikawa, Yuki Oyama, Masahito Ikawa, Noriko Koizumi, Naoki Okumura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo determine whether a Tcf4 CTG repeat knock-in mouse model of Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) recapitulates molecular features of repeat RNA toxicity and MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation in corneal endothelial cells in vivo.MethodsCorneal endothelial tissues from 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice were examined by contact specular microscopy and CUG-repeat RNA FISH with MBNL1 immunostaining. At 60 weeks, we used RT-PCR, quantitative PCR, Western blotting, and RNA-Seq analysis to assess Mbnl autoregulatory splicing, genome-wide alternative splicing, RNA-binding protein motif enrichment, and Tcf4 isoform composition; selected splicing events were compared with human FECD data.Results Tcf4 (CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice showed neither obvious guttae-like abnormalities nor nuclear RNA foci at 20 weeks; both were detected at 40 and 60 weeks, whereas wild-type mice showed neither at any age. Foci-positive nuclei and RNA foci–MBNL1 colocalization did not differ between 40 and 60 weeks. Although Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 transcript levels and MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein levels were unchanged, Mbnl2 exon 6 inclusion was reduced. RNA-Seq reanalysis identified 645 differential alternative splicing events, predominantly skipped exons, with MBNL1 motif enrichment among events with increased exon skipping. Representative MBNL-regulated events were only partially conserved between the mouse model and human FECD. Total Tcf4 expression was unchanged, whereas Tcf4-225 was selectively upregulated.ConclusionsThis model provides in vivo evidence that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion can induce molecular hallmarks of repeat RNA toxicity and downstream MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation, together with selective Tcf4 isoform alteration. It offers an in vivo platform for mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

Fuchs endothelial corneal dystrophy (FECD) is a common, progressive disorder of the corneal endothelium and one of the leading indications for corneal transplantation in older adults.1–4 It is characterized clinically by the formation of guttae, a progressive loss of corneal endothelial cells, and disruption of the regular endothelial mosaic, and it culminates in corneal edema and visual impairment in advanced disease. Although late-onset FECD is clinically and genetically heterogeneous, the predominant genetic risk factor appears to be expansion of an intronic CTG trinucleotide repeat (CTG18.1) within the transcription factor 4 (TCF4) gene, accounting for a substantial proportion of cases across multiple populations.2,5 This genetic finding has placed TCF4 repeat expansion at the center of efforts to define the molecular basis of FECD.2

The noncoding, intronic location of the CTG18.1 expansion has raised the possibility that FECD shares its pathogenic mechanisms with those of other noncoding repeat expansion disorders. For example, in myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1), which is the prototypical CTG/CUG repeat disease, expanded CUG-repeat RNA accumulates as nuclear RNA foci that sequester muscleblind-like (MBNL) splicing regulators, resulting in loss of MBNL activity and widespread dysregulation of MBNL-dependent alternative splicing.6–9 Features consistent with this form of RNA toxicity, including nuclear CUG RNA foci, colocalization of MBNL1 with these foci, and altered splicing of MBNL-regulated transcripts, have been reported in FECD corneal endothelial cells carrying the TCF4 repeat expansion.10–16 Together, these observations support a model in which RNA containing TCF4 repeats contributes to FECD through RNA toxicity and downstream MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation.

Important evidence linking TCF4 repeat expansion to RNA foci formation and splicing abnormalities has emerged from studies of human FECD corneal endothelial tissues10,14–21; however, these findings are difficult to interpret in isolation. Surgical specimens typically represent advanced disease and reflect the combined effects of aging, disease progression, endothelial cell loss, and secondary stresses (e.g., oxidative damage and endoplasmic reticulum stress), as well as genetic and environmental modifiers.2,3 Consequently, human tissue studies alone cannot establish whether TCF4 CTG repeat expansion is itself sufficient to initiate in vivo RNA foci formation and splicing dysregulation in the corneal endothelium. A further complication is that analyses of total TCF4 mRNA expression in FECD have yielded inconsistent results, likely reflecting the complex isoform structure of TCF4 and the limitations of assays that target only selected transcript regions.22–27 Together, these considerations underscore the need for an in vivo model in which the molecular consequences of Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion can be evaluated directly.

To address this need, we recently generated a CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in mouse model that carries 100 CTG repeats within the Tcf4 intron.28 These Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice develop FECD-like corneal endothelial phenotypes, including progressive guttae-like abnormalities and reduced endothelial cell density, indicating that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion on its own is sufficient to induce the key structural features of FECD in vivo. Therefore this model enables a direct examination of the molecular effects of Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion, independently of the clinical and molecular heterogeneity inherent in human surgical specimens. Nevertheless, whether this model recapitulates the molecular hallmarks of repeat RNA toxicity, including nuclear RNA foci formation, MBNL1 recruitment, and MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation, remains unknown.

In the present study, we used the corneal endothelium of our Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice to test whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion induces the molecular features of repeat RNA toxicity in vivo. We examined nuclear RNA foci formation, MBNL1 colocalization, Mbnl autoregulatory splicing, genome-wide alternative splicing profiles, and Tcf4 isoform-level expression and integrated the findings of these analyses. Our goal was to determine whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion is sufficient to induce RNA toxicity and downstream MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation in the corneal endothelium.

Material and Methods

Animal Studies

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of Doshisha University (approval no. A-23011, Kyoto, Japan) and Osaka University (approval no. Biken-AP-H30-01, Osaka, Japan). All procedures conformed to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Recombinant DNA experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional regulations and were approved by the Recombinant DNA Experiment Committee of Doshisha University (approval no. D23011).

Tcf4 (CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice, carrying a knock-in expansion of 100 CTG repeats in the Tcf4 intron, were generated previously using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing, as described elsewhere.28 Wild-type mice were used as controls. All mice were maintained under standard laboratory housing conditions with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle and free access to food and water. Unless otherwise stated, analyses were performed using 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old mice. Mice were obtained or maintained through CLEA Japan, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan), and Shimizu Laboratory Supplies (Kyoto, Japan), as appropriate.

In Vivo Corneal Endothelial Assessment

Corneal endothelial imaging was performed in 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (eight eyes from four wild-type mice and eight eyes from four Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice at each time point). The mice were anesthetized using a small-animal anesthesia system (KN-1071-S; Natsume Seisakusho, Tokyo, Japan) with sevoflurane (Nikko Pharmaceutical, Tokyo, Japan) at 5% for induction and 2.5%–4% for maintenance. After general anesthesia, topical anesthesia was administered using 2 µL of 0.4% oxybuprocaine hydrochloride ophthalmic solution (Benoxil; Santen Pharmaceutical, Osaka, Japan).

Corneal endothelial images were captured using a contact specular microscope (CellCheck C; Konan Medical, Hyogo, Japan). Focused still images were extracted from video recordings according to previously described methods, and panoramic images were generated by merging 100 extracted frames using AutoStitch software, originally developed at the University of British Columbia (Vancouver, BC, Canada).

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization

Corneal endothelial tissues from 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice were analyzed by FISH. After enucleation, the eyeballs were washed with RNase-free water, and the corneoscleral rims were dissected in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The rims were fixed in 4% PFA at room temperature for 10 minutes, washed in RNase-free PBS (AM9624; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and permeabilized with cold ethanol at 4°C for 30 minutes.

Samples were washed in Buffer 1 consisting of 2 × saline-sodium citrate (SSC) and 10% formamide. Prehybridization was performed in Buffer 2 consisting of 2 × SSC, 40% formamide, 0.02% BSA, and 100 mg/mL dextran sulfate. Hybridization was carried out overnight at 37°C in a humidified chamber using Buffer 3, identical in composition to Buffer 2, supplemented with a 5′-FAM–labeled 2′-O-methyl RNA probe targeting CUG-repeat–containing RNA (5′-FAM-CA(CAG)₆; Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA) at a final concentration of 20 ng/µL. Samples were subsequently washed in Buffer 1 prewarmed to 37°C.

After FISH, the flat-mounted specimens were subjected to immunostaining for MBNL1. Samples were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), blocked with 2% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich), and incubated with a primary antibody against MBNL1 (3A4; sc-47740; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) at 1:200 dilution for one hour at 4°C. After washing in PBS, the samples were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 594–conjugated secondary antibody (A-11005; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1:1000 dilution for one hour at room temperature. The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan).

Fluorescence images of corneal endothelial flat mounts were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SPE; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Z-stacks were acquired with a z-step interval of 0.1 µm, and maximum-intensity projections were used for representative images and quantitative analysis. Imaging settings were kept constant between wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 samples within each experiment.

For quantification of the RNA foci, the DAPI-stained nuclei were manually counted in four randomly selected fields per sample. All counting was performed by an investigator blinded to genotype and age. No predefined minimum focus-size threshold was applied; only discrete punctate signals located within DAPI-positive nuclei and clearly distinguishable from background fluorescence were counted as RNA foci. Nuclei containing at least one discrete intranuclear RNA focus in the FISH channel were considered foci-positive. The percentage of foci-positive nuclei was calculated for each field and averaged for each sample. Colocalization between RNA foci and MBNL1 signals was assessed using Fiji (ImageJ; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) with the Coloc 2 plugin. The Manders’ coefficient representing the fraction of RNA foci signal overlapping with MBNL1 signal was calculated after automatic thresholding using the Costes method. Because no RNA foci were detected in wild-type samples, the age-dependent statistical comparisons were restricted to Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 samples. For samples without detectable RNA foci, the Manders’ colocalization coefficient was assigned a value of 0. Each eye was treated as the unit of analysis. The percentage of foci-positive nuclei and Manders’ colocalization coefficients were compared among 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple-comparisons test with Holm adjustment.

RT-PCR and Quantitative PCR Analyses

Corneal endothelial tissues were collected from 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized using ReverTra Ace α (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

RT-PCR was performed to amplify selected transcript regions spanning alternatively spliced exons, including Mbnl1 exon 6, Mbnl2 exon 6, Mbnl2 exon 9, Numa1 exon 15, Ppfibp1 exon 18, Inf2 exon 22, Add3 exon 14, Esrp1 exon 14, Map2 exon 10, Homer1 exon 6, and Pgap2 exon 2. PCR amplification was performed using KOD FX Neo (Toyobo) under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 94°C for two minutes; 32 cycles of 98°C for 10 seconds, 66°C for 30 seconds, and 68°C for 15 seconds; and a final extension at 68°C for 5 minutes. The amplified products were separated by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels in TBE buffer and visualized using an Amersham Imager 600 system (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA). Primer sequences used for the RT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S1.29,30

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using cDNA prepared as described above to assess total mRNA expression in wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelial tissues. The reactions were carried out using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TaqMan Gene Expression Assays on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). TaqMan probes for Tcf4 (Mm00443196_m1) and Gapdh (Mm99999915_g1) were used, with Gapdh serving as the endogenous control. Relative Tcf4 expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot Analysis

Corneal endothelial tissues were harvested from 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. After enucleation, corneal buttons were excised at the corneoscleral junction, and the corneal endothelium with Descemet's membrane was peeled off and collected. The samples were washed in ice-cold saline solution, spun in a centrifuge, and lysed in RIPA buffer (5 µL per endothelium) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (1:100). For each genotype, corneal endothelial tissues from 40 eyes (20 mice) were pooled to generate one biological replicate. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Protein samples (20 µg per lane) were denatured at 95°C, separated on 10% polyacrylamide gels, and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The membranes were blocked with 3% skim milk (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) for one hour and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against MBNL1 (3A4, 1:1000; sc-47740; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), MBNL2 (3B4, 1:1000; sc-136167; Santa Cruz Biotechnology), or GAPDH (3H12, 1:5000; M171-3; MBL, Tokyo, Japan). After washing, the membranes were incubated for one hour at room temperature with an HRP-linked sheep anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (NA931V, 1:5000; Cytiva). The signals were detected using Chemi-Lumi One Ultra (Nacalai Tesque) and an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini system (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan). Because MBNL1, MBNL2, and GAPDH have similar molecular weights, their detection was performed using the same membrane, which was stripped and reprobed sequentially for each antibody.

RNA Sequencing Dataset and Read Processing

RNA-Seq data generated and deposited in our previous study were reanalyzed in the present study. The dataset consisted of corneal endothelial tissue samples from 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. The RNA-Seq data are available in the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE283422.28 The dataset included five biological replicates per genotype, with each RNA-Seq sample derived from one mouse.

For cross-species comparison, previously generated human corneal endothelial RNA-Seq data were also reanalyzed. Raw FASTQ files were obtained from the DNA Data Bank of Japan Sequence Read Archive (DRA; accession numbers DRA01023731 for non-FECD controls and DRA01507832 for FECD samples). The human dataset included seven non-FECD controls and 10 FECD samples, including four FECD samples without TCF4 CTG repeat expansion and six FECD samples with TCF4 CTG repeat expansion.

For the present reanalysis, raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control and adapter trimming using fastp software (v0.20.0).33,34 Cleaned mouse reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm39 using STAR (v2.7.11a)35 with Ensembl GRCm39 release 111 gene annotation. Cleaned human reads were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using STAR (v2.7.11a) with Ensembl GRCh38 release 111 gene annotation. The STAR-aligned output files were used for downstream alternative splicing analysis. Gene- and transcript-level quantification was performed using RSEM (v1.3.1)36 separately for the mouse and human datasets using the corresponding reference genomes and annotations. The RSEM-derived gene-level count data were used for gene expression analysis, whereas transcript-level abundance estimates were used for isoform-level analysis.

Gene-Level Expression Analysis

Gene-level expression analysis was performed using RSEM-derived gene-level count data from wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelial samples. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.50.2)37 with Wald testing, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Differentially expressed genes were defined as genes with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1.0. For Mbnl1 and Mbnl2, the expression levels were visualized as transcripts per million (TPM) values, whereas adjusted P values were obtained from the DESeq2-based gene-level analysis.

Alternative Splicing Analysis

Differential alternative splicing events were identified using rMATS-turbo (v4.3.0)38,39 based on STAR-aligned RNA-Seq data from wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelial samples. Five major classes of alternative splicing events were analyzed: skipped exon (SE), alternative 3′ splice site (A3SS), alternative 5′ splice site (A5SS), mutually exclusive exon (MXE), and retained intron (RI). For each event, the inclusion level difference was calculated between the wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 samples. The P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and events with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute IncLevelDifference > 0.2 were considered significant.

The number of significant events in each splicing category was summarized to characterize the overall distribution of alternative splicing alterations. Genes harboring significant splicing events were compared across the five event types and visualized using UpSetR (v1.4.0) in R (v4.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Principal component analysis was performed using prcomp in R, based on differential alternative splicing events, to evaluate the separation of samples according to genotype. Sashimi plots for Mbnl1 exon 6, Mbnl2 exon 6, Mbnl2 exon 9, Numa1 exon 15, Ppfibp1 exon 18, Inf2 exon 22, Add3 exon 14, Esrp1 exon 14, Map2 exon 10, Homer1 exon 6, and Pgap2 exon 2 were generated using rmats2sashimiplot (v3.0.0).

For cross-species comparison, alternative splicing of orthologous MBNL-regulated events was evaluated in human corneal endothelial RNA-Seq data using rMATS-turbo (v4.3.0). Pairwise comparisons were performed between non-FECD controls and FECD samples without TCF4 CTG repeat expansion, and between non-FECD controls and FECD samples with TCF4 CTG repeat expansion. The same significance criteria used for the mouse analysis were applied to the human dataset. The analyzed events were NUMA1 exon 16, PPFIBP1 exon 19, INF2 exon 22, and ADD3 exon 14. Sashimi plots were generated using rmats2sashimiplot (v3.0.0). The corresponding mouse events (Numa1 exon 15, Ppfibp1 exon 18, Inf2 exon 22, and Add3 exon 14) were selected for RT-PCR validation in 60-week-old mice. For additional validation, Esrp1 exon 14 and Map2 exon 10 were selected as the two top-ranked SE events showing increased exon inclusion, whereas Homer1 exon 6 and Pgap2 exon 2 were selected as the two top-ranked SE events showing increased exon skipping. Selection was limited to events for which RT-PCR primer design was feasible.

For SE events, the potential effects on protein features were assessed using NEASE (v2.0).40 This analysis evaluated whether alternatively spliced exons were predicted to affect annotated protein features, including protein domains and amino acid residues. The events were classified according to whether they were predicted to affect protein features, and affected features were further categorized by feature type.

Candidate RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) associated with the observed splicing alterations were identified using motif enrichment analysis and rMAPS2 (v2.2.0).41 Significant SE events were analyzed separately according to the direction of the splicing change (i.e., an increased exon inclusion or increased exon skipping) in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. Enrichment of RBP motifs around regulated exons and flanking intronic regions was evaluated using default rMAPS2 settings. The results were visualized as heatmaps using ggplot2 (v4.0.3) in R.

TCF4/Tcf4 Isoform Expression Analysis

For isoform-level analysis, RSEM-derived transcript-level data from the mouse and human RNA-Seq datasets were analyzed in R. Transcript-level differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 with Wald testing, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For the human dataset, differential expression was assessed between non-FECD controls and FECD samples with TCF4 CTG repeat expansion. Differentially expressed TCF4/Tcf4 isoforms were defined as transcripts with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1.0.

Row-scaled expression values were visualized using gplots::heatmap.2 (v3.3.0) with RColorBrewer (v1.1.3). Row clustering was performed using Euclidean distance and Ward's method. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2, and differentially expressed isoforms were labeled using ggrepel (v0.9.8).

For isoform composition analysis, RSEM-derived isoforms.results files were used to obtain TPM values. For the mouse dataset, the five most abundant Tcf4 isoforms based on mean TPM across all samples were displayed individually. In addition, Tcf4-225 (ENSMUST00000201091), the only Tcf4 isoform that met the predefined criteria for differential expression, was displayed separately. All remaining Tcf4 isoforms were grouped as “Other.” Relative isoform composition was visualized as a 100% stacked bar plot using ggplot2 with geom_col(position = "fill") and facet_grid by genotype. Data processing was performed using dplyr (v1.2.1) and stringr (v1.6.0).

Isoform Structure Comparison

The exon and coding sequence (CDS) organizations of Tcf4-225 and human TCF4-272 (ENST00000630720) were analyzed using Ensembl release 111 GTF annotations for GRCm39 and GRCh38 with rtracklayer (v1.70.1) in R. The numbers and lengths of exons and CDS blocks, as well as the predicted protein length, were extracted for each isoform. Schematic diagrams of the Tcf4 and TCF4 loci, transcript structures, and exon-derived organization of the predicted protein products were generated using ggtranscript (v1.0.0)42 and ggplot2 (v4.0.3).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R. Bar graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism (v11.0.1), whereas all other plots were generated using R. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise stated. Comparisons of qPCR-based gene expression between two groups were performed using Welch's t-test. Age-dependent comparisons of the percentage of foci-positive nuclei and Manders’ colocalization coefficients were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's multiple-comparisons test with Holm adjustment.

For RNA-Seq-based analyses, statistical significance was determined according to the criteria defined for each analysis. Differential alternative splicing events were considered significant at an FDR < 0.05 and an absolute IncLevelDifference > 0.2. Gene-level and isoform-level differential expression analyses were performed using Wald testing, and P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Differentially expressed genes and isoforms were defined as those with an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1.0. A P value or adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Nuclear RNA Foci Formation and MBNL1 Colocalization in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100

To determine whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion alone is sufficient to induce nuclear RNA foci formation in corneal endothelial cells in vivo, and to assess whether this phenotype changes with age, we examined corneal endothelial morphology and CUG-repeat RNA foci in 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. Contact specular microscopy showed age-dependent emergence of guttae-like endothelial abnormalities in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. No obvious guttae-like abnormalities were observed at 20 weeks; lesions were detectable at 40 weeks and more evident at 60 weeks. In contrast, age-matched wild-type mice showed no obvious guttae formation throughout the observation period (Fig. 1A).

We next performed fluorescence in situ hybridization for CUG-repeat-containing RNA combined with MBNL1 immunostaining in corneal endothelial flat mounts. Discrete nuclear RNA foci were detected in corneal endothelial cells from Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice at 40 and 60 weeks of age, but not at 20 weeks or in age-matched wild-type mice at any age (Fig. 1B). MBNL1 signals showed marked colocalization with these RNA foci at both ages (Fig. 1B). Additional representative FISH images demonstrating RNA foci formation and colocalization with MBNL1 in 20-, 40-, and 60-week-old Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice are provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Quantitative analysis showed no foci-positive nuclei in 20-week-old Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 corneal endothelium, whereas the mean percentages of foci-positive nuclei were 1.75% at 40 weeks and 2.02% at 60 weeks. No foci-positive nuclei were detected in wild-type corneal endothelium at any age (Fig. 1C). The Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test with Holm adjustment showed that the percentage of foci-positive nuclei was significantly higher at both 40 and 60 weeks than at 20 weeks (adjusted P = 2.09 × 10−3 and 8.25 × 10−4, respectively), whereas the difference between 40 and 60 weeks was not significant (adjusted P = 7.19 × 10−1).

The Manders’ colocalization coefficient for RNA foci and MBNL1 signals was assigned a value of 0 at 20 weeks because RNA foci were not detected, and the mean coefficients were 0.794 at 40 weeks and 0.816 at 60 weeks, indicating a high degree of spatial overlap between RNA foci and MBNL1 at the latter two ages (Fig. 1D). The Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test with Holm adjustment showed significantly higher coefficients at 40 and 60 weeks than at 20 weeks (adjusted P = 2.36 × 10−3 and 7.12 × 10−4, respectively), whereas the difference between 40 and 60 weeks was not significant (adjusted P = 6.65 × 10−1). These findings indicate that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion is sufficient to induce nuclear RNA foci formation and MBNL1 recruitment in corneal endothelial cells in vivo by 40 weeks of age, and that this phenotype is maintained at 60 weeks.

Altered Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 Autoregulatory Splicing

Because MBNL-dependent splicing dysregulation is a major downstream consequence of expanded CUG-repeat RNA in repeat expansion disorders,43,44 we next examined whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion altered Mbnl autoregulatory splicing in mouse corneal endothelial cells. MBNL proteins are known to regulate alternative splicing of their own pre-mRNAs, and our previous study showed an altered inclusion of MBNL1 exon 6, MBNL2 exon 6, and MBNL2 exon 9 in human FECD corneal endothelial cells with TCF4 trinucleotide repeat expansion.10,12,15,16 Therefore we evaluated Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 expression and the corresponding autoregulatory splicing events in corneal endothelial tissues from 60-week-old wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice.

RNA-Seq analysis showed no significant difference in Mbnl1 expression between wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (wild-type: 71.1 ± 49.3 TPM vs. Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100: 41.9 ± 12.4 TPM; adjusted P = 4.11 × 10−1). Likewise, Mbnl2 expression did not differ significantly between the two groups (wild-type: 68.1 ± 12.9 TPM vs. Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100: 52.8 ± 11.5 TPM; adjusted P = 6.12 × 10−1) (Fig. 2A). In agreement with the RNA-Seq expression data, Western blot analysis revealed no apparent change in the MBNL1 or MBNL2 protein levels between the two groups (Fig. 2B).

We then examined alternative splicing of Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 using RNA-Seq data. Sashimi plot analysis showed no apparent difference in Mbnl1 exon 6 inclusion between wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (Fig. 2C). In contrast, decreased inclusion of Mbnl2 exon 6 was observed in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (Fig. 2D), whereas Mbnl2 exon 9 inclusion did not markedly differ between the two groups (Fig. 2E). To validate the RNA-Seq–detected splicing patterns, we performed RT-PCR analysis of Mbnl1 exon 6, Mbnl2 exon 6, and Mbnl2 exon 9. Consistent with the Sashimi plot analysis, the RT-PCR data showed no apparent difference in Mbnl1 exon 6 inclusion, decreased inclusion of Mbnl2 exon 6, and no marked difference in Mbnl2 exon 9 inclusion in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice when compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 2F–H). Collectively, these data indicate that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion is associated with a selective alteration of Mbnl2 autoregulatory splicing without apparent changes in total MBNL1 or MBNL2 expression.

Genome-Wide Alternative Splicing Dysregulation in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 Mouse Corneal Endothelium

Because MBNL-dependent splicing abnormalities can extend beyond individual autoregulatory exons, we next asked whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion was associated with broader alterations in the corneal endothelial splicing landscape. We comprehensively evaluated alternative splicing alterations in wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice by performing rMATS analysis using RNA-Seq data from corneal endothelial tissues of 60-week-old mice.

Five major classes of alternative splicing events were analyzed: SE, A3SS, A5SS, MXE, and RI. In total, 645 differential alternative splicing events were identified among the five event types (SE: 311, A3SS: 92, A5SS: 76, MXE: 38, RI: 128). SE was the most frequent event type (Fig. 4A).

We then examined the overlap among genes affected by each class of alternative splicing event using an UpSet plot. SE-only events accounted for the largest single-event category, involving 208 genes (Fig. 4B). Principal component analysis based on alternative splicing profiles showed distinct clustering between wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (Fig. 4C). These results indicated that Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelium exhibits a global splicing profile distinct from that of wild-type corneal endothelium, with SE events representing the predominant class of altered splicing events.

Because SE events represented the predominant class of differential alternative splicing alterations, we used NEASE to assess whether the significant SE events identified by rMATS were predicted to affect annotated protein features. Although most differential SE events were not predicted to affect annotated protein features, 6% were predicted to affect these features (Fig. 4D). Among the events predicted to affect protein features, 70.0% involved protein domains and 30.0% involved amino acid residues.

We then performed RBP motif enrichment analysis to identify candidate regulators associated with these SE alterations. The SE events were divided according to the direction of splicing change, namely increased exon inclusion or increased exon skipping in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. In SE events with increased exon inclusion, motifs corresponding to IGF2BP, PCBP2, PTB, SRSF10, and U2AF2 were enriched (Fig. 4E). In contrast, in SE events with increased exon skipping, we observed enrichment of eight RBP motifs, including a motif corresponding to MBNL1 (Fig. 4F). These results identified MBNL1 as a candidate RBP associated with increased exon skipping events in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelium.

To experimentally validate representative SE events beyond the Mbnl autoregulatory exons and orthologous events described above, we selected the two top-ranked events with increased exon inclusion (Esrp1 exon 14 and Map2 exon 10) and the two top-ranked events with increased exon skipping (Homer1 exon 6 and Pgap2 exon 2) among events for which RT-PCR primers could be designed. Sashimi plots showed increased inclusion of Esrp1 exon 14 and Map2 exon 10 and increased skipping of Homer1 exon 6 and Pgap2 exon 2, and RT-PCR confirmed the direction of these RNA-Seq-based changes (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Selective Tcf4 Isoform-Level Alteration Without a Change in Total Tcf4 Expression

Previous studies in human FECD have reported inconsistent changes in total TCF4 mRNA expression, whereas RNA-Seq-based analyses have suggested that TCF4 repeat expansion may affect transcript usage in an isoform-dependent manner.45 We determined whether Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion similarly altered the Tcf4 transcript composition in this mouse model by first evaluating total Tcf4 mRNA expression in 60-week-old mice. The relative Tcf4 expression normalized to Gapdh did not differ significantly between wild-type and Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (P = 6.57 × 10−1; Fig. 5A), indicating that repeat expansion did not produce a detectable change in total Tcf4 transcript abundance.

We then examined isoform-level Tcf4 expression using RNA-Seq data. Heatmap visualization of detected Tcf4 isoforms showed variability in isoform expression across individual samples (Fig. 5B). Isoform-level differential expression analysis was then performed using DESeq2. In the volcano plot, most of the Tcf4 isoforms did not exceed the significance thresholds of adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change > 1.0. Tcf4-225 was the only isoform that met these criteria and was upregulated in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice (Fig. 5C).

Finally, TPM-based isoform composition was evaluated to determine how individual isoforms contributed to the overall Tcf4 transcript pool. The five most abundant Tcf4 isoforms were displayed individually, and Tcf4-225 was additionally displayed because it was the only isoform that met the predefined criteria for differential expression. All remaining isoforms were grouped as “Other.” The composition plot showed that the relative contribution of Tcf4 isoforms varied among individual samples (Fig. 5D). Tcf4-225 was barely detectable in wild-type samples but was observed in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice. Although Tcf4-225 accounted for only a small fraction of the total Tcf4 transcript pool, its selective increase was consistent with the isoform-level differential expression analysis. Together, these results indicate that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion does not markedly alter total Tcf4 expression but is associated with a selective isoform-level alteration in mouse corneal endothelial cells.

We next examined the structural organization of the selectively upregulated Tcf4-225 isoform. A locus-level schematic showed the position of Tcf4-225 within the mouse Tcf4 locus and the CTG repeat insertion site (Fig. 5E). The Tcf4-225 transcript contained eight exons and was predicted to encode a 271-amino-acid protein comprising 8 exon-derived segments (Fig. 5F). The coding exon lengths of Tcf4-225 were 69, 106, 134, 133, 68, 79, 74, and 150 nucleotides (Supplementary Table S2).

In the human RNA-Seq dataset, TCF4-272 was significantly upregulated in FECD samples with TCF4 CTG repeat expansion compared with non-FECD controls (Supplementary Data S5). To place mouse Tcf4-225 in the context of human TCF4 isoform structure, we compared it with human TCF4-272. Human TCF4-272 contained nine exons and was predicted to encode a 315-amino-acid protein (Supplementary Fig. S4). The first seven coding exons had identical lengths in human TCF4-272 and mouse Tcf4-225 (69, 106, 134, 133, 68, 79, and 74 nucleotides). However, the terminal coding region differed: human TCF4-272 contained two terminal coding exons, whereas mouse Tcf4-225 contained one, resulting in a longer C-terminal region in the human isoform. These findings indicate that human TCF4-272 and mouse Tcf4-225 share broadly similar coding exon organization, with species- and isoform-specific differences in the terminal coding region (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

This study provides in vivo evidence that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion can induce molecular features of repeat RNA toxicity in corneal endothelial cells. In a genetically defined Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse model, we identified nuclear RNA foci, MBNL1 recruitment, and alternative splicing dysregulation, together with selective Tcf4 isoform-level alteration. These findings extend previous observations in human FECD tissues by demonstrating that CTG repeat expansion at the Tcf4 locus is sufficient to trigger these molecular abnormalities in the corneal endothelium in vivo.

Nuclear RNA foci are a hallmark molecular feature of CTG18.1 expansion-positive FECD.10,11,13–15 Mootha et al.11 reported abundant CUG RNA nuclear foci in endothelial keratoplasty specimens from patients with advanced FECD carrying the CTG18.1 expansion, with foci-positive cells ranging from 33% to 88%. By comparison, foci-positive nuclei accounted for only 1.75% and 2.02% of the nuclei in 40- and 60-week-old Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelium, respectively, representing a substantially lower foci burden than that reported in advanced human FECD. RNA foci were already detectable at 40 weeks, and neither the proportion of foci-positive nuclei nor the degree of MBNL1 colocalization differed significantly between 40 and 60 weeks, indicating that the association between RNA foci and MBNL1 is established relatively early and remains stable across this interval. The lower foci burden relative to human FECD may reflect several factors, including species differences, repeat length and genomic context, disease stage, tissue sampling, and FISH-based quantification. Repeat-length dynamics may be particularly relevant, because CTG18.1 expansion shows somatic instability, and age- and tissue-dependent changes in repeat length have been proposed to contribute to FECD progression.46–49 In humans, this somatic expansion accumulates over decades, far exceeding the interval between 40 and 60 weeks examined here. Thus the low and stable foci burden in our model may reflect an early disease stage with limited opportunity for age-related repeat instability, rather than the advanced, long-standing disease typical of human surgical specimens. Nonetheless, RNA foci were absent in wild-type corneal endothelium but readily detectable in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mice at both ages and showed marked colocalization with MBNL1. Thus, although this model does not quantitatively reproduce the high foci burden of advanced human FECD, it provides qualitative in vivo evidence that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion itself is sufficient to induce detectable MBNL1-associated nuclear RNA foci in corneal endothelial cells.

The colocalization of MBNL1 with nuclear RNA foci is consistent with a link between repeat RNA aggregation and downstream splicing dysregulation. In DM1 and related repeat expansion disorders, expanded CUG-repeat RNA recruits MBNL proteins into nuclear foci and disrupts MBNL-dependent pre-mRNA splicing.6–9 A similar mechanism has been proposed in FECD, whereby human corneal endothelial cells with TCF4 repeat expansion show altered MBNL autoregulatory splicing, including events in MBNL1 exon 6, MBNL2 exon 6, and MBNL2 exon 9.10,12,15,16 In the present mouse model, the total Mbnl1 and Mbnl2 transcript levels and MBNL1 and MBNL2 protein levels were not detectably altered, whereas Mbnl2 exon 6 inclusion was selectively reduced. Rather than reflecting broad MBNL depletion, this pattern indicates that Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion is sufficient to induce a detectable alteration in Mbnl autoregulatory splicing in corneal endothelial cells in vivo.

Consistent with this, our current analysis revealed a splicing profile in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 corneal endothelium that was distinct from wild-type, with skipped exon events predominating. Among exons with increased skipping, an MBNL1-binding motif was enriched. Together with the selective alteration of Mbnl2 exon 6, this motif enrichment supports the possibility of early or partial impairment of MBNL-associated splicing regulation in this model. However, MBNL1 motif enrichment was observed in only a subset of the differential splicing events, whereas motifs corresponding to several other RNA-binding proteins, including PTB and SRSF family members, were also enriched. Therefore the 645 differential alternative splicing events cannot be attributed solely to MBNL dysfunction and may also reflect the involvement of other RNA-binding proteins and secondary transcriptional or splicing regulatory responses. Accordingly, these findings support MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation but do not establish functional MBNL loss or a direct causal relationship between MBNL sequestration and the genome-wide splicing changes. Because the RNA-Seq analysis was performed on bulk corneal endothelial tissue, the present study cannot determine whether the observed splicing alterations occur preferentially in foci-positive cells or reflect broader cellular responses associated with Tcf4 repeat expansion. Although most skipped exon events were not predicted to affect annotated protein features, a subset involved protein domains or amino acid residues, suggesting potential functional consequences. Cross-species comparison showed only partial conservation of individual splicing events. Some changes observed in human FECD were absent or differed in direction in mice, indicating that the model captures the broader MBNL-associated splicing phenotype rather than the full human pattern. Taken together, these findings provide in vivo evidence that TCF4/Tcf4 repeat expansion itself can perturb MBNL-associated splicing regulation in corneal endothelial cells while also highlighting important differences from human FECD.

The unchanged total Tcf4 expression, together with the selective upregulation of Tcf4-225, suggests that the transcript-level effects of TCF4/Tcf4 repeat expansion are better captured by isoform-level analysis than by total gene expression alone. Previous studies of human FECD have reported increased, unchanged, or decreased total TCF4 mRNA, likely reflecting differences in assay design and the complex isoform structure of TCF4.22–25 More recent analyses based on RNA-Seq have shown that TCF4 repeat expansion is associated with isoform-dependent transcript dysregulation and altered exon usage in human FECD corneal endothelial cells.24,45,50 In the present model, Tcf4-225 was selectively upregulated, whereas human studies have implicated other TCF4 isoforms, such as TCF4-277, in association with the repeat expansion.45 Although Tcf4-225 is a low-abundance transcript, its selective increase was consistent across both the differential expression and isoform composition analyses and may reflect a cis-acting effect of the repeat on local Tcf4 transcript usage. Structural comparison with human TCF4-272 revealed a related coding exon organization but species- and isoform-specific differences in the terminal region. The biological function of Tcf4-225 has not been established, and the present data do not determine whether its upregulation is disease-promoting, compensatory, or functionally neutral. Moreover, because the TCF4/Tcf4 isoform repertoire differs between humans and mice, the selective increase in Tcf4-225 may represent a species-specific consequence of CTG repeat expansion rather than a direct counterpart of the isoform alterations reported in human FECD. These observations should not be interpreted as a direct isoform-by-isoform reproduction of human FECD; rather, they support the broader concept that CTG repeat expansion at the TCF4/Tcf4 locus can alter transcript composition without necessarily changing total TCF4/Tcf4 expression.

This study has several limitations. First, the frequency of foci-positive nuclei in Tcf4(CTG)100/(CTG)100 mouse corneal endothelium was lower than that reported in advanced human FECD, indicating that this model does not quantitatively reproduce the foci burden of late-stage disease. Second, the homozygous mouse model differs from human FECD in repeat length, zygosity, species-specific transcript structure, and aging background. Third, although selected splicing alterations were validated by RT-PCR and supported by motif analysis, most genome-wide alternative splicing events were not experimentally confirmed, and their functional and disease-relevant consequences remain to be defined. Finally, the colocalization of MBNL1 with RNA foci and the enrichment of an MBNL1 motif support MBNL involvement but do not directly establish functional MBNL sequestration or a causal role in the observed splicing changes. Future studies incorporating long-read RNA-Seq, repeat-length analysis in the corneal endothelium, and MBNL-targeted rescue or perturbation experiments will help clarify how Tcf4 repeat expansion contributes to FECD pathogenesis.

Conclusions

Tcf4 CTG repeat expansion can induce molecular hallmarks of repeat RNA toxicity in mouse corneal endothelial cells in vivo, including nuclear RNA foci and MBNL1 recruitment, together with MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation and selective Tcf4 isoform-level alteration. These findings support RNA toxicity and downstream MBNL-associated splicing dysregulation as molecular consequences of the repeat expansion and establish this model as a mechanistically informative in vivo platform for future therapeutic studies.
