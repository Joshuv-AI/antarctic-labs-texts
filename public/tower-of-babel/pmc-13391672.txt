Prevalence of HCV and HIV infections and evaluation of ALT Levels in Patients with transfusion-dependent thalassemia: a cross-sectional study from Nanning, China
Authors: Bin Li, Linbin Huang, Xipeng Yan, Xinwei Wang, Jinlian Li, Rongji Lai, Mingshuang Lai, He Xie, Jujun Sun, Baoren He, Limin Chen, Shilin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) patients encounter a substantial threat of transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs), specifically hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), stemming from their chronic dependence on blood transfusions. Consequently, ensuring the safety and availability of clinical blood resources is paramount. To assess the prevalence of HCV and HIV within the TDT patients in Nanning, China, and to evaluate the efficacy of the “Three-Designated” transfusion model (Designated Locations, Designated Patients, and Designated Schedules) for TTD patients in safeguarding blood safety and supply, we collected 766 demographic information and 546 blood specimens from TDT patients across nine hospitals in Nanning between July 2020 and March 2021. We screened for anti-HCV antibodies, HIV-1/2 antibodies, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels. Our findings revealed that all specimens were negative for HCV and HIV antibodies, Abnormal ALT(≥ 50 U/L) stood at 19.05% (104/546), no statistically significant variations in abnormality rates were observed regarding sex, ABO blood type groups, birth of years groups, or hospitals. This study suggesting that the “Three-Designated” transfusion model successfully ensured an “adequate, safe, and effective” blood supply and markedly reduces TTI risks among TDT patient.

Introduction

Thalassemia, a hereditary disorder of hemoglobin, arises from mutations in the α- or β-globin genes, leading to ineffective erythropoiesis and persistent anemia. Clinically, it is classified into two types: non-transfusion-dependent thalassemia (NTDT) [1] and transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) [2]. The latter requires continuous red blood cell transfusions throughout the patient’s life to ensure survival [3] and to prevent complications, such as skeletal deformities and growth retardation [4, 5]. TDT includes conditions such as β-thalassemia major and HbE/β-thalassemia. While transfusions are critical for life support, patients received blood transfusions before 2015 faced a significant risk of acquiring transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) [3, 6], particularly hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) [6], due to inadequate donor screening protocol and suboptimal viral detection methods. Fortunately, since 2015, the implementation of a voluntary donation system in China coupled with universal nucleic acid testing (NAT) for blood donors [7] have substantially minimized the risk of TDT patients contracting TTIs to a very low level [8].

The prevalence of thalassemia gene abnormality in the general population of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region ranges from 20.0% to 25.0%, making it the region with the highest incidence in China [9]. Furthermore, Guangxi is recognized as a hyperendemic zone for β-thalassemia within southern China, with a carrier rate of 6.34% [10]. This condition shows notable ethnic and geographic variations in carrier rates and mutation types [10]. The autosomal recessive nature of the disorder means there is a 25% probability of having children with TDT when both parents are carriers [11], Such children typically present with β-thalassemia major (β-TM) [12]. This condition requires lifelong blood transfusions unless hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is performed [13, 14]. Although prenatal prevention initiatives in Guangxi have achieved remarkable success—evidenced by the increase in rates of fetal interventions for severe thalassemia from 39.04% in 2010 to 98.88% in 2019—cases of persistent TDT remain [9]. This ongoing issue can be attributed to the region’s ethnic genetic diversity, particularly c.-103_-100delAAAC, c.124_127delTCTT and c.316–197 C > T [15], as well as limitations of first-generation screening technologies and inadequate compliance with premarital screening protocols. Consequently, patients with TDT continue to endure lifelong complications, including iron overload and hepatic dysfunction [16], thereby highlighting the urgent need for improved transfusion safety strategies [3].

HCV and HIV are RNA viruses [17]. Extensive research has documented the global incidence and patterns of TTIs [18], which include HCV, HIV, and various other pathogens. Patients diagnosed with TDT are at increased risk of acquiring TTIs and developing liver damage [6]. Blood transfusions have potential to transmit HCV and HIV [19]. Previous studies indicate that the prevalence of HCV infection among individuals with TDT or β-TM exhibits a wide global range, spanning from as low as 1.7% in certain regions of Iran [20] to as high as 69% in Egypt [21]. However, the prevalence of HIV infection in TDT patients remains insufficiently studied. Blood screening techniques vary significantly between developed and developing countries. Although NAT technology has been implemented for donor screening since 2015 in China [22], the window period—the interval between infection and the appearance of detectable markers—still hinders complete elimination of transfusion-transmissible infection risks. Patients with TDT who receive frequent blood transfusions are particularly vulnerable to HCV and HIV infections due to repeated exposure [19], despite enhanced screening efforts. Furthermore, the serological profile of recipients may indirectly reflect the safety of the blood supply.

Similar to those in developed countries, China has established a voluntary blood donation framework where all clinical blood is procured exclusively from voluntary and unpaid donors [23]. Blood collection and supply centers operate as non-profit organizations tasked with maintaining a sufficient supply of blood. In addition to their primary role, these entities engage in research focused on blood safety [24], utilizing various omics technologies, such as genomics and proteomics, to identify TTIs [25]. To meet the blood supply needs and ensure the safety of patients with TDT patients, Nanning, Guangxi, has instituted a “Three-Designated” transfusion model for TTD patients. This strategy ensures a reliable blood supply from blood banks to hospitals that cater to TDT patients. The model comprises three critical components: “Designated Locations”, “Designated Patients” and “Designated Schedules”. Specifically, “Designated Locations”: Designated hospitals assess the transfusion needs of TDT patients, review their eligibility for blood use, and then submit “Thalassemia-specific Blood Use Applications” to blood bank. The blood bank implement a targeted allocation of blood specifically for TDT patients to these designated hospitals. “Designated Patients”: TDT patients must complete Registration Procedures at the blood bank before transfusion and establish medical records at the hospitals (designated locations) to ensure that the thalassemia-specific blood is used for them. Except under exceptional circumstances, hospitals cannot allocate this blood to unregistered TDT patients or other clinical patients. “Designated Schedules”: hospitals (designated locations) must administer regular transfusions to TDT patients based on clinical indications to sustain optimal health and quality of life. “Application Forms for Blood Use to TDT Patients” must be submitted to the blood bank 10 to 15 days in advance, detailing the TDT patient’s registration code, blood type and blood transfusion volume. To ensure the blood supply for TDT patients, blood banks formulate a monthly “Thalassemia Blood Usage Plan” based on “Application forms for blood use to TDT patients” and current blood inventory level. Blood bank distribute blood to different hospitals at “designated schedules”. However, usually the blood is sent to different hospitals at different times to prevent surges in demand from causing inventory strain and blood type shortages. “Three-Designated” transfusion model for TTD patients is also key aspects of our work in ensuring blood supply and blood safty for TTD patients. It is also a model pioneered in China to date.This approach is aimed at enhancing the effectiveness of repeated transfusions for TDT patients.

This study aims to

(1) Characterize the demographic characteristics (age, sex, ABO blood type) of TDT patients in Nanning; (2) Determine the seroprevalence of HCV and HIV; (3) Identify predictors of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) abnormalities; and (4) Evaluate the efficacy of “Three-Designated” transfusion model for TTD patients in ensuring transfusion safety.

Materials and methods

Study participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted from July 2020 to March 2021. It involved transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) participants registered across nine designated medical institutions (hospitals) in Nanning City, Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China. The inclusion criteria required participants to have a confirmed diagnosis of TDT and a history of regular transfusions. A total of 766 registered TDT participants were enrolled across the nine hospitals during the study period. Of these participants, 546 provided blood samples. Using structured questionnaires, demographic data including sex, birth year, and ABO blood type were collected. Blood samples (5-10mL) were collected under sterile conditions using EDTA tubes. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1500 g for 20 min and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Prior to data and sample collection, the written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Additionally, the study received ethical approval from the Nanning Blood Center (Ethics approval number: 202001).

Viral infection detection

Anti-HCV and HIV serological tests

The detection of anti-HCV and HIV-1/2 antibodies was conducted using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. Specifically, the anti-HCV assay was performed with the InTec ELISA kit (InTec PRODUCTS, INC., Xiamen, China; Lot No. 2020055810). The HIV-1/2 antibodies were assessed using the Bio-Rad ELISA kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, France; Lot No. OD0493). Automated sample processing was carried out with the Hamilton STAR 1256 automated liquid handling system (Hamilton Company, Switzerland) to ensure precise reagent and sample dispensing. The FAME 3848 automated enzyme immunoassay analyzer (Hamilton Company, Switzerland) managed incubation, plate washing, substrate addition, and optical density measurement, which standardized the procedures and enhanced reproducibility. The use of the automated ELISA systems, STAR 1256 and FAME 3848, minimized human error in high-throughput serological testing.

ALT testing

ALT levels were measured using the Roche P800 biochemical analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Germany). Plasma samples with ALT values exceeding 50 U/L were categorized as abnormal. This threshold is established to indicate hepatic injury and to determine temporary ineligibility for blood donation among voluntary donors.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables, such as sex and blood type, were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variable ALT, if the Shapiro-Wilk test confirms a non-normal distribution (P < 0.05), it should be described using “median (interquartile range)”. For comparisons among multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis H test is used. If the difference is statistically significant, the Mann-Whitney U test is further employed for pairwise comparisons, and the p-value is corrected using the Bonferroni method. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., USA).

Results

Blood samples were collected from 766 TDT patients across 9 hospitals, yielding 546 valid test results. All samples tested negative for HIV and HCV antibodies. ALT levels (≥ 50 U/L) were observed in 19.05% (104/546) of the blood samples. No significant differences were observed among gender, blood group, birth year cohorts and hospital groups (Table 1; Figs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6).

Discussion

Due to the requirement for frequent blood transfusions, TDT patients are at a higher risk of TTIs, especially HCV infection [6]. HCV infection rates among thalassemia patients vary significantly across different regions, largely because of differences in blood screening practices and the quality of blood collection agencies [6, 26]. This study shows that Nanning City has successfully lowered the risk of HCV and HIV infections by implementing “Three-Designated” transfusion model for TTD patients. ALT is recognized as a significant biomarker indicative of liver injury or inflammation [27]. TDT patients increases in ALT levels can be attributed to several factors: (1) Iron overload [28]: The elevation of ALT levels is associated with both the frequency and volume of blood transfusions in TDT patients. Chronic transfusion therapy results in the accumulation of iron, which lead to hepatocellular injury [29]. The implementation of iron chelation therapy is essential for the prevention or reversal of iron deposition in organs; (2) HCV infection [30]: Previous exposure to HCV or the presence of occult HCV infection (OCI) [31] may lead to sustained increases in ALT levels among TDT patients; (3) Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [32]: Although the prevalence of HBV among Chinese TDT patients is often underreported [33], individuals in high-risk categories—such as those receiving hemodialysis—are more susceptible to HBV infection due to frequent exposure to blood products. Additionally, ongoing risks remain from mother-to-child transmission and iatrogenic spread of HBV in China; (4) Other viral infections: Infections with Torque Teno virus (TTV) may arise from repeated transfusions. While the clinical significance of TTV infection is not clearly defined, various TTV genotypes have been identified in TDT patients [34]. Additionally, some transfusion-transmitted pathogens are not routinely screened in Chinese blood donors such as mosquito-borne pathogens, including Dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) [35]. These pathogens can also cause liver inflammation leading to increased ALT levels in TDT patients. The complex interplay of these factors highlights the necessity for thorough monitoring in the management of TDT.

TDT patients require sufficient, safe, and effective blood to sustain their lives and well-being. The ultimate goals of clinical blood management for TDT patients are to extend patient lifespan, reduce symptom burden, improve transfusion compliance, decrease transfusion dependency, enhance patient quality of life, and increase social care. The “greatest risk” for TDT patients in transfusion is “blood unavailable” followed by TTIs and then transfusion-related immune reactions. The proportion of blood transfusions for TDT patients in 2020 and 2021 was 10.43% (21,449.5U/205,618.5U) and 10.69% (23,760.0U/222,338.5U), respectively, which largely satisfies the transfusion needs of 766 TDT patients. No HIV or HCV positivity was detected in any tested samples, indicating an absence of transfusion-transmitted HIV or HCV events among local TDT patients. The “Three-Designated” transfusion model for TDT patients is a key aspect of our work in ensuring blood supply and blood safety for TDT patients. It is also a model pioneered in China to date.

Results from our current study indicated that 19.05% of patients with TDT patients presented with elevated ALT levels (≥ 50 U/L), consistent with the globally reported prevalence of hepatic injury ranging from 20% to 40% within TDT cohorts [36]. Notably, despite the lack of statistical significance, the G1 cohort (born before 2000) and the G5 cohort (born from 2016 to 2021) exhibited high ALT levels [G1: 100.00 (68.00-107.50); G5: 80.50 (59.50-148.50)]. Findings in the G1 cohort align with existing literature, showing a positive correlation between ALT elevation and the number of blood transfusions. The G5 cohort is characterized by unexpectedly elevated ALT levels in young TDT patients, a phenomenon largely attributed to inconsistent standards in the application of iron chelation therapy (ICT) for TDT patients in China. ICT is considered a primary intervention to reduce iron overload and mitigate liver injury, specifically ALT elevation [37], in patients with β-TM [29]. Due to the lack of standardized ICT guidelines, Chinese physicians must consider factors such as the patient’s transfusion history, growth indicators, physiological tolerance to iron overload, and socioeconomic status when applying ICT to TDT patients. This variability highlights the urgent need to establish a consensus-based ICT protocol specifically for the pediatric TDT population.

Several methodological constraints should be acknowledged in this study: (1) Study design constraints: The observational nature of this research prevents definitive conclusions regarding causation and temporal associations between transfusion events and ALT level alterations. (2) Limited pathogen screening: The assessment of infection risks remains incomplete due to unavailable data concerning other transfusion-related pathogens including HBV and Treponema pallidum(TP). (3) Mechanistic knowledge gaps: The study did not systematically examine potential etiologies for ALT elevation or its relationship with critical clinical parameters like transfusion volume and iron accumulation. (4) Sampling limitations: The final analytical cohort (n = 546) represented a significant reduction from the original dataset (n = 766), and the exclusive focus on a single geographic region may affect external validity. (5) Analytical considerations: The statistical approach would benefit from incorporating multivariate models to account for potential confounding variables such as patient age, transfusion history, and iron chelation treatment status.

Conclusion

No cases of transfusion-transmitted HCV or HIV infection were tested among 546 TDT patients in Nanning from July 2020 to March 2021, and the proportion of ALT abnormalities in TDT patients is within the range reported in the literature. The effectiveness implementation of the “Three-Designated” transfusion model ensured blood safety for TTD patients.
