The interplay of academic procrastination, self-generated stress, and self-reported bruxism among medical and dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Osman Babayiğit, Fatma Nur Büyükkalaycı, Seray Altun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcademic procrastination (AP), a common behavior among students, is influenced by factors such as fear of failure, task aversiveness, anxiety, and stress. Self-generated stress (SGS) refers to the subjective experience of tension that occurs in response to an existing situation. Individuals often overestimate the difficulty and attainability of a task, resulting in increased stress and extended stress duration. SGS can worsen mental and physical health problems, including bruxism. Therefore, this study investigates the possible underlying connections by examining the relationship between AP, SGS, and self-reported (SR) bruxism in medical and dental students.MethodsThe present cross-sectional study was undertaken among a sample of 379 students pursuing medical and dentistry degrees in Turkey. The questionnaire, including the Academic Procrastination Scale-Short Form, SR bruxism Scale, and SGS Scale, was administered to the students. According to the responses given to the SR bruxism Scale, 3 types of SR bruxism groups were created as sleep, awake, and compound. A comparative analysis was conducted on AP, SGS, and SR bruxism among medical and dental students. Furthermore, the associations among AP, SGS, and SR bruxism were assessed using the utilization of correlation tables and regression analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of awake bruxism was higher among dental students (46.6%) compared to medical students (34.7%), suggesting a faculty-dependent trend. A significant positive correlation was observed between SGS and AP (r = 0.212, p < 0.001). Median AP values differed significantly based on awake bruxism (p = 0.007) and compound bruxism (p = 0.013), but not sleep bruxism (p = 0.352). SGS exhibited statistically significant differences across all SR bruxism types: sleep (p < 0.001), awake (p < 0.001), and compound (p < 0.001). Multiple regression analysis identified SGS as a significant risk factor for SR bruxism, with a one-unit increase in stress score elevating the SR bruxism risk by 1.086 times.ConclusionsThis study revealed that self-generated stress significantly increases the risk for the development of SR bruxism. A significant relationship was found between academic procrastination and self-generated stress, and this relationship was particularly associated with an increased risk for the development of compound and awake bruxism. Stress-reducing therapy methods should be recommended for students to both increase academic success by reducing academic procrastination and prevent the negative effects of stress-related SR bruxism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02105-w.

Introduction

Procrastination is a widespread habit that hinders the completion of daily and academic work. Academic procrastination (AP) is a prevalent form of procrastination behavior observed among students [1]. It refers to the tendency of individuals to postpone the initiation or completion of academic work and assignments, particularly those with specified deadlines [2]. Students employ procrastination as a means of managing the adverse emotions triggered by undesirable assignments, prioritizing immediate mood improvement over task fulfillment. This transient alleviation of negative emotions also jeopardizes long-term objectives. Engaging in academic procrastination exacerbates stress levels when the deadline for academic tasks draws near [3]. Over 70% of university students exhibit a tendency to procrastinate consistently [4]. Research indicates that procrastination is a consistent personality trait influenced by internal and external factors, including personal, cognitive, emotional, and motivational elements [5]. This propensity is also influenced by conditions such as perfectionism, poor time management, poor motivation, environmental and social influences, and stress [6]. The psychological processes that contribute to academic procrastination are currently under investigation. Studies suggests that the primary cause of procrastination may be the fear of failure, whereas other studies suggest that deficiencies in self-regulation are the principal factors contributing to procrastination [7, 8]. Recent research indicates that academic procrastination may be a fundamental characteristic of conscientiousness [9]. The psychological and physiological mechanisms underlying the adverse effects of academic procrastination are not fully understood. Academic procrastination correlates with anxiety and apprehension, leading students to delay task completion. Students may procrastinate to evade feelings of anxiety regarding task difficulty or negative consequences (low self-efficacy) [10]. Nonetheless, this procrastination can increase stress levels, resulting in elevated anxiety. Self-generated stress activates the body’s stress response. This response is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and increases the release of the stress hormone cortisol. Elevated cortisol levels can adversely impact both mental and physical well-being [11]. Stress induces muscle tension. Prolonged stress can increase muscle tension throughout the body, including the masticatory muscles, such as the masseter and temporalis. This may result in bruxism (the grinding or clenching of teeth). Prolonged academic procrastination can escalate self-generated stress into a chronic stress condition over time. Chronic stress impairs the body’s overall functioning, disrupting sleep patterns, inducing concentration difficulties, and ultimately resulting in physical health issues such as bruxism.

Stress is an inherent feature of every person’s existence that impacts all areas and probably cannot be entirely eradicated [12]. Everyday discourse and the media frequently touch on the subject of stress. Consequently, several scientific studies have been conducted to analyze and understand its origins and effects [13]. The notion of stress generation is highly pertinent to perfectionism. Hewitt and Flett (1993) initially proposed that the connection between perfectionism, stress, and depression was partially attributable to perfectionism’s tendency to generate stress [14]. The self-generated stress scale, a concise self-assessment tool, has been linked to perfectionism, cognitive perfectionism, and perfectionistic self-presentation. In addition, stress that is caused by oneself is linked to feelings of distress, physical symptoms related to the mind-body connection, and unpleasant emotions [15]. The American Psychological Association provides a definition of stress as the bodily or mental reaction to pressures originating from within or outside the individual [16]. Excessive, overwhelming, and unregulated stress can have detrimental effects on our well-being and lead to heightened susceptibility to illnesses such as cardiovascular disease and stroke, as well as mental disorders like depression, bruxism and posttraumatic stress disorder [17, 18].

Bruxism is the deliberate act of tightly clenching the teeth and forcefully grinding or gnashing them, whether it occurs during awake or sleep [19]. Self-reported (SR) bruxism is identified through an individual’s personal responses to questionnaires, rather than through clinical examinations or objective assessments. SR Awake bruxism refers to the occurrence of teeth in contact or motions such as clenching, grinding, and thrusting while a person is awake. On the other hand, SR sleep bruxism refers to the chewing muscle actions observed during sleep. SR bruxism has been closely linked to stress. Research suggests that stress triggers hyperactivity in the jaw muscles, leading to involuntary teeth grinding, especially during sleep [16].

Dental and medical education is crucial for fostering the advancement of healthcare systems globally [20]. The area of healthcare presents a demanding setting characterized by growing responsibilities. Pursuing a degree in dentistry or medicine necessitates extensive dedication to both theoretical and practical coursework, resulting in elevated levels of stress, uncertainty, and apprehension regarding academic and professional progress [21]. This stress-filled atmosphere can impact medical and dental students’ overall well-being and potentially negatively impact their mental health, causing SR bruxism [22].

Muscle activity caused by stress is a subconscious coping mechanism to manage anxiety and tension. Similarly, academic procrastination may also be indirectly related to SR bruxism. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the relationship between SR bruxism and the academic procrastination habits and self-generated stress levels of medical and dental students. By quantifying these variables, we seek to elucidate the potential interconnections and underlying mechanisms linking procrastination, stress, and SR bruxism in this specific student population.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on 379 medical and dental students from 35 different universities in Turkey. The students participating in the study were included in the study on a voluntary basis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participating in the study. Exclusion criteria were determined as those under 18 years of age, edentulous patients, those who were unable to participate in the survey and those who were unable to answer the survey, or those who refused to answer the survey.

Data were collected between April 2024 and July 2024. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the 2013 revision of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry of Necmettin Erbakan University (Decision no. 2024/402).

Statistical analysis

The power of the sample size was determined using the G*power software with the following input parameters: number of dental students = 281; number of medical students = 98; two-tailed α-error p = 0.05; effect size d = 0.8. The estimated power of sample size (1-β error p) was 0.95. The statistically acceptable power for the sample size is 0.8.

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS V23. The suitability for normal distribution was examined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, which indicated substantial deviations from normality in certain groups. Consequently, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparing quantitative data instead of the independent t-test. Categorical data were examined using the Pearson chi-square test. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was employed for correlation analysis due to the non-parametric nature of the data and the violations of normality and linearity assumptions. Binary logistic regression was used to identify the risk factors affecting SR bruxism. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted.

This gender distribution was consistent throughout both the medicine and dentistry faculties, with 60.2% of students in medicine and 67.2% of students in dentistry being female.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to compare SR bruxism with academic procrastination and the self-generated stress scale in medical and dental students. The findings of our study indicate a significant interrelationship among these three primary issues.

Numerous studies have documented a correlation between SR bruxism and various factors such as stress, temporomandibular joint disorders, and anxiety [25–27]. These studies also investigated the prevalence of SR bruxism across different populations [27–29]. In the study conducted by Yıldırım and her colleagues, they found that the self-reported SB rate was 24% among dental students [30]. In the study conducted by Serra-Negra et al., the prevalence rates of SB and AB among dental students were 21.5% and 36.5%, respectively [31]. In our investigation, we discovered higher rates of 39.2% and 46.6% for SB and AB, respectively [30, 31].

The study conducted by Huhtela et al. found that the prevalence of SB in women and men was 21% and 12.5%, respectively. However, the occurrence of AB was not influenced by gender. In our study, while the prevalence of AB (48.8%) was higher in women, there was no significant difference in terms of SB (41.5%). Consistent with previous research, our study found that women had a higher prevalence of AB and CB compared to men [27, 30, 31]. Women’s increased likelihood of being bruxists could originate from elevated levels of stress and anxiety, hormonal fluctuations, and greater social responsibilities.

No comparative study on SR bruxism between dental and medical students has been found in the literature. The study conducted by Phuong et al. revealed that the prevalence rates of SB, AB, and CB among medical students were 38.2%, 23.4%, and 10.4%, respectively [32]. The rates of SB, AB, and CB among medical students in our study were all approximately 35.7%, 34.7%, and 24.5%, respectively. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of AB among dental students, observed in our study, may be attributed to the longer and more challenging practical tasks that are involved in this field, which demand greater physical force during hands-on training. The absence of difference between SR bruxism and class years indicates that there is a uniformity throughout the entire duration of both dentistry and medical schools. It is crucial to initiate research aimed at enhancing student well-being from the beginning of their first year.

There are numerous studies comparing the stress, anxiety, mental health, and depression symptoms of students studying dentistry, medicine, and non-medical fields [33–36]. Rezaei-Gazki et al. suggested that anxiety has a greater impact on academic procrastination in dental and medical students when compared to perfectionism [37]. In a study conducted by Fan et al. on university students, it was concluded that state anxiety significantly and positively predicted academic procrastination and ego depletion partially mediated the relationship between state anxiety and academic procrastination [38]. Our study revealed no statistically significant disparity in academic procrastination and self-generated stress levels between medical and dental students. The results indicate that both cohorts of students encounter comparable levels of self-generated stress and have comparable inclinations towards academic procrastination. This indicates that both occupational groups have comparable academic and personal stressors.

Our study found a positive correlation between self-generated stress and academic procrastination, which aligns with previous researches. Ragusa and his colleagues have demonstrated a favorable correlation between procrastination and academic stress and anxiety [39]. A study conducted by Pardo et al. involving 100 psychology students from Colombian colleges has established a direct correlation between the level of procrastination and the intensity of anticipated anxiety [40]. In a study conducted by Vahedi et al., it was demonstrated that there is a positive correlation between degrees of procrastination and levels of academic anxiety among a population of 246 students [41]. Sirois and Tosti’s study demonstrated a positive correlation between high levels of stress and increased procrastination among students as a coping mechanism [42]. Furthermore, a study conducted by Tice and Baumeister has revealed that stress diminishes individuals’ willpower, leading to ego depletion, which in turn increases procrastination behavior [3]. Research constantly demonstrates that procrastination is linked to various aspects in the academic setting, including anxiety, anxiety about learning, and stress or tension prior to upcoming academic tasks [39]. Moreover, it can also have detrimental effects on one’s physical well-being [43].

The categorization of stress into various branches, including emotional stress, psychological stress, and stress related to life events or experiences, along with the utilization of diverse stress measuring scales such as the Perceived Stress Scale, 12-item General Health Questionnaire, Stress Symptoms Inventory, Depression Anxiety Stress Scale present challenges in establishing conclusive evidence regarding the association between SR bruxism and stress [44]. The Self-Generated Stress Scale utilized in our research measures the individual’s inclination to perceive themselves as someone who creates and contributes to their own personal stress, exacerbating tough situations or amplifying life problems [15, 45].

The majority of studies have found a significant relationship between increased stress and SR bruxism [29, 46, 47]. Our findings, in line with these studies, showed that there was a significant difference between SGS and all types of SR bruxism, despite using a different stress measurement scale. It was observed that students with SR bruxism had higher SGS levels. The reason for this is that the psychological and physiological stress factors linked to SR bruxism might lead students to generate additional internal tension. Moreover, SR bruxism might impact the stress management strategies of students facing academic life stressors, potentially leading to heightened self-perception and overall stress levels.

As this is the first study exploring the relationship between academic procrastination and SR bruxism, there is a lack of existing data in the literature for comparison. Our study revealed that students with awake bruxism and compound bruxism exhibited more academic procrastination behavior. The possible cause of this could be the adverse impact of stress and anxiety related to SR bruxism on academic achievement [48]. Students who procrastinate can often experience increased stress and anxiety as deadlines approach, which can lead to increased cases of SR bruxism as their bodies react to this tension. Moreover, the procrastination cycle has the possibility of creating a feedback loop. Stress can lead to procrastination, which exacerbates stress levels and can further exacerbate SR bruxism symptoms [3].

Although both academic procrastination and self-generated stress were identified as factors increasing the risk of SR bruxism in the univariate analysis, only self-generated stress was found to be a significant risk factor in the multiple regression analysis, indicating that the impact of academic procrastination may be intertwined with the influence of self-generated stress, and therefore may not have a significant independent effect. This suggests that the potential relationships between academic procrastination and self-stress may be more complex and that the effects of these factors on SR bruxism may interact.

The study’s limitations include the inability to determine cause-and-effect relationships due to reliance on self-reported data from students, which may introduce bias, particularly in sensitive behaviors such as bruxism and academic procrastination. This reliance on self-reporting can lead to inaccuracies, as participants may underreport or overreport their behaviors due to social desirability or lack of self-awareness. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer causal relationships between self-generated stress, SR bruxism, and academic procrastination. It is crucial to emphasize that this design captures data at a single point in time, making it challenging to ascertain the directionality of these relationships. Moreover, the current study does not account for other associated factors, such as perfectionism and other personality traits, that could contribute to the connection between these variables. Another limitation is the exclusion of university students from non-health-related disciplines, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, further research with longitudinal designs, larger and more diverse populations, and inclusion of students from various fields is necessary to confirm and expand upon our study results.

Conclusion

This study revealed that self-generated stress significantly increases the risk for the development of SR bruxism. Increased self-generated stress levels increase the likelihood of presenting SR bruxism both during awake and sleep. Furthermore, a significant relationship was found between academic procrastination and self-generated stress, and this relationship was particularly associated with an increased risk for the development of compound and awake bruxism. Differences between dental and medical schools also suggest that awake bruxism rates vary depending on the faculty. Universities can implement various strategies to identify these issues and achieve effective solutions. Mobile applications, commonly utilized and readily accessible to university students, can offer self-help modules, time management strategies, relaxation exercises, and bruxism monitoring. Incorporating mindfulness-based stress reduction programs into academic counseling sessions can assist students. Furthermore, students who self-report bruxism and demonstrate academic procrastination should be observed and referred to cognitive-behavioral therapy workshops. Moreover, peer support programs can promote student engagement in group discussions centered on stress management strategies, time management, and effective coping mechanisms. By breaking the cycle of stress, bruxism, and academic procrastination, students can improve their academic performance, mental health, and overall quality of life.
