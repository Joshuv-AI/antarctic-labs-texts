Spatial variation in HIV test non-uptake among antenatal care–attending pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa: a cross-sectional study using demographic and health survey data

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study assessed the spatial distribution of HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women who attended antenatal care (ANC) in sub-Saharan Africa.DesignCross-sectional study design.SettingSub-Saharan Africa (SSA) region. 24 SSA countries were included in this study.Data sourceDemographic and Health Survey (DHS), 2016–2024.Participants82 397 women who were pregnant in the last 2 years preceding the survey.Outcome measureHIV test non-uptake, which is a legacy indicator of HIV test among pregnant women.ResultThe HIV test non-uptake among ANC attending pregnant women was 39.6% (95% CI 39.27% to 39.93%). The spatial autocorrelation test revealed that HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women was clustered. The global Moran’s I value was 0.48 with a p value <0.01. Hotspot areas were those with high rates of HIV testing non-uptake. These hotspot areas were located in most parts of Mali, Mauritania, Madagascar, Guinea, Senegal, central parts of Angola, eastern part of Senegal and northern parts of Ethiopia. Prioritising hotspot areas with high rates of HIV test non-uptake for spatially targeted interventions is essential. Policymakers, health professionals, and other stakeholders should focus on improving women’s formal education, expanding health insurance coverage, and increasing ANC contacts to ensure that each visit includes HIV screening. Moreover, special attention should be given to younger women to enhance HIV testing uptake among those attending ANC in sub-Saharan Africa.Keywords: Geographical mapping, Pregnant Women, Africa South of the Sahara, HIV & AIDS

Introduction

HIV remains a major public health concern, with an estimated 42.3 million HIV-related deaths reported globally. The African region accounts for approximately 65% of all HIV cases.1 In sub-Saharan Africa, HIV incidence is higher among females than males,2 and the rate of mother-to-child transmission is estimated at 16.9%.3 According to the 2024 UNICEF report on HIV among children and adolescents, Eastern and Southern Africa bear the highest global burden, contributing to 42% of new HIV infections among children.4 The prevalence of HIV infection among pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa ranges from 5.3% to 8.5%5–9, which is substantially higher than in developed regions; for example, in North America it is 0.6%.10

The expansion of treatment coverage and preventive measures has reduced the HIV burden worldwide, including in many sub-Saharan African countries.11 Globally, HIV infection rates have declined over the past two decades due to interventions such as antiretroviral therapy (ART), prophylaxis, and the prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Despite these advances, an estimated 1.3 million new HIV infections were reported, with women and girls accounting for approximately 44% of the cases.12

Among the four prongs of the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) framework, the third focuses on preventing HIV transmission from mother to infant.13 Effective implementation of PMTCT interventions can substantially reduce the incidence of new HIV infections among newborns.14 However, limited access to maternal and child health services and gaps in the delivery of interventions continue to hinder efforts to eliminate HIV transmission during pregnancy, delivery, and the postpartum period in low- and middle-income countries.15

HIV screening among pregnant women during antenatal care (ANC) visits is a key strategy for preventing mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of HIV and facilitating early initiation of treatment for infected infants, who are among the hardest groups to reach with HIV testing.16 The administration of antiretroviral therapy (ART) to HIV-positive pregnant women can reduce the risk of MTCT to less than 2%, compared with substantially higher rates without treatment. Without intervention, the probability of HIV transmission ranges from 15% to 30% during pregnancy, labour, or delivery, and from 5% to 15% during breastfeeding.17

More than 20 African countries, including Angola, Ethiopia, Ghana, Malawi, Mozambique, and Uganda, have updated their national guidelines to adopt the most recent recommendations of the WHO, which emphasise the integration of Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) services into maternal and child health programmes, with routine HIV testing during antenatal care (ANC) visits underscored as a key component.18 19 This integration has demonstrated synergistic benefits, enhancing the effectiveness of maternal healthcare.20 Nevertheless, HIV testing is still not routinely offered,13 and uptake among pregnant women remains low despite expanded antenatal care coverage.21–23

A study conducted in the Sub-Saharan Africa region found that women from the poorest wealth quintile were disadvantaged in accessing HIV testing, underscoring the influence of socio-economic inequalities.24 Additionally, previous studies have identified multiple factors associated with HIV testing uptake among women, including age, educational status, marital status, skilled ANC attendance, number of antenatal care visits, occupation, husband’s education, household wealth, number of children, place of residence, sex of household head, distance to health facilities, HIV knowledge, pre-test counselling and media exposure.25–28

Although several studies have studied factors influencing HIV testing uptake among women attending antenatal care (ANC), thus far, no study has assessed the spatial distribution of HIV test uptake and its associated factors. Therefore, this study aims to assess the spatial distribution of HIV test non-uptake among ANC-attending pregnant women and its associated factors in Sub-Saharan Africa.

The findings of this study have important implications for advancing progress towards Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, which aims to end the HIV/AIDS epidemic and reduce maternal and child mortality in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). By identifying hotspot areas with high rate of HIV test non-uptake, the results can guide the design of spatially targeted interventions. Furthermore, applying multi-scale geographically weighted regression to examine the spatially varying effects of contributing factors across regions enhances the interpretability and practical relevance of the results. Consequently, these findings can help policymakers, health professionals and other stakeholders in prioritising specific geographic areas for implementing tailored interventions.

Methods

Study setting, study design and data source

This study was conducted in 24 sub-Saharan Africa countries (online supplemental information 1). These countries were selected based on study year, having a national DHS after 2015, and availability of study variables (outcome variable and key independent variables identified through a literature review) in the dataset (online supplemental information 2).

bmjopen-16-6-s001.pdf

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted between 2016 and 2024 in Sub-Saharan Africa countries. The data used in this study were secondary data obtained from the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) website.29 DHS used two stages of sampling: stratified cluster sampling to select clusters from urban and rural areas and systematic sampling techniques to select households from selected clusters.30 We used individual records from each country’s DHS data for this study, and the study population was all women who had a live birth 2 years preceding the survey.

The spatial data for this study were obtained from the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), which collected georeferenced information alongside standard survey data. Cluster-level spatial data were captured in the field using GPS receivers during the survey sampling and listing process. The GPS coordinates for clusters were accurate to within 15 m, allowing precise geo-referencing of the centres of populated areas within sampled clusters. In accordance with DHS guidelines, cluster coordinates were randomly displaced to protect participant confidentiality. Urban clusters were displaced by up to 2 kilometers, rural clusters by up to 5 kilometers, and a randomly selected 1% of rural clusters were displaced by up to 10 kilometers.31

Sample size and sampling procedure

The study sample was selected based on the DHS guideline-recommended denominator population for estimating HIV testing uptake among ANC-attending pregnant women. Specifically, we included women who had given birth in the 2 years preceding the survey and had attended ANC visits. After excluding women with no ANC contact and those in clusters with missing coordinates, a total of 82 397 weighted samples were included in the analysis (online supplemental information 3).

Study variables

Dependent variable

HIV screening uptake is a dichotomous variable categorised as ‘No’ and ‘Yes’. Pregnant women who were tested for HIV during antenatal care and received their test results were categorised as ‘Yes’, whereas women who were not tested for HIV or women who were tested for HIV but did not receive their test result were categorised as ‘No’. HIV testing and receiving the results were one of the legacy indicators of HIV testing among pregnant women.

Explanatory variables

Based on literature review, we identified different explanatory variables of HIV testing uptake among women: age of the women, number of children, educational status, wealth index, place of residence, exposure to mass media, marital status, sex of household head, decision-making on healthcare, distance to health facility, health insurance coverage and number of antenatal care contacts. For this study, mass media exposure was created by combining exposure to magazine, radio and television. Women were considered to have mass media exposure if they had at least 1 day in a week exposure to magazine or radio or television. Here, we categorised the number of antenatal care contacts into two categories: less than four ANC and four or more ANC.

Data collection procedures

DHS developed and used standard model questionnaires. The model questionnaire has been reviewed and modified in each phase of the DHS questionnaire. The data were collected by interviewing eligible participants from selected households. Cluster coordinates were collected in the field using a GPS receiver during the survey sampling list process, and the GPS coordinates for the cluster were accurate to less than 15 m.32

Data management and analysis

We used Stata 17 software for data extraction, cleaning and data management. First, we performed exploratory data analysis to identify outliers, missing data and data distribution. We weighted our sample before any statistical analysis using the weighting variable V005 (the individual weight for women) to consider the unequal selection probability of samples according to DHS guidelines. Missing data were managed according to DHS guidelines. All descriptive summary analyses were conducted using Stata 17 software.

For the spatial analysis, the Africa shapefile was downloaded from the Open Africa website. Individual country DHS datasets were cleaned and merged with cluster coordinate files using DHS cluster numbers. Clusters with missing or zero coordinates were excluded, and unique cluster identifiers were assigned prior to combining all countries into a single dataset using Stata’s append command. The final cleaned dataset was imported into ArcGIS Pro version 3.2, using the GCS WGS 1984 geographic coordinate system, and projected with the Albers Equal Area Conic projection for spatial analysis.

Spatial analysis

To depict geographic patterns, spatial analysis was conducted for the HIV test non-uptake category, which served as the outcome for all descriptive and analytical spatial analyses. ArcGIS Pro version 3.2 was used for descriptive and analytical mapping, while SaTScan version 10.1 was employed to perform spatial scan analysis.

Global Moran’s I was used to assess the spatial autocorrelation of HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women. Moran’s I values range from –1 to 1, where values near –1 indicate spatial dispersion, values near one indicate spatial clustering, and values near 0 indicate a random spatial distribution. The Getis-Ord General G statistic was used to assess local clustering of HIV test non-uptake. Based on Z-scores, high values indicate clustering of high HIV test non-uptake, while low values indicate clustering of low values. Additionally, Anselin Local Moran’s I was applied to detect spatial outliers, high clusters surrounded by low clusters (High–Low) and low clusters surrounded by high clusters (Low–High), and to identify statistically significant hotspots (High–High) and cold spots (Low–Low).34

Hotspot analysis (local Getis-Ord Gi*) was used to identify hotspot and cold spot areas.35 In this study, hotspots represented high rates of HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women, whereas cold spots represented low rates of HIV test non-uptake during antenatal care.

Spatial interpolation was employed to estimate HIV test non-uptake in unsampled areas based on observed values from sampled areas.36 Different interpolation techniques were compared using root mean square error (RMSE) to identify the best-fitting method.

Spatial scan analysis was performed using SaTScan version 10.1 with the Bernoulli model to identify statistically significant clusters of high HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women. Cases were women with no HIV test uptake during antenatal care, and controls were women with HIV test uptake. A maximum cluster radius of ≤100 km and the default 50% window were used to detect primary and secondary clusters. Cluster significance (p-values) and log-likelihood ratios were determined based on 999 Monte Carlo replications.

Variable selection for spatial regression was conducted using a stepwise procedure. Theoretically relevant variables were first identified through a literature review. Inter-variable relationships and multicollinearity were assessed using correlation matrices and scatter plots. Exploratory regression analyses, testing all combinations of candidate variables similar to classical stepwise regression, were performed to identify significant predictors (p < 0.05), assess multicollinearity (VIF > 7.5) and evaluate OLS model diagnostics.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is a global model that estimates a single coefficient for each factor across all geographical locations. As a type of linear regression, OLS relies on several assumptions, which were assessed using diagnostic statistics. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values >7.5 indicating redundancy and prompting variable removal. The Koenker statistic tested the consistency (stationarity) of explanatory-dependent variable relationships, and the Jarque-Bera statistic assessed residual normality. Overall model significance was determined using joint Wald statistics when the Koenker statistic indicated non-stationarity, and p-values <0.05 were used to identify statistically significant factors associated with HIV test non-uptake.

Explanatory variables for the local spatial model were specified based on the global OLS model as diagnostic statistics were not available for local models in the ArcGIS software outputs. HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women

Among study samples 32 603 (39.6%, 95% CI 39.27% to 39.93%) out of 82 397 pregnant women had no HIV test uptake during their ANC contact (online supplemental information 4). Furthermore, among pregnant women who attended four or more antenatal care (ANC) visits, 20.6% had never undergone HIV testing, compared with 31.0% of those with fewer than four visits. At the country level, among women with four or more ANC visits, Mauritania, Madagascar and Guinea had particularly high proportions of women who had never been tested for HIV. In some countries, such as Mauritania, the difference in the proportion of women who had never been tested between those with four or more visits and those with fewer visits was minimal (online supplemental information 5).

Spatial distribution of HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women

The spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed that HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women was clustered. The global Moran’s I was 0.48 (p<0.01), and Getis-Ord General statistics detected that local clustering was from high rate clusters (online supplemental information 6). Hotspot analysis using Getis-Ord Gi* statistics identified hotspot areas with high rates of HIV testing non-uptake and coldspot areas with low rates. Dark and light red indicate hotspots located in Mali, Mauritania, Madagascar, Guinea, Senegal, central Angola, eastern Senegal, and northern Ethiopia, whereas dark and light green indicate cold spots (figure 1A). Local clustering and outliers were also assessed using Anselin Local Moran’s I, with detailed results provided in online supplemental information 7.

Ordinary kriging interpolation was used after comparing multiple techniques based on root mean square error, as it had the lowest error (online supplemental information 8). On the interpolated map, a green-to-red gradient depicts increasing HIV test non-uptake, with red indicating the highest rates in Mali, Mauritania, Madagascar, Guinea, Senegal, central Angola, eastern Senegal and northern Ethiopia (figure 1B).

In the spatial scan analysis, the primary window was located in Mauritania, Guinea, Mali, Burkina Faso, Sierra Leone and Senegal, with a log likelihood ratio of 6180.6 and a relative risk (RR) of 2.39. This indicates that pregnant women in the spatial window were more than twice as likely to miss HIV testing during ANC compared with those outside it (online supplemental information 7).

Factors associated with HIV test non-uptake

Spatial regression models were fitted to identify significant factors associated with HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women, who had antenatal care contact during their pregnancy. Initially, the global model, ordinary least squares (OLS) regression, was fitted following the selection of independent variable combinations through exploratory regression. Only variables with a variance inflation factor (VIF) below 7.5 and p-values less than 0.05 were included in the OLS model. Hence, age group 15–19 years, no formal education, no health insurance and less than four antenatal care contacts were the explanatory variables in the fitted OLS model. The OLS model explained 54.9% of the variation in HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women. The Koenker statistic was significant, which indicated spatial non-stationarity. The Jarque-Bera statistic was significant, which indicated non-normality of residuals distribution. The residuals of the OLS model were identified as clustered after conducting spatial auto-correlation using Moran’s I (online supplemental information 9). Due to the non-stationarity of the Koenker test and residuals spatial-autocorrelation, we fitted local spatial models, geographical weighted regression (GWR) and multiscale geographical weighted regression (MGWR). After fitting the MGWR, the residuals' spatial-autocorrelation was resolved (online supplemental information 10).

Finally, the MGWR model was the best fitted model since it had the lowest Akaike Information Criterion with correction (AICc) and highest Adj-R2 (table 2). The model explained 91% of the variation in HIV test non-uptake among pregnant women. Detailed MGWR results, including model diagnostics, a summary of explanatory variable estimates and optimal bandwidth statistics, are provided in online supplemental information 10.

Discussion

This study examined the spatial variation of HIV test non-uptake and its associated factors among ANC-attending pregnant women in SSA. The prevalence of HIV test non-uptake in this population was 39.6% (95% CI 39.27 to 39.93%), substantially higher than in developed countries such as Germany and France, where it remains below 10%.39 40 This high magnitude underscores the need for targeted interventions to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV. A possible explanation for the higher rate of HIV test non-uptake in the SSA region could be shortages of HIV testing kits, inadequate health infrastructure and limited health personnel, as well as individual-level factors such as fear of HIV test results, poor knowledge about HIV and HIV-related stigma. Collectively, these factors negatively influence testing uptake during ANC visits.41

There was a high rate of women who had never been tested for HIV in the SSA region. Even among women who attended four or more ANC visits, the proportion who had never been tested for HIV remained high. In some countries, such as Mauritania, more than 80% of pregnant women had never been tested for HIV despite attending four ANC visits. This indicates missed opportunities for HIV testing and suggests weak integration between HIV testing services and maternal healthcare.24 42

The spatial autocorrelation test revealed that HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women was clustered. The global Moran’s I value was 0.48 with a p value <0.01. Hotspots, areas with high rates of HIV testing non-uptake, were located in most parts of Mali, Mauritania, Madagascar, Guinea, Senegal and central parts of Angola, eastern parts of Senegal and northern parts of Ethiopia. The spatial variation in HIV test non-uptake among ANC-attending women may be due to differences in health infrastructure, the availability and quality of maternal health services, socio-cultural and economic conditions and variations in service delivery by healthcare professionals.43–45

Hotspot areas with high HIV test non-uptake among ANC-attending women were mainly in West Africa, aligning with UNAIDS reports that the region has experienced the slowest decline in new HIV infections among children compared with eastern and southern Africa, many of which could be prevented through antenatal HIV screening. Madagascar was also identified as a hotspot in this study and is classified as a lower-performing country in East Africa for preventing new HIV infections.46 These patterns likely reflect gaps in the coverage and provision of maternal health services across different geographical areas and may also be influenced by disruptions in service delivery, such as those experienced during the Ebola outbreak in West African countries.47 48

Based on the final MGWR model, age group 15–19 years, no formal education, no health insurance and less than four antenatal care contacts were significantly associated with HIV test non-uptake.

Younger women showed higher rates of HIV test non-uptake during antenatal care. This may be attributed to their economic dependence on families or guardians, which can restrict access to healthcare services. Additionally, individuals under 18 years often lack the legal autonomy to make independent healthcare decisions and may experience heightened fear of stigma or judgement from both the community and healthcare providers. Furthermore, as pregnancy among adolescents is frequently perceived as socially and legally unacceptable, many young women may avoid antenatal care altogether, thereby missing essential health counselling and HIV testing opportunities.49–52

Women with no formal education had higher rates of HIV test non-uptake. This may be attributed to limited health literacy, which can restrict access to information and reduce understanding of the benefits of HIV testing during pregnancy, as well as the consequences of non-testing for both mothers and their children. Additionally, women who have not attended formal education may have limited autonomy in healthcare decision-making, and their economic dependence on partners may further hinder HIV testing uptake.53 54

Not having health insurance was associated with HIV test non-uptake. This may be explained by the financial constraints faced by uninsured women, which can limit their routine healthcare utilisation compared with those with insurance. In contrast, women with health insurance can access services with greater confidence, without concern for unexpected expenses. Uninsured women often seek care only for acute health issues, rather than for preventive services or regular follow-up, which may reduce opportunities for HIV testing during pregnancy.55–57

Women who attended fewer than four antenatal care (ANC) contacts had higher rates of HIV test non-uptake. Each ANC contact represents an opportunity for HIV testing; therefore, women with fewer than four visits are more likely to miss these opportunities due to workload pressures or assumptions regarding test provision. Additionally, fewer ANC contacts reduce interactions with healthcare providers, limiting access to health education on HIV testing and opportunities to discuss sensitive issues confidentially.18 58 59

The spatial variations in associated factors and their corresponding coefficients highlight the need for geographically targeted interventions. Such strategies should prioritise areas with the highest rates of HIV test non-uptake and consider the projected impact of interventions based on the identified determinants, particularly in resource-constrained settings, such as the SSA.

Strengths and limitations of this study

This study has several strengths. Nationally representative, multi-country survey data from 24 SSA countries were used, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. In addition, the use of MGWR allowed neighbourhood effects to vary across explanatory variables, providing a more nuanced understanding of spatial heterogeneity.

Nevertheless, the study has limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the data limits the ability to establish causal relationships between dependent and independent variables. Self-reported data collected through interviews may be subject to recall bias, potentially affecting the results. Furthermore, the DHS does not capture new HIV infections among women who missed ANC testing, preventing estimation of potential HIV acquisitions in this group.

Conclusion and recommendation

There was a high non-uptake of HIV testing among pregnant women in SSA. There was also a spatial variation on HIV test non-uptake in SSA. The hotspot, high rate of HIV testing non-uptake areas located in Madagascar, Angola, Benin, Guinea, Sierra Leone, Gambia, Senegal, Mali and Mauritania. Not attending formal education, not covered by health insurance coverage, four or more antenatal care contacts and being younger age group (15–19 years) was associated with HIV testing non-uptake among pregnant women.

The findings of this study have important implications for reducing HIV test non-uptake among ANC-attending women through spatially targeted interventions. Recommended strategies include improving women’s formal education, expanding health insurance coverage, increasing ANC contacts to maximise HIV testing opportunities, and addressing the needs of young women and adolescent girls through targeted counselling and support. These strategies should be integrated into programme design, and stakeholders such as policymakers, health organisations, and healthcare professionals should prioritise geographically targeted interventions to improve HIV testing uptake among ANC-attending women in resource-limited settings such as SSA. We recommend that future studies assess the impact of missed HIV testing during ANC by estimating the number of potential infections missed during untested visits.
