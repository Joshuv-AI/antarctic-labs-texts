TCF19 promotes gastric cancer progression through MCM6
Authors: Jinglei Zhang, Ya Liu, Siai Chen, Yidan Pang, Jin Zeng, Juan An
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide with limited therapeutic options. Identifying molecular drivers of GC progression is crucial for developing effective strategies. Transcription Factor 19 (TCF19), a hub gene implicated in various cancers, has not been well reported in GC. Understanding the role of TCF19 in GC could provide novel insights into tumorigenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets.MethodsTCF19 expression was analyzed using data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), a cohort of 35 GC patients, and 3 GC cell lines (AGS, HGC-27 and MKN45). To investigate the roles of TCF19, gene knockdown and overexpression were performed using TCF19-targeted siRNAs and TCF19-encoding plasmids, respectively. Additionally, the roles of TCF19 in GC growth were examined in vivo. Gene expression profiles were obtained through RNA sequencing in control and TCF19-deficient HGC-27 cells to identify the potential downstream targets.ResultsAnalysis of the TCGA cohort revealed that TCF19 expression is significantly elevated in GC tissues. This finding was further validated by immunohistochemistry in 35 clinical GC specimens. In vitro experiments showed that TCF19 expression was elevated in 3 GC cell lines, compared to immortal gastric epithelial GES-1 cells. Silencing TCF19 significantly inhibited cell proliferation and migration. Conversely, overexpression of TCF19 produced the opposite effects. In vivo assays in nude mice revealed that TCF19 knockdown markedly reduced the growth of xenografted AGS cells. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis of TCF19-expressing and TCF19-depleted GC cells identified MCM6 as one of the top co-regulated genes. Rescue experiments, in which TCF19 was knocked down while MCM6 was simultaneously overexpressed, provided strong evidence for the pivotal oncogenic role of the TCF19-MCM6 axis in GC.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.ConclusionTCF19 promotes GC progression via regulating MCM6, and the TCF19-MCM6 axis is the potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04531-5.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide and is often diagnosed at advanced stages [1]. In recent years, both the mortality and incidence rates of GC in China have declined, accompanied by improvements in survival rates. However, a notable gap persists when compared to Japan, South Korea, and other East Asian countries [2]. Given China’s large population and the challenges associated with widespread GC screening, targeting high-risk populations for endoscopic examination using biomarkers appears to be a more practical approach [3].

Transcription factor 19 (TCF19), also known as SC1, was first identified as a growth-regulated gene in human, mouse, and hamster cells [4]. Subsequently, Krautkramer et al.. showed that TCF19 knockdown led to cell cycle arrest during G1/S transition, promoting also apoptosis and susceptibility to endoplasmic reticulum stress [5]. The metabolic regulation mediated by TCF19/p53 was reported as critical in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma [6]. Consistent with an ubiquitous pro-oncogenic role, high TCF19 expression has also been reported in lung cancer [7], liver cancer [8, 9], colorectal carcinoma [10], and head and neck carcinoma [11]. In a study on GC patients, it was reported that TCF19 is the target of the tumor suppressor factor VEZT [12]. However, the role of TCF19 in the initiation and progression of gastric cancer (GC) remains poorly understood. To address this gap, the present study utilized bioinformatics approaches alongside in vitro and in vivo experiments to assess the expression of TCF19 and investigate its potential contribution to GC pathogenesis.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics

The Xiantao Xueshu platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com) was accessed to analyze TCF19 expression levels in 375 GC specimens and 32 normal gastric tissues from The Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) database. Clinical baseline data for GC cases were analyzed using the online database Xiantao Xueshu platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com).

Clinical samples and immunohistochemical analysis

A total of 35 pairs of GC and corresponding normal gastric tissues were collected from the Pathology Department of Qinghai University Affiliated Hospital from March 2020 to July 2022. Prior to surgery, none of these patients had received GC-targeted therapy. All patients signed informed consent forms, and this study received approval from the Ethics Committee (PJ202002-28) of Qinghai University Medical College.

An immunohistochemical assay kit (Elabscience Biotech., China) was applied to examine TCF19 expression. A TCF19 antibody (1:1000; ab230005, Abcam) was purchased from Shanghai Aibo Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd (China). Two independent pathologists evaluated staining intensity on a scale of 0 (no staining), 1 (faint yellow), 2 (brown yellow), and 3 (dark brown). In high-power fields (400×), the quantity of positive cells was classified as 0 (< 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (26–50%), or 3 (>50%), with scores added for final quantification. A score >3 points defined the high TCF19 expression group and a score ≤ 2 points the low TCF19 expression group [13].

Cell culture and transfection

Human GC cells (AGS, HGC-27, and MKN45) and human normal gastric epithelial cells (GES-1) were obtained from the Beijing Cancer Research Institute. All cell lines were authenticated using Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling by Beijing Cancer Research Institute. All cell cultures used in this study were confirmed to be mycoplasma-free by routine PCR testing. AGS Cells were cultured in F12 medium (Wuhan Punosa Life Technology Co., Ltd., China) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HGC-27 and MKN45 Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). All cells were kept in a 37℃ incubator with 5% CO2.

Two siRNAs targeting TCF19 (siTCF19#1: 5’- GCCAAGGUACUUGUCAA-3’, siTCF19#2: 5’-GCUGGAAGUUCCCACUUU-3’), one siRNA targeting MCM6 (siMCM6: 5’- CCTAACTACTTGCTCGAAGAT-3’), and a negative control (siCtrl: 5’- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’) were synthesized by Hippo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Huzhou, China). The pcDNA3.1 empty vector, as well as TCF19 and MCM6 overexpression plasmids, were purchased from Beijing Syngenbio Co., LTD (Beijing, China). A total of 3 × 105 cells/well were plated into 6-well plates and Lipo8000 (5 µL/well; Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd) was used for transfection of siRNAs (50 nM/well) or overexpression plasmids (5 µg/well), as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from transfected GC cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription and cDNA quantification were carried, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using a SuperReal PreMix Color (SYBR Green) kit (Beijing Tiangen Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.). The 2− ΔΔCq method was used to estimate relative TCF19 expression. The primer sequences were: TCF19: forward, 5’- GGGGCGGTGATCTCTACAC-3’; reverse, 5’-GGGAGTCGGACATTATTGACCA-3’; MCM6: forward, 5’- GAGGAACTGATTCGTCCTGAGA-3’; reverse, 5’-CAAGGCCCGACACAGGTAAG-3’; β-actin, forward, 5’-CATGTACGTTGCTATCCAGGC-3’; reverse, 5’-CTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGAT-3’.

Western blot analysis

To assess the protein expression of TCF19 and MCM6, Western blot analysis was performed following standard procedures. Briefly, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. The protein concentration of the cell lysates was determined using the BCA protein assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels and separated by electrophoresis. The proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to TCF19 (1:1000, Abcam, ab155391) and MCM6 (1:1000, Proteintech, 67989-1-PBS). The membranes were then washed three times with TBST and incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Sigma) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Thermo Scientific), and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Vinculin (1:1000, Proteintech, 66305-1-PBS) was used as a loading control.

Cell proliferation and colony formation assays

Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) assay was used to analyze cell viability. Briefly, transfected AGS, HGC-27, and MKN45 cells were plated onto 96 well plates, cultured for 24, 48, 72, or 96 h, and incubated with CCK-8 solution (10 µL/well) for 1 h. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm in a microplate reader. For cell colony formation assays, a total of 1000 AGS, HGC-27, or MKN45 cells were plated into individual wells of 6-well plates. After 14 days of culture, the cells were thoroughly rinsed with PBS, fixed with 100% methanol for 15 min, and stained with a 0.2% crystal violet solution for 30 min.

Cell migration assay

Transwell chambers were used for cell migration assays. A total of 8 × 104 HGC-27 cells in 200 µL of DMEM without FBS were seeded on the upper chambers, and the lower chambers were filled with 500 µL of complete DMEM with 10% FBS. After incubation at 37℃/5% CO2 for 24 h, the cells remaining on the upper surface of the insert were removed using a cotton swab, and those that traversed to the lower surface were fixed in 100% methanol, stained with 0.2% crystal violet, and quantified under a microscope.

Cell cycle and apoptosis assays

Cell cycle analysis was conducted on GC cells cultured for 48 h and fixed with 70% alcohol on ice for 3 h, using a Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, the cells were incubated with RNase A and PI staining solution at 37℃ for 15 min, and cell cycle distribution assessed by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter). The data were analyzed with CytExpert software (version 2.4.0.28; Beckman Coulter). For apoptosis detection, an Annexin V-FITC/PI Cell Apoptosis Detection kit (Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) was used. Briefly, GC cells were cultured for 48 h, rinsed with PBS, and subsequently stained with PI and Annexin V at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Cell apoptosis was analyzed on a CytoFLEX cytometer.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from HGC-27 cells transfected with siTCF19 or siCtrl using TRIzol, and RNA concentration and purity measured using a NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, the samples were sent to Beijing Allwegene Gene Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for RNA sequencing. Gene expression levels were normalized based on fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values, and differential expression analysis was performed using differential exon usage (DEU). In addition, we used GO and KEGG assay to explore the pathway and functional mechanism of differential gene enrichment in the TCF19 knock down cells.

Luciferase reporter assay

Sequence of MCM6 promoter was cloned into the pGL3 Luciferase vector (Promega) at the upstream of luciferase reporter gene. TCF19 gene was inserted into the pGL3 promoter vector and transfected into AGS cells with pGL3 luciferase vector. After incubating with 48 h, Dual Luciferase Reporter Assay System purchased from Promega was applied to measure the activity.

Chip-qPCR

ChIP assay was performed with IgG and Flag antibody by using SimpleChIP enzymatic chromatin IP kit (Cell Signaling), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. qPCR was carried out to check the relative enrichment of promoter fragments in each group. The primers designed were as follows: MCM6 promoter F: 5’- GTGTAAAGGGTCGCCCAAGA-3’; MCM6 promoter R༚ 5’- CGCCTCTCCTTCAGTGAGTC-3’.

Mouse xenograft model

Ten female mice (BALB/c-nu, 6 weeks old, 16–20 g) were purchased from Charles River Co., Ltd. (China). All procedures were conducted according to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [14] and institutional guidelines, with approval granted by the Ethics Committee of Qinghai University ((PJ202002-28). The shCtrl and shTCF19 lentiviruses were purchased from Beijing Syngenbio Co., LTD. Lentiviral infection of AGS cells was carried out according to the provided protocol. The mice were randomly divided into two groups and inoculated into the right armpit with ~ 1 × 107 AGS cells stably expressing shCtrl (n = 5) or shTCF19 (n = 5). Tumor volumes were measured on the 25th day post-implantation and then twice a week. After 40 days, the mice were killed by cervical dislocation and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.0 was used for statistical analysis. The correlation between TCF19 expression and clinical variables was analyzed by chi-square test. Differences between two groups were compared through students’ t-test, and ANOVA was used for comparison of multiple groups. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, P-values were adjusted using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction method of Benjamini and Hochberg. All P-values reported in the results are FDR-adjusted. A corrected P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

TCF19 is highly expressed in GC

Analysis of gene expression data from TCGA datasets revealed significantly elevated TCF19 mRNA levels in gastric cancer (GC) samples compared to normal gastric tissue (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A) and adjacent non-tumoral gastric tissue (P < 0.01, Fig. 1B). Additionally, TCF19 expression was positively correlated with both patient age and tumor histological grade (Table 1). Immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed the upregulation of TCF19 in GC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumoral samples from an internal cohort of 35 GC cases (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C; Table 2). Furthermore, TCF19 expression was correlated with tumor size, patient age, and tumor histological grade (Table 3). Western blot analysis similarly demonstrated significantly increased TCF19 levels in human GC cell lines (AGS, MKN45, and HGC-27) compared to normal human gastric epithelial GES-1 cells (P < 0.01, Fig. 1D).

TCF19 knockdown suppresses GC cell progression

To further investigate the functional role of TCF19, two siRNAs targeting TCF19 were used to knock down its expression in AGS and HGC-27 cells. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by both RT-qPCR and Western blotting (Figs. 2A, B). In vitro assays assessing cell viability and clonogenicity demonstrated that TCF19 downregulation significantly reduced cell proliferation (Fig. 2C) and colony formation (Fig. 2D) in AGS and HGC-27 cells. Additionally, Transwell migration assays revealed that TCF19 knockdown impaired the migratory capacity of both AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 2E). Flow cytometry analyses further showed that TCF19 knockdown induced apoptosis (Fig. 2F) and caused cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 phase (Fig. 2G). We also observed that cell proliferation was inhibited in TCF19 knockdown AGS and HGC-27 cells using EdU staining assay (Figs. 2H and J). Collectively, these findings suggest that TCF19 inhibition suppresses cell proliferation and migration, while promoting apoptosis in gastric cancer cells.

TCF19 overexpression promotes GC cell progression

To further evaluate the role of TCF19 in GC cells, MKN45 cells were transfected with control or TCF19 overexpression plasmids. RT-qPCR and western blotting confirmed that TCF19 mRNA and protein levels were prominently upregulated following TCF19 overexpression (Figs. 3A, B). CCK-8 and clonogenic assays showed that overexpression of TCF19 significantly increased the cell viability (P < 0.01, Fig. 3C) and colony-forming capacity (P < 0.01, Fig. 3D) of MKN45 cells. Through flow cytometry, we observed that TCF19 overexpression decreased apoptosis (Fig. 3E) and induced the accumulation of MKN45 cells in S phase (Fig. 3F). Our results showed that cell proliferation was increased in TCF19 overexpression MKN45 cells using EdU staining assay (Fig. 3G). These results indicated that TCF19 expression promotes proliferation and apoptosis resistance in GC cells in vitro.

TCF19 knockdown suppresses GC cell growth in vivo

To investigate the role of TCF19 on GC growth in vivo, a tumor xenograft model was constructed by subcutaneously injecting AGS cells infected with either control shRNA (shCtrl) or TCF19-targeted shRNA (shTCF19) into the right armpits of nude mice. Upon sacrifice 40 days after tumor cell implantation, mean tumor volume was significantly reduced in the shTCF19 group compared to the shCtrl group (Figs. 4A-B). Accordingly, tumor weight was significantly lower in the shTCF19 group compared to the shCtrl group (Fig. 4C, P < 0.05). Our IHC results showed that the expression of TCF19 was decreased in shTCF19 group (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that knocking down TCF19 expression inhibits the in vivo tumorigenic ability of GC cells.

MCM6 is downstream target of TCF19

To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying TCF19’s role in GC, RNA sequencing was performed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between siCtrl- and siTCF19-transfected HGC-27 cells. A total of 3485 DEGs were detected, including 1816 upregulated and 1669 downregulated genes in TCF19-silenced cells (Fig. 5A). GO and KEGG analysis revealed that multiple pathways were dysregulated after TCF19 silencing (Fig S1A, B). Notably, the top 10 down-regulated genes in the TCF19 knock down group were validated by RT-qPCR, we observed that the most significantly downregulated gene was minichromosome maintenance complex component 6 (MCM6), a critical regulator of eukaryotic genome replication (Fig. 5B). Consistent with these findings, MCM6 expression was positively correlated with TCF19 expression in GC cases from the TCGA database (Fig. 5C). To further confirm this relationship, RT-qPCR was performed to assess MCM6 expression in GC cells after TCF19 overexpression or knockdown. The results showed that siRNA-mediated TCF19 silencing suppressed MCM6 mRNA levels in both AGS and HGC-27 cells (Fig. 5C). Conversely, forced expression of TCF19 in MKN45 cells led to upregulation of MCM6 mRNA (Fig. 5D). Western blot analysis produced similar results, with TCF19 downregulation resulting in reduced MCM6 protein levels, and TCF19 upregulation enhancing MCM6 protein expression (Fig. 5E). Our IHC results showed that the expression of MCM6 was decreased in shTCF19 group of xenograft model (Figs. 5F, G). We observed that TCF19 could directly binding to the MCM6 promoter using luciferase reporter assays (Fig. 5H) and ChIP-PCR assay (Fig. 5I). These findings suggest that MCM6 expression is directly regulated by TCF19.

TCF19 regulates GC progression through MCM6

To investigate the role of MCM6 in TCF19-mediated gastric cancer (GC) progression, cell viability and migration assays were conducted in GC cells following TCF19/MCM6 overexpression and knockdown. Western blot analysis (Fig. 6A) confirmed successful MCM6 overexpression in siTCF19-transfected HGC-27 cells. CCK-8 and clonogenic assays showed that MCM6 upregulation partially rescued cell viability (P < 0.05; Fig. 6B) and promoted colony formation (P < 0.001; Fig. 6C) in HGC-27 cells with TCF19 knockdown. Additionally, Transwell migration assays demonstrated that MCM6 overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of siTCF19 on cell migration (P < 0.01; Fig. 6D). In contrast, after confirming successful inhibition of MCM6 protein expression in TCF19-overexpressing MKN45 cells co-transfected with siMCM6 (Fig. 6E), CCK-8 assays showed that the proliferative effect of TCF19 overexpression was significantly inhibited by MCM6 knockdown (Figs. 6F, G). Transwell results showed that cell migration was decreased in MCM6 knockdown HGC-27 cells, and this ability was rescued in siMCM6 + TCF19 HGC-27 cells (Fig. 6H). Furthermore, we performed TCF19 overexpression and TCF19 overexpression + siMCM6 in MKN45 cells, the expression of TCF19 and MCM6 was confirmed using western blot (Fig. 6I). Our CCK8 results showed that cell viability was increased in TCF19 overexpression MKN45 cells compared to control, while was decreased TCF19 overexpression + si MCM6 MKN45 cells (Fig. 6J). We also observed that similar results in colony formation assays (Fig. 6K). These findings suggest that upregulation of MCM6 plays a critical role in promoting TCF19-mediated GC cell proliferation and migration.

Discussion

Our study shows that TCF19 expression is increased in GC, a finding supported by both TCGA gene profiling data and immunohistochemistry in clinical GC samples. Furthermore, TCF19 upregulation, relative to normal human gastric epithelial GES-1 cells, was detected in three human GC cell lines. Gain- and loss-of-function assays were implemented to explore the role of TCF19 on GC proliferation, migration, and survival. Indicating a tumor promoter role for TCF19 in GC, our in vitro experiments demonstrated that TCF19 knockdown suppressed proliferation and migration in GC cells, while TCF19 overexpression had opposite effects. Animal experiments also confirmed the oncogenic role of TCF19 on GC growth, suggesting TCF19 could be an effective therapeutic target for GC.

A disrupted cell cycle balance and sustained cell proliferation are basic characteristics of cancer cells [15]. TCF19 was shown to function as a regulator of normal G1/S cell cycle transition [4]. In genome wide association studies, it was reported that TCF19 increases the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma by activating a G1/S phase shift during the cell cycle [8, 9, 16]. Research on GC and insulinoma cells showed that the expression of G1 (A-type and E-type) cyclins and G2/M (B-type) cyclins is regulated by TCF19 [12]. In this study, we found that inhibiting TCF19 expression promoted G0/G1 arrest and thus halted the transition through the S phase, whereas forced expression of TCF19 reversed this effect. Evan et al.. pointed out that the accumulation of oncogenic mutations in cancer cells is caused by deregulated proliferation, with cell survival sustained by the deactivation of pro-apoptotic genes and/or increased anti-apoptotic protein expression or activities leading to apoptosis suppression [17]. Studies have shown that downregulation of TCF19 induces apoptosis [18, 19]. Accordingly, we showed that following TCF19 knockdown and overexpression, the apoptosis rate of GC cells was respectively increased and decreased.

Employing RNA sequencing to screen for DEGs between control and TCF19 knockdown HGC-27 cells, we identified MCM6 as a potential downstream target of TCF19. MCM6 is part of the MCM complex, which possesses DNA helicase activity and plays a crucial role in genomic DNA replication, particularly in the initiation and elongation phases [20]. MCM6 was found to be overexpressed in GC [21, 22], colorectal carcinoma [23], lung carcinoma [24], breast cancer [25, 26], hepatocellular carcinoma [27], glioma [28], and was proposed to be a sensitive and specific cancer biomarker and therapeutic target [29].

We observed a positive relationship between TCF19 and MCM6 expression in GC samples in the TCGA database. In turn, MCM6 mRNA and protein levels were decreased after TCF19 knockdown in AGS and HGC-27 cells, and increased instead following TCF19 overexpression in MKN45 cells. These outcomes indicated that MCM6 may be a downstream target of TCF19. Supporting this possibility, we found that MCM6 overexpression partially rescued cell viability and migration ability in TCF19-silenced HGC-27 cells, whereas MCM6 knockdown reduced the proliferative capacity of MKN45 cells overexpressing TCF19. These results suggest that TCF19 regulates GC cell proliferation and migration through MCM6. Despite their widespread use in gastric cancer research, the AGS, HGC-27, and MKN45 cell lines exhibit inherent limitations, including divergent molecular profiles, variable metastatic potential, and lack of representation of gastric cancer heterogeneity, which may constrain their translational relevance.

Limitations of our study primarily stem from the scope of our in vivo experimental models. Specifically, the functional validation of our findings was limited by the lack of knockout mouse studies, which would have provided critical insights into the physiological relevance and systemic impact of the target gene or pathway in a whole-organism context. Furthermore, the absence of patient-derived xenograft (PDX) experiments in mice restricts the clinical translatability of our results. Future studies incorporating genetically engineered mouse models and PDX assays would be essential to strengthen the mechanistic and translational conclusions of this work.

Conclusions

In summary, our study shows that TCF19 expression is characteristically high in GC tissues and cells. Our gain- and loss-of-function experiments indicate that TCF19 acts as an oncogenic factor in GC, stimulating GC cell proliferation and migration through upregulation of MCM6. Although this study enhances our understanding of TCF19 function in GC, it has some limitations. First, the direct interaction between TCF19 and MCM6 was not investigated. Second, rescue studies to evaluate the impact of MCM6 expression on the growth of TCF19-deficient GC cells in vivo are still required to validate our in vitro findings. Third, further analysis of RNA sequencing data is warranted to identify additional pathways regulated by TCF19 in GC. Lastly, correlation analyses assessing the relationship between TCF19 and clinical outcomes will be necessary to establish TCF19 as a prognostic biomarker and treatment target in GC.
