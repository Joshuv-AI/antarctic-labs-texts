Fluid responsiveness in ARDS: current evidence, knowledge gaps, and future directions — a scoping review of the literature
Authors: Jorge Iván Alvarado-Sánchez, Juan Daniel Caicedo-Ruiz, María José Torres-Martínez, Juan José Diaztagle-Fernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPredicting fluid responsiveness in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains a clinical challenge. While dynamic indices such as pulse pressure variation (PPV), stroke volume variation (SVV), and functional tests like passive leg raising (PLR), end-expiratory occlusion test (EEOT), and tidal volume challenge (VTC) are widely used in critical care, their reliability in ARDS—characterized by altered lung mechanics and heterogeneous pathophysiology—has not been clearly established.ObjectiveTo systematically map the available evidence on predictors of fluid responsiveness in ARDS, including their performance under different ventilatory settings, the use of extracorporeal support, and the influence of respiratory mechanics and phenotypic variability.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following PRISMA-ScR and Arksey & O’Malley guidelines. A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library was performed from January 1999 to October 2024. Studies were included if they evaluated any fluid responsiveness predictor in adult ARDS patients and reported performance metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, or area under the curve (AUC). Data were extracted in duplicate and synthesized narratively.ResultsTen studies were included. PPV was the most frequently evaluated predictor, followed by SVV, EEOT, VTC, and PLR. Only two studies assessed patients in the prone position, and two included patients supported with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO). No study included spontaneously breathing patients or stratified results by ARDS phenotype. Diagnostic performance varied substantially across clinical contexts. Unadjusted PPV showed inconsistent diagnostic performance, particularly in patients with low respiratory system compliance or under prone positioning, whereas adjusted PPV, defined as PPV normalized to respiratory mechanics (e.g., driving pressure, transpulmonary pressure, respiratory system compliance, and chest wall elastance), demonstrated improved diagnostic performance in selected cohorts. Overall, predictor performance was highly dependent on ventilatory strategy, extracorporeal support, and validation methodology.ConclusionThe diagnostic performance of fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS is highly variable and context-dependent, influenced by ventilatory strategy, respiratory mechanics, extracorporeal support, and methodological heterogeneity. Important evidence gaps persist, particularly in prone ventilation, VV-ECMO, and spontaneously breathing patients. Future studies should integrate measures of fluid tolerance and stratify patients by ARDS phenotype to support individualized fluid management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03910-z.

Introduction

Intravenous fluid administration remains a cornerstone of resuscitation in critically ill patients, aiming to restore effective circulating volume and improve tissue perfusion. A positive fluid response is commonly defined as an increase in cardiac output (CO) of ≥ 15% following a fluid challenge or preload responsiveness test [1–5]. However, current guidelines emphasize that fluid therapy should be reserved for patients with clinical signs of tissue hypoperfusion—such as hypotension, oliguria, elevated lactate levels, or poor peripheral perfusion—rather than administered empirically. Inappropriately administered fluids can exacerbate organ dysfunction and increase morbidity and mortality, particularly in syndromes marked by capillary leak and pulmonary edema, such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [6]. Importantly, beyond predicting whether cardiac output will increase, clinicians must also consider fluid tolerance, defined as the capacity of a patient to receive intravenous fluids without developing signs of congestion, worsening oxygenation, right ventricular overload, or increased extravascular lung water.

To improve the precision of fluid management, several dynamic predictors of fluid responsiveness have been developed. These include pulse pressure variation (PPV) [7] and stroke volume variation (SVV) [8], which leverage cardiopulmonary interactions during mechanical ventilation, as well as functional maneuvers such as passive leg raising (PLR) [9], the end-expiratory occlusion test (EEOT) [10], and the tidal volume challenge (VTC) [11]. While these tools are supported by robust evidence in general ICU populations, their validity and performance in ARDS remain uncertain due to the profound physiological alterations characteristic of this syndrome.

ARDS is defined by widespread endothelial injury, alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, leading to impaired oxygenation and decreased respiratory system compliance [12]. These mechanical changes limit the transmission of airway pressure fluctuations to the cardiovascular system, which can reduce the diagnostic performance of dynamic preload indicators. Furthermore, ARDS is frequently complicated by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular dysfunction, and the need for high levels of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP)—all of which distort the physiological conditions required for accurate fluid responsiveness prediction. Intra-abdominal hypertension and the altered hemodynamic interactions induced by prone positioning and extracorporeal support (e.g., veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [VV-ECMO]) further confound the interpretation of conventional predictors [12].

Clinical trials have underscored the importance of a cautious approach to fluid therapy in ARDS. The landmark Fluid and Catheter Treatment Trial (FACTT) demonstrated that a conservative fluid strategy improves lung mechanics and reduces ventilator days, although without a clear mortality benefit [6]. More recently, the recognition of distinct biological subphenotypes within ARDS has further challenged the concept of uniform fluid management. In particular, the study by Famous et al. revealed differential responses to fluid strategies among hyperinflammatory and hypoinflammatory ARDS phenotypes, suggesting that fluid needs and tolerance are not homogeneous across all patients [13].

Given the physiological complexity of ARDS, this scoping review aims to map the existing literature on fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS patients, evaluating their applicability across different ARDS definitions, ventilatory strategies (supine vs. prone positioning), and ECMO use. Additionally, we aim to identify knowledge gaps and propose future research directions, with a particular emphasis on shifting from a fluid responsiveness-centered approach to a fluid tolerance-based strategy that prioritizes individual patient physiology.

This review was developed using the study selection and search strategy originally designed for a systematic review and meta-analysis (currently unpublished), which aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of these predictors. While that project focused on statistical synthesis, the current scoping review offers a broader clinical and physiologic perspective, exploring the settings, limitations, and heterogeneity in the application of these tools within the ARDS population.

Methods

Design

This scoping review was conducted by a multidisciplinary research team composed of experts in critical care, hemodynamic monitoring, and ARDS fluid management. The literature search and study selection process were originally developed for a systematic review and meta-analysis (data not yet published) aimed at evaluating the diagnostic performance of fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS [14]. However, the current scoping review was independently conceptualized to explore broader clinical and physiologic themes, following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines [15, 16]. Five key stages were followed: identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, selecting eligible studies, charting the data, and collating, summarizing, and reporting the results.

Identifying the research question

This scoping review was guided by the question: “What fluid responsiveness predictors have been studied in ARDS, and what are the knowledge gaps in their application across different clinical settings?”. To address this, we categorized studies based on:

Title and abstract relevance screening

A two-stage screening process was applied. In stage 1, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts. If abstracts were unavailable, the full text was retrieved for screening. Eligibility criteria were displayed within RAYYAN to enhance reviewer consistency. Conflicts were resolved through consensus meetings.

In stage 2, full-text articles were reviewed independently by two reviewers, applying eligibility criteria.

Two reviewers performed independent data extraction in batches of five studies. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings.

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

Extracted data were exported to Excel (Microsoft Corporation) for descriptive analysis. Findings were summarized in a narrative synthesis, categorizing predictors based on ventilation strategy (supine vs. prone), ARDS definition, and ECMO use. The review also identified gaps in the literature.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool [17]. This framework evaluates four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Each domain was rated as having low, high, or unclear risk of bias.

Additionally, applicability concerns were evaluated across three domains—patient selection, index test, and reference standard—using the same classification.

Results

Summary of studies evaluating fluid responsiveness in ARDS

Fifteen studies were initially considered eligible after full-text review; however, five were excluded following final eligibility verification (one due to study design and four because ARDS patients were not analyzed as a distinct cohort), leaving 10 studies for inclusion in the final synthesis. Reasons for exclusion were documented in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). A total of 10 studies met the inclusion criteria [18–27], evaluating various fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS patients (Table 1). These studies differed in ARDS definitions, ventilatory strategies, fluid challenge volumes, and hemodynamic assessment methods. While two studies applied the AECC definition [18, 19], the remaining eight studies used the Berlin definition, and none utilized the newest ARDS definition proposed in 2023. Regarding ventilatory strategy, eight studies assessed fluid responsiveness in mechanically ventilated patients in the supine position, while only two studies incorporated prone positioning [23, 25]. No studies evaluated fluid responsiveness in spontaneously breathing ARDS patients. Fluid challenge volumes ranged between 250 mL and 500 mL, using either colloids, normal saline, or lactated Ringer’s solution; however, four studies did not perform a fluid challenge, relying instead on alternative hemodynamic tests to assess preload dependency [21, 25–27].

In all included studies, preload tests and reference standard assessments were performed in the same body position in which patients were being ventilated (supine or prone).

The most commonly evaluated predictors were Pulse Pressure Variation (PPV) and Stroke Volume Variation (SVV), analyzed in eight [18–20, 22–25, 27] and three studies [18, 24, 26], respectively. Other dynamic indices assessed included Passive Leg Raising (PLR) in one study [20], End-Expiratory Occlusion Test (EEOT) in three studies [21, 23, 25], and Tidal Volume Challenge (VTC) in two studies [23, 25]. Additionally, some studies incorporated alternative hemodynamic parameters, such as ΔCCI TREND (one study) [23], ΔVmaxTP (one study) [26], ΔVTI_TP (one study) [26], ΔIVC (two studies) [26, 27], ΔSVC (two studies) [26, 27], and ΔVmaxAO (two studies) [26, 27]. Several studies further adjusted PPV by incorporating additional physiological parameters such as driving pressure, transpulmonary pressure, compliance of the respiratory system, and chest wall elastance [19, 22, 24]. Two studies examined fluid responsiveness in VV-ECMO patients [20, 26].

Performance of fluid responsiveness predictors

The diagnostic performance of the different predictors varied significantly (Supplementary Material, table S1):

Pulse Pressure Variation (PPV)

PPV was the most frequently evaluated predictor, assessed in eight studies across different ARDS definitions and ventilatory contexts [18, 19, 22–25, 27]. Two early investigations applied the AECC definition [18, 19], whereas subsequent studies used the Berlin criteria [18, 19, 22–25, 27].

Most analyses were conducted in invasively mechanically ventilated patients in the supine position, using protective tidal volumes between approximately 6 and 8 mL/kg of predicted body weight [16, 20, 22, 25]. Two studies evaluated PPV in the prone position (Yonis et al. [21]; Shi et al. [23]). When reported, respiratory system compliance varied substantially across cohorts [16, 17, 20, 23].

In supine mechanically ventilated patients, unadjusted PPV demonstrated moderate to good diagnostic performance, with AUC values ranging approximately from 0.67 to 0.85 [18, 22, 24, 27]. Some studies also evaluated adjusted PPV indices, normalized to respiratory mechanics parameters (e.g., driving pressure, transpulmonary pressure, respiratory system compliance, and chest wall elastance) [19, 22, 24].

In prone positioning, reported AUC values ranged from approximately 0.49 to 0.85 [23, 25]. Validation methods differed among studies, with some assessing preload responsiveness using a fluid challenge [23] and others using reversible preload maneuvers [25].

Overall, PPV demonstrated variable diagnostic performance across ventilatory settings and study designs, with reported AUC values ranging from poor to good depending on the cohort and validation strategy.

Stroke volume variation (SVV)

SVV was evaluated in three studies conducted in mechanically ventilated ARDS patients [18, 24, 26]. Protective tidal volumes ranged from approximately 6 to 7.5 mL/kg of predicted body weight across studies, and when reported, respiratory system compliance spanned from moderately reduced to relatively preserved values [18, 24, 26].

Overall, SVV demonstrated variable diagnostic performance. In supine mechanically ventilated patients, Chen et al. [24] reported moderate diagnostic performance of SVV, with AUC values generally between approximately 0.75 and 0.79, depending on PEEP level and adjustment strategy. In contrast, results were less consistent in earlier cohorts [18], where diagnostic performance was modest.

In patients supported with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO), diagnostic performance appeared limited. Loosen et al. [26] reported poor diagnostic performance when SVV was derived from echocardiographic measurements (SVV_Echo), with AUC values close to 0.5. When assessed using pulse contour analysis (SVV_PCA), diagnostic performance improved but remained variable and threshold-dependent, reaching moderate levels at selected cutoffs.

Passive Leg Raising (PLR)

PLR was evaluated in a single study conducted in patients supported with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [20]. In this cohort, several hemodynamic responses to PLR were analyzed, including changes in stroke volume (PLR-SV), cardiac output (PLR-CO), pump outflow (PLR-PO), and pulse pressure (PLR-PP).

Among these parameters, only PLR-induced changes in stroke volume demonstrated relevant diagnostic performance, reaching good diagnostic performance at selected thresholds. In contrast, PLR-PO and PLR-PP showed limited or no diagnostic performance. Although multiple cutoffs were explored for PLR-SV and PLR-CO, comprehensive AUC reporting was not consistently provided across all thresholds, limiting detailed comparison.

End-Expiratory Occlusion Test (EEOT)

EEOT was evaluated in three studies conducted in mechanically ventilated ARDS patients [21, 23, 25]. Protective tidal volumes ranged between approximately 6 and 7 mL/kg of predicted body weight across studies, and when reported, respiratory system compliance was moderately reduced [21, 23, 25].

Diagnostic performance varied across cohorts, with Silva et al. [20] reporting high diagnostic performance, with AUC values ranging from approximately 0.90 to 0.96 depending on PEEP level. Similarly, Shi et al. [25] described strong diagnostic performance, with AUC values above 0.90 at selected thresholds. In contrast, Yonis et al. [23] reported lower diagnostic performance, with AUC values in the moderate range and reduced sensitivity.

Validation strategies differed among studies. Yonis et al. [23] assessed preload responsiveness using a fluid challenge, whereas Silva et al. and Shi et al. used reversible preload maneuvers as reference tests [21, 25]. One of the included studies was conducted in the prone position [23], while the others evaluated patients in the supine position [21, 25].

Tidal Volume Challenge (VTC)

VTC was evaluated in two studies conducted in mechanically ventilated ARDS patients [23, 25]. In both investigations, baseline tidal volume was set at 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight and transiently increased to 8 mL/kg to assess preload responsiveness. When reported, respiratory system compliance ranged between approximately 30 and 32 mL·cm H₂O⁻¹ [23, 25].

Reported diagnostic performance differed between studies, with Shi et al. [25] reporting high diagnostic performance when VTC-induced changes in PPV were used to assess fluid responsiveness, with sensitivity and specificity values exceeding 0.85 and an AUC in the high range. In contrast, Yonis et al. [23] reported lower overall diagnostic performance, with high sensitivity but reduced specificity and a moderate AUC value.

Validation strategies differed between studies, with Yonis et al. [23] assessing preload responsiveness using a fluid challenge, whereas Shi et al. [25] used reversible preload maneuvers.

Other predictors of fluid responsiveness

Other predictors were evaluated less frequently and demonstrated variable performance across studies.

The change in continuous cardiac index during the Trendelenburg maneuver (ΔCCI TREND) was assessed in one study [23]. In this cohort, ΔCCI TREND demonstrated good diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and specificity values above 0.85 and an AUC of approximately 0.90.

Two studies evaluated echocardiographic-derived indices [26, 27]. In patients supported with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, Loosen et al. [26] assessed ΔSVC, ΔIVC, ΔVmaxAO, ΔVmaxTP, and ΔVTI_TP. Reported AUC values for these parameters ranged from approximately 0.55 to 0.67, indicating modest diagnostic performance.

In a non-ECMO population, Joseph et al. [27] evaluated ΔSVC, ΔIVC, and ΔVmaxAO. Reported AUC values ranged from approximately 0.63 to 0.76, with variable sensitivity and specificity across indices.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes the available evidence on fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS and demonstrates substantial heterogeneity in their reported diagnostic performance. Across the ten included studies, PPV and SVV were the most frequently evaluated indices, followed by EEOT, VTC, and PLR. Diagnostic performance ranged from poor to high depending on ventilatory setting, extracorporeal support, and validation strategy. Variability in ARDS definitions, mechanical ventilation parameters, respiratory system compliance, measurement techniques, and reference standards further contributed to differences in reported diagnostic performance. These findings underscore the combined influence of clinical context and methodological design on the performance of fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS.

Methodological heterogeneity and reference standards

A central finding of this review is the substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies evaluating fluid responsiveness in ARDS. Variability was observed not only in the predictors assessed but also in the reference standards used to define preload responsiveness, measurement techniques, ventilatory settings, and patient selection.

This heterogeneity can be conceptually organized into three major domains: [1] variability in ventilatory settings, including tidal volume, positive end-expiratory pressure, and driving pressure; [2] differences in respiratory system mechanics, particularly compliance; and [3] heterogeneity in validation strategies and reference standards. These domains interact to influence the reliability of dynamic predictors, as they directly affect the magnitude and transmission of cardiopulmonary interactions.

Reference standards differed notably. Some studies confirmed responsiveness using a true fluid challenge [18–20, 22–24], whereas others relied on reversible preload maneuvers or surrogate dynamic tests [21, 25–27]. Given that indices such as EEOT and VTC are themselves preload-modifying interventions, validation against other reversible tests may introduce partial circularity and affect reported diagnostic performance.

In addition, variability in the threshold used to define fluid responsiveness (ranging from ≥ 10% to ≥ 15% increase in cardiac output or stroke volume) and differences in fluid challenge protocols (type, volume, and use of reversible preload maneuvers) may directly influence reported sensitivity and specificity. These differences may lead to inconsistent classification of responders and non-responders, thereby limiting comparability across studies and contributing to the observed heterogeneity in diagnostic performance.

Beyond reference standard variability, additional methodological differences were observed across studies. Measurement approaches were also inconsistent. SVV was derived using pulse contour analysis in some cohorts [24] and echocardiographic methods in others, particularly under VV-ECMO support [26]. Similarly, echocardiographic indices varied in acquisition and thresholds [26, 27]. Study populations differed as well, including supine mechanically ventilated patients [18, 19, 21, 22, 24, 27], prone cohorts [23, 25], and VV-ECMO populations [20, 26].

Most studies were small, single-center diagnostic investigations with variable threshold definitions and incomplete reporting of respiratory mechanics. Together, these methodological differences limit direct comparability across cohorts and likely contribute to the heterogeneity observed in reported diagnostic performance.

The structured risk-of-bias assessment provides additional context for interpreting these findings. Although most studies were classified as having a low overall risk of bias, concerns identified in the index test and reference standard domains may partially explain the variability in reported diagnostic performance. In particular, inconsistent threshold definitions and differences in validation strategies likely contributed to heterogeneity across studies. Therefore, the observed variability should not be interpreted solely as physiological but also as reflecting underlying methodological differences.

Therefore, differences in reported AUCs across supine, prone, or VV-ECMO settings should be interpreted cautiously, as part of the observed variability may reflect methodological heterogeneity rather than true physiological differences in diagnostic performance.

Physiological determinants in ARDS

From a physiological perspective, ARDS introduces several challenges to the accurate prediction of fluid responsiveness.

ARDS is characterized by a heterogeneous inflammatory process involving epithelial and endothelial injury [28], leading to regional differences in lung mechanics and ventilation distribution, which may impair the transmission of airway pressure to the intrathoracic compartment. These limitations can be understood within a physiology-driven framework involving three main mechanisms: [1] impaired transmission of airway pressure to the intrathoracic compartment due to reduced respiratory system compliance; [2] alterations in pleural pressure and venous return dynamics, particularly under high PEEP and prone positioning; and [3] right ventricular loading conditions and ventriculo–pulmonary interactions. Together, these factors disrupt the fundamental assumption that cyclic changes in intrathoracic pressure are reliably transmitted to cardiac preload, thereby limiting the performance of dynamic fluid responsiveness indices.

Reduced pulmonary compliance may limit the transmission of airway pressure variations to the cardiovascular system [12], attenuating heart–lung interactions and thereby reducing the sensitivity and specificity of dynamic indices such as PPV and SVV [29, 30].

In the included studies, cohorts with relatively preserved compliance—such as Lakhal et al. [19] and Liu et al. [22] —tended to report more favorable PPV performance, whereas those with lower compliance values, such as Yonis et al. [23], demonstrated more variable or attenuated results. Although prior meta-regressions [2, 3] have linked reduced compliance with diminished PPV performance, respiratory mechanics were not consistently reported across all ARDS cohorts included here, and compliance ranges frequently overlapped. Therefore, compliance should be viewed as one contributing physiological determinant—alongside ventilatory settings, right ventricular loading, and extracorporeal support—rather than a sole explanatory mechanism. Notably, when PPV was adjusted for respiratory mechanics (e.g., driving pressure or chest wall elastance), its diagnostic performance improved in some cohorts [19, 22, 24], suggesting that integrating mechanical context enhances the interpretability of dynamic indices.

Abdominal hypertension may also interfere with fluid responsiveness testing, particularly for maneuvers such as passive leg raising. Increased intra-abdominal pressure can alter venous return and modify preload redistribution. However, none of the included studies—including the PLR cohort evaluated by Guinot et al. [20] —systematically measured or reported intra-abdominal pressure, limiting assessment of its potential influence.

Prone positioning represents another relevant physiological modifier. Although prone ventilation improves oxygenation, promotes a more homogeneous distribution of ventilation, enhances ventilation–perfusion matching, and modifies pleural pressure gradients [31]—thereby altering cardiopulmonary interactions—it also affects venous return, right ventricular loading conditions, and intrathoracic pressure gradients [32]. These changes may modify the hemodynamic response to transient preload variations and contribute to variability in predictor performance under prone conditions, as observed in cohorts studied by Yonis et al. and Shi et al. [23, 25].

Similarly, patients receiving veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) constitute a distinct physiological context. Although VV-ECMO does not replace intrinsic cardiac function, it substantially modifies venous return, preload dynamics, and flow distribution [33]. Negative drainage pressures generated by the venous cannula may reduce effective preload and induce partial venous collapse. In addition, extracorporeal circulation can alter flow patterns and attenuate arterial pulsatility, particularly under deep sedation or neuromuscular blockade. The coexistence of native and artificial circulations introduces non-physiological pressure–flow relationships that may impair the assumptions underlying dynamic preload indices such as PPV and SVV, as reflected in ECMO cohorts evaluated by Guinot et al. and Loosen et al. [18, 26].

In addition, right ventricular dysfunction is common in ARDS due to increased pulmonary vascular resistance, hypoxic vasoconstriction, and high intrathoracic pressures. Most dynamic preload indices primarily reflect variations in left ventricular stroke volume and may not adequately capture right ventricular overload. In this context, left ventricular filling may appear preload-dependent while the right ventricle is already volume-stressed. Under such circumstances, indices such as PPV could suggest fluid responsiveness from a left-sided perspective, yet fluid administration might worsen right ventricular dilation and impair overall hemodynamics. Importantly, detailed assessment of right ventricular function and arrhythmia prevalence was inconsistently reported across studies, further limiting physiologic interpretation [34].

Taken together, ARDS is characterized by altered cardiopulmonary interactions that challenge the physiological foundations of dynamic fluid responsiveness markers. Reduced compliance [19, 22, 23], changes in ventricular loading conditions during prone positioning [23, 25], and extracorporeal flow dynamics [20, 26], may all limit the reliability of conventional preload-dependent indices in this population.

Understudied populations and knowledge gaps

This scoping review identified several important gaps in the current literature regarding the assessment of fluid responsiveness in patients with ARDS, which represent not only areas for future research but also key limitations of the current body of evidence.

First, no study evaluated fluid responsiveness in spontaneously breathing ARDS patients. All included investigations were conducted in fully controlled invasive mechanical ventilation, with or without ECMO support [18–27]. Importantly, this gap extends not only to non-intubated patients—such as those receiving high-flow nasal cannula under the updated ARDS definition [35] —but also to intubated patients who retain spontaneous breathing activity while receiving assisted ventilatory support. This limitation is particularly relevant, as spontaneous breathing profoundly alters cardiopulmonary interactions and may invalidate the physiological assumptions underlying dynamic preload indices. Therefore, the applicability of these predictors to a substantial proportion of ARDS patients in real-world settings remains uncertain.

Second, evidence during prone positioning remains limited. Only two studies assessed fluid responsiveness under prone ventilation [23, 25], as discussed previously. Given the widespread use of prone positioning in moderate-to-severe ARDS, this limited representation restricts the external validity of current findings. Further validation in this context is warranted.

Third, patients supported with veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation are underrepresented. Only two investigations included ECMO populations [20, 26], and dynamic predictors have not been systematically validated under extracorporeal support [33]. Given the widespread use of prone positioning in moderate-to-severe ARDS, this limited representation restricts the external validity of current findings. Further validation in this context is warranted.

Fourth, heterogeneity in validation methods persists across studies. Although fluid challenge was commonly used as a reference standard [18–20, 22–24], some investigations relied on surrogate preload maneuvers [19, 21, 23–27]. This variability may lead to inconsistent classification of responders and directly affect estimates of diagnostic performance, further limiting comparability across studies. Standardization of reference methods remains necessary to improve comparability.

Fifth, although most studies applied the Berlin definition, none stratified diagnostic performance according to ARDS severity (moderate vs. severe). Respiratory system compliance was inconsistently reported, and in several cohorts mechanical impairment appeared relatively preserved. As a result, it remains unclear whether differences in predictor performance reflect severity-related physiological alterations or other contextual factors. This limitation constrains interpretation, as patients with more severe mechanical impairment may exhibit fundamentally different cardiopulmonary interactions. The performance of dynamic indices in patients with severely reduced compliance therefore remains insufficiently characterized.

Sixth, none of the included studies stratified analyses according to ARDS biological subphenotypes [13], despite increasing recognition of inflammatory and recruitability-based heterogeneity. These pathophysiological differences likely contribute to variability in predictor performance, and future studies should incorporate phenotype stratification to improve the precision of fluid management strategies. Moreover, emerging evidence—including data from our group—suggests that the diagnostic performance of fluid responsiveness predictors is influenced not only by ventilatory mechanics but also by vascular tone and vasopressor exposure, particularly norepinephrine [3, 36]. These findings indicate that systemic hemodynamic conditions may substantially modulate predictor reliability in critically ill patients.

Finally, no study integrated markers of fluid tolerance, such as extravascular lung water index (ELWI), venous congestion scores, or pulmonary edema burden. As a result, current evidence focuses exclusively on detecting the probability of a hemodynamic response, without evaluating whether this response can be safely pursued [37]. This represents a critical limitation, as fluid responsiveness does not necessarily equate to fluid tolerance in ARDS patients.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that current evidence is derived from highly selected and controlled conditions, which may not fully reflect the complexity of real-world ARDS populations.

Addressing these gaps may contribute to a more physiologically informed and individualized approach to fluid management in ARDS.

Implications of evolving ARDS definitions

None of the included studies adopted the 2023 ARDS definition [35], which broadens diagnostic criteria and incorporates patients receiving high-flow nasal cannula. All available data were derived from invasively mechanically ventilated cohorts defined according to AECC or Berlin criteria [18–27]. As ARDS definitions evolve and encompass less invasively supported populations, the physiological substrate underlying fluid responsiveness assessment may differ from that of deeply sedated, fully ventilated patients.

The expansion of ARDS criteria toward earlier and potentially less severe stages raises important questions regarding the applicability of traditional dynamic indices, which were largely developed and validated under controlled mechanical ventilation. Future investigations should examine whether preload responsiveness testing remains reliable and feasible across the broader clinical spectrum defined by contemporary ARDS frameworks.

Limitations of the present review

This review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although a structured risk-of-bias assessment (QUADAS-2) was performed, no quantitative synthesis of bias impact was conducted, which limits formal grading of evidence.

Second, the number of included studies was limited, reflecting both strict eligibility criteria and the scarcity of ARDS-specific validation cohorts. Although a comprehensive search strategy was applied, it is possible that studies evaluating ARDS subgroups within broader ICU populations were not captured if ARDS-specific data were not separately reported or identifiable during screening. This may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant data. In addition, grey literature sources (e.g., conference abstracts and trial registries) were not systematically searched, which may have further limited the identification of unpublished or difficult-to-identify studies.

Third, substantial heterogeneity in definitions, thresholds, ventilatory settings, reference standards, and type and volume of fluid administered (crystalloids vs. colloids) precluded meta-analysis and limits direct comparability across cohorts. Variability in fluid composition may influence the magnitude of preload increase and, consequently, the apparent diagnostic performance of responsiveness tests. Reported diagnostic performance should therefore be interpreted within the context of individual study design.

Fourth, diagnostic accuracy studies are inherently susceptible to publication bias, as studies reporting higher diagnostic performance are more likely to be published, which may lead to an overestimation of the true diagnostic performance of fluid responsiveness predictors. Fifth, several physiologically relevant variables—such as intra-abdominal pressure, detailed right ventricular function, and standardized respiratory mechanics reporting—were inconsistently measured or reported across studies. As a result, the ability to fully contextualize predictor performance is constrained by the available data.

Finally, all included studies were single-center diagnostic performance investigations with relatively small sample sizes, which may limit external validity.

Despite these limitations, this review provides a structured synthesis of the current evidence and identifies critical gaps that warrant further investigation.

Clinical implications and conclusion

The assessment of fluid responsiveness in ARDS should not be interpreted in isolation from the underlying pathophysiology. Although dynamic predictors estimate the probability of increasing cardiac output, they do not determine whether fluid administration is clinically appropriate or safe in a syndrome characterized by capillary leak and reduced pulmonary reserve.

In this context, fluid tolerance becomes as important as fluid responsiveness. An intervention that improves stroke volume may simultaneously worsen oxygenation, increase pulmonary edema, or aggravate right ventricular stress. Therefore, dynamic indices should be integrated within a broader hemodynamic evaluation that considers respiratory mechanics, ventricular function, and markers of congestion.

In summary, the performance of fluid responsiveness predictors in ARDS is highly context-dependent. While several indices show potential under selected conditions, their generalizability across the heterogeneous spectrum of ARDS remains uncertain. Future research should aim to integrate responsiveness and tolerance within a more individualized and physiologically grounded approach to fluid management.
