Inference on High Dimensional Selective Labeling Models
Shakeeb Khan, Elie Tamer, Qingsong Yao

Abstract




Abstract

The paper reconsiders the problem of inference on parameters in binary outcome models when these outcomes are subject to possibly endogenous censoring. Recently, these models have gained increasing interest in the computer science and machine learning literatures where the issue of endogenous sample selection is referred to as the selective labels problem. Such models are relevant in diverse empirical settings, including criminal justice, healthcare, and insurance. Notable recent studies in this area include
Lakkaraju et al. (2017)
,
Kleinberg et al. (2018)
, and
Coston, Rambachan and Chouldechova (2021)
, which examine judicial bail decisions—where the outcome of whether a defendant fails to appear in court is observed only if the judge grants bail. Inference on such model parameters can be computationally challenging for two reasons. One is the nonconcavity of the bivariate likelihood function,
and the other is the large number of covariates in each equation. Despite these hurdles, we propose a novel distribution free estimation procedure
that is computationally friendly especially in the many covariates settings. The new method combines the semiparametric batched gradient descent algorithm introduced in
Khan, Lan, Tamer and Yao (2024)
with a novel sorting algorithm incorporated to control for selection bias. Asymptotic properties of the new procedure are established under increasing dimension conditions in both equations, and its finite sample properties are explored through a simulation study and an application using Stanford Open Policing Project data set
(
Pierson et al. 2020
)
. Extensions to models with endogenous treatment are also proposed.

Key Words:
Selective Label Models, Semiparametric Batched Gradient Descent, Selection Bias.

JEL Codes:
C14, C31, C35, C63.

Introduction

This paper addresses the challenge of inference on large-dimensional selective labeling models with binary outcomes. These models emerge in numerous domains where the observed binary outcomes result from the choices made by one of the agents within the system. Recently, they have garnered significant attention in the fields of computer science and machine learning, where this issue is known as the “selective labels problem”, or as an endogenous sample selection problem. 2.1
The First-Step Estimator

We first introduce the algorithm for estimating
\bm{\delta}_{0}
. Define
\bm{\phi}_{\bm{\delta},q_{\bm{\delta}}}(\cdot)=\left(\phi_{0}(\cdot),\cdots,\phi_{q_{\bm{\delta}}}(\cdot)\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
, where
\phi_{0}(\cdot),\phi_{1}(\cdot),\cdots
are a sequence of basis functions, and
q_{\bm{\delta}}
is the order of sieve approximation used in the first-step estimation. For the choice of basis functions, see
Chen (2007)
and
Khan et al. (2024)
. The algorithm is described as follows.

Algorithm 0 for estimating
\bm{\delta}_{0}

1.

Start with
k=0
and
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{0},\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{0},\widehat{F}_{U}^{0}(\cdot)
, where
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{0}\in R^{p_{Z}}
is the initial guess of
\bm{\delta}_{0}
,
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{0}\in R^{q_{\delta}+1}
is the initial guess of the pseudo true sieve coefficient, and
\widehat{F}_{U}^{0}(\cdot)
is the initial guess of
F_{U}(\cdot)
, the CDF of
U_{i}
.

2.

With
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k}
, define
\widehat{Z}_{i,k}=z_{0,i}+\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k}
, and update the sieve coefficient to
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{k+1}
using the following

\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{k+1}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i,k})\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i,k})^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i,k})D_{i}\right)

3.

With
\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{k+1}
, update
\widehat{F}_{U}^{k}(\cdot)
to
\widehat{F}_{U}^{k+1}(\cdot)
by
\widehat{F}_{U}^{k+1}(\cdot)=\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\cdot)^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\pi}}^{k+1}
.

4.

With
\widehat{F}_{U}^{k+1}(\cdot)
, update
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k}
to
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k+1}
using

\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k+1}=\widehat{\bm{\delta}}^{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{F}_{U}^{k+1}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i,k}\right)-D_{i}\right)\bm{Z}_{i}

\gamma_{k}>0
is learning rate.

5.

Set
k=k+1
and go back to Step 2 unless some terminating conditions are satisfied.

2.2
The Second-Step Estimator

Denote the first-step estimator as
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
. With
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
in hand, we now consider estimating
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. As in this SBGD, we will need to control for selection bias by explicitly estimating the selection correction function. To provide some intuition, suppose that we know the joint CDF of
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
, then the probability of
V_{i}<v
conditioned on
U_{i}<u
is given by

P(V_{i}<v|U_{i}<u)=\frac{F(u,v)}{F_{U}(u)}\equiv G(u,v).

Define
Z_{0,i}=z_{0,i}+\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
X_{0,i}=x_{0,i}+\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}_{0}
, we have that

E(Y_{i}|\bm{Z}_{e,i},\bm{X}_{e,i},D_{i}=1)=G(Z_{0,i},X_{0,i}).

The key observation here is that
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is increasing with respect to its second argument. Suppose further that we also know
\bm{\delta}_{0}
. Define
\widehat{X}_{i,k}=x_{0,i}+\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}
, then batch gradient descent algorithm based on the loss function in
Khan et al. (2024)
immediately leads to the following iterative algorithm for estimating
\bm{\beta}_{0}
using only observations with
D_{i}=1
,

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k+1}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\left(G\left(Z_{0,i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)-Y_{i}\right)\bm{X}_{i},

(2.1)

where
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
is the number of observations whose first-step outcome is 1. Obviously, update (
2.1
) takes the index in the selection equation into consideration, so effectively controls for the selection bias.

However, since both
F(u,v)
and
\bm{\delta}_{0}
are unknown, the above algorithm is indeed infeasible. Note that the second issue can be easily resolved by plugging in our first-step estimator
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
, while the first remains unsolved. An intuitive solution to such issue is to obtain an estimator for the conditional expectation
G(u,v)
and then plug such estimator into update (
2.1
). In this section we propose two methods to estimate such conditional expectation, one being local in nature and the other global.

The first local estimator uses matching to control for selection bias similar to
Ahn and Powell (1993)
. To provide some intuition, suppose that the first-step estimator
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
is consistent and
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{k}
, the starting point in the
k
-th iteration, is close to
\bm{\beta}_{0}
. Define

\widehat{Z}_{i}=z_{0,i}+\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
, so long as
G
is smooth enough, we have that

E\left(\left.Y_{i}\right|\bm{Z}_{e,i},\bm{X}_{e,i},D_{i}=1\right)=G(Z_{0,i},X_{0,i})\approx G(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}).

The above result implies that for arbitrary

is close enough to
(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k})

can be used as a (noisy) replacement for

Ahn and Powell (1993)

We next propose an algorithmic approach which controls for selection bias by nonparametrically estimating the selection correction function globally
using method of sieves. This was done for the standard selection model with linear outcome equation in
Das, Newey and Vella (2003)
and
Newey (2009)
. Let
\bm{\Phi}_{q}(\cdot,\cdot)

be a
(q+1)^{2}
-dimensional vector of basis functions. Note that we can decompose
D_{i}Y_{i}
as follows

D_{i}Y_{i}=D_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Pi}_{q}+\mathcal{E}_{i,q,k},

(2.3)

where
\bm{\Pi}_{q}
is the unknown pseudo true sieve parameter vector
6
6

6

Note that for any sequence of sieve functions
\{\Phi_{st}(u,v)\}_{s,t=0}^{\infty}
that is complete in
C(R^{2})
space and any function
G(u,v)\in C(R^{2})
, there exists a sequence of sieve coefficients
\{\pi_{st}\}_{s,t=0}^{\infty}
such that
G(u,v)=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}\pi_{s,t}\Phi_{st}(u,v)
. Then
\bm{\Pi}_{q}
is the vector of first
(q+1)^{2}
sieve coefficients. See

for more detailed discussion.
, and
\mathcal{E}_{i,q,k}
is the error that can be decomposed as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{q,k,i}

\displaystyle=\underset{\text{Error due to truncation of the sieve space}}{\underbrace{D_{i}\left(G\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)-\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Pi}_{q}\right)}}+\underset{\text{Error due to first-step estimation}}{\underbrace{D_{i}\left(G\left(Z_{0,i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)-G\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)\right)}}

\displaystyle+\underset{\text{Error due to second-step estimation of the $k$-th round}}{\underbrace{D_{i}\left(G\left(Z_{0,i},X_{0,i}\right)-G\left(Z_{0,i},\widehat{X}_{i,k}\right)\right)}}+\underset{\text{Sampling randomness}}{\underbrace{D_{i}\left(Y_{i}-G\left(Z_{0,i},X_{0,i}\right)\right)}}.

When

is smooth enough, the first term on the right side of the above equation will be small as long as

is large. Moreover, suppose again that the first-step estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}^{k+1}_{q}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}\right)\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{-1}\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}Y_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}\right)\right],

(2.4)

and the unknown conditional expectation function

estimator. In particular, we assume that
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
has the following asymptotic linear
representation.

Condition 2
.

The first-step estimator
\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
satisfies

\left\|\sqrt{n}\left(\widehat{\bm{\delta}}-\bm{\delta}_{0}\right)-\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{\delta}}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\left(\bm{Z}_{e,i}\right)\left(D_{i}-F_{U}\left(Z_{0,i}\right)\right)\right\|=o_{P}\left(1\right),

where
\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{\delta}}
is a
p_{Z}\times p_{Z}
invertible
matrix and
\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\left(\cdot\right)
is a
p_{Z}\times 1

nonrandom function. Furthermore, there hold
0<\inf_{n}\underline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\right)<\sup_{n}\overline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\right)<\infty
and
\sup_{n}\|\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\|_{\infty}/\sqrt{p_{Z}}<\infty
.

Remark 2
.

The asymptotic linear representation in
2
simply repeats the results of Theorem 8 in
Khan et al. (2024)
; more primitive conditions that guarantee such condition can
be found therein. According to Theorem 8 of
Khan et al. (2024)
, we
have that
\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\left(\bm{Z}_{e}\right)=\bm{Z}-E_{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}_{e}}(\widetilde{\bm{Z}}|\widetilde{Z}_{0}=Z_{0})

and

\bm{\Psi}_{\delta}=E\left(\nabla F_{U}\left(Z_{0}\right)\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\left(\bm{Z}_{e}\right)\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)
, where
Z_{0}=z_{0}+\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}_{0}
,
\widetilde{Z}_{0}=\widetilde{z}_{0}+\widetilde{\bm{Z}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}_{0}
,
\widetilde{Z}_{e}
is an independent copy of
\bm{Z}_{e}
, and
E_{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}_{e}}
computes the expectation with respect to
\widetilde{\bm{Z}}_{e}
. Moreover, under
2
,
E\|\bm{\Psi}_{\bm{\delta}}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\psi}_{\bm{\delta}}\left(\bm{Z}_{e,i}\right)\left(D_{i}-F_{U}\left(Z_{0,i}\right)\right)\|^{2}\lesssim p_{Z}
, so
\|\widehat{\bm{\delta}}-\bm{\delta}_{0}\|=O_{P}(\sqrt{p_{Z}/n})
.

The next condition regulates the data generating process.

Condition 3
.

For all
n
, there hold: (i)
\mathscr{Z}_{e}=\left[0,1\right]^{p_{Z}+1}
and
\mathscr{X}_{e}=\left[0,1\right]^{p_{X}+1}
;
(ii) There exists some constant
C_{0}>0
such that
\mathscr{D}\subseteq[-C_{0},C_{0}]^{p_{Z}}
and
\mathscr{B}\subseteq[-C_{0},C_{0}]^{p_{X}}
;
(iii) There exists a positive constant
C_{G}>0
such that
\left\|\nabla_{u}G(u,v)\right\|_{\infty},\left\|\nabla_{v}G(u,v)\right\|_{\infty}
,
\left\|\nabla_{uu}G(u,v)\right\|_{\infty}
,
\left\|\nabla_{uv}G(u,v)\right\|_{\infty}
, and
\left\|\nabla_{vv}G(u,v)\right\|_{\infty}
are upper bounded by
C_{G}
; (iv)
There exists some constant
C_{D}>0
such that
P_{D}\equiv P(D_{i}=1)=E(F_{U}(Z_{0,i}))\geq C_{D}
.

Remark 3
.

3
(i) simply normalizes the feature space. In principle our results apply to any scenarios with bounded feature space.
3
(iii) requires that the unknown conditional expectation
G(\cdot,\cdot)
is smooth enough so can be nonparametrically estimated. Finally,
3
(iv) requires that
S_{n}
increases roughly at the rate at
O(n)
. In particular, we have that
P(S_{n}>P_{D}n/2)>1-1/C_{D}^{2}n
.

3.1
Statistical Properties of Matching-Based Estimator

This subsection studies the statistical properties of the matching-based BGD algorithm. To ease exposition, define
Z(\bm{\delta})=z_{0}+\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}
,
Z_{i}(\bm{\delta})=z_{0,i}+\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}
,
X(\bm{\beta})=x_{0}+\bm{X}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}
, and
X_{i}(\bm{\beta})=x_{0,i}+\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}
. For any
\bm{\delta}\in\mathscr{D}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}
, define
\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta}}=\{Z(\bm{\delta}):\bm{Z}_{e}\in\mathscr{Z}_{e}\}
and
\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}=\{X(\bm{\beta}):\bm{X}_{e}\in\mathscr{X}_{e}\}
. We next introduce some additional technical conditions.

Condition 4
.

For each pair of
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\beta}
, denote the joint density of
Z(\bm{\delta})
and
X(\bm{\beta})
conditioned on event
D=1
as
\iota\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\delta},\bm{\beta}\right)
. There exists a sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
\alpha_{n}>0
for all
n
, and
\inf_{\bm{\delta}\in\mathscr{D},\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\inf_{\nu_{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta}},\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}}\iota\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\delta},\bm{\beta}\right)\geq C_{\iota}\alpha_{n}
, where
C_{\iota}>0
.

Remark 4
.

4
requires that for each sample size
n
, the joint density function of
Z(\bm{\delta})
and
X(\bm{\beta})
conditioned on
D=1
is lower bounded by positive constant that may depend on
n
. Such condition guarantees that with large probability, for any pair
(Z(\bm{\delta}),X(\bm{\beta}))
, we can find observations whose indices constructed based on
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\beta}
are sufficiently close to such pair as long as the sample size is large enough. For the case of fixed dimensionality, such restrictions are also imposed in
Ichimura (1993)
. We note that in some cases, the lower boundedness may fail to hold. But as long as we can find subsets of
\mathscr{Z}_{e}
and
\mathscr{X}_{e}
such that the condition holds, we can replace the above condition by introducing trimming to our algorithm. See
Khan et al. (2024)
for more details.

Condition 5
.

For any
\bm{\delta}\in\mathscr{D}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}
, define

\mathcal{Y}\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\delta},\bm{\beta}\right)=E\left(\left.Y\right|Z\left(\bm{\delta}\right)=\nu_{Z},X\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\nu_{X},D=1\right),\nu_{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta}},\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}

For any
\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{D}
,
\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}
,
\nu_{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta}}
,
\nu_{Z}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta^{\prime}}}
,
\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}
, and
\nu_{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta^{\prime}}}
, there holds

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{Y}\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\delta},\bm{\beta}\right)-\mathcal{Y}\left(\nu_{Z}^{\prime},\nu_{X}^{\prime},\bm{\delta}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\mathcal{Y}}\cdot\left(\left|\nu_{Z}-\nu_{Z}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\nu_{X}-\nu_{X}^{\prime}\right|+\sqrt{p_{Z}}\left\|\bm{\delta}-\bm{\delta}^{\prime}\right\|+\sqrt{p_{X}}\left\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\right\|\right),

where
C_{\mathcal{Y}}
is a positive constant.

Remark 5
.

We have that

\displaystyle E\left(\left.Y\right|D=1,Z\left(\bm{\delta}\right)=\nu_{Z},X\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\nu_{X}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left(\left.E\left(\left.Y\right|D=1,Z\left(\bm{\delta}\right)=\nu_{Z},X\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\nu_{X},\bm{Z}_{e},\bm{X}_{e}\right)\right|D=1,Z\left(\bm{\delta}\right)=\nu_{Z},X\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\nu_{X}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left(\left.G\left(Z_{0},X_{0}\right)\right|D=1,Z\left(\bm{\delta}\right)=\nu_{Z},X\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\nu_{X}\right).

We finally introduce a condition that can be used as a sufficient condition for contraction mapping.

Condition 6
.

For any
\bm{Z}_{e},\bm{X}_{e}
,
\bm{\beta}
, and
\varsigma\in[0,1]
, define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}_{M}\left(\bm{Z}_{e},\bm{X}_{e},\bm{\beta},\varsigma\right)=

\displaystyle E_{\widetilde{\bm{Z}}_{e},\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{e},\widetilde{D}}\left(\left.\nabla_{v}G\left(Z_{0},X_{0}+\varsigma\left(\bm{X}-\widetilde{\bm{X}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\beta}\right)\bm{X}\left(\bm{X}-\widetilde{\bm{X}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right|\widetilde{Z}_{0}=Z_{0},\widetilde{X}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=X\left(\bm{\beta}\right),\widetilde{D}=1\right),

and

\bm{\Psi}_{M}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}E_{\bm{Z}_{e},\bm{X}_{e},D}\left(\left.\widetilde{\bm{\Psi}}_{M}\left(\bm{Z}_{e},\bm{X}_{e},\bm{\beta},\varsigma\right)\right|D=1\right)d\varsigma

There hold
\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\overline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{M}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)+\bm{\Psi}_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{M}}<\infty

and
\inf_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\underline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{M}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)+\bm{\Psi}_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\geq\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{M}}>0.

Remark 6
.

6
is the key condition that guarantees the validity of iteration-based estimator. Under such assumption, we have that
\|\left(\bm{I}_{p_{X}}-\gamma\bm{\Psi}_{M}(\bm{\beta})\right)\bm{a}\|\leq C_{\gamma}\|\bm{a}\|
for some
0<C_{\gamma}<1
and arbitrary
\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}
and
\bm{a}\in R^{p_{X}}
. This guarantees the contraction map of the proposed algorithm. In general,
6
is more likely to hold when at least one regressor is continuous, which excludes the case where all the regressors are discrete. For examples of data generating processes satisfying
6
, see Remark 4 in
Khan et al. (2024)
.

Given the above conditions, we now state our theorem regrading the convergence rate of the matching-based estimator, whose proof is provided in Appendix, Section
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
1
–
6
hold,
n\alpha_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, and
\mathfrak{P}\leq n
. If we choose
m
such that
m/\log(n)\rightarrow\infty
and
m/n\rightarrow\infty
and a constant learning rate
\gamma_{k}=\gamma<\min\{\overline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{M}}^{-1},\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{M}}/2p_{X}^{2}C_{G}^{2}\}
,
then there holds

\sup_{k\geq k_{M}\left(n,m,\gamma\right)+1}\|\Delta\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}\|=O_{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathfrak{P}(\mathfrak{P}+m)\cdot\log\left(n\right)}{n\alpha_{n}}+\frac{\mathfrak{P}^{2}\log(n)}{m}}\right),

where

k_{M}(n,m,\gamma)=\frac{\log\left(\sqrt{\frac{\mathfrak{P}(\mathfrak{P}+m)\cdot\log\left(n\right)}{n\alpha_{n}}+\frac{\mathfrak{P}^{2}\log(n)}{m}}\right)-\log\left(\left\|\Delta\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{1}\right\|\right)}{\log(1-\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{M}}\gamma/4)}.

Theorem 1
states that the matching-based estimator will be consistent when the number of nearest-neighbor points
m
and the learning rate
\gamma_{k}
are properly chosen. For the rate of convergence, the first term corresponds to the bias and the second term variance. It’s intuitive that the bias increases with
m
, while the variance decreases with
m
. The optimal rate of
m
is
\sqrt{\mathfrak{P}n\alpha_{n}}
, and the corresponding convergence rate of the matching-based estimator is
\sqrt{\log(n)\left(\frac{\mathfrak{P}^{2}}{n\alpha_{n}}+\frac{\mathfrak{P}\sqrt{\mathfrak{P}}}{\sqrt{n\alpha_{n}}}\right)}
. Note that due to the sizable bias, the resulting estimator is not guaranteed to be
1/\sqrt{n}
-consistent. This issue will be resolved for the sieve-based estimator. Nevertheless, the matching based estimator is easy to implement so can be used as a computationally efficient first-step estimator.

3.2
Statistical Properties of Sieve-Based Estimator

This section studies the statistical properties of the sieve-based BGD algorithm
proposed in the previous section. We further introduce some technical conditions.

Condition 7
.

The vector of basis functions
\bm{\Phi}_{q}
satisfies:
(i) Let
\Phi_{j}(u,v)
denote the
j
-th argument of
\bm{\Phi}_{q}

\max\{\|\nabla_{u}\Phi_{j}\|_{\infty},\|\nabla_{v}\Phi_{j}\|_{\infty}\}\leq C_{\Phi,1,q}

\max\{\|\nabla_{uu}\Phi_{j}\|_{\infty},\|\nabla_{uv}\Phi_{j}\|_{\infty},\|\nabla_{vv}\Phi_{j}\|_{\infty}\}\leq C_{\Phi,2,q}
, where
C(0,q),C_{\bm{\Phi},1,q}
and
C_{\bm{\Phi},2,q}
are all positive constants that depend on
q
only, and moreover,
\log(\max\{C(0,q),C_{\bm{\Phi},1,q},C_{\bm{\Phi},2,q}\})=O(\log(n))
; (ii) Define
\bm{\Gamma}_{q}(\bm{\beta})=E[\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(Z_{0,i},X_{i}(\bm{\beta})\right)\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(Z_{0,i},X_{i}(\bm{\beta})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}|D_{i}=1].

There exist
0<\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Phi}}\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Phi}}<\infty

such that
\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Phi}}\leq\inf_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\underline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{q}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\leq\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\overline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Gamma}_{q}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Phi}}

for all

;
(iii)
\left\|G\left(u,v\right)-\bm{\Phi}_{q}(u,v)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Pi}_{q}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\mathscr{R}(q)

Remark 7
.

7
provides standard restrictions on the basis functions for sieve estimation.
7
(i) requires that the sieve functions are bounded and are twice continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives. The upper bounds, which are functions of
q
, increasing at best at poly-
n
rate (polynomials of
n
).
7
(ii) allows us to provide uniform convergence rate for the estimators of sieve coefficients. Finally,
7
(iii) guarantees that the unknown function
G
can be uniformly approximated by the basis functions. Note that when
q
is fixed, the sieve approximation error rate
\mathscr{R}(q)
decreases with the increase of
G

Chen (2007)
.

Condition 8
.

For any
\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}
,
\nu_{Z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\bm{\delta}_{0}}
and
\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}

\mathcal{X}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})=E(\bm{X}|Z_{0}=\nu_{Z},X(\bm{\beta})=\nu_{X},D=1)
. There hold:
(i) Let
\mathcal{X}_{j}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})
denote the
j
-th argument of
\mathcal{X}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})
. There exists a positive constant
C_{X}
such that for all
1\leq j\leq p_{X}
, any
\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}
,
\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta}}
, and
\nu_{X}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}_{\bm{\beta^{\prime}}}
, there holds

\left|\mathcal{X}_{j}\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta}\right)-\mathcal{X}_{j}\left(\nu_{Z}^{\prime},\nu_{X}^{\prime},\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\right)\right|\leq C_{X}\cdot\left(\left|\nu_{Z}-\nu_{Z}^{\prime}\right|+\left|\nu_{X}-\nu_{X}^{\prime}\right|+\sqrt{p_{X}}\left\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{\prime}\right\|\right);

(ii) For each
\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}
, there exists
\bm{\Pi}_{q}^{X}(\bm{\beta})\in R^{(q+1)^{2}}
such that

\sup_{\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta}}\|\mathcal{X}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})-\bm{\Pi}_{q}^{X}(\bm{\beta})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Phi}_{q}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X})\|\leq\mathscr{R}_{X}(q).

Remark 8
.

8
(i) restricts the smoothness of
\mathcal{X}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})
.
8
(ii) requires that for each
j
,
\mathcal{X}_{j}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})
can be uniformly approximated by linear combinations of sieve functions. Similar to the previous condition, the approximation error
\mathscr{R}_{X}(q)
depends on both the order of sieve functions and the smoothness of
\mathcal{X}(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\bm{\beta})

We finally introduce a condition that is similar to
6

Condition 9
.

Define

\bm{\Psi}_{S}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)=\int_{0}^{1}E\left(\left.\nabla_{v}G\left(Z_{0,i},X_{0,i}+\varsigma\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\beta}\right)\left(\bm{X}_{i}-\mathcal{X}\left(Z_{0,i},x_{0,i}+\bm{X}^{\mathrm{T}}_{i}\bm{\beta},\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right|D_{i}=1\right)d\varsigma.

There hold
\sup_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\overline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{S}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)+\bm{\Psi}_{S}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\leq\overline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{S}}<\infty

and
\inf_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}}\underline{\lambda}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{S}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)+\bm{\Psi}_{S}^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\bm{\beta}\right)\right)\geq\underline{\lambda}_{\bm{\Psi}_{S}}>0.

Define

\Xi_{1,n}=\sqrt{p_{X}}\mathscr{R}(q)+q^{2}C(0,q)^{2}\mathscr{R}_{X}(q)+\frac{\sqrt{p_{X}}q^{4}C(0,q)^{3}(p_{Z}C(1,q)+C(0,q)\sqrt{\log(n)p_{X}}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Under the above conditions, we have the following result, whose proofs are provided in the Appendix, Section
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose that
1
–
3
and
7

9

q

\Xi_{1,n}\rightarrow 0

then there holds

\sup_{k\geq k_{S}\left(n,\gamma\right)}\|\Delta\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}\|=O_{P}\left(\Xi_{1,n}\right),

Based on

Theorem 3

\displaystyle\Delta\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{k+1}

Corollary 1

Let all the conditions in

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{k}

\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Psi}_{S}^{-1}\left(\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\left(\bm{X}-\mathcal{X}\left(Z_{0},X_{0},\bm{\beta}_{0}\right)\right)\rightarrow_{a.s.}\mathfrak{X}_{\mathcal{W}}

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(P_{D}^{-1}D_{i}\mathfrak{X}_{\mathcal{W},i}\varepsilon_{i}-\mathfrak{Z}_{\mathcal{W},i}\left(D_{i}-F_{U}\left(Z_{0,i}\right)\right)\right)+o_{P}\left(1\right)\Longrightarrow N\left(0,\Sigma_{\mathcal{W},1}+\Sigma_{\mathcal{W},2}\right).

The above corollary states that the linear combinations of arguments of
\bm{\widehat{\beta}}
is

-consistent and

\widehat{X}_{i}=x_{0,i}+\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}

\widehat{\bm{\pi}}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i})\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(\widehat{Z}_{i})D_{i}\right),

\widehat{F}_{U}(u)=\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(u)^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\pi}},\ \ \widehat{\nabla F}_{U}(u)=\nabla\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}(u)^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\pi}},

\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{\delta}\left(\bm{Z}_{e}\right)=\bm{Z}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{Z}_{i}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i}\right)\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{\delta,q_{\delta}}\left(z_{0}+\bm{Z}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\delta}}\right),

\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{\delta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\widehat{\nabla F}_{U}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i}\right)\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{\delta}\left(\bm{Z}_{e,i}\right)\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right),

\widehat{\mathcal{X}}_{n}\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\right)=\frac{1}{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\bm{X}_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{n,q}^{-1}\left(\widehat{\bm{\delta}},\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\right)\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\nu_{Z},\nu_{X}\right),

\widehat{\bm{\Pi}}_{q}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}\right)\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\right]^{-1}\times\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}Y_{i}\bm{\Phi}_{q}\left(\widehat{Z}_{i},\widehat{X}_{i}\right)\right],

We consider four competing methods, two being parametric and two semiparametric.
The first method is parametric estimation using nonlinear least squares (two-step NLS), which is similar to Heckman’s two-step estimation but accounts for the binary response in the second stage. To apply such method, we assume that
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) have zero mean and unit variance, and are jointly normally distributed with covariance
\rho
.
Then we can jointly estimate
\bm{\delta}_{0}
,
\bm{\beta}_{0}
together with
\rho
. In particular, let
F_{1}(\cdot)
and
F_{2}(\cdot,\cdot,\rho)
denote the CDF’s of univariate standard normal distribution and bivariate normal distribution with covariance matrix
[\sigma_{ij}]_{2\times 2}
, where
\sigma_{11}=\sigma_{22}=1
, and
\sigma_{12}=\sigma_{21}=\rho
. Also define
\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}=\left(1,z_{0,i},\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\overline{\bm{X}}_{e,i}=\left(1,x_{0,i},\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\overline{\bm{\delta}}=\left(c_{\delta,0},c_{\delta,1},\bm{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
,
\overline{\bm{\beta}}=\left(c_{\beta,0},c_{\beta,1},\bm{\beta}^{\mathrm{T}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}
. In the first step, we minimize the following loss function

L_{1,n}(\overline{\bm{\delta}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(D_{i}-F_{1}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\delta}}))^{2}

and obtain the minimizer
\widehat{\overline{\bm{\delta}}}
. Then in the second step, we minimize the following loss function

L_{2,n}(\overline{\bm{\beta}},\rho)=\frac{1}{S_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}\left(Y_{i}-\frac{F_{2}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\overline{\bm{\delta}}},\overline{\bm{X}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\beta}},\rho)}{F_{1}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\widehat{\overline{\bm{\delta}}})}\right)^{2}

and obtain the minimizer
\widehat{\overline{\bm{\beta}}}
. Then the two-step NLS estimators for
\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}
are given by
\widehat{c}_{\delta,1}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
and
\widehat{c}_{\beta,1}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
.

The second method is parametric maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Like in the first method, we also assume that
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) are jointly normally distributed, then the log-likelihood function is then given by

\displaystyle L_{3,n}\left(\overline{\bm{\delta}},\overline{\bm{\beta}},\rho\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\left((1-D_{i})\log(1-F_{1}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\delta}}))+D_{i}Y_{i}\log\left(F_{2}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\delta}},\overline{\bm{X}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\beta}},\rho)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+D_{i}(1-Y_{i})\log\left(F_{1}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\delta}})-F_{2}(\overline{\bm{Z}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\delta}},\overline{\bm{X}}_{e,i}^{\mathrm{T}}\overline{\bm{\beta}},\rho)\right)\right).

Suppose the MLE estimators are given by
\widehat{\overline{\bm{\delta}}}
and
\widehat{\overline{\bm{\beta}}}
, then the MLE estimators for
\bm{\delta}_{0}
and
\bm{\beta}_{0}
are given by
\widehat{c}_{\delta,1}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\delta}}
and
\widehat{c}_{\beta,1}^{-1}\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
.

The third method is semiparametric estimation based on matching. In particular, we first obtain the estimator of
\delta_{0}
in the first step, then we conduct Algorithm 1. To improve the computational efficiency, in the first and second step estimation, we use nearest neighbor matching with
m=[(\log(n))^{1.1}]
and
m=[(\log(S_{n}))^{1.1}]
, where recall that
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
. We update 2000 times for both first- and second-step estimation.

The fourth method is semiparametric estimation based on series approximation. For the sieve functions, we consider the Legendre polynomials used in
Khan et al. (2024)
, and use tensor products of one-variate sieve functions as the sieve functions for bivariate functions. The order of sieves is chosen to be 31 for the first-step estimation and 15 for the second-step estimation. Finally, the stopping rule is the same as in that of
Khan et al. (2024)
with the tolerance being
10^{-6}
.

We report the bias and the root mean squared error (RMSE) of the second-step estimator for all methods. Let the simulation be repeated for
R
times, and in the
r
-th round of repetition the estimator of
\beta_{j,0}
be
\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{r}
. Then the bias of the estimator is given by
B_{j}=|\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{r}-\beta_{j,0}|
, and the RMSE is given by
RMSE_{j}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}(\widehat{\beta}_{j}^{r}-\beta_{j,0})^{2}}
. We report the bias and RMSE for
\beta_{1,0}
through
\beta_{8,0}
. We also report the total bias and RMSE, which are defined as
B_{\text{all}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{X}}B_{j}
and
RMSE_{\text{all}}=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p_{X}}RMSE_{j}^{2}}
. We choose
R=100
. Results are reported in
Table 2
to
Table 4
.

Table 2
through
Table 4
correspond to fours setups of the error terms. The first three setups feature model misspecification, while the last one features correct specification. Moreover, in the first setup both
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
are symmetric and are positively correlated. For the second step,
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
are also positive correlated but are both heavily skewed. The third setup corresponds to the case in which
U_{i}
and
V_{i}
are both bounded and are strongly (negatively) correlated with each other. Several insights can be drawn from the simulation results. First of all, when the model is misspecified, that is, the error terms
U_{i},V_{i}
are not jointly normally distributed, parametric estimators always have nonvanishing sizable bias, while the semiparametric sieve-based estimator constantly has minimal bias which is close to zero. This highlights the fact that the key advantage of semiparametric estimation lies in the robustness to model misspecifications. Further comparisons of the RMSE between different methods reveal that even though parametric estimators have
O(1/\sqrt{n})
standard deviation, the sizable bias significantly contaminates the RMSE so that the sieve-based estimator always outperforms two parametric estimators in terms of RMSE. Notably, when the model is correctly specified (
Table 4
), both two-step NLS and joint MLE estimators have trivial bias, and joint MLE estimators have smallest RMSE among all methods. However, even in this case, the sieve-based semiparametric estimator is competitive compared with two-step NLS in terms of RMSE. We finally point out that the performance of matching-based semiparametric estimator is less promising compared with the sieve-based on in terms of both bias and RMSE. This may be explained by limited number of iterations (2000 iterations for both steps) or small neighborhood size
k
(

In this section we apply our new algorithm to analyze the Stanford Open Policing Project data set
(
Pierson et al. 2020
)
. This large-scale data set records traffic stops made by police officers and various features related to the stops so can be used to provide insights into the potential racial disparities during police stops, which has been widely studied in the existing literature
(
Goel et al. 2016
)
.

In this paper we use the data set from Nashville, which contains a total of 3092351 raw observations and 2608109 observations after data clearing. To analyze this data set, we define the first-stage binary outcome as, after a stop has been made, whether the police officer further decides to conduct a search for the person or vehicle. Conditioned on that a search has been made, the second stage outcome is whether the police officer found any contraband items such as drugs or weapons. For the first stage equation, we consider a series of regressors including police precinct, the reason for the stop, as well as the gender, race, and age of the stopped subject. For the second stage equation, we consider all the regressors in the first stage but the precinct so that the precinct is used as the exclusion restriction. We provide detailed description of the variables in
Table 5
.

When conducting SBGD estimation, we set the coefficient of Reason1 to be 1 for both selection and outcome equations, whose reason is detailed as follows. When police officer stops individuals to conduct investigation, it’s highly likely that the police officer notice something unusual and hence a search is more likely to follow and contraband items are more likely to be found. This implies that the coefficients of Reason1 in both selection and outcome equations should be positive. We also note that the ratio of maximum and minimum eigenvalues of the covariate matrices for both selection and outcome equation is extremely large, which may lead to poor numerical performance during iterations. For example, in this case small learning rate
\gamma_{k}
has to be chosen during the estimation of both stages to guarantee convergence of the algorithms, which leads to extensive rounds of iterations. To solve this issue, we use Gram-Schimidt method to orthogonalize the covariate matrix so that the resulting covariate matrix has unit column variance and zero column correlation, and the first column of the orthogonalized covariate matrix is equal to Reason1. See
for more details of the empirical setups. The estimation results are reported in
Table 6
.

Several insights can be drawn from the results. First of all, age is negatively related to the conditional probability of being searched after a stop is made; it is also negatively associated with the probability that illegal items are found. Second, compared with male individuals, female individuals are less likely to be searched after the stop, and conditioned on that the stopped individual is searched, female individuals are less likely to be found carrying illegal items. Finally, for racial disparities during stops, we find that the coefficient of Black in the selection equation is significantly positive, while that of white is negative. This implies that when other conditions are held constant, individuals are more likely to be searched (compared with Hispanic individuals) if they are black, while white people are less likely to be searched. We further notice that for the outcome equation, the coefficients of Black and White almost coincide with each other, implying that conditioned on being searched, black people are almost as likely as the white people to be found carrying contraband items. Such a result implies that even though black community may confront certain disparities in terms of a search decision, the hit rate (of finding illegal items) does not differ across races.

Selective Labeling with Endogenous Treatment

This section extends the previous estimation method to selective labeling models with endogenous treatments. In his seminal work,

Lee (2009)
considers partial identification of the treatment parameters in treatment effect models with attrition but does not allow for explanatory variables.
Semenova (2025)
extends
Lee (2009)
’s bound and studies the asymptotic properties of the proposed bounds under both fixed and high dimensionality. For more discussion on
Lee (2009)
’s model and its recent development, see
Semenova (2025)
and references therein.

Extending our selective labeling model to allow for endogenous treatment status which is denoted by
T_{i}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle T_{i}=I\left(r_{0,i}+\bm{R}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{0}>W_{i}\right),

(6.1)

\displaystyle D_{i}=I\left(z_{0,i}+\bm{Z}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\delta}_{0}+\tau_{1,0}T_{i}>U_{i}\right),

(6.2)

\displaystyle Y_{i}=D_{i}\cdot I\left(x_{0,i}+\bm{X}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\beta}_{0}+\tau_{2,0}T_{i}>V_{i}\right),

(6.3)

where
\bm{R}_{e,i}=(r_{0,i},\bm{R}_{i}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}\in R^{p_{R}+1}
and
\bm{\varphi}_{0}\in R^{p_{R}}
. In the above system of equations, the observed binary variable

indicates
whether or not the
i
-th agent outcome variable is observed in the sample,

denotes the observed outcome variable for the selected sample, with selection governed by

denotes an observed binary variable indicating treatment status, whose coefficient in the outcome equation,

’s original model by explicitly modeling the determination of the treatment status. Moreover, since the unobserved random errors

are potentially mutually correlated,
the treatment status is allowed to be endogenous and correlated with both the selection status

and the outcome

. We also note that

The above system of selective labeling model with endogenous treatment nests many models in important work in the literature.
In a model where for

was linear and there were no regressors besides

considered
partial

Khan et al. (2023)

Chen et al. (2024)

However, none of the methods proposed in the above papers are applicable to the model above where there are endogenous treatment, attrition, and selective labeling on the same time, even for low dimensional models. In contrast, the methods we introduced in Section
2

can be extended to estimate

, and
\tau_{1,0},\tau_{2,0}

This paper considers estimation and inference for large dimensional semiparametric selective labeling models. Statistically these models have a similar structure to sample selection models with binary, as opposed to linear outcome equations in the second stage. It is this binary/binary structure which makes computation of the model particularly difficult when compared to the standard selection model, especially for large dimensional (i.e many regressor) models.
\par To address this problem we propose novel algorithmic procedures
which are computationally fast, and derive their asymptotic properties even for the case where the dimension increases with the sample size. We demonstrate the finite sample properties of our proposed procedures by a simulation study.
\par Our work here motivates areas for future research. For example to further ease implementation, a bivariate penalization scheme
would be useful for model selection in this settings, and its asymptotic validity would need to be proven. Furthermore, the usefulness of our methods in other empirical settings in economics, biostatistics and medicine would be worthy of exploration.

\par\@proof Write $\bm{\varphi}_{0}=(\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}^{\mathrm{T}},\bm{\varphi}_{d,0}^{\mathrm{T}})^{\mathrm{T}}$. To show the point identification of $\bm{\varphi}_{0}$, we note that since \autoref{condition11} (iii) holds, $\nabla_{r_{0}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e})$ and $\nabla_{\bm{R}_{c}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e})$ can be consistently estimated uniformly for all interior point $\bm{R}_{e}\in\mathcal{R}_{e}$, so in the following analysis we can assume that $E(T|\bm{R}_{e})$ is known for all all $\bm{R}_{e}\in\mathcal{R}_{e}$, and both $\nabla_{r_{0}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e})$, and $\nabla_{\partial\bm{R}_{c}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e})$ are known for interior points $\bm{R}_{e}\in\mathcal{R}_{e}$. According to \autoref{condition11} (iv) we can find interior point $\bm{R}_{e}^{1}$ such that $\nabla_{r_{0}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e}^{1})\neq 0$. Note that $\nabla_{r_{0}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e}^{1})=\nabla_{w}F_{W}\left(r_{0}^{1}+(\bm{R}^{1})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{0}\right)$ and
$\nabla_{r_{c,j}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e}^{1})=\nabla_{w}F_{W}\left(r_{0}^{1}+(\bm{R}^{1})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{0}\right)\varphi_{c,j,0}$ for $1\leq j\leq p_{R}^{c}$,
where $p_{R}^{c}$ is the dimension of $\bm{R}_{c}$. This implies that we can identify $\varphi_{c,j,0}$ by
$$\varphi_{c,j,0}=\frac{\nabla_{r_{c,j}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e}^{1})}{\nabla_{r_{0}}E(T|\bm{R}_{e}^{1})},\ 1\leq j\leq p_{R}^{c}$$To identify $\bm{\varphi}_{d}$, we first state a useful result. For any function $h(t)$ which is nondecreasing and has continuous derivative, if $\nabla h(t)>0$ for $t=t_{0}$, then $h(t_{0})=h(t_{1})$ must imply $t_{0}=t_{1}$. To prove such result, we note that
$h(t_{1})-h(t_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla h(t_{0}+\tau(t_{1}-t_{0}))d\tau(t_{1}-t_{0})$. Since $h$ has continuous derivative, $\nabla h(t)$ is lower bounded by some positive constant for a neighborhood of $t_{0}$. Then since $\nabla h(t)\geq 0$, we have that $\int_{0}^{1}\nabla h(t_{0}+\tau(t_{1}-t_{0}))d\tau>0$. So if $t_{1}>t_{0}$, we have that $h(t_{1})>h(t_{0})$, which leads to a contradiction. Given the above result, we note that when \autoref{condition11} (iv) and (v) hold, we have that $r_{0}^{2}+(\bm{R}_{c}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}-r_{0}^{j}-(\bm{R}_{c}^{j})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}=(\bm{R}_{d}^{j}-\bm{R}_{d}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{d,0}$ for $3\leq j\leq p_{R}^{d}+2$, so
$$\begin{pmatrix}r_{0}^{2}+(\bm{R}_{c}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}-r_{0}^{3}-(\bm{R}_{c}^{3})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}\\
\vdots\\
r_{0}^{2}+(\bm{R}_{c}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}-r_{0}^{p_{R}^{d}+2}-(\bm{R}_{c}^{p_{R}^{d}+2})^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\varphi}_{c,0}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}(\bm{R}_{d}^{3}-\bm{R}_{d}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\\
\vdots\\
(\bm{R}_{d}^{p_{R}^{d}+2}-\bm{R}_{d}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}\bm{\varphi}_{d,0}$$So nonsingularity of $(\bm{R}_{d}^{3}-\bm{R}_{d}^{2},\cdots,\bm{R}_{d}^{p_{R}^{d}+2}-\bm{R}_{d}^{2})$ guarantees identification of $\bm{\varphi}_{d,0}$.
\par When \autoref{condition12} further holds, using the previous method we can identify $\bm{\delta}_{0}$ so we can deal with the case where $R_{0}$ and $Z_{0}$ are known. For any $R_{0}$ and $Z_{0}$, $E(T|R_{0})=F_{W}(R_{0})$, $E(D|T=0,R_{0},Z_{0})=(F_{U}(Z_{0})-F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}))/(1-F_{W}(R_{0}))$, where $F_{U}$ is the marginal CDF of $U$, and $E(D|T=1,R_{0},Z_{0})=F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}+\tau_{1,0})/F_{W}(R_{0})$. Also, note that
$$-\nabla_{R_{0}}[E(D|T=0,{R}_{0},{Z}_{0})(1-E(T|R_{0}))]=-\nabla_{R_{0}}(F_{U}(Z_{0})-F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}))=\nabla_{w}F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}),$$and
$$\nabla_{R_{0}}[E(D|T=1,{R}_{0},{Z}_{0})E(T|R_{0})]=\nabla_{R_{0}}F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}+\tau_{1,0})=\nabla_{w}F_{W,U}(R_{0},Z_{0}+\tau_{1,0}),$$Note that $\nabla_{w}F_{W,U}(w,u)$ is nondecreasing with respect to $u$ because $\nabla_{wu}F_{W,U}(w,u)$ is the joint density of $W$ and $U$ and is nonnegative.
