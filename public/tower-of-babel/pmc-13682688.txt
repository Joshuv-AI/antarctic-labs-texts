How Patients' Inquiry Shapes Meaning in Primary Care Consultations: A Qualitative Study Using Own‐Point‐of‐View Video‐Stimulated Recall
Authors: Louis‐Baptiste Jaunay, Anne‐Sophie Ivanoff, Corentin Philippot, Emmanuel Triby, Thierry Pelaccia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleMedical consultations are collaborative encounters in which physicians and patients jointly address health concerns and make decisions. While most research has focused on physicians' clinical reasoning, decision‐making is increasingly viewed as a form of situated cognition, distributed across individuals and their interactions. However, the patient's perspective on how they make sense of their health problems remains underexamined.Aim and ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to investigate how patients experience and make sense of their health problems before and during a primary care consultation.MethodsWe conducted video‐stimulated recall interviews with patients after their consultations. Consultations were recorded from the patient's perspective using wearable camera glasses. Each interview comprised two parts: (a) an explicitation interview exploring the patient's experience and reasoning before the consultation, and (b) a video‐based stimulated recall to analyze their experience during the consultation. We performed an abductive thematic analysis using Dewey's theory of inquiry.ResultsTwelve interviews were analyzed. Patients engaged in an inquiry process before the consultation, involving the identification of a problematic situation, hypothesis generation and the exploration of potential actions and interactions. During the consultation, patients interpreted the physician's messages as confirmations, refinements or refutations of their own hypotheses. They attempted to infer the physician's reasoning and its underlying assumptions. Patients' acceptance and integration of medical explanations depended on the coherence between these explanations and the trajectory of their own inquiry.ConclusionPatients interpret the physician's messages in light of their own inquiry, which can be described according to John Dewey's pattern of inquiry. The physician's ability to recognize where the patient stands within their inquiry and to make clinical reasoning explicit appears central to knowledge sharing and decision‐making. These findings suggest new orientations for communication training in primary care, emphasizing physicians' capacity to identify and support patients' inquiry processes.

Background

Medical consultations are fundamentally oriented toward addressing the patient's health problems [1]. These problems may be raised by the physician (the doctor's agenda) or by the patient (the patient's agenda) [2]. Throughout the consultation, the interaction shapes and is shaped by the evolving reasoning processes of both the patient and the physician [3]. This is one pathway through which consultations can affect patients' health positively [4, 5] or negatively [6, 7].

Although many studies have examined physicians' clinical reasoning during consultations, recent approaches increasingly view decision‐making as arising from a situated, distributed process involving patients and physicians, the interactions between them and the context in which those interactions occur [8, 9, 10]. Knowledge thus emerges through exchanges of meaning and action, making knowledge sharing central to decision‐making—not as decontextualized information transfer, but as situated co‐construction between actors who make sense of the situation differently [11, 12, 13, 14].

However, one limitation identified in the patient‐centered care literature is its focus on discrete aspects of patient experience, such as reasons for consultation, and on outcomes such as satisfaction, rather than on experience as an unfolding process [15]. In response, calls have been made for a shift toward relationship‐centered care (RCC), which foregrounds interaction itself as an unfolding relational process through which patients' experiences are shaped [16]. Understanding their sense‐making may help clinicians align care with experiential knowledge and expectations, while recognizing patients' capacity to contribute to both the management of their health problems and patient safety [17].

Patients' experiences and sense‐making processes remain largely opaque because they involve substantial implicit and tacit activity [17, 18]. Their decisions—which are central to models of shared decision‐making—therefore cannot be regarded as entirely intentional or fully conscious [16]. Yet few studies have examined the patient's own process of making sense of their health problems during a consultation. The influence of this process is particularly salient in primary care, where patients typically make decisions that lead to appointments, shape their engagement during the consultation and influence their subsequent behaviors, including adherence.

This study sought to answer the following question: How do patients experience and make sense of the health problems that lead them to seek primary care, before and during the consultation?

Methods

Theoretical Approach

Because no single established framework fully captures how patients make sense of their health problems as an unfolding process, we adopted an abductive approach combining conceptualization and field observations [19, 20]. We sought to understand patients' experience of their health problems by drawing upon John Dewey's philosophy of experience [21].

According to Dewey, experience refers to the significance that emerges through interactions with the environment over time [22, 23]. When individuals identify a disruption in the continuity of experience, they engage in inquiry to transform a problematic situation into one sufficiently determinate to guide action. They generate hypotheses and examine their anticipated consequences, which shape the subsequent course of the interaction. Inquiry involves several partially overlapping phases: (1) identifying a problematic situation, (2) defining the problem, (3) developing hypotheses, (4) examining their consequences and (5) testing solutions through action [21].

This framework is currently widely used in the French‐speaking field of experience studies to analyze work activity and professional development, particularly in healthcare [24]. It is particularly well‐suited to examining how patients experience and make sense of their health problems because Dewey conceived his theory of inquiry as applicable to diverse problematic situations and as encompassing the active and passive, bodily and cognitive, episodic and prolonged, as well as implicit and explicit dimensions of experience. Previous studies have illustrated its value for studying patients' perspectives. [25, 26, 27, 28, 29].

Setting and Recruitment

We conducted a multicenter qualitative study between March 2023 and September 2025 in France. We initially recruited general practitioners (GPs) through convenience sampling because participation required acceptance of video recording. The researcher was not familiar with the selected sites beforehand. Participating physicians provided written consent to be filmed during the consultation and identified patients willing to participate. As analysis progressed, patient recruitment was iteratively adjusted through purposive sampling to diversify participant profiles. Patients were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, fluent in French, capable of providing informed consent and willing to wear camera glasses during the consultation and take part in an audio‐recorded interview. Recruitment continued until we judged the data sufficient to support the thematic analysis. We conducted and reported our research following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (see Appendix S1) [30].

Data Collection

We collected data through interviews with patients after their consultation with a GP. The consultation was recorded from the patient's subjective perspective using a head‐mounted camera, allowing the reconstruction of the human natural field of view (Figure 1) [31]. Only the GP was filmed. The choice to use a subjective‐perspective recording via camera glasses (rather than by an external camera filming the patient and physician from an observer's point of view, as in video elicitation [32], for example) aimed to avoid participants' tendency to focus on the judgment of their own behavior [33].

Video and audio recording device during consultation: patient wearing camera glasses/image captured (GP).

Prior to their appointment, patients provided written consent and were equipped with camera glasses to wear upon entering the GP's office. We conducted the interview in a dedicated enclosed area, immediately after the consultation or within 8 days, at the patient's convenience, in accordance with previous studies [34, 35, 36].

The interview comprised two parts. The first part aimed to explore the patients' experience of their health problems prior to the consultation, using the explicitation interview (EI) method [37]. This method guides participants to relive a specific past experience situated in time and space through open questions focused on their perceptions and actions [38, 39]. EI explores both the active and passive dimensions of patients' experience, enables detailed descriptions of mental and behavioral activity, makes implicit aspects of reasoning explicit and limits retrospective interpretations [23, 40]. It is considered by some authors to be the most appropriate interview method for exploring lived experience [41] and has yielded significant results in understanding patient experience across various conditions [42, 43, 44, 45, 46].

The second part of the interview focused on the experience during the consultation, using the video recording of the encounter (Figure 2). This recording supported the interview according to the method known as subjective re‐situ interview, derived from EI [33, 47, 48]. This video capture aimed to (a) act as a mnemonic trigger, stimulating the patient's re‐experiencing of the event, (b) provide a shared observable for both the patient and the researcher, allowing the latter to understand the context and (c) provide a video record of the physician's observable activity [33]. The video was paused at the patient's request, or whenever they reacted verbally or nonverbally, as well as when the researcher identified elements requiring exploration.

Interviews were audio‐recorded, fully transcribed, anonymized and sent to participants for feedback. The interview guide was refined iteratively. All interviews were conducted by L.‐B.J., who has qualitative research experience and formal EI training (Groupe de recherche sur l'Explicitation, GREX).

Data Analysis

Our research team consisted of a male GP with 10 years of practice and experience in conducting and supervising qualitative research and engaged in a PhD in educational science on the topic of this article (L.‐B.J.), a female GP with 15 years of practice and experience in conducting qualitative research (A.‐S.I.), a male GP resident involved in a medical doctoral thesis about patient experience (C.P.), a male professor of emergency medicine with 25 years of practice, holding a PhD in educational science (T.P.) and a male emeritus professor in education, former vice‐dean of the Faculty of Educational Sciences (E.T.).

We conducted an abductive thematic analysis informed by the data and Dewey's theory of inquiry [19, 20]. During the interpretative phase, the emerging categories were examined in relation to Dewey's conceptualization of inquiry, allowing us to organize themes according to the temporal unfolding and phases of patients' inquiry while remaining grounded in participants' accounts. The interviews were first coded independently by L.‐B.J., A.‐S.I. and C.P., then analyzed with T.P. and E.T. to discuss interpretations. Disagreements about coding were discussed and revised until consensus was reached.

Ethical Approval

This project was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee for Research Integrity and Education in Health Professions of the International Francophone Society for Medical Education (SIFEM) on 22 March 2023.

Themes

Our analysis was informed by Dewey's theory of inquiry. Specifically, the two overarching themes represent the temporal unfolding of patients' inquiry before and during the consultation, whereas the subthemes reflect the dynamic and iterative process of inquiry described by Dewey: the identification and definition of a problematic situation, the generation of hypotheses and anticipation of their consequences, the testing of solutions through action and the construction of meaning through interactions (Table 3).

We observed that when consulting, patients were already engaged in an inquiry concerning their health problem: they had identified disruptions in their usual experience, constructed hypotheses grounded in experiential knowledge, explored actions or interactions (including that of making an appointment) and anticipated what the consultation might provide. During the consultation, patients interpreted the physician's reasoning through the lens of this prior inquiry. Their reception of the physician's messages depended on (1) how well these messages resonated with their ongoing inquiry and (2) how they interpreted the physician's reasoning and the underlying hypotheses.

To illustrate these findings, we selected verbatim excerpts. Quotes from participant P8 (Zoe, 18, student; acute febrile illness) are used to provide a narrative thread, while excerpts from other participants broaden the illustration of each code.

This identification was intertwined with the development of explanatory hypotheses, as the following section shows.

Emergence of Knowledge

Discussion

This study examined how patients experience their health problems before and during a primary care consultation, drawing on John Dewey's theory of inquiry as an interpretative framework. It highlights the importance of patients' own inquiry in shaping meaning within primary care consultations. This inquiry began before the consultation, when disruptions in patients' usual experience prompted hypotheses, actions, interactions and expectations. At first, however, patients underwent this process rather than deliberately initiating it, and much of it remained implicit.

Our results bring attention to the early stages of health‐problem emergence—identification and framing—which have received limited attention in the decision‐making literature, where problems are often treated as objective data to be obtained from patients [49]. Some studies support our findings by showing the importance of patients' ability to detect and name their problems [50], as well as the role of their engagement in interactions with relatives, health professionals or online resources in shaping how these problems are constructed [51].

During the consultation, their inquiry shaped how they received the physician's messages and assessed the physician's problem identification, reasoning and underlying hypotheses. When the physician's reasoning remained unexplained, misunderstandings could arise; when it was made explicit, patients could integrate medical explanations with previously tacit experiential knowledge and anticipate the evolution of their condition. Examining, from the patients' perspective, how the physician's messages became integrated with their experiential knowledge provides insights into processes of knowledge sharing [52] and negotiation of meaning [18].

These findings support reconsidering the central role assigned to physicians' decisions in models of clinical reasoning and shared decision‐making. From the patient's point of view, the GP's influence operates chiefly through the messages conveyed by the physician and the positions that these messages imply for the patient's inquiry. This may help explain how consultations influence adherence and contribute to therapeutic effects through interaction. Such effects may arise when consultations enable patients to become aware of previously tacit experiential knowledge and coherently relate medical knowledge to their own experience. Supporting the emergence of patients' tacit knowledge and their formulation of the problem appears to be a key clinical skill for shared understanding [18, 49, 53], yet it is only marginally addressed in competency frameworks [22]. Communication techniques derived from EI could help physicians support patients in becoming aware of and articulating their health problems, thereby informing both the patient's and the physician's inquiry [23].

This study reframes decision‐making not as the physician's reasoning supplemented by patient input, but as the coordination of the patient's and physician's interacting inquiry processes, consistent with RCC [16] and distributed cognition [10]. Which factors determine whether practical decisions stem more from the physician's or the patient's inquiry? Recent studies suggest that this influence depends on (1) who holds the relevant information (awareness and knowledge of the problem) and (2) who performs the action [54]. In primary care, patients typically initiate the consultation, possess crucial information through their experiential knowledge, and perform most of the actions outside of consultations. In this context, the physician's influence may lie less in their decisions or prescriptions than in how they support the patient's inquiry [4, 18]. These findings suggest that communication training should teach GPs to recognize and engage with patients' ongoing inquiry.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study lie in its theoretical framework and its original methods for eliciting patients' experience in real‐life situations while avoiding decontextualized declarative knowledge. Dewey's theory enabled us to analyze patients' inquiries without presupposing their underlying basis. The EI and video‐based stimulated recall enabled us to examine the ‘black box’ of patients' thoughts nested within the ‘black box’ of the consultation, providing insight into patients' actions and meaning‐making while bringing implicit dimensions of their experience to light.

The limited number of participants may be considered a limitation of this study. However, purposive sampling produced diverse patient, physician and clinical problem profiles, and recurring patterns were identified across interviews.

As with any abductive approach, data may be fitted to a theory. Dewey's framework should therefore be viewed as an interpretive lens rather than a comprehensive account of patient experience; it has nonetheless been productively applied across diverse contexts [24].

The experimental setup and video recording modified the consultations studied. However, most participants reported forgetting the camera glasses, consistent with previous findings [55] and possibly because the camera was unobtrusively embedded in the glasses.

Conclusion and Implications

The patient interprets the physician's messages through the lens of their ongoing inquiry, which can be described according to Dewey's inquiry framework.

Our findings highlight the central role of physicians in recognizing where patients stand in their inquiry and in making their own reasoning explicit. When patients face an unclear situation, physicians can help them identify the problems, possible courses of action and their likely consequences. When patients seek to understand the problems identified, physicians can work with them to develop and discuss hypotheses, notably through diagnostic and therapeutic actions. By doing so, physicians help patients make sense of their situation in a more structured and actionable way, enabling them to integrate experiential and medical knowledge into a coherent understanding of their condition. Conversely, when physicians leave their reasoning implicit, misunderstandings may arise and disrupt the patients' inquiry.

This study also demonstrates the value of combining EI with own‐point‐of‐view video to examine patients' tacit and situated activity. This method could be used to investigate multiple dimensions of patients' experience across diverse healthcare settings. In particular, it could support research into how clinicians' recognition and active support of patients' inquiry affect patients' understanding and subsequent actions.
