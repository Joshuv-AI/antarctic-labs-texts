Enhanced seam segmentation for automated welding robot in construction through transfer learning: addressing limitations of bilateral segmentation network
Authors: Keonvin Park, Yong Ann Voeurn, Hyeokjun Kweon, Doyun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reliable weld seam segmentation is essential for autonomous robotic welding in construction, where severe illumination changes, specular reflections, and thin weld geometries frequently degrade segmentation performance. This paper proposes a lightweight post-training optimization framework that systematically improves existing real-time semantic segmentation networks without modifying their architectures. Starting from an Online Hard Example Mining (OHEM)-pretrained checkpoint, the proposed framework combines controlled fine-tuning with a hybrid Cross-Entropy–Lovász objective to enhance pixel-level classification, region-level seam continuity, and recovery from reflection-induced segmentation failures while preserving real-time inference efficiency. Extensive experiments demonstrate that the proposed framework substantially improves segmentation performance. Using BiSeNetV2, Joint IoU increases from 59.40% to 81.76% (+22.36 percentage points), while mIoU reaches 90.73% without increasing parameter count, FLOPs, inference latency, or memory consumption. Furthermore, the proposed framework successfully recovers 96.33% of severe zero-IoU failure cases caused by strong reflections, substantially improving the operational reliability of downstream robotic perception. To evaluate the generality of the proposed optimization framework, additional experiments were conducted using U-Net, DeepLabV3+, SegFormer-B0, and PIDNet-S under multiple fine-tuning configurations. The results reveal that the effectiveness of post-training optimization is strongly architecture-dependent: lightweight real-time segmentation networks, particularly BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S, consistently benefit from the proposed framework, whereas larger semantic-oriented architectures exhibit comparatively smaller and less stable improvements. In robotic welding experiments, the proposed BiSeNetV2 model was the only evaluated approach capable of consistently generating a valid weld seam trajectory, achieving an average joint-center offset of 2.47 ± 0.78 mm during robotic path planning, corresponding to an approximately 9.8 × reduction compared with the previous BiSeNetV2-based robotic welding system. Overall, the results demonstrate that carefully designed post-training optimization provides a practical and computationally efficient alternative to architectural redesign, substantially improving failure recovery, seam continuity, and robotic trajectory generation for autonomous welding in reflective construction environments.

Introduction

Automated robotic welding in construction

Welding is a fundamental process in structural steel fabrication, prefabrication, and on-site assembly, directly affecting the quality, safety, and durability of steel structures. However, the construction industry is increasingly challenged by shortages of skilled welders, rising labor costs, and growing demands for safer and more productive construction processes1–4. Consequently, robotic welding systems have attracted considerable attention as an effective solution for improving productivity while reducing human exposure to hazardous environments.

Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled vision-based robotic welding systems capable of automatically detecting weld seams, generating robot trajectories, and performing autonomous welding operations in complex construction environments5–7. In particular, semantic segmentation provides dense pixel-level seam information that can be directly utilized for seam localization, centerline extraction, and robotic path planning8–10. Lightweight real-time segmentation networks such as BiSeNetV2 have demonstrated an attractive balance between segmentation accuracy and computational efficiency, making them suitable for deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms11.

Despite these advances, reliable weld seam segmentation remains challenging in practical construction environments. Metallic workpieces frequently produce severe specular reflections, saturated highlights, illumination variations, and low-contrast seam boundaries, resulting in fragmented seam predictions and unstable centerline extraction12–14. Such segmentation failures directly affect downstream robotic localization and trajectory generation, ultimately reducing the reliability of autonomous welding systems9,10,15,16.

Research gap

Most existing deep learning-based weld seam segmentation methods improve performance by designing new network architectures, incorporating attention mechanisms, or introducing multi-scale feature fusion modules17–20. Although these approaches have achieved promising segmentation accuracy, they typically increase model complexity and computational cost, limiting their deployment in real-time robotic welding systems.

An alternative direction is to improve existing lightweight segmentation models through post-training optimization rather than architectural redesign. While transfer learning and region-aware objectives such as the Lovász loss have been widely adopted for semantic segmentation21–23, they are generally applied as standard optimization techniques without systematically investigating how post-training strategies influence robustness under severe metallic reflections. Moreover, the interaction between optimization strategy and segmentation architecture remains insufficiently understood for robotic welding applications.

Our previous work developed a real-time robotic welding perception system based on BiSeNetV2 and demonstrated its feasibility for autonomous construction welding8. Nevertheless, strong metallic reflections frequently produced fragmented seam predictions and complete seam omissions, indicating that segmentation performance is limited not only by network architecture but also by the optimization strategy used during post-training.

Proposed framework

Motivated by these observations, this study proposes a lightweight post-training optimization framework for weld seam segmentation instead of developing a new network architecture. Starting from an OHEM-pretrained checkpoint, the framework performs a short post-training stage using controlled fine-tuning and a hybrid Cross-Entropy–Lovász (CE–Lovász) objective to improve both pixel-level classification and region-level seam continuity while preserving the computational efficiency of the original segmentation model.

Rather than assuming that identical optimization strategies benefit all segmentation networks equally, this study systematically evaluates the proposed framework across representative semantic segmentation architectures, including BiSeNetV2, PIDNet-S, U-Net, DeepLabV3+, and SegFormer-B0. This analysis provides practical insight into how post-training optimization interacts with network architecture under severe reflective welding environments.

Background & related work

Vision-based welding automation

Computer vision has become a core enabling technology for robotic welding automation, supporting seam detection, joint tracking, weld inspection, and robotic trajectory generation4–6. Early welding perception systems primarily relied on geometric and photometric methods such as edge detection, Hough transforms, laser-stripe sensing, and structured-light projection15,16. Although these approaches achieved acceptable performance in controlled laboratory environments, their robustness deteriorated significantly in real construction scenarios characterized by dynamic illumination, metallic reflections, occlusions, and vibration. Recent advances in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have enabled data-driven semantic segmentation frameworks capable of learning hierarchical seam representations directly from images24,25. Architectures such as U-Net24, DeepLabV3+25, and BiSeNetV211 have demonstrated strong segmentation capability while preserving boundary information and real-time inference performance. In particular, BiSeNetV2 achieves an effective trade-off between accuracy and efficiency through its dual-branch design, making it attractive for edge-deployed robotic systems. However, despite the progress of deep-learning-based seam segmentation, robust operation under severe specular reflection remains an unresolved challenge in robotic welding environments7,12. Metallic glare, saturated highlights, and irregular illumination frequently cause fragmented seam predictions and unstable boundaries, which can propagate into inaccurate seam centerline extraction and unstable robotic trajectories. Therefore, achieving reflection-robust and trajectory-stable seam representation is essential for practical robotic welding deployment.

Segmentation of metallic and industrial surfaces

Deep learning-based segmentation has also been widely studied in metallic-surface inspection tasks including corrosion detection, defect segmentation, crack detection, and microstructure analysis13,26,27. These studies provide important insights into handling reflective textures and low-contrast boundaries in industrial environments. Muñoz-Rodenas et al.13 applied DeepLabV3+ for steel microstructure segmentation and demonstrated improved fine-grained texture recognition. Chen and Jahanshahi26 evaluated deep segmentation networks for corrosion detection and showed that specular highlights and illumination variation remain major failure sources. Zhang et al.17 proposed SME-DeepLabV3+ with multiscale feature extraction for irregular metallic surfaces, while Cumbajin et al.12 highlighted through a systematic review that reflective artifacts continue to limit the generalization capability of CNN-based industrial segmentation systems. These studies collectively indicate that conventional segmentation architectures often overfit to dataset-specific illumination statistics and struggle to generalize to real-world reflective environments. Furthermore, many previous approaches focus primarily on architectural expansion through deeper encoders, attention modules, or multiscale fusion, while relatively few studies explicitly address reflection robustness from the perspective of learning stability and optimization strategy. In contrast, the present study focuses on reflection-aware seam localization through transfer learning and hybrid loss optimization rather than increasing architectural complexity. This enables improved seam continuity and stable representation under strong metallic reflections while preserving lightweight real-time deployment capability.

Reflection and illumination challenges in construction welding

Among various environmental factors affecting robotic welding perception, specular reflection is one of the most critical sources of segmentation instability9,10. Reflective metallic plates generate intense glare regions and false edges that frequently resemble seam structures, thereby confusing both traditional vision algorithms and deep segmentation networks. In construction welding environments, these challenges become more severe due to dynamic lighting conditions, welding sparks, sensor vibration, and cluttered backgrounds4,27. Even small discontinuities or reflection-induced false positives may propagate into unstable seam trajectories during downstream robotic path planning. Therefore, robust seam continuity is essential not only for segmentation accuracy but also for reliable robotic execution. Several recent studies attempted to mitigate reflection effects using attention mechanisms or specialized architectures. Guo and Chen18 proposed HAMS-Net with heterogeneous attention modules for reflective welding environments, while Zhao et al.19 introduced an improved U-Net framework for metallic object segmentation under challenging illumination conditions. Other studies utilized active sensing systems such as structured light or laser scanners20; however, these approaches often increase hardware complexity, calibration burden, and deployment cost for mobile robotic systems. Compared with prior work, this study approaches the reflection problem from a different perspective. Rather than introducing a new architecture, we investigate whether reflection robustness can be improved through learning stabilization using transfer learning and CE–Lovász optimization. This perspective reframes reflection robustness as a learning-stability problem rather than solely a model-capacity problem.

Transfer learning in industrial vision

Transfer learning has become increasingly important in industrial vision tasks where labeled datasets are limited and domain shifts between source and target environments are substantial21,22. Pretrained encoders trained on large-scale datasets such as ImageNet28 and COCO29 provide robust low-level and semantic feature representations that can significantly improve downstream segmentation performance. Dippel et al.21 showed that transferring encoder weights while selectively adapting downstream layers improves convergence and segmentation quality. Karimi et al.22 further demonstrated that transfer learning is particularly beneficial in data-scarce segmentation tasks. In addition, domain adaptation techniques such as ADDA and appearance-level adaptation methods have been used to mitigate distribution mismatch between training and deployment domains30,31. In robotic welding environments, domain shifts caused by specular reflection, surface finish variation, and illumination inconsistency make generalization especially difficult. Therefore, this study adopts pretrained encoder initialization combined with reflection-aware fine-tuning to stabilize feature representations under reflective metallic conditions. Importantly, the proposed framework improves performance without modifying the network architecture or increasing parameter count, making it suitable for real-time robotic deployment with fixed computational budgets.

Loss function optimization for weld seam segmentation

Loss-function design critically affects segmentation continuity and boundary stability in thin-structure segmentation tasks such as weld seam detection. Standard Cross-Entropy (CE) loss provides stable pixel-wise supervision but often struggles with highly imbalanced seam regions. Dice and Tversky losses improve overlap-based optimization but may produce unstable gradients near thin reflective boundaries32. Online Hard Example Mining (OHEM)33 emphasizes difficult pixels and improves learning under imbalance conditions, but in reflective environments it can overweight noisy or ambiguous regions caused by glare. Lovász-Softmax23 directly optimizes IoU-related objectives and improves region-level consistency, making it effective for preserving elongated seam continuity. Recent studies have also explored boundary-aware objectives34; however, their effectiveness under severe reflection remains limited. In this work, we employ a hybrid CE–Lovász formulation to jointly optimize local pixel accuracy and global region continuity. The proposed optimization improves seam reconstruction, suppresses reflection-induced artifacts, and stabilizes thin seam boundaries without increasing computational complexity. Importantly, experimental results demonstrate that reflection robustness can be substantially improved through optimization strategy alone while preserving identical FLOPs, parameter count, and inference graph. This suggests that robust seam segmentation is fundamentally related to learning stability rather than network capacity. This observation is particularly important in reflective robotic welding environments, where severe segmentation failures may occur only infrequently but can critically disrupt downstream robotic operations. Similar to rare-event prediction problems in high-risk systems35, isolated catastrophic failures may be substantially more important than average segmentation performance because even a single zero-IoU seam prediction can propagate into endpoint-detection failure or unstable robotic trajectory generation. Therefore, the proposed framework explicitly emphasizes recovery of severe reflection-induced failure cases in addition to improving average overlap metrics.

Data limitations and confidence stability

Real-world welding datasets exhibit severe class imbalance and annotation uncertainty. Weld seams occupy only a small fraction of image pixels, while reflective metallic backgrounds dominate the scene12,13. In addition, strong glare frequently obscures seam boundaries, introducing ambiguity during manual annotation. To improve robustness against these challenges, this study applies reflection-aware augmentation strategies including exposure variation, illumination perturbation, and reflection simulation during transfer learning. Combined with pretrained encoder reuse, these augmentations improve generalization under varying lighting conditions. Furthermore, confidence-map visualization is incorporated as an additional analysis tool. Prior to transfer learning, prediction confidence often spreads into reflective artifacts, whereas after fine-tuning, confidence becomes concentrated along true seam regions. This calibration behavior provides additional evidence that the proposed framework improves reflection robustness and structural seam consistency. Nevertheless, the present study remains subject to several limitations, including limited welding-scene diversity, potential annotation uncertainty under severe reflective conditions, and possible domain bias associated with specific lighting environments and metallic surface characteristics, which may affect generalizability to broader robotic welding scenarios.

Comparative analysis and research positioning

Table 1 summarizes representative approaches to welding and metallic-surface segmentation and positions the proposed framework relative to existing reflection-robust methods. Previous studies have primarily addressed challenging metallic environments through geometric or structured-light sensing, architectural modifications such as attention mechanisms and multiscale feature extraction, or specialized encoder–decoder designs. Although these approaches can improve segmentation robustness, they generally achieve this improvement by modifying or expanding the underlying perception architecture, which may increase model complexity and deployment requirements.

In contrast, the present study investigates whether reflection robustness can be improved through post-training optimization while preserving the original segmentation architecture. Specifically, the proposed framework combines reflection-aware transfer learning with a hybrid Cross-Entropy (CE)–Lovász objective. The methodological distinction is therefore not the introduction of a new segmentation architecture or an individually new loss function, but the use of complementary pixel-level and region-level optimization to refine an already-trained segmentation model under severe reflective welding conditions.

The mechanism of the CE–Lovász objective can be explained by the different optimization properties of its two components. This property is useful in reflective welding images because specular glare, saturation, and low-contrast regions can alter local appearance and cause seam pixels to be confused with surrounding metallic surfaces. CE penalizes these local classification errors and maintains discrimination between seam and non-seam pixels. However, because the CE objective is accumulated over individual pixels, it does not directly optimize the spatial overlap or connectivity of the predicted seam. This limitation becomes particularly important for weld seams because the foreground occupies only a small fraction of the image and forms a thin and elongated structure. A relatively small number of spatially concentrated errors can therefore disconnect a seam or remove an entire local segment even when the majority of pixels are correctly classified.

Lovász–Softmax provides a complementary optimization signal by minimizing a convex surrogate of the discrete intersection-over-union (IoU) loss. Unlike CE, its optimization is related to the ranking of prediction errors and their contribution to region-level overlap. Consequently, errors that substantially reduce the intersection or increase the union between the predicted and ground-truth seam regions directly affect the optimization objective. For thin weld seams, reflection-induced false negatives can remove contiguous portions of the seam, while false positives caused by glare can introduce spurious seam-like regions. Both types of errors deteriorate IoU and are therefore explicitly reflected in the Lovász objective. This region-level optimization encourages greater agreement between the predicted mask and the complete ground-truth seam structure.

The complementary behavior of the two objectives provides a theoretical explanation for the effectiveness of their combination under strong reflections. CE preserves local class discrimination and supplies dense gradients for ambiguous seam and background pixels, whereas Lovász introduces an overlap-oriented optimization signal that is sensitive to structural degradation of the predicted seam. Thus, the hybrid objective can be expressed conceptually as a balance between local classification fidelity and global region consistency. When reflection causes errors only within a localized region, CE penalizes the corresponding pixel-level misclassification. When these local errors accumulate into a fragmented, shortened, or completely missing seam segment, the associated degradation in region overlap is additionally penalized through the Lovász component. The two losses therefore address different but complementary aspects of reflection-induced segmentation error.

This interaction is particularly relevant to severe zero-IoU failures. Because weld seams are narrow relative to the background and plate regions, localized prediction errors can have a disproportionately large effect on seam IoU and structural continuity. Pixel-wise optimization alone may not sufficiently reflect the operational severity of such structural failures, whereas the region-oriented Lovász term increases sensitivity to the resulting overlap degradation. Combining the two objectives consequently reduces the likelihood that local reflection-induced ambiguities propagate into fragmented or completely missing seam predictions. Importantly, the hybrid loss does not physically suppress or remove reflections from the input image; rather, it improves the optimization behavior of the segmentation model against prediction errors induced by those reflections.

The experimental ablation results support this interpretation. For BiSeNetV2 under full fine-tuning, CE-only and OHEM optimization achieved Joint IoUs of 78.78% and 79.06%, respectively, whereas the CE–Lovász objective achieved 81.76% Joint IoU and a 96.33% Recovery Rate for baseline zero-IoU failures. These results indicate that the benefit of the hybrid objective is particularly evident in recovering structurally severe segmentation failures rather than merely improving already successful pixel predictions. The observed improvement is therefore consistent with the complementary pixel-level and region-level optimization mechanisms described above.

In addition to the loss formulation, the proposed framework systematically investigates the interaction between post-training optimization and network architecture. The same optimization strategies are evaluated across BiSeNetV2, PIDNet-S, U-Net, DeepLabV3+, and SegFormer-B0 using different fine-tuning configurations. The experimental results demonstrate that the effectiveness of post-training optimization is architecture-dependent, with lightweight architectures that preserve fine-grained spatial information, particularly BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S, showing more consistent improvements than heavier semantic-oriented architectures. This observation indicates that reflection robustness depends not only on the optimization objective itself but also on its compatibility with the spatial representation characteristics of the underlying segmentation network.

Furthermore, unlike conventional evaluations that primarily emphasize average segmentation overlap, the present study explicitly considers severe reflection-induced zero-IoU failures through the Recovery Rate metric. These catastrophic failures are particularly relevant to robotic welding because a completely missing seam prediction can directly prevent reliable seam tracking, endpoint detection, centerline extraction, and trajectory generation. The proposed framework therefore targets both average segmentation performance and operational failure recovery while preserving the original inference architecture, parameter count, and computational requirements.

Overall, the proposed framework reframes reflection-robust weld seam segmentation as a learning-stability and structural-consistency problem rather than solely a model-capacity problem. Results

Quantitative comparisons

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed reflection-robust transfer-learning framework, extensive experiments were conducted using multiple semantic segmentation architectures under identical training and evaluation conditions. Performance was evaluated using Joint IoU, BG+Plate IoU, mIoU, Dice/F1 score, Precision, Recall, and Recovery Rate, defined as the percentage of baseline zero-IoU failures recovered after post-training. Since weld seams occupy only a very small fraction of the image and are highly vulnerable to specular reflections, Joint IoU was treated as the principal evaluation metric throughout this study.

Table 2 first analyzes the influence of different pre-training objectives on BiSeNetV2 before downstream transfer learning. Unlike general semantic segmentation tasks, weld seam segmentation involves severe class imbalance, thin structural targets, and strong reflection-induced noise. Under these conditions, CE-only and Lovasz-only optimization exhibited unstable convergence and significantly degraded seam reconstruction performance. In contrast, OHEM pre-training achieved the best overall initialization performance, reaching 59.40% Joint IoU and 79.66% mIoU.

These observations indicate that hard-example-focused optimization is particularly important during the initial convergence stage of weld seam segmentation, where reflective artifacts frequently dominate the foreground seam region. Consequently, the OHEM-pretrained checkpoint was adopted as the initialization point for all subsequent transfer-learning experiments.

To further investigate the influence of transfer-learning strategies, Table 3 presents comprehensive ablation experiments across multiple architectures using decoder-only finetuning, partial finetuning, and full finetuning settings. The proposed framework was evaluated using CE-only, OHEM-only, and CE–Lovasz hybrid optimization under identical experimental conditions.

Among all evaluated configurations, BiSeNetV2 combined with full finetuning and CE–Lovasz optimization achieved the best overall performance. Joint IoU improved from the baseline 59.40% to 81.76%, corresponding to a substantial gain of +22.36 percentage points. Similarly, mIoU increased from 79.66% to 90.73%, while Recall improved from 69.86% to 94.10%.

More importantly, the proposed framework achieved a Recovery Rate of 96.33% under severe reflective conditions. This result is particularly meaningful because many baseline failure cases correspond to fragmented or completely missing seam predictions (zero-IoU failures), which may directly propagate into endpoint-detection failure, unstable centerline extraction, and downstream robotic trajectory instability. Therefore, the proposed framework not only improves average segmentation overlap metrics but also substantially restores severe reflection-induced failure cases that are critical for practical robotic welding applications.

The qualitative results shown in Fig. 4 further support this observation. In particular, Fig. 2(b) demonstrates an extreme glare scenario in which the baseline prediction becomes severely fragmented, whereas the proposed framework reconstructs a geometrically stable curved seam structure closely aligned with the ground truth. Although the numerical IoU value remains relatively modest due to thin-structure sensitivity and boundary ambiguity, the restored seam continuity is sufficiently stable for downstream robotic processes such as seam tracking, centerline extraction, and trajectory generation. This observation highlights an important limitation of relying solely on overlap-based metrics in robotic welding environments involving thin reflective structures.

Interestingly, the effectiveness of the proposed transfer-learning framework was architecture-dependent. Lightweight real-time segmentation architectures, including BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S, consistently benefited from transfer learning, whereas heavier semantic-oriented models such as DeepLabV3+ and the transformer-based SegFormer-B0 exhibited comparatively smaller and less stable improvements under several fine-tuning settings. In particular, DeepLabV3+ frequently showed unstable optimization during decoder-only and partial fine-tuning, while SegFormer-B0 demonstrated inconsistent behavior under CE–Lovasz optimization.

These observations suggest that preserving fine-grained spatial continuity is more important for weld seam segmentation than relying primarily on large-scale semantic-context modeling. More importantly, the primary objective of this work is not to maximize segmentation accuracy using increasingly larger backbones, but to develop a perception module that can be reliably deployed in real-time robotic welding systems. In the proposed application, weld seam segmentation constitutes the first stage of a sequential pipeline including seam tracking, path planning, laser-guided refinement, and robotic trajectory execution. Therefore, low-latency inference is an essential system requirement rather than merely a computational advantage. Previous studies on automated robotic welding have likewise emphasized real-time vision processing to enable continuous robot control throughout the welding pipeline9.

From this perspective, the heavier architectures were included primarily as benchmarking baselines to evaluate the architectural generalizability of the proposed transfer-learning framework. In contrast, lightweight real-time networks are considerably more relevant for practical deployment. BiSeNetV2 achieved the largest improvements across nearly all evaluation metrics, including Recovery Rate, while PIDNet-S also demonstrated consistent gains across multiple fine-tuning strategies. These results indicate that the proposed optimization strategy is particularly effective when combined with architectures specifically designed for efficient real-time dense prediction.

This behavior is likely attributable to architectural differences. BiSeNetV2 explicitly preserves high-resolution spatial information through its bilateral branches, while PIDNet-S maintains a dedicated detail branch that effectively retains thin boundary structures. These characteristics enable accurate localization of narrow weld seams under severe reflections while maintaining real-time inference speed. Consequently, lightweight real-time segmentation networks provide a more practical balance between segmentation robustness, computational efficiency, and reliable robotic operation than heavier semantic-oriented architectures for autonomous robotic welding.

Robotic welding evaluation: laser scanning result via path planning and tracking

To further validate the engineering applicability of the proposed framework, robotic welding experiments were conducted in an indoor laboratory environment under normal ambient lighting conditions. The experimental platform consisted of a Yaskawa HC10 industrial robotic manipulator equipped with an LMI Gocator 2618 A laser profile scanner and an Intel RealSense D455 RGB-D camera. All sensors and the robotic system were integrated and controlled using the ROS 2 framework, while robot motion planning and execution were performed using MoveIt.

As shown in Fig. 2, the predicted weld seam mask was converted into a continuous centerline through skeletonization and endpoint detection. The extracted centerline (yellow) represents the planned welding path, while the detected start and end points (red and green circles) define the scanning direction. The centerline is subsequently transformed into a sequence of waypoints for robotic path planning and trajectory execution.

The generated trajectory was then executed by the robotic manipulator while the laser profile scanner continuously acquired two-dimensional (2D) cross-sectional profiles of the weld seam. Figure 3 illustrates the laser-guided robotic path tracking process during trajectory execution. The red profile represents the measured laser scan of the workpiece, from which the weld seam center is estimated. The positional offset is defined as the distance between the estimated weld seam center and the optical center of the laser scanner (yellow reference line). This offset was computed at each scanning step and used to compensate the robot trajectory before the actual welding process.

The positional offset directly reflects the accuracy of the segmentation-based path planning. A smaller offset indicates that the predicted weld seam more accurately represents the true weld seam and provides a more reliable robotic trajectory. Impact of hyperparameters

The influence of the CE–Lovász weighting parameter λ was evaluated over the range \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0.00 \le \lambda \le 0.20$$\end{document}, where the hybrid objective is defined as1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {L} = \lambda \mathcal {L}_{CE} + (1-\lambda )\mathcal {L}_{Lovasz}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The weighting parameter was selected exclusively according to validation performance and was not optimized using the held-out test set. For BiSeNetV2, the validation performance reached its maximum at λ =0.04, corresponding to CE and Lovász weights of 0.04 and 0.96, respectively. The selected value was subsequently fixed before final evaluation on the test set.

The Lovász-dominant weighting can be explained by the geometric and statistical characteristics of weld-seam segmentation. Weld seams occupy only a small fraction of the image and generally appear as thin and elongated structures. Under this severe foreground–background imbalance, pixel-wise CE can be dominated by the substantially larger background and plate regions. Although CE provides stable local supervision, it optimizes individual pixels independently and does not directly account for the overlap and structural continuity of the predicted seam. Consequently, relatively small but spatially concentrated errors can fragment a seam or remove an important seam segment while contributing only a limited proportion of the total pixel-wise loss.

In contrast, Lovász–Softmax directly optimizes a convex surrogate of the IoU objective and therefore provides an overlap-oriented learning signal that is particularly relevant to thin seam structures. A Lovász-dominant objective consequently places greater emphasis on preserving the complete seam region, while the smaller CE component retains complementary pixel-level discrimination in locally ambiguous regions. The selected λ =0.04 should therefore be interpreted as an empirically determined balance between local pixel classification and region-level seam consistency for BiSeNetV2 rather than as a universally optimal constant.

Importantly, the optimal CE–Lovász balance was not assumed to be identical across segmentation architectures. Different architectures generate different spatial representations and exhibit different optimization dynamics during fine-tuning. Therefore, λ was selected independently for each architecture and fine-tuning configuration according to validation performance. This procedure explains the architecture-specific CE ratios reported in Table 3. The test set remained isolated from this hyperparameter-selection process and was used only for final performance evaluation.

These results demonstrate that the CE–Lovász weighting is architecture-dependent and should be treated as a validation-selected post-training hyperparameter. Cross-domain generalization on an independent welding dataset

To further evaluate whether the proposed model generalizes beyond the WJ3600 domain, an additional cross-domain experiment was conducted using the independently collected Weld-Joint-Segments dataset publicly available through Kaggle38. The external dataset contains 1,597 welding images with visual characteristics distinct from WJ3600, including differences in metallic surface appearance, illumination, background, and imaging conditions.

No images or annotations from this external dataset were used during training, fine-tuning, hyperparameter selection, or model adaptation. Instead, the final BiSeNetV2 model trained exclusively on WJ3600 was directly applied to all 1,597 external images without any additional optimization, thereby providing a zero-shot evaluation under domain shift.

Because the semantic annotation scheme of the external dataset differs from the six-class labeling scheme used in WJ3600, quantitative evaluation was performed using a harmonized binary Plate/non-Plate representation. In the external ground-truth masks, Plate pixels were defined as the positive class, whereas Background and Joint/Seam pixels were treated as the negative non-Plate class. Correspondingly, the six-class BiSeNetV2 predictions were converted into the same binary representation by retaining class 5 as Plate and merging classes 0–4 as non-Plate. This conversion was applied only for evaluation and did not involve retraining or modification of the model.

All 1,597 external images contained valid ground-truth masks and were included in the analysis. Plate IoU, non-Plate IoU, binary mIoU, Plate Dice/F1, Precision, and Recall were computed independently for each image and subsequently averaged across the complete external dataset. The proposed model achieved a mean Plate IoU of 50.17%, a mean non-Plate (Background + Joint) IoU of 88.54%, a mean binary mIoU of 69.36%, a mean Plate Dice/F1 score of 54.58%, and a mean Plate Precision of 94.41%.

These results demonstrate that the model retains meaningful segmentation capability when transferred directly to an independently collected welding-image domain without target-domain adaptation. In particular, the high mean Plate Precision of 94.41% indicates that false-positive Plate predictions remain relatively limited under the external-domain conditions. At the same time, the moderate Plate IoU and Dice/F1 values indicate that substantial differences in metallic surface appearance, illumination, background characteristics, imaging conditions, and annotation definitions remain challenging under zero-shot domain transfer.

Because WJ3600 and the external dataset differ in both annotation scheme and acquisition characteristics, these cross-domain metrics should not be interpreted as a direct numerical comparison with the six-class in-domain WJ3600 results. Rather, this experiment provides an independent assessment of whether the representation learned from WJ3600 retains useful segmentation capability when applied directly, without adaptation, to an unseen welding-image domain. Overall, the results provide evidence of cross-domain transferability while also highlighting the remaining limitations associated with substantial domain shift.

Discussion

Why does the proposed framework improve weld seam segmentation?

The proposed framework substantially improves weld seam segmentation by combining complementary pixel-level and region-level supervision. While the Cross-Entropy loss stabilizes local boundary prediction, the Lovász loss directly optimizes region overlap, encouraging continuous seam structures despite severe specular reflections and illumination variation. Their complementary roles effectively suppress fragmented predictions and improve seam continuity, resulting in a significant increase in Joint IoU while maintaining stable background segmentation.

An important finding is that the effectiveness of the proposed optimization strategy depends strongly on network architecture. Among the evaluated models, lightweight real-time architectures such as BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S consistently achieved the largest performance gains, whereas DeepLabV3+ and the transformer-based SegFormer-B0 exhibited comparatively smaller and less stable improvements under several fine-tuning configurations. These results indicate that optimization strategies should be selected according to architectural characteristics rather than assuming that identical post-training procedures universally improve all segmentation networks.

Figure 6 illustrates the effect of the CE–Lovász weighting parameter λ on validation performance under full fine-tuning. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the validation mIoU increases as λ increases, reaches its maximum at λ =0.04, and gradually decreases thereafter. A similar trend is observed for Joint IoU in Fig. 6(b), where the highest value is also achieved near λ =0.04. Validation Recall (Fig. 6(c)) likewise reaches its maximum at λ =0.04, indicating improved detection completeness. In contrast, the Recovery Rate shown in Fig. 6(d) remains consistently high across a broad range of λ values, suggesting that recovery from severe segmentation failures is relatively insensitive to the precise loss weighting. Based on these observations, λ =0.04 was selected for all subsequent experiments because it achieved the highest validation mIoU while simultaneously providing the best or near-best performance across the remaining evaluation metrics.

Recovery of critical failure cases

Average overlap metrics such as IoU and Dice summarize overall segmentation quality but do not adequately capture catastrophic failures in which the weld seam is completely missed. In robotic welding, such failures are considerably more critical than moderate reductions in average accuracy because subsequent perception modules depend on the detected seam geometry.

To better evaluate robustness against complete seam omission, this study introduces the Recovery Rate. Severe failure cases are defined as test images whose baseline prediction produces a Joint IoU of zero. The Recovery Rate measures the proportion of these identical samples that recover to a valid seam prediction (Joint IoU > 0) after transfer learning. Unlike conventional overlap metrics, the Recovery Rate directly evaluates whether a failed prediction can be restored to a usable seam representation. This criterion is particularly relevant for robotic welding because successful trajectory generation requires reliable seam localization rather than a high average IoU alone.

Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed framework successfully restores most catastrophic failures observed in the baseline model. For BiSeNetV2 under full fine-tuning, 96.33% of 121 baseline zero-IoU cases were recovered. Qualitative results further confirm that most recovered predictions provide geometrically identifiable seam structures suitable for downstream robotic localization.

Despite the high Recovery Rate, a small number of catastrophic failures remained after transfer learning. Figure 7 presents representative unrecovered cases identified through examination of the complete test results. In Fig. 7(a), both the baseline and proposed models fail to overlap with the thin curved ground-truth seam, resulting in a Joint IoU of 0%. The extremely small seam area and weak local contrast relative to the surrounding metallic surface appear to provide insufficient discriminative information for reliable localization. In Fig. 7(b), the proposed model produces fragmented false-positive responses along a visually similar horizontal structure while failing to overlap with the annotated seam, again resulting in a Joint IoU of 0%. This case suggests that competing edge-like structures and local appearance variations in metallic environments can still cause spatial mislocalization even after transfer learning.

From an operational perspective, these remaining zero-IoU predictions are not suitable for reliable centerline and subsequent path extraction because the predicted seam is either absent or spatially inconsistent with the true joint location. These residual cases demonstrate that the proposed framework substantially reduces, but does not completely eliminate, catastrophic segmentation failures. Loss-balance sensitivity

The analysis of the loss-balance parameter λ provides additional insight into the interaction between pixel-level and region-level supervision. As shown in Fig. 6, the validation performance reaches its optimum near λ =0.04, indicating that a Lovász-dominant optimization regime provides the most effective balance between global seam continuity and local boundary refinement.

As the Cross-Entropy contribution increases beyond this region, segmentation performance gradually decreases. This trend suggests that excessive pixel-wise supervision weakens global structural consistency for thin weld seams under challenging reflective conditions. Although moderate Cross-Entropy supervision remains beneficial for stabilizing local predictions, the empirical optimum consistently occurs when region-level optimization dominates.

These findings indicate that preserving globally consistent seam geometry is more beneficial than emphasizing pixel-wise classification accuracy alone for robotic weld seam segmentation.

Practical implications for robotic welding systems

The proposed framework provides a favorable balance between segmentation accuracy and deployment efficiency. Robotic experiments using the vision-based welding platform shown in Figs. 2 and 3 demonstrate that the predicted seam masks can be successfully converted into centerlines for waypoint generation and laser-guided trajectory tracking. As summarized in Table 5, the proposed BiSeNetV2 achieved an average joint-center offset of 2.47 ± 0.78 mm. Compared with the previous BiSeNetV2-based robotic welding system, which reported an average joint-center offset of 24.12 mm9, the proposed transfer-learning framework reduced the localization error by approximately 9.8 ×. In contrast, the remaining segmentation models failed to generate valid seam trajectories under the same experimental conditions, preventing reliable robotic path planning.

Another notable result is that these improvements are achieved without additional computational cost. The proposed BiSeNetV2 framework improves Joint IoU by 22.36 percentage points while maintaining identical FLOPs, parameter count, inference speed, latency, and memory consumption. This demonstrates that substantial performance gains can be obtained through optimized learning strategies while preserving real-time deployment capability.

Overall, the results indicate that improving the training strategy of an existing lightweight segmentation network can be more effective than increasing architectural complexity for weld seam segmentation under challenging reflective conditions. The proposed framework therefore provides a practical solution for real-time robotic welding systems that require both reliable seam localization and computational efficiency.

Methodology

Overview of the proposed post-training optimization framework

This study proposes a lightweight post-training optimization framework for weld seam segmentation in automated construction welding. Rather than developing a new segmentation architecture, the proposed framework systematically improves existing real-time segmentation models through a controlled post-training stage. Starting from an Online Hard Example Mining (OHEM)-pretrained checkpoint, the framework applies a hybrid Cross-Entropy (CE)–Lovász objective to improve segmentation robustness under severe specular reflections and illumination variation while preserving the original network architecture and computational efficiency.

Figure 1 illustrates the overall workflow of the automated mobile robotic welding system, with the proposed post-training optimization stage highlighted in red. An OHEM-pretrained source model is refined through controlled decoder-only, partial, or full fine-tuning using the hybrid CE–Lovász objective. The resulting seam masks are subsequently converted into centerlines through skeletonization and endpoint extraction for waypoint generation and robotic path planning following the procedures described in39–41.

The robotic welding system follows a sequential perception and control architecture established in our previous studies39–41. RGB-based semantic segmentation is used only during the initial perception stage to detect the weld joint, extract its centerline and endpoints, and generate a coarse trajectory for robotic laser scanning. Consequently, segmentation failure or inaccurate seam localization can directly affect the ability to generate and execute the initial laser-scanning path. During the subsequent phase, the laser scanner acquires the cross-sectional joint profile while the robot follows the image-derived coarse trajectory. The actual joint center is then estimated from the laser-scanned profile, and the robotic trajectory is corrected based on the measured joint-center offset. Once the laser scan has been acquired, the previous segmentation mask and RGB-image information are no longer used for downstream path refinement or welding execution. Therefore, final trajectory-tracking accuracy, welding-path deviation, repeatability, and welding quality depend additionally on laser sensing, calibration, robotic control, and welding-process parameters and are beyond the scope of the present study, which focuses specifically on improving the image-segmentation stage.

The proposed methodology therefore focuses on systematic post-training optimization of existing segmentation models rather than architectural redesign. Its effectiveness is evaluated using segmentation accuracy, Recovery Rate, computational efficiency, cross-architecture validation, and downstream robotic trajectory generation.

Robotic system integration and edge-deployment analysis

To evaluate the practical deployability of the proposed framework, the offline training environment was explicitly distinguished from the hardware used for real-time robotic execution. Model training, post-training optimization, hyperparameter selection, and offline evaluation were performed on a high-performance computing server equipped with an NVIDIA A100 Tensor Core GPU with 40 GB of VRAM. The A100 GPU was used only for offline model development and was not used during robotic deployment. In contrast, real-time robotic deployment was evaluated using a mobile workstation integrated into the robotic welding platform, equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i5-12450HX CPU (2.40 GHz), 20 GB of system RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 Laptop GPU with 6 GB of VRAM. The onboard system operated under Ubuntu 22.04 LTS with ROS 2 Humble.

Importantly, the proposed post-training optimization is applied exclusively during offline training and does not modify the deployed inference architecture. The baseline and proposed BiSeNetV2 models therefore have identical network architectures, parameter counts, FLOPs, and inference graphs. Accordingly, the statement “without increasing computational cost” refers specifically to the absence of additional model-level inference complexity introduced by the proposed optimization. Any deployment hardware capable of executing the baseline BiSeNetV2 therefore incurs essentially the same model-level inference requirements when executing the proposed model.

The robotic welding system follows a sequential perception and control architecture established in our previous studies9. The real-time perception-to-execution pipeline is implemented through ROS 2. An Intel RealSense D455 RGB-D camera provides the initial visual input, and incoming RGB frames are processed by the proposed BiSeNetV2 model for semantic segmentation. The resulting weld-joint mask is processed to extract the joint centerline, contour, and endpoints in image coordinates. Using the camera calibration parameters and the corresponding depth information, the extracted image coordinates are transformed into three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates in the robot reference frame. The resulting seam geometry is then converted into a sequence of waypoints defining a coarse trajectory for robotic laser scanning. Robot motion planning and trajectory execution are performed using MoveIt 2 and a Yaskawa HC10 collaborative robotic manipulator.

RGB-based semantic segmentation is therefore used only during this initial perception and coarse path-generation stage. Segmentation failure or inaccurate seam localization can directly affect whether a valid laser-scanning trajectory can be generated and executed. During the subsequent scanning phase, an LMI Gocator 2618 A laser profile scanner continuously acquires cross-sectional profiles of the weld joint while the robot follows the image-derived coarse trajectory. The actual joint center is estimated from the measured laser profile, and the robotic trajectory is corrected according to the resulting joint-center offset. Once the laser profile has been acquired, the previous RGB image and segmentation mask are no longer used for downstream trajectory refinement or welding execution. Thus, final trajectory-tracking accuracy, welding-path deviation, repeatability, and welding quality additionally depend on laser sensing, sensor calibration, robotic control, and welding-process parameters and are beyond the scope of the present segmentation-focused study.

The practical computational performance of the proposed BiSeNetV2 was further profiled directly on the onboard RTX 3050 Laptop GPU. In standalone PyTorch FP32 inference, the model achieved an average inference latency of 9.49 ms per image, corresponding to a throughput of 105.4 FPS. When executed within the live perception loop, including image preprocessing, GPU inference, mask resizing, and post-processing, the inference stage required approximately 19.61 ms per frame, corresponding to approximately 51.0 FPS. The complete perception-to-waypoint pipeline, including camera ingestion, segmentation inference, joint-geometry extraction, waypoint generation, and ROS 2/GUI processing, exhibited an end-to-end processing latency of 33.7 ms per frame, corresponding to approximately 29.7 FPS. This processing rate is compatible with the nominal 30-FPS camera input used by the robotic perception system.

Resource profiling further confirmed the feasibility of onboard execution. The live model-inference process consumed approximately 234 MiB of GPU VRAM, while the total GPU memory usage during execution was approximately 0.62 GiB. The complete perception pipeline consumed approximately 1.39 GiB of system RAM and utilized approximately 4.5 CPU cores. GPU compute utilization during standalone inference was approximately 50–58%. Welding joint dataset: WJ1000 and WJ3600

The experiments were conducted using the WJ3600 welding joint dataset, which extends the earlier WJ1000 dataset developed for vision-based robotic welding in construction environments9. The original WJ1000 dataset contains 1,320 manually annotated RGB images, with 330 images for each of four welding-joint categories: straight-line butt joints, L-shaped butt joints, round butt joints, and corner joints. Together with Plate and Background, these categories constitute six original semantic classes. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 25:5:3.

The original WJ1000 images were captured using a Samsung Galaxy Note 9 camera at a resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1{,}024 \times 512$$\end{document} pixels, with an F2.4 aperture, a 1/750-s shutter speed, ISO 800, and AF-S focus mode. The weld-joint and steel-plate regions were manually delineated using polygonal annotations, which were stored in JSON format as pixel coordinates defining the corresponding semantic regions.

WJ3600 expands the training set to 3,600 images while preserving the original validation and test sets9. Specifically, the expanded training set retains 1,200 original WJ1000 training images and introduces substantially greater background and appearance variability through synthetic compositing and data augmentation. A total of 2,340 construction images from the Alberta Construction Image Dataset and 60 industrial welding-environment images were used as additional backgrounds. Steel-plate regions extracted using the polygonal annotations of WJ1000 were composited with these backgrounds to generate additional training samples. Data augmentation further included random scaling, gamma adjustment, shadow generation, horizontal and vertical flipping, rotation, channel shuffling, brightness and contrast adjustment, and additive Gaussian noise.

The datasets were not originally stratified into predefined reflection-severity categories. Therefore, a numerical distribution among low-, moderate-, and severe-reflection conditions is not reported. Nevertheless, both WJ1000 and WJ3600 contain substantial illumination variation and metallic glare, including challenging cases involving severe specular reflections. Although the expanded dataset contains images with severe illumination changes and specular reflections, it does not include samples in which the weld seam is completely obscured by glare. All retained images remain sufficiently interpretable to permit reliable manual annotation. Images in which reflections completely obscured the weld seam and prevented reliable manual annotation were excluded during dataset construction.

Figure 4b presents one of the most challenging specular-reflection cases in the WJ1000 dataset, which is also included in WJ3600. In this example, although the central portion of the weld seam is partially occluded by strong reflections, the visible seam endpoints and surrounding geometric structure provide sufficient information to consistently identify the underlying weld seam. This example therefore illustrates the upper range of reflection difficulty represented in the annotated dataset rather than a case of complete visual obscuration.

All models were optimized using the six original semantic labels. During evaluation, the four weld joint categories were merged into a single Joint class, while Plate and Background were treated as non-joint regions. This evaluation protocol reflects the downstream objective of robotic welding, where accurate weld seam localization is more important than discriminating individual joint geometries. The detected joint regions are subsequently skeletonized to generate seam centerlines for robotic trajectory planning.

The same WJ3600 images and annotations used in the previous study9 were retained throughout this work. Consequently, the reported performance differences originate from the proposed post-training optimization strategy rather than modifications to the dataset.

Segmentation architecture selection

Five semantic segmentation architectures—BiSeNetV2, PIDNet-S, U-Net, DeepLabV3+, and SegFormer-B0—were selected to evaluate whether the effectiveness of the proposed post-training optimization depends on the structural characteristics of the underlying network. These architectures were not selected solely as additional benchmarking models; rather, they represent distinct segmentation design paradigms with different mechanisms for spatial-detail preservation, semantic-context modeling, and computational efficiency.

BiSeNetV2 was selected as the primary architecture because it is designed specifically for real-time semantic segmentation and provides an effective balance between spatial-detail preservation and semantic representation through its bilateral structure. Its detail branch retains high-resolution spatial information, while its semantic branch captures contextual information at lower computational cost. These characteristics are particularly relevant to robotic weld-seam segmentation, where the target is thin and spatially localized and where low-latency inference is required for downstream robotic operation.

PIDNet-S was included as a second lightweight real-time architecture. Its multi-branch design explicitly separates complementary feature-processing functions and includes detail-preserving processing for fine spatial structures and boundaries. This makes PIDNet-S an informative comparison with BiSeNetV2 for determining whether post-training optimization particularly benefits architectures that explicitly retain high-frequency spatial information.

U-Net was selected to represent the conventional encoder–decoder segmentation paradigm. Its skip connections transfer high-resolution encoder features to the decoder and are widely used for recovering fine spatial details during segmentation. Evaluating U-Net therefore provides a reference for determining whether the proposed optimization remains effective in a classical architecture that preserves spatial information through encoder–decoder feature fusion rather than a dedicated real-time branch.

DeepLabV3+ was selected to represent convolutional architectures that emphasize multiscale semantic-context modeling. Its atrous spatial pyramid pooling and encoder–decoder structure provide a substantially different feature representation from the lightweight detail-oriented architectures. Because weld seams are narrow structures for which precise spatial localization may be more important than broad semantic context, DeepLabV3+ provides a useful comparison for examining how context-oriented feature extraction interacts with the proposed post-training strategy.

Finally, SegFormer-B0 was selected to represent Transformer-based semantic segmentation. Its hierarchical Transformer encoder captures long-range contextual dependencies without relying exclusively on conventional convolutional operations. Including SegFormer-B0 therefore extends the evaluation beyond CNN-based architectures and allows the proposed optimization strategy to be examined under a fundamentally different feature-representation paradigm.

Together, these five architectures cover lightweight bilateral and multi-branch real-time networks, a conventional encoder–decoder model, a multiscale atrous-convolution model, and a Transformer-based segmentation model. This diversity enables a systematic analysis of whether the proposed post-training strategy provides architecture-independent improvements or whether its effectiveness depends on how each network preserves spatial detail and models semantic context.

Restricting optimization to a single epoch minimizes representation drift while isolating the effect of the proposed optimization framework.

Hybrid CE–Lovász loss

During post-training, model parameters are optimized using a hybrid objective combining Cross-Entropy (CE) loss and Lovász–Softmax loss.

Specular reflections frequently generate high-intensity artifacts that fragment seam boundaries and destabilize local predictions. The optimal value was selected solely based on validation-set performance without using the test set during hyperparameter selection.

Implementation details

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch 2.2 and executed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40 GB of memory.

The proposed optimization framework consists of two stages. First, an architecture-specific baseline model was trained on the WJ3600 dataset for 1000 epochs. BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S employed OHEM-based training, whereas DeepLabV3+, U-Net, and SegFormer-B0 were initialized from publicly available pretrained weights. For each architecture, the checkpoint achieving the highest validation mIoU was retained as the initialization for post-training.

Second, each baseline checkpoint underwent one additional epoch of post-training under decoder-only, partial, and full optimization configurations. Weighted CE, OHEM, and the proposed CE–Lovász objective were compared under identical optimization settings. For the hybrid objective, the coefficient λ was selected according to the validation procedure described in Algorithm 1

All RGB images and segmentation masks were resized to 512× 512 pixels. Bilinear interpolation was used for RGB images and nearest-neighbor interpolation was used for segmentation masks. Input images were normalized using the ImageNet mean and standard deviation.

A batch size of 2 was used for BiSeNetV2 and PIDNet-S, whereas a batch size of 4 was adopted for DeepLabV3+, U-Net, and SegFormer-B0 when GPU memory permitted. Post-training employed the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-5}$$\end{document}. Because the optimization stage was intentionally limited to one epoch, no additional learning-rate scheduling was applied.

Class imbalance was addressed using weighted CE. The class weights for the six original labels were [1.6, 11.2, 11.2, 11.2, 11.2, 3.2] for Background, the four weld joint categories, and Plate, respectively. During evaluation, the four weld joint classes were merged into a single Joint category.

All experiments were repeated using random seeds 41, 42, and 43. For each seed, the checkpoint with the highest validation mIoU was retained. Final test performance is reported as the mean ± standard deviation over the three runs.
