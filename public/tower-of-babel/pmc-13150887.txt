Sexual violence disclosure in Canadian postsecondary educational institutions: a scoping review
Authors: Jori Jones, Rachelle Ashcroft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesAcross Canadian postsecondary educational institutions, sexual violence persists as a serious yet largely undisclosed harm with profound impacts on students’ mental, physical and academic well-being. Canadian scholarship has not been systemically synthesised to clarify how sexual violence disclosure processes are framed or experienced within postsecondary contexts despite disclosure often being the primary way students seek support. The aim of this scoping review was to: (1) characterise existing perceptions of disclosure within Canadian postsecondary sexual violence evidence; (2) identify key sources and perspectives informing the Canadian postsecondary sexual violence evidence; (3) synthesise reported impacts of disclosure for students pertaining to Canadian postsecondary sexual violence and (4) highlight gaps and research priorities for Canadian postsecondary sexual violence.DesignA scoping review conducted using Arksey and O’Malley framework, with incorporated enhancements from Levac and colleagues.Data sourcesDocuments published between 1 January 2014 and 7 March 2025 were reviewed. These were located through searches in PsycINFO, ERIC, Sociological Abstracts, Criminal Justice Abstracts and SCOPUS, along with targeted searches of organisational and government websites.Eligibility criteriaPeer-reviewed and grey evidence in English, published from 2014 onward that addressed sexual violence in Canadian postsecondary settings were included. Eligible evidence included empirical studies, theoretical papers, reviews, dissertations, commentaries, books, book chapters and organisational reports. Letters to the editor, book reviews and conference abstracts were excluded.Data extraction and synthesisExtraction and synthesis examined how disclosure is defined, its purpose, associated barriers and outcomes; the role of intersectionality and power relations; and recommendations to improve sexual violence responses.ResultsA total of 224 documents (164 peer-reviewed articles and 60 grey literature documents) were included in the review. Results indicate disclosure is a complex, iterative process, shaped by systems of power that differentially affect students across intersecting identities. Barriers to disclosure were most often linked to both institutional and structural inequities that influence students’ experiences and outcomes. Findings clarify that advancing the safety of sexual violence response and research depends on centring student perspectives and expertise.ConclusionsThis scoping review provides an overview of the power dynamics and risk involved in sexual violence disclosures among students in Canadian postsecondary institutions. Meaningful and transparent application of intersectional and trauma-informed approaches is critical to supporting epistemic safety and cultivating institutional cultures of care rather than perpetuating further harm for Canadian students.Keywords: Sexual Violence, Post-Secondary Institutions, Survivor Disclosure, Canadian Higher Education, Trauma-Informed Research

Introduction

Sexual violence continues to be a significant concern across Canadian postsecondary educational institutions, critically impacting students’ mental, physical and academic well-being. Sexual violence is defined as ‘any sexual act that happens without consent, including threats, attempts to obtain sex, or unwanted sexual comments or advances’.1 Although physical sexual assault is a form of sexual violence, sexual violence can also manifest through multiple forms of harm beyond physical such as psychological and emotional offences that are sexual in nature or directed at an individual’s sexuality. These forms of violence include—but not limited to—sexual harassment, sexual exploitation and technology-facilitated abuse.2 Over the last two decades, reported violent victimisation in the USA has decreased, falling from 79.8 per 1000 in 2003 to 22.5 by 2023.2 The percentage of rape or sexual assault victimisations reported to police increased significantly during this time, rising from 21% in 2012 to 46% in 2023.2 In Canada, however, sexual violence persists as the only category of violent crime that has not improved since 1999.3 Furthermore, it also remains the type of violent offence that is least reported to authorities.4 A similar pattern is evident within Canadian postsecondary institution settings. Despite roughly 71% of students in Canadian postsecondary institutions having endorsed witnessing or experiencing unwanted sexualised behaviours,5 6 upwards of 90% of these incidents are never disclosed to postsecondary institutions.7 The lack of disclosure is concerning and acts as a barrier to receiving adequate support after experiencing sexual violence as there are increased risks of depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation and compromised physical, emotional and spiritual well-being.5 6 8 It is essential for postsecondary institutions to serve as sites of both prevention and support, as experiences of harm not only compromise academic outcomes but also student well-being.

Each student’s experience of disclosing sexual violence is deeply personal, yet pervasively influenced by the social, cultural and structural contexts in which they live.9 Critically, certain groups of students encounter disproportionately higher rates of harm and more complex barriers to support in postsecondary institutional settings.10,12 For example, international students, Indigenous students, 2SLGBTQ+ individuals and students with disabilities are at heightened risk, often navigating multiple forms of discrimination that may reinforce vulnerability and silence.13 14 Understandably, postsecondary student survivors may hesitate to seek support due to a myriad of barriers, including institutional mistrust, fear of retaliation, limited availability of culturally safe services. This scoping review maps the extent to which the nuances and impact of disclosure experiences for students across different intersecting identities exist in current scholarship. Such insights are fundamental for advancing trauma-informed, equitable and inclusive student-survivor centred responses across Canadian postsecondary institutions.

Differentiating disclosure and reporting of sexual violence

Disclosure refers to the act of sharing personal information with others to seek support and plays a central role to identity formation, affirming one’s perspectives and cultivating intimacy in relationships.15 16 In therapeutic contexts, disclosure is understood as fundamental for building therapeutic rapport9 and commonly assumed to support well-being.17 However, Chaudoir and Fisher9 18 warn against assuming that disclosure is always inherently healing. For those who experience marginalisation, the act of disclosure may involve further risks, particularly when the process exposes them to further structural violence.

Despite prominent campaigns such as the global ‘Me Too’ movement and Canada’s Ending Violence Association ‘Speak Out’ initiative, which aimed to motivate individuals to disclose sexual violence and access supports—such as trauma counselling, case management and crisis support—many available resources continue to remain underused.5 7 The decision to disclose is often complex and may even involve potential negative consequences such as the risk of retraumatisation or not being believed, which may deter postsecondary students from seeking help.11 19 20 For some, disclosing may feel less liberating and instead a potential source of harm.21 22 Recognising both the reluctance to disclose and the reasons driving that decision is critical for postsecondary institutions attempting to create trauma-informed and antioppressive environments that do not perpetuate harm for students who strive to disclose.23

Since 2016, several Canadian provinces have required postsecondary institutions to establish sexual violence policies outlining the protocol for reporting, investigation and academic accommodations.24 25 Yet without a national standard, practices can vary considerably. Disclosure involves the choice of sharing an experience of sexual violence with another person, such as a peer, faculty member or counsellor, typically with the hope of receiving validation and support. Reporting, in contrast, initiates a formal institutional procedure where an incident is submitted to an appointed authority, potentially resulting in interim measures or accountability expectations. Students may also choose to report incidents to campus security or local police. An important caveat is that disclosure does not require a reporting process unless the student survivor elects to do so, or the university is legally required to act. It is at this juncture, when disclosures activate institutional or legal obligations, that a student may experience a shift in the control over their disclosure.

Legal frameworks discern the circumstance in which confidentiality cannot be maintained following a sexual violence disclosure. For example, Title IX requires institutions to report and respond to incidents of sexual violence in the USA, at times over-riding student survivor’s preferences pertaining to formal reporting.26 In Canada, several provincial statutes, including Ontario’s Ministry of Training, Colleges and Universities Act, Ontario Human Rights Code and Occupational Health and Safety Act, define the conditions under which institutions must take action to respond to a disclosure.25 27 28 These obligations can be enacted, for example, in situations where the alleged perpetrator is a university employee, when the conduct constitutes a criminal offence or when there is an imminent threat to an individual or the broader campus community. Growing evidence from the USA suggests that broad mandatory reporting policies may actually act to dissuade student survivors from seeking support or confiding in trusted staff members.29 Moreover, even when disclosures result in formal reports, American colleges proceed with formal investigations in only one of four cases.30 Such evidence suggests that compelled or forced disclosures do not actually lead to formal investigations, hearings or sanctions. So, while these legal frameworks and policies may be designed to protect students who experience sexual violence, they may also unintentionally create new challenges to accessing necessary support.29 It is critical to consider that there is also a legacy of colonial power and structural violence in Canada that further differentially impacts students’ access and trust with sexual violence supports.

Canada’s legacy of colonialism and sexual violence

Students from equity-deserving groups, including racialised students, Indigenous students, students with disabilities and international students endure distinct barriers when considering disclosure, challenges that are further compounded by intersecting factors such as race, gender and immigration status.11 Improving institutional responses to sexual violence in Canadian postsecondary institutions depends on acknowledgement of the country’s colonial history of violence, where sexual violence was weaponised as a form of domination and assimilation. These colonial systems still reverberate within higher education, contributing to systemic inequalities such as the ongoing underrepresentation of Indigenous faculty, staff and students.31

The enduring impacts of colonialism are evident in the violent displacement and oppression of Indigenous peoples across Turtle Island.32 Residential schools signify this legacy of colonial violence, where Indigenous children and youth were exposed to extensive sexual, physical and psychological violence producing trauma across generations and communities. Legislation such as the Indian Act (1876), systematically ostracised Indigenous women, Two-Spirit people and girls by denying them rights and protections, ultimately increasing their disproportionate risk to violence.33 The ongoing crisis of Missing and Murdered Indigenous Women, Girls and Two-Spirit People34 35 exemplifies how these injustices frame safety, belonging and trust today. Together, these historical and ongoing colonial harms undergird disclosure experiences for Indigenous students and various equity-deserving groups, deepening mistrust of institutional and government systems.

Although postsecondary institutions in Canada provide supports such as trauma-informed counselling and academic accommodations, these services remain underused by the students most at risk of sexual violence.36 Structural factors, including systemic racism, colonial legacies and precarious immigration conditions, continue to compromise equitable access to support.36 Understanding the motivation behind non-disclosure among equity-deserving students is imperative to designing institutional responses and policies that truly meet the needs of all student survivors.

Purpose and rationale

Prior systematic and scoping reviews in North America have focused on campus-level contributors to sexual violence,37 institutional prevention and response efforts, students’ experiences of harm38 and the academic consequences of sexual violence.39 40 To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to focus specifically on sexual violence disclosure in Canadian postsecondary institutions. By exploring this overlooked topic, the review clarifies the potential effects of disclosure on students and identifies pathways for institutions to reduce structural barriers and provide more student-centred care.

Objective

The aim of this scoping review was to: (1) characterise existing perceptions of disclosure within Canadian postsecondary sexual violence evidence; (2) identify key sources and perspectives informing the Canadian postsecondary sexual violence evidence; (3) synthesise reported impacts of disclosure for students pertaining to Canadian postsecondary sexual violence and (4) highlight gaps and research priorities for Canadian postsecondary sexual violence.

Methods

This study applied scoping review methods to map the range of existing evidence on sexual violence disclosure in Canadian postsecondary settings and to identify gaps in current literature and provide guidance to postsecondary institutions on how to improve supports for students seeking to disclose sexual violence. Scoping reviews are well suited to broad research topics that are underexplored or conceptually complex because they allow for the inclusion of diverse forms of evidence, including grey literature such as institutional and community reports.41 This is an important advantage, as grey literature often captures perspectives that are under-represented in empirical studies.42 Consistent with this inclusive approach, we use the term ‘evidence’ to broadly refer to both research and non-research, in line with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.43 44

The review followed the five-stage scoping review framework outlined by Arksey and O’Malley45 which included: (1) identifying the research question; (2) identifying relevant evidence; (3) study selection; (4) extracting and charting data; and (5) data summary and synthesis of results. Stage five was further refined using enhancements developed by Levac and colleagues46 which included: (1) conducting quantitative descriptive analysis and qualitative thematic analysis, (2) reporting the results within the context of the research question and purpose and (3) interpreting the findings within the context of future research, practice and policy. The review adhered to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines37 43 (see online supplemental file 1) and the published study protocol,47 with minor modifications to the inclusion criteria described below.

Step 1: identify the research question

This scoping review was guided by the research question: ‘What are the perceptions and experiences of disclosure processes of sexual violence in Canadian postsecondary institutions?’.

Step 2: identifying relevant evidence

In consultation with a health and social sciences librarian at the University of Toronto a comprehensive search strategy was developed across APA PsycINFO (as the primary database), ERIC, Sociological Abstracts, Criminal Justice Abstracts and SCOPUS. The searches targeted terms related to sexual violence and the Canadian postsecondary institution setting in titles, abstracts and author-supplied keywords. Search terms included combinations of keywords such as “sexual violence”, “gender-based violence”, “post-secondary institutions”, “Canada” and “students”. The term ‘disclosure’ was intentionally not included as a core search concept. Instead, disclosure related content was identified during the screening and data extraction, to ensure that evidence addressing disclosure indirectly, such as through terms like ‘assessment’, ‘identification’ or as an incidental finding, were captured. Online supplemental file 2 provides the full search strings for each database.

The search was refined to the Canadian context by adding geographical parameters for provinces and major cities (eg, Toronto, Vancouver) and by using existing library filters, such as the University of Alberta library filters for Canada, as cross checks. While the search strategy was intentionally broad to capture all potentially relevant evidence, the study selection process applied stricter inclusion criteria to ensure the final evidence focused on Canadian postsecondary institution settings, regardless of the authors’ geographical location or place names appearing in research tools (eg, instruments like the Montreal Cognitive Assessment).

The included evidence was English language and published from 1 January 2014 onward, reflecting a period marked by significant developments in Canadian postsecondary policy, including Ontario’s Sexual Violence and Harassment Action Plan Act (2016)25 and the broader influence of the ‘Me Too’ movement. Eligible evidence comprised of empirical studies, theoretical papers, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, dissertations, commentaries and relevant grey literature (eg, documents, books, book chapters and reports from national postsecondary bodies) addressing sexual violence in Canadian postsecondary settings. Although the published protocol indicated that book chapters would be excluded, books and book chapters were ultimately included because they contributed conceptual and contextual discussions of disclosure processes in Canadian postsecondary settings. Grey literature was identified through targeted searches of organisational and government websites using key and relevant terms including ‘sexual violence’ and ‘post-secondary’. Websites of national and provincial postsecondary and student organisations including the Canadian Association of College and University Student Services and the Ontario Undergraduate Student Alliance were systematically searched. This process was repeated for provincial and territorial government ministries responsible for postsecondary education across Canada. Letters to the editor, book reviews and conference abstracts were excluded because they typically offer limited methodological detail, incomplete findings or duplication of content.

Step 3: study selection

Study selection proceeded in two stages: (1) title and abstract screening and (2) a full-text review. The principal investigator (JJ) and a research assistant independently screened all records of evidence, with a supervising faculty member (RA) available to review screening decisions. Covidence was used to organise and track the screening process. To ensure consistency, both reviewers screened the initial 25 abstracts and reached the recommended calibration of at least 75% agreement on sorting decisions.48 The remaining title/abstract and full text screening was completed independently. Inclusion criteria was guided by several factors. During title and abstract screening, evidence was kept if it: (1) centred on sexual violence and included ‘sexual violence’ or a related term; (2) focused on postsecondary students and/or institutions; (3) was published on or after 1 January 2014; and (4) was written in English. At the full-text stage, evidence also needed to be situated in the Canadian context, determined either by the first or last author being affiliated with a Canadian institution or through explicit mention of Canadian postsecondary students within the document. As per Levac’s recommendation,35 the team communicated as needed to discuss selection decisions and maintain consistency throughout the process.

Step 4: data summary and synthesis of results

A data extraction chart was developed to support consistency and comprehensive extraction across all included evidence. The chart included key fields relevant to the research question, including author details, study location, methodological approach, theoretical perspectives, definitions of disclosure and how disclosure was considered or analysed. The principal investigator (JJ) completed data extraction under the supervision of the third reviewer, faculty member (RA). Particular attention was given to identifying perspectives informing the current understandings of sexual violence disclosure in Canadian postsecondary institutions. All data included for this review was sourced from publicly available content.

Step 5: summarising and presenting data

A reflexive thematic analysis was implemented, drawing on Braun and Clarke’s guidance.49 50 The analysis proceeded through their six steps42: (1) becoming familiar with the data, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) writing the report. Throughout this process, memo writing was used to document reflections and analytical insights, supporting reflexivity. Summary tables and charts were created for all extraction items to map how many articles addressed each subcomponent. An intersectional framework, informed by work from Sherman and colleagues,51 guided coding and interpretation by attending to how health, resource access, privilege, oppression and historical and current contexts influence experiences of disclosure. Close attention was given to how identities and power relations were represented, categorised and analysed within the selected evidence.52 53

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Description of sample

There were 1701 citations from the literature search (see figure 1). After removing duplicates, 1172 abstracts were screened and 904 were excluded for not having a central focus on either sexual violence or the postsecondary setting. A full-text review was completed for 268 documents, 37 were excluded for not being situated in a Canadian setting, 6 did not meet the study type inclusion criteria and 1 was excluded for not being written in English. Subsequently, 224 documents (164 peer-reviewed, 60 grey literature) were included in the review. The number of publications increased over time, from 4 (1.8%) in 2014 to a peak of 32 (14.2%) in 2022, before declining in the following years. Most literature was based in Canada (n=192, 85.7%) (see figure 2), with nearly half of first authors being situated in Ontario (n=102, 45.5%). Other Canadian provinces represented included British Columbia (n=18, 8.0%), Quebec (n=16, 7.1%), Nova Scotia (n=13, 5.8%), Alberta (n=12, 5.4%), Saskatchewan (n=6, 2.7%), New Brunswick (n=6, 2.7%), Manitoba (n=2, 0.9%), the Northwest Territories (n=1, 0.4%) and Newfoundland and Labrador (n=1, 0.4%). 15 publications (6.7%) were set in Canada but did not specify a province. Although publications focused on Canadian settings, some were authored internationally, including from the USA (n=28, 12.5%), the UK (n=3, 1.3%) and from South Korea (n=1, 0.5%). In terms of geographical focus of the document, nearly half were of Canadian national focus (n=105, 46.9%), followed by a specified lens on Central Canada (Ontario and Quebec) (n=55, 24.6%) and North America (Canada and USA) (n=39, 17.4%). Nine documents (4.0%) addressed Eastern Canada (New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, Prince Edward Island, and Newfoundland and Labrador), six (2.7%) focused on Western Canada (British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba) and one (0.4%) examined the Northern regions (Yukon, Northwest Territories and Nunavut). When considering community settings, nine documents (4%) explicitly specified an urban context and six (2.7%) concentrated on rural or remote settings.

Affiliated region of first author.

Peer-reviewed publications (n=164, 73.2%) were mostly empirical studies (n=127, 77.4%), alongside literature review (n=10, 6.1%), perspective or commentaries (n=10, 6.1%), book chapters (n=9, 5.5%), conceptual (n=4, 2.4%) pieces, policy analyses (n=2, 1.2%), a programme report (n=1, 0.6%) and a book (n=1, 0.6%). Grey literature (n=60, 26.8%) mainly consisted of policy briefs (n=17, 28.3%), toolkits (n=8, 13.3%), institutional reports (n=6, 10%) and practice recommendations (n=6, 10%). Most empirical studies employed quantitative designs (n=66, 52%) with the majority using survey methods (n=64, 51.6%). Qualitative approaches (n=49, 38.6%) primarily involved interviews (n=21, 42.9%), focus groups (n=12, 24.5%) and policy analyses (n=7, 14.3%), while 12 studies (9.4%) adopted a mixed-methods design. Not all studies incorporated student perspectives within their methodology. 66 documents (29.5%) did not include any explicit input from students in their design or analysis. For the remaining 158 documents that included students in their design, 68 studies (24%) relied primarily on closed-ended or scaled survey instruments. Nearly half of the documents (n=103, 46%) did not specify a theoretical framework guiding their analysis. For those that did, intersectionality (n=51, 22.8%) was most frequently applied, followed by feminist (n=36, 16.1%), trauma-informed (n=15, 6.7%) and antiracism approaches (n=10, 4.5%).

Definition and purpose of disclosure

More than half of the reviewed documents (n=138, 61.6%) do not use the term ‘disclosure’ in their study of sexual violence in postsecondary contexts. Instead, related terms such as ‘reporting’, ‘help-seeking’, ‘complaint’ or ‘identification’ were used to describe instances in which sexual violence occurred (see figure 3). When disclosure processes were discussed, 68 documents (30.4% of the total sample) indicated that disclosures are directed towards general postsecondary services, 34 (15.2%) to friends or peers, 15 (6.7%) to law enforcement and 14 (6.3%) to health professionals or clinical settings. Smaller proportions identified disclosures to campus human rights or sexual violence response offices (n=12), community organisations (n=9), campus authorities (n=6) or family members (n=1).

Terminology use associated with disclosure.

In examining how disclosure was discussed in the literature, we identified three themes: (1) disclosure as a complex contextual process, (2) disclosure as a form of decision-making and risk navigation and (3) disclosure as an institutional encounter and response.

Disclosure as a complex and contextual process

Disclosure was described as an individualised, iterative and uncertain process framed by social and institutional contexts. Of the 86 documents that explicitly used the term ‘disclosure’ in relation to sexual violence support, the concept was often characterised as complex. Contributing factors included procedural barriers and limitations (n=40), confusion and complexity surrounding definitions of sexual violence and available support pathways (n=34), the influence of social identity impacting disclosure experiences (n=29), institutional mistrust (n=24) and stigma and fear (n=20).

Disclosure as a form of decision-making and risk navigation

Disclosure was often described as a deliberative process involving the balance of risks and available support. Non-disclosure was at times positioned as a deliberate strategy for self-protection. Related themes included safety concerns (n=27), the need for support (n=26), agency and control (n=26), conflicted motivations and hesitations (n=18) and silence or non-disclosure as an act (n=18). Conflicted motivations and hesitations can reflect the tension between seeking support and concerns about how disclosure might affect others involved, relationships with peers or lead to unintended academic consequences.

Disclosure as an institutional encounter and response

There were also documents that position disclosure within the institutional system focusing on how the disclosure experience is shaped by organisational response and structure. Common themes included the quality of staff responses to disclosure, referring to whether a response was perceived as supportive and trauma-informed or dismissive and procedural (n=39), the impacts of disclosure, including situations where students became involved in formal investigations they had not anticipated (n=27), secondary victimisation and institutional betrayal (n=19) and non-disclosure of victimisation in research contexts, often characterised as a research burden or limitation (n=7).

Barriers and outcomes to disclosure in postsecondary settings

There were roughly 48 (21.4%) documents that acknowledged or discussed barriers to disclosures that were explicitly connected to the postsecondary institution. Themes that surfaced from these documents included (1) institutional self-preservation, (2) student power imbalances and (3) stigma and structural marginalisation.

Institutional self-preservation

Documents referenced students’ lack of confidence in policies/protocol (n=28), institutional betrayal (n=26), mandatory reporting (n=10), fear of authority (n=3) and experiences of procedural humiliation (n=3) as barriers to feeling confident about disclosing sexual violence to postsecondary institutions. Procedural humiliation referenced experiences such as being required to repeatedly recount details of violence or perceiving those intimate details of harm needed to be disclosed to meet institutional definitions or criteria for support.

Student power imbalances

The concept of relational power differentials was described as a barrier to disclosing. Documents framed unequal dynamics by naming such things as limited institutional resources and capacity (n=27), students having less power in procedural pathways for reporting harm to postsecondary institutions (n=19), a fear of not being believed (n=19), lack of trust that the postsecondary institution will hold their information confidentially (n=6) and fear of authority (n=3).

Stigma and structural marginalisation

Documents discussed disclosure in relation to broader systemic inequities embedded within institutional practices. Stigma and social identity were mentioned as factors influencing students’ sense of safety and perceived legitimacy of disclosure. Fear of discrimination and retaliation (n=24) and the normalisation or minimisation of sexual violence (n=8) within campus life were also noted.

Most documents (n=138, 61.6%) did not discuss outcomes or consequences related to the disclosure of sexual violence. Of the 86 documents that did, reported outcomes varied. Positive outcomes included the relief of mental health symptoms when disclosures were met with compassionate responses12 which were described as fostering healing.54 Negative outcomes included mentioned secondary victimisation,55 stigma56 and institutional betrayal57 due to experiences of blame, disbelief, breaches of confidentiality or exclusion from institutional processes. These impacts were associated with declines in academic performance,58 increased post-traumatic stress symptoms and maladaptive coping.59 Several documents described mixed or ambivalent impacts, where students perceived disclosure both as an opportunity to access support pathways and as exposure to new risks and costs, particularly when institutional processes were felt to limit student survivor agency or control.

Intersectionality and power

An intersectional framework was explicitly mentioned in 51 (22.8%) documents. Intersectionality was most referenced in respect to the uneven distribution of vulnerability among student populations. When social identity was examined in relation to sexual violence and institutional response, it was linked to dynamics of power and privilege (n=36), compounding oppressions (n=28), differential disclosure outcomes (n=26), silencing mechanisms (n=24) and perceived safety or risk (n=16). Almost one-third (n=68, 30.4%) of documents did not acknowledge any dimension of identity in relationship to the disclosures process or sexual violence response. 54 documents (24.1%) involved one vector of identity in their work, while fewer included two vectors (n=32, 14.3%). A larger proportion (n=75, 33.5%) engaged with three or more intersecting identity dimensions. Gender was the most referenced (67.9%), followed by race (36.2%), LGBTQ2S+ identity (32.6%), disability (20.1%) and Indigeneity (19.2%). Fewer documents addressed citizenship or immigration status (15.6%), class (7.6%) or community context (rural or remote settings; 3–4%). Mentions of age, religion, housing status or professional status were mentioned the least (below 3%) (see figure 4).

Considered dimensions of identity.

Most documents (n=169, 75.4%) did not explicitly acknowledge power, hierarchy or institutional authority in relation to disclosure processes or sexual violence responses in postsecondary institutions. From those that did (n=55), most described power operating at the organisational level (n=38, 69%), followed by systemic hierarchies (n=34, 61.8%) and individual power (n=22, 40%). The types of power mentioned included student-staff power imbalance (n=28, 50.9%), postsecondary procedural control (n=23, 41.8%), the normalisation/minimisation of sexual violence (n=19, 34.5%), silencing (n=18, 32.7%), misrepresentation or erasure of identity (n=13, 23.6%), and the gatekeeping of access to resources (n=12, 21.8%).

Recommendations to improve disclosure and sexual violence response

In practice

Recommendations for improving students’ experience of support and increasing their confidence that disclosure would lead to appropriate support were evident across several documents. Common recommendations included expanding trauma-informed and inclusive disclosure response training for faculty and staff.60,63 Enhanced accessibility and culturally responsive supports were highlighted, such as multilingual materials, providing materials using multiple types of platforms, and confidential or anonymous services.64 65 Equity focused practices were also named to address service gaps for groups like male students, student survivors in rural or remote areas and other marginalised groups through Gender-based Analysis Plus, culturally competent policies and tailored interventions.66 67

In research

Recommendations for future research also aligned with equity-oriented access and inclusion. Authors called for more studies to explore barriers to help-seeking among students with disabilities, international students and those with stigmatised or intersecting identities.12 68 69 Several documents described a need for deeper examination of disclosure processes and the ways power and perceptions of institutional trustworthiness influence disclosure decisions.70 71 Methodological recommendations included moving beyond self-report surveys by incorporating behaviour-specific questions, content warnings and narrative approaches to increase student involvement and representation of marginalised voices.72 73 At the policy level, there were suggestions to standardise risk assessment tools and to study how policies influence disclosure outcomes, educational trajectories and career.74 75

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised current evidence on how sexual violence disclosure is conceptualised within Canadian postsecondary institutions. The aim was to clarify the perspectives informing existing scholarship, describe reported impacts of disclosure for students and identify key gaps and research priorities to advance safe, equitable and trauma-informed disclosure processes. Much of the reviewed literature reflects institutional and researcher-driven perspectives, with comparatively limited attention to student perspectives, a gap noted in several studies and reports.59 71 73 Few documents explicitly addressed the disclosure process itself.66 74 When discussed more broadly, disclosure was often conflated with institutional reporting processes76 and rarely analysed as a relational and contextual experience,77 creating barriers to equitable access to support and justice.78 Overall, most reviewed literature is procedural and compliance-oriented, with fewer documents considering broader structural and sociocultural dimensions such as identity, safety and power.12 57 64 Grounded in an intersectional framework and epistemic justice lens, this discussion explores how power dynamics and methodological choices determine whose voices and knowledge are represented, and how these patterns impact the accessibility and safety of sexual violence disclosures within Canadian postsecondary settings.

Disclosure as complex and contextual

Reviewed documents that explicitly examined disclosure processes revealed its complex and rich contextual nature, often shaped by student survivors’ experiences of safety and trust with their postsecondary institution.12 64 66 67 74 75 79 Collectively, descriptions across the literature characterised disclosure as both a potential pathway for healing and a source of risk for further harm. As noted by Sit and Schuller,80 the process can elicit either positive responses (eg, emotional support or referral to resources) or harmful reactions (eg, blame, stigma or procedural exclusion). When disclosure is poorly distinguished from reporting, students may feel compelled to prioritise credibility over healing, fearing disbelief and pressure to be clear and defendable of their experience before seeking.57 81 82 These terms ‘disclosure’ and ‘reporting’ carry specific social meanings linked to safety and control. When used interchangeably, they obfuscate students’ understanding of their rights and options for support. Epistemic injustice refers to situations where knowledge may be constructed or applied in ways that marginalise certain experiences, resulting in incomplete understandings.83 In this context, conceptual vagueness creates hermeneutical gaps83 whereby unclear definitions and procedures compromise student survivors’ ability to predict what disclosure will involve, anticipate how it will be received and maintain control over the information they share.

Understanding disclosure as a complex and iterative process requires an intersectional framework that considers how student survivors are uniquely positioned within societal judgements of their intersecting identities, influencing their disclosure decisions, experiences and outcomes. Moving beyond the performative naming of intersectionality, documents that meaningfully applied the framework12 67 84 demonstrated disclosure processes and sexual violence responses are filtered through dimensions of intersecting identities. Minimal consideration was given to trust and perceived risk in disclosures, however the few documents that did highlight this critical factor highlighted that black, Indigenous and racialised students often face compounded safety concerns when considering disclosure, including cultural mistrust of institutions such as law enforcement56 and fear that naming perpetrators could reinforce stigma against their communities.85 Disclosure of sexual violence may also indirectly expose marginalised aspects of students’ identity, such as their sexuality, potentially intensifying feelings of vulnerability and safety.86 Critical black feminist scholars note that racialised and gender-based harm are often inseparable,87 yet from this review several documents noted that institutional policies and certain research methodologies require students to compartmentalise their identity and experiences.59 69 88 89 Recent work calls for extending intersectional analyses beyond the ‘Big Eight’ identity dimensions to include factors like age, body size, neurodiversity, housing, mental health, first language and citizenship.90 Combined, these findings highlight that intersectional analysis is essential to better understanding the differential complexity of sexual violence disclosure.

Postsecondary institution-specific barriers to disclosure

When barriers to disclosure were mentioned in the literature, they were often described through student experiences of structural power at both institutional and systemic levels.55 65 70 91 At the institutional level, power was described as operating through student-institution hierarchies, procedural control and policy language. Hierarchal power was particularly evident when students disclosed harm perpetrated by faculty,60 92 where disclosures may trigger forced reporting processes, negating student agency in the name of community safety. Several documents raised issue about how procedural jargon and vague definitions of sexual violence in postsecondary policy can limit students’ ability to identify and credibly name experiences of harm to their school with confidence.84 93 94 This ambiguity lends itself to epistemic exclusion,83 95 leaving student survivors uncertain about whether their experiences ‘count’ as violence and jeopardises their sense of control over their disclosure.

At a systemic level, sexual violence was at times perceived as normalised across broader social and campus cultures. Colpitts’ intersectional analysis of sexual violence polices across Ontario demonstrated how institutional responses can mirror rape culture, racism, gender discrimination and inequities inherent in the criminal justice system, reinforcing the minimalisation of sexual violence.84 Stigma also surfaced as a structural barrier in that being labelled as a ‘difficult’ graduate student, a ‘victim’ or a ‘complainant’ can threaten academic, professional or social standing, including reputational relationships with supervisors.13 70 The disclosure process within postsecondary institutions becomes a negotiation of power and risk for student survivors.

Impacts of disclosure on students

The extent to which postsecondary sexual violence policies and procedures in Canada account for how students’ intersecting social identities shape their risks, needs and outcomes following harm is underexplored. Few documents explicitly acknowledge that student survivors may experience differential impacts and outcomes related to their sexual violence disclosure. From the documents that did capture impacts of disclosure, positive outcomes were linked to compassionate and trauma-informed responses.12 54 80 Whereas, while negative outcomes or conflicting feelings reflected institutional betrayal96 or secondary victimisation94 97 as evidenced by such things as blame, disbelief, breaches to confidentiality and exclusion procedural processes.58 59 98

This review also considered the methodological choices of researchers to understand which sources of data and knowledge are prioritised. Within research contexts, participants asked to recall and disclose experiences of sexual violence similarly risk secondary victimisation. Many studies relied on closed ended surveys (eg, Sexual Experiences Survey) and Likert-scale instruments. While these methods facilitate large-scale data collection, such as campus climate surveys, there is limited opportunity to monitor participants’ well-being during and after participation. Documents that demonstrated stronger trauma-informed approaches prioritised proactive measures to support participant safety and choices such as clear content warnings, trained researchers able to respond to activation and accessible safety features throughout participation (eg, quick exit buttons).99,102 Studies that provided safe spaces for debriefing, psychoeducation, crisis lines, peer-based or local supports further exemplified trauma-informed principles.99101,104

Future directions

Evidence throughout the reviewed documents reflect a growing awareness that advancing sexual violence response in Canadian postsecondary settings requires improving the safety conditions of disclosure, acknowledging structural power and enhancing intersectional representation in both policy and research.13 70 82 105 106 Intersectional frameworks39107,109 and trauma-informed approaches63 64 78 were most recommended as strategies to guide these efforts. There is a call to action at both individual-level disclosure response training and institutional-level attention to clarify systemic influences shaping institutional culture, accountability and trust. Overall, advancing sexual violence response necessitates centring students and drawing on their knowledge and diverse experiences to cultivate epistemic, procedural and structural safety in both sexual violence support practice and research.

Strengths and limitations

This review offers several key strengths. The final sample of 224 documents provided rich and diverse data for synthesis. A comprehensive search strategy spanning multiple databases and grey literature sources supported a broad consideration of evidence that included both research and organisational documents related to sexual violence disclosures in the Canadian postsecondary context. Including grey literature was critical to ensure representation of both student and institutional perspectives that may not appear in peer-reviewed publications, supporting an expansive understanding of disclosure processes and responses in Canadian postsecondary contexts. Additionally, the publication of a review protocol enhanced transparency and methodological rigour by indicating the review objectives, eligibility criteria and analytical approach prior to conducting the review.

This review can be interpreted with the following limitations in mind. Relevant or contradictory perspectives may have been omitted due to the exclusion of non-English sources, older publications or grey literature missed through the search strategy. Furthermore, consistent with scoping review methodology, the study did not include a formal assessment of the methodological quality of all included evidence. The findings are a mapping of existing evidence, not an evaluation of the strength of evidence.

Conclusion

This scoping review provides an overview of the power dynamics and risk involved in sexual violence disclosures among students in Canadian postsecondary institutions. Advancing safety and equity in sexual violence responses requires centring diverse student perspectives and experiences across research, policy and practice. Meaningful and transparent application of intersectional and trauma-informed approaches is critical to supporting epistemic safety and cultivating institutional cultures of care rather than perpetuating further harm for Canadian students.
