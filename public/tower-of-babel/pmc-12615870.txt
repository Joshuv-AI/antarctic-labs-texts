Proportion and predictors of postoperative depression among elderly urology patients in Bangladesh
Authors: Nayan Mony, Md Monir Hossain Shimul, Saimum Arafat Pantho, Md Shah-paran Islam Probal, Salamat Khandker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative depression is a frequently underdiagnosed condition, particularly in elderly surgical patients, and is associated with poorer recovery outcomes. This study assessed the proportion and risk factors of postoperative depression in elderly patients undergoing urological surgery in Bangladesh.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary level hospital in Dhaka, from June to September 2024. A total of 130 patients aged 55 years and above, scheduled for urological surgery, were recruited using systematic sampling. Data were collected preoperatively and at discharge using a structured questionnaire, using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify associated risk factors.ResultsPostoperative depression was present in 69.2% of patients, with 34.6% experiencing moderate and 2.3% severe symptoms. Significant predictors included age 56–65 years (OR = 3.10, p = 0.024), housewife occupation (OR = 4.48, p = 0.027), sleep problems (OR = 8.16, p = 0.056), surgery duration ≥ 121 min (OR = 13.60, p = 0.025), high pain scores (OR = 0.065, p = 0.023), and perceived stress (OR = 12.82, p = 0.010). Depression was also significantly linked with shorter surgeries, hospital stays over 10 days, functional impairment, and elevated anxiety and stress levels, indicating a strong interplay between psychosocial and surgical factors.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that anxiety and depression are common among elderly urological patients, highlighting the importance of integrating psychosocial support into routine perioperative care.Keywords: Postoperative depression, Elderly, Urological surgery, Bangladesh, Risk factors, Hospital anxiety and depression scale, Sleep disturbance, Surgical outcomes

Introduction

Depression affects over 300 million individuals globally and is projected to be the leading cause of disability by 2030 [1, 2]. The condition affects all age groups but tends to be severe among older adults, especially those experiencing medical comorbidities or undergoing surgical procedures [3].

Postoperative depression, a subtype of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), refers to the emergence or exacerbation of depressive symptoms following surgical procedures. It is part of the broader category of postoperative neuropsychiatric disorders (POND), which also includes cognitive dysfunction and delirium [4]. This mental health condition is frequently overlooked in surgical care, despite its association with delayed recovery, decreased functional status, prolonged hospital stays, poor treatment adherence, and increased mortality [5]. Global data suggest that between 10% and 30% of patients may experience depressive symptoms after surgery, with elderly individuals [6, 7].

Urological surgerymay result in long-term complications such as urinary incontinence, sexual dysfunction, or catheter dependency, all of which can negatively impact the patient’s mental health [8]. Furthermore, the surgical stress response, anesthesia, and potential postoperative complications such as pain, infections, and immobility act as additional psychosocial stressors contributing to depression [9]. For example, pooled analyses suggest depression prevalence of 25–30% among elderly surgical patients in South Asia [10, 11], underscoring the regional public health importance of this issue.

In Bangladesh, the burden of non-communicable diseases including urological disorders is rising and the issue of postoperative depression is of increasing importance [12]. Taken together, the global evidence on the mental health burden among surgical patients, the increasing prevalence of non-communicable diseases in Bangladesh, and the scarcity of local studies on elderly urological patients clearly establish the rationale for conducting this study.

However, local data regarding the incidence of postoperative depression among elderly surgical patients are scarce. Mental health services are often limited in under-resourced surgical wards, and the focus is typically placed on managing physical outcomes such as infection, wound healing, or mortality [13].This gap in routine assessment and follow-up of mental health in postoperative care potentially leaves a significant number of patients untreated, thereby compounding their suffering and impairing their overall recovery.

Previous international studies have identified various risk factors for postoperative depression, including older age, female gender, low educational attainment, pre-existing neuropsychiatric conditions, chronic pain, sleep disturbances, poor functional status, and lack of social support [14–16]. In the Bangladeshi context, however, the role of cultural stigmas, socioeconomic instability, and limited access to mental health services may further amplify the risk yet remain poorly understood or unaddressed. Additionally, factors specific to the surgical experience such as the type and duration of surgery, the presence of postoperative complications, and length of hospitalization may influence the psychological well-being of patients [17].

Despite the growing recognition of mental health as a critical component of overall health outcomes, postoperative depression remains largely overlooked in Bangladeshi hospitals. Although the number of elderly surgical patients is steadily increasing in Bangladesh, there is a notable scarcity of research on postoperative depression in this population. Elderly patients undergoing urological surgery represent a particularly vulnerable group, as they often face both physical frailty and psychosocial stressors, making them prone to anxiety and depression. Yet, this subgroup has been understudied in Bangladesh. Although ≥ 60 years is a commonly used international cutoff, we adopted ≥ 55 years based on Bangladeshi demographic studies and national geriatric policy frameworks [18, 19], ensuring cultural and contextual relevance.

This study aims to fill the existing knowledge gap by investigating the proportion and associated risk factors of postoperative depression in elderly patients undergoing urological surgery in Bangladesh. The central hypothesis is that sociodemographic factors lifestyle behaviors, and surgical variables are associated with postoperative depression. The findings from this study are expected to provide future guidelines for clinical practices by encouraging the integration of psychological assessment into perioperative care.

Methodology

Study design and settings

This was a hospital-based cross-sectional study; patients were enrolled at admission and observed until discharge, with exposures and outcomes assessed within the same admission episode. The study was conducted from June to September 2024. The research took place at the Department of Urology of a tertiary referral center located in Dhaka, Bangladesh. This institute performs an average of 1000–2000 urological procedures annually, making it an appropriate and representative site for the study of postoperative depression in elderly urological patients.

Study population

The study population comprised elderly patients aged 55 years and above who were admitted at the selected hospital for elective or emergency urological surgeries. The selected patients represented both urban and rural regions of Bangladesh.

Sample size and sampling technique

The required sample size was calculated using the single proportion formula (n = Z2pq/d2), assuming a 12.7% prevalence of postoperative depression among urological patients [17], with a 90% confidence level (Z = 1.645) and 5% margin of error.This yielded a minimum required sample size of 139. Although the study hospital had a finite patient pool, the formula without finite population correction was used to preserve external validity for similar institutions nationwide. The use of a 90% confidence level was chosen to ensure feasibility within the available time and resource constraints; however, all subsequent analyses were performed at 95% confidence. This approach has been adopted in prior clinical studies from comparable settings [3]. Due to institutional formalities, 130 eligible participants were ultimately enrolled. A formal power analysis was not performed; however, the achieved sample size was adequate for logistic regression, allowing ~ 10 events per variable. A systematic random sampling technique was employed, wherein every third patient listed in the admission registry of the selected hospital for surgery was approached for participation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All patients aged ≥ 55 years that underwent elective or emergency urological surgery at the selected hospital during the study period were included. Patients with history of known preoperative psychiatric disorders, preexisting depression, communication impairments, and altered mentation at the time of recruitment, or frequent history of surgical interventions were excluded.

Data collection tools

Eligible participants were identified preoperatively, and written informed consent was obtained in Bangla. Data were collected using a structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire translated into Bangla from English. The instrument included sections on sociodemographic information, comorbidities, perioperative factors, and mental health status. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), a 14-item tool with separate subscales for anxiety and depression. The HADS was translated into Bangla using forward–backward translation by two bilingual experts, following WHO translation guidelines. Content validity was reviewed by a panel of three psychiatrists to ensure cultural and clinical appropriateness. The tool has been validated in Bangladesh previously, showing good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82 for anxiety and 0.78 for depression) [20]. Based on the depression subscale (HADS-D), scores were categorized as follows: 0–7 indicating no depression, 8–10 mild depression, 11–14 moderate depression, and 15–21 severe depression. A score of 11 or higher was considered the threshold for identifying probable cases of postoperative depression, reflecting moderate to severe symptom levels.

Data collection and quality control

Baseline data were collected before surgery and follow-up depression assessment was performed on the day of hospital discharge. Data collectors were trained on the study objectives, ethical considerations, and proper questionnaire administration. A pretest was conducted on 5% (n = 6) of patients to ensure tool clarity and interviewer reliability. The principal investigator conducted daily reviews of collected data to ensure accuracy and completeness. Confidentiality was maintained throughout by anonymizing participant data.

Data analysis

All data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions and measures of central tendency, were used to summarize patient characteristics. The Shapiro–Wilk test was performed to test data normality. Bivariate analysis using chi-square tests was conducted to assess associations between independent variables and depression status. Variables with p < 0.2 in bivariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Although the sample size calculation applied a 90% confidence level, post-hoc assessment indicated that the achieved sample size ensured adequate statistical power (> 80%) for analyses at the 95% confidence level, thereby minimizing the risk of Type II error and preserving methodological rigor.

Results

Among the 130 respondents, 64.6% were aged between 56 and 65 years (mean age 61.48 ± 7.78), with males comprising 71.5% (Table 1). Most had secondary education (45.4%) and were engaged in business (46.2%). Rural residence predominated (66.2%), and 80.8% of families earned less than 25,000 BDT monthly. Hypertension (81.5%) and diabetes (15.4%) were common comorbidities. The BMI distribution showed that 47.7% had normal weight, while 36.2% were overweight and 13.2% obese (mean BMI 25.31 ± 4.63). While 58.5% slept ≥ 6 h per day, 41.5% slept less. Tobacco use was reported by 35.4%, with smokeless tobacco being the most common. Physical inactivity affected 40% of participants, and nearly half (49.2%) had hospital stays of fewer than 5 days, while 40.8% stayed for 5–10 days and 10% for more than 10 days.

Based on the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), the overall distribution of depressive symptoms among the 130 respondents indicated that 30.8% had no depression, 32.3% experienced mild depression, 34.6% had moderate depression, and only 2.3% exhibited severe depression (Table 2). When stratified by sex (Fig. 1), male respondents (n = 93) reported slightly higher rates of no depression (35.5%) compared to females (18.9%), while the proportions of mild and moderate depression were lower among males (30.1% each) than females (37.8% and 45.9%, respectively). Severe depression remained uncommon in both groups, affecting 2.2% of males and 2.7% of females.

Table 3 presents the frequency of depression in relation to sociodemographic characteristics and health related information of respondents. Statistically significant associations with depression were observed for education level (p = 0.016), profession (p = 0.002), tobacco use (p = 0.009), sleep problems (p < 0.001), and sleep duration (p = 0.014). Participants with lower education levels (≤ 10 years) were more frequently depressed, and those engaged in business (50%) or housewives (31.1%) had higher depression rates compared to others. Notably, sleep disturbances were significantly associated with depression, with 41.1% of depressed individuals reporting sleep problems compared to only 7.5% in the non-depressed group.

Those sleeping less than 6 h were more likely to be non-depressed (57.5%) than their depressed counterparts (34.4%), suggesting better sleep duration is linked to lower depression risk. While tobacco use overall was associated with depression, smokeless tobacco users (23.3%) and dual users (6.7%) were more likely to be depressed. Variables like age, sex, income, place of residence, chronic disease, BMI, and physical activity were not significantly associated with depression in this sample.

Table 4 reveals several significant associations between depression and various surgical and hospital characteristics among elderly patients undergoing urological surgery. Depression was significantly more prevalent in patients who underwent minor surgeries (40% vs. 15%, p = 0.005) and those whose surgeries lasted less than 60 min or up to 120 min (p = 0.039). Postoperative complications such as acute renal failure and lab-confirmed infections, though rare, were found exclusively among depressed patients (p = 0.048). Notably, cognitive dysfunction and ischemic stroke occurred only in the depressed group, while delirium was more common in non-depressed patients (p = 0.005). Functional impairment was higher among depressed patients, with only 80% being fully functional postoperatively compared to 100% of non-depressed patients (p = 0.010). Hospital stays longer than 10 days were also exclusively associated with depression (p = 0.000), along with higher pain scores (≥ 8) in the depressed group (57.8% vs. 7.5%, p = 0.000). Furthermore, depression was significantly associated with higher levels of anxiety (p = 0.002) and stress (p = 0.001), indicating a strong psychosocial burden. However, disability index (HAQ-DI) and readmission rates did not show significant differences between the two groups.

In the bivariate analysis, individuals aged 56–65 had significantly higher odds of experiencing severe depressive symptoms compared to those aged ≤ 55 (OR = 3.100, p = 0.024). Housewives were more likely to report severe depression than unemployed individuals (OR = 4.480, p = 0.027). Tobacco smoking showed a protective association (OR = 0.188, p = 0.010), while those with sleep problems had markedly increased odds (OR = 8.610, p = 0.001), although this lost significance in the multivariate model (p = 0.056). Notably, patients undergoing longer surgeries (≥ 121 min) had significantly higher odds of severe depression both in bivariate (OR = 5.409, p = 0.034) and multivariate analyses (OR = 13.595, p = 0.025). Pain scores ≥ 8 were strongly associated with higher depression severity (OR = 0.111, p = 0.001; multivariate OR = 0.065, p = 0.023), indicating that higher pain levels significantly influenced depressive symptoms. Furthermore, stress levels remained a strong predictor of depression severity in both bivariate (OR = 3.692, p = 0.001) and multivariate models (OR = 12.824, p = 0.010). Moderate and severe anxiety levels were inversely associated with depression severity (p = 0.018 and p = 0.013, respectively), potentially suggesting diagnostic overlap or symptom misclassification (Table 5).

Discussion

This study revealed a notably high proportion of postoperative depression 69.2% among elderly patients undergoing urological surgery, which is align with previous research linking prolonged surgery and stress with depression, though the strength of association was notably higher in our cohort. The study confirms that advanced age, poor sleep, longer surgery duration, postoperative pain, and perceived stress significantly contribute to the onset of depression. These results are consistent with previous studies, that showed the correlation between prolonged surgery and extended hospital stays with greater risk of depression among elderly patients [21]. Similarly, another study found that symptoms of depression persisted up to six months’ post-surgery in European surgical patients, emphasizing the long-term mental health implications of invasive interventions [17].

In the present study, sleep-related issues emerged as strongly associated with depression of depression. Patients with sleep problems were eight times more likely to suffer from moderate-to-severe depression, corroborating findings that noted the bidirectional relationship between anxiety, depression, and sleep deprivation [22]. Furthermore, sleep disruption in hospital settings due to shared wards, routine night-time procedures, and noise was also emphasized as contributing to patient discomfort and poor mental outcomes [23], reinforcing the need for sleep-promoting interventions in surgical units.

Patients experiencing severe pain were disproportionately related by depressive symptoms. This is supported by findings that observed a clear association between untreated pain and post-surgical depression in elderly cohorts [24]. In this study, higher pain scores significantly increased the odds of depression, echoing prior research in orthopedics and urology, which found opioid use for pain to be a confounding factor for mood changes postoperatively [25].

In this study psychological stress and anxiety levels were strongly associated to depression which was similar to previous study reports that stress increased the likelihood of depression twelvefold, affirming the stress-depression nexus [17, 26]. These findings substantiate the utility of preoperative screening using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) to identify at-risk patients and intervene early [20].

Sociodemographic variables such as education and profession were also significant. Individuals with lower education and non-service professions, such as housewives and businesspersons, were at higher risk likely due to reduced health literacy, financial insecurity, and lower social mobility. These findings are consistent with large-scale survey data that emphasized lower education and poor social support as major determinants of depressive disorders, especially in resource-constrained settings like Bangladesh [27, 28].

Interestingly, tobacco useparticularly cigarette smoking appeared to have a complex association. The finding that smokers had lower depression rates may reflect self-medication or selection bias. This contradicts broader evidence and should be interpreted cautiously [4]. Further studies are required to better understand this paradox.The type and duration of surgery were statistically significant risk factors. Major operations and those lasting over two hours were associated with more severe depressive symptoms. Previous findings suggest that the invasiveness of surgery, blood loss, and prolonged exposure to anesthesia may impair neurological pathways, leading to postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction, which often overlaps with depressive symptoms [29]. However, in this study, the type of anesthesia was not independently associated with depression, aligning with findings from multicenter studies of urological surgeries [24].

Functional status and neuropsychiatric complications such as delirium and cognitive impairment also contributed to poor psychological outcomes. Recent reviews of postoperative neurological disorders in elderly patients suggest that these conditions form a syndromic cluster that includes depression [4], emphasizing the importance of comprehensive mental health evaluations in surgical aftercare.

Importantly, comorbid conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, although prevalent, were not significantly associated with postoperative depression in this study. This contrasts with global evidence that identifies comorbidity burden as a predictor of depression [5]. The findings support the study’s hypothesis and fulfill its objectives highlighting both the proportion and multifactorial etiology of postoperative depression. As a pioneering study in the Bangladeshi context, it adds significant local insight to a field often dominated by Western data. Routine screening for depression using validated scales like HADS, integrating psychological counseling into surgical care, and tailoring pre- and postoperative education to at-risk groups could significantly improve outcomes.

Limitations

Being single-centered, its generalizability is limited. Recall and reporting bias are possible due to the self-reported data collection method. Factors like preoperative psychiatric screening, and social support systems were not robustly assessed. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study, causal relationships cannot be confirmed. Depression symptoms may precede or result from surgery-related stressors.This study occurred post-pandemic, which may influence stress levels and healthcare delivery factors that warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study reveals a high prevalence of postoperative depression among elderly urological patients in Bangladesh, driven by factors such as sleep disturbances, surgical duration, pain, and stress. Socio-demographic variables also showed strong associations. These findings support the need for routine psychological screening, integrated perioperative mental health care, and targeted interventions.Also highlight the need for policymakers to consider integrating mental health into surgical care. Multicenter, longitudinal studies withmultidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, anesthesiologists, psychologists, and rehabilitation specialists is recommended to mitigate the burden of postoperative depression in elderly urological patients.
