Case report: dual pathology of LMX1B-associated nephropathy and iga nephropathy in a middle-aged woman
Authors: Ahsan Sajjad, Rida Amer, Mohammad Alsayed, Muhammad Daoud Butt, Sreedhar Mandayam, Mobeen Sajjad, Attique Ur Rehman Masood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProteinuria is a common manifestation of glomerular disease. Advances in genetic testing have improved recognition of hereditary nephropathies such as LMX1B-associated nephropathy. Coexistence with immune-mediated glomerulonephritis is exceptionally rare.Case reportWe describe a 58-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, and prior pulmonary embolism who presented with persistent proteinuria. Urinary protein-to-creatinine ratios ranged from 1679 to 3944 mg/g, while renal function remained preserved (estimated GFR 69–103 mL/min/1.73 m²). Genetic testing identified a pathogenic LMX1B mutation consistent with nail–patella syndrome, along with variants of uncertain significance in CYP11B1, ABCC8, and PLG. Kidney biopsy revealed IgA nephropathy with mesangial IgA deposition and ultrastructural findings of podocyte myelinosomes, characteristic of LMX1B-associated nephropathy. Chronicity was minimal (3 of 23 glomeruli globally sclerotic, no segmental sclerosis, and no interstitial fibrosis). Management included renin–angiotensin system blockade and metabolic optimization.ConclusionThis case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of dual glomerular pathology and underscores the importance of integrating genetic testing with histopathology in patients with atypical proteinuria.Keywords: LMX1B-associated nephropathy, Nail–patella syndrome, IgA nephropathy, Podocyte dysfunction, Dual glomerular pathology

Introduction

LMX1B nephropathy, typically manifesting in patients with nail-patella syndrome (NPS), is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by heterozygous mutations in the LMX1B transcription factor gene. This syndrome classically presents with nail dysplasia, patellar hypoplasia or aplasia, iliac horns, elbow anomalies, glaucoma, and variable renal involvement. Renal manifestations occur in approximately 30–50% of NPS patients, with around 15% progressing to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Histologically, affected individuals may demonstrate focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) or irregular thickening with “moth-eaten” appearances of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), with collagen fibril deposition visible on electron microscopy [1, 2]. Notably, a phenotype characterized predominantly by renal involvement LMX1B-associated nephropathy also exists, often lacking extrarenal signs and occasionally masquerading as Fabry-like disease due to similar ultrastructural features [3, 4].

In contrast, IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, especially in Asia. It is characterized by dominant or codominant mesangial IgA deposition, often associated with hematuria and varying degrees of proteinuria [5, 6]. The disease course is heterogeneous: roughly 80% maintain stable function over 10 years, while 25–50% progress to ESRD over two decades. Histopathological assessment even using the Oxford classification assists in prognostication; mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary proliferation, segmental sclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, and crescents independently predict renal outcomes [5, 7].

The concurrent presence of LMX1B-associated nephropathy and IgAN is exceptionally rare. To our knowledge, no previous cases of this dual pathology have been reported. The diagnostic overlap FSGS-like lesions and GBM abnormalities from LMX1B mutations, along with mesangial IgA deposits may obscure recognition of both conditions. Reporting such a case emphasizes the need for integrated histologic, immunologic, and genetic evaluation and expands current understanding of potential pathogenic interactions between podocyte transcription factor mutations and immune complex deposition in the mesangium. Case presentation

A 58-year-old woman of Caucasian descent was referred for evaluation of persistent proteinuria detected during routine follow-up. Her medical history included type 2 diabetes mellitus (diagnosed 10 years prior; HbA1c 9.2% in 2024), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, bilateral pulmonary embolism (treated with anticoagulation), and breast cancer in remission. She reported foamy urine for several months, fatigue, pruritus, dry eyes, and intermittent joint discomfort. There was no history of gross hematuria, dysuria, flank pain, or peripheral edema. Family history was negative for kidney disease, skeletal anomalies, or nail abnormalities. The patient had an absence of Patella and presence of Iliac horns bilaterally. Family history was negative for kidney disease, skeletal anomalies, or nail abnormalities.

Physical examination

Blood pressure was 125/85 mmHg, heart rate 78 bpm, BMI 25.5 kg/m², and oxygen saturation 97% on room air. No edema was noted. Nails appeared brittle, and there were no obvious skeletal deformities. Cardiovascular and abdominal examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory findings

Serial serum creatinine ranged from 0.67 to 0.96 mg/dL, with estimated GFR between 69 and 103 mL/min/1.73 m². Proteinuria was significant, with urine protein-to-creatinine ratios ranging from 1679 to 3944 mg/g over two years (Table 1). Urinalysis revealed 2 + protein, no hematuria, and bland sediment. Serum albumin was 3.8 g/dL; lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides (210 mg/dL). ANA, complements (C3, C4), hepatitis B/C serologies, and HIV was negative.

Imaging

Renal MRI demonstrated normal-sized kidneys with preserved cortical thickness and a 2 cm proteinaceous cyst in the left kidney. No hydronephrosis or structural anomalies were observed.

Differential diagnosis

The primary clinical consideration was diabetic kidney disease (DKD), given the patient’s decade-long history of type 2 diabetes mellitus. However, several features of the presentation were atypical for a standard metabolic progression, prompting a broader diagnostic search. Most notably, the patient exhibited persistent, nephrotic-range proteinuria with protein-to-creatinine ratios reaching 3944 mg/g, which was disproportionately high compared to her relatively preserved renal function and the absence of other microvascular complications like diabetic retinopathy.

Primary IgA nephropathy (IgAN) remained a high clinical suspicion due to its global prevalence, yet the complete absence of hematuria and a “bland” urinary sediment made it an atypical presentation for an isolated inflammatory glomerulonephritis. Furthermore, hereditary nephropathies such as Alport syndrome or Fabry disease were considered. While the family history was uncompromised and the patient did exhibit classic extrarenal manifestations such as the skeletal or nail abnormalities typically seen in nail–patella syndrome, the “moth-eaten” appearance of the glomerular basement membrane occasionally seen in LMX1B variants can masquerade as other basement membrane disorders. Ultimately, the mismatch between the severity of the proteinuria and the mild diabetic chronicity necessitated a kidney biopsy to differentiate between metabolic, immune-mediated, and structural genetic pathologies.

Renal biopsy

The biopsy specimen contained 23 glomeruli, of which 3 were globally sclerotic. Light microscopy demonstrated mild mesangial hypercellularity without segmental sclerosis or crescents. The glomerular basement membrane (GBM) appeared focally irregular and thickened. The tubulointerstitial compartment showed minimal chronicity, with no significant interstitial fibrosis or tubular atrophy. Arteries exhibited mild intimal fibrosis (Fig. 1A).

Histologic and ultrastructural features of dual glomerular pathology

Immunofluorescence demonstrated mesangial IgA deposition (2+), trace C3, and light chain staining (kappa 1+, lambda 2+), consistent with IgA nephropathy. Notably, the absence of significant endocapillary proliferation or segmental sclerosis suggested that the IgA component contributed less to the overall clinical presentation than the underlying structural podocytopathy.

Electron microscopy demonstrated irregular GBM thickening with a characteristic “moth-eaten” appearance, diffuse podocyte foot process effacement, and numerous podocyte myelinosomes—hallmarks of LMX1B-associated nephropathy (Fig. 1B). These ultrastructural changes confirmed the genetic diagnosis and explained the persistent proteinuria despite minimal chronicity.

Genetic testing

A comprehensive Renasight panel identified a pathogenic heterozygous mutation in LMX1B, consistent with nail–patella syndrome (NPS). Additional variants of uncertain significance were detected in CYP11B1, ABCC8, and PLG genes, as detailed in Table 2. Despite the confirmed genetic diagnosis, the patient exhibited no overt skeletal or nail abnormalities, suggesting a renal-predominant phenotype of NPS. This genetic finding provides a definitive molecular basis for the pathognomonic “moth-eaten” GBM and podocyte myelinosomes observed on electron microscopy.

Management and follow-up

The patient was initiated on losartan (100 mg daily) for proteinuria and blood pressure control, fenofibrate for dyslipidemia, and optimized glycemic management with metformin. An SGLT2 inhibitor was added sequentially, starting with empagliflozin. After clinical reassessment and persistence of proteinuria, empagliflozin was later switched to dapagliflozin rather than combined therapy, in accordance with current practice recommendations. Immunosuppressive therapy was deferred given minimal inflammatory activity and preserved renal function. She was counseled on lifestyle modification and referred for nephrology and endocrinology follow-up. At 6-month review, proteinuria persisted (protein/creatinine ratio ~ 2800 mg/g), but renal function remained stable (eGFR 95 mL/min).

Discussion

This case demonstrates the rare coexistence of LMX1B-associated nephropathy and IgA nephropathy (IgAN), two distinct glomerular disorders with different etiologies. LMX1B mutations, typically linked to nail–patella syndrome (NPS), impair podocyte transcriptional regulation, resulting in abnormal glomerular basement membrane (GBM) architecture and podocyte dysfunction [8, 9]. Electron microscopy findings such as irregular GBM thickening and podocyte myelinosomes are considered pathognomonic for this genetic nephropathy [10]. Conversely, IgAN is an immune-mediated disease characterized by mesangial IgA deposition and variable mesangial hypercellularity [11].

The coexistence of these two pathologies is exceptionally rare. While IgAN is the most common primary glomerulonephritis globally, LMX1B-associated nephropathy accounts for a small proportion of hereditary kidney diseases [7]. To our knowledge, no prior reports have documented this dual pathology. In our patient, heavy proteinuria with preserved renal function and minimal chronicity prompted genetic testing, which confirmed a pathogenic LMX1B mutation. Renal biopsy revealed mesangial IgA deposits alongside ultrastructural features typical of LMX1B-related disease, confirming the dual diagnosis.

A critical consideration in this case is the relative contribution of each pathology to the patient’s clinical presentation. While renal biopsy confirmed mesangial IgA deposits (2+), the light microscopy showed only mild mesangial hypercellularity without significant endocapillary proliferation, segmental sclerosis, or crescents. Given that IgA deposits can occasionally be found as clinically irrelevant bystanders, the structural podocyte dysfunction and “moth-eaten” GBM probably changes driven by the LMX1B mutation were the primary drivers of the nephrotic-range proteinuria. The minimal inflammatory activity suggests that the IgAN may have been an incidental finding or a secondary contributor, rather than the primary cause of the significant protein leak.

The late-adulthood clinical presentation observed in this 59-year-old patient raises the question of whether such a delayed diagnosis is typical for LMX1B-associated disease. While classic Nail-Patella Syndrome is often identified in childhood due to overt skeletal and nail anomalies, the renal-predominant phenotype lacking extrarenal manifestations is increasingly recognized as a cause of “idiopathic” proteinuria in adults. In these cases, the absence of hematuria and a negative family history can frequently lead to a delayed diagnosis or misdiagnosis as diabetic nephropathy or FSGS. This case reinforces that LMX1B mutations can remain clinically indolent for decades before presenting with persistent proteinuria in middle age.

Pathophysiologic interactions

The mechanism underlying this coexistence remains unclear. One hypothesis is that podocyte dysfunction due to LMX1B mutation compromises glomerular integrity, potentially facilitating immune complex deposition. Alternatively, this may represent a coincidental overlap, given the high prevalence of IgAN in Asian populations [11]. Further research is needed to determine whether genetic defects in podocyte transcription factors predispose to secondary immune-mediated injury.

Therapeutic decisions and outcomes

Management focused on renin–angiotensin system blockade and SGLT2 inhibitors for proteinuria and metabolic control. Immunosuppressive therapy was deferred due to minimal inflammatory activity and preserved renal function. At six months, renal function remained stable despite persistent proteinuria, consistent with the indolent course often observed in LMX1B-related disease [9].

Clinical implications

This case underscores the importance of integrating genetic testing with histopathology in patients with atypical proteinuria. Genetic counselling should be offered to affected individuals and their families. Long-term surveillance should include monitoring for chronic kidney disease progression and screening for extrarenal manifestations of NPS. Reporting such cases expands awareness of complex nephropathies and informs personalised management strategies.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of diagnostic vigilance when evaluating patients with persistent proteinuria, particularly in the absence of overt clinical clues. The exceptionally rare coexistence of LMX1B-associated nephropathy and IgA nephropathy highlights the necessity of a comprehensive diagnostic framework that integrates detailed renal histopathology with advanced genetic testing to unravel complex dual pathologies. Notably, the identification of a pathogenic LMX1B mutation in this 59-year-old patient despite a negative family history and the absence of classic extrarenal manifestations suggests that renal-predominant phenotypes of nail–patella syndrome may be an under-recognised cause of “idiopathic” or “atypical diabetic” proteinuria in the adult population.

Despite the presence of significant, nephrotic-range proteinuria, the patient maintained preserved renal function, emphasizing that the structural podocyte dysfunction and basement membrane changes characteristic of LMX1B nephropathy may follow an indolent clinical course regardless of secondary immune-mediated findings like IgA deposition. This distinction is vital for clinical management, as it justifies the deferral of aggressive immunosuppression in favor of optimized metabolic and renin–angiotensin system blockade.

Future research should explore the prevalence of genetic variants such as LMX1B in patients with IgA nephropathy, the potential pathogenic interplay between podocyte transcription factor mutations and immune-mediated injury, and the long-term renal outcomes in cases of dual pathology. Broader implementation of genetic screening in atypical glomerular disease presentations may refine diagnostic accuracy and guide precision therapy.
