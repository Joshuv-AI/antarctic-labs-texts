One-year longitudinal cohort study of chemosensory recovery and plasma biomarker dynamics in SARS-CoV-2 survivors
Authors: Benson W M Lau, Sharon M W Chung, Freddy M H Lam, Kwok-Kuen Cheung, Chi-Ming Wong, Clare C W Yu, Siu-Ngor Fu, Timothy K H Fung, David W K Man, David H K Shum, Shirley P C Ngai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the recovery of smell and taste after SARS-CoV-2 viral-clearance and explored biological factors associated with chemosensory outcomes. Participants were enrolled at 3, 6, or 12 months (Group 1, 2 and 3) after viral-clearance (Visit 1) and re-evaluated three months later (Visit 2). Chemosensory functional tests were performed, and biomarkers related to protein aggregation and neurodegeneration, astroglial activation, neuronal injury, and oxidative stress were measured. 120 participants were recruited. In the cohort, gustatory function had largely normalised at the first visit across all groups and remained stable at follow-up. In contrast, significant improvement in olfactory function was observed within 3–6 months post–viral clearance (Group 1), while Groups 2 and 3 exhibited stable olfactory performance at subsequent follow-up assessments. Biomarker α-synuclein is significantly correlated with olfactory performance, however, plasma concentration did not exhibit a significant decline until approximately one-year post-recovery (Group 3), suggesting a delayed biomarker response relative to clinical symptom resolution. These findings suggest that protein aggregation–related processes or underlying susceptibility may be associated with SARS-CoV-2–related olfactory dysfunction. Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has produced more than 770 million confirmed infections and over 7 million deaths worldwide, making it a persistent global health challenge1. Among its early and often defining clinical features are olfactory and gustatory disturbances: up to 70% of infected individuals report partial or complete loss of smell (hyposmia/ anosmia) and or taste (hypogeusia/ ageusia), which in most cases, resolve within 8–10 days2,3. Nevertheless, a sizable minority experience protracted or permanent chemosensory deficits, and biological determinants of this variability remain poorly understood.

Acute SARS-CoV-2 olfactory impairment is thought to result primarily from infection of sustentacular cells in the olfactory epithelium, rather than direct infection of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs)4. Sustentacular cells provide metabolic and structural support to OSNs, and their loss, along with damage to Bowman’s gland cells, could potentially lead to OSN damage or degeneration, thereby impairing olfactory perception5,6. In the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection, pronounced inflammatory responses may further disrupt olfactory function by impeding effective odorant conduction or by altering the activity of OSNs and neurons within the olfactory bulb6. Gustatory dysfunction, by contrast, may arise from viral replication within taste receptor cells, evidenced by SARS-CoV-2 co-localisation with type II taste cell markers and the ACE2 receptor within taste buds7,8.

When chemosensory dysfunction persists beyond the acute phase, neuropathological mechanisms may be involved. Persistent chemosensory dysfunction is also recognised as an early clinical feature and prognostic marker in several neurodegenerative diseases9–11. In these conditions, olfactory dysfunction is frequently accompanied by neuronal damage, protein aggregation and neuroinflammation, for instance, increased α-synuclein (α-syn) in Parkinson’s disease and elevated amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42) levels in Alzheimer’s disease10,12. Emerging evidence indicates that SARS-CoV-2 can interact with and promote aggregation of these pathogenic proteins, potentially exacerbating neurodegenerative processes13,14. The prolonged or permanent chemosensory disturbances in post-SARS-CoV-2 patients raises the hypothesis that shared or overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie these persistent deficits. In addition to neuropathological mechanisms, other proposed mechanisms include (i) ongoing localised immune activity with chronic T-cell infiltration, which disrupts olfactory epithelium function15, (ii) microvascular injury with axonal degeneration in the olfactory bulb16, and (iii) evidence of sustained inflammatory signalling with decreased olfactory sensory neurons15.

Given the previously reported association between olfactory and gustatory function17, this study also sought to examine whether biomarker levels are associated with gustatory performance, as research directly correlating these biomarkers with taste function remains limited. This approach aimed to explore whether similar pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie dysfunction in both sensory modalities.

Despite growing interest in SARS-CoV-2-related prolonged chemosensory impairment, longitudinal investigations combining objective assessments of post-viral clearance olfactory and gustatory performance with circulating biomarkers of neural injury and inflammation over a one-year post-recovery period remain scarce. To address this gap, this study aimed to characterise the trajectory of objectively measured olfactory and gustatory function during the first year following SARS-CoV-2 viral clearance and to evaluate associations between plasma biomarker profiles and chemosensory performance. It is hypothesised that SARS-CoV-2 alters specific biomarker profiles, which in turn contribute to prolonged chemosensory dysfunction.

Materials and methods

All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations provided by the ethical approval body, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Institutional Research Board of the Hong Kong Polytechnic University [HSEARS20210906006]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection.

Participants

Individuals who had recovered from SARs-CoV-2, confirmed by conversion from positive to negative rapid antigen (lateral flow) test or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test, were recruited from the community. Those with a confirmed diagnosis of neurodegenerative diseases and blood-borne diseases were excluded. Participants were categorised into three groups based on the time since recovery: Group 1 (3 months), Group 2 (6 months), and Group 3 (12 months).

Experiment design

Eligible participants were invited for two visits with 3 months apart (Visit 1: V1 and Visit 2: V2). This longitudinal design allows evaluation of within‑group recovery trajectories, direct comparison of improvement magnitudes across the three post‑recovery groups, and examination of how baseline levels and temporal shifts in the biomarkers track with chemosensory outcomes.

At V1, demographics such as age and gender were recorded. Self-reported symptoms during the acute infection phase were also documented at V1. On each visit, participants completed psychophysical smell and taste tests to assess their chemosensory performance. Following informed consent, 5 mL blood samples were collected from 107 consented participants by licensed phlebotomists. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation at 1,500 × g for 15 min and stored at − 80 °C until analysis for detecting six plasma biomarkers representing four mechanistic pathways, with details listed in the section on outcome measures.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures

Olfaction function

Olfactory function was assessed by The University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) (Traditional Chinese version) (Sensonics International, USA)18. UPSIT contains 40 odorant strips embedded in booklets that require participants to scratch and sniff. Participants need to answer a multiple-choice question for each odorant strip. A lower total score indicates a worse sense of smell. Microsomia, a diminished sense of smell, is identified by a test score of lower than 2918.

Gustatory function

The gustatory function was assessed by ODOFIN™ Taste Strips 50 (ODOFIN™, USA)19. It is a validated test where strips contain one of four tastes: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, or none. Strips were presented to participants in a pseudorandomised sequence according to the instruction manual. After placing a strip on the tongue with the mouth closed, participants are asked to indicate the taste they sense on an answer sheet, if any. Each taste modality was tested using four strips of increasing concentrations, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 16. A lower score indicates worse taste function. Hypogeusia, a diminished sense of taste, is identified by a test score of lower than 9.

Secondary outcomes

Biomarkers

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) was conducted to quantify α-syn, Aβ42, and GFAP concentrations in plasma. ELISA kits were used per the manufacturer’s protocols to determine levels of α‑synuclein (α‑syn; Lot. Ab260052, Abcam Limited, UK), amyloid-beta 42 (Aβ42; Lot. ab289832, Abcam Limited, UK), and Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP; Lot. EH0410) (FineTest, China). Additionally, plasma concentrations of Neuron-specific Enolase (NSE), Ferritin, and S100B were measured using ProcartaPlex™ immunoassays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The following kits were utilised according to the manufacturer’s instructions: ProcartaPlex™ Basic Kit (Lot. EPX010-10420-901), NSE Simplex Kit (Lot. EPX010-12335-901), and Ferritin Simplex Kit (Lot. EPX010-12184-901). The data are presented as median values.

The six plasma biomarkers were clustered into four mechanistic clusters: (i) misfolding/aggregation-prone proteins (α‑syn and Aβ₄₂), (ii) astroglial activation markers (GFAP and S100B), (iii) acute neuronal injury marker (NSE), and (iv) an indicator of iron‑mediated oxidative stress (ferritin). These biomarkers have been implicated in neuronal damage, reactive gliosis or oxidative neuro-inflammation20–24, processes that have in turn, been associated with olfactory dysfunction25–28. Given the previously reported association between olfactory and gustatory function17, biomarker levels were also examined in relation to gustatory performance. By investigating these biomarker clusters, we aimed to elucidate whether these processes influence the recovery or persistence of post-SARS-CoV-2 olfactory and gustatory dysfunction.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorised into the normosmia group or hyposmia/anosmia group (in smell test); and the normogeusia group or hypogeusia/ageusia group (in taste test). Demographic variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while biomarker plasma concentrations are presented as median values with interquartile ranges (Q1–Q3). Between-group differences in self-reported symptoms during the SARs-CoV-2 period were assessed by the Chi-squared test. Group differences and within-group changes of olfactory and gustatory function across two visits were assessed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For significant main or interaction effects, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction. McNemar’s test was employed to evaluate changes in categorical group (i.e., anosmia/hyposmia and ageusia/hypogeusia) between visits.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used to investigate the time effect and group effect on biomarker concentrations, with post-hoc Mann–Whitney U tests performed for between-group comparisons at each visit. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate associations between plasma biomarker concentrations and olfactory and gustatory function scores. To further explore the relationships among post-recovery time, age, biomarker levels, and olfactory function, a multivariable linear regression model was constructed. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 30.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The level of statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval.

Chemosensory function

Olfactory function

Two-way ANOVA of UPSIT scores, reflecting olfactory function, revealed a significant main effect of group (F (2,117) = 7.323, p < 0.001) and visit (F (1, 117) = 21.03, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons showed that at V1, Group 3 exhibited significantly higher scores than Group 1 (p < 0.001), whereas no significant between-group differences were observed at V2. Within recovery groups, Group 1 has increased significantly by 3.58 correct responses (p < 0.001) from V1 to V2. Although Group 2 and Group 3 have increased by 1.1 and 0.95 correct responses, respectively, the increase did not reach significance (p = 0.185; p = 0.125). Across all recovery groups at V1, 49 out of 120 (40.8%) participants reported microsomia in smell perception, of whom 29 (59.2%) were in Group 1. By V2, cases of microsomia decreased to 34 out of 120 participants (Table 1). In Group 1, 72.5% exhibited microsomia, of whom, 32.5% improved from fail to pass by V2. Groups 2 and 3 showed no significant change in the microsomia population. Results of Chi-Square tests revealed a statistically significant association between transition outcomes from V1 to V2 (Fail–Fail, Fail–Pass, Pass–Fail, Pass–Pass) and recovery group (χ² (6, N = 120) = 26.530, p < 0.001).

Gustatory function

In Group 1, 10 participants (25%) reported hypogeusia at V1, which reduced to 7 participants (17.5%) at V2. Group 2 showed a reduction from 30% to 15%, while Group 3 showed no change across visits. Despite these trends, a two-way ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of recovery group or within-group differences on ODOFIN™ scores. Additionally, a Pearson Chi-Square test found no statistically significant association between the results of fail/pass transition from V1 to V2 and recovery group (χ²(6, N = 120) = 7.232, p = 0.300).

Biomarkers

Misfolding or aggregation-prone proteins

Plasma concentrations of α-syn and Aβ42 were quantified at V1 and V2 and compared across post-recovery time strata. When participants were stratified by months since viral clearance, α-syn only showed between‑group differences at V2 (V1: p = 0.170, V2: p = 0.023, Table 2; Fig. 1a). Post-hoc Mann-Whitney U test indicated a significant difference between Group 1 and Group 3 at V2 (p = 0.007).

Astroglial activation biomarkers

GFAP plasma concentration did not differ between recovery groups at either visit (V1: p = 0.985, V2: p = 0.663, Table 2; Fig. 1c). No significant differences were observed within any recovery groups (Table 2).

Likewise, S100B demonstrated no between-group (V1: p = 0.85, V2: p = 0.60, Table 2; Fig. 1f) and within-group differences (Table 2).

Acute neuronal injury

NSE plasma concentration did not differ between recovery groups at either visit (V1: p = 0.538, V2: p = 0.825, Table 2; Fig. 1d). No significant differences were observed within any recovery groups (Table 2).

Iron-handling/oxidative-stress marker

Ferritin plasma concentration did not differ between recovery groups at either visit (V1: p = 0.266, V2: p = 0.179, Table 2; Fig. 1e). No significant differences were observed within any recovery groups (Table 2).

Factors associated with chemosensory performance

Spearman correlation analyses revealed a significant negative correlation between plasma α-syn levels and UPSIT scores at V1, indicating that lower olfactory performance was associated with higher α-synuclein levels (ρ = − 0.258, p = 0.007, Table 3). However, this correlation was no longer evident at the 3-month follow-up (ρ = − 0.023, p = 0.817, Table 3). No significant correlations were observed between α-syn and gustatory function at either visit (Table 3). Other biomarkers, including Aβ₄₂, GFAP, S100B, NSE and ferritin showed no significant correlations with olfactory or gustatory function at either time point. These associations were directionally inconsistent across visits and did not reach statistical significance (Table 3).

Discussion

Chemosensory function recovery trajectory

Olfactory function

In this cohort, only Group 1 demonstrated a statistically significant improvement from V1 to V2, however, the mean gain did not exceed the four-point minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for UPSIT29. Nevertheless, the greatest difference in the UPSIT scores was observed between the early visit (Group 1 V1) and the last recovery visit (Group 3 V1), an increase of 4.7 UPSIT score. This improvement exceeds the MCID threshold, suggesting that while the magnitude of improvement reached clinical significance only within the first six months post-recovery, objective olfactory recovery continues at a slower pace for at least twelve months following viral clearance. Comparable trajectories have been documented across other cohorts. The SECURe study documented a negligible transition from hyposmia to normosmia between 6 and 12 months in hospitalised cases30. Likewise, Leung et al.31 observed 29% of the studied cohort were still suffering from impaired olfaction at 12 months post-infection, closely matching the 25% observed in our cohort31.

Longer-term follow-ups reinforce a slow and asymptotic trajectory. Boscolo-Rizzo et al.32) still identified 13.6% patients with olfactory impairment at 36 months, while Saccardo et al.33 reported 7.7% at 48 months32,33. Collectively, these findings indicate that the largest gains occur within the first 3–6 months post-recovery, with gradual improvement continuing into the fourth and fifth years after infection.

Gustatory function

For gustatory performances, previous research has documented gustatory dysfunction in 48.6% of hospitalised SARS-CoV-2 patients3. By contrast, in our cohort, mean ODOFIN™ scores consistently exceeded 9 at all assessments, suggesting that gustatory impairments present during the acute phase had largely resolved within 3 months of viral clearance.

Dynamics of biomarker concentrations following recovery

Misfolding/aggregation-prone proteins

Among the misfolding/aggregation markers examined, only α-syn exhibited a statistically significant association with olfactory performance at V1. A statistically significant within-group decline was detected exclusively in Group 3. Notably, despite this group exhibiting the lowest α-syn concentrations in this cohort, their levels remained higher than previously reported reference ranges34. This suggests that reductions in α-syn concentrations may occur gradually and could extend beyond the 12 month recovery period.

Persistent elevation might be explained by in vitro evidence that SARS‑CoV‑2 spike or N‑proteins accelerate α‑syn aggregation and synaptic toxicity13,15,35, raising the possibility that viral infection may act as a trigger for the sustained α-syn pathology seen in this cohort. Given that α-syn aggregation is strongly implicated in olfactory dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease and related synucleinopathies25, one might anticipate that declining α-syn levels would parallel improvements in olfactory performance. However, in this cohort, a significant direct correlation was observed only at V1, but not at V2. At V1, the shared early-phase pattern across all groups between both visits yielded a negative cross-sectional association. By V2, olfactory function had significantly improved in the early recovery group, whereas α-syn levels declined significantly in the cohort assessed approximately one year later. The asynchronous trajectory disrupted the cross-sectional alignment, thereby eliminating the overall correlation. This dissociation suggests that reductions in α-syn concentrations may lag behind perceptible functional recovery and may not directly translate into further measurable olfactory gains once recovery has plateaued. Longer-term follow-up is needed to determine whether α-syn levels eventually normalise.

In the multivariable regression analysis, α-syn remained an independent predictor of olfactory performance (B = -0.162, p = 0.020; Table 4). The combined evidence of direct correlation and regression significance supports the role of α-syn in post-SARS-CoV-2 olfactory dysfunction, albeit with temporal decoupling between biomarker normalisation and functional recovery. Nevertheless, the well-established underlying relationship between α-syn and olfactory function, independent of viral infection, should also be taken into account, and may have predated SARS-CoV-2 exposure in some participants.

Few studies have directly examined the relationship between α-syn and gustatory function. Existing evidence predominantly implicates α-syn in olfactory dysfunction, with subsequent alterations in gustatory perception likely mediated by established interactions between retronasal-olfaction and gustation11. In our cohort, plasma α-syn concentrations showed no significant association with gustatory performance in both visits. These findings suggest that gustatory function may be less influenced by neurotoxic mechanisms involving α-syn pathology. Furthermore, the normalisation of ODOFIN™ scores at six months post-recovery in our cohort further supports that gustatory dysfunction from SARs-CoV-2 is short-lived and likely to recover independently of longer-term α-syn dynamics.

In contrast, plasma Aβ42 levels remained low and stable throughout the study period, consistently staying below reference concentrations36. Aβ42 is continuously produced via cleavage of amyloid precursor protein in peripheral tissues, resulting in relatively stable circulating levels under steady-state conditions37. Although transient post-infection elevations have been reported within two months of SARS-CoV-19 diagnosis38, the long post-recovery interval in our cohort (3 to 15 months) may have allowed any acute changes to normalise. The lack of any association between Aβ₄₂ and olfactory or gustatory symptoms in our data suggests that amyloidogenic mechanisms, if activated during acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, subsided well before 3 months post-viral infection. Therefore, Aβ42 appears unlikely to play a significant role in persistent chemosensory dysfunction during the chronic recovery phase.

Astroglial activation / glial‑derived injury proteins

Previous study of intensive-care cohort has reported serum GFAP peaks during the acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 and return to baseline by 225 days39. This aligns with our cohort, where no within-group and between-group differences were observed, indicating that any systemic astroglial activation triggered by SARS-CoV-2 resolves within 6 months. Because post-recovery GFAP kinetics were irrespective of initial disease severity39, the rate of astroglial normalisation appears to be governed predominantly by other intrinsic repair mechanisms rather than by the magnitude of the acute illness. Hence, persistent anosmia beyond six months is unlikely to be driven by ongoing astrocytic injury. Although elevated serum GFAP has been linked to olfactory dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease27, we detected no correlation between GFAP concentrations, UPSIT and ODOFIN™ scores, suggesting it has little contribution to chemosensory dysfunction in post-SARS-CoV-2 recovery.

S100B has no between- and within-group differences, or any direct correlation with smell. Yet, it emerged as a significant predictor in the multivariable regression model. This discrepancy likely reflects a suppressor effect, where S100B improves model validity by accounting for overlapping variance with other factors, thereby revealing an association with olfaction that was not evident in isolation40. This also suggests S100B concentrations already influence olfactory function before SARS-CoV-2 infection. If S100B levels had normalised by the time of measurement, their predictive effect may suggest that individuals with initially elevated levels were predisposed to poorer olfactory recovery. Given the established link between S100B and neurodegenerative pathology41, this subset of participants may reflect early neurodegenerative processes influencing chemosensory recovery.

Acute neuronal damage

Previous reports have reported a 4-fold increase in cerebrospinal fluid NSE in SARS-CoV-2-infected patients; elevated serum NSE levels were also reported in ICU patients23,42. However, NSE levels were stable and within reference norms in all groups43. NSE is a glycolytic isoenzyme which enters the bloodstream during neuronal necrosis or marked membrane leakage and is therefore a canonical marker of acute cytolytic injury and neuroinflammation42. However, the absence of elevated NSE levels at 3 months post-infection suggests that any neuronal membrane damage that occurred during the acute phase of infection had likely resolved by this time. Furthermore, the lack of association between NSE concentrations and chemosensory function suggests the ongoing chemosensory deficits in this cohort are not attributable to persistent acute neuronal injury. Thus, circulating NSE does not affect chemosensory function within 3–15 months of viral clearance.

Iron-handling /oxidative-stress biomarker

In acute-phase studies, ferritin rises sharply during SARS-CoV-2 infection as a classic acute‑phase reactant driven by IL‑6 and hepcidin‑mediated iron sequestration44. Similarly, Mumoli et al. (2021) reported markedly elevated ferritin levels at hospital admission, followed by a pronounced decline within 3 months post-recovery45. Our cohort was sampled 3–15 months post‑infection; hence, the flat trajectory observed likely reflects a return to iron‑handling homeostasis rather than an absence of earlier oxidative stress. Moreover, no associations were detected between ferritin and chemosensory outcomes, indicating circulating ferritin is not a major determinant of chemosensory function within 3–15 months of viral clearance.

Other factors affecting chemosensory performance

In the multivariable regression model, age also emerged as a significant negative contributor to olfaction (B = 0.258, p = 0.004; Table 4). This finding is consistent with the well-documented decline of olfaction across the lifespan46, as well as reduced neuroplasticity in older individuals, which may limit their capacity to recover after neurological or inflammatory injury47. Interestingly, although mean age did not differ significantly between recovery groups, regression analysis still identified age as an independent predictor of olfactory performance. This reflects the difference between categorical group comparisons and continuous regression modelling, while age-matched group averages may obscure variability, regression captures subtle within-group trends and adjusts for the influence of other variables. Thus, even in the absence of group-level age differences, older individuals consistently exhibited lower smell scores, confirming age as an important determinant of olfactory outcomes.

Study limitation

A potential limitation arises from the use of self-reported symptom data. As participants were enrolled at varying intervals following viral clearance, recall bias may have been introduced, particularly among those with longer post-recovery durations and older age48. Consequently, self-reported acute symptoms may not accurately reflect objective chemosensory function.

Secondly, the absence of a pre-infection baseline and a non-infected healthy control group may limit this study from making direct comparisons with unaffected individuals. Due to the unprecedented nature of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, prospective pre-infection chemosensory and biomarker assessments were not feasible. The prevalence of asymptomatic infection also complicates confident identification of truly infection-free individuals. Hence, our analyses focused on the recovery trajectories within the infected cohort rather than between-group comparisons with healthy controls.

Furthermore, all biomarkers were quantified in systemic blood, which may underestimate localised molecular processes within the olfactory epithelium, olfactory bulb, or central olfactory pathways. While no associations were observed between most systemic biomarkers and chemosensory outcomes, compartmentalised neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative changes may still contribute to persistent olfactory dysfunction. Future studies, such as nasal epithelial biopsies, may better elucidate the relationship between molecular pathology and chemosensory recovery.

Conclusion

In this cohort of COVID-19 survivors, olfactory recovery occurred predominantly within the first six months after viral clearance, with only modest additional gains thereafter. Gustatory dysfunction largely resolved by three months. Among the biomarkers studied, elevated plasma α-syn and S100B emerged as independent predictors of poorer olfactory outcomes, alongside older age, while other markers showed no consistent associations. These findings suggest that persistent olfactory dysfunction may reflect both the delayed resolution of α-syn pathology and pre-existing susceptibility as indicated by S100B. Mechanistic studies and standardised psychophysical follow-up beyond 12 months are needed to define longer-term biomarker dynamics and chemosensory recovery.
