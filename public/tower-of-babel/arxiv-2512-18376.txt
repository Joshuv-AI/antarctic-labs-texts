Explicit harmonic and wave maps into variable-curvature surfaces
A. Fotiadis, G. Polychrou

Abstract
. We construct explicit harmonic and wave maps between pseudo-Riemannian surfaces of variable curvature. For a broad class of target metrics, including nonconstant curvature surfaces such as ellipsoids, the harmonic and wave map equations admit a reduction to integrable ordinary differential equations under a natural ansatz. This yields explicit solutions beyond the classical constant-curvature and symmetric-space settings. The method applies uniformly in both elliptic and hyperbolic regimes.

1.
Introduction

Although the qualitative theory of harmonic and wave maps is highly developed, the construction of
explicit
solutions remains a difficult and largely open problem, except in very symmetric settings. Explicit formulas are valuable because they provide concrete test cases for analytic theory, illuminate underlying geometric mechanisms, and often reveal hidden structures that are otherwise inaccessible. Outside a small number of highly structured situations, however, the harmonic map equations quickly become too nonlinear to solve in closed form.

Harmonic maps have played a central role in geometric analysis since the work of Eells and Sampson
[
2
]
; in two dimensions, their conformal invariance links them to complex analysis, Teichmüller theory, and geometric structures on surfaces, see, for example,
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
and the references therein.

Most known explicit constructions in two dimensions rely on strong symmetry assumptions on the target, most notably constant curvature or symmetric-space structures. In these cases, the harmonic map equations admit additional integrability properties, such as Lax pair formulations or reductions to classical integrable systems. When the curvature of the target varies, these structures typically break down, and the methods developed for constant-curvature targets no longer apply.

Consider metrics of the form

g=e^{f(x,y)}\big(dx^{2}-\epsilon^{2}dy^{2}\big),\qquad\epsilon\in\{1,i\},

on the domain
M
. We identify a broad class of variable-curvature target
metrics for which the harmonic and wave map equations admit a systematic
reduction to ordinary differential equations. More precisely, we consider
metrics of the form

h=A(R)\,dR^{2}-\delta^{2}B(R)\,dS^{2},\qquad\delta\in\{1,i\},

on the target
N
, where
A
and
B
are smooth positive functions. For this class of targets,
the harmonic map equations admit a partial separation of variables under a
traveling-wave ansatz, leading to a reduction to integrable first-order
equations.

The main result shows that for any target metric of the above form, the harmonic map equations reduce to a separable equation for the
R
-component and a quadrature for the
S
-component. The reduction applies uniformly in both the elliptic and hyperbolic regimes. As a result, we obtain explicit harmonic and wave maps into genuinely variable-curvature geometries, extending
[
3
,
4
,
9
]
beyond the constant curvature setting.

Theorem 1
.

Let
a,b,\kappa,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be such that
b^{2}\neq\epsilon^{2}a^{2}
and set
t=ay-bx
. Consider the map
u=(R,S):(M,g)\rightarrow(N,h)
,

R(x,y)=R(t),\quad S(x,y)=ax+by+H(t),

where

(1)

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(t)=\frac{2\lambda\delta^{2}\left(b^{2}+\epsilon^{2}a^{2}\right)+4\kappa\delta^{2}ab+ab(a^{2}+b^{2})(\epsilon^{2}+1)B(R)}{(b^{2}-\epsilon^{2}a^{2})(a^{2}+b^{2})B(R)},

(2)

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(t)^{2}=\frac{c_{1}B(R)^{2}+c_{2}\kappa B(R)+c_{3}\lambda B(R)+c_{4}}{(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}(b^{2}-\epsilon^{2}a^{2})^{2}A(R)B(R)},

and

c_{1}=\delta^{2}\epsilon^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})^{4},\quad c_{2}=4(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2}(b^{2}+\epsilon^{2}a^{2}),

c_{3}=8\epsilon^{2}ab(a^{2}+b^{2})^{2},\quad c_{4}=4\delta^{2}\big(2\kappa ab+\lambda(b^{2}+\epsilon^{2}a^{2})\big)^{2}.

Assume that
R(t)
is a smooth solution of the above equation. Then

u
is a harmonic map.

In Section 4, we apply our results and provide some examples. A particularly noteworthy case is the construction of a harmonic map whose target is an
ellipsoid
.
Ellipsoids serve as canonical deformations of the round sphere and possess non-constant curvature.
Explicit harmonic maps into ellipsoids are very scarce in the literature because the variable curvature typically destroys the integrability present in symmetric spaces. Recently, in
[
1
]
, the authors studied harmonic self-maps of ellipsoids in all dimensions. In this work, we construct harmonic maps from a Riemannian surface to an ellipsoid in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space.

Furthermore, when both the domain and target carry Lorentzian metrics, our construction yields new explicit
Lorentzian wave maps
. These are maps from a Lorentzian surface to an elliptic hyperboloid of one sheet in the 3-dimensional Minkowski space.
Such examples are valuable for nonlinear hyperbolic PDE, where explicit solutions are scarce.
These examples, and an explicit example in mixed signature, demonstrate that our main result provides a class of closed-form harmonic and wave maps in variable-curvature geometries.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we establish the notation and derive the harmonic map equations.
Section 3 contains the proof of Theorem
1
. Finally, Section 4 presents illustrative examples, including the harmonic map into an ellipsoid and new explicit Lorentzian wave maps.

In summary, this work introduces a broad class of target metrics that admit explicit harmonic map constructions and provides a unified treatment of harmonic and wave maps between surfaces of arbitrary signature. It extends previous constant curvature results to a large family of variable-curvature geometries and produces explicit solutions that were previously unavailable, including harmonic maps into ellipsoids, Lorentzian wave maps into hyperboloids, and an explicit mixed signature example.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish the notation and geometric setup used throughout the paper. Let
u:(M,g)\longrightarrow(N,h)

be a map between pseudo-Riemannian surfaces. We write
u=(R,S)
in local coordinates.

2.1.
Isothermal coordinates and metric conventions

On the domain surface
M
, we choose isothermal coordinates

v=x+\epsilon y,\qquad w=x-\epsilon y,

where
\epsilon\in\{1,i\}
so that
\epsilon=1
corresponds to a Lorentzian surface and
\epsilon=i
to a Riemannian one. In these coordinates the metric takes the conformal form

g=e^{f(x,y)}\left(dx^{2}-\epsilon^{2}dy^{2}\right)=e^{f(v,w)}\,dv\,dw,\qquad\epsilon\in\{1,i\}.

Similarly, we endow the target surface
N
with coordinates
(R,S)
and consider metrics of the form

h=A(R)\,dR^{2}-\delta^{2}B(R)\,dS^{2},\qquad\delta\in\{1,i\},

where
A(R)
and
B(R)
are smooth, positive functions. This class includes a wide range of classical variable-curvature geometries, such as surfaces of revolution (catenoids, paraboloids, tori), as well as Lorentzian models arising in general relativity and cosmology. Tables
1
and
2
summarize several representative examples.

2.2.
Energy density and Euler–Lagrange equations

The energy density of the map
u
is given by

e(u)=\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{i}u^{\alpha}\partial_{j}u^{\beta}=e^{-f(v,w)}\Big(A(R)\,R_{v}R_{w}-\delta^{2}B(R)\,S_{v}S_{w}\Big),

where

R_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{x}+\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}R_{y}\right),\quad R_{w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{x}-\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}R_{y}\right),

S_{v}=\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{x}+\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}S_{y}\right),\quad S_{w}=\frac{1}{2}\left(S_{x}-\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}S_{y}\right).

The total energy of
u
is

E(u)=\int_{M}e(u)\,dM=\int_{M}\left(A(R)R_{v}R_{w}-\delta^{2}B(R)S_{v}S_{w}\right)dv\,dw.

We call
u

harmonic
if it is a critical point of
E(u)
. In our ansatz, the Jacobian is proportional to
R^{\prime}(t)
so nondegeneracy reduces to the case
R^{\prime}(t)\neq 0.

We now compute the Euler–Lagrange equations associated to the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(v,w,R,S,R_{v},S_{v},R_{w},S_{w})=A(R)\,R_{v}R_{w}-\delta^{2}B(R)\,S_{v}S_{w}.

Lemma 2
.

A map
u=(R,S):(M,g)\to(N,h)
is harmonic if and only if it satisfies

\displaystyle 2A(R)R_{vw}+A^{\prime}(R)R_{v}R_{w}+\delta^{2}B^{\prime}(R)S_{v}S_{w}=0,

\displaystyle 2B(R)S_{vw}+B^{\prime}(R)\left(R_{v}S_{w}+R_{w}S_{v}\right)=0.

Proof.

The result follows from a direct computation of the Euler–Lagrange equations associated to the energy functional. We omit the routine details.
∎

Because the energy is conformally invariant in dimension two, the
Euler–Lagrange ,depends only on the conformal class of
g
.

2.3.
Reduction to a first integral

Following
[
4
]
, the Euler–Lagrange system admits a useful first integral.

Proposition 3
.

A map
u
is harmonic if and only if there exist smooth functions
F(v)
and
G(w)
such that

(3)

\displaystyle A(R)R_{v}^{2}-\delta^{2}B(R)S_{v}^{2}=F(v),

(4)

\displaystyle A(R)R_{w}^{2}-\delta^{2}B(R)S_{w}^{2}=G(w).

Proof.

The identities follow by differentiating the expressions in (
3
) and (
4
) and using the Euler–Lagrange equations. Conversely, assuming (
3
) and (
4
) and using the nondegeneracy of the Jacobian yields the Euler–Lagrange system. Details are routine and omitted.
∎

We simplify the expressions by choosing

(5)

F(v)=\kappa+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\lambda,\qquad G(w)=\kappa-\frac{1}{\epsilon}\lambda,\qquad\kappa,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

2.4.
Ansatz reducing the system to ODEs

To achieve separation of variables and find explicit solutions, we employ the following ansatz for the map components
u=(R,S)
:

(6)

\displaystyle R(x,y)

\displaystyle=R(ay-bx)=R(t),

(7)

\displaystyle S(x,y)

\displaystyle=ax+by+H(ay-bx)=ax+by+H(t),

where
t=ay-bx
and
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
are constants.

Substituting this form into the first integrals (
3
)–(
4
) reduces the governing partial differential equations to a manageable system of ordinary differential equations for the functions
R(t)
and
H(t)
. The closed-form solutions derived from these ODEs constitute the explicit family of harmonic maps presented in Theorem
1
.

3.
Proof of Theorem 1

In this section, we prove our main result. The proof consists in substituting the ansatz into the first integrals and solving the resulting algebraic system, which reduces the harmonic map equations to integrable ordinary differential equations.

Proof.

Substituting the ansatz
(R,S)
from (
6
)–(
7
) into the system (
3
)–(
4
), and considering (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle(a^{2}+\epsilon^{2}b^{2})A(R)R^{\prime}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle=4\epsilon^{2}\kappa+\delta^{2}B(R)\Big[(b+aH^{\prime}(t))^{2}+\epsilon^{2}(a-bH^{\prime}(t))^{2}\Big],

\displaystyle-abA(R)R^{\prime}(t)^{2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}B(R)(a-bH^{\prime}(t))(b+aH^{\prime}(t))+2\lambda.

Expanding the quadratic terms and collecting coefficients yields the system

(8)

\displaystyle(a^{2}+\epsilon^{2}b^{2})K

\displaystyle=4\epsilon^{2}\kappa+\delta^{2}B(R)\big(b^{2}+\epsilon^{2}a^{2}+2(1-\epsilon^{2})abH^{\prime}(t)\big),

(9)

\displaystyle abK

\displaystyle=-2\lambda+\delta^{2}B(R)\big((b^{2}-a^{2})H^{\prime}(t)-ab\big),

where

K=A(R)R^{\prime}(t)^{2}-\delta^{2}B(R)H^{\prime}(t)^{2}.

Solving the linear system (
8
) - (
9
) for
H^{\prime}(t)
, we obtain (
1
). Substituting
H^{\prime}(t)
into (
9
) yields (
2
) by direct computation. Since the right–hand side depends only on
R

R
in (
1
), we find
H
by integration and thus we find
S
. The resulting map

is a harmonic map.
∎

Remark 1:
In the Theorem we assume that
b^{2}-\epsilon^{2}a^{2}\neq 0,
to allow the formulas for
R^{\prime}(t)
and
H^{\prime}(t)
to be well defined. Note that the case where
b^{2}=\epsilon^{2}a^{2}
is degenerate since it forces
B(R)
to be a constant and so

is a constant function and hence the Jacobian of

Remark 2:
The same method applies when the target metric has the form

h_{N}=A(S)dR^{2}-\delta^{2}B(S)dS^{2},\qquad\delta\in\{1,i\}.

In particular, our framework recovers the explicit harmonic maps constructed in
[
11
]
between infinite strips equipped with conformal metrics. We omit the details, which follow by an analogous reduction.

4.
Examples of harmonic maps

In this section we illustrate the scope of our main result by means of representative examples. Our aim is not to exhaust all possible cases, but rather to highlight the geometric meaning of the construction and to demonstrate that it applies to genuinely variable-curvature targets and to both elliptic and hyperbolic regimes.

4.1.
Harmonic maps into ellipsoids

We first consider the Riemannian case. An interesting case to consider is when the target surface is an ellipsoid in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space and the domain is a Euclidean surface equipped with isothermal coordinates. We denote by
E_{c}
the ellipsoid

\frac{x^{2}}{c^{2}}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1,\quad c>1,

endowed with the induced metric. In suitable coordinates
(R,S)
this metric takes the form

h=(c^{2}\sin^{2}R+\cos^{2}R)dR^{2}+\sin^{2}RdS^{2}.

Ellipsoids provide a canonical family of deformations of the round sphere. Unlike spheres or hyperbolic planes, ellipsoids do not admit transitive isometry groups and their curvature varies along the surface. As a consequence, many techniques for constructing harmonic maps that rely on symmetry or constant curvature fail in this setting.

The explicit harmonic maps constructed here demonstrate that the variable curvature of the ellipsoid can still be accommodated within our framework.

Proposition 4
.

The map
u
from a Riemannian surface M to an ellipsoid Riemannian surface
E_{c}
, i.e.

u=(R,S):(M,e^{f(x,y)}(dx^{2}+dy^{2}))\rightarrow(E_{c},(c^{2}\sin^{2}R+\cos^{2}R)dR^{2}+\sin^{2}RdS^{2}),

where

R(x,y)=\arccos\left(\cos\theta\sin\left(\frac{ay-bx}{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}}\right)\right),

S(x,y)=ax+by+\arctan\left(\sin\theta\tan\left(\frac{ay-bx}{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}}\right)\right),

with
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
, is a harmonic map.

Sketch of proof.

For the ellipsoid metric one has

A(R)=c^{2}\sin^{2}R+\cos^{2}R
and
B(R)=\sin^{2}R
.
Substituting these functions into (
2
),
the equation for
R(t)
becomes separable and integrates explicitly to
the stated expression. The function
S
is then recovered by quadrature.
∎

4.2.
Lorentzian wave maps into hyperboloids

We now consider an example when
\epsilon=\delta=1
. An interesting case to consider is when the target surface is an elliptic hyperboloid of one sheet in the 3-dimensional Minkowski space and the domain is a Lorentzian surface equipped with isothermal coordinates. We denote by
F_{c}
the elliptic hyperboloid

\frac{x^{2}}{c^{2}}+y^{2}-z^{2}=1,\quad c>1,

endowed with the induced Lorentzian metric. In appropriate coordinates, this metric again takes the form required by our main result.

The following proposition holds.

Proposition 5
.

The map
u
from a Lorentzian surface
M
to the elliptic hyperboloid Lorentzian surface
F_{c}
, i.e.

u=(R,S):(M,e^{f(x,y)}(dx^{2}-dy^{2}))\rightarrow(F_{c},(c^{2}\sin^{2}R+\cos^{2}R)dR^{2}-\sin^{2}RdS^{2})

where

R(x,y)=\arccos\left(\cosh\theta\sin\left(\omega(ay-bx)\right)\right),

S(x,y)=ax+by+\frac{2ab}{b^{2}-a^{2}}(ay-bx)+\arctanh\left(\sinh\theta\tan\left(\omega(ay-bx)\right)\right),

with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
and

\omega=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{b^{2}-a^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}}
, is a harmonic map.

The proof follows the same steps as in the Riemannian ellipsoid case. Since no new phenomena arise, the details are omitted.

4.3.
A mixed-signature example

Finally, we briefly indicate that the construction remains valid when the domain and target have different causal structures. Thus, we now consider the case
\delta\neq\epsilon
which implies
\delta^{2}\epsilon^{2}=-1
.

Proposition 6
.

The map
u=(R,S)
from pseudo-Riemannian surface
M
that is equipped with the metric
dx^{2}-\epsilon^{2}dy^{2}
to the pseudo-Riemannian surface
N
that is equipped with the metric

h=dR^{2}-\delta^{2}\tanh^{2}RdS^{2},

where

R(x,y)=\operatorname{arcsinh}\left(\frac{\sin{\theta}\cdot y-\cos{\theta}\cdot x}{\cos^{2}{\theta}-\epsilon^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta}}\right),

S(x,y)=\cos{\theta}\cdot x+\sin{\theta}\cdot y+\frac{(1+\epsilon^{2})\cos{\theta}\sin{\theta}}{(\cos^{2}{\theta}-\epsilon^{2}\sin^{2}{\theta})}(\sin{\theta}\cdot y-\cos{\theta}\cdot x),

with
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{4})
, is a harmonic map.

This example illustrates the flexibility of the method and confirms that the reduction mechanism is insensitive to the relative signatures of the domain and target. The above metric provides an example of a variable-curvature surface.
