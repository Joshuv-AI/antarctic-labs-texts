Cannabinoid receptor 2 affect the inflammatory response in periodontitis by regulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization
Authors: Shu-Yue Yuan, Fei Zhao, Ran Tao, Bing Liu, Chun-Yan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated the effect of cannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2R) on macrophage polarization and inflammatory severity in periodontitis, elucidating CB2R’s potential role in the disease.MethodsChronic periodontitis was induced by ligating maxillary second molars with orthodontic filaments in wild-type (WT) and CB2R knockout (CB2R-/-) mice. Unligated mice served as controls. After 3 weeks, maxillae were harvested. Micro-CT evaluated modeling success and quantified alveolar bone resorption. HE staining assessed periodontal tissue destruction. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyzed macrophage polarization, while RT-qPCR measured mRNA levels of macrophage markers and inflammatory cytokines.Results1. Micro-CT revealed significantly greater alveolar bone resorption in both CB2R-/- and WT periodontitis groups versus controls (P < 0.05), with CB2R-/- mice exhibiting more severe resorption than WT mice.2. HE staining demonstrated increased bone loss, inflammatory infiltration, and tissue destruction in periodontitis groups, most pronounced in CB2R-/- mice.3.IHC indicated elevated M1/M2 macrophage markers in periodontitis groups. M1 marker iNOS expression was significantly higher in CB2R-/- versus WT mice (P < 0.05), while M2 marker Arg-1 showed no intergroup difference (P < 0.05)0.4.RT-qPCR confirmed upregulated macrophage markers and inflammatory cytokines in periodontitis groups, with significantly higher expression in CB2R-/- mice (P < 0.05). CB2R-/- mice also exhibited elevated M1/M2 and IL-6/IL-10 ratios versus WT mice (P < 0.05).ConclusionsCB2R deficiency exacerbates alveolar bone resorption and inflammatory severity in periodontitis, potentially by dysregulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization and inflammatory cytokine expression.

Background

Periodontitis is a multifactorial disease characterized by chronic progressive destruction of periodontal hard tissues, primarily driven by bacterial activity. It manifests clinically as gingival inflammation and alveolar bone resorption, leading to tooth mobility, displacement, and eventual loss, representing the leading cause of adult tooth loss worldwide. Since its proposal as a distinct category of periodontitis in 1999, chronic periodontitis was used clinically for an extended period. To accommodate advances in biological and clinical research knowledge, periodontitis was reclassified in 2017 into four stages and three grades according to the current classification scheme [1–3]. Studies indicate a global periodontitis prevalence as high as 62% between 2011 and 2020 [4]. Furthermore, periodontitis increases systemic disease risks including diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, rheumatoid arthritis, and Parkinson’s disease [5].Research confirms that periodontal tissue destruction occurs through host-mediated immune responses triggered by bacteria, highlighting host immunity’s critical role in pathogenesis [6, 7].

As key innate immune cells, macrophages contribute significantly to periodontal tissue homeostasis and defense through phagocytosis, bacterial killing, and debris clearance [8]. These cells exhibit remarkable plasticity, differentiating into classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) phenotypes in response to environmental stimuli. M1 macrophages exert pro-inflammatory effects by secreting inflammatory cytokines, promoting inflammation progression, and stimulating osteoclast formation to accelerate bone resorption. Conversely, M2 macrophages demonstrate anti-inflammatory properties, secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines to counteract M1 responses while promoting tissue repair and bone formation [5, 9]. Importantly, M1/M2 macrophage imbalance correlates with periodontitis-associated alveolar bone resorption, with the M2-to-M1 phenotype shift potentially driving tissue destruction [10, 11].

The endocannabinoid system (ECS), comprising cannabinoid receptors, endocannabinoids, and their metabolic enzymes, regulates numerous physiological processes. CB2R, a cannabinoid receptor component, is inflammation-inducible in both humans and mice [9] and predominantly localizes to peripheral and immune cells including microglia, macrophages, and neutrophils [10]. Studies demonstrate CB2R’s key anti-inflammatory role in various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions such as neuroinflammation, inflammatory nephropathy, and periodontitis. Specifically, CB2R agonists reduce periodontitis-associated inflammatory cytokines and bone resorption [12–15]. CB2R activation also regulates macrophage M1/M2 polarization to ameliorate alcoholic hepatitis and suppresses skin inflammation by inhibiting M1 polarization [16–18].

Studies demonstrate that CB2R activation alleviates periodontal inflammation by modulating downstream signaling pathways (e.g., the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway), thereby reducing macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. For instance, in in vitro models (e.g., LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells), CB2R agonists (e.g., JWH-133) significantly suppress M1 polarization and cytokine production. These effects are reversible upon treatment with CB2R antagonists (e.g., SR144528) or in CB2R-knockout (CB2R⁻/⁻) mice, confirming CB2R as an essential regulatory factor [19]. Similarly, in obesity-related inflammation models, CB2R agonist JWH-133 attenuates systemic inflammation by inhibiting macrophage activation a mechanism potentially extendable to periodontitis [18, 20]. CB2R-dependent actions involve multiple pathways, including: Suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, Regulation of ROS production, Modulation of G protein-coupled receptor signaling [21, 22]. Within the periodontal microenvironment, macrophage polarization is governed by these pathways. CB2R agonists (e.g.,β-caryophyllene, BCP) exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects through selective CB2R activation, mitigating tissue damage [22, 23]. Furthermore, CB2R modulation positively influences bone metabolism primarily by inhibiting osteoclastogenesis. Given CB2R’s high expression in bone-associated cells, its activation reduces ROS accumulation (e.g., NOX1-mediated ROS), consequently suppressing osteoclast differentiation—a mechanism likely operative in periodontitis [24].

Compelling evidence documents CB2R’s role in regulating macrophage polarization in alcoholic liver failure (ALF) models: GW4064 pretreatment significantly reduces hepatic macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization while suppressing the release of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β. These protective effects are blocked by the CB2R antagonist SR144528. Parallel outcomes were confirmed in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, validating CB2R-mediated macrophage modulation [19]. This indicates a conserved regulatory mechanism whereby CB2R attenuates inflammation through M1 polarization suppression, providing a theoretical foundation for periodontitis applications. In periodontitis, M1 macrophage polarization is driven by pathways including the NLRP3 inflammasome [25]and TLR4/MyD88 signaling [26].Notably, CB2R-regulated inflammatory axes (e.g.,TNF-α/IL-1β) in ALF [19] exhibit substantial overlap with periodontitis mechanisms, supporting its applicability within analogous immunopathological contexts. Given CB2R’s high expression in immune cells and well-established anti-inflammatory properties, the mechanistic rationale for its regulatory function in the specific microenvironment of periodontitis remains robust.

Despite this evidence, whether CB2R modulates periodontitis inflammation through macrophage M1/M2 polarization remains unexplored. Therefore, this study tests the hypothesis that CB2R influences periodontitis inflammatory responses by regulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The experimental animals were purchased from Beijing Ji cui Pharmaceutical Kang Biotechnology Co., LTD., and were raised on the public service platform for experimental animals of Hebei Medical University. The animal experimental protocol was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Review Committee of Hebei Medical University (Number: 20220301).

Animals

CB2R defective (CB2R-/-) C57BL/6 mice were constructed using CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology. Thirty specific pathogen free (SPF) wild type (WT) male mice and thirty CB2R-/- male mice were selected from 6 to 8 weeks and weighed about 20 g.

Periodontitis model and grouping

Thirty selected WT and CB2R-/- male mice each aged 6–8 weeks and weighing about 20 g were divided into two groups according to genotype, and each group was subdivided into an experimental group P and a control group NP.

Periodontitis mouse models were established via ligature placement. After mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.8% sodium pentobarbital (0.008 mL/g), the right maxillary second molar of mice in both groups was ligated with 0.12 mm orthodontic ligature wire and knotted palatally to establish the periodontitis model, and the modeling group was P group [27–29]. The unmodeled mice of both genetic groups were blank control group, i.e., NP group. During the modeling period, the ligature wire was checked for shedding, and if it was shed, the mouse was eliminated. The mice were euthanized 3 weeks after modeling for sampling. We administered a triple anesthetic dose of sodium pentobarbital to mice via intraperitoneal injection, supplemented by cervical dislocation to euthanize the mice (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Mouse periodontitis model established by ligating wire

Mouse periodontitis model established by ligating wire

Sample collection

All WT and CB2R−/− mice maxillae were sampled, and after removing the ligature wires on the modeling side, the maxillae of the P and NP groups were sorted by genotype, and some of them were placed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for fixation and used for Micro-CT scanning, HE staining, and immunohistochemistry staining; and some of the maxillae were placed in the refrigerator at −80℃ for freezing and storage, and were used for extracting tissue RNA.

Alveolar bone absorption measurement

The maxilla fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were scanned by cone beam CT using PINGSENG Healthcare Inc. ‘s Nemo Micro-CT (NMC-200) device. When scanning, put the maxilla into the equipment sample chamber, set the Micro-CT tube voltage to 90 kV and tube current to 40uA.The maxillary molar region was scanned and then images were reconstructed with FDK 2 K on Avatar software (version 2.0.11.0, PINGSENG Healthcare Inc.) with a pixel size of 8*8*9um. The distance from the alveolar bone crest (ABC) to the cementa-enamel junction (CEJ) in the buccal and palatal sides of the second molar was measured and averaged, and the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and trabecular bone thickness (Tb.Th) in this region were analyzed [30–32].

HE staining analysis of alveolar bone tissue

Maxilla of mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h, then the tissue was transferred to 10% EDTA for decalcification for 1 month, dehydrated by gradient ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and finally made into 4 μm thick sagittal sections. After HE staining, the infiltration of inflammatory cells, the level of attachment loss and the resorption of alveolar bone in periodontal tissues were observed under a microscope.

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC) analysis of alveolar bone

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC) was performed on the above alveolar bone sections to analyze the expression of M1 and M2 macrophages in periodontal tissues. In this study, anti-inducable nitric oxide synthase antibodies (iNOS, SCBT, SC-7271, 1:100) and anti-arginase 1 antibodies (Arg-1, BD, 610708, 1:50,) were used. After tissue sections were stained, the stained areas were photographed using Olympus BX63, and the iNOS and Arg-1 positive cells in the pictures were counted and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Alveolar bone of RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from alveolar bone using Trizol method (Biyuntian, R0016). After measuring the concentration and purity of the extracted RNA using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (NanoDrop2000, Thermo), the extracted total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA according to the instructions of the reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1622). RT-qPCR was performed with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara). The reaction primers included GAPDH, iNOS, Arg-1, IL-6, IL-10, 1 L-4 and IL-1β, all of which were synthesized by Sangong Bioengineering (Shanghai) Co., LTD. The sequence of primers was shown in Table 1. GAPDH was used as the internal control, and the mRNA expression levels of iNOS, Arg-1, IL-6, IL-10, 1 L-4 and IL-1β in alveolar bone were calculated by 2−△△Ct method.Table 1Primer sequences of the biomarkers examined in this study

Statistical analysis

The IHC-stained positive cells were counted and analyzed using ImageJ software. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and the experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for inter-group samples, unmatched T-test was used for two samples in the group, and statistical analysis was performed by GraphPad Prism 8 statistical software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, by establishing a mouse model of periodontitis in vitro, we found that the M1 and M2 polarization of macrophages were enhanced during the progression of periodontitis, and CB2R knockout affected the M1 and M2 polarization of macrophages during the progression of periodontitis, which in turn affected the degree of periodontitis inflammation.

Studies have shown that periodontitis is a result of the interaction between bacteria and host immunity, and macrophages, as common intrinsic immune cells, play an important role in periodontal inflammation [33]. M1 macrophage polarization is the main form in the process of periodontitis. It is induced by interferon gamma (IFN-γ) produced by T helper cell 1 (Th1) and microbial related factors dominated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and stimulates the production of proinflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-23 and TNF-α, which promote inflammatory response [5, 9]. In contrast, M2-type macrophages, which mainly play a role in anti-inflammation and promotion of tissue recovery, are generated by macrophage differentiation under the stimulation of helper T cell 2 (Th2)-associated cytokines IL-4 and IL-13, and produce anti-inflammatory factors such as IL-4, IL-10, and TGF-β1 [34–36]. The dynamic balance of M1 and M2 macrophages plays an important role in tissue homeostasis. Moreover, it has been found in previous studies that some gene regulation, inflammatory vesicles, etc., could aggravate the level of macrophage M1 polarization and the level of inflammation in periodontitis [25, 37–41]. An in vivo and in vitro study of macrophage polarization in periodontitis also confirmed that the polarization of macrophages M1 and M2 was enhanced under periodontal inflammation, as well as the M1/M2 ratio [11, 42]. In diabetes-associated periodontitis glipizide attenuates the pathogenicity of periodontitis by inhibiting angiogenesis, osteoclastogenesis, and M1/M2 macrophage ratio in periodontal tissues [43]. The results of the present study also confirmed the enhanced polarization of M1-type macrophages and the increased M1/M2 ratio in the inflammatory state.

The anti-inflammatory effects of CB2R in certain diseases have been widely reported [12–14, 16, 44]. The CB2R agonist JWH-133 was found to inhibit M1 macrophage polarization and attenuate chronic inflammation in diet-induced obese mice through the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway [18]. In a mouse model of skeletal muscle ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, CB2R partially protects against skeletal muscle regeneration by modulating macrophage M1/M2 polarization [17]. CB2R was demonstrated in a mouse skin wound model to attenuate the inflammatory response during skin wound healing by affecting macrophage M1 polarization [16]. Recent studies have also shown that CB2R agonist GW405833 could alleviate acute liver failure in mice by inhibiting HIF-1α-mediated glucose metabolism reprogramming, macrophage proliferation and M1 polarization [19]. Other studies have also confirmed that CB2R inhibited pro-inflammatory cytokine production and attenuates the inflammatory response by reducing tissue destruction and alveolar bone resorption in periodontitis [45]. The CB2R agonist HU-308 attenuated LPS-induced bone resorption in periodontitis, providing anti-inflammatory and osteoprotective effects [15]. It has not been reported whether CB2R affects the degree of periodontal inflammation by regulating the polarization of macrophages in periodontitis. Therefore, this study verified from in vitro experiments that CB2R can affect the inflammatory response of periodontitis by regulating M1/M2 polarization of macrophages.

In the present study, we first established a CB2R knockout model, and then a periodontitis model was established in the second molar of the right maxilla in WT mice and CB2R−/− mice, respectively, with ligature wire. Micro-CT and HE results showed that the bone resorption in periodontitis was more severe in both CB2R−/− group mice than in WT group mice. This is consistent with previous studies, indicating that CB2R itself plays a positive feedback role in the process of periodontitis, and when its receptor is knocked out, it fails to fulfill its role and aggravates the inflammatory response [15, 19, 46–48]. In order to verify whether CB2R−/− can affect the polarization of macrophages and thus affect periodontitis, we also detected the expression of iNOS and Arg-1 protein and mRNA on the surface markers of M1 and M2 macrophages in the periodontal tissues of WT mice and CB2R−/− mice. The results showed that the expression of related markers increased on the periodontitis side of both groups of mice. The expression of CB2R−/− group was more than that of WT group. We also examined the mRNA expression of relevant pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory factors in the two groups of mice, and both showed an increase in the expression of the relevant factors, and the CB2R−/− group showed more increased expression than the WT group. The results of the M1/M2 ratio and the associated inflammatory factor ratio showed a higher ratio in the CB2R−/− group than in the WT group. These results indicated that at the overall level, CB2R knockdown enhanced the M1 polarization of macrophages and the expression of related inflammatory factors, which aggravated periodontitis. In addition, there was also an enhancement of M2-type macrophage polarization and related anti-inflammatory cytokine expression in our findings, which was consistent with some previous studies [11, 49, 50]. Although it has been shown in some studies that M2-type macrophage expression was reduced in the periodontitis state, this may be related to the fact that macrophage polarization is a dynamic equilibrium process, as well as its great plasticity [11, 50].

Potential explanations for discrepancies between IHC and RT-qPCR results: The observed discrepancies between IHC and RT-qPCR results may be attributed to several factors, primarily spatial heterogeneity. IHC detects protein localization and distribution within specific tissue regions, whereas RT-qPCR measures the average gene expression level across the entire tissue sample. Studies [51, 52] demonstrate regional heterogeneity in macrophage polarization states within periodontitis lesions. IHC can spatially resolve polarization markers, an advantage not afforded by RT-qPCR, potentially leading to result inconsistencies. Secondly, post-transcriptional regulation and protein stability contribute to the divergence. Discordance between mRNA levels and protein expression arises from translational efficiency control and differential protein degradation. For instance, Study [53] reports elevated PTEN gene expression in periodontal macrophages, yet its protein activity is regulated by phosphorylation. Study [54]indicates hat Porphyromonas gingivalis infection triggers ubiquitin-mediated degradation of M2 markers, resulting in detectable mRNA by RT-qPCR but absent protein signals via IHC. Additionally, the dynamic nature of macrophage polarization plays a role. Macrophage polarization is reversible studies [55, 56]. RT-qPCR can capture transient mRNA fluctuations, while IHC-detected protein expression exhibits a temporal lag. Such temporal differences may introduce bias. Therefore, future studies should employ complementary methodologies to minimize these potential biases and validate findings comprehensively.

This experiment also has some limitations, as the specific mechanism by which CB2R regulates macrophage polarization to aggravate periodontal inflammation is still unclear. Moreover, the present experiment is also limited in using only a single time point to induce inflammation, and the mechanism can be more thoroughly investigated by increasing the modeling time points and increasing the number of in vitro experiments in the future.
