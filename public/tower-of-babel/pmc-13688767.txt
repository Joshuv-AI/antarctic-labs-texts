Exploring palliative care clinical skills and practice capability among rural allied health professionals: a learning needs analysis
Authors: Emily Mitchell, Laurelle Stalker, Kristen Ranse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPalliative care improves quality of life for people with serious illness and their families by relieving suffering. In rural areas, access to specialist services is limited, with generalist clinicians often providing care. Allied health professionals in these settings may have varying levels of confidence and knowledge, which can affect care quality. Understanding their learning needs is essential to develop targeted education and improve service delivery.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify the learning needs and education preferences of rural allied health clinicians in delivering specialist palliative and end-of-life care.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was distributed using convenience and snowball sampling to allied health professionals in two health services spanning four rural regions in Queensland, Australia. The survey collected demographic data and assessed self-reported confidence, knowledge, skills and preferred learning formats. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarise survey responses and identify areas of knowledge gaps, consolidation and strength.ResultsThirty-one allied health professionals participated in the survey. The majority had over two years of experience and routinely provided palliative care within their generalist roles. Live, recorded online sessions were the preferred format for education, and all participants expressed interest in attending locally delivered in-person training. Knowledge gaps were identified in 94% of items across six domains—Clinical Practice, Psychosocial and Supportive Care, Quality of Life and Functional Activity Engagement, Palliative Symptom Management, End-of-Life Care, and Communication. Items pertaining to energy conservation and fatigue management and pressure area care and prevention were identified as areas for knowledge consolidation. Home environment assessment and equipment prescription were considered knowledge strengths. Most participants reported confidence in their understanding of allied health roles but variability was evident in regards to understanding the roles of other members of the health care team. Two-thirds of participants were receptive to information on self-care.ConclusionsThis study provides a baseline of learning needs and education preferences among rural allied health professionals from two health services across four rural regions of Queensland, Australia. These insights can guide the development of targeted training to strengthen palliative care delivery in rural communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02251-5.

Background

Palliative care focuses on enhancing the quality of life for patients with serious illnesses and their families by preventing and alleviating suffering [1]. However, people living in rural areas often struggle to access specialised palliative care services, relying instead on generalist healthcare providers, including allied health professionals [2]. These healthcare professionals, such as physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech therapists, and social workers are integral to a holistic, interprofessional approach to addressing the needs of patients and their families [3]. While they play a vital role in delivering care, their understanding of palliative care principles can vary, potentially affecting the consistency and quality of care provided [4].

Research on the knowledge and confidence of rural allied health professionals in delivering palliative and end-of-life care is limited. A survey of 121 allied health professionals in the Loddon Mallee region of rural Victoria, Australia found that approximately 40% of these clinicians were involved in caring for patients with terminal illnesses [4]. Although these clinicians reported high confidence in delivering general allied health interventions, such as managing physical decline and fatigue, they expressed lower confidence in addressing end-of-life symptoms, including the dying process and related physical and existential distress [4].

Ristevski et al. emphasized that identifying the strengths and knowledge gaps of the rural workforce in palliative care can inform training and planning, ultimately contributing to effective educational programs [5]. Rural clinicians face unique challenges, including geographical isolation and limited resources, which can hinder their confidence and competence in providing quality palliative care [3, 6, 7]. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 10 aims to reduce inequality, including healthcare inequality, both within and between countries [8]. The World Health Organization highlights the necessity of equitable access to palliative care [1], while the National Palliative Care Standards for Specialist Palliative Care Providers in Australia highlight the need for targeted training [9]. Despite this, empirical data on the specific needs of allied health professionals in rural settings remains scarce.

Rodda and Barrett suggest that it is essential to identify the learning needs of rural clinicians to offer targeted education, information, and resources that enhance their skills [4]. Identification of the specific learning needs of rural clinicians has the capacity to inform the development of targeted educational programs and support systems that consider the unique challenges faced by these professionals in accessing education. Closing these gaps is crucial for improving palliative care delivery in rural communities, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes and an enhanced quality of life for individuals facing serious illness.

Methods

Aim

To identify the learning needs and education preferences of rural allied health professionals who deliver palliative and end of life care to patients and families in their local communities.

Design

This study utilised a cross-sectional online survey design. The Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-surveys (CHERRIES) guided reporting of this study [10].

Population, setting and sample

The target population comprised allied health professionals employed in two health services spanning four rural regions in Queensland Australia, covering a total population of approximately 277 000 and a land area of approximately 396 000 square kilometres [11]. These are generalist allied health professionals who cover all aspects of health care, including palliative care. Allied health teams are based at various health services over these regions. They provide both inpatient and outpatient professional allied health services, face to face and via telephone. Home visits often include travelling long distances. To recruit participants, the study employed convenience and snowball sampling approaches. Allied health team leaders in each of the four rural regions were asked to provide a list of email addresses for allied health professionals employed in a clinical capacity. A total of 93 email addresses were received. Recruitment involved sending an email invitation with a survey link to allied health professionals requesting their participation. Additionally, the email encouraged recipients to share the survey link with their colleagues, to expand the potential participant pool.

Data collection

REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted by Queensland Health provided the platform for the online survey [12]. This survey was informed by the Gippsland Professional Development and Skills Matrix (PD SMaRT) which had previously been piloted with a regional nursing cohort [13]. The PD SMaRT survey and associated clinical practice domains were adapted to represent the broad rural generalist allied health professional scope of practice. Although the adapted survey was not formally validated or tested for reliability prior to use, its adaption was informed through the critical application of universal, standardised frameworks and subsequent consultation with specialist palliative care and rural generalist allied health clinicians and educators across Queensland. Frameworks reviewed included the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) [14], the Palliative Care Australia National Palliative Care Standards for Specialist Palliative Care [9], and the National Palliative Care Standards for All Health Professional and Aged Care Services [15]. These frameworks ensured contemporary clinical relevance and supported a focused, comprehensive representation of the core interprofessional domains relevant to rural generalist allied health professionals delivering palliative care. The consultation process involved initially sharing the adapted survey with eight specialist palliative care allied health clinicians for feedback. The survey was then shared with seven rural generalist allied health clinicians for further feedback from their perspective. This feedback affirmed the interprofessional framework and prompted the inclusion of the “not relevant” response option.

The survey comprised of 6 demographic questions, 10 questions about learning goals and preferences, and 67 Likert scale questions (see Supplementary material 1). These Likert scale items covered the following domains: (1) Clinical Practice, (2) Psychosocial and Supportive Care, (3) Quality of Life or Functional Activity Engagement, (4) Palliative Symptom Management, (5) End of Life Care, (6) Communication, (7) Collaborative Practice, and (8) Well-being and Self-care. Items in domains 1 to 6 requested participants to rate themselves across Benner’s five developmental stages of clinical competence from “No knowledge or experience” through to “Can support & Teach others” [16]. Unlike the PD SMaRT survey [13], this survey included an additional likert scale option of “Not Relevant” allowing participants to select this response if an item was not relevant to their role. Items in the domain Collaborative Practice required participants to rate their level of agreement on a 5-point rating scale (strongly agree to strongly disagree) to a series of statements regarding their understanding of the roles of other members of the interprofessional care team. Lastly, the Well-being and Self-Care domain required participants to indicate how well they managed their wellbeing and self-care on a 3-point scale (not well, well or very well).

Participants were able to review and change their responses through a review or back buttons. There were no procedures in place to prevent multiple entries, however as the survey was voluntary and participants were self-reporting their learning needs and preferences, completing multiple surveys presented no gain to participants. No incentives were offered to participants to complete the survey. The survey was open for a period of six weeks between January and March 2025, with two reminder emails sent in mid-February and March 2025.

Data analysis

Survey responses were exported from RedCap into Microsoft Excel for analysis. Frequency and percentages were calculated to summarise participant demographic information, learning goals, education preferences, collaborative practice and self-care.

The recommendations for data interpretation from PD SMaRT [13] were used to identify the knowledge gaps, areas of consolidation and areas of strength. A knowledge gap was identified if more that 25% of the participants self-reported either no knowledge or basic knowledge. If more than 25% of the participants self-reported that they could provide interventions with support, it was identified that consolidation of knowledge was required. Areas of strengths were identified if more than 50% of the participants self-reported that they could provide interventions independently or could support and teach others. When interpreting data using the PD SMaRT recommendations [13], only valid percentage scores were used, excluding participant responses that marked an item as not relevant to their role.

Ethical considerations

Approval for the study was granted by the Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) (HREC/2024/QCC/113168) and University HREC (GU Ref No: 2025/052). Participation in the study was voluntary. Participants were provided with a participant information sheet that outlined the aims of study, the purpose and that the data would be used to inform the learning needs of allied health clinicians. As per the ethics approval, consent to participate was implied if participants chose to complete the survey.

Research data were stored electronically, password protected, and access restricted to the research team. Data will be retained for five years as per the National Health and Medical Research Council guidelines [17].

Results

Overall, 31 allied health professionals completed the online survey from a total of 93 invitations (33% response rate). All surveys were complete with no missing fields or data. One nurse completed the online survey, however as this survey was for rural allied health professionals’ this survey data was excluded from the data analysis and respondent count. Notably one third of the participants were occupational therapists, with most of the participants having at least 2 years or more experience in their chosen allied health profession and were experienced in providing palliative care.

Learning goals and education preferences

Clinicians primarily valued engagement in education for professional growth (16/31, 52%) or to enhance their knowledge and skills (10/31, 32%). Key enablers for participation included funding support (16/31, 52%) and access to self-directed learning opportunities (10/31, 32%). Preferred learning formats were self-paced (20/31, 65%), interactive or practical in nature (20/31, 65%), while didactic lectures (9/31, 29%) and written materials were less favoured (9/31, 29%).

Online live sessions (e.g., via Teams or Zoom) (25/31, 81%) were the most preferred mode of delivery, followed by recorded modules and workplace-based learning (e.g., clinical support or shadowing) (17/31, 55%). Preferences for the frequency of educational engagement were evenly distributed across at least once per month (9/31, 26%), three to four times per year (10/31, 32%), and twice per year (8/31, 26%), with fewer clinicians opting for education only when needed (4/31, 13%).

All allied health professionals were interested in attending a face-to-face, interprofessional palliative care workshop delivered in their local region. Preferred formats for this workshop included half-day (17/31, 55%) or full day sessions (11/31, 35%) held annually (14/31, 45%), biannually (8/31, 26%) or every 3 months (7/31, 23%).

Gaps

The majority of items in domains 1 to 6 were identified as knowledge gaps (50 out of 53; 94%). Refer to Fig. 1. A knowledge gap is where participants indicated they had either no knowledge or only basic knowledge of a specific domain. All items within the domains of Clinical Practice, Psychosocial and Supportive Care, End of Life Care, and Communication were classified as gaps. Individual items rated as a knowledge gap (no knowledge or basic knowledge) by the most participants included the ability to recognise and respond to medical emergencies (94%), common pharmacological or medication management used in palliative care (80%), and communication with family or carers about the dying process (76%). Items with lower percentages included identifying and responding to acute distress (29%), aggression (29%), and anger (29%); however, all exceeded the PD SMaRT benchmark of 25% required to be classified as a knowledge gap [13].

Areas of consolidation

Two items met the criteria for consolidation. These items were from the domains of Quality of Life or Functional Activity Engagement and Palliative Symptom Management and were specifically energy conservation and fatigue management (28%) and pressure area care and prevention (30%), respectively.

Strengths

There was one item identified as a strength. This item was in the domain of Quality of Life or Functional Activity Engagment and was undertaking a home environment assessment and prescribing equipment (57%).

Collaborative practice

Most participants felt confident in their understanding of allied health roles, with 74% agreeing or strongly agreeing (see Table 1). Understanding of the grief & bereavement co-ordinator role was more varied, with nearly half agreeing (48%) but over a quarter (26%) disagreeing. For the doctor and nurse roles, responses were mixed, with 58% and 48% respectively agreeing or strongly agreeing, while 23% disagreed in both cases. Understanding of the First Nations health liaison officer role showed similar trends, with 55% agreeing or strongly agreeing and 23% disagreeing. No participants strongly disagreed with any role (see Table 2).

Self-care

Twenty-one participants (68%) expressed interest in accessing education on self-care. Self-reported ratings across eight well-being items revealed that most participants felt they were managing “well” or “very well” in regards to physical (77%), emotional (71%), work (84%), personal (71%), social (81%), spiritual (81%), practical (94%), and environmental (84%) aspects (see Table 3). However, notable proportions reported managing “not well” in emotional (29%) and personal (29%) domains, suggesting potential areas for targeted support and education.

Discussion

This online cross-sectional survey examined the palliative care learning needs and education preferences of allied health professionals across four rural regions in Queensland, Australia. The findings of this study provide insight into how a rural generalist allied health workforce contributes to palliative care across broad scopes of practice, while identifying areas where targeted capacity building is needed to support service delivery in resource-constrained settings [2, 3, 19, 20].

Strengths in rural practice

Participants reported confidence in pressure care prevention, patient handling, energy conservation and fatigue management, home assessment, and equipment prescription. These competencies reflect core allied health roles, particularly for occupational therapists and physiotherapists, who comprised over half of the sample. High confidence in these interventions suggests that rural allied health professionals are already integrating palliative care principles into routine practice, including teaching and supporting peers in clinically relevant areas [4, 18, 20].

Learning needs and service delivery

Although few items met PD SMaRT thresholds for strengths [13], almost 90% of participants reported providing palliative care daily, weekly, or monthly, highlighting the extent to which generalist allied health professionals sustain palliative care delivery in rural communities [19, 20]. Knowledge gaps were identified across clinical practice, psychosocial and supportive care, end-of-life care, and communication, aligning with prior research showing that rural allied health clinicians often feel less confident in palliative care domains [2–5].

When examining the domains where the knowledge gaps were identified, a higher proportion of participants identified gaps related to the provision of end-of-life and palliative care interventions. Tasks involving more generalist skills, for example within the communication domain, such as responding to aggression, anger, and managing depression or withdrawal, were also identified as knowledge gaps, but by a smaller proportion of participants. Although communication skills are usually considered core generalist competencies, they can be more challenging in the palliative care context because they are often shaped by symptom burden, emotional distress and end-of-life concerns [21]. This suggests that future palliative care education should include these domains, even when the gaps appear smaller.

Importantly, this study identified a prevalence of knowledge gaps despite substantial professional and palliative care experience amongst participants, suggesting that experience alone does not ensure confidence in applying palliative care principles across the full breadth of rural practice. These findings therefore reinforce the need for ongoing education that is relevant to rural generalist roles and responsive to the realities of limited specialist access and broad clinical scope [4, 19, 22].

Role clarity and collaboration

Variation in participant responses to interprofessional role items in this study suggested differing levels of role clarity. Role clarity is highly relevant in rural settings where collaborative practice and skill sharing are central to sustainable and responsive care delivery [23, 24]. This reinforces the need for interprofessional education that strengthens understanding of team roles, improves coordinated patient centered care across professions, and supports evidence-based collaborative rural service delivery [23, 25]. Rural service access constraints also shape what can be prioritised in practice, with clinicians often focusing on the most feasible and high-value interventions. Skill sharing, safe delegation, and workforce redesign approaches such as the Calderdale Framework, a structured framework used to analyse tasks, identify competencies and support safe role extension [26], are therefore important for sustaining palliative care delivery across broad rural scopes of practice [19, 24, 27–29].

Self-care and sustainability

Interest in self-care education was substantial, and while most participants reported managing well across several wellbeing domains, emotional and personal self-care emerged as areas of concern for some participants. This is significant in rural palliative care, where professional isolation and repeated exposure to emotionally demanding work can increase the risk of burnout and affect retention [24, 30]. Self-care therefore needs to be considered as part of workforce sustainability, alongside skills development and organisational support, particularly in rural settings where workforce capacity is already stretched [15, 30].

Education and workforce capacity

Education preferences pointed clearly toward flexible, practical, and locally relevant formats. Clinicians valued professional growth, self-directed learning, and online delivery, with strong interest in face-to-face interprofessional workshops in their local region. These findings are directly useful for workforce development because they indicate that education should be designed around rural service realities, including geographic distance, limited access to specialist support, and the need to fit learning around broad caseloads [19, 22, 31]. In this context, targeted education is not only about filling knowledge gaps, but also about strengthening workforce resilience and capability, improving confidence, and supporting sustainable service delivery [24, 28, 29].

Implications for education and service delivery

Overall, the findings support a needs-based approach to rural workforce development that is flexible, interprofessional, and grounded in local service realities. The adapted survey provided a more detailed picture of clinician capability than broader confidence measures and can inform the design of graduated professional development pathways [4, 13]. By linking capability, role clarity, self-care, and preferred learning formats, the study offers practical direction for capacity building that is contextually relevant to rural allied health practice and to the delivery of palliative care in underserved communities [22, 24, 28].

Methodological considerations

Methodologically, the use of a survey adapted from PD SMaRT [13] with 67 nuanced practice domain competency items relevant to rural generalist allied health professional practice allowed a more detailed characterisation of clinician capabilities and learning stages than the broader confidence and practice measures used by Rodda and Barrett [4]. This granularity supported identification of specific learning gaps, consolidation areas, and strengths, which is valuable for designing graduated and targeted professional development. However, this enhanced specificity also introduced challenges, including reduced direct comparability with the original tool, increased respondent burden, and potential variability in role interpretation, which may affect the applicability of findings to other rural contexts and highlights the need for further research [4, 13, 23].

Study limitations

A key limitation of this study was the small sample size and uneven representation across allied health professions, with some professions represented by only one respondent. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the views or learning needs of the broader rural allied health workforce. Nevertheless, within a rural generalist transdisciplinary model of care, the results still provide useful direction for shared palliative care capacity building and interprofessional education. Further research with larger samples that are representative of allied health professional groups is needed to strengthen confidence in the applicability of these findings and to inform more targeted workforce development [22]. Additional limitations include regional specificity, potential selection bias through established networks, and limited representation from First Nations health workers, highlighting important areas for future research.

Conclusion

This study identified important learning needs in palliative care-specific domains among rural allied health professionals, while also demonstrating that these clinicians are already central to palliative care delivery in rural practice. The findings highlight the need for flexible, interprofessional, and context-specific workforce development that builds confidence across palliative care domains and is responsive to the realities of rural service provision. Strengthening access to targeted education and support may improve equitable palliative care delivery for people living in rural Australia. Furthermore, this work supports global efforts to reduce health disparities and promotes alignment with the SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities [8], emphasising the importance of equitable healthcare access for underserved rural populations.
