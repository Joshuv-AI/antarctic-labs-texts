Existence of critical tiltings and local limits of general size-conditioned Bienaymé-Galton-Watson multitype trees
Rémy Poudevigne, Paul Thévenin

Abstract
We are interested in the structure of multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson (BGW) trees conditioned on integer linear combinations of the numbers of vertices of given types. We show that, under regularity assumptions on the offspring distributions, it is always possible to find a critical BGW tree having the same conditional distribution. This allows us to prove the existence of local limits for noncritical BGW trees, under a large variety of conditionings. Our proof is based on geometric considerations on the set of the so-called exponential tiltings of a family of offspring distributions.

Introduction

Multitype Bienaymé-Galton-Watson trees (or, in short, BGW trees) are a model of random trees, where each vertex is given a type and has offspring according to a distribution that only depends on its type. The asymptotic structure of large BGW trees under various conditionings (total size, number of leaves, number of vertices of a given type) has attracted a lot of interest (see e.g.
[
13
,
20
,
4
,
22
,
14
,
1
]
), in order to tackle questions coming from theoretical mathematics, biology or physics.

We consider here trees whose set of types is
[K]:=\{1,\ldots,K\}
.
In the monotype case (
K=1
), which has been extensively studied, let
\mu
be a distribution on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, assumed to be critical (that is, of mean
1
). Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the BGW tree with offspring distribution
\mu
, conditioned on having
n
vertices, provided that this occurs with positive probability. Abraham and Delmas
[
2
]
proved that the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
converges in a local sense to a random discrete infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{*}
; that is, for all
k\geq 1
, the
k
-neighbourhood of the root of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
converges in distribution to the
k
-neighbourhood of the root of
\mathcal{T}_{*}
. The tree
\mathcal{T}_{*}
is called Kesten’s tree, and was first characterized in
[
12
]
.
In another direction, under the additional assumption that
\mu
has finite variance
\sigma^{2}
, Aldous
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
proved that
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, seen as a metric space where all edges have length
(\sigma\sqrt{n})^{-1}
, converges in distribution to a random compact limit metric space called the Brownian Continuum Random Tree (in short, CRT)
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. This tree is referred to as the scaling limit (i.e. limit after rescaling) of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

In the case where
K\geq 2
, similar partial results were obtained. Miermont
[
16
]
proved that the BGW tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}_{n}
, which is the multitype tree with root of type
i
conditioned on having
n
vertices of type
i
, admits the CRT as its scaling limit, given that the offspring distribution is critical (in a multitype sense that we define later) and has small exponential moments. The latter assumption was then lifted by Haas and Stephenson
[
9
]
, who only assume a finite variance condition.

On the other hand, let
(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}\backslash\{0,\ldots,0\}
. Stephenson
[
21
]
showed, under an assumption of small exponential moments, the local convergence of a critical multitype BGW tree
\mathcal{T}
conditioned on
\sum_{i=1}^{K}\gamma_{i}N_{i}(\mathcal{T})=n
(for
n
going to
+\infty
along a subsequence on which these events have positive probability) towards a multitype version of Kesten’s tree (see
[
21
, Definition 2.7]
). Here and everywhere in the paper,
N_{i}(\mathcal{T})
stands for the number of vertices of type
i
in the tree
\mathcal{T}
.

In all the previously mentioned results, the criticality assumption turns out to be fundamental, and proving results outside of criticality can be challenging. In the monotype case, Janson
[
11
]
proved the following: a (non necessarily critical) distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
being given, under some regularity assumptions, there exists a unique critical distribution
\tilde{\mu}
such that, for all
n
, the
\mu
-BGW tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
has the same distribution as the
\tilde{\mu}
-BGW tree
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
(again, conditioned to have the same number
n
of vertices). This allows to prove the local and scaling convergences of the noncritical tree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as well.

Little is known in the multitype case, outside of criticality. Let us mention Pénisson
[
18
]
, who showed that - under some regularity assumptions - for all
n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}
, the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}
(the BGW tree conditioned to have
N_{i}(\mathcal{T})=n_{i}
vertices of type
i
for all
i\in[K]
) is distributed as a critical BGW tree under the same conditioning. Recently, Abraham, Delmas and Guo
[
3
]
showed the local convergence of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}
, provided that the asymptotic proportions of vertices of each type satisfy a specific relation. In another direction, Abraham, Bi and Delmas
[
1
]
considered the case where the multitype tree has the structure of a monotype BGW tree, and where the type of a vertex only depends on its number of children. In this specific case, they characterized the local limit of the tree
\mathcal{T}_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{K}}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
n_{i}\sim\alpha_{i}n
for all
i
, for some
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K})\in[0,1]^{K}
. Outside of criticality, they managed to obtain a detailed picture similar to Janson’s in the monotype case, and to characterize the cases in which the limit is a nondegenerate multitype Kesten tree.

Finally, in the general multitype case and under a general size-conditioning, the second author obtains partial results
[
23
]
: the size-conditioned tree
\mathcal{T}
is distributed as a critical tree
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
under the same size-conditioning, under some rather strong assumptions on the generating functions of the offspring distributions.

Our aim in this paper is to extend all these results, and generalize
[
23
]
.

2
Background on random trees

We recall here the definitions of multitype plane trees and multitype BGW trees.

2.1
Plane trees

We first recall the definition of plane trees using Neveu’s formalism
[
17
]
. Let
\mathcal{U}:=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{k}
represent the set of finite sequences of positive integers, where we adopt the convention that
\mathbb{N}^{0}=\{\varnothing\}
. To simplify notation, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, an element
u
of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
is denoted as
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{k}
, with
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we use
ui
to represent the element
u_{1}\cdots u_{k}i\in\mathbb{N}^{k+1}
and
iu
to represent the element
iu_{1}\cdots u_{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{k+1}
, with the convention that
\emptyset i=i\emptyset=i
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

A plane tree
t
is defined as a subset of
\mathcal{U}
satisfying three conditions:

•

(i)
\varnothing\in t
(indicating the presence of a root in the tree);

•

(ii) if
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\in t
, then, for all
k\leq n
,
u_{1}\cdots u_{k}\in t
(these elements are referred to as ancestors of
u
);

•

(iii) for any
u\in t
, there exists a nonnegative integer
k_{u}(t)
such that, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
ui\in t
if and only if
1\leq i\leq k_{u}(t)
(
k_{u}(t)
is called the number of children of
u
, or the outdegree of
u
,
u
is called the parent of
ui
and
ui
is called a child of
u
).

The elements of
t
are referred to as vertices, and we denote the total number of vertices of
t
by
|t|
. Finally, we denote the set of plane trees by
\mathbb{T}
. We always consider a plane tree
t
as a metric space by connecting each non-root vertex to its parent, with the usual graph distance
d_{t}
on it (that is, all edges have length
1
. For
a>0
, we also define
ad_{t}
as the distance on
t
for which all edges have length
a
. Furthermore, we endow a plane tree
t
with the uniform measure
m_{t}
on its vertices.

2.2
Multitype plane trees

Fix
K\in\mathbb{N}
. A
K
-type plane tree is a pair
T:=(t,\mathbf{e}_{t})
where
t\in\mathbb{T}
is a plane tree and
\mathbf{e}_{t}:t\mapsto[K]
. For
u\in t
,
\mathbf{e}_{t}(u)
is called the type of the vertex
u
. We let
\mathbb{T}^{(K)}
be the set of
K
-type plane trees, and, for
i\in[K]
, we denote by
\mathbb{T}^{(K,i)}
the subset of
\mathbb{T}^{(K)}
of trees whose root has label
\mathbf{e}_{t}(\varnothing)=i
. We also denote by
N_{i}(T)
the number of vertices
u
of the tree
t
which are of type
i
, that is, such that
\mathbf{e}_{t}(u)=i
, and we set
\mathbf{N}(T):=(N_{1}(T),\ldots,N_{K}(T))^{\intercal}
. Finally, we call
t
the
shape
of the multitype tree
T
.

2.3
Multitype BGW trees

Let us now define
K
-type BGW trees. For
K\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the set
\mathcal{W}_{K}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}[K]^{n}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}:=(\zeta^{(i)})_{i\in[K]}
be a family of probability distributions on
\mathcal{W}_{K}
. For
i\in[K]
, we define a probability distribution on
\mathbb{T}^{(K,i)}
as follows. Consider a family of independent variables
(X_{u}^{i},u\in\mathcal{U},i\in[K])
with values in
\mathcal{W}_{K}
, such that for all
(u,i)\in\mathcal{U}\times[K]
,
X_{u}^{i}
follows the distribution
\zeta^{(i)}
. We recursively construct a random
K
-type tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}:=(t,\mathbf{e}_{t})\in\mathbb{T}^{(K,i)}
as follows. First,
\varnothing\in t
and
\mathbf{e}_{t}(\varnothing)=i
; then, if
u\in t
and
\mathbf{e}_{t}(u)=j
, then, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
uk\in t
if and only if
1\leq k\leq|X_{u}^{j}|
and in this case
\mathbf{e}_{t}(uk)=X_{u}^{j}(k)
.

In other words, the root of
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
has type
i
, and vertices of type
j
in
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
have children independently according to
\zeta^{(j)}
. We refer to
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
as a
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
-BGW tree with root type
i
.

An objet of importance is the
projection
of the family
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. For any
w\in\mathcal{W}_{K}
and
j\in[K]
, let
w^{(j)}
be the number of
j
’s in
w
. We call projection of
w
the element
p(w)=(w^{(1)},\ldots,w^{(K)})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}
. For
i\in[K]
, denote by
\mu^{(i)}
the probability distribution on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}
defined as follows: for all
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}
,

\displaystyle\mu^{(i)}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in\mathcal{W}^{K}\\
p(w)=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})\end{subarray}}\zeta^{(i)}(w).

The family
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=(\mu^{(i)},i\in[K])
is called the
projection
of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

The generating function
\phi^{(i)}
of
\mu^{(i)}
is the series

\displaystyle\phi^{(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})=\sum_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}}\mu^{(i)}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{K}^{k_{K}}.

We also define the mean matrix
M=(m_{i,j})_{i,j\in[K]}
as the
K\times K
matrix such that

\displaystyle m_{i,j}=\sum_{\textbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}}z_{j}\mu^{(i)}(\textbf{z}),

with the convenient notation
\textbf{z}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{K})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}
. In other words,
m_{i,j}
is the expected number of children of type
j
of a vertex of type
i
.

Numerous asymptotic structural properties of
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
depend only on the projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Let us mention some of them.

•

(Critical) We say that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is critical if the spectral radius
\rho(M)
of
M
is equal to
1
, subcritical if
\rho(M)\leq 1
, and supercritical if
\rho(M)\geq 1
.

•

(Entire) We say that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire if, for all
i
, the generating function
\phi^{(i)}
of
\mu^{(i)}
is entire.

•

(Finite) A family
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is called finite if, for all
i\in[K]
:

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(i)}|<\infty\right)>0,

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
is a
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
-BGW tree for some
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
whose projection is
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

•

(Nondegenerate) We say that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is nondegenerate if it satisfies:

\exists i\in[K],\mu^{(i)}\left(\left\{\textbf{z},\sum_{j\in[K]}z_{j}\geq 2\right\}\right)>0.

(2)

•

(Nonlocalized) A projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is called nonlocalized if, for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{K}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
, there exists
i\in[K]
such that, if
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})
is distributed according to
\mu^{(i)}
, then
\sum_{j=1}^{K}X_{j}k_{j}
is not deterministic.

•

(Irreducible) We say that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is irreducible if
M
is irreducible, that is, for all
i,j\in[K]
there exists
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
M^{p}_{i,j}>0
.

We also say that a family
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is critical (resp. supercritical, subcritical, entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized, irreducible) if its projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is.

The Perron-Frobenius theorem states that, when
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is irreducible,
\rho(M)>0
is an eigenvalue of
M
and is simple. Up to multiplicative constants, its left and right
\rho(M)
-eigenvectors (respectively
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
) are unique and have positive coefficients. We always normalize them so that

\sum_{i\in[K]}a_{i}=\sum_{i\in[K]}a_{i}b_{i}=1.

2.4
Size-conditioned BGW trees

Our main object of interest is multitype BGW trees conditioned on their size being large, for a rather general notion of size.

Definition 2.1

(Size-conditioned tree)
.

Let
\ell\geq 1
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let also
\mathbf{g}:=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{\ell})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
be a family of distributions on
\mathcal{W}_{K}
. We define the tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}
as the tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
conditioned on

\Gamma\left(\begin{array}[]{c}N_{1}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(i)}\right)\\
\vdots\\
N_{K}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(i)}\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}g_{1}\\
\vdots\\
g_{\ell}\end{array}\right),

(3)

provided that (
3
) holds with positive probability.

One of the main goals of the paper is to answer the following question: does there exist a multitype BGW tree
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
whose offspring distribution is critical, and such that, for all
\mathbf{g}
such that (
3
) occurs with positive probability, we have the equality in distribution:

\mathcal{T}^{(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}\overset{(d)}{=}\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}?

When it is the case, then, the same way as Janson
[
11
]
in the monotype case, we can understand the behaviour of the tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}
as
|g_{1}|,\ldots,|g_{K}|\rightarrow\infty
by studying the critical tree
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^{(i)}
instead of
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
.

2.5
Local limit of trees

We say that a tree
T
is locally finite if, for all
x\in T
, for all
r>0
, the number of vertices of
T
at distance
\leq r
from
x
is finite.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of (random) finite trees of respective roots
(\varnothing_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{*}
be an a.s. locally finite infinite tree of root
\varnothing_{*}
. We say that
\mathcal{T}_{*}
is the local limit of the sequence
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
if, for any fixed
r\geq 1
, the following convergence holds in distribution:

B_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{(d)}{\rightarrow}}B_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{*}),

where
B_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is the ball of radius
r
around
\varnothing_{n}
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and
B_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{*})
is the ball of radius
r
around
\varnothing_{*}
in
\mathcal{T}_{*}
. We write, in this case:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\overset{(d),loc}{\rightarrow}}\mathcal{T}_{*}.

3
Statement of the results

We define here our main object of study, which is a family of projections that we call exponential tiltings of a projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Our main tool is a function
\chi
which we define in Section
3.2
, before stating our main results in Section
3.3
.

3.1
Exponential tiltings and asymptotic directions

In this paper, being given a projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, we are interested in a certain family of projections, which we call exponential tiltings of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

Definition 3.1

(Exponential tiltings)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=(\mu^{(i)})_{i\in[K]}
be a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{K}
. For any fixed
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
we define the measures
(\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{(i)})_{i\in[K]}
as follows: for all
i\in[K]
, for all
k_{1},\ldots,k_{K}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
:

\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{(i)}(k_{1},\dots,k_{K})=\mu^{(i)}(k_{1},\dots,k_{K})\frac{e^{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}k_{i}}}{\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{(i)}}(e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cdot\mathbf{k}})},

(4)

where in the expectation
\mathbf{k}:=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})
is distributed according to
\mu^{(i)}
. We set
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:=(\mu^{(1)}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\ldots,\mu^{(K)}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
, and call it an exponential tilting of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
.

Observe that the tilting operation is consistent with the projection. Indeed, if
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is the projection of a family
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we can define the exponential tilting
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
the same way as
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
by setting, for all
\textbf{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{K}
, all
i\in[K]
:

\displaystyle\zeta^{(i)}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\textbf{w})=\zeta^{(i)}(\textbf{w})\frac{e^{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}w^{(i)}}}{\sum_{\tilde{\textbf{w}}\in\mathcal{W}_{K}}\zeta^{(i)}(\tilde{\textbf{w}})e^{\sum_{i=1}^{K}\theta_{i}\tilde{w}^{(i)}}}.

We let
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}
be the generating function of
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}
, that is,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}:\mathbb{R}^{K}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is such that

\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})=\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{K}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\mu^{(i)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})x_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots x_{K}^{k_{K}},

and we set
\phi^{(i)}:=\phi_{\mathbf{0}}^{(i)}
for all
i\in[K]
.

Note that, then, (
4
) rewrites: for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(i)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})=\frac{\phi^{(i)}\left(x_{1}e^{\theta_{1}},\ldots,x_{K}e^{\theta_{K}}\right)}{\phi^{(i)}\left(e^{\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\theta_{K}}\right)},

if these quantities are well-defined.

We denote by
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
the mean matrix of
\left(\mu^{(i)}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)_{i\in[K]}
and by
\rho_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
the spectral radius of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. For all
i\in[K]
, we also define
\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)}
as the multitype BGW tree with distribution
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and root of type
i
.

When
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is critical, it turns out that there is a favoured direction that we call the asymptotic direction.

Definition 3.2

(Asymptotic direction)
.

For any critical and irreducible measure
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, we call asymptotic direction the (renormalized)
1
-left eigenvector
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, that is, the vector
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
with positive coordinates such that
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})=\mathbf{0}
, renormalized so that
||X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{1}=1
. This vector exists and is unique by the Perron-Frobenius theorem.

3.2
The function
\chi
and
\Gamma
-equivalence

We are particularly interested in an equivalence relation on the set of exponential tiltings. To define this equivalence relation, we first introduce a function
\chi
, similar to the log-Laplace transform of the functions
(\phi^{(i)})_{1\leq i\leq K}
which will play an important role throughout the paper.

Definition 3.3
.

The function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
is defined by
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=(\chi_{1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\ldots,\chi_{K}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
where, for all
i\in[K]
:

\chi_{i}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{R}^{K}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}\\
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto\log(\phi^{(i)}(e^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))-\theta_{i},\end{array}\right.

where we recall that
e^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=(e^{\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{\theta_{K}})
.

We call
\mathcal{C}_{image}:=\chi(\mathbb{R}^{K})
the image of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
by the function
\chi
.

Observe that the function
\chi
is not a bijection (see Appendix
9
). Its interest is twofold: first, it naturally appears in the following definition of equivalent tiltings; second, its Jacobian is equal to
M_{\theta}-I_{K}
, which helps provide a characterization of critical measures in terms of
\chi
.

We now introduce the notion of equivalent tiltings, which are tiltings under which the distribution of the size-conditioned tree does not vary.

Definition 3.4

(
\Gamma
-equivalent tiltings)
.

Let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
, for some
\ell\geq 1
. We say that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
are
\Gamma
-equivalent if

\displaystyle\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})\in\left(\ker\,\Gamma\right)^{\perp}.

If this holds, we write
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
.

For convenience and with a slight abuse of notation, we will sometimes write that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is
\Gamma
-equivalent to
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
whenever
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
.

In the rest of the paper, unless explicitly mentioned, we fix an integer
\ell\geq 1
and a matrix
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
. For any
\mathbf{g}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\ell}
, any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, any

, define
\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}
as the tree
\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)}
conditioned on
\Gamma\ \mathbf{N}(\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)})=\mathbf{g}
.
The interest of the Definition

lies in the following result.

Proposition 3.5
.

Fix
i\in[K],\ {\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
, and

such that

\mathbb{P}(\Gamma\ \mathbf{N}(\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)})=\mathbf{g})>0

(i)

We have
\mathbb{P}(\Gamma\ \mathbf{N}(\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime},(i)})=\mathbf{g})>0

(ii)

\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}\overset{(d)}{=}\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime},(i)}_{\Gamma,\mathbf{g}}

Proof.

The proof of (i) is clear (and it actually holds for any

4.1

Definition 4.2

Let

(ii)

be the asymptotic direction associated to

is maximal at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\boldsymbol{\mu}
iis the s nonlocalized, this maximizer is unique.

To simplify notations, we will write for any

To prove (i), it suffices to compute the Hessian

at

\forall i,j\in[K],H_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(i,j)=-\sum_{k\in[K]}X_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}g^{(k)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

In particular, for any vector

Notice that

is strictly concave.

Let us now prove (ii). For any

(5)

M_{s\mathbf{1}}(i,j)=\frac{e^{s}M(i,j)}{\mu^{(i)}(\mathbf{0})+e^{s}\sum_{k\in[K]}M(i,k)}.

(8)

Let
(Z_{\infty}(1),\ldots,Z_{\infty}(K))
be any subsequential limit of
(Z_{s}(1),\ldots,Z_{s}(K))
. By (
7
) and (
8
), we obtain that, for all
i\in I
:

Z_{\infty}(i)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}M(i,k)Z_{\infty}(k)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}M(i,k)}.

In particular, by irreducibility of
M
, we get that
Z_{\infty}(i)=1
for all
i\in I
. Thus,
(Z_{s}(1),\ldots,Z_{s}(K))\rightarrow\mathbf{1}
as
s\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, for
s
large enough,
\inf_{i\in I}Z_{s}(i)\geq 3/4
. We now use the following fact. Since
Z_{s}(j^{\prime})=1
for all
j^{\prime}\in J
, if
I\neq\emptyset
, we have:

\forall j\in J,\sum_{k\in[K]}M_{s\mathbf{1}}(j,k)Z_{s}(k)\geq\big(\inf_{i\in I}Z_{s}(i)\big)\sum_{k\in[K]}M_{s\mathbf{1}}(j,k)

(9)

Therefore, for
s
large enough, for all
j\in J
,
(M_{s\mathbf{1}}Z_{s})(j)\geq 3/4\times 3/2\geq 1=Z_{s}(j)
and (
6
) also holds for any
j\in J
. Hence, we have that
(M_{s\mathbf{1}}Z_{s})(i)\geq Z_{s}(i)
for all
i\in[K]
. Moreover,
M_{s\mathbf{1}}
has nonnegative coefficients. Thus, we get that for all
n\in\mathbb{N},\big(M_{s\mathbf{1}}^{n}Z_{s}\big)(i)\geq\big(M_{s\mathbf{1}}Z_{s}\big)(i)>0
. This implies that
\rho_{s\mathbf{1}}\geq 1
and therefore
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{s\mathbf{1}}
is supercritical.

∎

4.3
\mathcal{M}_{crit}
is a manifold

We prove here that the set
\mathcal{M}_{crit}
has a manifold structure.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}
be entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Then, the set
\mathcal{M}_{crit}
is a differentiable manifold of dimension
K-1
.

This is shown by exhibiting a local diffeomorphism (see Lemma
4.10
) around each element of
\mathcal{M}_{crit}
. The next lemma allows us to simplify the study of critical matrices.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}
be entire and irreducible. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
. Then, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathcal{M}_{crit}\cap B_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime},\det(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}-I_{K})=0\right\}\cap B_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is well-defined for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Fix
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
. Assume that there exists a sequence
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)},n\geq 1)
converging to
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, such that, for all
n
,
\det(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}-I_{K})=0
but
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}
is noncritical. Since
1
is an eigenvalue of
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}
, necessarily
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}
is supercritical (but noncritical). In particular, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem, for all
n
the largest eigenvalue of
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}
is a real number
z_{n}>1
. Up to extracting a subsequence, we can assume that
z_{n}
converges to some
z\geq 1
. Indeed,
z_{n}\leq\sum_{i,j}\left|\left(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(n)}}\right)_{i,j}\right|
which is a bounded continuous function of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, so that
(z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is bounded. In particular
z
is an eigenvalue of
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
by continuity of the determinant. If
z>1
, then
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is noncritical. On the other hand,
z=1
cannot occur since, in that case,
1
would be an eigenvalue of multiplicity at least 2 of
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. This is impossible by the Perron-Frobenius theorem. The result follows.
∎

Now let
\pi
be a linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{K}
to
\mathbb{R}^{K-1}
of kernel
\mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}
.
We define the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
as follows:

F({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\left(\pi(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})),\det(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})\right),

where the first
K-1
coordinates of
F
are
\pi(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, nonlocalized and irreducible. The function
F
is a local diffeomorphism around any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical.

Clearly, Lemmas
4.9
and
4.10
along with Proposition
4.4
imply Proposition
4.8
.

Proof of Lemma
4.10
.

Fix
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical. We will show that
F
is a local diffeomorphism around
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which boils down to proving that its Jacobian matrix at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is invertible. First, observe that the Jacobian matrix
J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
of the function
\chi
at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
satisfies
J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K}
. Let us prove that
\ker(\pi\circ J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\ker(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
(seeing
J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
as a map from
\mathbb{R}^{K}
to
\mathbb{R}^{K}
). This is equivalent to showing that
\text{Im}(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\cap\ker(\pi)=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Let
Z\in\text{Im}(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\cap\ker(\pi)
and assume that
Z\neq\mathbf{0}
. By definition of
\pi
,
Z\in\mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}
, so that
Z
has only negative coordinates or only positive ones. On the other hand, we have
\text{Im}(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\ker(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\intercal})^{\perp}
. By the Perron-Frobenius theorem,
\ker(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{\intercal})
is a one-dimensional vector space generated by the vector
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
which has positive coordinates (see Definition
3.2
). Since
Z\in\text{Im}(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
, we would have
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\cdot Z=0
which is not possible because
Z
has coordinates of the same sign and
Z\neq\mathbf{0}
. Hence,
\ker(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\ker(\pi\circ J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
.

By Jacobi’s formula, we have:

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\det(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})=\text{Tr}\left(\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})^{\intercal}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right),

(10)

where
\text{com}(A)
denotes the cofactor matrix of
A
. Since
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical,
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K}
is of rank
K-1
and
\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})
is of rank
1
. Therefore, there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})^{\intercal}=\lambda YX_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}
, where
Y\in(0,+\infty)^{K}
is a 1-right eigenvector of
J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. (remember that
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}
and
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}Y=Y
).

We now have all the tools to prove that the Jacobian matrix
J_{F}
of
F
is invertible at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, that is,
\ker(J_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\{\mathbf{0}\}
. To this end, let
U\in\ker(J_{F}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
and assume by contradiction that
U\neq\mathbf{0}
. By definition, we have
U\in\ker\pi(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\ker(J_{\chi}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))
, so
U=\nu Y
for some
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. In addition, if
U\in\ker(J_{F})
, we have by (
10
):

\sum_{i}U_{i}\text{Tr}\left(\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})^{\intercal}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)=0.

We use again the notation
g^{(i)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=\log(\phi^{(i)}(e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}))
, so that in particular
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(i,j)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}g^{(i)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
. Computing the left-hand term, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{i}U_{i}\text{Tr}\left(\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})^{\intercal}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\nu Y(i)\text{Tr}\left(\lambda YX_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\nu\sum_{i}Y(i)\sum_{j,k}X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(j)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)_{j,k}Y(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\nu\sum_{i}Y(i)\sum_{j,k}X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(j)Y(k)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{k}}\big(g^{(j)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\nu\sum_{j}X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(j)\sum_{i,k}Y(i)Y(k)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{k}}\big(g^{(j)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\nu\sum_{i,j}Y(i)Y(j)\left(H(-f_{X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})\right)_{i,j},

where
H(-f_{X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})
is the Hessian of the function
-f_{X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
, where
f_{X}
is defined in Definition
4.2
.
Since
H(-f_{X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})
is positive definite by Lemma
4.3
(i) (using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is nonlocalized) and
Y\neq\mathbf{0}
, the right-hand side cannot be equal to
0
. This implies that
\ker(J_{F})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, so
J_{F}
is invertible at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
∎

5
Properties of the function
\chi

In this section, we show some properties of the function
\chi
, which will be useful in the proof of our main results.

5.1
Structure of the asymptotic cones

Here we prove a general result about asymptotic directions, which may be of independent interest - namely, the asymptotic cone
\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
(see Definition
3.2
) is open whenever it does not contain
\mathbf{0}
. More generally, the structure of the asymptotic cone strongly depends on whether
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
or not. The following lemma allows us to characterize the former case precisely.

Lemma 5.1
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Let
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
of rank
\ell
. The following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
;

(iii)

\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical,
\Gamma X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\mathbf{0}
and
\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\implies{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Assume that
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical,
\Gamma X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\mathbf{0}
and
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\implies{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})
. Then, by definition of the asymptotic cone,
\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
, so (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii).

It is also clear that (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).

We only have to prove that (i)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Assume that
\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
. This means that there exists
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
critical such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}=\mathbf{0}
. By definition of
\Gamma
-equivalence, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, we have
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
and thus there exists a vector
R\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\Gamma^{\intercal}R
. This in turn means that the following holds:

\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\exists R\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell},\ X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R=\mathbf{0}^{\intercal}R=0.

(11)

On the other hand, using Lemma
4.3
(ii) and the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is nonlocalized, we get
that for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\not={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
, we have:

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))<0.

(12)

This implies that, necessarily,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\implies{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
, which implies (iii).
∎

We now consider the second case, where
\mathbf{0}\not\in\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
. In this case, it turns out that the set
\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
is open.

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Let
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
of rank
\ell
. Then, either
\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
or the set
\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
is open and
\mathbf{0}\not\in\mathcal{D}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}_{asy}
. In particular, the set
\mathcal{D}_{asy}
is open.

The proof of Proposition
5.2
uses the following lemma, which states that the measures associated to two different critical parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\not={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
cannot have the same asymptotic direction. For any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
, define

\displaystyle E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\text{ and }\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\text{ are collinear}\}.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}
be an entire, nonlocalized and irreducible measure, and let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z})
for some
\ell\geq 1
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

|E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|\in\{1,2\}
.

(ii)

If
|E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|=2
and
E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\}
then there exists
\lambda<0
such that
\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}=\lambda\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.

(iii)

In addition, if
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
, then
E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}
.

An immediate corollary is the following.

Corollary 5.4
.

For any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
,
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\not=X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
.

Proof of Corollary
5.4
.

By Lemma
5.3
(iii) to
\Gamma=I_{K}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{K,K}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
,
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
are not collinear. Since
||X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{1}=||X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}||_{1}=1
and both have positive coordinates, it is equivalent to saying that
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\neq X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

Fix
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\neq{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
, and assume that
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
are collinear. Without loss of generality, we can assume that there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\lambda\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
. By Lemma
4.3
(ii), we have:

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}\big(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big)>0.

Similarly we have:

X^{\intercal}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}\big(\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\big)<0.

By definition of
\Gamma
-equivalence (using that for any matrix
A
,
\ker(A)^{\perp}=\text{Im}(A^{\intercal})
), there exists
R\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\Gamma^{\intercal}R
. We can thus write

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R<0<X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R=\lambda X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R.

(13)

In particular, if
\lambda\geq 0
then there is no solution.

Let us prove (i) and (ii). Assume that
|E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|\geq 3
and choose
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{3}\in E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
distinct. Then, necessarily there exists
i\neq j
and
\lambda^{\prime}\geq 0
such that
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}}=\lambda^{\prime}\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}}
, which is impossible. Hence,
|E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}|\leq 2
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, we get (i), and (ii) follows again from the fact that (
13
) has no solution if
\lambda\geq 0
.
We finally prove (iii). Let
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\ell,K}(\mathbb{Z}_{+})
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in E^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. Since
\Gamma
is not the null matrix and has nonnegative coefficients and
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
have only positive coefficients (by the Perron-Frobenius theorem) then both
\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
have nonnegative coefficients and are not
\mathbf{0}
. This means that necessarily
\lambda>0
, which is not possible if
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\not={\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
by (
13
). This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
5.2
.

Proof of Proposition
5.2
.

Consider
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
such that
\mathcal{D}_{asy}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
. Let
P
be a linear map from
\mathbb{R}^{K}
to
\mathbb{R}^{K-\ell}
with kernel
\ker{P}=(\ker{\Gamma})^{\perp}=\text{Im}(\Gamma^{\intercal})
. We define the function
G^{\Gamma}:(0,+\infty)\times\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
by:

G^{\Gamma}(\lambda,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=(\lambda\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},P\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})).

Let us prove that this function is continuous. Since
\chi
is continuous,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow P\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is continuous. To see that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is continuous, observe that, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
, we have the expression

\displaystyle\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})=\frac{D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}{Y_{\ell}^{\intercal}Y_{r}}Y_{\ell}Y_{r}^{\intercal},

where
D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is any nonzero
(K-1)
-minor of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-I_{K}
and
Y_{\ell},Y_{r}
denote respectively the
1
-left eigenvector and the
1
-right eigenvector of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with positive coordinates satisfying
||Y_{\ell}||_{1}=||Y_{r}||_{1}=1
. Such vectors exist by the Perron-Frobenius theorem. This implies that
X^{\prime}:=\frac{\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})\mathbf{1}}{||\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})\mathbf{1}||}
is a
1
-left eigenvector of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
satisfying
||X^{\prime}||_{1}=1
, whose coordinates are either all positive or all negative. Using the notation
|Y|:=(|Y_{1}|,\dots,|Y_{n}|)^{\intercal}
, the asymptotic direction
X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
can be written as

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\frac{|\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})\mathbf{1}|}{||\text{com}(M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-I_{K})\mathbf{1}||_{1}}.

(14)

Now, since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto D_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is continuous and always nonzero, it is either always positive or always negative. It is clear from (
14
) that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a continuous function of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto\Gamma X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(and thus
G^{\Gamma}
) is also continuous.

Recall now that, by Proposition
4.8
(ii), the set
\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
is a
(K-1)
-dimensional differentiable manifold. Hence, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
there exists a diffeomorphism
\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
from
(-1,1)^{K}
to an open subset
U_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
U_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\cap\mathcal{M}_{crit}=\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}((-1,1)^{K-1}\times\{0\})
and
\psi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{0})={\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
We can then define, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, the function
\tilde{G}^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\varepsilon}:(0,+\infty)\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{K-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
as:

\tilde{G}^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\varepsilon}(\lambda,x)=(\lambda\Gamma X_{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(x)},P\chi(\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(x))).

Since
\mathcal{D}_{asy}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}
, by Lemma
5.1
we have
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\not=0
. Thus, one can find
\varepsilon>0
small enough so that, for all
x\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)^{K-1}\times\{0\},\ \Gamma X_{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(x)}\not=0
. By Lemma
5.3
(i), for
\varepsilon
small enough,
\tilde{G}^{\Gamma,{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\varepsilon}
is injective. It is also continuous and therefore by the invariance of domain theorem, its image is open.

Finally, notice that
\mathcal{D}_{asy}=\mathcal{D}^{I_{K},\mathbf{0}}_{asy}=\mathcal{D}^{I_{K},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{asy}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Since
X_{\mathbf{0}}\neq\mathbf{0}
, Proposition
5.2
implies that
\mathcal{D}_{asy}
is open.
∎

5.2
The function
\chi
on
\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}

In this section, we show that the set
\mathcal{C}_{image}
is closed and convex and that
\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
is sent by
\chi
to the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{image}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Then the set
\mathcal{C}_{image}
is closed and convex.

Proposition 5.6
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Let
\partial\mathcal{C}_{image}
be the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{image}
. Then
\chi
realizes a bijection between
\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
and the boundary
\partial\mathcal{C}_{image}
.

We start with a technical lemma that adds some quantitative information to Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Let
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathcal{M}_{crit}
. Then, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b>0
such that:

\forall\,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\ X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq a+b||\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})||_{2}.

Proof.

Denote by
(e_{1},\ldots,e_{K})
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. By Proposition
5.2
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all
i\in[K]
, there exists
(\lambda_{i}^{\pm},\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{i}^{\pm})\in(0,+\infty)\times\mathcal{M}_{crit}
such that
\lambda_{i}^{\pm}X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{i}^{\pm}}=X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\pm\sqrt{K}\varepsilon e_{i}
.
In particular the Euclidean ball
B_{\varepsilon}(X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})
is included in the convex hull of
\{\lambda_{i}^{\pm}X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{i}^{\pm}}\}
, and hence any
Y\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}})
is a linear combination of them with nonnegative coefficients. Combining it with Lemma
4.3
(ii), we get, letting
A:=\min\{\lambda_{i}^{\pm}X^{\intercal}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{i}^{\pm}}\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{i}^{\pm})\}
:

\forall Y\in B_{\varepsilon}(X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}),\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\ Y^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq A.

By taking
Y=X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}-\varepsilon\frac{\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{||\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})||_{2}}
we get:

\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\ X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq A+\varepsilon||\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})||_{2}.

∎

We now show that, if
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is on the boundary of

, then

is critical.

Lemma 5.8
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Fix
\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\ell,K}^{*}(\mathbb{Z})
and
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
\mathbf{0}\not\in\mathcal{D}_{asy}^{\Gamma,\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
. Fix

such that
\Gamma X\not=\mathbf{0}
. Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and assume that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
maximizes
f_{X}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto-X^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
on the set
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
is critical and there exists
\lambda>0
such that
\Gamma X=\lambda\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
.

In the specific case where
\Gamma=I_{K}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
is critical and its asymptotic direction is
X
(up to a multiplicative constant).

Proof.

Assume that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
is subcritical but not critical. The Jacobian of
\chi
at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
is
M_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}-I_{K}
, which is invertible. Hence,
\chi
is locally invertible around
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
. Now, let
Y
be such that
Y^{\intercal}\Gamma X>0
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})=\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\varepsilon\Gamma^{\intercal}Y
. This means that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
and
f_{X}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})=f_{X}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\varepsilon X^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}Y<f_{X}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
cannot be a maximizer of
f_{X}
on the set
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim_{\Gamma}\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\}
.

Now, assume that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
is supercritical but not critical. By Lemma
4.5
, there exists a subcritical (but not critical)
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}
such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime})=\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})
and in particular
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}
. By the previous argument,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime\prime}
cannot be a maximizer of
f_{X}
, so
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
cannot either.
In the end, necessarily
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
is critical.

Now let
X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
be the asymptotic direction of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
5.2
, for any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, there exists
\lambda>0
and
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}\sim_{\Gamma}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}
is critical and
\lambda\Gamma X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}=\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon\Gamma X
.
Assume that
\Gamma X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}
and
\Gamma X
are not collinear. Then
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\neq\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}
.
Observe now that, by Lemma
4.3
(ii):

X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime})<X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})\text{ and }X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})<X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}).

(15)

By definition of
\Gamma
-equivalence, there exists a vector
R\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})-\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime})=\Gamma^{\intercal}R
. This means that we have:

\lambda X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}(\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}))=\lambda X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R=\big(X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon X\big)^{\intercal}\Gamma^{\intercal}R=\big(X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}}+\varepsilon X\big)^{\intercal}(\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})).

(16)

Furthermore, by assumption

X^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})\leq X^{\intercal}\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}),

(17)

so by using the second inequality of
15
and the inequality
17
in Equation

we get:

\lambda X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime}}^{\intercal}(\chi(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\prime})-\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}))>0.

which is incompatible with the first inequality of Equation

. We thus get the desired result.

Assume now that
\Gamma=I_{K}
. Then, necessarily
\mathbf{0}\notin\mathcal{D}_{asy}^{I_{K},\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}=\mathcal{D}_{asy}
by definition of the asymptotic direction. We therefore get the desired result.
∎

The next lemma provides an affine bound to the function
\chi
. It will be used to show that, for
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, if
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and

are close to each other then
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}
cannot be too far apart.

Lemma 5.9
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, nonlocalized and irreducible. Let
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
be such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
is critical. Then, there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
and

such that:

\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K},\ \ X^{\intercal}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\,\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq a+b||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}||_{2}.

Proof.

Let

b:=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in S^{K-1}}\left(f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right),

where
S^{K-1}
is the unit sphere for
||\cdot||_{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
As
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is the unique global maximizer of
f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
,
f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}
is continuous and
S^{K-1}
is compact, we get that
b>0
.
For any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that
||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}\leq 1
, we have:

f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-b(||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}-1).

On the other hand, by Lemma
4.3
(i), the function
f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mapsto-X^{\intercal}_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is strictly concave. Thus, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that
||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}>1
, we get:

\displaystyle||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}^{-1}f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+(1-||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}^{-1})f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}\left(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right)\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-b,

which implies

\displaystyle f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-b||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}\leq f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-b(||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}||_{2}-1).

Setting
a:=-f_{X_{\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}}(\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})-b
, we get the result.
∎

We now turn to the proof of Proposition

.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

We first show that the set
\mathcal{C}_{image}
is closed. Let
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence such that
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})
converges to some

. Fix also
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}\in\mathcal{M}_{\text{crit}}
. By Lemma

, there exist constants
a,b
with

, depending only on

, such that:

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})\geq a+b||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}||_{2}.

(18)

Since the sequence
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})
converges, it is bounded. Hence, (
18
) implies that the sequence
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is also bounded. We can therefore extract a subsequence that converges to some limit
\overline{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
. By continuity of the function
\chi
, we have

and therefore the set

is closed.

We now prove that the set
\mathcal{C}_{image}
is convex. Define the set
\mathcal{C}^{-}
as the image by

of the set of subcritical (but not critical) parameters

:

\mathcal{C}^{-}:=\{\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\ \rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}<1\}.

We first show that
\mathcal{C}^{-}
is convex. To this end, let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
such that
\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}},\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}}<1
. We define the set
E
as follows:

E:=\{s\in[0,1]\ |\ \exists{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\text{ with }\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}<1\text{ such that }\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=s\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})+(1-s)\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2})\}.

Our goal is to show that

. By definition,
\{0,1\}\subset E
. We will now show that
E
is both open and closed in
[0,1]
, which will imply the desired result. First,
E
is open in

by the implicit function theorem, since the Jacobian of
\chi
at any

\rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}<1
. To show that

is closed, we use Lemma

. Set

\forall i\in\{1,2\},\ m_{i}:=X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}).

In particular, for any

X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=sm_{1}+(1-s)m_{2}.

By Lemma
5.9
, there exist constants
a,b
with

depending only on

such that:

sm_{1}+(1-s)m_{2}=X_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}}^{\intercal}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geq a+b||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}||_{2}.

This implies that:

||{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{c}||_{2}\leq\frac{\max(m_{1},m_{2})-a}{b}.

(19)

Now, fix
s\in(0,1)
and assume that there exist two sequences
({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
subcritical for all

, such that

\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ s_{n}\in[0,1]\text{ and }\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})=s_{n}\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1})+(1-s_{n})\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{2}),

. By (

and its eigenvalues. By continuity of

We now define the two sets

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{+}:=

\displaystyle\{\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\ \rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}>1\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{1}:=

\displaystyle\{\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\ \rho_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=1\}.

for the closure of

. Indeed by Proposition
4.8
,

is a differentiable manifold of dimension

. Hence, for any

.

Proof of Proposition
5.6
.

First, by Proposition

, the set

6
Proof of the main result

It turns out that, when

, by Proposition

. However, we have proved (see Corollary

The goal of this section is to characterize which directions are possible asymptotic directions of a critical
\Gamma
-equivalent tilting of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and for which parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. This will help us prove Theorem
3.7
.

Let us first define the notion of accessible (asymptotic) direction.

Definition 6.1
.

Fix a projection
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and let
I\subseteq[K]
be the set of types that have no child under
\boldsymbol{\mu}
with positive probability (that is,
I=\{i\in[K],\mu^{(i)}(\mathbf{0})>0\}
). Let
X\in[0,+\infty)^{K}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
. We say that the direction
X
is:

•

accessible
if there exists a tree
T
such that

–

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\mathcal{T}=T)>0
;

–

T
has at least
2
vertices;

–

the root of

has type
i\in I
;

–

at least one of its leaves has the same type
i
as the root;

–

the vectors
\tilde{\mathbf{N}}(T):=(\tilde{N}_{1}(T),\dots,\tilde{N}_{K}(T))^{\intercal}
and
X
are collinear, where
\tilde{N}_{j}(T)
is the number of nodes of type
j
in
T
(counting the leaves but excluding the root).

•

strongly accessible
if it is in the interior of the convex hull of the accessible directions.

We define
\mathcal{D}_{sad}
as the set of strongly accessible directions (up to a positive multiplicative constant).

The set of strongly accessible directions turns out to be exactly the set of asymptotic directions (up to a multiplicative constant).

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is entire, finite, nondegenerate, nonlocalized and irreducible. Then, the set

is both open and convex and

After stating some preliminary results in Section

6.1

Lemma 6.5

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(1)}]=\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(j)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}.

Proof.

Observe that we have the following relations:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(j^{\prime})}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{j^{\prime}=j}+\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{K}\geq 0}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(j^{\prime})}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})\sum_{i=2}^{K}\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(i)}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{j^{\prime}=j}+\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{K}\geq 0}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(j^{\prime})}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})\sum_{i=1}^{K}k_{i}\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(i)}]-\sum_{k_{1},\ldots,k_{K}\geq 0}\mu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(j^{\prime})}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{K})k_{1}\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(1)}]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{j^{\prime}=j}+\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(i)}]M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(j^{\prime},i)-\mathbb{E}[N_{j}^{(1)}]M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(j^{\prime},1).

\displaystyle P=I_{K}+PM_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}-P^{(1)}M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal,(1)}.

Multiplying to the right by

, we get

\displaystyle PX_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+P^{(1)}M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal,(1)}X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle=X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+PM_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle PX_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)P^{(1)}

\displaystyle=X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+PX_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\displaystyle P^{(1)}=\frac{1}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}}X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}.

The result follows.
∎

We can now prove Proposition

Proof of Proposition

.

In this proof, for convenience, we write

for

. We know by e.g.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},(1)})=N_{n}\right)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}cN_{n}^{-3/2}.

In the rest of the proof, we let

. Consider a sequence of i.i.d.

of random variables distributed as
(N_{j}^{(1)})_{1\leq j\leq K}
and fix

. Using the cycle lemma (see e.g.
[

, Lemma 15.3]
), we have that, for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(N_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{(1)})=N,N_{i}(\mathcal{T}^{(1)})=\ell\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}\xi_{1}^{(p)}=N-1,\sum_{p=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{(p)}=\ell\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{(p)}=\ell\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(\xi_{i}^{(p)}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{(p)}]\right)=\ell-N\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}\right)\text{(by Lemma \ref{lem:expectationinblob})}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(\xi_{i}^{(p)}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{(p)}]\right)\Big|\geq\Big|\ell-N\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}\Big|\right).

\displaystyle\sum_{\big|\ell-N\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}\big|\geq\varepsilon N}\mathbb{P}\left(\Big|\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left(\xi_{i}^{(p)}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}^{(p)}]\right)\Big|\geq\Big|\ell-N\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}\Big|\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq N\left(\frac{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(i)}{X_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1)}-\varepsilon\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2D}N\right)+\sum_{a\geq 0}\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2D}N-\frac{\varepsilon}{2D}a\right)

(21)

for some

7.1
Multitype Kesten trees

We construct here the infinite discrete trees that appear as local limits of critical multitype BGW trees. It turns out that they all share a common structure: a unique end (infinite spine), on which are grafted independent multitype trees that are identically distributed conditionally on the type of their root. In regard of Kesten’s seminal work
[
12
]
, we will call these trees multitype Kesten trees. This multitype construction was first introduced in
[
15
]
, see also
[
21
, Proposition
3.1
]
for a proof in the broader case of mutitype forests.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
be a
K
-type critical distribution, and recall that
b
denotes the renormalized right
1
-eigenvector of the mean matrix
M
. Denote by
\hat{\zeta}
the biased family of distributions defined as:

\displaystyle\forall j\in[K],\forall\textbf{x}\in\mathcal{W}_{K},\hat{\zeta}^{(j)}(\textbf{x})=\frac{1}{b_{j}}\sum_{k=1}^{|\textbf{x}|}b_{x_{k}}\zeta^{(j)}(\textbf{x}),

where
|\textbf{x}|
denotes the length of
x
. In particular,
\hat{\zeta}^{(j)}(\varnothing)=0
.
Given a type
i\in[K]
, we define the tree
\mathcal{T}_{*}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
as follows: it is made of a spine, which is an infinite branch starting from the root. On this infinite branch, vertices have distribution
\hat{\zeta}
. Given an element
v
of the spine, denote by
\textbf{w}_{v}
its ordered list of offspring types. Then, the probability that the child of
v
belonging to the infinite spine is
vj
(that is, the
j
-th of its children) is proportional to
b_{\ell(vj)}
- that is, equal to

\displaystyle\frac{b_{\ell(vj)}}{\sum_{i=1}^{|\textbf{w}_{v}|}b_{\ell(vi)}},

where we recall that
\ell(u)
is the type of the vertex
u
.

Finally, for all
i\in[K]
, on any offspring of type
i
of a vertex of the spine that is not itself on the spine, we graft a tree
\mathcal{T}^{(i)}
with root of type
i
, which is independent of all the rest of the tree.

In the monotype case, the child of a vertex on the spine that will be itself on the spine is just chosen uniformly at random. Observe also that, since
\hat{\zeta}^{(j)}(\varnothing)=0
for all
j\in[K]
, the spine is indeed infinite.

has positive coordinates for

Appendix: a counter-example when
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is not entire

We construct here an example of a supercritical probability measure that does not admit a critical equivalent. No such example exists in the monotype case, showing that the multitype case is more intricate.

The idea is as follows: start from an entire supercritical distribution, and add a small well-chosen non-entire perturbation. It turns out that one can do it without changing too much the functions
\phi^{(i)}
within the domain of convergence. By doing this, we can make some critical tiltings ”leave” the domain of convergence. We consider the following function, defined on
(-\infty,0)
:

g(\theta):=\sum_{n\geq 3}\frac{2}{n(n-1)(n-2)}e^{n\theta}

It has the following properties.

Lemma 8.1
.

We have, for any
\theta<0
:

g(\theta)=-\left(e^{\theta}-1\right)^{2}\log\left(1-e^{\theta}\right)+e^{\theta}\left(\frac{3}{2}e^{\theta}-1\right).

In addition, for all
\theta\leq 0
, we have
0\leq g(\theta),g^{\prime}(\theta)\leq 2
and

\displaystyle g(\theta),g^{\prime}(\theta)\underset{\theta\rightarrow-\infty}{\rightarrow}0.

Proof.

It suffices to observe that, for all
n\geq 3
,

\frac{2}{n(n-1)(n-2)}=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{2}{n-1}+\frac{1}{n-2},

and check that

g^{\prime}(\theta)=-2e^{\theta}(e^{\theta}-1)\log(1-e^{\theta})+2e^{2\theta}.

∎

We now consider, for any
\varepsilon,A>0
, the
2
-type tree whose two offspring distributions have generating functions:

\begin{cases}\phi^{(1)}(e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=&\frac{1}{2+2e^{-2A}+\varepsilon g(-1)}\left(1+2e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+e^{2\theta_{2}}+\varepsilon g(\theta_{1}-1)\right)\\
\phi^{(2)}(e^{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=&\frac{1}{2+e^{-2A}}\left(1+e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+e^{2\theta_{2}}\right)\\
\end{cases}

Denote by
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{A,\varepsilon}
the associated projection of offspring distributions, and by
\chi^{A,\varepsilon}
the function associated to
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{A,\varepsilon}
by Definition
3.3
.

Proposition 8.2
.

For a good choice of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}},A,\varepsilon
and for
\Gamma:=(1\ 1)
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{A,\varepsilon}
is supercritical, but has no critical
\Gamma
-equivalent tilting.

In order to prove this, we start with a first lemma.

Lemma 8.3
.

For any
c_{1},C_{2}>0
, there exist
A_{0},\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for any
A>A_{0}
, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for which
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{A,\varepsilon}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is critical, we have
\theta_{1}-A<-C_{2}
and
|\theta_{2}|<c_{1}
.

Proof.

Fix
A,\varepsilon>0
. For any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
critical,
1
is an eigenvalue of
M_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and we have:

\left(\frac{4e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+\varepsilon g^{\prime}(\theta_{1}-1)}{\gamma^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}-1\right)\left(\frac{2e^{2\theta_{2}}}{\gamma^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}-1\right)-\frac{2e^{2\theta_{2}}}{\gamma^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}\frac{2e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}}{\gamma^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}=0,

where

\begin{aligned} \gamma^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=&1+2e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+e^{2\theta_{2}}+\varepsilon g(\theta_{1}-1),\\
\gamma^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=&1+e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+e^{2\theta_{2}}.\end{aligned}`

This can be rewritten as:

\left(4e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)}+\varepsilon g^{\prime}(\theta_{1}-1)-\gamma^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)\left(2e^{2\theta_{2}}-\gamma^{(2)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right)=4e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)+2\theta_{2}}.

Using the notation
X=e^{2(\theta_{1}-A)},Y=e^{2\theta_{2}}
and
\delta=g(\theta_{1}-1)-g^{\prime}(\theta_{1}-1)
we get:

\left(2X-1-\varepsilon\delta-Y\right)\left(Y-1-X\right)=4XY.

This can be rewritten as:

Y^{2}+(X+\varepsilon\delta)Y-(1+X)(1-2X+\varepsilon\delta)=0.

(27)

This is equivalent to:

\bigg(Y+\frac{X+\varepsilon\delta}{2}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{(X+\varepsilon\delta)^{2}}{4}+(1+X)(1-2X+\varepsilon\delta)

(28)

Observe that the radius of convergence of
g\circ\log
is
1
. Hence, if
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
is critical, we have
\theta_{1}<1
and in particular, if
A
is large enough so that
A\geq C_{2}+1
, we have
\theta_{1}-A\leq-C_{2}
. Using the fact that
g,g^{\prime}
are bounded on
\mathbb{R}_{-}
(Lemma
8.1
), we get that
\delta
is bounded. We have that
X\leq e^{-2(A-1)}
. For
X,\varepsilon>0
small enough, (
28
) has one positive solution
Y(X,\varepsilon)
and, as
X,\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,
Y(X,\varepsilon)\rightarrow 1
. This means that by taking
A
large enough and
\varepsilon
small enough we can ensure that any positive
Y
that satisfies (
28
) also satisfies
|\log(Y)|<c_{1}
. The result follows.
∎

Let us show how it implies Proposition
8.2
.

Proof of Proposition
8.2
.

Observe that, for any choice of
A,\varepsilon
, for any
s
large enough,
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}
are well-defined at
(-s,s)
. The constants
A
and
\varepsilon
being fixed, as

goes to
+\infty
, we can compute the following asymptotics:

\chi^{A,\varepsilon}(-s,s)=(3s,s)+O_{s}(1).

Using this along with Lemma

, for any
A>0
large enough, any
\varepsilon>0
small enough, there exists a constant
C:=C(A,\varepsilon)>0
such that we have:

(a)

||\chi^{A,\varepsilon}(-s,s)-(3s,s)||_{1}\leq C
,

(b)

if
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{A,\varepsilon}
is critical then
\theta_{1}-A\leq 0
and
||\chi^{A,\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-(-\theta_{1},0)||_{1}\leq C
.

Now fix
A

\varepsilon>0

such that (a) and (b) hold, and take

and assume that there exists

It follows from (a) and (b) that

(by definition of

To conclude, observe that, for

large enough, the measure is supercritical. Indeed, by definition, a node of type

has two children of type

as

.

Appendix: number of pre-images for
\chi

We know by Proposition
5.6
that
\chi
induces a bijection between
\mathcal{M}_{crit}
and the boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{image}
.
We also know by Lemma
4.5
that an element in the interior of
\mathcal{C}_{image}
may have multiple pre-images by
\chi
. It turns out that there is actually no upper bound to the number of pre-images of an element by
\chi
.

To show this we consider the following example for some
N\geq 3
:

•

There are
K:=2N
types,

•

a vertex of type
i\in[2N]
has no child with probability
1/4
, 2 children of type
i
with probability
1/2
or 1 child of each type (so,
2N
children in total) with probability
1/4
.

In this setting we have for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N}
and for all
i\in[2N]
:

\phi^{(i)}(e^{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1+e^{\sum_{k}\theta_{k}}+2e^{2\theta_{i}}}{4}.

(29)

The reason we look at this case is that the Jacobian of
\chi
at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}
is equal to the matrix with
1/4
on every entry. This means that it should be far from locally invertible and we can hope that some values have many pre-images by
\chi
.

To simplify notations, write
\gamma:=e^{\sum_{k}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{k}}
. We want to find values of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
for which
\chi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\mathbf{0}
.

To this end, let
\delta
be a solution in
(0,1)
of

\displaystyle\delta=\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)^{N}.

Let
I
be a set of
N
elements of
[2N]
, and let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{2N})
be such that

e^{\theta_{i}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}&\mbox{if }i\in I\\
1-\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

In this case, we get

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{k}\theta_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}\right)^{N}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{1-\delta}{2}}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+\delta}{2}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle=\delta

In addition, for all
i\in[2N]
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e^{2\theta_{i}}-e^{\theta_{i}}+\frac{1+\delta}{4}=0.

This can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\forall i\in[2N],\frac{1+e^{\sum_{k}\theta_{k}}+2e^{2\theta_{i}}}{4}=e^{\theta_{i}}.

We get from that:
