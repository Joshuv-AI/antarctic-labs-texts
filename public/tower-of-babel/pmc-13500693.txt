A novel method for estimating the atmospheric turbulence vertical structure using coherent doppler wind lidar data from cone scanning
Authors: Yalin Hu, Chenbo Xie, Hui Zhou, Hao Yang, Yingjian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A novel methodology has been developed in this study for retrieving turbulence parameters in the atmospheric boundary layer using conical scanning data from a Coherent Doppler wind Lidar (CDL). The vertical distribution of turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rates (\varepsilon) is estimated based on the Azimuth Structure Function (ASF) approach. These dissipation rates are subsequently applied to spectral analysis of the three-dimensional wind field, where the frequency ranges for both spectral fitting and integration are dynamically adjusted to enhance the accuracy of power-law exponent and turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) estimations. Validation experiments were conducted through comparisons with measurements obtained from ultrasonic anemometers. The results demonstrate strong consistency in the structural variation trends of \varepsilon derived from both methods. Additionally, the distribution of power-law exponent fitting residuals (ranging from 0.4 to 1.2) and the sensitivity of TKE estimations to frequency adjustments (ranging from 5 to 24%) were analyzed across different altitudes for the three wind components (U, V, and W). Moreover, continuous turbulence observation experiments under varying weather conditions revealed distinct spatiotemporal patterns of directional TKE and their corresponding responses to variations in the power-law exponent. This study offers a methodological advancement that improves the characterization of atmospheric turbulence in the boundary layer.

Introduction

Atmospheric turbulence is one of the fundamental forms of atmospheric motion, playing a crucial role in governing the transport of energy and matter through its dynamic processes 1,2. Accurate detection of atmospheric turbulence characteristics, particularly their vertical structure, is essential for applications in wind energy development, meteorological forecasting, and global climate change research 3,4. The turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rate (\varepsilon) serves as a key parameter in characterizing turbulence, as it dictates the cascade process in which turbulent energy is transferred from large-scale inhomogeneities to smaller scales within the inertial subrange, following Kolmogorov’s theory 5. Moreover, the power-law exponent and turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) provide valuable insights into the energy distribution and dynamic properties of turbulence. The power-law exponent describes the energy transfer across different scales, while TKE quantifies the conversion of energy into heat at the microscale 6. Accurate measurement of these parameters is important for understanding turbulence variability and improving wind energy applications such as wind farm siting and power forecasting. However, the strong spatial and temporal inhomogeneity of turbulence poses significant challenges for its continuous and precise measurement. Coherent Doppler wind Lidar (CDL) has emerged as a promising tool for obtaining reliable atmospheric turbulence estimates across the entire atmospheric boundary layer 7,8. With its high spatial and temporal resolution, real-time capabilities, long-range measurement potential, and adaptability to complex atmospheric conditions, CDL enables routine and continuous detection of turbulence across the full vertical extent of the boundary layer. These advantages make it a powerful instrument for investigating boundary layer wind fields and retrieving turbulence characteristics 9,10.

In previous studies, researchers have focused on developing remote sensing methods, particularly Lidar-based approaches, for estimating the \varepsilon 11–13. These methods can generally be categorized into three main types: the Doppler spectrum width method, the time spectrum method, and the structure–function method 14. Among them, the structure–function method provides quantitative information on turbulence intensity and is well-suited for stable atmospheric stratification. However, its accuracy and applicability are limited under unstable conditions or in non-homogeneous turbulence fields. I. N. Smalikho 15 estimated the dissipation rate within the inertial subrange of atmospheric turbulence using the azimuthal structure function of radial velocity measured by CDL. His findings demonstrated a high degree of consistency between the dissipation rate estimated from Lidar data and the values obtained simultaneously at the same altitude using sonic anemometers. However, no comprehensive analysis has yet been conducted to evaluate the errors associated with this method under various atmospheric conditions. These methods rely on Kolmogorov’s local isotropy assumption and the well-known -5/3 power law, making them inherently indirect and thus limited in applicability. In contrast, spectral analysis provides a more direct approach to retrieving atmospheric turbulence parameters. This method estimates the dissipation rate by analyzing high-frequency turbulence velocity measurements, typically involving the transformation of velocity spectra into the frequency domain 16. Turbulent kinetic energy is initially contained in the larger energy-containing motions and is transferred across scales through the inertial cascade toward progressively smaller eddies. At sufficiently small scales, viscous dissipation becomes dominant and converts kinetic energy into heat. The characteristic sizes of these scales are flow-dependent and vary with Reynolds number, atmospheric stability, shear conditions, and boundary-layer depth. Under homogeneous and isotropic turbulence conditions, Kolmogorov’s hypothesis provides a statistical representation of the turbulence energy spectrum within the inertial subrange 5. By invoking Taylor’s frozen turbulence hypothesis, the spectrum can be linked to length scales 17. The portion of the spectrum within the inertial subrange can be fitted to a -5/3 power law, enabling the estimation of the dissipation rate 18.Using wind Lidar measurements, the spectrum of the three-dimensional wind field can be obtained after Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) processing, which allows the estimation of TKE through spectral integration over the selected inertial-subrange frequency interval and enables the determination of the power-law exponent, thereby making spectral analysis applicable to diverse atmospheric conditions. Xian et al. 19 validated the accuracy and applicability of this approach. Recent advances in atmospheric turbulence measurements have increasingly focused on resolving fine-scale turbulence structures and spectral characteristics using in-situ instrumentation. For example, Winiarska et al. 20 investigated turbulent kinetic energy spectra and momentum transport across heterogeneous canopy-edge flows using high-resolution constant-temperature anemometry. Goldshmid et al. 21 developed a next-generation combined sonic–hot-film anemometer with deep-learning-based automated calibration, enabling continuous field measurements of fine-scale turbulent fluctuations. In addition, Freire et al. 22 analyzed atmospheric small-scale turbulence in the boundary layer using three-dimensional hot-film data and emphasized the importance of calibration and spectral consistency in high-frequency measurements. These studies provide important advances in local turbulence spectral measurements and help contextualize the present Lidar-based approach within the broader framework of atmospheric turbulence observation techniques. Compared with these point-based in-situ methods, the coherent Doppler wind Lidar approach adopted in this study provides continuous remote sensing observations of the vertical structure of turbulence, enabling the retrieval of altitude-dependent spectral parameters and TKE across the atmospheric boundary layer. Recent studies have further advanced Lidar-based turbulence retrieval and spectral analysis. For example, Päschke et al. 23 systematically assessed the performance of scanning Doppler Lidar systems for turbulence measurements and discussed the effects of scanning geometry, temporal resolution, and measurement uncertainty on turbulence retrieval.

Based on the above analysis, it is evident that previous studies have predominantly focused on estimating the \varepsilon at different altitudes using the structure function method. This approach assumes atmospheric turbulence is isotropic, making it an indirect method with limited applicability, as it cannot directly obtain the power-law exponent. In contrast, spectral analysis methods can directly determine both the power-law exponent and TKE, making them more suitable for different atmospheric conditions. Traditional spectral methods are based on Kolmogorov’s turbulence theory, which assumes that within the inertial subrange, the TKE spectrum follows a power-law distribution. However, in real-world observations, variations in atmospheric conditions lead to differences in the vertical distribution of turbulence intensity, causing the extent of the inertial subrange to be non-uniform. In particular, the Kolmogorov frequency is influenced by both the \varepsilon and air viscosity. If the selected fitting frequency range is inappropriate, it may result in either an overextension into the dissipation range or the omission of the valid inertial subrange, thereby compromising the accuracy of the analysis.

To address this issue, this study proposes an improved method for retrieving atmospheric turbulence characteristics based on wind field data obtained from conical scanning. In Section "Methodology", we introduce this novel approach, which utilizes Kolmogorov’s theory and the azimuth structure function (ASF) to calculate the turbulence dissipation rate at various altitudes. Additionally, a dynamic adjustment strategy for the fitting frequency range is proposed based on altitude-dependent variations in the dissipation rate. This ensures that the selected fitting range remains within the inertial subrange, preventing contamination from the dissipative region and thereby enhancing the accuracy of spectral analysis. To validate this method, a series of comparative experiments are conducted. First, the turbulence dissipation rate estimated using this approach is compared with measurements obtained from ultrasonic anemometers to verify consistency. Second, the results from the proposed method are compared against those derived from conventional spectral analysis without frequency range adjustments, demonstrating the improved capability of the new approach in resolving finer turbulence structures. Finally, long-term observations under various atmospheric conditions are analyzed to examine the vertical characteristics of atmospheric turbulence derived from CDL data. The results confirm that the proposed method enables continuous retrieval of TKE variations in all three wind components (U, V, and W), with corresponding variations in the power-law exponent. This provides strong evidence for the reliability and applicability of the method, as discussed in Section "Results and discussion". The key conclusions of this study are summarized in Section "Conclusions". The proposed method is adaptable to different turbulence conditions, allowing for dynamic matching of turbulence characteristics across varying altitudes. This improves the estimation accuracy of both TKE and the power-law exponent, enabling continuous and reliable detection of the vertical structure of atmospheric turbulence. The findings of this study not only enhance the accuracy of wind energy resource assessment and optimize wind farm site selection strategies but also strengthen meteorological models’ ability to describe boundary-layer turbulence processes. Consequently, this research provides a novel technological approach to improving the efficiency of renewable energy utilization and advancing environmental monitoring.

Consequently, this assumption limits the method’s ability to accurately describe the actual evolution of turbulence. In conventional power spectrum fitting for deriving the power-law exponent and integrating to obtain TKE, a fixed frequency range is typically selected. Within the inertial subrange, turbulence characteristics generally follow Kolmogorov’s theory, where the power spectral density exhibits a power-law distribution 26. However, due to the significant variation in \varepsilon with altitude, the extent of the inertial subrange also changes accordingly. Since turbulence characteristics at different altitudes correspond to different frequency ranges, using a fixed frequency range fails to accurately capture turbulence properties, thereby affecting the estimation of turbulence parameters. To address this limitation, this study proposes a method for dynamically adjusting the frequency range, enhancing the accuracy of spectral fitting and integration by incorporating altitude-dependent turbulence characteristics. This method dynamically adjusts the frequency range to accommodate turbulence characteristics at different altitudes. In regions with higher turbulence dissipation rates, the frequency range is expanded to enhance coverage of the inertial subrange. Within the dynamically adjusted frequency range, turbulence spectra are fitted and integrated, where the extent of coverage over the inertial subrange directly influences the estimation accuracy. The adaptive frequency range is determined from the turbulence dissipation rate at each height through the Kolmogorov scale and is then applied in the spectral fitting and integration process to ensure that the selected frequency interval corresponds to the inertial subrange. Therefore, by dynamically adapting the frequency range, this method improves sensitivity to turbulence variations at different altitudes, ensuring more precise spectral fitting and integration results.

Methods

The detection method and parameter retrieval process for wind field measurements using a scanning Lidar system are illustrated in Fig. 2. When analyzing the radial wind speed array obtained from conical scanning, employing a continuous conical scanning approach in combination with the Von Kármán model from atmospheric turbulence theory can introduce measurement inaccuracies. Specifically, errors in radial wind speed estimation and computational uncertainties associated with noise components may lead to increased measurement errors in the estimation of turbulence dissipation rates 27. Therefore, in the method of inverting the turbulent energy dissipation rate based on Lidar wind speed data, we use a discontinuous cone scanning method, with a complete cone scanning time of about 5 min, an accumulation time of the Lidar raw data of ∆t = 1 s, a number of rays of a complete scanning of M = 120, and an azimuthal resolution of ∆θ = 3°. Conclusions

This study proposes a novel method for estimating atmospheric boundary layer turbulence parameters using radial wind velocity arrays obtained from conical scanning with CDL. Comparative validation experiments are conducted, and the method is applied for continuous detection of the vertical structure of atmospheric boundary layer turbulence under varying meteorological conditions. The key conclusions are summarized as follows:

The radial velocity array measured by CDL is utilized to estimate the \varepsilon at different altitudes using the azimuth structure function method, in combination with spectral analysis. The frequency range is dynamically adjusted to adequately cover the inertial subrange while minimizing the inclusion of the dissipative subrange, thereby ensuring adaptability to varying turbulence characteristics at different heights. A series of comparative experiments are conducted to validate this approach. First, the \varepsilon obtained using this method is compared with measurements from ultrasonic anemometers, showing a consistent trend over time. The results effectively capture temporal variations in turbulence intensity, providing a solid basis for the subsequent dynamic frequency adjustment. Second, the sensitivity of the power-law exponent fitting residuals and TKE adjustments is analyzed across three velocity components (U, V, and W) at different altitudes. The residual analysis indicates that for the U component, residuals increase beyond 1.0 below 300 m but remain relatively stable (< 0.8) within the 500–700 m range. The V component exhibits greater residual fluctuations between 100 and 500 m (peaking at 1.2) but stabilizes above 600 m (< 0.6). The W component shows an increase in residuals up to 1.2 at 700–800 m, while at other altitudes, residuals range between 0.4 and 0.8. After adjusting the frequency range for each direction, the fitting performance varies slightly with altitude but maintains relatively low residuals across all cases. The sensitivity analysis of integrated TKE adjustments reveals that the relative differences in the U component reach up to 24% in the lower layers and over 10% in the upper layers, while the V component remains within 5%–15%, and the W component varies between 5%–10% at most altitudes, with only localized low-altitude regions exceeding 20%. The observed sensitivity variations at different altitudes confirm the effectiveness of frequency range adjustments for \varepsilon estimation. Finally, the proposed method is applied for continuous atmospheric turbulence detection under different weather conditions. The temporal and spatial variations of TKE in different directions are observed and found to correspond with variations in the power-law exponent. The refined depiction of \varepsilon variations across turbulence fields of varying intensity further validates the applicability and stability of this dynamic adjustment approach.

This study introduces a novel approach for the precise estimation of atmospheric turbulence, offering a dynamic adjustment method for \varepsilon that adapts to the spectral characteristics of turbulence at different altitudes. This method provides a reliable analytical framework for investigating complex atmospheric turbulence, enabling the effective capture of the vertical structure and spatiotemporal variations of boundary-layer turbulence. The findings contribute to improving meteorological models, optimizing renewable energy assessments, and enhancing the accuracy of climate change predictions. Long-term, continuous observations of the vertical structure of atmospheric turbulence under various weather conditions, combined with other atmospheric parameters, can further reveal the energy transfer and dissipation mechanisms of turbulence across different atmospheric environments. This will be a key focus for future research.
