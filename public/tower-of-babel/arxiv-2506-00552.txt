Drawdowns, Drawups, and Occupation Times under General Markov Models
Pingping Zeng, Gongqiu Zhang, Weinan Zhang

Abstract
Drawdown risk, an important metric in financial risk management, poses significant computational challenges due to its highly path-dependent nature. This paper proposes a unified framework for computing five important drawdown quantities introduced in Landriault et al. 2015 and Zhang 2015 under general Markov models. We first establish linear systems and develop efficient algorithms for such problems under continuous-time Markov chains (CTMCs), and then establish their theoretical convergence to target quantities under general Markov models. Notably, the proposed algorithms for most quantities achieve the same complexity order as those for path-independent problems: cubic in the number of CTMC states for general Markov models and linear when applied to diffusion models. Rigorous convergence analysis is conducted under weak regularity conditions, and extensive numerical experiments validate the accuracy and efficiency of the proposed algorithms.



Keywords
: Continuous-time Markov chain approximation; Drawdowns; Drawups; Occupation times; Markov models

Introduction

Drawdown, defined as the magnitude of decline of the asset price from its historical peak, has emerged as a widely adopted downside risk metric in financial markets. Its prominence is evidenced by growing scholarly and industrial interest, particularly in applications such as performance evaluation of investment funds and algorithmic trading strategies, where maximum drawdown is routinely reported as a key robustness indicator. Notably, empirical studies increasingly associate irrational trading behaviors, including panic selling and momentum overreaction, with exposure to extreme drawdown events.

Various drawdown quantities have been systematically examined in the academic literature, including the first passage time of drawdown processes

;

;

)
, along with drawdown-linked insurance products and derivatives

;

Among the extensive literature on drawdown and related derivatives, seminal works by

These drawdown quantities are especially useful in financial engineering applications and of theoretical importance in stochastic modeling. However, these foundational studies predominantly assume a diffusion process or even a Brownian motion, as the underlying model, fail to capture the empirical reality of financial markets where discontinuous price movements (jumps) frequently occur. To address this limitation and enable robust evaluation of these drawdown quantities under general Markov models, we develop a novel computational framework based on the continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) approximation.

For a stochastic process
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, the drawdown process
\{D_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and drawup process
\{\widehat{D}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
can be formally defined as

D_{t}=\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}X_{s}-X_{t}~~\text{and}~~\widehat{D}_{t}=X_{t}-\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}X_{s},\qquad t\geq 0,

respectively. The drawdown time is then defined as the first passage time of the drawdown process

\tau^{X}_{a}=\inf\{t\geq 0:D_{t}\geq a\}

\widehat{\tau}^{X}_{b}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\widehat{D}_{t}\geq b\}

n
-th drawdown time without recovery; (5) The
n
-th drawdown time with recovery. More specifically, we first approximate the Markov process
X
by a CTMC and then price the target quantities under the CTMC. The CTMC approximation method has been extensively used for derivative pricing under Markov models. Notable applications include European and barrier options
(
Mijatović and Pistorius 2013
)
, American options
(
Eriksson and Pistorius 2015
)

(

(
Zhang and Li 2023a
)

(
Zhang and Li 2023b
)

(
Cui et al. 2024
)

(
Zhang et al. 2021

Zhang and Li 2023c
;
Li et al. 2024
)

Working under the CTMC model, we can establish rigorous characterizations of these five target quantities through dedicated linear system formulations and develop efficient recursive procedures to reduce the complexity of solving these linear systems. For most drawdown quantities, our methodology achieves computational complexity scaling as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
with
N
CTMC states under general Markov models, while attaining complexity
\mathcal{O}(N)
when applied to diffusion models, thereby achieving computational parity with path-independent valuations (e.g., European option pricing) under equivalent model configurations. Finally, we perform rigorous convergence analysis under weak regularity conditions, and conduct extensive numerical experiments to demonstrate the accuracy and efficiency of the proposed algorithms.

Our contributions to the literature are three-fold:

•

We have proposed efficient algorithms for evaluating five important drawdown quantities under general Markov models, overcoming the restrictive Lévy process/diffusion process/Brownian motion assumptions prevalent in existing literature (e.g.,
Mijatović and Pistorius 2012

•

We have established the convergence analysis of the proposed algorithms. The analysis adopts the techniques used in
Zhang and Li 2023c

•

We contribute to ample financial engineering applications of drawdown quantities. Specifically, we price various financial products, including digital options associated with the occupation times of the Markov/drawdown processes and insurance products associated with the frequency of relative drawdowns with/without recovery.

Preliminaries

Consider a 1D time-homogeneous Markov process
X
living on an interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with its inﬁnitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
acting on
g\in C^{2}_{c}(I)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}g(x)=b(x)g^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(x)g^{\prime\prime}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(g(x+y)-g(x)-yg^{\prime}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}\big)\,\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),

(1)

where
b(x)
is the drift,
\sigma(x)
is the diffusion coefficient, and
\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
is the state-dependent jump measure satisfying
\int_{|y|\leq 1}y^{2}\,\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)<\infty
for all
x\in I
.
The class of models characterized by (
1
) is very general as it subsumes diffusions, jump-diffusions, and pure-jump models,
and allows the jump intensity to be state-dependent with ﬁnite or inﬁnite jump activity and ﬁnite or inﬁnite jump variation.

The process
X
can be approximated by a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)
Y
with the finite state space

\mathbb{S}=\{y_{0},y_{1},\cdots,y_{N}\},

where
y_{0}<y_{1}<\cdots<y_{N}
and
y_{0}
and
y_{N}
are absorbing states.
The details regarding the construction of the CTMC can be found in Appendix
A
. Denote by
\bm{G}
the transition rate matrix of
Y
, whose elements satisfy that
G(x,y)\geq 0
for
y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}
and
G(x,x)=-\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}G(x,y)
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}
.
The infinitesimal generator of the CTMC
Y
is then formulated as:
for any function
g:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}g(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}}G(x,y)g(y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{S}.

(2)

In the following, for any
x
, define by
x^{+}=\min\{y\in\mathbb{S},~y>x\}
the right grid point next to
x
.

2.1
The First Passage Time of the CTMC

For any
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the first passage time of the CTMC
Y
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle T_{S}:=\inf\{t>0:Y_{t}\notin S\}.

(3)

We consider the following quantity:

\displaystyle P_{S}(k,x;f):=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-\int_{0}^{T_{S}}k(Y_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{S}<\infty\}}f(Y_{T_{S}})\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x\big],

(4)

where
k,f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 1
.

The quantity
P_{S}(k,x;f)
satisfies the following linear system:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(k(x)-\mathcal{G})P_{S}(k,x;f)=0,\qquad&x\in\mathbb{S}\cap S,\\
P_{S}(k,x;f)=f(x),\qquad&x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash S,\end{cases}

(5)

where
\mathcal{G}
is the infinitesimal generator of the CTMC specified by (
2
).

The proof of Lemma
1
is analogous to that of Proposition 3.1 in
Zhang and Li 2023c
and hence is omitted here. Following
Zhang and Li 2023c
, the linear system above admits a closed-form solution. More specifically, define
{\boldsymbol{P}}_{S}(k;f)=\big(P_{S}(k,x;f)\big)_{x\in\mathbb{S}},

then

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{P}}_{S}(k;f)=({\boldsymbol{I}}_{S^{c}}+{\boldsymbol{I}}_{S}\operatorname{diag}({\boldsymbol{k}})-{\boldsymbol{I}}_{S}\,{\boldsymbol{G}})^{-1}{\boldsymbol{I}}_{S^{c}}{\boldsymbol{f}}.

(6)

Here,
{\boldsymbol{f}}=(f(x))_{x\in\mathbb{S}}
,
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k(x))_{x\in\mathbb{S}}
,
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{S}=(\mathbf{1}_{\{z=x,\,x\in S\}})_{x,\,z\in\mathbb{S}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{I}}_{S^{c}}=(\mathbf{1}_{\{z=x,\,x\in S^{c}\}})_{x,\,z\in\mathbb{S}}
. It is noteworthy that the dependence of the solution on
f
is linear.

Remark 1
.

The Laplace transform of the first passage time
T_{S}
can be obtained as a special case by setting
k(x)\equiv q
and
f(x)\equiv 1
.

2.2
The First Passage Time of the Drawdown Process of the CTMC

Write
\overline{Y}_{t}=\sup_{0\leq u\leq t}Y_{u}
and denote by
\tau_{a}=\mathrm{inf}\{t\geq 0:\overline{Y}_{t}-Y_{t}\geq a\}
the first passage time of the drawdown process of the CTMC
Y
to the level
a>0
. We call
\tau_{a}
the drawdown time. Define

\displaystyle Q_{a}(q,x;f):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-q\tau_{a}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{a}<\infty\}}f(Y_{\tau_{a}})\big],

(7)

where
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
,
f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,Y_{0}=x,\,\overline{Y}_{0}=x]
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
and
x\in\mathbb{S}
, the quantity
Q_{a}(q,x;f)
satisfies the following linear system:

\displaystyle Q_{a}(q,x;f)=P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;f(\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq x-a\}})+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y>x}P_{(x-a,x]}\big(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}}\big)Q_{a}(q,y;f).

(8)

The proof of Lemma
2
is very similar to that of Theorem 3.1 in
Zhang and Li 2023c

and hence is omitted here. The computationally efficient recursive algorithm proposed by
Zhang and Li 2023c
can be employed for solving the linear system (
8
). The key steps of their approach can be summarized as follows:

•

Step 1:
Q_{a}(q,y_{N};f)=0
as
y_{N}
is an absorbing state.

•

Step 2: Assume
x=y_{\eta_{x}}
for some integer
\eta_{x}
. Calculate
Q_{a}(q,y_{i};f)
by running the backward from
i={N-1}
to
i=\eta_{x}
:

–

Compute the quantities
P_{(y_{i}-a,y_{i}]}(q,y_{i};f(\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq y_{i}-a\}})
and
P_{(y_{i}-a,y_{i}]}\big(q,y_{i};\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y_{j}\}}\big)
for all
j>i
via (
6
);

–

Compute
Q_{a}(q,y_{i};f)
via (
8
).

Significant reduction of the computational cost can be achieved in the following two cases.

Case 1: the Markov process
X
is a Lévy process. A CTMC
Y
with the state space
\mathbb{S}=h\mathbb{Z}
is chosen to approximate
X
so that
Y
itself is also a Lévy process. Here,
h>0
and
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the set of integers. In this case,
P_{S}(q,x;f)=P_{S-x}(q,0;f(x+\cdot))
and (
8
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{a}(q,x;f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y\leq x-a}P_{(x-a,x]}\big(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}}\big)f(y)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y>x}P_{(x-a,x]}\big(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}}\big)Q_{a}(q,y;f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y\leq-a}P_{(-a,0]}\big(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}}\big)f(y+x)+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y>0}P_{(-a,0]}\big(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}}\big)Q_{a}(q,y+x;f).

Obviously, it suffices to compute
P_{(-a,0]}\big(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y_{i}\}}\big)
for
y_{i}\leq-a
and
y_{i}>0
via (
6
) to obtain
Q_{a}(q,x;f)
recursively.

Case 2: the Markov process
X
is a diffusion process. The resulting CTMC is a birth-death process and (
8
) can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{a}(q,x;f)

\displaystyle=P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=(x-a)^{\ominus}\}})f((x-a)^{\ominus})+P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=x^{+}\}})Q_{a}(q,x^{+};f),

(9)

where
(x-a)^{\ominus}=\sup\{y\in\mathbb{S}:y\leq x-a\}
. In this case,
P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=(x-a)^{\ominus}\}})
and
P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=x^{+}\}})
can be constructed from two independent solutions
\psi^{\pm}
as follows: (see
Zhang and Li 2023c
)

\displaystyle P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=x^{+}\}})=\frac{\psi^{+}(q,x)\psi^{-}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})-\psi^{+}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})\psi^{-}(q,x)}{\psi^{+}(q,x^{+})\psi^{-}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})-\psi^{+}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})\psi^{-}(q,x^{+})},

(10)

\displaystyle P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=(x-a)^{\ominus}\}})=\frac{\psi^{+}(q,x^{+})\psi^{-}(q,x)-\psi^{+}(q,x)\psi^{-}(q,x^{+})}{\psi^{+}(q,x^{+})\psi^{-}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})-\psi^{+}(q,(x-a)^{\ominus})\psi^{-}(q,x^{+})}.

(11)

Here,
\psi^{\pm}
satisfy the following linear systems:

\displaystyle(q-\mathcal{G})\psi^{\pm}(q,x)=0,\qquad x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\},

(12)

with boundary conditions:
\psi^{+}(q,y_{0})=\psi^{-}(q,y_{N})=0
and
\psi^{-}(q,y_{0})=\psi^{+}(q,y_{N})=1
.

Drawdowns, Drawups, and Occupation Times

In this section, we derive linear systems for five drawdown quantities
under the CTMC and then propose efficient algorithms for solving these linear systems.

3.1
The Drawdown Time Preceding the Drawup Time

Write
\underline{Y}_{t}=\inf_{0\leq u\leq t}Y_{u}
and denote by
\widehat{\tau}_{b}=\mathrm{inf}\{t\geq 0:Y_{t}-\underline{Y}_{t}\geq b\}
the first passage time of the drawup process of the CTMC
Y
to the level
b>0
.
\widehat{\tau}_{b}
is called the drawup time. We consider the drawdown time
\tau_{a}
and the drawup time
\widehat{\tau}_{b}
, especially when the former precedes the latter, and define the following quantity:

\displaystyle A(q,x,y):=\mathbb{E}_{x,y}\big[e^{-q\tau_{a}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{a}<\widehat{\tau}_{b}\}}f(Y_{\tau_{a}})\big].

(13)

Here,
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
,
f:\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{x,y}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\,|\,Y_{0}=x,\,\overline{Y}_{0}=x,\,\underline{Y}_{0}=y]
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
b\geq a
. For any
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
,
x\in\mathbb{S}
, and

y\in\mathbb{S}\cap(x-a,x]
, the quantity
A(q,x,y)
satisfies the following linear system:

\displaystyle A(q,x,y)=

\displaystyle P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;f(\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq x-a\}})

(14)

\displaystyle+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\cap(x,y+b),\,w\in\mathbb{S}\cap((x-a)\vee(z-b),y]}R_{(x-a,x]}(q,x,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=z,\,\tilde{\cdot}=w\}})A(q,z,w),

where

\displaystyle R_{(\ell,r]}(q,x,y;g):=\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-qT_{r}^{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{\ell}^{-}>T_{r}^{+}\}}g(Y_{T_{r}^{+}},\underline{Y}_{T_{r}^{+}})\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x,\,\underline{Y}_{0}=y\big],

(15)

which satisfies the following linear system: for
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
with
x\geq y
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}qR_{(\ell,r]}(q,x,y;g)-\sum\limits_{z\in\mathbb{S}}G(x,z)R_{(\ell,r]}(q,z,y\wedge z;g)=0,\qquad&x\in(\ell,r],\\
R_{(\ell,r]}(q,x,y;g)=g(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{\{x>r\}},\qquad&x\notin(\ell,r].\end{cases}

(16)

Here,
\ell<r
,
T_{r}^{+}:=T_{(-\infty,r]}
, and
T_{\ell}^{-}:=T_{(\ell,\infty)}
.

Proof.

The proof of Proposition
1
is given in Appendix
B.1
.

.

Remark 2
.

The complexity of Algorithm
1
is dominated by the calculations of inverse matrices and matrix multiplications.
For each fixed
i
, when
j
ranges from
i_{a}+1
to
i
, the matrix
\bm{G}_{i,j}-q\bm{I}_{i-j+1}
in each recursion step can be expressed as the matrix in the previous step plus a sum of rank-one matrices.
This observation enables us to utilize the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula
(
Woodbury 1950
)
to efficiently compute
(\bm{G}_{i,j}-q\bm{I}_{i-j+1})^{-1}
from the inverse matrix in the previous recursion step,
see
Zhang and Li 2023c
for more details. Consequently, the complexity of calculating inverse matrices in the inner loop is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. Furthermore, when
i
decreases from
N-1
to
\eta_{x}
, the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula can be applied again, resulting in a complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
for the whole loops. Consequently, the overall complexity of computing inverse matrices is
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
.

The complexity in the calculations of matrix multiplications is of order
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
instead of
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
.
Take matrix multiplications
\bm{G}_{i,j}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j}^{-}
with
i=N-1,\dots,\eta_{x}
and
j=i_{a}+1,\dots,i
for example. The computational cost can be reduced as follows.
Introduce two new matrices
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}
and
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-}
by expanding
\bm{G}_{i,j+1}^{-}
and
\bm{G}_{i,j}^{-}
as follows:

\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{G}_{i,j+1}^{-}\\
\bm{0}_{1\times(j-i_{a}+1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad\quad\quad\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-}:=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{G}_{i,j}^{-}&\bm{0}_{(i-j+1)\times 1}\end{bmatrix},

(17)

where
\bm{0}_{m\times n}
is an
m\times n
zero matrix.
By construction,
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-}
is a sparse matrix as only the last row and column contain non-zero elements. The sparsity ensures that
the calculation of the matrix multiplication
(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-})\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}
only requires
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations. Moreover, notice that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}=

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-})\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}+\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}-\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j}^{-})\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}+\bm{G}_{i,j}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j}^{-}.

As a result, once
\bm{G}_{i,j}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j}^{-}
has been calculated, the complexity of evaluating
\widetilde{\bm{G}}_{i,j+1}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}
is
\mathcal{O}(N)
. So is
\bm{G}_{i,j+1}^{-}\bm{R}_{i,j+1}^{-}
. When
j
increases from
i_{a}+1
to
i
and
i
decreases from
N-1
to
\eta_{x}
, the computations of these matrix multiplications are of
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
complexity.

Therefore, the overall complexity of Algorithm
1
is
\mathcal{O}(N^{4})
. In particular, when the Markov process
X
is a diffusion process, the overall complexity of Algorithm
1
can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
. In this case, similar to (
10
), the quantity

A(q,y_{i},y_{j})

can be represented in terms of the vector
\{\psi^{\pm}(q,y_{m})\}_{m=0}^{N}
, which requires
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations by solving the linear systems (
12
). Thus, one circumvents the computations of inverse matrices and matrix multiplications,
as previously discussed. When
i
decreases from
N-1
to
\eta_{x}
and
j
ranges from
i_{a}+1
to
i
, the overall complexity is
\mathcal{O}((N-\eta_{x})(i-i_{a}))+O(N)
, which simplifies to
O(N^{2})
.

Remark 3
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
,
x\in\mathbb{S}
, and

y\in(x-b,x-a]

satisfies the following simpler linear system:

A(q,x,y)=P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;f(\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq x-a\}})+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\cap(x,y+b)}P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=z\}})A(q,z,y),

(19)

which can be solved in analogous to the quantity

.

Remark 4
.

The case
b<a
is more complicated and we leave it for future work.

Remark 9
.

As revealed from Algorithm

is analogous to that of

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
since the inverse matrix
(\bm{G}_{i}-q\bm{I}_{i-i_{a}+1})^{-1}
is independent of the recursion w.r.t.
k
, whereas the matrix multiplications cost
\mathcal{O}(nN^{2})

increases from

to

\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{max}(n,N)N^{2})

\mathcal{O}(nN)
for diffusion processes.

Consider an insurance policy that offers a protection against relative drawdowns. Assume the seller pays the buyer 1 at each relative drawdown time without recovery as long as it occurs before the maturity
T
; see
Landriault et al. 2015
. The price of the insurance policy can be defined as
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-r_{f}\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}<T\}}\big]
, whose Laplace transform w.r.t.
T
is given by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-qT}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-r_{f}\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}<T\}}\big]\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}T=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-(q+r_{f})\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}}\big]=\frac{H(q+r_{f},x)}{q}.

(33)

Here,
r_{f}
is the risk-free rate and
H(q,x):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[e^{-q\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}}\big]
.
The pricing of the insurance product reduces to evaluating
H(q,x)
as follows.

Corollary 1
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
and
x\in\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle H(q,x)

\displaystyle=~\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y\leq x-a}P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})(1+H(q,y))+\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S},\,y>x}P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})H(q,y).

(35)

Remark 10
.

Define
\bm{H}:=\{H(q,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{S}}
,
\bm{P}:=\{P_{(x-a,x]}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})\mathbf{1}_{\{y\in(-\infty,x-a]\cup(x,\infty)\}}\}_{x,y\in\mathbb{S}}
, and

(\bm{I}_{N+1}-\bm{P})\bm{H}=\widetilde{\bm{P}},

Here,

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})

for more details. Therefore, the overall complexity to solve the linear system (

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})

If the Markov process

(36)

\mathcal{O}(n_{a}^{3})

Remark 11
.

The complexity analysis of Algorithm
5
is the same as that of Algorithm
4
and hence is omitted. The complexity of Algorithm
5
for general Markov processes is
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{max}(n,N)N^{2})
and can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(nN)
for diffusion processes.

Consider an insurance product that the seller pays the buyer 1 at each relative drawdown time with recovery as long as it occurs before the maturity
T
. The price of the insurance product can be defined as
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-r_{f}\tau_{a,k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{a,k}<T\}}\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x,\overline{Y}_{0}=y\big]
, whose Laplace transform w.r.t.
T
is given by

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-qT}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-r_{f}\tau_{a,k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{a,k}<T\}}\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x,\overline{Y}_{0}=y\big]\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}T=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-(q+r_{f})\tau_{a,k}}\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x,\overline{Y}_{0}=y\big].

We define
J(q,x,y):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[e^{-q\tau_{a,k}}\,\big|\,Y_{0}=x,\overline{Y}_{0}=y\big]
. The pricing of the insurance product reduces to evaluating
J(q,x,y)
as follows.

Corollary 2
.

For any
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
\Re(q)>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
, the quantity
J(q,x,y)
satisfies the following linear system:

\displaystyle J(q,x,y)=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S},\,z\geq y}P_{(-\infty,y)}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=z\}})J(q,z,z),

(39)

\displaystyle J(q,y,y)=

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S},\,z\leq y-a}P_{(y-a,y]}(q,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=z\}})(1+J(q,z,y))

\displaystyle+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S},\,z>y}P_{(y-a,y]}(q,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=z\}})J(q,z,z).

Remark 12
.

Similar to Remark
10
, the complexity of solving the linear system (
39
) for general Markov processes is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
.
If the Markov process
X
is a Lévy process, we have
J(q,x,y)\equiv J(q,0,y-x)
, which leads to the following simplified solution to the linear system (
39
):

\displaystyle J(q,x,y)

\displaystyle=P_{(-\infty,y-x)}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\geq y-x\}})J(q,0,0),

(40)

\displaystyle J(q,0,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{(-a,0]}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq-a\}})}{1-P_{(-a,0]}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot>0\}})-P_{(-a,0]}(q,0;P_{(-\infty,0)}(q,\cdot;\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{\cdot}\geq 0\}})\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\leq-a\}})}.

Since a CTMC
Y
with the state space
\mathbb{S}=h\mathbb{Z}
is chosen to approximate the Lévy process
X
, the quantity
P_{(-\infty,y-x)}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\geq y-x\}})
satisfies an infinitely large linear system. To resolve this difficulty, we select a large
A>a>0
to approximate
P_{(-\infty,y-x)}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\geq y-x\}})
by
P_{(-A,y-x)}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\geq y-x\}})
. Similarly, let
n_{A}
denote the number of grid points within the interval
(-A,0]
, then the complexity in the computation of the quantity
P_{(-A,y-x)}(q,0;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\geq y-x\}})
is
\mathcal{O}(n_{A}^{3})
,
which results in
\mathcal{O}(n_{A}^{3})
order for the overall complexity of solving the linear system (
40
).

If the Markov process
X
is a diffusion process, the linear system (
39
) can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle J(q,x,y)=

\displaystyle P_{(-\infty,y)}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})J(q,y,y),

(41)

\displaystyle J(q,y,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{(y-a,y]}(q,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y^{+}\}})J(q,y^{+},y^{+})+P_{(y-a,y]}(q,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=(y-a)^{\ominus}\}})}{1-P_{(y-a,y]}(q,y;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=(y-a)^{\ominus}\}})P_{(-\infty,y)}(q,(y-a)^{\ominus};\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})}.

In this case,
P_{(-\infty,y)}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})
can be approximated by
P_{[y_{0},y)}(q,x;\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot=y\}})
, where
y_{0}
is a boundary point. Thus, by taking advantage of (
10
), the linear system (
41
) can be solved recursively with
\mathcal{O}(N)
computational complexity.

4
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we establish the convergence of the CTMC approximation for five quantities analyzed in Section
3
.
Let
\{Y_{t}^{(n)}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
be a sequence of CTMCs to approximate
X_{t}
, where the superscript
(n)
denotes the number of grid points and
\delta_{n}
represents the corresponding mesh size. We assume
\delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Sufficient conditions for
Y^{(n)}\Rightarrow X
in
D(\mathbb{R})
are laid out in
Mijatović and Pistorius 2013
, where
\Rightarrow
stands for the weak convergence of stochastic processes and
D(\mathbb{R})
is the space of real-valued C
\grave{\text{a}}
dl
\grave{\text{a}}
g functions endowed with the Skorokhod topology.

Denote by
\omega
a path of the Markov process
X
and introduce the following subsets of
D(\mathbb{R})
:

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=\{\omega\in D(\mathbb{R}):\inf\{t\geq u:\overline{\omega}_{u,t}-\omega_{t}\geq a\}=\inf\{t\geq u:\overline{\omega}_{u,t}-\omega_{t}>a\}~\text{for all}~u\geq 0\},

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle:=\{\omega\in D(\mathbb{R}):\inf\{t\geq u:\overline{\omega}_{u,t}\geq L\}=\inf\{t\geq u:\overline{\omega}_{u,t}>L\}~\text{for all}~u\geq 0\},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}

\displaystyle:=\{\omega\in D(\mathbb{R}):\text{for all}~t>s>0,~\text{if}~\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}(\overline{\omega}_{s,u}-\omega_{u})<a,~\text{there exists a}~\delta\in(0,s)~\text{such that}

\displaystyle\qquad\sup_{s-\delta\leq u\leq t}(\overline{\omega}_{s-\delta,u}-\omega_{u})<a\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle:=\{\omega\in D(\mathbb{R}):\text{for all}~t>u>s\geq 0,~\text{if}~s~\text{and}~u~\text{are consecutive drawdown times with }

\displaystyle\qquad\text{recovery and}~\overline{\omega}_{s,u}>\overline{\omega}_{u,t},~\text{there exists a}~\delta\in(0,u-s)~\text{such that}~u-\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\setminus J(\omega)~\text{and}

\displaystyle\qquad\overline{\omega}_{s,u-\delta}>\overline{\omega}_{u-\delta,t}\}.

Here,
a>0,~L\in\mathbb{R},~J(\omega):=\{t\geq 0:\omega_{t}\neq\omega_{t-}\},
and
\overline{\omega}_{s,t}:=\sup_{s\leq u\leq t}\omega_{u}
.

We first impose the following assumptions on the paths of
X_{t}
.

Assumption 1

(
Zhang and Li 2023c
)
.

(1)
X_{t}
does not have monotone sample paths. (2)
X_{t}
is quasi-left continuous, i.e., there exists a sequence of totally inaccessible stopping times that exhausts the jumps of
X_{t}
. (3) For all
x
, the Lévy measure
\nu(x,\cdot)
is either absolutely continuous or if it is discrete,
X_{t}
must have a diffusion component, i.e.,
\sigma(x)>0
for all
x
attainable in the state space. Furthermore, if the Lévy measure
\nu(x,\cdot)
has finite activity for any
x
,
X_{t}
must have a diffusion component.

Assumption
1
ensures that
\tau_{a}^{(n)}
converges in distribution to
\tau_{a}
as
n\to\infty
; see
Zhang and Li 2023c
for more details. In the following analysis, the quantities bearing the superscript
(n)
are defined under the CTMC
Y^{(n)}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumption
1
holds and
Y^{(n)}\Rightarrow X
.

(1)

\Big(\tau_{a}^{(n)},\widehat{\tau}_{b}^{(n)},Y_{\tau_{a}^{(n)}}^{(n)}\Big)
converges in distribution to
(\tau_{a},\widehat{\tau}_{b},X_{\tau_{a}})
as
n\to\infty
.

(2)

Suppose
k(\cdot)
is a bounded and piecewise continuous univariate function, and there exists a subset
I\subseteq D(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
P(X\in I)=1
and for any
\omega\in I

\textbf{T}_{dis}:=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}

\Big(\tau_{a}^{(n)},\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}^{(n)}}k(Y_{s}^{(n)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,Y_{\tau_{a}^{(n)}}^{(n)}\Big)

.

(3)

Suppose

satisfying
P(X\in I)=1
and for any
\omega\in I

\textbf{T}_{dis}:=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}

\Big(\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}^{(n)},Y_{\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}^{(n)}}^{(n)}\Big)

(\tilde{\tau}_{a,k},X_{\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}})

.

(5)

Suppose
\tau_{a,k}<\tau_{a,k+1}

\Big(\tau_{a,k}^{(n)},Y_{\tau_{a,k}^{(n)}}^{(n)}\Big)

.

5
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we conduct a series of comprehensive numerical experiments to assess the accuracy and efficiency of our proposed five algorithms. Specifically, we price five types of financial derivatives and insurance products
under different Markov models.
Utilizing the CTMC approximation, the Laplace transforms of these prices can be expressed through the quantities analyzed in Section
3
. The prices themselves can be computed by performing Laplace inversions via the Abate-Whitt algorithm
(
Abate and Whitt 1992
)
.

Let
S_{t}
denote the asset price at time
t
with the interest rate
r_{f}
and dividend yield
d
. We set
S_{0}=1
,
r_{f}=0.5
, and
d=0.02
. We consider five quantities associated with the relative drawdown/drawup of the asset price
S_{t}
, equivalently, the drawdown/drawup of the log-asset price
X_{t}=\ln S_{t}
, under the following models:

•

The Black-Scholes (BS) model
(
Black and Scholes 1973
)
:

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{t}=(r_{f}-d)S_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma S_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t},

where
\{W\}_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion. We set
\sigma=0.3
.

•

The constant elasticity of variance (CEV) model
(
Cox 1996
)
:

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{t}=(r_{f}-d)S_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma S_{t}^{1+\beta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

We set
\sigma=0.3
and
\beta=-0.25
.

•

The double exponential jump-diffusion model (DEJD)
(
Kou 2002
)
:

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S_{t}}{S_{t-}}=(r_{f}-d-\lambda\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W_{t}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}(V_{i}-1)\right),

where
\{N_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Poisson process with the jump intensity
\lambda
,
\{V_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with the density of
\ln V_{i}
given by
f_{\ln V}(y)=p^{+}\eta^{+}e^{-\eta^{+}y}\mathbf{1}_{\{y\geq 0\}}+p^{-}\eta^{-}e^{\eta^{-}y}\mathbf{1}_{\{y<0\}}
, and
\zeta=\mathbb{E}[V_{i}]-1=\frac{p^{+}\eta^{+}}{\eta^{+}-1}+\frac{p^{-}\eta^{-}}{\eta^{-}+1}-1
. We set
\sigma=0.3,~p^{+}=p^{-}=0.5,~\eta^{+}=\eta^{-}=10,
and
\lambda=3
.

•

The variance Gamma (VG) model
(
Madan et al. 1998
)
:

S_{t}=S_{0}e^{(r_{f}-d)t+\theta\gamma_{t}(1;\nu)+\sigma W_{\gamma_{t}(1;\nu)}+\ln(1-\theta\nu-\sigma^{2}\nu/2)/\nu t},

where
\{\gamma_{t}(1;\nu)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a Gamma process with the mean 1 and variance
\nu
that is independent of
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
. We set
\theta=-2.206,~\sigma=0.962,
and
\nu=0.00254
.

over the interval

.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a unified framework for computing five important drawdown quantities under general Markov models. Utilizing the CTMC approximation, we derive linear systems for the associated quantities defined under the CTMC and develop algorithms to solve these systems. In particular, by exploiting the inherent spatial homogeneity of Lévy models and birth-death characteristic of diffusion models, we construct more efficient algorithms under these two types of models for a majority of quantities. Notably, we circumvent the challenge posed by highly path-dependent quantities and conduct rigorous convergence analysis of our algorithms under mild conditions. Extensive numerical experiments are presented to demonstrate the good performance of our algorithms. Specifically, in most cases, our algorithms achieve remarkable accuracy with relative errors below 1% within just a few seconds. Even in the worst case, the CPU time remains under 30 seconds. The accuracy can be further enhanced with the aid of the extrapolation technique.

For future research, we can extend our framework in two directions. First, we can consider more complex quantities involving both drawdowns and drawups simultaneously, such as the drawdown time
\tau_{a}
preceding the drawup time
\tau_{b}
under a more complicated case
b<a
, as discussed in Remark
4
.
Second, model assumptions can be generalized beyond Markovian models to incorporate non-Markovian models including rough stochastic local volatility models. The CTMC approximation for rough stochastic local volatility models was constructed in
Yang et al. 2025
.

Appendix A
Construction of the CTMC Approximation

This section is devoted to constructing a CTMC
Y
with the state space
\mathbb{S}
to approximate a Markov process
X
whose inﬁnitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
is depicted in (
1
). It suffices to specify the transition rate matrix
\bm{G}
with elements
G(x,y)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
, such that the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{G}
of
Y
is an approximation of
\mathcal{L}
in the sense that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}g(x)\approx\mathcal{L}g(x)

(42)

for any function
g\in C^{2}_{c}(I):I\to\mathbb{R}
and any
x\in\mathbb{S}
.

To this end, the derivative terms and the integral term in (
1
) are approximated by the central difference method and a summation over the discrete state space
\mathbb{S}
, respectively.
More specifically, the derivative terms are approximated as follows:

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\Delta g(x):=\frac{\nabla^{+}g(x)-\nabla^{-}g(x)}{\delta x},

(43)

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\approx\nabla g(x):=\frac{\delta^{-}x}{2\delta x}\nabla^{+}g(x)+\frac{\delta^{+}x}{2\delta x}\nabla^{-}g(x),

(44)

where

\displaystyle x^{+}=\inf\{y\in\mathbb{S}:y>x\},~\delta^{+}x=x^{+}-x,\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{N}\},

(45)

\displaystyle x^{-}=\sup\{y\in\mathbb{S}:y<x\},~\delta^{-}x=x-x^{-},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0}\},

(46)

\displaystyle\delta x=\frac{\delta^{+}x+\delta^{-}x}{2},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\},

(47)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\pm}g(x)=\pm\frac{g(x^{\pm})-g(x)}{\delta^{\pm}x},\qquad

\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\}.

(48)

On the other hand, the integral term is approximated as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(g(x+y)-g(x)-yg^{\prime}(x)\mathbf{1}_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}\big)\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)

(49)

\displaystyle\approx~\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(x)\int_{-\delta^{-}x/2}^{\delta^{+}x/2}y^{2}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)-g^{\prime}(x)\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash[-\delta^{-}x/2,\delta^{+}x/2]}y\mathbf{1}_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)

(50)

\displaystyle~~~~+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}\big(g(z)-g(x)\big)\int^{z+\delta^{+}z/2-x+\infty\mathbf{1}_{\{z=y_{N}\}}}_{z-\delta^{-}z/2-x-\infty\mathbf{1}_{\{z=y_{0}\}}}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),

(51)

where the first term approximates the integral for small jumps and the resulting
g^{\prime\prime}(x)
is then discretized by the central difference approximation as before.

For brevity, we introduce

\displaystyle\overline{b}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash[-\delta^{-}x/2,\delta^{+}x/2]}y\mathbf{1}_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),

(52)

\displaystyle\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\delta^{-}x/2}^{\delta^{+}x/2}y^{2}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),

\displaystyle\overline{\nu}(x,z)=\int^{z+\delta^{+}z/2-x+\infty 1_{\{z=y_{N}\}}}_{z-\delta^{-}z/2-x-\infty 1_{\{z=y_{0}\}}}\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y).

Thus, the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{G}
of
Y
can be chosen as follows: for
x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}g(x)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma^{2}(x)+\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)\big)\Delta g(x)+\big(b(x)-\overline{b}(x)\big)\nabla g(x)+\sum_{z\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}\overline{\nu}(x,z)\big(g(z)-g(x)\big),

(53)

otherwise,
\mathcal{G}g(x)=0.

The generator
\mathcal{G}
implies that the transition rate matrix
G
satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle G(x,x^{+})=(b(x)-\overline{b}(x))\frac{\delta^{-}x}{2\delta^{+}x\delta x}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma^{2}(x)+\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)\big)\frac{1}{\delta^{+}x\delta x}+\overline{\nu}(x,x^{+}),~~~~~~x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\},

(54)

\displaystyle G(x,x^{-})=-(b(x)-\overline{b}(x))\frac{\delta^{+}x}{2\delta^{-}x\delta x}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\sigma^{2}(x)+\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)\big)\frac{1}{\delta^{-}x\delta x}+\overline{\nu}(x,x^{-}),~~~~x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\},

(55)

\displaystyle G(x,z)=\overline{\nu}(x,z),~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~x\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{y_{0},y_{N}\},~z\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x,x^{+},x^{-}\},

(56)

\displaystyle G(x,x)=-\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}G(x,y),\qquad x\in\mathbb{S},

(57)

and all other elements of
G
are zeros.

Remark 13
.

For Lévy processes, it is desirable to construct a CTMC that preserves the property of independent and stationary increments. To this end,
a CTMC
Y
with the state space
\mathbb{S}=h\mathbb{Z}
is chosen to approximate the Lévy process
X
so that
Y
itself is also a Lévy process. Here,
h>0
and
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the set of integers.
In this case, for any
x\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

x^{\pm}=x\pm h
and
\delta^{\pm}x=\delta x=h.

Moreover,
b(x),~\sigma^{2}(x),
and
\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
in (
1
)
are independent of
x
,
which enables us to write
b=b(x),

\sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}(x),
and
\nu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)=\nu(x,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
.
Therefore, we introduce

\overline{b}=\int_{\mathbb{R}\backslash[-h/2,h/2]}y\mathbf{1}_{\{|y|\leq 1\}}\nu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),

\overline{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}y^{2}\nu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y),
and
\overline{\nu}(z-x)=\int^{z-x+h/2}_{z-x-h/2}\nu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)
.
It follows that the transition rate matrix
G
is characterized by

\displaystyle G(x,x+h)=\frac{b-\overline{b}}{2h}+\frac{\sigma^{2}+\overline{\sigma}^{2}}{2h^{2}}+\overline{\nu}(h),

(58)

\displaystyle G(x,x-h)=-\frac{b-\overline{b}}{2h}+\frac{\sigma^{2}+\overline{\sigma}^{2}}{2h^{2}}+\overline{\nu}(-h),

(59)

\displaystyle G(x,z)=\overline{\nu}(z-x),~~~~z\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x,~x\pm h\},

(60)

\displaystyle G(x,x)=-\sum_{y\in\mathbb{S}\backslash\{x\}}G(x,y).

(61)

Remark 14
.

For diffusion processes, we have
\overline{b}(x)=0
,
\overline{\sigma}^{2}(x)=0
, and
\overline{\nu}(x,z)=0
for all
x,z\in\mathbb{S}
. The resulting CTMC is a birth-death process.

B.6
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

For brevity, we write
s^{\prime}=\tau_{a}(\omega)
,
s^{\prime(n)}=\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})
,
\overline{\omega}_{s,t}^{(n)}=\sup_{u\in[s,t]}\omega_{u}^{(n)}
,
MD_{s,t}=\sup_{u\in[s,t]}(\overline{\omega}_{s,u}-\omega_{u})
,
MD_{s,t}^{(n)}=\sup_{u\in[s,t]}(\overline{\omega}_{s,u}^{(n)}-\omega_{u}^{(n)})
,
M_{k}=\sup_{u\in[\tau_{a,k-1}(\omega),\tau_{a,k}(\omega)]}\omega_{u}
,
M_{k}^{(n)}=\sup_{u\in[\tau_{a,k-1}(\omega^{(n)}),\tau_{a,k}(\omega^{(n)})]}\omega_{u}^{(n)}
,
T_{M_{k}}=\inf\{t>\tau_{a,k}(\omega):\omega_{t}\geq M_{k}\}
, and
T_{M_{k}}^{(n)}=\inf\{t>\tau_{a,k}(\omega^{(n)}):\omega_{t}^{(n)}\geq M_{k}^{(n)}\},
where
0\leq s<t
and
k=1,2,\dots
. Define
\tau_{a,0}(\omega)=\tau_{a,0}(\omega^{(n)})=0
.
In the subsequent analysis, we focus on establishing the continuity of the mappings w.r.t. the sample path
\omega
. Combined with the weak convergence
Y^{(n)}\Rightarrow X
, this continuity property ensures
the quantities defined under the CTMC
Y^{(n)}
converge in distribution to their counterparts defined under the Markov process
X
.

(1)

It suffices to show that
\omega\mapsto(\tau_{a}(\omega),\widehat{\tau}_{b}(\omega),\omega_{\tau_{a}(\omega)})
is a continuous mapping almost surely. Since the continuity of the mapping
\omega\mapsto(\tau_{a}(\omega),\omega_{\tau_{a}(\omega)})
has been verified by
Zhang and Li 2023c
, it remains to prove the mapping
\omega\mapsto\widehat{\tau}_{b}(\omega)
is continuous. Observe that the running minimum satisfies the identity
\inf_{0\leq s\leq t}X_{s}=-\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}(-X_{s})
. Following the methodology of
Zhang and Li 2023c
, we directly verify the continuity of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\widehat{\tau}_{b}(\omega)
.

(2)

As discussed above, it suffices to show the continuity of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
. Suppose
\omega^{(n)}\to\omega
under the Skorokhod topology. For any
\epsilon>0
, denote by
\bm{T}_{\epsilon}=\cup_{1\leq i\leq m}(t_{i}-\epsilon,t_{i}+\epsilon)
the neighbourhood of
\bm{T}_{dis}
. By
Jacod and Shiryaev 2013
, for
s\in(0,\tau_{a})\setminus\bm{T}_{\epsilon}
, the convergence
\omega_{s}^{(n)}\to\omega_{s}
holds as
n\to\infty
, ensuring the convergence
k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\to k(\omega_{s})
. Denote by
U_{k}
the upper bound of the function
k(\cdot)
. Notice that
\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})\to\tau_{a}(\omega)
as
n\to\infty
, then it follows that

\left|\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})}k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq U_{k}\left|\tau_{a}(\omega)-\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})\right|\to 0.

On the other hands,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\bm{T}_{\epsilon}}\left|k(\omega_{s})-k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\int_{(0,\tau_{a}(\omega))\setminus\bm{T}_{\epsilon}}\left|k(\omega_{s})-k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 4mU_{k}\epsilon+\int_{(0,\tau_{a}(\omega))\setminus\bm{T}_{\epsilon}}\left|k(\omega_{s})-k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\right|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\to 4mU_{k}\epsilon.

The second inequality is established by the boundedness of the function
k(\cdot)
and the last line follows from an application of the dominated convergence theorem.
Since
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small, combining the above two results yields that
\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})}k(\omega_{s}^{(n)})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\to\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
as
n\to\infty
.

(3)

The proof of the continuity of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s},\overline{\omega}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
is analogous to that of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\int_{0}^{\tau_{a}(\omega)}k(\omega_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
and hence is omitted.

(4)

According to
Zhang and Li 2023c
,
\tilde{\tau}_{a,1}(\omega^{(n)})=\tau_{a}(\omega^{(n)})\to\tau_{a}(\omega)=\tilde{\tau}_{a,1}(\omega)
as
n\to\infty
.
Therefore, it suffices to show the continuity of the mapping
\omega\mapsto\tilde{\tau}_{a,2}(\omega)
, and the continuity of the mappings
\omega\mapsto\tilde{\tau}_{a,k}
for
k=3,\dots
can be proved by mathematical induction.

Assume
s^{\prime}<\tilde{\tau}_{a,2}(\omega)<s,~\omega\in U
, and

, then
MD_{s^{\prime},s}>a
.
By the right-continuity of the path
\omega

s^{\prime}

inherits this property and is also right-continuous at

Zhang and Li 2023c
implies that
