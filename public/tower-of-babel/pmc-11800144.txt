Impact of residential hot water heater type and water stagnation on drinking water quality within a full-scale premise plumbing system
Authors: Helen Y Buse, Josh Steenbock, Sara Okum, Nicole Sojda, Sharon Kidney, Johnny Brossart, Dave Elstun, Eric Weaver, Sue Witt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Premise plumbing systems (PPSs), which are connected to the main drinking water distribution system via service lines, are a primary source for human exposure to chemical and microbial contaminants through inhalation, ingestion, and skin contact. To understand the occurrence and distribution of drinking water contaminants in these systems, this study utilized a full-scale PPS and monitored operational parameters and water quality changes for 26 weeks. The PPS contained natural gas- and electric-powered instantaneous and tank hot water heaters (HWHs), each supplying separate sinks and a single shower stall. Total water, gas, and electric usage was monitored daily. Over 400 water samples were collected at 11 timepoints across a 26-week period. Samples represented 4 types: Supply, Hot, Cold, and Shower water, with either stagnant (after an 8-hour period of no water usage), 10–15 second, and/or 5–10-minute flushed samples collected. Lower heterotrophic plate count (HPC) levels were observed in Shower and Hot water samples compared to those in the Supply Line, with most Hot water samples displaying decreases in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels post flushing. Water quality differences were also observed between Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous and tank HWHs, such as temperature, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), conductivity, chlorine levels, and US regulated disinfection byproducts (DBPs). Spearman tests indicated very strong to perfect negative correlations between DBPs, ORP, and free chlorine in Hot and Shower water samples. Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering of Cold and Hot water samples, supplied either by instantaneous or tank HWH samples, which were driven by differences in temperature, HPC/ATP, DBPs, conductivity, and disinfectant residual. These differences were also impacted by water stagnation as observed by the separate clustering of respective stagnant and flushed HWH samples. Collectively, HWH system and water age (i.e., stagnation) and type strongly influenced chemical and microbial water quality and furthers the understanding of the impacts PPS design and engineering parameters have on water quality.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, over 6 billion people, or 73 % of the global population, had access to a safely managed drinking water service (e.g., protected springs or dug wells; packaged or delivered water; piped water) that is free from contamination, available when needed, and located on premises (WHO, 2023). However, even in affluent countries where ≥ 90 % of the population has access to high-quality water, the disease burden associated with contaminated drinking water is high and can range, for example, from 37–18,250 gastrointestinal illness cases per 100,000 due to microbial contamination and 1.2–2.9 cancer cases per 100,000 depending on the chemical contaminant and cancer type (Lee et al., 2023). Other illnesses and adverse health outcomes associated with microbial and chemical drinking water contamination include respiratory illnesses, hepatitis, meningoencephalitis, impaired fertility, neurocognitive damage, cancer, and death (WHO, 2022).

In the United States (US), between 1971–2020, there has been an overall increase in the percentage of drinking water associated outbreaks where the contributing factors were premise plumbing deficiencies (in 1971–2006, 13 %; 2007–2014, 59 %; 2015–2020, 82 %) (Beer et al., 2015, Brunkard et al., 2011, Craun et al., 2010, Hilborn et al., 2013, Kunz et al., 2024, McClung et al., 2017, Yoder et al., 2008). A premise plumbing system (PPS) is defined as the portion of a water system connected to the main drinking water distribution system via service line(s); and encompasses both hot and cold-water supplied through various plumbing appurtenances (NRC, 2006). For the US drinking water associated outbreaks cited above, premise plumbing deficiencies included point of use contamination at taps, hoses, or containers; cross-connections; contamination of devices and equipment using or distributing water, such as decorative fixtures, cooling towers, ice machines, and drink mix machines; inadequate disinfection; occurrence of Legionella spp.; and water temperatures (≥ 30°C or 86°F) that are conducive to growth of Legionella and other opportunistic pathogens, such as nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) and Pseudomonas (Craun et al., 2010, Kunz et al., 2024).

PPSs serve as an important source of exposure to drinking water contaminants via inhalation, ingestion, and dermal contact. However, in many countries, water quality management in these systems falls outside the responsibilities of the drinking water supplier or utility (WHO, 2011, WHO, 2022). Specifically, in the US, potable water quality in PPSs is not regulated by the US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) except for the Lead and Copper Rule Revisions and to some extent the Revised Total Coliform Rule (40 C.F.R. § 141, 2024). Building water quality is one of the nine foundations for Healthy Buildings proposed by the Harvard School of Public Health as well as being part of the USEPA’s Healthy Buildings, Healthy People initiative, all aimed at improving indoor environments for benefits to health, productivity, the economy, and sustainability (Allen et al., 2017, USEPA, 2024). Moreover, the US General Services Administration (GSA) recently issued an order requiring all Federally owned facilities and leased space to effectively manage and oversee drinking water to ensure the well-being of building occupants, contractors, and visitors (1000.7A PBS, 2023). Specifically, the directive states that the Public Building Service of the GSA has the responsibility to provide potable water at point-of-use outlets that meets USEPA’s National Primary Drinking Water Rules (NPDWRs) per the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) Sanitation Standard (29 C.F.R. § 1910.141, 2024, 40 C.F.R. § 141, 2024). Thus, there is a need for ensuring premise plumbing water quality and understanding the complex factors that impact it is of vital importance for public health protection.

Numerous studies have reported field data on PPS water quality, e.g., physiochemical parameters, concentrations of disinfectant residuals, disinfection byproducts, corrosion, nitrification, lead, copper, and opportunistic pathogens utilizing either grab samples or real-time monitoring (Buse and Mistry, 2024, Buse et al., 2020, Edwards et al., 2000, Edwards et al., 2009, Inkinen et al., 2014, Kalan et al., 2022, Lautenschlager et al., 2010, Logan-Jackson et al., 2021, Saetta et al., 2021, Salehi et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2009, Zheng et al., 2015). These studies describe the changes and/or degradation of water quality within PPSs due to the system’s design and type which will impact other characteristics such as water age, temperature, flow velocities, mixtures of plumbing materials and sizes, variety and quantities of plumbing fixtures and devices, fluctuating temperatures, and levels of disinfectant residuals (WHO, 2011, WHO, 2022). Due to these complex factors, premise plumbing model systems have been used to evaluate the impact of stagnation, flushing, water age, temperature, pipe material, and disinfection on water quality (Gomez-Alvarez et al., 2023, Masters et al., 2015, McCuin et al., 2022, Salehi et al., 2020, Tolofari et al., 2021). Thus, there is a recognized research need for a holistic, systematic, and controlled evaluation of the complex effects and interplay of various engineering and operational parameters (e.g., upstream distribution system water quality, water usage and patterns, stagnation, temperature, energy usage and consumption, plumbing materials and fixture types, cold and hot water networks, hot water heater system type, etc.) on PPS water quality and developing strategies to improve it and protect public health (Persily et al., 2023).

To address these knowledge gaps, this study utilized a newly constructed, full-scale model PPS that was operated to simulate average household water usage in the United States of approx. 220 gallons or 833 liters per day and household average of 2.65 people (DeOreo et al., 2016). In comparison, total water consumption per day per person can range from 13 gallons (50 liters) to >59 gallons (225 liters) depending on the European country (Schumann et al., 2021). Assuming a similar household average of 2.65 people, water usage in Europe can thus range from 35 gallons (132 liters) to 157 gallons (596 liters) per day per household, compared to 220 gallons (833 liters) in the United States. In this study, over 400 water samples were collected over 6 months across 11 sampling events and analyzed for physiochemical, chemical, and microbial water quality parameters, in addition to daily water, electric, and gas usage. Information from this study will aid in the improved operations, management, and maintenance of water quality in PPSs.

Methods

Description of the full-scale premise plumbing system

Main room

A full-scale premise plumbing system (PPS) was constructed in an approximately 425 square foot (ft2) or 39 square meter (m2) space (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1). This space was divided up into two rooms: a 315 ft2 (29 m2) main area housing utility sinks and hot water heaters (Main Room, Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A and B) and a 110 ft2 (10 m2) room containing a shower stall, two toilets, a urinal, and a utility sink (Shower Room Fig. 1A, Fig. S1C, D, and E). Municipally treated water was supplied to the entire PPS via a 2 inch, or 5.1 centimeter (cm), diameter schedule 80 polyvinylchloride (PVC) pipe outfitted with a PVC ball check valve to prevent backflow (Hayward Flow Control, Charlotte, NC, USA) (Supply Line, Fig. 1A). See Section 2.1.4 for source water characteristics. Fig. 1A depicts six separate utility sinks (numbered 1 through 6), where each was supplied with cold and hot water from separate plumbing lines (Fig. S1A). Each of the six sinks are fed hot water from their respectively numbered hot water heater (HWH) (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1B).

HWH1 is a natural gas powered, instantaneous, or tankless, water heater (model RU160iN, Rinnai America Corporation, Peachtree City, GA, USA); HWH2 and HWH3, are natural gas powered, 50 gallon (189 liters) tank water heaters (model 9050–38F3, Rheem Manufacturing Company, Atlanta, GA, USA and model 6–50-NBRT 400, Reliance Water Heater Company, Ashland City, TN, USA, respectively); HWH4 and HWH5 are electric powered, 50 gal (189 L) tank water heaters (model E6–50H45DV 120, A. O. Smith Corporation, Milwaukee, WI, USA and model XE50M06ST45U1, Rheem Manufacturing Company, Atlanta, GA, USA, respectively); and HWH6 is an electric powered, instantaneous, or tankless, water heater (model RETEX-27, Rheem Manufacturing Company, Atlanta, GA, USA). These HWHs were chosen based on their average cost, common availability in United States based hardware stores and retail chains, and the need to have representation of gas and electric powered instantaneous and tank HWH types in the full-scale PPS.

Shower room

The shower and utility sink, depicted in Fig. 1A, was supplied hot water from each HWH using separate plumbing lines (Fig. S1E). Three additional outlets (2 supplying hot and 1 supplying cold water) were also installed on the side of the shower room sink to simulate water usage from appliances such as a dish and clothes washer (SDW and SCW, respectively, Fig. 1B). Two toilets, one with a tank and one without (model K782760, Kohler, Kohler, WI, USA and model 3043, American Standard, Piscataway, NJ, USA, respectively), and a urinal (model 6550001, American Standard, Piscataway, NJ, USA) were installed in the Shower Room on a platform to facilitate future wastewater and aerosol work (Fig. S1E). However, for this study, their primary function was for water consumption from a relevant fixture to simulate residential water usage. The automatic flushing mechanism for each were comprised of a metal loop wrapped around the flush handle and a pulley mechanism controlled by a solenoid (model MED 24×2.400, Deltrol Controls, Milwaukee, WI, USA) and timer (model H5S, Omron, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. S1B and D, blue box).

Plumbing

Common water pipe materials, copper (Cu), chlorinated polyvinyl chloride (cPVC), polyvinylchloride (PVC), and cross-linked polyethylene (PEX), were used uniformly throughout the PPS (Fig. S1A). All PVC material was schedule 80 (ASTM, 2021). Cold water from the Supply Line was delivered via a 17.3 ft (5.3 m) PVC line which branched off into six separate plumbing lines for each sink. Each of these separate cold water sink lines, in order of installation, consisted of 8.4 ft (2.6 m) of Cu pipe, 8.9 ft (2.7 m) of PVC pipe, and 24 ft (7.3 m) of PEX pipe. Similarly, each hot water heater delivered hot water to their respective sinks via a 9.8 ft (3 m) of Cu pipe, 8 ft (2.4 m) of cPVC pipe, and 18.8 ft (5.7 m) of PEX pipe. Each of these 12 cold and hot water lines incorporated a 3 ft (0.9 m) section of removable duplicate 0.5 ft (0.15 m) Cu, PVC or cPVC, and PEX pipe segments to facilitate future biofilm work. All pipes used within the PPS had an inner diameter of 0.5 inches (1.3 centimeters).

For the Shower Room, cold water from the Supply Line was delivered to the shower fixture, via two segments of PEX lines totalling 7.1 ft (2.2 m), and to the cold water sink faucet, via two segments of PEX lines totalling 12.6 ft (3.8 m). Each hot water heater delivered hot water to a solenoid shower manifold via a 8.1–20.2 ft (2.5–6.2 m) PEX line (Fig. S1E). From the manifold, hot water was delivered to the simulated dishwasher (SDW), simulated clothes washer (SCW), sink faucet, and shower fixture via two segments of PEX lines totalling 11.5 ft (3.5 m), 10.2 ft (3.1 m), 12 ft (3.7 m), and 5.4 ft (1.7 m), respectively.

Source water

Municipally supplied drinking water was derived from river water treated by coagulation, flocculation, and sedimentation; sand and gravel filtration; granular activated carbon (GAC) filtration; and chlorination with sodium hypochlorite. Several water quality parameters and contaminants measured in this study were also monitored within the distribution system by the local water utility and published in the annual Consumer Confidence Report (CCR) as per the USEPA NPDWR (40 C.F.R. § 141, 2024). For the year covering the 26-week study period, the CCR indicated a 1.04–1.2 mg/L detection range for total chlorine, 1.60–3.49 mg/L for total organic carbon (TOC), 0.03–0.11 nephelometric turbidity units (NTU) for turbidity, 14.4–52.8 μg/L for total trihalomethanes (TTHMs), and 2.2–12.6 μg/L for the five regulated haloacetic acids (HAA5). Under the USEPA NPDWRs, the maximum residual disinfectant level goal for chlorine (as Cl2) is 4 mg/L; for this system, NTU must be ≤ 0.3 NTUs in at least 95 percent of the samples in any month; and the maximum contaminant level (MCL) for TTHMs is 80 μg/L and for HAA is 60 μg/L. Under the USEPA Stage 1 and Stage 2 Disinfectants and Disinfection Byproducts Rules, this system must be compliant with the TOC removal requirements with source water TOC levels ≤ 4 mg/L and in monthly treated water samples, < 2 mg/L.

Operation of the PPS

Fixtures

Faucets installed in each sink had dedicated cold and hot valves (model FL014107CP, Project Source, Lowe’s Corporate, Mooresville, NC, USA). Cold valves were set to deliver a flow rate between 1.9–2.3 gallons per minute (gpm), or 7.3–8.5 liters per minute (lpm) and hot valves between 1.2–1.8 gpm, or 4.6–6.6 lpm. The shower fixtures included a handheld, detachable showerhead with a metal finish exterior and acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) plastic interior and a 5-ft (1.5 m) vinyl lined metal hose (model XDL-769ME, Waterpik, Fort Collins, CO, USA) and a single cold and hot water lever handle (model 82604, Moen Incorporated, North Olmsted, OH, USA) which was adjusted to deliver shower water temperatures between 43–45°C (109 – 113°F) at 1.5 gpm or 5.8 lpm (Fig. S1B, green box).

Controllers

Water usage was automated and controlled by electrically operated two-way low lead brass solenoid valves (model 21WN5K1E190-T5, Assured Automation, Roselle, NJ, USA) and 2-channel timers (model H5S, Omron, Tokyo, Japan). A total of 28 solenoids and 14 timers were used throughout the PPS. The controller box in the Main Room contained six timers which operated 12 solenoids installed in the 6 cold and 6 hot plumbing lines for the sinks (Fig. S1A). The controller box in the Shower Room contained 8 timers, which operated 16 solenoids installed in the 10 hot plumbing lines for the shower, sink, and simulated dish and clothes washer; 3 cold plumbing lines for the shower, sink, and simulated clothes washer; and 3 for the automated flushing mechanisms for the toilets and urinal (Fig. S1B–E)

Water demand and usage

The water flow schedule and usage of the system is described in Table S1. The automated programming consisted of a shower run once per day for 15 min, a simulated bathtub run once per week for 15 min, and simulated clothes and dishwasher run once per day for 2 and 11.5 min, respectively. The urinal and toilets were each flushed 5 times per day and each sink in the main room was run 17 times for both cold and hot water. The automated flushing program (e.g., frequency and duration) for the various fixtures occurred over a 16-hr period which allowed for an overnight 8-hr stagnation period. Water usage was calculated based on the flow rates measured at each fixture type (i.e., volume collected over time) as well as duration and frequency of use. Cold water usage was estimated to be 241.2 gallons per day (gpd) or 913 liters per day (lpd), hot water usage was 186.0 gpd (704.1 lpd), and mixed cold and hot water usage was 159.2 gpd (602.6 lpd), with a total estimated water usage of 586.4 gpd (2,219.8 lpd) for the entire PPS (Table S1).

Water usage, energy consumption, and pipe temperature monitoring

Water usage

An inline paddle wheel digital flow meter was used to monitor total daily water usage in the PPS (model WWM-P-200-PT, Assured Automation, Roselle, NJ, USA). This device was installed in line with the 2-inch (5.1-cm) diameter PVC pipe supplying municipally treated water to the PPS (Fig. S2A, green arrow). At week (wk) 14, an ultrasonic transit time water flow meter (model FDT-25, Omega Engineering, Inc., Norwalk, CT, USA), was also installed the main copper supply pipe (Fig. S2A and B, blue box). Digital readings of total water flow (in gallons) were recorded on regular business days during the entire study (Fig. S2C).

Energy consumption

Gas usage of HWH1, 2, and 3 was separately monitored using diaphragm gas submeters (model NG4, Norgas, Fairfield, Ohio, USA) installed inline approximately 1 m above each HWH (Fig. S2D and E). Electric usage of HWH4, 5, and 6 was separately monitored using watthour submeters (model ESP3, Ohio Semitronics, Inc., Hilliard, Ohio, USA) installed at the respective branch circuits (Fig. S2F). Meter readings were recorded on regular business days during the entire study.

Temperature monitoring

Water temperatures were indirectly measured and recorded every 10 seconds using resistive temperature devices (RTDs, model SA1-RTD, Omega Engineering, Inc., Norwalk, CT, USA). These surface temperature sensors were adhered around the exterior of copper plumbing pipes at 14 different locations (highlighted with yellow boxes in Fig. S3): around the outlet of each hot water heater (n = 6, Fig. S3A); the main Supply Line feeding the PPS (n = 1, Fig. S3B); one of the cold water lines supplying Sink4, used to represent Sink1–3, 5, and 6 (n = 1, Fig. S3C); and on each hot water lines supplying Sink1–6 (n = 6, Fig. S3D). In a 24-hour period, each sensor recorded approximately 8,640 temperature readings.

Sample collection

Table 1 describes the types of bulk water samples collected in this study. Three locations were sampled (notation is denoted in parentheses): Supply Line (Sup), Sink (C, for Cold water; H, for Hot water), and Shower (S). Sample notations were followed by a number 1 through 6, indicating the hot water heater number associated with, and/or supplying the hot water, at that sampling location. Sample notations were also followed by a lowercase a (indicating a first draw, or stagnant, water sample after an 8-hour overnight period of no water demand in the system) or lowercase b (indicating a flushed sample).

Approximately 1.5 L of Cold, Hot, and Shower water samples were collected in sterile 2 L plastic bottles. For each sample, two 50mL portions were immediately processed for pH, temperature, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), and conductivity as described in Section 2.5. Two 5-mL portions and a 20-mL portion were analyzed for free and total chlorine, and turbidity, respectively, as described in Section 2.5. One mL was placed into a microcentrifuge tube containing 10 μL of 10 % w/v sodium thiosulfate to neutralize any disinfectant residual. This neutralized sample was analyzed for heterotrophic plate count as described in Section 2.6. Aliquots of the bulk water samples were also placed into appropriate vessels and analyzed for total organic carbon (40 mL glass vials, with no headspace), disinfection byproduct (40 mL amber glass vials and 250 mL amber bottles, with no headspace), and adenosine triphosphate levels (50 mL conical tubes) as described in Section 2.7.

Once operation and water flow into the newly constructed PPS was initiated, bulk water samples were collected within 48 h during that first week of operation (time point wk1); then collected weekly, biweekly, or monthly thereafter (wk2, wk3, wk5, wk7, wk8, wk12, wk14, wk16, wk20, wk26) (Table 2). Table 2 also shows the type of sample collected and the water quality parameters analyzed for each type. Measured parameters are described in Sections 2.5, 2.6, and 2.7.

Water quality measurements

Oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) and conductivity were measured using portable handheld meters (model HI991003 and HI991301, respectively; Hanna Instruments Inc., Smithfield, RI, USA). Both instruments were capable of recording pH and temperature. Turbidity was measured using a portable turbidimeter (model 2100Q, Hach, Loveland, CO, USA). Total organic carbon was analyzed following United States Environmental Protection Agency Method 415.3 using an ultraviolet/persulfate analyzer (USEPA, 2009). Free and total chlorine concentrations were measured using the N,N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DPD) method (TNT 867 0.05–2.00 mg/L Cl2, Method 10232, Hach, Loveland, CO, USA) and the Hach DR900 portable colorimeter following manufacturer’s protocols.

Microbial analyses

Culturable heterotrophs in the bulk water samples were enumerated using the heterotrophic plate count (HPC) spread plate method. Briefly, samples were plated on Reasoner’s 2A (R2A) agar plates (Difco Laboratories, Detroit, MI, USA) and incubated at 28°C for 7 d (APHA AWWA WEF, 2023). The limit of detection for this method was 0.7 logarithmic colony forming units per milliliter (log10 CFU/mL).

ATP concentrations were measured using the Dendridiag SW test system (GLBiocontrol, Clapiers, France) following manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, 50 mL of the bulk water sample was passed through a 0.45-micron pore size syringe filter. The filter was exposed to a lysing agent and the contents were then placed in a tube containing an enzymatic mixture to release ATP energy in the form of light (bioluminescence). The emitted photons were measured using a handheld luminometer (model PD30, Kikkoman, Tokyo, Japan) where relative light units were converted to picograms of ATP per milliliter (pg/mL) based on an internal standard. The limit of detection for this method was 0.002 pg/mL.

DBP analysis

Bulk water samples were also collected for analysis of (i) THM4, including chloroform (TCM), bromodichloromethane (BDCM), dibromochloromethane (DBCM), and bromoform (TBM), following USEPA Method 524.2 (USEPA, 1995); and (ii) HAA9, including monochloroacetic acid (MCAA), dichloroacetic acid (DCAA), trichloroacetic acid (TCAA), monobromoacetic acid (MBAA), dibromoacetic acid (DBAA), tribromoacetic acid (TBAA), bromochloroacetic acid (BCAA), bromodichloroacetic acid (BDCAA), and dibromochloroacetic acid (DBCAA), following USEPA Method 552.3 (USEPA, 2003). Collected samples were preserved and shipped overnight, on ice, to a state-certified commercial laboratory and analyzed within 14 days.

Statistical analyses

Linear interpolation was used to calculate missing values for daily water, gas, and electric usage that were not recorded due to weekends and federal holidays. The Shapiro–Wilk D’Agostino-Pearson normality test was conducted to determine distribution of the data throughout that sampling period. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was conducted for hot water heater energy readings, water usage, and for each water quality measurement. Nonparametric Spearman correlation tests were conducted to determine relationships between paired water quality parameters (e.g., pH and temperature, free and total chlorine, HPC and ATP). Spearman correlation matrix analysis was performed between all water quality parameters and a heat map was generated based on the Spearman correlation coefficient (rs). Principal component analysis (PCA) was also performed to assess patterns and relationships between sample types and water quality parameters. P-values < 0.05 (at the 95 % confidence interval) were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism v10.2.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Evaluating the steady-state operation of the full-scale premise plumbing system (PPS)

As described in Section 2.3, water, gas, and electric usage was monitored daily in the PPS to assess the operation of timers, solenoid valves, and controllers; consistency and adherence to the water demand schedule; and performance of sensors, meters, and plumbing fixtures.

Water usage

Daily water usage within the PPS was estimated to be 586.4 gallons per day (gpd) or 2219.8 liters per day (lpd) (Table S1). From wk1 to wk14, an inline, paddle wheel based, water flow totalizer monitored daily water usage in the PPS (Fig. 2A, dotted blue line). However, usage was not being accurately measured due to the flow rates in the Supply Line not reaching the minimum specification range of the meter (≈ 20 gallons per minute [gpm], or 76 liters per minute [lpm]), even during periods of water demand. This inaccuracy resulted in large fluctuations observed for daily water usage values with minimum, maximum, and mean ± standard deviation (SD) being 0, 571, and 183±99 gpd or 0, 2161, and 693±375 lpd, respectively (Fig. 2A, dotted blue line). Thus, an ultrasonic, transit time flow meter was installed and brought online during wk14 to monitor daily water usage (Fig. S2B). The minimum, maximum, and mean±SD daily water usage values obtained between wk14 to wk26, were 350, 601, and 502 ± 56 gpd or 1325, 2275, and 1900±212 lpd (Fig. 2A, black line), which was within 76–95 % of the estimated daily water usage.

Energy usage

Fig. 2B shows the gas usage, in cubic feet (CF), for the instantaneous and two tanked gas hot water heaters - HWH1 (gray line), HWH2 (blue line), and HWH3 (orange line), respectively. The minimum, maximum, and mean±SD daily gas usage values for HWH1 were 12, 34, 23±4 CF; for HWH2 20, 52, 35±5 CF; and for HWH3 26, 60, 43±7 CF (or 0.3, 1.0, 0.6±0.1 for HWH1; 0.6, 1.5, 1.0±0.1 for HWH2; and 0.7, 1.7, 1.2±0.2 cubic meters for HWH3, respectively). Differences in gas usage between the two tanked HWHs were statistically significant (HWH2 vs HWH3, P < 0.0001) as well as usage between the instantaneous and both tanked heaters, as expected (P < 0.0001). Fig. 2C shows the electric usage, in kilowatt-hour (kWh), for the two tanked and the instantaneous electric hot water heaters – HWH4 (pink line), HWH5 (green line), and HWH6 (black line), respectively. No electric usage data were available between wk1 and wk9 due to recording errors with the watthour submeters. Thus, for wk10 through 26, the minimum, maximum, and mean±SD values for HWH4 were 4.8, 8.5, 6.7±1.0 kWh; for HWH5 4.5, 7.5, 5.8 ±0.8 kWh; and for HWH6 4.4, 8.3, 6.4±1.0 kWh.

Water temperature monitoring using resistive temperature devices (RTDs)

Externally mounted RTDs were installed throughout the PPS to monitor water temperatures every 10 seconds. In Fig. 3, representative temperature profiles are shown for a 24-hour time period during wk2 and wk24, which represent early and late sampling time points evaluated in this study. Profiles associated with Sink3 and HWH3 are shown in Fig. 3, with the rest shown in Fig. S4. Each panel displays the temperature profiles of the Supply Line water (gray line) and Cold Line water (dark blue line). Notably, the mean±SD values of the Cold Line water temperature, during the entire 24-hour period at wk2 and wk24 were 21.7±1.0 and 21.5±0.8°C, respectively, while the differences in the Supply water temperature at wk2 and wk24 were statistically significant (P < 0.0001, 25.0±1.0 vs 17.8±1.1°C, respectively). The multiple peaks observed in the Cold Line coincided with the temperature spikes detected during the programmed runs for Sink1 through Sink6 (Fig. 3A–F, dark blue line, and Table S1) with the peak profiles reflecting seasonal temperature effects.

The PPS is an enclosed facility within a much larger facility. The Supply Line is contained within the mostly stable temperature environment of this larger facility (Fig. S2A–B), while the enclosed PPS facility is climate controlled, which caused differences in the temperature peak profiles of the Cold Line noted above. Specifically, at wk2, which coincided with the warm season in the US, warmer, cold water from the Supply Line entered the PPS causing an increase in temperature during the periods of cold water demand in the air-conditioned PPS (Fig. 3A, wk2 dark blue line vs gray line). Conversely, at wk24, which coincided with the cooler season, colder, cold water from the Supply Line entered the PPS causing a decrease in temperature during the periods of cold water demand in the heated PPS (Fig. 3B, wk24 dark blue line vs gray line).

At the outlets of HWH1 through HWH6, the temperature spikes detected expectedly coincided with the sink runs, along with the two additional peaks due to the morning shower run (Fig. 3 and Fig. S4, black down arrow) and early-late afternoon dish and clothes washer runs, which ran consecutively, thus appearing as a single peak (Fig. 3 and Fig. S4, blue down arrow).

Water quality measurements

Bulk water samples (described in Section 2.4) were analyzed for pH, temperature, total organic carbon (TOC), turbidity, oxidation-reduction potential (ORP), and conductivity (Table 1). The mean±standard deviation (SD) for water quality measurements at all time points are plotted in Fig. 4 and listed in Table S2.

pH and temperature

There were no statistical differences in pH levels between stagnant (C1–6a) and flushed (C1–6b) Cold water samples (Fig. 4A and Table S2) with mean±SD values higher than those observed in the Supply Line (8.85±0.05). Moreover, mean±SD Cold water pH levels, for all stagnant and flushed samples (8.92±1.16 and 8.91±0.20, respectively), were generally higher than pH levels in the stagnant and flushed hot water samples (H1–6a, 8.92±0.24 and H1–6b, 8.82±0.24, respectively) and the 10–15 sec and 5–10 min flushed Shower water samples (S1–6b, 8.81 ±0.23 and S1–6c, 8.65±0.22, respectively). Fig. 4A also displays temperatures measured during bulk water collection. Statistical increases were observed between respective H2–5a and H2–5b, and S1–6b and S1–6c samples (P < 0.0001, Table 2).

Total organic carbon (TOC) and turbidity

Fig. 4B displays TOC and turbidity measurements for all bulk water samples except S1–6c samples which were not analyzed for these parameters. There were no statistical differences in TOC or turbidity levels noted between samples collected in this study. Mean±SD TOC level and turbidity in Supply Line samples were 0.90±0.34 mg/L and 0.13±0.05 NTU (Fig. 4B and Table S2). TOC levels in both stagnant Cold and Hot water samples were slightly higher than their respective flushed samples, except for the C1a and C1b samples, but not statistically different. For turbidity, flushed Hot water samples had higher NTU levels than their respective stagnant samples, except for the H1a and H1b samples supplied by the gas instantaneous HWH (Fig. 4B and Table S2); however, these trends were not statistically significant and correlations to HWH type could not be made. H5b samples displayed the highest mean±SD NTU at 0.98±0.39 due to four high measurements at wk1 (1.51 NTU), wk14 (2.26 NTU), wk16 (1.64 NTU), and wk20 (4.06 NTU), without which H5b levels would be 0.18±0.03 NTU. C1a and b, C2a and b, and C3a and b samples showed an increase in turbidity after flushing, but C4a and b, C5a and b, and C6a and b showed a decrease (Fig. 4B and Table S2).

Oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) and conductivity

The mean±SD for ORP and conductivity levels of the Supply Line samples were 444±60 millivolts (mV) and 437±43 μS/cm, respectively (Fig. 4C and Table S2). ORP levels increased in the C1–6b and S1–6c samples compared to their respective C1–6a and S1–6b samples; however, only C3a vs C3b and C6a vs C6b samples were statistically significant (P < 0.001, Table S2). Conductivity levels between each C1– 6a, H1–6a, and S1–6b samples were also not statistically different from their respective C1–6b, H1–6b, and S1–6c samples (Fig. 4C and Table S2). However, changes in ORP and conductivity levels in stagnant vs flushed hot water samples seemed to correlate with HWH type. Specifically, increases in ORP and decreases in conductivity were observed between stagnant and flushed Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous HWHs (H1a vs H1b and H6a vs H6b), while decreases in ORP and increases in conductivity were observed in samples supplied by tanked HWHs (H2–5a vs H2–5b) (Fig. 4C and Table S2).

Disinfectant residual and microbial water quality measurements

Free and total chlorine measurements

Fig. 5A displays free and total chlorine levels for all samples with mean±SD Supply levels at 0.76±0.20 and 0.78±0.20 mg/L, respectively (Table S3). Despite an overnight water stagnation period of 8 hours, levels between respective stagnant and flushed Cold water samples were not statistically different, except between C5a and C5b samples (P < 0.05). Residual levels in all flushed Cold water samples were not statistically different from Supply Line levels, except free and total chlorine levels in C2b samples (0.54±0.24 and 0.57±0.24 mg/L, respectively, P < 0.05) and C6b free chlorine levels (0.85±0.22 mg/L, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A and Table S3). In contrast, levels in all stagnant and flushed Hot water samples were statistically lower than Supply levels (P < 0.05), except H1b and H6b samples since hot water for these samples was supplied from instantaneous HWHs and flushed samples would be drawn from the Supply Line. Supply disinfectant levels were also significantly higher than Shower water samples supplied from tanked HWHs (Supply vs S2b, S3b, or S5b, P < 0.05; except Supply vs S4b, P = 0.24) (Fig. 5A).

Free and total chlorine levels in both stagnant and flushed H1 and H6 samples were significantly higher than those in Hot water samples supplied by tanked water heaters (H2–5, P < 0.05). Moreover, disinfectant levels in H2a, H2b, H3a, and H3b samples (supplied by tanked, gas powered HWHs) were lower than those in the respective stagnant and flushed H4a, H4b, H5a, and H5b samples (supplied by tanked electric powered HWHs) (Fig. 5A). Free and total chlorine levels in combined stagnant H2a and H3a samples (0.24±0.15 and 0.26±0.15 mg/L, respectively) vs H4a and H5a samples (0.34±0.15 and 0.37±0.15 mg/L, respectively) were statistically different (P < 0.001). Similar free and total chlorine level trends were observed in combined flushed samples: H2b and H3b (0.25±0.19 and 0.27±0.18 mg/L, respectively) vs H4b and H5b (0.35±0.22 and 0.38±0.21 mg/L, respectively) (P < 0.05).

Heterotrophic plate count (HPC) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) measurements

To monitor changes in microbial water quality, culturable heterotrophs were enumerated in bulk water samples using the heterotrophic plate count (HPC) method. Fig. 5B displays mean±SD log10 CFU/mL HPC levels with mean±SD levels in the Supply Line measured at 3.82 ±0.40 log10 CFU/mL. There were no significant differences in HPC levels between respective stagnant vs flushed Hot water (P > 0.29) or Cold water samples (P > 0.99) (Fig. 5B and Table S3). However, significantly lower HPC levels were observed in all Shower and Hot water samples compared to those in the Supply Line (P < 0.02); except for S1b, S5b, H4a, and H5a samples (P > 0.17). There were significant differences between Cold and Hot samples for approximately half of the sample types (e.g., C1a vs H1a, C1b vs H1b, C3a vs H3a, C3b vs H3b, C4b vs H4b, C6a vs H6a, and C6b vs H6b, P < 0.01). However, there were no significant data differences or patterns to make inferences regarding impact of HWH type or stagnant and flushed samples on observed HPC levels.

Active microorganisms were indirectly quantified via measurements of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) molecules, which are the source of energy used for many essential cellular processes. Fig. 5B displays mean ±SD picograms of ATP per milliliter (pg/mL) with levels in Supply Line samples at 0.29±0.17 pg/mL (Fig. 5B and Table S3). There were no significant differences in ATP levels between respective stagnant vs flushed Cold water samples (P > 0.35) or between respective stagnant vs flushed Hot water samples (P > 0.54), except H3a vs H3b samples (0.07 ±0.06 vs 0.02±0.01 pg/mL, P < 0.02). Generally, most Hot water samples displayed decreases in ATP levels post flushing, except H2b; while mixed results were observed in Cold water samples (e.g., C1b, C2b, and C6b samples showed increases in ATP levels and C3b, C4b, and C5b showed decreases post flushing) (Fig. 5B).

Disinfection byproduct (DBP) analyses

As described in Table 2, DBPs were measured in select bulk water samples collected at five timepoints throughout the study period: wk1, wk2, wk7, wk14, and wk26. The focus for this study was to monitor DBP levels in Hot and Shower water samples; however, stagnant Cold water samples were collected once at wk1 to serve as an initial reference point for DBP levels between Cold water samples collected at each respective sink. Levels of total trihalomethane (TTHM) levels and nine haloacetic acids (HAA9), which includes the regulated 5 (HAA5) and 4 unregulated HAAs, are shown in Fig. 6A.

Regulated total trihalomethane (TTHM) quantification

There were no statistical differences between Supply TTHM levels (27.9±5.1 μg/L) and stagnant and flushed Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous HWHs: H1a, 29.4±2.5; H1b, 29.5±5.0; H6a, 29.3±3.6; and H6b, 23.8±2.0 μg/L (P > 0.99, Fig. 6A). However, Supply levels were statistically lower than those in stagnant and flushed Hot water samples supplied by tanked HWHs: H2a, 51.7±14.2; H2b, 56.3±7.2; H3a, 50.8±15.4; H3b, 55.5±7.3; H4a, 45.1±10.0; H4b, 50.9±8.1; H5a, 46.9±10.7; and H5b, 53.4±7.2 μg/L) (P < 0.0001, Fig. 6A and Table S4). Fig. 6B shows mean±SD TTHM levels in μg/L for each sample type with stacked bars displaying levels of the four chemicals included in the TTHM group - bromodichloromethane, bromoform, dibromochloromethane, and chloroform. The differences in TTHM levels between these samples were driven by the statistically higher levels of chloroform (P < 0.0002), as there were no significant differences in bromodichloromethane, bromoform, or dibromochloromethane levels between respective Supply and Hot water samples (P > 0.99, Table S4). For H2–5 samples derived from tanked HWHs, TTHM levels generally increased post-flushing from 45.1–51.7 μg/L in H2–5a samples to 50.9–56.3 μg/L in H2–5b samples (Fig. 6A), but was not significant, with the instantaneous HWH samples, H1 and H6, displaying mixed results.

There were no significant differences between Supply TTHM levels and each Shower sample (Supply vs S1–6b, P > 0.58). However, there were significantly lower levels in S2–5b samples compared to their respective flushed H2–5 samples (P < 0.01, Fig. 6A). Additionally, compared to Supply levels, all stagnant Cold water samples had higher levels of chloroform (12.0–14.9 vs 8.8 μg/L), lower levels of bromodichloromethane (6.6–7.0 vs 8.1 μg/L) and dibromochloromethane (4.6–5.2 vs 8.3 μg/L), and absence of bromoform (not detected vs 2.8 μg/L) (Fig. 6B and Table S4). However, only one set of Cold water samples was analyzed and thus, no conclusive interpretations could be made.

Haloacetic acid (HAA) quantification

Water samples were also analyzed for 9 HAAs, collectively referred to as HAA9, which includes the group of 5 regulated HAAs, termed HAA5, and is comprised of monochloroacetic acid, MCAA; dichloroacetic acid, DCAA; trichloracetic acid, TCAA; monobromoacetic acid, MBAA; and dibromoacetic acid, DBAA. HAA9 also includes a group of four unregulated HAAs: bromochloroacetic acid, BCAA; bromodichloroacetic acid, BDCAA; dibromochloroacetic acid, DBCAA; and tribromoacetic acid, TBAA. Fig. 6C shows the levels of each as stacked bars for each sample type. MCAA, MBAA, and TBAA were not detected in any samples.

No statistical differences were observed in the levels of HAA5, four unregulated HAAs, and HAA9 between any sample type (P > 0.9). HAA5 mean±SD μg/L levels in Supply (6.1±2.3), H1a and b (7.9±3.8 and 6.6 ±2.8), H6a and b (9.0±3.6 and 5.9±2.1), S1b (5.6±1.4), and S6b (4.9 ±2.0) samples were generally lower than those observed in H2–5a (mean ranging from 10.5–10.8 μg/L), H2–5b (10.4–12.6 μg/L), and S2–5b (8.2–10.3) samples (Fig. 6C and Table S5).

C1–6a samples had higher HAA5 levels compared to levels in their respective Hot and Shower water samples and the Supply (Fig. 6C). Similar HAA9 levels were observed between Cold water and the respective Hot and Shower water supplied by tanked HWHs; while higher HAA9 levels in Cold water samples were observed when compared to the respective Hot and Shower water samples supplied by instantaneous HWHs (Table S5). However, as noted above, only one set of Cold water samples was analyzed and thus, no conclusive interpretations could be made.

Temporal effects were also noted for certain sample types. DBCAA was detected only once in the Supply at wk1 (Fig. 6C, pink bar). TCAA was detected in C1–6a and H1a samples collected at wk1, in H2–6a collected at wk1 and wk2, and in H2–5b samples collected at wk2 (Fig. 6C, light blue bar). BDCAA was only detected in the Supply, C2a, C4a, H4a, C6a, and H6b samples collected only at wk1 and twice in H1a samples collected at wk1 and wk26 (Fig. 6C, white bar). DCAA, DBAA, and BCAA were consistently detected in all samples and respective sampling time points (Fig. 6C, brown, gray, and dark blue bars, respectively).

Correlations between sample type and water quality parameters

To determine if there were significant associations between water quality parameters analyzed in the different sample types, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated for Cold, Shower, and Hot water samples (Fig. 7A–C, respectively). As expected, there were perfect positive correlations between free and total chlorine levels in all samples (r = 1.00, P < 0.01). There were also strong positive correlations between ORP and free and total chlorine (r > 0.60) and strong negative correlations between HPC and temperature (r < −0.59) for each sample type, but those correlations were only statistically significant for Cold and Hot water samples (P < 0.05) and not Shower samples (P = 0.10).

For Cold water samples only, significant positive correlations were observed between temperature and free and total chlorine (r = 0.64 and 0.62, P = 0.03), pH and HAA5 (r = 0.94, P < 0.02), and HPC and TTHM (r = 0.94, P < 0.02), while negative correlations were observed between temperature and both HAA groups (r = −0.94 and −0.93, P < 0.02), HPC and free and total chlorine (r = −0.62 and −0.59, P < 0.05) and between HPC and ORP (r = −0.78, P = 0.002) (Fig. 7A). For Shower samples only, negative correlations were observed between pH and temperature (r = −0.75, P = 0.006) and ATP and TOC (r = −0.89, P = 0.03) (Fig. 7B). For Hot water samples only, positive correlations were observed between turbidity and TTHM (r = 0.67, P = 0.02) and between conductivity and each DBP group (r = 0.64–0.67, P < 0.03), while a negative correlation was observed between TOC and turbidity (r = −0.69, P = 0.017) (Fig. 7C).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to further delineate patterns and relationships between water quality measurements and sample type. Fig. 7D is a PCA biplot showing the principal component scores of each water sample (colored dots) and the loading plots of each water quality parameter as vectors. Samples with similar water quality profiles are clustered together in three distinct groups notably encompassing all Cold stagnant samples (open oval), Shower and Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous (gray filled oval), and Shower and Hot water samples supplied by tank HWHs (dark gray filled oval) (Fig. 7D). No clear separation of samples supplied from electric- and gas-powered tank HWHs was observed.

Discussion

In this study, the duration and frequency of use for each fixture type in the full-scale premise plumbing system (PPS) was modeled based on a published survey of over 23,700 homes across the United States and Canada (DeOreo et al., 2016). The survey reported the percentage of indoor household water usage by fixture type (with the percentage modeled in this study in parentheses) as: shower 20 % (21 %), bath 3 % (3 %), dishwasher 1 % (6 %), clothes washer 17 % (30 %), toilet flushing 24 % (15 %), faucet 19 % (24 %), leaks 12 % (−), and other 4 % (−), which includes evaporative cooling, humidification, water softening, and other uncategorized indoor uses (DeOreo et al., 2016). Leaks and other uses were incorporated into the simulated dish washer (SDW) and simulated clothes washer (SCW) water usage. Although 15 flushes were programmed daily over a 14-hr period, water use for flushing was lower in this study (15 vs 24 %) most likely due to more water efficient toilets and the inclusion of a urinal (T2, Fig. 1), which generally uses less water than toilets. The gallons per flush (gpf) (with liters per flush, lpf, in parentheses) of T1, T2, and T3 were 1.28 (4.8), 0.75 (2.8), and 1.35 (5.1), respectively (Fig. 1), which were much lower than the average of 2.6 gpf (9.8 lpf) reported in 2016 and 3.65 gpf (13.8 lpf) reported in 1999 (DeOreo et al., 2016). The flushing frequency remained the same at 5.0 flushes per person in 1999 and 2016.

Total daily water usage in the model PPS was estimated to be approximately 580 gallons per day (gpd) or 2195 liters per day (lpd) (Table S1). However, considering only the water usage of a single hot water heater (HWH) and its associated sink and excluding Shower, SDW, and SCW fixture runs from all other HWHs, the total water usage, reflecting a single household, would be 110.8 gpd (419.4 lpd), close to the reported 138 gallons per household day (gphd), or 522 liters per household day (lphd) (DeOreo et al., 2016). Total household hot and cold water usage was also reported at 45.5 and 92.5 gphd, or 172.2 and 350.2 lphd, respectively, compared to total hot and cold water usage in this study of 43.2 and 67.6 gpd, or 163.5 and 255.9 lpd, respectively. However, this study did not incorporate outdoor residential usages such as landscape irrigation, filling swimming pools, and washing pavements and cars.

Water heating accounts for approximately 23, 18, and 15 % of residential energy consumption in Australia, the United States, and European Union, respectively (DCCEEW, 2020, Eurostat, 2023, USDOE, 2024) with a global estimate of 44 % (IEA, 2023). Although heat pumps water heaters are a more energy efficient and low carbon technology with increasing market penetration (Rosenow et al., 2022, USEPA, 2022), most residential homes in the US continue to utilize storage and tankless HWHs (USEIA, 2023, USEPA, 2022). Specifically, of the estimated 123,530,000 homes in the US based on a 2020 survey, 93 % of homes utilized tank HWHs and 6.5 % tankless, or instantaneous HWHs; and the type of energy used for all water heaters was 48 % natural gas, 46 % electricity, and 6 % other (propane, fuel oil, kerosene, and other fuel types) (USEIA, 2023).

Gas usage for HWH1, HWH2, and HWH3 averaged 23, 35, and 43 cubic feet (CF), respectively, from wk1 to wk 26 (Fig. 2B), while electric usage for HWH4, HWH5, and HWH6 averaged 6.7, 5.8, and 6.4 kilowatt•hour (kWh), respectively (Fig. 2C). Excluding delivery and distribution charges and riders, gas rates during this study period were $0.0045 per CF, while electric rates for demand and energy charges were $4.58 per kW and $0.05756 per kWh, respectively. Thus, average daily energy costs for HWH1 were $0.10, HWH2 $0.16, HWH3 $0.19, HWH4 $1.66, HWH5 $1.43, and HWH6 $1.59. The higher energy costs for electric powered HWHs agree with other studies reporting higher annual operating, maintenance, and life-cycle costs of, and total greenhouse gas emissions from, electric instantaneous and tank HWHs compared to gas powered and heat pump HWHs (Keinath and Garimella, 2017, Kumar and Mathew, 2018). Thus, based on the design, components, and operation of the PPS used in this study, this system represents a relevant and comprehensive model for systematic investigations of drinking water quality, contamination, and exposure in residential premise plumbing environments.

Premise plumbing water quality is impacted by upstream distribution system water quality. Discoloration, release of heavy metals, increased corrosivity, and changes in microbial profiles within PPSs have been associated with changes in source water and water treatment processes, loss of disinfectant residual, switch in disinfectant type, pressure fluctuations, distribution system residence times, and main breaks, to list a few (Buse and Mistry, 2024, Craun et al., 2010, Edwards and Dudi, 2004, Kunz et al., 2024, Liu et al., 2017). Moreover, as discussed in Section 1, inherent characteristics and deficiencies in PPSs can lead to illnesses and fatalities, such as from Legionella infections; and due to the heightened awareness of these risks, there are numerous guidance and technical documents, standards, and toolkits on PPS water quality management (ASHRAE, 2020, ASHRAE, 2021, ASHRAE, 2023, CDC, 2021, ECDC, 2017, NASEM, 2020, WHO, 2022).

Specifically for Legionella control, water temperatures should be maintained outside the range of 25–50°C (77–122°F), preferably 20–50°C (68–122°F), to prevent growth of the organism (WHO, 2022). Additionally, a HWH setpoint of >60°C (140°F) with hot water temperatures >50°C (122°F) throughout the pipe network or >55°C (131°F) at the point of use has been recommended (NASEM, 2020, WHO, 2022), but there is a significant risk of scald injuries to the elderly and children (Shields et al., 2023, USCPSC, 2020). Most homeowners will either (1) defer to the installer or plumber, who may or may not be knowledgeable about premise plumbing water quality issues, current standards and guidance, or applicable plumbing and public health codes; (2) follow the HWH manufacturer’s set point recommendations (typically between 49–60°C or 120–140°F); and/or (3) adjust the setpoint based on their priorities (e.g., hot water usage needs, concerns about scalding, energy savings) (IPC, 2021, Lévesque et al., 2004, Wilson et al., 2015).

In this study, each HWH had a manufacturer default setting of 49°C (120°F), except for HWH3 due to the temperature control knob also being used to light the pilot; thus, the default setpoint was used. At wk2 and wk24, the temperature data from the RTDs installed around each HWH outlet showed that HWH1 outlet temperatures reached a maximum of 49.0°C (120°F), for HWH2 53.2°C (128°F), for HWH3 56.7°C (134°F), for HWH4 46.2°C (115°F), for HWH5 46.7°C (116°F), and for HWH6 49.8°C (122°F) (Fig. 3, Fig. S4). The lower temperatures for the electric tank HWHs (HWH4 and HWH5) are consistent with other studies reporting 25–40°C temperature gradients between the top and bottom of electric tank HWHs, even at a higher set point of 60°C (140°F), and with water temperatures (~30–40°C, 86–104°F) at the bottom being highly predictive of Legionella contamination (Alary and Joly, 1991, Brazeau and Edwards, 2013, Healy, 2008, Lévesque et al., 2004).

For the samples in this study, mean levels of each parameter and contaminant did not exceed the highest level reported by the utility in their CCR (Section 2.1.4), except for turbidity, TTHM, and HAA5 (STable 2–5). Mean NTU levels for all samples were higher than 0.11 NTU, except for H6a, which had a mean of 0.11 NTU. H2b, H3b, and H5b samples displayed mean TTHM levels of 56.3, 55.5, and 53.4 μg/L, respectively, and H2b had a mean HAA5 level of 12.6 μg/L (Fig. 6, Table S4–5). As stated in Section 3.3.1, free and total chlorine levels were the only parameters that showed statistical differences between gas and electric tank HWHs. Free and total chlorine levels in H2–3a and H2–3b samples (supplied by gas tank HWHs) were statistically lower than the respective H4–5a and H4–5b samples (supplied by electric tank HWHs), which could be due to the large temperature gradients within electric tank HWHs as mentioned previously, resulting in overall lower water temperatures and slower decay of disinfectants compared to the environment within the gas tank HWHs (Hua et al., 1999). Other factors such as presence of organics, sediment, biofilm, and corrosion products, can also impact chlorine decay (Fisher et al., 2012) especially within the HWH environment but were not investigated in this study.

Highest TTHM levels were observed in both stagnant and flushed hot water samples supplied by gas and electric tank HWHs (46.9–56.3 μg/L), while HAA5 and HAA9 levels in all samples ranged from 4.9–13.3 and 7.5–17.9 μg/L, respectively (Fig. 6, Table S4–5). Among the HAA9, the first five are currently regulated by the USEPA as HAA5, though the other 4 HAAs are of regulatory interest due to potential health impacts, especially for BCAA and BDCAA, which are mixed bromine-chlorine species, that have shown some carcinogenic effects in rodents (Peterson et al., 2023) and were the two unregulated HAAs that were detected most often in study samples (Table S5). DBP trends in this study were similar to those in a previous study showing higher average increases in TTHM levels (Δ+64 μg/L) and lower changes in HAAs (Δ+26 μg/L) when comparing residential flushed hot water samples collected at temperatures of 55°C (131°F) to distribution system DBP levels (Dion-Fortier et al., 2009). Another study also reported DBP levels in residential stagnant (morning first draw) and flushed (afternoon collection) hot water samples (53–64°C [127–147°F]) and observed TTHM levels in stagnant and flushed of 121 and 120 μg/L, respectively; and HAA9 in both stagnant and flushed, 88 μg/L (Chowdhury et al., 2011). However, in both studies, distribution system TTHM and HAA9 levels were higher (ranges of 23–84 and 28–108 μg/L, respectively) than those reported in the CCR associated with this study, thus, possibly contributing to higher DBP levels observed in their PPS samples.

DBP formation and concentration is dependent on multiple factors such as pH, temperature, TOC, disinfectant type, and residence time with formation potential of TTHM reportedly increasing under basic pH conditions and HAA increasing under acidic pH conditions (Krasner, 2009, Nikolaou et al., 2004, Sinha et al., 2021). However, there were only weak to moderate positive correlations, which were statistically insignificant, between pH or temperature with each DBP group in Hot and Shower water samples (r = 0.26–0.49, P > 0.12), but strong to very strong negative correlations were observed between TTHM, HAA5, and HAA9, free and total chlorine (r ≤ −0.82, P < 0.02), and ORP in those samples (r ≤ −0.77, P < 0.03, Fig. 7B–C). ORP has been used to assess various treatment processes and for chemical dosing control during water treatment; and decreases in ORP levels can be due to microbial respiration (i.e., oxygen consumption) and microbial responses to heat, starvation, or hypoosmotic stress (Copeland and Lytle, 2014, Oktyabrskii and Smirnova, 2012, Yu and Cheng, 2003).

ORP increased post flushing for all Cold, Shower, and Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous HWHs, but decreased in samples supplied by tank HWHs (H2–5b) (Fig. 4C, Table S2). Mean ORP levels in stagnant samples started out at significantly lower levels in samples supplied by tanked HWHs compared to instantaneous HWHs (combined H2–5a, 239 mV, vs combined H1a and H6a, 425 mV, P < 0.0001), with levels decreasing significantly further in flushed samples (combined H2–5b, 158 mV, vs combined H1b and H6b, 466 mV, P <0.0001). This trend was also noted for the 10–15 sec flushed shower water samples (combined S2–5b, 343 mV, vs combined S1b and S6b, 514 mV, P < 0.01). However, ORP levels increased in all S1–6c samples, most likely due to mixing with cold water, but levels in the 5–10 min flushed shower samples supplied by tanked HWHs were still significantly lower than those supplied by instantaneous HWHs (combined S2–5c, 403 mV, vs combined S1b and S6b, 531 mV, P < 0.05).

Microbial concentrations were enumerated by HPC analysis and quantification of cellular ATP levels. Mean HPC concentrations in combined stagnant Hot water samples from both instantaneous and tank HWHs were higher than in flushed samples, with combined values associated with tank HWHs being statistically significant (mean log10 CFU/mL: combined H1a and H6a, 2.86, vs H1b and H6b, 2.80; and H2–5a, 3.17 vs H2–5b, 2.68, P = 0.0002). Similarly, mean ATP levels in stagnant and flushed Hot water samples from instantaneous HWHs were 0.12 and 0.7 pg/mL, respectively, and from tank HWHs were 0.10 and 0.04 pg/mL, respectively. Although there were weak to moderate positive correlations observed between HPC and ATP levels in all water samples, they were not statistically significant (r =0.36 to 0.44, P > 0.07, Fig. 7A–B). Higher HPC levels in stagnant cold water has been reported previously for residential and building water systems, with mean ranges of 2.2–3.0 log10 CFU/mL in stagnant cold water and not detected-1.0 log10 CFU/mL in flushed cold water (Lautenschlager et al., 2010, Park et al., 2023). In those same studies, the reported mean ATP levels ranged from 6–39 pg/mL and 1–20 pg/mL in stagnant and flushed cold water samples, respectively (Lautenschlager et al., 2010, Park et al., 2023). The large ranges of ATP detected in this study and others are most likely due different sample volumes processed and commercial ATP kits that vary in cell lysis methodologies, reagents, and sensitivity. However, comparisons of relative differences and changes in ATP concentrations between sample types can be a useful tool for rapid microbial monitoring and water quality assessments (van der Wielen et al., 2023, Vang et al., 2014).

Supportive conditions for microbial growth in stagnant water include disinfectant decay, ambient temperatures, type of fixture and usage history, undisturbed flow, and prolonged residence time (Lautenschlager et al., 2010, Lipphaus et al., 2014, Montagnino et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2022). Concordantly, although (1) temperatures for the Cold and Hot water lines ranged between 22–23°C (72–73°F) for 8 hours prior to sample collection (Cold Line vs Sink1–6, Fig. 3); (2) pipes were of the same material (Section 2.1.3, Fig. S1A); and (3) fixture usage frequency was identical (Table S1), stagnant Cold water samples contained higher disinfectant and HPC levels compared to all Hot stagnant samples (Fig. 5, Table S3). These results collectively suggest that microbial activity (e.g., ATP, HPC) and processes (e.g., potentially indicated by ORP), water temperature and usage, DBP formation potential, and disinfectant decays within plumbing, devices, and appliances are interconnected and that interactions between variables can influence others and indicates dynamic and intricate processes that warrant further investigations. Other water quality monitoring was conducted in this study that focused on culture, isolation, and molecular analyses of opportunistic pathogens, such as Legionella, nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), and Pseudomonas, as well and yeast and fungi, to further evaluate the impact of PPS engineering and operational parameters on microbial water quality. Those results will be reported in forthcoming publications.

Moreover, future research utilizing this full-scale PPS will examine long-term biofilm accumulation and community structure established on pipe sections made of different plumbing materials and evaluation of water quality management strategies and technology (e.g., flushing, point of use and point of entry devices) to ensure water quality. Like drinking water distribution system infrastructure, PPSs can become contaminated with chemical or biological agents after an intentional or accidental contamination event. This full-scale PPS will be a useful and critical tool for the continued holistic evaluation of the engineering and operational parameters, that along with the research results presented here, will help fill knowledge gaps regarding contaminant occurrence and persistence in PPSs so that water quality can be better managed and maintained.

Conclusions

Physicochemical (pH, temperature, conductivity, oxidation-reduction potential, total organic carbon, turbidity), chemical (free and total chlorine, disinfection byproducts), and microbial (heterotrophic plate count, adenosine triphosphate) water quality parameters were monitored in this study for over 26 weeks. Water quality differences revealed that samples clustered into three distinct groups: all Cold stagnant samples, Shower and Hot water samples supplied by instantaneous HWHs (dark gray filled oval), and Shower and Hot water samples supplied by tank HWHs, with the latter group showing no separation based on the energy source used by the HWH. Water quality of Cold and Hot water samples, supplied either by instantaneous or tank HWH samples, were distinct and driven by differences in temperature, HPC/ATP, DBPs, conductivity, and disinfectant residual. Strong negative correlations between disinfection byproducts, ORP, and free chlorine were also observed in Hot and Shower water samples. Moreover, water quality was impacted after an 8-hr period of water stagnation, as physiochemical, chemical, and microbial differences were observed between respective stagnant and flushed HWH samples. Thus, water age and temperature, fixture type, and HWH system type heavily influenced chemical and microbial water quality. Results from this study and others will continue to help inform industry, practitioners, trade groups, researchers, regulators, and end users on how premise plumbing engineering and operational parameters can be designed and managed to ensure water quality and minimize human exposure to drinking water contaminants.
