Microbiota as a modulator of drug response: targeting microbial–drug crosstalk in cancer therapy
Authors: Tianhao Zhao, Mingren Liu, Lei Qiu, Hao Zhang, Nan Zhang, Zaoqu Liu, Zhiwei Xia, Peng Luo, Jun Huang, Quan Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years, accumulating evidence has established mechanistic connections between the microbiota and the efficacy of anticancer treatments, encompassing microbial communities residing in the gut, within tumors, and across other tissue sites. Across diverse malignancies, distinct compositional and functional profiles of these microbial ecosystems have been associated with differential responses to chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted agents, as observed in both clinical settings and experimental systems. It is now understood that microbial communities exert influence on drug activity through multiple pathways: they may compromise therapeutic efficacy by metabolic inactivation or structural modification of anticancer compounds, or, conversely, potentiate responses by enhancing tumor cell susceptibility. Furthermore, these communities modulate the tumor immune microenvironment, fostering antitumor immunity through augmented antigen presentation and effector cell function, or constraining immune responses via regulatory networks and immunosuppressive myeloid polarization. The crosstalk between microbial cues and signaling networks in malignant and surrounding stromal tissues is widely thought to influence therapeutic outcomes. Key challenges ahead involve proving causation, elucidating context-dependent mechanisms, and translating patient heterogeneity into robust biomarkers and targeted therapies. Anticancer interventions can reportedly alter microbial community structure and function, with therapy-associated dysbiosis potentially contributing to primary or acquired resistance. Microbiota-directed strategies, including transplantation, defined microbial consortia, targeted delivery systems, and lifestyle interventions, are under investigation, yet challenges remain in establishing causality, ensuring safety, and enabling patient stratification. Collectively, these insights establish a translational framework for rationally designed microbiota‒drug combination strategies aimed at enhancing anticancer therapeutic outcomes.Keywords: Gut microbiota, intratumoral microbiota, drug resistance, tumor immunotherapy, microbiota metabolites, microbiota-targeted therapy

Introduction

The human microbiota comprises bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea that inhabit multiple body sites, including the gut, oral cavity, respiratory tract, urogenital tract, and skin. The gut microbiota (GM) is the largest community and exhibits substantial diversity in composition.1 Rather than serving only as a commensal companion, the microbiota acts as a functional partner that shapes host immunity, metabolism, and inflammatory signaling.2-4 When this community is disrupted, dysbiosis has been linked to cancer development and progression, affecting malignant growth, metastatic dissemination, and responses to anticancer therapies.5,6

Neoplasms are commonly divided into solid and nonsolid tumors. Nonsolid malignancies disseminate through the bone marrow and bloodstream, are not amenable to resection, and depend on drug treatment. Solid tumors are more often treated with combinations of surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic agents. Immunotherapy and chemotherapy remain the main drug-based approaches in modern oncology,7 and they have produced meaningful benefits in colorectal cancer (CRC), liver cancer, melanoma, lung cancer (LC), and other malignancies.8-10 Immunotherapy has reshaped cancer care by enabling durable responses in a subset of patients. For example, checkpoint blockade restores T-cell activity by inhibiting Programmed Death-1 (PD-1)/Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1) or Cytotoxic T-Lymphocyte-Associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) signaling, while CD47-targeted therapies disrupt the “don't-eat-me” signal and promote macrophage-mediated tumor phagocytosis.11-13 Even so, many patients experience primary resistance or relapse after an initial response.14 Growing evidence links this variation in efficacy to differences in the gut and intratumoral microbiomes, which can influence how patients respond to treatment.

Current evidence indicates that the GM and intratumoral microbiota (IM) actively influence drug efficacy and resistance, and the IM has become a major focus in cancer research.12,15,16 Distinct bacterial communities are often found in tumors compared with adjacent normal tissues, supporting the view that intratumoral microbes can contribute to resistance and motivating efforts to define the underlying mechanisms.17-19 Microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and secondary bile acids, can also shift treatment outcomes by shaping immune surveillance, affecting drug bioavailability, and altering tumor-related signaling programs.20 These findings suggest that engineering the GM, the IM, or metabolite profiles may help address therapeutic resistance (Figure 1). Microbiome-directed approaches now extend beyond antibiotics (ATB) and probiotics to include fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), biomaterial-based delivery strategies, and lifestyle interventions, each with the potential to reshape microbial communities and improve drug response. In this review, we summarize mechanistic studies that support a causal role for the microbiota in anticancer therapy through host–drug–microbe interactions. We also discuss the coupled feedback in which microbes influence drug efficacy and toxicity, while anticancer agents and concomitant medications reshape microbial composition and function. Finally, we highlight key barriers and future priorities for translating microbiota-based interventions into clinical practice.

Microbiota orchestrate tumor progression and regression

The microbiota can either promote or restrain tumor development through multifaceted interactions with the host. Such effects have been reported across gut, intratumoral, and extraintestinal communities, and across tumor sites including the colorectum, lung, liver, and pancreas, involving both bacterial and fungal members. As the underlying mechanisms become clearer, these communities are also being considered as targets for therapeutic intervention.

Microbiota promote tumor progression

The microbiota can influence cancer biology, and cancer patients often show microbial patterns that differ from those of healthy individuals.21,22 In CRC, specific gut microbes such as Bacteroides fragilis and Fusobacterium nucleatum have been linked to tumorigenesis. Bacteroides fragilis can drive chronic inflammation, weaken the epithelial barrier, and perturb host signaling, whereas Fusobacterium nucleatum supports tumor cell growth and survival by altering immune responses. Together, these findings point to microbiota-driven effects on CRC progression through inflammation and immune regulation.23-26 In addition, Peptostreptococcus stomatis has been reported to activate oncogenic pathways, including MAPK signaling, and to foster a pro-inflammatory milieu that supports increased proliferation in CRC.27

Interest has expanded beyond the gut microbiota to the IM, and recent work has clarified how it can influence tumor progression.28 Microbes within the tumor microenvironment (TME) have been shown to shape local immune responses and, in turn, affect how patients respond to therapy.29-31 Tumor tissues also harbor diverse microbial communities, and their composition can vary with tumor site.32 In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), for example, fungal colonization was found to accelerate tumor progression, particularly due to the enrichment of Malassezia species, which activate the mannose-binding lectin (MBL) pathway to promote cancer progression.33 Recent studies also report that intratumoral fungi, including Malassezia globosa and Alternaria alternata, can promote tumor growth by triggering IL-33–dependent activation of TH2 cells and ILC2s, which supports immune suppression in the TME. Antifungal treatment has been shown to reduce the accumulation of these cells and decrease tumor burden, suggesting a potential role for antifungal approaches in PDAC.34

In addition to the GM and IM, microbial communities in other body sites, including the respiratory tract, oral cavity, and biliary tract, also contribute to cancer progression by modulating the immune system and promoting tumor dynamics.35-37 This suggests that microbial infiltration and expansion in tumor tissues are facilitated by tumor-induced immunosuppression, emphasizing the microbiota's role in shaping the cancer microenvironment.30,31

Microbiota suppresses tumor progression

Certain microbes have been linked to tumor suppression, raising the possibility that they could be leveraged therapeutically and warranting further study. In PDAC, microbiome variation has been associated with prognosis independent of medication use. A comparison of the IM between patients with short-term survival (STS) and long-term survival (LTS) showed that intratumoral microbial diversity predicts outcome. Saccharopolyspora, Pseudoxanthomonas, Streptomyces, and Bacillus clausii were more abundant in LTS patients than in STS patients, and these taxa were associated with increased CD8+ T-cell recruitment and activation, supporting antitumor immunity and longer survival. Together, these findings suggest that microbiome composition may serve as a prognostic indicator and a therapeutic entry point in PDAC.38

Microbial metabolites also shape cancer biology. SCFAs, including butyrate (Btr), propionate, and acetate, are generated during fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates such as dietary fiber and contribute to intestinal homeostasis.39 In a CRC mouse model, Clostridium butyricum, a Btr-producing probiotic, was found to suppress tumor progression by inhibiting the Wnt signaling pathway and promoting the enrichment of a higher proportion of SCFAs-producing bacteria.40 Btr has additionally been reported to restrain tumor growth through multiple routes: activating TLR4 and downstream MAPK/NF-κB signaling to promote CRC cell death,41 dampening the PKM2-driven Warburg program to rewire glucose metabolism,42 and inducing ROS-linked autophagy in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).43 Clinically, reduced levels of Btr and its producers (especially Faecalibacterium) have been correlated with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) progression,44 and can promote inflammatory B-cell lymphoma via the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB pathway.45 Another SCFA, propionate, was found to inhibit LC cell proliferation by modulating survivin and p21.29 These findings highlight the important role of microbial metabolites in regulating tumor progression and immune responses, suggesting that modulating the microbiome could be a valuable therapeutic strategy.

Evidence now supports causal links between specific microbes and multiple cancers, with microbial metabolites and antitumor immunity contributing to these effects. Beyond these pathways, interactions between microbiota-derived signals and treatment response are increasingly implicated in resistance to immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted agents, with clear implications for efficacy and prognosis. We discuss these drug–microbiota interactions in detail in the later section on anticancer therapies and the microbiota.

3 The role of microbiota in immunotherapy

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has significantly improved the prognosis of many patients with refractory cancers, offering renewed hope. However, the clinical benefit remains limited to a subset of patients, as primary and acquired resistance mechanisms hinder their full efficacy. Increasing evidence highlights the GM and IM as key modulators of ICI responses. Mechanisms of microbiota-ICI crosstalk include shifts in specific bacterial populations, microbe-derived metabolites, remodeling of the immune microenvironment, and alterations in tumor-intrinsic cellular signaling pathways. Targeting these microbial interactions provides a promising strategy to overcome resistance and enhance patient responses to immunotherapy.

Colorectal cancer

Emerging clinical evidence suggests that GM can significantly impact the effectiveness of ICIs in CRC patients (Figure 2, Table 1). Fusobacterium nucleatum, a well-known anaerobic bacterium, is strongly linked to CRC progression and resistance to immunotherapy.46-48 High levels of Fusobacterium nucleatum colonization are associated with reduced progression-free survival (PFS) compared to patients with low bacterial burden.49 Additionally, specific microbiota, such as Prevotella sp. CAG:485 and Akkermansia muciniphila, are linked to better responses to immunotherapy, while Bacillus S24-7 and CAG:927 are associated with poorer efficacy.47 These beneficial strains enhance the effects of anti-PD-1 therapy through glycerophospholipid metabolism, which upregulates IFN-γ/IL-2, further suggesting that specific microbiota modulate immune responses to ICIs.50 Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to enhance IL-2 immunotherapy across various tumor models, including mouse melanoma and colorectal cancer, with this effect partly mediated by its outer membrane protein Amuc via TLR2 signaling.51 Oral administration of Lactobacillus plantarum L168 has also been reported to boost anti-tumor immunity in CD8+ T cells in a CRC mouse model through its metabolite indole-3-lactic acid (I3A). I3A promotes IL-12 production by enhancing H3K27 acetylation in dendritic cells (DCs), opening the IL12a enhancer region, while simultaneously inhibiting Saa3 expression by modifying chromatin accessibility.52 In another study, oral administration of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG synergized with anti–PD-1 monoclonal mAb, improving tumor control. This gut bacterium activates the cGAS/STING pathway in DCs, stimulating IFN-β–dependent CD8+ T cell infiltration into the tumor and amplifying antitumor immunity.53 Similarly, Lactobacillus acidophilus cell lysates enhanced the efficacy of CTLA-4 mAb by expanding CD8+ and effector memory T cells, while reducing Tregs and M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment.54 Together, these studies show that manipulating gut probiotics can rewire systemic and local immune circuits to potentiate ICI responses in CRC.

Alistipes finegoldii, a beneficial gut commensal, has been linked to prolonged survival in CRC patients, with similar associations observed in lung cancer and neuroendocrine tumors.55 In mouse models, Alistipes finegoldii enhances antitumor immunity and improves anti–PD-1 therapy efficacy in tumors with primary resistance to ICIs. This effect is driven by increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into the TME, with CXCR6 playing a critical role in recruiting highly cytotoxic, apoptosis-resistant CXCR6⁺CD8⁺ T cells. On a molecular level, the bacterial lipoprotein LIPOAF from Alistipes finegoldii binds directly to TLR2 on DCs, stimulating CXCL16 secretion and activating downstream antitumor immune responses. Both intravenous LIPOAF protein and oral administration of engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 expressing LIPOAF significantly enhanced anti–PD-1 therapy efficacy. These findings highlight gut-derived LIPOAF/TLR2 signaling as a promising target for GM–drug crosstalk in adjunctive therapy, with a clinical trial (NCT05576961) already underway. Supporting the notion that gut communities can be engineered to modulate ICI responses, Tanoue et al. identified an 11-strain bacterial consortium from healthy human feces that synergized with anti–PD-1 therapy in a murine CRC model. This defined GM consortium greatly expanded tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and, remarkably, was sufficient to suppress tumor growth on its own.84 These results suggest that gut-derived bacterial consortia can be engineered as living adjuvants to reshape host–drug–microbe interactions in CRC.

Furthermore, GM-derived metabolites are increasingly implicated in improved responses to immunotherapy (Figure 3, Table 1). In CRC, Btr has been repeatedly linked to better outcomes. Several Btr-producing species, including Clostridium butyricum, Agathobacter M104/1, Blautia SR1/5, and Roseburia intestinalis, have been associated with enhanced efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.56,85,86 Mechanistic work in CRC suggests that Btr can potentiate anti–PD-1 treatment by increasing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, in part through MYC downregulation and suppression of MYC-dependent thymidylate synthase (TYMS), which may also help alleviate 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) resistance.85 In chemotherapy-refractory metastatic CRC, enrichment of Agathobacter M104/1 and Blautia SR1/5 has correlated with improved immunotherapy response, although the mechanisms remain unclear. Roseburia intestinalis has likewise been reported to restrain tumor growth and augment anti–PD-1 activity by promoting functional CD8+ T cells.56 Together, these studies support a role for Btr and other short-chain fatty acids in tuning antitumor immunity and improving CRC treatment outcomes.

Besides Btr, inosine, a purine nucleoside derived from adenosine, also plays a significant role in modulating immune responses and enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy.57 Produced by specific GM, such as Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, inosine activates the A2AR-cAMP-PKA signaling pathway, which in turn promotes CREB activation, upregulates IL12Rβ2 and IFN-γ expression, and facilitates Th1 cell differentiation. These effects enhance the therapeutic efficacy of anti-CTLA4 monoclonal antibodies, as demonstrated in mouse models of small intestinal adenocarcinoma, bladder cancer, and melanoma.87 Recent metagenomics studies have shown that the gut probiotic Lactobacillus gallinarum is significantly depleted in CRC patient feces, suggesting that its loss may impair the host's antitumor defense. In CRC mouse models, oral administration of Lactobacillus gallinarum leads to rapid colonization and the production of indole-3-carboxylic acid, which suppresses the IDO1/Kyn/AHR axis. This suppression prevents CD4+ Treg differentiation and intratumoral infiltration, ultimately enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in both MSI-high and MSI-low tumors.58

In addition to the GM, the IM play crucial roles in modulating immune responses to cancer therapies (Figure 4, Table 2). Tumor tissues from various cancers, including CRC, LC, breast cancer (BC), and liver cancer, have significantly higher bacterial loads compared to adjacent normal tissues, suggesting that tumors are more densely populated by bacteria.17 For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum is notably more abundant in CRC tissues than in normal tissues or benign lesions.18 Fusobacterium. nucleatum, one of the key IM in CRC, has been shown to enhance anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy in microsatellite stable CRC (MSS-CRC) via Btr and the HDAC3/8-H3K27ac-TBX21-PD-1 signaling pathway.88 At the same time, commensal members of the IM can act as allies to enhance the efficacy of cancer therapies. For example, Bifidobacterium enrichment in CRC tumors activates the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway, significantly boosting the effectiveness of anti-CD47 immunotherapy.12 Additionally, a proteomic study by Yao et al. identified bacterial peptides in CRC tissues, including Bacteroides fragilis-derived B2 and B5 peptides, that costimulate peripheral blood mononuclear cells and trigger strong immune responses.89 These findings suggest that IM can utilize unique bacterial peptides to enhance antitumor immunity, providing a novel approach to improving immunotherapeutic outcomes.

Liver cancer

Although the liver is inherently sterile, it maintains a close molecular interaction with the intestinal microbiota via the gut–liver axis. In murine HCC models, bacterial translocation along this axis chronically activates LPS/TLR4 signaling, driving inflammation and carcinogenesis. In contrast, gut sterilization significantly reduces the incidence of HCC.98 With the increasing prevalence of both intrinsic and acquired resistance to first-line systemic therapies, the GM, acting through the gut–liver axis, is increasingly recognized as a key factor in determining drug efficacy and a potential target for therapeutic intervention99 (Figure 2, Table 1). Certain microbial taxa are closely associated with immunotherapy response and long-term survival of HCC patients. The enrichment of Akkermansia muciniphila, Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae bacterium-GAM79, Alistipes sp. Marseille-P5997, Ruminococcus calidus, and Erysipelotrichaceae bacterium-GAM147 correlates with enhanced antitumor immunity and longer OS and PFS. In contrast, the dominance of Proteobacteria or Veillonellaceae is associated with refractory disease and shorter PFS/OS.59,60 Recent studies have highlighted Phocaeicola vulgatus as a major driver of immunotherapy resistance in HCC. This bacterium is selectively enriched in nonresponders and impairs anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy in preclinical models by disrupting tryptophan metabolism and reducing microbial production of indole-3-acetic acid (3-IAA). The resulting shortage of 3-IAA weakens IFN-γ⁺ and granzyme-B⁺ CD8⁺ T cell responses. Exogenous supplementation of 3-IAA has been shown to restore cytotoxic T-cell activity and resensitize tumors to checkpoint blockade61 (Figure 3, Table 1). This microbe–metabolite–drug axis illustrates a bidirectional microbial–drug interaction, where the efficacy of systemic therapy depends on microbiota-derived metabolites, and therapeutic resistance can be reversed through metabolite supplementation. ATB exposure provides a complementary example of pharmacologic disruption of the microbiota–drug crosstalk. Broad-spectrum ATBs induce gut dysbiosis, depleting beneficial microbes and destabilizing the gut–liver ecosystem. Clinically, ATB-induced dysbiosis has been shown to impair therapeutic outcomes in HCC, increasing resistance to the standard combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab, and significantly reducing both OS and PFS.100 This demonstrates how nononcologic drugs like ATBs can indirectly affect responses to cancer therapies by altering the microbiota, further emphasizing the importance of considering the broader pharmacologic context rather than focusing on individual drugs.

Most of the evidence for interactions between the microbiota and immunotherapy comes from preclinical mouse models. Several microbes with known immunomodulatory effects, including Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Clostridium scindens, various Lactobacillus species, and Roseburia intestinalis, have been documented mainly in animal studies. In contrast, human cohort studies have identified specific associations, such as between Fusobacterium nucleatum and Ruminococcus species and the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, although the exact mechanisms remain undefined in animal models. Only a few key species, such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes finegoldii, and Clostridium butyricum, have been validated in both human and mouse studies. This disparity in evidence underscores a significant translational gap: microbes that are effective only in animal models need validation in human cohorts, while species identified as beneficial or harmful in human studies should be further examined in mouse models to clarify their mechanisms, providing a solid foundation for microbiota-targeted adjuvant therapies.

Unlike the GM, which communicates with the liver mainly through the gut–liver axis, the IM within liver tumors represents a more localized form of microbial–drug interaction (Figure 4, Table 2). In advanced liver cancer, IM has been shown to influence immunotherapy efficacy by activating the LPS/TLR4–CXCL1/JNK/MAPK pathway, promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and creating an immunosuppressive environment that limits the effectiveness of ICIs.101 Clinically, patients with a higher abundance of Pseudomonas species in their tumors tend to have better outcomes, suggesting that certain IM members may positively modulate checkpoint blockade responses.102 Enrichment of Streptococcaceae in HCC activates the NF-κB pathway, which upregulates peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1) and promotes excessive glycolysis, leading to increased lactate in the TME. This excess lactate significantly suppresses CD8⁺ T-cell cytotoxicity while raising the levels of immune checkpoint molecules such as TIM-3 and LAG-3, which impairs antitumor immunity and reduces the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy.97 These findings suggest that while much of the current research on microbiota–immunotherapy interactions in HCC focuses on the GM, intratumoral communities also play a significant role in microbial–drug interactions. However, data on IM remain limited and largely correlative, emphasizing the need for more clinical and mechanistic studies to better understand how both GM and IM can be safely and effectively manipulated to improve immunotherapy outcomes in liver cancer.

Melanoma

GM has been closely associated with melanoma immunotherapy efficacy, resistance, and adverse events (AEs).8,65,103-105 A growing body of work indicates that GM and ICIs engage in bidirectional crosstalk (Figure 2, Table 1). On the one hand, certain commensals can potentiate antitumor immunity: Bifidobacterium species enhance DCs function and promote CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into the TME, thereby improving the efficacy of anti–PD-L1 therapy.62 On the other hand, ICIs themselves can reshape GM behavior. In preclinical melanoma models, ICI treatment promotes the translocation of specific gut species, such as Enterococcus faecalis and Lactobacillus johnsonii, from the intestine to tumor-draining lymph nodes, where they activate DCs and T cells and enhance antigen presentation and systemic antitumor responses.63 In a related mechanism, Enterococcus produces bioactive muropeptides via its SagA enzyme, which are sensed by host NOD2 and further augment the antitumor efficacy of ICIs.64 Together, these studies illustrate a dynamic GM–drug crosstalk in which ICIs alter microbial localization and signaling, while microbiota-derived molecules feedback to shape ICI responsiveness. Not all GM exert beneficial effects on melanoma immunotherapy. Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection impairs responses to ICIs by suppressing DCs activity and CD8⁺ T cell activation.65,66 Similarly, antibiotic-induced disruption of gut microbiome homeostasis blunts systemic immunity and undermines the therapeutic potency of ICIs, resulting in significantly shorter PFS.106 Mechanistic studies in mouse melanoma models have further identified the PD-L2/RGMb axis as a critical node of microbiota–drug interaction. In this context, commensal microbiota enhance responses to anti–PD-1/PD-L1 therapy by blocking PD-L2–RGMb interactions, downregulating PD-L2 and RGMb expression, and increasing the CD8⁺ T-cell to Treg cell ratio, thereby overcoming tumor drug resistance.107

Btr, which is often linked to antitumor effects in other settings, shows context-dependent associations in melanoma immunotherapy. One study reported that higher Btr levels were accompanied by reduced benefit from ipilimumab, with weakened antitumor immunity that was attributed to fewer memory T-cell clusters and altered T-cell function. In that work, Btr was proposed to impair DC maturation and interfere with CD28 signaling, a pathway required for effective CTLA-4–based therapy.67 By contrast, a recent murine melanoma study found that Btr supplementation improved responses to anti–PD-1 therapy, whereas lower Btr levels were associated with poorer treatment outcomes. In this setting, Btr increased histone H3K27 acetylation at the promoter regions of PDCD1 and CD28 in human CD8⁺ T cells, thereby promoting their expression and ultimately augmenting the antitumor effect of anti–PD-1 therapy.68 Together, these seemingly contradictory findings suggest that Btr participates in a nuanced metabolite–drug crosstalk in melanoma, in which its impact on treatment resistance and sensitivity may depend on the specific ICI target (CTLA-4 versus PD-1), treatment context and epigenetic programming of T cells. Further work is needed to clarify how Btr can be harnessed or avoided in tailored microbiota–metabolite–ICI combination strategies.

In melanoma, IM can shape antitumor immunity through T-cell metabolic changes and, in some settings, by supplying bacterial neoantigens (Figure 4, Table 2). A higher intratumoral abundance of Lachnoclostridium has been associated with increased CD8+ T-cell infiltration, suggesting that certain tumor-resident bacteria influence the local immune milieu and support antitumor responses.108 Lactobacillus reuteri has also been reported to migrate to and establish within melanoma lesions, where it converts dietary tryptophan into indole-3-carboxaldehyde (I3C). I3C then activates aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling in CD8+ T cells, elevates IFN-γ production, and improves responses to anti–PD-1 therapy.94 Additionally, bacterial peptides presented by tumor-resident bacteria, such as Lactobacillus reuteri, have been shown to function as tumor neoantigens presented by HLA class I and II molecules. This provides an exciting new class of potential targets for immunotherapy, expanding the repertoire of microbial–drug crosstalk that can be exploited to personalize and enhance melanoma treatment.109,110

Lung cancer

Akkermansia muciniphila, a key GM species, has emerged as an important prognostic biomarker in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Its abundance correlates with improved clinical responses to ICIs, particularly anti-PD-1 therapy (Figure 2, Table 1). In NSCLC mouse models, Akkermansia muciniphila enhances PD-1 blockade efficacy by working with the immune system to reduce tumor growth. Mechanistically, this microbe contributes to TME remodeling by disrupting the CXCL3–PD-L1 axis, which mediates communication between cancer-associated fibroblasts and PD-L1+ neutrophils. This interaction helps reverse CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, further boosting the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy.69-71 Both endogenous and supplemented Akkermansia muciniphila have been shown to influence cancer progression, highlighting its critical role in modulating immune responses through microbial–drug interactions.111 Gut-derived probiotics, such as Clostridium butyricum and Bifidobacterium, have been shown to enhance the effectiveness of ICIs in LC. Clostridium butyricum improves ICI efficacy in LC patients by restoring the gut microbiota balance often disrupted by therapies like proton pump inhibitors, leading to better prognosis.72-74 Similarly, Bifidobacterium has been found to boost the activity of ICIs and oxaliplatin in LC mouse models, potentially through increased IFN-γ production and enhanced antitumor immune responses.112 Ruminococcus species are common gut bacteria involved in carbohydrate fermentation and nutrient absorption, including the breakdown of complex polysaccharides like cellulose.113 A higher abundance of Ruminococcus has been linked to longer survival and better responses to ICIs in NSCLC patients from both U.S. and Japanese cohorts.114,115 However, a study of Chinese LC patients found that treatment responders had higher levels of Alistipes putredinis, Bifidobacterium longum, and Prevotella, while non-responders had increased Ruminococcus levels.116 These findings suggest that ethnic differences in immunotherapy response may be shaped by variations in gut microbiota composition, potentially influenced by diet or lifestyle factors. Further research is needed to understand how these differences affect cancer outcomes, highlighting the potential of microbiota profiling in personalizing therapies. ATB treatment has been shown to reduce the efficacy of ICIs, likely by inducing intestinal dysbiosis, which weakens systemic immune responses and alters how the microbiome influences immunotherapy outcomes.117-120 This disruption underscores the complexity of microbial–drug interactions. Regarding GM metabolites, clinical data show that NSCLC patients who respond to therapy have higher serum levels of SCFAs compared to non-responders.68 A recent prospective study of 106 advanced NSCLC patients receiving immunotherapy combined with cisplatin-based chemotherapy demonstrated that specific GM, such as Faecalibacterium and Subdoligranulum, influence immune responses and cytokine profiles through SCFAs (Figure 3, Table 1). In preclinical models, including the Lewis LC mouse model, these GM were shown to activate effector T cells via SCFAs, enhancing antitumor immunity and improving the efficacy of immunochemotherapy.75

Pancreatic cancer

Pancreatic cancer patients generally show poor responses to ICIs. However, preclinical studies suggest that coadministering the oral bacterium Megasphaera sp. XA511 with PD-1 blockade can enhance tumor suppression in PDAC models.76 Additionally, the microbial metabolite trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) plays a critical role in modulating ICI efficacy. 16S rRNA profiling has shown that responders to anti-PD-1 therapy are enriched in CutC-expressing strains, such as Clostridium tetani, Clostridium tepidum, Enterococcus asini, and Enterococcus phoeniculicola, as CutC is the key enzyme responsible for TMAO production. In PDAC models, TMAO supplementation enhances ICI efficacy by reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment. This includes the activation of immunostimulatory macrophages, amplification of type I interferon signaling, and activation of effector T cells, leading to reduced tumor growth and prolonged survival.77 In the orthotopic murine PDAC model, disruption of the intestinal barrier has been linked to increased levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a component of the Gram-negative bacterial cell wall, found in both the bloodstream and tumor tissue. LPS activates the TLR4/MyD88/AKT/NF-κB signaling pathway, directly driving the transcription of PD-L1, resulting in its increased expression on tumor cells. While LPS alone can promote tumor growth by impairing T-cell function, its upregulation of PD-L1 becomes a therapeutic target when combined with PD-L1 blockade therapy. Under these conditions, inhibiting PD-L1 can quickly reverse T-cell exhaustion and shift an immunosuppressive environment toward antitumor activity, demonstrating the crosstalk between microbiota and drug therapy in cancer treatment.78 IM have been shown to influence immunotherapy responses in PDAC (Figure 4, Table 2).121 In PDAC mouse models, depletion of the Col1 homotrimer increases T-cell infiltration and enhances the efficacy of anti–PD-1 therapy. This effect is linked to the inability of anaerobic Bacteroidales-enriched IM in hypoxic, immunosuppressive tumors to activate the Col1 homotrimer-α3β1 integrin signaling pathway, which is essential for driving oncogenic signaling, cancer cell proliferation, and tumor growth.96 These results suggest that both GM and tumor-resident bacteria can modulate immunotherapy effectiveness by reshaping the immune microenvironment through metabolic and signaling mechanisms.

Urinary system cancer

In renal cell carcinoma (RCC), enrichment of the gut bacterium Akkermansia muciniphila has been linked to better responses to anti–PD-1 therapy (Figure 2, Table 1). Mechanistically, Akkermansia muciniphila activates the MHC-II⁺ plasmacytoid DC–Th1 CD4⁺ T cell axis, increasing levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and granzyme H, which enhances T-cell trafficking and cytotoxicity within the TME.69,79 In contrast, in immunologically “cold” prostate cancer, androgens promote a microbiome-mediated resistance program. The GM, like Clostridium scindens, convert cortisol into precursors of androgen receptor ligands, which supports proliferative signaling and reduces the effectiveness of treatment.80 In some ICI-responsive prostate cancer cohorts, reduced levels of Akkermansia muciniphila have been observed, suggesting that its effect on treatment response may depend on the context and tumor type, and be influenced by the endocrine environment.122 These findings indicate that both endocrine signaling and the GM participate in a hormone–microbe–drug interaction, which can either enhance or hinder immunotherapy in urologic cancers. In bladder cancer, clinical data further highlight the negative impact of antibiotic-induced dysbiosis. In the PURE-01 trial, prior antibiotic use significantly lowered the pathological complete response rate (15% vs. 50% in nonusers) and 24-month recurrence-free survival (63% vs. 90%), suggesting that antibiotic-driven disruption of the gut microbiota contributes to resistance to ICIs.123 Fecal 16S rDNA sequencing in bladder cancer patients revealed a reduced abundance of Parabacteroides. Supplementation with Parabacteroides distasonis in MB49 tumor-bearing mice, combined with anti–PD-1 therapy, significantly improved immunotherapy responses, slowed tumor growth, and increased intratumoral CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration.81 These findings suggest that restoring beneficial taxa like Parabacteroides distasonis may help counteract antibiotic-induced dysbiosis and re-establish a favorable microbial–drug interaction in bladder cancer.

Beyond the GM, the IM also influence immunotherapy responses in urinary system cancers (Figure 4, Table 2). In prostate cancer, the uropathogenic Escherichia coli strain CP1 has shown significant potential in reshaping the tumor immune landscape. After intraurethral administration, CP1 ascends from the urethra to the bladder, selectively colonizing prostate tumors without reaching the kidneys or causing systemic toxicity. In mouse models, combining CP1 with PD-1 inhibitors increases intratumoral CD8⁺ T cells, Th17 cells, mature DCs, M1 macrophages, and NK cells, while decreasing Treg cells and the pro-angiogenic factor VEGF, collectively overcoming resistance to PD-1 blockade.16 Taken together, these observations position both GM and IM as active participants in immunotherapy outcomes in urinary system cancers, providing multiple entry points for rational manipulation of microbial–drug crosstalk to enhance clinical benefit.

Ovarian and breast cancers

Ovarian cancer (OC) and breast cancer (BC) are generally considered low-immunogenic tumors, with only a subset of patients benefiting from ICIs. Recent studies suggest that both GM and IM, along with their metabolites, can help “activate” these tumors by reshaping T-cell responses and the tumor immune microenvironment, influencing the effectiveness of immunotherapy (Figure 2, Table 1). In OC, evidence is growing that the GM and its metabolites can modulate responses to ICIs and chemotherapy (Figure 3, Table 1). For instance, in OC patients who respond to ICIs, 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples has identified an enrichment of Ruminococcus and Leuconostoc mesenteroides. These bacteria produce lactate, which enhances CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell responses and boosts the effectiveness of both immunotherapy and chemotherapy by promoting cytokine production.82 Metabolomic analyzes in these responders also show a distinct systemic metabolite profile, including elevated levels of lipids such as phosphatidylcholines and triacylglycerides or diacylglycerides, as well as amino acids like serine and several bacterial-derived metabolites.82 In OC mouse models, oral mannose supplementation targets Faecalibaculum rodentium in the gut, further influencing treatment responses. Its derived metabolites, propionate and Btr, promote histone acetylation, stimulating the expansion and differentiation of progenitor-exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, ultimately improving immunotherapy response.83 This suggests the potential of mannose supplementation in targeting specific microbial taxa to enhance OC immunotherapy outcomes.

BC is likewise often considered immunologically “cold”, with immunotherapy demonstrating meaningful benefit only in selected subtypes, particularly triple-negative BC. In this context, the immunological fate of BC is critically shaped by the IM. In animal models, the bacterial load in normal mammary tissue is significantly lower (1.7 × 10⁴ bacterial equivalents per gram) compared to tumor tissue, where the bacterial load is nearly ten times higher (1.35 × 10⁵ bacterial equivalents per gram).19 Fusobacterium nucleatum uses its Fap2 lectin to bind Gal-GalNAc moieties on tumor cells, facilitating colonization and establishing a niche that excludes CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, thereby accelerating tumor progression and metastasis.124 In contrast, Achromobacter yanoikuyae, which is markedly depleted in cancerous tissue, can reactivate antitumor immunity by deploying glycolipid ligands that activate CD1d-restricted invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells.125 From a translational perspective, tumor-associated Clostridiales species elevate systemic levels of the microbial metabolite TMAO, which triggers PERK-mediated endoplasmic reticulum stress, remodels the immune microenvironment and significantly potentiates immunotherapy efficacy.30 Together, these observations suggest that intratumoral bacteria and their metabolites form a BC-specific microbial–drug crosstalk axis that can either suppress or restore antitumor immunity, offering tractable metabolic and microbial entry points to overcome immune checkpoint blockade resistance (Figure 4, Table 2).

Esophageal cancer

In many solid tumors, GM features linked to ICI response are being progressively mapped, but comparable data in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) are limited. IM in ESCC, however, has begun to draw attention, and emerging evidence suggests that certain taxa may influence treatment response (Figure 4, Table 2). The enrichment of Streptococcus within ESCC lesions has been associated with higher CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and better outcomes with anti–PD-1 therapy, suggesting that some IM members may increase local immune activity in tumors that are otherwise weakly immunogenic.92 By contrast, a higher intratumoral burden of Fusobacterium nucleatum correlates with poorer response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and greater bacterial abundance has been linked to treatment failure.93 These findings support a model in which intratumoral bacteria participate in microbe–drug interactions that can either favor ICI benefit or promote chemoresistance, and they suggest that selectively reducing detrimental taxa such as Fusobacterium nucleatum may improve outcomes.

Recent studies on microbiota and immunotherapy interactions mostly come from preclinical mouse models, where GM species like Bifidobacterium pseudolongum, Clostridium scindens, Lactobacillus, and Roseburia intestinalis have shown immunomodulatory effects. However, these findings are primarily based on animal studies, while human cohort studies have found associations between Fusobacterium nucleatum and Ruminococcus species and ICI efficacy, though the mechanisms are not fully understood. Additionally, IM such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Bifidobacterium influence immune responses and tumor immunity in both animal and human studies. These microbes can either enhance or inhibit ICI efficacy by modifying the TME, often through metabolites like Btr. Notably, only a few microbes, including Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes finegoldii, and Clostridium butyricum, have been validated in both human and mouse models, bridging the gap between GM and IM in immunotherapy. These microbes affect both systemic and local immune responses, influencing the success of anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapies. The current gap in evidence highlights the need for further validation of microbes identified in animal models within human cohorts and for more exploration of human-associated microbes in mouse models. This dual approach will lay the groundwork for microbiota-targeted therapies.

4 The role of microbiota in chemotherapy

Microbiota, encompassing both the GM and IM, are increasingly recognized as influential modulators of chemotherapeutic responses. Building on earlier evidence that GM shapes patient outcomes through drug metabolism, immune regulation and barrier integrity, recent studies further demonstrate that IM can directly modulate drug sensitivity within the TME. In this chapter, for each cancer type, we first summarize GM-mediated effects on chemosensitivity, chemoresistance and treatment-related toxicity, and then outline the emerging contributions of IM.

Colorectal cancer

In CRC, specific GM can directly modulate the cytotoxic activity of chemotherapy (Figure 5, Table 3). Bacteroides species are Gram-negative commensals that contribute to carbohydrate breakdown, microbial homeostasis and host immune regulation. Multiomics analysis of CRC patients receiving 5-FU identified Bacteroides vulgatus as a driver of 5-FU resistance by upregulating DNA repair and nucleoside transport-related genes that support nucleotide biosynthesis, thereby enabling tumor cells to better withstand 5-FU-induced damage.126 Conversely, several probiotic strains have shown the capacity to restore chemosensitivity in resistant CRC. In experimental models, combining Lactobacillus plantarum supernatant with 5-FU inhibits cancer cell survival by upregulating caspase 3 and suppressing Wnt/β-catenin signaling, suggesting its potential as an adjuvant strategy to overcome 5-FU resistance.127 Likewise, Lactobacillus rhamnosus R0011 enhances the efficacy of capecitabine in colon cancer bearing mice, leading to significantly reduced tumor size, which is associated with increased Bax and caspase 3 expression and decreased Bcl-2 expression compared with untreated controls.128 In contrast to these beneficial effects, ATB treatment markedly reduces the antitumor efficacy of 5-FU in CRC mouse models by disrupting GM homeostasis and impairing the microbiota supported chemosensitive state.129

Gut microbial metabolites represent a key mechanism through which GM influences chemosensitivity in CRC (Figure 3). The Btr has been shown to enhance the efficacy of multiple chemotherapeutic agents in ways that partially mirror its effects on immunotherapy. Through the GPR109a–AKT signaling axis, Btr suppresses the glucose transporter GLUT1 and the nucleotide synthesis rate limiting enzyme G6PD in tumor cells, thereby markedly potentiating 5-FU-induced apoptosis. At the same time, it activates an ID2 dependent interleukin 12 pathway that promotes cytotoxic CD8⁺ T-cell expansion and further augments the antitumor activity of oxaliplatin.130,131 Additionally, Btr synergizes with irinotecan to reduce tumor cell viability and increase apoptosis by increasing the BAX/BCL2 ratio and downregulating p21, p53 and β-catenin.132 Together, these data illustrate a metabolite-centered microbial–drug interaction axis in which Btr remodels both tumor intrinsic metabolism and antitumor immunity to overcome chemoresistance. Beyond Btr, other GM-derived metabolites also modulate chemotherapy response in CRC. Urolithin A (UroA), a microbial metabolite produced from dietary ellagic acid and ellagitannins, has been reported to enhance mitochondrial function and cellular longevity.154 In CRC models, UroA boosts the anticancer effect of 5-FU by arresting the cell cycle through cyclin A and cyclin B1, and by triggering apoptosis via caspase activation.133 The FOXO3–FOXM1 axis is a key regulator of tumor drug resistance, with FOXO3 acting as a tumor suppressor that inhibits FOXM1; impairment of FOXO3 leads to FOXM1 upregulation and chemoresistance.134,135 UroA can increase the sensitivity of CRC cells and mouse tumors to 5-FU and reduce resistance by upregulating FOXO3 and downregulating FOXM1.136 At the level of bacterial taxa, Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to enhance the efficacy of first line CRC chemotherapy with FOLFOX (5-FU, leucovorin and oxaliplatin), potentially through dipeptides containing branched chain amino acids identified by metabolomic analyzes.137 However, not all GM–derived metabolites counter drug resistance; in some settings, they can instead foster a chemoprotective niche that favors tumor persistence. Using spatial metabolomics together with 16S rRNA sequencing, investigators observed an increased abundance of colibactin-producing Escherichia coli (CoPEC) in specimens from patients with right-sided colon cancer,138 and colibactin was linked to a glycerophospholipid-enriched tumor microenvironment with lower immunogenicity, accompanied by reduced infiltration of IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T lymphocytes and an association with chemotherapy resistance.138 Consistently, studies using CRC mouse models and in vitro experiments have shown that CoPEC promotes epithelial mesenchymal transition and the emergence of highly drug-resistant cancer stem cells, further enhancing chemoresistance.139 In addition to CoPEC, Prevotella species, anaerobic Gram-negative bacteria that participate in the breakdown of complex plant polysaccharides,155 have also been implicated in reduced chemotherapy efficacy. In CRC models, Prevotella decreases the anticancer efficacy of first-line FOLFOX therapy (5-FU, leucovorin and oxaliplatin) through its metabolite 3-oxocholic acid (3-Oxo).140 Although 3-Oxo normally facilitates fat digestion and nutrient absorption, in the context of cancer therapy it counteracts the effects of FOLFOX and induces drug resistance by modulating the P-EGFR/P-ERK/c-MYC and LOX pathways.140 These studies illustrate that GM can also drive chemoresistance by generating metabolites that remodel the TME, suppress antitumor immunity and activate pro-survival signaling in tumor cells.

Beyond the GM, the IM are increasingly recognized for their role in modulating chemotherapy efficacy (Figure 4, Table 2). In CRC, a high abundance of Fusobacterium nucleatum within the tumor is strongly linked to chemoresistance. This bacterium activates the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB signaling pathway, upregulates the autophagy-related gene BIRC3, and inhibits the caspase cascade, thereby preventing apoptosis and promoting resistance to chemotherapeutic agents like 5-FU and oxaliplatin.26,90 Interestingly, LaCourse et al. proposed that part of the effectiveness of 5-FU might stem from its ability to inhibit Fusobacterium nucleatum, counteracting the tumor-promoting effects of this IM species.26,90 In contrast, other intratumoral bacteria, such as Escherichia coli, can metabolize 5-FU into an inactive form, bypassing its anticancer effects and ultimately reducing treatment efficacy.91 Together, GM and IM in CRC are involved in a complex microbial–drug crosstalk network that regulates chemosensitivity, providing complementary targets for strategies designed to overcome chemoresistance.

Lung cancer

In LC, increasing evidence suggests that GM can influence the effectiveness of platinum-based chemotherapy (Figure 5, Table 3). A randomized controlled trial (NCT03158129) on operable NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy found that different treatment regimens resulted in variations in the major pathological response (MPR) rate.141 Patients achieving MPR showed higher levels of Akkermansia muciniphila and a reduced presence of proinflammatory, potentially harmful microorganisms, indicating that Akkermansia muciniphila may have synergistic antitumor effects that contribute to deeper pathological responses and improved prognosis.141 In LC mouse models, Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to enhance chemotherapy sensitivity, as its combination with cisplatin suppresses tumor growth more effectively than cisplatin alone.142 This combination decreases Ki-67, p53, and FasL expression in tumor cells, while increasing Fas levels, thus mitigating cisplatin resistance at the cellular level.142 Additionally, it boosts proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, interleukin 6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), lowers interleukin 10 (IL-10), and remodels the immune microenvironment by reducing immunosuppressive regulatory T cells and modulating Th17 cells.142 These findings suggest that Akkermansia muciniphila enhances chemotherapy efficacy through a dual mechanism involving tumor cell signaling alterations and immune reprogramming, illustrating GM–drug interactions in LC. Other GM species also contribute to chemotherapy-related immune modulation.143 Enterococcus hirae contributes to microbial balance in the mammalian gut, while Barnesiella intestinihominis colonizes the colon and can regulate immunity by activating intestinal γδ T cells to produce IFN-γ. Both species have been reported to enhance the efficacy of the chemotherapeutic drug cyclophosphamide by shaping antitumor immune responses: Enterococcus hirae migrates to gut draining lymph nodes and increases the CD8⁺ T cell to Treg cell ratio in tumors, whereas Barnesiella intestinihominis accumulates in the colon and triggers intratumoral γδ T cells to secrete IFN-γ.143 This cooperative microbial–drug crosstalk has been associated with prolonged progression-free survival in LC and ovarian cancer patients, although the precise mechanisms remain under active investigation. Moreover, chemotherapy is often combined with targeted therapies like erlotinib to improve outcomes in NSCLC. Erlotinib targets EGFR in EGFR-mutated lung cancer, enhancing treatment precision. Recent studies suggest that GM can influence the efficacy of erlotinib. Higher levels of Bacteroides ovatus and Bacteroides xylanisolvens are linked to better treatment responses. In mouse models, supplementing with these bacteria improved erlotinib's efficacy by increasing CXCL10 and IFNγ expression, suggesting that GM enhances treatment effects by boosting immune cell recruitment. This highlights the potential of GM in modulating the response to targeted therapies.

However, disruption of GM by antibiotics can have the opposite effect on chemotherapy outcomes. In Lewis LC mouse models, antibiotic administration disturbs GM balance and induces resistance to cisplatin.144,145 Transcriptomic analyzes revealed that ATB treatment upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) and downregulates BAX and CDKN1B expression in tumor cells, thereby attenuating the antitumor effect of cisplatin at the tumor cell level. In contrast, coadministration of Lactobacillus with cisplatin promotes the expression of key effector molecules such as IFN-γ, granzyme B (GZMB) and perforin 1 (PRF1) in CD8⁺ T cells, whereas ATB use reduces the levels of these molecules.144,145 These findings indicate that GM disruption by antibiotics can shift microbial–drug crosstalk toward a chemoresistant state, while targeted probiotic supplementation may help restore a chemosensitive immune landscape in LC.

Pancreatic cancer

In PDAC, GM perturbations can substantially alter the efficacy of standard chemotherapy (Figure 5, Table 3). In PDAC mouse models, diabetes-associated hyperglycemia induces GM dysbiosis that enhances resistance to gemcitabine and paclitaxel, in part by increasing CD133 positive cells and thereby enriching a drug-resistant compartment.146 Clinically, however, several studies indicate that targeted or empirical ATB use can improve outcomes in specific settings. In PDAC patients treated with gemcitabine or 5-FU, concomitant ATB use was associated with better prognosis compared with chemotherapy alone.147 A large-scale cohort study of elderly PDAC patients further confirmed that those receiving combined chemotherapy and ATB had an approximately 11% higher survival rate, whereas no significant benefit was observed in patients treated with chemotherapy alone.156 These observations suggest that bacteria can be critical drivers of chemoresistance in PDAC and that, in some contexts, eliminating key resistance-promoting taxa through broad-spectrum nonpenicillin β lactam ATB may partially rewire GM–drug crosstalk in favor of chemosensitivity, although the specific species responsible for this survival benefit remain to be identified. Consistent with observations in other cancers, gut probiotics can exert antiresistance effects in PDAC. In PDAC model mice, combining gemcitabine with Lactobacillus paracasei reduces the grade of pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia and suppresses vimentin and Ki-67 expression, indicating attenuation of EMT and proliferation under chemotherapy.148 These findings support the concept that GM manipulation, whether through ATB or probiotics, can steer microbial–drug crosstalk toward a more chemosensitive state in PDAC. Microbial metabolites also play a central role in shaping chemotherapy response in PDAC.157 Mechanistically, this combination attenuates the proliferation of tumor-associated macrophages, reduces stromal production and thereby disrupts the desmoplastic barrier that normally supports PDAC progression.158 In parallel, the FOLFIRINOX regimen combined with indole 3 acetic acid (3-IAA), a tryptophan metabolite produced by Bifidobacterium, inhibits PDAC growth by promoting reactive oxygen species accumulation and suppressing autophagy; this effect depends on neutrophil myeloperoxidase-mediated oxidation of 3-IAA.149 These studies delineate a metabolite-centered layer of GM–drug crosstalk in PDAC, whereby microbial products remodel stromal and stress response pathways to restore chemosensitivity.

At the local level, IM in PDAC can directly inactivate chemotherapeutic agents. γ-Proteobacteria within pancreatic tumors express a long isoform of cytidine deaminase (CDDL) that metabolizes gemcitabine into an inactive form, thereby conferring marked chemoresistance (Figure 4, Table 2). Targeting this IM component with ATB such as ciprofloxacin has been shown to counteract this metabolic inactivation and partially restore gemcitabine sensitivity.95 This example illustrates an IM centered microbial–drug crosstalk in which tumor resident bacteria metabolically deactivate chemotherapy, but can also be pharmacologically targeted to reverse resistance.

Glioma

The GM interacts with the central nervous system and participates in regulating its development and homeostasis. Central nervous system tumors have become one of the leading causes of cancer-related death, with glioma being the most common and having an extremely poor prognosis. Current evidence indicates that alterations in microbiota composition or its metabolites can influence glioma growth by modulating the tumor immune microenvironment.159,160 Despite current multimodal treatment involving surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for glioblastoma patients remains very poor, and recurrence is inevitable. Temozolomide (TMZ) is the standard alkylating agent for glioma, but its high resistance rate (response rate less than 50%) severely impacts efficacy and prognosis, with no effective sensitization strategies currently available.

Recent studies suggest that GM composition and its metabolites influence TMZ sensitivity. A Phase I/II trial (NCT03174197) combining radiotherapy, TMZ, and anti-PD-L1 therapy analyzed fecal samples from GBM patients and found that changes in GM were associated with overall survival (OS), suggesting that GM-targeted interventions could serve as an essential adjunct to current treatments161 (Figure 5, Table 3). Furthermore, significant differences in GM composition have been observed between TMZ-sensitive and TMZ-resistant mouse models. In Chemotherapy-responsive models, Akkermansia muciniphila was enriched, while Alloprevotella, Muribaculum, and Desulfovibrio were relatively less abundant in resistant models. Notably, after TMZ treatment, the GM in sensitive mice enhanced systemic inflammation by increasing blood TNF-α and IL-1β levels through tryptophan metabolism. This inflammatory cascade promoted the accumulation and proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells and macrophages, thereby enhancing the efficacy of TMZ.162 Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to work synergistically with TMZ, further improving its antitumor effects. Additionally, gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Anaerotruncus have been found to cooperate with TMZ, enhancing its therapeutic impact by modulating immune responses.153 These studies highlight the dual role of GM and its metabolites in influencing glioma treatment outcomes, underscoring the importance of microbial–drug interactions. They also suggest that GM-targeted approaches could improve TMZ efficacy and help overcome resistance mechanisms in glioma.

Breast cancer

The GM plays a crucial role in influencing the effectiveness of chemotherapy in breast cancer (Figure 5, Table 3). In patients undergoing the AC-D regimen (doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and docetaxel), longitudinal microbiome analysis revealed significant changes in microbial communities over time. The Lachnospiraceae NK4A214 group, Lachnospiraceae UCG-005 group, Christensenellaceae R-7 group, and Marvinbryantia declined during chemotherapy but rebounded after treatment. Conversely, Proteobacteria and unclassified Enterobacteriaceae expanded markedly during chemotherapy and then fell below baseline levels, while Lactobacillus steadily increased throughout the entire treatment course.163 These changes indicate that chemotherapy significantly alters GM composition, suggesting a microbial–drug crosstalk in which chemotherapy drugs directly influence microbiota abundance and diversity. In metastatic breast cancer patients treated with capecitabine, fecal metagenomic analysis revealed that Blautia obeum was positively correlated with longer PFS, whereas Slackia was associated with shorter PFS, further emphasizing the role of specific GM taxa in chemotherapy outcomes.150 In this regard, chemotherapy is often combined with targeted therapies like trastuzumab in HER2-positive breast cancer. While chemotherapy broadly kills rapidly dividing cells, trastuzumab specifically targets the HER2 receptor, enhancing treatment efficacy. Recent studies suggest that GM can influence the efficacy of trastuzumab.164,165 Moreover, ATB impair immune responses by disrupting GM, reducing T-cell recruitment, hindering dendritic cell activation, and lowering IL-12p70 release.164 Consistent with this, clinical studiey shows that nonresponders to trastuzumab have reduced gut microbial diversity, with lower levels of Lachnospiraceae, Bifidobacteraceae, and others, compared to responders.165 This confirms that GM plays a direct role in regulating the efficacy of targeted therapies by modulating immune responses, highlighting the potential of GM in enhancing treatment outcomes. Although the precise mechanisms by which microbiota modulate BC chemoresistance remain elusive, microbial metabolites are believed to play a key role. For instance, UroA, identified as a specific substrate of the breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP/ABCG2), dose-dependently inhibits the transport of mitoxantrone, thereby promoting chemoresistance.166 These findings underscore the growing evidence of a microbial–drug crosstalk that critically shapes chemoresistance.

Genitourinary tumors

The GM also plays a significant role in chemotherapy resistance in genitourinary cancers (Figure 5, Table 3). In advanced urothelial carcinoma, a combination of gemcitabine and cisplatin with probiotics (Lactobacillus casei Shirota and Bifidobacterium breve) improves therapeutic outcomes through the gut–tumor axis. This combination increases infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and DCs into the TME, while decreasing CD4⁺ T cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts, suggesting that probiotics can enhance chemotherapy responses by modulating the immune landscape.151 In contrast, in an epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) mouse model, ATB treatment led to significant resistance to cisplatin. Mice treated with ATBs showed reduced tumor cell apoptosis, enhanced DNA repair, active angiogenesis, and an increased population of cancer stem cells compared to untreated controls.152

In summary, most studies on GM–chemotherapy interactions have relied on preclinical mouse models, with human studies primarily showing correlative data. Certain microbes, such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Enterococcus hirae, and Barnesiella intestinihominis, have demonstrated positive effects in both human and mouse studies. However, many potential probiotics, including Anaerotruncus spp., Bifidobacterium longum, and Lactobacillus spp., have mostly been observed in animal models, and their clinical efficacy remains unverified. IM, such as Escherichia coli and γ-proteobacteria, contribute to chemotherapy resistance in both animal and human studies by metabolizing drugs into inactive forms or promoting tumor progression. In particular, CoPEC (colibactin-producing Escherichia coli) resides in the TME and contributes to chemotherapy resistance by altering lipid composition and reducing tumor immunogenicity. In mouse models, CoPEC promotes resistance by inducing EMT and the formation of cancer stem cells. Human studies have identified microbial markers such as Bacteroides vulgatus, Blautia obeum, Slackia spp., and the CoPEC group, although the mechanisms behind these associations are still unclear. Future research should focus on bridging animal model findings to human applications and elucidating the causal relationships behind these associations.

Microbiota-targeting strategies

As the crosstalk between the microbiota and drugs is increasingly recognized as a critical factor influencing cancer treatment outcomes, microbiota-targeting strategies have emerged as a promising approach to improve therapeutic efficacy and overcome resistance. Several strategies have been explored to precisely target the microbiota in cancer treatment, including FMT, probiotics, prebiotics, drug delivery systems, lifestyle interventions, and even antibiotic use. While antibiotics are commonly used to treat infections, their effects on the microbiota are typically broader and less selective, often disrupting microbial balance. Research has shown that antibiotic treatment can sometimes reduce drug efficacy, although a few studies suggest that, in certain contexts, antibiotics might enhance therapeutic outcomes. These aspects have been discussed in detail in Sections 3 and 4 of this review. In contrast, strategies such as FMT, probiotics, prebiotics, drug delivery systems and lifestyle interventions allow for more targeted modulation of the microbiota. These approaches aim to restore or promote the growth of beneficial microbial communities, adjust metabolic pathways, and enhance immune responses—ultimately improving cancer treatment outcomes and reducing resistance. In the following section, we will elaborate on these strategies in more detail.

FMT

FMT has shown promise in enhancing the efficacy of pharmacological treatments by reprogramming the gut microbiota. This therapeutic procedure involves transplanting fecal material from healthy donors into the gastrointestinal tract of patients, aiming to restore a balanced microbial community. While FMT has demonstrated improvements in the effectiveness of anticancer drugs, particularly ICIs, its clinical outcomes remain heterogeneous and controversial (Figure 6).

In CRC, early-phase trials have shown that FMT can enhance responses to PD-1-based therapies. A randomized controlled trial (NCT04130763) combining oral FMT capsules from “responder-like” microbiota with anti-PD-1 treatment in advanced CRC patients led to improved clinical responses, increased α-diversity, and higher frequencies of IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T cells.167 In mouse models, FMT from healthy donors shifted key taxa like Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron and Bacteroides fragilis, with metabolomic profiling identifying metabolites like punicic acid and aspirin as contributors to enhanced PD-1 blockade efficacy.168 FMT combined with anti-VEGF therapy and anti-PD-1 in MSM-CRC models significantly reduced tumor progression, associated with microbiota changes, including Proteobacteria and Sphingomonadaceae, and T cell receptor clonal expansion.169 These findings highlight FMT's potential to overcome primary resistance to PD-1-based therapies in CRC. Similarly, in melanoma, FMT has also shown promise in enhancing ICI efficacy. The MIMic Phase I trial (NCT03772899) reported a 65% objective response rate (ORR) in advanced melanoma patients receiving FMT combined with anti-PD1 therapy. After a median follow-up of 20.7 months, 80% of patients remained progression-free. Microbiome analysis revealed that microbial metabolism, particularly Btr metabolism and glycan degradation, contributed to early immune activation, boosting anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy.170,171 In PD-1-refractory melanoma, FMT improved response to Pembrolizumab, with post-treatment microbiome changes leading to increased CD8⁺ T cell activation and reduced IL-8-producing myeloid cells.172 Another study showed that patients receiving Microbial Ecosystem Therapeutics 4 (MET4) with anti-PD1 therapy had enriched microbiota linked to ICI responses, including Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, and Enterococcus.173 Additionally, FMT enhanced ipilimumab efficacy in melanoma by activating IL-12-dependent Th1 immune responses via Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron and Bacteroides fragilis.174 A recent randomized trial (NCT03353402) demonstrated that FMT reshaped both the intestinal and TME, improving gene expression profiles and cancer treatment efficacy.175

In independent cohorts of advanced melanoma and NSCLC patients, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii was associated with better clinical responses to ICIs. Oral administration of the EXL01 strain (Faecalibacterium prausnitzii) in MCA205 tumor-bearing mice restored antitumor responses to ICIs and enhanced T cell activation, without altering microbiota composition, suggesting direct immunomodulatory effects.176 FMT from ICI responders into gnotobiotic mouse models of lung cancer (LC) reduced tumor growth and increased antitumor cell phenotypes, with enriched genera including Bacteroides, Blautia, Akkermansia, and Faecalibacterium.114 A clinical trial involving 13 patients with digestive system tumors (NCT04264975) showed that FMT enhanced anti-PD1 therapy efficacy, even in resistant cases. Of the 16 enrolled patients, 6 (46.2%) achieved clinical benefit, including 1 partial response and 5 with stable disease. FMT also induced sustained microbiota changes, increased CTLs, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines. Prevotella merdae was detected in FMT responders' feces, promoting CTL infiltration and enhancing T cell activity, while suppressing tumor growth in mice.177 In summary, FMT improves clinical outcomes by remodeling the immune microenvironment, enhancing T cell function, and increasing beneficial microbiota. However, the molecular mechanisms remain unclear, and future clinical applications will require tailored FMT strategies for different cancer types. Despite promising results, a randomized controlled trial (NCT03817125) showed no clinical benefit from FMT with SER-401 (a Firmicutes-based spore preparation).178 Sequencing analysis of immune-related gene expression profiles revealed the activation of signaling pathways associated with increased resistance to anti-PD1 therapy in the SER-401 group.178 Elevated PD1 expression and the presence of Tregs and effector T cells coexpressing TIGIT and PD-1 suggested a TME shift unfavorable for antitumor immunity. The addition of vancomycin to the SER-401 regimen complicated the interpretation of results. Although the trial failed to meet its objectives, it underscores the complexity of modulating the tumor microbiota and the challenges in microbiota manipulation.

Current research indicates that FMT benefits only a subset of patients, with limited efficacy in those resistant to immunotherapy. Moreover, most studies involve transplanting human feces into mice and lack direct application to human subjects, making the evidence for FMT still preliminary and necessitating further research (Figure 6).

Probiotics and prebiotics

Probiotics are gaining increasing attention in the context of cancer therapy. Among them, species of Lactobacillus, Clostridium butyricum, Akkermansia muciniphila, and Bifidobacterium are currently the most intensively studied. As discussed above, these probiotics can directly enhance the efficacy of anticancer drugs, but their ability to modulate gut microbial homeostasis is equally important. By selectively promoting the expansion of beneficial commensals and/or suppressing potentially pathogenic taxa, they reshape the gut microbiota and thereby influence drug metabolism, antitumor immunity, and overall treatment response. For example, administration of Lactobacillus rhamnosus Probio-M9 to a CRC mouse model enhanced the efficacy of ICIs by modulating the gut microenvironment. This probiotic promoted the growth of beneficial gut microbes, including Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium animalis, leading to increased production of beneficial metabolites such as Btr, and the systemic accumulation of α-ketoglutarate, N-acetyl-L-glutamate, and pyridoxine. Consequently, these changes promoted the infiltration and activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) while suppressing the function of Tregs within the TME.179 Similarly, in a CT26 murine CRC model, oral administration of an exopolysaccharide derived from Lactobacillus plantarum YT013 (YT013-EPS-1) modulated the gut microbiota and enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of locally delivered cisplatin hydrogel, resulting in marked inhibition of tumor growth and attenuation of cisplatin-induced systemic toxicity.180 16S rRNA sequencing further showed that this probiotic intervention enriched short-chain fatty acid–producing beneficial taxa, including Roseburia and members of the Lachnospiraceae family, thereby synergizing with the antitumor activity of cisplatin.180 Probiotic-based platforms, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG coated with a gallium-polyphenol network (LGG@Ga-poly), have been developed to selectively target intratumoral Proteobacteria and lipopolysaccharides, reshaping the IM and potentiating immune checkpoint blockade.181 This example demonstrates how probiotic-based platforms can eliminate harmful microbes while enhancing antitumor immunity.

In parallel, prebiotics provide fermentable substrates that selectively support the growth and function of such beneficial bacteria, forming an integrated microbiota-targeted strategy to optimize the therapeutic effects of anticancer agents. Substances that are selectively utilized by host microorganisms and confer health benefits are defined as prebiotics. Prebiotics are typically nondigestible carbohydrates, including inulin, fructo-oligosaccharides, galacto-oligosaccharides, resistant starch, and natural polyphenols. After reaching the intestine, they are selectively metabolized and fermented by gut microbes to produce beneficial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, thereby promoting intestinal health and modulating immune responses. In the context of cancer, these compounds exert antitumor effects and can act synergistically to enhance the efficacy of anticancer drugs. In addition, prebiotics can enrich gut microbial taxa such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, probiotics that have been shown in various cancer settings to improve the therapeutic effectiveness of anticancer agents. For instance, formulating inulin as a colon-retentive oral gel enables localized modulation of the colonic microbiota, reshaping the microbial community and increasing the relative abundance of key commensal species and their SCFA metabolites.182 This, in turn, induces a systemic memory T cell response, expands the pool of IFN-γ⁺ CD8⁺ T cells, and promotes the accumulation of TCF-1⁺ PD-1⁺ stem-like CD8⁺ T cells within the TME, ultimately leading to a marked improvement in the therapeutic efficacy of α-PD-1 immunotherapy.182 Furthermore, in CRC, prebiotic nanoparticles combined with capecitabine not only promote the growth of beneficial gut bacteria but also increase SCFA production, enhancing antitumor immunity and extending survival in mouse models.183 Similarly, a lipid nanoparticle system (FLSK) engineered by modifying 5-FU-loaded liposomes with prebiotic xylan derivative Sxy and probiotic Akkermansia muciniphila-derived phospholipid effectively extended the intestinal transport and release of 5-FU, ensuring sustained high drug concentrations at the tumor site, resulting in significant suppression of tumor growth.184 These findings underscore the significant potential of probiotics and prebiotics in combination with anticancer therapies, highlighting their ability to modulate the gut microbiome, enhance drug efficacy, and improve therapeutic outcomes in cancer treatment.

Drug delivery systems

In addition to the previously discussed systems that combine probiotics, prebiotics, and drugs to create new delivery platforms aimed at promoting gut microbiome balance and enhancing drug efficacy in Section 5.2, microbiota-targeted drug delivery systems also utilize the unique distribution and functions of bacteria within tumors and the gut to enhance anticancer efficacy and overcome resistance (Table 4). These systems can be broadly categorized based on their mechanisms, including targeting pathogenic bacteria, modulating the tumor microbiome, enhancing drug delivery, and improving immune responses. For instance, the PTE@CDDP system is designed to target and kill Fusobacterium nucleatum within colorectal cancer tumors, thus alleviating bacteria-induced inflammation. This, in turn, helps to reverse chemotherapy resistance and inhibits tumor growth.185 Similarly, the PYT@ZIF8@siRNA nanoparticles target Porphyromonas gingivalis, a bacterium linked to the progression of oral squamous cell carcinoma, and, by eliminating both the tumors and intratumoral Porphyromonas gingivalis, synergize with PD-1 blockers to enhance antitumor effects.186 Furthermore, LTA-MSN, which utilizes lipoteichoic acid antibody-conjugated mesoporous silica nanoparticles, recognizes bacteria-rich regions within tumors and releases chemotherapeutic agents locally upon binding. This localized drug release enhances drug accumulation and antitumor activity while sparing healthy tissues.17 Moreover, the oxaliplatin-encapsulated hydrogels system, which encapsulates oxaliplatin in hydrogels, targets the oral microbiome, activates antitumor immunity, and improves chemotherapy efficacy by modulating microbial populations within the TME.187 In addition, dextran nanoparticles conjugated with azide-functionalized bacteriophages have been shown to effectively deliver irinotecan, suppressing Fusobacterium nucleatum growth while enhancing chemotherapy through microbial modulation.188 These studies highlight the potential of microbiota-targeted drug delivery systems not only to enhance drug delivery but also to reshape the TME, offering novel strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance and optimize cancer treatment (Table 4).

Lifestyle

Lifestyle factors, such as diet and exercise, can significantly influence the efficacy of cancer therapies. Research has shown that these factors can impact the GM and immune responses, thus potentially enhancing the outcomes of cancer treatments. A study examining the impact of dietary sucralose on patients with advanced melanoma, NSCLC, and those receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy found that sucralose disrupted the GM, reducing therapy efficacy. This effect was linked to decreased microbiota-accessible arginine, essential for immune function. Tumor mouse models showed that supplementing with amino acids or using FMT could reverse drug resistance, restore T-cell function, and improve both immunotherapy and chemotherapy outcomes.189 In contrast, a high-fiber diet enhanced the efficacy of both immunotherapy and chemotherapy. Melanoma patients on a high-fiber diet had improved PFS and higher levels of beneficial bacteria like Ruminococcaceae and Faecalibacterium. Mouse models confirmed that a high-fiber diet increased interferon-gamma-positive cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in the TME, enhancing anti-PD-1 therapy response. Clinical trials in metastatic NSCLC patients showed similar benefits, with increased alpha diversity in the GM and enrichment of Alistipes and Bacteroides salyersiae. Metabolomic analysis revealed that the high-fiber diet promoted propionate production, which boosted immune responses by downregulating DNA repair mechanisms and upregulating adaptive immunity.190,191 Exercise also improves the efficacy of both immunotherapy and chemotherapy, particularly in melanoma. Exercise activates microbial one-carbon metabolism, increasing formate levels, which enhances cytotoxic CD8+ T cell (Tc1) function and improves treatment outcomes. Formate promotes Tc1 differentiation and tumor-specific immunity, with Nrf2 identified as a key mediator in this process. These findings suggest that exercise enhances both immunotherapy and chemotherapy by promoting Tc1-mediated antitumor immunity.192 The summary table of research on the impact of lifestyle on antitumor drugs is shown in Table 5.

Perspective and conclusions

The microbiota, encompassing both GM and IM, has emerged as an important regulator of tumorigenesis and therapeutic response. Across treatment modalities, microbial communities can either augment drug efficacy or blunt responses, and they can contribute to both the development and mitigation of resistance. Importantly, the microbiota is not a passive bystander during pharmacological intervention; it actively shapes host physiology and immune contexture in ways that influence outcomes. Conversely, anticancer therapies can remodel microbial composition and function, and these therapy-driven shifts may feed back to affect subsequent responses. Within tumors, treatment may reveal previously undetected taxa or expand pre-existing populations, and such IM changes can, in turn, modulate drug efficacy. Collectively, these observations support a bidirectional drug–microbiota interplay in which reciprocal constraints or synergy can shape overall therapeutic benefit.

Mechanistically, microbiota influence therapy mainly through immune regulation, metabolite production, and signal transduction. Microbial communities can remodel the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating antigen presentation, DC activity, and cytotoxic T-cell priming, ultimately promoting CD8⁺ T-cell expansion and tumor cell killing. In CRC, for example, certain GM states have been linked to cGAS–STING activation in DCs or other antigen-presenting cells, supporting T-cell priming and synergy with immunotherapy. Beyond immune modulation, microbiota-derived metabolites can reprogram the tumor immune landscape and reshape cellular metabolism, thereby influencing drug sensitivity. Notably, the same metabolite can have context-dependent effects across tumor types; For instance, Btr has been associated with beneficial effects in some settings (e.g. CRC and PDAC) yet potentially unfavorable associations in others such as melanoma, underscoring the complexity of metabolite-mediated regulation.

Despite rapid progress, context-specific—and sometimes opposing—microbiota effects complicate translation. The same taxon can exert divergent effects across therapies: in CRC, Fusobacterium nucleatum has been linked to improved ICI activity via immune modulation, yet it may also promote chemoresistance through autophagy- and apoptosis-related pathways. Microbiota associations can also vary across tumor types and clinical contexts: Akkermansia muciniphila has been associated with improved ICI responses in CRC, NSCLC, RCC, and HCC, whereas prostate cancer cohorts have reported contrasting patterns, including reduced abundance in responders. Moreover, outcomes are rarely dictated by a single organism; dysbiosis can shift the relative influence of beneficial versus harmful taxa, altering efficacy and resistance trajectories.

Additional barriers include limited reproducibility of microbiome signatures across cohorts and population-level variability, which constrain biomarker development and clinical interpretation. Nevertheless, microbiota-targeting strategies—such as rational antibiotic use, probiotics/postbiotics, FMT, improved delivery platforms, and lifestyle interventions—offer potential routes to enrich beneficial taxa or suppress detrimental ones and thereby improve treatment responses. However, most evidence remains preclinical, clinical datasets are still limited, and patient benefit is variable. Moving forward, clarifying causal microbiota–host–drug relationships, standardizing sampling and analytical pipelines, and validating findings across multiple cohorts will be essential to enable mechanism-guided, patient-tailored microbiota modulation and improve long-term outcomes.
