High-Dimensional Tensor Classification with CP Low-Rank Discriminant Structure
Elynn ChenYuefeng Han ♯, Jiayu Li

Abstract
Tensor classification has become increasingly crucial in statistics and machine learning, with applications spanning neuroimaging, computer vision, and recommender systems. However, the high dimensionality of tensors presents significant challenges in both theory and practice. To address these challenges, we introduce a novel data-driven classification framework based on linear discriminant analysis (LDA) that exploits the CP low-rank structure in the discriminant tensor. Our approach includes an advanced iterative projection algorithm for tensor LDA and incorporates a novel initialization scheme called Randomized Composite PCA ( rc-PCA ). rc-PCA , potentially of independent interest beyond tensor classification, relaxes the incoherence and eigen-ratio assumptions of existing algorithms and provides a warm start close to the global optimum. We establish global convergence guarantees for the tensor estimation algorithm using rc-PCA and develop new perturbation analyses for noise with cross-correlation, extending beyond the traditional i.i.d. assumption. This theoretical advancement has potential applications across various fields dealing with correlated data and allows us to derive statistical upper bounds on tensor estimation errors. Additionally, we confirm the rate-optimality of our classifier by establishing minimax optimal misclassification rates across a wide class of parameter spaces. Extensive simulations and real-world applications validate our method’s superior performance.



Keywords:

Tensor classification;
Linear discriminant analysis;
Tensor iterative projection;
CP low-rank;
High-dimensional data;
Minimax optimality.

This paper investigates tensor classification within the framework of high-dimensional tensor linear discriminant analysis (HD Tensor LDA). We consider a high-dimensional tensor predictor
{\cal X}
of dimension
{d_{1}\times d_{2}\cdots\times d_{M}}
and a class label
Y\in[K]

Vectorizing tensor observations not only leads to a multiplicative increase in dimensionality (see Remark

To tackle the challenge of high-dimensionality and preserve the unique tensor strcuture, we introduce the concept of a CP low-rank structure in the discriminant tensor and design a novel high-dimensional (HD) CP-LDA algorithm with theoretical guarantees, exploring the previously unstudied CP low-rank structure in the discriminant tensor. This low-rank discriminant structure implies that the principal discriminant direction lies in a low-rank tensor subspace, marking a significant departure from the sparsity assumptions employed in both high-dimensional vector LDA
(
Cai and Liu,, 2011
;
Fan et al.,, 2012
;
Cai and Zhang,, 2019

and tensor LDA
(

)

The HD CP-LDA algorithm directly estimate the discriminant tensor through iterative refinement of an initial estimate, which can be viewed as a perturbed version of the true discriminant tensor.
Unlike existing tensor estimation literature that typically assumes i.i.d. (sub-)Gaussian noise and imposes restrictive eigen-ratio conditions, our setting involves perturbation error with dependent entries and may not satisfy the conventional eign-ratio conditions. Consequently, current initialization methods and theoretical guarantees are inadequate for our context.

To address these challenges, we propose Randomized Composite PCA (
rc-PCA
), a novel initialization scheme for the CP bases of the discriminant tensor.
rc-PCA
relaxes key assumptions of existing initialization algorithms
(
Anandkumar et al., 2014b,
;
Sun et al.,, 2017
;
Han et al.,, 2024
)
. It accommodates non-i.i.d. noise entries, eases eigen-ratio conditions, and permits non-orthogonal CP bases.

Despite these relaxations, we have developed new theoretical results demonstrating that
rc-PCA
provides satisfactory initialization, ensuring that the tensor iterative projection algorithm converges to the global optimum.
Furthermore, we establish the convergence rate of HD CP-LDA, accommodating correlated entries in the noise tensor through new perturbation analysis.

Our proposed algorithms and theoretical results extend recent advancements in tensor classification theory.
Notably, tensor logistic regression is examined as a special case of generalized tensor estimation in
Han et al., 2022b ()
. Our work relaxes their assumption of independent elements within the tensor observation.
In the context of Tensor Gaussian Mixture Models (TGMM),
Pan et al., (2019)
investigates tensor LDA with an overall sparsity assumption on the discriminant structure.
Li et al., (2024)
assumes a Tucker low-rank structure for the discriminant tensor, incorporating group sparsity within the loading matrices.
Our work, however, focuses on the CP low-rank structure with potentially non-orthogonal CP bases, offering a novel perspective in tensor classification.
Despite existing work in empirical tensor classification
(
Guo et al.,, 2016
;
Liu et al.,, 2020
)
and theoretical tensor learning literature
(
Chen et al.,, 2024
;
Xia et al.,, 2022
;
Luo and Zhang,, 2022
)
, our HD Tensor LDA setting and the proposed HD CP-LDA algorithm address a crucial gap in tensor classification with theoretical guarantees.
The subsequent section will provide a comprehensive review of related literature, highlighting our distinctive contributions to the field.

Our contributions are twofold. Methodologically, we introduce the novel HD CP-LDA algorithm, which pioneers the use of CP low-rank structure for the discriminant tensor under minimal assumptions. We also develop a new initialization scheme,
rc-PCA
, for the CP bases of the discriminant tensor.
rc-PCA
operates under relaxed assumptions and provides a beneficial warm start close to the global optimum.
The
rc-PCA
has broader applications in tensor CP decompositions and regressions, making it of independent interest.
Extensive simulations and real data analyses demonstrate our algorithms’ superior performance over existing tensor LDA methods.
Theoretically, we establish sharp convergence rates for the initializations and the tensor iterative projection algorithm when applied to the discriminant tensor, accommodating correlated entries in the noise tensor. This advancement effectively addresses challenges posed by the complexity of tensor data, our CP low-rank structure, and spectrum perturbation analysis amid correlated noise entries. This result surpasses most current analyses in tensor decomposition, which typically assume i.i.d. entries in the noise tensor
(
Zhang and Xia,, 2018
;
Wang and Li,, 2020
)
. Additionally, we establish the first minimax optimal classification rate in the tensor classification literature, thereby advancing the theoretical foundations of the field.

High-dimensional vector discriminant analysis.

High-dimensional discriminant analysis has been widely studied in the vector domain, with much of the work rooted in Fisher’s discriminant analysis

Our CP low-rank approach captures complex interactions in tensor data that one-dimensional discriminant vectors cannot fully represent. By leveraging the intrinsic low-rank nature of tensor data, our method provides a more comprehensive analysis than traditional vector-based methods.

2  The Model

2.1
Notation and Preliminaries

An
M
-th order tensor is a multi-dimensional array
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
, with
{\cal I}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{M})
denoting the index of an element. The inner product
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\rangle=\sum_{{\cal I}}{\cal X}_{{\cal I}}{\cal Y}_{{\cal I}}
induces the Frobenius norm
\|\mathcal{X}\|_{\rm F}=\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}\rangle^{1/2}
. For
\mathnormal{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\widetilde{d_{m}}\times d_{m}}
, the mode-
m
product
\mathcal{X}\times_{m}\mathnormal{A}
yields an
M
-th order tensor of size
d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m-1}\times\widetilde{d_{m}}\times d_{m+1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}
. Elementwise,
(\mathcal{X}\times_{m}A)_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m-1},j,i_{m+1},\ldots,i_{M}}=\sum_{i_{m}=1}^{d_{m}}\mathcal{X}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{M}}\cdot A_{j,i_{m}}
. The mode-
m
matricization
\text{mat}_{m}(\mathcal{X})
is a
d_{m}\times\prod_{k\neq m}d_{k}
matrix, where
\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}\dots i_{M}}
maps to element
(i_{m},j)
, with
j=1+\sum\nolimits_{k\neq m}(i_{k}-1)\prod_{l<k,l\neq m}d_{l}
. For
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,M\}
, the multi-mode matricization
{\rm mat}_{S}({\cal X})
is a
\prod_{m\in S}d_{m}
-by-
\prod_{m\notin S}d_{m}
matrix with elements mapped as:

i=1+\sum_{m\in S}(i_{m}-1)\prod_{\ell\in S,\ell<m}d_{\ell}
,

j=1+\sum_{m\notin S}(i_{m}-1)\prod_{\ell\notin S,\ell<m}d_{\ell}
. Vectorization is denoted as
\text{vec}(\mathcal{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d=\prod_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}
. For a comprehensive review of tensor algebra and operations, we refer to
Kolda and Bader, (2009)
.
For a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, the matrix Frobenius norm is denoted as
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{\rm F}=(\sum_{ij}a_{ij}^{2})^{1/2}
and its spectral norm is denoted as
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|_{2}
. For two sequences of real numbers
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
, write
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
(resp.
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
) if there exists a constant
C
such that
|a_{n}|\leq C|b_{n}|
(resp.
1/C\leq a_{n}/b_{n}\leq C
) for all sufficiently large
n
, and write
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=0
. Write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
(resp.
a_{n}\gtrsim b_{n}
) if there exist a constant
C
such that
a_{n}\leq Cb_{n}
(resp.
a_{n}\geq Cb_{n}
). We use
C,C_{1},c,c_{1},...
to denote generic constants, whose actual values may vary from line to line.

The Tensor Normal (TN) distribution generalizes the matrix normal distribution to higher-order tensors
(
Hoff,, 2011
)
. Let
{\cal Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
be a random tensor with i.i.d.
N(0,1)
elements. Given a mean tensor
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
and mode-specific covariance matrices
\Sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{m}}
for
m\in[M]
, we define
{\cal X}={\cal M}+{\cal Z}\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{1/2}
.

Then,
{\cal X}\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}:=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
. The probability density function is:

f({\cal X}|{\cal M},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\left(\prod\nolimits_{m=1}^{M}|{\Sigma_{m}}|^{-\frac{d}{2d_{m}}}\right)\exp\left(-\left\lVert({\cal X}-{\cal M})\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}/2\right).

(1)

Importantly,
{\cal X}\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
is equivalent to
\vect({\cal X})\sim{\cal N}(\vect({\cal M});\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1})
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product.

2.2
Tensor High-Dimensional Linear Discriminant Analysis

We explore the classification of tensor variables using the Tensor Gaussian Mixture Model (TGMM), which extends the Gaussian Mixture Model to accommodate tensor-variate
{\cal X}
drawn from
K
classes
\{{\cal C}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
. The TGMM distribution for
({\cal X},Y)
, where
Y
is the class label, is specified by:

{\cal X}\;|\;Y=k\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{k};\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}),\quad\pi_{k}:=\mathbb{P}(Y=k),\quad\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}=1,\quad\text{for any }k\in[K],

(2)

where
{\cal M}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
is the mean tensor of the
k
th class,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}
represents the within-class covariance matrices, and
0<\pi_{k}<1
is the prior probability for class
k
. In this paper, we focus on the binary classification case (
K=2
) with equal covariance structure
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}:=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
for all
k\in[K]
. Given independent observations
{\cal X}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,{\cal X}_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
{\cal X}_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,{\cal X}_{n_{2}}^{(2)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, our goal is to classify a future data point
{\cal Z}
into one of the two classes.

In the ideal setting where all parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},{\cal M}_{1},{\cal M}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
are known, Fisher’s linear discriminant rule for Tensor LDA is given by:

\Upsilon({\cal Z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\langle{\cal Z}-{\cal M},\;{\cal B}\rangle+\log\Big(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1}\Big)\geq 0\right\},

(3)

where
{\cal M}=({\cal M}_{1}+{\cal M}_{2})/2
,
{\cal D}=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})
, and the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}={\cal D}\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1}
. The indicator function
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
assigns
{\cal Z}
to class
{\cal C}_{1}
(value 0) or
{\cal C}_{2}
(value 1). This rule, generalizing the classical vector case
(
Anderson,, 2003
)
, is optimal under the TGMM
(
Mai et al.,, 2021
)
. The optimal misclassification error is:

R_{\text{opt}}=\pi_{1}\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})-\Delta/2)+\pi_{2}(1-\phi(\Delta^{-1}\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})+\Delta/2)),

where
\phi
is the standard normal CDF and
\Delta=\sqrt{\langle{\cal B},\;{\cal D}\rangle}
is the signal-to-noise ratio. Building on the framework in vector LDA
(
Cai and Liu,, 2011
;
Mai et al.,, 2012
;
Cai and Zhang,, 2019
)
, this paper focuses on the estimation of the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
while enforcing a CP low-rank structure.

Remark 1
.

The classification rule can also be derived from Bayes’ Theorem. Let
f_{k}({\cal Z})\equiv\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{X}\;|\;Y=k)
be the conditional probability density function (
1
) for the
k
th class. Bayes’ Theorem states that
\mathbb{P}(Y=k\;|\;\mathcal{X})=\pi_{k}f_{k}({\cal Z})/(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}f_{k}({\cal Z}))
. The Bayes’ rule classifies an observation to the class maximizing
\mathbb{P}(Y=k\;|\;\mathcal{Z})
, achieving the lowest possible error rate
(
Domingos and Pazzani,, 1997
)
:
\widehat{Y}=\mathop{\argmax}_{k\in[K]}\mathbb{P}(Y=k\;|\;\mathcal{Z})=\mathop{\argmax}_{k\in[K]}\pi_{k}f_{k}({\cal Z})
. For binary classification (
K=2
), this simplifies to:

\Upsilon(\mathcal{Z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\pi_{2}f_{2}(\mathcal{X})-\pi_{1}f_{1}(\mathcal{X})\geq 0\right\}
. Rearranging and taking logarithms yields:

\Upsilon(\mathcal{Z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\log(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1})+\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{M}_{1})\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left\|(\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{M}_{2})\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}^{-1/2}\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}\geq 0\right\}.

The equivalence of
\Upsilon(\mathcal{Z})
to (
3
) can be demonstrated through tensor algebra.

Remark 2
.

Vectorizing tensor-variate
{\cal X}
to apply high-dimensional vector LDA leads to the discriminant rule:

\Upsilon(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbbm{1}\left\{(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{z}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}}{2})+\log\Big(\pi_{2}/\pi_{1}\Big)\geq 0\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{z}=\vect({\cal Z})
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}=\vect({\cal M}_{k})
. Studies in high-dimensional LDA
(
Cai and Liu,, 2011
;
Mai et al.,, 2012
;
Cai and Zhang,, 2019
)
estimate the discriminant direction
\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1})^{\top}(\Sigma_{M}\otimes\cdots\otimes\Sigma_{1})^{-1}
directly under sparsity assumptions. However, this approach may overlook tensor structures and face ultrahigh-dimensionality issues. For instance,
Cai and Zhang, (2018)
’s adaptive estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
requires solving linear programming (LP) problems. For a tensor
{\cal X}\in\mathbb{R}^{30\times 30\times 30}
, the resulting optimization involves 27,000 inequalities, becoming computationally extremely intensive.

Remark 3
.

While
Pan et al., (2019)
utilize group sparsity of
{\cal B}
with a group LASSO penalty, we recognize and utilize that the
multi-dimensional
discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
often exhibits richer low-rank structures, which captures complex dependencies among tensor dimensions beyond simple sparsity. Our proposed decomposable discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
will be elaborated upon later. One could employ the method from
Han et al., 2022a ()
by framing the problem as an optimization task using the negative log-likelihood function
{\cal L}
of logistic regression,

\widehat{{\cal B}}=\arg\min_{{\cal B}\;\text{is Tucker low-rank}}\mathcal{L}({\cal B};{\cal X}_{1}^{(k)},...,{\cal X}_{n_{k}}^{(k)},k=1,2).

However, measuring the statistical noise of the model—a crucial plug-in quantity in their theoretical framework for estimation errors—is non-trivial. This challenge hinders the validation of theoretical guarantees under their method. Additionally, their work typically assumes that
{\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}
has i.i.d. elements, a requirement that is often too stringent in practice. Consequently, our work necessitates the introduction of a new estimation procedure and the development of novel perturbation analysis to establish theoretical guarantees. Moreover, our approach diverges by imposing a CP low-rank structure on
{\cal B}
, relaxing the orthogonality assumption, and providing a novel perspective in the field of tensor classification.

3  Estimation of CP-Low-Rank Discriminant Tensor

In this paper, we propose an efficient method for estimating the discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
, utilizing the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) low-rank structure:

{\cal B}=\sum\nolimits_{r=1}^{R}w_{r}\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}_{r1}\circ\boldsymbol{a}_{r2}\circ\cdots\circ\boldsymbol{a}_{rM}),

(4)

where
R
is the CP low rank,
\circ
denotes the tensor outer product,
w_{r}>0
represents signal strength, and
\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}
is a unit vector of dimension
{d_{m}}
. Our estimation strategy encompasses two key components: firstly, developing an initial estimator that closely approximates the true discriminant tensor, ensuring a high probability of achieving an optimal solution; and secondly, algorithmically devising a computationally efficient method to refine this estimator, with provable convergence guarantees.

Remark 4
.

An alternative decomposition of
{\cal B}
is the Tucker low-rank structure:

{\cal B}={\cal F}\times_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{1}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{M}\boldsymbol{U}_{M},

where
{\cal F}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}\times\cdots\times r_{M}}
is the core tensor and
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}\times r_{i}}
are loading matrices. However, the CP low-rank structure of
{\cal B}
offers several advantages
(
Han et al.,, 2024
;
Erichson et al.,, 2020
)
. Unlike Tucker decomposition, which suffers from ambiguity due to invertible transformations, CP structure is uniquely defined up to sign changes, facilitating interpretation. Furthermore, while CP can be viewed as a special case of Tucker with a superdiagonal core tensor, our work allows for non-orthogonal CP bases
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{rm},r\in[R]\}
. This generalization, though more challenging, offers greater flexibility compared to the common orthonormal representation in Tucker decomposition. Additionally, CP’s sequence of signal strengths is more parsimonious than Tucker’s redundant core tensor, enhancing modeling, interpretation, and practical estimation. These characteristics render the CP low-rank structure superior for our discriminant tensor analysis.

In the subsequent sections, we introduce the sample discriminant tensor and explore the advantages of applying low-rank structures. We propose two algorithms:
(i) an iterative projection algorithm utilizing power iteration techniques to ensure linear local convergence, and
(ii) a method to generate a beneficial warm start from the sample discriminant tensor, which facilitates global convergence with high probability when used as the initial CP bases in the refinement process.

3.1
Sample Discriminant Tensor

For each class
{\cal C}_{k}
, let
n_{k}
be the sample size,
{\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}
the
i
-th sample, and
\overline{{\cal X}}^{(k)}=(n_{k})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}{\cal X}_{i}^{(k)}
the sample mean. We define the sample discriminant tensor as:

\widehat{\cal B}=(\overline{{\cal X}}^{(2)}-\overline{{\cal X}}^{(1)})\times_{m=1}^{M}\widehat{\Sigma}_{m}^{-1},

(5)

where
d=\prod_{\ell}d_{\ell},d_{-m}=d/d_{-m}
,

The sample discriminant tensor

, viewed as a perturbed version of the true

, no data-driven method can mimic the optimal misclassification error

in high-dimensional settings (

3.2
Refinement of the Discriminant Tensor

The core of the refinement procedure is iterative tensor projection (Algorithm
1
). Each iteration performs simultaneous orthogonalized projections across tensor modes to refine the CP components
\boldsymbol{a}_{rm},\;r\in[R]
, followed by estimating signal strengths
w_{r},\;r\in[R]
. This approach, based on the CP structure, preserves the signal strength of
{\cal B}
while reducing the perturbation
\widehat{\cal B}-{\cal B}
, thereby improving the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and estimation accuracy..

To illustrate the functionality, consider estimating mode-
m
CP bases
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}\}_{r\in[R]}
given true mode-
\ell
CP bases
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{r\ell}\}_{r\in[R]}
for all
\ell\neq m
. Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{1\ell},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{R\ell}]
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell})^{-1}=[\boldsymbol{b}_{1\ell},...,\boldsymbol{b}_{R\ell}]
as a right inverse of
\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}
, where
\boldsymbol{a}_{k\ell}^{\top}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}=\mathbbm{1}\{k=r\}
. Projecting the noisy
\widehat{\cal B}
with
\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}
on all modes
\ell\neq m
extracts the signal component containing only
\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}
:

\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}=\widehat{\cal B}\times_{\ell=1,\ell\neq m}^{M}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}^{\top}=\underbrace{w_{r}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}}_{\text{signal}}+\underbrace{(\widehat{\cal B}-{\cal B})\times_{\ell=1,\ell\neq m}^{M}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}^{\top}}_{\text{noise}}.

(6)

The signal term retains the strength
w_{r}
along
\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}
, while the noise term is filtered through projections. When the true
\{\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell},\;\ell\neq m\}
are known, the relative magnitude of the noise term is small compared to the signal term. This characteristic enables the distinction of the signal component with theoretical guarantees, utilizing perturbation theory
(
Wedin,, 1972
)
. In practice, with unknown
\{\boldsymbol{B}_{\ell},\;\ell\neq m\}
, we update CP bases
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{rm},\;r\in[R],m\in[M]\}
at the
t
-th iteration:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\cal B}\times_{1}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r1}^{(t)\top}\times_{2}\cdots\times_{m-1}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r,m-1}^{(t)\top}\times_{m+1}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r,m+1}^{(t-1)\top}\times_{m+2}\cdots\times_{M}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{rM}^{(t-1)\top},

\displaystyle\widehat{}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}^{(t)}/\|\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}^{(t)}\|_{2}.

The projection error is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}-\boldsymbol{z}_{rm}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=w_{r}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}-\sum_{i=1}^{R}\widetilde{w}_{i,r}\boldsymbol{a}_{im}+\boldsymbol{e}_{rm}-\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{e}_{rm},

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\widetilde{w}_{i,r}

\displaystyle=w_{i}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\left[\boldsymbol{a}_{i,\ell}^{\top}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}^{(t)}\right]\prod_{\ell=m+1}^{M}\left[\boldsymbol{a}_{i,\ell}^{\top}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}^{(t-1)}\right],

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{rm}=(\widehat{\cal B}-{\cal B})\times_{\ell=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{b}_{r,\ell}^{\top}

\displaystyle\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{e}_{rm}=(\widehat{\cal B}-{\cal B})\times_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r,\ell}^{(t)\top}\times_{\ell=m+1}^{M}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r,\ell}^{(t-1)\top}.

The multiplicative measure in
\widetilde{w}_{i,r}
decays rapidly as
\boldsymbol{a}_{i,\ell}^{\top}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{b}_{r\ell}^{(t)}
approaches zero for
i\neq r
with increasing iterations, and
\widetilde{w}_{i,r}
has a product of
M-1
such terms. The remaining terms are projected noise, controlled by the signal-to-noise ratio. Specifically, we can establish upper bounds on the estimation error of
\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}
, ensuring the accuracy of our recovered CP bases.

3.3
Initialization of CP Bases

The refinement procedure in Algorithm
1
requires initialization of CP bases
\{\widehat{}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}^{(0)},r\in[R],m\in[M]\}
. Given the non-convex nature of this problem and its propensity for multiple local optima, a carefully designed initialization is crucial for convergence to the optimal solution. We propose a novel Randomized Composite PCA (
rc-PCA
) initialization method, detailed in Algorithm
2
. This method efficiently produces initial estimates within the convergence zone of the iterative procedure, typically requiring only a polynomial number of attempts to achieve global convergence, as confirmed in Theorem
4.2
.

Our initialization strategy addresses two scenarios: one with a significant gap between signal strengths
\{w_{r},r\in[R]\}
, and another where all signal strengths are of similar magnitude. Consider the multi-modes matricization:

{\rm mat}_{S}({\cal B})=\sum\limits_{r=1}^{R}w_{r}\vect(\circ_{m\in S}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm})\vect(\circ_{m\in S^{c}}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm})^{\top},

where

are complementary subsets of
[M]
. For orthogonal tensor components, signal strengths directly correspond to singular values.
In the non-orthogonal case, Proposition
3.1
shows that under ‘mild’ non-orthogonality, the difference between signal strengths and singular values remains relatively small. Thus, we use the gap between singular values as a proxy for signal strength differences in Algorithm
2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}_{S}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1,S},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{R,S})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{S}\times R}

\displaystyle\|\widehat{}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}^{\rm rcpca}\widehat{}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}^{\rm rcpca\top}-\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}^{\top}\|_{2}

(18)

where

\displaystyle\phi_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{d_{1}d_{S_{1}}}+\sqrt{d_{1}d_{S_{1}^{c}}}}{\sqrt{n}w_{R}}+\frac{w_{1}}{w_{R}}\max_{1\leq k\leq M}\sqrt{\frac{d_{k}}{nd_{-k}}}.

(19)

Remark 6
.

We improve the existing initialization methods in tensor CP decomposition from several aspects. First, compare to CPCA in the literature
(
Han and Zhang,, 2023
;
Han et al.,, 2024
)
, the proposed initialization method allows for repeated singular values in tensor CP decomposition.
Second, our
rc-PCA
in Algorithm
2
provides weaker incoherence condition for the non-orthogonality of the CP basis vectors under small CP rank. For example, for 3-way tensors, the initial estimator in
Anandkumar et al., 2014b ()
requires the incoherence condition
\max_{i\neq j}\max_{m}\boldsymbol{a}_{im}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{jm}\leq{\rm polylog}(d_{\min})/\sqrt{d_{\min}}
. In comparison,
rc-PCA
initialization
\psi_{0}\lesssim 1
leads to
\max_{i\neq j}\max_{m}\boldsymbol{a}_{im}^{\top}\boldsymbol{a}_{jm}\lesssim R^{-2}
. Compared with the previous work, this is a weaker incoherence condition when
R\lesssim d_{\min}^{1/4}
.

Next, we develop the misclassification error rates of high-dimensional tensor LDA for data with low-rankness structure. We first obtain the upper and lower bounds for the excess misclassification risk of the Algorithm
1
. Then, the upper and lower bounds together show that the Algorithm
1
is rate optimal.

Recall that we classify tensor variables under the TGMM, with the tensor LDA rule defined in (
7
).
The classifier’s performance is evaluated by its misclassification error

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(label({\cal Z})\neq\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}({\cal Z})\big),

where
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
denotes the probability with respect to
{\cal Z}\sim\pi_{1}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\pi_{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
and
label({\cal Z})
denotes the true class of
{\cal Z}
. In this paper, we use the excess misclassification risk relative to the optimal misclassification error,
{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, to measure the performance of the classifier
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}
.

Theorem 4.3

(Upper bound of misclassification rate)
.

Assume the conditions in Theorem
4.1
all hold. Assume
w_{1}\sqrt{\max_{m}d_{m}^{2}R/(nd)}+\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}R/n}=o(\Delta)
with
n\rightarrow\infty
, where
\Delta=\sqrt{\langle{\cal B},\;{\cal D}\rangle}
is the signal-to-noise ratio.

(i) If
\Delta\leq c_{0}
, for some
c_{0}>0
, then with probability at least
1-n^{-c}-d_{1}^{-c}-\sum_{m=2}^{M}e^{-cd_{m}}
, the misclassification rate of classifier
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}
satisfies

{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq C\left(\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}R}{n}\right),

(20)

for some constant
C,c>0
.

(ii) If
\Delta\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, then there exists
\vartheta_{n}=o(1)
, with probability at least
1-n^{-c}-d_{1}^{-c}-\sum_{m=2}^{M}e^{-cd_{m}}
, the misclassification rate of classifier
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}
satisfies

{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq C\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{1}{8}+\vartheta_{n}\right)\Delta^{2}\right\}\left(\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}R}{n}\right),

(21)

for some constant
C,c>0
.

Compared with Theorem
4.1
, the excess misclassification rates in (
20
) and (
21
) only contain one part. The error induced by the estimation accuracy of the mode-
m
precision matrix becomes negligible in the excess misclassification rates.

To understand the difficulty of the tensor classification problem, it is essential to obtain the minimax lower bounds for the excess misclassification risk. We especially consider the following parameter space of CP low-rank discriminant tensors,

\displaystyle{\cal H}=\Big\{\;

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=({\cal M}_{1},{\cal M}_{2},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}):{\cal M}_{1},{\cal M}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}},\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M},\;\Sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times d_{m}},\;\text{for some}\;C_{0}>0,

\displaystyle C_{0}^{-1}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\otimes_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m})\leq\lambda_{\max}(\otimes_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m})\leq C_{0},\;{\cal B}=\sum_{r=1}^{R}w_{r}\circ_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}\;\text{with}\;\|\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}\|_{2}=1\Big\}.

The following lower bound holds over
{\cal H}
.

Theorem 4.4

(Lower bound of misclassification rate)
.

Under the TGMM, the minimax risk of excess misclassification error over the parameter space
{\cal H}
satisfies the following conditions.

(i) If
c_{1}<\Delta\leq c_{2}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, then for any
\gamma>0
, there exists some constant
C_{\gamma}>0
such that

\inf_{\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\cal H}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\gamma}\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}R}{n}\right)\geq 1-\gamma.

(22)

(ii) If
\Delta\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, then then for any
\gamma>0
, there exists some constant
C_{\gamma}>0
and
\vartheta_{n}=o(1)
such that

\inf_{\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\cal H}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\gamma}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{1}{8}+\vartheta_{n}\right)\Delta^{2}\right\}\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}R}{n}\right)\geq 1-\gamma.

(23)

Combined with the upper bounds of the excess misclassification risk in (
20
) and (
21
), the convergence rates are minimax rate optimal.

5  Simulation

This section presents extensive simulations demonstrating the superiority of the DISTIP-CP algorithm in estimating the discriminant tensor, leading to exceptional tensor classification accuracy. We explore various experimental configurations and employ multiple evaluation metrics to substantiate our theoretical findings. The simulations primarily focus on classifications involving order-3 tensor predictors, with results for order-4 tensor predictors provided in Appendix
C
.
We incorporate state-of-the-art classification methods as competitors, including the Tensor LDA rule with the sample discriminant tensor as a benchmark. Additionally, we include the CATCH estimator
(
Pan et al.,, 2019
)
and the Discriminant Tensor Iterative Projection using Tucker low-rank discriminant tensor (DISTIP-Tucker) algorithm for a thorough comparison.

5.1
Data Generation

We generate training and testing sets from tensor normal distributions
{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{k};\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
with
k\in[2]
. The training set comprises
n_{k}
samples of
{\cal X}^{(k)}
, while the testing set consists of 500 samples per class. Common covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
are either identity matrices or have unit diagonals with off-diagonal elements
2/d_{m}
.
CP bases for
{\cal B}
are generated as either orthogonal or non-orthogonal. Orthogonal bases
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times R}
are derived from random matrices with i.i.d.
U(0,1)
entries and orthonormalized via QR decomposition. Non-orthogonal bases
\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
are constructed using
\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{a}_{1m}=\boldsymbol{a}_{1m}
and
\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}=(\boldsymbol{a}_{1m}+\eta\boldsymbol{a}_{rm})/\left\lVert\boldsymbol{a}_{1m}+\eta\boldsymbol{a}_{rm}\right\rVert_{2}
for
r\geq 2
, where
\eta=(\vartheta^{-2/M}-1)^{1/2}
and
\vartheta=\delta/(r-1)
. The discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
is then configured with these bases and the signal strengths
\{w_{r},r\in[R]\}
. For order-3 tensors, we explore four configurations combining orthogonal/non-orthogonal CP bases with identity/general covariance matrices. Across all settings, we maintain
d_{1}=d_{2}=d_{3}=30
,
R=5
,
n_{1}=n_{2}=200
, and perform 50 repetitions. We vary signal strengths
{w_{r}}
as follows:

•

Equal strengths:
w_{\max}/w_{\min}=1
with
w_{\max}\in{1.5,2.5,3.5,5.0}
;

•

Unequal strengths:
w_{r+1}/w_{r}=1.25
with
w_{max}\in{3,4,6}
.

For identity covariance matrices, we set
{\cal M}_{1}=0
and
{\cal M}_{2}={\cal B}
. For general covariance matrices, we set
{\cal M}_{2}={\cal B}\times_{m=1}^{M}\Sigma_{m}
. For non-orthogonal CP bases, we set the parameter
\delta=0.1
in the data generating process.

Comparison with other methods

In this subsection, we compare the proposed DISTIP-CP method with CATCH

, DISTIP-Tucker, and the sample discriminant tensor for binary classification tasks. We also attempted to implement the adaptive estimation by

Comparison with Sample Discriminant Tensor
{\cal B}
: DISTIP-CP significantly outperforms the sample discriminant tensor in both estimation accuracy (Tables

) and classification performance (Tables
3
and
4
). This superiority stems from DISTIP-CP’s incorporation of the low-rank CP structure, which effectively reduces noise and captures the essential features of the discriminant tensor. The advantage is particularly pronounced in low-signal scenarios, where the sample estimator struggles with high-dimensional noise. As signal strength increases, the performance gap narrows, but DISTIP-CP consistently maintains an edge.

Comparison with DISTIP-Tucker
: DISTIP-CP outperforms DISTIP-Tucker, particularly at smaller signal strengths, due to its superior capture of the underlying tensor structure.

Comparison with CATCH
: Although the estimation error of the discriminant tensor using CATCH appears competitive, this perception is misleading due to the choice of metric. CATCH outputs a sparse discriminant tensor with most entries being zero, which keeps the metric
\|\widehat{\cal B}-{\cal B}\|_{\rm F}/\|{\cal B}\|_{\rm F}
close to 1. However, its classification performance is poor, even compared to the sample discriminant tensor
{\cal B}
(Tables
3
and
4

DISTIP-CP better captures the underlying low rank tensor structure, leading to more accurate estimations and, consequently, more precise classifications.

In summary, DISTIP-CP with
rc-PCA
initialization excels in discriminant tensor estimation and classification across diverse scenarios, demonstrating its robustness to varying signal strengths, CP basis orthogonality, and covariance structures.

7  Conclusion

This paper introduces a novel tensor classification framework that addresses critical gaps in the existing literature. The proposed approach employs a CP low-rank structure for the discriminant tensor, which provides a more flexible and parsimonious representation than current methods. This structure effectively captures complex interactions in tensor data that traditional sparse tensors fail to model, marking a significant innovation in the field.

To capitalize on this structure, we developed the Tensor HD CP-LDA algorithm, incorporating a novel Randomized Composite PCA (
rc-PCA
) initialization scheme and an iterative refinement algorithm. The CP-LDA algorithm achieves global convergence, particularly in challenging scenarios involving intricate signal strengths and non-orthogonal CP bases, substantially advancing the algorithm design.

Moreover, the paper establishes sharp bounds for estimating the CP low-rank discriminant tensor. Our analysis accounts for correlated noise tensor entries during perturbation analysis, offering a more realistic framework for practical applications where noise correlations are common. This approach can be applied to other tensor learning analyses. Additionally, we derived minimax optimal misclassification rates, providing the first comprehensive theoretical guarantees in tensor classification.

Despite the significant advancements made by the proposed method, several directions for future research remain: (1) Investigating the application of this framework to non-Gaussian tensor data is particularly promising. Previous work by
Shao et al., (2011)
has demonstrated the optimality of LDA rules for elliptical distributions in vector classification, suggesting that our tensor classification framework could perform well in non-Gaussian settings. (2) Additionally, exploring the integration of our CP low-rank approach with other tensor learning tasks, such as regression and clustering, could yield valuable insights.

is non zero for all

Denote the loading matrices

for each mode

.

First, let
\boldsymbol{A}_{m}
be a fixed matrix where the
(i,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,R
, are set to one and all other elements are zero. Denote

\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{M}\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{M-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
. According to basic tensor algebra, this setup implies that

\vect({\cal M})=\boldsymbol{A}\vect({\cal F})
and
\|{\cal M}\|_{\rm F}=\|\vect({\cal F})\|_{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
be the basis vector in the standard Euclidean space whose first entry is 1 and 0 elsewhere, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}=[\boldsymbol{I}_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M}
.
Define the following parameter space

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{0}=

\displaystyle\big\{\theta=({\cal M}_{1},\;{\cal M}_{2},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}):\;{\cal M}_{1}={\cal F}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{m},\;{\cal M}_{2}=-{\cal M}_{1};\ \vect({\cal F})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{f}\in\{0,1\}^{R},\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0\big\},

where

,

and

is chosen to ensure that
\theta\in{\cal H}
such that

\displaystyle\Delta=({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}({\cal M}_{2}-{\cal M}_{1})=4\|\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\|_{2}^{2}=4\epsilon^{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}^{2}+4\lambda^{2}.

In addition to
{\cal H}_{0}
, we also define
\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}
, for
\ell\neq m
, as fixed matrices where the
(i,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,R
, are set to one and all other elements are zero. Let
{\cal F}
be a diagonal tensor such that the
(i,...,i)
-th elements,
i=1,...,R
, are set to one, and all other elements are zero. It implies that
\|{\cal M}\|_{\rm F}=\|\boldsymbol{A}_{m}\|_{\rm F}
. For
m=1,...,M
, define the following parameter spaces

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{m}=\big\{

\displaystyle\theta=({\cal M}_{1},\;{\cal M}_{2},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}):\;{\cal M}_{1}={\cal F}\times_{k=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{k},\;{\cal M}_{2}=-{\cal M}_{1};\ \vect(\boldsymbol{A}_{m})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{g}_{m}+\lambda_{m}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{g}_{m}\in\{0,1\}^{d_{m}R},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0\big\},

\epsilon=c/\sqrt{n}
,

and
\lambda_{m}
is chosen to ensure that

. We shall show below separately for the minimax risks over each parameter space
{\cal H}_{\ell}
.

First, consider
{\cal H}_{0}
. By Lemma
B.13
, we can construct a sequence of
R
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{N}\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, such that
\boldsymbol{f}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0
,

, and

, where

denotes the Hamming distance.
To apply Lemma
B.12
, for
\forall\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}\in{\cal H}_{0},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}\neq\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}},
we need to verify two conditions:

(i)

the upper bound on the Kullback-Leibler divergence between

and
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}}
, and

.

We calculate the Kullback-Leibler divergence first. For
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\{0,1\}^{R}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=0

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}}={\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\times_{m=1}^{M}\boldsymbol{A}_{m},\;\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}})=\epsilon\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{u}}+\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\;\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}=\big({\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;-{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\;\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\big)\in{\cal H}_{0}.

and consider the distribution

.
Then the Kullback-Leibler divergence between

and

can be bounded by

In addition, by applying Lemma

, we have that for any

\displaystyle L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{u}}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})+L_{\theta_{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\left\lVert\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{u}})-\vect({\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{v}})\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\sqrt{\frac{R}{8}\cdot\frac{c^{2}}{n}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\sqrt{\frac{R}{n}}.

So far we have verified the aforementioned conditions (i) and (ii). Lemma
B.12
immediately implies that, there exists some constant

, such that

\inf_{\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(L_{\theta}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})\geq C_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\sqrt{\frac{R}{n}}\right)\geq 1-\gamma

64
) and (
63

\inf_{\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\frac{R}{n}\right)\geq 1-\gamma.

(65)

Similarly, for each
{\cal H}_{m}
,

\inf_{\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp}}\sup_{\theta\in{\cal H}_{m}}\mathbb{P}\left({\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\Upsilon}_{\rm cp})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq C_{\gamma}\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\frac{d_{m}R}{n}\right)\geq 1-\gamma.

(66)

Finally combining (

This implies that if

The following lemma presents the tail bound for the spectral norm of a Gaussian random matrix.

Lemma B.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{E}
be an
p_{1}\times p_{2}
random matrix with
\boldsymbol{E}\sim{\cal M}{\cal N}_{p_{1}\times p_{2}}(0,\;\Sigma_{1},\;\Sigma_{2}).
Then for any
t>0
, with constant
C>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(78)

Let
\mathbb{O}_{p_{1},r}=\{\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times r},\;\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\}
be the set of all
p_{1}\times r
orthonormal columns and let
\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}
be the orthogonal complement of
\boldsymbol{U}
. Denote
\boldsymbol{E}_{12}=\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp},\;\boldsymbol{E}_{21}=\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{V}
, where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{O}_{p_{1}\times r},\;\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{O}_{p_{2}\times r}.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(79)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{12}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq\exp(-t^{2})

(80)

Proof.

Similar to (
77
), using
\varepsilon
-net argument for unit ball (see e.g. Lemma 5 in
Cai and Zhang, (2018)
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq 3u\right)\leq 7^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\cdot\max_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,\|y\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y|\geq u\right).

Decompose
\boldsymbol{E}=\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}
, where
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}},\;\boldsymbol{Z}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}N(0,1).

Then,

\displaystyle x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y=\left(y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\otimes x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\right)\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z})\sim N\left(0,y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}y\cdot x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}x\right).

By Chernoff bound of Gaussian random variables,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}y|\geq u\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{y^{\top}\Sigma_{2}y\cdot x^{\top}\Sigma_{1}x}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{u^{2}}{\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}}\right).

Setting
u\asymp\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)
, for certain
C>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}\|_{2}\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+\sqrt{p_{2}}+t)\right)\leq 2\cdot 7^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\exp(-c(p_{1}+p_{2})-t^{2})\leq\exp(-t^{2}).

For
\boldsymbol{E}_{21}
, following the same
\epsilon
-net arguments, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|_{2}\geq 3u\right)\leq 7^{(p_{1}-r)+r}\cdot\max_{\|x\|_{2}\leq 1,\|y\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathbb{P}\left(|x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{21}y|\geq u\right).

Note that

\displaystyle x^{\top}\boldsymbol{E}_{21}y

\displaystyle=x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{Z}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}y=\left(y^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\otimes x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\right)\vec 1(\boldsymbol{Z})

\displaystyle\sim N\left(0,y^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\Sigma_{2}\boldsymbol{V}y\cdot x^{\top}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}x\right).

By Chernoff bound of Gaussian random variables, setting
u\asymp\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{E}_{21}\|\geq C\|\Sigma_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}(\sqrt{p_{1}}+t)\right)\leq 2\cdot 7^{p_{1}}\exp(-c(p_{1})-t^{2})\leq\exp(-t^{2}).

Similarly, we can derive the tail bound of
\|\boldsymbol{E}_{12}\|_{2}
.

\varepsilon

,

. And

\displaystyle T=

Lemma B.8
.

Suppose that
{\cal X}_{1},...,{\cal X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
are i.i.d.
{\cal T}{\cal N}(\mu,\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}
, and
\overline{{\cal X}}
is the sample mean. Then, with probability at least
1-e^{-c\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<\overline{{\cal X}}-\mu,{\cal V}>\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r}{n}}.

(85)

Proof.

For any CP low rank tensor
{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}
with
w_{i}>0
and
\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1
, we can reformulate a Tucker type decomposition,
{\cal V}={\cal S}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m}
with
{\cal S}=\diag(w_{1},...,w_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times\cdots\times r}
and
U_{m}=(u_{1m},...,u_{rm})
. Let
N_{0}=\lfloor(1+2/\epsilon)^{r}\rfloor
and
N_{m}=\lfloor(1+2/\epsilon)^{d_{m}r}\rfloor
. There exists
\epsilon
-nets
{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times\cdots\times r}
with diagonal elements
{\cal S}_{j_{0},k,...,k}^{*}\neq 0
and all the off-diagonal elements being 0,
\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,j_{0}=1,...,N_{0}
, and
U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{m}\times r}
with
\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,j_{m}=1,...,N_{m},1\leq m\leq M
, such that

\displaystyle\max_{\|{\cal S}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1}\min_{1\leq j_{0}\leq N_{0}}\|{\cal S}-{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq\epsilon,\qquad\max_{\|U_{m}\|_{2}\leq 1}\min_{1\leq j_{m}\leq N_{m}}\|U_{m}-U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,1\leq m\leq M.

Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1
is equivalent to
\|{\cal S}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1
. Let
{\cal Y}=\overline{X}-\mu
.
Then by the “subtraction argument",

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal V}>\right|-\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
j_{0}\leq N_{0},j_{m}\leq N_{m},\forall 1\leq m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal V}>\right|-\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,j_{0}\leq N_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m}>\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
j_{0}\leq N_{0},j_{m}\leq N_{m},\forall m\leq k\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{k}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\times_{m=k+1}^{M}U_{m}>\right|\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.-\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
j_{0}\leq N_{0},j_{m}\leq N_{m},\forall m\leq k+1\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{k+1}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\times_{m=k+2}^{M}U_{m}>\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(M+1)\epsilon\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal V}>\right|

Setting
\epsilon=1/(2M+2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal V}>\right|\leq 2\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
j_{0}\leq N_{0},j_{m}\leq N_{m},\forall 1\leq m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|.

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal V}>\right|\geq x\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\|_{\rm F}\leq 1,\|U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}\|_{2}\leq 1,\\
j_{0}\leq N_{0},j_{m}\leq N_{m},\forall 1\leq m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|\geq x/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{M}N_{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|\geq x/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(4M+5)^{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r+r}\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|\geq x/2\right).

Since
{\cal Y}=\overline{X}-\mu\sim{\cal T}{\cal N}(0,\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, we can show
|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>|
is a
n^{-1/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\|\Sigma_{m}\|_{2}^{1/2}
Lipschitz function, and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>|\right]\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}n^{-1/2}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\|\Sigma_{m}\|_{2}^{1/2}
. Then, by Gaussian concentration inequalities for Lipschitz functions,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>\right|\geq{\mathbb{E}}\left[|<{\cal Y},{\cal S}_{j_{0}}^{*}\times_{m=1}^{M}U_{m,j_{m}}^{*}>|\right]+t\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{\prod_{m=1}^{M}\|\Sigma_{m}\|_{2}}\right).

Setting
x\asymp t\asymp\sqrt{(\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r+r)/n}
, in an event with at least probability at least
1-e^{-c\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\cal V}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}\circ_{m=1}^{M}u_{im}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\|u_{im}\|_{2}=1,\forall i\leq r,m\leq M\end{subarray}}\left|<\overline{{\cal X}}-\mu,{\cal V}>\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}d_{m}r}{n}}.

∎

The following lemma gives an inequality in terms of Frobenius norm between two tensors.

Lemma B.9
.

For two
M
-th order tensors
\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{M}}
, if
\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o(\|\gamma\|_{\rm F})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, and
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\geq c
for some constant
c>0
, then when
n\rightarrow\infty
,

\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\rangle\asymp\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
{\cal E}=\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma
, when
\left\lVert\gamma-\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o(\|\gamma\|_{F})
and
\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\geq c,
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\widehat{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\cdot\left\lVert\gamma+{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}-\langle\gamma,\;\gamma+{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}\sqrt{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}+2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}}-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle\asymp\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}\big(1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle+\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}\big)-\left\lVert\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}-\langle\gamma,\;{\cal E}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\lVert{\cal E}\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}}{2}\asymp\left\lVert\widehat{\gamma}-\gamma\right\rVert_{\rm F}^{2}.

∎

The following lemma illustrates the relationship between the risk function
{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and a more “standard” risk function
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
, which fulfills a role similar to that of the triangle inequality, as demonstrated in Lemma

B.10
and
B.11

Cai and Zhang, (2019)

Lemma B.10
.

Let
{\cal Z}\sim\frac{1}{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{1};\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})+\frac{1}{2}{\cal T}{\cal N}({\cal M}_{2};\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
with parameter
\theta=({\cal M}_{1},\;{\cal M}_{2},\;\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=[\Sigma_{m}]_{m=1}^{M}

\widehat{\delta}
satisfies

as

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\widehat{\delta})-{\cal R}_{\rm opt}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\Delta}{8}e^{\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot L_{\theta}^{2}(\widehat{\delta}).

Lemma B.11
.

Let
\theta=({\cal M},\;-{\cal M},\;[I_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M})
and
\tilde{\theta}=(\tilde{{\cal M}},\;-\tilde{{\cal M}},\;[I_{d_{m}}]_{m=1}^{M})

\left\lVert{\cal M}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\left\lVert\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=\Delta/2

\delta

\left\lVert{\cal M}-\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}=o(1)

\displaystyle L_{\theta}(\delta)+L_{\tilde{\theta}}(\delta)\geq\frac{1}{\Delta}e^{-\Delta^{2}/8}\cdot\left\lVert{\cal M}-\tilde{{\cal M}}\right\rVert_{\rm F}.

Although
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
is not a distance function and does not satisfy an exact triangle inequality, the following lemma provides a variant of Fano’s lemma. It suggests that it suffices to provide a lower bound for
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
, and
L_{\theta}(\widehat{\delta})
satisfies an approximate triangle inequality (Lemma
B.11
).

Lemma B.12

(
Tsybakov, (2009)
)
.

Let
N\geq 2
and
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{N}\in\Theta_{d}
. For some constants
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1/8),\gamma>0
and any classifier
\widehat{\delta}
, if
{\rm KL}(\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{j}})\leq\alpha_{0}\log N/n
for all
1\leq i\leq N

implies
L_{\theta_{j}}(\widehat{\delta})\geq\gamma
for all
0\leq i\neq j\leq N
, then

\displaystyle\inf_{\widehat{\delta}}\sup_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{i}}(L_{\theta_{i}}(\widehat{\delta}))\geq\gamma)\geq\frac{\sqrt{N}}{1+\sqrt{N}}(1-2\alpha_{0}-\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha_{0}}{\log N}})>0.

Lemma B.13

(Varshamov-Gilbert Bound,
Tsybakov, (2009)
)
.

Consider the set of all binary sequences of length m:
\Omega=\big\{\omega=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}),\omega_{i}\in\{0,1\}\big\}=\{0,1\}^{m}

\rho_{H}(\omega^{(i)},\omega^{(j)})\geq m/8,\forall 0\leq i<j\leq N
