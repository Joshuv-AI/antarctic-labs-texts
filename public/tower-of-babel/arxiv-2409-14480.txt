Large-Time Asymptotics for the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili I Equation
Samir Donmazov, Jiaqi Liu, Peter Perry

Abstract
. We prove large-time asymptotics for solutions of the KP I equation with small initial data. Our assumptions on the initial data rule out lump solutions but enable us to give a precise description of the radiation field at large times. Our analysis uses the inverse scattering method and involves large-time asymptotics for solutions to a non-local Riemann-Hilbert problem.



1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to determine the large-time asymptotic behavior of solutions to the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili I equation on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with small initial data using inverse scattering methods. The Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation is the dispersive equation

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left(u_{t}+u_{xxx}+6uu_{x}\right)_{x}&=3\lambda u_{yy}\\
u(0,x,y)&=u_{0}(x,y).\end{aligned}\right.

for a function
u=u(t,x,y)
describing long weakly dispersive waves in two space dimensions. The case
\lambda=1
is the KP I equation, and the case
\lambda=-1
is the KP II equation. The KP equation is integrable in the sense that a smooth function
u=u(t,x,y)
solves the KP equation if and only if
u
satisfies the comptability condition for the system

(1.2)

\displaystyle\left(\sigma\partial_{y}+\partial_{x}^{2}+u\right)\psi

\displaystyle=0

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+4\partial_{x}^{3}+6u\partial_{x}+3\left(u_{x}-\sigma\partial_{x}^{-1}\partial_{y}u\right)\right)\psi

\displaystyle=0

(where
\sigma=i
for KP I and
\sigma=1
for KP II) . The linear problem (
1.2
) defines “scattering data” which, by (
1.3
) and the compatibility condition, evolves linearly under the flow defined by the KP equation (
1.1
). Inverse scattering for the KP I equation was studied by Zakharov and Manakov
[
29
]
, Manakov
[
22
]
, and Fokas-Ablowitz
[
10
]
. A rigorous analysis of the inverse scattering method for KP I was given by Zhou
[
30
]
and was further studied by Fokas-Sung
[
11
,
12
]
and Sung
[
25
]
.

To solve (
1.1
) by inverse scattering, one computes time-zero scattering data from (
1.2
) with
u=u_{0}
, evolves the scattering data linearly in time, and recovers
u(t,x,y)
via an “inverse scattering map” from the time-evolved scattering data to the solution.

One of the strengths of the completely integrable method is its potential to analyze long-term behavior of solutions. For completely integrable systems in one space dimension, the method of nonlinear steepest descent developed by Deift and Zhou
[
6
]
, based on the formulation of the inverse scattering map as a matrix Riemann-Hilbert problem, facilitated a complete analysis of asymptotic behavior for the modified Korteweg-de Vries (mKdV) equation
[
6
]
, the Korteweg-De Vries (KdV) equation
[
5
]
, and the defocusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation
[
7
]
.

The inverse scattering method has also led to proofs of the soliton resolution conjecture, which asserts that, at large times, the solution of a dispersive PDE separates into solitons and radiation. Equations for which soliton resolution holds include the KdV equation (Eckhaus-Schuur
[
9
]
), the mKdV equation (Chen and Liu
[
3
]
), the cubic NLS equation (Borghese, Jenkins, McLaughlin
[
2
]
equation, and the derivative nonlinear Schrödinger (dNLS) equation (Jenkins, Liu, Perry, and Sulem
[
17
]
).

One might expect that rigorous use of the inverse scattering method for the KP I and KP II equations would produce similar insights into asymptotic behavior of these models. As we will explain, our paper constitutes a first step in this direction for KP I. For results on KP II, we refer to the paper of Kiselev
[
18
]
, which we discuss in what follows, and more recent work of Derchyi Wu
[
27
]
on large-time asymptotics for solutions of the KP II equation for small initial data.

The KP I and KP II equations have some similarities but also some important differences. Both take the form (
1.1
) with linear term

(1.4)

\mathcal{L}=\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{3}-3\lambda\partial_{x}^{-1}\partial_{yy},

and, from the asymptotics of the linear equation
\mathcal{L}v=0
, one might expect
\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-1}\right)
decay of radiation in
L^{\infty}
norm. However the KP II equation has no soliton-like solutions (see deBouard and Saut
[
4
]
), while the KP I has well-studied “lump” solutions–solutions which translate in time and have algebraic spatial decay (see, for example, Villarroel and Ablowitz
[
26
]
and references therein, and see
[
20
, Chapter 5.7]
for a comprehensive survey of results up to 2021). It is known that there is a small-data threshold for lump solutions to occur (see Segur
[
24
]
), and our assumptions on the initial data will rule out lump solutions. What we can hope to recover is the
\mathcal{O}\left(t^{-1}\right)
decay of solutions for such small data.

The KP I equation differs from most of its counterparts in one space dimension in ways that make the inverse scattering approach to large-time asymptotics substantially more challenging. Unlike the inverse scattering maps for KdV, mKdV, NLS, and dNLS, the inverse scattering map for KP I is defined by a
nonlocal
Riemann-Hilbert problem for which there is, at present, no “nonlinear method of steepest descent’ and not even a well-developed theory comparable to the comprehensive literature on matrix Riemann-Hilbert problems. In this paper, we obtain–for the first time, to our knowledge–sharp asymptotic estimates on solutions to the nonlocal Riemann-Hilbert problem for KP I that enable us, via the reconstruction formula by inverse scattering, to obtain good asymptotics of the solutions to KP I under our hypotheses on initial data.

The KP I and KP II equations have been extensively studied by PDE methods: we refer the reader to the chapter 5 of the monograph of Klein and Saut
[
20
]
for a comprehensive discussion of results on the KP equation via both PDE and inverse scattering approaches. We will largely confine our remarks on the PDE literature here to global well-posedness results that we will use in our analysis of KP I and to preceding work on large-time asymptotics for KP I.

Ionescu, Kenig, and Tataru
[
16

(1.5)

\mathbf{E}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\{\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}:\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathbf{E}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}<\infty\}

\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathbf{E}_{1}}\coloneqq\left\|\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\partial_{x}\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\partial_{x}^{-1}\partial_{y}\varphi\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

We will work in a subspace
\mathbf{E}_{1,w}
of
\mathbf{E}_{1}
with
more stringent decay and regularity hypotheses. We define

(1.7)

\mathbf{E}_{1,w}=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbf{E}_{1,w}}<\infty\}

(1.8)

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbf{E}_{1,w}}

\displaystyle=\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4,5}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\partial_{x}^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}+\left\|\partial_{x}^{-1}\partial_{y}u\right\|_{L^{2}_{x}L^{2,1}_{y}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Here

\mathbf{H}^{4,5}(\mathbb{R}^{2})=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4,5}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}<\infty\right\}

\left\|u\right\|_{\mathbf{H}^{4,5}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\\

A number of authors have studied asymptotics for the KP equations by PDE methods. Building on previous analysis of the generalized KP equation due to Hayashi, Naumkin and Saut
[
14

\left\|\partial_{x}u\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim(1+t)^{-1}

u_{x}(t,x,y)
, and find a scattering asymptote for
u_{x}
in
L^{\infty}
. Harrop-Griffiths, Ifrim, and Tataru
[
13
]
study solutions of KP I in a natural weighted space with Galilean-invariant norms: they obtain a similar
L^{\infty}
bound on
u_{x}
and also prove scattering of
u
in
L^{2}
for small data. Their approach is motivated in part by an analysis of the Hamiltonian flow associated to (
1.1
) and its behavior in the “propagation region” which is essentially the region
a<0
described in our main result, Theorem
1.1
, stated below (see their discussion in §1 of
[
13
]
). Mendez, Mũnoz, Poblete and Pozo
[
23
]
study long-time asymptotics for large data. More recently, Alegria, Chen, Muńoz, Poblete, and Tardy
[
1
]
have studied soliton structures for the KP II equation. They study classes of solutions of KP II of the form
u(x,y,t)=2\partial_{x}^{2}(F(\Theta))
, where
F:[s_{0},\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a profile and
\Theta
is a phase. They establish an equivalence between various types of soliton solutions and corresponding classes of amplitudes and phases.

So far as we are aware, however, there are no results in the PDE literature on the
L^{\infty}
behavior of
u
itself, even for small initial data, which show
t^{-1}
decay in time.

On the other hand, large-time asymptotics for solutions of both the KP I and KP II equation have also been studied via inverse scattering. Kiselev
[
18
]
derived leading asymptotics for the KP II equation under regularity and small data assumptions on the time zero scattering data for KP II. Manakov, Santini and Takhtajan
[
21

[
19

), the results of Manakov, Santini and Takhtajan are confined to only one of several space-time régimes for KP I.

(1.10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}v&=0\\

Introduce “slow” variables

\zeta=\frac{x}{t},\quad\eta=\frac{y}{t}.

The Cauchy problem (

\displaystyle v(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{it(\zeta p+\eta q+(p^{3}+3\lambda q^{2}/p))}\widehat{v_{0}}(p,q)\,dp\,dq

p=l-k,q=-(l^{2}-k^{2})

\displaystyle v(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int e^{itS_{0}(k,l;\zeta,\eta)}\widehat{v}_{0}(l-k,-(l^{2}-k^{2}))|l-k|\,dl\,dk

where the phase function is

(1.14)

\displaystyle S_{0}(k,l;\zeta,\eta)

\displaystyle=\zeta(l-k)-\eta(l^{2}-k^{2})+4(l^{3}-k^{3})

A similar analysis for the linear KP II equation with the change of variables

\displaystyle v(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{C}}e^{itS(k,\overline{k};\zeta,\eta)}i(k+\overline{k})\widehat{v}_{0}(-(k+\overline{k}),i(k^{2}-\overline{k}^{2}))dk\wedge d\overline{k}

We consider the scattering solutions

Here and in what follows,

We will sometimes make a change of variables to obtain

To prove (

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose that

and
\widetilde{u}\in L^{1,1}_{l}(\mathbb{R})

\left\|\widetilde{u}*f\right\|_{L^{2,1}_{l}}\leq\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{1,1}_{l}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2,1}_{l}}.

Proof.

From the usual Young’s inequality, we have

(2.39)

\left\|\widetilde{u}*f\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{1}_{l}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}.

Next, we write

l(\widetilde{u}*f)(l)=\int(l-l^{\prime})\widetilde{u}(l-l^{\prime})f(l^{\prime})\ dl^{\prime}+\int\widetilde{u}(l-l^{\prime})l^{\prime}f(l^{\prime})\,dl^{\prime}.

Applying (
2.39

\widetilde{u}
and

(2.40)

\left\|l(\widetilde{u}*f)\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq\left\|l\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}+\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{1}}\left\|lf\right\|_{L^{2}}.

We conclude that
(
2.38
) holds.

\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{1,1}_{l}L^{1}_{y}}<\sqrt{2\pi}.

Next, we compute

\displaystyle g_{k,u}^{\pm}(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{y}e^{-il(l+2k)(y-\eta)}\widetilde{u}(l;\eta)\,d\eta,

\displaystyle=\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{y}e^{-il(l+2k)(y-\eta)}l\widetilde{u}(l;\eta)\,d\eta.

From (

) and (

Lemma 2.7
.

Suppose that
\widetilde{c}<1

and
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}\cap L^{2,1}_{y}L^{2,1}_{l}

with

Proof.

Direct calculation gives

(2.45)

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta)+\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}\right](\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp})+g_{k,u}^{\pm}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right).

We first make a translation:

(2.46)

g_{k,u}^{\pm}(f)(l;y)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{0}e^{-il(l+2k)(\eta)}(\widetilde{u}*f)(l;y+\eta)\,d\eta,

so that, in particular,

\displaystyle g_{k,u}^{\pm}(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{0}e^{-il(l+2k)\eta}\widetilde{u}(l;y+\eta)\,d\eta,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{0}e^{-il(l+2k)\eta}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}(l;y+\eta)\,d\eta.

We then deduce that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}(\sqrt{2\pi}\delta)\right\|_{X}\leq\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}}.

For the other inhomogeneous term in (
2.45
),

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}\right](\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp})(k,l;y)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\pm l\cdot\infty}^{0}e^{-il(l+2k)\eta}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}*\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right)(l;\eta)\,d\eta.

We then estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}g_{k,u}^{\pm}\right](\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp})\right\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}*\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right)(k,\cdot,\eta)\right\|_{L^{2}_{k}L^{2}_{l}}\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|\left\|\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}*\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right)(k,\cdot,\eta)\right\|_{L^{2}_{l}}\right\|_{L^{2}_{k}}\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\|\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right\|_{L^{2}_{k}L^{2}_{l}}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}(\cdot;\eta)\right\|_{L^{2,1}_{l}}\,d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2,1}_{y}L^{2,1}_{l}}\left\|\widetilde{\mu}^{\pm}_{\sharp}\right\|_{X}.

The rest of the proof is an application of the resolvent bound
(
2.43
).

3.2.
The Nonlocal Riemann-Hilbert Problem

We now proceed to derive the nonlocal Riemann-Hilbert problem for the inverse problem. We first define two integral operators:

(3.22)

\displaystyle C_{T_{x,y}}

\displaystyle:=C_{+}\mathcal{T}^{-}_{x,y}+C_{-}\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y},

(3.23)

\displaystyle C_{R_{x,y}}

\displaystyle:=C_{+}\mathcal{R}^{-}_{x,y}+C_{-}\mathcal{R}^{+}_{x,y}.

Proposition 3.2
.

[
30
, Proposition 4.10]

A vector
\mu^{l}
respectively
u^{r}
is a fundamental solution of the Riemann-Hilbert problem

\displaystyle\mu^{\pm}

\displaystyle=(I\pm\mathcal{T}^{\pm}_{x,y})\mu^{l},

\displaystyle\mu^{\pm}

\displaystyle=(I\pm\mathcal{R}^{\pm}_{x,y})\mu^{r}

respectively if and only if it satisfies the equations

\displaystyle\mu^{l}

\displaystyle=1+C_{T_{x,y}}\mu^{l},

\displaystyle\mu^{r}

\displaystyle=1+C_{R_{x,y}}\mu^{r}

respectively and the homogeneous solutions of these equations are exactly the vanishing solutions of the Riemann-Hilbert problem.

Proof.

Notice that

\displaystyle\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}=\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{l}+\mathcal{T}^{-}_{x,y}\mu^{l}

hence taking into account the normalization condition, we obtain from Plemelj’s formula

(3.24)

\mu^{\pm}=1+C_{\pm}\left[\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{l}+\mathcal{T}^{-}_{x,y}\mu^{l}\right]

(3.25)

\mu^{\pm}=1+C_{\pm}\left[\mathcal{R}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{r}+\mathcal{R}^{-}_{x,y}\mu^{r}\right],

and in particular

\displaystyle\mu^{+}

\displaystyle=1+C_{+}\left[\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{l}+\mathcal{T}^{-}_{x,y}\mu^{l}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(I+\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y})\mu^{l}=1+\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{l}+C_{+}\mathcal{T}^{-}_{x,y}\mu^{l}+C_{-}\mathcal{T}^{+}_{x,y}\mu^{l}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mu^{l}=1+C_{T_{x,y}}\mu^{l}.

We have similarly

\mu^{r}=1+C_{R_{x,y}}\mu^{r}.

∎

The following proposition deals with the solvability of the nonlocal RHP:

Proposition 3.3

[

\displaystyle C_{\widetilde{T}_{x,y}}C_{T_{x,y}}=C_{+}{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{x,y}^{-}}\left(C_{-}\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{+}+C_{+}\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{-}\right)+C_{-}{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{x,y}^{+}}\left(C_{-}\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{+}+C_{+}\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{-}\right).

We then make use of the fact that

\displaystyle\quad+C_{+}{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{x,y}^{-}}C_{-}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{+}+\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{-}\right)+C_{-}{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{x,y}^{+}}C_{+}\left(\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{+}+\mathcal{T}_{x,y}^{-}\right)

We consider scattering data

5.
The Reconstruction Formula

In this section we prove the asymptotic estimates on the solution
u(t,x,y)
asserted in Theorem
1.1
. Recall the decomposition of
u(t,x,y)
into local and nonlocal parts:

(5.1)

\displaystyle u(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(t,x,y)+u_{2}(t,x,y)

where

(5.2)

\displaystyle u_{1}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int e^{itS_{0}(k,l;\zeta,\eta)}i(l-k)f(k,l)\,dl\,dk,

and

(5.3)

\displaystyle u_{2}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\int e^{itS_{0}(k,l;\zeta,\eta)}f(k,l)\left(\mu^{l}(l,x;y,t)-1\right)\,dl\,dk

with
f(k,l)
given by (
1.29
). In (
5.2
) and (
5.3
),
S_{0}
denotes the phase function

(5.4)

S_{0}(k,l;\zeta,\eta)=(l-k)\zeta-(l^{2}-k^{2})\eta+4(l^{3}-k^{3}).

In (
5.3
),
\mu^{l}=\mu^{l}(k,x;y,t)
denotes the unique solution of the nonlocal Riemann-Hilbert problem

(5.5)

\mu^{l}=1+\mathcal{C}_{T}(\mu^{l})

with
\mu^{l}(\cdot,x;y,t)-1\in L^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{R})
.
Here

(5.6)

\mathcal{C}_{T}=C_{+}\mathcal{T}^{-}+C_{-}\mathcal{T}^{+}

where
C_{\pm}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
are Cauchy projectors and

\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
are the integral operators

(5.7)

\mathcal{T}^{\pm}(f)(k)=\int e^{itS_{0}}T^{\pm}(k,l)f(l)\,dl.

The nonlocal Riemann-Hilbert problem considered here differs from the one considered in section
3.2
(see Proposition
3.2
) by the addition of time evolution for the scattering data: the time-zero scattering data
T^{\pm}(k,l)
is replaced by the time-evolved scattering data
T^{\pm}(k,l)e^{4it(l^{3}-k^{3})}
, giving rise to the third term in (
5.4
).

Given the decomposition (
5.1
), our analysis naturally divides into two steps: the analysis of the local term
u_{1}
in §
5.1
, and the analysis of the nonlocal term in §
5.2
. In each step, the analysis has three parts, dictated by the form of the phase function
S_{0}
: the cases of no critical points, nondegenerate critical points, and the transition region where the critical points degenerate to a single critical point and disappear.

The local term (treated in §
5.1
) involves an oscillatory integral whose amplitude is the scattering data

(5.8)

f(k,l)=T^{+}(k,l)+T^{-}(k,l),

where
T^{\pm}
is given by (
3.3
),
and whose phase function is
S_{0}
(see (
5.4
)).
For the analysis, we consider in turn the cases where the phase function
S_{0}
has no stationary points (see §
5.1.1
),
where the phase function has nondegenerate stationary phase points (see §
5.1.2
), and in the transition region where the phase function passes a point of degenerate stationary phase (see §
5.1.3
). The functions
T^{\pm}(k,l)
are discontinuous along the line
l=k

it follows that

\left\|\mathcal{T}^{\pm}\right\|_{L^{2}_{k}\to L^{2}_{l}}\leq\frac{1}{1-c}\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}}

since the Hilbert-Schmidt norm bounds the operator norm. Using

we conclude that

\left\|\mathcal{C}_{T}\right\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{1-c}\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}}.

Hence

(5.91)

\left\|\mathcal{C}_{T}\right\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}<\frac{1}{2}\text{ whenever }\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}}<\frac{1-c}{4}

Next, we have the estimate

and (
5.86

The right-hand norms are bounded under the hypotheses of the lemma.

∎

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\left\|\frac{\partial^{3}\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial l^{3}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\left\|l^{3}\widetilde{u}(l,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\left\|\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial l^{2}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\left\|l^{2}\widetilde{u}(l,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\left(\left\|\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial l^{2}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\left\|l^{4}\widetilde{u}(l,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}+\left\|\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial l}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\left\|l^{3}\widetilde{u}(l,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+l^{4})(1+y^{4})\widetilde{u}(l,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{3/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left\|\frac{\partial^{i}\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial l^{i}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|(1+y^{4})(1+\partial_{x}^{4})u(x,y)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{3/2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{4}\left\|{x^{i}u(x,y)}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{1/2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}}{\partial y}\right\|_{L^{2,2}_{y}L^{2,2}_{l}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}y^{4}l^{4}\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}(l,y)}{\partial y}\frac{\partial\overline{\widetilde{u}(l,y)}}{\partial y}dydl

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|y^{4}\frac{\partial^{4}u(x,y)}{\partial x^{4}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|\frac{\partial^{2}u(x,y)}{\partial y^{2}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|y^{3}\frac{\partial^{4}u(x,y)}{\partial x^{4}}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\left\|\frac{\partial u(x,y)}{\partial y}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

The singular low-frequency bounds in (i) can be obtained by using the

\partial_{x}^{-1}
terms in the definition of
\mathbf{E}_{1,w}
. Indeed,

\partial_{x}^{-1}u\in L^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|l|^{-1}\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{l,y},

and

\partial_{x}^{-1}\partial_{y}u\in L^{2}_{x}L^{2,1}_{y}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|l|^{-1}\frac{\partial\widetilde{u}}{\partial y}\in L^{2,1}_{y}L^{2}_{l}.

These estimates imply

\widetilde{u},\partial_{y}\widetilde{u},y\partial_{y}\widetilde{u}\in L^{2}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}
, using the weighted
L^{2}
bounds away from
l=0
.

The bound
\widetilde{u}\in L^{2,1}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}
follows from Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2,1}_{y}L^{2,-1}_{l}}^{2}\leq\left\||l|^{-1}\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\,\left\|\widetilde{u}\right\|_{L^{2,2}_{y}L^{2}_{l}}.

The first factor is controlled by
\left\|\partial_{x}^{-1}u\right\|_{L^{2}}
, and the second by
\mathbf{H}^{4,5}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
