Optimization landscape of ℓ 0 \ell_{0} -Bregman relaxations Thanks: August 10, 2026
Jonathan Chirinos-Rodríguez 1,2, Cédric Févotte 1, Emmanuel Soubies 1

Abstract
In this paper, we study (noisy) linear systems, and their ℓ 0 \ell_{0} -regularized optimization problems, coupled with general data fidelity terms. Recent approaches for solving this class of problems have proposed to consider non-convex exact continuous relaxations that preserve global minimizers while reducing the number of local minimizers. Within this framework, we consider the class of ℓ 0 \ell_{0} -Bregman relaxations, and establish sufficient conditions under which a critical point is isolated in terms of sparsity, in the sense that any other critical point has a strictly larger cardinality. In this way, we ensure a form of uniqueness in the solution structure. Furthermore, we analyze the exact recovery properties of such exact relaxations. To that end, we derive conditions under which the oracle solution (i.e., the one sharing the same support as the ground-truth) is the unique global minimizer of the relaxed problem, and is isolated in terms of sparsity. Our analysis is primarily built upon a novel property we introduce, termed the Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity. Finally, we specialize our general results to both sparse Gaussian (least-squares) and Poisson ((generalized) Kullback–Leibler divergence) regression problems. In particular, we show that our general analysis sharpens existing bounds for the LS setting, while providing an entirely new result for the KL case.

Introduction

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
,
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
, be a linear operator (typically wide, with
M\ll N
) and let
\mathbf{x}^{*}
be a sparse vector with
k^{*}\ll N
nonzero components. We consider the inverse problem of recovering
\mathbf{x}^{*}
from the measurements
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{M}
given by

\mathbf{y}=\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{y}^{*}),\text{ with }\mathbf{y}^{*}=\iota(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}),

(1)

where the –possibly nonlinear– map
\iota
represents a deterministic transformation (i.e., the sigmoid function in logistic regression),
\mathcal{R}
stands for a random operation such as additive (Gaussian) or signal-dependent (Poisson) noise, or a probabilistic “labeling” operation as in logistic regression. Finally, the set
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
depends on the problem we are dealing with. For instance,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
in Gaussian regression,
\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}
in logistic regression, and
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
in Poisson regression. In order to tackle the above problem, it is common to consider the following
\ell_{0}
-regularized problem

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}J_{0}(\mathbf{x});\quad J_{0}(\mathbf{x}):=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})+\lambda_{0}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0},

(2)

where
G_{\mathbf{y}}
corresponds to a general convex data fidelity term (or loss function), and ensures that the solution remains close to the available measurements. Famous examples are the least-squares (or quadratic) loss (associated to Gaussian regression problems), the generalized Kullback–Leibler divergence (common in Poisson regression problems), or the logistic loss (a natural choice for logistic regression problems). The notation
\|\cdot\|_{0}
stands for the so-called
\ell_{0}
pseudo-norm (or
\ell_{0}
-norm for simplicity), and measures the amount of nonzero components of a given vector. The scalar
\lambda_{0}>0
, known as regularization parameter, aims to balance the relative importance between fitting the data and imposing sparsity of solutions, induced by the
\ell_{0}
-norm. Finally, we consider in this work problems for which the constraint set
{\mathcal{C}}
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, and this depends on the fidelity term that we choose.

Due to the discontinuous and non-convex nature of the
\ell_{0}
-norm, these problems are inherently NP-hard
[
20
,
21
]
. As a result, a common strategy is to replace the
\ell_{0}
-norm with continuous relaxations, as they yield more tractable optimization problems. Among these, the
\ell_{1}
-norm is particularly popular, as its convexity permits the use of fast and efficient optimization algorithms
[
27
]
. Beyond the
\ell_{1}
convex relaxation, a variety of non-convex alternatives have been proposed. Notable examples include the capped-
\ell_{1}
penalty
[
30
]
, the
\ell_{p}
-norms,
0<p<1

[
14
]
, and the minimax concave penalty (MCP)
[
28
]
. More recently, non-convex
exact
continuous relaxations have been proposed
[
3
,
9
,
12
,
25
,
26
]
. These relaxations enjoy the following theoretical property: it can be shown that the relaxed problem shares the same global minimizers as the original
\ell_{0}
-regularized formulation
eq.

2
, while admitting fewer local minimizers. As a result, these formulations offer a more favorable optimization landscape, potentially improving the effectiveness of numerical algorithms.

In particular, as we are interested in dealing with fidelity terms that may be non-quadratic, we will focus on the class of
\ell_{0}
-Bregman relaxations (B-rex), which has been recently introduced in
[
12
]
. The corresponding exact relaxation takes the form

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}J_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x});\quad J_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}):=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})+B_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}),

(3)

where, given
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
,
B_{\Psi}
is known as the B-rex functional, and is associated to the Bregman divergence of the function
\Psi
. We will dedicate
Section

3
to further delve into the details of the above functional: from its definition to its exact relaxation property.

The case of a quadratic fidelity term
G_{\mathbf{y}}
and
\Psi=(1/2)\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
has been extensively studied in the literature, and the exact relaxation properties of the associated relaxed problem have been analyzed in
[
25
,
26
,
9
]
. Moreover, a deeper analysis of the relaxation’s optimization landscape was conducted in
[
7
]
. This work provides conditions under which critical points are isolated in terms of sparsity, meaning that any other critical point admits a much larger cardinality. The authors further demonstrate that, under suitable assumptions, it is possible to determine an interval for the regularization parameter
\lambda_{0}
such that the oracle solution is the unique global minimizer of the relaxation and, additionally, isolated in terms of sparsity. Subsequent works analyzed the optimization landscape of exact relaxations for rank-regularized least-squares
[
8
]
and for
\ell_{0}
-
\ell_{1}
regularized least-squares
[
15
]
. Finally, the results of
[
7
]
have been specialized to the case where
\mathbf{A}
is a Fourier measurement matrix in
[
23
]
.

In this work, we will consider two main instances of Model
eq.

1
, by varying the deterministic transformation
\iota
and the random noise described by
{\mathcal{P}}
.

Example 1
(Gaussian regression).

Let
\iota
be the identity mapping,
\iota=\mathrm{Id}
, and let
\mathcal{R}
denote an additive Gaussian noise. With this, we rewrite Model
eq.

1
as

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Id}})
,
\sigma>0
being its standard deviation, and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{M}=\mathbb{R}^{M}
. The inverse problem corresponds to a sparse Gaussian regression problem, where the negative log-likelihood reduces to the data fidelity term being the least-squares loss, which is given for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
by

G^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}):=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}.

(4)

Finally, and for further purposes, we note that
G^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\mathbf{y}}
has Lipschitz gradient with Lipschitz constant
L=1
.

Example 2
(Poisson regression).

Let
\iota=\mathrm{Id}+\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{M}
modeling some known background
[
13
]
, and let
\mathcal{R}={\mathcal{P}}
denote a Poisson noise. With this, we rewrite Model
eq.

1
as

\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{y}^{*}),\;\text{with }\;\mathbf{y}^{*}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^{*}+\mathbf{b}.

where
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
,
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{M\times N}
, and
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{M}=\mathbb{Z}^{M}_{\geq 0}
Here, we aim to solve a sparse Poisson regression problem where, as above, the negative log-likelihood reduces to the fidelity term given, for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{M}
, by

G^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}):=\sum_{j=1}^{M}g_{y_{j}}^{\mathrm{KL}}(w_{j}+b_{j}),

(5)

with
g_{y}^{\mathrm{KL}}(z):=z+y\log(y/z)-y
the (generalized) Kullback–Leibler divergence. As above, notice that
G^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathbf{y}}
has Lipschitz gradient with constant
L=\max_{j}{y_{j}}/{b_{j}^{2}}
, see e.g.
[
16
, Lemma 1]
.

Contributions and outline.
In this work, we analyze the optimization landscape of
J_{\Psi}
in
eq.

3
for general –not necessarily quadratic– data fidelity terms. First, we introduce in
Section

2
the Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity (BRSC) property, that will be central in our manuscript and, as we will see, naturally generalizes well-known quantities such as the Lower Restricted Isometry Property (LRIP)
[
4
]
(which is basically a lower estimate of the so-called RIP
[
6
]
), or the Restricted Strong Convexity (RSC) property
[
24
]
. In particular, we show that, while the KL data fidelity term fails to satisfy the RSC property, it satisfies the proposed BRSC property for a suitable choice of Bregman generating function. Next, in
Section

4
we provide sufficient conditions for a critical point of
J_{\Psi}
to be isolated in terms of sparsity. Additionally, recalling that
J_{\Psi}
is an exact continuous relaxation of
J_{0}

[
12
]
, our setting allows us to show that such an isolated critical point turns out to be the unique global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
(and so of
J_{0}
) for suitable
\lambda_{0}
. In
Section

5
, we study exact support recovery results. Specifically, we derive sufficient conditions, that can be stated as

\lambda_{0}\in(\underline{\Lambda},\bar{\Lambda}),

under which the oracle solution
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
is both isolated in terms of sparsity and the unique global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
(hence the global minimizer of
J_{0}
too). We conclude by specializing these results to our two examples in
Section

6
, with the following outcomes.

1.

In the Gaussian regression (least-squares) setting, our analysis allows to obtain improved bounds with respect to existing literature
[
7
]
, in the sense that we obtain a larger interval
(\underline{\Lambda},\bar{\Lambda})
with respect to
[
7
]
.

2.

In the Poisson regression ((generalized) Kullback-Leibler) setting, we provide a completely novel result by constructing a valid interval
(\underline{\Lambda},\bar{\Lambda})
of regularization parameters
\lambda_{0}
in which our result holds. Naturally, we see that the same interpretation as in Gaussian regression problems can be obtained: the derived interval enlarges whenever the level of noise is small enough with respect to the magnitude of the nonzero components of the true solution. In this sense, we show that the asymptotic behavior that one would expect holds theoretically too.

Notation, definitions and assumptions.
In the following,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
will denote the spaces of real and non-negative real numbers. Given a vector
\mathbf{x}
,
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
denotes its support; that is,
\sigma(\mathbf{x}):=\{i\colon x_{i}\neq 0\}
. Additionally, given a set of indices
\omega
, we denote by
\#\omega
its cardinality and, given
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{x}_{\omega}=(x_{\omega[1]},\ldots,x_{\omega[\#\omega]})
denotes the restriction of
\mathbf{x}
to the entries indexed by
\omega
. In this context, given
\mathbf{A}
a matrix,
\mathbf{A}_{\omega}
defines the matrix restricted to the columns indexed by
\omega
:
\mathbf{A}_{\omega}:=(\mathbf{a}_{\omega[1]},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{\omega[\#\omega]})
, where
\mathbf{a}_{\omega[j]}
,
j=1,\ldots,\#\omega
, denotes the
\omega[j]^{\mathrm{th}}
column of
\mathbf{A}
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Id}}
the identity matrix, whose dimension should be clear from the context. Finally, we use the Loewner order for matrices: we say that
\mathbf{A}\succcurlyeq 0
(resp.
\mathbf{A}\succ 0
) if
\mathbf{A}
is positive semidefinite (resp. positive definite). We further recall some useful definitions.

Definition 1
(Zero padding
[
22
]
).

The zero padding operator
Z_{\omega}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\#\omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined, for any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\#\omega}
, as

(Z_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}))_{i}:=\begin{cases}u_{j},&\text{for the unique }j\in\{1,\ldots,\#\omega\}\text{ such that }i=\omega[j],\\
0,&\text{if }i\notin\omega.\end{cases}

In short,
Z_{\omega}
embeds a vector of smaller dimension into a larger one by padding it with zeros outside the index set
\omega
. For example, in
\mathbb{R}^{6}
, given
\mathbf{u}=(1,2,3)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\omega=\{2,4,5\}
, we have that
Z_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})=(0,1,0,2,3,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
.

Definition 2
(Restricted function).

Given
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we define for any
\omega\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
and any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-\#\omega}
the restricted function of
F
,
F_{\omega,\mathbf{v}}
, as

F_{\omega,\mathbf{v}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{\#\omega}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\};\quad F_{\omega,\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{u}):=F(Z_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})+Z_{\omega^{c}}(\mathbf{v})).

The above definition corresponds to the restriction of a function
F
to the variables indexed by
\omega
, the other being fixed to the values in
\mathbf{v}
. Additionally, we simply write
F_{\omega}
instead of
F_{\omega,\boldsymbol{0}}
whenever
\mathbf{v}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Now, we recall the definition of the (symmetric) Bregman divergence.

Definition 3
((Symmetric) Bregman divergence).

Given a proper, convex and differentiable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we define the Bregman divergence associated to
F
,
D_{F}
, to be

D_{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}):=F(\mathbf{x})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\rangle,

for any
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{dom}F
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
, and
+\infty
elsewhere, and where
\nabla F(\mathbf{x})
denotes the gradient of
F
at
\mathbf{x}
. Moreover, the symmetric Bregman divergence of
F
, denoted by
D^{\ \mathrm{symm}}_{F}
, is defined as

D_{F}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}):=D_{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+D_{F}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})=\langle\nabla F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\nabla F(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\rangle,

for any
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
, and
+\infty
elsewhere.

Notice that, in the above definitions,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
(and
\mathbf{x}
too in the symmetric case) must belong to
\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
as the gradient of
F
may not necessarily be defined in the border of the domain.

Next, we introduce a general set of assumptions for the –not necessarily quadratic– data fidelity terms
G_{\mathbf{y}}
that are considered in this work.

Assumption 1.1
.

The data fidelity term
G_{\mathbf{y}}
is assumed to be coordinate-wise separable; i.e., given
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{M}
,

G_{\mathbf{y}}\colon(\operatorname{dom}g_{y_{1}})\times\cdots\times(\operatorname{dom}g_{y_{M}})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0};\quad G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}):=\sum_{j=1}^{M}g_{y_{j}}(w_{j}).

where
g_{y}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a nonnegative function such that, for any
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

(i)

g_{y}
is strictly convex, proper, twice differentiable on
\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}g_{y})
, and coercive,

(ii)

g_{y}
has
L
-Lipschitz gradient for some
L>0
.

Moreover, when
\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
we assume that

(iii)

\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}))\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
, where
\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}):=\{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}:\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}\}
,

(iv)

\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}})}\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|<\infty
.

The first assumption is the one employed in
[
12
]
to guarantee that
eq.

2
admits solutions and ensure that
eq.

3
is an exact continuous relaxation of
eq.

2
. The second assumption is common in the optimization literature to ensure convergence of several iterative algorithms. Importantly, both assumptions are naturally satisfied by most loss functions of interest and, in particular, by both of the fidelity terms in
example

1
and
example

2
. Finally, note that both the third and the fourth assumptions are satisfied for our KL example as
\mathrm{cl}(\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}}))\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{M}\subset(-\min_{j=1,\ldots,M}b_{j},\infty)^{M}\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G^{\mathrm{KL}}_{\mathbf{y}})
and
\lim_{w\to\infty}(g_{y_{j}}^{\mathrm{KL}})^{\prime}(w)=1
for all
j=1,\ldots,M
.

We now dedicate the following section to present the central tool of our work, which we call the Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity (BRSC) property and, as we will see, extends existing concepts in the literature.

2
The Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity Property

We recall that the objective of this work is to show that the functional
J_{\Psi}
defined in
eq.

3
, although non-convex, exhibits a favorable optimization landscape. As we already mentioned, the quadratic setting has been studied in depth in
[
7
]
. This work is strongly based on a very specific assumption on the matrix
\mathbf{A}
: the so-called LRIP condition, introduced in
[
4
]
. In the following, we aim to extend such definition to our Bregman setting.

2.1
From LRIP/RSC to BRSC

We start by giving a proper definition of the LRIP condition: we say that
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the LRIP property if there exists a constant
\delta_{K}^{-}<1
, for some
K\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, such that

1-\delta_{K}^{-}=\inf\left\{\frac{\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\mid\mathbf{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\text{ and }\|\mathbf{x}\|_{0}\leq K\right\}

or, equivalently, that

\mathbf{A}_{\omega}^{T}\mathbf{A}_{\omega}\succcurlyeq(1-\delta_{K}^{-})\boldsymbol{\mathrm{Id}},\quad\text{ for all }\omega\text{ with }\#\omega\leq K.

(LRIP)

For instance, when
\mathbf{A}
is a Gaussian matrix, a common choice in the compressed sensing literature, then
eq.

LRIP
holds with “overwhelming” probability
[
5
]
. Since this condition is closely associated with quadratic fidelity terms, it is natural to seek for a corresponding definition that extends existing analyses to general non-quadratic fidelity terms. To this aim, a first approach would be to consider the so-called Restricted Strong Convexity (see
[
24
]
and references therein): given a proper, convex and differentiable function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we say that it satisfies the RSC property if there exists a constant
\mu_{K}>0
such that, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\operatorname{dom}F
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
with
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq K
, we have

D_{F}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq\frac{\mu_{K}}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},

which is equivalent to having, for any
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
with
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq K
:

D_{F}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq\mu_{K}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

(RSC)

Notice that both of the above expressions are simply equivalent definitions of strong convexity of
F
with the additional assumption that the distinct points must satisfy
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq K
. Additionally, the above condition trivially recovers the LRIP property on the matrix
\mathbf{A}
, whenever
F=G_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
, by observing that can be equivalently defined as follows: we say that
F
satisfies the RSC property whenever the quantity

\mu_{K}:=\inf\left\{\frac{D_{F}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})}{\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}\mid\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\text{ and }\|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\mathbf{x}\|_{0}\leq K\right\}

is positive. The relation between both constants
\mu_{K}
and
\delta_{K}^{-}
when
F=G_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
is
\mu_{K}=1-\delta_{K}^{-}
. In fact, it is possible to relate
eq.

RSC
and
eq.

LRIP
beyond the least-squares data term.

Proposition 4
.

Let
G\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper, differentiable, and strongly convex function with parameter
\nu>0
. Assume in addition that
\mathbf{A}
satisfies
eq.

LRIP
with constant
\delta_{K}^{-}<1
. Then,
F=G(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
satisfies
eq.

RSC
with constant
\mu_{K}:=\nu(1-\delta_{K}^{-})
.

We point out that the above result is natural, and has already been proved in the full row rank case in
[
29
, Theorem 1]
(notice that, in our setting, requiring
eq.

LRIP
corresponds to
\mathbf{A}_{\omega}
, for
\#\omega\leq K
, being full column rank). For clarity, we dedicate
Section

7.1.1
to prove the result.

However,
eq.

RSC
turns to be irrelevant for data fidelity terms
F=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
where
G_{\mathbf{y}}
is not strongly convex. This is the case, for instance, for the (generalized) Kullback–Leibler divergence
G_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{KL}}
from
example

2
. To see this, as it is separable, it suffices to consider the one dimensional case: given
y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have that
(g_{y}^{\mathrm{KL}})^{\prime\prime}(z)=y/(z+b)^{2}
tends to zero as
z\to\infty
, that is
\nu=0
and thus
\mu_{K}=0
for any
K
in
Proposition

4
. Motivated by this observation, we introduce in this work what we call the
Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity
(BRSC) property which, we anticipate, allows to obtain positive results for
G_{\mathbf{y}}^{{\mathrm{KL}}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
(cf.
theorem

10
).

Definition 5
.

Let
F,\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, convex and differentiable functions over the interior of their domains. We say that
F
satisfies the
Bregman Restricted Strong Convexity (BRSC)
property with respect to
\Phi
,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)\cap\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}\Phi)
, and
K\leq N
, if there exists a constant
C_{K}>0
such that, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)\cap\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}\Phi)
with
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{x}
and
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq K
, we have

D_{F}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\geq C_{K}D_{\Phi}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(BRSC)

Remark 6
.

Note that, although we use the notation
C_{K}
, this constant also depends on
\mathcal{X}
,
F
, and
\Phi
. However, for a given instance of Problem
eq.

2
, these elements are fixed, whereas we may be interested in analyzing different values of
K
.

The above definition will serve as the main technical tool in this work, and can be seen as a restricted version of the
\Phi
-strong convexity property in
[
1
]
or the relative strong convexity in
[
18
]
.

We now provide some additional remarks to clarify its implications. First, observe that it generalizes existing notions; for instance, the choices
\Phi=(1/2)\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
and
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)
recover
eq.

RSC
with
\mu_{K}=C_{K}
and, consequently
eq.

LRIP
as well (with
1-\delta_{K}^{-}=C_{K}
), whenever
F=G_{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{LS}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
. In the following section, we present some properties of our BRSC, that will be useful throughout the manuscript.

2.2
Properties of BRSC

To begin with, we show in the following proposition an interesting monotonicity property of our BRSC, which also holds for both LRIP and RSC. Roughly, we prove that the smaller the set
\mathcal{X}
and/or
K
is, the larger the BRSC constant.

Proposition 7
(Monotonicity of BRSC).

If
F
satisfies
eq.

BRSC
with respect to
\Phi
,
\mathcal{X}
, and
K
, then it also satisfies
eq.

BRSC
with respect to
\Phi
, any
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, and any
\tilde{K}\leq K
. Moreover, we have
C(\tilde{K},\tilde{\mathcal{X}},F,\Phi)\geq C(K,\mathcal{X},F,\Phi)
.

Proof.

The results follows from the fact that, for any
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and any
\tilde{K}\leq K
we have

\left\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)\cap\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}\Phi),\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq\tilde{K}\right\}\\
\subseteq\left\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{X}},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)\cap\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}\Phi),\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq K\right\}

which are the sets over which the
eq.

BRSC
inequality needs to hold true.

In the following result we consider restrictions over a subset of variables indexed by
\omega\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
F
satisfy
eq.

BRSC
with respect to
\Phi
, a convex
\mathcal{X}
, and
K
. Let
\omega\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}
such that
\#\omega\leq K
and define
\mathcal{X}_{\omega}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\#\omega}|{Z}_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})\in\mathcal{X}\}
. Then, the restrictions
F_{\omega}
and
\Phi_{\omega}
are such that

(i)

h(\mathbf{u}):=F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})-C_{K}\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})
is convex over
\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
.

(ii)

For all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
, we have that

F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})\geq F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\left<\nabla F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right>+C_{K}D_{\Phi_{\omega}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}).

Proof.

First of all, by its definition,
\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
is convex as
\mathcal{X}
is.
Then, noticing that
\nabla h(\mathbf{u})=\nabla F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})-C_{K}\nabla\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})
, we get from the definition of the symmetric Bregman divergence and
eq.

BRSC
that, for all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
,

D_{h}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=\left<(\nabla F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})-C_{K}\nabla\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}))-(\nabla F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-C_{K}\nabla\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right>\geq 0,

which proves
item

(i)
(monotone gradient condition). Then, given that
h
is convex over
\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
, we have that, for all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{\omega}
,

h(\mathbf{u})\geq h(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\left<\nabla h(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right>.

Equivalently,

F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})\geq F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})+\left<\nabla F_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right>+C_{K}\left(\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u})-\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})-\left<\nabla\Phi_{\omega}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\right>\right),

which completes the proof by definition of
D_{\Phi_{\omega}}
.

To start this section, let us emphasize that our

BRSC

Proposition 9
.

Let

\mathcal{X}\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{N}

We defer its proof to
Section

7.1.2
. In short, by the monotonicity property given in

. The above result demonstrates that, if

Theorem 10
.

Let

(6)

The
\ell_{0}
-Bregman relaxation (B-rex)

In this section, we recall the results of
[
12
]
, namely the definition of the
\ell_{0}
-Bregman relaxation, some relevant properties, and the associated exact relaxation result.

Definition 11
.

Let
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
\psi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\operatorname{dom}\psi_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
, be a family of scalar functions such that

(i)

\psi_{i}
is strictly convex, proper, and twice differentiable over
\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{C}})
,

(ii)

the map
x\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x)x-\psi_{i}(x)
is coercive,

(iii)

when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}
,
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
is odd and, when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0)\geq 0
.

The Bregman-generating function
\Psi
is defined, for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
, as

\Psi\colon{\mathcal{C}}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\Psi(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}(x_{i}).

Then, given
\lambda_{0}>0
, the
\ell_{0}
-Bregman relaxation (B-rex) associated to
\Psi
is defined, for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
, as

B_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}):=\sup_{\rho\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{C}}^{N})}\{\rho-D_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\ :\ \rho-D_{\Psi}(\cdot,\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{0}\|\cdot\|_{0}\},

where, since
\Psi
is separable,
D_{\Psi}
can be written as the sum of one-dimensional Bregman divergences
d_{\psi_{i}}
; i.e., for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
and any
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{C}}^{N})
,

D_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}d_{\psi_{i}}(x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i}),

being
d_{\psi_{i}}(x,x^{\prime}):=\psi_{i}(x)-\psi_{i}(x^{\prime})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})(x-x^{\prime})
for all
x\in{\mathcal{C}}
,
x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{C}})
.

Note that both
item

(i)
and
item

(ii)
in
definition

11
are the same as in
[
12
]
. In particular,
item

(ii)
has been included so that the
\lambda_{0}
-sublevel sets of each

are bounded convex sets, and so of the form
[-\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}]
when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}
or
[0,\alpha_{i}]
when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Here, we drop the superindices
+
and
-
of
[
12
]
as, if
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then simply
\alpha_{i}^{-}=0
and, from
item

(iii)

\alpha^{-}_{i}=-\alpha^{+}_{i}
whenever
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}
. Finally, notice that, although
item

(iii)
is specific to this work, it is satisfied by each of the Bregman-generating functions
\psi_{i}
considered in
[
12
, Table 2]
. Finally,
B_{\Psi}
is separable,
B_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x_{i})
, where each
\beta_{\psi_{i}}
is defined, for all

, as

\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x):=\begin{cases}\psi_{i}(0)-\psi_{i}(x)+\mathrm{sign}(x)\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})x,&\text{ if }|x|\leq\alpha_{i},\\
\qquad\lambda_{0},&\text{ if }|x|>\alpha_{i}.\end{cases}

(7)

For further purposes, we recall the closed-form expression of the Clarke’s subdifferential of the B-rex penalty. As it is separable, we have that

\partial B_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=\partial\beta_{\psi_{1}}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times\beta_{\psi_{N}}(x_{N}),

where, when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}

\partial\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x)=\begin{cases}-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x)+\mathrm{sign}(x)\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}),&\text{if }0<|x|\leq\alpha_{i},\\
[-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0),\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)],&\text{if }x=0,\\
\qquad 0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and, when
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,

\partial\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x)=\begin{cases}-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x)+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}),&\text{if }x\in[0,\alpha_{i}],\\
\qquad 0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It turns out that the B-rex relaxed problem

3
is an exact continuous relaxation of
J_{0}
for suitable choices of
\Psi

Theorem 12
.

[
12
, Theorem 9]
Let
\Psi
be such that the following concavity condition is satisfied : for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
and

,

g(t):=J_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}+t\boldsymbol{e}_{i})\text{ is strictly concave on }(-\alpha_{i},0)\text{ and }(0,\alpha_{i}),

(CC)

where, for any

\mathbf{x}^{(i)}+t\boldsymbol{e}_{i}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},t,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{N})

4
Optimization landscape analysis of
\ell_{0}
-Bregman relaxations

The current section will be divided into three parts. First, we provide a simple characterization of the critical points of
J_{\Psi}
that will be useful along the rest of this manuscript. Second, we focus on analyzing the optimization landscape of the relaxed functional
J_{\Psi}
, providing sufficient conditions so that there is a global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
that is (what we call)
isolated in terms of sparsity
. Throughout this part, we fix
F:=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
. Moreover, in all our examples we have
{\mathcal{C}}^{N}\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}F)\cap\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}\Psi)
and we will thus simply use
{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
in place of this intersection in what follows.

4.1
Characterizing the critical points of
J_{\Psi}

In order to find the critical points of the relaxation
J_{\Psi}
, we first define, for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
, the following auxiliary function:

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x}):=B_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})+\Psi(\mathbf{x}),

and observe that, by
[
12
, Proposition 11]
,
\mathcal{H}
is the lower semicontinuous convex envelope of
\lambda_{0}\|\cdot\|_{0}+\Psi
; i.e. the largest convex function below
\lambda_{0}\|\cdot\|_{0}+\Psi
. It follows that

J_{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})-\Psi(\mathbf{x})+G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}).

In this way,
J_{\Psi}
is written as the sum of two terms: the convex, non-differentiable function
\mathcal{H}
(due to the non-differentiability of
B_{\Psi}
), and the non-convex, differentiable part
-\Psi+G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
. Critical points of
J_{\Psi}
are characterized via first-order optimality conditions:
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
is a critical point of
J_{\Psi}
if and only if

\boldsymbol{0}\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})-\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{A}^{T}\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}),

where
\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the subdifferential operator of
\mathcal{H}
at
\mathbf{x}
. Rearranging the terms, we get that

\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{A}^{T}\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x})\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x}).

(8)

For further convenience, we will associate to any
\mathbf{x}
the left hand side of the formula above by defining the following quantity

\mathbf{z}:=\nabla\Psi(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{A}^{T}\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}),

(9)

and in this way the optimality conditions derived in
eq.

8
simply write
\mathbf{z}\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
is a critical point for
J_{\Psi}
if and only if
\mathbf{z}\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
. To prove the upcoming results, we will exploit the monotonicity of the operator
\partial\mathcal{H}
. This is the motivation behind the introduction of the convex
\mathcal{H}
above, so as to separate the non-differentiability and non-convexity of
J_{\Psi}
in two different terms.

Before delving into the next section, and for further purposes, we investigate the subdifferential of
\mathcal{H}
. First, notice that
\mathcal{H}
is separable: for all
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
we have
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}(x_{i})
, with
h_{i}:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
h_{i}(x):=\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x)+\psi_{i}(x)
for any
x\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Therefore, it holds true that, for every
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}
,

\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})=\partial h_{1}(x_{1})\times\cdots\times\partial h_{N}(x_{N}),

where, if
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\partial h_{i}(x)=\partial\beta_{\psi_{i}}(x)+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sign}(x)\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}),&\text{if}\quad 0<|x|\leq\alpha_{i},\\
[-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}),\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})],&\text{if}\quad x=0,\\
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(10)

In the constrained case
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, we get

\partial h_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i}),&\text{if}\quad 0\leq x\leq\alpha_{i},\\
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To conclude, we point out that, as
\partial\mathcal{H}
is separable, the condition
\mathbf{z}\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
can be equivalently analyzed element-wise:
\mathbf{z}\in\partial\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{x})
, with
\mathbf{z}
defined as in
eq.

9
, if and only if
z_{i}\in\partial h_{i}(x_{i})
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.

\sigma^{*}:=\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{*})
, its length as
k^{*}=\#\sigma^{*}
, and we let
\mathbf{u}^{*}=\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\sigma^{*}}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}
; i.e.,
\mathbf{u}^{*}
denotes the true solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}
restricted to its support. Throughout this section, we set
F=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
and we assume that the restricted matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
has full rank. Next, we define the
oracle solution
of
eq.

1
as
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}=Z_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})
, with

\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}}F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}),

(13)

where we recall that the restricted function
F_{\sigma^{*}}
reads

F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}):=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}\mathbf{u}).

Note also that
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}
is unique as
F_{\sigma^{*}}
is a strictly convex and coercive function because
G_{\mathbf{y}}
is strictly convex and coercive, and
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
has full rank. Moreover, as
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}
is the minimizer of
F_{\sigma^{*}}
, it holds true that for all
\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}
,

\left<\nabla F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}),\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}\right>\geq 0.

(14)

Moreover, on the oracle support
\sigma^{\mathrm{or}}:=\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}})\subseteq\sigma^{*}
we have
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{C}}^{\sharp\sigma^{\mathrm{or}}})
, and thus

\nabla F_{\sigma^{\mathrm{or}}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})=\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{\mathrm{or}}}^{T}\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{\mathrm{or}}}\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})=\boldsymbol{0}.

(15)

Finally, by construction,
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
is a local minimizer of
J_{0}

[
12
, Proposition 2]
.

Remark 16
.

Notice that asking for
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
to be full rank has several implications. First, it serves to define the oracle solution within our context. Additionally, and as we aim to show that the oracle solution is a global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
(and hence of
J_{0}
too), there would be no hope to show this if
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
were not full rank. Indeed, by
[
12
, Theorem 4]
, all global minimizers of
J_{0}
are strict. Moreover, by
[
12
, Theorem 3]
, any local minimizer
\mathbf{x}
of
J_{0}
is strict if the restricted matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma(\mathbf{x})}
is full rank. Therefore, if
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
is not full rank,
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
cannot be a global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
.

Next, on top of the concavity condition
eq.

CC
that ensure
J_{\Psi}
to be an exact relaxation of
J_{0}
, we make an additional structural assumption on
F
.

Assumption 5.1
.

We assume that
F
satisfies
eq.

BRSC
, with respect to
\Psi
,
K\geq 2k^{*}
, and a compact set
\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}},\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
. Additionally, we require that its construction does not depend on
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
. For instance, a valid choice for
\mathcal{X}
is

\mathcal{X}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}\mid\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{x})\subseteq\sigma^{*}\text{ and }F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{x}_{\sigma^{*}})\leq F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{x}^{*}_{\sigma^{*}})\}.

(16)

The required conditions on the set
\mathcal{X}
in the above assumption are motivated by three underlying considerations. First, the subsequent analysis builds on the results of
Section

4.2
, which must be applied at both
\mathbf{x}^{*}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{X}
is required to contain both points. For instance, if we consider
\mathcal{X}
given by
eq.

16
, the inclusion of
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is trivial, while that of
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
follows from its definition. Second, it should be as small as possible to get better BRSC constants (cf.
proposition

7
). In our example, this is achieved with the sublevel-set constraint. Moreover, the compactness assumption is particularly important for guaranteeing nonzero BRSC constants in cases such as the KL fidelity term (cf.
Theorem

10
). Notice that, by coercivity of
F_{\sigma^{*}}
,
\mathcal{X}
given by
eq.

16
is compact. In contrast, for the quadratic case (
F=G^{\mathrm{LS}}_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{A}\cdot)
), this compactness assumption does not affect BRSC constants (as it holds globally) and can therefore be omitted. Lastly, we aim to derive conditions that depend only on the true solution
\mathbf{x}^{*}
, and not on the oracle
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
. As such,
\mathcal{X}
should depend only on
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

The remainder of this section will be divided into two parts. First, we will show that, under certain conditions, it is possible to ensure that the oracle solution
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
, which is a local minimizer of
J_{0}
by definition, is also a local minimizer of the exact relaxation
J_{\Psi}
.
Such result will be presented in
theorem

19
. Next, we will analyze whether these conditions can be completed in order to show that
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
is the global minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
and is also isolated in terms of sparsity. We present this result in
theorem

20
.

5.1
The oracle solution is a local minimizer of
J_{\Psi}

As already anticipated, we investigate in this section the geometrical properties of the oracle solution
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
.
To that end, we first introduce the notion of
safe oracle region
, borrowing the terminology from the safe screening literature
[
10
]
.

Definition 17
(Safe oracle region).

A subset
S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}
is said to be a safe oracle region if it contains
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}
. Moreover, it should be defined without knowledge of
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}
.

Clearly, the restriction of
\mathcal{X}
(defined in
5.1
) to
\sigma^{*}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}_{\sigma^{*}}
, defines a safe oracle region. However, its definition involves a sublevel-set constraint that is difficult to handle in the subsequent analysis, since it couples all variables through the operator
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma^{*}}
in
F_{\sigma^{*}}
. In contrast, the proposition below introduces a safe oracle region whose construction only involves separable functions.

Proposition 18
.

Under
5.1
, the following set

\mathfrak{S}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}\mid F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{*})\geq C_{K}D_{\Psi_{\sigma^{*}}}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{u})\},

(17)

where
D_{\Psi_{\sigma^{*}}}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{u}):=\sum_{j=1}^{k^{*}}d_{\psi_{\sigma^{*}[j]}}(u^{*}_{j},u_{j})
, is a safe oracle region.

Proof.

Using
proposition

8

item

(ii)
with
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}
and
\mathbf{u}^{*}
, we get that

F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{*})-F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})\geq\left<\nabla F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}),\mathbf{u}^{*}-\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}\right>+C_{K}D_{\Psi_{\sigma^{*}}}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}).

Then, since
0\leq F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})\leq F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{*})
and
\left<\nabla F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}),\mathbf{u}^{*}-\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}\right>\geq\mathbf{0}
by
eq.

14
, we get

D_{\Psi_{\sigma^{*}}}(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}})\leq\frac{F_{\sigma^{*}}(\mathbf{u}^{*})}{C_{K}},

showing that
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{or}}\in\mathfrak{S}
, as desired.

In
Section

6.1
, we will see that, for the choice
\Psi=(1/2)\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
, this set reduces to an Euclidean ball (
proposition

22
). We are now ready to provide sufficient conditions under which
\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
is a local minimizer of
J_{\Psi}
.

Theorem 19
.

Let
5.1
be satisfied and let
S\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}
be a safe oracle region. Define
\Omega:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k^{*}}\Omega_{j}
where, for every
j=1,\ldots,k^{*}

\Omega_{j}:=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{k^{*}}\mid u_{j}\in[-\alpha_{\sigma^{*}[j]},\alpha_{\sigma^{*}[j]}]\right\}.

Then, under the conditions,

\displaystyle S\cap\Omega=\emptyset,

(18)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{a}_{i}\|_{2}\sqrt{2\tilde{L}F(\mathbf{x}^{*})}\leq\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0),\text{ for all }i\in{\sigma^{*}}^{c},

(19)

(with

\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}}
is a local minimizer of

and
\sigma(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{or}})=\sigma^{*}
.

We present below a sketch of the proof, with full details deferred to
Section

7.3
.

Sketch of the proof.

From
[
12
, Proposition 10]
, we get that a local minimizer

of

is preserved by

if and only if

(i)

for any

(ii)

for any

.

where

and
\ell_{i}^{-}:=-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha_{i})-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0)

or

. Hence, a simple geometrical sufficient condition for

to satisfy

(i)

19
to ensure that

Next, notice that, while Condition

,
\Psi

, rather than with the valid example provided in

.

Remark 21
(On the noiseless setting).

Let us consider the extreme case in which no noise is present in the observations; i.e., when

. Additionally, we set

, the safe oracle region given in

. Now, observe that Conditions

C_{K}=\min_{\#\omega\leq K}C_{\omega}
by analyzing the above infimum for
k=K
.
Our goal now is to show that this constant is positive; that is, the ratio
\Gamma
never vanishes on the considered constraint set. To that end, let
\mathbf{u}\in[0,Q]^{K}
and
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}
with
\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\neq\mathbf{u}
. We distinguish two cases: either
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq B
or
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}>B
with
B:=4\sqrt{K}Q
.

Case
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq B
.
Define
\delta_{4}:=\min_{i=1,\ldots,K}\eta_{i}
and observe that, as
\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, then clearly
\Delta_{i}>0
for at least one
i=1,\ldots,k
. We have that

0<\sum_{i=1}^{K}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i}}\leq\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}}{\delta_{4}},

leading to

\Gamma(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})\geq\frac{\delta_{4}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\delta_{2}}\geq\frac{\delta_{4}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}B+\delta_{2}}>0,

concluding the proof of the result.

Case
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}>B
.
By equivalence of norms in finite dimension, we have
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\geq{\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}}/{\sqrt{K}}>4Q
. This implies that the set of indices
T:=\{i:u^{\prime}_{i}>2Q\}
contains at least one element. We denote
T^{c}
its complement. Then observe that, for all
i\in T
,

\Delta_{i}^{2}=(u_{i}^{\prime}-u_{i})^{2}\geq(u_{i}^{\prime}-Q)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}u_{i}^{\prime 2},

(43)

where we used that
u_{i}\leq Q
for all
i=1,\ldots,K
and
u_{i}^{\prime}>2Q
for
i\in T
. It then follows that

\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i\in T}\Delta_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\in T^{c}}\Delta_{i}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i\in T}u_{i}^{\prime 2}=\frac{1}{4}\left(\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-\sum_{i\in T^{c}}u_{i}^{\prime 2}\right).

Then, by definition of
T^{c}
we have that, for
i\in T^{c}
,
u_{i}^{\prime}\leq 2Q
if and only if
-u_{i}^{\prime 2}\geq-4Q^{2}
, from which we deduce the bound

\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-4(\#T^{c})Q^{2}\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}-4KQ^{2}\right)\geq\frac{3}{16}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},

where we used that
(\#T^{c})\leq K
and
-4KQ^{2}=-B^{2}/4>-\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}/4
. We now analyze the quantity
D(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime}):=\sum_{i=1}^{K}{\Delta_{i}^{2}}/(u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i})
involved in the definition of the ratio
\Gamma
. Again, we split the indices of the sum in
T
and
T^{c}
to get

•

for all
i\in T
, we have that
\Delta_{i}^{2}=(u_{i}^{\prime}-u_{i})^{2}\leq u_{i}^{\prime 2}
(as
u_{i}^{\prime}>2Q>Q\geq u_{i}
) and

\sum_{i\in T}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i}}\leq\sum_{i\in T}\frac{u_{i}^{\prime 2}}{u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i}}\leq\sum_{i\in T}u_{i}^{\prime}\leq\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{K}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}.

•

for all
i\in T^{c}
, we have
u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i}\geq u_{i}^{\prime}+\delta_{4}\geq\delta_{4}
and
|\Delta_{i}|\leq u_{i}^{\prime}+u_{i}\leq 3Q
, and

\sum_{i\in T^{c}}\frac{\Delta_{i}^{2}}{u_{i}^{\prime}+\eta_{i}}\leq\frac{(\#T^{c})9Q^{2}}{\delta_{4}}\leq\delta_{5}:=\frac{9KQ^{2}}{\delta_{4}}.

It then follows that

D(\mathbf{u})\leq\sqrt{K}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\delta_{5}.

Combining all these bounds we obtain that, for all
\mathbf{u}\in[0,Q]^{K}
and
\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}>B
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{16}\frac{\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\delta_{2})(\sqrt{K}\|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}\|_{2}+\delta_{5})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{16}\frac{B^{2}}{(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}B+\delta_{2})(\sqrt{K}B+\delta_{5})}>0

where we used the fact that the function
x\mapsto x^{2}/((ax+b)(cx+d))
for
a,b,c,d>0
is increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

In all, we have shown that

C_{K}\geq\frac{\delta_{1}(1-\delta^{-}_{K})}{\delta_{3}}\min\left\{\frac{\delta_{4}}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}B+\delta_{2}},\frac{3}{16}\frac{B^{2}}{(\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|_{2}B+\delta_{2})(\sqrt{K}B+\delta_{5})}\right\}>0

which completes the proof.

Lemma 29

Let

\mathbf{z}

9

Notice that the notation

Proof.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

45

\partial h_{i}
in
eq.

10

eq.

45

C_{K}<1
, and
\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})>\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0)
(
\psi_{i}^{\prime}
increasing), we get that

z_{i}>\frac{\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)}{C_{K}}+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)>\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})

Then, necessarily

and so
x_{i}>\alpha_{i}
. We next aim to minimize the quotient

Q:=\frac{(z_{i}-z_{i}^{\prime})(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})}{d_{\psi_{i}}^{\ \mathrm{symm}}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime})}=\frac{z_{i}-z_{i}^{\prime}}{\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})},

and show that it is greater than

. To do so, and motivated by the expression of the subgradient given in
eq.

10
, we distinguish three cases. If
x_{i}^{\prime}=0
, then by
eq.

10

and the term in the right hand side is greater than

if and only if

45

0<x_{i}^{\prime}\leq\alpha_{i}
, we get that
z_{i}^{\prime}=\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})
and so

Q=\frac{z_{i}-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})}{z_{i}-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})}.

Now, we fix

and observe that, as a function of

is minimized for the smallest admissible

z_{i}=(\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0))/C_{K}+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)

Q>\frac{(\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0))/C_{K}+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(\alpha_{i})}{(\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0))/C_{K}+\psi_{i}^{\prime}(0)-\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})},

C_{K}<1
, we have that
(\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0))/C_{K}\geq\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})-\psi^{\prime}_{i}(0)>0

Finally, if
x_{i}^{\prime}>\alpha_{i}

z_{i}^{\prime}=\psi_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}^{\prime})
leading to

Let
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{N}

\mathbf{z}

9

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
be such that

7.3
Proofs of

We provide in this section the proof of
theorem

19
and
theorem

20
. In contrast with the previous section (i.e.
Section

7.2
), extending the results of
[
7
, Section 4.3]
is much more involved, since their analysis crucially relies on the fact that they have access to a closed-form expression of the oracle solution. As this is not available in our setting, the methods therein must be suitably adapted. We start with the following general lemma, which will be useful in the proof of both results, and is a direct consequence of the so-called descent lemma.

Lemma 31
.

Under
1.1
, there exists a constant
\tilde{L}\geq L
such that, for any
\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}})
,

\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|\leq\sqrt{2\tilde{L}G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})}.

(47)

In particular, when
\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\tilde{L}=L
is valid.

Proof.

Since
G_{\mathbf{y}}
has
L
-Lispchitz gradient by assumption, it satisfies the descent lemma
[
2
, Theorem 18.15 (iii)]
, which writes, for any
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
,

G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{z})\leq G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})+\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})^{T}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w})+\frac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now, we claim that there exists
\eta>0
such that, for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
,
\mathbf{z}:=\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
. Then we derive that

\displaystyle 0\leq G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}))

\displaystyle\leq G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})-\eta\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})-\left(\eta-\frac{L\eta^{2}}{2}\right)\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|_{2}^{2}

where we note that the left hand side above is non-negative by assumption. Taking
\eta<\tfrac{2}{L}
and rearranging the terms, we conclude that

\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(2\eta-L\eta^{2}\right)^{-1}G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w}).

(48)

In particular, one can observe that the concave quadratic function
\eta\mapsto 2\eta-L\eta^{2}
attains its maximum at
\eta=\tfrac{1}{L}
, which would be the choice leading to the best (smallest) bound.

To complete the proof, it remains to prove the claim; i.e., that there exists
\eta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{L}]
such that
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{w}-\eta\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
for all
\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
. While this is trivial when
\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbb{R}^{M}
(all
\eta
are fine), more care is needed when
\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
. Let
\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
. First, from
Item

(iii)
of
1.1
, we can define a uniform margin,

\delta:=\inf_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}})}\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{w},\partial(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}))>0

where
\partial(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
denotes the boundary of the domain of
G_{\mathbf{y}}
. Additionally, from
Item

(iv)
of
1.1
we have
\theta:=\sup_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{A}|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{N}})}\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|<\infty
. Taking
\eta<\tfrac{\delta}{\theta}
leads to

\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|=\|\eta\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|<\frac{\delta}{\theta}\|\nabla G_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{w})\|\leq\delta,

which in turn implies that
\mathbf{z}\in\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
since, if
\mathbf{z}\notin\mathrm{int}(\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}})
, then necessarily
\|\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{w}\|\geq\delta
. Hence, we have shown that there exists
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{L},\frac{\delta}{\theta}\right\}
such that (
48
) holds true, which can be equivalently expressed as the existence of
\tilde{L}\geq L
such that (
47
) holds. The fact that
\tilde{L}=L
is valid when
\operatorname{dom}G_{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbb{R}^{M}
is direct from the above derivations.

We are now ready to prove
theorem

19
.
