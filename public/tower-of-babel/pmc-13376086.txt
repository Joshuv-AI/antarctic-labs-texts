Ondansetron oral soluble pellicles vs. azasetron for preventing nausea and vomiting in patients receiving antitumor therapies: a comparative observational study
Authors: Cheng Zhao, Haibao Cao, Shuowen Yan, Runsen Sun, Lina Hu, Hui Yu, Cui Bai, Kai Cheng, Xiaoping Wu, Xiaoyi Pan, Qiang Zhang, Bin Jiang, Huiqian Liu, Simin Tao, Jianfeng Zhang, Meng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOndansetron oral soluble pellicles (OSP) show satisfactory effects on preventing nausea and vomiting following antitumor therapies. This study aimed to compare the antiemetic efficacy and safety profiles of ondansetron OSP vs. azasetron in patients receiving antitumor therapies.MethodsA total of 110 patients who received antitumor therapies were included in this comparative observational study. According to patient willingness, disease status, and tolerance, 55 patients received ondansetron OSP, and the other 55 patients received intravenous azasetron. During acute and delayed phases, the function living index emesis (FLIE) scale score and degrees of nausea and vomiting [high (46–63 points), moderate (19–45 points), or low (0–18 points)] were assessed. Adverse reactions were collected.ResultsDuring the acute phase, FLIE-nausea (5.7 ± 8.9 vs. 17.9 ± 13.6) and vomiting (6.9 ± 7.8 vs. 17.6 ± 13.9) domain scores were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (both P < 0.001). The degrees of nausea and vomiting were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (both P < 0.05). During the delayed phase, FLIE-nausea (5.0 ± 7.9 vs. 10.8 ± 9.1) and vomiting (4.2 ± 6.6 vs. 9.2 ± 8.7) domain scores were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (both P < 0.001). Regarding adverse reactions, the incidence of constipation was lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (7.3% vs. 30.9%) (P = 0.003), but the incidence of headache did not differ (P = 0.113).ConclusionOndansetron OSP possess superior antiemetic effects and safety profiles to azasetron in patients receiving antitumor therapies.Keywords: Ondansetron oral soluble pellicles, Azasetron, Nausea, Vomiting, Adverse reactions

Introduction

Nausea and vomiting are prevalent and distressing side effects in patients undergoing antitumor therapies, severely impairing their quality of life (Park et al. 2025). Effective prevention of these symptoms is essential to improve the management of cancer patients (Henson et al. 2020; Navari 2020; Farhat et al. 2025). Azasetron, a 5-hydroxytryptamine-3 (5-HT3) receptor antagonist, has been used as an antiemetic regimen after antitumor therapies in some Asia Pacific countries (Piechotta et al. 2021).

Ondansetron, another 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, is one of the most recommended regimens for preventing antitumor therapy-induced nausea and vomiting according the guidelines (Hesketh et al. 2020; Herrstedt et al. 2024). This drug can be administered either orally or via intravenous injection ((CSCO) 2019; Care et al. 2024). Its oral formulation can avoid the risk of several complications induced by intravenous injection, such as phlebitis and infection (Zhu et al. 2015). Ondansetron oral soluble pellicle (OSP) is a novel formulation that dissolves rapidly in the mouth without water, offering a convenient antiemetic treatment option for patients receiving antitumor therapies (Dadey 2015). A previous study indicated that ondansetron OSP exhibited good antiemetic efficacy with satisfactory safety profiles in cancer patients who received chemotherapy (Sun et al. 2025). However, to better understand the efficacy and safety of ondansetron OSP for the prevention of nausea and vomiting, a comparison with other antiemetic agents like azasetron is necessary.

Accordingly, this study aimed to compare the antiemetic efficacy and safety between ondansetron OSP and azasetron in patients receiving antitumor therapies.

Methods

Patients

From November 2023 to July 2024, 110 patients who received antitumor therapies were included in this comparative observational study. The inclusion criteria were: (1) received ondansetron OSP or azasetron to prevent nausea and vomiting caused by antitumor therapies; (2) aged more than 18 years old; (3) complete data on the function living index emesis (FLIE) scale. Patients who were diagnosed with nausea and vomiting due to other causes (e.g., brain metastases, hypercalcemia) or received anti-emetic therapy other than ondansetron OSP or azasetron were excluded from this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anqing Petrochemical Hospital of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital Group with number of (2023) 032 accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from each patient or his/her family.

Data collection

Clinical characteristics, including patient demographics, smoking, drinking, comorbidities, history of tumor surgery, and diagnosis, were collected. The emetic risk of antitumor drugs was also collected. According to the incidence of acute nausea and vomiting caused by antitumor drugs without prophylactic treatment, the emetic risk was divided into 4 levels: (1) high (incidence of acute nausea and vomiting > 90%); (2) moderate (incidence of acute nausea and vomiting 30 ~ 90%); (3) low (incidence of acute nausea and vomiting 10 ~ 30%); (4) minimal (incidence of acute nausea and vomiting < 10%). More details can be found at [www.csco.org.cn].

Treatment

Patients received ondansetron OSP or azasetron to prevent nausea and vomiting caused by antitumor therapies. Notably, ondansetron was administered sublingually, whereas azasetron was administered intravenously. The ondansetron OSP or azasetron regimen was individualized according to patient willingness, disease status, and tolerance. The recommended regimen was based on the emetic risk, and the recommended dosage of ondansetron OSP was 8 mg sublingually, twice daily; and azasetron was 10 mg intravenously, twice daily.

Assessment

All patients filled out the FLIE scale, which was used to evaluate the impact of nausea and vomiting on patients’ daily lives. The FLIE scale included the nausea domain and vomiting domain. Each domain was divided into acute phase and delayed phases; the former was defined as a period within 24 h after taking antitumor drugs, and the latter was defined as a period after 24 h. Each domain scored from 0 to 63 at each phase. Besides, the degree of each domain at each phase was assessed according to the following criteria: (1) high (46 ~ 63 points); (2) moderate (19 ~ 45 points); (3) low (0 ~ 18 points). Moreover, adverse reactions, including constipation and headache, were collected.

Statistical analyses

SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, USA) was used for data analysis. Comparison analyses of continuous variables between ondansetron OSP and azasetron were conducted using a student t-test. Comparison analyses of counting variables between ondansetron OSP and azasetron were calculated using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. However, a comparison analysis of the degree of nausea and vomiting between ondansetron OSP and azasetron was performed using the Wilcoxon rank sum test because the data were ordered categorical variables and there was an ordered rank relationship between them. A P value < 0.05 was defined as statistical significance.

Results

Comparison of clinical features

Clinical features, including age, sex, smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of tumor surgery, and diagnosis, were not different between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron (all P > 0.05). Emetic risks also did not differ between these two types of patients (P = 1.000); in detail, 0 (0.0%), 0 (0.0%), 48 (87.3%), and 7 (12.7%) patients receiving ondansetron OSP had minimal, low, moderate, and high emetic risks; while 0 (0.0%), 1 (1.8%), 48 (87.3%), and 6 (10.9%) patients receiving azasetron had minimal, low, moderate, and high emetic risks (Table 1).

Comparison of FLIE-nausea and vomiting domain scores during the acute phase

FLIE-nausea domain scores during the acute phase were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (5.7 ± 8.9 vs. 17.9 ± 13.6) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). The percentages of low, moderate, and high degrees of nausea during the acute phase differed between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron (P = 0.028); overall, the degree of nausea during the acute phase was lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (Fig. 1B).

Comparison of nausea during the acute phase. Comparison of FLIE-nausea domain scores (A) and the percentages of patients in different degrees of nausea (B) during the acute phase between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron

Similarly, compared with patients receiving azasetron, FLIE-vomiting domain scores during the acute phase were decreased in patients receiving ondansetron OSP (6.9 ± 7.8 vs. 17.6 ± 13.9) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). The percentages of low, moderate, and high degrees of vomiting during the acute phase differed between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron (P = 0.006); the vomiting degree during the acute phase was lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (Fig. 2B).

Comparison of FLIE-nausea and vomiting domain scores during the delayed phase

FLIE-nausea domain scores during the delayed phase were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (5.0 ± 7.9 vs. 10.8 ± 9.1) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). However, the percentages of low, moderate, and high degrees of nausea during the delayed phase did not differ between patients receiving ondansetron OSP or azasetron (P = 0.282) (Fig. 3B).

Comparison of nausea during the delayed phase. Comparison of FLIE-nausea domain scores (A) and the percentages of patients in different degrees of nausea (B) during the delayed phase between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron

By comparison with patients receiving azasetron, FLIE-vomiting domain scores during the delayed phase were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP (4.2 ± 6.6 vs. 9.2 ± 8.7) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4A). Nevertheless, the percentages of low, moderate, and high degrees of vomiting during the delayed phase did not differ between patients receiving ondansetron OSP or azasetron (P = 0.187) (Fig. 4B).

Comparison of adverse reactions

The incidence of constipation was lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron (7.3% vs. 30.9%) (P = 0.003). However, the incidence of headache was not different between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron (1.8% vs. 10.9%) (P = 0.113) (Table 2).

Discussion

Antitumor therapy-induced nausea and vomiting are troublesome side effects, occurring in approximately 20–50% of patients (Jahn et al. 2022). These two symptoms often cause physical discomfort, decreased appetite, increased fatigue, impaired social interactions, and reduced mental health, which severely impair the quality of life in cancer patients (Henson et al. 2020; Heckroth et al. 2021). Nausea and vomiting are regulated by the coordinated action of the central and peripheral nervous systems (Zhong et al. 2021; Johns et al. 2024). Antitumor therapies, such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy, stimulate the release of serotonin from enterochromaffin cells in the gastrointestinal tract, which activates 5-HT3 receptors on the vagal nerve endings (Jahn et al. 2022; Ullah et al. 2023; Scotté 2025). This signal is then integrated in the brainstem vomiting center, triggering nausea and vomiting (Ullah and Ayaz 2023). 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, such as ondansetron and azasetron, selectively block these receptors, preventing nausea and vomiting (Machu 2011; Rojas et al. 2014; Belluomini et al. 2024). In the current study, we discovered that during the acute phase, nausea and vomiting were attenuated by ondansetron OSP compared to azasetron in patients receiving antitumor therapies, as evidenced by lower FLIE-nausea and vomiting scores, as well as lower degrees of nausea and vomiting. During the delayed phase, FLIE-nausea and vomiting scores were lowered by ondansetron OSP compared to azasetron. Overall, our study provided a novel insight that the antiemetic effect of ondansetron OSP was superior to azasetron after antitumor therapies. In clinical practice, the satisfactory antiemetic effects of ondansetron OSP may assist in improving the quality of life in patients undergoing antitumor therapies. Therefore, ondansetron OSP could be recommended following antitumor therapies.

The choice of antiemetic regimen should be determined based on the risks of nausea and vomiting (Athavale et al. 2020; Belluomini et al. 2024). For patients with a low emetic risk, the single antiemetic agent could be administered, while for patients with moderate or high emetic risks, the combined antiemetic regimen is required (Care et al. 2024; Herrstedt et al. 2024). Of note, regardless of the emetic risk classifications, 5-HT3 receptor antagonists serve as the cornerstone in the antiemetic regimes (Care et al. 2024; Herrstedt et al. 2024). In our subgroup analyses, we found that in patients with a high risk of nausea and vomiting, the antiemetic effect of ondansetron OSP was superior to azasetron during the acute and delayed phases, as evidenced by lower FLIE nausea and vomiting domain scores and lower degrees of nausea and vomiting. However, in patients with low to moderate risk, only the FLIE-nausea and vomiting domain scores during the acute and delayed phases were lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than those receiving azasetron. Our findings disclosed that ondansetron OSP might be recommended for patients with a high risk of nausea and vomiting. It should be clarified that the number of patients with a high emetic risk was relatively small. Therefore, this finding should be further validated.

Constipation is a common adverse reaction following 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, which is caused by the inhibition of 5-HT3 receptors in the gastrointestinal tract, leading to reduced gut motility (Mawe et al. 2013; Browning 2015; Gilmore et al. 2018). In this study, we found that the incidence of constipation was lower in patients receiving ondansetron OSP than in those receiving azasetron. This finding was in line with a previous study (Lee et al. 2014). Headache is another common adverse reaction following 5-HT3 receptor antagonists (Gilmore et al. 2018). We found that the incidence of headache did not differ between patients receiving ondansetron OSP and those receiving azasetron. Overall, our findings suggested that ondansetron OSP might possess better safety profiles than azasetron due to its ability to reduce the risk of constipation.

Patients receiving highly emetogenic antitumor therapies should be treated with additional antiemetic agents, such as corticosteroids or neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptor antagonists, according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Clinical Practice Guideline Update on Antiemetics (Basch et al. 2011). Of note, concomitant antiemetic therapy may influence the observed efficacy outcomes. However, at the time this study was conducted, NK1 receptor antagonists were not available at our institution. In addition, as the majority of patients in this study were classified as having moderate emetogenic risk, and considering the potential adverse effects associated with corticosteroids, no additional antiemetic agents were administered. Therefore, all patients received monotherapy with either ondansetron OSP or azasetron.

Several limitations should be mentioned. (1) The number of patients in each group was relatively small, which might impair the statistical power. (2) A randomized controlled trial was required to validate the findings of this study. (3) This study was conducted in China, which limited the generalizability of our findings. (4) Comparison of ondansetron OSP with other 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, such as granisetron and palonosetron, could be performed by subsequent studies. (5) Ondansetron was administered sublingually whereas azasetron was administered intravenously. Differences in the route of administration might contribute to variations in drug absorption and bioavailability, potentially influencing the comparative outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ondansetron OSP shows better antiemetic effects and safety profiles compared with azasetron in this observational study of patients receiving antitumor therapies. Clinically, ondansetron OSP may serve as a potential alternative to prevent nausea and vomiting after antitumor therapies. However, our findings require further confirmation in well-designed randomized controlled trials.
