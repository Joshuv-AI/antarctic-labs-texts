Single-cell and machine learning-integrated bulk RNA-seq analysis reveals TKT as an oxidative stress-associated diagnostic biomarker in acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Jiayi Wang, Shouwen Zhang, Fahong Li, Yanjin Wei, Ming Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) represents one of the most severe clinical manifestations of coronary artery disease, characterized by rapid onset, aggressive progression, and high mortality. Despite considerable advances in reperfusion strategies and pharmacological interventions in recent years, AMI remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide [1, 2] . The onset of AMI is not only directly associated with atherosclerotic plaque rupture and thrombus formation, but also closely linked to both local and systemic inflammatory responses. Excessive activation of immune cells and the release of inflammatory mediators play crucial roles in myocardial ischemic injury and subsequent tissue repair [3–5]

Oxidative stress (OS) is widely recognized as a critical pathogenic mechanism in the development of AMI. During myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury, excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and DNA damage in cardiomyocytes. These molecular events subsequently trigger apoptosis, necrosis, and the activation of inflammatory signaling cascades, resulting in sustained myocardial injury [6–9] . Increasing evidence suggests that OS also modulates the recruitment, differentiation, and functional states of immune cells, thereby contributing to the amplification of local inflammation and remodeling of the immune microenvironment in AMI. However, the spatiotemporal expression patterns and dynamic regulation of key OS-related molecules in AMI particularly within peripheral immune cell populations remain poorly characterized.

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled high-resolution dissection of cellular composition, transcriptional heterogeneity, and intercellular communication in complex diseases. By profiling peripheral immune cells at the single-cell level in AMI patients, it is possible to uncover stress-associated immunological changes and identify key molecular drivers linked to disease progression. Concurrently, the increasing adoption of machine learning (ML) approaches in biomedical research has provided powerful tools for biomarker discovery from high-dimensional transcriptomic datasets. Techniques such as multi-model cross-validation, feature selection, and ensemble learning have been shown to enhance the robustness and reproducibility of candidate gene identification, thereby facilitating the discovery of clinically translatable biomarkers.

In this study, we integrated scRNA-seq data from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of AMI patients with multiple publicly available bulk RNA-seq cohorts. A curated list of 807 oxidative stress-related genes was used to construct a single-cell OS activity scoring system. Differential expression, correlation analysis, and univariate logistic regression were employed to identify preliminary high-risk OS-related candidates. Building on this, six mainstream machine learning algorithms LASSO, Random Forest, Boruta, Bayesian modeling, Learning Vector Quantization (LVQ), and Treebag-were used in cross-validation to screen for consensus diagnostic biomarkers. Further analyses, including single-cell expression profiling, pseudotime trajectory inference, and intercellular communication modeling, were performed to comprehensively characterize the expression dynamics and regulatory potential of candidate genes in AMI pathogenesis. Ultimately, we identified TKT as a robust oxidative stress-associated biomarker with diagnostic value in AMI. TKT exhibited distinct expression patterns in myeloid immune cells and appeared to mediate inflammatory signaling within the immune microenvironment. The graphical abstract in Fig. 1 provides a clear and comprehensive overview of our work. These findings provide a novel framework for identifying and functionally annotating OS-related genes in AMI and offer mechanistic insights into the immunometabolic coupling underlying post-infarction inflammation.

Results

Study design and cohort characteristics

To clarify the overall study design, we summarized all datasets used in this study (Table S1), including cohort ID, sample size, tissue source, platform, and key clinical metadata. The myocardial dataset was used to define the tissue-level oxidative stress context after AMI, the PBMC scRNA-seq dataset was used for single-cell immune profiling, and the bulk blood cohorts were used for candidate prioritization and external validation.

For the bulk cohorts, inclusion criteria were further refined during preprocessing. In GSE123342, only D0 samples were retained. In GSE59867, only admission-phase samples and controls were included. GSE123342 and GSE48060 were then combined as the training cohort, and GSE59867 was used as an independent validation cohort.

OS activity evaluation and single-cell profiling reveals ten major immune cell types in AMI peripheral blood

When evaluating oxidative stress (OS) activity in scRNA-seq data from myocardial tissue samples, we first assessed OS scoring with GO and KEGG validated 807 OS genes (Table S2, Figure S1H) across previously annotated cell types (Fig. 2A). This analysis revealed significant alterations in OS activity AMI patients and healthy controls (Fig. 2B). Moreover, global OS activity was markedly elevated in AMI samples (Fig. 2C), indicating a reversal of OS homeostasis following AMI onset. Given the limited accessibility of myocardial tissue and the clinical value of systemic biomarkers, we next concentrated our investigation to AMI bulk data and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). ssGSEA enrichment results revealed significantly elevated OS activity among AMI patients in cohorts GSE59867 and GSE48060 (Fig. 2D–E), while GSE123342 showed a non-significant increase in OS activity in AMI patients (Fig. 2F). Then, we performed a comprehensive analysis of oxidative stress (OS)-related features using the scRNA-seq dataset GSE269269, which includes peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from 10 AMI patients. Following rigorous quality control to remove low-quality cells (Figure S1A-B), obvious batch effect was observed (Figure S1C) resulting in batch correction using the Harmony algorithm, a total of 93,324 cells were retained for downstream analysis. UMAP visualization showed clear clustering of cells across all patients (Figure S1E).

Dimensionality reduction and clustering were based on the top 2,000 highly variable genes, resulting in 28 transcriptionally distinct clusters (Figure S1D & F). Using canonical marker genes and reference to the CellMarker 2.0 database, we manually annotated these clusters into 10 major immune cell types(Figure S1G), including B cells, monocytes, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), platelets, cycling cells, red blood cells and eosinophils (Fig. 2G). Expression of key marker genes for each cell type is shown in Fig. 2H. The relative abundance of each immune cell type was consistent across different patients (Fig. 2I).

OS activity landscape in AMI patients

To assess oxidative stress (OS) activity at the single-cell level, we performed enrichment scoring based on a curated list of 807 OS-related genes. Four independent scoring algorithms AUCell, UCell, singscore, and AddModuleScore were applied to each cell, and their standardized sum was used to calculate a composite OS score (Scoring). The scoring algorithms were positively correlated with each other, and the Scoring was strongly correlated with each individual method (Spearman r = 0.905–0.950), supporting the overall consistency and robustness of the scoring strategy. (Figure S 1I) This metric enabled quantification of the OS state across diverse immune cell populations.

Among all cell types, monocytes exhibited the highest OS activity, suggesting their central role in ROS-associated immune responses during AMI (Fig. 3A). Visualization of OS Scoring on UMAP further confirmed monocyte enrichment in the high-OS state (Fig. 3B). Using the Mclust algorithm, all cells were grouped into three OS activity subpopulations, designated as Low, Mid, and High (Fig. 3C).

Differential expression analysis between OS-high and OS-low cells identified 969 OS-related upregulated expressed genes (OS-DEGs) in the OS-high group (Fig. 3D, Table S3). To further refine candidates, we assessed the correlation between OS Scoring and gene expression across all 807 OS genes. This analysis yielded 83 positively correlated OS genes (Spearman r > 0.5, P < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). The intersection of OS-DEGs and correlated OS genes resulted in 73 OS activity genes (Fig. 3F).

Subsequently, univariate logistic regression was performed to evaluate the diagnostic potential of these genes in distinguishing AMI status. As a result, 37 key OS activity genes demonstrated statistically significant associations and were defined as Hub OS genes (Fig. 3G).

Although the algorithms varied in their selection strategies and sensitivity to feature noise, TKT (transketolase) was the only gene consistently identified across all six machine learning methods with cross validation, underscoring its robustness and diagnostic relevance (Fig. 4G). Diagnostic potential and immunological correlates of TKT in AMI

To further validate the diagnostic utility of TKT in AMI, we assessed its classification performance and expression levels in both training and independent validation cohorts. In the training set, ROC curve demonstrated that TKT could effectively distinguish AMI patients from healthy controls, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.736 (Fig. 5A). Differential expression analysis confirmed that TKT was significantly upregulated in the peripheral blood of AMI patients compared to controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 5B). In the independent validation dataset GSE59867, the diagnostic performance of TKT was further improved, achieving an AUC of 0.801 (Fig. 5C). Expression levels remained significantly elevated in AMI patients (P < 0.001, Fig. 5D), reinforcing the reproducibility of TKT as a diagnostic biomarker. Further Western Blot analysis with 10 clinical obtained PBMC samples revealed a significantly elevated protein level in AMI patients compared to healthy controls with 5 biological replicates per group and 3 technical replicates (Fig. 5E & S3A, Table S5). This difference was statistically validated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Fig. 5F).

With the aim of exploring the immunological relevance of TKT expression, we applied the MCP-counter algorithm to estimate immune cell abundance and correlated these scores with TKT expression. TKT levels showed the strongest positive correlations with monocytic lineage and neutrophils (P < 0.001, Fig. 5G). To further assess the potential confounding effect of immune-cell composition, we additionally performed MCP-counter analysis using cell markers derived from the scRNA-seq dataset. The results still showed significant positive correlations of TKT with monocytes and neutrophils (Figure S2 E). Moreover, univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses incorporating both TKT expression and immune-cell scores demonstrated that TKT remained closely associated with AMI occurrence and retained independent diagnostic value in the multivariable model (Figure S2 F-G). In addition, single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed after stratifying samples into TKT-high and TKT-low expression groups. Enrichment scores revealed that macrophages, neutrophils, and effector memory CD8⁺ T cells were significantly more abundant in the TKT-high group (P < 0.05, Fig. 5H).

Expression dynamics and functional annotation of TKT at the single-cell level

We next investigated the expression landscape of TKT across immune cell subsets at single-cell resolution. UMAP projection revealed that TKT was highly expressed in monocytes and neutrophils, while its expression was lower in lymphoid populations such as T cells, B cells, and NK cells (Fig. 6A–B). Platelets and dendritic cells (DCs) showed moderate expression levels.

We further stratified monocytes and neutrophils into TKT⁺ (normalized TKT expression > 0) and TKT⁻ (normalized TKT expression ≤ 0) cells according to the expression of TKT. A greater number of TKT-positive cells were observed among monocytes, neutrophils, and DCs (Fig. 6C). To assess the dynamic changes in TKT expression during immune cell activation or differentiation, pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted separately in neutrophils and monocytes-two lineages with the highest TKT expression. The dynamic expression of S100A8, S100A9, CXCR2 and FCGR3B in neutrophils and S100A8, S100A9, FCN1 and IL1B in monocytes was carried out to validate lineage inference (Figure S4A). In neutrophils, TKT expression exhibited a rise-then-fall pattern along the pseudotime axis, suggesting a role in functional maturation or transient activation (Fig. 6D). In contrast, monocytes displayed stable and sustained high expression of TKT throughout the pseudotime trajectory (Fig. 6E), implying a potential role in maintaining activation homeostasis.

To explore the biological significance of differential TKT expression, we performed subpopulation-level differential expression analysis between TKT⁺ and TKT⁻ cells within monocytes and neutrophils. Functional enrichment using Gene Ontology (GO) revealed that TKT⁺ neutrophils were enriched in pathways related to reactive oxygen species (ROS) response, chemotaxis, and cell migration. KEGG pathway analysis further highlighted associations with neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, cytokine–receptor interactions, and fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis (Fig. 6F).

In TKT⁺ monocytes, enriched functions included cytoplasmic translation, ribosome biogenesis, and antigen processing and presentation. Notably, Fc-γ receptor–mediated phagocytosis was enriched in TKT⁻ neutrophils, indicating a potential functional divergence based on TKT expression status.

To investigate the communication characteristics of TKT-high immune subsets in the immune microenvironment of AMI, we utilized CellChat to construct a ligand-receptor based intercellular signaling network. We specifically modeled interactions involving four key subpopulations: TKT⁺ neutrophils (TKT⁺ Neu), TKT⁻ neutrophils (TKT⁻ Neu), TKT⁺ monocytes (TKT⁺ Mono), and TKT⁻ monocytes (TKT⁻ Mono). Comprehensive communication networks were visualized for each subgroup, illustrating their bidirectional interaction intensity and partner diversity (Fig. 7A–D).

The results revealed that TKT⁺ monocytes exhibited frequent and strong interactions with multiple immune cell types, including T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and platelets. Likewise, TKT⁺ neutrophils demonstrated robust outgoing interactions with several cellular targets. The global cell–cell interaction network (Fig. 7E) highlighted widespread signal exchange between TKT-expressing subsets and other immune populations. Quantitative analysis showed that TKT⁺ Mono had the highest incoming interaction strength, whereas TKT⁺ Neu ranked among the top in outgoing signaling (Fig. 7F).

Pathway-level analysis further identified distinct signaling modalities associated with TKT⁺ cells. In terms of incoming signals, TKT⁺ monocytes exhibited a globally higher overall incoming communication strength than TKT⁻ monocytes, while TKT⁺ neutrophils exhibited almost the same as TKT− neutrophils (Figure S4B). More specifically, VISFATIN, GRN, CysLTs, LAIR1, and Histamine signaling pathways were preferentially activated in TKT⁺ monocytes. In neutrophils, the overall incoming communication contribution was largely comparable between TKT⁺ and TKT⁻ neutrophils, clear differences were observed at the pathway level. TKT⁺ neutrophils preferentially enriched MIF, ICAM, and CD23 signaling. By contrast, TKT⁻ neutrophils showed relative enrichment of the ADGRE pathway. (Fig. 7G). As signal senders, TKT⁺ subsets were enriched in multiple inflammation-related pathways. In monocytes, TKT⁺ monocytes displayed distinct pathway-selective outgoing signaling activity compared with their TKT⁻ counterparts, with specific enrichment of the APP, THBS, BAG, and LAIR1 pathways. In neutrophils, TKT⁺ neutrophils showed specific activation of the GRN signaling pathway relative to TKT⁻ neutrophils. (Fig. 7H).

Collectively, these findings suggest that TKT⁺ monocytes and neutrophils act as central communication hubs, actively orchestrating inflammatory crosstalk and leukocyte recruitment in the context of AMI.

Discussion

Oxidative stress (OS) has long been recognized as a critical contributor to myocardial injury and adverse cardiac remodeling following acute myocardial infarction (AMI). In our single-cell transcriptomic analysis of myocardial tissue, we observed a profound shift in OS activity across multiple cardiac cell types after AMI onset, highlighting the widespread redox imbalance in the infarcted heart. These findings are consistent with previous reports emphasizing the role of OS in exacerbating inflammatory responses, promoting cell death, and impairing tissue repair [10, 11] . However, due to the limited accessibility of myocardial tissue in clinical settings, there remains a pressing need to identify robust, blood-based OS-related biomarkers that can reflect the systemic oxidative burden and aid in the early detection or monitoring of AMI.We systematically integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with multi-cohort bulk RNA-seq datasets and employed multiple machine learning algorithms to identify and validate transketolase (TKT) as a key oxidative stress (OS)-related factor potentially involved in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). TKT was found to be consistently upregulated in AMI patients, exhibiting favorable diagnostic performance. Its elevated expression in myeloid-derived immune cells, particularly monocytes and neutrophils, suggests that TKT may contribute to AMI pathogenesis through modulation of immune responses.

Oxidative stress is one of the central molecular mechanisms underlying AMI. Prolonged overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) can induce myocardial cell injury, trigger inflammatory cascades, and promote immune cell infiltration, collectively exacerbating cardiomyocyte necrosis and cardiac dysfunction [12–14] . In this study, we applied multiple scoring algorithms to assess OS activity at the single-cell level and identified key genes showing strong correlations with both OS scores and differential expression. Among them, TKT emerged as a core candidate across all analyses, and was consistently selected by six mainstream machine learning algorithms as a robust diagnostic biomarker, demonstrating both stability and reproducibility.

Further analysis showed that TKT expression was primarily enriched in monocytes and neutrophils within AMI peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and was positively correlated with the abundance of myeloid immune subsets in bulk blood. This indicates that part of its diagnostic signal may reflect post-AMI immune-cell compositional changes. However, TKT remained significantly associated with AMI after adjustment for immune-cell scores, suggesting that its relevance extends beyond post-AMI immune-cell compositional changes alone. Notably, although neutrophils did not display the highest OS scores, TKT remained highly expressed in monocytes and neutrophils, indicating its potential role in shaping systemic oxidative stress rather than reflecting OS‑high states within a specific lineage. As a critical enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), TKT regulates NADPH production and cellular redox homeostasis. Its upregulation is often viewed as a compensatory response to excessive ROS levels [15–17] . Previous studies have reported that TKT contributes to redox regulation in various inflammatory conditions and is closely associated with macrophage polarization and neutrophil activation [18–20] . Our findings are consistent with this, showing that TKT⁺ immune cell populations exhibit theoretically higher immune activation scores and may functionally enriched in processes such as neutrophil chemotaxis, inflammatory cytokine secretion, and antigen presentation. These findings suggest that overactivated myeloid cells may contribute to post-ischemic injury through TKT associated oxidative inflammatory coupling.

Pseudotime analysis revealed dynamic upregulation of TKT during neutrophil activation, whereas its expression remained persistently high in monocytes across developmental states. This differential pattern suggests that TKT may participate in regulating the transition of neutrophils from a quiescent to an activated state, while maintaining redox homeostasis in monocytes to support their role in chronic inflammation. This aligns with previous reports that TKT can sustain PPP flux to promote M1 macrophage polarization and amplify inflammatory responses [20–23] .

Cell–cell communication analysis further revealed a central regulatory role for TKT⁺ immune cells within the AMI immune microenvironment. TKT⁺ monocytes in AMI are characterized by enhanced communication activity, with incoming enrichment of VISFATIN, CysLTs, Histamine, GRN, and LAIR1 signaling and outgoing activation of APP, THBS, BAG, and LAIR1, indicating closer involvement in metabolic stress sensing, leukocyte inflammatory recruitment, immune inhibition, and thrombo-inflammatory regulation [24–26] . TKT⁺ neutrophils were defined more by selective pathway remodeling than by stronger global communication, showing preferential incoming activation of MIF, ICAM, and CD23 and outgoing enrichment of GRN, which is consistent with enhanced inflammatory amplification, adhesion-related interactions, and immune modulatory signaling during acute myocardial infarction [27–29] .

TKT encodes a key enzyme in the PPP. Beyond its canonical roles in metabolic energy production and redox regulation, recent studies have revealed non-metabolic functions of TKT in modulating cell fate and immune activity. For example, under hypoxic conditions, TKT can stabilize HIF-1α to enhance cellular adaptation to ischemic stress [30, 31] . In models of atherosclerosis and cardiac fibrosis, TKT expression was positively correlated with ROS levels and promoted the expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and MCP-1 [32, 33] . These findings suggest that TKT may function as a metabolic-inflammatory hub that links oxidative stress to immune regulation in the context of AMI.

Despite the multi-omics integration and the application of advanced computational approaches in this study, several limitations remain. First, functional validation of TKT is lacking, particularly in myocardial tissue. Future studies are needed to clarify its localization and biological roles through gain and loss of function experiments. Second, heterogeneity in AMI disease stages and individual immune backgrounds may affect the generalizability of TKT expression as a biomarker. In addition, some clinical variables, such as STEMI/NSTEMI subtype and treatment status, were not consistently available across all public datasets. Multicenter prospective cohorts are needed for broader validation. Additionally, this study focused on evaluating TKT as a binary classifier for AMI versus healthy controls and its biological effects, rather than distinguishing MI subtypes or comparing with high‑sensitivity Troponin I. High‑sensitivity Troponin I is the clinical gold standard Future studies will be required to validate if TKT is specific in AMI rather than reflecting systemic inflammation/immune activation and assess whether TKT offers complementary diagnostic value, with larger and more diverse clinical cohorts. Moreover, upstream transcriptional or metabolic regulators of TKT expression, as well as its downstream signaling networks, remain to be elucidated.

In summary, this study comprehensively identified TKT as a high-confidence oxidative stress-related biomarker through multi-layer transcriptomic integration and single-cell resolution analysis. TKT demonstrates stable diagnostic potential and immunoregulatory relevance in AMI and provides a new theoretical basis for understanding oxidative stress-mediated myocardial injury and developing redox-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and bulk transcriptomic data to explore the oxidative stress landscape in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). By calculating oxidative stress activity across immune cell subsets and intersecting differentially expressed genes with oxidative stress-related gene sets, we identified a core panel of 32 candidate genes. Through a comprehensive machine learning framework combining six algorithms, transketolase (TKT) was consistently identified as a robust diagnostic marker for AMI. The expression and diagnostic value of TKT were validated across independent datasets.

Further analyses revealed that TKT is predominantly expressed in monocytes and neutrophils, where it may participates in pseudotime-dependent transcriptional programs associated with immune activation and redox regulation. Functional enrichment and intercellular communication analyses highlighted TKT⁺ immune cells as key mediators of inflammatory signaling.

Overall, our findings not only provide a single-cell level map of oxidative stress responses in AMI, but also identify TKT as a potential biomarker and functional regulator in the immunopathology of AMI. This work offers novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying oxidative stress in cardiovascular disease and presents TKT as a promising target for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

To investigate oxidative stress-related alterations during the progression of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), we retrieved publicly available transcriptomic datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and Zenodo dataset (https://zenodo.org/records/6578047). We obtained scRNA seq data of 20 myocardial tissue samples with 16 patients and 4 healthy controls from Zenodo 6578047. The scRNA-seq dataset GSE269269 [34] , comprising peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) samples from 10 patients, was also used for single-cell analysis. Three bulk RNA-seq datasets were included for comparative transcriptomic profiling and feature prioritization: GSE123342 [35] (65 AMI patients, 22 healthy controls), GSE48060 [36] (31 patients, 21 healthy controls), and GSE59867 [37] (111 patients, 46 healthy controls). For all three bulk cohorts, expression matrices and phenotype annotations were downloaded using the GEOquery package. Between-array normalization was performed using normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package. Based on the inspected expression distributions, no additional log2 transformation was applied in the final workflow. For probe-to-gene mapping, platform-specific annotation files were used for each dataset. When multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, the probe with the highest mean expression across samples was retained to generate a unique gene-level expression matrix. We obtained oxidative stress (OS)-related genes from the GeneCards database (version 5.10, https://www.genecards.org/) by using the keyword ‘oxidative stress’, applying a relevance score cutoff of ≥ 7 and filtering the protein coding genes. This previously reported criterion [38, 39] yielded a total of 807 OS-related genes, which were validated by GO-KEGG enrichment analysis and subsequently included in the analysis (Table S2).

Preprocessing of scRNA-seq data

Single-cell RNA-seq data from AMI patients were processed and analyzed using the Seurat package (v4.3.0) in R. Raw gene expression counts were first normalized using the NormalizeData function with the LogNormalize method. Mitochondrial and ribosomal gene percentages were calculated for each cell to assess quality. Cells with fewer than 1,000 or more than 8,000 detected genes, or with mitochondrial RNA content exceeding 25%, were excluded to ensure stringent quality control. The top 2,000 most variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures to capture major sources of transcriptional heterogeneity. These genes were used as the basis for scaling (ScaleData) and principal component analysis (RunPCA). PCA was performed with 10 PCs, followed by neighbor graph construction using dims = 1:30. The clustering resolution was set to 0.5. Batch effects among samples were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. Dimensionality reduction was performed using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) based on the top principal components. Clustering was carried out using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions (resolution = 0.5). Cell type annotation was performed manually based on canonical markers and reference to the CellMarker 2.0 database [40] . Visualization of major cell types and subclusters was conducted using DimPlot, Idents, and packages including scCustomize and SCP.

Characterization of oxidative stress activity at the single-cell level

To evaluate oxidative stress (OS) activity across different immune cell types, we employed the irGSEA [41] package to score individual cells based on the expression of 807 OS-related genes. Four enrichment-based scoring algorithms-AUCell, UCell, singscore, and AddModuleScore were applied independently. All resulting scores were standardized using the scale function, and their sum was used to calculate a composite OS score (referred to as Scoring) for each cell, serving as a quantitative proxy for OS activity. This composite score was used to reduce dependence on any single method and to capture the shared oxidative stress signal more robustly. To assess concordance across scoring algorithms, pairwise correlations among the individual scoring results were evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. The distribution of OS scores was visualized across cell types, and all cells were subsequently stratified into High, Mid and Low OS activity groups according to Mclust-based three-state grouping which was performed by the ‘mclust’ R package. Differential gene expression analysis between these High and Low OS activity groups was performed using the RunDEtest function in the SCP package. OS-related genes with |log2 fold change|> 1 and P < 0.05 were defined as differentially expressed (OS-DEGs). In parallel, correlation analysis was performed between the Scoring value and the expression levels of the 807 OS-related genes. Genes with a correlation coefficient > 0.5 and P < 0.05 were considered OS-correlated genes. The intersection of OS-DEGs and OS-correlated genes was then used to define a set of key OS activity genes.

Identification of candidate diagnostic markers using multiple machine learning algorithms

To prioritize robust OS-related biomarkers for AMI, GSE123342 and GSE48060 were merged as the training cohort after batch-effect correction using the ComBat method, whereas GSE59867 was used as an independent validation cohort.

Hub OS genes identified in the previous step were first pre-screened in the training cohort by univariate logistic regression. The retained candidate genes were then entered into six machine learning-based feature-selection algorithms, including LASSO, Boruta, LVQ, Treebag-RFE, Random Forest-RFE, and Naive Bayes-RFE. This supervised pre-screening step was designed to reduce feature dimensionality and improve downstream feature prioritization. Internal cross-validation within individual algorithms was used for parameter tuning or feature-selection stability, rather than as a formal nested framework for unbiased estimation of generalization performance.

To further assess robustness, the same feature-selection pipeline was also applied separately to GSE123342 and GSE48060 for cross-cohort consistency analysis. Finally, the independent cohort GSE59867 was used only for external validation of the final selected biomarker.

LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) applies an L1 penalty to shrink some regression coefficients to zero, thereby enabling feature selection and dimensionality reduction. In this study, LASSO was implemented using the cv.glmnet function with internal cross-validation, and the optimal penalty parameter was selected according to lambda.min..

Boruta was applied as a wrapper-based feature-selection method using its default shadow-feature framework, which iteratively compares original variables with randomized shadow features to identify robust predictors. In parallel, Random Forest-based recursive feature elimination (Random Forest-RFE) was performed to rank variables according to their contribution to classification and to identify informative features under internal cross-validation.

Bayesian was also conducted using recursive feature elimination combined with internal cross-validation.

LVQ (Learning Vector Quantization), a prototype-based supervised classification algorithm, was implemented in the caret framework using repeated fivefold cross-validation with three repeats, and variable importance scores were used for feature prioritization.

Treebag-RFE was performed using bootstrap aggregating combined with recursive feature elimination under internal cross-validation.

Genes identified by all six machine learning algorithms were considered Final OS genes, representing robust diagnostic candidates. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted in both training and validation datasets to evaluate their predictive performance. In addition, we compared their expression levels between AMI patients and healthy controls to assess diagnostic consistency. To reduce the potential impact of pseudoreplication in single-cell analyses, an additional patient-level aggregation analysis was performed in monocytes and neutrophils by summarizing Final OS genes expression and OS scores within each patient. Patients were stratified into cell-type specific OS-high and OS-low groups according to the median aggregated OS score within each cell type.

Western blot

10 PBMC samples were collected from AMI patients within one hour of hospital admission, and from age- and sex-matched healthy controls (Table S5). After centrifugation to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), the cells were washed twice with cold PBS and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beijing Solarbio, R0020) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail and PMSF (Solarbio, IP0280, Beijing, China). The lysates were kept on ice for 1 h and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected and stored at − 20 °C. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23,227, CA, USA).

For Western blotting, protein samples were mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Beyotime, P0286-15ml, Shanghai, China), denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, and separated using 10% SDS-PAGE gels. A pre-stained protein ladder (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26617, CA, USA) was used as the molecular weight marker. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010, Darmstadt, Germany) using a semi-dry transfer system (Junyibio, JY-ZY3, Beijing, China). Membranes were then blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk (prepared in PBS) for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking.

Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used included Transketolase Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech, 11039-1-AP; RRID: AB_2245927; 1:5000) and anti-β-actin (ZSGB-Bio, TA-09, RRID: AB_2636897; 1:2000). After three washes with PBST, membranes were incubated for 90 min at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-Bio, ZB-2301, RRID: AB_2747412; 1:3000). After further washes, signals were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit (sc-2048, Santa Cruz, CA, USA).

Band intensities were quantified using the analysis software of the Tanon 1600 automatic chemiluminescence imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Characterization of final OS genes in specific immune cell types

To assess the expression patterns of the Final OS genes across different immune cell types, we first applied the MCP-counter algorithm to the validation dataset (GSE59867) to estimate the relative abundance scores of various immune cell populations in each sample. Spearman correlation analysis was then performed between cell-type scores and the expression levels of Final OS genes, and the results were visualized using heatmaps. In addition, univariate logistic regression was performed for Final OS genes expression and each immune-cell score separately, and variables with statistical significance were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model to assess whether the association between Final OS genes and AMI remained after adjustment for immune-cell composition.

To investigate the cellular distribution of Final OS genes at the single-cell level, we used the FeaturePlot_scCustom function from the scCustomize package to generate customized feature plots. Additionally, the FeatureStatPlot function in the SCP package was used to statistically compare gene expression levels across annotated immune cell types.

Based on the expression intensity of Final OS genes, we selected the top two cell types exhibiting the highest expression. Within each of these cell types, cells were further stratified into Final OS-positive and Final OS-negative subpopulations according to their expression status, enabling downstream comparative analyses between these subgroups.

Pseudotime analysis

To explore the dynamic expression patterns of Final OS genes during cell differentiation or state transitions, we conducted pseudotime trajectory analysis using the Monocle package (v2.24.0). Gene expression matrices were extracted separately from the top two immune cell types exhibiting the highest Final OS gene expression. These matrices were converted into sparse format, and the associated metadata were used to construct the phenoData (cell annotations) and featureData (gene information), which were integrated into a Monocle CellDataSet object.

To normalize the data and model gene expression variability, we estimated size factors for each cell and dispersion values for each gene. Genes with high variability (mean expression ≥ 0.1 and empirical dispersion ≥ fitted dispersion) were selected as highly dispersed features for trajectory inference.

Dimensionality reduction was performed using the DDRTree algorithm, followed by cell ordering along the pseudotime trajectory. Cells were visualized based on their annotated Subcelltype, and the expression dynamics of the Final OS gene were plotted across the inferred pseudotime to identify phase-specific expression trends.

Cell–cell communication analysis

To investigate intercellular communication networks in the AMI immune microenvironment, we used the CellChat R package, which leverages a curated ligand-receptor interaction database for both human and mouse. Single-cell transcriptomic data annotated with cell type labels were used to infer signaling interactions between immune cell populations.

We initialized a CellChat object and utilized the CellChatDB.human reference to assess both outgoing (signal sending) and incoming (signal receiving) pathways among the identified cell clusters. Global communication patterns were visualized using the netVisual_circle function, which displays the number and strength of ligand–receptor interactions among clusters in a circular plot.

To further delineate dominant signaling modalities, we applied the selectK function to determine the optimal number of signaling patterns (K = 2 for both outgoing and incoming signaling). The resulting communication patterns were visualized using the netAnalysis_dot function, which summarizes the contribution of each pathway to cell-type-specific communication behavior.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1), and random seeds of all analysis was set ‘1234’. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Multiple-testing correction was applied to differential expression, correlation screening, and logistic regression. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
