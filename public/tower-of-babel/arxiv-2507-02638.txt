Homogenisation and spectral convergence of high-contrast convolution type operators
Mikhail Cherdantsev, Andrey Piatnitski, Igor Velčić

Abstract
The paper deals with homogenisation problems for high-contrast symmetric convolution-type operators with integrable kernels in media with a periodic microstructure. We adapt the two-scale convergence method to nonlocal convolution-type operators and obtain the homogenisation result both for problems stated in the whole space and in bounded domains with the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition. Our main focus is on spectral analysis. We describe the spectrum of the limit two-scale operator and characterize the limit behaviour of the spectrum of the original problem as the microstructure period tends to zero. It is shown that the spectrum of the limit operator is a subset the limit of the spectrum of the original operator, and that they need not coincide. Keywords: Homogenisation, Integral Operators, Spectral Convergence, High-contrast Problems Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 35B27, 45H99, 45M05, 45M15, 45P05

1
Introduction

This work is devoted to the homogenisation of high-contrast symmetric convolution-type operators with integrable kernels in periodic media. In the first part of the paper, we show that the two-scale convergence method (see
[
1
]
) applies to the class of operators under consideration and present several technical results that help us to homogenise this family of operators. We then analyse the corresponding spectral problems in two settings: problems posed in the whole space, and boundary value problems in bounded Lipschitz domains. In the latter case, we impose homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions in the complement of the domain.

The spectrum of the limit homogenised operator is non-trivial. Following the ideas of
[
30
]
we introduce an auxiliary Zhikov’s
\beta
-function, and, describe the spectrum of the limit two-scale operator in terms of this function.

Next we study the limit behaviour of the spectrum of the original operator in the whole space setting and characterize the Hausdorff limit of this spectrum as the microstructure period tends to zero. In particular, we show that the spectrum of the limit operator is always a subset of the said limit, and that the opposite inclusion need not hold, neither in the whole space nor in a bounded domain. We show that the additional limit spectrum is associated with the the quasiperiodic quasi-modes supported on the soft component. Remarkably, the soft component need not be infinite for this to happen (cf. the discussion below). Indeed, in the case of disconnected soft inclusions it is enough for the convolution kernel to have sufficiently large support in order that inclusions “communicate” with their neighbours.

For the generic Lipschitz domain we face the usual in periodic homogenisation problem of disagreement between the periodic microstructure and the domain boundary. In particular, the Hausdorff limit of the spectra does not exist in general in the presence of the boundary. However, the Hausdorff limit of the spectra may exist for domains of particular shape. To illustrate this, we study a special case when the domain is rectangular and assume that the small parameter
\varepsilon
goes to zero along a discrete subsequence so that the geometry of the microstructure in the boundary layer is self-congruent along this subsequence. We show that in this case the limit of the spectra exists in the Hausdorff sense and provide its characterisation.

Finally, in the whole space setting, adapting approach of
[
16
]
, we establish norm-resolvent convergence result with explicit bounds via scaled Gelfand transform, and, as a consequence, obtain bounds on the rate of spectral convergence.

Various processes in the models of population biology, porous media and chemistry of polymers
are often described in terms of evolution equations of the form
\partial_{t}u=Au
with a non-local convolution type operator
A
and the corresponding stationary equations. The non-locality of
A
reflects the non-local nature of the interaction in these models.
One of the models of this type, the so-called contact model in
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, has been actively studied in the existing literature, see
[
20
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
.

In a typical case the kernel of the operator

is a product of a convolution kernel

a(x-y)
and a coefficient
\Lambda(x,y)
. The kernel
a(x-y)
specifies the intensity of interaction in the model depending on the distance. It determines the localization properties of
A
. The coefficient
\Lambda(x,y)
represents
the local characteristics of the environment.
Raising the question of the long-time behaviour of these processes and of the macroscopic description of models with
a microstructure, we arrive at the upscaling or homogenisation problems for non-local convolution-type operators.

[

Sharp in order estimates for the rate of convergence in the operator norms have been obtained in
[

Homogenization problems for symmetric convolution-type operators with random statistically homogeneous coefficients have been considered in
[
25

operator with constant coefficients. In the ergodic media the limit operator is deterministic.

Non-local operators and functionals of convolution type in perforated domains have been investigated by the variational methods in
[
6
]
–
[

and, in the case of more complicated geometry, in
[

]

Homogenization result for a high-contrast convolution type evolution equation was obtained in the recent

[
28

, where the correctors and semigroup approximation techniques were used.

High-contrast differential equations with rapidly oscillating coefficients have been widely studied in the
existing literature starting from
[
4
]
. At present there are many works devoted to this topic. However, it turned out that the asymptotic behaviour of the spectrum
of these operators is a rather delicate problem. It was addressed successfully in
[
30

.

The approach developed in this article relies on the two-scale convergence technique.
However, the two-scale resolvent convergence result we obtain implies only “half” of the Hausdorff convergence of the spectra, namely, that the limiting spectrum contains the spectrum of the limit operator. The inverse inclusion (let us focus on the whole space setting at the moment, to avoid boundary layer effects) requires additional assumptions on the operator and / or geometry of the soft component, and is not true in general. For example, in the periodic setting of
[
30
]
,
[
31
]
the Hausdorff convergence of the spectra holds provided that the soft component is a collection of disconnected finite size inclusions. When such assumptions are not satisfied, it may happen that the limiting spectrum is strictly larger than that of the limit operator. In high-contrast problem this situation was first rigorously analysed in
[
14
]
, see also
[
16
]
, which provides deeper insight in the setting of
[
14
]
, where the “additional” spectrum is attributed to the quasiperiodic quasi-modes supported on the

[
15

13

5

A similar picture can be observed in other settings. In
[
9
]
the authors investigate the limit resolvent equation, limit spectrum and limit evolution for high contrast thin elastic plates. It turns out that in one of the regimes the limit spectrum is strictly larger than the spectrum of two-scale limit operator. The limit behaviour of the spectrum of high-contrast elliptic differential operators in random statistically
homogeneous environments was studied in
[
11
]
. There, the additional limiting spectrum not accounted by the two-scale limit operator is of a different nature and is due to stochastic fluctuations of arrangement of inclusions from the ergodic average. However, in case of a bounded domain this extra spectrum is not present in the limit
[
12
]
. We also mention
[
3
]
for results on semigroup convergence and the spectrum of the limit operator.

In the context of boundary layer spectrum we mention
[
2
]
, which focuses on the high frequency spectrum for moderate-contrast elliptic PDEs in a bounded domain.
Making use of the Floquet-Bloch transform the authors characterize the limit spectrum; they also characterize the limit boundary spectrum for a rectangular domain and a discrete subsequence of the microscopic parameter.

Finally, we note that this work contains new extension result, which is simpler and, in fact, more natural for the framework of integral operators, compared to the one used in previous works in the area. Moreover, the ‘minimal’ assumptions on the geometry of the soft / stiff components necessary for the extension result have been relaxed, in particular, they do not require any regularity of the boundary of the sets. We also develop a regularisation technique for the ‘bounded energy’ sequences of functions allowing for
H^{1}

Assumption 2.1
.

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a\Big(\frac{x-\eta}{\varepsilon}\Big)\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,\eta)(u(x)-u(\eta))(v(x)-v(\eta))dxd\eta,\quad u,v\in L^{2}(S).

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},v)-\lambda\int_{S}u_{\varepsilon}v=\int_{S}f_{\varepsilon}v\qquad\hbox{for all }v\in L^{2}(S).

(3)

Remark 2.3
.

We do not assume any regularity of the sets representing the stiff and the soft components beyond them being open. While not surprising, this is in a stark contrast with the PDEs case, where some boundary regularity is required to guarantee existence of extension from the stiff into the soft components with the control of
H^{1}
-norm. In the present setting, we only need to control the convolution energy, cf. (
144
). It turns out that a simple piecewise constant extension by local averages does the job! The only regularity we require in Assumption
2.2
is that the stiff component is “connected” through the convolution kernel
a
— no geometrical connectedness of the stiff component is required.

In the case of high-contrast PDEs (
-\nabla\cdot a_{\varepsilon}\nabla
) it is important for the structure of the limit spectrum whether the soft component comprises infinite connected sets or a collection of disconnected inclusions. In particular, the limit spectrum is strictly larger than the spectrum of the limit two-scale operator in the case of the former. In the present setting even if the soft component consists of disconnected inclusions they still may “communicate” with each other if the support of the convolution kernel
a
is sufficiently large, cf. operators
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}
below.

Remark 2.4
.

In the case of a bounded domain
S
, one can also study the problem with homogenous Neumann boundary condition. In this case, the integration in the bilinear form

is taken over the set
S\times S
rather than
\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle a_{\varepsilon}(u,v)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d+2}}\int_{S}\int_{S}a\Big(\frac{x-\eta}{\varepsilon}\Big)\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,\eta)(u(x)-u(\eta))(v(x)-v(\eta))dxd\eta,\quad u,v\in L^{2}(S).

The analysis and results for the Neumann problem would be analogous to the ones obtained in the case of the Dirichlet condition, including the analysis of the boundary spectrum, see the discussion below.

Remark 2.6

In the operator form the equations (

)-(
11
) read

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom}u-\lambda\left(u+\langle z\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}z(x,\cdot)-\lambda(u(x)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}}(\cdot)+z(x,\cdot))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(x,\cdot)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}}(\cdot),\quad u\in H_{0}^{1}(S),\ z\in L^{2}(S\times Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}).

In order to characterize the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}
we introduce the function
\beta:\mathbf{R}\setminus\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})\to\mathbf{R}
:

\beta(\lambda):=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\Big\langle(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}}\Big\rangle=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\langle b_{\lambda}\rangle,\quad\lambda\in\mathbf{R}^{+}_{0}\backslash\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}),

(14)

where we denote
b_{\lambda}:=(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}}
.
The spectrum of the limit two-scale operator can be fully characterised by the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom}
, function
\beta
and the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
:

Theorem 2.9
.

\Sp(\mathcal{A})=\{\beta(\lambda)\in\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom})\}\cup\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}).

(15)

Here and in what follows, when we write
\beta(\lambda)
we tacitly assume that
\lambda
belongs to the domain of
\beta
, i.e.
\lambda\in\mathbf{R}\setminus\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})
.

2.2
Spectral convergence

The spectral inclusion inverse to (
12
) is not always the case. For it to hold for high-contrast problems one needs some sort of locality property for the soft component. For example, in the case of periodic elliptic PDEs, the inverse to (
12
) inclusion holds only if the the soft component consists of disconnected inclusions, see
[
31
]
. In the present setting the inverse to (
12
) does not hold in general even under the mentioned geometric assumption. Indeed, if the support of the convolution kernel
a
is sufficiently large to guarantee the nearby inclusions to “communicate”, the limiting spectrum is strictly larger than
\Sp(\mathcal{A})
. In other words, the two-scale resolvent convergence is too restrictive and does not fully recover the asymptotic behaviour of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
. More specifically, instead of the ‘periodic’ operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
one needs to consider its whole space counterpart
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}
defined below. Moreover, in the case when
S
is a bounded domain, the spectrum arising from the interaction of the soft component with the boundary of
S
persists in the limit, but is not accounted for by the operator
\mathcal{A}
. It is, however, seems impossible to characterise the part of the limiting spectrum arising from the boundary for a general domain
S
. In what follows, we define relevant objects and summarise our main results concerning spectral convergence.

We define the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}:\,L^{2}(Y^{\#}_{\rm soft})\to L^{2}(Y^{\#}_{\rm soft})
as the symmetric operator associated with the bilinear form

\displaystyle a_{\rm soft}(z,b)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}a(\xi)p(y,y+\xi)(z(y+\xi)-z(y))(b(y+\xi)-b(y))\,dy\,d\xi,\quad z,b\in L^{2}(Y^{\#}_{\rm soft}).

This operator has important role in characterisation of the limiting spectrum in both cases: when
S
is bounded or
S=\mathbf{R}^{d}
.
Note that in contrast to the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
, which acts in the space of periodic functions
L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft})
, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}
acts in the space
L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft}^{\#})
.

It is not difficult to see that

\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})\subset\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}).

Indeed, if
\lambda\in\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})
the one can use a corresponding (approximate) periodic eigenfunction and the cut off function technique analogous to the one in the proof of Theorem
5.5
below, in order to construct an approximate
L^{2}
eigenfunction for
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}
. Alternatively, one can employ Gelfand transform resulting in
\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft})=\cup_{\theta}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta})
, see Section
6
for the definition of the quasi-periodic operators
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta}
. Then the claim follows directly from the observation that
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{0}
.

Further, we define the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon,\rm soft}:\,L^{2}(\varepsilon Y^{\#}_{\rm soft}\cap S)\to L^{2}(\varepsilon Y^{\#}_{\rm soft}\cap S)
via the associated bilinear form

a_{\varepsilon,\rm soft}(z,b)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)p\Big(\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\frac{x+\varepsilon\xi}{\varepsilon}\Big)\left(z(x+\varepsilon\xi)-z(x)\right)\left(b(x+\varepsilon\xi)-b(x)\right)\,dx\,d\xi,\\
z,b\in L^{2}(\varepsilon Y^{\#}_{\rm soft}\cap S).

(16)

(Note that in the case when
S=\mathbf{R}^{d}
the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon,\rm soft}
is unitarily equivalent to
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}
via the rescaling, hence they have identical spectra.)

The following two assertions hold.

Theorem 2.10
.

\{\beta(\lambda)\in\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom})\}\cup\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft})\subset\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}).

(17)

Theorem 2.11
.

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon})\subset\{\beta(\lambda)\in\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom})\}\cup\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon,\rm soft}).

In the case when
S=\mathbf{R}^{d}
, the inclusion (
17
) becomes equality, see Theorem
2.13
below. On the other hand, for a general domain
S
the “boundary layer” spectrum may behave unpredictably. In general, the task of characterising the boundary layer and the associated spectrum is extremely challenging. In Section
5
we provide its analysis for a special case when the set
S
is a rectangular box with vertices in
\mathbf{Z}^{d}
, see (
49
), and the sequence
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{N},N\in\mathbf{N}
, so that the geometry of the soft component in the boundary layer is congruent for all
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{N}
. In this case the limiting spectrum exists in the sense of Hausdorff, and we have the following

Theorem 2.12
.

{\rm H}\mbox{-}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N})=\{\beta(\lambda)\in\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm hom})\}\cup\left(\cup_{i=1,\dots,2^{d}}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{v_{i}})\right).

Here
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{v_{i}}
denotes the operator on the part of soft component associated with the
i
-th vertex of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
, see Section
5
and (
54
) below for the precise definition. Notice that
\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft})\subset\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{v_{i}})
for all
i
.

2.3
Norm resolvent and spectral convergence bounds for the case
S=\mathbf{R}^{d}

Periodic problems in the whole space is a standard premise for the Floquet-Bloch theory. Applying the scaled version of the Gelfand transform
G_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(Y^{*}\times Y),Y^{*}:=[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
,

(G_{\varepsilon}f)(\theta,y):=\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}f(\varepsilon(y+n)){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\varepsilon\theta\cdot(y+n)},

we obtain the decomposition

G_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(G_{\varepsilon})^{-1}=\int_{Y^{*}}^{\oplus}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta},

(18)

where the self-adjoint operators
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}
,
\theta\in Y^{*}
, are associated with the sesquilinear form

\begin{split}\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\Lambda(y,\xi)+p(y,\xi)\right)({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}u(\xi)-u(y))\overline{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}v(\xi)-v(y))}d\xi dy,\\
\quad\forall u,v\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y).\end{split}

The relation (
18
) implies that

\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon})=\cup_{\theta\in Y^{*}}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}).

Thus, in order to understand the limit behaviour of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
and its spectrum on can analyse the family of operators
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}
instead.

Following a new approach, recently developed in
[
16
]
, we show that
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}
can be approximated in the norm resolvent sense uniformly in
\theta\in Y^{*}
by a homogenised operator
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{h,\theta}
, associated with the sesquilinear form

\varepsilon^{-2}A^{\rm hom}\theta\cdot\theta\,z\overline{\widetilde{z}}\\
+\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)p(y,\xi)({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}(z+v(\xi))-(z+v(y)))\overline{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}(\widetilde{z}+\tilde{v}(\xi))-(\widetilde{z}+\tilde{v}(y)))}d\xi dy,\\
\quad\forall z+v,\widetilde{z}+\tilde{v}\in\mathbf{C}+L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft}).

(19)

Our results are as follows.

Theorem 2.13
.

There exists a positive function
\overline{h}
satisfying
\overline{h}(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
,
\lim_{t\to 0}\overline{h}(t)/t>0
, such that

\|(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}+1)^{-1}-(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{h,\theta}+1)^{-1}\|_{L^{2}(Y)\to L^{2}(Y)}\leq C\overline{h}(\varepsilon)

(20)

uniformly in
\theta\in Y^{*}
.
Moreover,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{G}:=\{\beta(\lambda)\geq 0\}\cup\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}),

and, for any
\Lambda>0
, one has

d_{H,[0,\Lambda]}\Big(\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}),\mathcal{G}\Big)\leq C(\Lambda)\max\{\overline{h}(\varepsilon),\,\varepsilon^{2/3}\},

where

d_{H,[0,\Lambda]}(A_{1},A_{2}):=\max\big(\operatorname{dist}(A_{1}\cap[0,\Lambda],A_{2}),\,\operatorname{dist}(A_{1},A_{2}\cap[0,\Lambda])\big).

The function
\overline{h}
depends essentially on the decay properties of the convolution kernel
a
at infinity, cf. (
98
), (
102
), (
119
) and (
120
) below. In case
a
has a finite third moment, i.e.
a(\xi)|\xi|^{3}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, then we can set
\overline{h}(t)=t
, see Remark
6.6
(cf. also
[
16
, Theorem 5.6]
).

Remark 2.14
.

It is not difficult to generalize the results of the paper to the case of
d
-dimensional periodicity lattice (see
[
19
]
)

\Xi=\{\ell=\sum_{j=1}^{d}k_{j}b_{j}:(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}\},

where
\{b_{1},\dots,b_{d}\}
is an arbitrary basis for
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. The associated unit cell is

Y=\{y=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\gamma_{j}b_{j}:\gamma_{j}\in[0,1),j=1,\dots,d\}\},

such that
\mathbf{R}^{d}
is the disjoint union of the translated cells
\ell+Y
, if
\ell
ranges over
\Xi
.
Y^{\#}
can be then defined as
Y^{\#}=\mathbf{R}^{d}/\Xi
with the quotient topology. For the case
\Xi=\mathbf{Z}^{d}
,

we obtain the case discussed here.

The limit two-scale operator via two-scale convergence

In this section we analyse the corrector problem (

Lemma 3.1
.

a.

The corrector problem (
8
) has a unique up to an additive constant solution.

b.

For
\chi:=(\chi^{1},\dots,\chi^{d})
and
\eta\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

\forall b\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y).

(21)

c.

The homogenised matrix of the stiff component
A^{\rm hom}
, cf. (
7

\tilde{\alpha}_{1}|\eta|^{2}\leq A^{\rm hom}\eta\cdot\eta,

for some
\tilde{\alpha}_{1}>0

Proof.

Claim a. follows from the Lax-Milgram theorem upon establishing the coercivity of the form

a_{\rm stiff}(\psi_{1},\psi_{2}):=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Lambda_{0}(y,y+\xi)\left(\psi_{1}(y+\xi)-\psi_{1}(y)\right)\left(\psi_{2}(y+\xi)-\psi_{2}(y)\right)\,d\xi\,dy\\
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm stiff})

for functions with zero mean on
Y_{\rm stiff}

A.5
with
\mathcal{M}=Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}
, and using the periodicity of
\psi

a_{\rm stiff}(\psi,\psi)\geq\frac{\alpha_{1}c_{a}}{(2k)^{d}}\int_{(2kY_{\rm stiff})^{2}\cap D_{r_{a}}}(\psi(y)-\psi(\xi))^{2}\,dy\,d\xi\\
\geq C\int_{(Y_{\rm stiff})^{2}}(\psi(y)-\psi(\xi))^{2}\,dyd\xi=C\left(\|\psi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm stiff})}-\left(\int_{Y_{\rm stiff}}\psi\right)^{2}\right),

(22)

which proves the first claim.

Claim b. is a straightforward consequence of the linearity of problem (
8
).

Now we address part c. From (
7
) and (
8

A^{\rm hom}_{ij}=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Lambda_{0}(y,y+\xi)\left(\xi_{i}+\chi^{i}(y+\xi)-\chi^{i}(y)\right)\left(\xi_{j}+{\chi}^{j}(y+\xi)-{\chi}^{j}(y)\right)\,d\xi\,dy,

which yields the symmetry of
A^{\rm hom}

Similarly, from (

A^{{\rm hom}}\eta\cdot\eta=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Lambda_{0}(y,y+\xi)\left(\xi\cdot\eta+\chi^{\eta}(y+\xi)-\chi^{\eta}(y)\right)^{2}d\xi\,dy

for every

for some

Note that one then has
\int_{y_{0}+(Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}\cap\square^{m/2})}y\cdot\eta dy=0

Let

We arrive at a contradiction due to the periodicity of

Proposition 4.1

\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})\subset\Sp(\mathcal{A}).

Proof.

\widetilde{u}_{0}+\widetilde{z}
, the problem on the soft component reads

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}\widetilde{z}-\lambda\widetilde{z}=\lambda\widetilde{u}_{0}{\mathbf{1}}_{Y_{\rm soft}}+g\psi.

The difference between
\widetilde{z}
and
\hat{z}:=\lambda\widetilde{u}_{0}\phi

satisfies

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}(\widetilde{z}-\hat{z})-\lambda(\widetilde{z}-\hat{z})=g\psi.

Multiplying the last equation by
{g}\|g\|^{-2}_{L^{2}(S)}
and integrating the resulting identity over
S,
we see that the function

\breve{z}:=\frac{\int_{S}(\widetilde{z}-\hat{z}\,){g}dx}{\|g\|^{2}_{L^{2}(S)}}

is a solution of

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}\breve{z}-\lambda\breve{z}=\psi.

Since
\psi\in L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})
is arbitrary, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I
acts onto, therefore, by the bounded inverse theorem one concludes that
(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I)^{-1}
is bounded. Indeed, since
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I
is onto and self-adjoint, the kernel of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}-\lambda I
is trivial, hence the operator is injective.
∎

It order to characterise the structure of the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
, it is natural to consider the following decomposition:

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,1}-\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2},

(33)

where the operators
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}
on
L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft})
are defined by (cf. (
13
))

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,1}z(x)=m(x)z(x),\quad\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}z(x)=\int_{Y}K(x,y)z(y)\,dy,\quad z\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft}),

with

m(x):=2\int_{Y}\tilde{a}(y-x)p(x,y)\,dy,\quad K(x,y):=2\tilde{a}(y-x)p(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}}(y),

(34)

and

\tilde{a}(x):=\sum_{j\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}a(x+j).

(35)

Since
a
is even,
\tilde{a}
is also an even function. Moreover, it is
Y
-periodic by construction.

The spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,1}
is purely essential and coincides with the essential range of the function
m\in L^{\infty}_{\#}(Y)
. We next argue that
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}
is compact. Recall the Schur test, see e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 6.18]
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)
and
(Y,\mathcal{N},\nu)
be
\sigma
-finite measurable space and let
K
be
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
measurable function on
X\times Y
. Suppose that there exists
C>0
such that
\int_{X}|K(x,y)|\,d\mu(x)\leq C
for almost every
y\in Y
and
\int_{Y}|K(x,y)|\,d\nu(y)\leq C
for almost every
x\in X
, and that
1\leq p\leq\infty
. If
f\in L^{p}(Y)
, the integral

\mathcal{T}f(x)=\int_{Y}K(x,y)f(y)\,d\nu(y)

is finite for almost every
x\in X
, the function
\mathcal{T}f
belongs to
L^{p}(X)
, and
\|\mathcal{T}f\|_{L^{p}(X)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{p}(Y)}
.

The following result is also well known, see e.g.
[
18
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)
be
\sigma
-finite measurable space and let

\mathcal{M}
be countably generated. Let

K
be
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
measurable function on
X\times X
such that
\int_{X}\int_{X}|K(x,y)|^{2}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)<\infty
. Then the operator
\mathcal{T}:L^{2}(X)\to L^{2}(X)
defined by

\mathcal{T}f(x)=\int_{Y}K(x,y)f(y)\,d\nu(y)

is of Hilbert-Schmidt class and thus compact.

Proposition 4.4
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}
is compact.

Proof.

It is easily seen that

\mathop{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}_{y}\int_{Y}|K(x,y)|\,dx=\mathop{\operatorname{ess\,sup}}_{x}\int_{Y}|K(x,y)|\,dy\leq 2\|p\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\tilde{a}\|_{L^{1}(Y)}=2\|p\|_{L^{\infty}}\|a\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

Hence, by Theorem
4.2
the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}
is bounded:

\|\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}\|_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})\to L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})}\leq 2\|p\|_{L^{\infty}}\|a\|_{L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

For each
n\in\mathbf{N}
we define

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{n}:=\tilde{a}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tilde{a}\leq n\}},\quad K_{n}(x,y):=\tilde{a}_{n}(y-x)p(x,y)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}}(x)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}}(y),

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2,n}z(x):=\int_{Y}K_{n}(x,y)z(y)\,dy,\quad\forall z\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft}).

By Theorem
4.3
the operators
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2,n}
are compact. Moreover,

\|\tilde{a}_{n}-\tilde{a}\|_{L^{1}(Y)}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0,\quad\int_{Y}|K_{n}(x,y)-K(x,y)|\,dx\leq C\|\tilde{a}_{n}-\tilde{a}\|_{L^{1}(Y)},\quad\forall y\in Y.

(36)

Then Theorem
4.2
and (
36
) imply

\|\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2,n}-\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\to 0.

Hence
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,2}
is compact.
∎

Thus, the operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
is a compact perturbation of the multiplication operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#,1}
, which implies the following characterisation of the spectrum.

\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})=\textrm{EssRan }m\cup\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\dots\},

where
\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots\}
is the discrete set (possibly empty or finite) that may have accumulation points only in
\partial(\textrm{EssRan }m)
.
If
\{\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime},\dots\}\subset\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots\}
is the set of all eigenvalues such that

\sup\{\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime},\dots\}\leq\inf\textrm{EssRan }m,
then we can enumerate the set
\{\mu_{1}^{\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime},\dots\}
in a non-decreasing order (accounting for multiplicity). In this case
\mu_{k}^{\prime}
can be characterised by the Rayleigh quotients:

\mu_{k}^{\prime}=\min_{V\subset L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft}),\textrm{ dim}V=k}\max_{z\in V}\frac{a_{\rm soft}^{\#}(z,z)}{\|z\|_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})}^{2}}.

(37)

Similarly, for the set
\{\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime},\dots\}\subset\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots\}
of all eigenvalues such that

\inf\{\mu_{1}^{\prime\prime},\mu_{2}^{\prime\prime},\dots\}\geq\sup\textrm{EssRan }m,
enumerated in the non-increasing order (accounting for multiplicity) one has

\mu_{k}^{\prime\prime}=\max_{V\subset L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft}),\textrm{ dim}V=k}\min_{z\in V}\frac{a_{\rm soft}^{\#}(z,z)}{\|z\|_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})}^{2}}.

(38)

We next study the properties of the function

Proposition 4.5
.

The function
\beta
can be naturally extended to
L^{c}
. It is differentiable on its domain and its derivative is always positive: more precisely,
\beta^{\prime}(\lambda)\geq 1-|Y_{\rm soft}|
. Moreover, if
\lambda_{0}\in L
is such that
\mu(\{\lambda_{0}\})>0
and there exists
\delta>0
with
(\lambda_{0}-\delta,\lambda_{0})\cap L=\varnothing
(respectively,
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\delta)\cap L=\varnothing
), then
\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{0}^{-}}\beta(\lambda)=+\infty
(respectively,
\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{0}^{+}}\beta(\lambda)=-\infty
).

Proof.

Using the spectral decomposition we obtain

\beta(\lambda)=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\int_{\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}(dE_{s}\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}^{\#}},\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm soft}})=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\int_{L}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}d\mu(s).

(39)

This formula naturally extends
\beta
onto
L^{c}
.
Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
the expression on the right-hand side of (
39
) yields

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2\lambda\int_{L}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}\,d\mu(s)+\lambda^{2}\int_{L}\frac{1}{(s-\lambda)^{2}}\,d\mu(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\int_{L}\,d\mu(s)+\int_{L}\frac{s^{2}}{(s-\lambda)^{2}}\,d\mu(s)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-|Y_{\rm soft}|.

In order to prove the second claim, assume that
(\lambda_{0}-\delta,\lambda_{0})\cap L=\emptyset
for some
\delta>0
(the other case is treated analogously) and consider the decomposition

\displaystyle\beta(\lambda)=\lambda+\lambda^{2}\int_{\{\lambda_{0}\}}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}d\mu(s)+\lambda^{2}\int_{(-\infty,\lambda_{0})}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}d\mu(s)+\lambda^{2}\int_{(\lambda_{0},+\infty)}\frac{1}{s-\lambda}d\mu(s).

Clearly, as
\lambda\to\lambda_{0}^{-}
, the integral

\int_{(-\infty,\lambda_{0})}(s-\lambda)^{-1}d\mu(s)

remains bounded,

\int_{(\lambda_{0},+\infty)}(s-\lambda)^{-1}d\mu(s)

is non-negative (possibly tends to
+\infty
), and

\int_{\{\lambda_{0}\}}(s-\lambda)^{-1}d\mu(s)

tends to
+\infty
.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem

, which characterises the spectrum of the limit two-scale operator.

4.2
Examples

The spectrum of the limit operator
\mathcal{A}
crucially depends on the spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
via the function
\beta
. In particular,
\Sp(\mathcal{A})
is guaranteed to have gaps in the case

if

\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#})

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
.

4.2.1
Example of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
with purely essential spectrum

In this section we construct a
1
-dimensional example of an operator
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
that has an empty discrete spectrum.
To simplify the notation, we will work with a shifted periodicity cell
Y=[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
in this example and take
Y_{\rm soft}=(-\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})
.
Define

a(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{y}{2}+\frac{3}{4}\right),&\hbox{if }y\in[-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2}],\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{4},&\hbox{if }y\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}],\\[5.69054pt]
\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{y}{2}+\frac{3}{4}\right),&\hbox{if }y\in[\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}],\\[5.69054pt]
0,&\hbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Clearly,
\tilde{a}(y)=\frac{1}{2}
for all
y
(cf. (
35
)).
We also set

p(y,\xi)=w(y)w(\xi)\big(1-\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}}(y)\mathbf{1}_{Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}}(\xi)\big),

(41)

where
w
is a continuous, positive,
1
-periodic function such that

\int_{Y}w(y)dy=1.

(42)

Then, see (
33
),(
34
),

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}z(y)=w(y)z(y)-w(y)\int_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(\xi)z(\xi)d\xi,\qquad y\in{Y_{\rm soft}},

and the quadratic form associated with
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
reads

a_{\rm soft}^{\#}(z,z)=\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)z^{2}(y)dy-\bigg(\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)z(y)dy\bigg)^{2}.

Denote

\delta:=\min_{Y_{\rm soft}}w,\qquad w^{+}:=\max_{Y_{\rm soft}}w.

Then the essential spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
is given by
{\rm EssRan}\,w=[\delta,w^{+}]
.

Clearly,

a_{\rm soft}^{\#}(z,z)\leqslant w^{+}\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})},\quad\forall z\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft}).

(43)

Next we will make a specific choice of the function
w
in such a way that

\delta\|z\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Y_{\rm soft})}\leqslant a_{\rm soft}^{\#}(z,z),\quad\forall z\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y_{\rm soft}).

(44)

Together with (
43
) this will imply that the discrete spectrum of
\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\#}
is empty for the choice of
w
.

Let
\gamma<\frac{1}{3}
be a small positive number. We introduce a sequence

b_{0}=\frac{1}{4},\quad b_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{k},\quad b_{j}=\gamma^{j-1}b_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=j}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\ \hbox{for }j\geqslant 2,

and consider the intervals
J_{j}=[b_{j+1},b_{j}]
, for
j\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
. Observe that
b_{j}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
and
|J_{j}|=\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{j}
for
j\geqslant 1
.
We define
w
as follows. On the interval
[b_{1},\frac{1}{2}]
we set
w\equiv w_{0}
, where
w_{0}
is a constant to be defined later, at each
b_{j},j\geq 2,
we set
w(b_{j})=w_{j}:=\frac{1}{j}+\delta
and require
w
to be affine on each interval
J_{j},j\geq 1
. Moreover, we define
w_{1}=w_{0}
for consistency of the notation. Finally,
we set
w(\frac{1}{2})=\delta
, extend
w
by symmetry to
[-\frac{1}{2},0]
, i.e.
w(-y)=w(y)
, and choose the constant
w_{0}
so that (
42
) holds.

One can easily check that
1<w_{0}<1+2\gamma
, for
\gamma<\frac{1}{4}
,
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
.
By construction, the function
w
is continuous,
\min w=\delta
,
\max w=w_{0}
.

Clearly, (
44
)
is equivalent to the inequality

\bigg(\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)z(y)dx\bigg)^{2}\leqslant\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}(w(y)-\delta)z^{2}(y)dy.

(45)

Since

\bigg(\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)z(y)dy\bigg)^{2}\leqslant\bigg(\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)|z(y)|dy\bigg)^{2},

it is sufficient to prove (
45
) for non-negative

only.
Denote

z_{j}:=\frac{1}{2|J_{j}|}\int\limits_{-J_{j}\cup J_{j}}z(y)dy.

Taking into account the inequality
w_{j+1}\leqslant w(y)\leqslant w_{j}
for

\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}w(y)z(y)dx\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|w_{j}z_{j}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\int\limits_{Y_{\rm soft}}(w(y)-\delta)z^{2}(y)dy\geqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|(w_{j+1}-\delta)z_{j}^{2},

where we have used the positivity of
z
and
w_{j}-\delta
,
j\in\mathbf{N}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that

\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|w_{j}z_{j}\Big)^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|(w_{j+1}-\delta)z_{j}^{2}.

(46)

\delta

\begin{gathered}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|w_{j}z_{j}\Big)^{2}\leqslant\frac{10}{9}\big(2|J_{0}|w_{0}z_{0}\big)^{2}+10\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2|J_{j}|w_{j}z_{j}\Big)^{2}\\

5.2
Characterization of
\text{H-}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N,\rm soft})
for the case
S=\Pi

As we already noted at the beginning of this section, the boundary spectrum, which comes essentially from the geometry of the soft component in the boundary layer, is difficult, if not impossible, to characterise in general. Moreover, one cannot get rid of the boundary spectrum by replacing the soft component near the boundary with the stiff one (as one could do in PDEs setting with dispersed/disconnected soft inclusions), even if the convolution kernel has finite support. In fact, this strategy will work only if the diameter of the support is smaller that the minimal distance between soft inclusions.

Yet, it should be possible to characterise the limit boundary spectrum in case for specific geometries of
S
.
In what follows we show that in the case when
S=\Pi
, cf. (
49
), the Hausdorff limit
\text{H-}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N})
exists and provide a detailed description of this limit. To this end we first characterize the limit of
\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N,\rm soft})
and then show that
\text{H-}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N,\rm soft})\subset\text{H-}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{1/N})
. A particular feature of this setting is that the geometry of the microstructure in the boundary layer remains congruent to itself (under appropriate scaling) for each
N\in\mathbf{N}
, thus rendering the boundary layer spectrum to be stable as
N\to\infty
.

Our technique can be easily adapted to a more general case when
S
is a polytope, provided that there exists a subsequence of
\varepsilon
such that the geometry of the soft component in the boundary layer in the vicinity of each vertex of the polytope is self-similar for all elements of the subsequence of
\varepsilon
. In this case, similarly to (
15
) below, the limiting spectrum of the soft component can be characterised as the union of spectra of the soft component in each vertex of the polytope.

We denote by

Proof.

\mathcal{K}(\eta_{L}u)-\eta_{L}\mathcal{K}u=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a_{L}(\xi)K(y,y+\xi)(\eta_{L}(y+\xi)-\eta_{L}(y))u(y+\xi)\,d\xi.

6
Norm resolvent convergence for the whole space setting

Recently a new abstract scheme for the norm resolvent estimates that applies to a wide range of problems with small parameter was developed in
[
16
]
.
Following
[
16
]
we use the scaled version of Gelfand transform
G_{\varepsilon}:L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(Y^{*}\times Y),Y^{*}:=[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
,

(G_{\varepsilon}f)(\theta,y):=\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}^{d}}f(\varepsilon(y+n)){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\varepsilon\theta\cdot(y+n)}.

Applying the transform to problem (
3
) and setting
\lambda=1
we obtain an equivalent family of problems for
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(\cdot):=(G_{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon})(\theta,\cdot)
: for a.e.
\theta\in Y^{*}
the function
u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y)
solves

\begin{split}\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\Lambda(y,\xi)+p(y,\xi)\right)&({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(\xi)-u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(y))\overline{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}v(\xi)-v(y))}d\xi dy\\
&+\int_{Y}u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(y)\overline{v(y)}dy=\int_{Y}f^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(y)\overline{v(y)}dy,\quad\forall v\in L^{2}_{\#}(Y),\end{split}

where
f^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}(\cdot):=(G_{\varepsilon}f)(\theta,\cdot)
. Adopting the notation from
[
16
]
we can write the above problem in the form

\varepsilon^{-2}a_{\theta}(u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta},v)+b_{\theta}(u^{\varepsilon}_{\theta},v)=(f,v),\quad\forall v\in L^{2}(Y),

(83)

where

a_{\theta}(u,v):=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\Lambda_{0}(y,\xi)({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}u(\xi)-u(y))\overline{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}v(\xi)-v(y))}d\xi dy,

and

b_{\theta}(u,v):=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)p(y,\xi)({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}u(\xi)-u(y))\overline{({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}v(\xi)-v(y))}d\xi dy\\
+\int_{Y}u(y)\overline{v(y)}dy.

(84)

(We drop for convenience the dependence of
f
on
\varepsilon
and
\theta
in (
83
).)

For every
\theta\in Y^{*}
denote by
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}
the bounded symmetric operator on

associated with problem (
83
):

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\theta}u(y)=2\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\Lambda(y,\xi)+p(y,\xi)\right)({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot(\xi-y)}u(\xi)-u(y))d\xi.

The approach presented in
[
16
]
works for a general class of problems of the form (
83
) and, upon verifying a number of abstract hypotheses, allows to approximate the original family of the operators by a more simple (homogenised) family in the norm-resolvent sense. When applying to a specific problem, like the one at hand, a lot of the argument can often be significantly simplified. In what follows we adapt some of the proofs from
[
16
]
to our setting while providing reference points to the original for reader’s convenience. We will verify hypotheses (H1)–(H4) from
[
16
]
, although in our setting the hypothesis (H4) is weaker, which manifests in the loss in the rate of the norm-resolvent convergence.

6.1
Basic properties of
a_{\theta}
and
b_{\theta}

We begin our analysis by verifying some basic properties of the sesquilinear forms in (
83
). Many bounds obtain in
[
16
]
are in terms of the family of the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\theta}
associated with the scalar product

(u,v)_{\theta}:=a_{\theta}(u,v)+b_{\theta}(u,v).

From the continuity of the integral operators involved we immediately conclude that these norms are equivalent to the standard
L^{2}
-norm uniformly in
\theta
:

\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\theta}\leq K\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(Y)}

for some
K>0
. Henceforth we will only use the
L^{2}
-norm in our bounds, rather than the whole family of norms
\|\cdot\|_{\theta}
, as in
[
16
]
.

The following bounds are trivial:

\begin{gathered}a_{\theta}(u,v)+b_{\theta}(u,v)\leq C\|u\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(Y)},\quad b_{\theta}[u]\geq\|u\|_{L^{2}(Y)}^{2},\quad\forall\theta\in Y^{*}.\end{gathered}

(85)

Further, the forms are Lipschitz continuous in
\theta
, i.e.

|a_{\theta_{1}}(u,v)-a_{\theta_{2}}(u,v)|\leq C|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\|u\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\quad\forall u,v\in{L^{2}(Y)},\quad\forall\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in Y^{*},

(86)

|b_{\theta_{1}}(u,v)-b_{\theta_{2}}(u,v)|\leq C|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|\|u\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\|v\|_{L^{2}(Y)}\quad\forall u,v\in{L^{2}(Y)},\quad\forall\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in Y^{*}.

(87)

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle a_{\theta_{1}}(u,v)-a_{\theta_{2}}(u,v)=\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\Lambda_{0}(y,\xi)\Big[({\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{2}\cdot(\xi-y)}-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta_{1}\cdot(\xi-y)})

\displaystyle u(\xi)\overline{v(y)}

\displaystyle+({\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta_{2}\cdot(\xi-y)}-{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\theta_{1}\cdot(\xi-y)})

\displaystyle u(y)\overline{v(\xi)}\Big]d\xi dy.

Then (
86
) follows by the application of the identity

{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\theta_{2}\cdot(\xi-y)}-{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}\theta_{1}\cdot(\xi-y)}=\pm{\rm i}(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1})\cdot(\xi-y)\int_{0}^{1}{\rm e}^{\pm{\rm i}(t\theta_{2}+(1-t)\theta_{1})\cdot(\xi-y)}dt

together with the first moment assumption on
a(\xi)
. The argument for (
87
) is analogous.

While
b_{\theta}
is coercive on
L^{2}_{\#}(Y)
, the form
a_{\theta}
is not and its kernel

V_{\theta}:=\{v\in{L^{2}_{\#}(Y)}\,|\,a_{\theta}[v]=0\},

plays an important role in this approach to homogenisation of high-contrast media. Notice that by the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality we have that

a_{\theta}(u,v)=a_{\theta}(v,u)=0,\quad\forall v\in V_{\theta},\forall u\in L^{2}(Y).

(88)

We also define the orthogonal complement of the kernel
V_{\theta}
with respect to the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\theta}
:

W_{\theta}:=\{w\in{L^{2}_{\#}(Y)}\,|(w,v)_{\theta}=0,\,\forall v\in V_{\theta}\}.

Proposition 6.1

(
[

, Hypothesis (H2)]
)

V_{\theta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{v\in{L^{2}_{\#}(Y)}\,|\,v=0\mbox{ in }Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}\},&\theta\neq 0,\\
\\
\mathbf{C}+\{v\in{L^{2}_{\#}(Y)}\,|\,v=0\mbox{ in }Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}\},&\theta=0.\end{array}\right.

(89)

Proof.

For

the expression

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi-y)\Lambda_{0}(y,\xi)|{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot\xi}u(\xi)-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot y}u(y)|^{2}d\xi

is periodic with respect to

a_{\theta}[u]\geq C\int_{(Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}\cap\square^{2k})^{2}\cap D_{r}}|{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot\xi}u(\xi)-{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot y}u(y)|^{2}d\xi dy,

where

is as in (
2

Denote
\phi(y):={\rm e}^{{\rm i}\theta\cdot y}u(y)
. Applying Lemma
A.5
with
\mathcal{M}=Y_{\rm stiff}^{\#}
and then the extension Lemma
A.7
, while taking into account the quasi-periodicity of

a_{\theta}[u]\geq C\int_{Y_{\rm stiff}\times Y_{\rm stiff}}|\phi(\xi)-\phi(y)|^{2}d\xi dy\geq C\int_{Y\times Y}|\tilde{\phi}(y+\xi)-\tilde{\phi}(y)|^{2}dyd\xi,

where
\tilde{\phi}
is the quasi-periodic extension of

into the soft component.
Rewriting this for the corresponding (periodic) extension

of

(i.e.

where

are the coefficients of the Fourier series for

on

. Then a direct calculation shows that for

(90)

When

a_{0}[u]\geq C\int_{Y_{\rm stiff}}\big|u-\fint_{Y_{\rm stiff}}u\big|^{2}.

(91)

This implies that the elements of

vanish on

for

Proof.

Assume first that

we rewrite (

Proposition 6.5

(
[

This readily implies (

Next we show the validity of (

is finite the assertion is trivial due to the Taylor expansion

(97)

where the

Remark 6.13
.

Since
\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft})=\cup_{\theta\in Y^{*}}\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta})
, understanding the structure of
\Sp(\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta})
helps us to characterize the spectrum of

\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{1}-\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{2},\quad\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta,1}-\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{\theta,2},

where for

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{1}z(x):=m(x)z(x),\quad\mathcal{A}_{\rm soft}^{2}z:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}K(x,y)z(y)\,dy,

\displaystyle m(x):=2\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)p(x,x+\xi)\,d\xi,\quad K(x,y):=2a(x-y)p(x,y)\,dy,

In what follows, for an integrable function

, the notation

stands for the mean value of

over
A
. A variant of the following lemma for the case

can be found in

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A,B\subset\mathbf{R}^{d}
have finite and positive Lebesgue measure. Then, for every
u\in L^{p}(A\cup B)
with
1\leq p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\int_{B}|u_{A}-u(x)|^{p}dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A|}\int_{A\times B}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy,

\displaystyle|u_{A}-u_{B}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|A||B|}\int_{A\times B}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy.

Proof.

Let
p>1
and denote by
p^{\prime}
the conjugate exponent of
p
. By Hölder’s inequality:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{B}|u_{A}-u(x)|^{p}\,dx=\frac{1}{|A|^{p}}\int_{B}\left|\int_{A}(u(y)-u(x))\,dy\right|^{p}\,dx

(135)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|A|^{p/p^{\prime}}}{|A|^{p}}\int_{B}\int_{A}|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}\,dy\,dx=\frac{1}{|A|}\int_{A\times B}|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}\,dx\,dy.

To prove (
A.1
) we proceed in a similar way utilising (
135
):

\displaystyle|u_{A}-u_{B}|^{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B|^{p}}\left|\int_{B}(u_{A}-u(x))\,dx\right|^{p}\leq\frac{|B|^{p/p^{\prime}}}{|B|^{p}}\int_{B}|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}|u_{A}-u(x)|^{p}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{|A||B|}\int_{A\times B}|u(y)-u(x)|^{p}\,dx\,dy.

The case
p=1
is straightforward.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
A,B
be subsets of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and let
|A\cap B|>0
. Then, there exists a linear continuous extension operator
\Phi:L^{p}(A\cap B)\to L^{p}(B)
such that,
for all
u\in L^{p}(A)
,

\Phi(u)=u\quad\mbox{in }\hskip 2.84544ptA\cap B,

\int_{B}|\Phi(u)|^{p}\,dx\leq c_{1}\int_{A\cap B}|u|^{p}\,dx,

\int_{B^{2}}\left|\Phi(u)(x)-\Phi(u)(y)\right|^{p}\,dx\,dy\leq c_{2}\int_{(A\cap B)^{2}}\left|u(x)-u(y)\right|^{p}\,dx\,dy,

(136)

where
c_{1}=1+\frac{|B\backslash A|}{|A\cap B|}
and
c_{2}=1+2\frac{|B\backslash A|}{|A\cap B|}
.

Proof.

We define

\Phi(u)(x):=\begin{cases}u(x),&x\in A\cap B,\\
u_{A\cap B},&x\in B\setminus A.\end{cases}

Using Jensen’s inequality we have:

\displaystyle\int_{B}|\Phi(u)|^{p}\,dx=\int_{A\cap B}|\Phi(u)|^{p}\,dx+\int_{B\backslash A}|\Phi(u)|^{p}\,dx=\int_{A\cap B}|u|^{p}\,dx+|B\backslash A||u_{A\cap B}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A\cap B}|u|^{p}\,dx+\frac{|B\backslash A|}{|A\cap B|}\int_{A\cap B}|u|^{p}\,dx=\left(1+\frac{|B\backslash A|}{|A\cap B|}\right)\int_{A\cap B}|u|^{p}\,dx.

Next we prove (
136
). Observing that

B^{2}=(A\cap B)^{2}\cup\left((A\cap B)\times(B\backslash A)\right)\cup\left((B\backslash A)\times(A\cap B)\right)\cup(B\backslash A)^{2},

we split the integral over
B^{2}
in four parts:

\int_{(A\cap B)^{2}}|\Phi(u)(x)-\Phi(u)(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy=\int_{(A\cap B)^{2}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy;

(137)

\int_{(B\backslash A)^{2}}|\Phi(u)(x)-\Phi(u)(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy=0;

(138)

Applying Lemma
A.1
we conclude:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\int_{(B\backslash A)\times(A\cap B)}|\Phi(u)(x)-\Phi(u)(y)|^{p}=\int_{(A\cap B)\times(B\backslash A)}|\Phi(u)(x)-\Phi(u)(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy

(139)

\displaystyle=|B\backslash A|\int_{A\cap B}|u(x)-u_{A\cap B}|^{p}\,dx\leq\frac{|B\backslash A|}{|A\cap B|}\int_{(A\cap B)^{2}}|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}\,dx\,dy.

Now (
136
) follows immediately from (
137
)-(
139
).

Appendix B
Compactness result

In this section we revisit the compactness theorem of
[
6
]
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\Omega
be an open set with Lipschitz boundary, and assume that for a bounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
family
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
the estimate

\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{r}}\left|\frac{u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}d\xi dx\leq M

(153)

is satisfied with some
k\geq r>0
. Then there exists a bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})
family
\{w_{\varepsilon}\}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}-w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{M}\varepsilon,\quad\|w_{\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})}\leq C(\sqrt{M}+\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}),

(154)

where
C
doesn’t depend on
\varepsilon
and
M
.
In particular, we have (up to a subsequence)

\displaystyle u_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to u

\displaystyle\mbox{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime}),

\displaystyle w_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\to u

\displaystyle\mbox{ strongly in }L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})\mbox{ and weakly in }H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime}),

for some
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. Here
\Omega^{\prime}
is and arbitrary open set satisfying
\Omega^{\prime}\Subset\Omega
, i.e.
\Omega^{\prime}
is bounded and compactly contained in
\Omega
.

Proof.

The following is a straightforward adaptation of the argument used in
[
10
, Theorem 2]
in a similar context.

Let
\varphi
be a radially symmetric mollifier,
i.e.

\varphi\geq 0
,
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{r})
,
\int_{B_{r}}\varphi\,dx=1
. We define

w_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{B_{r}}u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)\varphi(\xi)d\xi,

(155)

where
applying the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and utilising (
153
) yields (
154
):

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}-w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})}^{2}=\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}\left[\int_{B_{r}}\varphi(\xi)(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x))d\xi\right]^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}\left[\int_{B_{r}}\varphi^{2}(\xi)d\xi\int_{B_{r}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}d\xi\right]dx\leq CM\varepsilon^{2}.

This proves the first inequality in (
154
).
Next we show that
\nabla w_{\varepsilon}
is bounded. First we observe that since the mollifier is radially symmetric, its partial derivative
\partial_{i}\varphi(x)
is an odd function of
x_{i}
and is even with respect to all other variables (the second property is not essential, but will simplify the notation). Therefore we can write

\displaystyle\partial_{i}w_{\varepsilon}(x)=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B_{r}}\partial_{\xi_{i}}u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)\varphi(\xi)d\xi=-\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B_{r}}u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)\partial_{\xi_{i}}\varphi(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B_{r}\cap\{\xi_{i}>0\}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon\xi))\partial_{\xi_{i}}\varphi(\xi)d\xi.

Using Minkowski’s integral inequality in step two, applying the triangle inequality to the term
u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon\xi)
in step three, and then the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we obtain:

\displaystyle\|\partial_{i}w_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})}=\varepsilon^{-1}\left[\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}\left[\int_{B_{r}\cap\{\xi_{i}>0\}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon\xi))\partial_{\xi_{i}}\varphi(\xi)d\xi\right]^{2}dx\right]^{1/2}

(156)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{B_{r}\cap\{\xi_{i}>0\}}|\partial_{\xi_{i}}\varphi(\xi)|\left(\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon\xi))^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\||\nabla\varphi|\|\big._{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}\int_{B_{r}}\left(\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\||\nabla\varphi|\|\big._{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}|B_{r}|^{1/2}\left(\int_{\Omega_{k\varepsilon}}\int_{B_{r}}(u_{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon\xi)-u_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}d\xi dx\right)^{1/2}\leq CM.

As a consequence of (
155
), Young’s inequality and (
156
) we obtain the second inequality of (
154
).
The convergence properties are the direct consequences of the boundedness of
w_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\Omega_{k\varepsilon})
and (
154
).
∎

Appendix C
Two-scale convergence for convolution energies

In this section we provide technical statements on two-scale convergence that will be used in the derivation of the limit two-scale operator.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
a\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
\Gamma\in L^{\infty}_{\#}(Y\times Y),

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot):=\Gamma(\cdot/\varepsilon,\cdot/\varepsilon)
, and
\mu
be a bounded (signed) measure on
[0,1]
. Let
({u}_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
be a bounded sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that

u_{\varepsilon}\xrightharpoonup{2}u(x,y)\in L^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times Y)
.
Then

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Gamma_{\varepsilon}({x},{x+\varepsilon\xi})\int_{0}^{1}u_{\varepsilon}(x+t\varepsilon\xi)d\mu(t)\varphi(x)b({x}/\varepsilon)d\xi\,dx\\
\to\mathcal{I}:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Gamma(y,y+\xi)\int_{0}^{1}u(x,y+t\xi)d\mu(t)\varphi(x)b(y)d\xi\,dy\,dx,

(157)

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Gamma_{\varepsilon}({x},{x+\varepsilon\xi})\int_{0}^{1}u_{\varepsilon}(x+t\varepsilon\xi)d\mu(t)\varphi(x+\varepsilon\xi)b({x/\varepsilon+\xi})d\xi\,dx\\
\to\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\int_{Y}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\Gamma(y,y+\xi)\int_{0}^{1}u(x,y+t\xi)d\mu(t)\varphi(x)b(y+\xi)d\xi\,dy\,dx

(158)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
b\in C_{\#}(Y)
.

Proof.

Using a change of variables, we have

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{n}}u_{\varepsilon}(x)\Gamma_{\varepsilon}({x-t\varepsilon\xi},{x+(1-t)\varepsilon\xi})\varphi(x)b(x/\varepsilon-t\xi)dx\,d\xi\,d\mu(t)\\
+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}a(\xi)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u_{\varepsilon}(x)\Gamma_{\varepsilon}({x-t\varepsilon\xi},{x+(1-t)\varepsilon\xi})[\varphi(x-t\varepsilon\xi)-\varphi(x)]b(x/\varepsilon-t\xi)dx\,d\xi\,d\mu(t).

(159)

Note that for every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
t\in[0,1]
one has

\left|\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}u_{\varepsilon}(x)\Gamma_{\varepsilon}({x-t\varepsilon\xi},{x+(1-t)\varepsilon\xi})[\varphi(x-t\varepsilon\xi)-\varphi(x)]b(x/\varepsilon-t\xi)dx\right|^{2}\\
\leq 2\|\Gamma\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\|\varphi\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\|b\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}.

(160)

Furthermore, the quantity on the left-hand side of (
160
) vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
for every
\xi\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,
t\in[0,1]
. Therefore, by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, the second term on the right-hand side of (
