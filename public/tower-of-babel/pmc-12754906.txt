Prevalence and knowledge about cross-generational sexual relationships among undergraduates in a Nigerian tertiary institution
Authors: Love Bukola Ayamolowo, Praise Toluwani Ayowole, Damilola Jeremiah Ayowole, Bukola Abimbola Adesoji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCross-generational sexual relationships keep exposing more young persons to sexually transmitted infections, psychosocial and emotional trauma and various forms of abuse and have been linked with more cases of human immunodeficiency virus infection within the sub-Saharan Africa region; hence, the need to assess the knowledge and prevalence of cross-generational sexual relationships among undergraduates of a Nigerian institution.MethodologyThe study employed a descriptive cross-sectional survey design, utilising a four-stage sampling technique to select 390 undergraduates across the institution’s 13 faculties. Data were collected using tested, semi-structured and self-administered questionnaires. The data collected were analysed using IBM SPSS version 23. Chi-square tests were used to determine the associations at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 21.44 ± 2.62 years. The prevalence of cross-generational sexual relationships was 13.1%, 40.8% had poor knowledge, and 68.1% of the respondents practising cross-generational sexual relationships were unwilling to quit. Marital status (p = 0.008), family structure (p = 0.019), and level of study (p < 0.01) were significantly associated with the prevalence of cross-generational sexual relationships among the study population.ConclusionThis study reveals a significant prevalence and poor structure of cross-generational sexual relationships among undergraduate students, primarily driven by low-income family structure, relationship status and peer pressure. To address cross-generational sexual relationships, comprehensive sexual education, poverty alleviation initiatives, and mentorship programmes are recommended. These interventions can reduce prevalence, prevent harmful consequences, and promote healthier relationships among young individuals, especially in tertiary institutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25639-z.

Introduction

Cross-generational sexual relationships (CGSR), also referred to as age-disparate sex, are non-marital sexual relationships between heterosexual partners with an age range of at least 10 years [1, 2]. It occurs between older men and younger girls as well as between older women and younger boys [3–5]. The age-mixing pattern reported in these relationships was documented by the United Nations (UNAIDS) general population survey as young women who have had non-marital sex in the last 12 months with a man who is 10 or more years older than themselves [6]. Other words, such as age-disparate sex relationships and intergenerational sex relationships, have been used interchangeably to refer to cross-generational sexual relationships [1, 2].

The noticeable widespread of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection within the sub-Saharan Africa region has been linked to the prevalence of cross-generational sexual relationships in the region [7, 8], which are often transactional and have worse indices than those of their counterparts in other parts of the world [8]. This indicates that sex is exchanged for material support and other benefits [7]. In Nigeria, particularly in tertiary institutions, transactional sex is one of the factors that aid the spread of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [3, 9–11].

The 2013 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) revealed that 18% of never-married females aged 15-19years were in a relationship with a man who was at least 10 years older than they were [12]. In assessing the acceptance and morality of cross-generational sexual relationships, the intentions of the individuals involved played a defining role [13, 14]. Based on several studies, CGSR is perceived as mutually beneficial by the wider society [2, 15]. While young girl in transactional sex relationships states perceived socio-economic and psychosocial motivations and benefits as contributory factors towards their involvements in CGSR [2, 3, 5, 11, 16, 17]; the older men consider CGSR as a means of satisfying their sexual needs with the belief that the younger partners had reduced rate of harbouring sexually transmitted infections, especially HIV/AIDs [2, 15].

Cross-generational sexual relationships are fast becoming a norm and acceptable practice for economic survival among young adults and older people. Despite the expected awareness of the likely implications for the individuals involved in CGSR, the wider society and key stakeholders are not visibly frowning at the menace. It is alarming that CGSRs are practised by students of both secondary and tertiary institutions, exposing young girls and women with sugar daddies (wealthier, usually older men who provide financial or material support in exchange for sexual relationships) to physical, emotional and sexual abuse, unwanted pregnancy, single motherhood, psycho-emotional trauma and stigma [2, 18, 19]. Given the controversy surrounding CGSR, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and knowledge of CGSR among undergraduate students at a south-western Nigerian university.

Methods

Study area, design and population

This study is an institution-based cross-sectional study that used a quantitative approach and was conducted from February to March 2023 among undergraduate students of Obafemi Awolowo University (OAU), Ile-Ife, Nigeria. OAU is one of the first-generation universities in Nigeria, with thirteen faculties, two colleges, over 60 departments, and approximately 29,500 undergraduate students.

Target population and sampling

The target population comprised all undergraduate students from various faculties at the University. A four-stage sampling technique was employed to identify the study sample. In the first stage, four faculties were randomly selected from the 13 undergraduate faculties using a balloting method. In the second stage, three departments were selected from each of the chosen faculties through simple random sampling by balloting. In the third stage, proportionate sampling was used to determine the number of respondents to be selected from each department, based on the total sample size requested from the Head of Department of each department. At the final stage, students present in class on the day of data collection were selected via systematic random sampling. This approach ensured a representative and systematic selection of participants across different faculties and departments.

Using the formula N = Z2 pq/d2, where N = The desired sample size; Z = Standard normal deviation, usually set at 1.96, when the confidence limit is set at 95%, p = The prevalence of risky sexual behaviour among undergraduate students in Osun State, based on a previous study [20], was 65.8%; q = 1 – p; (i.e. 1–0.658 = 0.352) and d = Degree of accuracy desired, set at 0.05. The sample size initially calculated was approximately 346. To account for a 10% non-response rate, the sample size was adjusted to 384. It was finally rounded up to a final sample size of 390, ensuring the study remains statistically sound despite potential non-responses. Respondents were 30 students recruited from the 13 undergraduate faculties at the study setting, for a total of 390 selected students across the faculties.

Data collection instrument

Data was collected using a 23-item self-administered questionnaire, which was developed based on previous studies [9, 11, 14, 21], pretested among 20 undergraduate students of a private tertiary institution in Ile-Ife. Feedback from the pretest informed refinements to the wording and structure of items. Internal consistency of the knowledge scale was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.86, indicating good reliability. Participants were supervised by trained research assistants to ensure completion and minimise misunderstanding of the content of the questionnaire. Fifteen of the 23 items of the questionnaire specifically assessed knowledge, including understanding of definitions, associated risks, consequences, and preventive strategies of CGSR. Each correct response was scored as 1, and each incorrect response was scored as 0. Some items had multiple correct responses, bringing the maximum possible score to 23 points. The questionnaire was divided into three sections: socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge, and prevalence of cross-generational sexual relationships among respondents. Data collection was carried out during compulsory lecture periods, and all students present who had consented were supervised while completing the questionnaire, ensuring full participation among the 390 invited respondents. For every participant who declined, there was a direct replacement within the same department.

Data analysis

The data collected for the study included responses to a questionnaire administered to undergraduates in a tertiary institution, comprising demographic information (gender, faculty, etc.) and knowledge of CGSR. These data were then coded and analysed using the International Business Machines Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) version 23, using descriptive and inferential statistics. Bivariate associations were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square (χ²) tests. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The knowledge of CGSR was assessed using a series of questions in the questionnaire that evaluated the participants’ understanding of key concepts, definition and aspects of CGSR. The questions were scored using a dichotomous scale. For questions with multiple correct answers, each correct option was scored separately, with a ‘yes’ response receiving a score of 1 (correct) and a ‘no’ response receiving a score of 0 (incorrect). The total knowledge score was calculated by summing all question scores, with a possible range of 0 to the maximum. The knowledge mean score was determined by dividing the total score by the number of questions. The mean knowledge score was 14. Those who scored below 14 were regarded as having poor knowledge, those who scored between 14 and 15 as having fair knowledge, and those who scored above 15 as having good knowledge.

Further analysis revealed that 83.6% of respondents understood CGSR to constitute non-marital sexual affairs with older individuals, while 73.7% (n = 267) defined CGSR as a relationship between a student and someone at least 10 years older. The majority (83.1%) attributed the appeal of CGSR among students to financial gain and material gifts. Additionally, 64.1% of respondents believed that “big boys/girls” (well-to-do, rich, use sophisticated phones, cars, jewellery, etc.) share photos with club agents to facilitate connections with sugar daddies/mummies (rich older men/women who lavish gifts on young women/men in return for company or sexual favours).

More than half of the respondents (51.3%) reported that older students like themselves frequently attend clubs to meet wealthy older individuals for entertainment purposes. Furthermore, 67.7% (n = 264) viewed connections with multiple wealthy older individuals as a means to increase pocket money. Notably, only 28.4% of respondents recognised the risk of HIV/sexually transmitted infections as a potential consequence of CGSR, followed by unwanted pregnancy (25.2%). Discussion

Cross-generational sexual relationships are a significant issue in sub-Saharan Africa. The study revealed that the majority of respondents who engaged in CGSR were between 16 and 25 years old, aligning with previous studies [10, 22, 23], which found that younger women between 15 and 24 years old were predominantly involved in CGSR. This suggests that age is a critical factor, with younger individuals being more susceptible to engaging in CGSR due to various socio-economic pressures and developmental factors. The findings of this study also indicated that marital status, family structure, parent/guardian monthly income and monthly pocket money were significantly associated with the prevalence of CGSR. Specifically, being single, having a lower parent/guardian monthly income and coming from a single-parent family structure were linked to higher CGSR prevalence. These findings corroborated a previous study, which identified family support as a protective factor against transactional sex among adolescents and young adults [14]. This implies that socio-economic interventions aimed at improving family dynamics and economic conditions are essential.

Contrary to previous studies [11, 14], which found higher prevalence among female undergraduates, this study found no significant gender differences in CGSR prevalence. This suggests that both male and female undergraduates are equally affected by the factors driving CGSR, which warrants a gender-inclusive approach to addressing the issue. The study also revealed a notable discrepancy in the perceived social acceptability of CGSR. At the same time, 75.4% of the respondents rejected the notion that CGSR is socially acceptable. This diverged from the view of South African students who normalised and accepted CGSR as a standard practice [23]. This highlights the profound impact of cultural and regional differences on the social perception of CGSR, suggesting that intervention must be culturally sensitive and tailored to the specific social contexts of different regions. There was a complex interplay among age, socio-economic factors, family dynamics, and cultural perceptions that influenced CGSR among undergraduates. Comprehensive strategies addressing these multiple dimensions are crucial for effectively mitigating the prevalence and impact of CGSR.

The driving force behind the respondents’ disapproval appears to be rooted in religious beliefs, consistent with findings from another South African study where Christian participants viewed CGSR as sexually immoral [24, 25]. Moreover, the current study revealed a significant knowledge gap, with over a third (40.8%) of respondents demonstrating a poor understanding of CGSR, contrasting with findings from a study conducted in south-western Nigeria [26], where 60% of students displayed moderate knowledge. Thus, there is a need for improved, more targeted educational interventions to enhance understanding of CGSR among the respondents in the current study. Increased awareness and training programmes are needed to bridge the knowledge gap.

The study revealed that respondents engaged in CGSR not only to fulfil basic needs but also to acquire luxury items such as expensive clothing, jewellery, and phones. This finding aligns with previous research [19, 27] suggesting that CGSR often extends beyond basic necessities such as food and school books to include material goods and cash. This indicates that the motivation for engaging in CGSR is multifaceted, with students seeking both essential and non-essential items, reflecting a broader consumer culture among the respondents.

Furthermore, our study’s results confirmed that financial gain and material wealth were the primary motivations for undergraduates’ engagement in CGSR, similar to previous studies [16, 23] that identified poverty, family financial burdens, materialism, and the pursuit of a better lifestyle as driving factors. This suggests that economic pressures and the desire for an improved standard of living are significant influences on students’ participation in CGSR. The respondents in the current study cited peer pressure as a considerable influence, consistent with the results of similar studies [4, 13, 15, 19] that highlighted the role of social pressures in students’ involvement in CGSR. This underscores the impact of social dynamics and peer influence on the decision to engage in CGSR, indicating that interventions should also address these social factors.

The study further showed that a majority of the respondents were aware of the potential risks associated with CGSRs, including HIV, other STIs, and unintended pregnancies. Moreover, approximately one-fifth of the respondents perceived the risk of HIV and other STIs as a significant outcome of CGSR. This awareness and perception were corroborated by findings from a southwestern Nigerian study [26], where half of the respondents also acknowledged these potential consequences, with one-third emphasising the same risk. This suggests that while there is significant awareness of the health risks of CGSR, a substantial portion of the population still does not fully grasp their severity, underscoring the need for enhanced educational efforts.

However, a study conducted in Tanzania [1] showed that the respondents believed the benefits of CGSR outweighed the risk of infection, suggesting a potential disconnect between awareness of risks and perceived consequences. This highlights a critical gap between knowledge and behaviour: despite awareness of the dangers, the perceived immediate benefits of CGSR might drive continued engagement. It is crucial to address this disconnect in order to develop effective interventions that not only inform but also influence safer behavioural choices. Finally, the study indicated that only 13.1% of the sample had engaged in CGSRs. Notably, this prevalence of 13.1% is significantly lower than that reported in a similar study conducted in Nigerian universities (University of Ilorin and Nasarawa State University) [14], where it was more than twice as high. This difference suggests that factors such as regional, cultural, or institutional variations may influence the rate of CGSR engagement, underscoring the importance of context-specific strategies to address CGSR-related issues.

Limitation

This cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; all measures were self-reported and may be affected by recall or social desirability bias. The single-institution setting may limit generalizability to other contexts. Finally, although classroom administration optimised participation, it may still have introduced contextual influences on responses.

Conclusion

This study highlights that cross-generational sexual relationships occur among undergraduates in a Nigerian tertiary institution. A considerable proportion of respondents demonstrated limited knowledge, indicating persistent gaps in understanding of risks and consequences. These findings support implementing comprehensive sexual education, targeted socio-economic support, and mentorship programmes to address the drivers of cross-generational sexual relationships and mitigate their harms. Future research should focus on developing effective interventions and policies to combat CGSR.
