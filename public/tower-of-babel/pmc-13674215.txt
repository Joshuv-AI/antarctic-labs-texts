Interpreting antibody identification tests with large language models: Critical role of tabular data formatting
Authors: Jiwoo Lee, Yousun Chung, Dae‐Hyun Ko, Eun Young Song, Hyungsuk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibody identification testing is essential for preventing hemolytic transfusion reactions from unexpected red cell antibodies. Although automated interpretation is desirable to improve efficiency and consistency, conventional algorithmic approaches show limited accuracy, particularly in complex cases. Large language models (LLMs) offer a potential alternative; however, their performance is known to be sensitive to tabular data, which is a standard format for antigen profiles in antibody identification.Study Design and MethodsWe generated 100 simulated antibody identification cases comprising single alloantibodies, dual alloantibodies, autoantibodies, and unidentifiable patterns. Seven LLMs (GPT‐4o, GPT‐4.1, o3, GPT‐5, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro) were evaluated. Antigen profile tables were provided using five text‐based formats (Markdown, CSV, JSON, list‐based, and sentence‐based serialization) and as uploaded spreadsheet files. Each model interpreted all cases 10 times per format, and accuracy was assessed overall and by antibody category.ResultsReasoning‐capable LLMs significantly outperformed non‐reasoning models, achieving a median accuracy of 84.5%. Among text‐based formats, sentence‐based serialization yielded the highest accuracy, whereas CSV performed poorest. The maximum accuracy difference between table formats reached 40.5 percentage points. GPT‐5 and o3 performed comparably when antigen profiles were supplied as uploaded spreadsheet files, achieving accuracy similar to that of the sentence‐based format.ConclusionsReasoning LLMs demonstrated measurable baseline capability for interpreting antibody identification results when accompanied by appropriately structured antigen profile inputs. However, performance varied markedly with data representation and case complexity, and the use of LLMs in transfusion practice should remain limited to an assistive role under specialist supervision.Keywords: antibody identification, artificial intelligence, large language models, tabular data, unexpected antibody

INTRODUCTION

The antibody identification test is an essential component of transfusion practice, used to detect unexpected red cell antibodies and prevent hemolytic transfusion reactions or hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn. 1 Although interpretation is typically performed by trained transfusion specialists, test results can be difficult to utilize in settings where such expertise is unavailable. Even in laboratories with experienced specialists, tools that support preliminary interpretation may improve workflow, accelerate initial assessment, and promote consistency in routine practice. Automated or preliminary interpretation of antibody identification tests may therefore be potentially beneficial across diverse clinical settings, and several algorithmic approaches have been developed for this purpose. However, these tools have shown limited accuracy in complex cases, particularly those involving multiple alloantibodies. 2 Commercial software such as IH‐AbID (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, California, USA) is also available, although published validations have been limited to small datasets and have not assessed unidentifiable patterns. 3

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as an attractive alternative to traditional algorithmic approaches. Large language models (LLMs), a type of generative AI, are trained on vast textual datasets using deep learning techniques to generate natural language output in response to natural language input. 4 LLMs have been adopted across diverse fields due to their accessibility, user‐friendliness, low cost, and broad applicability. More recently, reasoning models that employ chain‐of‐thought reasoning prompting before generating responses have been introduced. 5 , 6 , 7 Although hallucinations remain a concern in some models, reasoning LLMs have shown substantial performance improvements in inference‐based tasks such as mathematics. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

LLMs have also been extensively explored in medicine, demonstrating considerable medical knowledge and accuracy despite being trained primarily on publicly available sources. 13 Several studies have evaluated GPT models on the United States Medical Licensing Examination, with one reporting an accuracy of approximately 90%. 14 , 15 Practical applications are also increasing, including explanation of radiologic findings to children and documentation of medical records from recorded conversations. 16 , 17 , 18 In transfusion medicine, one study demonstrated the potential of LLMs to manage clinical cases with complex transfusion requirements. 19 The authors suggested that LLMs may serve as reliable advisors for transfusion specialists, given their strengths in pattern recognition, esoteric knowledge, and interpretation of protocols and procedures—skills integral to blood bank operations. 19

A major challenge in applying LLMs to antibody identification is that antigen profiles are presented in tabular format. The optimal method for inputting tables into LLMs remains unclear, although tables are widely used for data organization. 20 Prior studies have mainly focused on approaches such as instruction tuning and continued pre‐training to improve LLM performance with tabular data. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 However, such approaches require advanced programming expertise, and many are limited to specific LLM architectures—an apparent contradiction to the accessibility considered one of LLMs' major advantages.

Multiple formats are available for serializing tables into text for LLM chat interfaces. Markdown tables use line breaks and vertical bars to distinguish rows and columns. 22 Comma‐separated values (CSV), which represent rows and columns using line breaks and commas, are among the most widely used open‐source formats. 27 Programming‐oriented serialization methods, such as JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) and Hypertext Markup Language (HTML), are also common in web documents and can be parsed by both humans and computers. 28 Although several studies have investigated LLM performance in understanding the content or structure of various table representations, 21 , 22 few have evaluated complex reasoning capabilities using these formats. In addition, although recent LLMs allow direct spreadsheet uploads, their performance with such inputs has not been systematically validated.

In this study, we evaluated multiple LLM versions for their ability to interpret antibody identification tests and compared their accuracy using antigen profile tables presented in various formats. We aimed to identify the table format most effectively processed by commercially available LLMs and to assess the feasibility of using LLMs to support antibody identification interpretation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case preparation

We created 100 test cases for antibody identification: 60 with a single identifiable alloantibody, 30 with dual alloantibodies, 4 with autoantibodies, and 6 with unidentifiable patterns. Each case included results from two antibody screening panels (including one auto‐control) and one antibody identification panel. For antibody screening, we used the Identisera Diana panel with two cells (Grifols, Sant Cugat del Vallès, Barcelona, Spain; Lot No. 24012) and the ID‐DiaPanel with three cells (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Inc.; Lot No. 45151.86). The antibody identification panel was ID‐DiaPanel/ID‐DiaPanel‐P (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Inc.; Lot No. 45161.96, 45171.96), with reaction patterns provided both with and without papain treatment. To minimize the risk of recognition from LLM pretraining data, panel lot numbers and manufacturer names were anonymized.

Autoantibody cases were defined as a positive auto‐control accompanied by a panreactive pattern on the antibody identification panel, in which all panel cells reacted both with and without papain treatment. Of the six unidentifiable cases, three were constructed by adding random reactions to a specific alloantibody profile to simulate the presence of an additional, unidentified antibody. The remaining three cases were designed with completely random reaction patterns with no specificity.

To reflect diverse clinical scenarios, reaction strengths ranged from weak to strong, incorporating dosage effects and papain sensitivity in accordance with the known characteristics of each alloantibody. In selected cases, reactivity was intentionally absent in heterozygous cells to simulate dosage effects. Complete case sets are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Formatting antigen profile tabular data

Antigen profile data from the test panels were converted into five text‐based formats. Three retained the original table structure: Markdown table, CSV, and JSON. For the other two, only the data content was serialized after removing the table structure. One represented antigen presence or absence in each sample as a list, whereas the other expressed the same information as complete natural language sentences; these were termed list‐based and sentence‐based serialization, respectively.

Prompt engineering

The query prompt included a role description, task objective, antigen profile data, and output format specifications. The prompt was designed to elicit a single, scorable interpretation for each case, avoiding the tendency of general‐purpose LLMs to refrain from providing definitive responses when explicitly asked for expert‐level medical judgment. To prevent this requirement from inducing hallucinations, the prompt also explicitly permitted the reporting of uncertainty when definitive identification was unsupported by the data.

After inputting this prompt, each case was presented to elicit a response. Six query prompts corresponding to the specific tabular data formats were constructed; they were identical except for the antigen profile data. Complete prompts are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Employed LLM versions

We evaluated seven LLMs from two providers: GPT‐4o, GPT‐4.1, o3, and GPT‐5 (OpenAI, San Francisco, California, USA), and Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google DeepMind, London, UK). Additional version details are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Models were categorized as reasoning or non‐reasoning. Reasoning models (o3, GPT‐5, and Gemini 2.5 Pro) were specialized for inference and could allocate tokens for “internal chain‐of‐thought” 5 or “thinking process.” 6 Non‐reasoning models (GPT‐4o, 4.1, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and Gemini 2.5 Flash) lacked reasoning‐specific functionality. Although Gemini 2.5 Flash includes a low level of “thinking process,” 6 this feature was disabled (thinkingBudget = 0) to isolate the effect of reasoning by enabling direct comparison with the contemporaneously developed Gemini 2.5 Pro.

We used the application programming interface (API) for each model and set temperature to 0 to maximize reproducibility. Exceptions were o3 and GPT‐5, for which temperature is not user‐modifiable. For these models, default settings (default temperature and medium reasoning effort) were used to align with their web versions. The maximum output token limit was set high enough to allow generation of complete antibody identification interpretations.

Experimental design

The study comprised three experiments evaluating factors influencing LLM performance in interpreting antibody identification test results. To ensure standardized testing, we developed a Python script to automate prompt delivery and response collection via API. If an API call returned an empty response (e.g., due to timeouts or internal errors), the case was automatically resubmitted using the identical prompt until a non‐empty response was obtained. For each model and condition, a single trial consisted of processing the complete set of 100 cases to obtain an overall accuracy rate. Within each trial, category‐specific accuracies were also calculated for single alloantibodies, dual alloantibodies, autoantibodies, and unidentifiable cases. Overall and category‐specific accuracy values from each repeated trial served as raw data for statistical analyses.

Performance comparison across LLMs

First, we compared the baseline accuracy of all seven LLMs to determine whether newer versions or reasoning capability were associated with improved performance. The sentence‐based format was used because natural language represents the primary training and input modality for LLMs. Ten trials were conducted for each LLM. Beyond overall accuracy, incorrect responses were examined to assess potential clinical risk. Misidentification—explicitly naming an incorrect alloantibody—may be more clinically concerning than responses stating that antibody specificity could not be determined, as the latter would typically prompt re‐evaluation.

Impact of table serialization formats

Second, we assessed the effect of data input format on interpretation accuracy using five formats: Markdown table, CSV, JSON, list‐based serialization, and sentence‐based serialization. Based on the findings from Experiment 1, the top‐performing reasoning models (o3, GPT‐5, and Gemini 2.5 Pro) were selected, and a non‐reasoning model (GPT‐4o) was included for comparison. Ten trials were conducted for each LLM‐format combination. To investigate whether table serialization formats influenced complex reasoning performance, we analyzed the accuracy gap between single and dual alloantibody cases. This approach was based on the assumption that identifying dual alloantibodies requires more complex reasoning, and that format‐dependent variation in this gap would indicate disproportionate impairment of intrinsic reasoning ability.

Efficacy of direct spreadsheet uploads

Finally, we evaluated LLM performance using raw spreadsheet files without text‐based serialization. Reasoning models supporting file uploads (o3 and GPT‐5) were used. Three Microsoft Excel (.xlsx) files containing antigen profile tables were uploaded, each labeled with an anonymized panel identifier. Because processing mechanisms for uploaded files may differ from those for text‐based inputs, model performance was also compared between API and web interfaces. For web versions, personalized memory function was disabled, and GPT‐5 was set to “Thinking mode,” as preliminary testing showed superior performance compared with “Auto” mode and accuracy comparable to the API version. Gemini 2.5 Pro was excluded because its API did not support direct spreadsheet uploads. 29 Five trials were conducted per condition.

Scoring criteria

Responses were scored as correct if the specific alloantibody was clearly identified, regardless of notation (e.g., anti‐Le(a), anti‐Lea, and anti‐Le^a^ were all accepted as correct for anti‐Lea). For dual alloantibody cases, responses were accepted only when both antibodies were correctly identified. An exception was made for cases involving anti‐C and anti‐D, in which anti‐G was accepted, as these specificities could not be fully distinguished using the provided data. Autoantibody cases were considered correct if any mention of an autoantibody was present. For unidentifiable cases, responses were scored as correct if they appropriately stated that antibody specificity could not be determined based on the available data (e.g., “unidentifiable with current information,” “inability to determine specificity,” or “antibody to a low‐prevalence antigen.”).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Microsoft Excel 365 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Because LLM accuracy could not be assumed to follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare reasoning and non‐reasoning models. The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to compare accuracy across multiple LLMs and table formats, followed by Dunn's post‐hoc tests with Bonferroni correction. All tests were two‐tailed, and p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations and use of AI

All cases were synthetic and did not involve human participants or patient‐derived data. Accordingly, the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Hospital confirmed that IRB review or ethics committee oversight was not required. The grammar and style of the English in the manuscript were improved with the assistance of ChatGPT 5.2 (Open AI) and Gemini 3 (Google DeepMind). The authors reviewed and edited the AI‐generated suggestions to ensure content accuracy and integrity and take full responsibility for the final manuscript.

RESULTS

Reasoning LLMs outperformed non‐reasoning models in antibody identification

Across LLM versions, reasoning models achieved significantly higher overall and category‐specific accuracy than non‐reasoning models (Table 1). Among reasoning models, GPT‐5 demonstrated the highest accuracy, significantly outperforming Gemini 2.5 Pro (median difference, 2 percentage points; p = .032) and o3 (median difference, 6.5 percentage points; p < .001) (Figure 1A). Within providers, OpenAI models (GPT‐4o, GPT‐4.1, o3, and GPT‐5) improved consistently over time. In contrast, Gemini 2.5 Flash with thinking disabled achieved lower accuracy than the older Gemini 2.0 Flash, indicating the intrinsic contribution of this mechanism. Reasoning ability also produced a large median accuracy difference of more than 65 percentage points between the concurrently released Gemini 2.5 Flash and 2.5 Pro (p < .001). In category‐stratified analyses (Figure 1B), GPT‐5 performed best for single alloantibody identification, with higher accuracy than o3 (p = .017) and Gemini 2.5 Pro (p < .001). For dual alloantibody cases, GPT‐5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro both outperformed o3 (p = .031 and p < .001, respectively), with no significant difference between them. For autoantibody cases, all models except Gemini 2.5 Flash achieved 100% accuracy.

Analysis of incorrect answer showed that the proportion of definite misidentifications among errors did not decrease with higher model accuracy (Supplementary Table 1) or over time (o3 vs. GPT‐5). In antibody‐specific analysis, all reasoning models struggled to distinguish single from dual antibodies sharing overlapping reaction patterns, such as anti‐c, anti‐e, and their dual combinations anti‐E + anti‐c, anti‐C + anti‐e (Supplementary Table 2). In unidentifiable cases, reasoning models generally identified specific co‐existing alloantibodies but often overlooked the unidentifiable components. Purely random unidentifiable patterns showed no consistent error trends. Non‐reasoning models performed relatively better for high‐prevalence antibodies such as anti‐E, for which ID‐panel cell configurations are often standardized. For other specificities, responses were inconsistent and biased toward common antibodies (e.g., anti‐E and anti‐C).

LLMs showed limitations in tabular data interpretation beyond natural language

LLM performance differed significantly across tabular data formats for all models (p < .001, Figure 2). Across reasoning models, a broadly consistent format‐dependent pattern was observed, although statistical significance varied by model. Sentence‐based serialization yielded the highest accuracy, followed by JSON, whereas CSV performed poorest. Excluding CSV, formats associated with significant performance drops differed: o3 underperformed with Markdown (p = .003), whereas Gemini 2.5 Pro struggled with list‐based serialization (p = .008). For GPT‐5, all formats except CSV showed comparable performance (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 3). In GPT‐4o, sentence‐based format significantly outperformed other representations (Figure 2, Supplementary Table 3), but this had limited practical value given its extremely low absolute accuracy. Category‐specific trends for single and dual alloantibody cases mirrored the overall accuracy ranking (Supplementary Figure 1). All reasoning models correctly identified autoantibody cases across nearly all formats, except for one o3 error in CSV. In contrast, accuracy for unidentifiable cases remained consistently low regardless of format. Finally, table format was not significantly associated with the accuracy gap between single and dual alloantibody cases, suggesting that format does not fundamentally alter intrinsic reasoning ability (Figure 3).

OpenAI reasoning LLMs interpreted antibody identification from uploaded spreadsheets

For spreadsheet‐based interpretations, o3 and GPT‐5 achieved accuracies comparable to the sentence‐based format across most answer types (Table 2). The only exception was unidentifiable cases, where GPT‐5 performed better with sentence‐based serialization than with spreadsheets. However, its practical significance remains uncertain because of o3's inconsistent results and generally low accuracy in this category. Comparing API and web versions using uploaded spreadsheets showed no significant differences in overall accuracy or across most categories (Table 2). Only o3's performance for dual alloantibody cases differed significantly (p = .032), with the web version outperforming the API.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate that reasoning LLMs can perform antibody identification with accuracy varying by case complexity, but their current performance does not yet meet the standard required for unsupervised clinical use. While non‐reasoning models were nearly unusable, reasoning models demonstrated measurable capability, with GPT‐5 achieving the highest median accuracy (86.5%). In category‐stratified analyses, performance varied with task complexity: GPT‐5 excelled in single alloantibody cases, whereas Gemini 2.5 Pro handled dual alloantibody cases better. Even so, all models had difficulty distinguishing single from dual antibodies with overlapping reaction patterns, suggesting reliance mainly on matching individual panel‐cell positivity. Because practical antibody identification also requires consideration of reaction strength and panel cell zygosity, current LLMs cannot yet fully replicate the reasoning involved in this task.

Most models correctly identified autoantibodies, suggesting that panreactive reaction patterns not requiring detailed interpretation of individual panel‐cell antigen profiles are generally easier to process. However, this finding should not be generalized, as the case set did not include low‐titer autoantibodies with incomplete panreactivity or other panreactive scenarios, such as antibodies to high‐prevalence antigens or autoantibodies with underlying alloantibody. In contrast, even reasoning models struggled with “unidentifiable” cases, and altering tabular data formats did not mitigate hallucinations. Misreporting an unidentifiable pattern as a specific antibody conceals the uncertainty that should have triggered specialist review. Because accurate recognition of such cases and appropriate further testing are critical in transfusion practice, LLMs should remain assistive tools under specialist supervision.

Tabular data format significantly influenced LLM accuracy, with a maximum median difference of 40.5 percentage points. CSV was surprisingly poorly handled despite its widespread use. Although Markdown tables may appear less robust, recent reasoning models demonstrated adequate capability to interpret them. Because Markdown is commonly generated when LLMs convert table images or spreadsheets into text, future models may directly process such data with high accuracy. JSON‐based prompts achieved the second‐highest accuracy after the sentence‐based format, likely because LLMs are extensively trained on open‐source data structured in JSON. JSON thus appears optimal for providing tabular data unless the table structure is completely deconstructed. HTML tables may offer similar advantages, but this requires further validation. The accuracy discrepancy between sentence‐based and list‐based formats, despite their structural similarity, indicates that natural language sentence structure itself influences performance. Because the query cases were described in a list‐like manner, describing antigen profiles in fully articulated natural language may have yielded even higher accuracy.

Using uploaded spreadsheets, o3 and GPT‐5 interpreted antibody identification results with accuracy comparable to the sentence‐based format. The web versions also showed accuracy equivalent to their API counterparts, suggesting that direct spreadsheet uploads may be preferable to text‐based serialization for current models. However, web version accuracy varied considerably under less strictly controlled conditions. We observed that o3 web accuracy declined by approximately 15% during our pilot study, particularly when multiple chat windows were used simultaneously or multiple cases were processed within a single session. Clinical implementation of web‐based LLMs thus requires further optimization and validation.

Our study has several limitations. First, simulated cases may not fully reflect real‐world conditions, although we attempted to include diverse reaction patterns and strengths. While clinical practice may involve more complex cases, additional testing applied in routine workflow, such as antigen typing or extended panel analysis, could also provide evidence supporting LLM‐based interpretation. Translation to clinical practice will require validation with real patient data and diverse test results. Second, prompt design is itself a methodological variable, and the prompt used in this study may not have fully elicited the LLMs' capabilities. Further prompt optimization could yield different performance profiles. Third, temperature settings were not uniform across models. Although temperature control has been considered essential for reproducibility in previous studies using LLMs, recent OpenAI reasoning models no longer allow such adjustment. While we observed no significant temperature‐related differences, this limitation may introduce variability. Fourth, because this study was conducted over a short period, performance stability under conditions such as server instability or heavy user traffic could not be assessed. Fifth, this study did not compare commercially available antibody identification software. Published validations of such software have not addressed unidentifiable patterns, and a meaningful comparison would require evaluating both approaches on a common case set covering a wide range of categories as assessed here. 2 , 3 Sixth, spreadsheet‐based experiments included models from only one company, requiring validation using models from other developers. Finally, although antibody identification data are highly anonymized and thus relatively free from ethical concerns, uploading real patient results to LLMs may raise ethical and privacy concerns. Therefore, this should be carefully addressed before clinical use.

In conclusion, this study systematically evaluated general‐purpose LLMs in antibody identification, without pre‐training or coding‐based prompt engineering. Reasoning LLMs demonstrated measurable baseline capability, but their performance does not yet meet the standard required for independent clinical use. Nevertheless, the marked dependence of performance on data input format highlights important methodological considerations for future LLM applications in antibody identification. These findings characterize the capabilities and limitations of general‐purpose LLMs in transfusion medicine and provide a framework for designing future validation studies. Until LLMs are validated against real patient data and a broader range of clinical scenarios, their use in transfusion practice should remain limited to an assistive role under specialist supervision.
