Controlled flight of high-thrust ultralight ion-propelled microrobot with integrated sensing
Authors: Qiannan Tao, Yang Gu, Xinshuai Wang, Zhi Chen, Fei Lv, Fu Xu, Tahir Bashir, Yu Zheng, Yunqi Cao, Xianfa Cai, Khadga Thakuri, Bin Han, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ion wind technology holds potential for the miniaturization of flying microrobots, generating thrust without mechanical moving parts, enabling simplified and light designs free from high-frequency actuators. Nevertheless, two fundamental challenges have limited its practical implementation: insufficient payload capacity and inadequate controllability, particularly in multi-degrees-of-freedom flight. Here, we demonstrated an inertial measurement unit-based closed-loop controlled flight of a light (36.7 mg) ion-propelled microrobot, achieving a thrust-to-weight ratio of 5:1 and 1-h long-endurance tethered hovering without mechanical actuators. Experimental validation indicates that the control strategy enhances stability, with a decrease of 83.11% and 89.21% in the root mean square error of pitch and roll angle, respectively. Leveraging origami-inspired design and cost-effective metal-polymer composites, our manufacturing approach enables the rapid assembly of complex microrobots at a disposable cost, overcoming a key barrier to practical swarm deployment. The microrobot’s high load capability allows it to carry a high-fidelity image sensor and a fiber Bragg grating sensor while maintaining sufficient maneuverability to complete predefined tasks such as environmental surveillance and material identification. This work introduces an approach to autonomous microrobotic swarm flight, suggesting potential applications in confined-space surveillance, disaster rescue, and hazardous environment exploration.

Introduction

In the challenging landscape of narrow, restricted access, or uncertain environments, flying microrobots provide advantages for exploration, rescue, inspection, and a spectrum of other critical tasks1–3. These microrobots may access previously inaccessible areas due to their aerial maneuverability. Driven by progress in materials and manufacturing, the field of microrobots has witnessed significant attention and ongoing breakthroughs in the research and development of flying microrobots4–7. Based on their propulsion mechanisms, flying microrobots are generally classified into three main types: propeller-driven fixed-wing8,9, rotorcraft10–13, and flapping-wing14–17. Unlike conventional propulsion systems, ion-propelled robots leverage ion wind for flight, representing an addition to the field of flying microrobots18,19. Ion wind propulsion has been confirmed for use in externally powered microrobots18, and untethered flight with ion wind has also been achieved in larger aircraft equipped with fully integrated onboard power systems19. Ion wind itself, as a natural propulsion mechanism, provides a streamlined and biomimetic solution for microrobot design. The straightforward generation of precisely controlled airflow via ion wind technology facilitates the circumvention of traditional propulsion limitations, introducing emerging pathways for creating lightweight, maneuverable, versatile systems20,21.

Corona discharge is the primary mechanism for ion-propelled flying microrobots, where strong electric fields ionize gas molecules, generating ion wind that subsequently produces thrust22. The use of ion wind in flying microrobots provides distinct advantages, as it negates the requirement for precision-engineered rotating components or flapping wings, thereby reducing system complexity, size, and weight23–25. This simplification enhances reliability and durability while also considerably reducing noise levels due to the absence of high-frequency motion. Ion-propelled flying microrobots are commonly centimeter-scale devices with masses of 30–50 mg and a thrust-to-weight ratio of around 326. Due to their limited payload capacity, these robots struggle to carry even lightweight payloads such as Microelectromechanical Systems (MEMS) devices, which restricts their practical applications. Enhancing the thrust-to-weight ratio has emerged as a main challenge for ion-propelled flying microrobots. Addressing this challenge demands a two-pronged optimization strategy: enhancing thrust while simultaneously reducing mass. On the one hand, thrust enhancement is determined by factors such as electrode configuration27–29, electrode spacing, and applied voltage30–32. Although experiments have confirmed that thrust increases with voltage, further increases in voltage face technical and safety barriers. Therefore, optimizing structure configuration, such as needle-wire, needle-ring, and needle-mesh, represents a more viable approach for thrust improvement33–35. On the other hand, in the pursuit of mass reduction, the use of light material types, as opposed to conventional materials like SOI silicon layers26,36 or metal foil37, holds the key to minimizing weight without compromising structural integrity. By selecting different materials and optimizing structural design, our recent study reported an ion wind thruster weighing 16 mg that achieved takeoff with a thrust-to-weight ratio of 5.5:138. Another persistent challenge for ion-propelled flying microrobots lies in achieving precise controllability. Three main challenges impede the controllability of this flying microrobot. The first challenge is the nonlinear relationship between voltage and thrust, which is further complicated by its susceptibility to disturbances such as temperature, humidity, and pressure. The second challenge stems from the microrobot’s low inertia and high sensitivity. Its low inertia makes it highly sensitive to thrust fluctuations, where minor thrust variations can lead to serious attitude deviation. Manufacturing and assembly present the third challenge for ion-propelled flying microrobots, as current methods involve costly materials like silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafers, high operational expenses for cleanroom facilities and advanced equipment, and time-consuming manual assembly of fragile microcomponents. The lack of simplified and standardized manufacturing and testing protocols amplifies process variations, leading to mismatches between designed and actual inertial properties in microrobots that ultimately compromise control stability. Current research strategies to address these challenges focus primarily on enhancing passive stability through structural design39,40 or developing intelligent algorithms for stabilization without actual flight validation41–43. However, robust closed-loop control implementations of ion-propelled flying microrobots—particularly in real-world experimental validations—remain underdeveloped. Although we have experimentally verified the feasibility of using multiple distributed ion thrusters for attitude control44, to date, no fully controllable and stable systems with inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based closed-loop control have been demonstrated, thus hindering their practical deployment compared to other types of flying microrobots45–47.

This paper proposes a light, low-cost ion-propelled microrobot with a high thrust-to-weight ratio for IMU-based closed-loop controlled flight. For its construction, the developed flying microrobot utilizes metal-coated polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film, capitalizing on its combination of metal conductivity, polymer flexibility, lightweight, and low cost to achieve further weight reduction, easy assembly, robustness, and cost-effectiveness. Through structural optimization design guided by multiphysics simulations, the microrobot achieves dimensions of 37.7 mm × 37.7 mm and a mass of 36.7 mg. Notably, the proposed flying microrobot features a low material cost (under $0.10 USD) and rapid assembly (less than 30 min), integrating onboard sensors and a circuit board. With four optimized ion wind thrusters arranged in a cross pattern, the microrobot achieves a thrust-to-weight ratio of 5:1, enabling it to carry various lightweight payloads, such as the IMU sensor, the image sensor, and the fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensor. Controlled flight is achieved via a closed-loop attitude-control method, with functionality validated through square-path and triangle-path trajectory tracking. Experimental results also demonstrate that the microrobot maintains high maneuverability when carrying an image sensor and an FBG sensor, enabling image recording and material detection tasks, respectively.

The microrobot achieves propulsion through electrohydrodynamic (EHD) ion wind generation, where asymmetric electrode configurations create macroscopic airflow via corona discharge. A unit of the ion wind thruster, located at one edge of the square configuration, comprises a group of emitters and collectors that generate thrust through ion wind. The working process of the thruster is illustrated in Fig. 1c. When a high voltage (HV) difference is applied between the emitter and collector electrode, it induces a corona discharge in the air molecules between them, generating a large number of excited ions near the sharp emitters. Under the influence of the electric field between the electrodes, these ions accelerate and move towards the collectors. In transit towards the collectors, the accelerated ions collide with surrounding neutral air molecules, transferring momentum and creating an ion wind that generates thrust.

As demonstrated by the fundamental principles of ion wind propulsion, the key advantage of ion-propelled microrobots stems from their inherently simplified architecture, which eliminates the need for complex mechanical components. Rotorcraft microrobots require heavy electromagnetic motors to control the rotors, making it challenging to reduce their overall weight to sub-gram levels. Although flapping-wing microrobots are amenable to miniaturization, they still rely on complex mechanical actuators and transmission mechanisms to drive high-frequency flapping motions, which may limit their thrust-to-weight ratios. In contrast, the ion-propelled flying microrobot developed in this work utilizes PET polymer films to achieve a thrust-to-weight ratio of 5:1, representing an improvement over existing flying microrobot platforms2,11,12,14–17,23–26,36,43,46,48–54 in this regard (Fig. 1d). Details of the robots are provided in Supplementary Table 8. Furthermore, the integration of an IMU enables the robot to achieve attitude control and subsequently realize trajectory tracking, surpassing the limitations of previous ion-propelled flying microrobots that were capable only of basic takeoff and landing, or solely theoretical control analysis36. With its high thrust-to-weight ratio, the flying microrobot demonstrates sufficient payload capacity, enabling integration of diverse functional modules, including image sensors and FBG sensors. The ability to adapt to specific mission requirements makes this microrobot particularly suitable for applications spanning infrastructure inspection, terrain mapping to parcel delivery, disaster rescue in harsh environments, and even deep-space exploration missions (Fig. 1e).

Characterization and analysis of ion thrusters’ performance

A basic actuating unit for the developed microrobot, comprising two needle emitter structures and seven collector structures on each side of its square structure, is investigated. To describe the distribution of the ion wind, a multiphysics coupling computational method is employed, which couples the electrostatic field, laminar flow field, and rarefied mass transfer field. The three-dimensional (3D) computational model, as shown in Fig. 2a, is constructed using Comsol Multiphysics to quantitatively calculate the velocity field of the ion wind generated by the developed ion-propelled flying microrobot. A voltage difference applied across the emitter and collector electrodes induces the corona discharge, generating a substantial amount of ionized particles. The rarefied mass transfer field is introduced to calculate the motion of activated ions, while the flow field captures the macroscopic air movement. The ion-neutral molecule interaction is simulated by applying a Coulomb force-derived volume force to the air molecules. Figure 2a demonstrates that the calculated airflow accelerates between the emitters and collectors, maintaining stable flow directionality, and further illustrates that varying the applied voltage (5 kV, 5.5 kV, and 6 kV) on the ion thrusters results in different airflow velocity profiles. The simulation results show that when the voltage difference between the emitter and collector rises to 6 kV, the velocity of the ion wind reaches the peak (1.71 m/s). The 3D simulation results further indicate that applying varying voltage differences to individual thrusters generated an uneven wind speed distribution, leading to a variation in thrust. This thrust variation can effectively be converted into a control torque, providing a theoretical basis for the attitude control of the flying microrobot. To experimentally validate the simulation results, we tested the ion-propelled flying microrobot in a wind tunnel (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Movie 1). The smoke stream can pass through the microrobot when the ion wind is off. The ion wind generation was controllably activated and terminated by modulating the voltage difference between the emitters and collectors. As the primary figure of merit for flying microrobot performance, the thrust-to-weight ratio can be maximized through coupled optimization of emitter geometry and collector architecture. The optimization to improve the velocity of ion wind, increase thrust, and reduce structural mass is conducted through both simulations and experiments. Although increasing the distance dg is an effective way to enhance lift, we chose not to increase the structure’s height due to the negative impact on flight stability. A taller structure adversely affects stability by elongating the frame and raising the center of gravity. Then the simulation-based optimization employs two-dimensional (2D) models evaluating two alternative configurations (Fig. 2c). Both models feature needle-shaped emitters and rectangular cross-section collectors. The distance dg is set to 4 mm. While style A employs a single emitter with two symmetric collectors, style B adds two outer collectors beyond the original pair. Figure 2d shows the velocity distribution corresponding to different spacings d1 between the emitter and the nearest collectors under a 5.5 kV voltage difference. Results show that the maximum wind speed on the collector plane occurred on the centerline of the emitters-collectors unit, both for style A and style B, with a single emitter. Simulation results indicate that when the spacing of collectors is 1.5 mm, the ion wind velocity on the collector plane reaches 1.49 m/s. As the spacing increases, the maximum ion wind velocity on the collector plane decreases. Further investigation reveals that increasing the number of collectors alters the wind speed distribution. As shown in the wind speed profile for style B in Fig. 2d, when extra collectors are added to both sides of a pair of collectors, the ion wind flow field shows a distinct convergence, resulting in a higher wind speed, which reaches 1.57 m/s at maximum. By comprehensively considering the enhancement of wind speed and the reduction in mass, the combination of d1 = 1.5 mm and d2 = 3.5 mm is determined as the optimal design solution. Furthermore, the simulation of the emitters’ numbers shows that the addition of extra emitters does not result in an increase in the maximum central wind speed with the same design of the collector structure, as illustrated in Fig. 2e. The explanation is as follows. When a single emitter is arranged symmetrically along the axis, the system can effectively absorb the ions around and generate a stable corona discharge. However, when multiple emitters are arranged symmetrically, the number of ionized air molecules is nearly the same as in the case of a single emitter. This observation can be attributed to the fact that when a single emitter is aligned along the thruster’s central axis, it effectively achieves maximum local ionization density by activating nearly all available air molecules within its effective range, while maintaining stable corona discharge. However, multiple emitter arrangements demonstrate no net gain in total ionized molecules compared to the single-emitter case. Each ion wind thruster demonstrates well-defined critical corona discharge voltages, which determine the onset of stable ionization and thrust generation. Upon reaching the critical voltage, the population of ionizable molecules approaches saturation. Multiple emitters not only fail to increase ion production beyond this saturation limit but also degrade the thrust-to-weight ratio due to an increase in mass. Consequently, a single emitter configuration delivers optimal system efficiency.

The final configuration uses style B with a single row of emitter (d1 = 1.5 mm, d2 = 3.5 mm) as one unit. To enhance thrust, the microrobot’s thruster incorporates two emitter-collector units arranged side by side. As shown in Fig. 2a, four thrusters form the ion-propelled microrobot. Experimental testing was conducted to determine the impacts of the spacing between the collectors on the ion wind velocity and thrust under actual working conditions. An anemometer was used to measure the ion wind velocity generated by the ion wind thruster. Furthermore, a precision balance was also used to measure the lifting force produced by the ion wind thrust, indicated by a reduction in weight. The thruster, mounted on a raised platform connected to a precision balance (resolution: 0.1 mg), was isolated within a Faraday cage-like enclosure. This electrostatic shielding prevented ionic wind interference and stray charges from compromising the balance’s internal electronics and measurement accuracy. Varying the distance d1 between the two inner collectors allowed us to obtain ion wind velocity and thrust for various thruster designs. According to the experimental data presented in Fig. 2f, g, a distance d1 of 1.5 mm yielded the highest measured ion wind velocity (2.0 m/s) and thrust (0.28 mN) across the tested designs. The configurations of style B were also tested. The data are shown in Supplementary Fig. 13. The experimental results are in agreement with the simulation analysis that a compact structure with extra collectors yielded higher wind velocity and thrust. Based on the findings, the thruster’s final structural parameters are set as d1 = 1.5 mm and d2 = 3.5 mm to achieve optimal performance. The optimal thruster generates a thrust of 0.31 mN, with a size of 12 mm × 12 mm × 4 mm, resulting in a thrust density of 538 N/m³, which is higher than that of the Airbus A320 (31.5 N/m³) and wire-wire ion wind thruster (110 N/m³)55. It should be noted that this data was obtained under quasi-static loading conditions. Increasing the voltage ramp rate is beneficial for enhancing the thrust, and further details are provided in Supplementary information.

Fabrication material impacts the performance of flying microrobots, requiring optimized mechanical properties to achieve both flight capability and structural integrity. Ideal materials must combine high stiffness, high thrust-to-weight ratios, and minimal density to satisfy the demands of high reliability and thrust-to-weight ratio. Furthermore, high elasticity and flexural strength are essential to resist thrust-induced bending moments while maintaining aerodynamic stability during aggressive maneuvers. Departing from conventional titanium or silicon-based microrobot fabrication, we propose a synergistic integration of metallic conductivity and polymeric advantages through metallized PET films. This hybrid material system combines the electrical functionality of thin metal coatings with PET’s intrinsic high elasticity, high stiffness, ultralight weight, and low-cost processability, etc. The resulting microrobots achieve over 60% mass reduction compared to titanium counterparts while exhibiting better robust structural integrity, simplified assembly via laser-cuttable layers, and scalable manufacturing. Three emitter variants were fabricated using distinct materials: titanium (metallic), silicon (inorganic non-metallic), and metal-coated PET (hybrid polymer). Comparative destructive testing was subsequently conducted to evaluate their mechanical performance under loading conditions, with complete experimental results presented in Fig. 3b and Supplementary Movie 2. To compare their mechanical properties, three different emitter materials were tested by applying a force with a 5 g weight. The silicon emitter fractured immediately upon loading. While the metal film emitter did not fracture, it underwent substantial permanent plastic deformation. In contrast, the metallized PET film demonstrated better performance, maintaining its linear elasticity and recoverability even under large deformations, which confirms its high strength, robustness, and reliability. Therefore, the 50-µm-thick metallized PET film was utilized as the optimal fabrication material, offering a combination of ultralight properties, low-cost manufacturability, and high thrust-to-weight performance. Additionally, to further enhance the bending resistance of the frame of the microrobot, diagonal supports are added between the thrusters (as shown in Fig. 3a); these diagonal supports increase the structural moment of inertia, thereby enhancing the bending resistance of the microrobot.

This study further investigates the performance of differentially metallized emitters, comparing aluminum and tungsten thin-film deposited via magnetron sputtering onto PET substrates. Both metals were uniformly coated to a thickness of 100 ± 10 nm, with precise control of deposition parameters (gas pressure, sputter power, time, etc). Corona discharge experiments were conducted under a voltage of 4.5 kV. Electrical characterization revealed high stability in tungsten-coated emitters, as evidenced by time-dependent current measurements (Fig. 3c). While the aluminum electrode suffered a 58% current reduction after 600 s of continuous corona discharge at 4.5 kV, the tungsten counterpart demonstrated high stability with only 8% current decay under identical conditions. Images of the emitter tips at different times during the corona discharge are shown in Fig. 3d. Comparative analysis revealed evident differences in electrode durability: the aluminum-coated emitter exhibited extensive surface erosion (∼90% area affected) after 600 s of operation, while the tungsten counterpart maintained structural integrity with almost imperceptible surface degradation under identical conditions. These results demonstrate that tungsten-coated PET emitters exhibit exceptional erosion resistance during ion wind generation. This performance stems from the intrinsic advantages of the emitter’s tungsten coating—including a high melting point (3422 °C), stable work function (4.5 eV), and low sputter yield ( < 0.1 atoms/ion)—which collectively mitigate ion bombardment damage and maintain consistent electron emission under high electric fields. All these factors will enhance the stability of the flying microrobot, thereby reducing the difficulty of control. Therefore, in this study, all conductive parts of the flying microrobot are sputtered using tungsten. The collective properties of tungsten-coated PET enhance flight stability by minimizing performance degradation, which in turn reduces control complexity. As a result, all conductive components in the microrobot were fabricated using tungsten-coated PET to fully leverage these benefits.

The manufacturing process of the microrobot, as illustrated in Fig. 3e, primarily involves constructing it on a 50-μm-thick PET film substrate. The emitters, collectors, and connectors are precisely cut from the PET film using an ultraviolet (UV) laser cutting system. Subsequently, a 100-nm-thick tungsten layer is sputter-deposited onto the emitters and collectors using a magnetron sputtering system. For assembly, an interlocking structure is employed. Firstly, the connector is inserted vertically into the positioning hole in the collector; the emitter is then placed onto the connector. The precise alignment is ensured by a snap-fit structure. A small amount of ultraviolet-curing adhesive is applied to reinforce the connections. Following manufacturing, a payload flight test was conducted on the microrobot, which verified that its thrust-to-weight ratio reached five and that it could maintain stable flight for over 15 min, as documented in Supplementary Movies 3 and 4. Without payload, a continuous tethered flight duration of 1 h was achieved, as shown in Supplementary Movie 5. In contrast, up-to-date sub-gram aerial microrobots typically have short lifespans, with hovering durations limited to under 10 s53.

Notably, this work introduces an origami-inspired manufacturing technique that monolithically integrates connectors, emitters, and collectors into a single foldable PET-tungsten composite structure (Fig. 3e), enabling rapid assembly of ion wind-propelled microrobots with fewer discrete components and high precision alignment through self-folding joints. During the magnetron sputtering process, the area of the connector is hard masked with polyimide film, ensuring that only the emitters and collectors are subjected to sputtering. Upon completion of sputtering, the emitters are folded inward, and the connectors are inserted into the small hole of the collectors to complete the assembly. The snap-fit structure guarantees accurate alignment during assembly. This integrated origami-inspired manufacturing technique effectively minimizes structural alignment errors. Both the interlocking and origami-inspired assembly designs improve manufacturing efficiency, enabling complete microrobot assembly in under 10 min. The material costs for each microrobot are maintained below $0.10 USD through the selection of low-cost components. This manufacturing approach satisfies the stringent requirements for the microrobot flight—including mechanical resilience, electrical functionality, and scalable production—while outperforming conventional metal and silicon alternatives in both weight savings and cost efficiency. It thereby facilitates the microrobot’s widespread adoption across a broad range of application scenarios.

Flight control design and implementation

Due to its low mass, the ion-propelled flying microrobot is dynamically unstable and highly vulnerable to disturbance during flight. The robot may quickly pitch and roll in the absence of attitude control. The implementation of effective attitude control is a fundamental enabling technology for the flight of ion-propelled microrobots. Owing to its high thrust-to-weight ratio, the proposed microrobot is capable of carrying devices with specific functions. Based on the feedback from an IMU, a closed-loop control algorithm is implemented to stabilize the flight attitude. The control algorithm continuously computes the pitch/roll angular error e and generates the corresponding stabilization thrust vector, which is then converted into precise voltage commands for the ion thrusters. The voltage amplifiers regulate the voltage difference between thrusters 2 and 4 (denoted as U2), and between thrusters 1 and 3 (denoted as U3), as dictated by the control signals. The control system achieves active attitude stabilization through real-time voltage modulation of the four ion thrusters, generating precise thrust outputs according to the PID control law.

The control system and circuit connections are illustrated in Fig. 4a. The negative high-voltage power supply (maximum: −30 kV) provides a reference voltage Vc of −4.5 kV for the collectors, serving as the voltage supply reference for takeoff. The reference voltage is denoted as control signal U1, which is associated with the height control strategy and is adjusted based on the feedback current. The emitter voltages are generated by a high-voltage operational amplifier (control range: 0–900 V). The amplifier is powered by a high-voltage Vw provided by the HV module with a basic voltage Va, and controlled by the control signals U2 and U3 from a microcontroller (Arduino). The control signal operates within a range of 0 to 4 V. Thus, the operational amplifier operates within a range of 0 to 400 V. Then the system maintains a stable inter-electrode potential difference of 4.5–4.9 kV across all four thrust units. Within this optimized voltage range, the thrust exhibits near-linear dependence on applied voltage, as quantified in Fig. 2f. Attitude feedback is provided by an IMU onboard the microrobot, delivering 3-axis angular rate and linear acceleration measurements. After the sensor data are processed, they are fed into a pre-programmed control algorithm in the microcontroller. The control algorithm compares the target attitude angle with the IMU feedback and outputs control signals to the operational amplifier. To achieve absolute coordinate trajectory control without the need for an expensive and complex motion capture system, we propose utilizing a binocular camera to effectively obtain real-time position feedback, enabling the capture and tracking of the flying microrobot’s position. Discussion

In this work, we present the development of the experimental demonstration of IMU-based closed-loop controlled flight on a centimeter-scale ion-propelled flying microrobot. The proposed ion-propelled flying microrobot generates thrust through ion wind created by corona discharge without mechanical actuators. This design reduces the reliance on complex mechanical components, decreases weight, and opens up possibilities for mass production. The ion-propelled flying microrobot is constructed using a 50-μm-thick tungsten-coated polymer film, innovatively combining the conductivity of the metal with the flexibility of the polymer material. Due to the use of this material, compared to other ion-wind flying robots26,36,37, the structure presented in this paper is more complex, but the weight has not increased. As shown in Fig. 1d, conventional rotorcraft requires electromagnetic motors and propeller structures, which typically results in a larger mass that often exceeds that of ion-propelled microrobots by two orders of magnitude. Even the lightest practical rotorcraft configurations have a mass (~1.6 g54) that is at least one order of magnitude greater than that of our ion‑propelled microrobot. Flapping-wing robots can achieve a lightweight mass on the order of tens of milligrams and have demonstrated a wide range of thrust-to-weight ratios. For instance, the Harvard RoboBee achieved a peak value of 4.1:149. However, ion wind propulsion technology, which operates completely without mechanical moving parts, offers a distinct advantage in reducing structural mass, thereby holding potential in several aspects for the further miniaturization of flying microrobots. To further enhance the flight performance, we also conducted design optimization for the ion-propelled flying microrobot through finite element analysis (FEA). By performing 2D simulations shown in Fig. 2c, the optimal structure (d1 = 1.5 mm and d2 = 3.5 mm) was identified for the collector electrodes. The simulation results also indicate that additional emitters have minimal impact on the ion wind velocity. Thus, a single emitter configuration was determined as the optimal design solution. Based on the optimization of the ion thruster, the ion-propelled flying microrobot proposed in this work, weighing 36.7 mg, accomplished the flight carrying the payload of 146.8 mg (Fig. 5a), achieved a high thrust-to-weight ratio of 5. The thrust density of our thruster reaches 538 N/m3. This value considerably exceeds the thrust density of 110 N/m3 reported for the wire-wire ion wind thrusters55. While our design shows a lower thrust density compared to recent studies on deeply optimized multi-stage thrusters32 (which can exceed 800 N/m3), our structural geometry is intentionally optimized for flight dynamics. It should be noted that increasing the distance between the emitter and collector in the structure, or adopting a more vertical multi-stage design, can further enhance the thrust. Since the only change in the structure leading to higher thrust is the lengthening of the connectors, with no significant increase in the overall structural weight, a higher thrust-to-weight ratio can be achieved. However, increasing the height of the structure is detrimental to flight stability due to the elevated center of gravity and elongation of the structure in the vertical direction, all of which contradict the conception of designing the microrobot with better stability. Furthermore, stability is also influenced by system damping; adding loads like a payload or a dedicated damping structure can effectively enhance it39. Also, the proposed microrobot has longer lifespans compared to other up-to-date sub-gram flying microrobots, since it could maintain stable flight in a tethered state for 1 h (recorded in Supplementary Movie 5). Compared to other types of robots in terms of weight, thrust-to-weight ratio, and long-duration flight capacity, the robot presented in this paper offers several advantages. Furthermore, 3D simulations of the ion-propelled flying microrobot with different voltage differences (5 kV, 5.5 kV, and 6 kV) applied to the emitter and collector electrodes show that the ion wind velocity can be altered by the voltage difference, and the voltage differences applied on different thrusters are independent (Fig. 2a). The optimization of the ion-propelled flying microrobot’s structural design through the multiphysics simulation not only enhances the thrust-to-weight ratio but also validates the feasibility of controlling its flight by adjusting the voltage difference.

The high thrust-to-weight ratio and thrust controllability enable the robot to carry an IMU for attitude adjustment through closed-loop feedback with the control strategy shown in Fig. 4a. This capability is critical in addressing the challenges of flying microrobots, particularly regarding flight stability. Traditionally, achieving stable flight in such small flying microrobots has been a challenging task due to their low weight and the inherent instability of ion propulsion at small scales. Using an onboard IMU sensor, the stability of the ion-propelled flying microrobot can be adjusted through real-time feedback56. This feedback regulates the emitter’s voltage signal, which in turn alters the EHD force (Fig. 4b). This allows different voltage signals to be applied to the different ion thrusters, enabling multi-degrees-of-freedom motion through the variation in the strength of corona discharge. While previous studies have demonstrated the ability to lift lightweight payloads (e.g., IMU boards) using ion thrusters36, none of those sub‑100 mg ion-propelled robots achieved closed‑loop controlled flight. This work mounts the IMU sensor on the ion-propelled flying microrobot, achieving stable flight (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Movie 6) and completing controlled path tracking (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movies 7 and 8). This work verifies the control system by conducting an ion-propelled flying microrobot to fly along rectangular (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Movie 7) and triangular (Supplementary Movie 8) trajectories with closed-loop control, and captures its real-time position using the binocular camera to demonstrate that the miniature ion-propelled microrobot has achieved closed-loop controlled flight with an onboard IMU. Through structural optimization and the design of feedback control systems, this work successfully achieved multi-degrees-of-freedom motion of the ion-propelled flying microrobot. Additionally, an origami technique is employed for the fabrication process of the ion-propelled flying microrobot, shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Leveraging the origami-inspired design with the cost-effective, high-performance metal-polymer composites, our manufacturing approach enables rapid, simple assembly, overcoming a barrier to swarm deployment.

The microrobot demonstrates a power consumption of approximately 0.45 W (4.5 kV, 0.1 mA), yielding a thrust-to-power ratio of around 2.8 mN/W. This efficiency is notably higher than that reported in several recent studies on ionic wind thrusters (e.g., 1.86 mN/W32). While this power consumption is relatively low for flying microrobots, under current technological constraints, it remains infeasible for these sub-gram microrobots to carry an onboard power supply sufficient for sustained flight. To bridge this gap, we propose a carrier-deployed system. In this configuration, a larger carrier aircraft provides the necessary power and houses the heavy control electronics, while the tethered microrobots, equipped with onboard sensors, are deployed to perform designated tasks in the confined spaces. This approach enables the flying microrobot to operate efficiently while bypassing the immediate constraints of limited energy capacity. Also, the miniaturization and integration of the control circuit present a significant challenge for flying microrobots. The control system currently relies on relatively heavy electronic components, such as the Apex PA97PC amplifier. Therefore, a key area for further research is to utilize lightweight, compact circuits to detect the environment and generate a controllable high voltage sufficient for regulating ion thrust. This will be essential for improving the performance and feasibility of such robots. The microrobot operates at voltages of 3 to 6 kV, which may raise initial safety concerns. However, the corresponding discharge current remains consistently below 1 mA under normal operating conditions. The voltage and current levels used in this work are already within safe limits for humans57. The potential applications of ion-propelled flying microrobots are vast and varied. This study explores several promising avenues for their deployment. In addition to the onboard IMU sensor, functional devices such as an image sensor and an FBG sensor are also mounted. Key areas of interest include payload delivery (Fig. 5a), real-time surveillance (Fig. 5c), and material detection (Fig. 5d) during flight. These applications are valuable in environments where traditional robotic solutions face limitations. When it comes to performing tasks in confined or difficult-to-access spaces, the microrobots demonstrated in this study offer distinct advantages. Their small size and high mobility allow them to accomplish complex tasks such as confined-space surveillance, disaster rescue, and hazardous environment exploration that are otherwise challenging for traditional robots.

Methods

Fabrication of the structure and electrodes

The microrobot’s main body was fabricated from 50-μm PET film using a cold-ablation UV laser system (Seaman SM-F10). To minimize heat-affected zones and prevent edge carbonization, we optimized the cutting parameters: 10 W UV power, 10 μm spot size, 10% laser-cutting accuracy, and 100 kHz pulse frequency at 1000 mm/s scan speed. The process required 60 repeat cycles to ensure clean through-cutting while preserving the structural integrity of the thin polymer. Following that, a 100-nm tungsten coating was deposited on the PET-made components via DC magnetron sputtering (Kurt J. Lesker PVD-75) using 140 W power for 10 min. Process parameters were optimized to achieve ±5% thickness uniformity across the 50-μm film while maintaining substrate temperature below 80 °C to prevent polymer deformation. The main body and components of the ion-propelled flying microrobot were assembled and structurally bonded at the joints using a localized UV-curable adhesive (EMI 10483). This bonding ensures the mechanical integrity essential for flight maneuvers. The complete fabrication sequence is detailed in Supplementary information.

Performance characterization of the ion wind thrusters

The experimental characterization of the ion wind thruster’s velocity and thrust is detailed in Supplementary information (Supplementary Figs. 7 and 13). Ion wind velocity was measured using a calibrated thermal anemometer (Testo 405i; 0.01 m/s accuracy) mounted on a manual translation stage and positioned 20 mm downstream of the thruster’s collector plane. A voltage difference was then applied between the collectors and emitters, causing the ion wind produced by corona discharge to be recorded by the anemometer.

Thrust measurements were performed using a precision balance (XIUILAB FA2204; 0.1 mg accuracy) with the ion thruster mounted above a grounded electrostatic shield that fully enclosed the balance. This shielding configuration effectively blocked ionic interference while allowing accurate force measurement of the thruster output. Additionally, a sufficient distance should be maintained between the ion wind thruster and the electrostatic shield to further prevent the ion wind from impacting the precision balance, thereby minimizing measurement errors. The net lifting force generated by the thruster was transduced into a measurable weight loss of the support structure, enabling direct thrust quantification. Then the weight loss was finally converted into thrust.

Ion wind flow was visualized in a custom low-speed wind tunnel (Re <100), revealing the three-dimensional flow structures generated by the microrobot during operation. A smoke generator was placed at the inlet of the wind tunnel, and a smoke extractor was placed at the outlet. Additionally, sponges were installed at both the inlet and outlet to ensure a laminar and uniform flow of the working fluid. During the experiment, the flying microrobot was suspended at the center of the wind tunnel using 30-μm enameled wire. Uniform smoke flow patterns were achieved by precisely balancing the smoke concentration at the inlet (10-15% by volume) with the extraction velocity at the outlet, enabling clear visualization of the ion wind interaction field surrounding the microrobot.

Closed-loop microrobot flight control

First, a negative bias voltage was applied to the collector to reach the corona discharge voltage level using a high-voltage power supply (DONGWEN DW-P503-4ACD2). The four thrusters were connected to four high-voltage operational amplifiers (APEX PA97PC) with a gain of 100, controlled by a microcontroller (Arduino Uno R3). The operational amplifiers were powered by a high-voltage module (DONGWEN DW-P102-1FN1), which required both a 24 V DC power supply and a 0–5 V Arduino control signal for precise voltage regulation. Effective control of the microrobot was achieved by amplifying a control signal of up to 4 V from the Arduino by a factor of 100 using high-voltage operational amplifiers, with the high-voltage module DW-P102-1FN1 providing a 400 V working voltage to the amplifiers. During flight, positive voltages were applied to the emitters through four fine (30-μm) enameled copper wires to minimize aerodynamic interference, while a single shared wire delivered negative voltages to all collectors. The wires are used exclusively for power supply and signal transmission. A shelf was mounted on the robot, with a flexible PCB housing a six-axis IMU (STMicroelectronics LSM6DS3TR) positioned at its center. Four 30-µm diameter enameled copper wires were employed to transmit data from the sensor to the Arduino, facilitating real-time monitoring of the microrobot’s flight attitude with an IMU sampling rate of 100 Hz. The Kalman filter was implemented in the Arduino program to estimate the system’s state, thereby mitigating the effects of drift and providing more accurate readings over extended periods of flight. The onboard control‑loop frequency for attitude regulation was also set to 100 Hz. A binocular stereo camera (WHEELTEC; <200 USD) was mounted 20 cm above the experimental arena to track the microrobot’s 3D absolute position with 0.1-mm spatial resolution at 60 Hz. The position control loop was executed at 10 Hz. The circuit is shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. In the experiments of surveillance and material characterization, the image sensor (Yichuang Electronics EZ-EN35L-S), FBG sensor (Tongwei), and interrogator (Tongwei, TV1600-mini) are required.
