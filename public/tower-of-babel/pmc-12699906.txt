Prevalence and genotypic characterization of human papillomavirus in Iraqi women: a retrospective study of 2,193 cases
Authors: Mohammed Tahir, Dlnya Mohammed, Chinar Sadiq
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a common sexually transmitted infection and is the leading cause of genital warts and cervical cancer. However, research on HPV prevalence within the Iraqi population is limited, with only a few published studies. The aim of this study was to investigate the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV among Iraqi women.MethodsIn this study, samples from 2,193 married women referred to PharmaGene laboratory for diagnostic evaluation between 2023 and 2025 were analyzed. HPV genotyping was performed using the HPV Direct Flow CHIP system.ResultThe overall positive rate was 12.7% (n = 279). Within the positive cases, 6.9% had only high-risk (HR-HPV) genotypes, 4.4% had only low-risk (LR-HPV) genotypes, and 1.4% were co-infected with both HR and LR-HPV genotypes. The most frequently detected HPV genotypes were HPV 18 (13.3%), HPV 11 (11.4%), HPV 16 (6.9%), HPV 62/81 (5.7%), HPV 39 (4.5%), HPV 51 (3.6%), and HPV 6 (3.6%). Among HPV-positive cases, the prevalence of single, double, triple, and quadruple or higher infections was 83.5%, 14%, 1.8%, and 0.7%, respectively.ConclusionThis study presents novel epidemiological data on the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV infection in Iraq, offering a valuable basis for enhancing cervical cancer screening and HPV prevention strategies. Furthermore, understanding the distribution of HPV genotypes may support the implementation of targeted vaccination programs.Keywords: Human papillomavirus, Cervical cancer, Married women, Genotype distribution and prevalence, vaccine

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most prevalent disease-causing organisms that causes infection in humans. Studies indicate that approximately nine out of ten sexually active men and eight out of ten sexually active women will be exposed to HPV at least once during their lives [1, 2]. HPV is implicated in a spectrum of clinical manifestations, from asymptomatic infections to both benign and malignant lesions of the vagina, penis, cervix, and vulva [3]. Additionally, the majority of cervical cancer cases are caused by HPV [4]. HPV is mainly transmitted via direct skin-to-skin contact during sexual activities, such as vaginal, anal, and oral intercourse.

Cervical cancer ranks fourth globally in incidence among women, with an estimated 570,000 new cases and 310,000 deaths each year [5]. Cervical cancer is more common in women residing in low- and middle-income countries due to limited access to vaccination, screening, and healthcare services [6]. In Iraq, cervical cancer ranks as the 12th most common cancer among women [7]. Moreover, recent data indicate a notable rise in cervical cancer incidence, from 0.6 per 100,000 individuals in 2010 to 2 per 100,000 individuals in 2016 [8]. Prolonged exposure to HR-HPV types, especially HPV 16 and 18, is the primary factor driving the progression from precancerous lesions to invasive cancer [9].

HPV is a small, non-enveloped virus with a circular double-stranded DNA genome of approximately 8,000 base pairs [10]. The viral genome consists of eight proteins: two late structural proteins (L1 and L2) responsible for forming the capsid, and six early non-structural regulatory proteins (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7) [11]. To date, more than 200 HPV genotypes have been detected and are typically categorized into LR-HPV and HR-HPV groups, depending on their oncogenic potential. LR-HPV types result in benign lesions, whereas HR-HPV types are linked to cancer development [12]. Women with HR-HPV types are particularly at risk for developing cervical cancer within three to five years after infection [12]. The most clinically relevant LR-HPV genotypes include types 6, 11, 40, 42, 44, 54, 55, 61, 62, 71, 74, 81, 84, 89, and 90, while the HR-HPV group comprises genotypes 16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, and 82. The most common HPV type in both healthy women and cervical cancer patients worldwide is HPV 16, which is followed by HPV 18, 6, and 11 [13]. Within HR-HPV genotypes, types 16 and 18 account for more than two-thirds of cervical cancer cases globally [4]. Additionally, HR-HPVs contribute to more than 40% of oral cancers and are linked to several penile, cervical, vulvar, anal, head, and neck carcinomas [14].

The rates of HPV infection and the distribution of HPV genotypes differ widely across different regions of the world, due to variations in population distribution, lifestyle, and education [15]. Understanding the regional distribution of HPV genotypes is essential for guiding effective cervical cancer screening, treatment strategies, and preventive measures. However, data on HPV prevalence and genotype distribution in Iraq remain scarce, with few existing studies and limited by small sample sizes. A recent study found that in women seeking gynecological care, the prevalence of HPV was 21.8% and HPV 6 was the most frequent genotype [16].

The recognition of the link between sustained HR-HPVs and the progression of cervical cancer has reshaped global approaches to cervical cancer prevention. The detection of viral DNA has become a valuable tool for identifying individuals at increased risk for cervical cancer development. In developed countries with limited vaccination programs, early HPV detection through viral DNA testing is an essential approach. The adoption of HPV vaccination has the potential to significantly reduce the prevalence of HPV infections [17].

The primary aim of this retrospective study was to evaluate the prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes and offer an overview of the frequently identified genotypes in the country. The findings of this study could provide valuable epidemiological data on HPV genotypes and prevalence, laying the foundation for promoting targeted vaccination and national vaccination planning.

Methodology

Study participants

This study was conducted between May 2023 and March 2025. A total of 2,193 married women from various provinces in Iraq, referred by gynecologists to PharmaGene Laboratory (Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory located in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq) for HPV DNA testing and genotyping, were enrolled. Participants meeting the following criteria were enrolled: those who were permanent residents of Iraq, aged 18 years or older, not pregnant, had not received an HPV vaccination, had not undergone a hysterectomy, had no history of cervical surgery, and had never received pelvic radiation therapy. Women who met the eligibility criteria were included in the study after providing informed consent.

Sample collection and HPV DNA extraction

Gynecologists exposed the cervix using a speculum and removed excess mucus with a cotton swab. A cervical brush was then inserted into the cervical canal and rotated gently five times in a single direction to collect cervical secretions and epithelial cells for further analysis. The collected samples were transferred into sterile tubes containing 5 mL of viral transport medium. All specimens were stored at 2–4 °C and processed within one week. DNA was extracted from the cervical samples using the AddPrep Viral Nucleic Acid Extraction kit (Addbio Inc., South Korea), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

HPV genotyping

The DNA flow technology was applied for the detection and genotyping of HPV DNA. Briefly, the method involves amplification of the HPV L1 gene region via polymerase chain reaction, subsequently followed by hybridization with genotype-specific oligonucleotide probes using DNA-flow technology. Once the target-probe interaction was completed, the hybridization signal was visualized through an enzymatic colorimetric reaction utilizing Streptavidin-conjugated alkaline phosphatase and NBT/BCIP substrates on the HybriSpot system, followed by computational analysis using HybriSoft software. The HPV direct flow CHIP kit (Master Diagnostica, Vitro S.A., Granada, Spain) was used for HPV genotyping. The assay detects both HR-HPV genotypes—including types 16, 18, 26, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, and 82—and LR-HPV genotypes, such as types 6, 11, 40, 42, 43, 44, 54, 61, 62, 67, 69, 70, 71, 72, 81, and 84. PCR amplification, hybridization, and result analysis were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2019 and GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, USA). Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the distribution and occurrence of HPV genotypes within the study population. The chi-square test was used to evaluate the significance of the difference between the specified groups. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The overall prevalence of HPV infection

A total of 2193 samples were collected from women referred by gynecologists for an HPV DNA test between 2023 and 2025. Participants were aged between 18 and 55 years. In total, 279 samples were positive for HPV, corresponding to an overall HPV prevalence of 12.7% within the study population. Of all 2,193 cases, 6.9% (n = 152) had pure HR-HPV genotypes, 4.4% (n = 97) had pure LR-HPV genotypes, and 1.4% (n = 30) tested positive for both HR-HPV and LR-HPV genotypes. A significantly higher frequency of HR-HPV (including co-infections) was observed compared to pure LR-HPV (χ² = 25.90, p < 0.0001). Distribution of HPV genotypes

In this study, 32 distinct HPV genotypes were found among the 279 patients who tested positive for HPV, including fifteen LR-HPV and eighteen HR-HPV genotypes. The top ten HPV genotypes with the highest detection rates were: HPV 18 (44, 13.3%), HPV 11 (38, 11.4%), HPV 16 (23, 6.9%), HPV 62/81 (19, 5.7%), HPV 39 (15, 4.5%), HPV 51 (12, 3.6%), HPV 6 (12, 3.6%), HPV 31 (11, 3.3%), HPV 45 (11, 3.3%), and HPV 59 (11, 3.3%). Of all patients, HPV 18 was the most frequently occurring genotype. Moreover, the top four prevalent HR-HPV genotypes were HPV 18, 16, 39, and 51. Prevalence of single and multiple HPV infections

A single HPV infection was observed in the majority of HPV-positive women (233/279, 83.50%), whereas multiple infections were detected in only 16.50% (46/279), indicating a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001). In cases of multiple infections, double infections were the most common, representing 14% (39/279), followed by triple infections at 1.80% (5/279). Furthermore, the incidence of more than three genotypes was quite rare, with only two cases observed, 0.70% (2/279). Discussion

HPV poses a primary global health concern due to its strong link to cervical cancer, which remains one of the leading causes of death affecting women across the world [17]. Data from the WHO global assessment for 2020 indicate that Asia accounts for the highest proportion of cervical cancer cases (58%), followed by Africa (20%), and Europe (10%) [18]. In Iraqi women, cervical cancer ranks as the 12th most common type of cancer [7]. The high incidence of cervical cancer cases is likely attributed to the absence of national HPV screening programs, delays in timely treatment, and public awareness. Evaluating the prevalence and regional distribution of specific HPV genotypes is essential for designing effective cervical cancer prevention and control strategies. This study provides a current overview of HPV prevalence and genotype distribution from women seeking diagnostic assessment for HPV in Iraq.

This study examined the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV in a cohort of 2,193 Iraqi women from 2023 to 2025. In total, 279 women tested positive for HPV, resulting in an overall prevalence of 12.7%. The HPV incidence rate found in this study aligns with findings from a study that reported a prevalence of 12.38% in the group of 856 Iraqi women [19]. Similarly, a study by Husain et al. in the Kurdistan region of Iraq in 2016 found an overall HPV prevalence of 12.5% [20]. Notably, research carried out at the Central Public Health Laboratory in Baghdad from December 2012 to December 2014 reported a lower HPV prevalence of 9% [21].

In contrast, a study conducted in Baghdad province between January 2021 and September 2022 among a cohort of 400 Iraqi women reported a markedly higher HPV incidence rate of 32% [22]. This variation could be attributed to several factors, including differences in the sensitivity of detection methods, the time of each study, or the sample size of study populations.

The overall HPV incidence rate of 12.7% in Iraq is remarkably lower than the rates reported in recent studies from North America (54,6%), the United States (40%), and China (22.3%) [23, 24, 25]. The lower HPV prevalence in Iraq may be attributed to differences in the study population and detection methods, as well as geographic and demographic variations, or cultural and behavioral factors. Sexual behavior patterns characterized by monogamy, delayed initiation of sexual activity, and lower prevalence of extramarital sexual relations may also contribute to the lower incidence of HPV.

Another aim was to assess the incidence of HR-HPV and LR-HPV within the studied population. Herein, from the 2,193 cases analyzed, 6.9% (n = 152) were positive for pure HR-HPV genotypes, 4.4% (n = 97) tested positive for pure LR-HPV genotypes, and 1.4% (n = 30) were concurrently positive for both HR and LR-HPV genotypes. The prevalence of HR-HPV in this study (6.9%) is slightly lower than the 9% reported in an earlier Iraqi study conducted on 500 patients from 2017 to 2022 [16]. Likewise, a cross-sectional study performed in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq from January 2018 to September 2020 reported an HR-HPV prevalence of 12.2% and an LR-HPV prevalence of 18.1% in a cohort of 320 patients [26]. Differences between the present findings and prior research may be attributable to variations in sample size and inclusion criteria. Therefore, caution is advised when interpreting prevalence comparisons across studies.

The prevalence of HPV genotypes shows considerable regional variation; however, HPV 16 and HPV 18 are consistently identified as the most predominant HR-HPV types involved in cervical cancer cases globally. These two types together are found in nearly 70% of all cervical cancer cases. A meta-analysis of 79 studies (5,954 cervical cancer cases) reported that HPV 16/18 made up about 66.9% of cervical cancer cases in Asia [27]. Consequently, the early detection of these genotypes is crucial in identifying individuals at an increased risk of developing cervical disease. In this study, HPV 18 was the predominant detected HR-HPV genotype, followed by HPV 16. Collectively, these two HR-HPV types were exhibited in 20.2% of HPV-positive cases. Prior research conducted in multiple Asian countries has consistently identified HPV 16 and HPV 18 as the most frequently detected genotypes. The highest incidence was reported in Turkey (40.0%) [28], followed by Saudi Arabia (37.5%) [29], China (32.4%) and Iran [30], (24.7%) [31]. In Iraq, consistent with the findings of this study, other research has demonstrated that HPV genotypes 16 and 18 collectively represent the leading detected HR-HPV types, with reported prevalence ranging from 24% to 52.7% across various studies [21, 26, 32]. The high prevalence of HPV 16/18 co-infection, which is strongly associated with cervical carcinogenesis, emphasizes the potential public health implications of HPV infection in this region. Therefore, considering the findings of this study and supporting data from previous research, the introduction of HPV vaccination programs focused on these HR-HPV genotypes, combined with early screening, is essential.

In this study, a total of 16 h-HPV genotypes, including (18, 16, 39, 51, 31, 45, 59, 68, 35, 66, 56, 33, 53, 73, 26, and 58), with 16 LR- HPV genotype including (71, 69, 72, 84, 40, 61, 70, 67, 54, 42, 44/55, 43, 6, 62/81, and 11) were detected. The most commonly identified HR-HPV genotypes were HPV 18, HPV 16, and HPV 39, with detection rates of 13.3%, 6.9%, and 3.6%, respectively. In contrast, HPV58 was the least prevalent HR-HPV type, detected in only four cases (1.2%). As for LR-HPV genotypes, the most frequent types were HPV 11 (11.4%), HPV 62/81 (5.7%), and HPV 6 (3.6%). Meanwhile, HPV 69 and HPV 71 were each observed in only a single case. Consistent with these results, a study by Intisar et al. reported that HPV 16 and HPV 18 were the most prevalent HR-HPV genotypes among 64 participants evaluated in Iraq [32]. A different study carried out in Mosul, Iraq, which investigated the distribution of HPV genotypes within 150 females with genital warts, found that 127 participants (84.6%) tested positive for HPV. Consistent with our results, the study identified HPV 11 as the most prevalent low-risk genotype (60.5%), while HPV 16 was the highest detected HR-HPV type [33]. Furthermore, two recent studies conducted in Iraq reported that HPV 6 and HPV 11 were the most frequently detected LR-HPV genotypes [16, 26]. These findings, in line with previous studies, indicate that HPV 11 and HPV 6 are the most frequently detected LR-HPV genotypes.

In a study by Sanna et al., out of 731 Iraqi women, 9% (n = 66) tested positive for HPV. The distribution of HPV genotypes in this population included HPV 16 (25%), HPV 59 (18%), HPV 56 (13%), HPV 18 (12%), and HPV 35 (9%) [21]. A large study conducted in Iraq, which analyzed 856 samples using real-time PCR, reported a HR-HPV DNA positivity rate of 12.4% (n = 106). Unlike our findings, the most prevalent HR-HPV genotypes were HPV 33, HPV 35, and HPV 56, each accounting for 18.6% of the positive cases [19]. The variation in HPV genotype prevalence may be attributed to differences in sample size, diagnostic methods, and the periods during which the studies were conducted.

In a study conducted in Iran, Mojarad et al. reported an HPV prevalence of 43.6% among 447 participants (431 female and 16 male). The five most frequently identified HR-HPV genotypes were HPV 16 (15.3%), HPV 56 (11.5%), HPV 52 and HPV 59 (each at 7.7%), and HPV 51 (7.1%). HPV 6 was the most common LR-HPV genotype, detected in 39.3% of cases [34]. El-Daly et al. reported an overall HPV infection prevalence of 15.1%. HPV 16 was detected at the highest rate (43.75%) within the HR-HPV genotypes. In the category of LR-HPV genotypes, HPV 6 ranked second in frequency, with a detection rate of 25% [35]. Moreover, a systematic review and meta-analysis comprising 296 eligible studies with a combined cohort of 199,233 outpatients reported an overall HPV prevalence of 52.45% within the Chinese population. Consistent with our findings, HPV 11 and HPV 16 were identified as the second (13.71%) and third (8.29%) most prevalent genotypes, respectively. However, in contrast to our results, HPV 6 (19.06%) was reported as the most frequently detected genotype in that analysis [36].

In this study, HPV genotypes 18, 11, 16, and 62/81 were among the top-detected genotypes. Consequently, adopting a vaccination strategy that includes immunization against these four genotypes may enhance protective efficacy in the Iraqi population. Notably, the quadrivalent HPV vaccine Gardasil®—which targets HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18 [37] is currently available in Iraq through private healthcare services.

Another aim of this study was to examine the prevalence of single and multiple HPV infections in HPV-positive individuals, especially given the increasing research focus on co-infections involving multiple HPV genotypes. The link between infections involving multiple HPV genotypes and the advancement of cervical cancer has not been definitively established, as existing research presents conflicting evidence. Some research has demonstrated that co-infection with multiple HPV genotypes is associated with an increased risk of cervical cancer development [38, 39, 40]. However, other studies have reported no significant difference between single and multiple HPV infections concerning the development of cervical cancer [41–43]. In the current study, single HPV infection was more common within HPV-positive Iraqi women, accounting for 83.5% of cases, whereas multiple HPV infections were identified in 16.5% of the positive samples analyzed. These findings align with previous research [44]. Determining the frequency of single versus multiple HPV infections can aid in optimizing screening protocols and HPV vaccination programs in this population. Further studies are needed to understand better how multiple infections influence cervical cancer development and patient outcomes.

The current findings must be evaluated in consideration of the study’s strengths and limitations. A key strength of the current study is its substantial sample size. To our knowledge, this is the largest HPV-related cohort study conducted in Iraq. Nonetheless, certain limitations of the study must be acknowledged. Firstly, Insufficient data regarding the regional distribution, demographic profile, and lifestyle behaviors of the participants restricts the capacity to explore potential associations between these variables and the prevalence of HPV infection. Secondly, due to the absence of complete age data, the correlation between age and HPV genotype distribution could not be assessed. Finally, the findings are based on samples submitted to our laboratory for diagnostic evaluation. Therefore, the data may not be nationally representative, as the women included in the study may not accurately reflect the broader population of women residing in Iraq. Nonetheless, we recommend that additional research involving both Iraqi men and women be conducted to gain a more complete understanding of HPV distribution within Iraq.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study analyzed the prevalence and genotype distribution of HPV among 2,193 Iraqi women, revealing a relatively low overall infection rate of 12.7%. The predominance of genotypes HPV 18, 11, 16, 62/81, 39, and 6 highlights the need for targeted vaccination strategies. The The results of this study can assist policymakers in formulating efficient and cost-effective interventions to strengthen national HPV vaccination and cervical cancer prevention efforts.
