How do the measurement properties of the broad-band scales for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in children perform based on the COSMIN guidelines?
Authors: Yueyue Chen, Qiuhan Cai, Siyuan Hu, Zehui Zhao, Jixuan Wang, Liqing Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study systematically evaluated the measurement properties of ADHD-relevant subscales (i.e., subscales measuring inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity) embedded within broad-band behavior rating scales for children with ADHD, using the COSMIN methodology. The aim was to provide evidence-based support for clinical screening, co-occurring assessment, and treatment monitoring.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we conducted a systematic literature search across PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ProQuest. Risk of bias and measurement properties were assessed with the COSMIN Risk of Bias Checklist and Updated Criteria for Good Measurement Properties, respectively. Evidence synthesis was performed through an adapted GRADE approach.ResultsThirty-six studies, covering 12 broad-band instruments, were included. The CBCL Attention Problems subscale was rated as "not recommended" due to insufficient cross-cultural validity (high quality evidence). The SDQ Hyperactivity-Inattention subscale demonstrated the most favorable evidence profile, with moderate quality evidence for structural validity and internal consistency, and high quality evidence for cross-cultural validity, reliability, and convergent/discriminant validity. The CADBI Inattention and Hyperactivity/Impulsivity subscales showed high quality evidence for structural and cross-cultural validity, and moderate quality evidence for internal consistency. The FTF Attention and Hyperactive/Impulsive subscales had moderate quality evidence for structural validity and internal consistency. The CABI Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity subscale had high quality evidence for reliability, but low quality evidence for structural validity and internal consistency. The remaining instruments (ANSER-PQ, CBAI, ECI-4, YI-4, DSMD, ABC, CSI-4) had limited or unsatisfactory evidence for their ADHD-related subscales.ConclusionsAmong the included broad-band instruments, given the evidence of insufficient cross-cultural validity, applying the COSMIN criteria to the CBCL Attention Problems subscale would lead to a recommendation against its use; users outside North America should be cautious. The SDQ Hyperactivity-Inattention subscale demonstrates comparatively favorable measurement properties, though this does not establish its overall superiority for ADHD assessment. Future studies should evaluate content validity, measurement error, and responsiveness following COSMIN guidelines.Trial registrationPROSPERO (CRD420250655032).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07661-1.

Background

As the most prevalent neurodevelopmental condition in pediatric populations, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is characterized by persistent inattention, hyperactive behaviors, and emotional impulsivity. Additional features encompass cognitive deficits, learning difficulties, and typically normal or intellectual functioning that falls within typical ranges [1]. ADHD exhibits substantial global prevalence. Epidemiological studies reveal distinct patterns across research methodologies: registry studies reported a 1.6% prevalence in children, whereas survey studies reported 5.0%, one-stage clinical studies 4.2%, and two-stage clinical studies 4.8%. Notably, preschool populations demonstrated elevated rates, with corresponding prevalence values of 4.2%, 6.4%, 4.9%, and 6.1% across these study designs [2]. Studies have shown that co-occurring oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder (CD), anxiety disorders, and depressive disorders are more frequent in preschool children with ADHD than in the general population [3, 4]. Longitudinal studies have further demonstrated that the risk of developing disruptive behaviors and substance use disorders increases with advancing age, often culminating in adverse clinical outcomes [5]. Therefore, early identification and timely intervention in children with ADHD are essential to mitigate adverse outcomes.

The evaluation of health measurement instruments requires rigorous methodological standards to ensure their reliability and validity in clinical and research settings. The Consensus-Based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) initiative was established to provide such standards, encompassing methodological quality assessment, rating of measurement properties against predefined criteria, and evidence synthesis using GRADE [6]. Although originally developed for Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs), the COSMIN framework's standards for measurement properties, risk of bias, and evidence grading are equally applicable to observer-reported outcome measures (ObsROs), including parent- and teacher-reported behavioral rating scales.

Given the absence of specific biomarkers for ADHD diagnosis and the prevalence of comorbid conditions, identification and diagnosis rely predominantly on behavioral symptoms. Consequently, the behavioral rating scales, primarily used as ObsROs in pediatric ADHD assessment, serve as the primary instruments for evaluating ADHD-related behaviors. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) indicates that ADHD assessment scales are primarily categorized into broad-band and narrow-band instruments [7]. Narrow-band scales, such as the Conners Rating Scales and the ADHD Rating Scale (ADHD-RS) IV, focus specifically on ADHD symptoms and are designed to quantify symptom severity and assist in diagnostic evaluation. Broad-band scales, such as the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) and the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL), provide a comprehensive assessment of children's overall behavior and psychosocial functioning. These scales enhance screening sensitivity, reduce misdiagnosis, identify co-occurring conditions and functional impairments, and provide objective evidence for clinical diagnosis and early intervention, thereby minimizing secondary damage to academic and social functioning in children with ADHD [8, 9].

Although broad-band scales offer these practical advantages, they are multidimensional instruments that measure several distinct constructs (e.g., internalizing problems, externalizing problems, prosocial behavior). Their usefulness for ADHD screening depends specifically on the psychometric performance of their ADHD-relevant subscales, that is, the subscales that measure inattention (IN) and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (HI). For example, the SDQ contains a hyperactivity/inattention subscale, the CBCL includes an attention problems subscale, and instruments such as the Child and Adolescent Disruptive Behavior Inventory (CADBI) provide separate IN and HI subscales. These subscales are embedded within a broader item pool and are intended to be scored and interpreted independently. Consequently, their measurement properties cannot be inferred from analyses of the total scale or from other subscales; each subscale must be evaluated separately, as emphasized by the COSMIN guidelines [6].

Despite the widespread clinical and research use of broad-band scales in children with ADHD, no systematic review has evaluated the measurement properties of their subscales according to modern psychometric standards. Existing evidence is fragmented, and it remains unclear whether these subscales possess adequate content validity, structural validity, reliability, and other properties to support their use in screening, co-occurring assessment, or treatment monitoring. This uncertainty poses challenges for clinicians and researchers.

The present study therefore aimed to systematically evaluate the measurement properties of ADHD-related subscales (i.e., subscales measuring inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity) embedded within broad-band behavior rating scales for children with ADHD, following the COSMIN guidelines. The results are intended to provide evidence-based recommendations for selecting optimal subscales for screening, diagnostic support, and co-occurring assessment. A separate systematic review of narrow-band scales is ongoing; together, the two reviews will inform an integrated, evidence-based assessment pathway for pediatric ADHD.

Method

The original PROSPERO protocol (CRD420250655032) was registered to evaluate broad-band scales as whole instruments [10]. During the conduct of the review, we recognized—consistent with the updated COSMIN guidelines (version 2.0)—that each subscale of a multidimensional instrument must be evaluated separately. Specifically, the COSMIN guidelines stress "the importance of evaluating the measurement properties of each subscale of a PROM separately" [11]. Following this methodological guidance, we refined the unit of analysis from the overall broad-band scale to ADHD-related subscales (i.e., subscales measuring inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity). No additional substantive changes were made to the protocol. This deviation is documented transparently to uphold the rigor and reproducibility of the review.

Search strategy

To identify studies assessing the psychometric measurement properties of ADHD broad-band instruments, systematic searches of the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ProQuest databases were conducted from each database’s inception to December 1, 2025.

A structured search framework was developed in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines, utilizing "attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)," "pediatric population," and "psychometric properties" as core search terms. This framework integrated the two-tiered filtering system devised by the COSMIN working group, comprising a targeted filter for identifying measurement-property studies and a non-target literature exclusion filter [12]. To maximize search sensitivity, three methodological strategies were systematically implemented: (1) citation tracking of seminal publications to identify associated studies through reference analysis; (2) omitting the term "broad-band" as a search limiter to avoid excluding measurement instruments, with target instruments subsequently identified through manual screening; and (3) reference chaining involving iterative screening of bibliographies to capture potentially relevant literature. The search strategy is detailed in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies must describe at least one measurement property of a subscale that explicitly measures inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity within a broad-band instrument; (2) the study population or reported subgroups must comprise children aged 18 years or younger with ADHD; and (3) language restriction was limited to English.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies using the scale solely as an outcome measure; (2) studies employing the scale exclusively as a control instrument for evaluating other instruments; (3) secondary studies, including reviews and systematic evaluations; (4) duplicate publications, with preference retained for versions with more comprehensive data or earlier publication dates; and (5) studies with unavailable full texts.

Literature screening

To ensure methodological rigor, two reviewers (CYY and CQH) independently screened all records using EndNote 20 (Clarivate Analytics) for reference management and duplicate removal. Titles and abstracts were screened against the predefined eligibility criteria, followed by independent full-text reviews of potentially eligible articles. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion or, when necessary, arbitration by a third reviewer (HSY).

Data extraction and quality evaluation

An independent dual-review protocol was maintained throughout data extraction and analysis. Two reviewers (CYY and CQH) independently performed data extraction, risk-of-bias and quality assessments, and evidence synthesis using the standardized COSMIN data extraction template in Microsoft Excel®. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussion.

Quality assessment was conducted following COSMIN guidelines in three sequential stages: (1) content validity was evaluated first, assessing the relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility of items for the target construct and population—instruments with high quality evidence of insufficient content validity were excluded from further analysis; (2) internal structure was then examined, including structural validity, internal consistency, and cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance; and (3) remaining properties (reliability, measurement error, criterion validity, hypotheses testing for construct validity [including convergent and discriminant validity], and responsiveness) were evaluated to assess overall scale performance. If there was high quality evidence that an instrument had insufficient results for any of these measurement properties, the instrument was rated as not recommended for use. In accordance with the COSMIN framework [11], each phase involved three sequential steps: (1) assessment of methodological quality using the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist across 10 domains (PROM development, content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance, reliability, measurement error, criterion validity, hypotheses testing for construct validity, and responsiveness), with ratings of very good, adequate, doubtful, or inadequate; (2) rating of study results according to the updated criteria for good measurement properties (+, -,?, ±), with prespecified thresholds for hypotheses testing for construct validity (≥ 0.50 for largely related constructs); and (3) grading of evidence quality via an adapted GRADE approach, with initial high quality assumptions downgraded for risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, or indirectness to yield final ratings of high, moderate, low, or very low.

To address heterogeneity in psychometric terminology, this study applied COSMIN definitions consistently to ensure conceptual alignment and maintain the rigor and comparability of the evaluation framework.

Results

Literature search results

This study followed the PRISMA guidelines strictly. A systematic search of the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and ProQuest databases yielded a total of 23,631 records, and an additional 4 studies were identified through citation tracking. After deduplication (n = 6,274), 17,361 records were screened based on titles and abstracts, of which 16,821 were excluded for failing to meet the inclusion criteria. Full-text evaluation was sought for 540 reports, of which 17 were not retrieved. Of the remaining 523 reports assessed for eligibility in the first pass, 403 were excluded: 190 did not assess psychometric measurement properties, 129 had a non-research structure, 62 were not patient-reported outcome measures, 16 had inappropriate study populations, and 6 were non-English language studies. The remaining 120 reports underwent a second-pass eligibility assessment, of which 84 were excluded due to the use of narrow-band scales. Ultimately, 36 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the review. SDQ

The SDQ, developed by Goodman, is a scale used to assess the mental health of children and adolescents. It is validated for use with children and adolescents aged 4–17 years and has been extensively utilized in both clinical practice and empirical research contexts. The scale consists of 25 items covering five subscales: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity-inattention, peer problems, and prosocial behavior. It utilizes a 3-point rating system (0–2) for responses [49].

Content validity

Three studies reported on SDQ development [13, 18, 19], which were rated as having doubtful methodological quality due to incomplete reporting of development procedures. No study specifically examined the content validity of the SDQ. Based on a comprehensive assessment, the SDQ demonstrated sufficient (+) relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility, though with low quality evidence.

Internal structure

Regarding structural validity, three studies demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [14–16]. However, two other studies were rated as having doubtful methodological quality because they utilized principal component analysis (PCA) to validate the structure of the SDQ. In these studies [17, 18], the explained variance failed to meet the target threshold (≥ 50%); consequently, quality of the measurement properties was rated as insufficient (-). One study utilized confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [19]; however, it was limited by a sample size less than seven times the required number of items. The methodological quality was rated as adequate, with sufficient (+) measurement properties. Due to inconsistencies in the results across these studies, the overall quality of evidence was downgraded to moderate.

Regarding internal consistency, six studies demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. However, the level of evidence was downgraded to moderate due to limitations in structural validity evidence [13, 15–19].

Cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance was assessed in two studies [14, 15], both of which demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Evidence quality was high.

Remaining measurement properties

For reliability, two studies assessed test–retest reliability, demonstrating adequate methodological quality, sufficient (+) measurement properties, and high quality evidence [13, 18].

Regarding convergent and discriminant validity, three studies assessed convergent validity via correlations between the SDQ and ADHD-related subscales of other instruments, including the Child Health and Illness Profile-Child Edition (CHIP-CE), the ADHD Rating Scale-Ⅳ (ADHD-RS-IV), and the CBCL (with its Teacher Report Form, TRF) [17–19]. Correlations with the CBCL aligned with expectations. These studies exhibited very good methodological quality with high quality evidence. Discriminant validity between ADHD and non-ADHD populations was sufficient (+) with high quality evidence. In contrast, discriminant validity by gender was downgraded to moderate owing to inconsistent findings.

CADBI

The CADBI was developed by Burns and colleagues. It was designed to assess disruptive behaviors and related competencies in children and adolescents. It comprises multiple subscales, including inattention, hyperactivity/impulsivity, ODD, academic competence, and social competence, with items rated on an 8-point Likert scale. The instrument is available in both parent- and teacher-report versions, allowing for multi-informant assessment across home and school settings. Its structured format makes it particularly suitable for rapid screening and progress monitoring of externalizing symptoms [50, 51].

Content validity

Three studies reported the development of the CADBI [22–24]; however, the development process was inadequately described. Methodological quality was therefore rated as doubtful. Nevertheless, measurement properties—including relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility—were rated as sufficient (+). Evidence quality was downgraded to moderate because of these methodological limitations.

Internal structure

Two studies of very good methodological quality reported sufficient (+) structural validity with high quality evidence [21, 22].

One study was rated as having inadequate methodological quality because it did not report Cronbach's alpha or item-total correlations; consequently, its measurement property was rated as doubtful (?) [21]. The remaining three studies exhibited very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties for internal consistency [22–24]. Due to inconsistent results, the overall quality of evidence was downgraded to moderate.

Measurement invariance was examined in four studies [20–22, 24], all of which demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. The CADBI showed robust invariance across time, gender, and national settings.

Remaining measurement properties

No relevant studies have been identified to date.

FTF

Developed collaboratively by a Nordic expert consortium, the FTF scale is designed to evaluate neurodevelopmental and behavioral challenges in pediatric populations aged 5–15 years, with primary applications in screening for ADHD, learning impairments, and social difficulties. The scale comprises 181 items, organized into 8 main domains and 22 subdomains. It employs a 3-point scoring system [25].

Content validity

Two studies reporting FTF development were rated as doubtful in methodological quality because they did not describe patient consultation on item comprehensibility, response options, or recall periods [25, 28]. Reviewers rated relevance and comprehensiveness as sufficient (+) and comprehensibility as doubtful (?). No independent study has assessed the FTF content validity. Evidence quality was therefore downgraded to moderate for relevance and comprehensiveness and to low for comprehensibility owing to risk of bias.

Internal structure

Structural validity was assessed in two studies [26, 27]. In one study, the methodological quality was rated as inadequate due to the use of PCA and a sample size fewer than five times the number of items [26]; however, its measurement property was rated as sufficient (+). The other study demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Due to discrepancies in methodological quality across the studies, the overall evidence quality was graded as moderate [27].

For internal consistency, two studies reported very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [25, 27]. Evidence quality was nevertheless downgraded to moderate due to limitations in structural validity evidence.

Remaining measurement properties

One study evaluated test–retest reliability. The methodological quality was rated as adequate, and the measurement property was graded as sufficient (+) [25].

The study assessed discriminant validity, demonstrating adequate methodological quality and insufficient discriminant validity across gender groups [25].

Another study examined convergent validity through comparative analysis with the CBCL (ADHD-related subscales). This study exhibited very good methodological quality, and its measurement property was rated as sufficient (+) [28].

CBCL

The CBCL was developed by Achenbach to assess behavioral and emotional problems in children and adolescents aged 4 to 18 years. It comprises 113 items organized into eight subscales, including internalizing problems, externalizing problems, and attention problems. The scale employs a 3-point scoring system [52].

Content validity

One study reported on CBCL development [30], which was rated as doubtful in methodological quality because of inadequate reporting regarding relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility. Reviewers rated measurement properties as sufficient (+). No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality for these dimensions was therefore downgraded to low owing to risk of bias and lack of independent validation.

Internal structure

One study assessed scale structure via CFA [30], demonstrating very good methodological quality but insufficient (-) measurement properties for structural validity. However, due to the lack of independent replication verification, it was impossible to rate the strength of the evidence.

For internal consistency, two studies showed very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [30, 31], though evidence quality was downgraded to moderate due to structural validity limitations.

Two studies examined cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance. While both studies exhibited very good methodological quality, the CBCL failed to demonstrate invariance between U.S. and Australian populations and between Dutch and Israeli populations. The evidence quality for this dimension was rated as high [32, 48].

Remaining measurement properties

One study examined the discriminant validity of the CBCL between children with ADHD and non-ADHD controls in Brazilian and German populations [29].According to the COSMIN guidelines for evidence synthesis, these findings were treated as two independent pieces of evidence because the study analyzed distinct population groups. Consequently, the quality of evidence for discriminant validity was ultimately rated as high.

CABI

The CABI was developed by Burns et al. as an expanded version of the CADBI. While the CADBI focuses primarily on externalizing behaviors and academic competence for rapid screening, the CABI extends its scope to internalizing symptoms and functional impairments, incorporating additional dimensions such as sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT). Thus, the CADBI is more suitable for rapid assessment of externalizing behaviors, whereas the CABI offers a comprehensive multidimensional evaluation across nine subscales. Symptoms are rated on a 6-point frequency scale, while social and academic impairments use a 7-point reverse-coded scale.

Content validity

Three studies reported CABI development [33, 34, 37], with adequate methodological quality. Reviewers rated relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility as sufficient (+). No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality for these dimensions was therefore downgraded to moderate.

Internal structure

For structural validity, one study using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was rated as adequate in methodological quality [34], but the explained variance was 48% (below the 50% threshold), yielding insufficient measurement properties (-). Three CFA studies were rated as inadequate due to insufficient sample sizes [33, 35, 36], yet showed sufficient (+) measurement properties. Collectively, four studies indicated sufficient (+) structural validity; evidence quality was nevertheless downgraded to low owing to methodological limitations and inconsistency.

Four studies assessed the internal consistency of the CABI. All demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Nevertheless, evidence quality for internal consistency was downgraded to low because of limitations in structural validity evidence [33–36].

One study evaluated measurement invariance of the CABI, demonstrating very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties, with robust invariance across gender groups [36].

Remaining measurement properties

Two studies assessed the test–retest reliability of the CABI. Both demonstrated adequate methodological quality, sufficient (+) measurement properties, and high quality evidence [33, 35].

One study examined convergent validity via correlations with the Vanderbilt ADHD Diagnostic Rating Scale (VADRS), Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scales-Parent Version (RCADS-P), and CBCL [37]. Although rated very good in methodological quality, correlations with VADRS and RCADS-P failed to meet expected thresholds, yielding insufficient (-) measurement properties, whereas correlations with CBCL (ADHD-related subscales) met expected thresholds, yielding sufficient (+) measurement properties.

ABC

The ABC was developed by Adams et al. in 1997 to assess core symptoms (inattention, hyperactivity/impulsivity) and related issues (e.g., conduct problems, academic difficulties, social deficits) in adolescents with ADHD aged 11–17 years. The scale consists of 44 items organized into 6 subscales and employs a 4-point Likert scale for self-reported behaviors over the preceding 6-month period [38].

Content validity

One study reported scale development [38], but inadequately described patient or expert involvement, yielding doubtful methodological quality. Reviewers rated relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility as sufficient (+). No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality for these dimensions was therefore downgraded to low.

Internal structure

One study examined the structural validity of the ABC [38]. The study was rated as adequate in methodological quality, but the structural-validity result was rated as insufficient (−) because the explained variance of 48% fell below the threshold. The same study assessed internal consistency, demonstrating very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [38].

Remaining measurement properties

In terms of the test–retest reliability of this study, the methodological quality was rated as adequate, and the quality of the measurement properties was rated as sufficient (+) [38]. For convergent validity, two studies demonstrated very good methodological quality. Correlations between ABC and Youth Self Report (YSR) met expectations, whereas those with Conners Parent Rating Scale-48 (CPRS-48) and CBCL were unexpected [38, 39]. Evidence quality was high. Both studies confirmed adequate discriminant validity between ADHD and non-ADHD populations, with high quality evidence [38, 39].

CSI-4

The CSI-4 is designed to assess symptoms of childhood mental disorders in clinically referred children and special education students aged 6–10 years. The scale consists of 77 items and comprises 12 subscales [53].

Content validity

No relevant development or content validity studies were identified. The reviewers rated the measurement properties of relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility as sufficient (+). The quality of evidence was rated as low.

Internal structure

Two studies assessed internal consistency [40, 41]. Both studies demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties, but the quality of evidence was downgraded by two levels to low due to the lack of structural validity studies.

Remaining measurement properties

In terms of convergent validity, CSI-4 was examined against the CBCL Teacher Report Form (TRF) [40, 41], demonstrating very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Evidence quality was high. One study examined the discriminant validity of the CSI-4 between ADHD and non-ADHD populations [41], with sufficient (+) measurement properties and high quality evidence.

ECI-4

The ECI-4 is a scale developed by Gadow and Sprafkin for assessing emotional and behavioral problems in preschool children. It is designed to assess emotional and behavioral problems in children aged 3 to 6 [54].

Content validity

No relevant development studies or content validity studies were identified. The reviewers rated the measurement properties of relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility as sufficient (+), and the quality of evidence was low [42, 43].

Internal structure

The methodological quality of the studies examining internal consistency for parent-reported and teacher-reported versions was rated as very good, with measurement properties rated as sufficient (+) and doubtful (?) due to incomplete reporting. Due to the lack of studies on structural validity, the quality of evidence was downgraded by two levels to low [42].

Remaining measurement properties

Studies on the convergent validity of the ECI-4-P with the CBCL, SSRS, and BASC-2 demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Studies on the convergent validity of the ECI-4-T with the TRF, SSRS, and BASC-2 demonstrated very good methodological quality, with measurement properties rated as sufficient (+) [42, 43]. Both versions demonstrated discriminant validity between ADHD and non-ADHD populations, with measurement properties rated as sufficient (+) [42].

Other tools

Evidence for four additional instruments (ANSER-PQ, CBAI, YI-4, DSMD) was identified [44–47]. However, as each instrument was supported by only a single study that failed to meet the minimum methodological threshold for evidence synthesis, a descriptive analysis was conducted for independent evaluation.

The ANSER-PQ, developed by Levine for children aged 7–12 years, lacks formal development studies and content validity assessments. Studies evaluating comprehensiveness demonstrated doubtful methodological quality owing to limited validation evidence from a single source. Relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility were rated as sufficient (+), doubtful (?), and sufficient (+), respectively; evidence quality for all three properties was rated as very low. The study of internal consistency had very good methodological quality and reported sufficient (+) measurement properties. Studies on the convergent validity of the ANSER-PQ with the CPRS-R demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [44].

Samarakkody's team developed the CBAI, a locally developed Sri Lankan instrument for screening externalizing behavioral problems in children aged 4–6 years. Although the development process was well-documented, the methodological quality of comprehensiveness was deemed doubtful due to reliance on a single validation study and persistent flaws in evaluating comprehensiveness. The measurement properties were rated as sufficient (+) for relevance and comprehensibility, and doubtful (?) for comprehensiveness. No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality was moderate for relevance and comprehensibility, and low for comprehensiveness. Studies on internal consistency and test–retest reliability demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties [45].

The YI-4 was developed for adolescents aged 11–18 years. However, the study did not describe the scale's development process in detail, resulting in a doubtful methodological quality rating. Based on researcher evaluations, the measurement properties of relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility were rated as sufficient (+), doubtful (?), and sufficient (+), respectively. No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality was low for relevance and comprehensibility, and very low for comprehensiveness. Studies on internal consistency demonstrated very good methodological quality and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Studies on test–retest reliability demonstrated adequate methodological quality but insufficient (-) measurement properties owing to correlations below the threshold (r < 0.7). Studies on convergent validity of the YI-4 with the YSR demonstrated very good methodological quality with expected correlations and sufficient (+) measurement properties. Studies on discriminant validity demonstrated adequate methodological quality, distinguishing ADHD from non-ADHD controls [46].

The DSMD, developed by Naglieri et al., was designed for children aged 5–12 years. Although the study outlined the scale's development background and target population, its methodological quality was rated as doubtful (?). Based on researcher evaluations, the measurement properties of relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility were rated as sufficient (+), doubtful (?), and sufficient (+), respectively. No independent content validity studies were identified; evidence quality was low for relevance and comprehensibility, and very low for comprehensiveness. Only one study examined the DSMD’s convergent validity with the CBCL. Only one study examined the DSMD’s convergent validity with the CBCL, demonstrating very good methodological quality, sufficient (+) measurement properties, and expected convergent correlations [47].

Discussion

Recommendations based on the COSMIN framework

The COSMIN guidelines recommend that a measurement instrument be rated as "recommendation against use" when high quality evidence demonstrates insufficient performance in one or more measurement properties [11]. Notably, although the CBCL is widely used in clinical practice as a broad-band scale for assessing behavioral problems, high quality evidence indicates insufficient cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance in ADHD populations.

The CBCL, developed using an original sample composed predominantly of North American participants, exhibits unstable factor structures and significant differential item functioning when applied to diverse cultural and linguistic contexts, thereby compromising cross-group measurement fairness. While extensive clinical use has generated substantial data, high utilization does not guarantee strong cross-cultural validity, nor does clinical popularity ensure superior psychometric performance. The reliability and validity of measurement instruments are not universal but vary with the cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic characteristics of the population. The low CBCL ratings demonstrate that a good instrument needs to be not only effective overall but also equally accurate across specific demographic groups.

In accordance with COSMIN recommendations, a measurement instrument is classified as "recommended" only when there is "high quality evidence for sufficient results across all relevant measurement properties" [11]. However, among the 12 instruments evaluated in this study, none were assessed for all measurement properties, and none demonstrated sufficient results for all such properties. Consequently, none of the scales met the COSMIN-recommended criteria. This may reflect the stringency of the COSMIN framework. Nevertheless, some instruments performed relatively well on the properties that were assessed.

Among the reviewed instruments, the SDQ hyperactivity/inattention subscale demonstrates comparatively favorable measurement properties: structural validity and internal consistency achieved sufficient ratings, with the evidence quality rated as moderate; cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance, reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity achieved sufficient ratings, with the evidence quality rated as high. Additionally, the SDQ is operationally efficient (brief format, consistent across parent/teacher/self-report versions) and broadly accessible (freely available in multiple languages).

However, the SDQ is a broad-band behavioral screening instrument, and its hyperactivity/inattention subscale should not be equated with a dedicated ADHD diagnostic measure. As a broad-band screener, it may be useful for initial case identification or assessment of comorbidity, but it is not a substitute for ADHD-specific instruments developed for diagnostic support or symptom severity assessment. Therefore, the SDQ may be considered an option for clinical use, pending further research that includes direct instrument comparisons and evaluation of predictive validity and diagnostic accuracy in the target population. Future studies should also investigate content validity, measurement error, and responsiveness.

Both the CADBI and FTF subscales showed favorable results across the measurement properties assessed to date; however, the evidence for each remained incomplete, with several key properties yet to be examined. By contrast, CABI—a recently developed broad-band instrument with an emerging body of literature—demonstrated reliability supported by high quality evidence. Its structural validity and internal consistency were rated as adequate based on low quality evidence, though these findings were constrained by methodological heterogeneity and insufficient sample representativeness. Future research on the CABI subscales should adopt more rigorous study designs and larger, more representative samples. Overall, CABI shows considerable promise and merits further development and validation.

ANSER-PQ, CBAI, ECI-4, YI-4, and DSMD generally lack detailed documentation of their developmental processes. Moreover, only a single study has examined the measurement properties of each instrument to date, precluding any meaningful evidence synthesis. For the ABC and CSI-4, only two studies have evaluated their measurement properties; however, both were conducted by the same research group, and the reported properties and corresponding evidence grades were largely unsatisfactory. Furthermore, the available literature for these instruments was published over a decade ago, potentially limiting their applicability in contemporary research and practice.

Current state of research on measurement properties

In this systematic review of 36 studies, significant disparities were identified in the assessment of measurement properties. Structural validity and convergent validity were examined in 16 studies (44.4%), and internal consistency was examined in 28 (77.8%). In contrast, discriminant validity (13, 36.1%), cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance (9, 25.0%), and test–retest reliability (8, 22.2%) were less frequently assessed. These findings indicate that existing literature has prioritized internal consistency, structural validity, and convergent validity, while other measurement properties remain underrepresented. Notably, no scale fully met the COSMIN guidelines for systematic evaluation. Beyond the absence of content validity (a core property), criterion validity, measurement error, and responsiveness also remain insufficiently validated.

Content validity, the core measurement property in the COSMIN framework, has not been rigorously established for existing ADHD broad-band scales. Specifically, formal assessment of item relevance, comprehensiveness, and comprehensibility is absent. Although some scales provide development documentation, most exhibit critical methodological limitations: poorly defined target populations and applicability scenarios, inadequate description of item generation and expert review processes, and insufficient transparency regarding interview protocols, moderator qualifications, and data saturation evidence. Moreover, the validity evidence for certain measurement instruments was rated solely by researchers, resulting in very low evidence quality. These findings suggest that current ADHD broad-band scales have significant deficiencies in normative instrument development and content validity validation, which urgently need to be addressed through standardized psychometric validation studies. The COSMIN guidelines suggest obtaining content validity evidence by searching the original research manual. However, for many of the older instruments included in this review (e.g., ANSER-PQ, DSMD), original development manuals or detailed documentation were not publicly available or could not be retrieved. This may reflect the fact that early development processes for some scales were kept in internal archives and not published publicly, limiting our ability to independently verify content validity evidence.

Criterion validity for ADHD broad-band subscales requires comparison with a gold-standard instrument; however, no universally accepted gold standard exists for ADHD, creating methodological challenges specific to this field. Many of these broad-band instruments were developed as general behavioral screeners rather than ADHD-specific measures, which complicates establishing criterion validity when their subscales are applied to ADHD assessment. These factors likely explain the absence of criterion validity studies across all included instruments. Furthermore, some measurement instruments are developed without a clear structure, further complicating criterion validity assessments. Measurement error analysis necessitates evaluating random error through repeated measurements, while responsiveness assessment demands longitudinal data tracking to determine an instrument's sensitivity to change over time. Both approaches are resource-intensive and time-consuming. Moreover, the iterative nature of scale development, wherein new versions may require years to accumulate sufficient validation data, contributes to the scarcity of such studies.

Limitations

First, the limited quantity of research and heterogeneous methodological quality across studies prevented any single instrument from fulfilling assessments across all COSMIN-recommended measurement properties, thereby limiting a comprehensive, multidimensional analysis. Second, restricting inclusion to English-language publications may have introduced publication bias and precluded full assessment of cross-cultural validity and measurement invariance; future updates should incorporate non‑English literature. Third, the COSMIN framework, while highly structured, does not fully accommodate certain methodological nuances. The framework does not differentiate between rotation methods or eigenvalue criteria in exploratory factor analyses, nor between types of correlation coefficients. In our review, studies using PCA or different rotation methods were evaluated under the same structural validity criteria, potentially masking analytical differences. Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha, widely reported in included studies, is increasingly considered less appropriate as a reliability index. The uniform COSMIN criteria may therefore not fully capture evolving psychometric standards. Fourth, as Geck et al. [55] highlighted, there is inherent subjectivity in applying the COSMIN framework despite its operationalized nature. Additionally, modern approaches such as network analysis or bifactor modeling are not accommodated within the current framework; however, few included studies employed these methods, and their exclusion did not alter the overall conclusions. Fifth, for several older instruments, the original development studies provided insufficient detail on content validity, and supplementary development manuals were not publicly available; consequently, these ratings relied heavily on reviewer judgment, which may have introduced subjectivity. Sixth, we evaluated the CBCL Attention Problems syndrome scale rather than the DSM-oriented ADHD scale, limiting conclusions about the CBCL's utility as an ADHD-specific screener.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated broad-band behavioral scales for ADHD, providing evidence-based guidance for clinical instrument selection. According to COSMIN criteria, the CBCL Attention Problems subscale raises concerns regarding suitability for users outside North America, as high quality evidence demonstrates insufficient cross-cultural validity/measurement invariance in ADHD populations. Among the other included instruments, the SDQ Hyperactivity-Inattention subscale demonstrated comparatively favorable measurement properties, including moderate quality evidence for structural validity and internal consistency, and high quality evidence for cross-cultural validity, reliability, convergent validity and discriminant validity. However, this does not establish its overall superiority for ADHD assessment. Future studies should evaluate content validity, measurement error, and responsiveness following COSMIN guidelines to further enhance the psychometric quality and clinical applicability of these instruments, ultimately improving early identification and long-term management of children with ADHD.
