Serum Lipidomics Profiling Identify Novel Biomarkers of Distal Symmetrical Polyneuropathy in Type 1 Diabetes
Authors: Tik Muk, Tina Okdahl, Marietta Kokla, Tommi Suvitaival, Gurjeet Kaur, Ismo M Mattila, Simon M Kienle, Christian Buhl, Knud Yderstræde, Troels S Jensen, Peter Rossing, Flemming Pociot, Birgitte Brock, Cristina Legido‐Quigley, Páll Karlsson, Christian S Hansen, Christina Brock, Joachim Størling, Karolina Sulek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDistal symmetrical polyneuropathy (DSPN) is a common complication of type 1 diabetes (T1D), yet validated biomarkers for early detection or prognosis are lacking. Metabolic disturbances driven by chronic hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia contribute to DSPN development and progression.MethodsWe used untargeted serum liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry lipidomics to identify DSPN biomarkers. The discovery cohort included 153 individuals with T1D (109 with and 44 without DSPN) and 50 non‐diabetic controls. The independent validation cohort included 99 individuals with T1D with established DSPN status. Key lipids were identified using multivariate modelling, followed by ANCOVA and adjusted post hoc tests.ResultsA total of 543 lipid species were identified in the discovery cohort. Among these, 14 lipids were associated with DSPN. Of which, 6 lipids, namely Cer(d42:1), PC(36:4), LPC(16:0), LPE(18:1), PE(36:2), and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4), showed a clear pattern among non‐diabetic individuals, people affected by T1D with and without DSPN. In the validation cohort, 3 of the 6 initially identified lipids showed the same directional changes as in the discovery cohort. A logistic regression model combining the six lipid biomarkers with HbA1c, diastolic blood pressure, and age achieved AUCs of 0.83 (95% CI, 0.76–0.91) in the discovery cohort and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.73–0.90) in the validation cohort for detecting DSPN in T1D. AUC improvement was not significant by DeLong's test in either cohort (p = 0.086 and 0.089) but was significant when combined using Fisher's method (χ2 = 9.7, p = 0.045). Additionally, 22 lipid species differed significantly between painful and painless DSPN.ConclusionsWe identified a reproducible lipidomic signature discriminating DSPN in type 1 diabetes. Painful DSPN was characterized by reduced unsaturated PC/PE and increased sphingolipids, offering new insight into disease pathophysiology.Keywords: distal symmetrical polyneuropathy, dyslipidemia, lipid biomarkers, lipidomics, painful neuropathy, serum lipid profiling, type 1 diabetes, untargeted liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry lipidomics

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects over 800 million individuals worldwide, with prevalence projected to increase further [1]. Distal symmetrical polyneuropathy (DSPN) is a chronic peripheral nerve complication affecting up to 50% of individuals with diabetes [2]. Early DSPN primarily involves small and partially myelinated nerve fibres, leading to sensory symptoms such as pain, tingling, or loss of sensation in the toes. With progression, neuropathy spreads proximally in a length‐dependent manner and may extend to the trunk. DSPN is a major contributor to foot ulcers, falls, and lower‐limb amputations in individuals with DM [2, 3].

Diagnosis of DSPN in individuals with DM remains challenging due to subtle, nonspecific symptoms, overlap with other neuropathies, poor screening adherence, and limited detection at reversible stages, often resulting in delayed diagnosis [4]. Similar to other diabetic complications, such delays are associated with irreversible nerve damage and increased risk of amputation [2]. Currently, no established curative treatment exists beyond general diabetes management strategies, largely due to incomplete understanding of DSPN pathogenesis [5]. Evidence suggests that metabolic alterations—including hypertension, hyperglycemia, and dyslipidemia—contribute to DSPN through excessive activation of pro‐inflammatory signalling pathways that promote neurotoxicity [6].

Although lipid metabolism‐related genes, such as apolipoprotein E, leptin, and peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) gamma, have been implicated in DSPN pathogenesis [6], the associations between dyslipidemia and DSPN are less consistent than those for other metabolic risk factors [7]. Furthermore, dyslipidemia is a broad term encompassing diverse lipid abnormalities, including variations in triglycerides, LDL cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol. The specific lipid impairment linked to DSPN remains uncertain and inconsistent across studies.

Lipidomics enables the large‐scale analysis of various lipid species in biological specimens. Previous studies on lipid fingerprinting and DSPN have primarily relied on cross‐sectional analyses of hospital‐based populations or animal models [8, 9, 10]. Few large longitudinal studies have investigated the association between specific lipid parameters and DSPN risk in type 2 diabetes cohorts while accounting for other metabolic risk factors [11]. While lipidomic research has provided insights into metabolic dysregulation in type 2 diabetes and other inflammatory conditions, lipidomic profiling in type 1 diabetes and DSPN remains largely unexplored. Here, we applied LC‐MS technology to investigate lipid alterations in individuals with type 1 diabetes with or without DSPN. Our study aimed to identify novel lipid biomarkers for risk stratification and potential therapeutic targeting.

Research Design and Methods

Clinical Study

This study combined two cohorts for discovery and validation phases. The discovery cohort comprised a cross‐sectional study involving 153 individuals with type 1 diabetes (109 with and 44 without DSPN), alongside a control group of 50 non‐diabetic individuals [12]. Of the 109 participants with DSPN enrolled in the discovery cohort, 108 were included in the lipidomics analyses due to sample availability. Additionally, a validation cohort consisting of 99 individuals with type 1 diabetes was utilized for biomarker validation [13]. All studies were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, local regulations, and International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practise guidelines. All subjects gave their written informed consent before participation. The protocol, amendments, and informed consent form were approved by The Central Denmark Region Committees (1‐10‐72‐22‐25) for the discovery cohort and The North Denmark Region Committee (N‐20170045, N‐20130077, N‐20090008) for validation cohorts on Health Research Ethics Denmark, respectively. Detailed exclusion and inclusion criteria are described in Tables S1 and S2. DSPN diagnosis was reached following the Toronto consensus criteria for definite neuropathy for the discovery cohort or standardized nerve conduction testing for the DSPN individuals in the validation cohort [14]. DSPN individuals from the discovery and validation cohorts were pooled into a DSPN group and classified as either painless or painful based on the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) or DN4 questionnaire (Douleur Neuropathique en 4 Questions), with an average score of ≥ 4 defining painful DSPN (n = 95 for painless and n = 57 for painful DSPN) [15]. Serum samples were collected and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Serum Lipidomics Profiling

Serum sample preparation for lipidomic analysis has been described previously [16]. Briefly, 10 μL serum was mixed on ice with 10 μL 0.9% (w/v) NaCl and 120 μL chloroform: methanol (2:1, v:v) containing internal standards. After vortexing, samples were incubated on ice for 30 min and centrifuged (3 min, 10 000 rpm). The chloroform phase (60 μL) was transferred to LC vials. Blank samples, pooled experimental samples, and in‐house pooled clinical serum standards were included as quality controls, with the latter used to confirm lipid identification across cohorts.

LC–MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 6200 LC‐Q‐TOF system (MassHunter Data Acquisition v.B.09.00) using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) with a matching guard column. Mobile phases consisted of (A) water and (B) acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1), both containing 1% 1 M ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid. Separation was conducted at 0.4 mL/min and 50°C using a gradient from 35% to 100% B over 9 min, held for 7 min, followed by 1 min re‐equilibration. Data were acquired in positive and negative ion modes using a dual Agilent Jet Stream Electrospray Ionization (AJS ESI) source.

Samples were analysed in randomized order with quality control samples injected at regular intervals. Lipidomics data were preprocessed using Skyline v.23.1.0.455 [17], normalized to internal standards, and quantified using lipid class–specific calibration (0.1–10 μg/mL) as previously described [18]. Data were matched against an in‐house library, identifying 590 lipid features across 22 lipid classes at identification levels 1 or 2. Lipids with > 20% missing values across all samples or relative standard deviation (RSD) > 20% in quality control samples were excluded. Peak tables from positive and negative ion modes were merged, retaining the polarity with higher signal where features overlapped.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis of Demographic Data and Clinical Cohort Characteristics

Demographic and clinical characteristics of individuals included in the lipidomic analysis were analysed. Categorical variables are presented as proportions, and continuous variables as medians (IQR). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and non‐normally distributed variables were log‐transformed. Group comparisons were performed using one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests, as appropriate. Post hoc analyses used Bonferroni or Dunn's multiple comparison tests. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Multivariate Variable Selection of Individual Molecular Lipid Species

To identify lipidomic signatures distinguishing type 1 diabetes patients with and without DSPN, we applied two machine learning approaches. Discovery samples were split into training and validation sets to enable unbiased model development and independent performance evaluation. Random forest‐based multivariate modelling with unbiased variable selection in R (MUVR) [19] was used to select informative features from 565 annotated lipid species, with 50 repetitions, 80% variable retention per iteration, 8 outer cross‐validation segments, and 25 permutations. Analyses were performed with and without non‐diabetic controls to identify lipid species specific to type 1 diabetes or DSPN. DSPN‐associated lipids were further evaluated using ANCOVA with adjustment for sex, age, HbA1c, and diastolic BP. These covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior evidence linking them to lipid metabolism and risk of nerve damage. MUVR findings were independently validated using XGBoost (XGB) [20].

To identify clusters of highly correlated, functionally related lipid species, weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) was applied to the lipidomic data [21]. WGCNA groups variables based on pairwise correlations to define biologically relevant modules. Analyses were performed on log2‐transformed data using a signed network, biweight midcorrelation, and average‐linkage hierarchical clustering, with a minimum module size of 13 and a dendrogram merge threshold of 0.2.

Lipids associated with DSPN in the discovery cohort were evaluated in the validation cohort using the same ANCOVA model with adjusting for the clinical covariates described above. An adjusted p‐value < 0.05 was considered significant. Classification performance was assessed using confusion matrices. Predictive performance of selected potential lipid biomarkers was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, with AUC and 95% CI reported. Models were tested with and without clinical parameters, and AUCs were compared using a bootstrap approach (pROC package). DeLong's test was calculated to compare the areas under the two ROC curves. Statistical significance of improvement in AUC over the two cohorts collectively was tested with Fisher's combined probability test (poolr package). Spearman correlations between significant lipid species and clinical characteristics relevant to diabetic neuropathy were calculated and visualized using heatmaps (corrplot R package), with correlations at FDR < 0.05 highlighted.

Painful Versus Painless Lipidomic Signature

To assess pain‐related lipidomic signatures, all type 1 diabetes participants with DSPN from both cohorts were pooled and compared between painful versus painless DSPN. Statistical analyses followed the methods described above, including MUVR modelling and ANCOVA. Lipid Ontology (LION) enrichment was performed using LION/web to identify enriched LION terms among pain‐associated lipids [22]. Significant pain‐related lipids were further correlated with inflammatory markers—including cytokines, chemokines, and soluble adhesion molecules—previously measured in this cohort [13], using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. All the statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.1.2).

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Cohorts

The study design is illustrated in Figure 1A and the participant characteristics for the discovery cohort are described in Table 1 and Figure S1. Participants with available serum samples were included and classified into three groups: (1) non‐diabetic individuals, (2) individuals with type 1 diabetes without DSPN (T1D group), and (3) individuals with type 1 diabetes and DSPN (T1D + DSPN group). Sex, BMI, smoking status, systolic BP, and diastolic BP were well matched between the T1D and T1D + DSPN groups. However, the T1D + DSPN group was considerably older (p < 0.001) and had a longer duration of diabetes (p = 0.001) than the T1D group. Moreover, patients with DSPN exhibited higher HbA1c levels compared to those with type 1 diabetes without DSPN (p < 0.001). The vibration perception test (biothesiometry) revealed significant differences between the groups (all p < 0.001). In the validation cohort, in addition to age (p = 0.01) and a longer diabetes duration (p < 0.001), the DSPN subjects had a significantly higher BMI (p = 0.01) and diastolic BP (p = 0.002) than people with type 1 diabetes without DSPN. Baseline characteristics of the 101 individuals with type 1 diabetes included in the validation cohort are noted in Table S3 and Figure 1A.

Lipid Profiling

A total of 575 lipid species (of a library screening > 667 lipids) spanning 22 individual lipid classes was identified and semi quantitated in the serum lipidome of the discovery cohort (n = 203) and validation cohort (n = 120), after removing peaks without satisfactory signal‐to‐noise ratios (< 3). 532 lipid species (92.5% of total annotated) were found in both cohorts (Figure 1B). The WGCNA identified 22 initial lipid modules, which were subsequently merged into 12 highly correlated modules (Figure 1C). Lipids displayed strong intra‐family correlations; however, none of the modules exhibited significant differences between the T1D and T1D + DSPN groups.

Lipid Profiles Associated With DSPN in Type 1 Diabetes

To identify individual lipids that differentiate type 1 diabetes individuals with DSPN from type 1 diabetes only and non‐diabetic controls, machine learning with multivariate modelling analyses were performed (Figure S2). Among the 575 lipids included in these models, we identified 14 altered lipid candidates for classifying people with type 1 diabetes and DSPN from type 1 diabetes without DSPN, and 37 altered lipid candidates for classifying type 1 diabetes with DSPN from type 1 diabetes without DSPN and controls in the discovery cohort (Figure 1D,E). The 14 DSPN‐related lipids, namely TG(42:2), PC(36:4), LPC(16:0), LPC(20:3), PE(36:2), PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4), LPE(18:1), Cer(d42:1), TG(52:8), FA(16:0), TG(55:5), PI(34:1), PE(34:1), and PG(40:4) were found to exhibit the highest permuted variable importance across both models. Six of the 14 lipids, that is, Cer(d42:1), PC(36:4), LPC(16:0), LPE(18:1), PE(36:2) and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4) exhibited a consistent directional (monotonic) trend across the three groups (Figure 2A). All relative level changes from the analyses are reported in Table S4. The changes were similar across all three patient groups and included higher levels of lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE), phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) and triglycerides (TG), lower levels of phosphatidylcholines (PC), lysophosphatidylcholines (LPC) and ceramides (Cer), also when adjusting for age, sex, HbA1c and diastolic BP.

The total 37 lipids associated with DSPN were taken for validation in the validation cohort. In the T1D versus T1D + DSPN group comparison, 11 of 14 lipids were detected (Figure 1E, Table S5), and three of the six lipids showing a monotonic trend across the three groups reached statistical significance in the validation cohort (Figure 2B). Specifically, LPC(16:0) and PE(36:2) were significantly downregulated, while PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4) was significantly upregulated (p < 0.05, Figure 2A,B). The remaining lipids, Cer(d42:1), PC(36:4), and LPE(18:1), did not differ significantly between groups (p > 0.05). All the selected lipid species were further validated by XGB modelling (Figure S3).

Associations Between Lipids and Clinical Parameters

We next examined the associations between the selected lipid features and various clinical outcomes. Of the 14 lipid features examined, 11 were significantly associated with sensory nerve fibre functionality‐related parameters in the discovery cohort, including biothesiometry (sensory loss of large fibres), DNPCheck amplitude (small fibres axonal damage), and conduction velocity (demyelination and axonal damage of large fibres), while 7 were linked to disease duration (Figure 2C). Notably, eight lipids, including LPE(18:1), PC(36:4), PG(40:4), TG(52:8), Cer(d42:1), LPC(16:0), PE(36:2), and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4), displayed at least two significant associations with nerve damage related parameters. Nine lipids were significantly associated with HbA1c, of which three exhibited positive correlations and six showed negative correlations. Additionally, six lipids were significantly correlated with BMI. Correlation analysis from the validation cohort further revealed that 7 of the 11 lipids were significantly associated with clinical lipids, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL, and HDL (Figure S4). These findings underscore how metabolic lipid changes reflected in lipid profiles may be linked to the status of diabetic neuropathy.

Lipid Panel Signature for DSPN Diagnosis in Type 1 Diabetes

To establish a concise lipid signature capable of discriminating between type 1 diabetes individuals with DSPN and those with type 1 diabetes only, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of the selected lipid markers. In the discovery cohort, we identified a six‐lipid molecular signature that, when combined with basic clinical parameters (HbA1c, diastolic blood pressure, and age), demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.76–0.91; Figure 2D). This panel signature comprises Cer(d42:1), PC(36:4), LPC(16:0), LPE(18:1), PE(36:2), and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4). The validation cohort confirmed the robustness of this six‐lipid panel, yielding a similarly high diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.73–0.90; Figure 2E). Compared to the combined lipid signature and clinical parameters, the use of clinical parameters alone resulted in lower diagnostic accuracy, with AUCs of 0.79 versus 0.83 in the discovery cohort (95% CI: 0.71–0.87) and 0.76 versus 0.81 in the validation cohort (95% CI: 0.66–0.85). DeLong's test for the difference between AUCs was Z = −1.71, p = 0.086, in the discovery cohort, and Z = −1.70, p = 0.089, in the validation cohort. When assessing these results from the two cohorts collectively, Fisher's combined probability test indicated statistically significant overall improvement in AUC (χ 2 = 9.7; p = 0.045).

Lipid Profiles and Painful DSPN

To further investigate lipidomic profiling changes between painful and painless DSPN, all individuals with type 1 diabetes and DSPN from the discovery and validation cohorts were pooled for a sub‐analysis. In total, 57 individuals with painful DSPN and 97 with painless DSPN were included, with their clinical characteristics summarized in Table S6. Participants with painful DSPN had a shorter disease duration (p = 0.027) but higher biothesiometry levels (p = 0.029) compared to those with painless DSPN. Additionally, there were more males with painless DSPN (p = 0.045).

In the pooled data, 35 out of 532 lipid species were selected by the model to classify painful and painless DSPN. Subsequent post hoc statistical analysis, adjusted for clinical confounders (age, gender, and HbA1c), revealed 22 lipid species that were significantly altered in painful DSPN (Figure 3A). Among these, all six sphingolipids, including three ceramides and three sphingomyelins, were elevated in painful DSPN. In contrast, the significantly regulated glycerophospholipids (GPLs) showed a mixed pattern. Most of the highly unsaturated GPLs were downregulated in association with pain. Interestingly, three lipids, namely PC(36:4), Cer(d42:1), and PI(34:1) were also identified as DSPN markers. The pain‐related lipids were further analysed using LION enrichment analysis (Figure 3B), which revealed that these lipids were highly enriched in species characterized by low transition temperature and high lateral diffusion. We finally assessed the associations between pain‐related lipids and circulating inflammatory markers (Figure 3C). Of 13 inflammatory markers measured, 6 (ICAM‐1, IL‐12p70, IL‐13, IP‐10/CXCL10, MCP‐1, and E‐selectin) showed significant correlations with more than four pain‐related lipids. Notably, Cer(d42:2), LPC(P‐16:0), LPC(20:3), and PE(42:8) exhibited the strongest associations with the inflammatory markers. Most correlations were positive, whereas E‐selectin was distinct in displaying negative correlations with LPC(P‐16:0) and PC(42:7).

Discussion

Novel DSPN Biomarkers in Type 1 Diabetes

We identified a signature of six molecular lipids and validated its performance in differentiating type 1 diabetes patients with DSPN from symptomatic controls in a cross‐sectional validation cohort. The combination of the lipidomic signature with clinical parameters resulted in higher AUCs than the model based on clinical parameters alone. The improvement was statistically significant across the two cohorts collectively, yet not in each of the cohorts individually, likely due to limited sample size. From a clinical translation perspective, these findings suggest that a signature comprising Cer(d42:1), PC(36:4), LPC(16:0), LPE(18:1), PE(36:2), and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4), together with established clinical risk factors, may provide incremental value for the reclassification of patients with DSPN from asymptomatic controls in the validation cohort. Participants that developed painful neuropathy also displayed a unique serum lipidomics signature versus participants that are painless. These findings indicate lipid changes may be related to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation in participants that develop peripheral neuropathy.

Biochemical Interpretation of the Lipid Biomarkers

In this study, we found that circulating Cer(d42:1) was significantly lower in type 1 diabetes patients with DSPN compared to type 1 diabetes only. A reduction in Cer(d42:1) may reflect disruptions in sphingolipid homeostasis, potentially leading to impaired myelin stability and increased susceptibility to oxidative stress [23]. Moreover, ceramide metabolism is closely linked to insulin signalling and lipid raft composition, and its dysregulation may exacerbate neuroinflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which are key contributors to nerve damage in diabetic neuropathy [24, 25]. Additionally, ceramides bound to LDL have been shown to stimulate the expression of inflammatory genes in macrophages and to increase insulin resistance in skeletal myocytes [26].

We identified decreased levels of the saturated lysophosphatidylcholine LPC(16:0) and the unsaturated phosphatidylcholine PC(36:4) in type 1 diabetes patients with DSPN compared with type 1 diabetes and non‐diabetic controls. LPC(16:0), a bioactive lipid generated from phosphatidylcholine hydrolysis via phospholipase A2, plays important roles in neuronal signalling, inflammation, and vascular function [27]. In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β‐cells leads to insulin deficiency and increased lipid oxidation, which may accelerate LPC metabolism and contribute to reduced circulating LPC levels [28]. Interestingly, previous studies have shown that elevated LPC(16:0) levels in addition to decreased levels of PC(36:4) are associated with the development of diabetic neuropathy in type 2 diabetes [10] and with various chronic pain states, including neuropathic pain [29, 30, 31]. PC(36:4), a major membrane phospholipid, has been reported as consistently reduced before type 1 diabetes onset [32], although increased levels have also been observed in antibody‐positive individuals [33]. In the discovery cohort, PC(36:4) and LPC(16:0) were both negatively associated with changes in BMI, pulse, and HbA1c levels, possibly reflecting their roles in metabolic regulation, lipid metabolism, and glycemic control. These findings underscore the potential of PC and LPC species (especially PC(36:4) and LPC(16:0)) as biomarkers for diabetic neuropathy and highlight the role of lipid imbalances in disease pathogenesis through mechanisms involving inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disrupted lipid signalling.

In this study, we also detected a depletion in serum PE(36:2), and elevations in LPE(18:1) and in the highly unsaturated PE plasmalogens (PE(P‐40:4)), PE ethers (PE(O‐40:5)) levels. The increase in LPE(18:1) may result from enhanced Lands cycle turnover of PE(36:2), suggesting altered phospholipid homeostasis or cellular stress in type 1 diabetes with DSPN. Abnormal levels of glycerophospholipids, usually plasmalogens, have been described in several pathologic conditions involving neurodegeneration. This suggests disruptions in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress responses, and neuroinflammatory pathways. PE depletion may impair membrane integrity and mitochondrial function, exacerbating neuronal damage [34]. Plasmalogen PEs, known for their antioxidative properties, may increase as a compensatory response to oxidative stress, but their dysregulation can contribute to lipid peroxidation and neurodegeneration [35]. Moreover, LPE(18:1) and PE(O‐40:5) or PE(P‐40:4) were positively correlated with HbA1c, while PE(36:2) was negatively associated with BMI and HbA1c levels. These alterations highlight lipid remodelling as a key driver in the pathogenesis of diabetic neuropathy, linking mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory lipid mediators.

Clinical Translation and Validation of Candidate DSPN Lipid Biomarkers

The present findings support the potential utility of a six‐lipid panel as candidate biomarkers for DSPN reclassification in type 1 diabetes. However, only three of the six candidate lipids with monotonic trends showed statistically significant differences between T1D and T1D with DSPN in the validation cohort. This partial validation may reflect differences in DSPN diagnostic methods, cohort characteristics, and sample size. DSPN diagnosis was based on the Toronto consensus criteria for definite neuropathy in the discovery cohort, whereas standardized nerve conduction testing was used in the validation cohort. The smaller validation cohort also limited the power to detect lipids with modest effect sizes. In addition, DSPN is a heterogeneous complication with multifactorial pathophysiology, and the partial validation may reflect biological variability in disease mechanisms and lipid metabolism.

Clinical translation of this lipid panel would require further assay development and validation. A targeted, standardized LC‐MS/MS method with appropriate authentic internal standards, quality‐control procedures, absolute quantification, and externally validated cut‐offs would be needed to ensure reproducibility across laboratories. Cost and turnaround time should also be considered, although a targeted lipid panel is likely to be more feasible for clinical use than untargeted lipidomics. In practice, such a panel would most likely serve as an adjunct to existing DSPN screening approaches rather than a stand‐alone diagnostic test, helping to identify individuals with type 1 diabetes who may benefit from closer neurological evaluation or more frequent follow‐up. Future prospective studies in larger, well‐characterized cohorts using harmonized DSPN assessment methods are warranted to determine the incremental predictive value, cost‐effectiveness, and feasibility of this panel for implementation in routine diabetes care.

Lipidomic Associations With Pain

We further extended these findings by demonstrating that the association between lipidome alterations and pain sensitization was particularly pronounced in patients with painful DSPN, as reflected by the differential regulation of 35 MUVR‐selected lipids, with 14 upregulated and 9 downregulated significantly compared to the painless DSPN group. The LION enrichment analysis identified lipid classes characterized by low transition temperatures and high lateral diffusion, biophysical properties typically attributed to highly unsaturated GPLs. In painful DSPN, a reduction in unsaturated PC and PE species, accompanied by an accumulation of sphingolipids, might indicate a compositional shift toward more ordered and rigid neuronal membranes with diminished fluidity, which may underlie altered axonal excitability and pain signalling. Notably, our data revealed that a specific subset of sphingolipids, including unsaturated Cer(d42:1), Cer(d42:2), SM(d42:2), and SM(d36:3), along with saturated Cer(d34:0) and SM(d36:0), was significantly upregulated in the painful DSPN group. Ceramides have previously been reported to bind to LDL and stimulate the expression of inflammatory related genes in macrophages, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐a, while also contributing to insulin resistance in skeletal myocytes [36]. In our study, these pain‐associated sphingolipids were positively correlated with several pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including IP‐10/CXCL10, MCP‐1, MIP‐1α, and MIP‐1β. Chemokines play essential roles in the nervous system, influencing neuroendocrine function, neurotransmitter signalling, and neurodegeneration [37]. Additionally, elevated ceramide levels have been linked to beta cell apoptosis, a key diabetogenic process contributing to insulin dysregulation [38]. The observed positive associations between sphingolipids and chemokines in painful DSPN may reflect a broader pathological interplay between lipid metabolism and inflammatory signalling pathways. Sphingolipids, particularly ceramides, are known to activate pro‐inflammatory cascades such as NF‐κB signalling, thereby amplifying the production of chemokines like MCP‐1 and IP‐10 [25]. Ceramides can also be transformed into ceramide 1‐phosphate, or they can undergo further degradation into sphingosine, which, in turn, can be phosphorylated to produce sphingosine 1‐phosphate (S1P). These molecules play a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory processes [25]. This, in turn, can promote macrophage and T‐cell recruitment, perpetuating local inflammation and exacerbating neuronal injury. Together, these interactions may contribute to the chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress that underlie peripheral nerve damage and pain sensitization in diabetic neuropathy.

Among the significantly regulated lipids, GPLs exhibited distinct patterns of upregulation and downregulation, suggesting disruptions in lipid homeostasis that may contribute to the pain sensation in DSPN. Upregulated long‐chain unsaturated species, including PC(35:1), PE(36:1), PI(34:1), and PI(38:3), may reflect compensatory mechanisms aimed at maintaining membrane fluidity and cellular signalling under metabolic stress. In contrast, several downregulated GPLs, including LPC(P‐16:0), PC(P‐36:5) or PC(O‐36:6), PG(36:3), PA(43:6), and PC(42:7), may indicate mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired membrane remodelling, or altered phospholipid biosynthesis. Notably, LPC(P‐16:0) exhibited positive correlations with pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐4, IL‐13, and IL‐17A, as well as a negative correlation with E‐selectin, suggesting its potential role in modulating immune responses and endothelial activation. Meanwhile, PG(36:3) showed a negative correlation with TNF‐α, which may reflect its role in anti‐inflammatory or mitochondrial protective pathways. These lipid‐cytokine associations highlight a complex interplay between lipid metabolism, inflammation, and vascular dysfunction in DN, potentially contributing to peripheral nerve injury and pain sensitization.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths and important limitations. One of the limitations of this study is its cross‐sectional design, which limited the assessment of causality, and the fact that patients with and without DSPN, as well as non‐diabetic controls, could not be matched by sex, age, HbA1c levels, or disease duration. However, we adjusted for sex, age, HbA1c, and diastolic blood pressure in all statistical models to control confounders. Additionally, the discovery and validation cohorts exhibited differences in clinical characteristics, reflecting real‐world conditions. This variability strengthens our findings, ensuring the presented results remain robust and generalizable.

Secondly, the number of patients in each disease category was based on the inclusion of each cohort, that is, the number of recruited patients during the study period, and was not predefined by sample size and power analysis.

Another limitation of this study is the potential for residual confounding. Participants with DSPN were older and had longer diabetes duration, both of which may influence lipidomic profiles. Although we adjusted for sex, age, HbA1c, and diastolic blood pressure, diabetes duration was not included in the primary model due to its collinearity with age and its close relationship with DSPN status. Other metabolic factors, including renal function, insulin sensitivity, and lipid‐lowering medications, may also affect lipid levels, but access to these data was limited in the present study. These findings should therefore be validated in larger cohorts with predefined study designs and more comprehensive adjustment strategies.

A key strength of this study is the serum lipidome profiling of two independent, well‐phenotyped type 1 diabetes cohorts from multiple Steno Diabetes Centers, which ensures strong representation of real‐world clinical practise and enhances the generalizability of the findings across clinical settings. The study is further strengthened by the use of a standardized questionnaire with strict diagnostic criteria to differentiate between painful and painless DSPN in type 1 diabetes individuals. To identify a diagnostic lipidomic signature and assess its diagnostic accuracy, we used supervised machine learning methodologies, which increase the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusions

We have identified a reproducible lipidomic signature that, in combination with clinical variables, robustly discriminates DSPN in type 1 diabetes. These biomarkers are mechanistically linked to pathways involving mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation. However, prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to assess the predictive capacity, establish causality, and evaluate whether modulation of these lipid pathways—potentially through targeted anti‐inflammatory or metabolic interventions—can prevent or mitigate DSPN.
