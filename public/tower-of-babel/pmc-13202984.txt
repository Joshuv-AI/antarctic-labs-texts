Eagle syndrome causing cerebral infarction attributed to the synergistic effect of mild compression of the styloid process and carotid artery arteriosclerosis: a case report
Authors: Taiki Tokuyama, Yuki Sakaeyama, Mitsuyoshi Abe, Shuhei Kubota, Chie Matsuura, Yutaka Fuchinoue, Sayaka Terazono, Nobuo Sugo
Published: 2026

Background

Eagle syndrome, which was first described by W.W. Eagle in 1937, is attributed to the physical compression of the surrounding structures caused by an elongated styloid process and/or a calcified stylohyoid ligament [1–3]. The length of the styloid process is typically between 20 and 30 mm, and a styloid process with a length > 30 mm is considered elongated [4–6]. The main symptoms of Eagle syndrome range from those related to the neck and face to severe neurological manifestations caused by cerebral infarction. A sensation of something being stuck in the back of the throat, which can lead to dysphagia, is a typical manifestation. Throat or ear pain and tinnitus are also often observed [3, 7]. The other common symptoms include neck pain and restricted neck movement. Due to compression of the glossopharyngeal or trigeminal nerves by the elongated styloid process or calcification of the hyoid ligament, headaches and facial pain may occur [7]. In particular, the important symptoms of Eagle syndrome are neurological abnormalities associated with cerebrovascular events, such as transient ischemic attacks and cerebral infarction, a condition known as stylocarotid syndrome [2, 8–11]. The mechanism by which cerebral infarction occurs in Eagle syndrome mainly involves impaired blood flow caused by direct compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process, and arterial dissection or thrombus formation [4, 10]. Herein, we report a rare case of Eagle syndrome in which mild compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process and stylohyoid ligament caused arterial tortuosity and severe stenosis, leading to cerebral infarction.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old Japanese female patient presented with a medical history of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (post-catheter ablation), and stage 4 chronic kidney disease. She was urgently transferred from another hospital after experiencing sudden-onset aphasia. Upon arrival, the patient’s neurological symptoms had resolved, and she did not exhibit aphasia nor limb paralysis, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4V5M6. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed upon arrival at the Emergency Room, and no screening computed tomography (CT) scan was conducted. Upon the initial examination, no new ischemic lesions were observed on diffusion-weighted imaging/MRI (Fig. 1a). However, first magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) revealed stenosis of the left middle cerebral artery (Fig. 1b). The second MRA, performed the day after the onset, showed that the stenosis of the left middle cerebral artery had resolved and returned to normal. Electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm without atrial fibrillation. In addition, an intracardiac thrombus was not detected on transthoracic echocardiogram. Therefore, the patient was treated conservatively with antiplatelet therapy. At 8 days after the initial episode, she did not exhibit neurological symptoms. However, a new cerebral infarction was observed in the left parietal lobe on diffusion-weighted imaging/MRI (Fig. 2a). The third MRA, performed at that time, showed no stenosis of the left middle cerebral artery (Fig. 2b). Carotid ultrasonography revealed mild stenosis at the left carotid bifurcation with an elevated peak systolic velocity at 190 cm/s. Digital subtraction cerebral angiography (DSA) showed that the tip of the styloid process and the calcified stylohyoid ligament were compressing a portion of the left internal carotid artery, causing mild stenosis (Fig. 3a). Further, in the proximal segment of the internal carotid artery, severe vascular stenosis attributed to vascular tortuosity was found (Fig. 3b). Mild compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process and calcified stylohyoid ligament caused tortuosity of the proximal internal carotid artery, which resulted in stenosis and the onset of cerebral infarction. A portion of the styloid process and calcified stylohyoid ligament was resected using the cervical approach by a neurosurgeon (Fig. 4). Postoperative DSA confirmed the resolution of mild stenosis caused by the styloid process and calcified stylohyoid ligament (Fig. 5a). In addition, the vascular tortuosity in the proximal segment improved, and the severe vascular stenosis normalized (Fig. 5b). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged without any residual neurological symptoms.

Discussion

Three-dimensional computed tomography angiography and DSA, which are diagnostic imaging methods for stylocarotid syndrome, a type of Eagle syndrome, are useful for evaluating the length of the styloid process and direct compression of the internal carotid artery [4, 10, 12]. If symptoms are reproduced by head rotation, dynamic carotid ultrasonography can be utilized to diagnose changes in blood flow as evidenced by parameters such as increased peak systolic velocity and pulsatility index [7]. In the current case, the elevated peak systolic velocity observed on carotid ultrasonography led to further investigation with DSA. The cerebral angiographic findings in this case emphasized that several factors complicated the diagnosis of stylocarotid syndrome. One factor is mild direct compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process on imaging. It is also challenging to determine on imaging alone whether the severe tortuosity and stenosis observed proximally to the site of compression are attributed to indirect changes caused by compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process or simply atherosclerotic changes. In addition, in elderly patients with a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and renal dysfunction, as in this case, the frequency of cerebral infarction owing to atherosclerosis is generally higher. Therefore, if mild direct compression of the internal carotid artery is observed, stylocarotid syndrome may not be considered as a potential diagnosis. Generally, the mechanism of cerebral infarction in stylocarotid artery syndrome is associated with the physical compression of the internal carotid artery by the elongated styloid process, leading to hemodynamic ischemia caused by restricted blood flow [2, 7, 10]. Head and neck movements can temporarily occlude blood flow, leading to the onset of ischemic symptoms. Rotating the head from side to side or bending the neck often results in carotid artery compression. In particular, if the head is turned toward the side of the elongated styloid process, the styloid process compresses the internal carotid artery, which restricts blood flow [2, 3, 13]. Tilting the neck toward the affected side can also bring the styloid process into contact with the internal carotid artery, thereby increasing the risk of compression [4]. Another mechanism involves the physical pressure applied by the elongated styloid process to the internal carotid artery, which damages the arterial wall. Thus, internal carotid artery dissection or pseudoaneurysm may develop, leading to thrombus formation within the lesion and subsequent cerebral infarction [3, 4]. In the current case, mild compression of the internal carotid artery by the styloid process and calcified stylohyoid ligament could have caused excessive kinking and severe stenosis in the proximal segment of the artery, which had reduced flexibility due to atherosclerosis. The postoperative cerebral angiography result can likely be an evidence for the resolution of severe arterial stenosis and vascular tortuosity, which supports this mechanism. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reported cases of Eagle syndrome causing indirect arterial stenosis in other regions attributed to the effects of atherosclerosis. This is a novel mechanism for the onset of cerebral infarction. Surgical treatment is the most effective approach for stylocarotid artery syndrome [4, 10]. The surgical goal is styloidectomy, and it can be performed via either the oral or cervical approach. The oral approach has a shorter operative time and a cosmetically favorable incision because the surgical site is not visible. However, it has a limited field of view and is associated with a risk of deep infections, which require cautious attention [4]. The cervical approach leaves an external incision but provides a broader field of view, thereby allowing the surgeon to identify key neurovascular structures [10]. In this case, the cervical approach was selected. By resecting the styloid process and the calcified stylohyoid ligament, the secondary tortuosity and stenosis of the internal carotid artery were relieved.

Conclusion

In the current case, the internal carotid artery was only slightly compressed by the styloid process. However, due to arteriosclerosis, the proximal segment of the internal carotid artery became tortuous and severely stenotic, leading to cerebral infarction. Mild compression of the styloid process and arteriosclerosis might have a synergistic effect, resulting in cerebral infarction in Eagle syndrome.
