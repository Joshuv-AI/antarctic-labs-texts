Reducing patient waiting times in humanitarian settings: a data-driven approach to improving healthcare access for vulnerable populations
Authors: Eslam Abo Alhawa, Elise Presser, Amin Zahwe, Sary Faraj, Samar Al-Hajj, Marwa Fares, Mohammad Fadi AlHalbi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProlonged patient waiting times in healthcare settings, particularly within humanitarian contexts, pose significant challenges to the provision and quality of care received. This study examines the persistent issue of extended waiting periods among patients, including Syrian and Palestinian refugees and Lebanese nationals seeking care at humanitarian clinics in Lebanon. The study aims to highlight the challenges faced by these populations and their impact on care and health outcomes.MethodsThis study analyzed secondary data for 111,998 patient records receiving care at Multi Aid Programs clinic between December 2018 and June 2023. Descriptive statistics were performed on the compiled data. Additionally, an Auto-Regressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model was developed to forecast patient waiting times over the next 14 days, providing a data-driven approach to understanding and optimizing healthcare service delivery.ResultsA total of 442,332 patient visits were analyzed. The vast majority of patients treated at the clinic were Syrian refugees (84.6%), Lebanese patients (15.3%), and Palestinian refugees(0.1%). Females accounted for 55% of visits, slightly outnumbering males (45%). Approximately 21.4% of patients left the clinic without receiving medical care, likely due to extended waiting times. The average waiting time was 1.4 h with an average of a 1-hour wait for emergencies and a 2-hour wait for orthopedic appointments. The study team, along with clinic staff and administration, observed patients waiting in overcrowded rooms. Little improvement has been reported in waiting times since 2019. The predictive ARIMA model for patient waiting time forecasting yielded a notably low RMSE of 0.18, indicating excellent fidelity between the modeled and true wait times.ConclusionPatient waiting time represents a significant issue in the humanitarian setting, jeopardizing patient access to medical care and often resulting in patients leaving clinics before being seen by a physician. Future research should explore the underlying causes and broader consequences of prolonged wait times, including their impact on patient satisfaction, healthcare provider burnout, and overall system efficiency.

Background

Waiting times represent a key indicator for the provision of timely care globally [1]. Prolonged patient waiting time constitutes a significant health issue and a major concern that impacts both patients’ experience and the efficiency of healthcare services. Numerous studies have shown that waiting times are inversely associated with patient satisfaction, access to services and the quality of care [2]. Nearly one-third of patients express dissatisfaction with the service they receive due to their long waiting times [29]. Multiple factors contribute to long patient waiting times, including limited healthcare staff, low bed and space capacity, and high health service demands with unexpected surges [30, 31].

Waiting times are particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare facilities frequently suffer from limited resources and high patient needs. In addition to the low-resource context, disasters and wars create humanitarian crises that add further layers of complexity to local health care systems in LMICs, exposing their existing vulnerabilities and straining their already limited capacity [6]. Globally, ongoing wars and conflicts have created the biggest displacement crisis in recent history, leading to a significant surge in the refugee population worldwide [7]. Nearly 35.3 million refugees in neighboring countries fled conflict zones and crossed borders to seek safety by the end of 2011 [7]. This massive influx of refugees has brought significant challenges in addressing the healthcare needs of these vulnerable populations. As countries struggle with the complexities of accommodating refugees, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) have stepped in to provide them with essential health services. Despite the additional support provided by NGOs, health clinics consistently experience extraordinarily high demand, frequently resulting in crowded waiting rooms filled with patients awaiting services [8].

The Syrian civil war in 2011 led to an estimated 5.5 million refugees who escaped war zones and settled in neighboring countries, including Lebanon, Jordan, and Turkey [9]. As a result, Lebanon hosted the largest number of refugees per capita in the world, accounting for nearly 30% of its population [10, 11]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that there was a 50% increase in demand for health services in Lebanon to accommodate refugees’ health needs [12]. In addition to this refugee crisis, Lebanon is experiencing an ongoing economic crisis, which in 2022 led to currency inflation of more than 170% and strained the infrastructure of its already fragile healthcare system [13]. These compounding crises have put tremendous pressure on the Lebanese healthcare system to function efficiently and meet the exponentially increasing demands for Syrian refugees and impoverished Lebanese populations [11].

Responding to the surge in patients’ healthcare needs led to inefficiency and bottlenecks in the overall patient flow and provision of care in Lebanon. A local study on patient satisfaction in inpatient healthcare settings in Lebanon revealed that 55% of patients expressed dissatisfaction due to extended waiting times [14]. Another study conducted in Malaysia indicated that the median waiting time at primary health care (PHC) facilities is 41 min [15]. However, refugees encounter notably lengthier waiting periods, particularly when seeking care at public hospitals, with waiting times reaching 4 h [16].

Statistical models have been adopted to reduce patient waiting times by analyzing patterns in patient flow and identifying factors that contribute to delays, thereby enhancing our understanding of how wait times impact healthcare access and experiences [17–19]. A limited number of studies have examined waiting time in the context of refugee health services [28]. This study aimed to examine patients’ waiting times in a humanitarian setting and Analyze patient waiting times and healthcare dynamics using quantitative methods to identify patterns and trends that could inform strategies for optimizing healthcare access and service efficiency.

Methodology

Study design

This study utilized secondary data analysis of patient records to develop a predictive mathematical model for patient waiting times among underprivileged patients seeking healthcare services. The primary aim was to analyze patterns and trends in waiting times and provide data-driven insights into healthcare dynamics within a humanitarian context. The findings are intended to inform strategies for optimizing healthcare service delivery. This clinic serves as both initial contact and referral centers, offering specialized services specialized services in ophthalmology, dentistry, radiology, echocardiography, kidney diseases, urology, echography, gastrointestinal diseases and laboratory testing that enable patients to consult doctors based on their symptoms, hence, the high patient influx seeking health services at this tertiary healthcare clinic.

Setting

The data for this study were acquired from the Ghirass Al-Khair, a specialized outpatient clinic, operated by Multi-Aid Programs (MAPs) in Lebanon. MAPs are refugee-led NGOs dedicated to providing health, relief, and education services to refugees and vulnerable Lebanese people. The Health Program Lebanon (HPL) was initiated by MAPs in 2013 to enhance healthcare access in the Bekaa Valley, northeast Lebanon, a region bordering Syria (Appendix) by offering affordable health services and minimizing barriers to care. The Specialized Clinic, launched in 2014, focuses on providing low-cost, specialized care to thousands of vulnerable patients.

Data collection and cleaning

Deidentified patient data were retrieved for the period from December 11, 2018, to June 21, 2023, consisting of daily records of patient visits. The patient records were entered digitally into the clinic’s system upon patient arrival, with the arrival times automatically generated as system timestamps. The data used was a backup of the system’s database in.BAK and.SQL formats, which were subsequently converted into.CSV files for analysis. The compiled data included patient demographics, age, disease history, number of clinic visits, and wait times. Wait time was defined as the duration from the patient’s arrival at the clinic to their initial consultation with a physician where patients first come to the registration check to confirm their arrival and wait for their turn. The data cleaning process involved importing tables into SQL Server, adjusting data types, and establishing relationships across fields, including encrypted and symbol-substituted entries. Calculated fields, such as patient age, visit count, and wait time, were created. Outliers were handled using Moving Average (MA) and ARIMA forecasting, ensuring data integrity without unnecessary deletions. This process resulted in two primary tables for analysis: Patients and Consultations. To enhance data accuracy, a 5-hour threshold was applied to exclude outliers, as wait times exceeding this limit fell outside the primary data cluster and were mainly recorded on the following day. Such extreme values likely reflect delayed or erroneous data entries, which could skew the analysis. This exclusion resulted in the exclusion of approximately 9% of the total 442,332 patient records.

Data analysis: forecasting and predictive models

The data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, focusing on the distribution of patient’s visits to the clinic by demographic factors such as nationality, age, and gender, as well as the type of health services required and patient wait times. This study adopted the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model to understand waiting times at a full-capacity NGO health system. ARIMA (AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average) models are widely used for time series forecasting, as they capture both autoregressive patterns and moving averages in data over time. This makes ARIMA especially suitable for our analysis, as it allows us to model fluctuations in patient waiting times, accounting for both short-term dependencies and variations.

Baseline forecasting models including naïve, drift, mean, and naïve seasonal were initially implemented. The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) was calculated for each model, with the naïve model achieving the best performance. The ARIMA model was subsequently employed to refine the accuracy. An iterative approach was used to determine the optimal ARIMA parameters (p, d, q), highlighting the necessity of differencing (d = 1) to achieve stationarity. The Kwiatkowski–Phillips–Schmidt–Shin (KPSS) test confirmed the stationarity of the data.

Results

Patient demographics and characteristics

A total of 111,998 patient records were analyzed. Most patients were Syrian refugees (84.6%), followed by Lebanese (15.3%) and Palestinian (0.1%) refugees. Females accounted for 55% (n = 241,513) of the total visits compared to their male counterparts 45% (n = 200,819). While 44% of patients visited the clinic only once, the average number of visits per patient is three times. The most common clinics visited by patients were Dentistry, Obstetrics and Gynecology (OBGYN), and Pediatrics. Approximately 60% of the patients aged 15–35 years were female, and 29% of the patients were younger than 14 years (Table 1).

AICc (Corrected Akaike Information Criterion) is a measure of model quality, where lower values indicate a better model fit. RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) measures the average magnitude of errors between predicted and actual values; lower RMSE values indicate better model performance, with a value of 0 signifying perfect predictions. MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) expresses prediction accuracy as a percentage, where lower values indicate higher accuracy. A MAPE of 0% indicates perfect accuracy.

Building on this, we implemented the ARIMA model to refine our understanding and forecasting of patient wait times, focusing on accurately capturing these fluctuations. After several iterations, the ARIMA(5,1,3) model emerged as the optimal choice for forecasting patient wait times, as it minimized prediction errors and aligned closely with observed data patterns. This model was selected through a balanced approach, incorporating both theoretical principles and empirical validation. Specifically, the parameters p = 5,d = 1, and q = 3 enable the model to capture short-term variations and medium-term dependencies in patient wait time data. By effectively balancing these elements, ARIMA(5,1,3) reduces forecasting errors and accurately reflects underlying patterns while mitigating noise and trend-based distortions. The baseline naïve model yielded an RMSE of 2.0, whereas the ARIMA(5,1,3) model produced an improved RMSE value of 0.186. This notably low RMSE underscores the ability of the ARIMA model to provide highly accurate predictions, aligning well with the scale of the data.

Discussion

This study focused on understanding and addressing the critical issue of prolonged patient waiting times in a humanitarian setting, specifically within a clinic providing health services to the underprivileged population in Lebanon. While Syrian refugees constitute the majority, the study also addresses waiting time dynamics among Lebanese and Palestinian patients, who represent a significant portion of the clinic’s patient base. The study results showed a persistent average waiting time of approximately 1.4 h per visit, aligning with previous research [20]. In this study, 21% of the visits were terminated without patients consulting a doctor, a phenomenon that exacerbates during peak afternoon hours, which accounted for 60% of the visits (See Table 5). These results suggest an association between extended waiting times and patients leaving before receiving care.

The high percentage of female visits in the age range of 15 to 35 years, approximately 60%, suggested that there might be factors influencing healthcare-seeking behavior specific to women in their childbearing years. Pregnancy-related healthcare needs could indeed be a significant contributor to this trend where approximately 12% of visits were for OB/GYN services related to obstetrics and gynecology services, including pregnancy follow-ups and other women-specific healthcare needs (see Table 1). This finding suggests potential age-related health considerations or healthcare-seeking behavior differences between genders. Approximately 29% of patients were aged less than 14 years, reflecting high demand for pediatric care, likely due to the high proportion of children in refugee families. As indicated by UNHCR data, 51% of the Syrian refugee population in Lebanon is under 18. This highlights the need for targeted pediatric health interventions in humanitarian settings [21].

Several factors, particularly poverty in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and resource-limited settings, significantly contribute to this challenge. A review of the literature confirms that multifaceted determinants influence waiting times, including patient load, workforce shortages, inefficient patient flow, infrastructure constraints, patient-related factors, healthcare system organization, and cultural norms [4, 5, 22]. Improving waiting times by optimizing appointment scheduling and resource allocation must take into account several cultural factors, including refugee health beliefs and experiences, literacy levels, refugee perceptions of negative attitudes of healthcare providers, and hierarchical and familial structures [23]. According to the Ministry of Health, approximately 60% of health clinics in Lebanon are operated by NGOs [24], a factor that contributes to the challenges associated with limited resources and capacity to deliver quality healthcare services. The reliance on nongovernmental organizations for a significant portion of healthcare facilities may lead to constraints in funding, staffing, and infrastructure, thereby impacting the overall ability to provide optimal and timely services [25]. Moreover, a lengthy waiting time significantly impacts patients’ decision to seek primary care. This highlights the critical importance of addressing and mitigating waiting times in healthcare settings to encourage timely and proactive healthcare-seeking behavior, ultimately fostering better health outcomes for vulnerable populations. The comprehensive analysis of patient records, which examined the distribution of waiting times, patient flow, and the relationship between waiting periods and patient satisfaction. Our findings indicate that longer waiting times correlate with decreased satisfaction and can hinder timely access to care, highlighting the necessity of improving waiting times to enhance healthcare delivery.

The high accuracy of the ARIMA model for forecasting waiting times in this study suggested that the model can be used to understand patient flow dynamics and support resource planning. Other studies have shown significant improvement in waiting times. For instance, task shifting results in a significant mean difference exceeding 2 h [26]. Furthermore, research employing advanced predictive models and schedule optimizations demonstrated noteworthy decreases in waiting times, with a 20% reduction achieved through random forest predictions and a substantial 45% decrease achieved by optimizing nurses’ schedules using simulations [27]. These findings collectively support the benefits of our model and its implications for improving patient access and reducing waiting times.

This study demonstrated that long waiting times are a prevalent issue among the vulnerable populations in Lebanon and suggested the potential of using data-driven approaches to understand clinic volume and optimize services. The literature has shown that using these models to adjust staffing could reduce waiting times by up to 45% [27]. While the specific modeling approach in [27] differed from our ARIMA/ARMA models, the underlying principle of using data-driven insights to improve healthcare efficiency remains consistent. Our study further supports this notion by demonstrating the accuracy of ARIMA/ARMA models in predicting patient volume, suggesting their potential to enhance the responsiveness and effectiveness of healthcare systems, particularly in diverse contexts like Lebanon.

This study has several strengths and limitations. The high accuracy of the ARIMA model, as demonstrated by its superior performance with an RMSE of 0.19, offers a potential avenue for improving resource planning. However, studies that utilize prediction to reduce waiting times in this unique context are limited. Therefore, comparing these results with those of existing models, which typically exhibit RMSE values of approximately 0.6, may not provide a fair or directly comparable benchmark [32]. Moreover, the scope of this study is limited to the services provided by the clinic in the Bekaa region of Lebanon, which consequently restricts the representation of patients and diagnoses to those specifically treated at the clinic. The volatile situation due to conflict within the country and the neighboring country (Syria) creates an influx of refugees who often face numerous challenges such as poor housing, nutrition and access to clean water and sanitation amenities, heightening disease exposure may exert unanticipated high demand for health care services significantly affect waiting times at various health clinics. Furthermore, the dip in visits observed in Fig. 1 is based on assumptions and could potentially be due to a system outage or other unidentified factors, representing a limitation in the analysis. While this study provides valuable insights into waiting time patterns and their implications for healthcare dynamics, the adopted methodology does not permit a direct assessment of the causal relationship between waiting times and outcomes such as patient satisfaction or health experiences. Our predictive model is accurate within the scope of the available data but may not account for these external factors, potentially leading to deviations from predictions in real-world scenarios.” We currently don’t have access to new data from the clinic but our model was tested on the last 14 records available in the database. We calculated the model’s accuracy by comparing its predictions with the actual results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the persistent challenge of long patient waiting times for vulnerable patients accessing healthcare services in a humanitarian setting. The high percentage of patients who leave clinics before being seen by physicians may be influenced by various factors, potentially including extended waiting times, which can impact patient retention and access to timely care. The ARIMA model offers an avenue for improving resource planning and underscores the potential of predictive models to streamline healthcare operations. This study provides data-driven insights into potential opportunities for enhancing patients’ healthcare experiences, particularly among refugees and vulnerable populations with comparable humanitarian settings.
