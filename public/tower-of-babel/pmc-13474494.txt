Obstructive shock from delayed blunt cardiac injury after chest trauma

Background

Blunt cardiac injury (BCI) represents a rare but clinically significant sequela of blunt thoracic trauma. It ranges in severity from hemodynamic instability to lethal injury [1]. Although most commonly associated with motor vehicle accidents, BCI can also result from falls, occupational trauma, or sports-related injuries. BCI remains an elusive diagnosis due to the lack of standardized evaluation protocols and the frequent presence of distracting injuries that may complicate the clinical picture.

Cardiac contusion is the most common type of BCI, exhibiting wide variability in presentation, severity, and associated findings [2]. As such, there is no standardized approach in evaluation [2]. A systematic review suggests electrocardiograms (ECG), cardiac troponin I and T, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) are useful initial tests, but possess limited ability to confirm the diagnosis [3]. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is recognized to have significant sensitivity for BCI but is less accessible in most emergency department settings [3].

Unlike blunt cerebrovascular injuries (BCVI), which have EAST guidelines to identify patients in need of screening and recommendations on screening modality, diagnostic approaches for BCI have been debated in recent years. Chest radiographs and ECGs have been the diagnostic tests of choice in hemodynamically stable patients, while computed tomography (CT) and echocardiography have traditionally been reserved for patients that are hemodynamically unstable [2]. POCUS has also been useful for identifying fluid in the pericardium, however, it remains difficult to discern which diagnostic test to use. With any diagnostic approach, prompt recognition of BCI remains is essential particularly for patients with pre-existing cardiac disease, as it can lead to sudden cardiac death, pericarditis, tamponade, or hemodynamic collapse.

Case presentation

History/physical

A 51-year-old female presented to the ED via helicopter Emergency Medical Services (EMS) for 10-hour history of worsening crushing central chest pain, shortness of breath, syncope, epigastric pain, nausea, and new-onset signs of poor peripheral perfusion. Her past medical history was significant for pancreatitis and gastric bypass with subsequent reversal. She relocated from Texas 1 week prior to presentation to the ED, where all of her previous medical care was performed. She was somnolent during examination and unable to provide a detailed history, however she intermittently responded, denying recent fevers, chills, vomiting, or diarrhea.

On presentation, her temperature was 97.0 °F, blood pressure 68/40, heart rate 78 beats per minute (BPM), and respiratory rate 20 breaths/min. Pulse oximetry was unobtainable due to cold, poorly perfused distal extremities. Peripheral vasculature access was unsuccessful on multiple attempts, and subsequently a femoral central line was emergently placed. Her physical exam was notable for distant heart sounds, poor extremity perfusion, and epigastric tenderness. She intermittently dry-heaved throughout evaluation without emesis.

Management

Through the femoral central line, she initially received 2 L of normal saline and had no significant improvement in blood pressure. Norepinephrine infusion was initiated and rapidly titrated before achieving normotension.

Her workup included cardiac biomarkers, serial ECGs, POCUS, and CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis. Further, a formal TTE was ordered. C-reactive protein (CRP) was moderately elevated to 13.5, however, troponin (7ng/L) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (pro-BNP) (140 g/mL) were within normal limits. Initial ECG revealed normal sinus rhythm and no acute changes (Fig. 1). Follow up ECG approximately 25 min later demonstrated normal sinus rhythm, with notably worsening QTc at 505 (Fig. 2). POCUS revealed pericardial effusion with grossly normal left ventricular function. CT confirmed a moderate pericardial effusion (Fig. 3). There was no pulmonary embolism identified. TTE was pending at time of patient admission to ICU.

The next morning in the intensive care unit (ICU), the patient revealed she was in a motor vehicle accident the week before. She was an unrestricted driver who she rear-ended another car going approximately 40 miles per hour while exiting the highway. Despite experiencing blunt force trauma to the chest, she did not present to the hospital for evaluation afterwards. She recalled experiencing intermittent, mild chest pain in the days following but was not concerned due to the infrequency. When chest pain became more diffuse and crushing, along with development of dyspnea and altered mental status, she was transported to the ED by EMS. This delayed symptom onset was consistent with evolving pericardial inflammation following blunt trauma, a phenomenon that may go unrecognized without high clinical suspicion. In the ICU, she was ultimately treated with colchicine and indomethacin before being deemed stable for discharge. Subsequentially, the patient did not return and was lost to follow up.

Clinical reasoning

The patient presented in extremis and had evidence of a pericardial effusion. Given these findings and clinical picture, there was initially strong concern for cardiac tamponade. Pericardial inflammation and effusion was initially seen on US and CT, and formal cardiology interpretation of the TTE confirmed the diagnosis of pericarditis. Of note, ECG changes were not consistent with pericarditis, and instead exhibited QTc prolongation. This may underscore increased risk of arryhthmia development in patients with any cardiac compromise, including pericarditi.

Ultimately, it was concluded the undisclosed BCI resulted in inflammation and accumulation of fluid in the pericardium in the subsequent week. The consequent increased pressure resulted in a progressively restrictive cardiac physiology. This led to diminished cardiac output and eventual transformation to obstructive shock as evidenced by hypotension requiring vasopressors, acute encephalopathy, and extremity hypoperfusion.

Discussion

Diagnosing BCI poses a significant clinical challenge due to heterogeneous presentations and the absence of standardized evaluation protocols. While most documented cases focus on immediate cardiac effects following trauma, this report adds to the limited literature addressing delayed complications such as evolving pericardial effusion and pericarditis.

Diagnosing BCI can be challenging due to its varied presentations and the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria. BCI encompasses a wide spectrum of injuries, ranging from minor myocardial contusions to life-threatening conditions such as cardiac rupture or tamponade.

In this case, the patient’s motor vehicle accident resulted in blunt force trauma to the chest. The patient remained hemodynamically stable for several days post-injury, until developing progressive symptoms consistent with obstructive shock. This temporal evolution highlights a diagnostic blind spot in trauma care: patients who are not initially evaluated or appear well post-injury may later decompensate from evolving BCI. Unfortunately, most screening paradigms focus only on the acute setting, with no consensus on surveillance strategies or follow-up imaging in patients with minor thoracic trauma.

The subtlety of symptoms in the initial days following trauma underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for BCI in patients with significant chest trauma, even if they do not initially seek medical attention. Early identification of BCI is critical to prevent catastrophic outcomes. The diagnostic workup for this patient was thorough and reflective of current best practices:

POCUS and CT imaging were instrumental in identifying pericardial effusion. Echocardiography—both point-of-care and formal TTE—remains the gold standard for evaluating cardiac function and detecting tamponade physiology. Current literature on the development of effusive-restrictive pericarditis following MVA is sparse. Only one other report describes post-MVA pericarditis occurring weeks after trauma, highlighting a rare but underrecognized delayed BCI complication [4]. A 32 year old male developed chest pain two months after an MVA and was initially misdiagnosed with gastroesophageal reflux disease until he developed dyspnea, tachycardia and hypotension. While this patient did not decompensate to shock, this case highlights the potential for delayed presentations and diagnostic challenges in BCI.

This case features key diagnostic tests for confirming BCI while ruling out other causes of obstructive as well as cardiogenic shock. It emphasizes the utility of POCUS as a preliminary examination tool as it readily discerned the presence of fluid in the pericardium— which was later confirmed by both CT and TEE. It also illustrates the importance of thorough physical examination when there is clinical suspicion of cardiac injury, especially when a patient’s ability to convey medical history is limited. Lastly, this case provides an important disease script for patients presenting with unexplained pericarditis and shock who may be unable to convey recent blunt force injury such as a motor vehicle accident.

Conclusion

BCI remains difficult to diagnose due to its variable and often delayed presentation. This case highlights the need for sustained clinical suspicion in patients with recent chest trauma—even when the injury appears minor or initial evaluation is absent. Prompt recognition and treatment of complications such as pericardial effusion and tamponade are critical. Utilizing a combination of physical examination, imaging, and laboratory findings can guide clinicians to more accurately diagnose BCI. By highlighting a rare but serious consequence of blunt chest trauma, this case adds to the limited literature on delayed BCI, underscoring the importance of structured diagnostic strategies in trauma care.
