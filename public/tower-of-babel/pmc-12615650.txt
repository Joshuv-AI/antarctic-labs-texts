Piezocatalytic activity of bismuth vanadium titanium oxide
Authors: Aditya Singh Thakur, Shivam Dubey, Amal Abdulrahman, Mohamed A Ismail, Abdelfattah Amari, Rahul Vaish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study reveals piezocatalytic activity of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 ceramic powder using Rhodamine B (RhB) dye. Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 ceramic powder was fabricated via solid state reaction method. The fabricated ferroelectric ceramic was characterized by X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, and Scanning electron microscopy. The band gap for Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 was calculated to be 2.25 eV using Tauc’s plot. Parametric studies were performed to determine catalyst performance in dye degradation under varying conditions such as catalyst dosage, dye concentration, and solution volume. During piezocatalysis experiments using an ultrasonicator, the maximum degradation was observed 77% in 240 min, and the rate constant (k) for this reaction is 0.0061 min⁻1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23589-5.Keywords: Ferroelectric material, Piezocatalysis, Dye degradationSubject terms: Materials for energy and catalysis, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Water pollution is a growing issue due to rising domestic sewage and industrial waste, threatening ecosystems and human health1. One of the main sources of contaminated water is industrial waste2. The extensive use of dyes in many different industries, such as paper, printing, and textiles, seriously pollutes the environment3. The contamination of natural water bodies such as rivers and lakes with organic dyes presents significant environmental concerns, posing serious risks to aquatic ecosystems and human health4. Different methods have been used to eliminate organic contaminants from wastewater. These include ion flotation, sedimentation, filtration, coagulation, adsorption, membrane technology, and biological treatment5–7. These techniques provide unique difficulties because the majority of them are inefficient, have large running costs, or occasionally cause secondary harmful contaminants to be released into the environment8. Treating wastewater using reactive oxygen species (ROS) is considered a promising and environmentally friendly approach for sustainable wastewater management9. In the last few years, the research on piezocatalysis for the organic degradation of dyes has gained popularity10,11. Piezocatalysts offer an economical and sustainable substitute for the degradation of complex compounds12. A piezocatalyst is a material with piezoelectric properties that can convert mechanical stress into electrical charges. These charges can be utilized for purposes like chemical synthesis, water splitting, and the removal of organic dyes13,14. When subjected to mechanical stress, a piezocatalyst induces charge separation within the piezoelectric material, generating electron and hole pair, which interact with water molecules and oxygen to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), like hydroxyl and superoxide radicals15,16. The charges transfer to the surface of catalyst, where they engage in redox participation with dye molecules17. Electrons reduce the dye molecules by transferring to them, while the holes oxidize the dye molecules by extracting electrons, thereby facilitating the degradation of the pollutants.

Ferroelectric materials are dielectric materials that exhibit permanent polarization, which persists even after the eternal electric field is removed18. Some known ferroelectric materials having intrinsic spontaneous polarization, including BaTiO3, BiFeO3, Pb(Zr0.52Ti0.48)O3, etc., are used in water cleaning applications through the process of piezocatalysis19–21.Aurivillius compounds hold great potential as a new class of lead-free and non-toxic piezocatalysts22. The lead-free, environmentally friendly bismuth-layer structure ferroelectrics (BLSFs) of different Aurivillius families are defined by the generic formula [Bi2O2]2+ [Am-1BmO3m+1]2-, where (Bi2O2) 2+ represents bismuth oxide layers, while (Am−1BmO3m+1)2− corresponds to the pseudo-perovskite layer. The parameter m denotes the number of BO6 octahedra in these perovskite layers (m = 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5). The A-site of this structure is typically occupied by a monovalent, divalent, or trivalent cation like Ba2+, Sr2+, Na+, Ca2+, Bi3+, and La3+. The B-sites are occupied by transition metal cations like Mo6+, W6+, V5+, Ti4+, Fe3+, Cr3+, Nb5+, and Ta5+23. The structural flexibility of the Aurivillius family enables the incorporation of a wide variety of cations. This results in diverse material properties such as ferroelectricity and piezoelectricity, with promising applications in photocatalysis and piezocatalysis for dye degradation24. Bismuth-containing materials belonging to the Aurivillius family, such as Bi2WO6, Bi2MoO6, and BiYWO6 have been found to have photocatalytic activity25. Similarly, piezocatalytic materials like BaTiO326, and Bi2MoO627 have shown the ability to degrade pollutants present in dyes due to the effect of mechanical vibrations. Bismuth-based materials with complex perovskite structures are gaining significant attention due to their piezoelectric and ferroelectric properties, particularly their high Curie temperature. Ferroelectric materials with a high Curie temperature retain their polarization capabilities even at high temperatures28. Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 is a member of the Bismuth Layered Structure Ferroelectric (BLSF) family, which is characterized by unique layered perovskite structures having the generalized formula (Bi2O2)2+(AmBm−1O3m+1)2−. This layered configuration makes a substantial contribution to the material’s dielectric and piezoelectric properties29. The m = 2 BLSF compounds, such as CaBi₂Nb₂O₉30, SrBi₂Nb₂O₉31, Bi₃NbTiO₉32, and BaBi₂Nb₂O₉33, have been extensively investigated for their favourable functional characteristics, such as low operating voltage, rapid switching speeds, strong retention capability, and low leakage current density. These features are particularly advantageous for piezoelectric and catalytic applications, where stable polarization and efficient charge separation are essential, all of which are important for efficient and sustainable performance in advanced electronic applications34. Additionally, the high transition temperatures of these compounds (often exceeding 900 °C) make them useful for high-temperature applications in fields such as aviation and aerospace35. They exhibit remarkable thermal stability and resistance to fatigue during polarization transitions. These qualities further enhance their potential for applications in piezoelectric engineering and information technology36. These properties encouraged us to explore ferroelectric materials for wastewater treatment. Recently, there is no research article reported on Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 catalyst for wastewater treatment. In this study, the piezocatalytic properties of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 have been investigated for the removal of RhB dye.

Experimental

The synthesis of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 ceramic powder was carried out using a solid-state reaction route involving the use of precursors Bi2O3, V2O5, and TiO2 with a purity index of more than 99%. These precursor materials were combined in their stoichiometric ratios and combined in the mortar and pestle for 30 min, using acetone as the mixing medium. After mixing, the mixtures were heated at 850 ˚C for the duration of 4 h to undergo calcination inside a Nabertherm electric furnace (Germany). Since some percentage of bismuth is lost during the calcination process, an additional 2% of Bi2O3 was added.

The structure characterization of the fabricated Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 ceramic was examined through XRD analysis which was carried out using Smart Lab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation). A scanning rate of 2˚ per minute was used to record the XRD data between 5–80˚. Raman spectroscopy using a HORIBA spectrometer was used to examine the vibrational modes of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 ceramic powder in the range of 200-1000 cm-1 at a wavelength of 532 nm. The morphology of the surface of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 powder was recorded using an FE-SEM specifically using FEI SEM NOVA Nanosem 450, Hillsboro. XPS was determined using an X-ray photoelectron spectrophotometer (NEXSA) to verify the elemental composition of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85. Using Tauc’s plot, which is obtained by diffuse reflectance spectrum (DRS) measured with a UV–visible spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU-2600), the energy band gap of the synthesized Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 composition was calculated. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to determine the specific surface area based on the nitrogen adsorption isotherm acquired using a Quantachrome ASiQwin device.

An aqueous solution of RhB dye was utilized to evaluate the piezocatalytic performances of the fabricated Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 catalyst powder. Ultrasonication was employed to stimulate the catalysts. The ultrasonicator generates pressure waves at 40 kHz within the bath. As the pressure waves propagate through the vessel and into the aqueous solution, they interact with the catalyst material. Initially, 0.1 g of catalyst powder was dispersed in 10 mL of a 5 mg/L aqueous RhB dye solution. The RhB dye strongly adsorbs onto the surfaces of catalyst particles (Supplementary figure S1). For the catalyst and dye molecules to reach the equilibrium of adsorption and desorption, the resultant solutions were stirred at 300 rpm with a magnetic stirrer for 12 h in a dark environment. Piezocatalytic activity was assessed using a 140 W ultrasonicator after adsorption was finished. To minimize the heat effects induced by ultrasonic vibrations, the temperature was kept constant between 15–20 °C by adding ice. The Piezocatalysis experiment was performed for 240 min, and the sample was collected at intervals of 30 min, with 1.5 mL extracted each time, then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm to extract the solution’s from solid catalyst. The solution of dye concentration was subsequently measured using UV–visible spectroscopy. For this SHIMADZU-2450, a double-beam UV-visual spectrophotometer was used. Following absorbance measurement, this extracted dye was returned to the original sample to ensure consistent volume during the experiment.

To assess the effect of each of three particular parameters on the piezocatalytic reaction, a parametric analysis was carried out. The study considered several factors, including the volume of the RhB dye solution, the dye’s initial concentration, and the amount of catalyst used. All other factors remained constant while each of these factors was changed during experimentation. This approach will provide insight into which parameter is the least and most effective during dye degradation.

Starting with a set of experiments on the different catalyst loading effects on dye degradation, we took 4 different catalyst dosages of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 as 0.05 g, 0.1 g, 0.15 g, and 0.2 g for the piezocatalytic dye degradation process. A higher quantity of catalyst results in the production of more reactive species per unit of time. An increased catalyst dose provides a larger surface area for interaction with the dye molecules, resulting in a higher concentration of free radicals/ml of the RhB dye solution. These free radicals, produced through piezocatalytic activity, are highly reactive and effective in breaking down dye molecules into smaller, less harmful components. An ultrasonicator was used to create ultrasonic vibrations in a solution of catalyst and RhB dye. The acoustic pressure generated by the ultrasonicator in the bath is transferred to the dye solution via the vessel wall. In the piezocatalysis process, an ultrasonicator generates vibrations that exert pressure on the Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 catalyst contained within the dye solution, inducing a piezoelectric effect as shown in Fig. 10. This process is highly sensitive to temperature, as elevated temperatures can independently cause the degradation of the organic dye. During ultrasonication, cavities form bubbles within the solution, which grow and collapse rapidly. This rapid collapse leads to a localized temperature spike reaching up to 4000–5000 K and generates shock waves with pressures up to ~ 108 Pa, resulting in the degradation of organic dye. The degradation of organic dye resulting from the combined effects of temperature and pressure is referred to as thermolysis or sonolysis. The sonocatalytic process is primarily governed by the formation of hot spots and sonoluminescence, both of which result from ultrasonic cavitation64. When ultrasonic waves of a specific frequency and intensity propagate through a liquid medium, they generate microscopic bubbles that undergo cyclic expansion and collapse65. This cavitation process induces significant physical and chemical transformations within the reaction system. These high-energy environments facilitate the emission of broad-spectrum light (sonoluminescence) and the formation of hot spots, which further enhance catalytic activity. The intense heat and pressure lead to the pyrolysis of water molecules, resulting in the generation of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (OH.)66. In sonocatalysis, the application of mechanical stress to the catalyst induces charge separation, generating an internal electric field on the catalyst surface67. An increase in temperature was avoided by using ice-cold water, which reduced the thermolysis contribution to the piezocatalysis process. The efficiency of the piezocatalytic process is also affected by the energy band gap and screening effects. The energy band theory explains that the band gap of the catalyst determines the efficiency of electron–hole pair generation. A suitable band gap allows efficient absorption of energy from ultrasonic vibrations, leading to the excitation of electrons from the VB to the CB. While the screening effect refers to the reduction in the effective electric field within the catalyst material, which can be caused by the presence of other charges or by the polarization of the material. Effective screening ensures that the charges remain separated long enough to participate in redox reactions, thus enhancing the degradation efficiency of the dye molecules. The formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is important for the degradation of organic dyes in the piezocatalysis process. The cavitation effect during ultrasonication generates localized high-energy “hot spots”, which induce mechanical strain on the Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 surface. This strain leads to the development of polarization charges, which create an internal electric field across the material. This field drives the separation of electron–hole pairs, directing them to move in opposite directions and thereby reducing the chances of recombination68,69. The exact origin of these charge carriers remains a topic of ongoing research. Some studies suggest that phenomena such as sonoluminescence, which involves the emission of light during ultrasonication, and localized temperature increases at the hot spots may play a role in charge generation70. Additionally, the combination of ultrasonication and polarization effects likely contributes synergistically to the observed activity.

Schematic representation of processes Piezocatalysis.

Polarization also induces band bending, leads to the migration of electrons and holes to the material’s surface71. Since piezocatalysis occurs in the absence of light, the generation of charge carriers here does not involve photogenerated processes. Instead, adsorbed water molecules on the surface are oxidized by the holes, producing hydroxyl radicals (OH.). Simultaneously, adsorbed oxygen reacts with the electrons to form superoxide radicals (O₂.-). These reactive oxygen species (ROS) are instrumental in degrading the methylene blue dye into non-toxic byproducts.

When vibrations are applied to the sample, the piezoelectric polarisation occurs on the balanced Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 catalyst, which causes the separation of negative and positive charges on the surfaces of the catalyst to form an imbalanced state (holes and electrons), as shown in Eq. 9.

The negative electrons and positive holes will react with dissolved oxygen and hydroxyl to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS constitute oxidizing chemicals, which are mainly divided into non-radical oxygen and oxygen-free radical species. Oxygen-free radicals include superoxide radicals and hydroxyl radicals, while non-radical oxygen species include hydrogen peroxide and organic hydroperoxide. In the Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 piezocatalytic reaction, superoxide and hydroxyl radicals are generated. O2− and OH. are highly reactive oxidants capable of undergoing redox reactions with adsorbed dyes in solution, enabling the degradation of dye molecules.

Scavenger tests were performed using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), benzoquinone (BQ), and isopropanol (IPA) to target particular radicals such as holes, superoxide radicals (O2.-), and hydroxyl radicals (OH.) to identify which reactive species were responsible for the breakdown of the RhB dye79,80. 0.1 g of Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 catalyst was introduced to a 10 ml solution containing approximately 5 mg/L of concentrated RhB dye. This mixture was stirred at 300 rpm for 12 h utilizing a magnetic stirrer to ensure adsorption–desorption equilibrium. For the scavenger tests, 10 mM of each scavenging solution (EDTA, IPA, and BQ) was added separately to 10 ml of solution. Conclusions

The synthesized Bi4V1.5Ti0.5O10.85 was prepared using the solid-state reaction method and calcined at 850 °C for 4 h. The initial study with 0.1 g of catalyst in 5 mg/L RhB dye solution achieved a degradation efficiency of 43% after 4 h. A comprehensive parametric study revealed that the catalyst amount is the most influential factor in enhancing the piezocatalytic activity. Increasing the catalyst amount notably improved the rate of reaction from 0.0017 min-1 to 0.0061 min-1 by expanding its surface area available for the adsorption -desorption processes, which in turn accelerated the generation of reactive species. Conversely, the initial concentrations and solution volume of RhB dye negatively impacted the degradation efficiency, as the higher number of dye molecule overwhelmed the reactive sites of the catalyst, suppressing its activity. Therefore, optimizing the catalyst dosage is crucial for maximizing the efficiency of the piezocatalytic degradation process, while careful management of dye solution parameters is necessary to avoid activity suppression.
