The influence of fine-needle aspiration on dual-layer detector spectral CT parameters of papillary thyroid carcinoma: a propensity-match analysis
Authors: Jiayi Yu, Liang Lv, Zuhua Song, Bi Zhou, Xiaodi Zhang, Jing Peng, Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDual-layer detector spectral computed tomography (DLCT) and fine-needle aspiration (FNA) are commonly used in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). However, whether FNA affects DLCT quantitative parameters remains uncertain. This study aimed to investigate the effect of FNA on DLCT parameters in PTC and to determine the optimal timing for DLCT examination.MethodsThis retrospective study included 689 patients with PTC, who were categorized into pre-FNA (n = 222) and post-FNA (n = 467) groups according to the sequence of FNA and DLCT. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance confounding variables. Sensitivity analyses were performed using inverse probability of treatment weighting and multivariate regression. DLCT parameters, including virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) at 40, 70, and 100 keV; spectral attenuation curve slope (λHU); iodine concentration (IC); normalized IC (NIC); effective atomic number (Zeff); and normalized Zeff (NZeff), were compared between groups in arterial (AP) and venous phases (VP). Post-FNA time intervals were ranked and divided into deciles. For each DLCT parameter, standardized differences from the pre-FNA baseline were plotted across deciles to propose temporal cutoffs, which were validated by comparing post-FNA subgroups with the pre-FNA group.ResultsCovariates were well balanced after PSM, with all standardized mean differences below 0.25. Sensitivity analyses confirmed consistent direction of effect estimates across methods. Two clinically relevant exploratory temporal landmarks were observed around 6 and 18 days. Within 6 days after FNA, significant differences were observed in AP VMIs (40, 70, 100 keV), λHU, IC, NIC, and VP VMI at 100 keV. Between 7 and 18 days, only VP-IC remained significantly altered. Beyond 18 days, no parameters differed from pre-FNA baselines.ConclusionsDLCT parameters showed a dynamic post-FNA temporal pattern, with early and later candidate recovery windows centered around approximately 6 and 18 days. From a clinical perspective, DLCT may be considered before FNA or after about 6 days, whereas a later interval around 18 days may be preferable when minimizing biopsy-related perturbation is particularly important.Keywords: Thyroid, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Fine needle aspiration, Dual-layer detector spectral computed tomography

Introduction

The global incidence of thyroid cancer has steadily increased, with annual rises of 3.6% in the United States [1] and 6.2% in men and 1.8% in women in China [2]. This trend, however, is largely attributed to increased detection of subclinical disease, particularly papillary thyroid microcarcinomas, rather than a true increase in disease occurrence [3]. Davies and Welch reported that between 1973 and 2002, 49% of the increase in thyroid cancer incidence consisted of tumors ≤ 1 cm, while overall mortality remained stable, suggesting significant overdiagnosis of indolent lesions [3]. The growing number of detected micro-lesions has heightened the clinical need for more reliable preoperative risk stratification tools to distinguish indolent from aggressive tumors. Although ultrasound serves as the primary diagnostic modality for thyroid evaluation, its well-recognized operator dependence and suboptimal sensitivity for central lymph node metastasis detection remain significant limitations. A meta-analysis [4] reported a pooled sensitivity of only 33.0% for this purpose, stressing the need for complementary imaging approaches.

Dual-layer detector spectral computed tomography (DLCT) represents a technological breakthrough that addresses the limitations of conventional CT in iodine quantification through its innovative dual-photon counting technology [5]. Growing evidence supports its diagnostic superiority in three key domains. First, in thyroid nodule characterization, our previous work developed DLCT-based nomograms to differentiate benign from malignant thyroid micro-nodules and distinguish microcarcinoma from micronodular goiter [6, 7], achieving area under the curve values of 85.8% and 88.0%. Recent studies further indicate DLCT’s capability to differentiate ultrasound-classified intermediate-high risk nodules from low-risk lesions, as well as fine-needle aspiration (FNA)-diagnosed Bethesda III/IV nodules [8, 9]. Second, DLCT shows superior diagnostic accuracy for challenging lymph nodal evaluations, including nodes with a short diameter of less than 0.5 cm, clinically negative nodes, and those in the lower cervical or upper mediastinal regions [10–13]. Third, integrating spectral parameters into machine learning models enhances clinical utility [14–16]. With recent advances in artificial intelligence and multimodal data fusion [17], DLCT is expected to find broader applications.

Despite these technological advancements, standardization gaps persist in clinical practice. Current American Thyroid Association guidelines lack specific recommendations regarding (1) the potential influence of FNA on CT quantitative parameters, and (2) the optimal temporal relationship between FNA and CT examinations. This oversight is significant given evidence that FNA induces histopathological alterations in thyroid tissue, including hemorrhage, granulation tissue formation, and fibrotic [18]. Crucially, the potential impact of these procedure-related changes on DLCT parameters remains unclear. If such effects exist, they could compromise both clinical reliability and the multicenter study reproducibility. These considerations highlight the need for an evidence-based clearance period between FNA and DLCT imaging.

We hypothesized that FNA-induced alterations in thyroid tissue may transiently affect DLCT parameters within a defined temporal window. To directly test this hypothesis, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to determine whether FNA affects DLCT parameters in PTC and the duration of such effects. This investigation addresses a critical gap in translating DLCT advancements into reliable clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective cohort study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Chongqing General Hospital (Approval No. KY S2023-002-01) with waiver of informed consent. From January 2022 to December 2023, 743 PTC patients undergoing DLCT were initially enrolled. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed PTC, (2) contrast-enhanced DLCT within 2 weeks prior to surgery, and (3) FNA performed either before (pre-FNA) or after (post-FNA) DLCT. Patients were excluded if: (1) images were significantly affected by artifacts or noise, or (2) there was a mismatch between nodule size on imaging and pathology. After applying these criteria, 689 patients were included. Clinical and pathological variables included gender, age, T stage (AJCC 8th edition), number of tumors, maximum tumor diameter from pathology (Dmax), capsule invasion, nerve invasion, vascular invasion, nodular goiter, chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (CLT), and lymph node metastasis (LNM).

DLCT protocol

All patients underwent unenhanced and contrast-enhanced cervical DLCT (IQon Spectral CT, Philips Healthcare) within two weeks before surgery. Scanning parameters were: tube voltage, 120 kVp (the standard setting for spectral data acquisition on this scanner); tube current, modulated with automated exposure control (DoseRight system, Philips Healthcare); detector collimation, 64 × 0.625 mm; field of view, 250 mm; matrix, 512 × 512; layer thickness, 3 mm; reconstruction thickness, 0.625 mm. A nonionic contrast agent (Loversol, 350 mgI/ml; Schering, Berlin, Germany) was administered via automatic injector at 1.1–1.3 ml/kg and 3–4 ml/s, followed by 20–30 ml saline flush. After unenhanced CT scanning, contrast-enhanced CT scanning was performed. The trigger threshold was 150 Hounsfield units (HUs) in the descending aorta at the level of tracheal bifurcation. Arterial phase (AP) and venous phase (VP) were acquired at 9 and 60 s post-trigger, respectively. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the timing of FNA relative to DLCT scans.

Quantitative parameter analysis of DLCT

Spectral base images from AP and VP were transferred to a workstation (IntelliSpace Portal, Version 10.1, Philips Healthcare). Based on their established clinical relevance and widespread use in thyroid imaging [5, 19–21], the following DLCT parameters were selected for analysis. Virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) at 40, 70, and 100 keV were reconstructed—energy levels most commonly reported in the literature, reflecting iodine sensitivity (40 keV), 120 kVp equivalence (70 keV), and artifact reduction (100 keV), respectively. Iodine concentration (IC) and effective atomic number (Zeff) maps were also generated to quantify tissue iodine content and elemental composition. A circular region of interest (ROI1) was manually placed at the center of the target lesion, encompassing the largest possible lesion area while avoiding calcification, necrosis, hemorrhage, or cystic components. In patients with multifocal PTC, only the lesion that had undergone FNA was delineated. A second ROI (ROI2) was positioned at the center of the ipsilateral common carotid artery at the same level to enable subsequent normalization.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared between pre-FNA and post-FNA groups using independent t-tests with Welch’s correction for unequal variances (verified by Levene’s test). Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Propensity scores were derived through multivariable logistic regression incorporating all baseline covariates. The symmetry of propensity scores between the two groups was assessed using back-to-back histograms. Full matching method was then performed to achieve covariates balance [22, 23]. The effectiveness of propensity score matching (PSM) was evaluated using standardized mean difference (SMD) scatter plots and line graphs. After matching, DLCT parameters were compared between groups using weighted linear regression models incorporating the matching weights, with cluster-robust standard errors calculated at the matched subclass level. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were performed using two analytical approaches: inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and multivariable regression.

The post-FNA time intervals were ranked and divided into ten parts of equal size, known as deciles. Temporal trends of DLCT parameters were visualized via adjusted residual line plots which providing a straightforward and data-driven approach to identify candidate thresholds. Potential cutoff points were proposed by examining dynamic patterns across deciles. Based on this threshold, the post-FNA group were subsequently categorized into subgroups. For validating the proposed temporal cutoffs, each post-FNA subgroup were compared with the pre-FNA group using weighted analysis based on generalized propensity score. Holm’s step-down procedure was used for multiple comparison adjustments, 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated to determine uncertainty and statistical significance of mean difference between comparison groups. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 defined statistical significance. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.0; www.R-project.org) with packages (Matchit, twang, and survey) [24–26].

Results

Baseline characteristics

The cohort included 689 patients: 222 patients underwent DLCT scans before FNA (pre-FNA group), and 467 patients underwent DLCT scans after FNA (post-FNA group). Prior to PSM, significant baseline imbalances were observed (Table 1). Patients in the post-FNA group were younger, with a lower proportion aged 55 years or older compared to the pre-FNA group (12.4% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.046). The pre-FNA group exhibited higher tumor multiplicity (47.3% vs. 30.8%, p < 0.001) and larger Dmax (11.9 mm vs. 9.5 mm, p < 0.001). In contrast, the post-FNA group had lower rates of capsule invasion (79.7% vs. 88.7%, p < 0.001) and LNM (45.0% vs. 65.8%, p < 0.001). Propensity score distributions exhibited distinct imbalance between the groups (Fig. 1), with median scores of 0.61 (IQR: 0.51, 0.73) for the pre-FNA group and 0.73 (IQR: 0.62, 0.82) for the post-FNA group.

Temporal changes in DLCT parameters following FNA

The time interval between FNA and DLCT scan ranged from 1 to 275 days, with the majority of patients (89.5%) undergoing imaging within 30 days. Based on adjusted residual line plots analysis, we observed that DLCT measurements at the 4th and 8th decile time points post-FNA (i.e., days 6 and 18 post-FNA) most closely approximated the pre-FNA baseline values (Fig. 3). According to the findings of Recavarren et al. [27], early post-FNA changes such as hemorrhage and granulation tissue formation are most prominent within the first 10 days, while reactive stromal and follicular cell proliferation become more evident after 11–20 days, peaking around 21–30 days. These histological patterns provide a biological rationale for the observed DLCT parameter. Accordingly, these two time points were proposed as exploratory candidate nodes with potential clinical relevance. Using these time points, post-FNA patients were stratified into three subgroups for comparative analysis: 1–6 days, 7–18 days, and > 18 days post-FNA. The AP measurements demonstrated significant temporal evolution across post-FNA phases (Table 5). During the early post-FNA phase (1–6 days post-FNA), all quantitative parameters showed marked reductions: VMIs at 40 keV (MD = -27.9, 95% CI: -46.8 to -9.0, adjusted p = 0.012), 70 keV (MD = -9.2, 95% CI: -14.8 to -3.7, adjusted p = 0.004), and 100 keV (MD = -5.6, 95% CI: -8.9 to -2.3, adjusted p = 0.003), λHU (40–100 keV) (MD = -0.4, 95% CI: -0.7 to -0.1, adjusted p = 0.035), λHU (40–70 keV) (MD = -0.6, 95% CI: -1.1 to -0.2, adjusted p = 0.029), IC (MD = -0.3, 95% CI: -0.5 to -0.1, adjusted p = 0.024), and NIC (MD = -0.3, 95% CI: -0.1 to 0, adjusted p = 0.009). These reductions attenuated during the intermediate recovery phase (7–18 days), though none reached statistical significance. By the late stabilization phase (> 18 days), all parameters had returned to baseline levels with no significant differences observed.

VP parameters (Table 6) exhibited similar but less pronounced temporal patterns. The intermediate recovery phase (7–18 days) was characterized by continued parameter reductions, with only IC showing statistically significant change (MD = -0.2, 95% CI: -0.40 to -0.05, adjusted p = 0.042). Discussion

This study revealed dynamic temporal changes in DLCT parameters of PTC following FNA. Our findings demonstrate that FNA-induced alterations in DLCT parameters persist for approximately six days, with complete resolution by 18 days post-procedure. To balance imaging reliability with clinical realities such as hospital stay and cost, DLCT can be considered either before FNA or after approximately 6 days. When pre-FNA imaging is not feasible, a 6-day post-FNA interval may provide a pragmatic compromise between image quality and workflow efficiency. For research requiring parameter values close to baseline, an around 18-day interval may be preferable. These insights may support optimized clinical workflow, reduced diagnostic errors in PTC, and enhanced rigor in future research.

FNA remains the gold standard for preoperative evaluation of thyroid nodules. However, its invasive nature can lead to hemorrhage, siderophagia, granulation tissue, papillary hyperplasia, fibrosis, and so on [18], which may potentially influence imaging outcomes. While previous studies have validated DLCT’s utility in predicting extrathyroidal extension, lymph node metastasis, and histopathological features [15, 28, 29], this study represents a novel investigation into the temporal impact of FNA on DLCT parameters.

To address potential confounders, we included clinicopathological covariates and applied PSM, achieving effective balance of variables between pre- and post-FNA groups. Given the sample size disparity between the two groups, the full matching method was employed for PSM to maximize sample size retention and enhance the robustness of our analysis. In the primary full-matching analysis, significant differences were observed mainly in VMIs at 70 keV and 100 keV in both the AP and VP, with all values being lower in the post-FNA group compared to the pre-FNA group. Sensitivity analyses using IPTW and multivariable regression showed attenuation of statistical significance for several parameters, but the general pattern of effect estimates remained similar. Among all parameters, VP-HU at 100 keV and VP-NZeff showed the most robust signals across analytical methods, whereas other parameters demonstrated directionally consistent but less statistically stable associations. Overall, these findings suggest that the main results are not driven by any single adjustment strategy, although the strength of evidence varies across parameters and analytic frameworks. Across the three analytical approaches, it is evident that FNA exerts an effect on certain DLCT parameter values. It is worth noting that for parameters showing no statistically significant differences between groups, caution interpretation is warranted. Given the dynamic pathological changes in thyroid tissue following FNA [27], a simple pre- vs. post-FNA comparison may oversimplify the temporal trajectory of parameter alterations. Therefore, it is necessary to observe the time-dependent changes in DLCT parameters after FNA and conduct subgroup comparative analysis to better validate these effects.

We chose adjusted residual line plots because they allowed direct visualization of post-FNA temporal patterns relative to the pre-FNA baseline. In this study, our aim was to propose and evaluate clinically relevant candidate time nodes rather than derive precise mathematical cutoffs. Therefore, visual assessment of temporal re-approximation to baseline was considered more appropriate than formal threshold-estimation approaches. Subgroup analyses revealed dynamic changes in adjusted DLCT parameters over time. Attenuation values of VMIs in the AP and VP were lower in the early post-FNA period (1–6 days), likely reflecting FNA-induced acute hemorrhagic changes. This aligns with histopathological reports demonstrating hemorrhage as the predominant early post-FNA alteration [27, 30]. Notably, PTC lesions typically exhibit higher CT values due to iodine-rich follicular epithelium and tumor angiogenesis. Whereas acute hemorrhage generally presents as hypodense regions, typically less than 90 HU, and with minimal enhancement. The differential time course of parameter recovery further supports this hypothesis: Attenuation values in the 7–18 days subgroup showed partial recovery compared to the 1–6 days group, eventually exceeding or near baseline levels in the > 18 days subgroup. This trajectory mirrors the temporal resolution of hemorrhage and subsequent fibrotic changes, consistent with previous observations of decreased hemorrhage incidence 20 days post-FNA [27].

The biphasic response of IC and NIC parameters warrants particular attention. While early-phase IC reduction (1–18 days) may reflect cellular damage impairing iodine uptake, one possible explanation for the paradoxical elevation beyond 18 days is delayed contrast clearance secondary to FNA-induced fibrosis [18, 31]. However, further studies are needed to confirm this potential mechanism. The minimal fluctuations in Zeff and NZeff parameters, suggesting that elemental composition alterations are less susceptible to procedural artifacts. Spectral curve analysis reflects the absorption and attenuation of X-rays and can be used for substance identification [6, 19]. Subgroup analyses revealed statistically significant decreases in values of λHU (40–70 keV) and λHU (40–100 keV) within the first 18 days, potentially serving as biomarkers for FNA-induced tissue changes. The subsequent non-significant rise in λHU beyond this period may indicate gradual return to baseline tissue characteristics.

Our findings should be interpreted considering three main limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design and modest sample size (particularly in subgroup analyses) necessitate validation through multicenter prospective studies. Second, while PSM mitigated selection bias, several unmeasured confounders could still influence DLCT parameters and were not controlled for in this study. These include technical factors related to the FNA procedure itself (e.g., needle gauge, number of passes), patient-specific medications (e.g., anticoagulants or antiplatelet therapy that may affect tissue perfusion), and underlying tumor or host characteristics (e.g., tumor vascularity, presence of Hashimoto thyroiditis). Future studies incorporating these variables are warranted to further validate our findings. Third, the absence of serial histopathological correlation limits our ability to directly link imaging changes to specific tissue alterations. Future studies using animal models with serial imaging and corresponding histopathological validation could help elucidate these relationships. Additionally, incorporating advanced imaging biomarkers (e.g., perfusion parameters) in such models may further clarify these relationships.

In conclusion, our results suggest clinically relevant temporal landmarks around 6 and 18 days after FNA, with arterial-phase DLCT parameters showing the greatest sensitivity to biopsy-related changes. To balance imaging reliability with clinical practicality, DLCT may be considered either before FNA or after approximately 6 days. When near-baseline measurements are desired, an interval of around 18 days may be preferable.
