Synthesis and efficiency of the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic in the removal of Gasoil from aqueous solutions
Authors: Elaheh Tajari, Hadis Bashiri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study presents the development of a novel cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic adsorbent derived from Prosopis farcta biomass for efficient Gasoil removal from aqueous solutions. The composite material was synthesized through a solvothermal method, combining the porous structure of ZIF-8, magnetic Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, and carbonized cellulose fibers. Comprehensive characterization using FT-IR, XRD, SEM-EDS, VSM, BET, and contact angle measurements confirmed the successful integration of components, revealing a hierarchical pore structure (micropores: 1–2 nm; mesopores: 14.84 nm average size). The adsorbent exhibited exceptional Gasoil adsorption capacity (7 g*g−1) and 100% removal efficiency under optimized conditions (0.1 g adsorbent dose, 0.5 g Gasoil, and pH 7.5), as determined by the Design of Experiment. ANOVA analysis highlighted the dominant effects of Gasoil weight (A), adsorbent weight (B) and, while pH showed negligible impact. Adsorption isotherms followed the Freundlich model (R² = 0.93), indicating multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Thermodynamic studies revealed spontaneous (ΔG < 0), exothermic (ΔH = − 259.98 J*mol−1), and entropy-driven (ΔS = 18.66 J*mol−1*K−1) adsorption. The material demonstrated rapid magnetic separation (< 10 s) and maintained 90% efficiency after 8 regeneration cycles. FT-IR spectra confirmed chemical interactions (π-π stacking, hydrophobic bonding) via peak shifts (C = C: 1558 → 1566 cm−1; new C-H stretches at 2853/2953 cm−1). Compared to conventional adsorbents, this aerogel outperformed in capacity, reusability, and emulsion prevention due to its unique structure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23556-0.

Introduction

The presence of Gasoil in water sources is particularly detrimental to plant/animal life and human health. Removing Gasoil from aqueous solutions is vital for protecting the safety and purity of water supplies. Various traditional techniques have been employed for pollution removal, such as adsorption1–3, photocatalysts4–6, sedimentation7,8, and ion exchange. However, these methods often suffer from restrictions such as low performance, cheapness, and complicated operation. Due to their promising adsorption capabilities and prospective uses in environmental remediation, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have engrossed much consideration in recent years as a unique class of porous materials. In this context, the extension of a highly efficient, profitable, and environmentally benign adsorbent based on MOFs is necessary for Gasoil removal from aqueous solutions9–11. MOFs are porous material with definite crystalline structure, large surface area, and pore size that can be modified by altering the concentration of metal ions or organic ligands. They have interesting characteristics that make them good candidates for many uses, such as gas storage, catalysis, and environmental remediation. The excellent thermal and chemical stability of Zeolitic Imidazolate Frameworks (ZIFs) has made them a prominent subclass within the larger family of MOFs12.

ZIF-8, made up of Zinc ions coupled by imidazole ligands, is a water-stable MOF with an explicit microporous structure. Heavy metal ion removal and dye removal from aqueous solutions are only two of the many uses that have prompted substantial research into the material13. The high surface area, broad pH stability, and feasible chemical interactions of ZIF-8 make it an ideal material for the adsorption of Gasoil and other contaminants14. Cellulosic fibrous materials derived from biomass have also shown potential as adsorbents for Gasoil removal. One such biomass is Prosopis farcta (PF), a renewable resource with abundant cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin content15. The magnetic properties of the composite material facilitate its easy separation and recovery from the aqueous solution, making it an ideal candidate for practical applications in water treatment16–18.

Recent studies have shown that CelloMOFs have multifaceted applications such as water treatment, antibacterial, gas separation, and high-performance flexible supercapacitors19–24. These MOFs are a type of MOF that incorporate magnetic nanoparticles into their structure, resulting in materials with unique properties and applications25–27. Magnetic MOFs can be utilized as sorbents to eliminate organic compounds from water. This category of MOFs has possible utilizations in various areas, including water treatment, catalysis, and biotechnology28.

In a study, LGN adsorbents prepared using luffa sponges and hollow CuFe2O4 nanospheres were used to remove various types of oil from water. The synthesized adsorbent had an adsorption capacity of 47.6–86.6 g*g−1. Through the capillary action, oil and organic solvents spontaneously penetrate into the interior of the aerogel, while the superhydrophobic surface prevents water from entering, thereby enabling the separation of oil and water. In addition, LGN can exhibit an oriented sorption performance by controlling the applied magnetic field, enabling sorbent recycling even under extreme environmental conditions29.

For removing Fosfomycin from pharmaceutical wastewater, a core-shell Fe3O4/PVP/ZIF-8 nanostructure adsorbent was generated30. The research evaluated the regeneration performance of the core-shell nanostructure adsorbent for Fosfomycin removal. The adsorption efficacy of sonochemically produced Fe3O4@ZIF-8 for the elimination of copper ions from water is shown in another work31. It has a high maximal adsorption capacity (345 mg*g−1), rapid adsorption time (10 min), and efficient elimination (95.3%). Heydari Moghadam et al. investigated zeolite imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) to remove trichloroethylene from aqueous solutions with an optimum elimination efficiency of 96.29%. The adsorption capacity was 136.33 mg*g−1. Trichloroethylene was demonstrated to be successfully removed from water by the ZIF-8 platform32. Khalilifard et al. used oleic acid- and graphene oxide-doped magnetic superhydrophobic polyurethane to separate organic species from aqueous solution. The magnetic sponges can be regulated and recycled using a magnet 15 times for oil spill cleaning without losing their adsorption ability. The modified sponge is an efficient adsorbent for treating oil in polluted water33.

This research presents a novel adsorbent, cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic, for Gasoil removal from aqueous solutions. It combines the advantages of biomass, magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and ZIF-8, resulting in a highly efficient, cost-effective, and environmentally friendly adsorbent material. The composite structure effectively overcomes the inherent limitations of ZIF-8, such as particle aggregation, and enables rapid Gasoil diffusion and adsorption. Design of Experiment (DOE) method, precisely the Box-Behnken method, is employed to optimize adsorption. Additionally, we investigate the adsorption isotherms, thermodynamics, and adsorption mechanism of the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic.

Experimental section

Materials and methods

Fe(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3.6H2O, 99.0%), ethanol (96%), sodium citrate, sodium acetate, ethylene glycol, mercaptoacetic acid (MAA), zinc nitrate, methanol, and 2-methylimidazole have been purchased from Merck Company. Fruits of PF were collected from meadows in central Iran. In this study, Gasoil (Diesel fuel; Auto diesel) has been used. Since Gasoil consists several substances and it is not a pure substance, Gasoil of different regions can have different percentages of existing compounds. To consider it, the used Gasoil in this research was prepared from a specific location.

Synthesis of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic adsorbent

The synthesized adsorbent in this research was named as cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic. The used cellulose is from the fruit of the Prosopis farcta and the magnetic property is due to the presence of Fe3O4 in it. The synthesis method is described below.

Preparation of cellulosic fibrous biomass

PF is a renewable biomass resource, which was used as the cellulose fiber material. A mechanical grinder was used to reduce the size of the material to a finer consistency. They were then given several methanol washes.

Synthesis of Fe3O4 nanoparticles

Fe3O4 Particles were successfully generated via a solvothermal approach. Using magnetic stirring for 30 min, 80 ml of ethylene glycol dissolved 2.7 g FeCl3.6H2O, 1.0 g sodium citrate, and 4.8 g NaAc. This mixture was sealed in a Teflon-lined stainless steel container and autoclaved at 473 K for 10 h. Consequently, microspheres of Fe3O4 were collected, washed many times in ethanol, and then vacuum dried at 50 °C. To make mercaptoacetic acid (MAA)-functionalized Fe3O4 nanoparticles, the following steps were taken: After shaking a mercaptoacetic acid (2.9 mM) ethanolic solution of 100 ml for 24 h, 0.5 g Fe3O4 was added. The MAA-Fe3O4 nanoparticles were collected and washed.

Synthesis of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic

First, 6.4 g of Zn(NO3)2 and 14 g of prepared biomass were added to 20 ml of methanol. Then, 1.0 g of MAA-Fe3O4 and 9.24 g of 2-methylimidazole were added and stirred for 2 h at room temperature. After that, it was dried in a vacuum at a 50 °C to obtain cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8. To prepare the carbon aerogel, the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 were placed in a tube furnace and pyrolyzed at 600 °C for 1 h at an N2 atmosphere. Finally, cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic was prepared.

Adsorption experiments

First, the weighed Gasoil was added the center of the water surface in the culture dish. The deionized water has been used to make the water-Gasoil mixtures. Then, a certain weight adsorbent was placed on the Gasoil-water mixture. After adsorption of the floating Gasoil by adsorbent, the adsorbent was separated from the water with a magnet and then, it was weighed. Adsorption of Gasoil onto the adsorbent occurs within 10 s. The schematic representation of the adsorption process is shown in Fig. 1. All adsorption experiments were conducted under static conditions to preserve the floating Gasoil layer’s integrity. The aerogel was carefully placed at the water-Gasoil interface without mechanical agitation, allowing natural capillary action to drive the adsorption process. The investigation of equilibrium adsorption isotherms is fundamental to understanding the interaction mechanisms between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate molecules at equilibrium. These models provide critical insights into the adsorption capacity, surface homogeneity, affinity, and energetic characteristics of the adsorption process. In this study, the equilibrium adsorption behavior of Gasoil onto the synthesized cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic composite, prepared under optimized conditions, was systematically evaluated using four nonlinear isotherm models: Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Azizian-Eris. The corresponding nonlinearized equations for each model are provided in the Appendix. At 30, 40, and 50℃, the nonlinear isotherms of Gasoil adsorption by cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic were investigated.

Batch adsorption experiments were conducted at a constant temperature and pH, with varying initial Gasoil concentrations ranging from 25 to 150 g*L−1 (i.e., 25, 50, 75, 100, 125, and 150 g*L−1).

Regeneration of adsorbent

The regeneration process involves magnetic separation of the saturated adsorbent was done using a magnetfrom the solution, followed by mechanical squeezing to remove the adsorbed Gasoil (as seen in Fig. 1). This method is efficient and cost-effective36,37.

Design of experiment: box-behnken method

Design of Experiment (DOE) is a method to select the experiments. Using this method, the maximum possible information can be obtained from each experiment, and the importance of independent variables can be statistically measured. The purpose of this method is to obtain the maximum amount of information possible by performing the minimum number of experiments in which the key variables are changed, simultaneously. In the DOE method, the effect of various factors as independent and controllable input variables on the output dependent variable, which is called the response, is measured, and a suitable mathematical relationship is calculated38.

The Box-Behnken DOE approach studied the effects of variables on the Gasoil removal by cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic in an aqueous solution. This method helps determine the optimal parameters for the adsorption process, such as the mass of adsorbent (A), the mass of Gasoil (B), and the pH value (C). By systematically varying these parameters, it is possible to identify the conditions that result in maximum Gasoil removal efficiency. After that, we followed the blueprint and coded the independent variables’ levels (−1, 0, 1 for low, medium, and high values) (Table 1). Results and discussion

FT-IR spectroscopy

cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic was subjected to Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) to determine the surface’s functional groups, and the results are shown in Fig. 2a. The peak at 1558 cm−1 can be attributed to C = C stretching due to α,β-unsaturated ketone compounds consisting of a carbon-carbon double bond, which is chemically reactive and capable of undergoing a variety of reactions, including addition reactions, conjugate additions, and polymerization.

Meanwhile, the peak at 1003.2 cm−1 in cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic can be attributed to the C-O stretching of an aromatic ester. Adsorbent contains peak at 2923 cm−1. This peak is caused by the stretching vibrations of the hydroxyl groups, which may be due to the presence of carboxylic groups in sample39. The band at 872.2 cm−1 represents the stretching vibration of the Fe-O bond40, indicating FeOx in the aerogel’s carbonaceous phase. The bands in the spectral region of 800–1500 cm−1 are assigned as the plane bending and stretching of the imidazole ring37. This band also shows the formation of the ZIF-8 structure. The functional groups identified in the sample can participate in the surface adsorption of Gasoil.

The cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic after adsorption of Gasoil was tested using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, and the result is shown in Fig. 2b. Also, Fig. 2c shows the FT-IR spectroscopy of Gasoil. The C = C stretching vibration of an α, β-unsaturated ketone from 1558 cm−1 (before) to 1566 cm−1 (after) carbon-carbon double bond next to a carbonyl group. Additional peaks at 1454 cm−1 are found after adsorption. These peaks are associated with C-H bending, which can be attributed to Gasoil and substituted hydrocarbons in the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic after adsorption. Lastly, an additional peak at 2853 cm−1 and 2953 cm−1 was found after Gasoil adsorption. This peak indicates that the availability of functional groups for chemical reactions, adsorption, or surface modification can be characterized by C-H stretching vibrations associated with hydroxyl groups.

Similar shifts were reported for Fe-O bonds (872 cm−1)41, and for ZIF-8 imidazole rings (800–1500 cm−1)42. The C-H stretches align with findings43 for hydrophobic adsorbents.

SEM and EDS analyses

The SEM images of the cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 magnetic carbon aerogel (Fig. 3) reveal a highly porous structure with abundant gaps and cavities, which are favorable for the adsorption of Gasoil from aqueous solutions. This morphology is consistent with previous studies on porous aerogels and metal-organic framework (MOF)-based composites. For instance, Zhang et al. in 2020 reported a similar porous structure in ZIF-8-modified cellulose aerogels, which enhanced the adsorption capacity for organic pollutants due to increased surface area and active sites44.

The EDS analysis (Fig. 4) confirms the presence of Fe, O, and Zn, indicating the successful incorporation of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles and ZIF-8 into the cellulose matrix. Comparable results were observed by Jiang et al. (2021), where Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 composites exhibited distinct Fe and Zn peaks in EDS, confirming the hybrid structure45. The uniform distribution of these elements suggests a well-dispersed nanocomposite, which is crucial for efficient adsorption.

Vibrating-Sample magnetometer (VSM)

The magnetic hysteresis loop tests a magnetic aerogel’s reaction to a magnetic field. It is shown as a curve of change of magnetization of the material concerning the applied area. The residual loop relies on magnetic field direction, sample ratio, sample extent, material type, and magnetic return method. Figure 6 demonstrates the hysteresis loop under the field − 15,000 to + 15,000 Oe. The cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic hysteresis curves showed supermagnetic adsorption. The measured saturation magnetization (Ms) and magnetic remanence (Mr) were 5.83 and 0.7081 emu*g−1, respectively. An external magnet can easily extract cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic from the solution. Bui et al. in 2022 reported Ms = 45 emu*g−1 for Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8, but this study’s lower value still ensured practical magnetic recovery48.

Also, the amount of zinc before adsorption is 3.88%, which reaches 3.2% after adsorption (Table 3). This finding demonstrates the durability and presence of magnetic characteristics in the adsorbent following the adsorption process.

BET measurements

The specific surface area and pore characteristics of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic have been estimated by N2 adsorption and desorption experiments. As shown in Fig. 7, a typical IV isotherm with a distinct hysteresis loop was observed for cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic, indicating their porous nature.

The specific surface area of ​​​​7.33 m2*g−1 was obtained, which seems to be due to the confinement of Fe3O4@ZIF-8 in the adsorbent cellulose structure. Using the BET method, the total pore volume and average pore size were calculated as 0.027 cm3*g−1 and 14.84 nm (Table 4). Zhao et al. in 2020 achieved higher surface areas (∼1200 m²*g−1) with MOF-derived aerogels but emphasized pore size (2–50 nm) as critical for hydrocarbon adsorption, corroborating this study’s pore efficiency41.

Contact angle and wettability of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic

As well known, the separation capacity of Gasoil/water mixtures largely depends on wettability49,50. To investigate the wetting property of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic, we characterized the contact angles and permeating behavior of water or Gasoil. The results can be seen in Fig. 8. In air, the water droplets infiltrated the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic rapidly with a contact angle of close to zero (Fig. 8a). While Gasoil droplets also spread on the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic contact angle of less 10° (Fig. 8b). Water droplets spread and permeate through the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic, whereas Gasoil droplets only spread on it. The water droplets would pass through the pores of adsorbent, while the Gasoil droplets are blocked in the pores of adsorbent. This property of the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic can be due to the hydrocarbon linker and cellulose in its structure.

Interestingly, the magnitude of ΔG becomes more negative with increasing temperature, indicating that higher temperatures enhance the spontaneity of the adsorption process. This behavior is characteristic of endothermic physical adsorption processes, where increased thermal energy facilitates molecular diffusion and access to deeper pore structures. The enthalpy change (ΔH) was determined to be − 259.98 J*mol⁻¹, indicating a very weak exothermic interaction between the Gasoil molecules and the adsorbent surface. This extremely low value of ΔH rules out strong chemical interactions (chemisorption) and confirms that the process is dominated by weak physical forces, such as van der Waals interactions, hydrophobic effects, and capillary condensation. Despite the weak enthalpic contribution, the positive entropy change (ΔS = + 18.66 J*mol−1*K−1) plays a crucial role in driving the process. The increase in entropy is attributed to the release of structured water molecules from the hydrophobic surface of the adsorbent upon Gasoil uptake — a phenomenon known as the “hydrophobic effect”.

When Gasoil molecules approach the hydrophobic surface of the cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic, the highly ordered water layer (clathrate-like structure) surrounding the nonpolar surface is disrupted and released into the bulk solution, leading to a significant increase in system entropy. This entropic gain compensates for the small enthalpic release and makes the overall ΔG negative, thereby driving the adsorption spontaneously.

Adsorption mechanism

The mechanism of Gasoil adsorption onto the cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic is primarily governed by physical interactions and thermodynamically driven by entropy gain, rather than strong chemical binding. This conclusion is supported by the thermodynamic data, FTIR analysis, and the hydrophobic nature of the adsorbent.

The extremely low enthalpy change (ΔH = − 259.98 J*mol⁻¹) indicates that the process involves negligible heat release, ruling out strong exothermic interactions such as chemisorption, hydrogen bonding, or covalent attachment. Instead, the adsorption is driven by weak van der Waals forces and, more significantly, by the hydrophobic effect—a well-known phenomenon in oil/water separation systems where the release of structured water molecules from hydrophobic interfaces provides the main thermodynamic driving force34,56.

The positive entropy change (ΔS = + 18.66 J*mol−1*K−1) suggests an increase in the system’s disorder during adsorption. This is attributed to the release of highly ordered water molecules from the hydrophobic surface of the aerogel upon contact with nonpolar Gasoil molecules. In aqueous media, water forms a structured, clathrate-like layer around hydrophobic surfaces—a phenomenon known as “hydration shell” or “ice-like structure”.When Gasoil molecules approach the surface, they displace these ordered water molecules into the bulk solution, resulting in a net increase in entropy. This entropic gain is the primary driving force for the spontaneous adsorption (negative ΔG), compensating for the minimal enthalpic contribution. This mechanism is widely reported for hydrophobic porous materials used in oil/water separation, where physical adsorption is entropy-dominated33. Figure 14 shows a schematic illustration of the adsorption mechanism by the adsorbent.

Furthermore, the hierarchical porous structure of the aerogel—derived from carbonized cellulose fibers, Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles, and ZIF-8—facilitates capillary condensation and pore filling. The interconnected micro- and mesopores act as nanochannels that rapidly draw in Gasoil through capillary forces, enabling high uptake capacity (7.00 g*g⁻¹) and fast kinetics (complete removal within 10 s). The low density and high porosity of the aerogel allow it to float on the water surface and directly interact with the floating Gasoil layer, maximizing contact efficiency51.

FTIR analysis shows minor shifts in C–H and C = C stretching vibrations after adsorption (from 1558 to 1566 cm−1 for C = C, and new peaks at 1454, 2853, and 2953 cm−1 for C–H bending and stretching), which can be attributed to weak dispersive interactions (e.g., van der Waals or π-π dispersion forces) between the aromatic/aliphatic components of Gasoil and the carbonaceous framework of the adsorbent. However, the absence of new covalent bond formation or significant peak broadening confirms the physical nature of the interaction. These observations are consistent with studies on similar MOF-based and biomass-derived aerogels, where adsorption of hydrocarbons occurs via physical entrapment and hydrophobic affinity rather than chemical reaction34,51.

These combined mechanisms make the adsorbent particularly effective for Gasoil spill remediation, where emulsion prevention is essential for efficient water purification. The prevention of emulsification also enhances the adsorbent’s reusability, as the absence of water-in-Gasoil emulsions allows for more complete Gasoil recovery during regeneration cycles. The material’s performance represents a significant advancement over conventional adsorbents that often struggle with emulsion formation during oil-water separation processes.

Regeneration and reusability

The regeneration of the spent adsorbent is a crucial economic factor in minimizing material costs. For long-term and practical applications, the regeneration and reusability of the cellulose@Fe₃O₄@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic composite are essential. The composite could be regenerated multiple times, ensuring sustained performance and cost-effectiveness in the removal of Gasoil. The regeneration process involves magnetically separating the saturated adsorbent from the solution using a magnet, followed by mechanical squeezing to remove the adsorbed Gasoil. The material is then dried at 80 °C for 2 h. The adsorption capacity of the regenerated adsorbent was evaluated over eight consecutive cycles. After eight uses, the adsorbent retained a high adsorption capacity, with less than 10% reduction (Fig. 15).

A significant advantage of this adsorbent is that its regeneration does not require solvent washing or high-temperature treatments33, making the recycling process simple, environmentally friendly, and energy-efficient19.

Evaluation of the Gasoil removal in different wastewaters

In wastewater treatment systems, mixed pollutant environments are particularly significant as they better represent real-world industrial effluents. To examine the adsorbent’s performance under complex conditions, we employed a municipal wastewater sample (initial turbidity: 250 NTU, COD: 400 mg*L−1) c as the baseline matrix. Since direct access to industrial Gasoil-containing wastewater was unavailable, we systematically spiked this municipal sample with 5000 mg*L−1 (5 g*L−1) of commercial Gasoil to simulate petroleum-contaminated wastewater. The adsorption experiments were conducted following the same protocol described in Sect. 2.4, maintaining identical adsorbent dosages (0.1–0.5 g) and contact times (10 s magnetic separation). The results showed that the adsorbent had an adsorption capacity of over 90% in removing Gasoil from wastewater samples with different pollutant compositions. The adsorbent was also able to reduce the COD and turbidity of the wastewater sample, which indicates the efficiency of the adsorbent.

Conclusion

In summary, the cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic adsorbent was synthesized through the in-situ growth of ZIF-8 on Fe3O4-modified cellulosic fibers, exhibits excellent Gasoil removal efficiency from aqueous solutions. The adsorption process was optimized using the Box-Behnken method, and the adsorption isotherms and thermodynamics were studied to understand the underlying mechanism. The adsorbent demonstrated good reusability and environmental compatibility, making it a promising candidate for Gasoil removal in real-world applications.

Under an external magnetic field, the saturated adsorbent is rapidly separated from the water without filtration or centrifugation. This adsorbent helps to save time, money, and materials. Only approximately 10% of the sorbent’s adsorption ability is lost after eight reuses, so it still has plenty of life.

The removal efficiency of Gasoil was studied using DOE to determine the optimal sorbent weight, starting Gasoil weight, and solution pH. By examining the impacts of numerous parameters on Gasoil removal, the starting concentration and adsorbent weight had the most significant influence. Using 0.5 g of Gasoil weight and 0.1 g of adsorbent weight is ideal for Gasoil removal. Under ideal circumstances, magnetic aerogels composed of cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic can remove all traces of Gasoil from the water. The Gasoil removal capability of the adsorbent is very high (7 g*g−1) in aqueous solutions.

The cellulose@Fe3O4@ZIF-8 carbon aerogel magnetic offers a cost-effective and eco-friendly solution for Gasoil removal, leveraging low-cost Prosopis farcta biomass and energy-efficient pyrolysis (600 °C). Its magnetic properties enable easy recovery and reuse (8 cycles, < 10% efficiency loss), reducing operational costs, while minimal metal leaching (Fe/Zn < 5%) ensures environmental safety. Compared to commercial adsorbents (e.g., activated carbon), its production cost and renewable feedstock align with circular economy goals, making it a scalable, sustainable alternative for oil-spill remediation.
