1
Introduction

We aim at studying a class of bounded, real valued processes
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

given by a
k
-term recurrence relation

X_{n+k}=\Phi_{0}(X_{n},\dots,X_{n-k+1})+\Theta_{n},

(1)

where
\Phi_{0}
is a real, nonlinear, deterministic function defined on

{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
,
X_{n}:(\Xi,{\cal T},\mathbb{P})\to{\mathbb{R}}
are real-valued random variables and

\Theta_{n}:(\Xi,{\cal T},\mathbb{P})\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a stochastic perturbation. We assume
that
\Theta=(\Theta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is i.i.d. and that the distribution

\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}
of one of the variables
\Theta_{n}
is absolutely
continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
m
, with density
H
.

When
\Theta
is equal to
0
, the recurrence relation is deterministic.
Such problems are the subject of
[
6
,
7
]
. In these articles, the
recurrence relation
is imbedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
in such a way as to give rise to a piecewise
expanding transformation of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. The properties of the transformation and
of its transfer operator imply results about the original process
X
.

Expanding maps and their ergodic properties have a long story.
In dimension
1
,
[
4
,
9
]
studied the transfer
operator of piecewise expanding transformations of an interval, applying
the result of
[
5
]
to prove its quasicompacity and the existence
of a spectral gap. This proved very fruitful, but in higher dimensions,
the method does not apply directly.
For example,

[
12
]
studies piecewise expanding transformations on a hypercube
which is partitioned in smaller hypercubes. In
[
13
]

the subdomains are polyhedral and the maps, linear. The domains are not so
regular in
[
10
,
11
]
, but the (nonlinear)
transformations satisfy very strict and complicated conditions.
In
[
15
]
, the
transformation and its properties are presented in a very axiomatized way,
which sheds a new light on the transformations and on the subdomains.
But it is extremely difficult to apply to a concrete example,
although
[
2
]
proved that such a decomposition in “towers”
is equivalent to good rates of decay of the correlations. Hence, in situations
where the rate of decay is known, the towers must exist.

The difficulty one faces in dimension superior to
1
is intrinsic:
[
14
]
constructs
an example of a piecewise expanding map in dimension
2
,
in which the successive images of
a (very well chosen) open rectangle have a diameter that converges to
0
,
contradicting the naive intuition that
the expanding property leads to
evergrowing iterates and even to exactness.

In view of the shape and nonlinearity of the recurrence relation, the technics developed in
[
11
]
seemed perfectly adapted. In this work,
the author studies expanding, piecewise defined
transformations
T
of a compact subset
{\Omega}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
.

Under
geometric conditions, he proves the existence of an absolutely continuous
invariant measure
\mu
(ACIM) for
T
. Moreover, if
T
is mixing,
the correlations decay at an exponential rate. That means that, for
convenient functions
f,h
, there exist
\Lambda\in[0,1[
and a
real constant
C(f,h)
such that, for any
n
,

\left|\int_{\Omega}f\circ T^{n}h\ d\mu-\int_{\Omega}f\ d\mu\int_{\Omega}h\ d\mu\right|\leq C(f,h)\Lambda^{n}.

In other words, if
Y_{0}\sim\mu
and if
Y_{n}=T^{n}Y_{0}
,

{\rm cov}(f(Y_{n}),h(Y_{0}))\leq C(f,h)\Lambda^{n}
.

Obtaining the mixing property is non trivial. The classical
conditions on the
transfer operator
or the more recent random covering condition
[
1
]
are difficult to
obtain. For example,
in
[
12
]
the author even obtains exactness, but the shape of the partition
is very regular. Therefore, the mixing condition has, up to now, to remain an
additional condition.

To apply the results of
[
11
]
in our situation,
one needs to derive a
transformation
T
of a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
from the function

\Phi_{0}
leading the process (
1
).
This was achieved, in the deterministic case,
in
[
6
,
7
]
for recurrence relations of all orders
k\geq 2
.

The present work is the following step.
It deals with the full problem
(
1
), with its stochastic perturbation
\Theta
.
Instead of treating
\Theta
as a perturbation, we study
a family
(\varphi_{\theta})_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}=(\Phi_{0}+\theta)_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}

of functions leading the process and
we consider that
\theta
is a realization of the random variable
\Theta
,
with (for example) normal distribution. Each
\varphi_{\theta}
gives rise
to a transformation
T_{\theta}
- to which we apply the results of
[
11
]
- and to its transfer operator
P_{\theta}
.

Following the lines of
[
1
]
, we
then study an
averaged transfer operator

P
which is,
in some sense, the expected value of
all the individual transfer operators
P_{\theta}
(under the
law of the perturbation
\Theta
). If the
individual transformations
T_{\theta}
satisfy the conditions in
[
11
]

uniformly in
\theta
, the operator
P
then satisfies the conditions of
[
5
]
. As a consequence, it
has a spectral decomposition, a spectral gap, all properties which ensure
the existence of an invariant measure in a certain sense.
This corresponds to the annealed point of view
and we are in the conditions of Example 2.7 of
[
1
]
.
The averaged operator is defined in the same way as the Foias
operator in
[
8
]
, section 12. But, since the transformation lacks
a continuity property (for a fixed value of the perturbation, our
transformation is piecewise
C^{2}
and not continuous), the conclusions
valid for the Foias operator do not hold in our case.

The article is organized as follows.

Section
2
gives the precise setting and hypotheses.
The functions
\varphi_{\theta}
are all additive perturbations of the same function

\Phi_{0}
defined on a compact hypercube

[-L,L]^{k}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Since each
\varphi_{\theta}
is supposed to take values in
[-L,L]
,
it is piecewise defined on
[-L,L]^{k}
and the precise definition domains
depend on
\theta
.
We then define the
compact set
{\Omega}
and the
transformations
T_{\theta}
derived from the
\varphi_{\theta}
and to which we apply
the results of
[
11
]
. The
T_{\theta}
and the set are defined using a
small parameter
\gamma
, which does not depend on
\theta
. To ensure its
existence, we need
\Phi_{0}
and its derivatives to satisfy
the precise, quantitative conditions (
17
).
We then give the main results. Theorem
1
states that the conditions of
[
11
]
are uniformly satisfied, Theorem
2
gives the decomposition of the averaged transfer operator
P
and Theorem
3
gives the invariant measure for the
k
dimensional process and for the original
{\mathbb{R}}
valued process.
The proofs are detailed in the following sections.

In Section
3
,
we use the same technics as in
[
6
,
7
]
to construct
the individual transformations
T_{\theta}
of a compact set
\Omega
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}

and thus to
imbed the recurrence relation into

{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
. Since the transformations depend on
\theta
,
we have to check carefully that
the conditions stated in
[
11
]
are satisfied uniformly with respect to

\theta
. This is particularly delicate when it comes to the precise
definition domains of
\varphi_{\theta}
. The results of this section are
summarized in Theorem
1
.

In Section
4
,
according to
[
1
]

we define and study an averaged transfer operator
P
.
The individual transfer operators
P_{\theta}

have uniformity properties, for example Section
3
proves that
they satisfy a Lasota-Yorke inequality
with constants independent of
\theta
. The same inequality holds for
P
.
Applying the result of
[
5
]
to the averaged
P
gives a spectral decomposition
of
P
as the
sum of projections on finite dimensional spaces and of a “small” residual operator
S
(small in that it has a small
spectral radius). The spectrum of
P
then consists of a finite number of
modulus
1
eigenvalues, all of them simple, and of the spectrum of

S
, which is contained in a ball of radius
<1
, centered on the origin. This particularity is called the spectral gap.

Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}
the tensor product
of the probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{\otimes{\mathbb{N}}}
.
One of the projections is associated with the eigenvalue
1
.
Applying it to the (normalized) constant function on
\Omega
gives an
invariant measure
\mu
, in the sense that the measure

\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu
is invariant for the mapping

\begin{matrix}F:&{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\Omega&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\Omega\\
&(\vartheta,x)&\longmapsto&\left(\mathcal{S}\vartheta,T_{\theta_{0}}(x)\right)\end{matrix}

(2)

where
\mathcal{S}
denotes the left shift. It remains to compute the marginal
distributions to get an invariant measure for the original
1
-dimensional
process.

2
Hypotheses and main results

2.1
Notations, concepts, hypotheses

We now present the problem more precisely.
Let
L>0
, let
the stochastic process
(X_{n})
, bounded and with values
in
[-L,L]
, be defined as follows.
Let
\beta
be a positive real number and
{\Phi_{0}}
be a real-valued,

C^{2}
application,
defined on a neighbourhood
(-L-\beta,L+\beta)^{k}

of
[-L,L]^{k}
.

We introduce a family
\{\varphi_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}
of applications
indexed by
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Each
\varphi_{\theta}
is defined piecewise on
[-L,L]^{k}
as an additive perturbation
of
\Phi_{0}
, with values in

[-L,L]
.
More precisely, let

(O^{\theta}_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}
be a family of open subsets of
(-L,L)^{k}

which split
[-L,L]^{k}
according to the values taken by
\Phi_{0}
and
denote by
S^{\theta}_{j}
the parts of their boundaries which are inside

[-L,L]^{k}
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle O^{\theta}_{j}=\{x\in(-L,L)^{k},\ (2j-1)L<\theta+{\Phi_{0}}(x)<(2j+1)L\}\\
\\
\displaystyle S^{\theta}_{j}=\{x\in[-L,L]^{k},\ (2j-1)L=\theta+{\Phi_{0}}(x)\}.\end{array}

(3)

For every
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and every
x\in[-L,L]^{k}
, set

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\varphi_{j,\theta}(x)&=&{\Phi_{0}}(x)+\theta\quad[-L,L]\\
&=&{\Phi_{0}}(x)+\theta-2jL,&j=\lfloor\frac{{\Phi_{0}}(x)+\theta+L}{2L}\rfloor,\end{array}

(4)

where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x
.
We define
\varphi_{\theta}
on
[-L,L]^{k}
(up to a null set) by

\varphi_{\theta}|_{O^{\theta}_{j}}=\varphi_{j,\theta},

(5)

provided
O_{j}^{\theta}
is not empty.

If, at time
n\geq k
, the perturbation
\Theta_{n}

takes the real value
\theta
,
the next state
X_{n+1}
is then given by

X_{n+1}=\varphi_{\theta}(X_{n-k+1},\dots,X_{n-k}).

(6)

We imbed the recurrence relation (
6
)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, setting

Y_{n}=(X_{n-k+1},\dots,\gamma^{j-1}X_{n-k+j},\dots\gamma^{k-1}X_{n}),

(7)

where
\gamma
is a small positive parameter, to be chosen.

The conditions (
17
) listed below ensure that there exists a

\gamma
, independent on
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and small enough to allow
us to apply Theorem 5.1 in
[
11
]
. The natural way, with

\gamma=1
, doesn’t allow the transformation
T_{\theta}
to
be expanding (condition (PE4) of Theorem
1
). Anyway,
condition (PE5) on a geometric constant
\eta
(see (
14
)) imposes
even stricter conditions.

Let us first define the applications and their domains. We give the convenient
value of
\gamma
in (
17
).

One sets

\Omega=\prod_{j=1}^{k}[-\gamma^{j-1}L,\gamma^{j-1}L].

(8)

One denotes by
\Gamma
the dilation linking
\Omega
and
[-L,L]^{k}
:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\Gamma:&{\mathbb{R}}^{k}&\rightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\\
&x=(x_{j})&\mapsto&(x_{j}\gamma^{-j+1}),\\
\end{array}

(9)

hence
\Omega=\Gamma^{-1}([-L,L]^{k})
.

One then sets

\begin{array}[]{llll}\displaystyle\forall r\in[0,\beta[,\quad\Omega_{r}=\Gamma^{-1}((-L-r,L+r)^{k})=\prod_{s=1}^{k}(-\gamma^{s-1}(L+r),\gamma^{s-1}(L+r)L),\\
\displaystyle U^{\theta}_{j}=\Gamma^{-1}(O^{\theta}_{j})=\left\{x\in\Omega_{0},\ (2j-1)L<\theta+\Phi_{0}(\Gamma(x))<(2j+1)L\right\}\\
\\
\displaystyle\Sigma^{\theta}_{j}=\Gamma^{-1}(S^{\theta}_{j})=\left\{x\in\Omega,\ (2j-1)L=\theta+\Phi_{0}(\Gamma(x))\right\}\end{array}

(10)

The open sets
\Omega_{r}
are neighbourhoods of
\Omega
and appear in
Section
3
.
The open set
U_{j}^{\theta}
is empty if and only if

O_{j}^{\theta}
is empty.
The properties of
\Omega
, of the
U^{\theta}_{j}
and of the slanted parts of
the boundaries, the
\Sigma^{\theta}_{j}

(regularity, number) do not depend on the value of
\gamma>0.

On the set
\Omega_{\beta}\setminus\{x\in\Omega_{\beta},\theta+\Phi_{0}(\Gamma(x))\in(2{\mathbb{N}}+1)L\}
,
the transformation
T_{\theta}
is defined by

T_{\theta}(u)=\left(\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma},~\dots,\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma},~\gamma^{k-1}\varphi_{\theta}(u_{1},\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma},~\dots,~\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma^{k-1}})\right),

(11)

with
\varphi_{\theta}
given by (
5
).

We introduce an auxiliary function
S
on
\Omega_{\beta}
:

S(u)=\left(\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma},~\dots,\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma},~\gamma^{k-1}\Phi_{0}(u_{1},\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma},~\dots,~\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma^{k-1}})\right)=\left(\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma},~\dots,\frac{u_{k}}{\gamma},~\gamma^{k-1}\Phi_{0}(\Gamma(u))\right).

(12)

We then define
T_{j}^{\theta}
on
\Omega_{\beta}
by

T_{j}^{\theta}(u)=S(u)+(0,\dots,0,\gamma^{k-1}(\theta-2jL)).

(13)

One sees that

\left.T_{\theta}\right|_{U_{j}^{\theta}}=\left.T_{j}^{\theta}\right|_{U_{j}^{\theta}}
.

From now on, we are interested in the
k
-dimensional valued process
defined by its first term
Y_{0}
and the relation

Y_{n+1}=T_{\theta}(Y_{n}),

where the relationship between the
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
valued processus
Y=(Y_{n})
and
the real valued
X=(X_{n})

is given by (
7
):

Y_{n}=(X_{n-k+1},\dots,\gamma^{j-1}X_{n-k+j},\dots\gamma^{k-1}X_{n}).

Lemma
9
below proves that

T_{j}^{\theta}
is a global diffeomorphism on

\Omega_{\beta}
onto its image
T_{j}^{\theta}(\Omega_{\beta})
. Hence,

T_{j}^{\theta}
is a diffeomorphism on

U^{\theta}_{j}
onto
T_{j}^{\theta}(U^{\theta}_{j})
. This holds for
any bounded open subset of

\Omega_{\beta}
.
Later on, we will need to restrict
T_{j}^{\theta}
to more specific open sets
(defined by (
32
)),
in order to apply directly
the results of
[
11
]
.

A central parameter in
[
11
]
is the
number of crossings
, that is,
the number of regular parts of the
boundaries of the open sets of
\Omega

which intersect at one point.
In the present case, the boundaries consist of parts of hyperplanes
(the boundaries of

\Omega
itself) and of the slanted parts
\Sigma_{j}^{\theta}
.
Whatever the value of the parameter
\gamma
, the
\Sigma_{j}^{\theta}
are
regular under the conditions (
17
) (see
Lemma
13
).
There are at most
k
hyperplanes
crossing at one point, and one slanted part (see Figure
1
in the
case when
k=2
). Since the

\Sigma_{j}^{\theta}
are common to
two open sets, they count double (see Lemma 2.1
[
11
]
), which
proves that the maximal number of crossings is

k+2
for a countable number of values
\theta
and
k+1

for the rest of the values.

Open sets
U_{j}^{\theta}
with boundaries and crossings.

Let
Y
be the maximal number of crossings. For a real number

\sigma>1
, we define

\eta_{0,Y}(\sigma)=\eta_{0}(\sigma,Y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}+\frac{4}{\sqrt{\sigma}-1}Y\frac{\gamma_{k-1}}{\gamma_{k}},

(14)

where
\gamma_{n}
is the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Let
S_{k}(Y)
be the real number for which

\eta_{0}(\sigma,Y)=1
:

S_{k}(Y)=\eta_{0,Y}^{-1}(1).

(15)

We choose the dilation coefficient
\sigma
:

\sigma>S_{k}(Y)>1

(16)

and two real constants
C_{1},C_{2}>1
. We now prescribe the conditions:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llllll}&\displaystyle\gamma^{-2}&=&C_{1}\sigma,\\
\\
\displaystyle\forall x\in(-L-\beta,L+\beta)^{k},&\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial{\Phi_{0}}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)\right)^{2}&\geq&C_{1}^{k-1}C_{2}\sigma^{k},\\
\\
\displaystyle\forall x\in(-L-\beta,L+\beta)^{k},\forall j\geq 2,&\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial{\Phi_{0}}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)\right)^{2}&\leq&\displaystyle\frac{(C_{1}-1)(C_{2}-1)}{k-1}\sigma.\end{array}\right.

(17)

This gives the value of the parameter
\gamma
, which, in turn,
defines the domain

\Omega
and the transformations.

The above conditions prove that each transformation (
11
)
is non singular in
L^{1}(\Omega,m)
, which implies the existence of
a Perron-Frobenius operator
P_{\theta}
. To study the transfer operators

P_{\theta}
or the averaged operator defined in (

), we use the
central result of
[
5
]
. Thus, we need to introduce
the following space
V_{1}
. We just recall here its definition.
Its properties are studied in
[
11
]
.

For every non-null Borel set
S
of

{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, for every
f\in L^{1}_{m}({\mathbb{R}}^{k},{\mathbb{R}})
, one sets :

\operatorname{osc}(f,S)=\supess_{S}f-\infess_{S}f

where
\supess_{S}
and
\infess_{S}
are the essential supremum
and infimum on
S
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
m
.

One then defines the semi norm
|\cdot|_{1}
by

|f|_{1}=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{1}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}{\rm Osc}(f,B(x,\varepsilon))\ dx

(18)

and the set
V_{1}
by

V_{1}(\Omega)=\{f\in L^{1}_{m}({\mathbb{R}}^{k},{\mathbb{R}}),~\operatorname{supp}f\subset\Omega\text{ and }\left|f\right|_{1}<+\infty\}.

(19)

Then, for
f\in V_{1}
, the norm
\|\ \|_{V_{1}}
is defined by

\|f\|_{V_{1}}=\|f\|_{L^{1}_{m}}+|f|_{1}.

2.2
Main results

Let
m
denote the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and its subsets. For

X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and
\varepsilon>0
, we denote by

the following
open set

B_{\varepsilon}(X)\coloneqq\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}:d(x,X)<\varepsilon\right\}

Theorem 1
.

Under the conditions of the preceding paragraph, there exist positive
real numbers
r_{0},\varepsilon_{m}
and
K
and a sufficiently small

\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, for every

\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the following conditions hold:

(PRE)

There exist a finite family
(U_{j}^{\theta})_{j}
of pairwise disjoint open subsets
of
{\Omega}
, a finite family
(V_{j}^{\theta})_{j}=B_{\varepsilon_{m}}(U_{j}^{\theta})

such that

\overline{U_{j}^{\theta}}\subset V_{j}^{\theta}
and
C^{2}
applications

T_{j}^{\theta}:V_{j}^{\theta}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
, satisfying all the following
conditions:

(PE1)

\forall j
,

\left.T^{\theta}\right|_{U_{j}^{\theta}}=\left.T_{j}^{\theta}\right|_{U_{j}^{\theta}}
and

B_{r_{0}}(T^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta}))\subset T_{j}^{\theta}(V_{j}^{\theta})
;

(PE2)

\forall j,T_{j}^{\theta}
is a

diffeomorphism on

onto its image.
Moreover,
the determinant of the Jacobian matrix
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous, in the following sense:

\forall j,\forall\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0}),

\forall z\in T_{j}^{\theta}(V_{j}^{\theta}),\forall x,y\in B(z,\varepsilon)\cap T_{j}^{\theta}(V_{j}^{\theta}),

\left|\det\left(D(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(x)\right)-\det\left(D(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)\right)\right|\leq K\left|\det\left(D(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(z)\right)\right|\varepsilon\ ;

(PE3)

m(\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{j}U_{j}^{\theta})=0
;

(PE4)

for all

in

such that

,

with

defined by (

).
The map
T_{j}^{\theta}
is thus expanding;

(PE5)

Set
G(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})=\sup_{x}G(x,\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})
with

G(x,\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})=\sum_{j}\frac{m\left((T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\left[B_{\varepsilon}(\partial T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta}))\right]\bigcap B\left(x,\left[1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\right]\varepsilon_{0}\right)\right)}{m\left(B\left(x,\left[1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\right]\varepsilon_{0}\right)\right)}.

Then
\eta
defined by

\eta(\varepsilon_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}+2\sup_{\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}}\frac{G(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})}{\varepsilon}\varepsilon_{0}

(20)

satisfies
\sup_{\delta\leq\varepsilon_{0}}\eta(\delta)<1
.

To sum up, the conditions of Theorem

in

are satisfied,
with constants

independent of

.
In particular, condition (PE2) proves that the transformations

are non singular. For each real number
\theta
, we may define the

by

\forall A\in{\cal B}(\Omega),\ \forall f\in L^{1}_{m}(\Omega),\quad\int_{T_{\theta}^{-1}(A)}f\,{\ \rm d}m=\int_{A}P_{\theta}f\,{\ \rm d}m.

One may check that

P_{\theta}f(y)=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}},U_{j}^{\theta}\neq\emptyset}{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta})}(y)f((T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y))\left|{\rm det}(D(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y))\right|.

, if

and

and all

Since these constants are expressed explicitely
in terms of

,

,

, they do not depend on
\theta
.

We formally define the averaged Perron-Frobenius operator associated with
this family of transformations by

P=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}P_{\theta}\,{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\theta)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}P_{\theta}\ H(\theta)\,{\ \rm d}\theta,

where

.

Now suppose that

B_{\varepsilon\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}}(\Sigma_{j}^{\theta})\cap B_{(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}})\varepsilon_{0}}(x)
.
We want to get an upper bound for the distance between the point

\|x-z\|\leq\|x-y\|+\|y-z\|\leq{\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\right)\varepsilon_{0}}+\varepsilon\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}},

with
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}

The set

D\Phi_{a}(x)^{-1}\Phi_{a}(B_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{j}^{\theta})\cap B_{(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}})\varepsilon_{0}}(x))\ \subset\ B\left(D\Phi_{a}(x)^{-1}H,\varepsilon\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}(1+M\varepsilon_{0}(1+\sigma^{-1/2}))\right):=F_{1}.

D\Phi_{a}(x)^{-1}\Phi_{a}(B_{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon}(\Sigma_{j}^{\theta})\cap B_{(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}})\varepsilon_{0}}(x))\subset F\cap F_{1}

We have seen, too, that this set

Then

The volume in maximal when the center is on

\begin{array}[]{llll}\displaystyle 2\underbrace{\varepsilon\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}(1+M\varepsilon_{0}(1+\sigma^{-1/2}))}_{{\rm height}}\times\gamma_{k-1}\underbrace{\left({(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}})\varepsilon_{0}}(1+M{(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}})\varepsilon_{0}})\right)^{k-1}}_{{\rm radius}=R}\\

According to

(55)

4
Proof of Theorems
2
and
3
: Properties of the average transfer operator

Seeing as
T_{\theta}:\Omega\to\Omega
is nonsingular for every
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we can define its (Perron-Frobenius) transfer operator

P_{\theta}
.

Our goal is to define an averaged form of the transfer operator:

\hskip 10.00002ptPf=\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\theta}f{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}(\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H(\theta)P_{\theta}f{\ \rm d}\theta,

(59)

for
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,m)
, where
H
is a density function. This definition raises the issue of measurability in the integrand, which we shall address before establishing spectral properties for
P
.

4.1
Measurability and
L^{p}
-boundedness

Proposition 16
.

Let
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,m)
.

1.

The mapping

\begin{matrix}Qf:&\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}\\
&(y,\theta)&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta})}(y)\left(f\circ(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\right)\!(y)\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{0}}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\Gamma(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)\right)\right|^{-1}\end{matrix}

(60)

is measurable.

2.

For all
(y,\theta)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
Qf(y,\theta)=P_{\theta}f(y)
.

3.

The mapping

\begin{matrix}\bar{Q}f:&\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}\\
&(y,\theta)&\longmapsto&H(\theta)P_{\theta}f(y)\end{matrix}

is measurable and integrable over
\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

1.

For fixed
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, since the set
\{(y,\theta)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}:y\in T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta})\}
is open, the map
(y,\theta)\mapsto{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta})}(y)
is measurable.
So are the maps
(y,\theta)\mapsto\left(f\circ(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\right)(y)
and
(y,\theta)\mapsto\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{0}}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\Gamma(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)\right)\right|^{-1}
.
Qf
is therefore a series of measurable functions. In addition, for fixed
\theta
, only finitely many of the
U_{j}^{\theta}
are nonempty, thus for every
(y,{\theta})\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
, all but a finite number of the series’ terms vanish. This makes the series pointwise convergent and thus
Q
measurable.

2.

For fixed
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
A\subset\Omega
open,

\int_{A}P_{\theta}f(y){\ \rm d}y=\int_{(T^{\theta})^{-1}(A)}f(x){\ \rm d}x=\int_{(T^{\theta})^{-1}(A)}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbf{1}}_{U_{j}^{\theta}}(x)f(x){\ \rm d}x

whence we get by substituting
x=(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)
in each term:

\displaystyle\int_{A}P_{\theta}f(y){\ \rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\left({\mathbf{1}}_{U_{j}^{\theta}}\circ(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\right)\!(y)\left(f\circ(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\right)\!(y)\left|\det DT_{j}^{\theta}\left((T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)\right)\right|{\ \rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbf{1}}_{T_{j}^{\theta}(U_{j}^{\theta})}(y)\left(f\circ(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}\right)\!(y)\left|\frac{\partial\Phi_{0}}{\partial x_{1}}\left(\Gamma(T_{j}^{\theta})^{-1}(y)\right)\right|^{-1}{\ \rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{A}Qf(y,\theta){\ \rm d}y

With this equality holding for open
A\subset\Omega
, a measure extension theorem allows us to induce that
P_{\theta}f(y)=Qf(y,\theta)
for every
(y,\theta)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
.

3.

\bar{Q}
is measurable as it is the product of two measurable functions, and for all
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\left|P_{\theta}f\right|\leq P_{\theta}\left|f\right|
for which Fubini-Tonelli yields:

\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}}H(\theta)P_{\theta}\left|f\right|(y){\ \rm d}\theta{\ \rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H(\theta)\left(\int_{\Omega}P_{\theta}\left|f\right|(y){\ \rm d}y\right){\ \rm d}\theta=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}},

hence the result.
∎

This ensures that the operator described in (
59
) is well-defined. Notice that the expression given for
P_{\theta}
in (
60
) allows us to establish an
L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}
bound:

\left|P_{\theta}f(x)\right|\leq N\left\|f\right\|_{L^{\infty}}C_{1}^{-\frac{k-1}{2}}C_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{k}{2}}

(61)

where
N
denotes the maximal number of nonempty open subsets
U_{j}^{\theta}
for fixed
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since this number has a bound that is uniform in
{\theta}
(see (
25
)), we are able to derive a bound for the average operator on
L^{\infty}
after establishing
L^{1}
-boundedness, which will be achieved by showing that
P
is a Markov operator.

We recall that, for some measure space
(X,{\cal T},\mu)
, a Markov operator over
X
in the sense of Lasota & Mackey (see
[
8
]
) is a linear operator
T:L^{1}(X,\mu)\to L^{1}(X,\mu)
such that for all
nonnegative

f\in L^{1}
,
Tf\geq 0
and
\left\|Tf\right\|_{L^{1}}=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}
.

Theorem 17
.

P
is a Markov operator.

Proof

Let
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,m)
such that
f\geq 0
. Thanks to
properties (3.2.4), (3.2.5) from
[
8
]
, the
P_{\theta}
are Markov operators, hence
Pf=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})P_{\theta}f{\ \rm d}{\theta}\geq 0
, and

\displaystyle\left\|Pf\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Pf(y){\ \rm d}y=\iint_{\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}}H({\theta})P_{\theta}f(y){\ \rm d}{\theta}{\ \rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left(\int_{\Omega}P_{\theta}f(y){\ \rm d}y\right){\ \rm d}{\theta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left\|P_{\theta}f\right\|_{L^{1}}{\ \rm d}{\theta}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}{\ \rm d}{\theta}=\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}},

establishing that
P
also is a Markov operator.
∎

Proposition 3.1.1 of
[
8
]
then allows us to establish that:

Corollary 18
.

For all
f\in L^{1}(\Omega,m)
,
\left\|Pf\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}
.

Theorem 19
.

For all
p\in[1,\infty]
,
P:L^{p}\to L^{p}
is a bounded linear operator.

Proof

By interpolation of (
61
) and Corollary
18
,
P
maps
L^{p}
onto itself and by setting
s=1-\frac{1}{p}
, one has:

\left\|P\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{p})}\leq\left\|P\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{1})}^{1-s}\left\|P\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{\infty})}^{s}\leq N^{s}C_{1}^{-\frac{(k-1)s}{2}}C_{2}^{-\frac{s}{2}}\sigma^{-\frac{ks}{2}},

and thus

.
∎

4.2
Lasota-Yorke inequality and spectral properties

Our aim now is to apply the Ionescu-Tulcea & Marinescu spectral decomposition theorem on a suitable
P
-stable subspace. The space of functions of weak oscillation, as defined in
[
1
,
11
]
, is one such subspace:

V_{1}(\Omega)=\left\{f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{k}):\operatorname{supp}f\subset\Omega,\;\left|f\right|_{1}\coloneqq\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int\operatorname{osc}\left(f,B_{\varepsilon}(x)\right){\ \rm d}x<+\infty\right\},

(62)

where for any non-null measurable set
A
and all
f\in L^{1}
, the oscillation of
f
over
A
is defined in
[
1
]
by

\operatorname{osc}(f,A)=\supess_{x,y\in A}\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|.

The first ingredient required for the decomposition theorem to apply is a Lasota-Yorke inequality on
P
. One such estimate was established in
[
11
]
which can be applied to our
P_{\theta}
:

Lemma 20
.

Under the conditions (
14
) to (
17
) of Section
2.1
,
there exist
\eta\in[0,1[
and
D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
such that, for all

\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\forall f\in V_{1}
,

|P_{\theta}f|_{1}\leq\eta|f|_{1}+D\|f\|_{L^{1}_{m}}.

(63)

Proof.

This is a consequence of the proof of Lemma 4.1 of
[
11
]
. For

\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
of Theorem
1
,
Lemma 4.1
gives a Lasota-Yorke inequality for the Perron-Frobenius operator
P_{\theta}
,
of the shape (
63
), with explicit
\eta
and
D
given by

\eta=\left(1+\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon_{0}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma}}+2\sup\frac{G(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0})\varepsilon_{0}}{\varepsilon}\right)

and

D=\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon+2\left(1+\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon\right)G(\varepsilon,\varepsilon_{0}).

With the notation (
58
), we get

\eta\leq\left(1+\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon_{0}\right)\overline{\eta(\varepsilon_{0})},\quad D\leq\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon_{0}+\left(1+\frac{K}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\varepsilon_{0}\right)\overline{\eta(\varepsilon_{0})}.

For
\varepsilon_{0}
sufficiently small,
\eta
is smaller than
1
.
Neither of them depends on
\theta
since the constants
K,\sigma
and the
function
\overline{\eta(\varepsilon_{0})}
do not depend on
\theta
.
∎

It follows from Lemma
20
that
V_{1}
is invariant under the operators
P_{\theta}
for all
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. In the following, we shall establish a similar estimate for
P
.

Lemma 21
.

For all
f\in V_{1}(\Omega)
and
\varepsilon>0
, the map

\left.\begin{matrix}{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}&\longrightarrow&{\mathbb{R}}\\
(x,\theta)&\longmapsto&\operatorname{osc}(P_{\theta}f,B_{\varepsilon}(x))\end{matrix}\right.

is measurable.

Proof

Let
a>0
and
O_{\theta}:(y,z)\in\Omega^{2}\mapsto\left|P_{\theta}f(y)-P_{\theta}f(z)\right|
. We shall denote by
\chi_{a}
the indicator function of the set

\left\{(x,y,z,{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times\Omega\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}:y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)\text{ and }O_{\theta}(y,z)>a\right\},

whose measurability is easily deduced from that of
({\theta},y)\mapsto P_{\theta}f(y)
.
Define

M_{a}:(x,{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto\iint_{\Omega^{2}}\chi_{a}(x,y,z,{\theta}){\ \rm d}y{\ \rm d}z

By the Fubini-Tonelli theorem,
M_{a}
is measurable, thus so is the set

\displaystyle\left\{(x,{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}:\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}O_{\theta}(y,z)>a\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{(x,{\theta}):m\left(\left\{(y,z)\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}:O_{\theta}(y,z)>a\right\}\right)>0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{(x,{\theta}):M_{a}(x,{\theta})>0\right\}=M_{a}^{-1}\left((0,+\infty)\right)

Hence the map
\displaystyle(x,{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\times{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}O_{\theta}(y,z)
is measurable, which concludes the proof.
∎

The proof for the main result in this section utilizes the following well-known estimate:

\sup_{x\in X}\int_{Y}g(x,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)\leq\int_{Y}\sup_{x\in X}g(x,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)

for all nonnegative, bounded
g\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})
. A measure-theoretic version shall also be utilized, for which we give a short proof.

Lemma 22
.

Let
(X,{\cal T}_{X},\mu_{X})
,
(Y,{\cal T}_{Y},\mu_{Y})
be
\sigma
-finite measure spaces, and consider a nonnegative essentially bounded
g\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mu_{X}\otimes\mu_{Y})
. The mapping
y\in Y\mapsto\supess_{x\in A}g(x,y)
is measurable, and

\supess_{x\in A}\int_{Y}g(x,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)\leq\int_{Y}\supess_{x\in A}g(x,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)

(64)

for all
A\in{\cal T}_{X}
with positive measure.

Proof

Measurability is established as in
Lemma
21
. Consider
f:(x,y)\mapsto\supess_{A}g(\cdot,y)-g(x,y)
and denote by
\chi
the indicator of
f^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{*}_{-})
. By Fubini-Tonelli,

\iint_{X\times Y}\chi=\int_{Y}\mu_{X}\left(\left\{x\in X:g(x,y)>\supess_{A}g(\cdot,y)\right\}\right){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)=0,

which entails that
f^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{*}_{-})
is null and thus that
f\geq 0
almost everywhere. Therefore, for
\mu_{X}
-almost every
x\in X
,

\int_{Y}g(x,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y)\leq\int_{Y}\supess_{A}g(\cdot,y){\ \rm d}\mu_{Y}(y),

hence (
64
).
∎

We may now move on to the proof of this section’s main result:

Proposition 23
.

There exist
\eta\in(0,1)
and

for all

Pf\in V_{1}
and

\left\|Pf\right\|_{V_{1}}\leq\eta\left\|f\right\|_{V_{1}}+C\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}

By Lemma
20
, for all

and
{\theta}\in{\mathbb{R}}

\left|P_{\theta}f\right|_{1}\leq\eta\left|f\right|_{1}+D\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}

do not depend on

or
{\theta}
. Furthermore, (

\displaystyle\left|Pf\right|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left|Pf(y)-Pf(z)\right|{\ \rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left(P_{\theta}f(y)-P_{\theta}f(z)\right){\ \rm d}{\theta}\right|{\ \rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left|P_{\theta}f(y)-P_{\theta}f(z)\right|{\ \rm d}{\theta}{\ \rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\supess_{y,z\in B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\left|P_{\theta}f(y)-P_{\theta}f(z)\right|{\ \rm d}{\theta}{\ \rm d}x

\displaystyle=\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}H({\theta})\operatorname{osc}\left(P_{\theta}f,B_{\varepsilon}(x)\right){\ \rm d}x{\ \rm d}{\theta}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left(\sup_{0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}}\varepsilon^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{k}}\operatorname{osc}\left(P_{\theta}f,B_{\varepsilon}(x)\right){\ \rm d}x\right){\ \rm d}{\theta}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left|P_{\theta}f\right|_{1}{\ \rm d}{\theta},

and it follows from (

\left|Pf\right|_{1}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}H({\theta})\left(\eta\left|f\right|_{1}+D\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}\right){\ \rm d}{\theta}=\eta\left|f\right|_{1}+D\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}.

(65)

Recall in addition that
\left\|Pf\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}
for all

; adding this to (

\displaystyle\left\|Pf\right\|_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\left|f\right|_{1}+D\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\eta\left|f\right|_{1}+D\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}+(\eta+1-\eta)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle=\eta\left\|f\right\|_{V_{1}}+(D+1-\eta)\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}},

and the result is established for
C\coloneqq D+1-\eta
.
∎

We now have every ingredient we need for Ionescu-Tulcea & Marinescu’s
theorem to apply, which proves points
2
,

3
, and the beginning of point
1
of Theorem
2
. The operator
P
decomposes as

P=S+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}\Pi_{j},

with the commutation relations of Theorem

. The fact that
\lambda_{1}=1
is proved in the following Lemma.

Lemma 24
.

The averaged transfer operator
P
has the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1
, and
h_{*}\coloneqq\Pi_{1}\left(\frac{1}{m(\Omega)}\right)
is a nonnegative fixed point.

Proof

Applying Lemma (4.1) of
[
5
]
with
c=1
, for all
f\in V_{1}

gives a function
\tilde{f}\in V_{1}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\tilde{f}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P^{k}f\right\|_{L^{1}}=0.

Taking
\lambda_{1}=1
, the projector
\Pi_{1}
from Theorem
2
on
V_{1}
is then defined
by setting
\tilde{f}=\Pi_{1}f
.
It is a positive operator, in the sense that, if
g\in V_{1}
and
g\geq 0
, then

\Pi_{1}g\geq 0
too (consider an a.e. converging subsequence of

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P^{k}g
,
which is a sequence of
\geq 0
functions).

One may check that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}P^{k}f\,{\ \rm d}m=\int_{\Omega}f\,{\ \rm d}m,

since
\int_{\Omega}Pf\,{\ \rm d}m=\int_{\Omega}f\,{\ \rm d}m
due to
P
being Markov.
Hence, if
f>0
,
\Pi_{1}f
is not the zero function because

\int_{\Omega}\Pi_{1}f\,{\ \rm d}m=\int_{\Omega}f\,{\ \rm d}m\neq 0
.
Moreover, the function
\tilde{f}=\Pi_{1}f
satisfies

P\tilde{f}=\tilde{f}
.

Thus
h_{*}=\Pi_{1}(\frac{1}{m(\Omega)})
is a fixed point of
P
and
it is nonnegative by positiveness of
\Pi_{1}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3

Here
\theta=(\theta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}=(\theta_{0},\check{\theta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
is a
real sequence. In (
2
), we defined a mapping
F
on

{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{N}}}\times\Omega
,
setting
F((\theta_{0},\check{\theta}),x)=(\check{\theta},T^{\theta_{0}}(x))
.
We seek to establish that
\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu
is invariant for
F
. It suffices to show that

(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)(A\times B)=(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)\left(F^{-1}(A\times B)\right)

for all
A=A_{0}\times...\times A_{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times...
with
A_{0},...,A_{n}\in\mathcal{B}({\mathbb{R}})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
, as sets of this form generate the Borel
\sigma
-algebra over
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\Omega
.

We have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)\left(F^{-1}(A\times B)\right)

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\Omega}{\mathbf{1}}_{A\times B}\left(F(\theta,x)\right){\ \rm d}(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)(\theta,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbf{1}}_{A}(S\theta)\left[\int_{\Omega}{\mathbf{1}}_{B}\left(T^{\theta_{0}}(x)\right){\ \rm d}\mu(x)\right]{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\iint_{{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{N}}}{\mathbf{1}}_{{\mathbb{R}}\times A}(\theta_{0},\check{\theta})\left[\int_{(T^{\theta_{0}})^{-1}(B)}{\ \rm d}\mu(x)\right]{\ \rm d}(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{\Theta})(\theta_{0},\check{\theta}),

thanks to the independence of the variables
(\Theta_{t})
, which allows us to split the integral as follows:

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)\left(F^{-1}(A\times B)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{(T^{\theta_{0}})^{-1}(B)}{\ \rm d}\mu(x){\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}(\theta_{0})\right]{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\check{\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{(T^{\theta_{0}})^{-1}(B)}h_{*}{\ \rm d}m(x){\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}(\theta_{0})\right]{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\check{\theta})

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{B}P_{\theta_{0}}h_{*}{\ \rm d}m(x){\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}(\theta_{0})\right]{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\check{\theta})

by definition of
\mu
and
P_{\theta_{0}}
. It then follows by applying Fubini’s theorem that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}\otimes\mu)\left(F^{-1}(A\times B)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{A}\left[\int_{B}\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\theta_{0}}h_{*}{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta_{0}}(\theta_{0}){\ \rm d}m(x)\right]{\ \rm d}\mathbb{P}_{\Theta}(\check{\theta})
