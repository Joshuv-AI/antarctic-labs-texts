Improvement of DCIS resection using intraoperative imaging with ICG-p28
Authors: Sunam Mander, Masahide Goto, Konstantin Christov, Albert Green, Tapas K Das Gupta, Tohru Yamada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), the earliest form of breast cancer, are treated with breast-conserving surgery (BCS) when feasible. The primary objective of BCS is to completely resect a lesion with a tumor-free margin in a single surgery, as positive margins are a risk factor for local recurrence. However, re-excision rates due to positive margins are high. Since reported positive margin rates after BCS for DCIS and invasive breast cancer are 20–81% and 15–47%, respectively, new intraoperative intervention for BCS represents a medical unmet need to achieve adequate resection margins. We previously established a new near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging probe ICG-p28 by utilizing indocyanine green (ICG) labeled with cell-penetrating peptide carrying a tumor-targeting motif, and demonstrated that our imaging approach accurately identified the tumor margins in invasive breast cancer animal models. We hypothesized that our imaging approach can be applied to DCIS and yield similar rates when compared with invasive breast cancer. Here, we report that the real-time imaging of DCIS mouse models using ICG-p28 showed significant improvement in tumor recurrence rates by clear tumor margin identification compared to a control agent, ICG alone. With the chemical and biological characteristics of ICG-p28, our promising approach holds translational potential for image-guided DCIS surgery, reducing re-excision and tumor recurrence rates through tumor margin identification.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Surgical oncology

Introduction

The incidence of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is increasing due to advanced diagnostic screening and accounts for approximately 20–25% of breast cancer cases diagnosed each year1,2. Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) is one of the treatments for DCIS, with the primary goal of removing the malignant tissue with clear margins while preserving a rim of normal tissue around the tumor in DCIS3. Current medical imaging technologies such as MRI, PET, and CT are useful as presurgical imaging, but not widely available in operating rooms4,5. Historically, reported positive margin rates after BCS for DCIS and invasive breast cancer are 20–81% and 15–47%, respectively3, and re-excision rates of DCIS have ranged from 31 to 46%6,7. Moreover, these provide a negative impact by delaying adjuvant therapy, elevating stress levels, degrading cosmetic outcomes, and increasing additional costs (average re-excision cost: $16,072)8,9. It indicates that a reduction of the BCS re-excision rates will substantially improve the current breast cancer care. Thus, there is a clinical need for an alternative, safe, and cost-effective technology for DCIS to precisely identify the margins during BCS.

To fulfill this need, research efforts with various approaches have been made for intraoperative margin assessment10. Molecular imaging is one of the technologies being developed to allow surgeons to visualize malignant tissue intraoperatively. It utilizes fluorescent contrast agents for the detection of cancer cells and has been described to identify positive margins11,12. Specifically, fluorophores with excitation and emission spectra in near-infrared (NIR) wavelength range (700–900 nm) have attracted the most attention due to their minimal intrinsic autofluorescence from tissues13 along with their safety by utilization of low energy light source10. Among them, indocyanine green (ICG) is an FDA-approved dye with an excellent safety record. Although ICG has been investigated in nontargeted applications14, its use in cancer imaging has been limited due to the lack of cancer-targeting properties15–17.

To address this, one approach to developing targeted delivery is to utilize delivery vehicles that recognize cancer-specific targets. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) have shown a promising ability for targeted delivery18. We identified CPP, p28 is a 28 amino-acid fragment of azurin from an opportunistic pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibiting preferential penetration to various human cancer cells19,20. Clinically, p28 (NSC745104) as a single therapeutic agent was tested in two phase I clinical trials21–23. In the trials, p28 showed preliminary efficacy without apparent adverse effects, toxicity, or immunogenicity in patients with advanced solid tumors and pediatric patients with recurrent and refractory CNS tumors21–23. Since p28 is a new class of CPP and an ideal compound to use as a tumor-targeting carrier, unlike other CPPs24–26, we previously developed an ICG-based probe, ICG-p28, composed of clinically nontoxic p28 and FDA-approved ICG. Image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 precisely identifies tumor margins and significantly reduces tumor recurrence rates in invasive breast tumor models27,28. Based on the results, we hypothesized that our imaging approach can be applied to DCIS and can yield similar rates when compared with invasive breast cancer.

In this preclinical study, we evaluated ICG-p28 in real-time image-guided surgery of xenograft DCIS and transgenic mouse models. Here, we show that ICG-p28 improves the positive margin identification with a significant reduction in residual cancer cells and tumor recurrence. Together, the findings of ICG-p28 in intraoperative margin assessment in DCIS breast cancer surgery indicate that our imaging approach has great translational potential in guiding DCIS tumor excision.

Materials and methods

Peptide synthesis and ICG conjugation

Peptide, p28, was synthesized by CS Bio, Inc. at > 97% purity. ICG and p28 were conjugated as described previously28. The final conjugated products were confirmed by HPLC and mass spectroscopy.

Cell cultures

Human DCIS MCF-10DCIS.com cells were purchased from Wayne State University and cultured in DMEMF/12 media supplemented with 5% horse serum, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. SUM-25529 and CCH-1 cells30 were kindly gifted by Dr. Behbod at University of Kansas Medical Center. SUM-225 was cultured in DMEMF/12 media supplemented with 5% FBS, 5 µg/ml insulin, 1 µg/ml hydrocortisone, 10 mM HEPES, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. CCH-1 was cultured in DMEMF/12 media supplemented with 5% horse serum, 20 ng/ml EGF, 10 µg/ml insulin, 0.5 mg/ml hydrocortisone, 100 ng/ml cholera toxin, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. Human fibroblasts (ATCC) were cultured in MEME media supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified chamber containing 5% CO2.

Confocal microscopy

Human MCF-10 DCIS.com, SUM-225, CCH-1, and fibroblasts (2 × 104 cells/well) were cultured on glass slides placed in 24-well plates. After overnight culture, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 20 µM Alexa Fluor 568-labeled p28 peptide in medium (5% FBS and without phenol red) for 2 h at 37 °C. Cells were fixed with 2% formalin for 10 min. After washing, slides were prepared and mounted with Vectashield with DAPI (Vector Laboratories, Catalog #94010). Slides were examined by an LSM 710 confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss). Images were processed using ZEN software (ZEISS ZEN Lite).

Flow cytometry

MCF-10 DCIS.com cells were separated stem-like cells and non-stem cells based on immunostaining with mammary stem markers, CD49f (APC-anti-human CD49f antibody, R&D systems), and CD24 (PE-anti-human CD24 antibody, BD Bioscience). Stem-like cells (CD49f+/CD24-) and non-stem (CD49f-/CD24+) DCIS cells were also subjected to ALDH (aldehyde dehydrogenase) activity assay (ALDEFLUOR™ kit, StemCell Technologies) measuring ALDH1 as a marker of breast cancer stem cells. In order to confirm the specific ALDH activity in the target population, cells were exposed to an ALDH inhibitor, DEAB. Each population of cells was isolated by a MoFlo Astrios EQ cell sorter (RRC, UIC). Each population of DCIS cells (1.0 × 106/500 µL PBS) was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with ICG-p28 (0.1-1 µmol/L) or medium alone followed by washing 3 times in PBS. Cells were then fixed in 2.5% formalin for 5 min and washed twice in PBS. Following resuspension in 200 µL PBS, cells were passed through a screen to obtain a single-cell suspension and analyzed with MoFlo Cell Sorter to evaluate ICG-p28 entry. The fold increase of the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) over background levels represents mean fluorescence.

Mouse intraductal model (MIND)

All studies involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Illinois and were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. The DCIS xenograft model was established by injecting the MCF-10 DCIS.com cells into mouse mammary ducts of the athymic mice31. Briefly, 4–5 weeks-old mice were anesthetized, and a Y-incision was made on the abdomen such that inguinal mammary fat pads were exposed. The nipple of the inguinal gland was then snipped followed by the insertion of a needle through the nipple. 4 µl of 1x 105 MCF-10DCIS.com cells, resuspended in PBS with 0.2% trypan blue were injected with 29G Hamilton syringe. An appropriate method of injection of the cells into the ducts was confirmed by visualization of trypan blue color. The skin was then repositioned and sutured.

Transgenic mouse model

The female MMTV-PyMT transgenic mouse model (The Jackson Lab) was used to evaluate ICG-p28. It carries the polyoma virus middle T-antigen under the control of the mouse mammary tumor virus (MMTV) promoter, and its modes of tumor progression stage are mammary intraepithelial neoplasia at week 8, early carcinoma at week 10, and late carcinoma at week 1232,33.

Intraoperative imaging of a DCIS and transgenic model by ICG-p28

For the DCIS xenograft model, after 21 days of intraductal injection of MCF-10 DCIS.com, mice developed tumors and were randomly divided into three groups: PBS, ICG alone, and ICG-p28 (N = 6 in each group). For the transgenic animal model, female MMTV-PyMT transgenic mice spontaneously developed mammary tumors. At 9, 10, and 11 weeks of age, these mice were randomly divided into ICG and ICG-p28 groups (N = 15 in each group). As determined previously28, ICG or ICG-p28 at 0.5 mg/kg was injected i.v. (tail vein) to the mice. Twenty-four hours after injection, all surgical procedures were started under sterile conditions. Following anesthesia administration, the PDE imaging system (Photodynamic Eye; Mitaka USA, UT) was used to monitor NIR fluorescence throughout the entire surgical procedure. It is an FDA 510(k)-cleared NIR camera composed of a charge-coupled device (CCD) camera unit that detects an 800 nm NIR signal. For the PBS, tumor tissues identified via visual and tactile cues were removed, whereas the ICG and ICG-p28 groups were excised following the displayed NIR images on the monitor. Two animal surgeons were involved and blinded to experimental conditions such as animal identification and treatment group. For all groups, whole tumor removal with an approximately 2 mm safety margin was performed. The remaining mammary tissues at the 6 and 12 o’clock positions were also resected with approximately 2 mm in each sample and group. Following tumor excision, the skin incisions were closed, and the mice were returned to their cages and kept for an additional four weeks and monitored for tumor recurrence. Two transgenic mice of age at 11 weeks in the ICG group after excision were sacrificed due to a large tumor burden from mammary glands other than excised tumor from the inguinal mammary gland.

Detection of human cancer cells in mouse tissue by Alu sequencing

A quantitative analysis using human-specific Alu sequence (hAlu) was performed on samples collected from the margins. The Alu-repeat DNA sequences are specific to human cells34 which allows the identification of human-derived tumors implanted into mice by detecting the presence of hAlu. As described previously28, the hAlu was measured by real-time PCR (5 ng genomic DNA, 0.5 µM each primer, and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix; Life Technologies, USA). Each reaction was performed in a final volume of 10 µl at 50 °C for 2 min and 95 °C for 2 min followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 sec, 63 °C for 30 sec, and 72 °C for 30 sec. The mouse GAPDH (mGAPDH) (sense: 5’- AGG TCG GTG TGA ACG GAT TTG − 3’ ; antisense: 5’-GGG GTC GTT GAT GGC AAC A -3’) was used as an internal control.

Stability studies of ICG-p28

For a stability assay of ICG-p28 in tissue sections, the DCIS model was established by injecting MCF-10DCIS.com or MDA-MB231 cells into mouse mammary ducts of female athymic mice31. Following the appearance of a tumor in the mouse, ICG or ICG-p28 at 0.5 mg/kg was injected i.v. (tail vein) to the mice. After 24 h of injection, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were harvested. The tumor was subsequently stored frozen at -80 °C. Following 24 h, the OCT blocks from the frozen samples were prepared, and serial sections of slides were stored at -80, -20, 4 °C, and room temperature. The tissues were then subjected to the Odyssey imaging system (Li-cor, NE) at various time points. For a stability assay of ICG-p28 in solution, ICG and ICG-p28 solution at 66 µM, equivalent to 0.5 mg/kg, were stored at -80, -20, 4 °C, and room temperature and was subjected to the Odyssey imaging system at various time points. Specific NIR signals at 800 nm were recorded. All samples were kept in the dark.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.0 was used for the statistical analysis of data. Two-tailed t-tests were used for single comparison, and group differences were evaluated using ANOVA. Data were presented as means±SD, and all experiments were performed in triplicates unless otherwise mentioned.

Results

ICG-p28 uptake by multiple human DCIS cell lines

Since ICG-p28 penetration is critically important for our imaging approach, we first investigated the p28 penetration ability in various subtypes of human DCIS cell lines in vitro. MCF-10 DCIS.com is a clonal breast cancer cell line derived from a xenograft originating from MCF10AT cells, representing transformed and noninvasive carcinoma35. SUM-225, a HER2 overexpressing line, is an immortalized cancer cell line derived from the chest wall recurrence of DCIS29. CCH-1 cells are characterized by its ability to progress towards invasive breast cancer36. Confocal analyses showed that p28 penetrated MCF-10 DCIS.com, SUM-225, and CCH-1 cells, but a lesser entry in the normal fibroblasts (Fig. 1a). p28 penetration was further confirmed by flow cytometric analyses (Fig. S1). DCIS is a heterogeneous disease with biological differences, exhibiting aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) expression associated with breast cancer stem-like cells phenotype in some populations of DCIS cells37. To confirm whether ICG-p28 enters the ALDH sub-populations, MCF-10 DCIS.com cells were isolated by staining CD49 and CD24 to gate CD49f−/CD24+/ALDH− and CD49f+/CD24−/ALDH+ cell populations based on DEAB-sensitive enzymatic activity (Fig. 1b), further observations were monitored by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analyses where ICG-p28 entered both ALDH (-) and ALDH (+) MCF-10 DCIS.com (Fig. 1c).

Cells were exposed to 20 µM of Alexa Fluor 568 labeled peptide for 2 h at 37 °C and images were recorded. Red: Alexa fluor dye-p28; Blue: DAPI for nucleus. Scale bars: 20 μm. (b) Isolation of CD49-/CD24+/ALDH- as represented by R4 and CD49+/CD24-/ALDH+ as represented by R3. Cells were treated with diethylaminobenzaldehyde (DEAB) as a negative control, and cells without DEAB treatment were the test group. (c) Flow cytometric analysis of the penetration of ICG-p28 into ALDH(+) and ALDH(-) MCF-10 DCIS.com cells. Mean ± SD. ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001 (ANOVA).

Real-time surveillance of surgical margin via ICG-p28 in xenografted mice

To evaluate the intraoperative application of ICG-p28 in a DCIS setting, we established a xenograft mouse model of DCIS by injecting MCF-10 DCIS.com into the mammary ducts of the mice. After 24 h systemic intravenous (i.v) administration of ICG-p28, ICG alone, or PBS control in the mice, real-time PDE imaging of the DCIS mouse model showed the preferential uptake of ICG-p28 by DCIS, unlike the ICG alone or PBS groups (Fig. 2a). With greater contrast images of the lesions compared to the surrounding normal tissue under the NIR PDE real-time monitoring, intraoperative images with ICG-p28 identified tumor margins that allowed tumor resection with a 2 mm safety margin at the 6 and 12 o’clock positions (Fig. 2b). To quantitatively assess the effectiveness of our imaging method in identifying surgical margins, we employed qPCR assays to quantify residual human cancer cells in the DCIS mouse model. The excised tumor and the surrounding tissue samples were collected and subjected to quantitative human-specific Alu-DNA (hAlu), which can identify human-derived MCF-10 DCIS.com tumors in mice, as Alu-repeated DNA sequences are specific to human cells28,34. The residual cancer cells were quantitatively determined using hAlu and the housekeeping gene mGAPDH. PCR results showed that significantly fewer residual cancer cells in the surrounding tissues were detected when image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 was used [log(hAlu DNA/mGAPDH DNA) = 9.12] than ICG alone [log(hAlu DNA/mGAPDH DNA) = 92.3] and PBS [log(hAlu DNA/mGAPDH DNA) = 96.3] (Fig. 2c). These values denote the averages from 6 o’clock and 12 o’clock positioned tissue surroundings in each group. This result indicates that image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 resulted in a 9-fold average reduction in residual normalized tumor DNA at the margin sites than controls.

The coefficient of variation (CV) also showed that the ICG-p28 group was superior to the control groups as it demonstrated a relatively small dispersion (Fig. S2).

Improvement of the tumor recurrence rate by image-guided surgery with ICG-p28

Next, we evaluated the recurrence rates in the DCIS xenograft model to determine whether the qPCR results correlate with tumor recurrence rates or tumor growth after four weeks of tumor removal. In both PBS and ICG alone control groups, the tumor recurrence rate was 100% (6 out of 6 mice). In contrast, 2 out of 6 mice (33%, p < 0.05 ANOVA) showed recurrence at the resection site in the ICG-p28 group. The average volumes of the recurrent tumors were 201.8, 141.5, and 9.16 mm3 in the PBS, ICG, and ICG-p28 groups, respectively, showing ICG-p28 had the smallest volume among the tested groups (Fig. 3a). The weight of the recurrent tumors from ICG-p28 was also significantly lower than the control groups (Fig. 3b). Images of recurrent tumors in each group also represent the efficacy of intraoperative imaging with ICG-p28 (Fig. 3c). Collectively, these findings show that by aiding in the precise identification of the DCIS tumor margins, ICG-p28 improved surgical outcomes by reducing both the incidence of tumor recurrence and the tumor volume/weight.

Real-time surveillance of surgical margin via ICG-p28 in transgenic mice

The development of various genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) has been instrumental in elucidating the molecular events involved in breast cancer initiation, progression, and metastasis38. In this study, we utilized the MMTV-PyMT (polyomavirus middle T antigen), a widely used GEMM since this transgenic model provides an excellent experimental framework for studying the natural progression of the heterogeneity of breast tumors33. Tumors arising from the luminal cells of the mammary gland progress through distinct histological stages that mimic human ductal breast cancer progression33. Here, we evaluated image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 in the transgenic mouse model at the early breast tumor development phase (9, 10, and 11 weeks old). Similar to the xenograft model, the real-time PDE imaging with ICG-p28 showed the preferential uptake of ICG-p28 by the early developmental stage of mammary tumors and greater contrast images of tumors compared to the surrounding tissue were observed, aiding the surgery of tumors developed on the right and left inguinal glands (Fig. 4a). The efficacy of ICG-p28 in identifying margin in the transgenic model was performed similar to the xenograft model (Fig. 2c). The residual cancer cells were determined quantitatively with PCR-based assay using hAlu. PCR results showed (Fig. 4b) that significantly fewer residual cancer cells in the surrounding tissues were detected in the ICG-p28 group [log(hAlu DNA/mGAPDH DNA) = 10.3] than surgery with the control agent ICG [log(hAlu DNA/mGAPDH DNA) = 95] (These represent the average values obtained from 9, 10 and 11 weeks old mice). Image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 resulted in a 9-fold average reduction in residual normalized tumor DNA at the margin sites than the ICG group.

Optical stability of ICG-p28 in solution and frozen tissue sections

The ICG solution, when stored at 4 °C, was previously recommended for use within a few days of its preparation39. We investigated the optical stability kinetics of ICG-p28 in solution by analyzing fluorescence intensity at 800 nm using the Odyssey imaging system. When ICG and ICG-p28 solutions were stored below − 20 °C, their fluorescence intensities were maintained for more than 2 months (Fig. 6a). The fluorescence intensity of ICG-p28 solution stored at 4 °C started decreasing after 15 days and lost ~ 30% on day 25 compared to day 1. The fluorescence intensity of ICG stored at 4 °C rapidly decreased after 2 days and lost ~ 60% on day 15 (Fig. 6a). At room temperature, the half-life (t1/2) values of fluorescence intensities of ICG and ICG-p28 were 1.6 and 19.6 days, respectively (p < 0.0001, two-tailed t-test) (Fig. 6a). These results indicate that the storage conditions at relatively higher temperature (> 4 °C) significantly alter their optical stability, but p28 conjugation stabilized the ICG fluorescence. Next, we evaluated the optical stability of ICG-p28 in frozen tissue sections. The mice with the intraductal injection of MCF-10 DCIC.com were treated with ICG-p28 or ICG and cryosections were prepared post-24 h injections. The time and temperature kinetics studies showed that ICG-p28 fluorescence intensity in the tumor slides stored at 4 °C and room temperature rapidly decreased to 20% after 48 h. The half-life of ICG-p28 fluorescence in the tumor slides was ~ 25 and 15 days at -80 °C and − 20 °C storage conditions, respectively (Fig. 6b). Similarly, the mice with the intraductal injection of invasive triple-negative breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with ICG-p28 or ICG and cryosections were prepared post-24 h injections. Results of time and temperature kinetics were consistent with DCIS (Fig. S3). At the frozen storage conditions (below − 20 °C), ICG-p28 fluorescence was maintained ~ 95% within 48 h.

Discussion

In this study, an NIR optical imaging fluorescent probe, ICG-p28, was evaluated in terms of its optical stability and its ability to clearly identify DCIS tumor margins in multiple DCIS preclinical models. Image-guided surgery with ICG-p28 improved DCIS margin identification and reduced the tumor recurrence rate in vivo.

We previously demonstrated that image-guided surgery with non-toxic ICG-p28 can identify small (≤ 1 mm) tumor lesions, and precisely identify tumor margins of invasive and metastatic breast tumors27,28. Based on the results, we hypothesized that our imaging approach can be used for DCIS and yield similar rates when compared with invasive breast cancer. Development of a new intraoperative imaging technique for DCIS is clinically important since (i) early diagnosis is clinically important, (ii) it comprises malignant cells1, (iii) its incidence is increasing, and (iv) it accounts for ~ 20–25% of breast cancer cases diagnosed each year2. Thus, in this study, we focused on DCIS which is malignant epithelial cells confined to the lumen of mammary ducts. Preclinically, MCF-10 DCIS.com cells injected into a mouse duct (MIND) are a widely utilized animal model30. This intraductal xenograft model allows precise control of tumor burden, location, and size, providing a reliable platform for studying DCIS biology. The MMTV-PyMT transgenic mouse model was also used in this study as it mimics human DCIS disease40, and spontaneously forms high-grade DCIS lesions towards IBC basal-like mammary tumors41. Although the understanding of the underlying clear mechanisms of progression from DCIS to IBC is still limited, DCIS has the potential of becoming invasive breast cancer (IBC) based on the evidence of gradual histological continuity observed in normal and abnormal breast tissues42,43. Several studies have reported a few genomic and epigenomic differences between DCIS and IBC43,44. In general, basal-like carcinomas are poorly differentiated invasive tissues, and presumably have a DCIS precursor with similar molecular characteristics45. It has been suggested that basal-like DCIS intimately admixes with invasive basal-like carcinoma and that the basal-like DCIS serves as a precursor lesion for invasive basal-like carcinoma46. Moreover, basal-like DCIS is reportedly to contain cancer stem-like cells (CSC) that might drive malignant progression which can be characterized by subpopulations of CD49f+/CD24- stem-like cells that possess aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH-1) activity, a metabolic stem cell marker47. A DCIS subpopulation with ALDH1 expression is suggested to have significantly enriched migratory capacity and is responsible for disease recurrence and progression47 with increased likeliness to progress to IBC48. Therefore, it is empirical for the detection and removal of ALDH1 expressing sub-population of DCIS during the surgery. We sought out the ALDH1 negative and ALDH1 enriched DCIS sub-population cells in this study. Flow cytometric analyses showed that the ICG-p28 probe penetrated both ALDH (-) and ALDH (+) DCIS cells. This suggests that intraoperative imaging using ICG-p28 can be applicable for identifying basal-like DCIS exhibiting cancer stem cell characteristics, which serves as an early-stage lesion leading to invasive basal-like carcinoma.

ICG, a water-soluble, amphiphilic cyanine dye, offers a broad range of clinical applications due to its low toxicity and capacity to absorb and emit in the NIR spectral range. Since human tissue has the lowest absorption coefficient in the NIR region of the spectrum, ICG is an ideal agent for imaging applications such as angiography and hepatic function, etc49. However, it has limitations such as low target specificity, a short half-life, and poor stability50,51. In our optical stability study, significantly higher stability of ICG-p28 solution was observed at 4 °C with over 50% intensity retention over 35 days as compared to the ICG which disintegrated to 50% intensity on day 15 (Fig. 6a). In frozen sections, fluorescence signals of ICG was rapidly decreased within 24 h (Fig. 6b). In contrast, the fluorescence signal from ICG-p28 was maintained ~ 97% at day 1, 93% at day 4 at the − 80 °C storage condition, suggesting that the frozen clinical specimens can maintain > 90% of specific fluorescent signal within 96 h. The stability effect was probably due to an interaction of ICG with p28 that can prevent self-quenching by reducing the formation of aggregates as p28 peptide (2,918 Da, ~ 11 nm) is relatively bulky compared to ICG (775 Da, ~ 3 nm)52,53. The additional experiments (e.g., cryogenic transmission electron microscopy) can be designed to visualize the structure and morphology of the aggregates. Nevertheless, ICG optical stability was enhanced by p28 conjugation in both solution and tissue sections.

Fluorophores that emit in the NIR range have become popular as in vivo NIR imaging agents because of the high signal-to-noise ratio (“low-noise”) due to minimal intrinsic autofluorescence from tissues13. In recent years, there are two fluorescent agents for image-guided surgery were approved by the FDA. Gleolan® (5-ALA, aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride) was developed for glioma surgery. The FDA approved Gleolan in 201754. It is an optical imaging agent that turns glioma tumors bright pink/magenta to increase their visibility under blue light, making them easier for surgeons to see during brain tumor removal. During glioma surgery, Gleolan is used with an operating microscope adapted with a blue light source (excitation at 375–440 nm and emission at 620–710 nm). Recently, another intraoperative imaging agent was approved; Cytalux® (Pafolacianine) which consists of a fluorescent dye conjugated to folate receptor α (FRα)55. FRα is overexpressed 10 to 100-fold in non-mucinous epithelial ovarian carcinomas compared to normal cells56. Cytalux received FDA approval as an intraoperative imaging agent for ovarian cancer in 202157 and approval for the expanded indication for lung cancer surgery in December 202258. As of writing, there is no FDA-approved intraoperative imaging agent for breast cancer surgery in 2023. FRα is a marker and its levels are high in specific malignant tumors such as ovarian cancer59,60. For breast cancer, estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and epidermal growth factor (HER2) are well-established markers and prognostic factors61,62. Based on our previous studies on primary breast cancer, p28 penetration is independent of these receptors20,28. In this study on DCIS, we used MCF DCIC.com and MMTV-PyMT transgenic mouse models that are ER-/PR-/HER2 low63–65. As SUM-255 is an HER2 overexpressed cell line and CCH-1 is ER-/PR-/HER2 low30, the results described above suggest that our real-time imaging approach can be applied to broad subtypes of DCIS, independent of the receptor status.

In summary, we demonstrated that NIR fluorescence intraoperative imaging with ICG-p28 can be used to visualize DCIS tumors prior to resection without changing the surgical field of view. Our imaging approach potentially enables surgeons to intraoperatively identify DCIS tumor margins and improves overall outcomes by reducing recurrence rates. Using ICG-p28 as a real-time intraoperative imaging agent in BCS is a promising direction for future clinical studies.
