MHCXGraph: a graph-based approach to detecting T-cell receptor cross-reactivity
Authors: Carlos D M S Simões, Rocío L B R Maidana, Samuel C de Assis, João V S Guerra, Helder V Ribeiro-Filho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe T-cell receptor (TCR) recognition of multiple peptides presented by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) is a key natural phenomenon, enabling the T-cell repertoire to respond to a broad array of antigens. Despite its importance to the immune response, T-cell cross-reactivity poses a major challenge for the development of novel T cell–based therapies. In this study, we present MHCXGraph, a graph-based computational approach for identifying conserved and immunologically relevant regions across multiple structures of peptides bound to MHC molecules (pMHC). Our approach provides three operational modes: Multiple, Pairwise, and Screening, with user-defined parameters, allowing flexible configuration according to specific scientific needs while delivering fully interpretable results through user-friendly interfaces. We evaluated MHCXGraph across several case studies, including peptides bound to classical MHC Class I, MHC Class II, unbound human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles, and pMHCs containing noncanonical amino acids, demonstrating its ability to capture conserved structural determinants beyond sequence similarity. By integrating structural information with efficient graph-based analysis, MHCXGraph addresses key limitations of sequence-based methods while maintaining computational scalability. Collectively, these results indicate that MHCXGraph can be readily integrated into computational pipelines for T-cell cross-reactivity discovery, especially in the context of pMHC engager design and T cell–based vaccine development.Keywords: T-cell receptors, MHC, cross-reactivity, graph theory

Introduction

T cells, through their surface-expressed T-cell receptors (TCRs), recognize short peptides presented by the major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I or class II molecules (hereafter referred to as pMHC) [1, 2]. The TCR binding to pMHC can trigger T-cell activation and constitutes one of the most critical events at the immunological synapse, initiating the adaptive immune response against pathogens and tumor-related antigens. Unlike antibodies, which undergo affinity maturation to enhance their specificity toward target antigens, TCRs exist as a diverse repertoire shaped in the thymus through positive and negative selection [3, 4]. T-cell maturation process ensures TCR tolerance to self-peptides while enabling recognition of wide range of nonself-peptides, including those derived from viral proteins or tumor-associated mutated proteins (i.e. neoantigens) [5, 6].

Although vital for immune surveillance, the ability of TCRs to recognize multiple peptides presented by MHC, a phenomenon known as cross-reactivity, often limits the therapeutic use of T cells, particularly those engineered with modified TCRs [7, 8]. Engineered TCRs may inadvertently recognize self-peptides in addition to their intended targets, leading to severe adverse effects. A well-known example is a TCR designed to target the melanoma-associated antigen MAGE-A3, which cross-reacted with a peptide derived from the cardiac protein Titin, leading to fatal cardiotoxicity in patients [8]. More recently, Artificial intelligence (AI)-based approaches have enabled the design of TCRs [9–11] and novel TCR-like binders to pMHC targets [12], further increasing the need for robust strategies to assess and mitigate cross-reactivity. These challenges also extend to vaccine development, where epitope similarity to self-peptides may lead to immune tolerance and reduced immunogenicity [13].

To address these issues, computational methods have been developed to identify and rapidly assess the potential for cross-reactivity, generally based on the assumption that shared patterns among pMHC complexes may drive recognition by the same TCR. Conventional approaches rely on sequence-based comparisons, in which a target peptide is evaluated against self-peptides derived from the human proteome and ranked according to sequence identity, biochemical properties, and immunopeptidomic data [14]. For instance, ICrossR performs position-wide comparisons of peptide sequences to assess identity [14], whereas sCRAP incorporates physicochemical and structure-derived information, excluding MHC anchor residues that are not accessible for TCR interaction [15]. CrossDome further refines this approach by applying biochemical profiles and weighting peptide positions that may be critical for TCR recognition [16].

Although sequence-based approaches focused on the peptide provide a rapid initial screening, relying solely on peptide sequence information has important limitations. First, the MHC polymorphism, which can strongly influence TCR specificity, is completely overlooked. In addition, while peptide binding to MHC involves conserved pockets that restrict variability in binding modes, shifts in peptide register can occur [17, 18], as well as structural adaptability in peptide conformation [19]. Importantly, because sequence-based methods rely on positional alignment between peptide residues, they are generally limited to comparisons between peptides of the same length [16], restricting their applicability across the broader diversity of peptides presented by MHC-I and MHC-II molecules. These factors are not captured by current sequence-based comparisons, especially when peptides of different lengths are involved. Thus, to achieve a more accurate comparison between pMHCs, 3D structural information can be employed, which is critical and urgently needed for the robust identification of potential cross-reactivity. This has become even more demanding given recent advances in accurate and high-throughput 3D protein structure prediction with AlphaFold and related structural prediction methods [20]. MatchTope [21] represents one such effort, focusing on pMHC class I and comparing electrostatic surface properties after structural alignment. However, a general, alignment-free framework capable of identifying physicochemical similarities in a structurally interpretable manner, without topological constraints, across diverse pMHC structures remains lacking.

Here, we introduce MHCXGraph, a versatile and interpretable computational tool based on graph-theoretical representations of pMHC 3D structures for the structural characterization and deterministic identification of pMHC surface similarities that may underlie TCR cross-reactivity, without relying on model training. MHCXGraph is designed to be integrated into cross-reactivity prediction pipelines and extend existing sequence-based approaches by enabling structure-driven analysis. MHCXGraph employs an alignment-free strategy based on maximum common subgraph detection, using geometric association graphs to approximate all possible spatial correspondences between protein structures [22–24]. This approach enables the identification of regions on the pMHC exposed surface that are identical or similar across a set of multiple structures. Without relying on topological constraints, MHCXGraph analysis can be focused on user-defined surface regions and support simultaneous analysis of multiple structures without requiring pairwise comparison. To optimize performance, the association graph is constructed using tokenized node triads embedded with discrete geometrical properties. This design enables comparison of input pMHC structures with different peptide lengths and MHC classes, including nonclassical alleles, as well as cases involving noncanonical amino acids or even water molecules. Collectively, MHCXGraph provides a flexible and scalable framework for structure-based cross-reactivity analysis in immunological and therapeutic applications.

Materials and methods

MHCXGraph is an open-source Python package that leverages graph-based operations to identify conserved exposed regions across multiple pMHC structures (Fig. 1). It supports multiple execution modes and offers fully adjustable parameters, enabling flexible configuration to suit diverse user needs. For result analysis, the package provides an interactive dashboard that facilitates data exploration through graph visualizations and projections onto 3D structures.

Algorithm and workflow overview

The package workflow consists of four main steps, detailed in the following sections (Fig. 1A). The first step is the construction of graph representations of pMHC surfaces named as a surface graph. In this step, selected solvent-exposed residues are represented as nodes, and edges are defined based on distance thresholds. To define appropriate thresholds for residue selection using relative solvent accessibility (RSA) and for edge construction, we performed a structural analysis of TCR:pMHC complexes, as detailed in the Supplementary Material (see Section “Characterization of pMHC structural interfaces with TCRs”). Instead of individual amino acids, users may choose to group amino acids into classes based on physicochemical properties, such as hydrophobicity, polarity, or charge, or according to any other user-defined grouping scheme. In this case, the amino acid identity associated with each node is replaced by its corresponding amino acid class.

The selection of residues is an important step in the workflow, enabling users to focus the analysis on regions most relevant for TCR interaction. To support this process, we provide a curated list of pMHC residues that have already been observed to interact with TCRs in experimentally solved structures (58 positions for MHC-I and 35 for MHC-II; see Section “Characterization of pMHC structural interfaces with TCRs” and Supplementary Table S1).

In the second step, each surface graph is decomposed into small, overlapping triads (three-node subgraphs), a critical step for computational efficiency. Each triad is encoded by a set of attributes, including inter-node distances, solvent accessibility, and chirality, capturing local structural features (Fig. 1B). All these attributes are discretized into tokens, forming a codebook of triads that enables more efficient identification of associated triads across graphs. All steps starting from triad generation are performed in chunks to control memory usage, as detailed in the Supplementary Material. Importantly, chunking does not affect the resulting frame graphs, regardless of the number of chunks used (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The third step constitutes the core of the workflow and involves the association of common graph triads across the input graphs. Association requires matching triads from different input structures within the same token. This matching involves comparing node amino acid identities, triad chirality, inter-node distances, and differences in node RSA, with the latter two constrained by local distance and RSA difference thresholds defined by the users. This process results in the construction of an association graph containing residues shared among the input structures. Multiple association graph components, representing disconnected subgraphs, may be generated in this step.

In the fourth step, we extract fully coherent subgraphs, named frame graphs, from the association graph by selecting only association nodes in which all nodes satisfy a global distance threshold relative to other nonadjacent association nodes. This process is performed using a modified depth-first search (DFS) algorithm combined with local clique identification (Fig. 1C). The result of this step is a set of frame graphs containing only fully coherent association nodes with consistent distances across all input structures.

MHCXGraph supports three running modes: Multiple, Pairwise, and Screening, all configurable via user-defined parameters (Fig. 1D). In Multiple mode, all input structures are analyzed simultaneously, resulting in frame graphs that represent conserved regions shared across all input structures. This mode is particularly useful when the objective is to identify regions conserved throughout an entire set of pMHC structures. Consequently, residue identities or classes must be matched across all analyzed structures. To ensure computational and memory efficiency, analyses involving multiple structures are performed in chunks, as detailed in the Supplementary Material. In Pairwise mode, all nonredundant pairs of input structures are analyzed independently, generating a separate frame graph for each comparison. This mode is useful for comparing individual pairs of structures and for constructing similarity relationships across a collection of pMHCs. It can also complement the Multiple mode when one or more input structures substantially diverge from the remaining structures. In such cases, Pairwise mode can first be used to identify groups of structurally similar pMHCs, after which Multiple mode can be applied within each group to maximize the identification of conserved regions. In Screening mode, a reference structure is compared independently against a set of target structures, generating one frame graph for each reference–target comparison. This mode is particularly useful for ranking the structural similarity of a reference pMHC against a collection of candidate pMHC structures. The graph-based algorithm is identical in all three modes; only the organization of the input structures differs according to the intended analysis.

In addition, users can focus the analysis on the peptide and its immediate surrounding MHC environment.

Input data and parameters

The MHCXGraph package is executed using a JSON configuration file (manifest.json) that defines a set of entries controlling its behavior. A comprehensive list of parameters and a sample manifest file are available in the Supplementary Material (Table S2 and “Example of Manifest file” Section, respectively).

Structure inputs can be provided in either PDB or CIF file formats. The minimal structural preprocessing recommended is the removal of TCRs or other macromolecule binders, as these can affect pMHC residue accessibility. Residue or chain selection can be specified using the selectors parameter. A predefined list of potential TCR-interacting residues, derived from structural analysis of TCR:pMHC complexes, is available (Supplementary Material, Table S1); however, its use requires MHC structures to follow IMGT numbering, which can be achieved using a preprocessing subcommand included in MHCXGraph package (see details in Supplementary Material). Optional parameters (include_waters, include_ligands, and include_noncanonical_residues) allow inclusion of nonprotein entities (e.g. water, ligands, and modified amino acids) in the analysis.

All processes related to reading, processing, and writing of 3D structures in MHCXGraph are handled using the Biopython package [25], while the secondary structure assignment is performed using the PyDSSP implementation of DSSP [26].

Triad chirality q is encoded as a discrete value (+1 or -1) computed using backbone and side-chain geometric information, as described in the Supplementary Material (see “Determination of chirality of node triads” Section). In this procedure, glycine residues are assigned a virtual Cβ atom, and triads involving other than canonical amino acids are assigned a chirality value of 0. Unlike restricting the analysis to a single chain, this approach preserves neighboring nodes from other chains, allowing the identification of structural similarities in the local environment of the target chain.

Generation of coherent frame graphs

The creation of association graphs introduces an important limitation: a potential loss of coherence between nonadjacent distant association nodes. Here, coherence refers to the property by which a corresponding pair of residues, represented as nodes from multiple structures, exhibits similar Euclidean distances across all inputs (Fig. 1). Formally, for two association nodes , coherence requires that the pairwise distances between their corresponding residues are consistent across all input graphs, i.e. for all .

During association graph construction, this property is guaranteed only locally, as coherence is verified solely for residue pairs involved in matched triads and for edges introduced during triad expansion. Consequently, while adjacent association nodes satisfy local geometric consistency, no such guarantee is imposed on distant, nonadjacent nodes within the same connected component.

To identify such subsets, we define the coherence graph in which edges represent pairwise coherence. A subset of nodes is mutually coherent if and only if it forms a clique in .

Frame generation is performed independently for each connected component of the association graph. Nodes are first ordered according to their degree, and each node is used as an initial seed. For a given seed node, its adjacency neighborhood in A defines a candidate set. Since adjacency alone does not guarantee mutual coherence, a coherence graph is implicitly constructed over this candidate set, and maximal cliques are extracted using the Bron–Kerbosch algorithm [30]. Each clique represents a subset of nodes that are pairwise coherent and serves as a valid seed for frame construction.

Once coherent seed nodes are identified, frame expansion is performed using array-level operations derived from the coherence matrix. At each step, candidate nodes are restricted to those that are both adjacent in A and coherent with all nodes currently in the frame. This restriction is efficiently implemented by intersecting the corresponding rows of the coherence relation, avoiding explicit pairwise comparisons.

The traversal of candidate nodes follows a DFS strategy (Fig. 1), iteratively expanding each frame while preserving both adjacency and global coherence constraints. Expansion continues until no additional nodes satisfy these conditions.

When expansion terminates, the resulting subgraph is evaluated as a candidate frame. Valid frames must satisfy structural constraints, including a minimum number of edges and the absence of isolated nodes. Finally, redundant frame graphs are removed by eliminating subgraphs that are strict subsets of larger frames. The remaining maximal subgraphs define the final set of coherent frame graphs.

MHCXGraph output and visualization

MHCXGraph generates a diverse set of outputs to allow users to easily access and interpret the results. Standard outputs include HTML reports displaying graphs from different stages of the workflow, including surface graphs, association graphs, and frame graphs. Additionally, the package generates PDB files mapping the identified nodes onto the original 3D structures, as well as raw data files for downstream analysis.

By activating the dashboard visualization, users can access all outputs on a single interactive interface (Fig. 2). The dashboard provides easy access to visualize graphs, map them onto 3D structures with full graphical control, and perform post-processing analyses.

Results

Here, we demonstrate the applicability and versatility of MHCXGraph, by evaluating the method across representative case studies covering distinct biological contexts relevant to T-cell recognition and cross-reactivity.

Identifying conserved surface regions across multiple prevalent HLA alleles

The success of therapies targeting MHC molecules, such as TCR-based immunotherapies and emerging MHC engager designs, may depend on the specific HLA allele expressed by an individual, owing to allele-specific differences in the MHC surface available for TCR interaction. Thus, as a first case study, we applied MHCXGraph to identify conserved residues on MHC surfaces across the most prevalent HLA alleles in human populations [32]. In this particular case, we intentionally excluded the bound peptides, as our objective was to focus exclusively on the polymorphic TCR-interacting surface of the MHC molecule.

For this purpose, we selected 11 alleles—HLA-A01:01, HLA-A02:01, HLA-A03:01, HLA-A11:01, HLA-A24:02, HLA-B07:02, HLA-B08:01, HLA-B15:01, HLA-B40:02, HLA-B44:03, and HLA-C*07:02—representing three different loci (i.e. A, B, and C). All selected alleles had experimentally solved 3D structures available in the unbound state (i.e. not complexed with a TCR). Peptides from the structures were not considered in this analysis.

We executed MHCXGraph in pairwise mode, considering all nonredundant structure pairs. As selectors for graph construction, we used a predefined set of MHC-I residues known to interact with TCRs. A similarity heatmap was generated using a coverage-based index (see Materials and methods Section), defined as the fraction of unique nodes in association graph frames relative to the total number of nodes in both input graphs (Fig. 3A). Hierarchical clustering showed that MHCXGraph grouped HLAs by gene locus, with a primary cluster containing HLA-A alleles and a second cluster comprising HLA-B and HLA-C alleles.

We then applied the multiple-analysis mode to the HLA-A subset to identify shared structural features. By analyzing the first graph frame from the first two association graph components (Fig. 3A), we identified large common regions across the alleles, spanning the helices from both α1 and α2 domains. Projection of the graph onto the 3D structure provides a more detailed visualization of the conserved exposed residues. Despite this large conserved region, a nonconserved region can be observed at the end of the α1 helix, encompassing residues known to participate in TCR interactions in experimentally resolved complexes (Fig. 3A; right panel). In the context of computational design of TCRs or protein binders targeting a specific group of HLA alleles, the identified conserved regions can serve as anchoring hotspots for positioning the binders, whereas nonconserved regions should be avoided when broad binding across all analyzed alleles is desired.

Analyzing Mel5 TCR cross-reactivity in cancer-associated epitopes

As a second case study, we analyzed a set of 10-mer peptides presented by HLA-A*02, including three cancer-associated peptides shown to be recognized by the same Mel5 TCR [33]. In experimental T-cell activation assays, peptides derived from the Melan-A (EAAGIGILTV), BST2 (LLLGIGILVL), and IMP2 (NLSALGIFST) proteins were identified as inducing a TCR response, with a weaker activation observed for the latter. Other peptides derived from ERBB2, DSE, IMP1, MRP3, MUC16ATS, and MUC16TSS did not show significant activation signals.

All peptides, whether cross-reactive or not, were modeled using the AlphaFold3 server [20] (see modeling details in Supplementary Material), bound to HLA-A*02, and analyzed with MHCXGraph in pairwise mode. Clustering based on the similarity index identified the highest surface residue conservation between the two cross-reactive peptides derived from Melan-A and BST2, while IMP2 formed a more distant cluster, consistent with its reduced experimental activation.

The graph representation of the first frame from the first association graph component (Fig. 3B) highlights conserved residues between the cross-reactive Melan-A and BST2 peptides. Structural mapping shows conservation in central peptide positions (e.g. GLY4, ILE5, and ILE7), which are known to participate in TCR interactions [33]. Of note, the Mel5 case study also demonstrates the capability of MHCXGraph to handle peptides of different lengths, beyond the typical 9-mers considered by sequence-based methods.

Analyzing MHC-II cross-reactivity in HIV-derived epitopes

As a third case study, we investigated cross-reactivity across different MHC-II alleles (HLA-DR1, HLA-DR11, and HLA-DR15) presenting the most immunodominant HIV-1 capsid epitope (Gag293) and its shorter version RQ13. The complexes, HLA-DR1 and HLA-DR11 presenting the Gag293, and the HLA-DR15 presenting the RQ13, were experimentally shown to interact with the same TCR, named TCR F24 [34]. Experimental 3D structures of HLA-DR11–Gag293 and HLA-DR15–RQ13 (PDB IDs 6CPL and 6CPO, respectively) were obtained from the PDB, while the HLA-DR1-Gag293 was modeled with AlphaFold3 server [20]. This case study demonstrates the ability of MHCXGraph to analyze pMHC-II complexes across different MHC alleles, a scenario not addressed by structural methods such as MatchTope, while also comparing peptides with substantially different lengths, which is currently not feasible using sequence-based methods such as CrossDome.

Using multiple-analysis mode with RSA features in the triad representation and all atom node granularity, MHCXGraph identified seven conserved peptide residues, six of which collectively interact across the CDR1α, CDR3α, CDR2β, and CDR3β loops of the F24 TCR [34], indicating a preserved binding register between the shorter RQ13 peptide and the full-length Gag293 peptide (Fig. 3C). Additionally, conserved residues from both the α and β MHC-II domains were also identified, indicating an extensive interface for TCR cross-reactivity despite allele differences (Fig. 3C).

Screening cross-reactivity of a virus-derived immunogenic peptide

To demonstrate the application of MHCXGraph within a cross-reactivity discovery pipeline and its potential integration with existing computational methods, we used as a case study a previously identified immunogenic alphavirus-derived peptide (APLQHTAPF) with potential application in the development of pan-alphavirus vaccines [35]. The peptide sequence was first screened against an HLA-A*02 immunopeptidome using CrossDome [16], and the 50 highest-ranked self-peptide candidates were modeled with AlphaFold3 and subsequently analyzed with MHCXGraph in Screening mode, with the filter_triads_by_chain parameter enabled to focus on the peptide, as all candidate peptides are presented by the same HLA-A*02 allele (Supplementary Fig. S4). By ranking the candidates according to the coverage-based similarity metric, we identified the pMHCs that most closely resembled the reference pMHC surface. The ranking produced by MHCXGraph did not strictly follow the sequence-based ranking, highlighting the potential complementarity of the two approaches. Notably, the peptide ALLQQTQPL, which was ranked second by CrossDome, was identified as the top-ranked candidate by MHCXGraph. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S4, this peptide presents three conserved solvent-exposed positions relative to the reference peptide that may contribute to the formation of a similar TCR-interacting surface and, consequently, potential cross-reactivity. Interestingly, although the top-ranked peptide identified by CrossDome exhibited the highest sequence similarity, structural modeling revealed that its first two N-terminal residues were buried and not solvent exposed, making them unlikely to be accessible for TCR recognition. This structural difference likely explains its lower coverage-based similarity score in MHCXGraph.

Discriminative performance, sensitivity analysis, and extended applications

Although the primary objective of MHCXGraph is to identify biologically relevant conserved regions on pMHC surfaces that can support the analysis and interpretation of potential TCR cross-reactivity, we also evaluated the ability of its coverage-based similarity metric to discriminate experimentally validated cross-reactive pMHC pairs from negative pairs generated by shuffling the cross-reactive groups while maintaining MHC allele balance (see Supplementary Material, Section 8). ROC-AUC analysis demonstrated a high discriminative performance for MHCXGraph when considering the complete pMHC surface, including both the peptide and the MHC residues (AUC = 0.883), with a false positive rate of 1.12%. The same analysis, but using the triad filtering mode (filter_triads_by_chain) to restrict the comparison to peptide residues and their directly neighboring MHC residues, maintained high discriminative performance (AUC = 0.857), with a false positive rate of 9.55%. All evaluated MHCXGraph scenarios showed higher performance than a baseline predictor based solely on MHC allele information. We further compared this performance with the sequence-based method CrossDome and the structure-based method MatchTope. Because CrossDome is restricted to peptides of length nine, only this subset of the benchmark dataset could be used for comparison. Using its relatedness score, CrossDome achieved discriminative performance comparable with MHCXGraph in the peptide-focused mode (AUC = 0.870), whereas MatchTope, using the Hodgkin metric, showed a lower ROC-AUC than the other methods (AUC = 0.691).

To evaluate the ranking capability of the methods, we used the same structural cross-reactivity dataset. For each pMHC, we compared it against all other pMHCs in the dataset, regardless of whether they belonged to the same cross-reactivity group, or only pMHCs sharing the same MHC allele, and ranked the resulting similarity scores for each method. The ranking was then evaluated using the Average Precision (AP), which measures the ability to retrieve cross-reactive pMHCs ahead of noncross-reactive ones (see Supplementary Material, Section 8). The Mean Average Precision (MAP) across all query pMHCs indicated high ranking performance for MHCXGraph (MAP = 0.863) in prioritizing cross-reactive pMHCs over noncross-reactive ones, with no substantial change when restricting the ranking to pMHCs within the same allelic group (MAP = 0.876) (Supplementary Fig. S5). A comparable MAP was obtained with CrossDome (using the restricted subset of 9-mer peptides), whereas MatchTope achieved a lower MAP across all evaluated scenarios (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Since MHCXGraph relies on a set of adjustable parameters, such as edge_threshold, local_distance_diff_threshold, and distance_bin_width, we also analyzed how variations in these parameters influence metrics such as the coverage-based similarity index. By analyzing all 32 pMHC possible pairs from the Mel5 case study and varying the edge_threshold from 7 Å to 10 Å, we observed similarity values ranging from 0.72 to 0.96, with substantial overlap for thresholds of 8.5, 9, and 10 Å (Supplementary Fig. S6). A reduction in similarity is expected for thresholds around 7–8 Å due to the possible loss of edges between nodes (as evidenced in Supplementary Fig. S1). Therefore, values between 8.5 and 10 Å allow the formation of connected graphs without introducing excessive edges. For the local_distance_diff_threshold, varying the parameter from 0.5 to 2 Å resulted in a large overlap in the distribution of similarity values, indicating a relatively small influence on the coverage metric (Supplementary Fig. S6). Importantly, values >3 Å may induce the matching of unrelated nodes in the association graph, such as residues at neighboring sequence positions. Finally, varying the distance_bin_width does not significantly affect the results, as this parameter is used solely to accelerate the calculations.

Finally, to showcase the extended capabilities of MHCXGraph in handling noncanonical amino acids and other ligands, we executed the Multiple mode using three pMHC complexes containing different phosphorylated peptides (phosphoserines) and crystallographic water molecules (Supplementary Fig. S7) [36]. MHCXGraph successfully identified the conserved phosphoserine residue together with a neighboring conserved arginine. In addition, a conserved cluster of crystallographic water molecules surrounding the phosphoserine were also identified, potentially indicating structurally important interface waters.

Runtime and memory usage benchmarking

To assess runtime and memory usage, we executed MHCXGraph in multiple and screening modes using two datasets, each comprising 100 pMHC structures. In the first dataset (Single HLA), all structures corresponded to the same MHC-I allele (HLA-A*02:01) bound to different peptides, whereas the second dataset (Diverse HLA) included different MHC-I alleles, each bound to distinct peptides. All experiments were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Core i7-14700 processor (2.10–5.40 GHz).

MHCXGraph analyzed 100 structures from both the Diverse and Single HLA datasets in a similar average runtime of 53 s, with a maximum peak memory usage of ∼1.2 GB (Fig. 4) in the multiple mode. A comparable performance profile was observed in screening mode, with an average runtime of 58 s for both datasets.

Although MatchTope exhibited lower memory usage, both methods required ≲300 MB of RAM, making them readily compatible with conventional workstations. To further reduce memory consumption, MHCXGraph also allows users to disable dashboard generation (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Discussion

The natural recognition of multiple MHC-presented peptides by a single TCR, despite being an essential process for the recognition of a broad array of antigenic peptides by the TCR repertoire, is one of the main challenges when developing TCR-guided therapies [7, 8, 37]. The understanding of similarities between peptides presented by MHC is also relevant in the context of vaccines based on T-cell responses [13]. For instance, in the development of pan-viral vaccines, one may want to select similar antigenic peptides for MHC presentation while ensuring their divergence from self-peptides [35].

The structural variability of peptide-exposed residues involved in TCR engagement, including register shifting [18], and the sequence and functional variability of MHC alleles presenting these peptides to TCR-specific populations [38], which may or may not lead to TCR cross-reactivity, create a particular need for structure-based comparison methods that sequence-based approaches alone cannot fully address [21].

In this context, we presented MHCXGraph, a graph-based method that can identify common regions across pMHC surfaces. MHCXGraph has several advantages and functionalities: it is alignment-free, it can focus on the most relevant TCR-interacting regions, it can be applied not only to the most common MHC-I but also to other MHC-like structures with or without peptide, and it can be directly used to analyze pairs or multiple structures. Importantly, MHCXGraph does not require model training, making it particularly suitable for applications where experimentally validated cross-reactivity data are scarce.

The basis of the MHCXGraph algorithm is the maximum common subgraph problem for detecting subgraph isomorphism, which has previously been addressed to identify surface or structural similarities between proteins [22, 23, 31]. In classical approaches, an association graph is first built from the product graph of two input graphs, followed by a maximum clique operation to detect the common subgraph [31]. Here, we employ a related strategy, but with key differences to improve computational performance and geometric accuracy. These include, for example, performing the Cartesian product at the level of tokenized triads and generating coherent subgraphs through a DFS algorithm coupled with a local clique search. The latter aims to avoid the computationally expensive (NP-hard in general) maximal clique problem. Comparing to individual node associations, the use of triads as the basic unit of association introduces stronger geometric constraints, capturing local structural context beyond individual residues and reducing spurious matches. Despite its advantages, the use of triads restricts the detection of common surface regions to those involving at least three connected residues. However, this is not a limitation for MHC interaction interfaces, which are typically large and comprise, on average, 18 residues, as shown in our structural analysis.

Although MHCXGraph has been applied to analyze hundreds of structures, as demonstrated in the computational time analysis, and can scale linearly to thousands of structures within minutes in screening mode, its structure-based nature makes it particularly suitable as a final step in cross-reactivity detection workflows. Starting from a set of pMHC candidates derived, e.g. from large-scale sequence-based proteome analyses, such as those implemented in the ARDitox pipeline [39], MHCXGraph can identify conserved surface regions and provide highly accurate structural validation and interpretability for cross-reactivity analysis.

Through different case studies, we demonstrated the usability of the method. MHCXGraph was able to identify conserved surfaces shared by pMHCs recognized by the Mel5 TCR [33] and cluster them in comparison with noncross-reactive cases. It was also capable of identifying shared regions across different HLA alleles, even without the bound peptide, and grouping them according to their similarity at the TCR interface. While the identification of conserved pMHC regions can be useful for avoiding cross-reactivity, the identification of conserved sites across multiple alleles can be useful when designing a pan-allelic binder and regulating alloselectivity [40]. Finally, we also demonstrate the capability of the method to work with MHCs other than classical MHC-I, such as MHC-II, which, through an open groove at both ends, presents peptides with a broader range of lengths and flexible flanking segments [41].

Although MHCXGraph was developed with a focus on pMHCs and involved extensive testing in this context, the graph-based approach presented here can be directly applied to any type of protein surface. A practical application not addressed in this study is the identification of conserved regions on the surfaces of different TCR structures. When focused on TCR CDRs, the method could identify shared residues across clonally expanded T-cell populations with TCRs that recognize the same pMHC [42].

Importantly, MHCXGraph was designed to identify similarities between pMHC surfaces that may lead to cross-reactivity by an unknown TCR or an unknown repertoire of TCRs, without requiring prior knowledge of the cross-reactive receptor. This fundamentally distinguishes MHCXGraph from TCR:pMHC prediction methods [43–47], which are designed to predict the interaction between a specific TCR and a pMHC complex and, consequently, can be used to infer the cross-reactivity of a known TCR. From a complementary perspective, MHCXGraph can enhance TCR prediction methods by providing structural and molecular interpretability for predicted cross-reactive pMHC pairs, helping to identify the conserved surface features that may underlie cross-recognition. Additionally, the structural clustering of pMHC surfaces with MHCXGraph could improve the separation between training and test sets in machine learning models aimed at predicting TCR interactions with unseen peptides [48]. The identification of poorly conserved pMHC surfaces by MHCXGraph could also assist in assigning better TCR:pMHC negative pairs generated from positive shuffling [49, 50]. Ultimately, high-quality data generated by MHCXGraph could be used to train novel machine learning models for predicting protein surface similarities.

Compared with other available methods developed for a similar purpose, such as CrossDome and MatchTope, MHCXGraph offers several advantages, including the incorporation of MHC information and independence from peptide length, overcoming important limitations of CrossDome and other sequence-based methods. In addition, on the evaluated benchmark datasets, MHCXGraph achieved both faster runtime and higher discriminative and ranking performance than MatchTope. Regarding the latter, despite the scarce availability of cross-reactivity data and the limitations associated with generating negative data, the coverage-based similarity index of MHCXGraph showed promising properties for discriminating cross-reactivity, although further studies with larger cross-reactivity datasets would improve our understanding of its discriminative performance. Importantly, as demonstrated in the alphavirus-derived immunogenic peptide case study, MHCXGraph can be used together with these available methods, leveraging, e.g. the large-scale screening capabilities of sequence-based methods such as CrossDome to prioritize a more restricted set of candidate cross-reactive pMHCs for subsequent structural analysis with MHCXGraph.

Conclusion

MHCXGraph package provides a versatile approach for studying, detecting, and interpreting potential cross-reactivity of peptides presented by MHC using structural information encoded in graph representations. MHCXGraph is expected to be integrated into computational pipelines for cross-reactivity discovery and to contribute to the development of safer TCR-based therapies and vaccines.
