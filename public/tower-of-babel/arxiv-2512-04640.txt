Abstract
. We consider a model Dirichlet problem with concave-convex and critical nonlinearity settled in Carnot groups. Our aim is to prove the existence of two positve solutions in the spirit of a famous result by Ambrosetti, Brezis and Cerami. To this aim we use a variational Perron method combined with proper estimates of a family of functions which are minimizers of the relevant Sobolev inequality. Due to the lack of boundary regularity, we also have to be careful while proving that the first solution found is a local minimizer in the proper topology.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
be an bounded and connected
open set with smooth enough boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
q\in(0,1)
, let

be the critical Sobolev exponent related to the Sobolev inequality in
\mathbb{G}
, and let

(

)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u&=\lambda\,u^{q}+u^{2^{\star}_{Q}-1}&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u&>0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=0&\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

We stress that

denotes here the sub-laplacian on
\mathbb{G}
which is a second-order differential operator with non-negative characteristic form that can be explicitly expressed as a sum of squares of vector fields satisfying the Hörmander condition, see e.g.
[
28
]
. We refer to Section
2
for more details, including the Folland-Stein Sobolev spaces we will work with.

Along the paper it will sometimes be useful to denote the above problem as (

)
λ

to make it clear the choice of the parameter.

)

The interest in studying existence of positive solutions to critical problems in the Carnot group setting,
is in the geometric significance of the purely critical problem in the model case of the Heisenberg group.
Indeed, when

[

showed that adding a perturbative term, linear in
[
15

is provided by the use of the Aubin-Talenti functions, whose analogue in

As long as one needs explicit knowledge of proper replacements of the Aubin-Talenti functions, the only other sub-Riemannian structure where they are known is that of groups of Iwasawa type, see

On the other hand, since the best constant in the Sobolev inequality is achieved in all Carnot groups (see

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect all the relevant notations, definitions and preliminaries needed in the rest of the paper.

2.1.
Carnot groups

A Carnot group
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\diamond)
of step
k
is a connected, simply connected Lie group whose finite dimensional Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of left-invariant (w.r.t.
\diamond
) vector fields admits a stratification of step
k
, namely there exist
k
linear subspaces
\mathfrak{g}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{g}_{k}
such that

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{k},\qquad[\mathfrak{g}_{1},\mathfrak{g}_{i}]=\mathfrak{g}_{i+1},\qquad\mathfrak{g}_{k}\neq\{0\},\qquad\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\{0\}\textrm{ for all }i>k.

In particular, this implies that Carnot groups are a special instance of graded groups.

We call
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
the horizontal layer. We denote by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
a basis of left-invariant vector fields of
\mathfrak{g}
such that the following holds:

•

X_{1},\ldots,X_{m_{1}}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
w.r.t. the scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}
;

•

for every
1<i\leq k
,
X_{m_{i-1}+1},\ldots,X_{m_{i}}
is a basis of of
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
;

•

m_{0}=0
and
n_{i}:=m_{i}-m_{i-1}=\dim\mathfrak{g}_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
;

•

m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=N
.

We define the homogeneous dimension of
\mathbb{G}
as

Q:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\cdot n_{i}.

The left translations
\tau:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
provides a family of automorphisms of
\mathbb{G}
, and are defined as follows

(2.1)

\tau_{h}(g):=h\diamond g,\quad\textrm{ for a given $h\in\mathbb{G}$,}

The anisotropic dilations
\delta_{\lambda}:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{G}
of
\mathbb{G}
are instead defined as

(2.2)

\delta_{\lambda}(g)=\left(\lambda^{\alpha_{1}}g_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\alpha_{N}}g_{N}\right),\quad\textrm{ for every }\lambda>0,

where
\alpha_{j}=i
if
m_{i-1}<j\leq m_{i}
.
We notice that
Q=\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{N}
.

Given a smooth horizontal vector field
V=v_{1}X_{1}+\ldots+v_{m_{1}}X_{m_{1}}
, we define its horizontal divergence as

(2.3)

\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}V:=X_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+X_{m_{1}}v_{m_{1}}.

Moreover, given a smooth enough scalar-valued function
u:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
, we can define
the horizontal gradient of
u
as

(2.4)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u:=(X_{1}u,\ldots,X_{m_{1}}u),

and the sub-Laplacian of
u
as

(2.5)

\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u:=\mathrm{div}_{\mathbb{G}}(\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u)=X_{1}^{2}u+\ldots+X_{m_{1}}^{2}u,

The Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{N}
coincides with the Haar measure of
\mathbb{G}
and hence is left-invariant and satisfies the following scaling property:

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}^{N}(\delta_{\lambda}(E))=\lambda^{Q}\mathcal{L}^{N}(E)\quad\textrm{ for every measurable set }E\subset\mathbb{G}.

Every integral in this manuscript has to be understood
with respect to the Haar measure, unless otherwise stated.

Every Carnot group can be endowed with several homogeneous norms. A homogeneous (quasi)norm
\rho:\mathbb{G}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-negative function further satisfying the following properties:

•

\rho(g)=0
if and only if
g=0
;

•

\rho(\delta_{\lambda}(g))=\lambda\,\rho(g)
for every
g\in\mathbb{G}
and for every
\lambda>0
;

•

\rho(h\diamond g)\leq C\left(\rho(h)+\rho(g)\right)
for every
g,h\in\mathbb{G}
and for some constant
C\geq 1
.

By a famous result of Folland
[
23
]
, there exists a homogeneous norm
|\cdot|_{\mathbb{G}}
on
\mathbb{G}
and a positive constant
C_{Q}>0
, depending only on
Q
, such that the function

(2.7)

\Gamma_{h}(g):=\dfrac{C_{Q}}{|h^{-1}\diamond g|^{Q-2}_{\mathbb{G}}},\quad\textrm{ with }Q\geq 3,

is a fundamental solution of
-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}
with pole at
h\in\mathbb{G}
.
Moreover, homogeneous norms can be used to define distances as follows:

d_{\rho}(g,h):=\rho(h^{-1}\diamond g).

We stress that there are other possible choices of distances (i.e. the so called
CC
-distance and many others) which are all equivalent.
Finally, we will denote by

B_{r}(g_{0}):=\{g\in\mathbb{G}:d_{\mathbb{G}}(g,g_{0})=|g_{0}^{-1}\diamond g|_{\mathbb{G}}<r\},

the open ball of radius
r>0
and center
g_{0}\in\mathbb{G}
.

We refer e.g. to
[
12
]
for a comprehensive introduction to the subject.

2.2.
The functional setting

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be an open set.
For every
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{O})
there exists a positive constant
C_{Q}>0
depending only on the homogeneous dimension
Q
such that the following Sobolev inequality holds true

(2.8)

\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2_{Q}^{\star}}(\mathcal{O})}\leq C_{Q}\,\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})},

where

(2.9)

2_{Q}^{\star}:=\dfrac{2Q}{Q-2},

denotes the (sub-elliptic) critical Sobolev exponent.
Thanks to (
2.8
),

\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f|\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
provides a norm on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
.
We define the Folland-Stein space
S^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})
as the completion of
C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathcal{O})
w.r.t. the above norm, and we set

\|u\|_{S^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})}=\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u|\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\quad\text{for every $u\in S^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{O})$}.

We explicitly observe that, owing to (
2.8
), we have

(2.10)

S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})=\big\{u\in L^{2_{Q}^{\star}}(\mathcal{O}):\,\text{$X_{i}u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})$
for all $1\leq i\leq m_{1}$}\big\},

where
X_{1}u,\ldots,X_{m_{1}}u
are meant in the sense of distributions.

We now remind a couple of basic properties of
S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
when
\mathcal{O}
is an open and bounded set, see e.g. to
[
24
]
.

•

S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
is endowed with a structure of real Hilbert space by the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})}=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}\qquad(u,v\in S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})),

whose associated norm is precisely
\|\cdot\|_{S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})}
.

•

S_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{O})
is continuously embedded into
L^{p}(\mathcal{O})
for every
1\leq p\leq 2_{Q}^{\star}
.
Furthermore, this embedding turns out to be
compact
when
1\leq p<2_{Q}^{\star}
.

We refer e.g. to
[
24
]
for more details.

We are now ready to properly set the definition of weak sub/supersolution of (

P

)
λ
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}
be an open, bounded and connected set.
We say that a function
u\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak subsolution (resp. supersolution) of (

P

)
λ
if it satisfies the following properties:

(i)

u>0
in
\Omega
.

(ii)

For every
0\leq\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, it holds that

(2.11)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\varphi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}\leq(\textrm{resp. }\geq)\int_{\Omega}\left(\lambda u^{q}+\ u^{2^{\star}_{Q}}\right)\varphi.

Finally, we say that
u\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (

P

)
λ
if it is both a weak subsolution and a weak supersolution of (

P

)
λ
without the non-negativity condition on
\varphi
.

Let us close this section recalling a few results on the Sobolev inequality (
2.8
).

Lemma 2.2
.

The best Sobolev constant in (
2.8
) (with
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{G}
) is achieved by a positive function
T\in S^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{G})
, and it is characterized as follows

(2.12)

S_{\mathbb{G}}:=\inf_{f\in S^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{G})}\dfrac{\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{G})}}{\|f\|^{2}_{L^{2_{Q}^{\star}}(\mathbb{G})}}.

Up to a constant,
T\in S^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{G})
is also a weak solution to

(2.13)

-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u=u^{2^{\star}_{Q}-1}\quad\textrm{in }\mathbb{G}.

Moreover, the following holds:

•

there exists a positive constant
M_{1}>0
such that

(2.14)

T(g)\leq M_{1}\,\min\{1,|g|_{\mathbb{G}}^{2-Q}\},\quad\textrm{ for every }g\in\mathbb{G},

•

there exists a positive constant
M_{2}>0
such that

(2.15)

T(g)\geq M_{2}\,\dfrac{|B_{1}(0)|}{(1+|g|_{\mathbb{G}})^{Q-2}},\quad\textrm{ for every }g\in\mathbb{G}.

Proof.

The fact that the best Sobolev constant in (
2.8
) (with
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{G}
) is achieved has been proved in
[
27
]
. We refer to
[
13
, Theorem 3.4]
for a proof of (
2.14
) and to
[
34
, Lemma 3.2]
for a proof of (
2.15
).
∎

As it is well known from the seminal paper
[
15
]
, a major role in finding solutions to critical problems is played by a suitable localized version of the family of minimizers of (
2.8
). To be more precise, let
T\in S^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{G})
be a minimizer of (
2.12
). For every
\varepsilon>0
define the rescaled function

(2.16)

T_{\varepsilon}(g):=\varepsilon^{(2-Q)/2}T\left(\delta_{1/\varepsilon}(g)\right).

Let further be
R>0
such that
B_{R}(0)\subset\Omega
and let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(B_{R}(0))
be a cut-off function such that
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and
\varphi\equiv 1
in
B_{R/2}(0)
. Finally, define the family of functions

(2.17)

U_{\varepsilon}(g):=\varphi(g)T_{\varepsilon}(g),\quad g\in\mathbb{G}.

Now, we have the following

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
T_{\varepsilon}
and
U_{\varepsilon}
be as above.
The following holds:

i)

Due to scaling invariance,

(2.18)

\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}T_{\varepsilon}|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{G})}=\|T_{\varepsilon}\|^{2^{\star}_{Q}}_{L^{2_{Q}^{\star}}(\mathbb{G})}=S_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q/2}.

ii)

The function
U_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the following estimates as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

(2.19)

\displaystyle\||\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}U_{\varepsilon}|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{G})}

\displaystyle=S_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q/2}+O(\varepsilon^{Q-2})

(2.20)

\displaystyle\|U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2^{\star}_{Q}}_{L^{2_{Q}^{\star}}(\mathbb{G})}

\displaystyle=S_{\mathbb{G}}^{Q/2}+O(\varepsilon^{Q}).

Proof.

We refer to
[
34
, Lemma 3.3]
for a proof of both (
2.19
) and (
2.20
).
∎

2.3.
Auxiliary sublinear problem

A major role in what follows will be played by the weak solution of the following problem

(2.21)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}u&=\lambda\,u^{q}&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u&>0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
u&=0&\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
q\in(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
. We say that
u\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (
2.21
) analogously to Definition
2.1
.
In this context, we have the following

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{G}

be a bounded open set. Moreover, let
q\in(0,1)
and
\lambda>0
.
Then, there exists a unique weak solution
\underline{u}_{\lambda}\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to (
2.21
). Moreover,
\underline{u}_{\lambda}
is a global minimizer in the
S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
-topology
of the functional

(2.22)

J_{\lambda}(u):=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}u|^{2}-\dfrac{\lambda}{q+1}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{q+1}.

Finally, we also have that
J_{\lambda}(\underline{u}_{\lambda})<0
.

The above result is actually a particular case of
[
10
, Theorem 1.1, Proposition 5.1]
, where Brezis-Oswald-type results have been proved for more general Hörmander operators.

We now state an
ad hoc
comparison principle for weak super and subsolutions of the model purely sublinear problem (
2.21
). This can be seen as a particulr instance, in the Carnot group setting, of
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
, which in turn was inspired by
[
14
]
. We refer to
[
41
]
for more general result whose proofs rely on the validity of suitable Picone-type inequalities.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\lambda>0
,
q\in(0,1)
and
v,w\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
weakly satisfy

(2.23)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}v&\leq\lambda v^{q}&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
v&>0&\textrm{ in }\Omega,\\
v&=0&\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

and

(2.24)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}w&\geq\lambda w^{q}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
w&>0&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
w&=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Then
w\geq v
in
\Omega
.

Proof.

We closely follow
[
4
, Proof of Lemma 3.3]
.
We choose first a smooth function
\theta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying the following properties

•

\theta(t)=0
for
t\leq 0
and
\theta(t)=1
for
t\geq 1
;

•

\theta
is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}
;

and we define (for every
\varepsilon>0
)

\theta_{\varepsilon}(t):=\theta\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)\in S_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

We further consider the variational formulation of both (
2.23
) and (
2.24
), namely

(2.25)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\varphi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}\geq\lambda\int_{\Omega}w^{q}\varphi\quad 0\leq\varphi\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

and

(2.26)

\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}\varphi\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}\geq\lambda\int_{\Omega}v^{q}\varphi\quad 0\leq\varphi\in S^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

We then test (
2.25
) with
\theta_{\varepsilon}(v-w)v
and (
2.26
) with
\theta_{\varepsilon}(v-w)w
. Finally, we subtract the latter to the former, getting

(2.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\left(w^{q-1}-v^{q-1}\right)\theta_{\varepsilon}(v-w)vw

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}w,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(v-w)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}v\,\theta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v-w)-\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(v-w)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}w\,\theta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v-w)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(v-w)\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}(v-w)\theta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v-w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}v,\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}(v-w))\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}=\int_{\Omega}(-\Delta_{\mathbb{G}}v)\,\gamma_{\varepsilon}(v-w),

where

\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t):=\int_{0}^{t}s\theta^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(s)\,ds.

By construction of
\theta_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that
0\leq\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\varepsilon
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, exploiting both (
2.27
), (
2.23
) and Hölder inequality, we find that

(2.28)

\int_{\Omega}\left(w^{q-1}-v^{q-1}\right)\theta_{\varepsilon}(v-w)vw\leq\lambda\,\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}v^{q}\leq C(\lambda,\|v\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},|\Omega|,q)\,\varepsilon.

By letting
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we find that

\int_{\{v>w\}}\left(w^{q-1}-v^{q-1}\right)vw\leq 0,

from which, recalling that
q\in(0,1)
, we conclude that
|\{v>w\}|=0
. This closes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.6
