Data as a relation: development of a blockchain-based platform for Indigenous data sovereignty
Authors: Tim K Mackey, Alec J Calac, Tiana McMann, Ken Miyachi, Samantha Hurst, Keolu Fox, Burt Dillabaugh, Kelle Dhein, Guthrie Ducheneaux, Joseph Yracheta
Published: 2026

In 2018, a staff attorney for the Native American Rights Funds described efforts by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) to collect DNA from under-represented American Indian and Alaskan Native (AI/AN) communities (All of Us Research Program) as an example of biocolonialism.1 Criticised for forgoing consultation with tribal leaders, agency officials rapidly convened an AI/AN workgroup to identify considerations for the collection, management, and use of AI/AN genomic data in this programme.2 Some of the primary concerns with the All of Us Research Program included storage of biospecimens in a privately-operated repository and no explicit representation of AI/AN researchers and tribal leaders on the NIH Data Access Committee overseeing requests for programme datasets.3,4 This criticism highlighted a fundamental challenge with data sovereignty in Indigenous communities: misuse of their data as governed by outside institutions, leading to mistrust in biomedicine and an absence of technology solutions specifically designed to enable Indigenous sovereignty.

Concurrently, discussions were ongoing with the Cheyenne River Sioux Nation and a Community Advisory Group to establish the first tribally managed genomics repository to drive advances in AI/AN and Indigenous health research. In 2019, these efforts were realised with the establishment of the 501(c)(3) Native BioData Consortium (NativeBio). Shortly thereafter, we began a collaboration with NativeBio to design a blockchain-based system to effectuate sovereign data governance of AI/AN data in alignment with the principles of Indigenous data sovereignty (IDS). IDS is defined as the authority of an Indigenous entity to govern the collection, ownership, and application of their data, whether related to research or other purposes.5 IDS is grounded in the principles of free, prior, and informed consent, as outlined in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples and also closely aligns with efforts to encourage community-based participatory research by directly engaging Indigenous communities in all stages of the research process.6

When working with any Indigenous community or organisation, purposeful outreach, listening, and relationship building are crucial before any scientific engagement. Our project likewise started with forming a community advisory board to identify and advise on the project objectives. A series of public meetings and presentations at Indigenous-led conferences then introduced the concept of blockchain and IDS and allowed for additional listening sessions with Indigenous groups and allies to further explore blockchain compatibility with IDS. We also conducted key informant interviews with AI/AN scholars and tribal leaders to explore specific themes and shared opinions about digital technologies and IDS that have informed technology development.5 Finally, prototyping of a blockchain platform named “b-IDS”, with Ethereum selected as the underlying protocol, was carried out with different iterations evaluated, refined, and adjusted based on feedback from NativeBio and our community advisory board.7 Critically, we framed prototyping as exploratory, in that we had no preconceived determinations about the design, choice, or purpose of a technology offering, or even the type of data a blockchain-based system would manage.8

What resulted from this process was different than what we anticipated. Although blockchain technology has potential for distributed and federated management of genomic and other health-care data and can promote community-specific data governance goals (eg, automated smart contracts to execute terms of IDS and tribal institutional review board requirements, dynamic consent structures for data use consent, and consensus mechanisms to allow community decision making),9 a more fundamental challenge of needing to ensure overall accountability and alignment when conducting research with AI/AN communities emerged. Hence, the project pivoted to building a blockchain-mediated workflow for shared decision making around research engagement with NativeBio and AI/AN genomic data (figure).

b-IDS workflow mediated by blockchain

The b-IDS platform allows consortium community member authorised and tribal member authorised nodes to vote on the process of: (1) approving researchers to join the network; (2) voting on research proposals to address community priority issues; (3) approval of research proposals and subsequent project milestones where community feedback can be incorporated; and (4) final community approval of research deliverables and final project outputs. b-IDS=Blockchain “b” for Indigenous Data Sovereignty. DAO=decentralised autonomous organisation. UCSD=UC San Diego.

What results from this workflow is a full and complete record of all decisions made through a more participatory process than the current status quo. This record includes immutable proof of community participation, decision making, and consensus to stages of the research process (eg, approval of research topics, proposals, milestones, and any final deliverables) that is also broadcasted to the b-IDS consortium community network to increase transparency and accountability. Put simply, decisions about the who, why, and how of research involving AI/AN genomic data go through a shared decision-making system that honours the core principles of IDS and community-based participatory research through smart contract execution, permission structures, and other blockchain-enabled architecture. However, our technology development needs further refinement and our engagement with NativeBio and Indigenous communities continues. This engagement includes exploring impactful use cases that can bring actual and tangible benefit to Indigenous communities when sharing their data and ensuring earth-friendly and sustainable implementation while recognising tribal infrastructure needs.
