A solvable microscopic model for the propagation of light in a dielectric medium
R. Dengler

Abstract
This work examines a dielectric medium consisting of small, polarizable classical atoms arranged on a cubic lattice. Using a regularized variant of the discrete dipole approximation (DDA) method, we derive the solution for the propagation of light in this medium in the long wavelength limit. Under typical conditions, the atoms remain effectively stationary, but acquire momentum. The analytical solution is used to calculate the mechanical contribution to the the signal momentum from the electromagnetic stress tensor, as well as from the Lorentz and Coulomb force. The results agree, but disagree with commonly accepted expressions. Experiments tend to support the conventional values. On the other hand, all calculations in this work are fairly straightforward and, in part, even generic. There remains the challenge to resolve the contradiction. At any rate, a solvable microscopic model still appears to be lacking, and this work is a detailed and clear step in this direction.

Exact solutions of idealized models based on first principles are

\section{The model}

We begin with an elementary derivation of the DDA equations \cite{Draine_1993},
treating atoms as finite-sized particles rather than point masses.
For simplicity we set the speed of light in vacuum to unity ($c=1$).

A simple microscopic model of a dielectric medium consists of atoms
treated as classical oscillators (akin to the Lorentz model) arranged
on a cubic lattice. The atoms have a finite radius $R$. In the limit
$R\rightarrow0$, this reduces to the discrete dipole approximation
\cite{Draine_1988,Draine_1993}, a formalism commonly used to study
light scattering by arbitrarily shaped dust particles. Unlike the
DDA, however, we retain a finite atomic radius to avoid ambiguities.

\subsection{Discrete dipole approximation}

The starting point is the inhomogeneous Helmholtz equation for the
electric field $E$, in SI units

\begin{align}
\left(\nablaˆ{2}+\omegaˆ{2}\right)E_{i} & =\tfrac{1}{\epsilon_{0}}\left(\nabla_{i}\rho-i\omega j_{i}\right).\label{eq:Helmholtz3D}
\end{align}
This equation follows directly from the Maxwell equations assuming
a factor $eˆ{-i\omega t}$ for the fields, the charge density $\rho$
and the current density $j$. We usually omit the factor $eˆ{-i\omega t}$
in the following. The source terms on the r.h.s. of Eq.˜(\ref{eq:Helmholtz3D})
are different from zero only in the polarizable matter, and in general
also depend on the fields.

A kind of decoupling occurs if the atoms have a radius $R\ll a\ll1/\omega$,
where in the vicinity of matter the derivative $\nablaˆ{2}\sim Rˆ{-2}$
dominates the $\omegaˆ{2}$. In other words, the electric field near
the matter is the quasi static \emph{near field}, related to $\rho$
and $j$ by an electrostatic problem. The strategy then is to first
solve the electrostatic problem, express the r.h.s. of the Helmholtz
equation (\ref{eq:Helmholtz3D}) in terms of the local electric field,
and then solve the resulting linear equation in Fourier space.

\subsection{Classical atoms}

As a classical (non-quantum-mechanical) model for an atom we use two
homogeneously charged rigid spheres of radius $R$, one with charge
$-Q$ and another \emph{heavier} sphere with charge $+Q$, which can
interpenetrate each other. Initially the spheres overlap. Rigid spheres
are not compatible with special relativity, however, the involved
speeds are small, and no problems arise. The force acting between
the spheres is due to the electric force attracting the spheres and
an additional potential energy, and can be made harmonic with any
desired force constant by choosing the additional potential energy
appropriately \footnote{Such an atom resembles a dielectric sphere for which, however the
electric susceptibility would be limited by the polarization catastrophe. }.

The negatively charged spheres have negligible mass and follow any
electric field in phase. Under the influence of a local electric field
$Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$ (not including the field of the atom itself) an
atom acquires a dipole moment

\begin{equation}
pˆ{\left(0\right)}=\epsilon_{0}\gamma aˆ{3}Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}=Qb,\label{eq:Dip_Moment}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma$ is the (dimensionless) susceptibility and $b$ the
displacement. We consider the limit of large $Q$ and small $b$.
The displacement $b$ generates a charge density on the surface of
the atom. The corresponding depolarization field within the sphere
has the well known value \cite{Jackson1975}
\begin{equation}
E_{P}=-pˆ{\left(0\right)}/\left(3\epsilon_{0}V_{R}\right),\label{eq:E_Depol}
\end{equation}
where $V_{R}$ is the volume of the sphere. This field generates an
attractive force
\[
F_{P}=E_{P}Q=-Qpˆ{\left(0\right)}/\left(3\epsilon_{0}V_{R}\right)\equiv-\lambda_{P}b,
\]
between the spheres with spring constant $\lambda_{P}=Qˆ{2}/\left(3\epsilon_{0}V_{R}\right).$

The polarization law (\ref{eq:Dip_Moment}), on the other hand, can
be written as $b=\left(\epsilon_{0}\gamma aˆ{3}/Qˆ{2}\right)QEˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$,
and thus implies a spring constant $\lambda_{\mathrm{tot}}=Qˆ{2}/\left(\epsilon_{0}\gamma aˆ{3}\right),$
larger than $\lambda_{P}$ by the factor
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{tot}}/\lambda_{P}=3V_{R}/\left(\gamma aˆ{3}\right).\label{eq:Spring_Ratio}
\end{equation}
In other words, to get a substantial susceptibility $\gamma$ one
needs an additional \emph{repulsive} potential compensating most of
the attractive Coulomb force. This plays a role if one wants to calculate
the energy of the system from the electric and the magnetic fields
alone.

The induced depolarization field (\ref{eq:E_Depol}) contributes to
the electric field in the sphere and thus
\begin{equation}
E\left(x=0\right)=\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma aˆ{3}}{3V_{R}}\right)Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}.\label{eq:E_0_3D}
\end{equation}
This field is much larger than $Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$ for $\gamma=O\left(1\right)$
and $R\ll a$.

\subsection{Electrostatics}

In the limit $R\ll a\ll\lambda$ the charge and current density on
the r.h.s. of Eq.˜(\ref{eq:Helmholtz3D}) can be deduced from the
quasi static limit, that is from the near field equations, where $\nablaˆ{2}\sim Rˆ{-2}$
dominates $\omegaˆ{2}\sim\lambdaˆ{-2}$. We now assume an electric
field with component $E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$ in $x_{2}$-direction.
Charge and current density of the atom at the origin then take the
form

\begin{align}
\rho_{0}\left(x\right) & \cong-\epsilon_{0}\gamma Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\tfrac{aˆ{3}}{V_{R}}\partial_{2}\theta\left(R-\left|x\right|\right),\label{eq:rho_0}\\
jˆ{\left(0\right)}\left(x\right) & =-i\omega\epsilon_{0}\gamma E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\hat{e}_{2}\tfrac{aˆ{3}}{V_{R}}\theta\left(R-\left|x\right|\right)\label{eq:j_0}
\end{align}
where $\theta$ is the step function and $\hat{e}_{2}$ the unit vector
in $x_{2}$ direction.

\subsubsection{Charge and current density}

The total charge and current densities are the superpositions of the
quantities (\ref{eq:rho_0}, \ref{eq:j_0}) for all lattice points
with the additional Bloch factor $eˆ{ikx}$. Of interest are the corresponding
Fourier series

\begin{align}
\rho\left(x\right) & =-i\epsilon_{0}\gamma E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}eˆ{iqx}q_{2}F\left(qR\right),\label{eq:rho_Fourier}\\
j\left(x\right) & =-i\omega\epsilon_{0}\gamma E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\hat{e}_{2}eˆ{ikx}\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}eˆ{iqx}F\left(qR\right),\label{eq:j_Fourier}\\
F\left(s\right) & =3\left(\tfrac{\sin s}{sˆ{3}}-\tfrac{\cos s}{sˆ{2}}\right)=1-sˆ{2}/10+O\left(sˆ{4}\right)...,\label{eq:F_s}
\end{align}
The function $F$ is defined in Eq.˜(\ref{eq:F_s_Def}) in the appendix.
It should be mentioned that simply adding the Bloch factor to Eq.˜(\ref{eq:rho_0},
\ref{eq:j_0}) is only valid in the long wavelength limit. The continuity
equation for $\rho$ and $j$ is not exactly satisfied, there remains
a term of order $O\left(ka\right)$. This term is negligible in the
long wavelength limit.

\subsubsection{Average electric field}

The continuous medium Maxwell equations use the average electric field
$Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}$, which we now want to relate to $Eˆ{loc}$. This
relation also is a direct consequence of the exact solution (\ref{eq:E_x_3D})
of the Helmholtz equation below. It may nevertheless be instructive
to derive the formula in the context of electrostatics.

The field $Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$, responsible for the polarization of
the atom at the origin, does not include the field from the atom itself.
It can be identified with the field generated by the dipoles at $\left|x\right|>\tilde{R}$
where $\tilde{R}$ is some radius much larger than the lattice spacing.
This field $Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}$ essentially is constant in the sphere
$\left|x\right|<\tilde{R}$. The dipoles at $\left|x\right|<\tilde{R}$
do not contribute to the field at the origin according to the usual
Clausius-Mossotti calculation˜\cite{Jackson1975}. However, they
also contribute to the average field. The average electric field in
a sphere with polarization $P$ (\emph{without} a polarizing external
field) is $E=-P/\left(3\epsilon_{0}\right)$ \cite{Jackson1975},
see also the similar Eq˜.(\ref{eq:E_Depol}). Combining the two
contributions and using (\ref{eq:Dip_Moment}) yields

\begin{equation}
Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}=\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}.\label{eq:E_avg}
\end{equation}
For the dipole moment (\ref{eq:Dip_Moment}) this implies $pˆ{\left(0\right)}=\epsilon_{0}Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\gamma aˆ{3}/\left(1-\gamma/3\right)$,
which is the Clausius-Mossotti relation.

\section{Formal solution}

The Bloch condition allows to write the electric field as a Fourier
series,

\begin{equation}
E\left(x\right)=eˆ{ikx}aˆ{-3}\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}E_{q}eˆ{iqx}.\label{eq:E_Fourier}
\end{equation}
The Helmholtz equation (\ref{eq:Helmholtz3D}) can then be solved
by inserting the Fourier series (\ref{eq:rho_Fourier}), (\ref{eq:j_Fourier})
and (\ref{eq:E_Fourier}). The result

\begin{align}
E_{q} & =\gamma aˆ{3}E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}\hat{e}_{2}-\left(q+k\right)q_{2}}{\left(q+k\right)ˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}F\left(qR\right),\label{eq:E_q_3D0}\\
E\left(x\right) & =\gamma E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}eˆ{iqx}\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}\hat{e}_{2}-\left(q+k\right)q_{2}}{\left(q+k\right)ˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}F\left(qR\right),\label{eq:E_x_3D0}
\end{align}
agrees with the DDA solution \cite{Draine_1993}, only the shape
function $F\left(qR\right)$ is new.

\subsection{Dispersion relation}

The dispersion relation can be deduced from a consistency condition:
the field (\ref{eq:E_x_3D0}) at the origin must agree with the field
(\ref{eq:E_0_3D}) at the center of the atom,

\begin{align}
E_{2}\left(0\right)/E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}} & =1-\tfrac{\gamma aˆ{3}}{3V_{R}}=\gamma\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}-\left(q+k\right)_{2}q_{2}}{\left(q+k\right)ˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}F\left(qR\right)\label{eq:DispRel}\\
& \cong\gamma\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}}{kˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\left(\sum_{q}F\left(qR\right)-1\right)=\gamma\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}}{kˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}-\tfrac{\gamma aˆ{3}}{3V_{R}}+\tfrac{\gamma}{3}.\nonumber
\end{align}
In the long wavelength limit $k$ is completely negligible against
any $q\neq$0. The sum $\sum_{q}F\left(qR\right)=aˆ{3}/V_{R}$ directly
follows from the definition (\ref{eq:F_s_Def}). There remains the
DDA result

\begin{align}
\omegaˆ{2} & =kˆ{2}/nˆ{2},\label{eq:Disp_3D}\\
nˆ{2} & =\frac{1+2\gamma/3}{1-\gamma/3}.\label{eq:n2_3D}
\end{align}
The dispersion relation (\ref{eq:DispRel}, \ref{eq:n2_3D}) implies
the Clausius-Mossotti formula (\ref{eq:n2_3D}) for the refractive
index $n$.

\subsection{Electric and magnetic field}

In the $q\neq0$ parts of the electric field (\ref{eq:E_x_3D0}) $q\sim2\pi/a$
dominates $k$ and $\omega$ in the long wave length limit and one
can write

\begin{equation}
E\left(x\right)=E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\left[\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)\hat{e}_{2}-\gamma\sum_{q\neq0}eˆ{iqx}\tfrac{qq_{2}}{qˆ{2}}F\left(qR\right)\right].\label{eq:E_x_3D}
\end{equation}
The $q=0$ part agrees with the average field $Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}$
from Eq.˜(\ref{eq:E_avg}) modulo the Bloch phase.

From Eq.˜(\ref{eq:E_x_3D0}) the magnetic field follows as

\begin{align}
B\left(x\right) & =\tfrac{1}{i\omega}\nabla\times E=\tfrac{1}{\omega}\gamma E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}eˆ{iqx}\tfrac{\omegaˆ{2}\left(k+q\right)\times\hat{e}_{2}}{\left(q+k\right)ˆ{2}-\omegaˆ{2}}F\left(qR\right)\label{eq:B_x_3D}\\
& \cong E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)\tfrac{k\times\hat{e}_{2}}{\omega}=-E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{ikx}n\hat{e}_{1}.\nonumber
\end{align}
Here we have used the dispersion relation (\ref{eq:Disp_3D}, \ref{eq:n2_3D}).

The magnetic field thus is constant apart from the factor $eˆ{ikx}$
in the long wavelength limit. This important fact has a generic explanation:
the fields in the near zone of electric dipole radiation (caused by
the atoms) are dominantly electric in nature˜\cite{Jackson1975}.
A substantial magnetic field only arises in the far zone, the total
magnetic field thus has negligible granular structure.

\subsection{Energy density}

The average energy density due to the electric field (\ref{eq:E_x_3D})
is

\begin{align}
\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{elec}}}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}aˆ{-3}\int_{aˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}x\left|E\right|ˆ{2} & =\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}\left[\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}-\tfrac{\gammaˆ{2}}{3}+\tfrac{\gammaˆ{2}}{3}\tfrac{aˆ{3}}{V_{R}}\right].\label{eq:uˆelec}
\end{align}
The evaluation of the integral is described in appendix \ref{subsec:u_em_calc}.
The contribution proportional to $1/V_{R}$ is due to the electromagnetic
field of the dipole, which agrees with the potential energy of an
oscillator with spring constant $\lambda_{P}$. The actual spring
constant is larger by a factor (\ref{eq:Spring_Ratio}), and the total
average ‘‘electric’’ energy density becomes

\begin{align}
\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{elec,tot}}}= & \tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}nˆ{2}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\right|ˆ{2}nˆ{2}.\label{u_elec_tot}
\end{align}
Here we have used Eq.˜(\ref{eq:n2_3D}) and the expression (\ref{eq:E_avg})
for the average electric field $Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}$. The expression
$\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{e,tot}}}$ in terms of $E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}$
agrees with the electric energy density of the Maxwell equations for
a homogeneous medium with refractive index $n$. This could have been
expected. The medium with waves with a long wavelength locally looks
like the medium in a capacitor, and energy and propagation speed only
depend on the capacitance, not on other details.

The magnetic contribution to the energy density directly follows from
Eq.˜(\ref{eq:B_x_3D}) and has the same value as (\ref{u_elec_tot}),
and thus as expected
\begin{equation}
\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\right|ˆ{2}nˆ{2}.\label{eq:u_tot}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Electromagnetic momentum density}

We now calculate the average of the electromagnetic momentum density
$\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{em}}=-\epsilon_{0}\Re E_{2}\Re B_{1}$ from the
fields (\ref{eq:E_x_3D}, \ref{eq:B_x_3D}). Since $B_{1}=-E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{ikx}n$
is $q$-independent there only contributes $E_{2}ˆ{q=0}\left(x\right)=E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{ikx}$
and thus
\begin{equation}
\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{em}}}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\right|ˆ{2}n.\label{eq:PI_em}
\end{equation}
This implies that the result (\ref{eq:PI_em}) is generic. The electromagnetic
momentum per energy follows as $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{em}}}/\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}=1/n$.

\subsection{Momentum density from stress tensor}

We want to calculate the momentum flow in propagation direction of
the plane wave with the fields (\ref{eq:E_x_3D}, \ref{eq:B_x_3D}),
given by the integral of the stress tensor component
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{33}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left(Eˆ{2}+Bˆ{2}-2E_{3}E_{3}\right)\label{eq:sigma_33_def}
\end{equation}
over a surface perpendicular to the propagation direction. The average
momentum flow is the same for any surface at $x_{3}\in\left[R/2,a-R/2\right]$.
The $q=0$ components of $E$ and $B$ directly give constant contributions
in Eq.˜(\ref{eq:sigma_33_def}), there remains the contribution from
$E_{q\neq0}\left(x\right)$ of the solution Eq.˜(\ref{eq:E_x_3D}).

For simplicity we use $x_{3}=a/2$. Since $x_{3}=a/2$ is far away
from the atoms, one can replace the atoms with point matter, that
is $F\left(qR\right)=1$.

Without the trivial factors $E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}eˆ{ikx}\gamma$ from
Eq.˜(\ref{eq:E_x_3D}) the granular part of the electric field is
\begin{align*}
\tilde{E}_{i}\left(x\right) & =\nabla_{i}\nabla_{2}\sum_{q\neq0}eˆ{iqx}\tfrac{1}{qˆ{2}}.
\end{align*}
One could evaluate this sum with the Ewald method \cite{Born_1954},
but there is a more direct way. The sum over $q_{3}$ can be performed
in closed form. With $Qˆ{2}=q_{1}ˆ{2}+q_{2}ˆ{2}\neq0$ one finds
(see appendix \ref{subsec:Sum_cosh_formula})

\[
\sum_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}\setminus0}eˆ{iqx}\tfrac{1}{qˆ{2}}=\tfrac{aˆ{2}}{4}\sum_{\left\{ q_{1},q_{2}\right\} \in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{2}\setminus0}eˆ{i\left(q_{1}x_{1}+q_{2}x_{2}\right)}\tfrac{\cosh\left(Q\left(\tfrac{a}{2}-x_{3}\right)\right)}{\tfrac{Qa}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Qa}{2}\right)}.
\]
For the plane at $x_{3}=a/2$ it follows

\[
\tilde{E}_{i}\left(x_{1},x_{2},\tfrac{a}{2}\right)=-\left(\delta_{i,1}+\delta_{i,2}\right)\tfrac{aˆ{2}}{4}\sum_{\left\{ q_{1},q_{2}\right\} \in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}\setminus0}eˆ{i\left(q_{1}x_{1}+q_{2}x_{2}\right)}\tfrac{q_{i}q_{2}}{\tfrac{Qa}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Qa}{2}\right)}.
\]
For the average of the stress tensor (\ref{eq:sigma_33_def}) on the
plane we need the constant

\begin{align}
M_{1} & =aˆ{-2}\int_{aˆ{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\left|\tilde{E}\right|ˆ{2}\label{eq:M_Madelung}\\
& =\tfrac{aˆ{2}}{16}\int_{aˆ{2}}\mathrm{d}x_{1}\mathrm{d}x_{2}\sum_{q_{1},q_{2},p_{1},p_{2}}eˆ{i\left(\left(q_{1}-p_{1}\right)x_{1}+\left(q_{2}-p_{2}\right)x_{2}\right)}\tfrac{q_{i}q_{2}}{\tfrac{Qa}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Qa}{2}\right)}\tfrac{p_{i}p_{2}}{\tfrac{Pa}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Pa}{2}\right)}\nonumber \\
& =\tfrac{aˆ{4}}{16}\sum_{\left\{ q_{1},q_{2}\right\} \neq0}\tfrac{\left(q_{1}ˆ{2}+q_{2}ˆ{2}\right)q_{2}ˆ{2}}{\left(\tfrac{Qa}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Qa}{2}\right)\right)ˆ{2}}\cong0.1715990251833.\nonumber
\end{align}
The integral over the area enforces the same wavevectors in the two
$\tilde{E}$ factors, there remains a rapidly converging two dimensionless
sum. The final expression for the stress tensor is

\begin{align}
\overline{\sigma_{33}} & =\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left(\overline{Eˆ{2}+Bˆ{2}}\right)=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}\left(\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}+\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}nˆ{2}+\gammaˆ{2}M_{1}\right).\label{eq:sigma_33}\\
& =\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|E_{2}ˆ{avg}\right|ˆ{2}\left(nˆ{2}+1+M_{1}\left(nˆ{2}-1\right)ˆ{2}\right).\nonumber
\end{align}
The number $M_{1}$ is relatively small and the contribution from
the $q=0$ components of the fields dominates.

We now consider a plane wave with a wave front. Into an undisturbed
interval of length $\ell$ (in a time $\ell n$) flows the momentum
$\sigma_{33}\ell n=\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}\ell$, which
gives the expression for the average total momentum density density
$\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}$ listed in Table˜\ref{tab:MomDensity}.
The mechanical momentum density $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}$,
also listed in the table, follows by subtracting the electromagnetic
momentum density (\ref{eq:PI_em}). The contributions to $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}$
can be divided into a Lorentz force part and a Coulomb force part.

The results are most easily compared with other values as momentum
per energy (\ref{eq:u_tot}), for example $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}/\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}.$
The ratios differ significantly from the usually assumed values. This
is discussed further below.

\subsection{Mechanical momentum from Lorentz force}

To get a more intuitive explanation for the mechanical momentum $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}$
we now calculate the Lorentz force $j\times B$ acting on the atoms
in the front of a light signal. This is not an ad hoc calculation,
the transfer of momentum to the atoms via the Lorentz force is one
of our equations of motion. To allow for the construction of propagating
wave packets we now keep the time dependence $eˆ{-i\omega t}.$

A key observation, not restricted to the DDA model, is that the magnetic
field (\ref{eq:B_x_3D}) contains only a $q=0$ component. This reflects
the fact that magnetic fields arise only in the far-field zone of
the atomic electric dipole radiation \cite{Jackson1975}, and therefore
exhibit negligible spatial granularity within the medium in the long
wavelength limit. As a result, the spatial integral of the Lorentz
force can be evaluated using the average current density,

\[
j_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\epsilon_{0}\left(nˆ{2}-1\right)E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{i\left(kx-t/n\right)}.
\]
Wave packets are superpositions of plane waves with wavevectors centered
around some wavevector $k$. The relevant electric field thus can
be written as

\[
E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{i\left(kx-t/n\right)}\rightarrow g\left(x_{3}-t/n\right)=\int_{k’\cong k}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}k’E_{2,k}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}eˆ{ik’\left(x-t/n\right)}.
\]
Such wave packets essentially are planes wave with a slowly varying
amplitude. The magnetic field (\ref{eq:B_x_3D} becomes

\[
B_{1}=-ng\left(x_{3}-t/n\right).
\]
The average Lorentz force density thus is

\[
\overline{f_{3}}\left(x_{3},t\right)=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left(nˆ{3}-n\right)\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\Re g\left(x_{3}-t/n\right)\right)ˆ{2}.
\]
Integrating over time and averaging locally over space (or time) yields
the corresponding mechanical momentum density
\begin{align}
\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{lorentz}}} & =\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{2}\left(nˆ{3}-n\right)\left.\overline{\left(\Re g\left(x_{3}-t/n\right)\right)ˆ{2}}\right|_{-\infty}ˆ{t}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left(nˆ{3}-n\right)\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\right|ˆ{2},\label{eq:PI_mech_Lorentz}
\end{align}
where we have assumed $g\left(\infty\right)=0$ for a signal with
a wave front. Eq.˜(\ref{eq:PI_mech_Lorentz}) explains the first
part of $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}$ in Table˜\ref{tab:MomDensity}.
The result (\ref{eq:PI_mech_Lorentz}) is generic, it does not depend
on the details of the model.

\subsection{Mechanical momentum from Coulomb force}

At a wave front the dipole moments of the atoms increase, schematically
like $\Uparrow\uparrow\shortuparrow$. This produces an electrostatic
force to the right on the atoms in the wave front. Every atom first
is in an environment with no polarization, then in an environment
with a polarization gradient and then the polarization is constant.

A linear distribution of dipole moments $p\left(x\right)=p_{0}+\left(\Delta p_{0}/\Delta x_{3}\right)x_{3}$
with a constant gradient generates a force in $x_{3}$ direction on
the atom at the origin,

\begin{align}
F_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{coulomb}} & =-\tfrac{p_{0}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{x\in\left(\mathbb{Z}a\right)ˆ{3}\setminus0}p\left(x\right)\left(\tfrac{3}{\left|x\right|ˆ{5}}-\tfrac{15x_{2}ˆ{2}}{\left|x\right|ˆ{7}}\right)x_{3}\Lambda\left(x_{3}\right)=-\tfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}p_{0}\tfrac{\Delta p_{0}}{\Delta x_{3}}aˆ{-3}M_{2},\label{eq:F3_Coul}\\
M_{2} & =\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}ˆ{3}\setminus0}\left(\tfrac{3}{\left|x\right|ˆ{5}}-\tfrac{15x_{2}ˆ{2}}{\left|x\right|ˆ{7}}\right)x_{3}ˆ{2}\Lambda\left(x_{3}\right)\cong2.156377\cong4\pi M_{1}\cong2.1563769475161.\label{eq:M2}
\end{align}
The constant $M_{2}$ is is another dimensionless Madelung constant.
The sum is only conditionally convergent, and it is crucial to use
the right anisotropic cutoff, represented by the function $\Lambda\left(x_{3}\right)=\theta\left(\lambdaˆ{2}-x_{3}ˆ{2}\right)$.
The oscillating amplitude of the dipoles in propagation direction
restricts the sum to a finite slice $-\lambda<x_{3}<\lambda$ (the
wave front extends over many wavelengths). The exact form of the cutoff
function is irrelevant, the sum rapidly converges in $x_{3}$ direction.
There is no such restriction in the transverse direction. The value
given in Eq.˜(\ref{eq:M2}) is the result of an evaluation with $\lambda=4$
on a computer, the (approximate) relation to the Madelung constant
$M_{1}$ (Eq.˜(\ref{eq:M_Madelung})) is as expected. We have not
attempted to strictly prove $M_{2}=4\pi M_{1}$.

The sum over the force (\ref{eq:F3_Coul}) along the signal front
gives the momentum transferred to the atoms,
\[
\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{coulomb}}=-\tfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\sum_{p_{0}}p_{0}\Delta p_{0}aˆ{-4}M_{2}=\tfrac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_{0}}p_{0}ˆ{2}aˆ{-4}M_{2}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{8\pi}\gammaˆ{2}\left|Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}M_{2}.
\]
where we have used Eq.˜(\ref{eq:Dip_Moment}). The average is
\begin{equation}
\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{coulomb}}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\gammaˆ{2}\left|Eˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}M_{1}=\tfrac{\epsilon_{0}}{4}\left|Eˆ{\mathrm{avg}}\right|ˆ{2}M_{1}\left(nˆ{2}-1\right)ˆ{2}n.\label{eq:PI_mech_Coulomb}
\end{equation}
This explains the second part of $\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}$
in Table˜\ref{tab:MomDensity}. The Coulomb contribution to the mechanical
momentum density also does not depend on the details of the model,
and in this sense also seems to be generic. At any rate, the Coulomb
force is smaller than the Lorentz force.

There is a simpler model with polarizable thin layers instead of atoms˜\cite{Dengler2025},
which can be exactly solved with a Bloch Ansatz. The results agree
with Table˜\ref{tab:MomDensity}, except that there is no contribution
from the Coulomb force. This is plausible since no charge accumulates
in the layers when the layers are polarized. What also is missing
is a Clausius-Mossotti relation.

\section{Discussion}

We have found the same mechanical momentum per energy
\begin{equation}
\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{mech}}}/\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(n-1/n+M_{1}\left(nˆ{2}-1\right)ˆ{2}/n\right)\label{eq:PI_mech_u_tot}
\end{equation}
with $M_{1}\cong0.171599$ from two rather different methods. The
conventionally accepted value is $n-1/n$, and there evidently is
a problem.

There is little doubt in the DDA solution (\ref{eq:E_x_3D}, \ref{eq:B_x_3D}).
Neglecting the movement of atoms is well motivated, and the (small)
atom radius $R$ does not enter the result. The electric field constitutes
a complete set of degrees of freedom, the magnetic field follows by
integrating the law of induction over time, the particle momentum
follows by integrating the Lorentz and Coulomb force acting on atoms
over time. The electromagnetic stress tensor in a plane between the
atoms is a well defined and relatively simple quantity.

Is the discrepancy due to the point-like nature of the atoms? There
exists a continuum of models from small $R$ to $R\cong a$, and more
realistic models with some larger $a$ are not at a definite end point
in this continuum. Accordingly, one would not expect something special
like $n-1/n$ in this case. Moreover, the direct calculations of the
Lorentz and Coulomb force acting on the atoms are quite generic, and
do not depend on the DDA details. Somehow these contributions would
have to cancel.

One might question the parameters of the model. We have used a large
charge $Q$ and a small displacement $b$ of the electron cloud, see
Eq.˜(\ref{eq:Dip_Moment}). What if the charge is $Ze$, where $e$
is the electron charge?

The order of magnitude of the displacement is $b\sim aˆ{3}\sqrt{\epsilon_{0}uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}/Qˆ{2}}.$
Using the Bohr radius for $a$ and the energy density $uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}\cong1.4\cdot10ˆ{-4}\unit{N}/\unit{m}ˆ{2}$
from the experiment by Jones and Leslie \cite{Jones_Leslie_1978}
yields $b\sim\unit[10ˆ{-20}]{m}/Z.$ This is a negligible shift, which
has no steric effect anyway if the electron clouds move in unisono.

In quantum mechanics an external electric field translates and \emph{distorts}
an electron cloud, except for an harmonic spherical potential, where
a translation is the exact solution. An induced anisotropy of the
order $b/a\sim10ˆ{-10}$ might have some steric effect for closely
packed atoms.

\subsection{Arguments in favor of the conventional values}

Arguably the most straightforward theoretical argument in favor of
the conventional values is the law of refraction $p_{\Vert}/\left|p\right|=np’_{\Vert}/\left|p’\right|$,
where $p$ is the total momentum of a signal, and the primed values
stand for the medium, the unprimed ones for the vacuum, and an anti-reflective
coating is assumed. If the component $p_{\Vert}$ of momentum parallel
to the boundary is conserved then Snell’s law requires $p’=np.$ The
only weak point in this argument is the constancy of $p_{\Vert}$.
The translational invariance in the direction parallel to boundary
only requires conservation of total momentum in this direction. And
in fact, the Lorentz force acts in propagation direction and has a
component parallel to the boundary.

Another argument relies on the well known quantum mechanical formula
$p=\hbar k$ relating momentum and wave vector of a quantum of a quasiparticle.
Since the wavelength is shorter in a medium by a factor $n$ this
also would lead to $p’=np.$ A problem here is that the wave packets
are not quasiparticles in the usual sense. Because of the displaced
atoms the center of energy moves away from the wave packet. This spoils
the usual connection between translations and momentum and makes the
argument questionable.

This then also applies to interference thought experiments connecting
the width of a slot or the distance between slots to a momentum.

\subsection{Experiments}

A serious challenge comes from experiments which tend to confirm the
conventional values. Instead of providing a summary of experimental
work here we refer to the reviews \cite{Brevik1979,Pfeifer_2007,BarnLoud2010}.
The signal momentum usually is small and results sometimes are controversial.

A unique result for liquids is due to Jones and Leslie \cite{Jones_Leslie_1978}.
The authors describe an elaborate repetition of an experiment by Jones
and Richards \cite{Jones_Rich_1954}, in which a torsion balance with
a mirror is submersed in air or a liquid. A laser ray is asymmetrically
directed to the mirror, and the angular deviation is measured. For
various liquids the authors find a momentum proportional $n$ with
an accuracy of $\unit[0.05]{\%}$.

There is no reason to think that liquids are something special. Furthermore,
for the used signal with a power $15\unit{mw}$ in a cross section
of $\left(\unit[0.6]{mm}\right)ˆ{2}$ the displacement (\ref{eq:Shift_Estimate})
of the atoms clearly is negligible. Independently of the details of
the reflection the DDA model predicts
\begin{align}
\overline{\Pi_{3}ˆ{\mathrm{tot}}}/\overline{uˆ{\mathrm{tot}}} & =\tfrac{1}{2}\left(n+1/n+M_{1}\left(nˆ{2}-1\right)ˆ{2}n\right)\label{eq:result_as_n-1}\\
& =1+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(4M_{1}+1\right)\left(n-1\right)ˆ{2}+O\left((n-1)ˆ{3}\right),\nonumber
\end{align}
in contrast to the experimental result $1+\left(n-1\right)$.

\section{Conclusions}

There is little doubt that some microscopic model can account for
the experimental results, and the author would welcome insights into
what might be missing or incorrect in the DDA model or solution. Could
it be that atoms must ultimately be treated quantum mechanically?

As emphasized above, several features of the DDA solution are quite
universal. The magnetic field originates from the far-zone of electric
dipole radiation, is non-granular, and combines with the universal
(non-granular, average) component of the electric field in both the
Poynting vector and the Lorentz force. Even the Coulomb interactions
between induced dipoles are fairly generic, provided that quadrupole
and higher-order moments are negligible.

It consequently remains to be explained how such contributions to
the mechanical momentum get canceled, leading to the experimentally
confirmed simple result. The most convincing way to resolve the enigma
is in terms of a solvable microscopic model.

\subsection{Conventions}

The conventions for the Fourier transform of periodic functions on
a cubic lattice spacing $a$ are
\[
f\left(x\right)=aˆ{-3}\sum\limits_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}eˆ{iqx}f_{q},\qquad f_{q}=\int_{aˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}xeˆ{-iqx}f\left(x\right).
\]
When using complex values for physical quantities what is meant is
the real part. Averages of sinusoidal bilinear quantities over time
and a cell can be calculated with the formula
\[
aˆ{-3}\int_{aˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{d}x\overline{\Re f\left(x\right)\Re g\left(x\right)}=\tfrac{1}{2}aˆ{-3}\int\mathrm{d}ˆ{d}x\Re\left(fgˆ{*}\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}aˆ{-6}\sum\limits_{q}\Re\left(f_{q}g_{q}ˆ{*}\right).
\]

\subsection{Shape function $F$}

The Fourier transforms of charge and current density contain the function
\begin{equation}
F\left(qR\right)=\tfrac{1}{V_{R}}\int_{\left[-a/2,a/2\right]ˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}xeˆ{-iqx}\theta\left(R-\left|x\right|\right)=3\left(\sin\left(qR\right)-qR\cos\left(qR\right)\right)/\left(qR\right)ˆ{3},\label{eq:F_s_Def}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ denotes the step function. The integral domain has
be chosen symmetrically around the origin.

\subsection{Electric field energy}

\label{subsec:u_em_calc}The average electric energy density (\ref{eq:uˆelec})
contains the integral $aˆ{-3}\int_{aˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}x\left|E\right|ˆ{2}$.
Inserting (\ref{eq:E_x_3D}) leads to
\begin{align*}
aˆ{-3}\int_{aˆ{3}}\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}x\left|E\right|ˆ{2} & =\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}\left(\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}+\gammaˆ{2}\sum\limits_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}\setminus0}\tfrac{q_{2}ˆ{2}}{qˆ{2}}Fˆ{2}\left(qR\right)ˆ{2}\right)=\left|E_{2}ˆ{\mathrm{loc}}\right|ˆ{2}\left(\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma}{3}\right)ˆ{2}-\tfrac{\gammaˆ{2}}{3}+\tfrac{\gammaˆ{2}}{3}\tfrac{aˆ{2}}{V_{R}}\right).
\end{align*}
Here we have used

\[
\sum\limits_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}\setminus0}\tfrac{q_{2}ˆ{2}}{qˆ{2}}Fˆ{2}\left(qR\right)=\tfrac{1}{3}\sum\limits_{q\in\left(2\pi\mathbb{Z}/a\right)ˆ{3}}Fˆ{2}\left(qR\right)-\tfrac{1}{3}=\tfrac{1}{3}\tfrac{aˆ{3}}{V_{R}}-\tfrac{1}{3},
\]
The remaining sum has been performed by inserting Eq.˜(\ref{eq:F_s_Def})
and using $\sum_{q}eˆ{-iqx}=aˆ{3}\sum_{m}\delta\left(x-x_{m}\right)$,
there remains the integral,

\begin{align}
\sum_{q}Fˆ{2}\left(qR\right)= & aˆ{3}V_{R}ˆ{-2}\int\mathrm{d}ˆ{3}x\thetaˆ{2}\left(R-\left|x\right|\right)=aˆ{3}/V_{R}.\label{eq:sum_int_F2}
\end{align}

\subsection{A formula for a sum over wavevectors}

\label{subsec:Sum_cosh_formula}The electromagnetic stress tensor
(\ref{eq:sigma_33}) in a plane between the atoms was evaluated with
the formula

\begin{equation}
f_{Q}\left(z\right)=\sum_{q\in2\pi\mathbb{Z}}\tfrac{eˆ{iqz}}{Qˆ{2}+qˆ{2}}=\tfrac{1}{4}\tfrac{\cosh\left(Q\left(z-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\right)}{\tfrac{Q}{2}\sinh\left(\tfrac{Q}{2}\right)}.\label{eq:sum_cosh}
\end{equation}
Symmetry properties are $f_{Q}\left(z\right)=f_{Q}\left(-z\right)$
and $f_{Q}\left(z\right)=f_{Q}\left(z+1\right)$. The expression written
down is for the interval $0\leq z\leq1$. To verify the formula it
suffices to check that $f_{Q}\left(z\right)$ satisfies the differential
equation

\[
\left(\partial_{z}ˆ{2}-Qˆ{2}\right)f_{Q}\left(z\right)=-\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\delta\left(z-m\right)
\]
and the symmetry conditions. The function $f_{Q}\left(z\right)$ is
a linear combination of the solutions $eˆ{\pm Qz}$ of the homogeneous
differential equation. The inhomogeneous equation is satisfied because
of $f_{Q}’\left(1\right)-f_{Q}’\left(0\right)=1$.
