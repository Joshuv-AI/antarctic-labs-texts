Optimization of Arthroscopic All‐Inside Meniscus Repair With a Probe
Authors: Hui‐Shan Angela Lim, Sir‐Young James Loh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeniscal preservation is essential for knee function and long‐term joint health. All‐inside meniscus repair achieves outcomes comparable to the inside‐out technique, but technical challenges remain, including limited working space, suture cut‐out in degenerative tissue, unfavorable suture angles, and risk of cartilage injury or arthroscope entanglement. The arthroscopic probe is used to assess tear pattern, tissue quality, stability, and working space, guiding portal selection or alternative approaches. Twirling the suture along the probe allows controlled loop positioning and tightening. Two strategies are employed: (1) countering the pulling force with the probe directly on the loop and meniscus, and (2) staged tightening using a short working length when resistance, angulation, or limited space hinders suture pulling. These maneuvers provide tactile feedback, reduce suture loop displacement, and minimize cartilage injury. Probe‐assisted techniques enhance control during loop positioning and incremental tightening, optimize tear margin apposition, and reduce risk of cut‐out in complex or degenerative tears. Incorporating these strategies contributes to successful all‐inside repair, maximizing meniscus preservation and overall surgical outcomes.

The menisci are essential for load distribution, shock absorption, and knee stability. Meniscal surgery remains one of the most frequently performed procedures, accounting for approximately 10% to 20% of all orthopedic surgeries annually. 1 Current knee preservation strategies emphasize repair whenever possible, 2 given the inevitable higher rates of cartilage degeneration and osteoarthritis with a meniscectomy. 3

All‐inside meniscus repair has achieved outcomes comparable to the gold‐standard inside‐out method, with shorter operative times and lower risk of nerve injury. 4 Nevertheless, technical challenges remain, including difficulty positioning and tightening the suture loop in a narrow tibiofemoral compartment, limited access for tears extending into the anterior meniscus, suture cut‐out in degenerative meniscal tissue, iatrogenic cartilage injury, and suture entanglement. 5 , 6

The arthroscopic probe, traditionally used to confirm tears, evaluate configuration and tissue quality, reduce displaced fragments, and assess repair stability, can also enhance repair. Twirling the suture along the probe redirects pulling forces, improving control of loop positioning and tension while reducing risks of displacement, cut‐out, cartilage injury, and entanglement with the arthroscope. Incorporating these probe‐assisted maneuvers contributes to the success of all‐inside meniscus repair.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The technique is described on a left knee with a complete medial meniscus tear involving the posterior horn and body in a middle‐aged patient (Video 1). The patient lies supine under general anesthesia with thigh tourniquet and contralateral calf pump. Standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals are created and alternated as working and viewing portals.

The torn edges of the meniscus are palpated with the probe to assess pattern, location, tissue quality, and stability, guiding preservation versus debridement (Figure 1). Unsalvageable fragments are excised and edges debrided and freshened. The probe is placed on the tear to determine the optimal direction for the all‐inside repair device (Fastfix Flex, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) (Figure 2). The accessibility of the tear to the repair instrument via the 2 standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals is compared. An accessory portal is made when the existing portals provide limited access and affects the placement of the suture anchors. The probe tip is used to gauge the working space to determine the accessibility of the repair instrument (Figure 3). The repair instrument (Fastfix Flex, Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is introduced with a half‐slotted cannula under direct visualization. The penetration depth required is determined based on the tear configuration assessed with the meniscal probe (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA). The Fastfix Flex device depth limiter is then adjusted to either 12, 16, or 20 mm maximum to prevent it from penetrating out of the skin and protect the posterior neurovascular bundle. The 2 anchors are directed and deployed perpendicular to the tear so that the suture loop is optimally positioned on the tear. If the meniscus is friable, the anchors are deployed superior and inferior to the meniscus to encompass its circumference to reduce the risk of cut‐out.

The suture is twirled on the probe several times, in either a clockwise or counterclockwise direction, so that when the suture is tensioned, it does not lift off from the probe. The twirling direction is such that the suture is posterior or below the probe tip from the surgeon's point of view. The suture loop is shifted to the most optimal position with the probe. When the suture is pulled, the probe tip counters the pulling force by pressing gently on the suture loop adjacent to the knot (Figure 3). The suture is seen moving up the length of the probe as it is pulled, with the suture loop tightening simultaneously. Once significant resistance is encountered during tightening, the probe tip is withdrawn from the suture knot, leaving approximately 10 mm of working suture between the probe tip and the suture loop. The short working length of suture is maintained as the probe pulls on the suture loop to position it optimally on the tear. The suture loop is subsequently tightened by pulling both the probe and suture in unison, restoring the short working length after each pull, until the suture loop is firmly tightened over the tear (Figure 4). By ensuring the suture is twirled along the length of the probe during tensioning, injury to the adjacent femoral condyle cartilage is avoided. The inner edge of the meniscus is reduced onto the tibial articular cartilage as the suture loop tightens. The stability of the repair is checked with the probe. Once stability of the repair is confirmed, the sutures are cut with a suture cutter. This process is repeated on the remaining length of the tear according to its pattern configuration and extent. When the tear extends anteriorly on the meniscal body and the optimally positioned suture loop is at an angle to the pulling force of the suture, the probe tip is used to redirect the line of pull to prevent displacement of the suture loop (Figure 5).

Portals are closed with nonabsorbable sutures. The knee is braced at 30° flexion and the distal neurovascular status are timely checked.

The surgery requires less than a 24‐hour inpatient stay. Patients with isolated meniscal repair mobilize non–weight‐bearing for 4 weeks, then partial for 2, progressing to full at 6 weeks. Range of motion is 0°‐90° for 4 weeks and then unrestricted. Protocols are modified for combined procedures.

DISCUSSION

With the evolution of all‐inside meniscus repair implant and instruments, its outcomes are comparable to the inside‐out repair in the long term. 4 Challenges remain, including positioning and tightening the suture loop within a confined space, suture cut‐out in degenerative meniscal tissue, pulling sutures at unfavorable angles, inadvertent femoral condyle cartilage injury, and suture entanglement with the arthroscope. Tips and Tricks

The probe is routinely used to measure as well as mobilize the torn meniscal margins and fragments (Figure 1). It also provides tactile feedback on meniscal tissue quality, particularly important in degenerative tears due to the higher cut‐out risk compared with acute tears in younger patients. These arthroscopic findings supplement and confirm magnetic resonance imaging scan findings.

Placing the probe tip on the tear allows visualization of the optimal directional approach for all‐inside repair (Figure 2). This helps determine whether to use an existing portal, create a new one, or employ an outside‐in technique for tears extending to the anterior meniscus.

A free length of suture may abrade the cartilage of the adjacent femoral condyle when tensioned upward (Figure 3). Twirling the suture onto the probe prevents this and also avoids entanglement with the arthroscope.

The probe tip enables quick estimation of the working space in the narrow tibiofemoral joint and determines whether the tear is accessible to the repair instrument (Figure 6). It needs to be borne in mind that the probe is rigid, with its tip perpendicular to its shaft, enabling access to areas that may be challenging for the malleable all‐inside device. Attempting to navigate the repair instrument through tight spaces can lead to kinking or device malfunction.

The suture is twirled onto the length of the probe, in a clockwise or counterclockwise direction, positioning it posteriorly or beneath the probe to enhance visualization during loop placement and tightening. Twirling the suture also shortens its working length, improving control over both positioning and tension during loop tightening.

Once the suture loop is optimally positioned on the tear, preventing displacement during tightening is critical. When performing an all‐inside repair on an anterior meniscal body tear, angulation of the pulling suture relative to the loop can cause the loop to shift, resulting in an obliquely oriented, unstable repair. The probe tip acts as a post to redirect the pulling force on the loop (Figure 5), allowing secure tightening without displacement.

There are 2 methods to tighten the suture loop. The first places the probe tip on the loop and adjacent meniscus, countering the pulling force as the suture slides up the probe. The tactile feedback from the probe regulates tension, reducing cut‐out and displacement.

However, there are occasions where there is significant resistance during loop tightening, there is angulation of the pulling suture relative to the loop, or limited working space may prevent placement of the probe tip directly on the loop. In such cases, a second method can be employed. The suture is secured on the probe, leaving a short working length of approximately 10 mm between the probe tip and suture loop. The probe and suture are pulled in unison over short distances to incrementally tighten the loop. The probe is subsequently repositioned to maintain this working length, and the process is repeated. This staged tightening provides better control and reduces the risk of suture cut‐out or loop displacement compared with a forceful, single‐step tightening. In addition, the suture loop tension can be assessed in intervals, monitoring for under‐ or overtightening. Undertightening due to unintended oblique position of the suture loop on the tear results in loss of apposition of the tear margins while overtightening results in distortion of the tear and buckling.

In conclusion, the probe remains indispensable in meniscal surgery, not only for assessment but also for optimizing all‐inside repair. Twirling sutures around the probe enhances loop positioning and tightening, prevents cartilage injury and entanglement, and stabilizes repairs, particularly in complex and degenerative tears. These maneuvers improve outcomes and support meniscus preservation.
