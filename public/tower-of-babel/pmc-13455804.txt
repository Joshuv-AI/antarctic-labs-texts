Psychometric Validation of the Persian Johns Hopkins Learning Environment Scale: A Cross-Sectional Validation Study Among Iranian Medical Students
Authors: Ali Asghar Hayat, Ziba Homayoon, Soolmaz Zare, Mitra Amini, Elham Navipour, Farzad Abaszadeh, Mobin Mottahedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA valid assessment of the learning environment is essential for improving the quality of medical education and supporting students’ academic and professional development. Although the Johns Hopkins Learning Environment Scale (JHLES) has demonstrated promising psychometric performance in several countries, no validated Persian version has been available. This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian JHLES among Iranian medical students.MethodsThis methodological cross-sectional validation study included 350 medical students from Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The JHLES was translated and culturally adapted using a standardized forward-backward translation process. Face, content, construct, convergent, and discriminant validity, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and measurement invariance were evaluated using established psychometric methods.ResultsThe Persian JHLES demonstrated good evidence of face and content validity (S-CVI/Ave = 0.96). Exploratory factor analysis supported a seven-factor structure explaining 70.7% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated acceptable model fit (χ2/df = 1.89, RMSEA = 0.048, CFI = 0.95, TLI = 0.94). Convergent and discriminant validity were satisfactory (CR = 0.81–0.91; AVE = 0.55–0.68; HTMT < 0.75). Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81–0.93) and test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.83–0.91) were also satisfactory.ConclusionThe findings provide evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the Persian JHLES in this sample of Iranian medical students. The instrument may be useful for evaluating learning environments and informing quality improvement initiatives within similar educational settings. Further multicentre validation across diverse Iranian medical schools is warranted before broader national implementation.Keywords: learning environment, medical education, Johns Hopkins learning environment scale, psychometric validation, Persian version

Introduction

A medical school’s learning environment is a critical determinant of how students think, behave, and ultimately develop as future physicians.1,2 Far beyond its physical or administrative components, the learning environment encompasses the interpersonal, cultural, and organizational conditions that shape motivation, well-being, and professional identity formation.3,4 Evidence from medical education research consistently demonstrates that supportive learning environments foster deeper engagement, strengthen professional values, and protect students against burnout.5,6 This is particularly important given that the global prevalence of burnout among medical students ranges from 7% to 75.2%, with an overall average of 37.23%.7 Conversely, unsupportive learning environments have been associated with reduced academic engagement, lower empathy, and poorer educational outcomes.8 As professional identity formation has become a central pillar of competency-based medical education, the valid assessment of the learning environment has assumed increasing importance.9

International accrediting bodies, including the Liaison Committee on Medical Education, emphasize systematic monitoring of the learning environment as an essential component of educational quality assurance.10 Among the available instruments, the Dundee Ready Educational Environment Measure (DREEM) has been the most widely used to assess learning environments in medical education.11,12

However, despite its widespread adoption, several studies have identified limitations in its ability to comprehensively capture contemporary dimensions of the learning environment, particularly domains related to mentoring, inclusion, students’ sense of community, and personal development.13,14 While DREEM remains a valuable instrument, these limitations have encouraged the development of more comprehensive and theory-informed measures that better reflect current educational frameworks and learners’ experiences.

The Johns Hopkins Learning Environment Scale (JHLES) was developed to address these gaps by providing a multidimensional assessment of the learning environment that includes peer relationships, faculty interactions, mentoring, inclusion and safety, meaningful engagement, and physical learning spaces, while being informed by contemporary theories of medical education and professional identity formation.15 The instrument has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric performance across diverse educational settings.5,13,16,17 Although learning-environment research has been conducted in Iran, available studies have relied predominantly on the Persian version of the DREEM,18,19 while no validated Persian version of the JHLES has been available. Therefore, cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the JHLES are necessary before its application in research and educational evaluation within Iranian medical schools.

This study therefore aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the JHLES among Iranian medical students. By providing evidence supporting its validity and reliability in this sample, the study seeks to facilitate the assessment of learning environments, inform educational quality improvement initiatives, and provide a foundation for future multicentre validation in Iranian medical schools.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

A methodological cross-sectional study was conducted to translate, culturally adapt, and psychometrically validate the Persian version of the JHLES. The study was carried out at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (SUMS), a major tertiary academic center in southern Iran.

Participants and Sampling

The target population consisted of medical students from both preclinical and clinical phases. Inclusion criteria included enrollment in the medical program, willingness to provide informed consent, and sufficient Persian proficiency. Exclusion criteria were: (1) over 20% missing responses, (2) straight-lining or patterned responses (eg, identical answers across >80% of items), and (3) logically inconsistent answering patterns detected during data screening. A convenience sampling approach, commonly used in psychometric validation studies, was applied. To ensure adequate sample size for both exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) while accounting for potential exclusions, a total of 350 students were recruited. The final sample was randomly divided into two independent subsamples: Sample A (n = 200) for EFA and Sample B (n = 150) for CFA. Although the CFA subsample meets the minimum recommended ratio (approximately 5 participants per item), it is considered relatively modest for a complex seven-factor model. Post-hoc power analysis using Monte Carlo simulation indicated adequate statistical power (>0.80) for detecting moderate factor loadings.20 This split allowed independent examination of the factor structure while maintaining sufficient sample size for each analysis. Additionally, 50 students were randomly selected from the full sample to complete the questionnaire again after a two-week interval for test–retest reliability assessment. A sample size of 50 aligns with the COSMIN recommendations for assessing temporal stability.21

Instrument

The JHLES is a 28-item self-report instrument assessing medical students’ perceptions of the learning environment across seven subscales: Community of Peers (6 items), Faculty Relationships (6 items), Academic Climate (5 items), Meaningful Engagement (4 items), Mentoring (2 items), Inclusion and Safety (3 items), and Physical Space (2 items).15 Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1–5),22 yielding a total score of 28–140.23 Items in the Inclusion and Safety domain are reverse-coded according to the original scoring guidelines. Subscale scores were computed by averaging their respective items.15 The original development study demonstrated a stable seven-factor structure using exploratory factor analysis and showed acceptable internal consistency for all subscales.15

Translation and Cross-Cultural Adaptation

A rigorous translation and cross-cultural adaptation protocol was followed based on the guidelines of Beaton et al24 and the World Health Organization (WHO).25 The process included the following steps:

Forward Translation

Two independent bilingual translators whose native language was Persian translated the original English version into Persian. One translator was a medical education faculty member familiar with the concepts underlying the instrument, while the other was a professional translator without a medical background to ensure both conceptual accuracy and natural language use.

Synthesis (Reconciliation)

The two forward translations were compared item by item by the research team. Discrepancies were systematically discussed with both translators, and consensus was reached through reconciliation meetings to produce a single synthesized Persian version. Particular attention was paid to preserving conceptual rather than literal equivalence.

Back-Translation

The reconciled Persian version was independently back-translated into English by two bilingual translators whose native language was English and who had no prior knowledge of the original JHLES or the study objectives. This procedure was performed to identify potential inconsistencies and verify conceptual equivalence with the source instrument.

Expert Committee Review

A multidisciplinary expert panel consisting of five medical education specialists, four psychometricians/methodologists, two linguists, and two translators reviewed all versions. Semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence were systematically evaluated. Disagreements between translators and experts were resolved through structured consensus meetings. Each discrepant item was discussed until agreement was achieved among at least 80% of the panel members. When consensus was not initially reached, the wording was revised based on committee recommendations and re-evaluated in a subsequent round.

Cognitive Debriefing

The pre-final Persian version was pilot-tested with 15 medical students representing both preclinical and clinical phases. Participants completed structured cognitive interviews and were asked whether each item was understandable, culturally appropriate, relevant to their educational experience, and reflected the intended meaning of the original instrument. Feedback identified only minor wording issues, resulting in small linguistic refinements without altering the underlying constructs.

Beyond linguistic and semantic equivalence, particular attention was paid to conceptual and cultural equivalence within the Iranian medical education context. The expert committee and cognitive debriefing explicitly considered the hierarchical structure of Iranian medical training and the relatively high power distance between students and faculty. Students were asked targeted questions regarding the relevance of items related to “Mentoring,” “Inclusion and Safety,” faculty–student relationships, and psychological safety. Feedback from both the expert panel and students confirmed that the core constructs of the JHLES were relevant in the Iranian context. Minor wording modifications included simplifying several expressions describing faculty–student interactions and replacing culturally unfamiliar expressions with more natural Persian equivalents while preserving the original concepts. No major conceptual modifications were deemed necessary.

The finalized Persian version of the instrument is provided in Supplementary File S1.

Validity Assessment

Face and Content Validity

Face validity was assessed both qualitatively and quantitatively. For qualitative face validity, 15 medical students evaluated the clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of the items, which led to minor refinements. Quantitative face validity was examined using the item Impact Score, with all items exceeding the minimum acceptable threshold of 1.5.26 Content validity was evaluated by an expert panel of 15 specialists. The panel consisted of diverse experts: five medical education experts, four psychometricians, three clinical faculty members, and three recent graduates. Following Lawshe’s method, the Content Validity Ratio (CVR) was calculated for each item, with all values exceeding the minimum acceptable value of 0.49 for 15 experts.27 The Content Validity Index was computed at both the item level (I-CVI ≥ 0.78) and scale level (S-CVI/Ave = 0.96, exceeding the 0.90 threshold), consistent with established psychometric standards.28,29 Qualitative feedback from both the expert panel and students was thematically analyzed. This process resulted in minor rewording of three items to enhance clarity and cultural relevance, without altering the underlying constructs.

Construct Validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis

Exploratory Factor Analysis was conducted on Sample A (n = 200) using principal axis factoring. Sampling adequacy was verified through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) index (KMO = 0.94) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2 = 3850.21, p <0.001), both of which confirmed excellent suitability for factor analysis. Although the original JHLES development study used Varimax rotation, both orthogonal (Varimax) and oblique (Promax) rotations were performed due to the theoretically expected correlations between the learning environment subscales (inter-factor correlations ranged from 0.39 to 0.63 in the current study). The Promax solution was considered more theoretically appropriate. A seven-factor solution emerged, explaining 70.7% of the total variance. All 28 items loaded saliently (≥ 0.55) on their hypothesized factors. Only moderate cross-loadings were observed between conceptually related subscales (eg, Faculty Relationships and Academic Climate). The detailed factor loading matrix (Pattern Matrix from Promax rotation) is presented in the Supplementary File S2. Factors with eigenvalues >1 were retained.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis was conducted on Sample B (n = 150) using Robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) as the primary estimator. Because the JHLES items were measured using five ordered response categories and the observed item distributions approximated continuous variables, MLR was considered appropriate despite the ordinal nature of the data. Although Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) is frequently recommended for ordinal indicators, MLR provides robust parameter estimates under mild departures from multivariate normality and facilitates the estimation of composite reliability, latent correlations, and measurement invariance. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, the CFA was repeated using WLSMV as a sensitivity analysis. The hypothesized seven-factor structure was tested. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: χ2/df < 3.0, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.08, CFI and TLI > 0.95. Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis was performed to test measurement invariance across gender and academic phase (configural, metric, and scalar invariance).

Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Convergent validity was supported by composite reliability (CR) values above 0.81 and average variance extracted (AVE) values ≥ 0.55 for all subscales. Although Meaningful Engagement demonstrated the lowest AVE among subscales (0.55), it exceeded the recommended threshold. Discriminant validity was confirmed using the Fornell-Larcker criterion and further supported by Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratios below 0.85 (all values < 0.75).

Reliability Assessment

Test–retest reliability was evaluated using Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement in a subsample of 50 students over a two-week interval. This interval was selected as an optimal balance between minimizing memory effects and avoiding substantial changes in students’ perceptions of the learning environment, consistent with previous validation studies of similar instruments. A sample size of 50 is in line with COSMIN recommendations for assessing temporal stability.

Data Analysis

Data were screened for missing values, patterned responses, logical inconsistencies, and multivariate outliers using Mahalanobis distance (p <0.001). Cases with more than 20% missing responses were excluded. For cases with ≤20% missing data, the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm was used for imputation after confirming that the data were missing completely at random (Little’s MCAR test, p >0.05). Univariate normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics, with values within ±2 considered acceptable. Multivariate normality was evaluated using Mardia’s coefficients of multivariate skewness and kurtosis. Although mild deviations from multivariate normality were observed, all univariate skewness and kurtosis values remained within acceptable limits, supporting the use of maximum likelihood estimation. To ensure robustness, sensitivity analyses were conducted using both Robust Maximum Likelihood (MLR) and WLSMV estimators, which are recommended for ordinal (Likert-type) data. For the exploratory factor analysis, the number of factors to retain was determined using multiple complementary criteria, including the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues >1), visual inspection of the scree plot, Horn’s parallel analysis based on 1000 randomly generated datasets, and the theoretical interpretability of the factor solution. Descriptive analyses and exploratory factor analysis were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26, whereas confirmatory factor analysis and measurement invariance testing were performed using Mplus version 8.4.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Recruitment

A total of 350 medical students participated in this study, yielding a response rate of 87.5% (350/400). As shown in Table 1, the sample included 189 females (54.0%) and 161 males (46.0%). The majority of participants were in the clinical phase (n=235, 67.1%), with a mean age of 23.8 ± 1.9 years. The sample demonstrated adequate heterogeneity in terms of gender and academic stage. Comprehensive data screening confirmed that no participant exceeded the 20% missing data threshold. Overall missing responses represented only 0.4% of all observations. Little’s MCAR test indicated that the missing data were completely at random (p >0.05); therefore, missing values (≤20% per case) were imputed using the EM algorithm. No cases were excluded because of patterned responses, logical inconsistencies, or multivariate outliers.

The translation and cultural adaptation process followed Beaton et al24 and WHO guidelines.25 Feedback from the expert committee and cognitive debriefing with 15 medical students confirmed satisfactory semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence. Targeted probing questions indicated that the core constructs were relevant in the Iranian medical education context. Only minor wording adjustments were made; no major conceptual changes were required.

Face validity was confirmed both qualitatively and quantitatively. All 28 items received Impact Scores above the 1.5 threshold (range: 2.1–4.5). Construct Validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis

EFA was performed on Sample A (n = 200). The KMO measure was 0.94 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2 = 3850.21, p <0.001), indicating excellent factorability. A seven-factor solution explaining 70.7% of the total variance was extracted. Although the original JHLES study used Varimax rotation, both Varimax and Promax rotations were examined. The Promax (oblique) solution was deemed more appropriate given the inter-factor correlations observed. All 28 items loaded saliently (≥ 0.55) on their hypothesized factors, with only moderate cross-loadings between conceptually related subscales (see Table 3 for a summary and Supplementary File S2, for the detailed pattern matrix). The corresponding inter-factor correlation matrix obtained from the Promax-rotated solution is provided in the Supplementary File S3). The scree plot supported the seven-factor structure. To further verify the number of factors to retain, Horn’s parallel analysis was performed using 1000 randomly generated datasets with the same sample size (n = 200) and number of variables.28 The observed eigenvalues obtained from the principal axis factoring solution were compared with the corresponding 95th percentile random eigenvalues. As presented in the Supplementary File S4 and Figure 1, the first seven empirical eigenvalues exceeded their corresponding random eigenvalues, whereas the eighth empirical eigenvalue (0.92) was lower than the corresponding random eigenvalue (1.33). These findings provided additional support for retaining the seven-factor solution and were fully consistent with the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues >1), the scree plot, and the theoretical structure of the original JHLES.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses using WLSMV estimator yielded highly consistent results (RMSEA = 0.051, CFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.042). Factor loadings remained stable (difference < 0.04 across all items), confirming the robustness of the seven-factor structure regardless of the estimation method.

Configural, metric, and scalar invariance were fully supported across gender. For academic phase, configural and metric invariance were supported, whereas partial scalar invariance was achieved after freeing four non-invariant item intercepts. These findings indicate that the Persian JHLES performs equivalently across the examined subgroups.

Reliability

The total scale and all subscales demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Table 7). Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.81 to 0.89 for the subscales and was 0.93 for the total scale. McDonald’s ω values were similarly high, ranging from 0.82 to 0.94. For the two-item subscales (Mentoring and Physical Space), average inter-item correlations were 0.71 and 0.68, respectively, indicating good item homogeneity. Test–retest reliability was assessed in a subsample of 50 students over a two-week interval. Excellent temporal stability was observed, with ICC values ranging from 0.83 to 0.91 (Table 6). Although the sample size for test-retest assessment is at the lower end of recommendations, it satisfies the minimum standards suggested by COSMIN guidelines. Furthermore, measurement error was quantified using the Standard Error of Measurement (SEM) and the Smallest Detectable Change (SDC). The SEM for the total scale was 4.1, with an SDC of 11.4, indicating acceptable precision of the instrument scores.

Descriptive Statistics and Scale Performance

Descriptive statistics for the total scale and all subscales are presented in Table 8. The mean total JHLES score was 110.5 (SD = 15.2), with observed scores ranging from 62 to 138 (theoretical range: 28–140). Among the seven subscales, Community of Peers showed the highest mean score (4.18 ± 0.62), whereas Mentoring had the lowest (3.62 ± 0.75). Skewness and kurtosis values for all subscales were within ±2, indicating acceptable univariate normality. Floor effects were negligible (0.0–1.1%), whereas ceiling effects ranged from 2.0% to 4.3%, remaining well below the commonly accepted 15% threshold for acceptable measurement performance.

Discussion

Summary of Principal Findings

The present study translated, culturally adapted, and evaluated the psychometric properties of the Persian version of the JHLES among medical students at Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The findings provide evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the instrument within this sample. The Persian JHLES demonstrated satisfactory content validity, a stable seven-factor structure, acceptable model fit, strong internal consistency, and high test–retest reliability. Overall, students reported generally positive perceptions of their learning environment. Floor effects were negligible across all domains, while modest ceiling effects were observed in several subscales, suggesting that although the instrument showed good discriminatory performance overall, highly positive perceptions in some domains may have slightly limited its ability to differentiate among students at the upper end of the scale.

Comparison with Other Studies

The psychometric findings of the present study are broadly consistent with those reported in previous international validation studies of the JHLES. The seven-factor structure identified in the original instrument developed by Shochet et al15 was retained in the Persian version, supporting the conceptual stability of the scale across different cultural and educational settings. Likewise, the observed levels of internal consistency and model fit were comparable with those reported in Polish,13 Brazilian,17 and Colombian30 validation studies. The consistency of these findings across culturally diverse populations suggests that the core domains measured by the JHLES, including peer relationships, faculty support, academic climate, mentoring, inclusion, and personal engagement, represent broadly applicable constructs of the medical school learning environment. At the same time, the need for only minor wording modifications during cultural adaptation indicates that the conceptual content of the instrument was largely transferable while requiring limited contextual refinement to improve clarity and cultural appropriateness.

Although the overall psychometric performance of the Persian JHLES was comparable to previous adaptations, some differences should be interpreted in light of methodological and contextual variations. For example, the proportion of explained variance observed in the present study was slightly higher than that reported in the Brazilian adaptation, whereas confirmatory model fit indices were generally comparable to those reported in the Polish validation. Such differences are expected in cross-cultural validation studies because sampling characteristics, educational systems, translation procedures, and analytical approaches may influence psychometric estimates without necessarily indicating meaningful differences in the underlying construct. Collectively, these findings provide further evidence supporting the cross-cultural applicability of the JHLES while highlighting the importance of validating the instrument within each educational context before widespread implementation.

An important finding of the present study was that the Mentoring subscale received the lowest mean score among the seven domains. Although the average score remained above the midpoint of the scale, it was consistently lower than scores for peer relationships, inclusion and safety, and faculty relationships. Mentoring is increasingly recognized as a key component of supportive learning environments because effective mentoring contributes to professional identity formation, academic development, career guidance, and psychological well-being. The comparatively lower score observed in this domain may therefore reflect opportunities to strengthen structured mentoring systems within Iranian medical schools. Possible explanations include limited availability of formal mentoring programs, high faculty workload, or the hierarchical nature of clinical education, all of which may reduce opportunities for sustained faculty–student interaction. Future qualitative and multicentre studies are warranted to determine whether this finding represents a broader feature of Iranian medical education or reflects institution-specific characteristics.

The present findings should also be interpreted alongside evidence from larger multicentre studies. Shahzad and Wajid31 evaluated the JHLES among 3400 students from six public and private medical colleges in Pakistan and demonstrated that institutional characteristics were associated with differences in students’ perceptions of the learning environment, despite a common national accreditation framework. Similarly, Huang et al32 examined 10,901 medical students across 12 Chinese universities and found that perceptions of the learning environment varied significantly across academic years, with students in the middle years reporting less favourable learning environments than first-year students. These studies illustrate that institutional context and stage of training can meaningfully influence JHLES scores. Compared with these large multicentre investigations, the present study provides evidence supporting the psychometric performance of the Persian JHLES within a single institutional context. Consequently, although the findings are consistent with previous international research, they should not be interpreted as establishing national generalizability. Additional multicentre validation across medical schools with diverse educational settings will be essential before broader implementation of the Persian JHLES throughout Iran.

Overall, the findings extend the existing international evidence on the JHLES by demonstrating that its conceptual framework and psychometric performance can be successfully adapted to Persian-speaking medical students. At the same time, the observed contextual differences, particularly in mentoring and the potential influence of institutional characteristics, underscore the importance of evaluating learning environments within their specific educational and cultural settings. Such context-sensitive assessment may better inform targeted educational quality improvement initiatives than relying solely on comparisons of overall scores across institutions or countries.

Strengths and Limitations

The present study has several methodological strengths. The translation and cross-cultural adaptation process followed established recommendations proposed by Beaton et al24 and the World Health Organization.25 A split-sample design was used to separately conduct exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses, and the psychometric evaluation included comprehensive assessments of validity, reliability, and measurement invariance in accordance with COSMIN recommendations. In addition, the high response rate (87.5%) and inclusion of students from different academic years strengthened the internal validity of the findings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, participants were recruited using convenience sampling from a single medical school, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other Iranian medical schools with different educational environments, institutional characteristics, and student populations. This limitation is particularly relevant for psychometric validation studies, where evidence from multiple institutions is generally recommended to establish broader applicability. Compared with large multicentre validation studies, such as those conducted by Huang et al32 across 12 Chinese medical schools (n = 10,901) and by Shahzad and Wajid23 in Pakistan (n = 3400), the present findings should be considered preliminary and require confirmation in more diverse educational settings. Furthermore, measurement invariance across institutions could not be examined because data were collected from a single university. Finally, criterion validity against established learning-environment instruments, such as the DREEM, and responsiveness to educational interventions were not evaluated. Future multicentre studies should address these limitations to strengthen the generalizability and practical applicability of the Persian JHLES.

Implications for Practice and Policy

The Persian JHLES provides educators and academic leaders with a culturally adapted instrument for assessing students’ perceptions of the learning environment. Its application may facilitate the identification of strengths and areas requiring improvement, particularly in domains such as mentoring and physical learning space. At the institutional level, the instrument may support educational quality improvement initiatives and complement existing quality-assurance processes. However, broader implementation across Iranian medical schools should be supported by additional multicentre validation.

Future Research Directions

Future research should focus on multicentre validation involving medical schools from different geographical regions and educational contexts across Iran to establish the generalizability of the Persian JHLES. Longitudinal studies are also warranted to examine the instrument’s responsiveness to changes in learning environments over time and across transitions from preclinical to clinical training. Comparative studies with established instruments, such as the DREEM, would further clarify the complementary strengths of the JHLES, while investigations of predictive validity using educational and well-being outcomes would provide additional evidence supporting its use. Exploring faculty perspectives through parallel adaptations may also contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of the medical school learning environment.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence supporting the validity and reliability of the Persian version of the Johns Hopkins Learning Environment Scale among medical students in this sample. The findings suggest that the instrument demonstrates satisfactory psychometric performance and may serve as a useful tool for assessing learning environments within similar educational settings. Nevertheless, given the single-centre design and convenience sampling, further multicentre validation across diverse Iranian medical schools is required before broader national implementation. Such studies will help establish the generalizability of the instrument and further support its application in educational evaluation, quality improvement, and future longitudinal research.
