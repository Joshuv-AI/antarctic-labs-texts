Controlled-Release Experiment to Optimize Emission Quantification of H2 Point Sources
Authors: Iris M. Westra, Hubertus A. Scheeren, Mareen J. Penninga, Steven M.A.C. van Heuven, Harro A.J. Meijer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global energy transition is expected to increase atmospheric hydrogen concentrations through fugitive emissions during production, transport, storage, and use, with loss rates potentially reaching up to 10% of total hydrogen production. This is of concern since atmospheric H2 oxidation lengthens methane’s lifetime, enhances tropospheric ozone, and increases stratospheric water vapor, making hydrogen an indirect greenhouse gas. Until recently, climate-relevant H2 emissions, producing downwind enhancements below 1 μmol mol–1 (ppm), were undetectable due to the lack of sensitive measurement techniques. Using our multiplatform active AirCore sampler with a newly developed high-resolution Agilent 8890 GC-PDHID system capable of simultaneously measuring H2 (±2 ppb), CH4 (±0.5 ppb), and CO2 (±0.3 ppm), we built upon our previous work and further optimize our sampling and emission estimation method through controlled H2 release experiments using an 8 kW electrolyzer emitting 1.1 ± 0.1 m3 h–1 (1.65 ± 0.15 g min–1 under standard atmospheric conditions). We investigate the role of different sampling conditions (i.e. sampling duration and plume coverage) on the accuracy of emission quantification. From a total of 14 downwind profiles collected using both UAV and ground-based sampling, we derived a weighted mean hydrogen emission rate of 0.94 ± 0.06 m3 h–1, with uncertainty driven by plume coverage, data resolution and spacing, and wind variability. The observed H2 plume behavior closely matches that of tracers such as CH4, indicating that established methane measurement strategies are transferable to hydrogen point sources. Our work facilitates future H2 emission measurements by demonstrating the versatility and accessibility of our proposed measurement method.

Introduction

Currently, more than 90% of hydrogen produced is gray hydrogen, derived primarily from steam methane reforming or coal gasification, both of which are carbon-intensive. Considering the global hydrogen budget, the primary natural source of atmospheric hydrogen is sunlight-driven production (e.g., via methane oxidation), contributing 38 ± 6 Tg yr–1 (about 55% of the total budget). The largest anthropogenic source of hydrogen is the combined contribution from biofuel burning and wildfires, amounting to 12 ± 4 Tg yr–1. Hydrogen’s indirect Global Warming Potential (GWP) leads to the lengthening of the lifetime of CH4 and ozone, and to increased levels of stratospheric water vapor (H2O). , As a result, the GWP of H2 over a 100-year time scale has been estimated at 11.6 ± 2.8, indicating that H2 is 12 times more potent than CO2. While this is about half the GWP of CH4 it underscores the significance of hydrogen’s indirect effects on climate when released into the atmosphere. Until recently, small but climate-relevant hydrogen emissions remained undetected along the entire hydrogen value chain. However, such hydrogen losses during production, transport, storage and consumption could lead to a greater atmospheric hydrogen burden. To illustrate the impact of hydrogen leakage, the HC2017 scenario presented in Hauglustaine et al. (2022) shows that, for a hydrogen mix of 30% blue (gray+CCS) and 70% green hydrogen (renewables) with leakage rates of 1–3%, the climate benefit is substantially reduced, with hydrogen CO2-equivalent emissions corresponding to 20 to 54% of the avoided CO2 (based on GWP100 and GWP20, respectively). At higher leakage rates (>10% for a 100-year horizon; > 5% for a 20-year horizon), this hydrogen mix results even in a net climate penalty, where CO2-eq emissions exceed the avoided CO2.

Thus, far, current loss rate estimates, ranging up to 10%, are solely based on bottom-up emission estimates (i.e., theory-based). − A limited amount of suitable measurement equipment and a current lack in actual measurements causes these estimates to have no validation.

Only recently, sensitive new techniques to detect low-level hydrogen emissions along the value chain on an atmospheric scale have become available. In our previous study we combined an established high-precision analysis method (gas chromatographpulsed discharge helium ionization detector; GC-PDHID) with a newly applied sampling method (active AirCore) deployed from various mobile platforms, to detect significant hydrogen emissions originating from a chemistry park in the Netherlands. In the meantime, Aerodyne Research Inc. (USA) has developed a laser-spectroscopy-based instrument with which atmospheric hydrogen can be measured continuously, through oxidation to water vapor and a subsequent optical measurement, achieving ∼5 ppb precision (per 5 s). , The Norwegian oil company Equinor, in collaboration with Advanced Monitoring Solutions (AdMS) and the Department of Chemistry (University of Oslo) in Norway, have developed a mass spectrometer system capable of measuring small atmospheric hydrogen mole fractions (<1 umol mol–1 i.e., ppm) with a detection limit of ∼10 nmol mol–1 (i.e., ppb) directly from dried ambient air. Hence, with these new developments in atmospheric hydrogen detection techniques, the hitherto unknown hydrogen emissions along the hydrogen value chain become visible.

In the field of emission quantification there is already a wealth of reported experiments for greenhouse gases (GHG), like methane (CH4), with the aim of making emission quantifications more reliable and accurate. These emission estimates help reduce uncertainties in bottom-up estimates of the greenhouse gas (GHG) budget.

Optimal airborne plume sampling for methane emissions from a point source depends on factors such as the downwind distance, maximum flight time, measurement precision, and atmospheric stability conditions. Especially during near-source sampling (<500 m downwind), accurate visualization of the plume in both the horizontal (width) and vertical (height) plane is critical for reducing significant discrepancies between measured emissions and inventory estimates. − Furthermore, Morales et al. showed that measurement uncertainties increase primarily under conditions of wind speeds below 2 m s–1, wind direction variability greater than 33°, and sampling at downwind distances greater than 75 m. So far, under the most favorable measurement conditions, CH4 emissions could be quantified with an overall uncertainty of at best 30%.

In this study, we use measurements from a controlled-release experiment to optimize the quantification of hydrogen point-source emissions by identifying the most influential parameters contributing to uncertainties in the emission estimates.

In the following, we first outline the sampling and analysis techniques, building on Westra et al., after which we give a site description and further necessary prior information. The observations obtained from ground-based and airborne measurements are analyzed using a Gaussian plume dispersion model combined with a Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis to estimate the emission rate. At the end of the paper the conclusions and outlook on our methodology will be given.

Materials and Methods

Active AirCore

For the in situ collection of ambient air samples, a long, thin coil with a passivated inner surface (length245−285m,316″OD) is used, referred to as the “AirCore”. The AirCore is an atmospheric sampling system that consists of a thin-wall stainless-steel (S.S.) tubing in the shape of a coil, invented and patented by Pieter Tans. The original design is used to obtain a vertical atmospheric profile by filling itself (passively) using the air pressure gradient in the atmosphere. In contrast, the “active” AirCore technique collects a profile of a trace gas of interest via the use of a micropump (KNF NMP015 KPDC-B 6V) in combination with a mass flow controller or a critical orifice to regulate the flow, after which it is conserved until analysis in the lab. , For a more detailed description the reader is referred to Westra et al. (the two AirCore samplers described in that work are the same as used in the present study). We used two different AirCore samplers with different sampling flow rates. For our ground-based measurements we used an AirCore of 4.3 L (at 1.6 bar) with a fixed sampling flow of ∼45 sccm. A second AirCore of 3.7 L was either used with a sampling flow of 100 ± 10 sccm for the ground-based sampling or at 200 ± 10 sccm for the airborne sampling. For the analytical methodology, the GC-PDHID system used a sample size of 36.6 ± 2 mL per sample, resulting in a collection of up to 181 and 118 (for airborne only 80% filling, up to 74 samples) discrete samples, respectively, from the two AirCore samplers described above. More details of the active AirCore sampling technique are described in Section S1.2.

Flasks

An independent complementary sampling method was employed, as described by Westra et al. allowing for measuring additional trace gases next to H2. This involved collecting 2.4 L glass flask samples (with two Louwers Hapert Viton sealed valves) at atmospheric pressure, using a magnesium perchlorate inlet, at places along the measurement trajectory. These flasks were analyzed for H2, CH4, and CO2 via the GC-PDHID and for CO2, CH4, CO, and H2O using a Cavity Ring-down Spectroscopy (CRDS) system (Picarro Inc., model G2401) to provide additional insight into potential colocated emission sources. For the CRDS measurements of the flasks, CO2 shows a precision of <0.1 ppm, CH4 < 0.7 ppb, and <2.0 ppb for CO. The scale uncertainty is ± 0.07 ppm for CO2, ± 1 ppb for CH4, and ± 2 ppb for CO.

Sampling Methods

To be able to conveniently sample at low “walking” speed a small pushcart (Figure ) was utilized, with the glass flasks at the front and the AirCore secured at the rear. The inlet of the AirCore, from which the ambient air is sampled, is located at a height of about ∼2 m, together with a 3D sonic anemometer (Gill Windmaster Pro). The measurement method for the UAV-based AirCore (Figure ) is similar as described in Westra et al. and illustrated in Tong et al.

Analysis Methods

The Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph pulsed discharged helium ionization detector (GC-PDHID) system setup was designed for the precise and accurate analysis of H2, CO2 and CH4 in dry ambient air. We chose a PDHID for its high sensitivity, reliability, and good linearity from low to high mole fractions for H2 (here typically ranging between 0.5 up to 5 ppm). The system setup enables, via a heart-cutting procedure (forward-flush mode of the O2 and N2 fraction), the simultaneous detection of CO2 and CH4, which can aid in the source apportionment of atmospheric H2.

Our system design was partly based on the Agilent setup 7890–0237 “Impurities in Monomers Analyzer by PDHID” and a GC setup by Wang et al. The PDHID system uses Helium 5.0 (99.9999% Vol. %, Westfalen) as carrier gas, for a combination of a HayeSep Q, (G3591–81004, 6 ft, 18 ” “, 2 mm, 80–100 mesh packed) pre-column, followed by a ShinCarbon (G3591–81187, 2 m, 18 ”, 2 mm, 50/80 mesh) analytical column for detection of H2, CH4 and CO2. For the sample analysis a 2 mL sample loop is flushed at a rate of 43 ± 2 mL min–1 for 0.01–0.67 min. One chromatogram lasts 15.5 min for the detection cycle of H2, CH4 and CO2, and 7.5 min for H2 only. Retention times for the H2–CH4–CO2-method are 2.8 ± 0.1 min for H2 with a 4.2 ± 1.0 s wide peak width (PW), 7.7 ± 0.1 min for CH4 with a 16.8 ± 1 s PW, and 13.9 ± 0.1 min for CO2 with a 78 ± 1 s PW, and for the single H2-method at 2.3 ± 0.1 min for H2 with a 4.8 ± 1.0 s PW. A more detailed description of the GC analysis is given in Section S1.1.

Our GC-PDHID measures H2 with a precision of ± 2 ppb, CH4 with ± 1.4 ppb, and CO2 with ± 0.8 ppm, and it is calibrated against a suite of in-house made dry whole-air working standards which themselves are calibrated against 3 primary standards. Hydrogen is linked to the international NOAA-H2-X1996 hydrogen scale (maintained by the Max Planck Institute for Biogeochemistry (MPI-BGC), Jena, Germany), CO2 to the WMO X2019 scale and CH4 to the X2004A scale. The absolute accuracy of H2 is <1.3 ppb, < 3.6 ppb CH4, and <0.4 ppm of CO2.

The further analysis and processing of the flasks and AirCore data is done in the same way as described in detail by Westra et al.

To realize the required precision of ± 2 ppb, a “WHS-S-S-S-SWLS-S-S-S-S-” sequence is used. In this sequence short series of samples (S-S-S-–S-S) are bracketed between known high, or low, H2 mole fraction (W)­orking standards (WH and WL, respectively), to account for instrumental drift and/or uncertainty. For the GC designed and used in this study the bracketing method was optimized from the previously used 3 repetitions to 5, thereby reducing the analysis time and minimizing the influence of diffusion on the samples in the AirCore over time. However, due to the additional analysis time of the H2–CH4–CO2-method, the diffusion effects became significant, therefore we decided to limit the AirCore analysis to a H2-only method. A detailed description on the study of H2 diffusion behavior and the associated experiments are provided in Sections S1.2 and S1.3.

Similarly to Westra et al., the analysis method of the GC, resulting in discrete samples, needed to be translated to the correctly corresponding GPS-coordinates of the mobile sampling along the ground-based and/or airborne trajectories. The assignment of time and location coordinates was performed as in Westra et al. So, using a 2 ml sample loop and a sample volume of 36.6 ± 2 ml, the spatial allocation of H2 was determined by dividing the sampling time by the number of samples, with each sample attributed to the corresponding spatial segment, the assigned location was at 95% of this segment (at 34 ± 2 mL), to incorporate for air losses due to the flushing of the sample loop. All the ground-based trajectories were done on foot (<1.5 km h–1), so the spatial uncertainty was limited to a few decimeters. For the UAV-based sampling, the low flight velocity allowed a mapping resolution of a few meters per altitude level. Further details on technical aspects, such as spatial allocation and sample size are provided in Section S1.2.

As in Westra et al., a DJI Matrice 600 UAV was used, with a payload capacity of 6 kg, sufficient for our AirCore system with a mass of 6 kg. With a sample flow rate of 200 ± 10 mL min–1 the maximum flight duration of ∼13 min results in an AirCore fill of ∼80% (2600 mL).

Further specifics like the GPS tracking, flight speed, altitude, and other technical parameters, during airborne profiles downwind from the electrolyzer, are recorded by the UAV. For more details we refer to Westra et al. (2024).

Measurement Site

Located on the North side of Groningen (The Netherlands), at the Zernike Campus (53.246994 N, 6.530236 E) (Figure ) an 8 kW polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) electrolyzer (HOGEN S40 PEM) is used for experimental purposes. To the North, East and West of the site, rural areas extend up to the coast, with distances up to ∼60 km until the Wadden Sea on the North and West and the Ems estuary on the East. The only potential nearby H2 enhancements from these wind directions originate from cattle emissions , as well as emissions from local traffic, with further away in the region the Delfzijl chemistry park and several landfill sites. However, this could be accounted for by considering the colocated emissions of CO2, CH4, and CO with H2 from the flasks data.

The south-side of the Zernike campus is connected to the urban area of the city of Groningen. Potential known sources of hydrogen emissions are a hydrogen fueling station, traffic (regular fossil fuel-based vehicles and hydrogen fueled buses), several small electrolyzers, and a landfill site. Note that the on-campus chemistry department may also contribute to local hydrogen emissions. However, since no measurements were done with southerly winds, these sources were not of influence on the results.

The electrolyzer acts as a single point source that continuously emits 1.1 ± 0.1 m3 h–1 of hydrogen, through a small vent, equivalent to a rate of 1.65 ± 0.15 g min–1. For safety reasons, the gas–liquid separator of the PEM electrolyzer is purged every ∼5 min to remove accumulated residuals. This “wet side” purge consists of water vapor with minor hydrogen traces and since it accounts for only ∼1% of the total hydrogen production, well within the 11% uncertainty of our measured production flow, it is considered negligible in the overall emission estimate.

During the controlled release experiments no other hydrogen emissions were present on site. Furthermore, the electrolyzer is surrounded by a minimum number of obstructions (∼3 m) which ensures a rather undisturbed plume during ground-based sampling. Since the UAV-based trajectories were conducted during late fall and late winter, the absence of leaves on the trees (Figure ) was assumed sufficient to prevent any influence by them on the plume from the electrolyzer.

The site was well-suited for this controlled-release experiment, thanks to direct coordination with plant operators, which enabled optimal sampling conditions. Furthermore, the continuous emissions from this electrolyzer allowed the atmospheric hydrogen mole fraction to build up gradually and reach a steady state within the plume, in contrast to for example tracer-release experiments, where for a shorter amount of time, a gas is discharged from a cylinder.

Field Experiments

A total of 14 sampling transects were sampled, including both ground-based and airborne profiles (Figure ), downwind of the electrolyzer on 7 separate days in the late fall and late winter period (Nov 2024–March 2025). From these 7 days, 2 were downwind UAV flights, flown up to ∼35 m in height of which one was sampled at ∼70 m and the other at ∼200 m distance from the source, both with a northeasterly wind direction (71 ± 39°). For the ground-based sampling for 5 separate days along a variety of distances (35, 100, and 200 m), transects were sampled with a northwesterly wind direction (322 ± 25°), from which additional information could be obtained regarding the dilution over distance of the plume. A detailed description of the meteorological conditions is given in Table S5 in Section S2.1.

The solar radiation and temperature were taken from the Royal Dutch Meteorological Institute

(KNMI) station in Eelde (KNMI station 280, Groningen, The Netherlands), as this was the KNMI station closest to the measurement site at ∼14 km to the southwest. In-situ wind measurements were done via the use of a 3D sonic anemometer (Gill Windmaster Pro) at 10 Hz frequency. The 3D-sonic with a sensor height set at 2 m above ground, was placed either in one stationary position during the entire course of a UAV experiment, e.g., downwind of the source and on the line along the UAV trajectory, or it was positioned on the pushcart and thus following the same trajectory as the AirCore for the ground-based experiments.

To obtain the best estimate for the wind speed and direction during the mobile ground-based sampling, the cart was parked for a period of minimally 20 s every other minute, to average out the 3D-sonic data over the course of the trajectory and to obtain a mean over the sampling time for the wind direction and speed.

Furthermore, due to the wind speed’s dependence on altitude (typically assumed to follow a power law), an additional drone flight, without an AirCore, was conducted to determine the vertical profile of wind direction and velocity from the internal sensor of the UAV. In Table S5 in the Section S2.1 we summarize the mean conditions of the different experiments used in this study, i.e., sampling flow, device, storage time, meteorological conditions and the stability class based on the Pasquill-Gifford scheme, which relies on surface wind speed and solar radiation for daytime sampling.

In the Gaussian dispersion model, enhanced H2 mole fractions [mol H2 mol air–1] C(x, y, z) (eq ), inside the plume at specific coordinates (x, y, z in meters) downwind from the electrolyzer are described as a function of the emission rate Q [g s–1] and the mean wind speed ũ [m s–1] along the plume direction. The coordinates x, y, and z are the downwind distance, the crosswind distance [m] and measuring height, respectively. The latter is always considered to be 2 m for the ground-based measurements. More information regarding the assigned values is provided in Section S1.4 and a sensitivity analysis is given in Section S1.6.

To be able to estimate the uncertainty in our results, a Monte Carlo approach (n = 500) was used, randomly varying wind speed, wind direction, emission rate and solar radiation within their uncertainties, and each time calculating the value for the emission Q that fitted the H2 mole fraction C in eq ). In this way, the Monte Carlo process calculated a normal distribution for Q with its mean and uniformly distributed standard deviation for each measurement.

Results and Discussion

Flask Results

Our flask-derived data provide insights into possible colocated emission sources associated with the atmospheric H2 enhancements at the measurement site. We consider two potential emission sources of H2 in the area that could affect our experiments: combustion of fossil fuels from vehicles passing-by (H2 and CO & CO2), or the agricultural sector from the nearby rural area (H2 and CH4). From the glass flask measurements presented in Figure , as indicated by the low R-squared, no significant correlation was found between the enhancements in H2 and the enhancements from CO, CO2, and CH4 for each field experiment.

Emission measurements from flask samples across all sampling days showing (a) the correlation between CO and H2, (b) CH4 and H2, and (c) CO2 and H2. As shown by the R-squared, no correlation is observed.

In contrast to previous measurements conducted in Westra et al. at the Chemistry Park Delfzijl, where multiple unknown emission sources resulted in observable correlations, the present study site was selected as an isolated single controlled point source. Hence, the absence of correlations supports the conclusion that the observed enhancements originate solely from the controlled H2 release, with no significant external influences.

The flask measurements did not consistently capture the maximum enhancements observed with the AirCore due to the low and “blind” sampling frequency (up to 10 flasks per experiment).

AirCore Results

The determined emission rates were achieved by applying the Gaussian dispersion model to the found H2 enhancements for each transect, and by taking into account the atmospheric conditions during sampling, following the approach outlined in Westra et al. Using an 8 kW electrolyzer (Figure ) with a known, continuous release rate of 1.1 ± 0.1 m3 h–1 as a controlled source, we analyzed 14 data sets to determine a mean weighted hydrogen emission rate of 0.94 ± 0.06 m3 h–1. This agrees well within uncertainty with the known source rate. The relative uncertainty (ratio of uncertainty over average) of the emission estimate is 6%, compared to 9% for the known source. The source-based uncertainty was derived from variability in the electrolyzer exhaust monitored over multiple hours. More information with regard to the uncertainty analysis can be found S1.5. All data gathered during the field experiments is given in Sections S2.1 and S2.4.

The most influential factor contributing to uncertainty, was in the first place the extent to which the plume cross-section was captured along the horizontal plane for both ground- and UAV-based measurements. For UAV-based measurements, capturing the plume height (i.e., along the vertical plane) was also of key importance. This was followed, in order of importance, by sampling frequency, and finally by atmospheric variability (wind speed, stability class, and wind direction). During the course of the experiments, the sample collection was optimized by using a higher sampling flow (from ∼45 sccm to 100 ± 10 sccm) resulting in a 2-fold increase in spatial resolution along the measurement trajectory (for a detailed explanation, see Section S1.2).

This led to a better representation of the plume’s cross section and minimized the variability in meteorological conditions, as is reflected in the decrease of spread of the boxplots from the first 2 days to the last 4 days shown in Figure . By filtering out the 45 sccm from the total data set, 9 transects remain resulting in a weighted mean of 1.01 ± 0.07 m3 h–1. However, Caulton et al. who studied the quantification of uncertainties from mobile-laboratory-derived emissions, stated that a data set of more than 10 mobile transects is required to constrain atmospheric variability, as it represents the largest contributor to total uncertainty.

Hence, in this study, 11 ground-based (+3 UAV) repeating measurement experiments were included to minimize the uncertainty in the weighted mean emission rate.

Additionally, crosswind walking paths were performed to ensure complete coverage of the plume width. , The challenge between reducing meteorology-induced measurement uncertainty by limiting sampling duration has been discussed in previous CH4 emission quantification studies. ,,,, In this study, the sampling time was reduced by increasing the sampling rate, allowing the AirCore to fill more quickly while maintaining the same number of samples.

In contrary to Morales et al., we did not observe a significant increase in measurement uncertainty in our emission estimates at downwind distances beyond 75 m.

For comparison, methane emission studies report a range of uncertainties, Yong et al. reported a total relative uncertainty of 39–45%, Fosco et al. found 40%, and Allen et al.) reported 51–61%. When comparing our relatively low uncertainty of 6% with these studies, several important distinctions should be noted. Typical methane emission studies focus on landfill, agricultural or industrial emissions, which represent diffuse, variable and spatially complex sources. In contrast, our study uses a single controlled point source with minimal diffusion. Compared to Westra et al., where the field experiments showed considerably higher uncertainties due to multiple complex and diffusive sources, our results demonstrate that under controlled conditions, the combination of our Gaussian plume model and sensitivity analysis, with a well-characterized emission source can yield low uncertainties. As such, the uncertainty reported in this study represents a lower-bound estimate achievable under ideal conditions. More importantly, it provides insight into how factors such as source diffusivity and increased meteorological variability can increase uncertainty, ultimately approaching more the emission estimate uncertainties reported in previous methane studies. −

On day 13–11–2024, the quality of the data collected along the first 100 m trajectory was lower due to a sampling flow rate of ∼45 sccm combined with a relatively high rate of displacement. As a result, the full plume width was not captured, and the spatial resolution of the measurements was insufficient. This limitation, consistent with the findings of Mohammadloo et al., led to the underestimation of the actual emission rate. Particularly for transect 100 m (1), where the plateau-shaped peak approximated a Gaussian peak, led the dispersion model to substantially underestimate the emission rate (Sections S1.6 and S2.3). Furthermore, the relatively large spread in data points observed throughout the remaining trajectories of 13–11–2024 can be attributed to the sampling time of the experiment, during which the atmospheric variability (i.e., wind speed and direction) impacted the uncertainty. As a result, the Monte Carlo simulations produced a wider range of solutions due to this variability, and thus a greater spread in the values. So, the impact of the atmospheric conditions could be reduced by minimizing sampling time and thereby reducing the atmospheric variability.

For day 14–11–2024 (Figure ), an average wind speed of 1.7 ± 1 m s–1 was measured. According to Andersen, wind speeds below 2 m s–1 lead to ill-defined wind directions, making the plume less likely to follow a stable path and thereby increasing uncertainty in the emission estimate. Hence, it is likely that the lower wind speed on this day contributed to less reliable results in the emission quantification. Moreover, for day 14–11–2024, the wider spread observed in the estimates (Figure ) is attributed to incomplete coverage of the plume width along the 100 m downwind transects.

The upwind hydrogen mole fraction shows small daily fluctuations, highlighting the need to measure the upwind value for each sampling day. Using a generic background would add unnecessary uncertainty to the emission estimate.

We used the data from the active AirCore UAV experiments to derive vertical cross sections of the downwind hydrogen plume, based on linear interpolation between measurement points. These contour plots allow for characterization of the plume’s cross-sectional profile and hydrogen’s dispersion behavior over distance. Unlike ground-based sampling, which is constrained to a single height and offers limited insight into whether the full plume height or core is captured, UAV-based measurements eliminate these constraints by allowing targeted sampling throughout the plume both vertically and horizontally.

At 70 m distance from the source on day 12–12–2024 (Figure ), the H2 mole fraction was 1027 ppb at the center of the plume, with the plume’s dimensions at 95% peak height, being 6 m in height and 30 m in width. On measurement day 21–03–2025, at 200 m downwind the center of the plume’s H2 mole fraction (Figure ) was 650 ppb, while the plume expanded significantly to 18 m height and 90 m in width. From the center of the plume, the plume’s radius in both directions, roughly increased by a factor of 3, due to dispersion over distance from the electrolyzer.

At these ranges, hydrogen’s dispersion behavior appears to be closely mirroring that of other greenhouse gases, with atmospheric turbulence dominating its atmospheric transport pattern (i.e., wind speed, direction, and buoyancy), indicating that established methane measurement strategies, and the extensive expertise developed in that field, can be directly transferred to hydrogen emission estimations.

To decide whether the UAV-based sampling is preferred over the ground-based, a separate weighted mean emission estimate was calculated for each sampling method. The weighted mean emission estimate derived from UAV data is 0.98 ± 0.11 m3 h–1 (n = 3) and has a slightly larger error than the ground-based weighted mean emission rate (without 45 sccm) of 1.04 ± 0.09 m3 h–1 (n = 6), most likely from the smaller data set size. However, both the UAV data and the ground-based emission rate are within the uncertainties of the source-based emission rate.

Since UAV-based sampling allows for capturing the cross section of the center of the plume, it reduces uncertainties associated with spatial coverage compared to ground-based sampling, where the plume’s vertical position (e.g., core) is unknown. However, atmospheric variability and data resolution still remain the two dominant sources of uncertainty for both methods. As a result, for a single point source as was used in this study, both methods still yield a valid weighted mean emission estimation. So, the negligible difference indicates that, when an UAV is unavailable, a walk-by trajectory can be sufficient for a single point source, provided the sampling strategy is iteratively adjusted based on the results.

For capturing the complete plume cross section, the measurement method would benefit from a lightweight mobile in situ H2 measurement device with sufficient precision to detect the plume in real time and guide the flight trajectory for optimal horizontal and vertical coverage. However, such a device, suitable for mounting on a UAV, does not exist yet. For now, our active AirCore based measurement method, applicable for UAV sampling, is the most suitable method. In addition, the recent availability of new sensitive in situ instruments for H2 allow for the direct analysis of the AirCore postflight, enabling multiple flights per day to ensure a data set which covers the complete plume width. , Further work will focus on expanding the inventory of other fugitive hydrogen sources along the hydrogen value chain.
