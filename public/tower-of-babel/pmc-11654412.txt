Fracture incidence in women: the impact of reproductive characteristics
Authors: Maryam Farahmand, Maryam Rahmati, Marzieh Saei Ghare Naz, Mina Amiri, Mahsa Noroozzadeh, Mahbanoo Farhadi-Azar, Fereidoun Azizi, Fahimeh Ramezani Tehrani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough endogenous estrogen exposure, influenced by reproductive factors (RFs), is negatively associated with fracture risk, there is limited and conflicting information on the association between these factors and the incidence of fractures. This study aimed to evaluate the association between RFs and fracture incidence (FXI) separately.MethodsThis longitudinal study commenced in 1999 and concluded in 2021. It is performed on women without previous fractures and adjusted for confounders. RFs, including age at menarche, parity, abortion, duration of breastfeeding (BF), hormonal contraceptive use, and age at menopause, were exposure variables. The incidence of fractures was the primary outcome. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to estimate the associations between RFs and FXI outcomes.ResultsThe median (interquartile range) of follow-up time was 15.0 (12.1–17.0) years. A total of 19.9% (1324/6653)of the women were menopausal at baseline, and 13% (865/6653) of the remaining participants reached menopause at the end of follow-up. At the end of the follow-up, 222 (3.3%) participants had fractures. The mean age of participants at the initiation of the study and last follow-up were 35.8 (15.5) and 50.0 (15.3) years, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, the HR of FXI increased by 10% per one extra delivery (HR: 1.10,95% CI: (1.03, 1.18); p = 0.01), and by 12% per one-year increase in age at menarche (HR: 1.12, 95% CI: (1.02, 1.23); p = 0.02). The HR of FXI decreased by 3% per month extra exclusive BF (HR: 0.97,95% CI: (0.94, 0.99); p = 0.04).ConclusionsThe results elucidate that a longer duration of exclusive BF has a protective effect on FXI. In contrast, increasing age at menarche and the number of parities increase the risk of FXI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20890-2.Keywords: Reproductive factors, Incidence of fracture, Menarche, Menopause, Lactation

Introduction

A significant public health issue is fracture [1], which leads to job absenteeism, decreased productivity, disability, diminished quality of life, health loss, and high healthcare costs. They burden individuals, families, and healthcare systems [2–4]. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 highlights that the prevalence, incidence, and years lived with disabilities due to bone fractures have increased significantly due to population aging and growth [5].

Sex differences are an influential factor in FXI, with postmenopausal women experiencing a significantly higher rate of fractures than older men. The National Osteoporosis Foundation estimates that 9.1 million women have osteoporosis and 26 million have low bone density, compared to 2.8 million men with osteoporosis and 14.4 million men with poor bone mass [6].

Women’s bone health is influenced by reproductive factors (RFs), with gaps in understanding the link between fracture incidence and these factors [7–10]. Pregnant and lactating women have the same calcium (Ca) requirements [11]. Daily Ca loss from bone during breastfeeding (BF) increases by 200–250 mg to maintain serum levels. After 6–12 months of lactation, bone mineral density (BMD) can drop by about 5–10% [12, 13]. However, six months post-weaning, lactation-related bone loss is significantly repaired. While the exact compensation for prolonged lactation is unclear [14], studies have shown that BMD is higher after BF, indicating more bone formation than loss during that time [15].

Besides, the number of pregnancies and age at delivery can influence osteoporosis. While studies on 30 grand multiparous women and others with 2,769 postmenopausal women present conflicting findings, some suggest frequent pregnancies without adequate intervals may reduce bone mass [16, 17]. Women aged 25–30 reach peak bone mass and pregnancy under this period may reduce bone metabolism [18]. Conversely, older age at delivery increases the risk of osteoporosis due to metabolic diseases [19, 20]. Additionally, studies indicate that hormonal contraceptives have an inconclusive link to fracture incidence in healthy pre and postmenopausal women [21–24].

Estrogen protects bone health, with studies showing an inverse link between endogenous estrogen and FXI [25–27]. For instance, a study on Chinese women found that late menarche and another study on 43,652 Japanese women aged 40–69 years showed early menopause, key RFs, and increased fracture risk [26, 27]. Study findings on the association between RFs and FXI have controversies [20–25]. Assessing the link between RFs and FXI is crucial. This longitudinal study explored how RFs relate to the incidence of hospitalized fractures in a large community population.

Methods

Sampling and participants

The participants in the present study were selected from among the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) participants. TLGS is an ongoing prospective study started in 1998 to specify the prevalence and incidence of risk factors for non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Fifteen thousand five people aged ≥ 3 years were selected via a multistage cluster sampling. Follow-ups were performed every almost three years to obtain data on demographic, anthropometric, reproductive, and metabolic characteristics, general physical examinations, and laboratory measurements. Furthermore, specific evaluations for all obstetric and reproductive conditions, including age at menarche and age at menopause, have been documented for every female participant. The details are published elsewhere [28].

The selection process for participants is shown in Fig. 1. For the present long-term prospective population-based study, all postmenarcheal women aged ten years or older, including reproductive and menopausal participants, who completed related examinations in the first phase of the TLGS (1998–2001) and attended at least one follow-up study, were included. Those with a lack of all appropriate data about reproductive characteristics (n = 875), those receiving hormone replacement therapy (n = 25), those with multiple pregnancies (n = 101), and those without any follow-up assessment (n = 2010) were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 6653 women met the eligibility criteria and were followed up to the date of incident fracture, censoring (e.g., death), or the end of the study period, whichever came sooner.

Measurements

Face-to-face interviews were performed, and a standard questionnaire was used by trained staff to gather all information on demographic and lifestyle variables, various risk factors for NCDs, and familial and individual past medical and reproductive histories.

Weight and height were measured with minimal clothing without shoes in a standing position, using standardized procedures and calibrated equipment for every participant. The waist circumference (WC) was measured centrally between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest at the level of the umbilicus at the end of a gentle expiration. An unstretched measuring tape with an accuracy of 0.1 cm was used to measure the hip circumference. To calculate the body mass index (BMI), weight in kilograms and height in meters squared (kg/m2) were used. Blood pressure (BP) was measured twice in the right arm after a 15-minute rest in a sitting position and was calculated based on the mean of the two measurements. The Modifiable Activity Questionnaire was used to assess physical activity. Accordingly, participants with fewer than 600 metabolic equivalent weekly task minutes were categorized as a low physical activity group [29]. After a 12- to 14-hour overnight fast, blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. The samples were analyzed on the blood-taking day. Other details for laboratory measurements are shown elsewhere [30].

Definitions

The World Health Organization (WHO) classification was used for the definition of “natural menopause” as the absence of spontaneous menstrual bleeding for > 12 months, for which no other source of pathologic or physiologic cause could be determined [31]. The time point of 1 year before the 12 months of menstrual bleeding cessation was considered the date of natural menopause. “Menarche is the first menstrual period in a female adolescent.” Parity was calculated as stated by the self-reported in such a way that the number of times a woman has given birth to a fetus with a gestational age of 24 weeks or more, regardless of whether the child was born alive or not, is known as parity.

The duration of hormonal contraception was calculated according to the total number of months of using any hormonal contraception for the prevention of pregnancy.

Abortion was calculated as stated by the self-reported total number of times a fetus was removed from the uterus before it reached the stage of viability, usually about the 20th week of gestation.

Participants indicated the duration of exclusive BF and the duration of subsequent BF that complementary foods attached to them, referred to as partial BF, by responding to two separate questions [32]. Exclusive BF was initially defined according to the WHO definition and Iranian national guidelines. Subsequently, after delivery, infants, until (maximum) six months of age, receive only breast milk until the end of six months of birth without receiving complementary foods or any solid food or other liquid, even water, except for vitamin drops or syrups, mineral supplements, and medications prescribed by the doctor [33, 34]. Complementary food should be added slowly and gradually to the infant’s diet. The total BF duration for each participant was calculated by summing all BF months [35].

Central obesity was defined as a WC ≥ 95 cm [36]. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7 mmol/L and/or 2-hour post-challenge plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L or the consumption of antidiabetic medications [37]. Hypertension (HTN) was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, or consumption of antihypertensive medications [38]. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as glomerular filtration rate (GFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for > three months, happening at any time during the follow-up period based on the Kidney Disease Outcome Quality Initiative guidelines. The abbreviated prediction equation provided by the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease study was used to estimate GFR as follows: GFR = 186 × (serum creatinine 2 − 1.154) × (age2 − 0.203) × 0.742. In this equation, estimated GFR is expressed as mL/min per 1.73 m2, and serum creatinine is expressed as mg/dL [39].

Fracture outcome

The method for assessment of outcomes that happened during the follow-up of TLGS has been explained in detail before [30]; in brief, participants were actively followed for each medical event that caused hospitalization within the previous year by a telephone call. A scientific committee, including an internist, endocrinologist, cardiologist, and epidemiologist, evaluated the collected documents, including the hospital record, to determine the type of outcome that occurred. The abstract was diagnosed with fractures. After one day of hospitalization, fractures were registered and adjudicated in any extremity (upper, lower, or other sites) by the outcome committee.

Statistical analysis

Our study had the power of 88.7% to detect a hazard ratio equal to 0.8 for the effect of EEE on fracture at a 0.05 significance level [25], considering an allocation ratio of 0.03. All continuous variables were examined in terms of normal distribution using the one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and were expressed as mean (standard deviation (SD)) or median (interquartile range (IQR) 25–75%) as appropriate. Categorical variables were also expressed as numbers (percentages).

The Cox regression model was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between reproductive characteristics, including age at menarche and menopause, duration of lactation and oral contraceptive use, and the number of pregnancies (if applicable), as exposure. The event date was defined as the date of the incident fracture, and the age at fracture was computed. For individuals with any incident fracture, survival time was defined as the interval between the age at entrance and the fracture age (in years). For the lost follow-up or censored participants who did not develop fractures by the end of the follow-ups, the survival time was defined as the difference between the age at the entrance and the age at the last follow-up.

Both unadjusted and adjusted Cox regression models were applied. Each RF was entered into a separate Cox model. Potential confounding factors, including age, BMI, central obesity, smoking status, education status, physical activity, marital status, CKD, cardiovascular disease (CVD), DM, HTN, and steroid and aspirin drug treatment, were examined by a univariate model, and those associated with the study outcome (p < 0.2) would be included in the multivariable Cox regression model. Finally, the hazard ratio plot was depicted for fracture outcome by each RF.

Statistical analysis was performed using STATA (version 13; STATA Inc., College Station, TX, USA), and the significance level of the test was set as 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

The characteristics of the participants according to baseline and the end of follow-up are shown in Table 1. The mean (SD) age of 6,653 subjects included in the study was 35.8 (15.5) and 50.0 (15.3) years at baseline and at the end of follow-up, respectively. Menopause occurred for 1,324 (19.9%) of women at baseline, and the mean (SD) for age at menopause was 48.9(6.2). By the end of the follow-up, 2,189 participants reached menopause; the mean (SD) age at menopause was 51.3(4.6). The median (IQR) of follow-up time was 15(12–17) years. During follow-up, 222 fracture events occurred, and the median (IQR) for age at fracture was 66.4(53.7–73.8) years.

Parity

Parity was positively associated with fracture risk in both unadjusted and adjusted models. The adjusted model revealed that per one increase in parity, the fracture hazard increased by 10% (HR: 1.10, 95% CI: (1.03, 1.18); p = 0.004).

BF duration

BF discloses a negative association with fracture events in unadjusted models. Specifically, the hazard of fracture decreased by 2% (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: (0.98, 0.99); p = 0.01), 6% (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: (0.92, 0.98); p = 0.001), and 2% (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: (0.98, 0.99); p = 0.01) per one month for total, exclusive, and partial BF duration, respectively. Interestingly, after adjustment, only the effect of the exclusive BF component remained significant (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: (0.94, 0.99); p = 0.04).

Age at menarche and age at menopause

In the unadjusted model, the association between age at menarche and menopause and FXI was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). After adjusting for potential confounders, the association between age at menopause and FXI remained unchanged. However, age at menarche showed a positive association with FXI in the adjusted model, so per one-year increase in age at menarche, the hazard of fracture increased by 12% (HR: 1.12, 95% CI: (1.02,1.23); p = 0.04).

Menopause status

We also considered menopause status as a separate factor in the Cox regression model. The corresponding HRs and 95% CIs in the unadjusted and adjusted models were HR: 4.14, 95% CI: (2.84, 6.05); (p < 0.001), and HR: 3.15, 95% CI: (2.01, 4.94); (p < 0.001), respectively.

Abortion

Increasing the number of abortions was associated with a significant increase in the hazard of fracture in the unadjusted model (HR: 1.12, 95% CI: (1.05, 1.20); however, this significant effect disappeared after adjusting for confounding variables (HR: 1.07, 95% CI: (0.98, 1.16); p = 0.11).

Duration of hormonal contraceptive use

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, the association between the duration of hormonal contraceptive use and FXI was not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Overall, no statistically significant relationship was observed between all types of RFs and the incidence of all types of fractures (p > 0.05).

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the associations of interest. The first analysis included all participants either in menopause or reached menopause during the study period (n = 2,189). The second analysis focused on participants who were already menopausal at the study’s baseline (n = 1,324). The total number of fractures, when considering all participants who were either postmenopausal or reached menopause during the study period, was 182 (8.31%). This number decreased to 118 (8.91%) when we limited the analysis to those already postmenopausal at baseline. However, the reduced sample size resulted in insufficient statistical power for this subgroup analysis.

We re-conducted our analysis by considering the specified variables, including parity, BF, and hormonal contraceptives, as categorical. The results of the unadjusted and adjusted models are presented in Table 2. The adjusted model indicates that the fracture risk is 1.67 times greater in women with three or more pregnancies than in nulliparous women (HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.04 to 2.66). The adjusted models for other categorical variables did not yield statistically significant results.

We also evaluated the effect of estrogen exposure (calculated as age at menopause minus age at menarche) on fracture. However, both unadjusted (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.98, 1.04), and adjusted (HR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.95, 1.03) models indicated no significant effect of estrogen exposure on fracture risk.

Discussion

Main findings

Our findings illustrated a direct association between the number of parity and the number of abortions and FXI; an inverse association was observed between the duration of BF (total, partial, or exclusive) and FXI. After adjusting for potential confounders including age, age at menarche (not in the model that menarche was an independent variable), BMI, central obesity, smoking, education, marital status, a steroid drug, an aspirin drug, CKD, DM, and CVD, there remained a direct association between parity and FXI, as well as age at menarche as the first RF that was shown to have a direct association with fracture. Furthermore, only an inverse association remained between the exclusive BF and FXI duration. As well, menopause status showed a strong association with FXI, increasing almost threefold.

BF duration and number of parity and abortion

Overall, the findings of a systematic review reported that the association between fracture risk with BF and pregnancy is unclear and controversial [40]. The protective effect of parity on BMD was reported before [41]; in contrast, parity has been reported as a risk factor for BMD [17, 42, 43]. Other studies found no an association between BMD and parity [44, 45]. However, the current study elucidated that increasing parity can lead to increasing FXI, so increasing parity by almost 10% increased fracture incidence.

However, BF duration overall showed a protective effect on FXI. Still, after adjusting for potential confounders, only the duration of exclusive BF remained a protective factor against fracture risk, so a one-month increase in exclusive BF can reduce fracture risk by 3%. In this regard, a study in Iran (2016) reported that gravidity and duration of lactation were the most influential RFs in osteoporosis [8], and the prevalence of osteoporosis increased with increasing parity and abortion [46]. Yet, in postmenopausal women, it has been reported that the duration of BF has an inverse association with the BMDs of the femoral neck and the lumbar spine [47].

During pregnancy, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-I) changes contribute to trabecular bone loss in women [48]. Intestinal calcium (Ca) absorption increases significantly, and the maternal skeleton supplies most of the Ca for BF, which is crucial for the infant’s growth [49]. Transient hip osteoporosis can occur in late pregnancy or early postpartum [50, 51]. Parathyroid hormone-related protein levels also rise during this time [49]. The body adapts through enhanced intestinal absorption [52], increased bone resorption, and reduced Ca excretion to meet the increased Ca needs [53–55]. While bone loss typically repairs within 6–12 months after BF [50], fragility fractures may be observed during the BF period. Notably, BMD tends to increase after weaning [15, 56, 57], often resulting in higher levels than before BF [15]. During BF, estrogen inhibits periosteal bone formation slightly while allowing periosteal expansion, leading to increased bone size after weaning [58]. Oxytocin secretion, heightened during BF and skin-to-skin contact post-childbirth, may enhance the success of exclusive BF [59]. This prolonged exclusive BF can raise oxytocin levels, which has anabolic effects on bone metabolism in females, supported by estrogen [60]. Overall, many studies on menopausal and reproductive women reported a neutral or beneficial effect of BF on BMD or fracture risk [15, 61–63], with few indicating potential harm from long-term BF [64].

Age at menarche

Our findings indicate that a one-year increase in age at menarche raises fracture risk by 12% after adjusting for confounders. Age at menarche is associated with various NCDs [65–67]. Late menarche is linked to osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures [26, 27, 68]. Women have endogenous estrogen from menarche to menopause, which helps reduce fracture risk and is influenced by RFs [25]. Therefore, delayed menarche may increase fracture risk. It is important to note that age at menarche had a statistically significant direct association with fracture risk only in the adjusted model. The association between all confounders and menarche was assessed, and results showed BMI had a negative association with menarche. This association can explain why, in the adjusted model, there is a statistically significant association between age at menarche and FXI risk.

Menopause

Our results showed a threefold risk for menopause, but between age at menopause and FXI, there was no significant association. In this regard, it has been reported that menopause status influences fracture risk. Indeed, the decrease in estrogen tends to decrease BMD and increase fracture risk. However, Banks et al. reported this effect a short time after menopause. It also seems that after menopause, aging is an influential factor instead of age at menopause [69, 70], so especially after the age of sixty years, increased age shows a direct association with FXI [71, 72].

Duration of hormonal contraceptive use

In addition, the duration of hormonal contraceptive use in our study did not show an association with fracture risk. Studies showed hormonal contraceptives have an ambiguous effect on BMD and an unclear association with the incidence of fractures [21–24, 68]. Recall bias in hormonal contraceptive consumption, especially in older women, is an important factor in assessing the association between hormonal contraceptive duration and FXI [68].

Limitations and strengths

The present study is one of the most extensive population-based retrospective studies, with almost two decades of follow-up exploring the effect of RFs on the risk of FXI. FXI as an outcome was directly assessed instead of, for example, BMD or replacement markers of fracture. Moreover, the present study reported a reliable estimation of FXI because it used hospital records instead of self-reporting. Furthermore, the most important potential confounders, such as comorbidities, which have been less considered in other studies, were adjusted.

Some limitations need to be considered as well. The main limitation of the present study is the lack of adequate statistical power to conduct subgroup analyses examining our findings among specific subsets of the study population. Specifically, the sample size was insufficient to assess the associations of interest separately among women who were postmenopausal at the initiation of the study and those who transitioned to menopause during the follow-up periods. Postmenopausal status is a critical modifier influencing the relationships between various exposures and health outcomes. Women who are postmenopausal at baseline may differ in important ways from those who transition to menopause throughout a longitudinal study. Recall bias represents a notable limitation of the present study. The data collection process involved the use of a self-report questionnaire to gather information regarding age at menarche, age at menopause, duration of BF, and hormonal contraceptive use. However, it is important to emphasize that these data were consistently recorded within the Tehran Lipid and Glucose Study (TLGS) cohort every three years. This systematic data collection approach helps mitigate the potential impact of recall bias and demonstrates high consistency in the data acquisition process. Third, milder and asymptomatic fractures may have been missed because only fractures requiring hospitalization were assessed during the follow-ups. Fourth, no difference between osteoporotic and traumatic fractures was diagnosed due to a lack of data. Next, the effect of nutritional factors such as vitamin D was not assessed. Although the study showed more than half of the Iranian population suffers from vitamin D deficiency [73], the lack of data on vitamin D in our data set would not affect our results. Finally, our findings from the urban area cannot be generalized to rural areas. Bone Mass Densitometry (BMD) is commonly used in the literature as a predictor of fracture risk; however, unfortunately, our study does not include BMD assessments.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates a significant association between late menarche and an increased risk of fractures, indicating a long-term risk factor for women that may be challenging to modify. In contrast, our findings suggest that childbirth does not significantly increase the long-term risk of fractures, providing reassurance for women contemplating motherhood. While these findings enhance our understanding of fracture risk factors in women, it is essential to acknowledge that the implications of late menarche may take time to be actionable. Therefore, while late menarche should be considered in clinical assessments, the positive outcome regarding childbirth may offer comfort to women in their reproductive decision-making.

Future research should investigate these associations and their implications for women’s health, focusing on identifying modifiable risk factors that can be targeted through clinical interventions and public health initiatives.
