Attention enhanced hybrid deep learning architecture with PCA-based feature fusion for banana leaf disease detection
Authors: Hongyi Du, Hua Yang, Zhidong Zhang, Jinjing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBanana is a key staple crop in the world, but its yield is very desperately affected by the leaf diseases like Sigatoka, Fusarium Wilt, and Cordana, which bring serious losses of yield and economy. The manual disease detection methods deployed in the past are time and labor-intensive and cannot be effective in the field, so powerful automated solutions are sought. In this paper, we have presented a hybrid deep learning architecture which combines MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 with attention, dilated convolutions, multi-scale feature pooling and PCA-based feature fusion to classify banana leaf disease accurately and efficiently. The Banana and Banana Leaf, Banana Disease Recognition, and Banana LSD three benchmark datasets have been trained and evaluated following an innovative preprocessing pipeline that consists of illumination correction, background suppression, denoising, and hi-tech data augmentation. The experimental findings have shown that the presented hybrid model is always better than ten state-of-the-art deep learning models, such as VGG16, ResNet50, DenseNet121, EfficientNet-B0, and Vision Transformer (ViT). It has reached an optimal accuracy of 98.28, precision of 98.18, recall of 98.77 and F1-score of 98.43, with only 12.7 M trainable parameters and convergence rate of only 15 epochs, which makes the model both high-accuracy and computationally efficient.Keywords: Banana leaf diseases, Deep learning, Convolutional neural networks, Transfer learning, MobileNetV2, ResNet101Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

Bananas, a staple food and a major source of income to millions of farmers in various parts of the world, is a heavily cultivated crop, especially in tropical and subtropical areas. Bananas, which are among the top five agricultural products, contribute significantly to the agricultural economy as well as the global food security, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)1. Nevertheless, several foliar diseases, which affect yield and post-harvest value, are a major threat to banana crop productivity and quality. Some of the most devastating banana leaf diseases include the black Sigatoka, Panama disease, Fusarium wilt, and Banana streak virus, which destroys the photosynthetic ability of the leaf, causing stunted growth, ripeness, and loss of large amounts of money2. Effective disease management and timely intervention coupled with sustainable cultivation of these diseases is therefore heavily dependent on early detection and proper classification of such diseases. In conventional systems of disease diagnosis, manual examination by agriculturalists can be subjective, time-consuming and not applicable to large plantations3.

In banana plantations, as well as in other places across the world, serious economic and yield losses are caused by banana leaf diseases, especially Black Sigatoka (Mycosphaerella fijiensis)4. The leaves of infected plants lose their photosynthetic ability causing abnormal ripening of fruits, low bunch size and low quality of fruit. Research has documented that Black Sigatoka by itself can lead to yield losses of 35–50% or in severe instances, yield losses of 100% in the event of uncontrolled cases. These diseases make production more expensive, as they highly depend on fungicides, which tend to be costly and polluting. Due to the predominance of banana production among smallholder farmers in some areas, such losses directly affect household income, food security, and export potential, and disease control is a crucial issue in maintaining banana production5.

Recent improvements in artificial intelligence and image processing have resulted in the creation of powerful banana leaf disease classification methods, and there is a heavy focus on deep learning models and transfer learning models. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)6 are the most commonly used because they can be used to extract features of leaf images automatically without feature engineering. Transfer learning has been applied to state-of-the-art architectures like ResNet7, DenseNet8, VGGNet9, and Inception to reach high accuracy in classifying various common banana diseases, including Black Sigatoka, Fusarium wilt, and Banana streak virus. Moreover, it has been noted that hybrid methods using CNNs with standard machine learning classifiers like Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests have been investigated to enhance the resistance to classifications. In addition to CNNs, recent research has examined the use of Vision Transformers (ViTs) and attention mechanisms10, which improve the capabilities of the model in targeting disease-related regions of the leaf. Improving the dataset imbalance as well as enhancing generalization has been achieved by data augmentation methods (rotation, flipping, synthetic image generation with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)) that augment the dataset with additional images11. In addition, lighter deep learning algorithms and mobile disease recognition systems are under development to facilitate real-time disease detection under field conditions making these methods more affordable to smallholder farmers. Taken together, these strategies are a step toward scalable, precise, and farmer-friendly solutions to early banana disease detection8.

Although there are various automatic techniques to classify banana leaf disease, several issues prevent the implementation of these techniques in the real agricultural field conditions. Available datasets usually are of limited size and are skewed in representation among disease groups and this limits the generalization capabilities of trained models. Further, the environment (including variations in lighting, background noise, the orientation of leaves, and occlusion) can greatly influence classification accuracy when models are evaluated under field conditions. Even though deep learning algorithms such as CNNs and Vision Transformers have shown to be very high-performing in controlled settings, their complexity prevents their implementation on resource-intensive systems such as smartphones, which are vital resource-heavy devices needed by smallholder farmers. Moreover, most of the existing techniques have difficulty in detecting the disease at its early stages when timely treatment is essential and do not usually work in cases of multiple diseases on the same leaf. Lastly, there is a gap between development of technology and its actual application because many of the available solutions are limited to scholarly research and are not converted into usable and affordable and easy-to-use tools.

To solve these problems, the present study will suggest a powerful and scalable banana leaf disease classification framework based on advanced deep learning methods and with a practical applicability in real-world agricultural settings. The methodology makes use of a highly edited and augmented dataset to address the drawbacks of data imbalance and optimize model generalization in different field settings. It uses a lightweight but precise deep learning model, optimized to run on mobile and edge devices, to deliver real-time disease detection to smallholder farmers. The structure also focuses on pre-empting the disease at an early stage through a combination of fine-grained feature extraction and attention, which secures the timely intervention of the disease before it can cause extensive crop damage. What is more, the model is created to deal with multi-disease classification, allowing the detection of multiple infections on the same leaf at the same time, which is closer to the real-life situation. Literature review

Various studies have also investigated various image processing and machine learning approaches in the realization of detecting banana leaf diseases. One was a method that would combine color segmentation, the Otsu method, with histogram thresholding to deal with images that had highly complex natural backgrounds. This procedure performed well in isolating healthy green leaf areas and disease spots, weeds, soil, and other noise with a segmentation accuracy of approximately 97% and an error of 2.3%, showing its consistency in future segmentation and recognition studies.

Other research combined CNN-based features extraction with the Support Vector Machines (SVM) to classify. This started with image preprocessing (denoising, resizing, and image quality) methods. The convolutional and pooling layers were used to extract features, and SVM was used to make the classification. This CNN-SVM hybrid method achieved approximately 94% in the detection of all common diseases such as Black Sigatoka, Cordana leaf spot and banana leaf speckle7. Another study project created a pipeline with image stitching, contrast equalization, segmentation and feature extraction. Experiments with the Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) and BruteForce (BF) matching identified the disease regions with the Otsu thresholding method. The HSV space color features were then used to detect brown spots, which is often a feature of Sigatoka disease12. The system used in conjunction with SVM was able to detect Sigatoka spots with approximately 90% accuracy. In other studies, K-means clustering was used to do segmentation, extract features using color, shape, and texture, and ultimately classify using SVM. This method proved efficient in identifying several diseases such as Panama disease, Bacterial Wilt, Cucumber Mosaic Virus (CMV) and Sigatoka with approximately 85% precision.

More recently, techniques that use a combination of histogram equalization with Fuzzy C-means clustering have been used to diseases like Black Sigatoka, Anthracnose and Freckle disease. Image quality was boosted by preprocessing including resizing, filter and elimination of Gaussian noise techniques and then a segmentation was performed using clustering followed by pattern recognition to classify the image. This technique, combined with Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), showed good results in identification of various diseases of banana leaves13.

As deep learning advances rapidly, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have become popular in measuring the symptoms of plant diseases, which are much more accurate and more efficient than alternative methods. As an example, Arun et al.14 trained CNNs on MalayaKew, the dataset of 44 plant categories, and showed that CNN-based models significantly outperformed conventional hand-crafted feature methods. In the same light, Yan et al. conducted a comparative study between manually engineered features and deep CNNs, which again confirmed the superiority of the latter15. Expanding on this, Nagachandrika et al.16 suggested a multi-scale fusion CNN architecture, which utilizes the resizing of images to various resolutions and then pass through independent CNNs; the learned features were then fused, and the network was trained to capture more visual information.

In addition to single-network approaches, ensemble-based approaches have also been proposed to enhance the robustness of classification. Pham et al.17 tested different CNN backbones on coffee leaf classification implementing early and late fusion strategies and noting that results were affected by the fusion scheme. Sarawathi et al.18 and Nagu et al.19 also investigated the concept of ensemble learning, with the latter used four CNN models in training, out of which two best were selected to create a weighted ensemble, providing more credible predictions.

Because high-quality labeled data acquisition is still one of the most significant challenges, scientists also explored algorithms that can work with a few annotations. Few-shot learning (FSL) has been suggested as an effective solution because it can train models to make predictions based on a limited number of samples that are labeled20. Thirumeninathanet al. showed this by testing a CNN with an SVM classifier on a source data set and freezing the CNN with fine-tuning of the SVM on the target data set only. They found that their ablation experiments were more accurate given more fine-tuning samples, with an accuracy of close to 90% when 80 samples were provided21. By applying a meta-learning method that employed a feature-level attention mechanism to a Vision Transformer (ViT), Saini22, further cut the number of samples per class to just five and therefore increased model robustness in the face of severe data scarcity.

The recent developments in deep learning in terms of detecting plant and leaf diseases have paid more emphasis on enhancing the classification accuracy, robustness, and interpretability of the models across a wide range of crop species. Some papers have investigated optimization and hybrid approaches to improve deep learning, e.g. wild horse and hybrid crossover oppositional firefly algorithms to optimize CNN parameters and promote feature extraction in plant disease classification, which has been shown to be more robust than standard deep learning-based networks23. In another study, it was suggested to increase classification accuracy and sustain the agricultural practice by developing gradient-weighted DenseNet models that can be easier to interpret and achieve higher performance in relation to their computational costs24. Attempts to design light and efficient models are also becoming more common; recent research in grapevine leaf disease recognition showed new methods to downsample features and to do hyperparameter optimization to obtain high detection but at a lower computational cost, suggesting the prospect of real-world and edge deployment25. Besides this, studies that use explainable AI methods like SHAP on deep neural networks have furthered the explanation of the plant disease diagnostics, showing significant visualization effects that govern the choice made by the model when faced with complicated field conditions26. Moreover, the use of hybrid machine-deep learning reviews highlights the combination of conventional ML and DL schemes to exploit global and local characteristics, which is a tendency in more comprehensive, data-adaptive models in precision agriculture27.

The detection of banana leaf disease has been investigated using a wide variety of image processing and machine learning methods, including color segmentation by Otsu thresholding and K-means clustering, as well as hybrid pipelines such as SIFT and HSV features and SVM classifiers with accuracies of between 85% and 97%. More complex methods used CNN feature extraction with SVM or CNN variants including dual-branch networks to learn shape and texture jointly, multi-scale fusion CNN to learn features at multiple scales, and an ensemble model to make stronger predictions. More recent studies have also examined approaches to few-shot learning and meta-learning to address the scarcity of labelled agricultural data, with encouraging results with CNN-SVM fusions and attention-enhanced Vision Transformers. Even with these developments, issues such as reduced performance when working in a complex field, reliance on large and heavily labeled datasets, lack of simultaneous performance across multiple diseases, and high computational complexity remain common, as do computational requirements that make the methods inaccessible to resource-limited farming systems.

Materials and methods

In this section, the datasets, preprocessing, and methodology framework used to classify banana leaf disease are described. The experiment used image data of healthy and infected banana leaves covering typical infections, including Black Sigatoka and Fusarium Wilt, and other foliar diseases. To train the model, several preprocessing procedures, such as resizing, noise reduction, and normalization, were utilized to standardize the input images and enhance feature extraction. Moreover, to augment the data used in improving the accuracy of the trained model, rotation as well as flipping and scaling were used to ensure the datasets were diversified and to help in reducing overfitting. The classification system was trained in deep learning architectures and mostly lightweight but accurate models that can be used in real-time detection. The subsections below describe the dataset, data preprocessing pipeline, model architecture, training configuration and evaluation measures in this study. Figure 1 shows the overall framework for proposed hybrid deep method for banana leaf disease classification. The architectural improvements utilized in this paper such as SE attention, dilated convolutions, GeM pooling, and PCA-based feature fusion were chosen with cautiousness to use their strengths. Channel-wise calibration of features is increased by SE attention, which increases the model capability to concentrate on informative features. The dilated convolutions increase the receptive field, which captures multi-scale contextual images without augmenting the computation cost. The feature maps are of variable size, which is well aggregated by GeM pooling, and multi-modes or high-dimensional features are well integrated by PCA-based fusion to eliminate redundancy. These elements were chosen over other methods that utilized early experiments which showed better performance in accuracy and computational efficiency hence the extension in the proposed architecture. The proposed model was made with lightweight and real-time constraints to facilitate the application. They have the benefit of a smaller number of parameters and the ability to extract features quickly and efficiently, which allows them to infer rapidly on resource-constrained edge and mobile devices without the need to have high-end GPUs. The simplified architecture and streamlined operations are likely to ensure the model has low inference latency and moderate power consumption, therefore, it can be used in continuous or near-real-time monitoring. These features indicate the possibility of the implementation of the offered method in practice where computation power, response speed, and energy consumption are very important factors.

Image preprocessing

A multi-stage preprocessing pipeline was designed to implement a strong and correct classification of banana leaf diseases beyond the traditional resizing and normalization methods. The pipeline under development starts with lighting correction based on adaptive histogram equalization to reduce the impact of light variation in field-acquired images. Background suppression follows, using a series of procedures that include conversion to color space (RGB to HSV) followed by selective masking to isolate the leaf area in an image with respect to soil, weeds, or other extraneous objects often found in the natural scene. A contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) filter28 is also used to maximize disease conspicuity (i.e., increase the strength of lesion patterns) without over-saturating the healthy areas.

Subsequently, a wavelet-based multi-scale denoising methodology is used to remove high-frequency noise but retain texture features essential to detecting disease. Morphological operations are then applied to the segmented leaf regions to eliminate small artifacts and emphasize the boundaries of lesions. Advanced data augmentation techniques (geometric transformations (rotation, flipping, scaling), photometric variations (brightness, contrast, hue changes), lesion-specific (region-masking) augmentation are used to balance the datasets and enhance generalization of the model, as well as, more importantly, learn more discriminative features29. Lastly, to find consistency in training, validation, and testing data, all images are standardized to a common resolution and scaled to a standard pixel intensity range. This new preprocessing pipeline does not only clarify the features of diseases but also boosts the strength of the classification structure during various real-life situations, including variations in light conditions, background noise and partial hiding. The suggested system allows real-time monitoring of the banana leaf disease with the help of a mobile device. The smartphone camera captures images, and they pass through the basic preprocessing stage and are sent to the lightweight attention-enhanced hybrid model (Fig. 2). The model conducts inference at the device to determine the condition of the disease, and the inferred outcome is shown immediately to the user. This test process illustrates the effectiveness and feasibility of the suggested solution to the real-world use, mobile-based agricultural applications.

It appears to be relatively small, but clear structure based on leaf spot diseases makes it useful in measuring disease-specific classification performance. In general, a combination of these datasets allows extensive experimentation, which means that the given framework could be tested in both balanced and imbalanced conditions and, therefore, become more robust and applicable in practice.

Hybrid deep feature extraction

This paper utilized deep feature extraction techniques with two currently state-of-the-art convolutional neural network (CNN) models, MobileNetV233 and ResNet10134, to obtain discriminative features of banana leaf diseases. The two models were pre-trained on ImageNet and then fine-tuned on the banana leaf datasets to fit the target domain.

MobileNetV2 based features

The efficiency and accuracy of MobileNetV2 make it one of the backbones of feature extraction because they are especially well-suited to agricultural settings, where time and resource constraints can be a limitation. In contrast to conventional CNNs, MobileNetV2 uses depth wise separable convolutions and inverted residual blocks with linear bottlenecks that significantly reduce the quantity of operations and parameters but have no effect on discriminative power. Attention weighted feature fusion

MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 learn to extract different but complementary features of the images of banana leaves. MobileNetV2 is small and capable of capturing finer lesion features with computational efficiency whereas ResNet101 offers more contextual information using its residual learning framework. In order to leverage both models, a feature fusion plan was developed such that the resulting representation is sensitive to local detail as well as at the semantic level.

During the first stage, feature vectors of both networks were normalized and added together to create a joint representation. In particular, the MobileNetV2 1280-dimensional vector and the ResNet101 2048-dimensional vectors were fused to form a 3328-dimensional fused one. Such a concatenation is guaranteed to retain the richness of both models, but it also opens the risk of overlap in the feature space which can adversely affect the efficiency of the classifier. To overcome this constraint, a modification was proposed which is the use of an attention-weighted fusion mechanism. Rather than averaging the two sets of features, a learnable coefficient was used to dynamically trade-off the contribution of MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 in training. By doing this, the mechanism facilitates the fusion process to focus on most applicable features on a specific disease category. As one example, in instances where finer lesion textures are more discriminatory, MobileNetV2 features are prioritized and those diseases are characterized by a larger change in leaf pattern, the ResNet101 feature is more heavily weighted.

where β ∈ [0,1] is a trainable coefficient that dynamically balances contributions from MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 during training. This ensures that the final representation emphasizes the most informative features depending on the dataset and disease type. Lastly, dimensionality reduction was also done to the fused vector using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to further enhance efficiency. PCA, having retained 95% of the variance, has not only reduced the computational complexity, it also assists in removing the redundant information, thus, the resultant feature space is more compact and discriminative. The result is a strong representation with the complementary strengths of the two architectures to achieve better classification performance in a wide range of conditions. Proposed deep neural network

In the present study pre-trained models were modified by freezing and altering the final classification layers. The completely connected layers of the base networks were substituted with redesigned ones depending on the set of data. Data preprocessing and augmentation methods were used to enhance robustness and generalization prior to the images (224 × 224 × 3) being fed to the pre-trained networks to extract features automatically. The tailored classification head, as shown in Fig. 1, is made up of a series of layers: the first layer is a batch normalization layer to normalize the input distribution, then there is a flattening layer to transform the feature maps into one-dimensional vectors. To increase flexibility of the model, two layers of 512 units with ReLU activation functions were added, and another layer of batch normalization was added to stabilize and speed up training. A dropout rate of 0.2 was added to minimize the overfitting, randomly deactivating neurons throughout the training. Lastly, the output layer consisted of two neurons whose activation function was a Softmax, which related to the categorization between banana leaf diseases.

Experimental setup

All the experiments were performed using a workstation with the NVIDIA GPU (e.g., RTX 3080 with 10 GB VRAM), Intel i7 processor, and 32 GB RAM with the Linux-based operating system. It was implemented in Python with TensorFlow and PyTorch frameworks alongside the supporting libraries of scikit-learn and OpenCV to preprocess and evaluation. The input images were scaled to 224 × 224 × 3 and normalized and then were fed into the models. To reduce the issue of class imbalance and improve generalization, data augmentation techniques of rotation, horizontal and vertical flipping, scaling and brightness adjustments were used. The training was done with the Adam optimizer, an initial learning rate of 0.0001, a 32-sized batch, and terminated by early stopping to avoid overfitting with a maximum number of 100 epochs. The proposed models, i.e., modified MobileNetV2, ResNet101 and the fusion framework were trained and tested in the same conditions to allow fair comparison. Stratified 80:20 train-test splits were used to evaluate model performance, and 5-fold cross-validation was performed to guarantee robustness and reproducibility. Measures of evaluation accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and confusion matrices were used to give a detailed evaluation of the performance in classification. A deployment workflow figure has been included and accompanied by a deployment feasibility table. The hybrid model has 12.7 M parameters, which is much less than the traditional architectures, and hence, it is applicable in real-time mobile inference. Ablation experiments

The findings of a study on ablation that investigated the effect of fine-tuning depth on the model performance in terms of accuracy and F1-score are presented in Fig. 8. With a frozen backbone, the model is associated with 94.5% accuracy and F1-score of 0.9201, which means a lack of flexibility. Semi-fine-tuning increases the accuracy to 95.23% and F1-score to 0.9432 which is a better feature refinement. Interestingly, full fine-tuning has a slightly higher accuracy, 96.01% but has a lower F1-score of 0.9152, which implies that it may be overfitting or imbalance handling. Hybrid fine-tuning to selectively fine-tune certain layers with frozen layers gives the highest accuracy of 98.28% and the maximum F1-score of 0.9843. These results demonstrate that hybrid fine-tuning is the best tradeoff approach in terms of generalization and specialization, as it works better than partial or full fine-tuning in the sense of utilizing pre-trained knowledge and adapting to task-related characteristics.

Effect of fine tuning on model performance.

Hybrid fine-tuning is an extension of the freezing technique where the low-level layers of the network (representing general features such as edges, shapes, and textures) are frozen, and the deeper task-specific layers are learned with discriminative high-level features. The method maintains the strength of pre-trained representations and customizes the model to novel domain-specific features without overfitting, which may happen during full fine-tuning. This selective adjustment enables this model to optimize the trade-off resulting in a higher level of accuracy and F1-score as shown in Fig. 5 than other fine-tuning approaches.

Figure 9 illustrates that input image resolution has an impact on model performance in accuracy and F1-score. However, the results indicate a clear rising pattern: performance is the weakest in 128 × 128 with an accuracy of 91.2. and F1-score of 89.0, probably because there is no detail in low-resolution pictures. The accuracy also increases to 93.5% and 95.1 as it reaches the resolution of 160/160, 192/192 with the respective F1-score of 90.5 and 94.3, suggesting that the finer disease patterns are recognized better. This is highest at 224 × 224 resolution where the accuracy and F1-score are 98.2 and 98.4%, respectively, which indicates that increased resolution is more informative to yield more dependable classification. These results point to the relevance of input resolution in improving the performance of the model which means that the bigger the image size, the more discriminative features are resolved by the network, and ultimately, the model will have a higher accuracy to detect the disease.

Conclusion and future work

This paper suggests a hybrid deep learning model that comprises MobileNetV2 and ResNet101 with attention-enhanced bottlenecks, dilated convolutions, multi-scale pooling, and feature fusion using PCAs to effectively and efficiently classification of banana leaf disease. The framework has been strictly tested on three benchmark datasets, such as Banana and Banana Leaf, Banana Disease Recognition and Banana LSD with a well-crafted preprocessing pipeline. The experimental outcomes have shown that the given method works much better than ten popular deep learning structures and some state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods. The hybrid model, with accuracy of 98.28, F1-score of 98.43 and AUROC of 0.9997 is proven to be highly accurate and computationally efficient as it only needs 12.7 M parameters, converging with only 15 epochs, thus making it appropriate in the real-world application in the precision agriculture system.

Although the suggested framework has been delivered to promise, it still has some weaknesses that leave open for further studies. To begin with, the experiments that have been conducted to date have been based on curated datasets; to achieve a wider applicability, it will be required to apply the method to large-scale field images captured in diverse lighting, occlusion and noise levels. Second, generative models, e.g., GANs or diffusion models, may be used to tackle the issues of data scarcity and class imbalance, which would also improve robustness. Third, the framework can be further simplified to a lightweight and edge-optimized implementation to be used in real-time on a mobile system, and IoT-based monitoring systems by farmers. In addition, it might be possible to involve multimodal sources of data, e.g., hyperspectral imaging, soil health indicators, and climatic conditions, to make disease prediction more robust than using leaf symptoms. Lastly, it can also be investigated in the future that self-supervised learning and domain adaptation techniques can be used to improve transferability between various varieties of crops and geographical locations.
