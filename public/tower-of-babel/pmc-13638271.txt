Serum versus lithium-heparin plasma for lipid testing: matrix-related bias and calculated LDL-C classification
Authors: Nejd Kouched, Lobna Jmal, Khedija Zaouche, Amel Dhieb, Raida Guidara, Aouatef Jmal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo assess whether serum and lithium-heparin plasma can be used interchangeably for routine lipid testing and whether matrix-related bias affects calculated low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol (LDL-C) classification near clinical decision thresholds.MethodsThis prospective paired-sample method-comparison study included 104 fasting adult ambulatory patients referred for routine lipid testing. Serum and lithium-heparin plasma were collected during the same venipuncture and analysed on a Cobas 6000 c501 analyser. LDL-C was calculated using the Friedewald equation when triglycerides (TGs) were <4.5 mmol/L (n=103). Agreement was assessed using paired testing, Passing-Bablok regression, Bland-Altman analysis, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), Cohen kappa and McNemar testing at selected decision thresholds. Interchangeability was interpreted using analytical bias, measurement uncertainty, limits of agreement and threshold discordance.ResultsLithium-heparin plasma yielded lower total cholesterol and calculated LDL-C than serum, with mean biases (plasma - serum) of −0.15 mmol/L (−3.06%) and −0.14 mmol/L (−4.77%), respectively. TGs and HDL-cholesterol did not differ significantly. Serum-plasma correlations and ICCs were high. LDL-C classification was discordant in 3/103, 2/103 and 7/103 paired samples at thresholds of 1.4, 1.8 and 2.6 mmol/L, respectively; all discordances classified plasma below and serum above the threshold.ConclusionsUnder these local analytical and pre-analytical conditions, the mean bias was modest but directional and sufficient to alter calculated LDL-C classification in a minority of samples near decision thresholds. Laboratories using lithium-heparin plasma for lipid testing should verify matrix agreement locally, document the specimen matrix and avoid alternating serum and plasma during longitudinal monitoring unless equivalence has been demonstrated.Keywords: CHOLESTEROL, LIPIDS, LIPOPROTEINS

Introduction

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol (LDL-C) remains the main therapeutic target in dyslipidaemia management, with increasingly low treatment goals in patients at high or very high cardiovascular risk.1 As decision margins become narrower, small analytical or pre-analytical differences may influence whether a result is interpreted as above or below a treatment threshold.

Routine lipid testing is often performed on either serum or lithium-heparin plasma. Lithium-heparin plasma can improve laboratory workflow because it allows several biochemical analyses to be performed from a single tube and may shorten turnaround time. However, manufacturer acceptance of more than one specimen type does not prove strict interchangeability under local pre-analytical and analytical conditions.2 3

We therefore compared paired serum and lithium-heparin plasma samples for total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C) and calculated Friedewald LDL-C on a Cobas 6000 system, and assessed the effect of matrix-related differences on classification at selected lipid decision thresholds.

Methods

This prospective paired-sample method-comparison study was conducted at a single tertiary-care hospital in December 2025. Adult ambulatory patients referred from consultations for routine lipid testing were included after a 12-hour fast. The study population comprised 104 patients (mean age 61.3±12.9 years, range 28–89 years; 72 women and 32 men). No formal sample-size calculation was performed; the sample size was determined by eligible paired routine samples collected during the study period. The study should therefore be interpreted as an exploratory local verification study performed according to CLSI EP09c principles rather than a universal equivalence study.4

For each participant, one 5 mL plain tube without clot activator or gel separator and one 2.5 mL lithium-heparin tube without gel separator were collected during the same venipuncture. Both tubes were manufactured by Plastique Gosselin Tunisie. Catalogue references were not available. The plain tube lot number was 240807, and the lithium-heparin concentration was 17 IU/mL. The serum tube was drawn before the heparin tube to minimise anticoagulant carryover, consistent with pre-analytical recommendations.5

Serum was allowed to clot for 30 min. Both tubes were centrifuged simultaneously at 3000 g for 10 min at 20 degrees C, and analyses were completed within 2 hours after centrifugation. Samples with haemolysis, icterus or lipaemia, assessed by analyser HIL indices according to assay-specific manufacturer criteria, were to be excluded; none met exclusion criteria.6–8

TC, TG and HDL-C were measured using homogeneous enzymatic assays on a Cobas 6000 c501 module (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany).6–8 Reagent lots were 938 182 for cholesterol, 916 901 for TG and 894 023 for HDL-C. Calibration was performed using C.f.a.s. lot 80551500; quality-control materials were PreciControl ClinChem Multi 1 lot 75 876 200 and PreciControl ClinChem Multi 2 lot 75 874 700. Internal quality-control means and coefficients of variation were: TC 2.55 mmol/L (CV 5%) and 4.49 mmol/L (CV 4%); TG 1.35 mmol/L (CV 5%) and 2.43 mmol/L (CV 6%) and HDL-C 0.763 mmol/L (CV 8%) and 1.47 mmol/L (CV 8%). Combined measurement uncertainty was 5.02% for TC, 5.08% for TG and 5.93% for HDL-C. National external quality assessment showed method-based ratings of A+ and A for cholesterol, A+ and A for TG, and B+ and B+ for HDL-C at normal and pathological levels, respectively.

LDL-C was calculated using the Friedewald equation (LDL-C=TC - HDL-C - TG/2.2) when TG was <4.5 mmol/L, leaving 103 valid paired LDL-C results.9 The estimated combined uncertainty for calculated LDL-C was 10.50% for serum and 10.96% for lithium-heparin plasma.

Agreement was assessed according to CLSI EP09c principles using paired comparisons, Passing-Bablok regression and Bland-Altman analysis.4 Relative bias was calculated using serum as the reference matrix. Interchangeability was judged by considering systematic bias, measurement uncertainty, 95% limits of agreement and threshold discordance at clinically relevant lipid decision thresholds. Discordance was evaluated at TC 5.2 mmol/L10 and LDL-C 1.4, 1.8 and 2.6 mmol/L. ICCs were calculated for continuous agreement; Cohen kappa and exact McNemar tests were used for categorical classification. Extreme discordant points were reviewed against the original analytical records. No sample identification error, transcription error or analytical interference was identified; therefore, these observations were retained in the analysis. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement: patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans for this laboratory method-comparison study.

Results

A total of 104 paired serum/plasma samples were analysed for directly measured analytes; one pair was excluded from LDL-C analysis because TG was >=4.5 mmol/L. Discussion

This paired-sample comparison showed that lithium-heparin plasma underestimated TC and calculated Friedewald LDL-C compared with serum, whereas TG and HDL-C were not significantly affected. The effect was modest in absolute terms, but it was directional and large enough to alter calculated LDL-C classification in a subset of samples near decision thresholds.

The practical issue is therefore not only statistical significance, but whether specimen matrix switching can mimic a clinically meaningful LDL-C reduction. A mean calculated LDL-C bias of −0.14 mmol/L would not be expected to change treatment in all patients. However, it is close to the approximate combined uncertainty at the 1.4 mmol/L decision level and the Bland-Altman limits of agreement for calculated LDL-C (−0.42 to 0.14 mmol/L) are potentially relevant near low LDL-C targets. In longitudinal monitoring, switching from serum to lithium-heparin plasma could give the appearance of improved LDL-C control, and in audit settings it could slightly increase the apparent proportion of patients achieving target.

Accordingly, the key message of this report is not that global agreement between matrices is poor because correlation and ICC values were high, but that a small systematic matrix effect can interact with fixed decision limits for calculated LDL-C. The separate threshold-discordance table therefore highlights the practical scenario most relevant to clinicians and laboratories: classification close to LDL-C targets and longitudinal monitoring when specimen matrix is changed.

These findings are consistent with earlier reports of slightly lower lipid concentrations in heparinised plasma than in serum.2 3 Several mechanisms may contribute including dilutional effects from anticoagulant, differences related to clotting and platelet activation, matrix effects and assay-specific behaviour. The larger effect for calculated LDL-C than for directly measured TG or HDL-C is also plausible because a matrix-related difference in TC is propagated through the Friedewald equation while analytical variability in TC, HDL-C and TG contributes cumulatively to the LDL-C estimate.9 11

Current lipid guidelines do not usually adjust numerical LDL-C targets for inter-assay or matrix-specific variability. They also emphasise relative LDL-C reduction from baseline and may support the use of alternative lipid markers such as non-HDL-C or apolipoprotein B when standard tests are unreliable or discordant.1 12 Our results should therefore not be interpreted as proving that every plasma-serum difference mandates treatment intensification. Rather, they show that laboratories should avoid assuming interchangeability between matrices unless this has been verified locally, particularly when values are close to decision thresholds.

The study has strengths relevant to laboratory practice: paired collection during the same venipuncture reduced biological variation, analyses were performed on the same analyser/reagent workflow and agreement was assessed with complementary methods recommended for method-comparison studies.4 However, the findings are method-specific. The study was single-centre and evaluated one analyser, reagent-lot workflow and lithium-heparin tube type. LDL-C was calculated rather than directly measured; therefore, the observed LDL-C difference reflects propagation of TC, HDL-C and TG measurements through the Friedewald equation and cannot automatically be extrapolated to directly measured LDL-C or newer LDL-C equations. Detailed lipid-lowering therapy and cardiovascular risk category were not systematically available, limiting direct assessment of therapeutic consequences. Finally, in this paired protocol the lithium-heparin tube was centrifuged at the same time as the serum tube after clotting; this does not fully reflect the workflow advantage of immediate lithium-heparin plasma centrifugation and should be considered a pre-analytical limitation. The results should therefore prompt local verification rather than universal correction factors.

Conclusion

On this analytical system, lithium-heparin plasma yielded systematically lower TC and calculated Friedewald LDL-C values than serum, whereas TGs and HDL-C remained comparable. The observed bias was modest but directionally consistent and sufficient to alter calculated LDL-C classification in a small proportion of samples near decision thresholds. Laboratories using lithium-heparin plasma for lipid testing should verify matrix agreement locally, document the specimen matrix and avoid alternating serum and plasma during longitudinal monitoring unless equivalence has been demonstrated.
