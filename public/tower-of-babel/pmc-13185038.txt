Evaluation of AI tool assisting primary healthcare physicians to diagnostic and treatment tasks
Authors: Tianran Wang, Jinyu He, Yiran Wang, Ning Zhang, Kaiyuan Chen, Wannian Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary healthcare (PHC) plays an important role in the healthcare system, yet the shortage of qualified primary healthcare physician (PHP) restricts the quality of diagnostic and treatment services provided by PHC institutions. The rapid development and prevalence of artificial intelligence (AI) offer a solution to this challenge. Existing research primarily focused on two themes: evaluating the performance of AI tools and investigating PHPs’ attitudes and use intention to such AI. There are three main modes used to evaluate the performance of AI tools: evaluation of single AI tool, comparison across different AI tools and comparison between AI tool and physicians. Confusion metrics and examination are two types of indicators primarily used to assess these products. PHPs generally hold positive attitudes and use intentions towards AI tools. According to the technology acceptance model, influential factors of PHPs’ attitudes and use intention can be categorised into usefulness and ease of use. Limitations and future research directions are also discussed in this article, which provides insights for future research and practice to improve AI tools assisting PHPs in diagnostic and treatment tasks and promote AI adoption in PHC.Keywords: Primary Health Care, General Practice, Family Medicine, Medical Informatics, Public Health Informatics

Introduction

Primary healthcare (PHC) is the basic healthcare service that is universally accessible to individuals in the community at an affordable cost, which is the first and most accessible point of contact for patients.1 Primary healthcare physicians (PHPs) are physicians working in PHC institutions. Due to limitations in salary and career development, medical students are reluctant to work as PHPs, resulting in a shortage of qualified PHPs in many PHC institutions, which constrains the service quality of PHC institutions.2

The rapid development and widespread adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) have offered a potential solution to this shortage.3 AI tools have been rapidly developed and increasingly used in PHC to assist PHPs in diagnosis and treatment.4 For example, AI tools can assist PHPs in identifying patients with heart failure who are at high risk of mortality and recommending timely referral to specialists for further treatment, thereby reducing the risk of mortality from heart failure.5 In addition, AI tools can help PHPs conduct community-based screening for diabetes, enabling earlier detection and timely intervention, which may alleviate patients’ suffering and economic burden.6 Another example is AI-based decision support systems that provide recommendations on whether a patient may have orofacial pain, thereby helping physicians improve diagnostic accuracy.7

Existing studies evaluated the performance of AI tools and PHPs’ attitudes and use intention towards AI.8 This paper focuses on AI tools that provide suggestions about diagnosis and treatment for PHPs and reviews existing studies evaluating such AI tools, which provide insights for policymakers, companies, researchers and PHPs.

Evaluation of AI performance

Evaluation modes

Existing research primarily adopted the following three modes to evaluate the performance and application effectiveness of AI tools assisting PHP in diagnosis and screening (figure 1).

Three modes used to evaluate AI tools. AI, artificial intelligence.

Evaluation of single AI tool

The most common evaluation approach adopted in existing research is to assess the performance of an AI tool in diagnosing specific diseases.9 In research developing AI tools, datasets were often divided into train, validation and test sets. Train and validation sets are used to train AI, and the test set is used to examine AI performance. If the performance of an AI tool meets a certain gold standard on one or more indicators, such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) or F1 score (eg, accuracy no less than 80%), the AI tool may be considered suitable for application in PHC practice.

Comparison across different AI tools

The comparison of different AI tools is also a common evaluation approach in existing research, which helps reveal the comparative performance of various AI tools.5 6 The results of this evaluation mode assist developers in identifying the strengths and weaknesses of their AI tools and in improving these products in a targeted manner. Many current studies employ this evaluation mode to compare the performance of the AI tools that they developed with alternative ones in specific tasks. If their AI tools demonstrate superior performance, it indicates application values and academic contributions of their works.

Comparison between AI tools and physicians

Existing research also evaluates AI tools by comparing their performance with that of different types of physicians.7 10 11 The physicians used for comparison primarily include expert specialists, novice specialists, PHPs and medical students. Such a comparison helps to assess the capability that a certain AI tool has achieved and identify which groups of physicians are appropriate to be supported by it. For example, an AI tool whose performance is superior to expert specialists but higher than PHPs might be suitable for assisting PHPs in clinical practice. The role of AI is not to replace PHPs, but rather to work as a decision-support system that provides suggestions for physicians. Moreover, higher accuracy of AI tools compared with PHPs does not definitely indicate that the AI tool is appropriate to assist PHPs. More indices, including CIs, sensitivity metrics and external validation criteria should be considered.

Evaluation criteria

Existing research primarily uses two categories of index to assess AI tools used in PHC.

Confusion metrics

Confusion metrics are the most commonly used tools in existing research to evaluate AI performance. This metric includes four values presenting the relationship between predicted results of AI and the actual: true positive, false positive, true negative and false negative (figure 2).

Confusion metrics.

In confusion metrics, ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ refer to AI’s predictions, while ‘true’ and ‘false’ indicate whether the predictions are correct or incorrect. Based on the four values, a series of indicators reflecting AI’s performance can be calculated. Commonly used indicators in existing research include accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, F1 score, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and area under the precision-recall curve.6 12 13 The definitions and formulas for these indicators are shown in table 1.

Standard examinations

Passing various examinations is an essential step for medical students to obtain the qualifications to become doctors, and some existing studies also evaluate AI tools based on their performance in examinations of relevant major.11 14 These studies required certain AI tools to answer questions from related examinations and accordingly judge the suitability of these AI tools for assisting PHPs in clinical practice. Findings from these studies suggest that an increasing number of AI tools are now able to pass examinations in relevant majors, such as general practice and family medicine. However, there is no evidence indicating that their performance in these examinations surpasses that of PHPs.

Physician attitudes and use intentions towards AI

AI sometimes encounters resistance from both organisations and individuals. For organisations, AI might be misaligned with organisational culture, raise ethical concerns or lead to overreliance on AI among staff. For individuals, concerns about job displacement might generate anxiety, and the ‘black box’ processes of AI might result in algorithmic aversion. Therefore, in the PHC context, it is necessary to accurately assess PHPs’ attitudes towards AI in order to refine AI tools and reduce resistance from PHC institutions and PHPs. Existing research also investigated PHPs’ attitudes and intentions towards AI.15 Although PHPs generally hold a positive attitude towards AI, they also express several concerns, such as potential bias and errors, increased workload resulting from AI adoption, the lack of patient-centred communication and unclear regulations regarding responsibility.815,17

Based on the technology acceptance model (TAM), this study integrates the factors influencing PHPs’ attitudes towards and intention to use AI. TAM categorises the factors influencing users’ attitudes towards and intentions to use digital products into two dimensions: usefulness and ease of use.18 Furthermore, TAM proposes that when users perceive having a higher level of usefulness and ease of use from a digital product, they tend to hold more positive attitudes towards it, and consequently show higher intention to use it.18 Although the TAM has not yet been applied to examine PHPs’ attitudes and use intention towards AI, it has been used to investigate other physicians’ attitudes and use intentions regarding AI. For example, existing research employed TAM examining the influence of factors, such as content quality and technology support, on physicians’ attitudes towards and use intentions regarding AI. Therefore, applying TAM to study physicians’ attitudes and use intentions towards AI in PHC settings is feasible.19 This article applies TAM to synthesise the factors influencing PHPs’ attitudes and use intentions towards AI, providing researchers and practitioners with an integrated framework to better understand how to promote AI adoption in the clinical practice of PHC (figure 3).

Usefulness

Usefulness refers to the extent to which PHPs perceive an AI tool can enhance their efficiency and effectiveness to complete diagnostic and treatment tasks, which mainly reflected in aspects in existing research.21

First, AI can provide reference information for PHPs to diagnose and treat diseases.22 Based on big data and algorithms, AI can predict the diseases of patients and accordingly provide treatment plans, which offers reference for PHPs. Compared with PHPs making independently handle diagnostic and treatment tasks, those supported by such reference information can complete these tasks with higher efficiency. Additionally, such references can help PHPs avoid some errors in diagnosis and treatment decisions, which enhances the effectiveness of their work.

Second, AI can assist PHPs to process complex information.23 PHPs sometimes need to process complex information, such as multiple laboratory results or medical images. However, PHPs generally have weaker capabilities to appropriately process such information compared with doctors in tertiary hospitals. AI can support PHPs by helping them process such information more quickly and accurately, thereby enhancing both their efficiency and effectiveness to handle diagnostic and treatment tasks.

Third, AI can automatically handle repetitive works for PHPs.24 PHPs often need to handle repetitive duties such as writing medical records and work reports. These mechanical and monotonous tasks are not technically demanding but consume a considerable amount of time. AI can assist PHPs by automatically handling such tasks, which reduces their workload and allows them to dedicate more time to providing diagnostic and treatment services for patients. This contributes to improving PHPs’ efficiency and effectiveness to diagnose and treat diseases for patients.

Ease of use

Ease of use refers to the extent to which PHPs perceive that it is easy to use AI in their works. The quality and functions of AI tools mainly influence PHPs’ attitudes and use intention towards AI through usefulness. To contrast, human-AI interaction and the management of PHC institutions primarily influence their attitudes and use intentions via ease of use. Based on existing research, main factors influencing AI’s ease of use can be sorted into seven categories.

Convenience of operation

The convenience of operating AI tools is an important factor influencing PHPs’ attitudes towards and intention to use these products.22 First, companies developing AI tools could adopt user-centred design to facilitate PHP to operate these products. For instance, some AI tools assisted diagnostic systems need PHPs to manually input patients’ chief complaints into, while others can automatically extract such information from conversations between PHPs and patients. PHPs are more likely to choose the latter, as it saves time and effort. In addition, it is easier for PHPs with higher levels of digital literacy to use AI. Therefore, PHC institutions can provide training programmes to enhance PHPs’ digital literacy, thus improving their perceived ease of use of AI.

Interpretability or transparency

Interpretability or transparency refers to the extent to which the process by which AI makes decisions and judgments can be understood by PHPs.25 A lack of transparency in how AI predicts diseases and proposes treatment plans may lead PHPs to distrust the information provided by AI. Developers can adopt explainable AI to present the reasoning and decision-making processes of AI to PHPs, thereby improving the transparency of these products and enhancing PHPs’ attitudes towards and intention to use them.

Integration

Patients’ information often needs to be transmitted across multiple systems used in PHC.8 For example, laboratory results and medical images need to be transmitted to the systems used by PHPs for writing medical records and issuing prescriptions. The prescriptions need to be transmitted to the pharmacy system. Therefore, AI tools used by PHP should be easily integrated into current information systems, which requires interfaces having high connectivity with other systems currently used in PHC institutions.

Safety

Concerns regarding data and privacy safety are also important factors influencing PHPs’ attitudes towards and intention to use AI.16 AI tools should handle patient and physician data appropriately in accordance with regulations and ethical standards, ensuring that such information is not disclosed or misused. In addition, AI tools should possess strong capabilities to resist external intrusions to ensure that stored data are safeguarded from hacking or theft.

Ethics

Ethical concerns are also barriers to AI adoption.15 The diagnostic results and treatment suggestions provided by AI are not completely accurate. When AI generates incorrect suggestions and PHPs accordingly make inaccurate diagnoses or provide inappropriate treatment plans, accountability and responsibility for the failure of health services remain unclear. Government should establish clear regulations to define responsibility in such cases. For instance, who would be held liable if AI makes an incorrect diagnosis, whether it be the AI developer, the PHP or the PHC institution.

Professional identity

Existing research found that when AI reflects strong capabilities in diagnosing and treating diseases, it might induce AI anxiety among PHPs, which means they are concerned about being replaced by AI and losing their jobs in the future.26 Such anxiety will negatively affect PHPs’ attitudes towards and intention to use AI. Therefore, companies designing AI tools should clearly position their products as assistants to PHPs, ensure PHPs’ control of AI and improve PHPs’ human-AI interaction experience, thereby reducing PHPs’ AI anxiety. For example, allowing AI to communicate with PHPs through a cartoon-like digital human image might help increase PHPs’ trust in AI and reduce their anxiety towards AI.

Economic cost

High economic cost that PHC institutions cannot afford is also a barrier to AI adoption.4 6 27 The economic cost of using AI does not only include the price of AI tools, but also the total costs of purchasing, installing, maintaining and operating AI tools, as well as providing training for PHPs.4 Therefore, PHC institutions need to weigh all these costs against their financial capacity and the potential benefits of AI adoption, thus determining whether to provide AI for their PHPs.6 27

Limitations and future research

Although existing studies have evaluated the performance and quality of AI tools used in PHC and explored PHPs’ attitudes and intentions towards AI adoption, several important limitations remain.

First, there is no standard dataset for evaluating AI performance. Most studies rely on internally generated test sets, but variations in dataset scale, representativeness and quality reduce comparability and undermine evaluation credibility.28 Future research should prioritise the development of benchmark case sets tailored to PHC settings to enable consistent, reproducible and clinically meaningful assessments of AI tools. Moreover, an additional index can also be employed to evaluate AI performance, such as the consistency of AI’s logic at each step (self-consistency), the efficiency of AI to process input data (contextual length ratio) and whether manual reviews can be conducted to optimise the decision-making process of AI (manual evaluation).

Second, the real-world effectiveness of AI in assisting PHPs is still unclear. Current studies mostly evaluate AI tools in isolation, rather than assessing whether AI-assisted PHPs perform better in diagnosis and treatment than those without AI support. Since AI itself cannot independently issue diagnoses or prescriptions, evaluating PHP-AI collaborative performance is more aligned with PHC practice. Future research should adopt robust designs, such as pragmatic trials or real-world observational studies, to examine the actual clinical impact of AI assistance on PHPs’ performance and patient outcomes.

Third, some frameworks and tools used to evaluate AI in comprehensive hospitals have not been applied in PHC. Existing research has developed evaluation frameworks and tools to assess the performance and feasibility of AI used in comprehensive hospitals. However, these frameworks and tools have not been used to assess AI tools applied in PHC. Future research could validate the effectiveness of these frameworks and tools in PHC contexts, thereby expanding the methods available for evaluating AI tools used in PHC.

Fourth, a critical yet underexplored limitation is the lack of governance for AI evaluation in PHC. Key elements, such as who should conduct evaluations, what qualifications evaluators should possess, what exactly should be evaluated (eg, algorithms, systems, workflows) and which evaluation methods are appropriate, remain insufficiently defined. Without clear governance mechanisms, it is difficult to establish an authoritative, transparent and trusted evaluation system, which in turn impedes the safe and scalable adoption of AI tools in PHC. Future research should develop multi-stakeholder governance frameworks, define evaluator accreditation standards, clarify evaluation typologies and design practical governance tools such as regulatory sandboxes and phased evaluation pathways.

Fifth, there is no standard scale to measure PHPs’ attitude and use intention towards AI. Existing research mainly uses self-developed scales to measure PHPs’ attitudes and use intention towards AI. For example, Oh et al used seven items to assess PHPs’ attitudes,29 and Chalutz Ben-Gal employed three items to measure PHPs’ use intention.30 However, there is a lack of widely accepted standardised scales to assess these constructs. Future research could develop appropriate standardised instruments for this purpose.

Conclusion

This article reviewed studies evaluating AI tools assisting PHPs in diagnostic and treatment tasks. Existing research has mainly focused on assessing the performance and quality of these AI tools, as well as PHPs’ attitudes towards and use intention of these AI tools. The current research has mainly employed three modes and two categories of indicators to assess the performance and quality of these AI tools. Although some concerns have been expressed, PHPs generally hold a positive attitude toward these AI tools and are willing to use them in their clinical work. According to TAM, the factors influencing PHPs’ attitudes towards and use intention of these AI tools can be classified into two categories: usefulness and ease of use. This article also identifies six limitations in existing research and accordingly proposes three suggestions for future studies.
