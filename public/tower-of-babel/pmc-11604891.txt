Comprehensive characterization of neddylation related genes in cutaneous melanoma identified a novel prognostic signature for treatment outcomes, immune landscape
Authors: Min Zhang, Jian Liu, Fangxu Zhang, Qian Liang, Zhiqiang Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Neddylation, as a post-translational modification, has garnered significant attention in various tumor types recently. Few studies have investigated the involvement of neddylation-related genes (NRGs) in cutaneous melanoma (CM). Our study aims to identify prognostic NRGs and investigate their potential roles in CM. The RNA-sequencing data and corresponding clinical data of CM patients were retrieved from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database, while the expression profiles of 812 normal skin specimens were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database. The neddylation-related genes (NRGs) were extracted from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). We identified differentially expressed NRGs in CM and determined neddylation-related prognostic genes through univariate Cox regression analysis. We constructed a novel NRGs signature using LASSO-COX. The accuracy and utility of the NRGs signature were evaluated via a variety of statistical methods. Bioinformatics tools were employed to investigate the differential biological functions and signaling pathways among distinct risk groups. The expression levels of NRGs were analyzed through RT-qPCR experiments conducted in vitro. Finally, we identified an 8-NRGs signature in CM. Our prognostic model exhibited a high predictive capability for outcomes. The differences in the proportions of immune cells among subgroups were statistically significant. The in vitro experiments indicated significant differences in the expression of our NRGs. The 8-NRGs signature serves as a prognostic model for CM. Importantly, the novel biological prognostic model holds potential for personalized therapy in CM patients.

Introduction

Cutaneous melanoma (CM) is an invasive malignant tumor primarily occurring in the skin and originating from melanocytes. Among all skin cancers, it has the highest mortality rate and is characterized by its propensity for metastasis and recurrence [1–3]. In 2018, there were 287,723 new cases of cutaneous melanoma, increasing to 301,694 cases in 2020. However, by 2022, there were 97,920 new cases of CM in the United States alone, with 7,650 of these patients succumbing to the disease [4, 5]. This indicates that the incidence of CM is steadily increasing on a global scale. Despite remarkable advancements in the treatment of CM, prognosis remains unfavorable for patients with deep primary CM, regardless of ulceration, for those in stages IIB-C, and for those in stages IIIA-D with local lymph node metastases, as these patient categories bear a high risk of recurrence of approximately 35% [6, 7]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to discern more effective objectives for need prognosis and therapeutic intervention.

Neddylation, a post-translational modification, involves the conjugation of neural precursor cell- expressed developmentally down-regulated protein 8 (NEDD8) to target proteins, significantly impacting their stability, function, and interaction with other proteins [8]. This pathway is fundamental to many cellular processes, including cell cycle regulation, DNA damage response, and apoptosis [9]. In cancer biology, perturbation of the neddylation pathway has been implicated in the development and progression of various malignancies. Cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligases (CRLs), major targets of neddylation, play key roles in protein degradation. Malfunction due to dysregulated neddylation can lead to the accumulation of oncogenic proteins, fostering tumorigenesis [10]. For instance, aberrant neddylation in colorectal cancer has been associated with disease progression [11]. Considering its pivotal role in tumorigenesis, the neddylation pathway has emerged as a promising target for the development of anticancer drugs, with several neddylation inhibitors currently in preclinical and clinical evaluation [12]. Given the precise influence of neddylation-related genes (NRGs) on the therapeutic approaches and prognostic outcomes in CM patients, it is imperative to identify prognostic NRGs specifically in these patients.

In our study, we obtained RNA-sequencing data for CM from the TCGA database and expression profiles of 812 normal skin specimens from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database (https://gtexportal.org/home/). Subsequently, we identified eight differentially expressed NRGs and used them to construct a prognostic model. Furthermore, we delved into the functions and underlying mechanisms of NRGs in CM using bioinformatics methods such as gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), immunoinfiltration analysis, and chemotherapy drug sensitivity analysis.

The aim of this study was to identify prognostic neddylation-related genes (NRGs) in CM, which can provide crucial insights into the underlying signaling pathways and mechanisms implicated in this phenomenon. Such information can be utilized to formulate tailored therapeutic approaches for clinical chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

Data collection

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinical information pertaining to patients with CM were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) on February 25, 2023. Samples without available survival information or with follow-up times of 0 were excluded, we subsequently obtained 1 non-tumor sample and 455 CM samples, along with the corresponding clinical data for 455 patients diagnosed with CM. To augment the sample size of normal skin specimens, expression profiles of 812 normal skin samples were obtained from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database (https://gtexportal.org/home/). RNA expression profiles in the two databases were merged for subsequent analysis after correcting for batch effects using the “ComBat” algorithm via the R package “sva” [13]. Simultaneously, 234 genes associated with neddylation, called as neddylation-related genes (NRGs), were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

In our study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation to identify potential prognostic genes associated with neddylation and performed differential expression analysis between non-tumor samples from GTEx SKIN and TCGA and tumor samples from CM. To perform this analysis, we utilized the R package “limma” [14]. In order to identify noteworthy candidates, this threshold was set as |logFC|> 1.0 and an adjusted p-value (adj. P) < 0.05.

Construction and validation of the neddylation-related genes prognostic signature

The total dataset was divided into training and testing sets using random allocation at a ratio of approximately 6:4. The training set was employed for conducting univariate Cox regression analysis to identify prognostic genes, with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. Subsequently, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was applied to minimize the number of prognostic genes, ensuring that NRGs were not overfitting. Following that, a prognostic model was formulated using multivariate Cox regression analysis, based on with the Akaike information criteria (AIC) value after 1000 times cross validation. The optimal prognostic model included a total of eight reliable NRGs. The risk score for patients with CM was calculated using the following formula: risk score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$= \sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{n} {({\text{expi}}*\upbeta {\text{i}})}$$\end{document}=∑i=1n(expi∗βi). In this formula, the terms “exp” and “β” represent the expression levels and corresponding coefficients of the identified genes, respectively. Patients in the training set, testing set and total set were categorized into low-risk and high-risk groups, respectively, based on the median score calculated within the training set. To validate the accuracy of the signature, the following analysis were conducted. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis, along with the log-rank test, was employed to examine overall survival (OS) using the R packages “survminer” and “survival”. The prediction accuracy and reliability were evaluated by analyzing the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) using the R package “timeROC” [15]. The distribution of the expression patterns within two groups was assessed using principal component analysis (PCA). Subsequently, we further validated the accuracy and stability of the signature in the testing set and total set, respectively. Furthermore, multivariate and univariate Cox regression analysis were utilized to determine whether the risk score and clinicopathological features (such as age, stage, and gender) could serve as independent predictors of prognosis for CM. Additionally, a nomogram, integrated the NRGs signature with other relevant clinical factors, was constructed to provide a comprehensive approach to evaluate clinical outcomes and assist in clinical decision-making using the R package “rms”. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were employed to evaluate the actual clinical utility of the nomogram, in order to ascertain whether the nomogram exhibits favorable applicability in clinical practice.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To explore potential alterations in signaling pathways between the high- and low-risk groups based on the NRGs signature, we conducted functional enrichment analysis using GSEA version 4.2.3 software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/login.jsp) [16]. The annotated gene set C2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt was selected as the reference gene set. Gene sets with P < 0.01 and a false discovery rate (FDR) q < 0.25 were regarded as significantly enriched.

Analysis of the tumor microenvironment and immune infiltration

To examine the immune cell infiltration landscape in CM, a comparative analysis was performed to assess the relative abundance of 22 distinct immunocyte types. This analysis included seven T cell types, naive and memory B cells, plasma cells, and NK cells, and was conducted between two separate subgroups in CM samples. The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed for this purpose [17]. The variances in the relative abundance of the 22 immune cells between the high-risk and low-risk groups were visually represented using boxplots and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Subsequently, to assess the tumor microenvironment, the stromal, immune, and ESTIMATE scores were computed for CM samples using the ESTIMATE algorithm implemented in the R package “estimation” [18]. Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was used to identify changes in 13 immune-related pathways between the two subgroups using the R package “gsva” [19]. Furthermore, the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm, available at http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/, was employed to investigate the potential effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy in CM patients [20].

Exploring the application of the NRGs signature in treatment with targeted therapeutics and chemotherapy drugs

Targeted therapy against the BRAF gene and chemotherapy can significantly improve the overall survival of melanoma patients, our research efforts were focused on the development of personalized targeted gene therapy and chemotherapy regimens guided by risk scores. The objective was to enhance the effectiveness of treatment for individuals diagnosed with CM. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) serves as an indicator of each patient’s response to chemotherapeutic agents. A lower IC50 value suggests that the drug is more effective in targeting and inhibiting tumor cells in that patient group. To evaluate the response to targeted therapy and chemotherapy response for each patient, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was estimated using the “pRRophetic” R package and the “oncoPredict” R package [21, 22], which incorporate the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer dataset (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) and cell line screening data (https://osf.io/c6tfx/).

Cell cultures

The human melanoma cell line SK5 and MNT-1, along with human normal melanocyte cell line (PIG1), were obtained from the Shanghai Cell Bank, which is affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai, China. The SK5 melanoma cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. The MNT-1 melanoma cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA),1X MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The PIG1 cell line was cultured in Opti-MEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cell confluence and morphology were monitored frequently, and subcultures were performed when the monolayers reached 75–80% confluence.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.13). The Wilcoxon test was employed to compare the proportional differences in immune-infiltrated cells within the tumor. Pearson correlation analysis was used to investigate the relationships between different variables. Survival analysis was conducted using the KM analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis were employed to identify significant prognostic factors and evaluate their independence. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the robustness of the prognostic signature for overall survival (OS). A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001.)

Results

Identification of DEGs and construction of a prognostic neddylation-related genes signature in CM

The complete clinical information and corresponding transcriptome data from 455 CM patients were obtained from the TCGA database, including 455 CM tissues and 1 nontumor tissue. Transcriptome data of 812 normal skin specimens were obtained from the GTEx database. We conducted a comparison of the expression levels of 234 NRGs in a dataset comprising both the GTEx and TCGA databases, consisted of 813 nontumor tissues and 455 tumor tissues. Based on the predefined criteria of |logFC|> 1.0 and adj. P < 0.05, we identified a total of 182 DEGs, which have the potential to serve as prognostic genes. Out of these, 100 genes showed upregulation, while 82 genes exhibited downregulation in the tumor group. Subsequently, 455 CM cases were randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 273) and a testing set (n = 182) using an approximately equal split of 6:4. Table 2 presents the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of each CM individual diagnosed in the training, testing, and total sets. Evaluation of the prognostic model

The risk score of each CM individual in each dataset was calculated using the formula of the risk score and organized in a sequence from lowest to highest. Following this, based on whether their scores were either above or below the median values derived from the training set, CM individuals from every dataset were respectively classified into two distinct groups: high-risk and low-risk. The survival outcomes, risk classifications, and expression intensities of NRGs for each patient were respectively represented in Fig. 3A–I. This was done separately for the training set, testing set, and total set. The findings revealed a gradual increase in patient mortality corresponding to an escalating risk score. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis uncovered a noticeably improved OS in the low-risk group in comparison to the high-risk group across all datasets (Fig. 3J–L). Our model demonstrated superior predictive precision for CM survival, as substantiated by the ROC curve analysis. This analysis revealed AUC values of 0.850, 0.722, and 0.746 for the timeframes of 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years, respectively (Fig. 3N). According to the median risk score, GSEA was executed to pinpoint significantly enriched pathways in both the high-risk and low-risk groups. Discussion

CM is a highly heterogeneous tumor. The molecular heterogeneity of CM poses challenges in early diagnosis and personalized treatment. Neddylation, a vital post-translational modification process, influences various biological activities, including cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and apoptosis, which plays a significant role in cancer pathogenesis by controlling the degradation, localization, and function of a wide array of oncogenes and tumor suppressors [8, 23]. Nevertheless, to date, no studies have investigated NRGs in CM.

In this study, a signature composed of eight NRGs was established, leveraging a plethora of statistical methods applied to the date of 455 CM patient, extracted from the TCGA database and GTEx databases. Subsequently, a series of statistical methods evaluated and validated the robustness and accuracy of the 8-NRGs signature. Both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that the risk score independently influenced OS of CM. The forest plot displaying hazard ratio (HR) values, where HR was greater than one, further corroborated the role of the risk score as an unfavorable prognostic indicator. Furthermore, the ROC-AUC analysis, compared to conventional clinical factors, substantiated the high reliability and effectiveness of the 8-NRGs signature (ASB7, DTL, FBXO27, KLHL2, PSMA6, PSMB9, SPSB1, and WSB2) in predicting the survival outcomes of CM patients. Additionally, an analytical tool in the form of a nomogram was developed, capable of delivering numerical prognoses to patients through straightforward computations. This nomogram integrates an 8-NRGs signature alongside clinical data. The results emphasized a notable correspondence between the projected OS and the observed OS, reflecting the substantial predictive capacity of this nomogram.

This finding implies that intrinsic processes may differ across risk score categories, potentially accounting for the documented occurrences. In order to understand these potential mechanisms, we extended our study to examine discrepancies in functional enrichment, immune cell types, tumor microenvironment, and immune-associated functions between at high and low risk groups. The analysis result revealed that the high-risk group demonstrated activation of numerous tumor-associated signaling pathways, such as lysine degradation, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, pyrimidine metabolism, base excision repair, purine metabolism, RNA polymerase, Alzheimer’s disease, glycerolipid metabolism, melanogenesis, glycolysis gluconeogenesis, Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, mTOR signaling pathway and glycerophospholipid metabolism, as identified in the GSEA analysis. In contrast, the low-risk group displayed activation of a range of signaling pathways associated with immune-related functions and tumor-related inhibitory pathways, such as antigen processing and presentation, cell adhesion molecules (CAMs), cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, chemokine signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, intestinal immune network for IgA production, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades and RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway. Additionally, the CIBERSORT analysis revealed that the levels of memory B cells, CD8 T cells, CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T (Tfh) cells, and M1 macrophages in the low-risk group were elevated in the low-risk group, and the quantities of M0 macrophages, M2 and resting mast cells were significantly increased. Our ESTIMATE analysis revealed that tumor cells in the low-risk group demonstrated greater infiltration of both immune cells and stromal cells compared to the high-risk group. The results of tumor microenvironment and immune infiltration in our study were similar to those of previous studies [24]. Several studies have suggested that mast cells and eosinophils, when activated, contribute to tumor development, and their heightened infiltration is linked with a better prognosis for cancer patients. Several studies have indicated that higher immunogenicity and immune responses are associated with improved prognosis in cancer patients [25–27]. These observations may explain the differences in prognosis observed between the two groups.

Immunotherapy, a groundbreaking advancement in cancer treatment, has significantly improved the prognosis for patients with CM [2]. Among the diverse array of immunotherapeutic techniques, the strategy of targeting immune checkpoints has emerged as a particularly persuasive therapeutic approach in cancer research [28]. Targeted treatment, particularly focusing on the PD-1/PD-L1 axis (such as nivolumab and ipilimumab), has been endorsed as an initial treatment strategy for patients suffering from recurrent or metastatic CM [29, 30]. In contrast to the high-risk group, the low-risk group exhibited increased expression of multiple immune checkpoints including PDCD1 (PD-1), Lymphocyte Activation Gene 3 (LAG3), and CTLA4. This heightened expression suggests that patients in the low-risk group may experience more robust responses to anti-PD-L1, PD-1, and CTLA4 therapy. As a result, based on our NRGs signature, we anticipate that CM patients in the low-risk group will achieve superior survival outcomes following to immunotherapy.

Next, to investigate the application of our prognostic model in targeted therapy and chemotherapy, we evaluated the association between the NRGs signature and chemosensitivity in CM patients by analyzing IC50 values. Our findings indicated that high-risk CM patients exhibited pronounced sensitivity to BRAF-targeted therapies such as nilotinib, sorafenib, and trametinib. Conversely, members of low-risk group were more responsive to conventional chemotherapy agents, such as paclitaxel and temozolomide, while high-risk individuals exhibited higher receptiveness to docetaxel. This implies that the NRGs signature, as a prognostic indicator, could guide the treatment of CM patients. Lastly, we assessed the basal expression levels of these NRGs in vitro, and the results highlighted their potential significance in CM.

However, like any study, ours has limitations that must be acknowledged. While we identified several NRGs with potential prognostic value, further in vivo and in vitro studies are necessary to elucidate the exact mechanisms by which these genes contribute to CM progression.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the prognostic value of the NRGs signature in CM, demonstrating its ability to stratify patient outcomes based on neddylation-related genes expression profiles. These findings not only support the use of this signature as a valuable prognostic tool but also suggest that targeting neddylation pathways may offer a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention. Future research should focus on validating these results in clinical settings and exploring the mechanisms by which neddylation influences CM progression.
