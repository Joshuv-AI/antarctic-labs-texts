Does thermal ablation plus chemotherapy improve survival in small-cell lung cancer ineligible for surgery?
Authors: Mao Sun, Yu Zhang, Yangyang Tang, Shubo Han, Wanyi Zhang, Ming Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThermal ablation (TA) is a safe and feasible alternative for the treatment of non-small cell lung cancer. However, data on the clinical efficacy of TA in patients with small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) are scarce. This study aimed to explore the clinical efficacy of TA plus chemotherapy (CHT) in treating SCLC.MethodsThis is a population-based retrospective cohort study based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database. We collected data from the SEER database for 51,273 SCLC patients who received CHT from 2000 to 2019. Using propensity score matching (PSM), we balanced covariate differences between two treatment groups: CHT alone and TA plus CHT. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for overall survival (OS) and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) were generated. Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess independent prognostic indicators.ResultsAfter PSM, the TA + CHT group exhibited superior OS and LCSS compared to the CHT-only group. Age subgroup analysis revealed that for patients aged ≥70 years, TA + CHT resulted in improved OS and LCSS compared to CHT alone. For patients <70 years, a survival benefit was observed, but the difference was not statistically significant. Tumor size subgroup analysis showed that for patients with tumor sizes of 0–3 cm, TA + CHT led to superior OS and LCSS compared to CHT alone, while no statistically significant differences were observed in other tumor size subgroups. N stage subgroup analysis indicated that for N0 and N1 patients, TA + CHT had better OS and LCSS compared to CHT alone, with no statistically significant differences in the N2 and N3 subgroups. Multivariable analysis indicated that TA + CHT was an independent favorable prognostic indicator for both OS and LCSS.ConclusionsTA + CHT may be a promising treatment option for patients with SCLC, especially those aged ≥70 years with tumor size ≤3 cm, and N0-1 disease.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Thermal ablation (TA) combined with chemotherapy (CHT) was associated with improved overall survival (OS) and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS) compared with CHT alone in small-cell lung cancer (SCLC).

• The survival benefit achieved clinically meaningful survival gains reported in landmark studies of immunotherapy plus CHT such as IMpower133 and CASPIAN.

What is known and what is new?

• TA is used increasingly in thoracic malignancies and may benefit selected patients in limited-stage settings; however, evidence for SCLC remains limited and patient selection criteria are not well established.

• This study provides additional comparative evidence suggesting that TA + CHT may reduce the risk of death, with better survival benefits in specific subgroups (e.g., smaller tumors and earlier N stage), after controlling for baseline confounders.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• TA + CHT could be considered a promising option for selected SCLC patients, particularly those with tumors with size ranging from 0 to 3 cm or N0-1 nodal stage.

• Larger prospective multicenter studies are required to confirm long-term survival outcomes, refine eligibility criteria, and define standardized TA protocols for SCLC.

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC), accounting for 13% to 15% of all lung cancer cases, represents a high-grade neuroendocrine tumor. It is characterized by aggressive behavior, a tendency for metastasis, genomic instability, and a poor prognosis (1-5). The Veterans Administration Lung Study Group (VALSG) categorizes SCLC into limited-stage SCLC (LS-SCLC) and extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) (3,6). Disturbingly, the 5-year survival rate for LS-SCLC patients, who comprise one-third of cases, ranges from 20% to 25%, while the remaining two-thirds of ES-SCLC patients face a bleak 5-year survival of less than 2% (7-10). The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system improves the accuracy of SCLC staging, assisting in treatment planning and prognosis assessment (3,11,12). Initial treatment for SCLC generally involves platinum-based chemotherapy (CHT) combined with thoracic radiation, yielding objective responses in 70% of patients. Unfortunately, many patients rapidly develop drug resistance or relapse within one year (8,11).

Recent advances in the treatment of SCLC have increasingly focused on integrating immunotherapy with traditional therapies, demonstrating potential improvements in patient outcomes. The combination of immune checkpoint inhibitors with CHT has shown improved response rates and prolonged survival in patients with ES-SCLC, achieving a median overall survival (OS) of approximately 12 to 13 months (13). While the combination therapy is associated with higher incidence of treatment-related toxicity, immunotherapy-related adverse events can be recognized early, appropriately diagnosed, and effectively managed. Therefore, treatment decisions should be guided by the overall net clinical benefit, balancing improved survival outcomes against the increased risk of toxicity and the potential need for treatment modification or discontinuation in selected patients (14). Given the aggressive nature of SCLC characterized by high rates of recurrence with 60% of patients relapsing within 3 months following the combination treatment of immunotherapy and CHT (13), there remains a critical need to explore alternative treatment strategies to reduce tumor burden and extend patient survival.

In recent years, thermal ablation (TA) has emerged as a minimally invasive, highly reproducible, and fast-recovery local treatment approach. It entails inserting ablation electrodes into the tumor under image guidance, leading to the coagulation of tumor proteins, tissue necrosis, and ultimately tumor eradication (15-17). TA has been extensively applied in treating malignant lung tumors (15,17-24). The latest National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines (25) recommend image-guided TA, which includes radiofrequency ablation (RFA), microwave ablation (MWA), and cryoablation, for patients with IA-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who are ineligible for surgical resection and have not undergone stereotactic radiotherapy or curative radiotherapy. Maxwell et al. (26) demonstrated the potential effectiveness of percutaneous TA in select patients with localized SCLC. Prior research indicates that TA combined with CHT can improve survival in advanced-stage NSCLC patients (24), suggesting that TA + CHT could present a novel potential treatment strategy for SCLC patients. However, the clinical significance of TA + CHT for SCLC patients remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the clinical efficacy of TA plus CHT in treating SCLC patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1382/rc).

Methods

Study population

The Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) database (https://seer.cancer.gov/), sponsored by the National Cancer Institute, collects data on cancer diagnosis, treatment, and survival for approximately 35% of the American population (23). This study utilized SEER*Stat 8.4.0.1 to gather information from 17 cancer registries. The follow-up endpoint for all subjects was December 31, 2019. Inclusion criteria included: (I) diagnosis between January 2000 and December 2019; (II) histological or pathological confirmation of SCLC; (III) receipt of CHT by all patients; (IV) with or without TA (including cryotherapy, electrocautery, fulguration, and laser ablation); (V) complete follow-up information. Exclusion criteria comprised: (I) multiple primary cancers; (II) surgical procedures beyond TA; (III) unknown or missing essential information such as staging, survival duration, cause of death, and treatment protocols (Figure 1). As the SEER database defines staging for SCLC patients based on an older version of staging guidelines, this study reclassified the staging of SCLC patients according to the 8th edition of the AJCC TNM staging system. Clinical data of the patients included demographic characteristics, tumor features, treatment details, and survival characteristics. Patients were divided into two groups based on different treatment modalities: TA + CHT group vs. CHT group. Primary outcome measures of interest were OS and lung cancer-specific survival (LCSS). OS was defined as the time from diagnosis to death or loss-to-follow-up. LCSS was the time from diagnosis to death caused by SCLC, excluding deaths from other causes.

Propensity score matching (PSM)

PSM is primarily employed in observational clinical research to simulate a randomized controlled trial by reducing selection bias, balancing confounding factors between groups, thereby enabling more reasonable comparisons of study outcomes. This technique aims to enhance the overall strength of the research evidence. To mitigate patient selection bias, PSM is used to harmonize the baseline characteristics of two groups. The matching factors include demographic characteristics (gender, age, race, marital status), tumor characteristics (primary tumor site, laterality, histological grade, tumor size, N stage, and M stage), as well as radiotherapy. Propensity scores are calculated using logistic regression models, and a 1:3 nearest-neighbor matching approach is employed, with a caliper of 0.05 for PSM analysis. McNemar’s test was conducted to assess the balances for categorical variables before and after PSM.

Ethics statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Since the SEER database is publicly available and contains anonymized data, no institutional review was required, and we were allowed to access the SEER database for research using the private SEER ID (10466-Nov2021) after signing the SEER Research Data Agreement. Consequently, the ethics committee of our hospital exempted this study from the requirements for ethics approval or written informed consent from the participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R version 3.5.0 (http://www.R-project.org). P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. PSM was adopted to mitigate confounding factors between groups. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate OS and LCSS, and the log-rank test was utilized to assess survival differences between groups. Subgroup analysis was conducted to compare hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) among different treatment subgroups, with the results visualized using a forest plot. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed for univariable and multivariable analyses to identify prognostic factors. Variables showing statistical significance in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable regression analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 51,273 eligible patients with SCLC were included in the study, and their demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 66 years (range: 19 to 98 years), with 34.25% being over 70 years old. A majority of the patients were white (87.04%), female (50.25%), married (51.82%), and had tumors located on the right side (58.24%). The median follow-up time for the cohort was 104 months, during which 46,141 patients died, including 41,711 deaths attributable to SCLC. The median OS and LCSS for the group were 10 and 11 months, respectively. The 1-, 2-, and 3-year OS rates were 39.88%, 17.88%, and 11.81%, while the 1-, 2-, and 3-year LCSS rates were 42.73%, 20.36%, and 14.11%, respectively.

Before PSM, compared to the CHT group, the TA + CHT group exhibited lower N stage (P=0.002) and M stage (P=0.006) distributions, and a higher frequency of radiation therapy (P=0.009). Differences were also observed in tumor location and histological grade. However, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of age, gender, race, marital status, tumor size, and laterality. Following 1:3 PSM, no statistically significant differences in the matched variables were observed between the two groups, indicating well-balanced data after PSM (Table 1).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for the survival analysis. Before PSM, the TA + CHT group demonstrated superior OS and LCSS compared to the CHT group. The 1- and 2-year OS rates were 56.72% vs. 39.36% and 24.55% vs. 17.85%, respectively (P<0.001, Figure 2A). The 1- and 2-year LCSS rates were 58.70% vs. 42.65% and 27.73% vs. 20.32%, respectively (P<0.001, Figure 2B). After PSM, the TA + CHT group continued to exhibit superior OS and LCSS compared to the CHT group. The 1- and 2-year OS rates were 56.72% vs. 43.20% and 24.55% vs. 21.43%, respectively (P=0.007, Figure 2C). The 1-year and 2-year LCSS rates were 58.70% vs. 46.62% and 27.73% vs. 24.48%, respectively (P=0.02, Figure 2D).

Subgroup analysis

To further investigate the impact of TA plus CHT on survival across various subgroups, we conducted Cox proportional hazards model analysis, presenting the results in a forest plot (Figure 3). Subgroup analysis indicated that TA + CHT was associated with improved OS and LCSS in most subgroups. In the subgroup analysis based on age, patients aged ≥70 years showed significant benefits from TA plus CHT in terms of both OS and LCSS compared to the CHT group. For patients under 70 years, although there was a survival benefit, the difference was not statistically significant. In the tumor size subgroup analysis, patients with tumors sized between 0–3 cm demonstrated significantly improved OS and LCSS in the TA + CHT group compared to the CHT group. Nonetheless, in other tumor size subgroups, the differences were not statistically significant. In the subgroup analysis based on N stage, patients in the N0 and N1 subgroups exhibited an improvement in OS and LCSS when treated with TA plus CHT compared to the CHT group, while in the N2 and N3 subgroups, the differences were not statistically significant. In the subgroup analysis based on M stage, patients in both the M0 and M1 stages experienced significantly better OS and LCSS in the TA + CHT group compared to the CHT group.

Univariable and multivariable analyses

The univariable analysis identified patient age, gender, race, marital status, primary tumor site, histological grade, tumor size, N stage, M stage, radiotherapy, and treatment modality as significant factors affecting OS and LCSS (Tables 2,3). These important variables from the univariable analysis were then included in a multivariable analysis. This further analysis established patient age, gender, race, marital status, tumor size, N stage, M stage, histological grade, primary tumor site, radiotherapy, and treatment modality as independent prognostic indicators for OS and LCSS (Tables 2,3). The TA + CHT group, in comparison with the CHT group, demonstrated a 20% lower risk of death (HR: 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.91, P<0.001) and a 19% decrease in the risk of tumor-related mortality (HR: 0.81, 95% CI: 0.71–0.93, P<0.001).

Discussion

In this population-based study, we explored the clinical efficacy of TA combined with CHT in treating SCLC patients. Our findings suggest that TA plus CHT could be a viable treatment option for patients with SCLC, particularly for those aged 70 years or older, with tumors measuring ≤3 cm, and having N0–1 stage disease.

Currently, LS-SCLC is primarily treated with concurrent chemoradiotherapy, whereas ES-SCLC is mainly managed through CHT-based comprehensive treatment (7,11). Traditional treatments, however, have not markedly enhanced the survival of SCLC patients. Many patients rapidly develop drug resistance or relapse within a year of initial treatment remission (8). Despite recent advances in immunotherapy, the treatment outcomes for SCLC remain inferior to those for NSCLC, possibly due to formidable therapeutic resistance and an immunosuppressive microenvironment that have historically hampered clinical development (25-27). Notwithstanding, a recent systematic review revealed that combination of CHT and immune checkpoint inhibitors improved survival among older patients with ES-SCLC (28). Importantly, SCLC resistance is driven largely by systemic cellular and molecular mechanisms; therefore, local interventions such as TA should be viewed as a complementary strategy aimed at improving local tumor control rather than as a direct solution to the underlying systemic challenge.

In recent years, the growing application of ablative therapeutic approaches, which focus on local tumor inactivation, has emerged as a feasible treatment alternative for high-risk patients who cannot tolerate definitive chemoradiotherapy or are otherwise unsuitable for standard treatment. This approach enhances the preservation of normal organ function. Techniques such as RFA, MWA, and cryoablation have demonstrated promising outcomes in treating various malignancies. These methods offer advantages including minimal invasiveness, symptom relief, prolonged survival, and improved patient prognosis (15,16). They achieve therapeutic benefits by inducing local tissue necrosis through either heating or freezing (16). TA has been widely used in the treatment of lung cancer for about two decades, primarily in stage I NSCLC patients (18,19,21,29). The latest NCCN guidelines (25) suggest considering TA for stage IA NSCLC patients who are ineligible for surgical resection and have not undergone stereotactic or definitive radiotherapy. TA has demonstrated clinical benefit of improved survival for patients with stages II–III NSCLC (30), and its combination with CHT can also improve the prognosis of stage IV NSCLC patients (24). However, the efficacy of TA in treating SCLC patients remains less documented. Huang et al. (31) performed RFA on 49 SCLC patients with 52 lesions, concurrently with needle biopsy, with an extensive 11-year follow-up, reporting a median survival of 32 months. Maxwell et al. (26) reported a small retrospective study of nine SCLC patients receiving either RFA or MWA. Local disease group (without lymph node metastasis) had longer median survival than the disseminated disease group (47.0 vs. 5.5 months). While these studies suggest potential feasibility and benefit of TA in selected SCLC patients with localized disease, direct comparisons with our study are limited. First, prior reports were based on substantially narrower inclusion criteria (e.g. ablation was performed without adjuvant therapy). Second, sample sizes particularly in Maxwell’s study were extremely small, increasing the likelihood of cohort-specific effects and wider uncertainty around survival estimates. In contrast, our study used a large registry database to evaluate TA combined with CHT in a broader real-world population. Therefore, differences in baseline patient selection, treatment context, and sample size may partially explain the heterogeneity in reported survival outcomes.

The present study revealed statistically significant differences across several parameters between the two patient groups prior to PSM, including primary tumor site, histological grade, N stage, M stage, and radiotherapy status. After PSM, no significant differences were observed in pathological data and treatment characteristics. These findings suggest that the combination of TA and CHT may increase survival rates in SCLC patients by mitigating confounding factors. Compared to the CHT group, the TA + CHT combination reduced the risk of death by 20% and the risk of tumor-related death by 19%. This survival advantage was also apparent in subgroup analyses stratified by gender and M stage. Moreover, age-based subgroup analysis indicated that TA plus CHT significantly increased the 1-year and 2-year OS and LCSS for patients aged 70 years or older. Although a survival benefit was observed in SCLC patients younger than 70 years, this difference did not reach statistical significance, which is consistent with previous studies (24). Several reasons may explain these results. First, older patients typically present with more comorbidities and experience physiological decline associated with aging, which can affect drug metabolism, immune function, and post-treatment recovery. As TA is a localized treatment, it reduces tumor burden while sparing surrounding healthy tissues, making it particularly suitable for older patients (24). Second, the clinical and molecular characteristics of SCLC may vary across age groups, potentially influencing treatment response. However, further studies are required to ascertain the specific influence of aging on the efficacy of TA treatment.

Regarding the tumor size, our subgroup analyses showed that for patients with tumors measuring 0–3 cm, the 1-year and 2-year OS and LCSS were significantly higher in the TA + CHT group compared to the CHT group, whereas for tumors larger than 3 cm, no statistically significant differences were observed. These results are consistent with prior observations in other thoracic malignancies, and they may reflect differences in the technical feasibility of achieving effective and duration ablation. For example, a population-based study (23) in pulmonary carcinoid tumors not amenable to surgery reported improved OS and LCSS in patients with tumors of 0–3 cm, with no clear survival advantage for larger tumors. Similarly, another retrospective study (24) evaluating TA plus CHT in advanced NSCLC revealed similar survival benefit among patients with tumors of 0–3 cm. While tumor size itself is not expected to be the dominant determinant of outcome in advanced disease, small lesions are more likely to be fully encompassed by the thermal field with adequate margins, thereby supporting more reliable local control. In contrast, larger tumors may be more difficult to ablate completely and may contain more heterogeneous tissue, potentially increasing the risk of disease recurrence (16,32-34). When performing subgroup analyses based on N staging, patients with N0 and N1 disease achieved better OS and LCSS after receiving the combination of TA and CHT than those receiving CHT alone. However, those with N2 and N3 disease did not show survival benefit with the addition of TA to CHT. First, this pattern is clinically plausible because TA is primarily a local therapy and its impact is less likely to overcome the systemic disease burden and extensive nodal involvement characteristic of N2/N3 disease. Moreover, in N2 and N3 stages, a more comprehensive treatment approach targeting disseminated lymphatic disease is generally required, and TA may not cover all nodal regions. Second, lymph node metastases in N2/N3 disease may reflect greater tumor heterogeneityand a higher likelihood of resistant subclones, which may further complicate the treatment response and result in less favorable anti-tumor effects. Nonetheless, non-significant subgroup findings in the present study should be interpreted with caution, as small subgroup sample sizes may result in insufficient statistical power to detect clinically relevant differences.

Several mechanisms may underlie the observed survival benefits of combining TA with CHT in the treatment of SCLC. First, TA modalities such as RFA and MWA create high intratumoral temperature that leading to rapid, irreversible tumor cell death predominantly via coagulative necrosis. This may reduce viable tumor burden, limit local progression, and potentially delay subsequent relapse events. Concurrently, CHT provides systemic control by targeting malignant cells at local and distant sites, thereby complementing the local ablation effect when administered in combination (35-37). Secondly, beyond direct cytotoxicity, TA may influence the host’s anti-tumor response by releasing tumor antigens and modulating the tumor microenvironment, which could be synergistic with systemic therapy (38-40). Thirdly, TA may reduce the likelihood that residual viable tumor cells at the ablation margin seed further spread when paired with systemic cytotoxic therapy (41-43). Therefore, these two therapeutic approaches may complement each other, maximizing the anti-tumor effects.

The significance of this study lies in its potential to introduce a treatment option for SCLC patients, particularly those who are older or present with smaller tumors. By demonstrating the efficacy of TA combined with CHT, this study could facilitate the development of more personalized treatment strategies for this challenging patient population. Nevertheless, critical knowledge gaps persist, particularly concerning the long-term outcomes of this combination therapy and its efficacy across various SCLC subtypes. Moreover, because immune checkpoint inhibitors are increasingly integrated into SCLC management, the combination of TA with immunotherapy is an emerging future direction. A recently published case report (44) suggests a potential rationale for combining cryoablation with immunotherapy for treatment of patients with medically inoperable NSCLC, although definitive conclusions could not be drawn yet. Therefore, multicenter prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings and to define optimal treatment protocols, including the potential role of TA in combination with immunotherapy across disease stages and potentially other cancer subtypes.

In the next five years, we anticipate significant advancements in this area, with ongoing research likely to refine the role of TA in SCLC treatment. As more data emerges, the establishment of standardized guidelines that incorporate TA into the treatment paradigm for SCLC, especially for high-risk patients, may become feasible. Furthermore, advancements in imaging and ablation technologies are expected to the precision and effectiveness of these treatments, potentially leading to improved survival outcomes.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature introduces inherent biases. The SEER database omits crucial data, including smoking status, family cancer history, comorbidities, and performance scores. It also lacks specific details about the CHT and radiation therapy regimens and sequencing, number of cycles and dosages, and other factors that could potentially influence survival. Second, TA encompasses various methods such as RFA, MWA, and cryoablation, and different ablation techniques may affect survival differently. However, the SEER database does not provide detailed information regarding the type of TA used. Further data collection is necessary to explore the impact of different TA methods on survival. Third, converting historical data before 2019 to the AJCC 8th edition staging system introduces potential misclassification bias due to differences in coding schemas over time and lack of granular anatomical or biomarker details in earlier SEER registry cycles. Although raw continuous metrics were used to standardize staging, missing granular details in early diagnostic years required excluding a subset of unclassifiable cases. Finally, SEER does not capture complete stage-specific clinical staging information and does not provide stage-stratified treatment decision data. As a result, our stage-related findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory within the constraints of the available SEER variables. In summary, TA + CHT may be a promising treatment option for patients with SCLC, particularly for those aged 70 years or older with tumors ≤3 cm in size and N0-1 stage disease.

Conclusions

In this study, the combination of TA and CHT demonstrated improved survival outcomes in SCLC patients compared with CHT alone. After balancing baseline characteristics using PSM, TA + CHT was associated with OS and LCSS survival. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent advantages across both non-metastatic (M0) and metastatic (M1) disease stages, as well as across tumor size strata (including smaller tumors, 0–3 cm) and nodal burdens (N0–N1). These findings suggest that TA + CHT may represent a clinically meaningful treatment strategy for selected SCLC patients. Further large-scale, prospective multicenter studies are warranted to confirm these results and to clarify the long-term survival benefit across different SCLC subtypes and patient profiles.
