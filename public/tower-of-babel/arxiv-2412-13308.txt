Generalized Scattering Matrix Formulation and its Relationship with TARC and Maximum Power Transfer Theorem
Majid Manteghi

Abstract
This paper presents a generalized framework for analyzing arbitrary passive matching networks using a Thévenin-Helmholtz equivalent circuit. Unlike prior formulations, which impose restrictive assumptions such as diagonal impedance matrices, the proposed approach accommodates arbitrary multiport networks, including interconnected and non-diagonal impedance configurations. The scattering matrix is derived directly from the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT), demonstrating its universal applicability to general passive network configurations. Additionally, a mathematical link between the Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC) and MPTT is established, proving that TARC inherently quantifies power transfer efficiency. This formulation unifies and extends existing scattering matrix approaches, providing a robust analytical foundation for studying complex multiport systems. The results enable more flexible and accurate analyses of modern multiport networks, particularly in applications such as phased arrays and passive antenna networks.

Introduction

The rigorous analysis and design of matching networks for multiport systems are fundamental to various applications, including phased arrays, multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems, and microwave circuits. A key challenge in this domain is bridging the mathematical foundations of power transfer and wave scattering with the physical realization of passive matching networks. This paper addresses this gap by presenting a unified framework that generalizes the scattering matrix formulation and establishes its direct connection to the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT). Unlike prior approaches, which impose restrictive assumptions such as diagonal impedance matrices, the proposed framework accommodates arbitrary passive multiport networks, offering new insights into the interplay between scattering matrix properties and power transfer efficiency.

The scattering matrix has been a cornerstone of network analysis since Kurokawa’s seminal work in 1965
[
1
]
. In
Power Waves and the Scattering Matrix
, Kurokawa
defined
the scattering matrix using diagonal source impedance matrices, an approach widely adopted for analyzing independent matching circuits. However, while effective, this formulation lacks a formal mathematical proof and does not account for interconnected or arbitrary impedance configurations. Similarly, Desoer’s proof of the MPTT for
N
-port networks
[
2
]
provided a fundamental theoretical basis for power transfer, yet its relationship with the scattering matrix remains largely unexplored—particularly in the context of arbitrary passive networks.

1.1
Contributions of This Work

This paper addresses these gaps with the following key contributions:

•

A generalized scattering matrix formulation for arbitrary passive matching networks, extending its applicability to multiport systems with interconnected and non-diagonal impedance matrices.

•

A rigorous mathematical connection between the scattering matrix and the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT), demonstrating that the Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC) inherently quantifies power transfer efficiency.

By bridging these concepts, this work extends the physical significance of the scattering matrix beyond traditional interpretations. It provides a robust theoretical framework for analyzing phased arrays, passive antenna networks, and other multiport systems.

2.1

We consider the Thévenin-Helmholtz equivalent model for an
N

Z

\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}
, as shown in Figure 1(
a

b

interconnected network

\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}
exhibits
loss

\Re(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})=H(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})=\frac{\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{H}}{2}\succ 0.

(1)

This property guarantees the invertibility of
\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}

[
3
]

\Re(\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{\pm 1/2}

Z

H(\mathbf{Z})=\frac{\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}^{H}}{2}\succeq 0.

(2)

Therefore we have:

H(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}+\mathbf{Z})=\frac{(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}+\mathbf{Z})+(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}+\mathbf{Z})^{H}}{2}\succ 0.

(3)

Since
H(\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}+\textbf{Z})
is positive definite, it guarantees invertibility, which is necessary for defining well-conditioned power transfer and scattering relationships in the multiport network.

Scattering Matrix

According to the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT)
[
2
]
, the forward power wave is based on the condition that the load impedance is conjugately matched to the source impedance (substitute
Z
by
\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}
) in
4

\textbf{R}_{\text{s}}=\Re(\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}})
and
(\textbf{Z}+\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}})
are invertible. The forward wave
\textbf{V}^{+}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{+}=\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{V}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{E}

(6)

We define the vector of the reflected voltages as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}-\mathbf{V}^{+}

(7)

Substituting (
4
) and (
6
) into (
7
) we have the following.

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}\mathbf{E}

(8)

One can factor
E
from the right side of (
8
) as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}\right]\mathbf{E}

(9)

Multiply the second term in the bracket (
9
) on the right side by the unity matrix,
\textbf{U}=(\textbf{Z}+\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}})(\textbf{Z}+\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}
as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\right]\mathbf{E}

(10)

Factoring from the right side of (
10
) the common term, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})\right](\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}

(11)

Multiplying the first term of (
11

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}})^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}\right](\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}

In (

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[(2\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}})^{-1}-\mathbf{U})\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}\right](\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}

By replacing

Factoring
(\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}})^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle=\left[(2\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}-\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}})\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{R}_{\text{s}}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}\right](\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}

Simplifying the expression inside the parentheses in

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-}

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}

3
Relationship Between TARC and MPTT

Based on the original definition of the Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC),
[
5
]
it is expressed as:

\text{TARC}=\sqrt{\frac{\text{Available Power}-\text{Radiated Power}}{\text{Available Power}}}

(28)

In an antenna array, the accepted power, given by (
\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{b}
), comprises both the radiated power (
\text{P}_{\text{r}}
) and the ohmic losses. The radiation efficiency (
\varepsilon_{\text{r}}
) is defined as the ratio of radiated power to accepted power. Substituting these into the original TARC definition (
28
), we obtain:

\text{TARC}=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{\text{r}}+\varepsilon_{\text{r}}\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}}=\sqrt{1-\varepsilon_{\text{r}}\left(1-\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}\right)}

(29)

In lossless antenna arrays (
\varepsilon_{\text{r}}=1
) or for multiport networks, the accepted power is the main objective. In this case, the expression simplifies to the familiar definition presented in
[
6
]
. The relationship between TARC and the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT) is established as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma=\text{TARC}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}}\Rightarrow 1-|\Gamma|^{2}=1-\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}=\frac{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\big(\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{S}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}\big)\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{a}}=\eta

(30)

Here,
\eta
is defined as the ratio of the power delivered to the available power:

1-|\Gamma|^{2}=\frac{\mathbf{E}^{\text{H}}(\mathbf{Z}^{\text{H}}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}}^{\text{H}})^{-1}(\mathbf{Z}^{\text{H}}+\mathbf{Z})(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})^{-1}\mathbf{E}}{\mathbf{E}^{\text{H}}\text{Re}(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{s}})\mathbf{E}}=\eta.

(31)

Although most radio systems use the same antenna array for both transmission and reception, it is possible to implement two different optimized matching networks for each side
[
7
]
: for the transmitter, the antenna array acts as the load,
\textbf{Z}=\textbf{Z}_{\text{A}}
, while the transmitter serves as the source,
\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}=\textbf{Z}_{\text{T}}
. However, for the receive array, the antenna array functions as the source,
\textbf{Z}_{\text{s}}=\textbf{Z}_{\text{A}}
, and the receiver acts as the load,
\textbf{Z}=\textbf{Z}_{\text{R}}
. This distinction allows for computing two different scattering matrices:
\textbf{S}_{\text{T}}
for the transmitter and
\textbf{S}_{\text{R}}
for the receiver. Consequently, one can determine two different total active reflection coefficients (TARC),
\text{TARC}_{\text{T}}
for transmission and
\text{TARC}_{\text{R}}
for reception.

Detailed proofs of these equations are presented in
[
8
]
, which introduces a comprehensive framework for analyzing multiport networks using generalized scattering matrix formulations. Specifically, a novel method is proposed to match antenna arrays by minimizing the maximum eigenvalue of
\textbf{S}\textbf{S}^{\text{H}}
, thereby reducing the worst-case power reflection. This approach ensures robust performance across a wide range of operating conditions, making it particularly effective in scenarios where strong coupling between array elements threatens efficiency.

Additionally, the interplay between the Array Coupling Index (ACI) and the Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC) has been explored in detail. In
[
9
]
, the ACI is introduced as a metric to quantify the degree of coupling between elements in an antenna array. Further,
[
10
]
demonstrates a trade-off between TARC and ACI: by allowing some degradation in TARC, it is possible to achieve a reduction in ACI. This trade-off is particularly relevant in the design of antenna arrays, where reducing coupling is critical to achieving desired radiation patterns and minimizing mutual interference.

These findings highlight the importance of balancing multiple performance metrics when designing multiport systems, emphasizing that improvements in one parameter, such as TARC, may come at the expense of others, such as ACI. Building upon these insights, this paper establishes a rigorous mathematical framework that formalizes these trade-offs and extends the scattering matrix formulation to arbitrary passive multiport networks.

4
Conclusion

This paper presents a rigorous and generalized framework that extends the scattering matrix formulation and establishes its direct connection to the Maximum Power Transfer Theorem (MPTT) for arbitrary passive multiport networks. By leveraging a generalized Thévenin-Helmholtz equivalent circuit, we derived the fundamental mathematical conditions that any passive matching network must satisfy. Our results demonstrate that these equations hold universally, even for interconnected matching circuits and non-diagonal source impedance matrices, thereby removing restrictive assumptions found in prior formulations.

A key contribution of this work is the formal proof that the Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC), when expressed in terms of the scattering matrix, provides a direct and quantifiable measure of power transfer efficiency. This result not only establishes a fundamental analytical link between power reflection and power transfer but also introduces a
generalized mathematical tool
for characterizing impedance matching efficiency in arbitrarily coupled multiport networks.

The implications of this work extend to both theoretical analysis and practical applications. By relaxing traditional source impedance constraints, our generalized formulations enable more flexible and accurate assessments of modern multiport systems. Furthermore, the demonstrated relationship between TARC and MPTT provides a powerful framework for optimizing impedance matching and power transfer efficiency in RF and microwave engineering.

Future research could explore extending these formulations to
nonlinear, active, or time-variant networks
, where dynamic impedance variations play a critical role in high-frequency system performance. Such advancements would further broaden the applicability of this approach, particularly in adaptive antenna arrays and reconfigurable RF front-end designs.
