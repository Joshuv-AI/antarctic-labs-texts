Structural Separation and Semantic Incompatibility in the P P vs. N ​ P NP Problem: Computational Complexity Analysis with Construction Defining Functionality
Yumiko Nishiyama

Abstract
We introduce the Construction Defining Functionality (CDF) framework, a structural approach to computational complexity grounded in a rigorous distinction between syntax and semantics aligned with mathematical logic. By analyzing how syntactic definitions transform into semantic interpretations—constructed within propositional logic—and further into logical consequences, CDF characterizes problems according to the coherence and complexity of these layered mappings. The framework identifies two fundamental classes: problems with stable, compositional generative structures ( ComCDF ) that allow polynomial-time resolution, and problems exhibiting intensional, non-local constructions leading to exponential semantic explosion ( ExpCDF ). This semantic explosion originates from the combinatorial complexity inherent in propositional semantic spaces induced by syntactic recursion and dependencies. Our results suggest that the separation of 𝖯 \mathsf{P} and 𝖭𝖯 \mathsf{NP} reflects a profound structural divide in the generative principles of problems themselves—beyond mere resource bounds. And through a canonical example such as 2-SAT versus 3-SAT, we illustrate how logical representation generates semantic complexity of problem.The CDF framework thus offers a unified foundation bridging syntax, semantics, and logic, opening new avenues for complexity theory and algorithm design.

Introduction

1.1
Basic Concept of Construction Defining Functionality (CDF)

In computational theory and logic, the distinction between
intensional
and
extensional
perspectives has long shaped our understanding of functions and problem representations. Extensional approaches characterize functions through observable input-output behavior—what a function
does
. Intensional perspectives, by contrast, focus on the internal syntactic rules or constructions that generate a function—what a function
is
.

While extensional models have provided the foundation for classical complexity theory, they often obscure the structural differences that make certain problems computationally hard. In particular, many
\mathsf{NP}
-complete problems resist extensional decomposition, not merely because they are large or complex, but because their internal
semantic structure
lacks the kind of compositional coherence that makes algorithmic reasoning tractable.

In this work, we introduce the
Construction Defining Functionality (CDF)
framework as a formal model for analyzing the layered structure of computational problems. CDF is designed to capture both the intensional constructions that define a problem and the extensional behaviors that emerge from them, using a logical and topological method.

At the heart of CDF is a tripartite architecture:

•

Syntactic space
(
S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)
) — the formal definition or rule specifying a function or problem instance;

•

Semantic space
(
S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)
) — the space of configurations or models that satisfy the syntactic constraints;

•

Logical space
(
S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)
) — the inferential outcomes or decision properties derived from the semantics.

These spaces are connected via structural mappings:

\alpha_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f),\quad\beta_{f}:S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{log}}(f),\quad\gamma_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{log}}(f).

The

of these mappings reflects how well a problem’s syntactic definition supports compositional reasoning. In this way, the CDF framework identifies a new axis of classification for problems: whether their internal structure supports stable semantic growth and logical deduction (
ComCDF

We show that this distinction aligns strikingly with the boundary between classes such as

The primary contribution of this paper is to develop and apply the CDF framework to reveal structural reasons behind computational tractability and intractability. By distinguishing between external behavior and internal construction, CDF offers a complementary and potentially foundational perspective on computational complexity—one grounded not in runtime bounds, but in the geometry and logic of how problems are built.

1.2
Intensionality and Extensionality in CDF

The notions of intensionality and extensionality in the CDF framework differ from those in classical set theory.

In set theory, the distinction is mainly representational:

•

Intensional
definitions describe a set via a property.

•

Extensional
definitions list the elements of the set.

By contrast, CDF interprets intensionality and extensionality as structural and generative:

•

Intensionality
refers to the internal rules or definitions that generate system behavior.

•

Extensionality
refers to the observable outcomes or behaviors resulting from that internal structure.

This distinction highlights how simple behaviors can arise from complex internal mechanisms. In CDF, intensionality and extensionality are not just different ways of expressing the same object—they represent different levels of abstraction and causality within formal systems.

1.3
Syntax and Semantics in the CDF Framework

The Construction Defining Functionality (CDF) framework integrates a clear structural distinction between syntactic and semantic reasoning, reflecting classical logical theory while elucidating computational complexity origins.

1.3.1
Classical Syntax–Semantics Distinction

•

Syntax
concerns formal symbolic manipulations governed by inference rules, focusing on provability and derivations without regard to meaning or truth values.

•

Semantics
evaluates the truth or falsity of propositions under interpretations or models, thus addressing meaning externally to syntactic formalisms.

This classical distinction underpins the semantic explosion phenomenon observed in complex computational problems.

1.3.2
CDF Mappings Corresponding to Syntax and Semantics

The CDF framework models this distinction explicitly through its structural mappings:

\begin{cases}\alpha_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}\to S_{\mathrm{sem}},\\
\beta_{f}:S_{\mathrm{sem}}\to S_{\mathrm{log}},\\
\gamma_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}\to S_{\mathrm{log}}.\end{cases}

1.3.3
Propositional Logic as the Semantic Foundation

Semantics in CDF is constructed via propositional logic, where

S_{\mathrm{sem}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prop}},

the space of propositional formulas. This reflects the classical model-theoretic view where meanings correspond to truth assignments of propositions.

The complexity inherent in propositional logic—especially as syntactic structures become deeply recursive or intensional—directly leads to semantic explosion: an exponential growth in the number and interdependencies of propositional interpretations that must be considered.

1.3.4
Syntactic vs Semantic Reasoning and Complexity

In the CDF framework, the contrast between syntactic and semantic reasoning surfaces naturally:

•

Syntactic reasoning
(
\gamma_{f}
) operates by manipulating formal proofs and derivations. While compositional and stable syntactic structures allow polynomial-time reasoning, complex recursive or intensional definitions quickly undermine compositionality, causing syntactic reasoning to become fragile.

•

Semantic reasoning
(
\alpha_{f}
,
\beta_{f}
) evaluates truth across propositional configurations. As the syntactic input structure increases, the semantic space grows combinatorially, leading to exponential computational complexity—a core source of the semantic explosion phenomenon.

Thus, the CDF mappings illuminate the boundary between syntactic tractability and semantic intractability, framing semantic explosion as a structural consequence that syntactic forms applied to complex propositional logic.

Basic Procedure of CDF framework

This section formalizes the core concepts of the CDF framework by defining the structural space generated by a function and the interrelated components that capture its syntactic, semantic, and logical properties.

Definition 2.1

(Structural Property Sets of CDF)
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a function. The
Construction Defining Functionality (CDF)
of
f
is defined as the triple of structural property sets:

S(f)=\left(S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f),\ S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f),\ S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)\right),

where

•

S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)
is the
syntactic property set
, describing symbolic patterns and generative structure of
f
’s operation.

•

S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)
is the
semantic property set
, encoding input-output relations and interpretations.

•

S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)
is the
logical property set
, reflecting computational or proof-theoretic realizations of
f
.

Definition 2.2

(Intercomponent Structural Mappings)
.

Given the structural space S(f),
we define the
intercomponent mappings

\alpha_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f),\quad\beta_{f}:S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{log}}(f),\quad\gamma_{f}:S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)\to S_{\mathrm{log}}(f).

These mappings represent the structural flow from syntax to semantics, semantics to logic, and syntax directly to logic, respectively. They may be partial and exhibit properties such as continuity, information preservation, or combinatorial complexity.

Definition 2.3

(Formal Construction Procedure of CDF)
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a function and fix a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
. The Construction Defining Functionality (CDF) of
f
, denoted by

S(f)=\left(S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f),S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f),S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)\right),

is generated via the following formal procedure:

1.

Syntactic Component

S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)
:

S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f):=\{f^{n}(x_{0})\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\},

representing the symbolic orbit generated by iterated applications of
f
from
x_{0}
.

2.

Semantic Component

S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)
:

S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f):=\{\phi(x,y)\mid\phi(x,y):=(y=f(x)),\ x\in X,y\in Y\},

which captures the input-output relation graph of
f
.

3.

Logical Component

S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)
:

S_{\mathrm{log}}(f):=\{T(x)\in\mathcal{T}\mid T(x)\text{ is a formal computation or proof tree deriving }f(x)\},

where
\mathcal{T}
denotes a suitable class of formal derivation objects.

The intercomponent mappings are given by:

\alpha_{f}(f^{n}(x_{0})):=\phi(f^{n-1}(x_{0}),f^{n}(x_{0})),

\beta_{f}(\phi(x,y)):=T(x\vdash y),

\gamma_{f}(f^{n}(x_{0})):=\text{computation or derivation of }f^{n}(x_{0}).

This procedure canonically lifts
f
into its structural analytic space
S(f)
, embedding it into the CDF framework.

Remark 2.4

(Note on Extensional/Intensional Terminology)
.

The notion of intension and extension used in CDF should not be confused with the classical set-theoretic distinction between intensional and extensional notation (e.g.,
\{x\mid P(x)\}
vs
\{a,b,c\}
). Instead, CDF refers to the generative and observable structural dimensions of functional behavior, aligned with deeper semantic and computational layers.

Remark 2.5

(On Proof Trees and Computation Trees)
.

The logical component
S_{\mathrm{log}}(f)
consists of formal derivation objects such as
proof trees
or
computation trees
. These are hierarchical structures that represent the process by which the value of
f(x)
is logically or computationally derived.

Each node in such a tree corresponds to an inference or computation step, and the tree as a whole traces the construction of
f(x)
from its base definitions and rules. In the case of recursive functions, these trees naturally express the nested structure of calls and their justifications, forming a concrete representation of the internal logic that governs the function’s behavior.

For example, in the case of the factorial function
f(n)=n!

proceeds as follows:

\displaystyle=3\cdot(2\cdot(1\cdot 1))

\displaystyle=3\cdot 2\cdot 1=6

(CDF of a Linear Function:
f(x)=x+1

.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
,
f(x)=x+1
, and

Example 2.7

(CDF of a Recursive Function: Factorial)
.

Consider
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
defined by

f(n)=\begin{cases}1&n=0,\\
n\cdot f(n-1)&n>0,\end{cases}

with basepoint
x_{0}=3
. Then, the components of the CDF for this function are:

1. **Syntactic Component**
S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)
:

S_{\mathrm{syn}}(f)=\{f(3),f(2),f(1),f(0)\},

reflecting the recursive calls made by
f
, such as
f(3)
,
f(2)
, and so on.

2. **Semantic Component**
S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)
:

S_{\mathrm{sem}}(f)=\{\phi(n,f(n))\mid n=0,\dots,3\},

which encodes the input-output relationships of the function. For example, we have
\phi(3,6)
,
\phi(2,2)
, and
\phi(1,1)
, representing the relations
f(3)=6
,
f(2)=2
, and
f(1)=1

3. **Logical Component**

:

where
T_{n}
is the formal derivation tree showing how
f(n)
is computed. For example, for
f(3)

T_{3}
shows how
f(3)=3\cdot f(2)
is derived from
f(2)=2\cdot f(1)
, and so on.

1. **Syntax to Semantics Mapping (
\alpha_{f}
)**:
This mapping describes how the syntactic steps (function applications) are related to the semantic input-output pairs. For example:

\alpha_{f}(f^{3}(3))=\phi(2,6),\quad\alpha_{f}(f^{2}(3))=\phi(1,2),\quad\alpha_{f}(f^{1}(3))=\phi(0,1).

2. **Semantics to Logic Mapping (
\beta_{f}
)**:
The semantic relations
\phi(x,y)
are mapped to formal computation trees
T(x\vdash y)
. For example:

\beta_{f}(\phi(3,6))=T_{3},\quad\beta_{f}(\phi(2,2))=T_{2},\quad\beta_{f}(\phi(1,1))=T_{1}.

Here,
T_{3}
,
T_{2}
, and
T_{1}
are the formal derivation trees showing how the values
f(3)=6
,
f(2)=2
, and
f(1)=1
are computed.

3. **Syntax to Logic Mapping (
\gamma_{f}
)**:
The syntax is mapped directly to the derivation or computation tree. For example:

\gamma_{f}(f^{3}(3))=T_{3},\quad\gamma_{f}(f^{2}(3))=T_{2},\quad\gamma_{f}(f^{1}(3))=T_{1}.

This mapping shows the derivation steps for each function application.

Thus, the mappings are defined analogously, ensuring that the structure of the recursive function’s syntactic, semantic, and logical components is stable when the input is fixed.

Remark 2.8

(Implications of Input Variability and Its Connection to NP Problems)
.

The structure and behavior of recursive functions become notably more complex as the input varies. In particular, when the input is not fixed, the syntactic, semantic, and logical mappings may no longer remain stable. This reflects the dynamic nature of recursive computations, where the depth of recursion, the number of calls, and the resulting computation tree can grow exponentially with respect to the input size.

•

Input Variability and Instability:

For recursive functions, changes in the input lead to significant variations in the computation process. In cases like the factorial function, larger inputs result in deeper recursive calls, which cause a dramatic increase in the complexity of the associated syntactic, semantic, and logical structures. These structures become unstable when input size grows, as the function’s behavior can no longer be predicted solely from the initial fixed values.

•

Connection to NP Problems:

The behavior of recursive functions under changing inputs bears a striking resemblance to the structure of NP problems. Many NP-complete problems, such as the traveling salesman problem or 3-SAT, exhibit exponential growth in complexity as the size of the input increases. This results in vast search spaces, similar to the deep recursive trees in the factorial example. Therefore, the instability of recursive mappings when inputs change mirrors the computational hardness observed in NP problems, where the solution space expands exponentially and requires complex exploration.

Thus, the interplay between recursive functions and NP problems emphasizes how the growth of computational complexity, driven by input variability, can lead to unmanageable computation and logical structures. This connection serves as an important bridge for understanding the deeper computational challenges faced in both theoretical and practical problem-solving contexts.

3.2
CDF-Completeness and Stability/Explosiveness Definitions

Definition 3.4

(CDF-Completeness)
.

Let
f:X\to Y
be a function. We say that

1.

The structural components
S_{\text{syn}}(f),S_{\text{sem}}(f),

The intercomponent mappings

.

A CDF diagram is said to be

\gamma_{f}=\beta_{f}\circ\alpha_{f}

In this case, the function

Structural Comparison Between 2SAT and 3SAT via Logical Form

This section formalizes the syntactic differences between 2SAT and 3SAT using their clause structures and explains how these differences induce semantic and logical expansion in the CDF framework.

2SAT: Local and Compositional Structure

Each clause in 2SAT takes the form:

C_{i}=(l_{1}\lor l_{2}),\quad\text{where }l_{1},l_{2}\in\{x_{j},\neg x_{j}\}

This can be equivalently rewritten as implications:

(l_{1}\lor l_{2})\equiv(\neg l_{1}\rightarrow l_{2})\wedge(\neg l_{2}\rightarrow l_{1})

This enables a translation into an implication graph. The solution is then determined by graph reachability and strongly connected components.

Example:

\varphi=(x_{1}\lor\neg x_{2})\wedge(\neg x_{1}\lor x_{3})

\Rightarrow(\neg x_{1}\rightarrow\neg x_{2}),(x_{2}\rightarrow x_{1}),(x_{1}\rightarrow x_{3}),(\neg x_{3}\rightarrow\neg x_{1})

•

Each clause produces two binary implications.

•

Logical reasoning path
\gamma_{f}
is stable and compositional.

•

Solvable in polynomial time.

3SAT: Non-local and Entangled Structure

Each clause in 3SAT takes the form:

C_{i}=(l_{1}\lor l_{2}\lor l_{3}),\quad\text{where }l_{i}\in\{x_{j},\neg x_{j}\}

Unlike 2SAT, this cannot be rewritten into binary implications. The only falsifying assignment is:

\neg(l_{1}\lor l_{2}\lor l_{3})=\neg l_{1}\land\neg l_{2}\land\neg l_{3}

Example:

\varphi=(x_{1}\lor\neg x_{2}\lor x_{3})

This clause is satisfied in 7 out of 8 truth assignments. The valid assignment space is non-local and scattered across the configuration space.

•

Clause evaluation requires checking
2^{3}=8
possibilities.

•

The semantic layer grows exponentially with
n
.

•

\gamma_{f}
is unstable and not efficiently computable.

The essential difference between 2SAT and 3SAT lies in the syntactic structure of their clauses:

•

In 3SAT, the clauses introduce non-local variable interactions that cannot be simplified or composed, resulting in exponential expansion in the semantic and logical layers.

4.2
Structural Origin of Semantic Explosion in 3SAT

The phenomenon of
semantic explosion
in
3SAT
originates from the structural mapping

\alpha_{f}:S_{\text{syn}}\rightarrow S_{\text{sem}},

which maps syntactic representations of formulas to their semantic configuration spaces. In the CDF framework, this mapping becomes
explosive
when the syntactic structure encodes highly entangled propositional dependencies.

Combinatorial Structure of 3SAT Clauses.

Each clause in 3SAT has the form:

(l_{1}\lor l_{2}\lor l_{3}),\quad\text{where each }l_{i}\text{ is a literal over propositional variable }x_{i}.

Unlike 2SAT clauses, which can be transformed into implication pairs (e.g.,
(\neg x\lor y)\Rightarrow(\neg x\rightarrow y)
), 3SAT clauses induce
non-local
logical dependencies among three variables simultaneously. This syntactic structure cannot be decomposed into independent local components.

Semantic Consequences.

The non-compositionality of 3SAT clauses leads to an exponential growth in the semantic space:

|S_{\text{sem}}(f)|=O(2^{n}),

where
n
is the number of variables. Each clause constrains a different region of the assignment space, and satisfying assignments must navigate an exponentially complex interaction among clauses.

Intrinsic Nature of Explosion.

The semantic explosion is not an artifact of inefficient algorithms but a direct structural consequence of the problem’s generative syntax. That is, the complexity resides in the definition of the problem itself. Any attempt to suppress this explosion requires altering the generative structure of 3SAT, thereby violating its structural identity.

Impossibility of Polynomial Stabilization.

In order to “make 3SAT tractable”, one would have to enforce a polynomially stable version of
\alpha_{f}
. However, such stabilization would require transforming 3SAT into a fundamentally different problem (e.g., 2SAT), thereby destroying the original problem’s identity. Hence, algorithmic improvements cannot bypass the structural explosion embedded in 3SAT.

Conclusion.

Therefore, in the CDF framework, the semantic intractability of 3SAT can be formally traced back to the syntactic non-locality embedded in its clause structure. The mapping
\alpha_{f}
becomes inherently explosive, reflecting the exponential combinatorics of propositional logic that arise from the problem’s intrinsic definition.
