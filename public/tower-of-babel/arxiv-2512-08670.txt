A constant rank theorem for linear elliptic equations on the sphere with applications to the mixed Christoffel problem
A. Colesanti, M. Focardi, P. Guan, P. Salani

Abstract
. We study the mixed Christoffel problem for C 2 , + C^{2,+} convex bodies providing sufficient conditions for its solution. Key to our approach is a constant rank theorem, following the approach developed in [ 13 ] to address the Christoffel problem, in order to ensure that the solution to a related second order linear PDE on the sphere is indeed geometric, that is, it is the support functions of a C 2 , + C^{2,+} convex body.



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
52A20, 35J15

1.
Introduction

In this paper we are interested in
(mixed) area measures
of
convex bodies
(compact and convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, with non-empty interior). Let us briefly explain how these measures are generated. It is well known that the volume (that is, the Lebesgue measure, here denoted by
V
) of the Minkowski linear combination

t_{1}\Omega_{1}+\dots+t_{m}\Omega_{m},\quad t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\geq 0,

of
m
convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is an homogeneous polynomial of degree
(n+1)
in the variables
t_{i}
’s:

V(t_{1}\Omega_{1}+\dots+t_{m}\Omega_{m})=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n+1}=1}^{m}V(\Omega_{i_{1}},\dots,\Omega_{i_{n+1}})t_{i_{1}}\cdots t_{i_{n+1}}.

The coefficients of this polynomial,
V(\Omega_{i_{1}},\dots,\Omega_{i_{n+1}})
, are the so-called
mixed volumes
. Hence, each mixed volume is a quantity depending on
(n+1)
convex bodies:

V=V(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n+1}).

Mixed volumes are among the fundamental notions in convex geometry (see for instance
[
21
]
). They include
quermassintegrals
as special cases: for a fixed convex body
\Omega
, mixed volumes of the form

V(\underbrace{\Omega,\dots,\Omega}_{\mbox{\tiny$k$-times}},\underbrace{B_{1},\dots,B_{1}}_{\mbox{\tiny$(n+1-k)$-times}})

where
B_{1}
is the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, are called
quermassintegrals of
\Omega
(or, with a different normalization, its
intrinsic volumes
).

Mixed area measures arise naturally in integral representations of mixed volumes. Given
n
convex bodies
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, there exists a finite Borel measure
S=S(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n};\cdot)
, defined on the unit sphere
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, such that, for any other convex body
\Omega
,

V(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n},\Omega)=\int_{{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}}u_{\Omega}(x)dS(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n};x),

where
u
is the
support function
of
\Omega
. We recall that the support function of
\Omega
(with respect to the origin) is the function
u_{\Omega}:\ {{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

u_{\Omega}(x)=\sup_{z\in\Omega}\langle x,z\rangle,

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the standard scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The measure
S=S(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n};\cdot)
is called the
mixed area measure
of
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n}
. Due to translation invariance of mixed volumes, area measures verify the following condition:

(1)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}x_{j}dS(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n};x)=0,\quad\forall j=1,\cdots,n+1

(here
x_{1},\dots,x_{+1}
are the coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
).

A special class of mixed area measures related to a convex body
\Omega
are its
area measures
,
S_{k}=S_{k}(\Omega;\cdot)
,
k=1,\dots,n
, defined by

S_{k}(\Omega;\cdot)=S(\underbrace{\Omega,\dots,\Omega}_{\mbox{\tiny$k$-times}},\underbrace{B_{1},\dots,B_{1}}_{\mbox{\tiny$(n-k)$-times}};\cdot).

In particular, the measure
S_{n}(\Omega;\cdot)
has the following simple geometric interpretation: for every Borel set
\eta\subset{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
,

S_{n}(\Omega;\eta)={\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}(\nu_{\Omega}^{-1}(\eta)),

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure, and
\nu_{\Omega}
is the Gauss map of
\Omega
.

The celebrated
Minkowski problem
requires to find a convex body
\Omega
with prescribed area measure
S_{n}(\Omega;\cdot)
. This problem is well-posed: simple necessary and sufficient conditions on the given measure, in order to have existence and uniqueness (up to translations) of the solution, are known, and a satisfactory regularity theory has now been developed, thanks to the contributions of many authors. The literature concerning this problem is vast; the interested reader is referred, for instance, to Chapter 7 in
[
21
]
, for an exhaustive account on this subject.

On the other hand, the problem of finding a convex body with given area measure of order one, that is
S_{1}(\Omega;\cdot)
, called the
Christoffel problem
, appears to be much more delicate. Necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution were established by Firey
[
12
]
and Berg
[
3
]
(see also Chapter 8 in
[
21
]
and
[
19
]
), but they are in general not easy to be checked in practice. The situation is even more complicated for the
Christoffel-Minkowski problem
, concerning the area measures of intermediate order,
S_{k}(\Omega;\cdot)
,
2\leq k\leq(n-1)
.

1.1.
The passage to PDE’s

A general observation, which is crucial for the present paper, is that, under regularity assumptions on the involved convex bodies, mixed area measures admit a density with respect to the uniform measure on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, and such densities can be explicitly written in terms of the derivatives of the support function. This allows, for instance, to reduce the Minkowski and the Christoffel problems to find a solution of a partial differential equation on the unit sphere, within the class of support functions of convex bodies.

We say that a convex body
\Omega
is of class
C^{2,+}
if its boundary is of class
C^{2}
, and the Gauss curvature is positive at every point of
\partial\Omega
. If
\Omega
is of class
C^{2,+}
, then its support function
u
belongs to
C^{2}({{\mathbb{S}}^{n}})
. We denote by
u_{i}
,
u_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
, the first and second covariant derivatives of
u
, with respect to a local orthonormal frame on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
.

Definition 1.1
.

For a convex body
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, of class
C^{2,+}
, with support function
u
, we define the inverse Weingarten form of
\Omega
as

(2)

W_{\Omega}(x)=(u_{ij}(x)+u(x)\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n},\quad x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}.

Note that
W
is independent of the choice of the point with respect to which the support function is defined (that is, is invariant with respect to translations of
\Omega
). Moreover,
\Omega
is of class
C^{2,+}
if and only if its inverse Weingarten form is positive definite at every point of
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
; see, for instance, Chapter 2 in
[
21
]
.

We will denote by
\mathscr{D}
the mixed discriminant operator, that is, the operator generated by the polarization of the determinant with respect to the usual addition of matrices.

\mathscr{D}
acts on
n
-tuples of
n\times n
symmetric matrices. With this notation, if
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n}
are convex bodies of class
C^{2,+}
, and
W_{i}
is the inverse Weingarten form of
\Omega_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, then their mixed area measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure restricted to
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, and its density is

(3)

dS(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n};x)=\mathscr{D}(W_{1},\cdots,W_{n})dx.

In particular

dS_{n}(\Omega;x)=\det(W_{\Omega}(x))dx

for every convex body
\Omega
of class
C^{2,+}
.

We now observe that the support function of the unit ball
B_{1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
(restricted to
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
) is the constant function
1
, and consequently

W_{B_{1}}\equiv I_{n},

where
I_{n}
is the identity matrix of order
n
. Hence

dS_{k}(\Omega;x)=\mathscr{D}(\underbrace{W_{\Omega}(x),\dots,W_{\Omega}(x)}_{\mbox{\tiny$k$-times}},\underbrace{I_{n},\dots,I_{n}}_{\mbox{\tiny$(n-k)$-times}})dx=\sigma_{k}(W_{\Omega}(x))dx,

where
\sigma_{k}(W_{\Omega})
denotes the
k
-th principal symmetric function of the eigenvalues of
W_{\Omega}
.

As a consequence, the Minkowski problem in the smooth setting can be reduced to solve a partial differential equation on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
: if
f
is the density of the given measure, the problem amounts to find a solution
u
to the Monge-Ampère equation

\det(u_{ij}(x)+u\delta_{ij}(x))=f(x),

such that its associated inverse Weingarten form is positive definite on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
:

(4)

W(x)=(u_{ij}(x)+u\delta_{ij}(x))>0\quad\forall\,x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}.

The latter condition ensures that
u
is the support function of a convex body (of class
C^{2,+}
). We will refer to this condition saying that
u
is a
geometric solution
of the relevant equation.

Similarly, the Christoffel problem is equivalent to find a geometric solution of the linear equation

(5)

\mbox{tr}(W)=\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}u+nu=f,

where
\Delta_{{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}}
denotes the spherical Laplacian. More generally, for an arbitrary
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the Christoffel-Minkowski problem is reduced to find a geometric solution of the following
Hessian equation

(6)

\sigma_{k}(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})=f.

Here, given a symmetric matrix
W
,
\sigma_{k}(W)
denotes the
k
-th elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues of
W
. In all cases, in view of (
1
), the compatibility condition

(7)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}x_{j}f(x)dx=0,\quad\forall\ j=1,\dots,n+1,

is a necessary condition on
f
. This condition is verified, for instance, when
f
(or equivalently
\mu
) is even. In the case of Minkowski problem (in the smooth setting), (
7
) is also sufficient. On the other hand it is known that such condition is not sufficient for the existence of a solution of the Christoffel problem (see
[
21
, Section 8.3]
).

1.2.
A sufficient condition for the Christoffel problem

Existence of solution to (
5
) follows from the standard theory of elliptic PDE’s. The main question is when such solution
u
is geometric, i.e.
u
is a support function of convex body. A progress was made in the paper
[
13
]
, where the authors established the following
constant rank
type theorem, for solutions of equation (
5
), under a fairly simple condition on
f
(in the same paper they proved a similar result for equation (
6
)).

Theorem A
.

Let
f\in C^{1,1}({{\mathbb{S}}^{n}})
be a strictly positive function satisfying (
7
).
Assume moreover that

(8)

the
1
-homogeneous extension of
\dfrac{1}{f}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, is convex.

If
u
is a solution to (
5
) with

W(x)=(u_{ij}(x)+u(x)\delta_{ij})\geq 0\quad\forall\,x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}},

then
W(x)>0
for every
x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Using the previous result and standard continuity argument, existence of a geometric solution to (
5
) can be proved if there is a homotopic path, described by a real parameter
t\in[0,1]
, connecting
f
and, say, the constant function
1
, such that the assumption (
8
) is valid for every
t
. For that purpose, Andrews and Ma used a curvature flow in an unpublished note
[
2
]
; see also a recent work by Bryan-Ivaki-Scheuer
[
7
]
. A direct homotopic path was constructed by Sheng-Trudinger-Wang in
[
22
]
. Therefore, as a corollary of Theorem
A
, conditions (
1
) (that is (
7
)) and (
8
) are sufficient for the existence of a solution of the Christoffel problem. Note that (
1
) is also a necessary condition.

1.3.
The mixed Christoffel problem

In this paper we study the following problem.

Problem 1.2

(
The mixed Christoffel problem).

Let
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n-1}
, be given convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
. Find a convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, such that

(9)

S(\Omega_{1},\dots\Omega_{n-1},\Omega;\cdot)=\mu(\cdot).

This problem is mentioned in the recent paper
[
15
]
. Moreover, necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution in the rotation invariant case have been recently established in
[
5
,
6
,
20
]
.

If
\Omega_{i}=B_{1}
for every
i=1,\dots,(n-1)
, then the problem reduces to the Christoffel problem. When
L
is a given convex
C^{2,+}
set (different from
B_{1}
) and
\Omega_{i}=L
for every
i=1,\dots,(n-1)
, then the problem can be interpreted as an
anisotropic Christoffel problem
, where
L
plays the role of the unit ball in place of
B_{1}
.

We first note that, due to (
1
), the following is a necessary condition for the existence of a solution:

(10)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}x_{j}d\mu(x)=0,\quad\forall j=1,\cdots,n+1.

We will treat the mixed Christoffel problem in the smooth setting: we will assume that
\Omega_{1},\cdots,\Omega_{n-1}
are convex bodies of class
C^{2,+}
, and that the measure
\mu
has a smooth density
f
with respect to the
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure restricted to
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
. In particular, condition (
10
) is equivalently expressed by (
7
).

We establish a sufficient condition involving
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n-1}
and
f
, for the existence of a solution to the mixed Christoffel problem.

In order to state our existence result, we need to recall the notion of mixed co-factor matrix. Let
M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1}
be
n\times n
symmetric matrices. For any further
n\times n
symmetric matrix
M=(m_{ij})_{i,j=1\dots,n}
, the mixed discriminant

\mathscr{D}(M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1},M)

is linear with respect to
M
. Therefore it can be written in the form

\mathscr{D}(M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1},M)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}c_{ij}m_{ij},

for suitable and uniquely determined coefficients
c_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
. The matrix

C(M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1})=(c_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n},

is called the mixed co-factor matrix of
M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1}
. Alternatively, the coefficients
c_{ij}
can be defined as

(11)

c_{ij}=\frac{\partial\mathscr{D}(M_{1},\dots,M_{n-1},M)}{\partial m_{ij}},\quad\forall\,i,j=1,\dots,n.

By (
3
), condition (
9
) is equivalent to

(12)

\mathscr{D}(W_{1},\cdots,W_{n-1},W)=f,\quad\mbox{on $\mathbb{S}^{n}$},

and the latter equation can be written in the form:

(13)

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{n}a_{\alpha\beta}(x)(u_{\alpha\beta}(x)+\delta_{\alpha\beta}u(x))=f(x)

(once that a local orthonormal coordinate system has been fixed on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
), where

(a_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n}

is the mixed co-factor matrix of
W_{1},\cdots,W_{n-1}
. Equation (
13
) is a linear second order equation in the unknown function
u
. The fact that
\Omega_{i}
is of class
C^{2,+}
for every
i
, implies that this equation is uniformly elliptic, which is equivalent to

(14)

(a_{\alpha\beta}(x))>0,\quad\forall\ x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}.

The previous conditions guarantee the existence of a solution to (
13
). Moreover, the solution is unique modulus the addition of linear functions (see, for instance,
[
14
]
). We also observe that if
\partial\Omega_{1},\cdots,\partial\Omega_{n-1}\in C^{4}
, then
a_{\alpha\beta}\in C^{2}
. Under these assumptions, the admissible solution
u
of (
13
) belongs to
C^{4,\gamma}
(but note that
C^{3,1}
would be sufficient for us to carry out convexity estimates in later sections).

We look for a solution
u
of (
13
) such that condition (
4
) holds. As in
[
13
]
, we will prove a constant rank theorem for (
13
). In order to state our result, we need the following remark.

Remark 1.3
.

Assume that
A
is a symmetric
(2,0)
tensor defined on an open subset
D
of
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, and that
u\in C^{2}(D)
. For a fixed local coordinate system on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, let

A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n},\quad W=(w_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n}=(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n}.

Then the quantity

{\rm tr}(AW)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}w_{ij},

is independent of the local coordinate system chosen on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
.

In the following statement
(a^{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n}
denotes the inverse matrix of
(a_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))\in C^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
be a positive
(2,0)
-tensor in an open and connected set
D\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
and let
f\in C^{2,\gamma}(D)
for some
\gamma>0
, with
f>0
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Suppose that, for every
1\leq l\leq n-1
, and every
q=1,\dots,n
,

(15)

\left(\delta_{ij}+\frac{\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}(\frac{a_{qq}}{f})}{\frac{a_{qq}}{f}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\nabla_{i}a_{qq}\nabla_{j}a_{qq}}{a^{2}_{qq}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{l}\frac{a^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_{i}a_{q\alpha}\nabla_{j}a_{q\beta}}{a_{qq}}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,n}\geq 0.

Let
u\in C^{2}(D)
be a solution of equation (
13
) in
D
. If

W=(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,n}\geq 0

in
D
, then
W
has constant rank in
D
. Moreover, if
D=\mathbb{S}^{n}
(and
n\geq 2
), then
W
has maximal rank, that is
W>0
in
D
.

In the case
n=2
, condition (
15
) can be written as follows: for every
q=1,2
,

(16)

\left(\frac{a_{qq}}{f}\delta_{ij}+\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\left(\frac{a_{qq}}{f}\right)\right)_{i,j=1,2}\geq 0.

Thank to (
16
), for
n=2
a simplified version of the previous result can be achieved. We need to introduce a definition, first. For an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)
the space of square and symmetric matrices of order
k
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

M\colon\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mbox{\rm Sym}(k).

We say that the function
M
is convex if

M((1-t)x_{0}+tx_{1})\leq(1-t)M(x_{0})+tM(x_{1}),

for every
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and for every
t\in[0,1]
. The above inequality has to be intended in the matricial sense: the difference between the right hand side and the left hand side is a positive semi-definite matrix.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))_{i,j=1,2}\in C^{2,\gamma}(D)
be a positive
(2,0)
-tensor defined in an open connected set
D\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and let
f\in C^{2,\gamma}(D)
for some
\gamma>0
, with
f>0
on
D
. Let
M
be the
1
-homogeneous extension of the tensor

\frac{A}{f}

to the cone

C=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\colon\frac{x}{|x|}\in D\right\}.

Assume that

(17)

M\mbox{ is locally convex in $C$}.

Let
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
be a solution of equation (
13
). If

W=(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1,2}\geq 0

in
D
, then
W
has constant rank in
D
. In particular, if
D=\mathbb{S}^{2}
then
W
has full rank in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Using the previous results, Theorem
A
and a continuity argument, we will deduce the following full rank theorem.

Corollary 1.5
.

Under the same assumptions of Theorem
1.1
(resp. Theorem
1.2
) on
A
and
f
, with
D={{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, assume moreover that
f
verifies (
8
). Let
u
be a solution of equation (
13
). Then

W=(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})>0\quad\mbox{on ${{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}$.}

As an application of Corollary
1.5
, we get the following existence result for the mixed Minkowski problem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n}
be convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, with boundary of class
C^{4,\gamma}
, for some
\gamma>0
, and positive Gauss curvature. For every
r=1,\dots,n
, let
u_{r}
be the support function of
\Omega_{r}
and

W_{r}(x)=((u_{r})_{ij}(x)+u_{r}(x)\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1\dots,n},\quad x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}},

be the inverse Weingarten form of
\Omega_{r}
. Let

A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))

be the mixed co-factor matrix of
W_{1}(x),\dots,W_{n-1}(x)
. Let
f\in C^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
, with
f>0
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, and assume that
f
verifies condition (
7
) and condition (
8
). Let
\mu
be the Borel measure on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
with density
f
with respect to the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure restricted to
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
.

Suppose that, for any
1\leq l\leq n-1
, and any
q=1,\ldots,n
,

(18)

\left(\delta_{ij}+\frac{\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}(\frac{a_{qq}}{f})}{\frac{a_{qq}}{f}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\nabla_{i}a_{qq}\nabla_{j}a_{qq}}{a^{2}_{qq}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{l}\frac{a^{\alpha\beta}\nabla_{i}a_{q\alpha}\nabla_{j}a_{q\beta}}{a_{qq}}\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,n}\geq 0.

Then there exists a convex body
\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, of class
C^{2,+}
, such that

S(\Omega_{1},\dots,\Omega_{n-1},\Omega;\cdot)=\mu(\cdot).

Moreover,
\Omega
is uniquely determined up to translations.

In the case
n=2
, condition (
18
) reads as (
16
).

Remark 1.6
.

If
A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))
is the identity matrix for every
x\in{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, that is, we are in the case of the ordinary Christoffel problem, condition (
18
) becomes

\left(\left(\frac{1}{f}\right)_{ij}+\delta_{ij}\frac{1}{f}\right)_{ij}\geq 0.

In other words, we get the sufficient condition (
8
) found by Guan and Ma in
[
13
]
.

Remark 1.7
.

Condition (
8
) is obviously verified if
f\equiv 1
.

1.4.
The mixed Christoffel problem in the
3
-dimensional space

The
3
-dimensional case (
n=2
), deserves a special treatment. The mixed Christoffel problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be formulated as follows: let
\Omega_{0}
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, such that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}xd\mu(x)=0.

The problem requires to find a convex body
\Omega
such that

S(\Omega_{0},\Omega;\cdot)=\mu(\cdot).

Assume that
\Omega_{0}
is of class
C^{2,+}
, with support function
h\in C^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
; let

W_{0}=W_{\Omega_{0}}=(h_{ij}+h\delta_{ij})_{i,j=1,2},

be the inverse Weingarten map of
\Omega_{0}
. Assume moreover that
\mu
has a density
f\in C^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Using Theorem
1.2
, or equivalently (
16
), we will derive two sufficient conditions for the existence of a solution, stated in the following Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a convex body with boundary of class
C^{4,\gamma}
, for some
\gamma>0
, and with positive Gauss curvature. Let
h
be the support function of
\Omega_{0}
. Let
f\in C^{2,\gamma}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
, with
f>0
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, be such that condition (

, and for every tangential direction
\alpha

\mathbb{S}^{2}

\left(\frac{h_{\alpha\alpha}(x)+h(x)}{f(x)}\right)_{\alpha\alpha}+\frac{h_{\alpha\alpha}(x)+h(x)}{f(x)}\geq 0,

where sub-indices denote covariant partial derivatives with respect to the direction

\Omega\in C^{2,+}
such that

S(\Omega_{0},\Omega,\cdot)=\mu(\cdot)\,,

where
\mu

d\mu(x)=f(x)dx
.

Example 1.8
.

Let us show an example of application of the previous result. Let

so that, in particular, condition (

) is verified. In this case (

(20)

h_{\alpha\alpha\alpha\alpha}+2h_{\alpha\alpha}+h>0,\quad\mbox{on $\mathbb{S}^{2}$.}

Assume that
h\colon{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}

where

verifies

\|\psi\|_{C^{4}(\mathbb{S}^{2})}<\frac{C}{4}.

Then (

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

where

.

Remark 1.9
.

In the assumptions of Theorem

\Omega_{0}

2.
The case
n=2

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case
n=2

The proof is based on the constant rank method.
We start with an observation which will be used also in the general case: using equation (
13
), the fact that the tensor
A
is positive definite, and the condition
f>0
, we deduce that the rank of
W
can not be
0
.
In particular, as
n=2
, the rank of
W
can be
1
or
2
.

If the rank equals
2
at every point, there is nothing to prove.

Hence, to proceed we assume that there exists a point
x_{0}
where the rank is
1
. In a sufficiently small neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
x_{0}
, one of the eigenvalues of
W
is larger than an absolute positive constant
C_{0}
, while the other one is close to zero, and can be assumed to belong to
[0,C_{0}/2]
, that is,

0\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{2}<C_{0}\leq\lambda_{2}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{U}\,,

where, for
z\in\mathcal{U}
,
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
denotes the eigenvalues of
W(z)
. Let us notice that, by the standard regularity theory for elliptic equations, the solution
u\in C^{4,\gamma}(D)
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then
\det(W),\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are of class
C^{2}({\mathcal{U}})
.
We set

(21)

\phi(x)=\det(W(x))=w_{11}(x)w_{22}(x)-w_{12}^{2}(x),\quad x\in{\mathcal{U}}.

We now fix
z\in{\mathcal{U}}
and choose a local orthonormal frame of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, such that
W(z)
is diagonal: note that this is possible since, by our assumptions, the quantity

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}w_{ij}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}(u_{ij}+u\delta_{ij})

is independent of the local coordinate system. In the next relations, all the involved functions will be computed at
z
. From the definition of
\phi
we have

w_{11}=\frac{1}{w_{22}}\phi,

so that

(22)

0\leq w_{11}\leq\frac{1}{C_{0}}\phi.

In the sequel we will denote by
C
a general constant independent of
z\in\mathcal{U}
, and depending possibly only on
\|u\|_{C^{3}({\mathcal{U}})}
,
\|\phi\|_{C^{2}({\mathcal{U}})}
,
\|A\|_{C^{2}({\mathcal{U}})}
and
C_{0}
(the value of
C
may change even in the same line).

Differentiating (
21
) with respect to
x_{1}
, we obtain

w_{111}w_{22}=\phi_{1}-w_{11}w_{221}+2w_{12}w_{112}=\phi_{1}-w_{11}w_{22}\frac{w_{221}}{w_{22}}=\phi_{1}-\phi\frac{w_{221}}{w_{22}}.

Whence, using again
w_{22}=\lambda_{2}\geq C_{0}
,

|w_{111}|\leq C(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

A similar inequality can be deduced for
w_{112}
. Therefore

(23)

|\nabla w_{11}|\leq C(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Our aim is to compute

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}(z)\phi_{\alpha\beta}(z).

We start observing that, as a consequence of the Codazzi formula, the quantity

(24)

w_{ijk},\quad i,j,k=1,\dots,n,

is independent of the order of
i,j,k
. We have, for
\alpha,\beta=1,2
,

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{11\alpha\beta}w_{22}+w_{11\alpha}w_{22\beta}+w_{11\beta}w_{22\alpha}+w_{11}w_{22\alpha\beta}-2(w_{12\alpha}w_{12\beta}+w_{12}w_{12\alpha\beta})

Taking into account (
22
) and (
23
), we deduce the following expression for the second order derivatives of
\phi
:

(25)

\phi_{\alpha\beta}=w_{22}\left(w_{11\alpha\beta}-2\frac{1}{w_{22}}w_{12\alpha}w_{12\beta}\right)+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|),\quad\alpha,\beta=1,2.

Here and throughout the paper, by
O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)
we mean a quantity which is bounded in absolute value by a constant
C
(as above) times
(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)
.

We also have the identities (see for instance
[
13
]
):

(26)

w_{ii\alpha\beta}=w_{\alpha\beta ii}+w_{ii}-w_{\alpha\beta},\quad\alpha,\beta,i=1,2,

so that

w_{11\alpha\beta}=w_{\alpha\beta 11}-w_{\alpha\beta}+w_{11}=w_{\alpha\beta 11}-w_{\alpha\beta}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Hence

\phi_{\alpha\beta}=w_{22}w_{\alpha\beta 11}-2w_{12\alpha}w_{12\beta}-w_{22}w_{\alpha\beta}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Next we compute

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}=w_{22}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}w_{\alpha\beta 11}-2a_{22}w_{221}^{2}-w_{22}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}w_{\alpha\beta}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|),

or, equivalently,

(27)

-\frac{1}{w_{22}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}=-\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}w_{\alpha\beta 11}+2\frac{a_{22}w_{221}^{2}}{w_{22}}+a_{22}w_{22}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Here, we have used (
22
) to absorb the term
a_{11}w_{11}
into
O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)
.

Next, we differentiate twice equation (
13
) with respect to
x_{1}
, and we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}w_{\alpha\beta 11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{11}-2\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta 1}w_{\alpha\beta 1}-\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta 11}w_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{11}-2a_{221}w_{221}-a_{2211}w_{22}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|),

where
a_{\alpha\beta 1}
and
a_{\alpha\beta 11}
denote the first and the second partial derivatives of
a_{\alpha\beta}
with respect to
x_{1}
, respectively, and we used (
22
) and (
23
). Replacing the last equality in (
27
) we obtain:

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{w_{22}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f_{11}+2a_{221}w_{221}+2a_{2211}w_{22}+2\frac{a_{22}w_{221}^{2}}{w_{22}}+a_{22}w_{22}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f-f_{11}+2\frac{w_{221}}{w_{22}}(a_{22}w_{22})_{1}+a_{2211}w_{22}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Using again the equation (
13
), we obtain

w_{22}=\frac{f}{a_{22}}-\frac{a_{11}}{a_{22}}w_{11}=\frac{f}{a_{22}}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Here we used (
22
) and the fact that, due to the positivity of the tensor
A
,
a_{22}\geq c
for some constant
c>0
depending on
A
only. Moreover, differentiating once (
13
) with respect to
x_{1}
and taking into account (
22
) and (
23
), we get

a_{221}w_{22}+a_{22}w_{221}=f_{1}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

Therefore

\frac{w_{221}}{w_{22}}=\frac{f_{1}}{f}-\frac{a_{221}}{a_{22}}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

For simplicity, let
a=a_{22}
,
a_{1}=a_{221}
and
a_{11}=a_{2211}
. From the previous steps we deduce

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{w_{22}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f-f_{11}+2\frac{f_{1}^{2}}{f}-2\frac{a_{1}f_{1}}{a}+\frac{fa_{11}}{a}+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f^{2}}{a}\left[\left(\frac{a}{f}\right)_{11}+\frac{a}{f}\right]+O(\phi+|\nabla\phi|).

By (
16
), the first term in the right hand side of the previous relation is non-negative:

(28)

\left(\frac{a}{f}\right)_{11}+\frac{a}{f}\geq 0.

Hence, we have the following differential inequality in
\mathcal{U}
:

\sum_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{2}a_{\alpha\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}\leq C(\phi+|\nabla\phi|),

for every
z\in{\mathcal{U}}
. As
\phi\geq 0
and
\phi(x_{0})=0
, it follows from the strong minimum principle that
\phi\equiv 0
in
D
, as
D
is connected. This is equivalent to say that the rank of
W
is constantly
1
in
D
.

The proof of the final part of the statement, relative to the case
D=\mathbb{S}^{2}
, is provided in Section 3, in the more general setting of arbitrary dimension
n
.

Let

be the

-homogeneous extension of

. Assume that

where

.

Proof of Theorem

.

We need to prove that condition (

) implies (

). Let us fix an orthonormal frame on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
, and

. By Remark

implies that the

-homogeneous extension of

to

.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By (
12
), the tensor
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,2}
appearing in (

) is given by

where

is the co-factor matrix of

. Let

W_{0}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b_{11}&b_{12}\\
b_{21}&b_{22}\end{array}\right).

Then, by (

) we see that:

(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}b_{22}&-b_{12}\\

In particular, in this case the matrix

\frac{1}{f}(a_{ij})_{i,j=1,2}

has the same diagonal entries of the matrix

up to a permutation. The convexity of the

-homogeneous extension of the latter matrix then implies that assumption

Proof of Theorem

The proof is based on an inspection of the proofs of Theorem

. In the final part of the proof of Theorem

\left(\frac{a_{22}}{f}\right)_{11}+\frac{a_{22}}{f}\geq 0.

Here, a local orthonormal coordinate system

has been fixed on

. On the other hand, as we have seen in the proof of Theorem

Note that the direction

may be completely arbitrary. We conclude that in Theorem

\left(\frac{h_{\alpha\alpha}(x)+h(x)}{f(x)}\right)_{\alpha\alpha}+\frac{h_{\alpha\alpha}(x)+h(x)}{f(x)}\geq 0,\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\,\forall\,\alpha,

.
∎

3.
The
n
-dimensional case

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.1
, when
n\geq 3
.

3.1.
Some preliminary facts

We assume throughout that
u
is a solution to (
13
) in
D
, and that the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
concerning the tensor
(a_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n}
and the function
f
, are verified. The tensor
W=W(x)
is defined in the statement of the theorem; as usual
w_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
, denote the entries of
W
. By assumption (
14
) and standard regularity theory of elliptic equations, we have
u\in C^{4,\gamma}({{\mathbb{S}}^{n}})
, for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Given a square symmetric matrix
M\in\mbox{Sym}(n)
and
j\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, let
\sigma_{j}(M)
denote the
j
th elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues of
M
. Note that, if
M
is positive semi-definite, then the rank of
M
is
l\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
if and only if

\sigma_{l}(M)>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sigma_{j}(M)=0\quad\forall\,j=l+1,\dots,n.

If

\sigma_{n}(W(x))>0\quad\forall\,x\in D,

then
W
has constant (and maximal) rank
n
in
D
. So, in the perspective to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will assume that there exists an integer
l
between
0
and
(n-1)
, with the following property:

\sigma_{l}(W(x))>0,\quad\forall\,x\in D,

and there exists
x_{0}\in D
, such that

\sigma_{l+1}(W(x_{0}))=0.

Remark 3.1
.

As already observed in the two-dimensional case,
l
is at least
1
by equation (
13
) and the strict positivity of
f
.

We now introduce the
test function

\phi
, which is one of the main tools of this proof. We remark that the choice of
\phi
for
n\geq 3
is different from the one in the case
n=2
. Define

(30)

\phi(x)=\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))+\frac{\sigma_{l+2}(W(x))}{\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))},

for every
x
such that
\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))>0
.

Remark 3.2
.

This test function was introduced in
[
4
]
, where the authors observed that, by the Newton-McLaurin inequalities,
\phi
can be continuously extended to the case
\sigma_{l+1}(W)=0
, setting

\frac{\sigma_{l+2}(W)}{\sigma_{l+1}(W)}=0\quad\mbox{if $\sigma_{l+1}(W)=0$.}

Therefore we complete the definition of
\phi
setting

(31)

\phi(x)=0,\quad\mbox{$\forall\,x$ s.t. $\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))=0$.}

The general strategy of the proof will be to show that, under the assumptions of the theorem,
\phi
verifies a differential inequality, to which the strong minimum principle can be applied. This will lead to the conclusion

\phi\equiv 0\quad\mbox{in $D$,}

that is,
W
has constant rank
l
in
D
.

Let
D^{\prime}
be an open and connected set compactly contained in
D
, and let
C_{0}>0
be such that

(32)

\sigma_{l}(W)\geq C_{0}\quad\mbox{in $D^{\prime}$.}

For any
z\in D^{\prime}
, let
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of the matrix
W
at
z
. As
u\in C^{3}(D)
, there exists a constant
C>0
, depending on
\|u\|_{C^{3}}
,
\|\phi\|_{C^{2}}
,
C_{0}
and
n
, such that

\lambda_{n}\geq\lambda_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n-l+1}\geq C,\quad\mbox{in $D^{\prime}$}.

We set

B=\{1,\dots,n-l\},\quad G=\{n-l+1,\dots,n\}.

We now recall a notation which is common in the context of constant rank type results. Given two functions
g
and
h
defined in
D^{\prime}
, and
y\in D^{\prime}
, we write

g(y)\lesssim h(y)

if there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, such that

g(y)-h(y)\leq c_{1}|\nabla\phi(y)|+c_{2}\phi(y).

We also write

g(y)\sim h(y)

if
g(y)\lesssim h(y)
and
g(y)\lesssim h(y)
. Moreover, we write

g\lesssim h\qquad\mbox{in $D^{\prime}$}

if this inequality holds with constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
independent of
y
and depending only on
\|u\|_{C^{3}}(D^{\prime})
,
\|\phi\|_{C^{2}}(D^{\prime})
,
C_{0}
and
n
.

As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the case
n=2
, we also write

g=O(\psi),

for some positive function
\psi
defined in
D^{\prime}
, if

|g|\leq C\psi\quad\mbox{in $D^{\prime}$,}

where, as above, the constant
C
depends only on
\|u\|_{C^{3}}
,
\|\phi\|_{C^{2}}
,
C_{0}
and
n
.

In view of the Codazzi property of
W
and arguments presented in
[
4
]
,
\phi
has the properties listed below.

I.

Once extended as indicated in (
31
),
\phi\in C^{1,1}
(see Corollary 2.2 in
[
4
]
).

II.

\forall\alpha,\beta=1,\dots,n
,

(33)

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\phi+\sum_{i,j\in B}|\nabla w_{ij}|\right)-\left[\frac{\sum_{i\in B}V_{i\alpha}V_{i\beta}}{\sigma^{3}_{1}(B)}+\frac{\sum_{i,j\in B,i\neq j}w_{ij\alpha}w_{ji\beta}}{\sigma_{1}(B)}\right]+

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in B}\left[\sigma_{l}(G)+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B|i)+\sigma_{2}(B|i)}{\sigma_{1}^{2}(B)}\right]\left[w_{ii\alpha\beta}-2\sum_{j\in G}\frac{w_{ji\alpha}w_{ij\beta}}{w_{jj}}\right],

where

V_{i\alpha}=\sigma_{1}(B)w_{ii\alpha}-w_{ii}\sum_{j\in B}w_{jj\alpha},

and
\sigma_{1}(B)
,
\sigma_{k}(B|i)
denote respectively the
1
st,
k
th elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues with indices in
B
,
B\setminus\{i\}
.
Relation (
33
) follows from Lemma 2.4 and Proposition 2.1 in
[
4
]
(see also the argument used to deduce (3.19) there).

III.

Let
m=|B|=n-l
. By Lemma 3.3 in
[
4
]
, there is a constant
C
depending only on
n
,
\|W\|_{C^{0}}
and
C_{0}
in (
32
), such that for each

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
l\geq 2
be an integer number and let
R,S,T\in{\mathcal{M}}_{l\times l}
. Assume in particular that
R
is symmetric and positive definite. Let

{\mathcal{D}}=\{W\in\mbox{\rm Sym}(l)\colon\langle R,W\rangle=1,W\geq 0\}.

Consider the function
\rho\colon{\mathcal{D}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\rho(W)=\langle S,W\rangle+\langle T,W\rangle^{2}.

Then there exists
W_{\rm min}\in\mathcal{D}
, with
rank(W_{\rm min})=1
, such that

\rho(W_{\rm min})=\min_{\mathcal{D}}\rho.

Proof.

Note that
\mathcal{D}
is convex and compact (the second claim follows being
R
positive definite), and that
\langle R,R\rangle>0
. Let

S^{\prime}=S-\frac{{\langle S,R\rangle}}{\langle R,R\rangle}R,\quad T^{\prime}=T-\frac{{\langle T,R\rangle}}{\langle R,R\rangle}R.

Clearly

\langle S^{\prime},R\rangle=\langle T^{\prime},R\rangle=0.

Now, using that
\langle R,W\rangle=1
,
\rho(W)
rewrites for
W\in{\mathcal{D}}
as

\displaystyle\rho(W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S^{\prime},W\rangle+\frac{\langle S,R\rangle}{\langle R,R\rangle}+\left[\langle T^{\prime},W\rangle+\frac{\langle T,R\rangle}{\langle R,R\rangle}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle S^{\prime},W\rangle+a+\left[\langle T^{\prime},W\rangle+b\right]^{2},

where
a
and
b
are constants. Hence, it suffices to prove the lemma for the function
\bar{\rho}
defined by

\bar{\rho}(W)=\langle S^{\prime},W\rangle+\left[\langle T^{\prime},W\rangle+b\right]^{2},\quad W\in{\mathcal{D}}.

As
\mathcal{D}
is compact, there exists
W_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}
, such that

\bar{\rho}(W_{0})=\min_{W\in\mathcal{D}}\bar{\rho}(W).

Let
k
be the rank of
W_{0}
, so
1\leq k\leq l
. If
k=1
, then the conclusion of the lemma is true. Hence consider the case
k\geq 2
. After a rotation, we may assume that the entries
w_{ij}
of
W_{0}
verify
w_{ij}=0
for every
i>k
. We may also embed
\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)
in
\mbox{\rm Sym}(l)
, with the convention that

\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)=\{W=(w_{ij})_{i,j=1,\dots,l}\in\mbox{\rm Sym}(l)|w_{ij}=0,\forall i>k\}.

Define

{\mathcal{D}}_{k}=\{W\in\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)\colon\langle R,W\rangle=1,W\geq 0\}.

Note that
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}\subset{\mathcal{D}}
is convex and compact. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the subspace of
\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)
defined by

{\mathcal{H}}=\{Y\in\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)\colon\langle Y,R\rangle=0\}.

In particular,
T^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{H}}
; moreover,
{\mathcal{H}}
has dimension

\frac{k(k+1)}{2}-1\geq 2,

as
k\geq 2
. Hence there exists
\bar{T}\in{\mathcal{H}}
such that

\langle T^{\prime},\bar{T}\rangle=0,\quad\langle\bar{T},\bar{T}\rangle=1.

As
\bar{T}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, the points of the form
W=W_{0}+x\bar{T}
, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, verify
\langle W,R\rangle=1
. Let

L=\{W\in\mbox{\rm Sym}(k)\colon W=W_{0}+x\bar{T}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Sigma=L\cap{\mathcal{D}}.

L
is a straight line in
\mbox{\rm Sym}(l)
, lying on the same hyperplane as
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
, and by the convexity of
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
,
\Sigma
is a segment containing
W_{0}
in its interior (because the rank of
W_{0}
is
k
), that is

\Sigma=\{W_{0}+x\bar{T}\colon x\in[-c,d]\}

for some
c,d>0
. In particular
W_{0}-c\bar{T}
and
W_{0}+d\bar{T}
belong to the relative boundary of
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
(meaning that they are points of
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
not belonging to the relative interior of
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
). Consider the function
\gamma\colon[-c,d]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\gamma(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(W_{0}+x\bar{T})=\langle W_{0}+x\bar{T},S^{\prime}\rangle+\left[\langle W_{0}+x\bar{T},T^{\prime}\rangle+b\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle W_{0}+x\bar{T},S^{\prime}\rangle+\left[\langle W_{0},T^{\prime}\rangle+b\right]^{2}.

Thus,
\gamma
is a linear function with a minimum point at

. Therefore
\gamma
is constant, which shows that the minimum of
\bar{\rho}
is attained (also) on the relative boundary of
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}
. In other words, there exists

such that

\bar{\rho}(\tilde{W}_{0})=\min_{W\in{\mathcal{D}}}\bar{\rho}(W).

Iterating the previous argument, we deduce that the minimum is attained on matrices with rank one.

with rank

. After a proper rotation, we may assume
W

\min_{\langle A,W\rangle=1,\ W\geq 0}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}(W)=\frac{a_{11}^{\prime\prime}}{a_{11}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a^{\prime}_{11}}{a_{11}}\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{1}\frac{a^{ij}a^{\prime}_{1i}a^{\prime}_{1j}}{a_{11}},

) as above by means of (

). In particular, note that in this case

\left(\frac{a^{\prime}_{11}}{a_{11}}\right)^{2}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{1}\frac{a^{ij}a^{\prime}_{1i}a^{\prime}_{1j}}{a_{11}}=0.

This concludes the proof in the case of a general subset
D\subset{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
.

We now consider the case
D={{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
and we show that constant rank of
W
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
implies that
W
has full rank, i.e.,
W>0
on
{{\mathbb{S}}^{n}}
. To this aim, we assume by contradiction that the (constant) rank is
l<n
; we know that
l\geq 1
. Thus,

\sigma_{l}(W(x))=:q(x)>0,\quad\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))=0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.

Let
\delta_{0}>0
be such that

Let
\Omega
be the convex body with support function

. We may assume

by moving the Steiner point of

to the origin. By Minkowski identities (see
[
21
, Section 5.3.1]
), there exists

, such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{n,l}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\sigma_{l+1}(W(x))dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}u\sigma_{l}(W(x))dx

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}uq(x)dx\geq\delta_{0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}udx.

As

, that is, a contradiction.

Remark 3.8
.

In general, for
l\geq 2
, if
WA=AW,\ W>0
,

\langle A^{\prime},W\rangle^{2}-\langle A^{\prime},WA^{\prime}A^{-1}\rangle\leq 0,

with equality holds if and only if
A^{\prime}=\lambda A
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. To see this, we first observe that for any matrix
B
,

(55)

\langle B,B\rangle=\langle B,B^{T}\rangle

where
B^{T}
the transpose of
B
. Write

A=A^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\frac{1}{2}},\ W=W^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}}.

As
\langle A,W\rangle=1
, by (
55
)

\langle A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}},A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle=1.

We also have

\langle A^{\prime},WA^{\prime}A^{-1}\rangle=\langle W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}},W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rangle,

and

\langle A^{\prime},W\rangle=\langle A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}},W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rangle.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\langle A^{\prime},W\rangle^{2}\leq\langle A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}},A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle\langle W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}},W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rangle=\langle W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}},W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rangle,

with equality holds if and only if

W^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\prime}A^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\lambda A^{\frac{1}{2}}W^{\frac{1}{2}}

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. As
WA=AW
, we get
A^{\prime}=\lambda A
. This is satisfied if
l=1
.
