Thermodynamic bounds and symmetries in first-passage problems of fluctuating currents
Adarsh Raghu, Izaak Neri

Abstract
We develop a method for deriving thermodynamic bounds for first-passage problems of currents with two boundaries. Using this method, we extend some results on first-passage times of fluctuating currents. Notably, we derive a thermodynamic bound on the rate of dissipation of a Markov process in terms of the splitting probabilities and the first-passage time statistics of a fluctuating current, which is a refinement of a previously derived inequality. We also show that the concept of effective affinity, originally developed for continuous-time Markov chains, naturally extends to discrete-time Markov chains. Furthermore, we analyse symmetries in first-passage problems of fluctuating currents with two boundaries. We show that optimal currents — those for which the effective affinity fully accounts for the dissipation — satisfy a symmetry property: the current’s average speed to reach the positive threshold equals the current’s speed to reach the negative threshold. The developed approach uses a coarse-graining procedure for the average entropy production at random times and utilises martingale methods to perform time-reversal of first-passage quantities.

Introduction

1.1
Setup

Nonequilibrium systems can sustain currents with nonzero average rates, such as particle, energy, or positional currents. A central objective of stochastic thermodynamics, and more broadly statistical physics, is to incorporate fluctuations in a theory of nonequilibrium thermodynamics (see, e.g., Chapter XII of Ref.
[
37
]
, and Refs.
[
74
,
56
,
40
]
).
Current fluctuations are conventionally studied at fixed times, e.g., with Green-Kubo relations
[
36
]
, fluctuation relations
[
27
]
, or thermodynamic uncertainty relations
[
5
,
25
]
. However, due to noise, in mesoscopic systems events of interest often take place at random times. For such processes, it is more natural to study fluctuations at random termination times.

In this paper we study current fluctuations through the first-passage problem

T={\rm inf}\left\{t\geq 0:J(t)\notin(-\ell_{-},\ell_{+})\right\},

(1)

where
J(t)
is the fluctuating current,
where
T
is the first time that the current exits a finite interval
(-\ell_{-},\ell_{+})
, and “
{\rm inf}
” is the infimum of a set; we use the convention that
J(0)=0
. This is a generalisation of the gambler’s ruin problem to the physically relevant case of fluctuating currents. Currents are in general non-Markovian processes, which complicates the analysis of their first-passage problem.
Key quantities of interest are the distributions
p_{T}(t|-)
and
p_{T}(t|+)
of the first-passage time
T
conditioned on
J(T)\geq\ell_{+}
or
J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}
, respectively, and the splitting probability

p_{-}
, denoting the probability that
J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}
; see Fig.
1
for a graphical illustration of this first-passage problem.

First-passage problems of the form (
1
) model the timing of discrete events in nonequilibrium mesoscopic systems. A good example is the directed stepping of molecular motors along one-dimensional substrates, such as motor proteins bound to biofilaments, for which
J
is a positional current. If
J(T)>0
then the motor steps forwards, while if
J(T)<0
then the motor steps backwards. Thus, the splitting probability
p_{-}
is the probability for molecular motors to step backwards, and
T
is the dwell time in between two steps
[
34
]
(see also
[
19
]
for an alternative model). Importantly, backward steps and dwell times are experimentally measurable quantities of molecular motors
[
53
,
1
,
64
]
, which implies that they can be used to infer thermodynamic and kinetic properties of molecular motors
[
73
,
47
,
45
]
, such as the efficiency of their chemomechanical coupling
[
34
]
.

More complex processes, such as, cell-fate decisions
[
75
,
16
]
, can also be modeled as first-passage problems of the type (
1
). Cell-fate decisions involve the initiation of gene transcription in response to extracellular ligand concentrations. In this case, the current represents the accumulated evidence tracked by the system through a chemical reaction, and the sign of the current at the first-passage time corresponds with the decision taken by a system.

We address two general classes of problems related to first-passage times of the form (
1
). The first concerns the relationship between the fluctuations of
T
and the rate of dissipation
\dot{s}
in the underlying process. The second involves the symmetry properties of the statistics of
T
at positive versus negative thresholds. Below, we provide a brief overview of these two problems, followed by a discussion of the novel contributions presented in this paper.

1.2
Trade-off relations between speed, accuracy, and dissipation

A generic feature of first-passage problems is the trade-off between speed, quantified by the mean first-passage time
\langle T\rangle
, and accuracy, quantified by the splitting probability
p_{-}
. For instance, increasing the thresholds
\ell_{-}
and
\ell_{+}
, the mean first-passage time
\langle T\rangle
increases and the splitting probability
p_{-}
decreases. This is the trade-off between speed-accuracy trade-off that is observed in a large number of decision making systems
[
12
,
9
]
.

A natural question in nonequilibrium thermodynamics is how dissipation, quantified by the entropy production rate
\dot{s}
, influences the speed-accuracy trade-off in first-passage problems. A couple of trade-off relations between speed, accuracy, and dissipation have appeared in the literature. These relations have been derived in the general setup of fluctuating currents in continuous-time Markov chains and in the limit of large thresholds.

Notably, for
\overline{j}>0
, Refs.
[
66
,
48
,
63
]
show that the following trade-off relation,

\dot{s}\geq\frac{\ell_{+}}{\ell_{-}}\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\langle T\rangle}(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)),

(2)

holds for arbitrayr currents,
where
o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)
is a function that converges to zero when
\ell_{\rm min}={\rm min}\left\{\ell_{+},\ell_{-}\right\}
diverges.

Applied to molecular motors, the inequality (
2
) relates probability of backward steps (
p_{-}
) and the mean dwell time (
\langle T\rangle
), to the rate of dissipation (
\dot{s}
).
Notably, the inequality (
2
) can be used to infer the efficiency of the chemomechanical coupling in molecular motors from the observation of the statistics of backward steps
[
34
]
. For example, using the statistics of Kinesin-1 backward steps, Ref.
[
47
]
estimated that Kinesin-1 converts Gibbs free energy into mechanical work with an efficiency that is smaller than
0.6
.

An equivalent form of the inequality (
2
) is

\dot{s}\geq\overline{j}a^{\ast},

(3)

where

\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\ell_{-}}=a^{\ast}

(4)

is the exponential decay constant of the splitting probability
[
63
]
.
Interestingly, in systems with coupled currents, the decay constant
a^{\ast}
exhibits properties similar to those of chemical affinities in uncoupled systems
[
63
]
, and therefore has been coined the
effective affinity
, see Refs.
[
59
,
60
,
50
,
63
]
. Notably the effective affinity determines the direction of the current (
\overline{j}a^{\ast}\geq 0
); captures a portion of the total amount of dissipation
\dot{s}
[Eq.
3
]; and it determines the statistics of the fluctuations of the currents against its average flow [Eq. (
4
)]. For systems with uncoupled currents
a^{\ast}
equals the chemical affinity
[
14
,
15
]
.

An alternative trade-off relation between speed, accuracy, and dissipation is the thermodynamic uncertainty relation for first-passage times times
[
24
]
, given by

\dot{s}\geq\frac{2}{\langle T\rangle_{+}}\frac{\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+}}{\langle T^{2}\rangle_{+}-\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+}}(1+o_{\ell_{+}}(1)),

(5)

where here and throughout the paper, we use the notation
\langle\cdot\rangle_{+}=\langle\cdot|J(T)\geq\ell_{+}\rangle
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{-}=\langle\cdot|J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}\rangle
to indicate averages conditioned on the first passage problem (
1
) terminating at the positive and negative threshold respectively. The main difference between the inequalities (
5
) and (
2
) is that the former quantifies accuracy with
\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+}/(\langle T^{2}\rangle_{+}-\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+})
while the latter quantifies it with the splitting probability
p_{-}
.

Thermodynamic bounds have also been derived for the distributions of first-passage times
T
in the limit of large thresholds.
In this limit, the first-passage time
T
satisfies a large-deviation principle at both thresholds with speeds
\ell_{+}
and
\ell_{-}

[
24
,
49
]
, respectively. The corresponding rate functions are defined by

\mathcal{I}_{+}(\tau)=-\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln p_{T/\ell_{+}}(\tau|+)}{\ell_{+}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{-}(t)=-\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln p_{T/\ell_{-}}(\tau|-)}{\ell_{-}},

(6)

where
\tau\geq 0
and where
p_{T/\ell_{+}}
(
p_{T/\ell_{-}}
) are the distributions of
T/\ell_{+}
(
T/\ell_{-}
).
The scaled cumulant generating functions

m_{+}(\mu)=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{+}}\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{+}\quad{\rm and}\quad m_{-}(\mu)=\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{-}}\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{-}

(7)

are the Fenchel-Legendre transforms of the rate functions
\mathcal{I}_{+}
and
\mathcal{I}_{-}
, respectively, viz.,

m_{+}(\mu)={\rm sup}_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}(\tau\mu-\mathcal{I}_{+}(\tau))\quad{\rm and}\quad m_{-}(\mu)={\rm sup}_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}(\tau\mu-\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)).

(8)

Using large deviation theory and martingale theory, it was shown that scaled cumulant generating functions satisfy the inequalities
[
24
]

m_{+}(\mu)\geq\frac{\dot{s}}{2\overline{j}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4\mu/\dot{s}}\right)

(9)

and
[
49
]

m_{-}(\mu)\geq\frac{\dot{s}}{2\overline{j}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4\mu/\dot{s}}\right)+a^{\ast}-\frac{\dot{s}}{\overline{j}}.

(10)

The first inequality yields the thermodynamic uncertainty relation for first-passage times, Eq. (
5
),
while, excluding special cases, a similar inequality does not apply at the negative threshold
[
49
]
.

Notice that we only covered the results that are directly relevant to this paper, and not results on, for instance, first-passage problems in semi-Markov processes or on first-passage problems of time-additive observables that are not fluctuating currents; for those topics we refer the interested reader to the general literature, see e.g., Refs.
[
22
,
55
,
29
,
84
,
42
,
4
,
43
]
. Also, several of these papers consider the case of first-passage problems with one-boundary, and not the two-boundary case as studied in this paper.

1.3
First-passage time symmetries

We say that
T
satisfies a first-passage symmetry when the statistics of first-passage times at the negative threshold equal those at the positive threshold i.e.,

\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)=\mathcal{I}_{+}(\tau)

(11)

for all
\tau\geq 0
.

While the trade-off relations (
4
) and (
5
) hold for arbitrary currents, the symmetry relation (
11
) is more specific, raising the question of which currents it applies to.
The first-passage symmetry (
11
) was first observed in Pascal’s gambler’s ruin problem, see e.g. Ref.
[
69
]
. In physics, interest in this symmetry increased after it was empirically observed in the dwell times between consecutive steps of kinesin motors
[
53
]
, a result that is interesting for describing time-reversibility in coarse-grained models of molecular motors
[
85
]
.
References
[
34
,
62
,
23
,
57
]
demonstrated that Eq. (
11
) applies to currents in unicyclic systems, while Refs.
[
51
,
35
]
derived an equivalent symmetry for one-dimensional overdamped Langevin processes with constant drift.
Given the link between dissipation and time-reversibility, one might expect a relation between first-passage time symmetries and entropy production. This connection was confirmed in Refs.
[
68
,
66
,
51
,
65
]
, which showed that the symmetry holds for currents proportional to the fluctuating entropy production. This result was further extended in Ref.
[
63
]
to include currents that are cycle-equivalent to the fluctuating entropy production, thereby encompassing arbitrary currents in unicyclic systems. Related findings include symmetry relations for cycle formation times
[
32
]
and for first-passage times of winding numbers
[
6
]
.

The symmetry relation (
11
) can be interpreted as a manifestation of the Gallavotti-Cohen-type symmetry
[
38
]
,

\mathcal{I}_{J}(j)=\mathcal{I}_{J}(-j)-a^{\ast}j,

(12)

where
\mathcal{I}_{J}
is the (large deviation) rate function of the current
J(t)
in the limit of large
t
, and
a^{\ast}
is the effective affinity. For currents that do not satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen-type fluctuation relation, the first-passage time statistics of fluctuating currents at positive and negative thresholds are not identical
[
49
,
70
]
.

Currents that are cycle-equivalent with the stochastic entropy production are also optimal currents, in the sense that the equality in (
3
) is attained. This suggests that there exists a relationship between optimality and symmetry. However, it should be noted that the symmetry relation (
11
) is not a sufficient condition for current optimality, and it remains an open question whether it is a necessary condition. Thus, establishing a clear connection between symmetry remains an open problem.

Since the symmetry relation (
11
) does in general not apply, one may wonder whether first-passage times of generic currents satisfy a generalised symmetry.
For edge currents, which are a specific class of currents that count the net number of transitions between two states in a Markov process, this is indeed the case. For edge currents the following generalised symmetry relation

\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau)

(13)

applies
[
59
]
,
where
\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau)
is the rate function of
T
at the positive threshold in a conjugate process. The first-passage symmetry (
13
) can be seen as a consequence of the generalised fluctuation symmetry
\mathcal{I}_{J}(j)=\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{J}(-j)-a^{\ast}j

[
59
]
,
where
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{J}
is the rate function of
J(t)
in a dual process defined by the effective affinity
a^{\ast}
. It is natural to ask whether the generalised symmetry (
13
) can be extended to arbitrary currents, and we address also this problem in this paper.

1.4
Results of this Paper

In this Paper we expand on the results above. Notably, we develop a method to derive the inequalities (
2
) and (
3
) that is different from derivations presented previously in the literature
[
48
,
63
]
. This new approach lends itself naturally to extensions and, in particular, allows us to extend the inequalities (
3
) and (
2
) in two meaningful ways.

First, we show that the inequalities (
2
) and (
3
) also apply for Markov chains in discrete time; this stands in contrast with the inequalities (
5
)-(
10
) that do not extend to the discrete-time setups
[
61
]
. Hence, the effective affinity
a^{\ast}
is also meaningful as an affinity-like quantity of coupled currents in discrete-time Markov chains.

Second, we derive the refined inequality

\dot{s}\geq\frac{\ell_{+}}{\langle T\rangle}\left(\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\ell_{-}}+\mathcal{I}_{-}\left(1/\overline{j}\right)\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(14)

Since the term
\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/\overline{j})>0
the inequality (
14
) implies the inequality (
2
).
The difference between the inequalities (
14
) and (
2
) is that the right-hand side of Eq. (
14
) also depends on the fluctuation properties of
T
through the term
\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/\overline{j})
.

Just as the first-passage inequality (
3
) can be expressed as the inequality (
2
) that involves the fluctuations of
J(t)
(through
a^{\ast}
), we can express the inequality (
14
) in terms of current fluctuations. Specifically, the inequality (
14
) is equivalent to

\dot{s}\geq I_{J}(-\overline{j})=\tilde{a}\overline{j}-\lambda_{J}(\tilde{a}),

(15)

where
\tilde{a}
is the positive value of
a
such that
\lambda^{\prime}_{J}(\tilde{a})=\overline{j}>0
, and with the prime indicating a derivative towards
a
. Note that if
\tilde{a}=a^{\ast}
, then the right-hand side of (
15
) equals the right-hand side of (
2
).

The inequality (
14
) clarifies the anticipated relation between symmetry and optimality (in the sense of attaining the equality in (
3
)). From the bound (
14
) it follows that optimal currents satisfy the symmetry relation

\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T\rangle_{-}}{\ell_{-}}=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T\rangle_{+}}{\ell_{+}},

(16)

i.e., the speed of reaching the positive threshold equals the speed of reaching the negative threshold; the symmetry relation (
16
) is equivalent to the condition
a^{\ast}=\tilde{a}
. However, the converse is not true, i.e., the symmetry relation (
16
) does not imply optimality. Also, note that general currents do not satisfy the symmetry relation (
16
), see Ref.
[
49
]
.

Furthermore, we show that all fluctuating currents satisfy a generalised symmetry relation of the form (
13
) for an appropriately defined dual process. The dual process is the time-reversal of a Markov process that has been tilted so that the time-averaged current is set to a specific value
\hat{j}
that is in general different from
\overline{j}

[
11
]
; the average rate
\hat{j}
is determined by setting the tilting parameter equal to the effective affinity
a^{\ast}
(
\hat{j}
equals the current
\overline{j}^{\ast}
of Ref.
[
49
]
).

From a methodological point of view, we extend procedures known at fixed time to random times. Specifically, we develop a method for coarse-graining the average entropy production at first-passage times,
\langle S(T)\rangle
, which extends existing methods for coarse-graining
\langle S(t)\rangle
(see, e.g., Refs.
[
26
,
67
,
28
,
80
,
8
,
18
,
21
]
) to the random-time setup. A key insight is that
\langle S(T)\rangle
can be expressed as a Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability distributions. Those probability distributions are defined on an unusual set consisting of all possible realisations of the process up to the first-passage time. By applying standard coarse-graining methods to this Kullback-Leibler divergence, we derive bounds on the rate of dissipation. However, doing so a complication arises, as the resultant bounds involve probabilities of first-passage events in the time-reversed dynamics, which are generally of less practical interest. Fortunately, using martingale theory, we can relate the first-passage quantities in the time-reversed dynamics to those in the forward dynamics
[
63
,
49
]
.

2
System setup: fluctuating currents and entropy production in Markov chains

We define the system setup that we use for studying first-passage times of currents. This setup consists of fluctuating currents in Markov chains. Subsequently, we discuss how entropy production can be defined in a Markov chain, relating the setup to nonequilibrium thermodynamics.

2.1
Markov chains

We consider a Markov process
X(t)\in\mathcal{X}
with
\mathcal{X}
a finite set and with
t\in\mathbb{I}
a time index that can be continuous
\mathbb{I}=\mathbb{R}^{+}
or discrete
\mathbb{I}=\mathbb{N}
. Without loss of generality we can set
\mathcal{X}=\left\{1,2,\ldots,|\mathcal{X}|\right\}
.

2.1.1
Discrete time.

In discrete time, the sample space
\Omega
consists of all trajectories of the form

x^{\infty}_{0}=(x(0),x(1),x(2),\ldots),

(17)

where
x(t)\in\mathcal{X}
for all
t\geq 0
.

The random variable
X(t)
is a map from
\Omega
to
\mathcal{X}
so that

X(t,x^{\infty}_{0})=X(t)
returns the value of the trajectory
x^{\infty}_{0}
at the time
t
. Let
f(X^{t}_{0})
be a function of the trajectory
X^{t}_{0}=(X(0),X(1),\ldots,X(t))
. The average value of
f
is given by

\langle f(X^{t}_{0})\rangle=\sum_{x^{t}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}\mathbb{P}\left(X^{t}_{0}=x^{t}_{0}\right)f(x^{t}_{0}),

(18)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(X^{t}_{0}=x^{t}_{0}\right)
is the probability to observe the trajectory
x^{t}_{0}
.

For a time-homogeneous Markov chain,
\mathbb{P}
is specified by the probability mass function of the initial configuration

p_{\rm init}(x)=\mathbb{P}\left(X(0)=x\right),

(19)

and the matrix of transition probabilities

\mathbf{q}_{yx}=\mathbb{P}\left(X(t)=x|X(t-1)=y\right).

(20)

It follows from the Markov property that

\mathbb{P}(X^{t}_{0}=x^{t}_{0})=p_{\rm init}(x_{0})\prod^{t}_{t^{\prime}=1}\mathbf{q}_{x(t^{\prime}-1)x(t^{\prime})}=p(x^{t}_{0}),

(21)

where we have introduced the simpler notation
p(x^{t}_{0})
for
\mathbb{P}(X^{t}_{0}=x^{t}_{0})
.

The transition matrix
\mathbf{q}_{xy}
satisfies
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{q}_{xy}=1
. We make two additional assumptions on
\mathbf{q}
. We assume that the graph of admissible transitions is strongly connected, and we assume that
\mathbf{q}_{xy}>0
implies that
\mathbf{q}_{yx}>0
, so that
X
is both ergodic and time-reversible.
Under these assumptions, there exist a unique probability mass function
p_{\rm ss}(x)
satisfying
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{\rm ss}(x)\mathbf{q}_{xy}=p_{\rm ss}(y)
.

2.1.2
Continuous time.

In continuous time, the sample space
\Omega
consists of all trajectories

x^{\infty}_{0}=\left\{x(t):t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\right\},

(22)

where
x(t)
is a right-continuous (i.e.,
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}x(t+\epsilon)=x(t)
) and piecewise constant function from
\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
\mathcal{X}
. As the function is piecewise constant and right-continuous, the trajectories
x^{\infty}_{0}
are specified by a discrete-time trajectory
(y(0),y(1),y(2)\ldots)
, with
y(j)\in\mathcal{X}
, and an increasing sequence of jump times
(t(0),t(1),t(2),\ldots)
with
t(0)=0
, so that
x(t)=y({\rm max}\left\{t(j):j\in\mathbb{N},t(j)\leq t\right\})
(see Chapter 3 of
[
54
]
). We write averages of functionals
f(X^{t}_{0})
as in Eq. (
18
), but now the sum has to be understood over all possible right-continuous trajectories
x^{t}_{0}
; this involves summing over all possible number of jumps
n
, summing over all possible sequences of states
y^{n}_{0}
, and integrating over all possible jump times
t^{n}_{0}
with
t(n)\leq t
.

The probability measure
\mathbb{P}
is in this case specified by the probability mass function

p_{\rm init}(x)=\mathbb{P}\left(X(0)=x\right),

(23)

and the transition rate matrix

\mathbf{q}_{xy}=\lim_{{\rm d}t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(X(t+{\rm d}t)=x|X(t)=y\right)}{{\rm d}t}

(24)

for all
x\neq y
; we set the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{q}
equal to
\mathbf{q}_{xx}=-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{q}_{xy}
.
Using the Markov property we can assign the following probabilistic weight to a trajectory
x^{t}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(X^{t}_{0}=x^{t}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{\rm init}(y(0))e^{\mathbf{q}_{y(n)y(n)}(t-t(n))}

(25)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod^{n}_{j=1}\left\{{\rm d}[t(j)-t(j-1)]\>\,e^{\mathbf{q}_{y(j)y(j)}[t(j)-t(j-1)]}\mathbf{q}_{y(j-1)y(j)}\right\}=:p(x^{t}_{0}),

where
n=n(x^{t}_{0})
is the number of jumps in the trajectory
x^{t}_{0}
.

As in discrete time, we assume that the graph of admissible transitions is strongly connected and we assume that
\mathbf{q}_{xy}>0
if and only if
\mathbf{q}_{yx}>0
, so that
X
is ergodic and time-reversible.

2.2
Fluctuating currents

A fluctuating current
J(t)
is a time-additive observable of the form

J(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\left\{x\right\}}c_{xy}N^{xy}(t),

(26)

with
N^{xy}(t)
the number of times that the process
X
has jumped from
x
to
y
in the time interval
[0,t]
, and
with the additional assumption that
c_{xy}=-c_{yx}
. If the coefficients
c_{xy}
are not antisymmetric in
x
and
y
, then we speak of a general time-additive observable that is not a fluctuating current.

We investigate in this Paper the properties of the first-passage problem (
1
) of fluctuating currents
J
of the form (
26
). To fully define
T
, we also need to specify that
T=\infty
if
J(t)\in(-\ell_{-},\ell_{+})
for all values of
t\geq 0
.

We consider currents with
\langle J(t)\rangle\neq 0
, as for such currents the first-passage times
T
at the positive and negative thresholds satisfy a large deviation principle with speeds
\ell_{+}
and
\ell_{-}
, respectively (see Introduction). For all such currents,
without loss of generality, we can assume that
\langle J(t)\rangle>0
.
The average rate of the current is defined as

\overline{j}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}t^{-1}\langle J(t)\rangle.

(27)

In discrete time the units of time are set so that the time interval between two steps equals one (otherwise we need to divide the right-hand side by the time interval).

The scaled cumulants of
J(t)
can be obtained from the derivatives of the
scaled cumulant generating function

\lambda_{J}(a)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln\langle e^{-aJ(t)}\rangle

(28)

at
a=0
. For example,
\overline{j}=-\lambda^{\prime}_{J}(0)
. The scaled cumulant generating function
\lambda_{J}
is determined by the Perron root of the tilted matrix
[
79
]

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_{xy}(a)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\exp(-ac_{xy})\mathbf{q}_{xy}&&x\neq y,\\
\mathbf{q}_{xx}&&x=y.\end{array}\right.

In particular, in continuous time
\lambda_{J}(a)
is the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
and in discrete time
\lambda_{J}(a)
equals the natural logarithm of the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
.

2.3
Entropy production

The rate of dissipation or entropy production of a Markov jump process is
[
71
]

\dot{s}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\left\{x\right\}}p_{\rm ss}(x)\mathbf{q}_{xy}\ln\frac{\mathbf{q}_{xy}}{\mathbf{q}_{yx}}.

(31)

The formula relies on the assumption that the process satisfies local detailed balance
[
44
]
, i.e., the environment is in thermal equilibrium at a temperature
\mathsf{T}_{\rm env}
. As with
\overline{j}
, in discrete time the unit of time in between two time steps is set equal to one.

The fluctuating entropy production quantifies the amount of dissipation along an observed trajectory
[
74
,
72
,
56
]
.
For stationary Markov jump processes, i.e., when the initial distribution equals the stationary distribution, the fluctuating entropy production is defined by

S(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\left\{x\right\}}\ln\frac{p_{\rm ss}(x)\mathbf{q}_{xy}}{p_{\rm ss}(y)\mathbf{q}_{yx}}N^{xy}(t).

(32)

Notice that
S(t)
is a fluctuating current of the form (
26
), with
c_{xy}=\ln(p_{\rm ss}(x)\mathbf{q}_{xy})/(p_{\rm ss}(y)\mathbf{q}_{yx})
the entropy produced when the process jumps for
x
to
y
. Taking the average we find

\dot{s}=t^{-1}\langle S(t)\rangle.

(33)

The fluctuating entropy production
S(t)
satisfies the property

\langle f(X(t))\rangle^{\dagger}=\langle f(X(t))e^{-S(t)}\rangle,

(34)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle^{\dagger}
are averages in the time-reversed Markov chain. The time-reversed Markov chain has a law
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
that is defined by the initial condition
p_{\rm ss}(x)
and the time-reversed transition rate matrix

\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{p_{\rm ss}^{-1}}\mathbf{q^{T}}\mathbf{p_{\rm ss}},

(35)

where
\mathbf{q^{T}}
is the transpose of the rate matrix
\mathbf{q}
,
\mathbf{p_{\rm ss}}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by
p_{\rm ss}(x)
, and
\mathbf{p_{\rm ss}^{-1}}
is the matrix inverse of
\mathbf{p_{\rm ss}}
.

2.4
Tilting and time-reversal

In this paper we use repeatedly the following equality

\mathbf{\tilde{q}^{\dagger}}(a)=\mathbf{p}_{\rm ss}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{T}(-a)\mathbf{p}_{\rm ss}^{-1}.

(36)

relating the tilted matrix of the time-reversed process,
\mathbf{\tilde{q}^{\dagger}}
, with the tilted matrix of the forward process
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}
; the tilted matrix
\mathbf{\tilde{q}^{\dagger}}
is defined as in Eq. (
2.2
), but with the entries of
\mathbf{q}
replaced by those of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}
.
Equation (
36
) follows readily from the definitions (
2.2
) and (
35
) and the fact that for fluctuating currents
c_{xy}=-c_{yx}
holds for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
.

A direct consequence of (
36
) is that

\lambda_{J}(a)=\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(-a),

(37)

where
\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(-a)
is the scaled cumulant generating function of the time-reversed process, i.e.,

\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(a)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\langle e^{-aJ(t)}\rangle^{\dagger}.

(38)

Indeed,
\lambda_{J}(a)
and
\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(-a)
are the Perron roots of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
and
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{\dagger}(-a)
, respectively, and due to (
36
) both matrices share the same eigenvalues.

Note that the property (
37
) does not apply to time-additive observables that are not currents (i.e., when
c_{xy}=-c_{yx}
does not hold for all
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
). The property (
37
) will prove to be important in this paper, and therefore the results are restricted to fluctuating currents.

3
Trade-off relation between speed, accuracy, and dissipation

We derive the inequality (
2
), which expresses a trade-off between speed, accuracy, and dissipation – quantified by the mean first-passage time
\langle T\rangle
, the splitting probability
p_{-}
, and the dissipation rate
\dot{s}
, respectively. The derivation presented here is more general than previous ones in the literature, which were restricted to continuous-time Markov chains (see Refs.
[
48
,
63
]
), as it applies to both continuous-time and discrete-time settings.

We organise the derivation of (
2
) in three steps. In Sec.
3.1
we focus on the quantity
\langle S(T)\rangle
, which is the average of the entropy production evaluated at the first-passage time
T

[
52
,
46
]
. We show that
\langle S(T)\rangle
decreases upon coarse-graining of the trajectories
X^{T}_{0}
. With a proper choice of the coarse-grained variable, we obtain a lower bound for
\langle S(T)\rangle
in terms of the splitting probabilities of
T
in the forward and time-reversed dynamics. In the following Sec.
3.2
, we take the large thresholds limit and we derive in this limit an asymptotic Wald equality that relates
\langle S(T)\rangle
to the rate of dissipation,
\dot{s}
, and the mean first-passage time,
\langle T\rangle
. Lastly, in Sec.
3.3
, we show that in the limit of large thresholds the splitting probabilities in the time-reversed dynamics are related to those in the forward dynamics, which completes the derivation.

Note that the restriction to continuous time in previous approaches stems from their reliance on the inequality
\lambda_{J}(a)\geq a\overline{j}(-1+a\overline{j}/\dot{s})

[
58
,
24
]
, which does not hold in discrete time
[
61
]
. In contrast, here we get the inequality in (
2
) by coarse-graining
\langle S(T)\rangle
, which applies in both continuous and discrete time

3.1
Inequality for
\langle S(T)\rangle
in terms of the splitting probabilities of
T

We show that the average entropy production
\langle S(T)\rangle
evaluated at a first passage time
T
is bounded from below by

\langle S(T)\rangle\geq p_{+}\ln\frac{p_{+}}{p^{\dagger}_{+}}+p_{-}\ln\frac{p_{-}}{p^{\dagger}_{-}},

(39)

where

p_{+}=\mathbb{P}\left(J(T)\geq\ell_{+}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad p_{-}=\mathbb{P}\left(J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}\right)

(40)

are the splitting probabilities of
T
in the forward dynamics, and where

p^{\dagger}_{+}=\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\left(J(T)\geq\ell_{+}\right)\quad{\rm and}\quad p^{\dagger}_{-}=\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\left(J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}\right),

(41)

are the splitting probabilities of
T
in the time-reversed dynamics.

To derive (
39
), we first show in Sec.
3.1.1
that
\langle S(T)\rangle
is a Kullback-Leibler divergence between two probability distributions defined on the set of realisations of the trajectories
X^{T}_{0}
. Then in Sec.
3.1.2
, we obtain (
39
) through a coarse-graining scheme applied to
X^{T}_{0}
.

3.1.1
Expressing
\langle S(T)\rangle
as a Kullback-Leibler divergence.

The definition (
34
) of
S(t)
implies that
\exp(-S(t))
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative process
[
72
,
51
,
87
]
,

e^{-S(t)}=\frac{p^{\dagger}(X^{t}_{0})}{p(X^{t}_{0})},

(42)

where
p(X^{t}_{0})

and
p^{\dagger}(X^{t}_{0})
are the probability distributions associated with the trajectories
X^{t}_{0}
in the forward and time-reversed dynamics.
For Markov chains in discrete time,
p(X^{t}_{0})
is the probability mass function defined in Eq. (
21
) with
p_{\rm init}=p_{\rm ss}
,
and
p^{\dagger}(x^{t}_{0})
is the corresponding path probability for the time-reversed Markov chain with transition probability
\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}
and initial state
p_{\rm ss}
. For Markov chains in continuous time,
p(X^{t}_{0})
is as defined in Eq. (
25
) with
p_{\rm init}=p_{\rm ss}
, and
p^{\dagger}
is the corresponding path probability distribution associated with the time-reversed Markov chain with transition rate matrix
\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}
and initial state
p_{\rm init}=p_{\rm ss}
.

The quantity
\langle S(T)\rangle
is the average of
S(t)
evaluated at the first-passage time
t=T(X^{\infty}_{0})
. In discrete time, it follows from the definition of
\langle\cdot\rangle
that the average of
S(T)
over all trajectories can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\infty}_{0}\in\Omega}p(x^{\infty}_{0})\ln\frac{p(x_{0}^{T(x^{\infty}_{0})})}{p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{T(x^{\infty}_{0})})},

(43)

where
p(x_{0}^{T(x^{\infty}_{0})})
is the path probability
p(x^{t}_{0})
for
t=T
(and similarly for
p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{T(x^{\infty}_{0})})
).
Note that
p(x^{\infty}_{0})
is a probability distribution over all trajectories in
\Omega
, but
S(T)
only depends on the part of the trajectory before the first-passage time. Therefore, we can ”marginalise” the distribution
p(x^{\infty}_{0})
in the right-hand side of Eq. (
43
) in the following way

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x^{\infty}_{0}\in\Omega}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\delta(t;T(x^{\infty}_{0}))p(x^{\infty}_{0})\ln\frac{p(x_{0}^{t})}{p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{t})}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\sum_{x_{0}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}\delta(t;T(x^{t}_{0}))p(x_{0}^{t})\ln\frac{p(x_{0}^{t})}{p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{t})},

where
\delta(\cdot;\cdot)
is a Kronecker delta function. The first equality introduces a Kronecker delta function to replace
T
by the summation index
t
. The last equality uses the stopping time property
T(x^{\infty}_{0})=T(x^{T}_{0})
of the first-passage time
[
86
,
65
]
, and then marginalises the distribution
p(x^{\infty}_{0})
by summing over all variables
x(t^{\prime})
with
t^{\prime}>t
. Note that we have used the notation
T(x^{t}_{0})=T(x^{\infty}_{0})
if
t\geq T(x^{\infty}_{0})
, and
T(x^{t}_{0})=\infty
if
t<T(x^{\infty}_{0})
. We can express the right-hand side of (
44
) into the more compact form

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\sum_{x_{0}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}^{\prime}p(x_{0}^{t})\ln\frac{p(x_{0}^{t})}{p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{t})},

(45)

where the prime indicates that we only sum over the trajectories for which
T(x^{t}_{0})=t
.

The Eq. (
45
) makes it clear that
\langle S(T)\rangle
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the probability distribution
p
from
p^{\dagger}
. Here, both probability distribution are defined on the set of realisations of the trajectories
X^{T}_{0}
. Note that these probability distributions are properly normalised as
\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\sum_{x_{0}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}^{\prime}p(x_{0}^{t})=1
. Hence, both
\langle S(T)\rangle
and
\langle S(t)\rangle
are Kullback-Leibler divergences between probability distributions of trajectories related to
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
, but the distributions are supported on different sets of trajectories.

The expression (
45
) generalises to continuous time

\langle S(T)\rangle=\int^{\infty}_{0}dt\sum^{\prime}_{x_{0}^{t}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}p(x_{0}^{t})\ln\frac{p(x_{0}^{t})}{p^{\dagger}(x_{0}^{t})}.

(46)

Here,
\sum^{\prime}_{x_{[0,t]}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}
denotes a generalised sum, understood as a combination of summations and integrals over all trajectories satisfying
T(X^{t}_{0})=t
. This summation can be expressed as an integral over all jump times with the condition that the last jump time equals
t
, i.e.,
t(n)=t
, and a sum over all discrete-time trajectories
y^{n}_{0}
with the constraint that the corresponding first-passage problem in discrete time satisfies
T(y^{n}_{0})=n
.
For simplicity, in what follows we perform the calculations in discrete time, using the formula (
45
) for
\langle S(T)\rangle
in discrete time. However, the derivations also hold in continuous time and can be obtained by replacing the summation over time with an integral in the steps that follow.

3.1.2
Coarse-graining of
\langle S(T)\rangle
.

The average entropy production
\langle S(t)\rangle
decreases under coarse-graining, see, e.g., Refs.
[
26
,
67
,
28
,
80
,
8
,
18
,
21
]
. This behaviour under coarse-graining follows from the fact that
\langle S(t)\rangle
is a Kullback-Leibler divergence.
As shown in the previous section, also
\langle S(T)\rangle
is a Kullback-Leibler divergence, and therefore we can also coarse-grain this quantity. However, while
\langle S(t)\rangle
involves probability distributions defined on the set of realisations of
X^{t}_{0}
, the
the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\langle S(T)\rangle
involves probability distributions defined on the set of realisations of the trajectories
X^{T}_{0}
. Consequently, when coarse-graining
\langle S(T)\rangle
, we need to use coarse
observables that are functionals of
X^{T}_{0}
. In what follows, we implement this procedure for a specific observable in discrete-time Markov chains that yields (
39
), and we refer to
A
for a derivation that applies to arbitrary observables in discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains.

We consider the coarse observable

D={\rm sign}\left\{J(T)\right\},

(47)

with
D=0
if
T=\infty
. Considering that
\mathbb{P}(D=0)=0
, we
can express the formula (
45
) for
\langle S(T)\rangle
as

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle=-\sum_{d\in\left\{\pm\right\}}p_{d}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\sum^{\prime}_{x^{t}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}\delta(d;D(x^{t}_{0}))\>p(x^{t}_{0}|D=d)\>\ln\frac{p^{\dagger}(x^{t}_{0})}{p(x^{t}_{0})},

(48)

where
p(x^{t}_{0}|D=d)=p(x^{t}_{0})/p_{d}
, and we recall that
p_{d}
are the splitting probabilities of
T
(with
d=+
or
d=-
). Note that in Eq. (
48
) we have used that
D
is a functional of
X^{T}_{0}
, as the sum in the right-hand side of (
48
) runs over all realisations of
X^{T}_{0}
.

Applying Jensen’s inequality
[
31
,
17
]
to the expectation value of a convex function, which here is
-\ln(\cdot)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}p_{d}\ln\left(\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\sum^{\prime}_{x^{t}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}\delta(d;D(x^{t}_{0}))p(x^{t}_{0}|D=d)\frac{p^{\dagger}(x^{t}_{0})}{p(x^{t}_{0})}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{d\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}p_{d}\ln\frac{p^{\dagger}_{d}}{p_{d}},

which we recognise as the inequality (
39
) with
p^{\dagger}_{d}
the splitting probabilities of
T
in the time-reversed dynamics. Note that the expectation value that we used for Jensen’s inequality is with respect to a probability measure defined on the set of realisations of the trajectories
X^{T}_{0}
and with probability mass function
p(x^{t}_{0}|D=d)
.

3.2
Limit of large thresholds

We show that in the limit of large thresholds,

\dot{s}\geq\frac{|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|}{\langle T\rangle}(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)),

(50)

where
o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)
is a function that converges to zero when
\ell_{\rm min}={\rm min}\left\{\ell_{+},\ell_{-}\right\}
diverges. Note that this inequality appeared before in Refs.
[
66
,
48
]
by applying results from the theory of sequential hypothesis testing to stochastic thermodynamics
[
77
]
.

As shown in
B.1
, in the limit of large thresholds

p_{-}=\exp\left(-a^{\ast}\ell_{-}[1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)]\right)

(51)

with
a^{\ast}>0
the effective affinity, i..e, the nonzero value of
a
for which
the scaled cumulant generating function
\lambda_{J}(a)=0
, i.e.,

\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast})=0.

(52)

As the average value of
J(t)
in the time-reversed process is negative,

p^{\dagger}_{+}=\exp\left(a^{\dagger,\ast}\ell_{+}[1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)]\right),

(53)

where
a^{\dagger,\ast}<0
is the effective affinity of
J
in the time-reversed dynamics. In other words,
a^{\dagger,\ast}
is the nonzero value of
a
for which
\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(a)=0
. Consequently, using the scaling laws (
51
) and (
53
), along with
p_{-}+p_{+}=p^{\dagger}_{-}+p^{\dagger}_{+}=1
, we find that in the limit of large thresholds (
39
) simplifies into

\langle S(T)\rangle\geq|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(54)

The left-hand side of (
54
) can be approximated using an asymptotic version of Wald’s equality
[
83
,
82
]

\langle S(T)\rangle=\dot{s}\langle T\rangle(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(55)

In
C
we derive (
55
) for first-passage times with two thresholds by using the assumed ergodicity of the process
X(t)
.
Combining (
55
) with (
54
) yields (
50
).

3.3
Splitting probabilities of fluctuating currents in the time-reversed process

We demonstrate here that due to the anti-symmetry of fluctuating currents under time reversal,

|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|=\frac{\ell_{+}}{\ell_{-}}|\ln p_{-}|(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)),

(56)

which together with (
50
) completes the derivation of the inequality (
2
). Note that the equality (
56
) was conjectured in Ref.
[
48
]
based on physical intuition, but a convincing derivation was missing (see section 5 of Ref.
[
48
]
). In the present paper we derive (
56
) by using recent results on martingales
[
63
,
49
]
.

As shown in
B.1
, the exponential decay constant
a^{\ast}
of
p_{-}
is the nonzero root of the equation

\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast})=0,

(57)

and analogously we find

\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(a^{\dagger,\ast})=0

(58)

for the exponential decay constant of
p^{\dagger}_{+}
.
Using the Eq (
37
) that relates
\lambda_{J}
with
\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}
, we readily recover from the definitions of

and

that the effective affinity changes sign under time reversal, i.e.,

a^{\dagger,\ast}=-a^{\ast}.

(59)

Using (
59
) in Eqs. (

) and (

), we obtain (
56
).

4
Refined inequality involving the statistics of
T
at the negative threshold

In the previous section we have derived a bound on the rate of dissipation
\dot{s}
by coarse-graining
\langle S(T)\rangle
with the observable
D(X^{T}_{0})
. This approach works more generally for observables that are functionals of
X^{T}_{0}
. Hence, by refining the observables that are being measured, we can obtain better bounds on the rate of dissipation. In the present section, we coarse-grain
\langle S(T)\rangle
by using the pair
(D,T)
of coarse observables. In this way, we will obtain the bound Eq. (

As in the previous section, we organise the derivation of (

) in three parts: in Sec.

at fixed time

4.1
Coarse-graining of the average entropy production
\langle S(T)\rangle

We show that

{\langle S(T)\rangle\geq p_{+}\ln\frac{p_{+}}{p^{\dagger}_{+}}+p_{+}\ln\frac{p_{+}}{p^{\dagger}_{+}}}+p_{+}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}p_{T}(t|+)\ln\frac{p_{T}(t|+)}{p^{\dagger}_{T}(t|+)}+p_{-}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}p_{T}(t|-)\ln\frac{p_{T}(t|-)}{p^{\dagger}_{T}(t|-)},

To derive (

\displaystyle\langle S(T)\rangle=-\sum_{d\in\left\{\pm\right\}}p_{d}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}p_{T}(t|D=d)\sum^{\prime}_{x^{t}_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{t+1}}\delta(d;D(x^{t}_{0}))\>p(x^{t}_{0}|D=d,T=t)\>\ln\frac{p^{\dagger}(x^{t}_{0})}{p(x^{t}_{0})}.

Applying Jensen’s inequality to the expectation of the convex function

4.2
Limit of large thresholds

We show that in the limit of large thresholds,

\dot{s}\geq\frac{\ell_{+}}{\langle T\rangle}\left(\frac{|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|}{\ell_{-}}+\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}(1/\overline{j})\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(62)

\langle S(T)\rangle\geq\left(|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|+p_{+}\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}p_{T}(t|+)\ln\frac{p_{T}(t|+)}{p^{\dagger}_{T}(t|+)}\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

For

\mathcal{I}_{+}(\tau)=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\ln p_{T/\ell_{+}}(\tau|+)|}{\ell_{+}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau)=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\ln p^{\dagger}_{T/\ell_{+}}(\tau|+)|}{\ell_{+}}.

By using in Eq. (

the asymptotic version of Wald’s equality, Eq. (
55
), we find that

\dot{s}\geq\frac{1}{\langle T\rangle}\left(\frac{\ell_{+}}{\ell_{-}}|\ln p_{-}|+\ell_{+}\int^{\infty}_{0}{\rm d}\tau\>p_{T/\ell_{+}}(\tau|+)\>[\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau)-\mathcal{I}_{+}(\tau)]\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(65)

Using that in the limit of large thresholds,
\ell_{+}\gg 1
, it holds that
p_{T/\ell_{+}}(\tau|+)\approx\delta(\tau-\langle T|+\rangle/\ell_{+})
, and using the following asymptotic version of Wald’s equality (see Appendix C in
[
63
]
)

\frac{1}{\overline{j}}=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T|+\rangle}{\ell_{+}}=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T\rangle}{\ell_{+}},

(66)

we obtain Eq. (
62
).

4.3
Time-reversal of the rate functions of
T

We show that for large enough thresholds the statistics of
T
at the negative threshold in the forward dynamics equal the statistics of
T
at the positive threshold in the time-reversed dynamics, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)=\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau),

(67)

which together with (
62
) completes the derivation of the inequality (
14
).
Just as was the case for the derivation of the equality (
56
), the property (
67
) relies on the anti-symmetry of fluctuating currents under time-reversal.

To derive (
67
),
we use the fact that the scaled cumulant generating function
m_{-}
[as defined in Eq. (
7
)] is the functional inverse of
\lambda_{J}

[
24
,
49
]
, viz.,

\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast}-m_{-}(\mu))=-\mu,

(68)

where
a^{\ast}
is the effective affinity. This result implies that the large deviation properties of
T
are determined by those of
J
, and can be derived using martingales see
B.2
.
Analogously, the scaled cumulant generating

m^{\dagger}_{+}(\mu)=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{+}}\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle^{\dagger}_{+}

(69)

is the functional inverse of
\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}
, i.e.,

\lambda^{\dagger}_{J}(a^{\dagger,\ast}+m^{\dagger}_{+}(\mu))=-\mu,

(70)

where
a^{\dagger,\ast}
is the effective affinity of
J
in the time-reversed dynamics. We recall that
a^{\dagger,\ast}=-a^{\ast}
[see Eq. (
59
)].
Using (
68
) and (
70
) in Eq.
37
, we find

a^{\ast}-m_{-}(\mu)=-a^{\dagger,\ast}-m^{\dagger}_{+}(\mu)
,
and thus from (
59
) we recover

m_{-}(\mu)=m^{\dagger}_{+}(\mu).

(71)

Since the large deviation rate functions
\mathcal{I}_{\pm}(\tau)
are given by the Legendre transforms

\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)={\rm max}_{\mu}(\mu\tau-m_{-}(\mu))\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}={\rm max}_{\mu}(\mu\tau-m^{\dagger}_{+}(\mu)),

(72)

the symmetry (
71
) implies (
67
), which is the equation we were meant to demonstrate.

4.4
Derivation of Eq. (
15
)

We derive the inequality (
15
) from (
14
). Using the Eq. (
4
) for the exponential decay rate of
p_{-}
and the asymptotic version of Wald’s equality, (
66
), we may rewrite (
14

\dot{s}\geq\overline{j}\left(a^{\ast}+\mathcal{I}_{-}\left(1/\overline{j}\right)\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(73)

Next, we use in Eq. (

[
24
,

]

\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/j)=\mathcal{I}_{J}(-j)-a^{\ast}{j},

(74)

which expresses the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{-}
of the first-passage time
T
at the negative threshold in terms of the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{J}(j)=\lim_{t\to\infty}|p_{J/t}(j)|/t
of
J(t)
, yielding

\dot{s}\geq\mathcal{I}_{J}(-j).

(75)

Since the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{J}
is the Fenchel-Legendre transform of
\lambda_{J}

[
79
]
,

\mathcal{I}_{J}(j)=\max_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\left(-aj-\lambda_{J}(a)\right),

(76)

we can express Eq. (
75
) as Eq. (
15
), which is what we were meant to show.

In Fig.
2
, we illustrate Eq. (

) by plotting
\lambda_{J}(a)
for a generic current
J(t)
and indicating the quantities
\overline{j}
,
a^{\ast}
, and
\tilde{a}
[as defined in (

5
Symmetries in the first-passage times of fluctuating currents

As mentioned in the introduction, it has previously been observed that optimality of currents is related to their symmetry
[
63
]
. In Sec.
5.1
we further clarify the connection between symmetry and optimality. Subsequently, in Sec.
5.2
we derive a symmetry relation that applies to the fluctuations of generic currents.

5.1
Symmetries for optimal currents

Optimal currents are defined as currents for which the equality in Eq. (
3
) is attained
[
63
]
, i.e.,

\dot{s}=\overline{j}a^{\ast}.

(77)

These currents optimise the trade-off relation between uncertainty, speed, and dissipation as expressed by Eq. (
3
). Furthermore, for optimal currents all the dissipation contained in the process
X
is captured by estimates based on the effective affinity using Eq. (
77
).

In Ref.
[
63
]
, it was shown that the fluctuating entropy production
S(t)
is optimal, and that currents belonging to the same cycle equivalence class as
S(t)
are also optimal. These currents also satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen type fluctuation symmetry (
12
) and the corresponding symmetry (
11
) for the statistics of first-passage times, which suggests a relation between symmetry and optimality. However, the Gallavotti-Cohen fluctuation symmetry is not a sufficient condition for optimality, as there exist currents that satisfy this symmetry but are not optimal
[
63
]
. In addition, it has not been proven that the Gallavotti-Cohen fluctuation symmetry is a necessary condition.

Here, we show that optimal currents satisfy the symmetry condition

\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T\rangle_{+}}{\ell_{+}}=\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T\rangle_{-}}{\ell_{-}},

(78)

and thus a necessary condition for optimality is that the average speed of reaching the positive threshold equals the average speed of reaching the negative threshold. Indeed, Eq. (
78
) readily follows from Eq. (
14
). The Eq. (
14
) together with the definition of optimality implies that
\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/\overline{j})=0
. Since
1/\overline{j}=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\langle T\rangle_{+}/\ell_{+}
, and since for a large-deviation rate function
\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)=0
if and only if
\tau=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\langle T\rangle_{-}/\ell_{-}
, the symmetry relation (
78
) follows from the necessary condition
\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/\overline{j})=0
. Note that Eq. (
78
) is a necessary and sufficient condition for the equality of the right-hand sides of the bounds (
2
) and (
14
).

Examples of currents that satisfy the symmetry (
78
) are currents that satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen fluctuation symmetry (
12
), but they are not the only kind of currents for which (
78
) holds. Indeed, for currents that satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen fluctuation symmetry the rate functions at the positive and negative thresholds are the same, see Eq. (
11
), whereas (
78
) is the weaker statement that the locations of the minima of the rate functions are the same.

We can also express the symmetry (
78
) as a property of
\mathcal{I}_{J}
or
\lambda_{J}(a)
. Specifically, the symmetry (
78
) is equivalent to

\mathcal{I}_{J}(-\overline{j})-a^{\ast}\overline{j}=\mathcal{I}_{J}(\overline{j})=0,

(79)

which we recognise as Eq. (
12
) for
j=\overline{j}
. In terms of
\lambda_{J}(a)
we get the condition

a^{\ast}=\tilde{a},

(80)

where
\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast})=0
and
\lambda^{\prime}_{J}(\tilde{a})=\overline{j}
,
as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

As we have seen, there exists a nontrivial relationship between current symmetries and optimality. We summarise this in Fig.
3
with a Venn diagram that represents the following sets of fluctuating currents:

•

The set
\mathcal{J}
is the set of all fluctuating currents of
X
as defined in Eq. (
26
), namely,

\mathcal{J}=\left\{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\left\{x\right\}}c_{xy}N^{xy}(t):\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}^{2},c_{xy}=-c_{yx}\in\mathbb{R}\right\};

(81)

Note that
\mathcal{J}
is isomorphic with
\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|/2}
, with
\mathcal{E}
the set of allowable transitions
(x,y)
with
c_{xy}\neq 0
.

•

The set
\mathcal{J}_{\rm v}
is the set of currents satisfying the weak symmetry Eq. (
78
), viz.,

\mathcal{J}_{\rm v}=\left\{J\in\mathcal{J}:\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\langle T\rangle_{+}/\ell_{+}=\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\langle T\rangle_{-}/\ell_{-}\right\};

(82)

•

The set
\mathcal{J}_{\rm sym}
is the set of currents that satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen type fluctuation symmetry,

\mathcal{J}_{\rm sym}=\left\{J\in\mathcal{J}:\lambda_{J}(a)=\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast}-a)\right\};

(83)

•

The set
\mathcal{J}_{\rm opt}
is the set of optimal currents,

\mathcal{J}_{\rm opt}=\left\{J\in\mathcal{J}:\dot{s}=\overline{j}a^{\ast}\right\};

(84)

•

The set
\mathcal{J}_{S}
is the set of currents that belong to the cycle equivalence classes
[kS_{t}]
with
k\in\mathbb{R}
, viz.,

\mathcal{J}_{S}=\cup_{k\in\mathbb{R}}[kS_{t}].

(85)

A cycle equivalence class is a set of currents for which the cycle coefficients
c_{\gamma}=\sum_{x_{j}\in\gamma}c_{x_{j}x_{j+1}}
with
\gamma
the cycles that belong to a fundamental cycle basis of the graph of admissible transitions of
X
(see Section 6.1 of Ref.
[
63
]
for details). In other words, these are the currents that have, up to a proportionality constant, the same cycle coefficients as the fluctuating entropy production
S_{t}
.

The stars in Fig.
3
indicate regions in the Venn diagram that are known to be nonempty (as we discuss in the examples of this paper), whereas the question marks indicate regions in the Venn diagram for which example currents have not yet been found.

5.2
Generalised symmetries for generic currents

We show that the symmetry (
13
) applies to generic currents
J(t)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{I}_{-}(\tau)=\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau),

(86)

where
\hat{\mathcal{I}}^{\dagger}_{+}(\tau)
is the rate function for the first-passage time
T
at the positive threshold
\ell_{+}
in a dual process associated with
J
. The dual process is the Markov chain described by the matrix
\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}
of transition probabilities (or transition rates). The matrix
\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}
is obtained in two steps. First, we define the matrix
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
, which is given by the Doob transform
[
11
]

\mathbf{\hat{q}}=\bm{{\phi}}_{a^{\ast}}^{-1}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(a^{\ast})\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}},

(87)

of the tilted matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a^{\ast})
, obtained by setting
a=a^{\ast}
in Eq. (
2.2
). Here,
\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by the elements of the right eigenvector
\phi_{a^{\ast}}(x)
associated with the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a^{\ast})
. Second, we time-reverse the matrix
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
(see Eq. (
35
)) to obtain
\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{\rm ss}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{q}}\bm{r}_{\rm ss}

(88)

\displaystyle=\bm{r}_{\rm ss}^{-1}\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}^{-1}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(a^{\ast})\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}\bm{r}_{\rm ss},

(89)

where
\mathbf{r}_{\rm ss}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by the steady state probability distribution
r_{\rm ss}(x)
of the Markov chain described by
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{\rm ss}^{-1}
is its inverse. Note that the
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
obtained from the generalised Doob transform describes a Markov process whose trajectory weights are asymptotically equivalent to the trajectory weights of the original process
X
conditioned on the value of the current
J(t)=t\lambda_{J}^{\prime}(a^{\ast})
for large values of
t

[
11
]
. Furthermore, note that Ref.
[
49
]
uses the notation
\mathbf{q}^{\ast}
for the Doob transform
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
at
a=a^{\ast}
.

The generalised first-passage symmetry (
86
) is equivalent to the following generalised fluctuation relation of the current
J
,

\hat{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{J}(a^{\ast}-a)=\lambda_{J}(a),

(90)

where
\hat{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{J}(a)
is the scaled cumulant generating function of the current
J(t)
in the dual process. Indeed, Eq. (
90
) along with Eq.(
68
) and Eq.(
72
) gives the generalised first passage time symmetry (
86
).

Next, we derive the generalised fluctuation symmetry Eq. (
90
). To do this, we consider the fluctuations of the current
J(t)
in the Markov chain determined by
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
. The scaled cumulant generating function
\hat{\lambda}_{J}(a)
in this Markov chain is the Perron root of the tilted matrix of
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
, i.e.,

\mathbf{\tilde{\hat{q}}}(a)=\mathbf{\hat{q}}\circ\exp(-a\mathbf{c}).

(91)

Here,
\circ
represents the element-wise product between two matrices,
\mathbf{c}
is the matrix with off-diagonal entries
c_{xy}
and diagonal entries equal to zero, and
\exp(-a\mathbf{c})
is matrix whose entries are given by
\exp(-ac_{xy})
, where we have employed a slight abuse of the conventional matrix notation for the exponential. Using the definition of
\mathbf{\hat{q}}
, given by Eq. (
87
), in Eq.
91
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{\tilde{\hat{q}}}(a)

\displaystyle=\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}^{-1}\big[\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(a^{\ast})\circ\exp(-a\mathbf{c})\big]\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}

(92)

\displaystyle=\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}}^{-1}\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(a^{\ast}+a)\bm{\phi}_{a^{\ast}},

(93)

where in the first equality we have used that the element-wise matrix product commutes with the diagonal transformation, and in the second equality, we have used the definition (
2.2
) of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}
. Equation (
93
) implies that
\mathbf{\tilde{\hat{q}}}(a)
and
\mathbf{\tilde{q}}(a^{\ast}+a)
are related by a similarity transformation, and thus these two matrices have the same eigenvalues. Hence, it holds that also their Perron roots are equal, giving

\hat{\lambda}_{J}(a)=\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast}+a),

(94)

as illustrated by panels (a) and (c) of Fig.
4
. Additionally, applying Eq. (
37
) to
\hat{\lambda}_{J}(a)
we find that

\hat{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{J}(a)=\hat{\lambda}_{J}(-a),

(95)

where
\hat{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{J}(a)
is the logarithmic moment generating function of
J(t)
in the time-reversed process with rate matrix
\mathbf{\hat{q}}^{\dagger}
(see panel (d) of Fig.
4
).
Combining Eqs. (
94
) and (
95
) we obtain the Eq. (
90
) that we were meant to show.

Note that in the above calculations there are two operations on matrices
\mathbf{q}
: the Doob transform
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
at the effective affinity
a^{\ast}
[given by Eq. (
87
)], and the time-reversal
\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}
[given by Eq. (
35
)]. One can show that these two operations commute, i.e.,
(\hat{\mathbf{q}})^{\dagger}=\widehat{(\mathbf{q}^{\dagger})}
, as two Markov matrices that are obtained from a diagonal transformations of a matrix have to be equal. This is illustrated in Fig.
4
in terms of the scaled cumulant generating function of the current
J(t)
in each of the processes described by the matrices
\mathbf{q}
,
\hat{\mathbf{q}}
,
\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}
, and
\mathbf{\hat{q}^{\dagger}}
.

6
Examples

In this section we illustrate the bounds on dissipation and the symmetry properties of currents in two toy examples of nonequilibrium processes.

The right-hand sides of the inequalities (
2
) and (
14
) determine the amount of the time-irreversibility, and thus also dissipation, captured by the random variables
T
and
D
. The right-hand side of (
2
) has been referred to as the first-passage ratio
[
47
,
50
]

\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}=\frac{\ell_{+}}{\ell_{-}}\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\langle T\rangle}

(96)

and determines how much of the dissipation
\dot{s}
in the process
X
is captured by the random variable
D={\rm sign}(J(T))
. The right-hand side of (
14
) considers the amount of dissipation captured by both
D
and the first-passage time
T
, and we call here the right-hand of (
14
) the improved first-passage ratio,

\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR}=\frac{\ell_{+}}{\langle T\rangle}\left(\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\ell_{-}}+\mathcal{I}_{-}\left(1/\overline{j}\right)\right).

(97)

The difference
\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR}-\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}
determines the additional dissipation captured by the first-passage time
T
that is not captured by
D
.

In the following we determine
\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}
and
\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR}
for two toy models, both of which are Markov chains in continuous time. In this way, we will quantify the amount of the dissipation contained in the time-irreversibility of the random variables
D
and
T
. Furthermore, we will find examples of currents that satisfy the symmetry condition (
16
) and discuss their place in the Venn diagram of Fig.
3
.

6.1
Biased random walker on a two-dimensional lattice

We consider a random walker that moves on a two-dimensional lattice with periodic boundary conditions (equivalent to a lattice on a torus)
[
47
]
. The process
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
denotes the position of the random walker, with
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
the two spatial coordinates of
X
. The coordinate processes are two independent jump process described by

\mathrm{d}X_{i}(t)=\mathrm{d}N_{i}^{+}(t)-\mathrm{d}N_{i}^{-}(t),\quad i\in\{1,2\},

(98)

where
N_{i}^{+}
and
N^{-}_{i}
are Poisson counting processes with rates
k_{i}^{+}
and
k^{-}_{i}
, respectively.
We parametrise the rates of the jump processes as

\displaystyle k_{1}^{+}=\frac{\exp(\nu/2)}{4\cosh(\nu/2)},

\displaystyle k_{1}^{-}=\frac{\exp(\nu/2)}{4\cosh(\nu/2)},

(99)

\displaystyle k_{2}^{+}=\frac{\exp(\rho\nu/2)}{4\cosh(\rho\nu/2)},

\displaystyle k_{2}^{-}=\frac{\exp(-\rho\nu/2)}{4\cosh(\rho\nu/2)},

(100)

where
\nu
and
\rho
are two parameters controlling the bias of the motion in the two directions. The parametrisation sets
k^{+}_{1}+k_{1}^{-}+k_{2}^{+}+k_{2}^{-}=1
, corresponding to a choice of time units such that the average time for the particle to move equals one. Moreover, this parametrisation sets
k^{+}_{1}+k^{-}_{1}=k^{+}_{2}+k^{-}_{2}
so that the mean jump time is the same in both directions.

Up to an irrelevant scaling constant, an arbitrary current in this model may be expressed as

J(t)=(1-\Delta)J_{1}(t)+(1+\Delta)J_{1}(t)

(101)

in terms of a single parameter,
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
J_{i}(t)=N_{i}^{+}(t)-N_{i}^{-}(t)
, with
i\in{1,2}
, are the currents in the two directions.

The average rate of entropy production for the random walker is

\dot{s}=\nu(k_{1}^{+}-k_{1}^{-})+\nu\rho(k_{2}^{+}-k_{2}^{-})

(102)

and the fluctuating entropy production equals

S(t)=\nu J_{1}(t)+\rho\nu J_{2}(t).

(103)

The fluctuating entropy production
S
is proportional to
J
when
\Delta=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}
.

Figure
5
shows the ratios
\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}/\dot{s}
and
\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR}/\dot{s}
as a function of the parameter
\Delta
, which specifies the current
J
for four different choices of
\rho
and
\nu
corresponding with four different processes
X
(left panels of the four subfigures). These plots reveal that the random variable
D
captures for a broad range of parameters
\Delta
a large fraction of the total dissipation
\dot{s}
, up to
100\%
of the total dissipation when
\Delta=(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)
. Instead, the random variable
T
in general captures a small fraction of the dissipation
\dot{s}
. This is more clearly visible in the right panels of the four subfigures, where
(\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}-\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR})/\dot{s}
is plotted as a function of
\Delta
. We observe that for most examples of currents the fraction of dissipation captured by
T
is negligible. Nevertheless, as shown in the panels with
(\rho,\nu)=(2,4)
and
(\rho,\nu)=(10,4)
, there do exist currents for which
T
captures up to
40\%
of the total dissipation. These are currents for which the statistics of first-passage times
T
is highly asymmetrical between both thresholds.

From the right-panels of the Figs.
5
, we can identify the currents for which
\hat{s}_{\rm FPR}-\hat{s}_{\rm iFPR}=0
, and thus the symmetry condition (
16
) is satisfied. The relevant currents are those with: (i)
\Delta=(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)
, which is proportional to
S(t)
, and thus belongs to the set
\mathcal{J}_{S}
. This is an example of an optimal current.; (ii) the currents with
\Delta\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
that satisfy the Gallavotti-Cohen-type fluctuation symmetry but are not optimal. These currents belong to the set
\mathcal{J}_{\rm sym}\setminus\mathcal{J}_{S}
. Note that in this model all currents that satisfy the weak symmetry condition
(
16
) also satisfy the stronger symmetry condition (
11
).

Markov jump process with four states

We illustrate the application of the bounds (

X(t)\in\mathcal{X}=\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}

C_{1}=(1,2,3,1)

Discussion

We elaborate here on a few remaining points that we think are worth discussing. In Sec.
7.1
, we review the assumptions underlying the derivation of the main results; in Sec.
7.2
, we examine the differences between first-passage problems in discrete and continuous time; and lastly, in Sec.
7.3
, we explore potential applications of the paper’s main results in physical chemistry.

7.1
Assumptions

The inequalities (
2
) and (
14
) rely on the following assumptions: we require that (i) the thresholds
\ell_{-}
and
\ell_{+}
are infinitely large; (ii) the process
X_{t}
is ergodic; (iii) the process
X_{t}
has even parity under time-reversal, i.e., the time reversed Markov chain is described by Eq. (
35
); (iv) the time-additive observable is a fluctuating current, i.e.,
c_{xy}=-c_{yx}
; and (v) the set
|\mathcal{X}|
is finite.

The first four conditions (i)-(iv) are crucial in deriving Eqs. (
2
) and (
14
). The condition (v) is less essential, as the inequalities Eqs. (
2
) and (
14
) apply in general to Markov jump processes defined on sets
\mathcal{X}
of infinite cardinality or for overdamped Langevin processes. Nevertheless, some of the results — such as Eqs. (
56
) — were derived under the assumption of finite cardinality. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extending these results to cases involving infinite cardinality. We will discuss this scenario in more detail in a future study.

If the assumptions (iii) and (iv) are not satisfied, then the main results of this paper do not hold. However, the following more general inequalities apply in this case:

\dot{s}\geq\frac{|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|}{\langle T\rangle}(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)),

(109)

and

\dot{s}\geq\frac{\ell_{+}}{\langle T\rangle}\left(\frac{|\ln p^{\dagger}_{+}|}{\ell_{-}}+\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}(1/\overline{j})\right)(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(110)

For example, Eq. (
109
) and (
110
) apply to time-additive observables that are not fluctuating currents, such as the total number of jumps from state
x
to state
y

[
22
]
(without subtracting the reverse jumps), or to fluctuating currents in systems with magnetic fields
[
13
]
.
The assumptions (iii) and (iv) are crucial for the derivation of Eqs.(
2
) and (
14
) because, without them, the symmetries expressed by Eqs.(
56
) and (
67
) do not hold. As a result, we cannot relate the time-reversed quantities
p^{\dagger}_{+}
and
\mathcal{I}^{\dagger}_{+}
to their counterparts in the forward dynamics. Instead, the main limitations on the validity of the general inequalities (
109
) and (
110
) stem from the assumptions required to derive Wald’s equality
\langle S(T)\rangle=\dot{s}\langle T\rangle(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1))
, namely, large thresholds, the ergodicity of the process
X
, and the fact that
T
is a stopping time (a random time that obeys causality).

The inequalities Eqs. (
109
) and (
110
), although less useful for the inference of kinetic properties of molecular systems, clarify some of the phenomenology on trade-offs between dissipation and accuracy as observed in the literature. These inequalities imply that the trade-off between dissipation and accuracy should be understood as a trade-off with the accuracy in the time-reversed process. The fact that for some systems this trade-off also involves the accuracy of the original process arises from the coincidence that in these systems the statistics of the forward and reverse processes are identical.
An illustrative example is the dynamics of a charged particle moving in a magnetic field and driven out of equilibrium by an external torque. As shown in Ref.
[
13
]
, increasing the magnetic field leads to a decrease in the rate of dissipation and an increase in the accuracy, and thus remarkably there is no trade-off between dissipation and accuracy. Nevertheless, according to Eq. (
110
), in the time-reversed process, where the sign of the magnetic field is inverted, accuracy should correspondingly increase. Therefore, the observed absence of a trade-off between dissipation and accuracy in systems with magnetic fields stems from the fact that the time-reversed dynamics differs from the forward dynamics, and thus accuracy should be measured in the system with the magnetic field inverted to observe the trade-off.

7.2
Discrete versus continuous time

The derivations in this paper also clarify distinctions and similarities between the first-passage time statistics of currents in discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains.

The
inequalities (
2
) and (
14
) apply to both discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains. This was not evident from previous derivations
[
48
,
63
]
, which relied on the parabolic bound on the large deviation rate function of
J(t)
[
58
,
25
]
– a result specific to continuous-time Markov chains
[
61
]
. Consequently, the effective affinity,
a^{\ast}
, also extends to discrete-time Markov chains, serving as a generalization of chemical affinity to systems with multiple coupled currents
[
63
]
.

Note that the thermodynamic uncertainty relation (
2
) for first-passage times does not apply to discrete-time Markov chains. Nevertheless, our analysis shows that the following discrete-time thermodynamic uncertainty relation holds for first-passage times in discrete time,

\exp(\dot{s})-1\geq 2\frac{\langle T\rangle_{+}}{\langle T^{2}\rangle_{+}-\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+}},

(111)

where, as before, we have assumed that the time unit between two consecutive time-steps equals one. The inequality (
111
) follows from the inequality (2) of Ref.
[
61
]
and the identities

\overline{j}=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ell_{+}}{\langle T\rangle_{+}}

(112)

and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle J^{2}(t)\rangle-\langle J(t)\rangle^{2}}{t}=\overline{j}^{3}\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle T^{2}\rangle_{+}-\langle T\rangle^{2}_{+}}{\ell_{+}}

(113)

that can be derived from Eqs. (
134
) by following derivations similar to those in continuous time Markov chains
[
49
]
.

7.3
Thermodynamic inference from first-passage time statistics

The results of this paper can be applied to infer the kinetic and thermodynamic properties of macromolecular systems from observations of a fluctuating current
J
, as well as, to develop coarse-grained descriptions of their dynamics. A good example are molecular motors, where
J
corresponds to the position, and
X
represents the different conformational and chemical states of the motor. In this context, experimental results on the fluctuations of
D={\rm sign}(J(T))
and
T
are available
[
53
,
1
,
64
]
.

The experiments on kinesin in Ref.
[
53
]
show that for molecular motors
\langle T\rangle_{+}\approx\langle T\rangle_{-}
(see Panel b of Figure 5 in Ref.
[
53
]
). Based on our results we can conclude that are two possible interpretations for this symmetry. Either (i) the positional current is optimal (it belongs to
\mathcal{J}_{\rm opt}
in Fig.
3
), in which case
a^{\ast}\overline{j}\approx\dot{s}
; or (ii) the positional current belongs to the set
\mathcal{J}_{\rm v}\setminus\mathcal{J}_{\rm opt}
, and thus the positional current satisfies

\langle T\rangle_{+}\approx\langle T\rangle_{-}
even though it is not optimal. Note that the symmetry in the fist-passage time of
J
is relevant for developing thermodynamically consistent coarse-grained of molecular motors
[
85
]
.

The inequalities (
2
) and (
14
) can be used to bound the efficiency of the chemomechanical coupling in motor proteins based on measurements of the fraction of backsteps (through
p_{-}
) and the fluctuations in the dwell times
T
(through
\mathcal{I}_{-}(1/\overline{j})
)
[
47
]
.
In this regard, the examples in Sec.
6
show that in general the improvement gained from the first-passage times
T
is small. It remains to be understood, also in the perspective of thermodynamically consistent coarse-graining
[
78
,
30
]
, what observable contains the part of
\langle S(T)\rangle
that is not already contained in
D={\rm sign}(J(T))
.

Lastly, let us compare the inequalities (
2
) and (
14
) with other approaches in the literature that bound the rate of dissipation based on partial observations in a Markov process
[
28
,
80
,
8
,
18
,
21
]
.
The inequality (
14
) has a formal similarity with the ”waiting-time” bounds of Refs.
[
28
,
80
]
, which also consist of two terms, one with and one without temporal information (for example, see Eq.(6) of Ref.
[
28
]
). Both approaches have advantages and disadvantages. Approaches as in
Refs.
[
28
,
80
]
require that the observer can measure transitions between individual states of the Markov process
X
. It is unclear how strong this approximation is for macromolecular systems, as simple proteins, such as myoglobin, have a very large number of
quasi-degenerate microscopic states
[
20
]
. Hence, a very good microscope would be required to measure the individual transitions. Instead, the approach in the present paper, based on the inequalities (
2
) and (
14
), does not assume that the observer can measure transitions between individual states of
X
. Instead, an observer just needs to be able to measure a fluctuating current, which is inline with experimental setups that measure the positional current of a molecular motor
[
53
]
. However, a drawback of the present approach is that we require the limit of large thresholds. This drawback may be less significant than it initially appears: case studies indicate that the asymptotic limit of large thresholds is rapidly reached when the system is far from thermal equilibrium, as in this regime
p_{-}
describes rare events even at low thresholds.
Overall, the different approaches are based on distinct approximate representations of experimental setups. Therefore, it is likely prudent to employ a number of different methods when inferring thermodynamic and kinetic properties from the fluctuations of a mesoscopic system.

Appendix A
Coarse-graining of
\langle S(T)\rangle
: general approach from a probability theoretic perspective

The coarse-graining of
\langle S(T)\rangle
that we use in Secs.
3
and
4

applies generically to the average entropy production
S(t)
evaluated at the stopping time
T
of a stochastic process
X
, as we discuss in this appendix. This follows from the fact that
\langle S(T)\rangle
can generically be expressed a Kullback-Leibler divergence, and coarse-graining applies generically to Kullback-Leibler divergences. To demonstrate this we use here a general probability theoretic approach, which relies on concepts developed in the theory of sequential hypothesis testing, see Ref.
[
77
]
. For terminology we refer to text books in probability theory
[
86
,
41
]
.

A.1
Defining
\langle S(T)\rangle
in a probability theoretical fashion

Consider a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, where
\Omega
is the set of outcomes,
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of
\Omega
,
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
is an increasing sequence of sub-
\sigma
-algebras (
\mathcal{F}_{s}\subset\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t>s
), and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure defined on
\mathcal{F}
. Furthermore, we consider a second probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
so that
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
are locally, mutually, absolutely continuous. This latter implies that
\mathbb{P}(\Phi)=0\Leftrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}(\Phi)=0
for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
and all
t\geq 0
.

For the construction of the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
of a Markov chain, as considered in the main text, see Refs.
[
54
,
39
]
. Briefly, in this case the elements of
\Omega
are the trajectories
x^{\infty}_{0}
. The sub-
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{F}_{t}
are those generated by the random variables
X(t^{\prime})
with
t^{\prime}\in[0,t]
. The probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathcal{F}
is constructed using the statistics provided by the
\mathbf{q}
-matrix and the probability mass function
p_{X(0)}
of the initial state.

We define the stochastic entropy production as

S(t)=\ln\frac{\left.d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}{\left.d\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}},

(114)

where

\frac{\left.d\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}{\left.d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}}=e^{-S(t)}

(115)

is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of the measure
\left.\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
with respect to
\left.\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
. Here, the measures
\left.\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
and
\left.\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
are the restrictions of the measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
. This means that
\left.\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(\Phi)=\mathbb{P}(\Phi)
for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
and
\left.\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}(\Phi)=0
for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

Let
T
be a stopping time, i.e., a random time
T
for which
\left\{T\leq t\right\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
. To each stopping time
T
, we can associate the optional
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{F}_{T}=\left\{\Phi\in\mathcal{F}:\Phi\cap\left\{\omega\in\Omega:T(\omega)\leq t\right\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t},\>\forall t\right\},

(116)

which is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{F}
. The entropy production
S(T)
is defined through the Radon-Nikodym derivative process

\exp(-S(T))=\frac{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}},

(117)

where now
\left.\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}
and
\left.\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}
are the restrictions of the measures
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}
to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{T}
.
The average entropy production
\langle S(T)\rangle
takes thus the form

\langle S(T)\rangle=\int_{\omega\in\Omega}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}.

(118)

In the case of a Markov chain the definitions of
\exp(-S(t))
and
\exp(-S(T))
as defined here correspond with those of Markov chains in the main text. Notably, in this case we get that

\exp(-S(t))=\frac{p^{\dagger}(X^{t}_{0})}{p(X^{t}_{0})},

(119)

with
p
as defined in Eqs. (
21
) and (
25
) in discrete and continuous time, respectively, and
p^{\dagger}
is the corresponding quantity for the time-reversed process determined by the matrix
\mathbf{q}^{\dagger}
. Analogously, it holds that

\exp(-S(T))=\frac{p^{\dagger}(X^{T}_{0})}{p(X^{T}_{0})}.

(120)

However, note that the formula (
117
) also applies to processes for which the Radon-Nikodym derivative cannot be straightforwardly expressed as the ratio of two path probability densities, as in Langevin processes with multiplicative noise
[
65
]
.

A.2
Coarse-graining of
\langle S(T)\rangle

We coarse-grain the quantity
\langle S(T)\rangle
by defining a random variable, say
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
that is
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable, where
\mathcal{Y}
is the set of possible values of
Y
. From the definition of a random variable it follows that it partitions the set
\Omega
as

\Omega=\cup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Phi_{y}

(121)

where

\Phi_{y}=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:Y(\omega)=y\right\}.

(122)

Furthermore, since
Y
is
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable, it holds that

\Phi_{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
. Note that
\Phi_{y}\cap\Phi_{y^{\prime}}=\phi
for all
y\neq y^{\prime}
.

To proceed, we follow a derivation similar to Lemma 3.2.1 of Ref.
[
77
]
to coarse-grainig
\langle S(T)\rangle
with respect to the random variable
Y
. Since the
\Phi_{y}
form a partition of
\Omega
, we can decompose the right-hand side of Eq. (
118
) as

\int_{\omega\in\Omega}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\omega\in\Phi_{y}}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}.

(123)

As
\Phi_{y}\in\mathcal{F}_{T}
and
\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}
is a
\mathcal{F}_{T}
-measurable, we can express the right-hand side of the previous equation by

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\omega\in\Phi_{y}}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\int_{\omega\in\Phi_{y}}\left.{\rm d}\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\omega)\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}.

(124)

Notice that the left-hand side of (
124
) contains an integral over the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
whereas the right-hand side is an integral over the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{T},\left.\mathbb{P}\right|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}})

[
76
]
. As
\ln
is a strictly concave function, we can apply Jensen’s inequality
to the expectation value with respect to the probability measure
\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\cdot)/\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})

[
31
,
17
]
to get

\displaystyle\int_{\omega\in\Phi_{y}}{\rm d}\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\omega)\ln\frac{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})\int_{\omega\in\Phi_{y}}\frac{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})}\ln\frac{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{{\rm d}\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}(\omega)

(125)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})\ln\frac{\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})}{\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})}.

Combining Eq. (
123
) with (
124
) and (
125
) we obtain

\langle S(T)\rangle\geq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})\ln\frac{\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})}{\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}(\Phi_{y})},

(126)

which is the final result.
The equality in Eq. (
126
) is attained when
\ln\frac{d\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{d\mathbb{P}^{\dagger}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}
is constant almost everywhere on
\Phi_{y}
.
Note that Eq. (
49
) in Sec.
3.1.2
is a special case of (
126
) when
Y=D
. In case of
Y=(D,T)
, the sum in Eq. (
126
) becomes an integral and we recover Eq. (
60
).

Appendix B
Large deviation principles for first-passage times

In this appendix, we develop the large deviation theory for the first-passage problem
T
defined in Eq. (
1
). Specifically, we derive the Eqs. (
51
), (
57
) and (
68
) from the main text that relate the large deviation properties of
T
to those of
J
.

The tools we use to derive the large deviation properties of
T
are based on martingale theory, see Refs.
[
63
,
49
]
. Specifically, we use that the process

M(t)=\phi_{a}(X(t))\exp\left(-aJ(t)-\lambda_{J}(a)t\right)

(127)

is a martingale, where
\phi_{a}
is the right eigenvector associated with the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
. Applying Doob’s optional stopping theorem to
M(T)

[
86
]
, we can relate the splitting probability
p_{-}
and the moment generating functions
m_{-}
and
m_{+}
in the limit of large thresholds to the scale cumulant generating function
\lambda_{J}
. These arguments date back to the seminal works of Ville
[
81
]
and Wald
[
83
,
82
]
, who analysed the first-passage problem (
1
) in the case when
J(t)
is a sum of independent and identically distributed random variables. In this case, the martingale
M(t)
is identical to
Wald’s martingale
[
82
,
2
]
for which
\phi_{a}=1
. In the present context of time-additive observables in Markov processes, martingales of the form (
127
) have been studied in Refs.
[
63
,
49
]
and similar martingales appear in the theory of Markov additive processes, see Refs.
[
3
,
2
]
and references therein.

Note that the martingales (
127
) apply to both continuous-time and discrete-time Markov chains. The distinction between both cases is in the definition of the tilted matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
. In continuous time, the
\mathbf{q}
-matrix in Eq. (
2.2
) is the transition rate matrix of a continuous-time Markov chain, and in discrete time the
\mathbf{q}
-matrix in Eq. (
2.2
) is the matrix of transition probabilities between states. Consequently, in continuous time
\lambda_{J}(a)
is the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
, while in discrete time
\lambda_{J}(a)
is the logarithm of the Perron root of
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}(a)
.

This Appendix is structured into two parts. In the first
B.1
we investigate the decay of the splitting probability
p_{-}
with the threshold parameter
\ell_{-}
, and in
B.2
we focus on the moment generating functions of
T
conditioned on hitting the positive (or negative) threshold first.

B.1
Splitting probability
p_{-}

We show that for
\overline{j}>0
the splitting probability
p_{-}
is an exponentially decaying function of
\ell_{-}
, i.e.,

\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\ln p_{-}|}{\ell_{-}}=a^{\ast}.

(128)

Additionally, we show that
a^{\ast}
is the nonzero solution of

\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast})=0,

(129)

with
\lambda_{J}
the scaled cumulant generating function of
J
. Note that in Eq. (
128
) the threshold
\ell_{+}
can take an arbitrary finite value, or can even be an arbitrary function of
\ell_{-}
.

We derive the aforementioned results following calculations that are
similar to the ones in Ref.
[
63
]
. However, the derivation here applies to both discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains, while Ref.
[
63
]
considered continuous-time Markov chains.

As
\mathbb{P}(T<\infty)=1
and
M(t)\in(-\ell_{-},\ell_{+})
for
t<T
, we can apply Doob’s optional stopping theorem
[
86
,
65
]

\langle M(T)\rangle=\langle M(0)\rangle=\langle\phi_{a}(X(0))\rangle

(130)

to the martingale
M_{t}
. Setting
a=a^{\ast}
, where
a^{\ast}
is defined as the nonzero solution to Eq. (
129
), we recover the identity

p_{-}\langle\phi_{a^{\ast}}(X_{T})\rangle_{-}e^{a^{\ast}\ell_{-}(1+o_{\ell_{-}}(1))}+p_{+}\langle\phi_{a^{\ast}}(X(T))e^{-a^{\ast}J(T)}\rangle_{+}=\langle\phi_{a^{\ast}}(X_{0})\rangle.

(131)

Here,
\langle\cdot\rangle_{-}=\langle\cdot|J(T)\leq-\ell_{-}\rangle
is the average conditioned on crossing the negative threshold first, and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{+}=\langle\cdot|J(T)\geq\ell_{+}\rangle
on crossing the positive threshold first. In the first term of (
131
) we have used that
J(T)=-\ell_{-}(1+o_{\ell_{-}}(1))
where
o_{\ell_{-}}(1)
represents an arbitrary function that converges to zero for large values of
\ell_{-}
. Using

\mathbb{P}(T<\infty)=p_{-}+p_{+}=1,

(132)

we can substitute the
p_{+}
in Eq. (
131
) by
1-p_{-}
. Next, taking the limit
\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty
and using that the set
\mathcal{X}
is finite so that
|\phi_{a}(x)|
can be bounded from above, we recover the Eq. (
128
) with
a^{\ast}
the solution to (
129
).

B.2
Generating functions
m_{+}
and
m_{-}

We show that the generating functions

m_{+}(\mu)=\lim_{\ell_{+}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{+}}\ln\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{+}\quad{\rm and}\quad m_{-}(\mu)=\lim_{\ell_{-}\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\ell_{-}}\ln\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{-}

(133)

are for
\overline{j}>0
the functional inverse of the scaled cumulant generating function
\lambda_{J}(a)

[
24
]
, viz.,

\lambda_{J}(m_{+}(\mu))=-\mu\quad{\rm and}\quad\lambda_{J}(a^{\ast}-m_{-}(\mu))=-\mu.

(134)

Equation (
134
) implies that the conditional distributions
p_{T}(t|+)
and
p_{T}(t|-)
satisfy a large deviation principle at both thresholds.

Note that Ref.
[
24
]
considers a first-passage problem with one threshold, while here we consider a first-passage problem with two thresholds. Here, we find that Eq. (
134
) applies for the generating functions as defined in (
133
). In this case, for
m_{-}
the threshold
\ell_{+}
can take an arbitrary finite value, or can even be an arbitrary function of
\ell_{-}
, and the converse is also true for
m_{+}
. The implication is that the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{+}
is independent of the threshold
\ell_{-}
, and analogously, the rate function
\mathcal{I}_{-}
is independent of
\ell_{+}
.

As in
B.1
, we use martingale arguments, this time following Ref.
[
49
]
. However, the derivation here applies to both continuous and discrete-time Markov chains, while in previous work the continuous time was considered.

Applying Doob’s optional stopping theorem to
M(T)
, we find

\displaystyle\langle M(T)\rangle

\displaystyle=p_{+}\langle\phi_{a}(X_{T})\exp(-aJ(t)-\lambda_{J}(a)T)\rangle_{+}

\displaystyle+p_{-}\langle\phi_{a}(X_{T})\exp(-aJ(t)-\lambda_{J}(a)T)\rangle_{-}=\langle\phi_{a}(X(0))\rangle

(135)

We take the parameter
a
to be a function of
\mu
so that

\lambda_{J}(a)=-\mu.

(136)

For a fluctuating current
J(t)
, this equation has two solutions, which we denote by
a_{+}(\mu)>-a_{-}(\mu)
.

For
a=a_{+}(\mu)
, we can express (
135
) as

\displaystyle e^{-a_{+}(\mu)\ell_{+}[1+o_{\ell_{+}}(1)]}\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{+}=\langle\phi_{a_{+}}(X(0))\rangle-p_{-}\langle\phi_{a_{+}}(X(T))e^{-a_{+}(\mu)J(T)+\mu T}\rangle_{-},

(137)

where we have used that
J(T)=\ell_{+}(1+o_{\ell_{+}}(1))
and that
|\phi_{a}(x)|
is bounded from above in the case of finite sets
|\mathcal{X}|
. Taking the logarithm of both sides and using the definition of
m_{+}(\mu)
in Eq. (
133
), we obtain in the limit of large
\ell_{+}
the identity

m_{+}(\mu)=a_{+}(\mu)(1+o_{\ell_{+}}(1)).

(138)

Plugging (
138
) into the Eq. (
136
) for the definition of
a_{+}
, we recover the first equality in Eq. (
134
).

Instead, for
a=-a_{-}(\mu)
, we can express (
135
) as

\displaystyle p_{-}e^{-a_{-}(\mu)\ell_{-}[1+o_{\ell_{-}}(1)]}\langle e^{\mu T}\rangle_{-}=\langle\phi_{-a_{-}}(X(0))\rangle-p_{+}\langle\phi_{-a_{-}}(X(T))e^{a_{-}(\mu)J(T)+\mu T}\rangle_{+}.

(139)

Taking the logarithm of both sides, we obtain in the limit of large threshold values
\ell_{-}
the identity

\displaystyle-a_{-}(\mu)=(a^{\ast}-m_{-}(\mu))(1+o_{\ell_{-}}(1))

(140)

where we have used Eq. (
128
) for
p_{-}
and the Eq. (
133
) for the definition of
m_{-}
. Plugging (
140
) into the Eq. (
136
) for the definition of
-a_{-}
, we recover the second equality in Eq. (
134
).

Appendix C
Wald’s equality for entropy production

We derive the following asymptotic version of Wald’s equation

\langle S(T)\rangle=\dot{s}\langle T\rangle(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1))

(141)

for processes
X
that are ergodic and for the first-passage time
T
in Eq. (
1
).

C.1
Wald’s equation

We use Wald’s equation for sums of independent and identically distributed random variables
[
82
,
83
,
7
]
. Let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
be an infinite sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables. Consider the sum

L_{N}=\sum^{N}_{j=1}Y_{j}

(142)

with
N
a stopping time of the process
Y_{j}
.
Then, Wald’s equation
[
82
,
83
,
7
]

\langle L_{N}\rangle=\langle N\rangle\langle Y_{1}\rangle

(143)

holds if
\langle N\rangle<\infty
and
\langle Y_{1}\rangle<\infty
. Note that for Wald’s equation it is not required that the
(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N})
are independent, but rather sequences
(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{n})
for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The condition
\langle N\rangle<\infty
is essential. For example, Wald’s equation does not apply to a simple random walker on
\mathbb{Z}
with one absorbing boundary.

In this Paper, we need a generalised form of Wald’s equation (see Theorem 12.9 in
[
33
]
). In this case, we consider a stopping time
N
of a stochastic process
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots)
, and a sequence
Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
of independent and identically random variables for which
Y_{j}
is a function of
X^{j}_{1}
. Wald’s equation (
143
) then also applies to the sum (
142
) if
\langle N\rangle<\infty
and
\langle Y_{1}\rangle<\infty
.

C.2
Derivation of Eq. (
141
)

We derive (
141
) by using Wald’s equation together with the fact that
X(t)
is an ergodic process on a finite set
\mathcal{X}
.

As
S(t)
is a time-additive observable, we can decompose it as

S(t)=S_{r}(t)+\sum^{N(t)}_{j=1}\Delta S_{j}

(144)

where
\Delta S_{j}=S(T_{j})-S(T_{j-1})
,
S_{r}=S(t)-S(T_{N(t)})
,
T_{j}
is the
j
-th time that the process
X(t)
returns to its initial state
X(0)
(i.e.,
X(T_{j})=X(0)
but
\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}X(T_{j}-\epsilon)\neq X(0)
), and
N(t)
is the number of times that
X(t)
has returned to the state
X(0)
in the time interval
[0,t]
. Notice that
T_{0}=0
.

As
X(t)
is a Markov process defined on a finite set
\mathcal{X}
, it satisfies the strong Markov property, and therefore the
\Delta S_{j}
are independent and identically distributed random variables
[
10
]
. Hence, we can apply Wald’s equation to the sum in (
144
) yielding

\langle S(T)\rangle=\langle S_{r}\rangle+\langle N(T)\rangle\langle\Delta S_{1}\rangle.

(145)

Analogously, we can decompose the stopping time
T
as

T=T_{r}+\sum^{N(T)}_{j=1}\Delta T_{j},

(146)

with
\Delta T_{j}=T_{j}-T_{j-1}
and
T_{r}=T-T_{j}
. Due to the strong Markov property the
\Delta T_{j}
are independent, and hence we can apply Wald’s equation to
T
to obtain

\langle T\rangle=\langle T_{r}\rangle+\langle N(T)\rangle\langle\Delta T_{1}\rangle.

(147)

Solving (
147
) towards
\langle N(T)\rangle
and substitution in (
145
) yields

\langle S(T)\rangle=\langle S_{r}(T)\rangle+(\langle T\rangle-\langle T_{r}\rangle)\frac{\langle\Delta S_{1}\rangle}{\langle\Delta T_{1}\rangle}.

(148)

Next, we use the fact that
X(t)
is an ergodic process. This property implies that the number of returns
N(t)
to the initial state diverges as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently, in the limit of
\ell_{\rm min}\rightarrow\infty
, the quantities
\langle N(T)\rangle
and
\langle T\rangle
diverge, while
\langle S_{r}\rangle\in o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)
and
\langle T_{r}\rangle\in o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)
. Thus, we obtain that

\langle S(T)\rangle=\langle T\rangle\frac{\langle\Delta S_{1}\rangle}{\langle\Delta T_{1}\rangle}(1+o_{\ell_{\rm min}}(1)).

(149)

Lastly, we use ergodicity to identify in Eq. (
149
)

\dot{s}=\frac{\langle\Delta S_{1}\rangle}{\langle\Delta T_{1}\rangle},

(150)

completing the derivation of (
141
).

We end this appendix with deriving (
150
). As before, we decompose the numerator and denominator in the definition of
\dot{s}
,

\dot{s}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\langle S(t)\rangle}{t},

(151)

in terms of returns to the initial state. The numerator is given by Eq. (
144
) and the denominator can be written as

t=T_{r}(t)_{+}\sum^{N(t)}_{j=1}\Delta T_{j},

(152)

which is similar to (
146
).
Using ergodicity and the law of large numbers, in the limit of
t\gg 1
we obtain

S(t)=\langle N(t)\rangle\langle\Delta S_{1}\rangle(1+o_{t}(1))

(153)

and

t=\langle N(t)\rangle\langle\Delta T_{1}\rangle(1+o_{t}(1)),

(154)

which combined with the definition (
151
) yields (
150
).
