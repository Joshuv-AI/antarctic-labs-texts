Vibration frequency drives oxidative membrane damage and metabolic remodeling underlying muscle quality deterioration in yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) under waterless transport
Authors: Guowei Huang, Tao Liao, Zhaoding Wang, Yansen Li, Liang Qiu, Lan Wang, Chan Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMechanical vibration is unavoidable during waterless live-fish transport, yet the chemical mechanisms driving muscle quality deterioration remain unclear. This study examined the effects of vibration frequency on muscle quality in yellow catfish Pelteobagrus fulvidraco during simulated waterless transport by integrating physicochemical traits, histology, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance, serum redox indices, and untargeted metabolomics. Higher vibration frequencies exacerbated water loss and texture deterioration, accompanied by progressive microstructural disruption and enhanced water mobility. Serum biomarkers indicated intensified oxidative stress, evidenced by increased malondialdehyde and glutathione depletion. Untargeted metabolomics annotated 1117 metabolites and revealed frequency-dependent metabolic remodeling, with pronounced disturbances in glycerophospholipid metabolism and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism. Key membrane phospholipids and glycosyl donors were depleted under high-frequency vibration, suggesting impaired membrane stability and structural maintenance. Results demonstrate that transportation vibration stress impairs muscle quality via regulating energy metabolism, membrane lipid stability, glycosylation processes, and cell apoptosis, thus providing a theoretical basis to optimize aquaculture transport and improve the quality of flesh quality resilience during waterless transport.Keywords: Waterless transport, Vibration stress, Membrane lipid remodeling, Water migration, Muscle quality

Introduction

Among animal-derived foods, meat products play an important role in the human diet because they provide high-quality protein, essential amino acids, and various micronutrients. With improvements in living standards and increasing health awareness, consumer demand for meat products is no longer limited to their function as sources of basic nutrition (Si et al., 2025); instead, consumers increasingly value their nutritional benefits, health-promoting properties, and sensory quality. Accordingly, freshness, nutritional value, and eating quality have become key factors influencing consumer choice. Compared with some livestock and poultry meats, fish muscle generally contains relatively high levels of protein and is rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids, making it a meat product with considerable nutritional and health value (Huang et al., 2024). Compared with chilled or frozen fish, live fish are generally perceived by consumers at the point of sale as a product form that better represents freshness, thereby conferring high market acceptance and economic value in aquatic product markets (Hua et al., 2025; Poveda-Arteaga et al., 2024).

Live fish are commonly transported from aquaculture production areas to consumer markets through circulation and logistics systems (Zhang et al., 2023). Current live fish transportation methods mainly include water-based and waterless transportation. Conventional water-based transportation is widely used, but it has several limitations, including high transportation loads, rapid water quality deterioration, and relatively low loading efficiency (Berka et al., 1986). Waterless transportation has been widely applied as an efficient strategy for the live delivery of fish, crustaceans, and other aquatic animals because, under appropriately controlled conditions, it can improve survival, minimize physiological stress, and limit flesh quality deterioration (Samet et al., 1996; Zeng et al., 2014). The waterless live transportation reduces fish activity and metabolic intensity through low-temperature induction or metabolic suppression, thereby enabling live transportation under low-water or waterless conditions. Owing to its advantages in water saving, load reduction, and improved transportation efficiency, this approach has attracted increasing attention (Wang et al., 2020). However, fish remain exposed to various stressors during transportation, including hypoxia, spatial restriction, noise, and mechanical vibration, which may induce physiological stress and further affect post-transport muscle quality. Previous studies have shown that the grouper (Epinephelus) body would be unable to scavenge free radicals in time after intense vibration in transportaion, intensifying the oxidative stress response and destroying the antioxidant system's equilibrium (Wu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023). It has also been rerported that insufficient dissolved oxygen impairs respiratory metabolism in Gadus morhua, leading to oxidative stress and liver tissue damage (Lavaud et al., 2019); prolonged transportation gradually reduces muscle shear force in Megalobrama amblycephala and disrupts the cellular and ultrastructural integrity of muscle fibers (Peng et al., 2023); noise exposure induces collagen degradation in the muscle of small yellow croaker and impairs muscle fiber integrity (Zhang et al., 2024); and transportation vibration causes early increases in serum stress-related indicators, including aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and cortisol, in pearl gentian grouper, accompanied by aggravated gill tissue damage (Fang et al., 2023). These findings indicate that transportation-related stressors not only disturb physiological homeostasis in fish but may also adversely affect muscle structure and quality attributes. Therefore, maintaining post-transport muscle quality has become an urgent issue in the quality-oriented circulation of live fish.

The vibration in transportation is one of the primary stressors during transportation, which is difficult to eliminate (Zhang et al., 2017). When transport vehicles travel on different road surfaces, such as unpaved, cement, and asphalt roads, vibrations of varying frequencies are generated because of differences in road smoothness and structural characteristics. These vibrations can be transmitted directly to the fish body through the chassis or supporting structure of the transport container, thereby imposing continuous mechanical loading on muscle tissue (Park et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that vibration stress can induce stress responses and disrupt physiological homeostasis in both aquatic and terrestrial animals (Ardian et al., 2025). Vibration stress during live transportation could cause the deterioration of fish muscle texture, which might be mainly related to the destruction of cell structure and muscle fiber structure (Peng et al., 2023). The increase of vibration frequency in Mandarin fish (Siniperca chuatsi) transportation also decreased protein content while increased lactic acid content of fish muscle (Cheng, 2015). However, previous studies have largely focused on general physiological stress markers or individual muscle quality attributes, while the chemical mechanisms linking vibration frequency to muscle quality deterioration remain poorly understood. Elucidating these mechanisms is therefore critical for optimizing vibration exposure conditions to preserve flesh quality during waterless transportation.

Yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) is a major freshwater species widely consumed in East and Southeast Asia (Lu et al., 2014). Owing to its scaleless skin and delicate muscle structure, it is particularly susceptible to mechanical disturbance during handling and transportation, making it a suitable model for investigating vibration-driven quality deterioration (Wang et al., 2022). Previous studies on yellow catfish transport have primarily emphasized survival performance and stress physiology, whereas mechanistic insight into vibration-related chemical instability of muscle remains limited (Wang et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2021).

Metabolomics is a widely used omics approach for investigating food quality and composition (Wang et al., 2023). Therefore, in this study, yellow catfish was used as the experimental model to investigate the effects of different vibration frequencies on muscle quality and metabolic characteristics under simulated waterless transportation conditions. By integrating muscle quality assessment, histological observation, and untargeted metabolomics, this study examined the potential associations among vibration-induced metabolic alterations, oxidative stress, and muscle quality deterioration. These findings are expected to provide new insights into the effects of transportation vibration on fish muscle quality and offer a theoretical basis for subsequent studies on the cumulative effects of vibration duration and the development of stress mitigation strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals materials and sample collection

Yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco; hereafter referred to as “fish”) were obtained from Qinfangxi Agricultural Science and Technology Development Co., Ltd. (Caidian District, Wuhan, Hubei Province, China). A total of 360 healthy fish of uniform body size were randomly selected for the experiment, with a mean body weight of 134.85 ± 22.69 g and a mean body length of 23.63 ± 1.94 cm. Before the experiment, the fish were acclimated in a recirculating aquaculture system and subjected to stepwise cooling until deep sedation was achieved. Specifically, the water temperature was reduced from 22.42 ± 0.46 °C to 10.16 ± 0.25 °C at a rate of 5 °C/h and then to 6.5 ± 0.3 °C at a rate of 2 °C/h. Our preliminary study identified deep sedation as the most suitable sedative state for the waterless live transportation of yellow catfish, and the sedation stage was assessed according to the criteria described by Zhou et al. (Zhou et al., 2022). The use of controlled cooling to induce dormancy during the waterless transportation of live fish has been considered a potentially sustainable transportation strategy because it reduces both metabolic rate and oxygen consumption (Zhang et al., 2017).

Before simulated transportation:after deep sedation, the fish were packaged, supplied with oxygen, and placed in transport boxes. 12 h of vibration transportation:based on the results of a preliminary temperature-screening experiment for waterless live transportation (see the Supplementary Material), the packaged fish were subjected to 12 h of simulated waterless transportation (vibration) at 11 °C using an agricultural product logistics testing system (DC-1000/THV-1200, Suzhou Sushi Testing Group Co., Ltd., China). To simulate vibration transportation over different road surfaces, four vibration-frequency treatments were established based on previous reports indicating that vehicle vibration frequencies generally range from 1 to 23 Hz (Gebresenbet et al., 2011; Yang et al., 2021). The treatments comprised 0 Hz (no vibration), 5 Hz (simulated asphalt road), 10 Hz (simulated concrete road), and 20 Hz (simulated unpaved road). An untreated blank control group (CK), which was maintained under normal aquaculture conditions and was not subjected to deep sedation, packaging, or simulated transportation, was also included. Post-transportation recovery: following vibration transportation, the fish were transferred to open containers containing clean water maintained at 22.15 ± 0.34 °C and allowed to recover for 24 h under continuous aeration.

Samples were collected immediately before simulated transportation (0 h), after 12 h of vibration transportation (12h), and after 24 h of post-transportation recovery (24-R). At each sampling time point, 6 fish were randomly selected from each treatment group and then anesthetized with 50 mg/L MS-222. Upon confirming deep anesthesia, fish were euthanized by severing blood. Blood samples were collected from the caudal vein using sterile 5 mL disposable syringes without anticoagulant and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting serum was collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent biochemical analyses. An additional 18 fish were randomly selected from each treatment group and and also anesthetized with 50 mg/L MS-222. Upon confirming deep anesthesia, fish were euthanized and dissected on ice to obtain dorsal muscle samples. Muscle samples from 12 fish were immediately used to determine muscle quality parameters, including color, texture profile, shear force, centrifugal loss, and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance characteristics. Muscle samples from five fish were placed in cryovials, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C for untargeted metabolomic analysis. Muscle samples from the remaining fish were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for hematoxylin and eosin staining and histological examination. Before the commencement of the study, all experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences on September 10, 2024 (approval no. HAAS-2024-0018). Each treatment was performed in triplicate.

Chemicals and reagents

Paraformaldehyde, tissue fixative, and hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining solution were purchased from Pinofei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Ethanol and xylene were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ultrapure water, methanol, and acetonitrile were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (China) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Formic acid was obtained from Shanghai ANPEL Laboratory Technologies Inc. (Shanghai, China), and L-2-chlorophenylalanine was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Commercial assay kits for measuring serum physiological indicators, including superoxide dismutase, malondialdehyde, reduced glutathione, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine, were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Determination of muscle quality parameters

Color

Color measurements were performed on the cut surface of fish dorsal muscle using a portable colorimeter (CR-10 Plus, Konica Minolta, Japan). The results were expressed as lightness (L*), redness/greenness (a*), and yellowness/blueness (b*) in the CIE L*a*b* color system. Shear force

Fish dorsal muscle samples were collected and trimmed into 2 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm pieces. Shear force was measured using a digital muscle tenderness meter (C-LM3, Mettler-Toledo Instruments (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., China). Each sample was measured five times, and the results were expressed as the mean value.

Centrifugal loss

Fish dorsal muscle samples were collected, and 2.00 g of each sample was accurately weighed and recorded as m₁. Each sample was placed at the center of filter paper, wrapped, sealed, and then transferred into a 10 mL centrifuge tube. The samples were centrifuged at 5000 r/min for 10 min using a centrifuge (3 K15, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). After centrifugation, the samples were removed, and surface moisture was gently blotted with filter paper before weighing; the resulting mass was recorded as m₂. Texture assay

Fish dorsal muscle samples were collected, and springiness and cohesiveness were determined using a texture analyzer (TA. XT Plus, Stable Micro Systems, UK). The samples were placed under a P/36R probe for texture profile analysis. The pre-test, test, and post-test speeds were set at 2, 0.5, and 2 mm/s, respectively. The compression distance was 5 mm, the trigger force was 49 N, and the calculation threshold was 20 g. Each replicate sample in each group was measured six times, and the results were expressed as the mean value.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance

Water distribution in fish muscle was determined using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analyzer (NMI20-025 V, Suzhou Niumag Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., China) according to the method described by Shi et al., with slight modifications (Shi et al., 2023). Fish dorsal muscle was cut into cuboids measuring 18 mm × 18 mm × 20 mm and placed in NMR tubes for analysis. The transverse relaxation time (T₂) was measured using the Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence. The obtained T₂ data were inverted using the CONTIN algorithm. The relaxation components were expressed as T₂₁, T₂₂, and T₂₃, corresponding to bound water, immobilized water, and free water, respectively. Six individual fish from each treatment were analyzed, with each fish considered an independent biological replicate.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E)

Fresh muscle tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and thoroughly washed with PBS buffer (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.4) to remove residual fixative. After trimming, the samples were placed in dehydration cassettes and sequentially subjected to gradient ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and paraffin infiltration at 65 °C, followed by paraffin embedding. The embedded tissues were sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices using a microtome (RM2016, Leica, Germany) and then processed for routine hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, including dehydration, clearing, and mounting with neutral resin. The stained sections were observed and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse TI-SR, Nikon, Japan), and the images were processed and analyzed using NIS-Elements software (Version 6.10.01, Nikon, Japan).

Determination of serum oxidative stress and metabolism-related parameters

The collected venous serum samples were analyzed using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Oxidative stress-related parameters included superoxide dismutase, which was determined using the xanthine oxidase method (SOD, A001–3); malondialdehyde, which was measured using the thiobarbituric acid method (MDA, A003–1); and reduced glutathione, which was determined using the DTNB method (GSH, A006–2-1). Metabolism-related parameters included blood urea nitrogen, which was determined using the urease–glutamate dehydrogenase method (BUN, C013–2-1), and creatinine, which was measured using the creatininase–sarcosine oxidase method (CR, C011–2-1).

UPLC–MS-based untargeted Metabolomic analysis of fish muscle

Approximately 50 ± 5 mg of muscle sample was accurately weighed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of one 6 mm grinding bead. Metabolites were extracted by adding 400 μL of methanol/water extraction solvent (4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine as the internal standard. The samples were homogenized using a cryogenic tissue grinder for 6 min at −10 °C and 50 Hz, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min at 5 °C and 40 kHz. The extracts were then kept at −20 °C for 30 min and centrifuged at 13,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred into autosampler vials with inserts for LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (TripleTOF 6600, AB SCIEX, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with an injection volume of 10 μL. Mobile phase A consisted of water/acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid, whereas mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5:47.5:5, v/v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid. The flow rate was set at 0.40 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 45 °C. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using electrospray ionization in both positive and negative ion modes. The mass scan range was set at m/z 50–1200. The ion spray voltage was 5500 V in positive ion mode and − 4500 V in negative ion mode. The declustering potential was 80 V. Ion source gases 1 and 2 were both set at 50 psi, and the curtain gas was set at 35 psi. The ion source temperature was maintained at 500 °C, and the collision energy was set at 20–60 V in rolling collision energy mode.

Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by pooling 20 μL of supernatant from each sample and were processed and analyzed using the same procedure as the experimental samples. During instrumental analysis, one QC sample was inserted after every 5–15 experimental samples to evaluate the repeatability and stability of the analytical system. After data acquisition, the raw LC-MS data were imported into Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) for baseline filtering, peak detection, peak integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. A data matrix containing retention time, mass-to-charge ratio, and peak intensity was generated. Metabolites were identified by matching MS and MS/MS spectral information against public databases, including HMDB and METLIN, as well as an in-house database.

Statistical analysis

Data on muscle quality and serum parameters were organized using Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and statistically analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). Before parametric analyses, data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene's test, respectively. Differences among vibration transportation treatments at different frequencies were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Differences between the blank control group and each treatment group were analyzed using an independent-samples t-test. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The annotated muscle metabolomics data matrix was uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; cloud.majorbio.com) for subsequent analysis. The data matrix was first preprocessed as follows. Missing values were filtered using the 80% rule, whereby variables with nonzero values in more than 80% of samples in at least one group were retained. The remaining missing values were imputed using the minimum value in the original data matrix. To reduce systematic errors caused by sample preparation and instrumental fluctuations, peak response intensities were normalized using the total-sum normalization method to generate a normalized data matrix. Variables with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% in QC samples were removed, and the data were then log10-transformed to obtain the final data matrix for subsequent statistical analysis. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed on the preprocessed data matrix using the ropls package in R software (Version 1.6.2), and model stability and reliability were evaluated by 7-fold cross-validation. Significantly different metabolites were screened based on the variable importance in projection (VIP) values obtained from the OPLS-DA model and the P values from Student's t-test. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and P < 0.05 were defined as significantly differentially expressed metabolites. Pathway annotation of the differential metabolites was performed using the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to identify the metabolic pathways in which these metabolites were involved. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using the scipy.stats package in Python software, and Fisher's exact test was used to identify biological pathways closely associated with the experimental treatments.

After 12 h of simulated waterless transportation, significant differences in muscle color were observed among the vibration-frequency treatments. The L* value exhibited a non-linear response, reaching its highest level in the 10 Hz-12 h group and its lowest level in the 20 Hz-12 h group, with significant differences among all four treatments (P < 0.05). Whiteness showed a similar pattern, with significantly higher values in the 5 Hz-12 h and 10 Hz-12 h groups than in the 0 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups. By contrast, b* was significantly lower in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups than in the lower-frequency groups, whereas a* did not differ significantly among treatments. These findings indicate that muscle color did not respond linearly to increasing vibration frequency. Moderate vibration (5–10 Hz) may have enhanced light scattering by inducing subtle changes in muscle structure and water distribution, thereby increasing L* and whiteness (Hughes et al., 2020; Stien et al., 2005). By contrast, excessive mechanical loading at 20 Hz may have promoted protein denaturation and pigment oxidation, resulting in reduced color stability and lower L* and whiteness values (Palanisamy et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023). The progressive decline in b* further suggests that higher vibration frequencies accelerated oxidative degradation of pigment-related compounds, whereas the relative stability of a* indicates that redness was less sensitive to vibration under the present experimental conditions.

Texture properties exhibited a clearer vibration-dependent response than color. Hardness differed significantly among all four vibration treatments at 12 h and was highest in the 20 Hz-12 h group, whereas shear force was significantly lower in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups than in the 0 Hz-12 h and 5 Hz-12 h groups (P < 0.05). Although chewiness increased numerically and springiness tended to decrease with increasing vibration frequency, neither parameter differed significantly among the treatments. The concurrent increase in hardness and decrease in shear force should not be interpreted as improved tenderness because these parameters reflect different mechanical properties of muscle. Hardness primarily reflects resistance to compression, whereas shear force represents resistance to cutting. Similar responses have been reported in transported Chinese sturgeon, channel catfish, and blunt-snout bream, in which transportation stress altered muscle hydration and myofibrillar integrity, resulting in increased hardness and reduced shear resistance (Refaey et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020). These observations indicate that medium- and high-frequency vibration adversely affected the mechanical properties of fish muscle and thereby reduced flesh quality.

Following 24 h of recovery, several muscle-quality parameters showed partial restoration. In the 5 Hz and 10 Hz groups, L* decreased from the post-transportation values but remained significantly higher than the corresponding 0 h values, whereas no significant temporal changes were observed in the 0 Hz group. Shear force increased significantly during recovery in the 5 Hz, 10 Hz, and 20 Hz groups but remained below the corresponding pre-transportation levels. Similarly, hardness declined after recovery but remained significantly higher than the corresponding 0 h values in all treatments. These results indicate that transportation-induced deterioration was only partially reversible, particularly under medium- and high-frequency vibration. Similar incomplete recovery of flesh quality after transportation has been reported previously, suggesting that severe transportation stress may exceed the short-term repair capacity of muscle tissue (Zhang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2021).

Overall, simulated waterless transportation significantly altered the color and texture of yellow catfish muscle, and the magnitude of these changes depended on vibration frequency. Low-frequency vibration (0–5 Hz) caused relatively minor quality deterioration, whereas medium- and high-frequency vibration (10–20 Hz) produced more pronounced alterations in both appearance and texture.

Centrifugal loss characteristics with microstructural evidence

Histological observation, low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR), and centrifugal loss (CL) analysis were jointly used to assess vibration-induced microstructural alterations and their effects on muscle water retention (Wang et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1A, muscle fibers in the CK group and the representative pre-transportation sample (0 h) were compact and regularly arranged, with narrow endomysial spaces and intact myofibrillar organization. After 12 h of simulated waterless transportation, structural damage became more pronounced with vibration frequency. The 0 Hz-12 h and 5 Hz-12 h groups retained relatively intact muscle architecture, whereas the 10 Hz-12 h group exhibited widened endomysial spaces and disrupted fiber alignment. The most severe lesions were observed in the 20 Hz-12 h group, characterized by extensive inter-fiber spaces, irregular fiber arrangement, and marked myofibrillar disruption. Following 24 h of recovery, partial restoration was evident in the 0 Hz-24-R and 5 Hz-24-R groups, whereas structural abnormalities persisted in the 10 Hz-24-R and especially the 20 Hz-24-R groups. These findings suggest that repeated mechanical loading progressively weakened the myofibrillar framework and exceeded the short-term repair capacity of muscle tissue at higher vibration frequencies. Similar transport-induced structural deterioration has been reported in fish exposed to prolonged handling and mechanical stress (Wang et al., 2020).

The LF-NMR relaxation profiles contained three components, T₂₁, T₂₂, and T₂₃, corresponding to bound, immobilized, and free water, respectively (Fig. 1B). The pre-transportation profiles were similar to that of the CK group, whereas clear alterations emerged after 12 h of transportation, particularly in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups. These changes were mainly reflected in the T₂₂ and T₂₃ regions, indicating altered muscle water status. After recovery, the 0 Hz-24-R and 5 Hz-24-R profiles tended to approach their pre-transportation patterns, whereas marked differences remained in the 10 Hz-24-R and 20 Hz-24-R groups. Because disruption of the myofibrillar network weakens protein–water interactions and promotes the migration of immobilized water toward more mobile compartments, the LF-NMR results suggest that medium- and high-frequency vibration impaired the water-retention properties of muscle (Bertram et al., 2002; Pearce et al., 2011).

Consistent with the LF-NMR findings, CL increased significantly after transportation in all frequency treatments relative to their corresponding 0 h values (P < 0.05; Fig. 1C). At 12 h, the between-frequency pattern was 20 Hz > 5 Hz ≈ 10 Hz > 0 Hz, with the highest CL observed in the 20 Hz-12 h group (P < 0.05). After 24 h of recovery, CL returned to the pre-transportation level only in the 0 Hz group. Partial recovery occurred in the 5 Hz and 10 Hz groups, whereas no significant reduction was detected between the 20 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-24-R groups (P > 0.05). As CL reflects the amount of water expelled from muscle under external force, its elevation indicates reduced water-holding capacity and increased water exudation. Such changes are commonly associated with disruption of the myofibrillar network and enlargement of extracellular spaces, which facilitate water migration from the intracellular compartment (Huff-Lonergan & Lonergan, 2005). Similar increases in drip loss and centrifugal loss have been reported in transport-stressed fish, including channel catfish and Chinese farmed sturgeon, where transportation-induced structural damage was accompanied by impaired water retention and deterioration of flesh quality (Refaey et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2020).

Overall, the histological, LF-NMR, and CL results consistently showed that increasing vibration frequency progressively impaired muscle structural integrity and water retention. These effects were largely reversible in the 0 Hz group, partially reversible at 5 and 10 Hz, and least reversible at 20 Hz, indicating that high-frequency mechanical loading was a major driver of early muscle quality deterioration during simulated waterless transportation.

Serum oxidative stress and metabolism-related parameters

Serum biochemical indicators were measured to evaluate the systemic physiological responses of fish to simulated waterless vibration transportation (Fig. 2). No significant differences in SOD, GSH, MDA, CR, or BUN were observed among the four pre-transportation groups (P > 0.05), indicating that cooling-induced deep sedation caused only limited initial physiological disturbance. After 12 h of transportation, SOD activity increased significantly in all treatments and followed the order 20 Hz-12 h > 10 Hz-12 h > 5 Hz-12 h > 0 Hz-12 h (P < 0.05; Fig. 2A). By contrast, GSH concentrations decreased with increasing vibration frequency and were lowest in the 20 Hz-12 h group (Fig. 2B). MDA concentrations also increased significantly after transportation, with the lowest value observed at 0 Hz and the highest at 20 Hz, although adjacent treatments were not consistently separated statistically (Fig. 2C). Collectively, these changes indicate that redox homeostasis became progressively disrupted as vibration frequency increased. SOD constitutes a major enzymatic defense against reactive oxygen species, whereas GSH provides non-enzymatic reducing capacity. Thus, increased SOD activity likely reflects a compensatory antioxidant response, while GSH depletion indicates increased consumption of antioxidant reserves (Hoseini et al., 2022; Nakano et al., 2014). The concurrent accumulation of MDA further suggests that this compensatory response was insufficient to prevent lipid peroxidation under more intense vibration (Halliwell et al., 2015; Refaey et al., 2018).

After 24 h of recovery, the redox indicators exhibited frequency-dependent reversibility. SOD, GSH, and MDA approached their pre-transportation levels in the 0 Hz group, and substantial recovery was also evident at 5 Hz. By contrast, SOD and MDA remained elevated, and GSH recovery was incomplete in the 10 Hz and particularly the 20 Hz groups. These findings suggest that low-frequency treatments induced predominantly transient oxidative responses, whereas medium- and high-frequency vibration imposed a sustained oxidative burden that was not fully resolved within 24 h. Similar persistent redox disturbances have been reported in transport-stressed fish, in which prolonged or severe stress depleted antioxidant reserves and intensified oxidative damage (Refaey et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2021).

CR and BUN showed broadly similar vibration-dependent responses (Fig. 2D–E). At 12 h, CR increased significantly across all frequency treatments, whereas BUN did not differ significantly between the 0 and 5 Hz groups but was higher at 10 Hz and highest at 20 Hz. During recovery, both indicators tended to decline; however, elevated values persisted in the higher-frequency groups, particularly at 20 Hz. CR and BUN are commonly used to assess physiological disturbance associated with transportation stress and altered protein metabolism in fish (He et al., 2020).Their accumulation may reflect increased protein catabolism together with disturbed metabolic and excretory homeostasis during transportation stress, rather than renal impairment alone (Refaey et al., 2018). Collectively, the increases in SOD, MDA, CR, and BUN, together with GSH depletion, indicate that high-frequency vibration imposed both oxidative and systemic metabolic burdens. The limited recovery observed at 10–20 Hz further suggests that intense vibration exceeded the short-term physiological compensatory capacity of the fish and may have contributed to muscle quality deterioration.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling of fish muscle following vibration transportation

Quality assessment of metabolomic data

Quality control (QC) is essential for ensuring the reliability, reproducibility, and analytical stability of untargeted metabolomics data (Liu et al., 2023). Low variability among QC samples reflects stable instrument performance and high analytical reproducibility, thereby supporting the overall quality of the metabolomic dataset. Analytical stability was evaluated by Pearson correlation analysis based on the relative abundances of metabolites in the QC samples. Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.85 and approaching 1 are generally considered indicative of robust analytical repeatability (Li et al., 2026). As shown in Fig. 3A, all pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients among the QC samples exceeded 0.998, confirming excellent analytical reproducibility and instrumental stability. These results satisfied established metabolomics quality-control criteria and minimized technical variation, thereby providing a reliable basis for subsequent metabolomic analyses (Pang et al., 2022).

Metabolite profiling and principal component analysis

Having confirmed the quality of the metabolomic dataset, unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate the overall metabolic variation among the treatment groups (Fig. 3B). All samples were distributed within the 95% confidence ellipse, while the QC samples clustered tightly, further confirming the robustness of the analytical platform. The first two principal components explained 43.6% of the total variance, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 31.6% and 12.0%, respectively. A clear separation trend was observed among the treatment groups, indicating that vibration transportation induced distinct alterations in muscle metabolic profiles. Notably, the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups were clearly separated from the other groups, suggesting that medium- and high-frequency vibration elicited more pronounced metabolic perturbations. In contrast, the 0 Hz-12 h and 5 Hz-12 h groups showed partial overlap and were located closer to the CK group, indicating relatively limited metabolic alterations under no or low-frequency vibration. To further characterize the overall metabolic composition, the identified metabolites were classified according to their chemical superclasses. A total of 5274 metabolic features were detected across all treatments, from which 1117 metabolites were identified, including 470 in positive-ion mode and 647 in negative-ion mode. Lipids and lipid-like molecules represented the largest superclass (46.28%), followed by organic acids and derivatives (15.69%), organoheterocyclic compounds (8.64%), organic oxygen compounds (7.71%), nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues (3.59%), benzenoids (3.59%), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (2.79%), and organic nitrogen compounds (1.33%) (Fig. 3C). The predominance of lipid-related metabolites is consistent with previous metabolomic studies of yellow catfish (Hu et al., 2022), supporting the reliability of the present dataset. Moreover, the broad metabolite coverage provided a robust foundation for subsequent multivariate statistical analyses, differential metabolite identification, and metabolic pathway enrichment.

Multivariate statistical analysis of muscle metabolite profiles

Orthogonal partial least squares–discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to compare the muscle metabolite profiles of the 5 Hz-12 h, 10 Hz-12 h, and 20 Hz-12 h groups with that of the 0 Hz-12 h group. By maximizing variation associated with group separation while filtering out orthogonal variation unrelated to classification, OPLS-DA facilitates the characterization of vibration-induced metabolic alterations (Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2024). All samples were located within the 95% confidence ellipse, and metabolites with variable importance in projection (VIP) scores greater than 1 were subsequently considered important contributors to group discrimination. The OPLS-DA score plots and corresponding permutation tests are presented in Fig. 4A–F. Clear separation was observed between each vibration treatment and the 0 Hz–12 h group in the score plots (Fig. 4A–C), indicating distinct muscle metabolic profiles among the vibration treatments. For the comparisons of 5 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h, 10 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h, and 20 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h, the model parameters were R2X = 0.600 and R2Y = 0.998, R2X = 0.680 and R2Y = 0.999, and R2X = 0.686 and R2Y = 0.999, respectively. The corresponding Q2 values were 0.781, 0.901, and 0.938, respectively. These high R2Y and Q2 values indicate strong model fit and predictive performance. Permutation testing further supported the validity of the models (Fig. 4D–F; P < 0.05), suggesting that the observed group separation was unlikely to have resulted from model overfitting (Luo et al., 2023). Collectively, these results demonstrate that vibration transportation induced marked and frequency-dependent alterations in the muscle metabolome of yellow catfish.

Differential metabolite analysis under vibration transportation

Pairwise comparisons were conducted between the 0 Hz-12 h group and the 5 Hz-12 h, 10 Hz-12 h, and 20 Hz-12 h groups. Differential metabolites were identified using the combined criteria of variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1, P < 0.05, and fold change ≥1.5 or ≤ 0.67 (Li et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Volcano plots were used to visualize the distribution of significantly altered metabolites, with red and blue points representing upregulated and downregulated metabolites, respectively (Fig. 5A–C). Relative to the 0 Hz-12 h group, the 5 Hz-12 h group contained 43 differential metabolites, including 9 upregulated and 34 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5A). The 10 Hz-12 h group contained 54 differential metabolites, including 3 upregulated and 51 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5B), whereas the 20 Hz-12 h group contained 86 differential metabolites, including 10 upregulated and 76 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5C). Downregulated metabolites predominated in all comparisons, and the largest number of differential metabolites was observed in the 20 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison. The progressive increase in the number of differential metabolites with vibration frequency suggests a frequency-dependent intensification of metabolic perturbation (Li et al., 2022).

To characterize the major metabolite changes, the 30 most discriminative annotated metabolites in each comparison were visualized using hierarchical clustering heatmaps under combined positive- and negative-ion modes (Fig. 5D–F) (Chen et al., 2025). Metabolites with KEGG annotations were grouped according to their chemical superclasses, and normalized abundances are represented from low to high by blue-to-red color gradients. The selected metabolites were mainly assigned to lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and derivatives, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, organoheterocyclic compounds, organic nitrogen compounds, organic oxygen compounds, and benzenoids. In the 5 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, 23 metabolites were downregulated and 7 were upregulated, with the upregulated metabolites distributed across four chemical classes and mainly comprising organic acids and derivatives. In the 10 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, all 30 selected metabolites were downregulated, including 15 lipids and lipid-like molecules. In the 20 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, downregulated metabolites were dominated by lipids and lipid-like molecules and phenylpropanoids and polyketides. The distinct clustering patterns among the three comparisons indicate progressively divergent metabolic responses with increasing vibration frequency. Lipid-related metabolites showed the most prominent alterations. Several glycerophospholipids and lysophospholipids, including phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), lysophosphatidic acid [LPA(18:2)], lysophosphatidylethanolamine [LPE(16:2)], and N-acyl phosphatidylethanolamine-related species, were predominantly downregulated in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups. Several fatty acids and oxygenated polyunsaturated fatty acid derivatives, including arachidonic acid, γ-linolenic acid, stearic acid, eicosadienoic acid, 13-HOTrE, 13-HpOTrE, and cis-9,10-epoxystearic acid, were also altered. These patterns suggest that medium- and high-frequency vibration substantially affected membrane lipid turnover, fatty acid metabolism, and lipid-mediated signaling. Changes in bile acid-related metabolites and putative fatty acid catabolism intermediates further indicate broader disruption of lipid homeostasis.

Fold-change analysis was subsequently used to identify the ten most strongly upregulated and downregulated metabolites in each comparison (Fig. 5G–I). In the 5 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, the upregulated metabolite set included carnitine-related compounds and amino acid derivatives, whereas the most strongly downregulated metabolites included oxygenated polyunsaturated fatty acid derivatives and phospholipid-related metabolites such as LPA(18:2) and LNAPE(17:1/N-18:1). In the 10 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, several ether-linked phospholipids and fatty acid-related metabolites were relatively enriched, whereas 13-HOTrE, 13-HpOTrE, cis-9,10-epoxystearic acid, LNAPE(17:1/N-18:1), and LPA(18:2) were markedly reduced. In the 20 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, the upregulated metabolites included amino acid derivatives, fatty acid-related compounds, and PC(O-23:5), whereas the downregulated metabolites were dominated by phospholipids and bile acid-related compounds, including LPA(18:2), LNAPE(17:1/N-18:1), LPE(16:2), LNAPE(20:5/N-4:0), and cholic acid-related metabolites.

Overall, the differential metabolites primarily comprised lipids, bile acid-related compounds, nucleotide sugars, amino acid derivatives, and steroids, consistent with the global metabolite composition. Lipid-related compounds accounted for approximately 56–75% of the differential metabolites across the vibration treatments, highlighting the high sensitivity of membrane lipid and energy metabolism to mechanical stress. Oxidative injury is known to alter cellular phospholipid and fatty acid profiles, thereby disrupting membrane organization and lipid homeostasis (Peterson et al., 2008). Likewise, metabolomic analysis of heat-stressed rainbow trout revealed marked disturbances in hepatic lipid metabolism, further supporting the responsiveness of lipid-related pathways to environmental stress in fish (Li et al., 2022). Many of the altered metabolites participate in membrane phospholipid turnover or serve as precursors of bioactive lipid mediators. Their reduced abundance may therefore reflect disturbed phospholipid remodeling and membrane turnover under vibration-induced stress.

Notably, LPA(18:2) and LNAPE(17:1/N-18:1) were consistently downregulated across all vibration treatments. Because lysophospholipids and N-acyl phosphatidylethanolamine-related lipids participate in membrane remodeling and lipid signaling, their sustained depletion may indicate increased utilization, impaired synthesis, or altered turnover of membrane lipids during vibration stress. However, the present metabolomic data do not directly distinguish among these possibilities. Recent studies have similarly reported that transportation and mechanical stress can disturb bile acid and membrane lipid metabolism in fish (Fang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). The increase in several carnitine-related metabolites in the 5 Hz-12 h group may reflect enhanced mitochondrial transport of fatty acids and greater reliance on lipid catabolism for energy production. Acylcarnitines are intermediates of mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, and their accumulation can indicate either increased fatty acid flux or incomplete β-oxidation under stress (Wang et al., 2020). Thus, the low-frequency response may represent an early energetic adaptation intended to support ion regulation and muscle homeostasis. By contrast, the broader depletion of phospholipids, oxygenated fatty acid derivatives, and bile acid-related metabolites in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups indicates more extensive disruption of membrane lipid homeostasis and energy metabolism. Several compounds annotated as tangeritin, rosamicin, oryzalexin A, and chalepin acetate were also markedly reduced. Because some of these compounds are more commonly associated with plants or microorganisms than with endogenous fish metabolism, these annotations should be interpreted cautiously and ideally confirmed using authentic standards or high-confidence MS/MS spectral matching. Their apparent changes should therefore not be directly interpreted as suppressed endogenous biosynthesis in fish. The 20 Hz-12 h group exhibited the largest number of differential metabolites and the greatest depletion of phospholipid- and bile acid-related compounds, indicating the strongest metabolic disturbance. Similar broad metabolic dysregulation has been observed in fish exposed to transportation, hypoxia, and other severe environmental stressors (He et al., 2020; Kumari et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings indicate that vibration did not elicit a uniform metabolic response but instead induced frequency-dependent metabolic reprogramming centered on lipid metabolism. Low-frequency vibration was associated mainly with adaptive energy mobilization, whereas medium- and high-frequency vibration produced progressively greater disruption of membrane lipid turnover, fatty acid metabolism, and bile acid-related pathways.

Metabolic pathway enrichment analysis

KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted to identify metabolic pathways affected by vibration transportation using a significance threshold of P < 0.05, while also considering the enrichment factor and biological relevance (Suh et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 6A–C, both the number and diversity of enriched pathways increased with vibration frequency, indicating progressively greater metabolic disturbance.

(A–C) KEGG enrichment analysis: (A) 5 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h; (B) 10 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h; and (C) 20 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h. (D) Schematic metabolic pathway map showing the major alterations in glycerophospholipid metabolism, biosynthesis of nucleotide sugars, and amino acid biosynthesis among the three comparison groups.

In the 5 Hz-12 h vs. 0 Hz-12 h comparison, differential metabolites were mainly enriched in nucleotide sugar biosynthesis, amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. 6A). Several choline-containing phospholipids, including LPC(20:2), LPC(16:1), LPC(18:3), PC(14:0/22:1), and glycerophosphocholine, were significantly decreased, suggesting early disruption of membrane phospholipid turnover under low-frequency vibration. Similar alterations in phosphatidylcholine metabolism have been reported under environmental stress and proposed as sensitive indicators of membrane lipid remodeling in fish (Chen et al., 2019). In parallel, enrichment of nucleotide sugar-related pathways suggests altered utilization of glycosyl donors required for glycoprotein and glycolipid biosynthesis, which may affect membrane-associated glycosylation and cellular homeostasis (Aebi, 2013; Cheng et al., 2015).

As vibration frequency increased, the range of affected pathways expanded. In the 10 Hz-12 h group, glycerophospholipid metabolism, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, linoleic acid metabolism, and autophagy- and ferroptosis-related pathways were significantly enriched (Fig. 6B), suggesting greater involvement of membrane lipid remodeling and redox regulation in the response to vibration stress (Kim et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021). At 20 Hz-12 h, additional enrichment of carbon metabolism, cofactor biosynthesis, amino acid biosynthesis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and apoptosis- and necroptosis-related pathways was observed (Fig. 6C), indicating broader perturbations in lipid metabolism, energy metabolism, and cellular stress responses under high-frequency vibration (Wang et al., 2023). Collectively, these findings reveal a clear frequency-dependent expansion of metabolic disturbance, with high-frequency vibration eliciting the most extensive response.

Among the enriched pathways, glycerophospholipid metabolism, linoleic acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids were consistently affected across all vibration treatments, highlighting membrane lipid metabolism as a major target of vibration stress. Linoleic acid metabolism is closely associated with the turnover and oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Ziarrusta et al., 2018). Compared with the 0 Hz-12 h group, γ-linolenic acid and GPCho(22:5/22:6) decreased progressively with increasing vibration frequency. Because γ-linolenic acid is an important intermediate in the ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid pathway, its depletion suggests altered PUFA metabolism, whereas changes in GPCho(22:5/22:6) may affect membrane fluidity, lipid signaling, and phospholipid homeostasis. Similar remodeling of linoleic acid metabolism has been reported in heat-stressed rainbow trout, further supporting lipid metabolism as a common target of environmental stress in fish (Li et al., 2022). Glycerophospholipid metabolism showed the strongest enrichment at 20 Hz. Glycerophospholipids form the structural framework of biological membranes and are essential for membrane stability, signal transduction, and cellular homeostasis (Cai et al., 2014). Environmental stress has also been shown to alter phospholipid-related metabolites, including phosphocholine, phosphoserine, betaine, and choline, consistent with the phospholipid remodeling observed in the present study (Peng et al., 2025).

High-frequency vibration also substantially affected central energy metabolism. In the 20 Hz-12 h group, alterations in ribose 5-phosphate and citrate suggested perturbation of the pentose phosphate pathway and TCA cycle. The pentose phosphate pathway is a major source of NADPH for antioxidant defense, whereas the TCA cycle supplies metabolic energy for cellular maintenance (Wang et al., 2021; Kang et al., 2021). Together with the enrichment of apoptosis- and necroptosis-related pathways, these changes suggest that severe vibration stress disrupted not only membrane lipid homeostasis but also cellular energy and redox balance.

To integrate these metabolic alterations, a KEGG pathway map focusing on glycerophospholipid metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and nucleotide sugar biosynthesis was constructed (Fig. 6D). Key membrane phospholipids, including LPC, PC, PE, and PS, together with intermediates such as 1,2-diacyl-sn-glycerol, were markedly depleted in the 10 Hz-12 h and 20 Hz-12 h groups. This depletion indicates extensive membrane phospholipid remodeling under vibration stress and is consistent with the elevated MDA concentrations, histological disruption, increased water mobility, and higher centrifugal loss observed in the present study. Similar stress-associated membrane lipid remodeling has been reported in aquatic organisms, where depletion of PC and PE is closely associated with lipid peroxidation and membrane destabilization (Nathaniel et al., 2025). Likewise, nucleotide sugars, including UDP-galactose, UDP-N-acetylgalactosamine, GDP-mannose, and N-acetylneuraminic acid, declined markedly at medium and high vibration frequencies, suggesting impaired glycosylation capacity and disturbed glycoconjugate biosynthesis (Aebi, 2013; Hadley et al., 2014). Previous studies have shown that disruption of amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism can compromise mucosal and immune barrier function in fish under environmental stress (Benktander et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022).

Overall, KEGG analysis indicated that vibration transportation induced frequency-dependent metabolic reprogramming centered on membrane lipid remodeling, nucleotide sugar metabolism, and energy metabolism. Low-frequency vibration caused relatively limited metabolic alterations, whereas medium- and high-frequency vibration produced broader disturbances involving phospholipid turnover, PUFA metabolism, antioxidant metabolism, and cell stress-related pathways. These pathway-level changes provide mechanistic support for the membrane destabilization, oxidative imbalance, impaired water retention, and muscle quality deterioration observed during simulated waterless transportation.

Correlation analysis

To clarify the mechanistic relationship between vibration-induced muscle quality deterioration and metabolic remodeling, Pearson correlation analysis was conducted between the major differential metabolites and key physicochemical traits, including color parameters (L*, a*, b*, and W) and texture attributes (shear force, hardness, chewiness, and springiness) (Fig. 7). Hierarchical clustering identified two major correlation modules, indicating that vibration-associated metabolic changes were closely linked to coordinated alterations in muscle appearance and texture. A lipid-dominated metabolite cluster, composed largely of glycerophospholipid-related species, showed strong associations with texture traits. Most of these metabolites were negatively correlated with hardness and chewiness but positively correlated with springiness, suggesting that disruption of membrane phospholipid homeostasis was associated with the development of a firmer and less elastic muscle phenotype under vibration stress (Cao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2006). This pattern is consistent with the deterioration in muscle structure and water retention observed under higher vibration frequencies and supports membrane lipid homeostasis as an important biochemical determinant of texture stability (Hao et al., 2024; Levental et al., 2020). Shear force showed positive correlations with several phosphatidylcholine-related metabolites, including LPC(20:2), LPC(16:1), LPC(18:3), and PC(14:0/22:1), but negative correlations with phosphatidylethanolamine species such as PE(18:0/20:4), PE(36:2), and PE(38:3). These opposing associations suggest that individual phospholipid subclasses may contribute differently to muscle structural stability and mechanical behavior. Phosphatidylcholine is a major membrane component involved in maintaining bilayer organization, whereas phosphatidylethanolamine participates in membrane curvature and autophagy-related processes. However, because correlation analysis does not establish causality, the negative associations between PE species and shear force should be interpreted as evidence of coordinated metabolic and structural changes rather than direct proof that PE-mediated autophagy caused myofibrillar degradation (Law et al., 2019; Semba, 2020). Color parameters also displayed metabolite-dependent correlation patterns. Lipid metabolites were generally negatively correlated with L* and W but positively correlated with b*, indicating that vibration-associated color changes were linked not only to optical effects but also to underlying alterations in lipid composition and muscle structure (Guerra-Rodríguez et al., 2025; Harlina et al., 2025). PUFA-rich phospholipids clustered near favorable texture traits and generally showed positive associations with springiness, suggesting that preservation of unsaturated membrane lipids may contribute to membrane organization and the viscoelastic properties of muscle tissue (He et al., 2025; Levental et al., 2020). Because the correlation with hardness was less consistent, these associations should be interpreted at the level of individual metabolites rather than generalized to all unsaturated fatty acids. Collectively, the correlation network indicates that vibration frequency-dependent deterioration in muscle quality was closely associated with coordinated remodeling of glycerophospholipid metabolism (Cao et al., 2025; Hao et al., 2024). The depletion or redistribution of membrane lipids was associated with reduced elasticity, increased hardness, altered shear resistance, and impaired color stability, supporting disrupted phospholipid homeostasis as a potential metabolic contributor to muscle quality deterioration during vibration transportation.

Conclusions

This study integrated muscle histology, serum biochemical parameters, and metabolomic analyses to investigate the effects of different vibration frequencies (0, 5, 10, and 20 Hz) on muscle quality, serum indices, and muscle metabolism in yellow catfish during simulated waterless transport. Vibration at 10–20 Hz caused more severe and less reversible quality deterioration, as evidenced by pronounced disruption of muscle microstructure, decreased shear force and whiteness, and increased water mobility, centrifugal loss, hardness, and chewiness. In parallel, GSH decreased and MDA increased with increasing vibration frequency, indicating progressively aggravated oxidative stress. These physiological and quality changes were closely associated with systemic redox imbalance and membrane lipid disruption. Vibration at 20 Hz induced pronounced metabolic disturbances, characterized by altered levels of d-ribose 5-phosphate and citric acid and the depletion of key membrane phospholipids, including lysophosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylcholine, and phosphatidylethanolamine. Pathway analysis further revealed substantial disturbances in glycerophospholipid metabolism and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism. Correlation analysis linked these metabolic perturbations to water loss and texture deterioration, supporting oxidative membrane damage and phospholipid remodeling as important contributors to vibration-related muscle instability. Overall, preserving membrane lipid integrity and redox homeostasis may represent promising targets for improving muscle quality resilience during waterless transport. Maintaining vibration frequencies below 10 Hz may help minimize metabolic disruption and cellular stress, whereas exposure to 20 Hz appears to trigger more severe and complex metabolic responses that are unfavorable for quality preservation. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying vibration-induced muscle quality deterioration in yellow catfish and may support the development of improved live-fish transportation protocols. Nevertheless, this study was conducted under controlled simulated transport conditions using fixed vibration frequencies and a defined transportation duration. Further studies under commercial transport conditions are needed to evaluate the combined effects of vibration amplitude, variable road conditions, transportation duration, and other environmental stressors.

Ethics statement

This study employed a simulated waterless transportation model for yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco), constituting a controlled departure from conventional water-based live-fish transportation and routine aquatic husbandry. Because the experimental procedure involved deliberate exposure to waterless conditions and mechanical vibration, its scientific necessity, the absence of adequate alternatives, the harm–benefit assessment, and the associated animal-welfare safeguards are described below.

Justification. The primary objective of this study was to elucidate how vibration frequency induces oxidative imbalance, membrane phospholipid disruption, metabolic remodeling, and subsequent muscle quality deterioration during waterless transportation. The vibration exposure was therefore not an incidental component of the experiment but the principal experimental variable required to address the research question. These effects depend on the integrated responses of intact fish to low-temperature-induced dormancy, waterless exposure, and mechanical vibration. Accordingly, a live-fish waterless transportation model was necessary to reproduce the essential features of commercially relevant transportation conditions under controlled experimental settings. Before transportation, healthy fish were acclimated and subjected to controlled stepwise cooling to induce a deeply sedated dormant state, thereby reducing spontaneous activity and metabolic demand in accordance with the refinement principle of animal welfare.

Absence of an adequate alternative. The scientific objective could not be adequately achieved using non-animal, in vitro, ex vivo, water-based transportation, or computational models. In vitro and ex vivo models cannot reproduce the integrated neuroendocrine, circulatory, redox, metabolic, microstructural, and water-distribution responses that connect whole-animal transportation stress with muscle quality deterioration. Water-based transportation models are not appropriate substitutes because the buoyancy and damping effects of water fundamentally alter vibration transmission, mechanical loading, and the physiological responses of fish. Furthermore, currently available computational models have not been validated to predict the coupled in vivo interactions among oxidative damage, membrane lipid remodeling, metabolic alterations, water migration, and muscle quality. Consequently, the use of live fish under simulated waterless vibration transportation conditions was necessary to achieve the stated scientific objective.

Appropriateness and harm–benefit assessment. The anticipated scientific and practical benefits were considered proportionate to the unavoidable stress imposed by the experimental procedure. Identification of vibration frequencies associated with sustained oxidative disturbance, membrane damage, metabolic remodeling, and muscle quality deterioration could provide an evidence base for optimizing commercial waterless transportation practices and reducing transportation-induced stress and quality loss in farmed fish. The tested vibration frequencies were restricted to the predefined road-relevant range of 0–20 Hz. The 0-Hz treatment served as a non-vibration waterless transportation comparator, allowing the effects of vibration to be distinguished from those of low-temperature waterless transportation itself. The exposure duration was limited to 12 h, corresponding to a practically relevant transportation period and avoiding exposure beyond that required to address the research objective. Detailed vibration settings and their selection criteria are provided in Section 2.1.

Several measures were implemented to minimize animal use and distress. Only healthy fish of relatively uniform size were included, and all fish were acclimated before experimentation. Controlled stepwise cooling was used to induce a deeply sedated dormant state before waterless transportation, and the transportation temperature was maintained at approximately 11 °C. Fish allocated to the recovery treatment were immediately transferred to clean, continuously aerated water after transportation. Animal numbers were determined before the experiment and restricted to those required for the prespecified analyses and independent biological replication.

Fish were monitored throughout the experimental period for body condition, opercular activity, responsiveness, visible injury, and other signs of severe distress. The predefined humane endpoints specified in the approved protocol were applied throughout the study. Any fish reaching a humane endpoint was immediately removed from the experiment and euthanized using an overdose of MS-222 in accordance with the approved protocol. Fish designated for terminal sampling were anesthetized with 50 mg/L MS-222 before blood collection and subsequently euthanized using an overdose of MS-222 before tissue collection. No fish was retained in the experiment after reaching a predefined humane endpoint.

Ethics approval. All experimental procedures, including controlled stepwise cooling, simulated waterless vibration transportation, post-transport recovery, animal monitoring, anesthesia, euthanasia, and biological sample collection, were reviewed and approved before the commencement of the study by the Animal Ethics Committee of Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences on September 10, 2024 (approval no. HAAS-2024-0018). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the institutional animal-welfare guidelines of Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition), the Guidance on Treating Experimental Animals issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People's Republic of China (2006), and Chinese national standard GB/T 35892–2018 (Laboratory Animal—Guideline for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare). The experimental design followed the 3Rs principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement.
