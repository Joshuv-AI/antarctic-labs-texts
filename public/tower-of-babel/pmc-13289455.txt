Artificial intelligence on chest X-ray for tuberculosis screening in Tanzania: a multicentre evaluation
Authors: Deogratias Mzurikwao, Twaha Kabika, Asa Kalonga, Lulu Sakafu, Simeon Mayala, Oluwarotimi Samuel, Sabina Mugusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tanzania has adopted artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted chest X-ray screening for tuberculosis (TB), including the use of CAD4TB version 6, which is registered by the Tanzania Medicines and Medical Devices Authority (TMDA). While GeneXpert, practical reference standard used in routine practice, remains the primary bacteriological confirmatory test in routine practice, there is currently no established national threshold for CAD4TB use in either active case finding (ACF) or passive case finding (PCF) settings. This study evaluates the implementation and operational use of CAD4TB version 6 within mobile TB screening units in Tanzania and highlights challenges affecting its effective use. We conducted a retrospective analysis of screening data from 11,923 individuals collected from mobile clinics equipped with digital X-ray, CAD4TB version 6, and GeneXpert systems. Comparisons were made between manual chest X-ray interpretation, CAD4TB scores, and GeneXpert results within the subset of individuals who underwent confirmatory testing. The findings reveal substantial inconsistencies in screening workflows, including non-uniform use of CAD4TB prior to GeneXpert testing, missing radiological records, and deviations from intended protocols across sites. Descriptive analysis showed that CAD4TB scores generally aligned with GeneXpert-positive cases within the tested subset; however, due to selective application of GeneXpert and incomplete data, these observations cannot be interpreted as measures of diagnostic accuracy. This study should be interpreted as an implementation and operational assessment of AI-assisted TB screening rather than a diagnostic accuracy or threshold-setting study. The findings highlight important gaps in protocol adherence, data completeness, and workflow standardization, underscoring the need for prospective, protocol-driven studies to establish validated national thresholds for CAD4TB use in Tanzania.

Background

According to an annual tuberculosis and leprosy epidemiological by the Tanzania’s National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Program 2023, the country has made progress in TB case notification but continues to face significant gaps in early detection and timely diagnosis [1]. Tanzania is among the 20 countries with the greatest burdens based on the absolute number of TB incident cases [2]. Tanzania remains one of the 30 high TB burden countries, and despite improvements, a considerable proportion of individuals are still diagnosed late, often after presenting with advanced disease, which contributes to ongoing transmission and poorer outcomes [3, 4]. The country has made substantial progress in reducing TB incidence and is among the few high-burden countries assessed as being on track toward the WHO End TB milestones [4], although further reductions are required to meet the long-term 2035 targets [5]of reducing TB incidence by 50% by 2035 [5, 6].

Although TB diagnosis and treatment are offered freely, the problem of underdiagnosis still occurs in Tanzania, as in many other developing countries [4]. Recent NTLP reports indicate that while case finding has improved compared to pre-COVID-19 levels, challenges remain in identifying individuals with subclinical or early-stage TB, particularly in community settings [1]. In 2023, an estimated 10.8 million people fell ill with TB worldwide, while only about 8.1 million new cases were diagnosed and notified, leaving around 2.7 million still undetected [7, 9]. Advanced disease at diagnosis remains common, not because people do not get tested, but because a substantial number still enter the health system only after symptoms become sever, underscoring the need for more efficient and community focused screening strategy [8, 9]. In many areas with high TB incidence, diagnostic performance has been substantially enhanced by automated nucleic acid amplification testing, notably the use of the GeneXpert® platform [10]; however, this approach adds considerable expense [11]. Even if the pretest probability of TB disease is high, most patients will have negative results [12].

In September 2021, the National TB and Leprosy Program (NTLP) under the Tanzania Ministry of Health introduced the use of mobile vans equipped with digital X-ray machines for TB screening. This initiative aimed to increase the detection rate of TB while reducing the expense of using GeneXpert. In addition, the ministry is also pioneering the use of digital X-rays for TB screening in medical facilities. Due to the low number of radiologists in the country who interpret chest X-ray images, this initiative could have had little impact on TB diagnosis and is prone to human error and unsuitable for mass screening [13, 14]. Hence, the program has adopted the use of AI-based computer-aided diagnosis software (CAD4TB). The software analyses the digital chest X-ray images for the detection of TB [15]. The software was created using a convolutional neural network, a deep learning algorithm for computer vision [16]. CAD4TB is aimed at detecting suggestive cases of TB that will later be subjected to GeneXpert for a confirmatory test [17, 18]. The initiative is expected to increase the number of people to be screened while reducing the use of GeneXpert, as only X-ray-based suggestive cases will be subjected to the GeneXpert test [19, 20].

Although CAD4TB has shown efficiency in detecting TB cases in many other countries, few studies have been conducted to assess the effectiveness of the method in comparison to manual reading, especially in resource-limited countries with a limited number of well-trained human readers. In addition, the CAD4TB score ranges between 0 and 100; the higher the score is, the more suggestive a case is [21]. There is no standard cutoff point for suggestive and nonsuggestive cases, and each country/setting must establish its cutoff point. This study aimed to assess the effectiveness of CAD4TB in screening for pulmonary TB in comparison to the manual reading and lights on the necessity of the country to develop its own threshold value to be used in the vans for digital X-ray-based pulmonary TB screening in Tanzania.

Methodology

Study area

This study used data from four (4) mobile vans operating in four (4) different zones in Tanzania. These zones are the coastal zone (which includes the regions of Dar es Salaam, Lindi, Mtwara, Morogoro, Pwani, and Tanga), the central zone (which includes regions of Dodoma, Tabora, and Singida regions). The other zones are the Lake Victoria zone (which includes the regions of Bukoba, Mwanza, Mara, Simiyu, Shinyanga, Geita, Kagera, and Kigoma), and the southern highlands of Tanzania zo ne (including the regions of Mbeya, Iringa, Njombe, Ruvuma, Rukwa, Katavi, and Songwe). The van operating in the northern zone (which includes regions of Kilimanjaro and Arusha) was not included because CAD4TB was not installed at the time of this study. During the study, the coastal zone van was stationed in Dar es salaam, the southern highland van was stationed in Mbeya, the central zone van was stationed in Dodoma, and the Lake zone van was stationed in Mwanza. In this study reporting, the vans names will be referred as per the regions they were stationed during the study. Each van was operated by a radiographer, responsible for taking and reading the X-ray images and a lab technician for conducting the GeneXpert test. Each Van was installed with Perlove digital Xray machine. Analysis of the data was conducted at the Emerging Technologies for Health Research and Development Laboratory (ETH), which is under the Department of Biomedical Engineering at the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences, Mloganzila campus.

Study design

This was a retrospective study to analyse the effectiveness of CAD4TB for the screening of pulmonary TB on digital chest X-ray. We collected and analysed data from September 2021 to March 2022 on TB screening from the mobile van used for TB screening in Tanzania.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included all the data in the TB vans from September 2021 to March 2022. In line with national active case-finding (ACF) guidelines, all individuals presenting for community screening, whether symptomatic or asymptomatic, were eligible for chest X-ray and CAD4TB assessment. This aspect is essential because ACF strategies aim to detect both clinical and subclinical TB, which represent a large proportion of missed cases in high-burden settings. The study excluded individuals who were not subjected to GeneXpert. Data from the Dodoma van were excluded due to relatively poor quality, with considerable missing data and technical issues related to the van itself. The developers of CAD4TB used in this study recommend that the software be used for people above four (4) years of age. It is important to note that different CAD4TB software versions may require recalibration of threshold values, and performance can also vary across age groups; therefore, specifying the use of CAD4TB version 6 and the ≥ 4-year age eligibility criterion is essential for accurate interpretation of the findings. This paper acknowledges the poor recording of data in the vans as having no basic information on patient demographics and proposes a way forward.

Study population

This study was conducted by using data collected from a total of 11,923 individuals screened by using four mobile vans (the Dar es salaam, Dodoma, Mbeya and Mwanza vans). These were individuals screened for pulmonary TB in the communities using AI-assisted chest X-ray.

Data collection

In each van, there was a patient registry that included the names of all patients screened, the type of tests conducted, and their screening results. Delft uses a small mini supercomputer, which is where the CAD4TB software was installed. To interface with a mini supercomputer, the company used a software called Thirona installed on a tablet. Thirona software had the option to download all the CAD4TB scores in a CSV file format with the CAD4TB score of everyone. The files were downloaded to a laptop and copied into a password-protected external hard drive. Research assistants extracted the CAD4TB score of all individuals, together with their lab results for those who were subjected to GeneXpert. According to the Vans protocol, radiographers were required to read the X-ray images first and get a second opinion from the CAD4TB. Each radiographer had over 10 years of experience, with a diploma certificate. Only suspected cases were subjected to GeneXpert for confirmatory examination. GeneXpert testing was not applied uniformly across all screened individuals. In some screening sites, confirmatory testing was performed only among individuals classified as radiologically or clinically suggestive, while in others all screened individuals underwent GeneXpert testing. This non-uniform application of the reference standard introduces verification bias and reflects inconsistencies in adherence to the intended screening protocol across mobile units. Hence, it was possible to obtain both manual reading and CADTB scores. The approval for data access was provided by the National Tuberculosis and Leprosy Program (NTLP), who are custodian of the data. Although culture is the gold-standard confirmatory test for TB, the process is slow and requires weeks to yield results. For practical screening and routine programmatic use, the NTLP therefore uses GeneXpert as the primary confirmatory test due to its rapid turnaround time and high diagnostic accuracy.

Their softcopy was then copied into the password-protected external hard drives. The lab results were then combined with the GeneXpert results into a single CSV file for analysis. Screening workflow across mobile vans

Screening workflows varied across the mobile vans due to differences in operational practices and adherence to national guidelines. In the intended protocol, individuals undergo chest X-ray followed by CAD4TB analysis, and only those classified as suggestive are referred for GeneXpert testing. However, in practice, deviations were observed: in some vans, GeneXpert testing was performed selectively among suggestive cases, while in others all screened individuals were directly subjected to GeneXpert irrespective of X-ray or CAD4TB findings. In addition, inconsistencies in documentation were noted, including missing radiological readings in some sites. These variations reflect real-world implementation challenges but introduce heterogeneity in the dataset.

Data analysis

Data cleaning and handling of missing values

Data analysis began with a structured preprocessing workflow designed to ensure data quality and consistency across the four mobile vans. All records were first anonymised, and variables were inspected to standardise their representation (e.g., harmonising categorical labels and screening outcome entries). Missing values were then assessed: individuals lacking GeneXpert results, which are required as the reference standard, were excluded from analyses involving diagnostic performance. Because GeneXpert results were not available for all screened individuals, the dataset does not represent a fully verified population. As a result, analyses involving comparisons with GeneXpert are subject to verification bias and should be interpreted with caution. Records with missing manual X-ray readings or missing CAD4TB scores were retained but omitted from analyses requiring those specific variables, as the missingness was operational in nature and not amenable to statistical imputation.

Duplicate entries were identified using unique patient identifiers and cross-checked against screening dates, CAD4TB scores, and GeneXpert outcomes. When multiple entries existed for the same individual, the record with the most complete and internally consistent set of results (aligned with van registries) was retained. Entries with irreconcilable discrepancies, such as conflicting GeneXpert outcomes recorded for the same day, were removed to prevent bias. Following this cleaning process, the initial dataset of 14,548 entries was reduced to 11,923 analyzable records.

Identification of outliers

CAD4TB scores outside the expected 0–100 range were examined and classified as outliers. Negative scores were found to correspond to non-chest X-ray images mistakenly uploaded to the CAD4TB platform. These entries were excluded from threshold analysis as outliers but reported descriptively to highlight operational challenges in the use of AI-based tools by van operators.

Analytical approach and statistical methods

The analytical phase aimed to evaluate the performance of CAD4TB in comparison with manual chest-X-ray interpretation and GeneXpert results. Descriptive statistics were computed for CAD4TB scores, manual X-ray interpretations, and GeneXpert outcomes. Distributions of CAD4TB scores among GeneXpert-positive vs. GeneXpert-negative individuals were examined using scatterplots, boxplots, and frequency distributions to visualize patterns, detect outliers, and explore score separability across true-positive and true-negative cases.

Threshold exploration was performed by assessing the proportion of GeneXpert-positive cases detected at different CAD4TB score cutoffs, with particular focus on the manufacturer’s default threshold of 50 and a lower empirically derived threshold. Although ROC curve construction is typically recommended for determining optimal thresholds, inconsistencies in screening implementation, such as the lack of standardized use of CAD4TB prior to GeneXpert testing and incomplete X-ray recording in some vans, limited the feasibility of robust ROC analysis for the full dataset. Instead, threshold comparisons were conducted descriptively to illustrate operational implications and to support the recommendation that a prospective, protocol-adherent study is needed to establish validated national thresholds.

The overall analytical approach is consistent with the constraints of using secondary programmatic data but allows meaningful observation of CAD4TB’s behaviour in real-world settings.

Although one of the initial aim was to determine and recommend a threshild value of CAD4TB in community screening, because of incomplete adherence to the screening protocol, particularly the absence of X-ray results in Dar es Salaam and the practice of sending all clients directly for GeneXpert in Mwanza and Mbeya limited the analysis. It was not possible to construct an unbiased ROC curve across the full CAD4TB score range. These data limitations restricted our ability to perform a formal threshold optimisation analysis.

Ethical clearance

This study obtained ethical clearance from the MUHAS ethical review board.

The Dar es Salaam van did not have manual X-ray results recorded in the registry, although it screened the largest number of individuals. The absence of X-ray readings is likely due to inconsistencies in documentation practices and incomplete adherence to the standard screening protocol, which resulted in radiological interpretations not being routinely entered into the logbook. In contrast, the Mbeya and Mwanza vans recorded X-ray results for all participants and also performed GeneXpert testing on every individual screened. This approach led to all clients being subjected to GeneXpert testing, resulting in avoidable costs and prolonged screening workflows.

For the Mwanza and Mbeya vans, sending all participants directly to GeneXpert despite having chest X-ray results suggests that human readers may not have been using either the CAD4TB outputs or their manual interpretations to triage clients before confirmatory testing. As shown in Table 1, only 2.12% of screened individuals in Mbeya and 30% in Mwanza were classified as suggestive by manual radiology, indicating a low concordance between manual X-ray interpretation and confirmatory outcomes. These differences in screening workflows across vans limit direct comparability of results between sites and should be considered when interpreting the observed patterns.

To align with NTLP recommendations and WHO’s End TB targets, these findings highlight the need to strengthen training for van operators on the correct screening protocol—conducting a manual X-ray read, confirming with CAD4TB as a second reader, and referring only suggestive cases for GeneXpert testing. This workflow is the intended purpose of introducing CAD4TB: to reduce the burden on human readers, improve screening efficiency, and minimize unnecessary GeneXpert tests.

Exploratory evaluation of CAD4TB score thresholds

As stated earlier, the CAD4TB provides scores from 0 to 100, and the greater the score, the greater the number of suggestive TB. CAD4TB scores are unitless likelihood values and should not be interpreted as percentages. Our study revealed that a considerable number of positive cases remained when a threshold value of 50 was used. As shown in Fig. 5, eight (8) GeneXpert positively confirmed cases were left out, marked with an orange cross in the figure.

To address this uncertainty, we analysed the dataset to understand the distribution and variability of CAD4TB scores against GeneXpert results, as shown in Fig. 6. The distribution of CAD4TB scores against GeneXpert results showed that in positively confirmed GeneXpert patients, the CAD4TB scores were normally distributed, with a minimum score of 31.96 and a maximum score of 98.71, which accounted for approximately 99 (44 patients out of 47) of the positive cases. However, for negative results, CAD4TB scores are normally distributed within the range of 0.08 to 39.13. We also identified the presence of outliers that comprised data points outside the specified ranges above.

Discussion

This section provides a general discussion of the analysed results. In both vans we visited, we realized that they did not have proper and uniform ways of recording test results in registries. This led to many missing data, such as the absence of manual readings of chest X-ray results in the Dar es Salaam van and the poor quality of data in the Dodoma van. In both vans, our study also found non-chest X-ray images in the van data collections, which contradicts the CAD4TB software supplier’s prohibition of.

using the software for non-chest X-ray image analysis [22]. Although our analysis.

Shows the potential of using CAD4TB AI-based software; proper use of the technology may bring more value [23]. In addition, we found many images in the CAD4TB boxes, 5375 images in the Dar es Salaam van, 4912 images in the Mwanza van, and 4261 images in the Mbeya Van. This large amount of data may affect the functionality of the CAD4TB Box and, hence, the software installed in the box. Proper storage of data may not only save the computing energy of the tools but also safely keep the data in a proper and safe manner. This can be done by leveraging data centres already available in the country [24].

The improper use of technology can also be witnessed in the number of.

clients who underwent GeneXpert testing was greater than those who underwent X-ray analysis, which may mean that several clients did not undergo X-ray analysis as the initial test for TB, hence increasing the use of GeneXpert. Additionally, our study found a poor performance of human readers compared to CAD4TB has shown efficiency, as out of 818 cases detected as suggestive by X-ray (analysed by humans), only 15 cases were confirmed to be positive by GeneXpert. This demonstrates the potential use of CAD4TB in resource-limited countries as an assistive tool for human readers. Although the poor performance of human readers can be justified based on the level of training, as many of them were radiographers and not radiologists [25], CAD4TB may still stand as a potential tool for chest X-ray image interpretation. This is because the tool can interpret many images at once, and can be used even with low-level trained personnel, in a remote location, important for resource-limited countries like Tanzania. Also, one of the weaknesses is the use of GeneXpert as a confirmatory test, yet it has a low sensitivity and specificity compared to culture [26]. Finally, in the comparison between CAD4TB and GeneXpert, this study suggests further analysis to be done with proper data collection to determine a new threshold value for community screening using AI in Tanzania. Although our exploratory analyses suggested that a score of 30 captured the majority of GeneXpert-positive cases in this dataset, this value is not proposed as an operational threshold for Tanzania. Given the inconsistencies in data completeness and deviations from the intended screening workflow, these findings cannot substitute for a properly designed prospective threshold-setting study. We emphasise that a validated national CAD4TB threshold should only be established through a controlled study using complete datasets and ROC-based optimisation. The absence of complete CAD4TB–GeneXpert pairs across all screening sites prevented the calculation of diagnostic accuracy measures such as sensitivity and specificity. A prospective study with strict adherence to the screening protocol is needed to obtain these metrics reliably. This study is best interpreted as a programmatic evaluation of CAD4TB implementation under routine operational conditions, rather than a formal diagnostic accuracy study. The findings highlight important gaps in protocol adherence and data completeness, which should be addressed in future prospective evaluations.

Another important limitation of this study is the relatively small number of microbiologically confirmed TB cases. This limits the statistical power of the analysis and reduces the reliability and stability of observed performance patterns. As a result, any apparent alignment between CAD4TB scores and GeneXpert-confirmed cases should be interpreted with caution. The findings should therefore be considered exploratory and indicative of programmatic trends rather than definitive evidence of diagnostic performance.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, the use of CAD4TB has been shown to play a significant role in TB case detection. The number of cases that can be quickly analysed by CAD4TB compared to manual reading by human radiologists demonstrates the efficiency of the technology in resource-limited countries such as Tanzania. Additionally, CAD4TB is cost-efficient in TB programs because its use reduces the number of patients who were directly subjected to the expensive process of GeneXpert, an expensive but confirmatory test for TB diagnosis, as has been confirmed in other studies [19]. As the CAD4TB is case subjective, and it is recommended that each country develop a threshold, this study highlights the importance of further in-depth study to be conducted to get a threshold score for Tanzanian settings. Larger studies with a higher number of confirmed TB cases are needed to enable more robust evaluation of CAD4TB performance.
