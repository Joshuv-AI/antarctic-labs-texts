The U-shaped association between serum direct bilirubin and incident mild cognitive impairment in hemodialysis patients: a multicenter study
Authors: Yuqi Yang, Qian Li, Qian Zhang, Jing Yuan, Yan Zha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have suggested an association between low serum bilirubin concentrations and increased risk of cognitive impairment. This study aimed to explore the association and dose-response relationship between serum direct bilirubin (DBIL) concentrations and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) among hemodialysis patients.MethodsThis is a multicenter cross-sectional study with patients undergoing hemodialysis from 22 dialysis centers in Guizhou Province, China. The outcome was mild cognitive impairment (MCI), measured with a Mini-Mental State Examination. The association and dose-response relationship between serum DBIL and MCI incidence were examined using multivariate logistic regression analysis, restricted cubic spline, and subgroup analysis to explore the association of serum DBIL concentrations with MCI.ResultsOf the 4223 enrolled patients (mean age 55.2 ± 15.3 years, 60.4% males), 1187 (28.1%) had MCI. Serum DBIL of 0.10-1.67umol/L [multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (OR) 1.32, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.08–1.60, P = 0.005], 2.31-3.20umol/L (OR = 1.22, 95%CI: 1.00-1.49, P = 0.047), and > 3.21umol/L (OR = 1.32, 95%CI: 1.08–1.61, P = 0.006) had increased risk of MCI compared with 1.68-2.30umol/L. The dose-response analysis between serum DBIL and MCI showed a U-shaped relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.009), and the serum DBIL concentrations with the lowest risk of MCI was 2.01umol/L. As the serum DBIL concentrations were lower than the reference, the risk of MCI decreased by 49% per standard deviation (SD) increase in serum DBIL (OR = 0.51, 95%CI: 0.29–0.89, P < 0.001); when the concentration exceeds 2.01umol/L, a rise per SD increased the risk of MCI by 9% (OR = 1.09, 95%CI:1.01–1.17, P < 0.001).ConclusionOur findings suggest a U-shaped association between serum DBIL and MCI among hemodialysis patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-024-06189-x.

Background

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), especially those underlying hemodialysis, has become a significant concern in recent years [1]. MCI has been reported as a high prevalence between 30 and 60% [2, 3], approximately three-fold higher than the age-matched general population [4]. Previous studies have shown that MCI is an independent risk factor of all-cause mortality, with up to 2.5-fold increased death rate compared to those with normal cognitive function [5, 6]. However, knowledge about the underlying pathogenesis of MCI in hemodialysis patients is still limited. Considering a transitional state between average age-related cognitive decline and dementia, MCI is more likely to maintain or even improve through timely and proper preventive strategies [7]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors is of paramount significance in detecting MCI before progressing to irreversible stages.

Bilirubin, the end product of heme catabolism through a complex sequence of reactions, possesses potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties in the body [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that serum bilirubin concentrations are critical in renal progression, increased risk of cardiovascular events, and all-cause mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients [9–11]. Additionally, bilirubin has been shown to play critical roles in counteract oxidative stress in several chronic inflammatory conditions [12–14], including cognitive impairment [15]. Previous studies showed that serum bilirubin concentrations were decreased and positively correlated with cognitive scores in patients with MCI [16, 17]. Based on the above research, serum bilirubin concentrations may influence cognitive function in patients undergoing hemodialysis, a state of persistent severe oxidative stress.

Few studies have investigated the relationship between serum direct bilirubin (DBIL) and MCI in hemodialysis patients. The role that serum DBIL plays in the development and progression of cognitive impairment remains unclear. Therefore, in the present study, we conducted a multicenter cross-sectional study to evaluate the relationship between serum DBIL and MCI in hemodialysis patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicenter, cross-sectional study recruited patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis from 22 hemodialysis centers in Guizhou Province, China, between June 1, 2019, and September 30, 2021, and has been described in previous publications [18]. Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis were included in the analysis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: a). aged < 18 years, b). the duration of hemodialysis less than 3 months; c). prior receipt of dialysis or organ transplant; d). with severe mood disorders or psychotic disorders; e). with hepatobiliary diseases, abnormal alanine transaminase (ALT, ≥ 80U/L) or aspartate transaminase (AST, ≥ 80U/L); f). missing bilirubin and cognition measurements. The flow chart is shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Flow chart of the study

Flow chart of the study

The current study conformed to the principles set by the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Guizhou Provincial People’s Hospital (Approval number: [2020]208). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study.

Clinical assessment

Demographic and clinical characteristics were obtained by trained interviewers used with a well-designed general questionnaire, including age, sex, educational level, smoking, drinking alcohol status; mean arterial pressure (MAP), body mass index (BMI); dialysis vintages, the type of vascular access; the presence of comorbidities (Hypertension, diabetes mellitus). Details have been shown in Method S1.

Cognitive assessment

Professional doctors assessed cognitive function using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) questionnaire. The MMSE consists of 30 items referring to orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, and language. The total score of MMSE was 30 points, and the lower score indicated worse cognitive function. If the MMSE score of a patient is less than 27, the patient can be diagnosed with MCI [19–21].

Serum measurement

Fasting venous blood from patients was collected to measure biochemical parameters. The concentrations of serum total bilirubin (TBIL), DBIL, indirect bilirubin (IBIL); ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase (ALP); total cholesterol, triglyceride, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDLC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC) were measured by automatic biochemistry analyzer according to routine protocols in the hospital medical laboratories.

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics were compared among serum DBIL quartile groups using One-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis H-tests for normally and not normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test for normality. When appropriate, the chi-square test or exact chi-square test was used for dichotomous variables. Pearson’s correlation analyses evaluated the correlations between DBIL, IBIL, TBIL, and MMSE and their domains. The univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the relationship between DBIL and MCI. The adjusted variates in the multivariate stepwise logistic regression models came from potential confounding factors that achieved a P-value < 0.05. In Model 1, we adjusted for age and sex; in Model 2, we adjusted educational concentrations, smoking, drinking, hemodialysis vintage, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and MAP based on Model 1; in Model 3, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDLC, HDLC, ALT, AST, ALP, and antilipidemic drug were adjusted based on Model 2. The odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used for non-linear relationships of serum DBIL concentrations after adjusting potential confounders, which were the same as those in multivariate logistic regression models. The likelihood ratio test was used for the nonlinearity tests. If the relationships were non-linear, the difference of relationships at the threshold was performed using two piecewise linear regression models. An additional turning point for serum DBIL was determined by curve fitting the cognitive impairment corresponding to the turning point in the graph. The risk associated with MCI is reported with per standard deviation (SD) of continuous serum DBIL. On the other hand, the subgroup analysis was conducted by age (≤ 54 or > 54 years), sex (male or female), educational level (low or high), smoking status (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), and diabetes (yes or no). The potential confounders were the same as those in the multivariate logistic regression models.

All statistical analyses were performed by IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0, Chicago, USA) and the statistical packages R (The R Foundation; http://ww.r-project.org; version 4.0.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically different.

Results

Study participants and baseline characteristics

The flow chart (Fig. 1) shows that 4223 hemodialysis patients were included in the current study. The mean age was 55.2 ± 15.3 years, and 60.4% were female. The median hemodialysis duration was 41.0 (20.0–73.0) months, and the mean DBIL concentration was 2.6 ± 1.4umol/L. The mean MMSE score was 27.5 ± 3.5, and 1187 (28.1%) patients were identified as MCI. Participants were categorized into four groups according to serum DBIL quartiles: Q1 (0.10-1.67umol/L, n = 1059), Q2 (1.68–2.30, n = 1154), Q3 (2.31–3.20, n = 1003), and Q4 (3.21–9.90, n = 1007). The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. Along with the increase in baseline DBIL concentrations, it showed a higher percentage of older male patients, longer dialysis vintages, lower MAP, BMI; lower levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDLC, and higher levels of ALT, AST, ALP, TBIL, and IBIL. Patients in group Q2 had the lowest percentage of MCI compared with other groups.

Associations between serum DBIL concentrations and incident MCI risk

Considering the lowest concentration of serum DBIL with the lowest risk of MCI was 2.01umol/L, and it fell within the range of the Q2 group (DBIL, 1.68–2.30 umol/L), we regarded the Q2 group with the reference group. As shown in Table 2, compared with those in Q2 group, hemodialysis patients in other quartiles showed a higher risk of MCI, with ORs (95%CIs) of 1.25 (1.04–1.51) for Q1 group, 1.25(1.04,1.52) for Q3 group and 1.31(1.08–1.58) for Q4 group (all P value < 0.05). After adjusting covariates, significant statistical trends existed in three groups (all P < 0.005). The fully adjusted ORs (95%CIs) for incident MCI of the other three quartiles of DBIL were 1.32(1.08–1.60), 1.22(1.00-1.49) and 1.32(1.08–1.61), respectively.

The dose-response relationship between serum DBIL concentrations with incident MCI risk

This dose-response relationship was further demonstrated with RCS, and a U-shaped relationship was observed between DBIL and incident MCI risk after adjustment for covariates (P for non-linear trend = 0.008, Fig. 2). According to the lowest OR value attained from the RCS curve, the serum DBIL concentration with the lowest MCI risk was 2.01umol/L. Compared with 2.01umol/L as a reference, the multivariate model showed that higher range and lower limit of serum DBIL concentrations increased the risk of MCI. We further fitted the relationship between DBIL and MCI using the two-piecewise logistic regression model (Table 3). Among the participants whose DBIL ≤ 2.01umol/L, there was a significant trend toward decreasing odds of MCI with increasing DBIL (OR 0.51, 95%CI 0.29–0.89, P < 0.001) in the fully adjusted model. Inversely, among those with DBIL > 2.01umol/L, there was a significant trend toward increasing odds of MCI with increasing DBIL (OR 1.09, 95%CI 1.01–1.17, P < 0.001).

Stratified analyses for associations between DBIL and incident MCI risk

The exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to assess the association between DBIL and the prevalence of MCI according to the turnpoint of serum DBIL concentrations (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2). In the group with serum DBIL ≤ 2.01umol/L, the significant dose-response relationship between DBIL and MCI incidence was more pronounced in females aged ≤ 54, with low educational concentrations, and non-smoking. In the group with serum DBIL > 2.01umol/L, the significant dose-response relationship was more pronounced in males aged ≤ 54 years with high educational concentrations, hypertension, and without diabetes. No significant interactions were found between DBIL concentrations and the above covariates in this population (all P > 0.05).

Subgroup analyses for serum DBIL concentrations predicting MCI among hemodialysis patients

We also conducted subgroup analyses among four groups of participants. The significant relationship between DBIL and MCI incidence was more pronounced in patients younger than 54 years of age, with low educational concentrations, non-smoking, and subjects without hypertension or diabetes. All the subgroup analyses showed no statistical interactions, as seen in Supplemental Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

This study presented the relationship between serum DBIL concentrations and incident MCI among hemodialysis patients for the first time. Hemodialysis patients generally had an increased risk of MCI, and serum DBIL concentrations may be an independent predictor of MCI in these patients. The association between serum DBIL and risk of MCI incidence demonstrated a U-shaped tendency, with the lowest event rate among those with serum DBIL concentrations of 2.01umol/L. Subgroup analyses found that the significant dose-response relationship between DBIL and MCI incidence was more pronounced in females aged ≤ 54, low educational concentrations, and non-smoking.

Similar to previous studies [16, 22, 23], the current study found that lower DBIL concentrations (< 2.01umo/L) were associated with the increased risk of MCI. An earlier prospective study demonstrated that serum concentrations of TBIL at baseline were lower in the patients with MCI, as calculated by MMSE scores, compared with those with normal cognitive function [17]. The study showed that low TBIL concentrations had a synergistic effect with serum albumin on the risk of MCI. However, they did not evaluate the direct impact of TBIL on MCI. A cross-sectional study including 1121 healthy Japanese adults found that low total bilirubin concentrations could be associated with a high prevalence of severe deep white matter lesions (DWMLs) related to impaired cognitive function [22]. The analysis revealed that the lower total bilirubin (< 17.1umol/L) indicated up to 2.36-fold increase in the prevalence of severe DWMLs compared with the high total bilirubin (≥ 17.1umol/L). In addition, declined serum TBIL concentrations were positively correlated with memory scores in schizophrenia patients [23, 24]. All the above studies indicated that TBIL was more likely a protective factor within the normal range for cognitive impairment, and the linear relationship of negative correlation they found may only be part of the U-type relationship.

The hypothesis that defects in the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory defense system might be involved in cognitive impairment might support this result [25]. Oxidative stress is involved in the hypofunction of NMDA receptors due to its redox-sensitive nature [26], and the decreased activity of NMDA receptors has been identified to affect the development of cognitive impairment [27]. Bilirubin can scavenge reactive oxygen species produced in oxidative stress, inhibit the aggregation of inflammatory cells, improve nitric oxide bioavailability, and play a protective role in cognition [28, 29]. In addition, neural bilirubin can scavenge superoxide to prevent NMDA receptor excitotoxicity as the endogenous antioxidant [30]. Hence, the deficiency of bilirubin could lead to cognitive impairment due to a relative lack of antioxidants or oxidative stress-induced bilirubin consumption.

However, it is worth noting that when the DBIL concentration is approximately 2.01umol/L, the protective effect of DBIL on MCI will no longer be enhanced in this study. When the bilirubin concentration exceeds this range, the protective effect will weaken, and a dangerous effect will appear. This implies that the MCI risk would no longer decrease when baseline DBIL increased to a relatively higher concentration. Meanwhile, there was a series of evidence showing that higher DBIL concentrations indicated neural injuries, with the possibility of progressing into chronic bilirubin encephalopathy, and the latter was proven to be associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment [31–33]. Therefore, the actual U-shaped association might be the combination of antioxidation and neural toxicity effects of DBIL.

We could not directly compare our results with those of previous studies because no data shows the effect of DBIL on MCI incidence among hemodialysis patients. The U-shaped curve was consistent with earlier studies about relationships between bilirubin and some chronic diseases [34–38]. Yang et al. found that serum total bilirubin concentrations played an independent role in predicting 3-year mortality among patients receiving regular peritoneal dialysis, and patients with serum total bilirubin 0.5–0.6 mg/dL had the lowest mortality rate (24%) [34]. A five-year cohort based on 5323 male diabetic patients showed a U-shaped association between bilirubin and diabetic retinopathy incidence, which was independent of the control status of diabetes and other related covariates [35]. A total of 10,900 Chinese male hypertensive participants were assessed in a real-world observational study, the China H-type hypertension Registry Study, and the U-shaped association between total bilirubin concentrations and the prevalence of peripheral arterial disease, with the inflection point of 11.48umol/L [36]. Another sizeable Chinese cohort, the Dongfeng-Tongji cohort, demonstrated that total bilirubin and indirect bilirubin had U-shaped associations with incident coronary heart disease.

In contrast, the association with DBIL was dose-response [37]. A recent meta-analysis including seven prospective studies depicted a U-shaped dose-response relationship between bilirubin concentrations and coronary heart disease. In the study, when the bilirubin concentrations were in the range of 3.42-13umol/L, the protective effect of bilirubin on CHD was enhanced with increasing bilirubin concentrations. When the bilirubin concentrations exceeded 13umol/L, the protective effect of bilirubin weakened, and a dangerous effect gradually appeared with further increases in bilirubin concentrations [38]. All the above diseases studied seem to share a similar inflammatory status with MCI or share an underlying cause, such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, which seems to provide evidence to support the results of our study.

A major strength of this study is that it was based on a large sample-size multicenter database with relatively complete medical information. Anyway, there are still some limitations to this study. First, as the present study is cross-sectional, the causal relationship between bilirubin concentrations and MCI is unknown. Further, longitudinal studies are needed to clarify their causality. Second, this observational study measured DBIL concentrations only at baseline, so it could not reflect the actual fluctuations. Third, even though we have taken a lot of potential confounders into account in this study, residual confounding by unmeasured or unknown confounders cannot be completely ruled out. Finally, given that the study was limited to Chinese hemodialysis patients, further studies need to broaden the generalization of our findings.

Conclusions

Serum DBIL concentrations are measurable in daily clinical practice around the world. This study demonstrated a U-shaped association of serum DBIL concentrations with MCI among hemodialysis patients, especially in female patients aged ≤ 54 with low educational levels and non-smoking. It suggests that serum DBIL levels are an excellent potential marker for estimating the risk of MCI, which could be related to the development of dementia. Early evaluation of DBIL may be helpful in early documentation of the risk of MCI among hemodialysis patients in clinical practice. Further research is needed to elucidate the possible molecular mechanisms between serum DBIL and MCI.
