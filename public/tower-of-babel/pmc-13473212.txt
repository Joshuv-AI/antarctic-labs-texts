Aging-Related Choroidal Heterogeneity and Loss: An Investigative Global Human Choroidal Thickness Review (ARCHLIGHT Study)
Authors: Abbie Lai, Mariam Issa, Ahmad Ali, Austin Pereira, Alaa Al Ali, Jay Chhablani, Peng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To synthesize current evidence on the pathophysiology, clinical relevance, and implications for age-related choroidal thinning.Methods:A systematic literature search of relevant publications was conducted across MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Library, up to and including May 2024. Identified studies reported choroidal thickness changes with aging. Data on subfoveal choroidal thickness, choroidal vascularity index, and associated factors (ethnicity, systemic disease, axial length) were analyzed (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251041101).Results:Choroidal thickness declines with age, with ethnicity-specific rates ranging from −0.93 µm/year in Spanish cohorts to −4.00 µm/year in Chinese populations. Age-related choroidal thinning correlates with retinal and neurodegenerative pathologies, including glaucoma (nasal choroidal thinning >250 µm in older adult patients), myopic maculopathy (subfoveal choroidal thickness <100 µm in high myopia), and Alzheimer disease (subfoveal choroidal thickness 169 µm vs 253 µm in control patients). Enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography (OCT) and swept-source OCT reveal preferential vascular loss (choroidal vascularity index <30%) and stromal atrophy. Systemic risks, such as hypertension or smoking, accelerate choroidal thinning.Conclusions:Age-related choroidal thinning exists on a continuum between physiologic aging and pathology, with implications for retinal and systemic health. Future prospective studies will help determine diagnostic thresholds (subfoveal choroidal thickness <200 µm in nonmyopic adults >60 years or rapid thinning beyond ethnic normative values), management recommendations for high-risk cohorts (glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration, high myopia), and therapeutic considerations for monitoring of choroidal thickness changes from moderate- to low-certainty evidence.

Introduction

Age-related choroidal changes occur across healthy and diseased populations globally. There are several distinct pathologic processes involving choroidal atrophy, including age-related choroidal atrophy, which was coined in 2009 in reference to degeneration of the choroid associated with choroidal thickness less than 125 µm, tessellated fundus appearance, macular pigmentary changes, and reduction of choroidal vessel density of the macula. 1 Before this, age-related changes in the choroid were recognized as part of broader chorioretinal pathology, particularly in the context of age-related macular degeneration (AMD). 2 Given the association of choroidal atrophy with many vision-threatening diseases, including primary open-angle and normal tension glaucoma, there is growing interest in understanding the role of normal age-related changes in vitreoretinal pathology.3,4

Imaging techniques, such as enhanced depth imaging optical coherence tomography (OCT) and swept-source OCT (SS-OCT), have allowed for better visualization of vascular and stromal structures of the choroid, enabling more precise examination of choroidal layers to better understand the progression of aging-associated choroidal atrophy. The choroidal vascularity index has also emerged as a valuable quantitative tool for evaluating choroidal vascular status with the use of enhanced depth imaging OCT. 3

Regional patterns of choroidal thinning are increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers of disease progression and overall ocular health. Understanding these variations can help differentiate physiologic aging processes from pathologic changes. Other factors, including axial length, refractive error, ethnicity, sex, and systemic disease, have been implicated in the modulation of age-related choroidal changes.

Despite a growing awareness of age-related choroidal changes, the literature remains limited in comprehensively characterizing normative values, risk factors, pathophysiology, and optimal management strategies. This review aims to synthesize the current evidence on age-related choroidal changes, with a focus on patterns of regional choroidal thinning, subfoveal and mean choroidal thickness, and associations with ocular and systemic diseases. Furthermore, correlations with axial length, refraction, sex, and ethnicity will be explored. By consolidating existing research, we seek to enhance understanding, inform management and prevention strategies, and provide a clearer framework for distinguishing age-related choroidal changes from other retinal pathologies, ultimately contributing to improved patient care and guiding future research endeavors in the field of age-related eye diseases.

Methods

This study used the 2020 PRISMA statement and is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews database (ID: CRD420251041101). Ethics approval was not required due to the nature of the study.

A literature search of Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted from inception to May 2024 (Supplementary Table A). Figure 1 displays the detailed PRISMA flowchart. Our search focused on capturing aging-associated structural decline and included the terms ‘age-related choroidal loss’, ‘choroidal atrophy’, and their synonyms. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), population studies, case controls, or observational studies that reported choroidal changes that occur with age. Included studies discussed the correlation between choroidal thickness and age. Two independent reviewers (M.I., A.A.) examined the studies and extracted data, including but not limited to study identifiers, baseline characteristics of participants and treated eyes, ocular history, OCT device and technique, choroidal thickness measurements in various regions, choroidal vascular diameter, and more, if available. Quality of studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklists. A third independent reviewer (A.L.) resolved conflicts in the data. The primary outcomes were subfoveal choroidal thickness and mean choroidal thickness. Secondary outcomes included choroidal vascularity index, axial length, macular layers, subretinal layers, and choroid vessel measurements. The quality of evidence was summarized using the GRADE approach. 5

Methods of Choroidal Thickness Measurement

Measurements of subfoveal choroidal thickness were taken from the outer border of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) to the junction between the choroid and sclera at the subfoveal point.6 –10 In a previous study, the central 1 mm subfoveal choroidal zone was used. 11 Both automated and manual approaches were used for measurement. Automated segmentation with manual verification was employed in 2 previous studies.8,11 Manual methods involved direct measurement at the central fovea, 9 manual layer segmentation, 7 or caliper-based measurement. 10 OCT devices used for subfoveal choroidal thickness measurement included the Spectralis OCT with enhanced depth imaging,4,6,7,12 –14 Topcon DRI Triton with SS-OCT,5,10,11,15,16 Zeiss Cirrus HD-OCT,17,18 and the Optovue Avanti RTVue XR OCT. 9 The wavelength range for OCT devices used to assess subfoveal choroidal thickness was between 840 nm and 1310 nm, with a median operating wavelength of approximately 990 nm across 8 studies reporting this parameter.

Mean choroidal thickness was similarly measured from the RPE to the choroidoscleral junction, typically calculated from multiple choroidal measurements at or near the fovea. Of the presented values, 2 studies used the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) grid to obtain circular zones at 1, 3, and 6 mm from the center of the macula.8,11 One study computed mean choroidal thickness as the average of 9 locations: 1 subfoveal, 3 nasal (N1–N3), and 5 temporal (T1–T5) points, each spaced 1000 μm apart. 10 Another study did horizontal scans every 500 μm from the fovea, extending up to 2500 μm temporally. 18 Jiang et al 16 averaged values from 900 microgrids within a 6-by-6 mm macular area. Two studies calculated the mean choroidal thickness from 9 ETDRS values, and 3 ETDRS zones (at 1, 3, 6 mm) were used in 1 study for calculations. Two studies calculated the mean choroidal thickness up to 1.50 mm from the fovea at regular intervals.3,8,17,19 –21 OCT devices used to measure mean choroidal thickness included Topcon,8,10,11,15,16 Zeiss, 18 Optovue, 9 and Spectralis OCT with enhanced depth imaging. 12 The wavelength range of devices used for measurement of mean choroidal thickness ranged from 840 nm to 1180 nm, with an average of approximately 997 nm across 7 reported studies.

These studies demonstrate methodologic heterogeneity, including differences in OCT platforms, wavelength ranges, and segmentation approaches.

Results

Our search yielded 2941 studies, 20 of which were eligible to be included in the analysis, for a total of 3439 patients. All included studies were cross-sectional in design. One (Gattoussi et al 6 ) drew its cross-sectional choroidal thickness data from a longitudinal population-based cohort (the ALIENOR/Three-City Study). In terms of study population, 4 studies involved Egyptian adults; 2 examined American and Turkish adults; 2 included Indian adults, while 1 comprised Indian adolescents; and 1 study each examined populations from Japan, Korea, Italy, Iran, Taiwan, Nigeria, Portugal, France, and China. In addition, 1 study assessed individuals of all ages from Spain.

Using Joanna Briggs Institute checklists, all studies were deemed of acceptable quality (moderate and above). A visual summary of the domain-level assessments is provided in Supplementary Table B. The most common sources of potential bias were related to unclear reporting of outcome measurement and identification of confounding variables. Using the GRADE approach, evidence of moderate certainty supports decreased age- and ethnicity-related subfoveal choroidal thickness (Table 1). Evidence of lower certainty links age-related choroidal thinning to glaucoma, high myopia, and Alzheimer disease. The heterogeneity in choroidal thickness methodology, combined with inconsistent axial-length correction, further limits cross-study comparability of absolute choroidal thickness values and contributed to downgrading for inconsistency and indirectness.

Subfoveal Choroidal Thickness

Subfoveal choroidal thickness in healthy populations was reported in 13 studies (n = 1167). Across all studies and populations, subfoveal choroidal thickness declined with increasing age (Table 2). The physiologic rate of thinning varied by population but generally ranged from −0.93 to −4.0 µm/year, with slower rates observed in Western cohorts and a more rapid decline reported in East Asian populations10,22 (Table 3). Subfoveal choroidal thickness was generally greater than 320 µm in children under 10 years old 10 and similarly high in young adults (<40 years).3,8,10,13,23

Regional Patterns in Choroidal Thinning

Regional variation in choroidal thickness was reported in many of the included studies. The subfoveal region was consistently thickest,9,11,12,18 with Okonkwo reporting a mean choroidal thickness of 265.4 ± 76.2 µm at the central subfovea. The nasal quadrant was consistently the thinnest region,4,11,13,18 with rapid tapering near the optic disc or peripapillary area. However, Jiang et al 16 reported the most pronounced thinning was temporal. Comparing rates of thinning, Ruiz-Medrano et al 10 used SS-OCT to quantify rates of decline of −2.98 µm/year subfoveally, −2.50 µm/year nasally, and −2.25 µm/year temporally. Temporal choroidal thickness was typically thicker in younger, healthy individuals.18,27

Axial Length

Subfoveal and nasal thinning were associated with axial length elongation. 16 However, 2 studies reported no significant effect of axial length on choroidal thickness.12,27 Heirani et al 12 reported an 8.71 µm reduction in choroidal thickness per diopter of myopia and a 10.25 µm increase per diopter of hyperopia, along with a 13.48 µm decrease in subfoveal choroidal thickness for every 1 mm increase in axial length.

Associations With Systemic and Ocular Pathologies

Subfoveal choroidal thickness in populations with systemic or ocular comorbidities was reported in 4 studies (n = 128) (Table 5). In patients with age-related choroidal atrophy, the lowest reported mean subfoveal choroidal thickness was 96.45 µm in Turkish adults. 4 Cunha et al 21 found that Portuguese adults with Alzheimer disease had a mean subfoveal choroidal thickness of 169.02 µm, significantly lower than age-matched controls, suggesting potential neurovascular involvement. In cases of high myopia (≥ −6.00 diopter), Nishida et al 14 reported subfoveal choroidal thickness values of 172.9 µm in Japanese patients and 113.3 µm in American patients, suggesting both disease-related and population-specific influences on choroidal thinning. Toprak et al 17 examined patients with primary open-angle glaucoma, observing a decline in subfoveal choroidal thickness from 302.87 µm in individuals younger than 70 years to 250.70 µm in those older than 70 years, demonstrating the additive effects of aging and glaucoma on choroidal thinning. Current evidence does not establish directionality of these associations.

Population and Ethnic Variations

Ethnicity significantly influenced choroidal thickness patterns across studies (Figure 2). Okonkwo et al 9 reported a mean choroidal thickness of approximately 265 µm in Black individuals, comparable to values seen in Chinese (262 µm) and Indian populations, but notably thinner than those in Japanese cohorts (354 µm). Similarly, Kim et al 7 found that Korean individuals had a mean choroidal thickness of 307 µm, higher than in Chinese (261 µm) or Western populations (287 µm), yet still lower than in Japanese populations (354 µm). In Iranian participants, choroidal thickness ranged from 330 to 363 µm, slightly above global averages, while also highlighting significant intra-ethnic variability influenced by age and refractive status. 12 Akhtar et al 15 observed that values in Indian adults were approximately 50 µm thinner than those reported in Japanese studies. Chiu et al 27 noted the underrepresentation of healthy Taiwanese individuals in the research, with emerging data suggesting a mean choroidal thickness of around 248 µm, which is among the lower values reported globally.

Sex and Choroidal Thickness

The effect of sex was reported in 8 studies, with 5 reporting sex had no significant impact on choroidal thickness.7,10,12,15,27 Two studies reported higher subfoveal choroidal thickness in men compared with women,13,16 while 1 study reported that menopause caused a decline in choroidal thickness. 12 In addition, 2 studies reported postmenopausal women had reduced choroidal thickness.6,9 As previously reported by Kavroulaki et al, 28 Gattousi et al suggested a hormonal hypothesis, while Okonkwo et al discussed the controversy about the effect of estrogen on choroidal thickness and the likelihood of age being the primary factor causing the thinning.

Choroidal Vascularity and Layer-Specific Changes

The choroidal vascularity index has emerged as a valuable quantitative marker for assessing the balance between vascular (luminal) and stromal compartments of the choroid. In a previous study, the choroidal vascularity index was significantly reduced, suggesting that age-related choroidal thinning may primarily reflect a selective loss of the vascular component. 4 Other studies using SS-OCT and enhanced depth imaging OCT have quantified individual layers more specifically. Choroidal thinning was found to disproportionately affect choroid microvasculature layers, suggesting that microvascular compromise may precede or exceed gross stromal loss in the aging choroid. 23 Similarly, a study using enhanced depth imaging OCT found a positive correlation between choroidal thickness and the thickness of vascularized inner retinal layers, including the ganglion cell and inner plexiform layers, further suggesting a perfusion-dependent process contributing to age-related choroidal thinning. 20 Age-related choroidal thinning predominantly affected Haller’s layer, the large choroidal vessel layer seen on spectral domain-OCT images. 10

Conclusions

This review consolidates emerging evidence that changes in age-related choroidal thickness are progressive and biologically programmed, marked by a predictable decline in choroidal thickness. Drawing on published data, we report thresholds for normal and pathologic thinning (Table 7). Proposed parameters, such as subfoveal choroidal thickness values <200 µm in older nonmyopic adults or thinning rates exceeding population-specific norms, should be viewed as exploratory signals suggested by current observational evidence. These values are not diagnostic thresholds and require validation through standardized, longitudinal studies.

Several mechanisms of choroidal thinning have been proposed, including vascular insufficiency and intrinsic age-related changes. Studies have demonstrated a pattern of choroidal changes characterized by slight choroidal thickness in younger individuals, stabilization in middle age, and significant decline in older adults. 3 Because the choroid is the primary vascular supply for the retina, subtle vascular caliber or density changes can have downstream consequences on retinal health and visual function.4,10 Thinning of specific choroidal layers, particularly Haller’s layer, highlights the vascular contribution to overall choroidal atrophy. 10 Clinically, progressive choroidal thinning may lead to impaired delivery of nutrients, increased retinal vulnerability, and eventual decline in visual function, emphasizing the importance of monitoring choroidal integrity as a part of routine ocular assessments in aging patients.

Change in choroidal thickness is emerging as a valuable clinical biomarker in neovascular AMD (nAMD), with implications for treatment planning and prognosis. Patients with thin choroid demonstrate early anatomic response to antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy at 3 months but show poorer anatomic and visual outcomes at 12 months compared with those with thicker choroid. 29 These findings suggest that a thin choroid may reflect a more degenerative process that is less responsive to anti-VEGF treatment than exudative processes. However, interpreting longitudinal changes in choroidal thickness is complicated by emerging evidence that anti-VEGF agents may themselves accelerate choroidal thinning via potential anti–VEGF-induced choroidal ischemia.30,31 Even so, treatment-naïve patients may be stratified by subfoveal choroidal thickness to help predict treatment burden, with thinner choroid associated with requirement of fewer injections. 32 These insights may help to inform prognosis and optimize therapy for patients with nAMD.

Beyond ocular implications, detection of changes in choroidal thickness may function as a noninvasive biomarker for systemic macrovascular diseases. Studies have shown that choroidal thinning and reduced vascularity correlate with early vascular dysfunction in conditions such as Alzheimer disease, mild cognitive impairment, 33 and coronary artery disease. 34 These observations supplement emerging evidence that retinal and choroidal imaging biomarkers can mirror underlying neurodegenerative and vascular brain pathology, with OCT angiography showing peripapillary and ganglion cell inner plexiform layer alterations in patients with Lewy body dementia. 35

Regional variations in choroidal thickness provide important insights into the progression of age-related choroidal thinning. The subfoveal region consistently demonstrates the greatest thickness, while the nasal quadrant is thinnest.9 –11,18 In Spanish individuals, the most rapid age-related thinning occurs in the central subfovea, while in Chinese individuals, thinning was most pronounced temporally.10,16 These differences highlight possible population-specific regional differences. In general, the rate of age-related thinning is most pronounced in the central subfovea, followed by the nasal and temporal regions. 10 These regional patterns may reflect differential perfusion demand and help explain the susceptibility of choroidal subfields to disease involvement. Recognizing these spatial differences can aid in interpreting OCT findings and identifying early localized atrophy in clinical practices.

Sex was found to have a modest impact on choroidal thickness given mixed significance of results.6,9,12,19,20 Both age and sex or menopausal status have been proposed as possible factors for thinning. Gattoussi et al6,32 proposed a hormonal hypothesis involving estrogen that is supported by studies 36 showing decreased choroidal blood flow after menopause and increased choroidal thickness during pregnancy due to elevated hormone levels. Further investigation is warranted given patterns of choroidal thinning seen in postmenopausal women.6,9

The observed ethnic differences in baseline choroidal thickness and thinning rates reaffirm the importance of population-specific normative values.7,14,24 These variations have direct implications for clinical interpretation of OCT findings and for accurate diagnosis of healthy choroidal thickness changes across diverse populations. Accordingly, we propose clinical recommendations for targeted screening and longitudinal monitoring based on OCT findings and associated ocular and systemic risk factors, and ethnicity-informed clinical recommendations to support risk stratification and minimize diagnostic error (Table 9). Incorporating these benchmarks into practice is essential for distinguishing physiologic aging from pathologic choroidal atrophy in ethnically diverse patient cohorts.

The current literature on changes in age-related choroidal thickness is limited by several methodologic challenges. Most included studies are cross-sectional, restricting the ability to draw causal inferences or track individual progression over time. There is also considerable variability in imaging methodology, including inconsistent use of enhanced depth imaging OCT and SS-OCT and a lack of standardized refractive and axial length adjustments, which are all factors known to influence choroidal thickness measurements. 14 Confounding variables such as diurnal variation, hydration status, and systemic hemodynamic fluctuations are often unaccounted for, further complicating interpretation. Population-specific patterns, such as the more pronounced temporal thinning seen in Chinese cohorts, remain unexplained, as existing studies do not determine whether these differences arise from refractive, anatomic, or methodologic factors.

To address these gaps, future research may prioritize longitudinal, multimodal imaging studies and standardized protocols. The development of ethnically stratified normative databases with prospective longitudinal studies will also enhance diagnostic accuracy, confirm or revise our proposed preliminary thresholds, and help determine directionality between choroidal thinning and disease states. Most available studies examined normal choroidal aging or diseases associated with thinner choroid, while conditions characterized by thicker or atypical choroidal profiles were underrepresented. Studies reporting on choroidal thickness in disease-specific states were also outside the scope of this review. Future work may focus on other disease groups to provide a more complete understanding of the full spectrum of age-related choroidal changes. A deeper understanding of choroidal aging may ultimately reveal early structural biomarkers of systemic vascular dysfunction, enabling preclinical detection and personalized risk assessment.
