Chemotherapy and age stratified survival outcomes in colorectal cancer based on a population analysis from the surveillance epidemiology and end results program 2010 to 2020
Authors: Guoshuai Xu, Wenqiang Li, Nan Yao, Fuzhou Han, Guoyong Yu, Jun Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of chemotherapy in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment, particularly in elderly and very young patients, remains controversial. This study aims to evaluate the survival benefits of chemotherapy across different age groups in CRC patients using real-world data.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study using data from the SEER database from 2010 to 2020. A total of 256,320 CRC patients were included in the final analysis. Chemotherapy status (yes/no) was the primary exposure. Outcomes included overall survival (OS), cause-specific survival, and cumulative incidence of cancer- and non-cancer-specific deaths. Statistical analyses involved multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, and competing risks models, adjusted for key demographic and clinical covariates.ResultsChemotherapy significantly improved OS in patients aged 45 years and older, with the most substantial benefit in those aged 75–84 years (hazard ratios [HR] = 0.54, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.52–0.56) and ≥ 85 years (HR = 0.60, 95% CI = 0.57–0.64). Patients younger than 45 years did not show a significant survival benefit (HR = 1.02, 95% CI = 0.93–1.12). Survival benefit was consistent across tumor locations and most pronounced in stage III (HR = 0.40) and stage IV (HR = 0.39) disease. Competing risks models confirmed reduced cancer-specific mortality in older age groups. Sensitivity analyses excluding early deaths further validated the robustness of these findings.ConclusionChemotherapy conferred a survival benefit in patients aged ≥ 45 years, including the very elderly, when selection was based on functional status rather than age alone, whereas no clear benefit emerged in younger patients. This association persisted after multivariable adjustment and across stage-stratified and sensitivity analyses. Given SEER’s lack of regimen, timing, comorbidity, and targeted-therapy detail, cautious interpretation is warranted.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite improvements in early detection and treatment, the prognosis of CRC patients remains highly variable and is influenced by factors such as patient age, tumor location, cancer stage, and the chosen therapeutic strategies. Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone in the management of advanced CRC, with evidence supporting its role in prolonging survival and improving quality of life [1, 2].

Administering chemotherapy to elderly CRC patients poses distinct clinical challenges due to age-related factors such as increased comorbidities, polypharmacy, reduced organ function, and heightened risk of treatment-related toxicity. These concerns often lead to underutilization of chemotherapy in older adults, despite potential therapeutic benefits. However, a growing body of literature suggests that many elderly patients, particularly those with preserved functional status, can tolerate and benefit from chemotherapy when appropriately assessed and managed [3, 4]. Tools such as comprehensive geriatric assessments (CGA), performance status scoring, and individualized dose modifications have proven valuable in optimizing treatment decisions [5]. As the global population continues to age, addressing age-related disparities in cancer care has become an urgent priority to ensure that elderly patients are not unjustly excluded from potentially life-prolonging therapies [6].

Younger CRC patients, particularly those under the age of 45, represent a distinct subgroup with unique tumor biology and often more aggressive disease patterns. The efficacy of chemotherapy in this group remains unclear, with existing studies offering conflicting conclusions [7, 8]. Understanding the therapeutic value of chemotherapy for young patients is essential for developing age-appropriate treatment protocols. To address these knowledge gaps, we performed a comprehensive, population-based analysis using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database from 2010 to 2020. This study aims to enhance the current understanding of chemotherapy efficacy in CRC and provide evidence to support more individualized and equitable cancer care decisions.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

We used data from the U.S. National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program for 2010–2020. SEER covers ~ 34.6% of the U.S. population and records detailed information on cancer incidence, patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatments, and survival [9]. Authorization to use the SEER database was obtained; see Fig. 1. All analyses adhered to SEER data-use policies.

Flowchart of patient selection from the SEER database (2010–2020)

Patient selection

A total of 393,628 patients diagnosed with CRC were initially identified. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) survival time of zero months or unknown; (2) diagnosis limited to in situ disease (Tis) or T0 stage; (3) presence of other malignant tumors; or (4) diagnosis of multiple primary CRC tumors. After exclusions, 256,320 patients were included in the final analytic cohort.

Exposure and outcome definitions

The primary exposure was receipt of chemotherapy, categorized dichotomously (yes vs. no/unknown). The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow-up (censoring at the vital status date; administrative cutoff December 31, 2021). Secondary outcomes included cause-specific survival and cumulative incidence of cancer-specific versus non-cancer-specific mortality.

The definitions of the covariates

In multivariable analyses, we adjusted for clinically relevant covariates available in SEER: age at diagnosis; sex; race (White, Black, Other); marital status (married, other, unknown); tumor location (right colon, left colon, rectum); American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage (I–IV); surgical intervention (yes/no); carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level (positive, negative, unknown) and radiotherapy. The SEER 2010–2020 research dataset does not provide patient-level genomic or molecular tumor markers for colorectal cancer (e.g., MSI/dMMR, KRAS/NRAS, BRAF, HER2, tumor mutational burden, or germline findings), nor details on chemotherapy regimen, dose intensity, duration, or adherence. Accordingly, models adjust only for the available covariates listed above.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between chemotherapy and non-chemotherapy groups using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier methods were used to estimate overall survival, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for overall survival, adjusting for the aforementioned covariates. Competing risks analyses were performed using both cause-specific hazard models (treating non-cancer deaths as censored) and sub-distribution hazard models (treating them as competing events). The cumulative incidence function (CIF) was used to describe the time-dependent probability of each type of death. During follow-up, non–cancer–related deaths may act as competing events, potentially biasing the accuracy of the outcomes. To address this, we applied both cause-specific (CS) and sub-distribution (SD) competing risks models to adjust for the impact of non-cancer mortality. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, tumor site, cancer stage, and surgery, CEA and race. Interaction terms were used in Cox models to assess effect modification. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Sensitivity analyses

To minimize bias from early mortality unrelated to treatment efficacy, sensitivity analyses excluded patients who died within the first three months after diagnosis. Additional stratified analyses were conducted to evaluate consistency across demographic and clinical subgroups.

Ethical considerations

As the SEER data are publicly available and de-identified, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval and informed consent. The research adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 256,320 CRC patients included in the final analysis, 112,287 (43.8%) received chemotherapy while 144,033 (56.2%) did not. Patients in the chemotherapy group were generally younger (mean age 59.91 ± 12.59 years vs. 66.28 ± 14.46 years), more likely to be male, and more often had advanced-stage disease (Stage III or IV). Statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in marital status, race, tumor location, surgery status, CEA levels, and receipt of radiation (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Chemotherapy and CRC patient survival

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significantly improved OS among patients who received chemotherapy, with a median OS of 84 months compared to 62 months in those who did not receive chemotherapy (log-rank p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). Consistent with these findings, the competing risks analysis using CIF further confirmed the protective effect of chemotherapy on patient prognosis (Fig. 2B).

Survival outcomes of colorectal cancer patients by chemotherapy status. (A) Kaplan–Meier survival curves; (B) Cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves

Cox regression models confirmed the survival advantage associated with chemotherapy, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.61 (95% CI: 0.60–0.62, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

The protective effect of chemotherapy remained statistically significant across all examined subgroups, including sex, tumor location, cancer stage, surgical status, CEA levels, and race, indicating the broad and consistent efficacy of chemotherapy in diverse patient populations (Fig. 3).

Stratified analysis by age

Chemotherapy conferred a significant survival benefit in patients aged ≥ 45 years, particularly those aged 75–84 years (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.52–0.56) and ≥ 85 years (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.52–0.59). However, in patients < 45 years old, no significant improvement in survival was observed (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.05–1.26), indicating a lack of benefit in this subgroup (Table 2).

Discussion

This population-based study provides robust evidence supporting the survival benefits of chemotherapy in CRC patient. Notably, the greatest improvements in survival were observed among elderly patients, including those aged 75–84 and ≥ 85 years. These findings challenge traditional concerns about overtreatment and toxicity in older adults and suggest that age alone should not be a limiting factor in the administration of chemotherapy.

Our results show that elderly patients with CRC—especially those aged 75 and above—not only benefit from chemotherapy in terms of survival but are also capable of tolerating treatment when carefully selected. Traditionally, oncologists have approached systemic therapy in older adults with considerable caution, largely due to concerns about frailty, comorbid conditions, and heightened sensitivity to toxicity [10, 11]. The ongoing undertreatment of older adults stems in large part from the mistaken assumption that age equates to physiological decline [12]. Although older adults often present with comorbidities, many maintain adequate functional status and tolerate chemotherapy well, especially when supported with appropriate dose adjustments and side-effect management strategies [12, 13].

This conflation often leads to therapeutic inertia, depriving many patients of potentially beneficial interventions. Compounding this issue is the historical underrepresentation of older patients in randomized controlled trials [14], leaving clinicians with limited age-specific evidence to guide decisions and often reinforcing overly cautious approaches. In practice, however, the older population is far from homogenous. While some individuals in their 80s maintain robust physical and cognitive function, others in their 60s may present with considerable frailty. The Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) has become an essential tool for distinguishing between these patient profiles [12]. By evaluating multiple domains—including functional ability, comorbidity, cognition, nutrition, emotional health, and social circumstances—CGA facilitates a more accurate assessment of biological age and enables clinicians to tailor treatment strategies accordingly. In addition to CGA, several validated instruments—such as the Cancer and Aging Research Group (CARG) chemotherapy toxicity tool, the G8 screening questionnaire, and the Vulnerable Elders Survey-13 (VES-13)—are being increasingly integrated into routine oncology practice [10]. These tools help anticipate treatment tolerance and guide therapy choices, supporting a more personalized and evidence-based approach to care in older adults. Notably, our findings align with data from observational studies and prospective cohorts showing that older patients, when adequately supported through interventions like prehabilitation and early symptom management, can experience outcomes comparable to their younger counterparts [15]. In fact, the most pronounced survival benefit in our analysis was observed among patients aged 85 and older—an often-overlooked group—suggesting that fit individuals in this demographic may derive substantial gains from chemotherapy.

In contrast, our analysis found no significant survival benefit from chemotherapy in patients under 45 years of age. This unexpected finding warrants further investigation. Several potential explanations exist, including the possibility that younger patients may have distinct tumor biology—such as a higher prevalence of hereditary syndromes (e.g., Lynch syndrome) or microsatellite instability—that influences treatment response [7, 16]. Additionally, younger patients may receive more aggressive multimodal therapies, potentially diminishing the relative effect of chemotherapy alone [17]. The heterogeneity and relatively small sample size in this subgroup could also contribute to the observed lack of benefit. Future research should focus on molecular profiling and treatment stratification tailored to young-onset CRC [18]. Crucially, the absence of a statistically significant association in this registry should not be interpreted as a rationale to withhold systemic therapy from very young patients. Early-onset CRC differs in case mix (e.g., rectal predominance; mucinous/signet-ring histology) and may harbor molecular features not captured by SEER (e.g., MSI/dMMR, RAS/RAF, HER2 alterations, germline predisposition). Treatment decisions should remain individualized and guideline-concordant, integrating stage, operative findings, patient goals and tolerance, and molecular profiling. These findings are hypothesis-generating and underscore the need for datasets with detailed regimen, timing, dose intensity, adherence, toxicity, and genomic information before firm therapeutic recommendations can be made for this subgroup.

Our results confirm that chemotherapy provides substantial survival advantages across tumor locations, including right-sided, left-sided, and rectal cancers, with slightly greater effects observed in left colon cancers. This aligns with prior studies suggesting that tumor sidedness reflects underlying molecular differences affecting treatment sensitivity [19, 20]. Right-sided tumors are often associated with worse prognosis and more frequent MSI-H or BRAF mutations, which may modify the effect of standard chemotherapy [21, 22].

This study has several strengths—including a large sample, comprehensive age stratification, and advanced methods (e.g., competing-risks analysis)—that provide real-world insight into chemotherapy outcomes across the CRC spectrum. Nonetheless, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, registry-based design may introduce selection bias, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., performance status, comorbidity burden) remains possible. Second, treatment granularity in SEER is limited: the database does not distinguish neoadjuvant, adjuvant, or palliative/first-line chemotherapy; does not capture regimen, dose intensity, duration, toxicities, or adherence; and provides only coarse surgical detail (procedure type/extent, curative vs. palliative intent, emergency vs. elective status, margin status, perioperative complications). Third, SEER does not capture reasons for non-receipt of chemotherapy (e.g., patient preference, pregnancy/fertility considerations, toxicities/contraindications, postoperative complications, clinical-trial allocation to non-cytotoxic strategies, or social/coverage barriers); therefore, treatment intent or appropriateness cannot be inferred from registry codes. Fourth, SEER lacks patient-level genomic and molecular tumor markers, which limits exploratory assessments of biology-driven heterogeneity—particularly in younger patients—though this does not affect ascertainment of the main exposure and outcome. Finally, despite adjustment for available demographic and tumor variables (including stage, surgery category, CEA, radiotherapy, and calendar year), findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Future work—ideally using linked registry–claims–EHR data or prospective cohorts with richer treatment and tumor-biology detail—will be important to validate and refine these observations [18, 23–25].

Conclusion

Chemotherapy improved OS in CRC patients aged ≥ 45 years, including the very elderly, when guided by functional status rather than age, with no clear benefit in younger patients. The effect was robust across adjusted and sensitivity analyses. Given SEER’s lack of regimen, timing, comorbidity, and targeted/immunotherapy data, findings should be interpreted cautiously. Treatment decisions should integrate functional status, tumor biology, and sociodemographic context.
