Improving the average dilation of a metric graph by adding edges
Sariel Har-Peled, Eliot W. Robson

Abstract
For a graph G G spanning a metric space, the dilation of a pair of points is the ratio of their distance in the shortest path graph metric to their distance in the metric space. Given a graph G G and a budget k k , a classic problem is to augment G G with k k additional edges to reduce the maximum dilation [ FGG05 , GW21 ] . In this note, we consider a variant of this problem where the goal is to reduce the average dilation for pairs of points in G G . We provide an O ⁡ ( k ) O(k) approximation algorithm for this problem, matching the approximation ratio given by [ GW21 ] for the maximum dilation variant.

Introduction

We consider a weighted, undirected graph
G=(V,E)
and a metric
space
\mathcal{X}=(X,d_{\mathcal{X}{}})
such that
V=X
, so both

G
and
\mathcal{X}
are defined over the same ground set.
For two points

u,v\in X
,
d_{\mathcal{X}{}}(u,v)
is the distance between
u
and
v
as
measured in the metric space
\mathcal{X}
, and
d_{G}(u,v)
is the distance
between
u
and
v
in the shortest path metric of
G
(i.e. only traversing edges of
G
). Unless otherwise stated, we will assume that

G
is connected. For any edge
e=(u,v)\in E
, we require that

w(e)=d_{\mathcal{X}{}}(u,v)
(the weight of edges in the graph is
taken from the underlying metric space
\mathcal{X}
).

1.1
Basic definitions

Definition 1.1
.

For a pair of vertices
u,v\in V
in a graph
G
over an
underlying metric space
\mathcal{X}
, the
dilation
of
the pair is

s_{G}(u,v)=\frac{d_{G}(u,v)}{d_{\mathcal{X}{}}(u,v)}.

Definition 1.2
.

The
average dilation
of a graph
G
with
n
vertices is

s_{\avg}(G)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V}s_{G}(u,v).

In this note, we study the following problem.

Problem 1.3
.

Given a positive integer
k
and a graph

G=(V,E)
over a metric space
\mathcal{X}
, compute a set
F

of
k
edges realizing

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|{F}\right|=k\\
F\subseteq V\times V\end{subarray}}s_{\avg}(G\cup F)=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left|{F}\right|=k\\
F\subseteq V\times V\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\sum_{u,v\in V}s_{G\cup F}(u,v).

1.2
Additional definitions

We state some definitions specific to our main result.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
F
be a set of edges used to augment some graph
G=(V,E)
,
and let
V_{F}\subset V
be the set of endpoints of edges of

F
. Define the function

\delta_{F}:V\times V\rightarrow(V_{F}\times V_{F})\cup\emptyset
such that, for vertices
a,b\in V
,
\delta_{F}(a,b)

returns a tuple
(v_{a},v_{b})
where
v_{a}
is the first visited
endpoint of the first edge of
F
in the shortest path between
a

and
b
in
G\cup F
, and
v_{b}
is the last visited endpoint of
the last edge of
F
in the same shortest path. If no edges from

F
are visited in the shortest path, then

\delta_{F}(a,b)=\emptyset
. The quantity
\delta_{F}(a,b)
is
the
signature
of
(a,b)
in
F
.

The function
\delta_{F}
has
O(\left|{F}\right|^{2})=O(k^{2})
distinct
values it might return.

Definition 1.5
.

With respect to the average dilation function
s_{\avg}
and graph

G
, the
benefit
of a set
F
is

B(F)=s_{\avg}(G)-s_{\avg}(G\cup F)=\sum_{u,v\in V}b_{F}(u,v)
, where
b_{F}(u,v)=s_{G}(u,v)-s_{G\cup F}(u,v)
. Similarly, the
restricted benefit
for
a,b\in V

is the quantity

B|_{(a,b)}(F)=\sum_{u,v\in V:\,\delta_{F}(u,v)=(a,b)}b_{F}(u,v)
.

The restricted benefit is the contribution from a single signature to the total benefit in the optimal solution. From this, the following
observation is immediate.

Observation 1.6
.

For some graph
G=(V,E)
, we let
P
be the set of all pairs of
distinct vertices in
V(F)
. Then, we have

B(F)=\sum_{(a,b)\in P}B|_{(a,b)}(F)
.

1.3
Our contribution

We analyze the greedy algorithm for placing
k
additional edges. Assume the algorithm already computed a set of
i-1
shortcut edges
F_{i-1}
.
In the
i
th iteration, the algorithm picks the edge
e_{i}
that
maximizes
B(F_{i-1}\cup\left\{{e_{i}}\right\})
. The algorithm then sets

F_{i}=F_{i-1}\cup\left\{{e_{i}}\right\}
(here
F_{0}=\emptyset
).

Our main result is that the output of this algorithm is an
O(k)

approximation in the benefit for
Problem
1.3
.

Theorem 1.7
.

After
k
steps, we have

B(F_{k})\geq B(F^{*})/8k.

After
4k^{2}
steps, we have

B(F_{4k^{2}})\geq B(F^{*})/2.

3
Approximation ratio of greedy algorithm

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S
be any set of shortcut edges, and
F^{*}
be the optimal set
of
k
shortcuts. Then, either
B(S)\geq B(F^{*})/2
, or there
exists an edge
e
, such that
B(S+e)\geq B(S)+B(F^{*})/8k^{2}
.

Proof:

Assume
B(S)<B(F^{*})/2
. Observe that

B(S\cup F^{*})\geq B(F^{*})
. The are
k=|F^{*}|
edges, and they
have
2k
endpoints, and thus overall there are

2k(2k-1)+1\leq 4k^{2}
signatures. One of the signatures, in
relation to
S
, must have benefit
\geq(B(S\cup F^{*})-B(S))/(4k^{2})
,
as the total benefits of all the signatures exceeds
B(S\cup F^{*})
. Thus, for the edge
e
maximizing
B(S\cup e)
, we have

B(S\cup e)\geq B(S)+\frac{B(S\cup F^{*})-B(S)}{4k^{2}}\geq B(S)+\frac{B(F^{*})-B(F^{*})/2}{4k^{2}}\geq B(S)+\frac{B(F^{*})}{8k^{2}}.

Restatement of
Theorem
1.7
.

After
k
steps, we have

B(F_{k})\geq B(F^{*})/8k.

After
4k^{2}
steps, we have

B(F_{4k^{2}})\geq B(F^{*})/2.

Proof:

After
k
steps of the greedy algorithm, we have by
Lemma
3.1
that

B(F_{k})\geq B(F_{k-1})+\frac{B(F^{*})}{8k^{2}}\geq\cdots\geq B(F_{0})+k\frac{B(F^{*})}{8k^{2}}=0+\frac{B(F^{*})}{8k}.

The second part follows by similar argumentation.
