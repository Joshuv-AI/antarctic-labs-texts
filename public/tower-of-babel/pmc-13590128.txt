Exploring the Anticancer Potential of Novel Piperidine‐Embedded Isoxazol–Triazole Conjugates Against MCF‐7 Human Breast Adenocarcinoma Cell Line: Design, Synthesis, In Silico, and In Vitro Investigations
Authors: Jay R Ghonia, Jigisha Parikh, Bharatkumar Z Dholakiya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer remains a major global health challenge, particularly as several tumors develop resistance to current therapies. Although doxorubicin is widely used to treat various cancers, its effectiveness is often limited by adverse effects, including cardiotoxicity and nephrotoxicity, which restrict its dosing. In search of safer and more effective options, a series of novel piperidine–embedded isoxazol–triazole conjugates (6a‐6o) were designed, synthesized, and biologically evaluated against the MCF‐7 cell line in this study. The synthesized compounds 6a‐6o were characterized using FTIR, HRMS, and 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy analysis to confirm their structure and verify successful synthesis. The GI50 (Growth Inhibition 50%) values of synthesized compounds 6a‐6o were determined using the SRB assay. Among all synthesized compounds, 6m exhibited the most potent antiproliferative activity against MCF‐7 cells, having GI50 <10 µg/mL (SI >5), comparable to adriamycin (doxorubicin, GI50 <10 µg/mL). Overall, 6m is a novel anticancer agent with promising abilities against MCF‐7 human breast cancer. The results support the compounds relevance as a biologically active agent with effective proliferation‐suppressive properties.Keywords: anti‐breast cancer activity, in silico evaluation, isoxazol, piperidine, triazole

Introduction

Cancer remains the second most prominent cause of mortality despite significant advances in research and treatment and poses a serious risk to global health [1, 2], which is caused by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. Distinct normal cells, cancer cells proliferate uncontrollably, occupy surrounding tissues, and can metastasize to distant organs [3]. Targeted treatments for cancer treatments can carry toxicity risks, drug resistance, and reduced effectiveness over time [4]. The development of anti‐cancer agents that are effective and have the least side effects is still a most important issue in modern medicine, typically because tumor cells become resistant to many treatments [5]. Several cancers, including breast cancer, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, and malignant lymphoma, generally use doxorubicin (Adriamycin, DOX) hydrochloride, an effective chemotherapeutic agent. However, its continued clinical use causes severe adverse effects such as cardiotoxicity, neurotoxicity, and nephrotoxicity [6, 7, 8]. 5‐Fluorouracil use also has side effects, including diarrhea, vomiting, mucositis, nausea, neutropenia, and hand‐foot syndrome that significantly affect a patient [9, 10].

Heterocyclic compounds are essential in developing potent anticancer drugs because of their structural diversity, which makes them fundamental scaffolds in drug development. Their presence in several FDA‐approved treatments highlights their capability to overcome resistance and advance the effectiveness of cancer treatment [11, 12]. Many medicines were originally made directly from medicinal plants; they are now frequently made using chemical synthesis. The majority of medicines function by interacting with particular biological targets referred to as macromolecules in the body. Usually, this interaction alters the way a certain cell component works, triggering a series of physiological and biochemical processes that result in the drug's desired outcome [13]. The integration of computational approaches has emerged as a valuable strategy in heterocyclic drug design, providing valuable insights into molecular structure, electronic properties, and intermolecular interactions, thereby supporting the rational design and optimization of novel therapeutic agents [14, 15].

Nitrogen‐containing heterocyclic rings have gained consideration for their bioactive characteristic, resulting in several applications in pharmaceuticals and the agriculture sector [16]. The various biological activities of isoxazole rings, including anticancer, neuroprotective, anti‐obesity, antidiabetic, antidepressant, and anti‐inflammatory properties, have recently attracted significant interest. Additionally, research demonstrates that adding isoxazole groups to synthetic or natural molecules can increase their anticancer potential [17]. The development of anticancer drugs has benefited greatly from isoxazole‐based molecules. Valdecoxib, leflunomide, sulfamethoxazole, and tivozanib are among isoxazole‐containing approved drugs that exhibit their therapeutic usefulness [18].

1,2,3‐triazoles have also grown in importance in medicinal chemistry because of their many uses and simple synthesis through CuAAC “click” reactions. This approach provides high yield and selectivity, which makes it potential to synthesize 1,4‐disubstituted triazoles. These substances display diverse biological functions, and their therapeutic potential is often improved by adding the triazole ring to another pharmacophore [19]. Triazole systems exhibit distinctive electronic properties and molecular interactions that can influence their reactivity and biological activity [20].

Piperidine is a six‐membered nitrogen‐containing heterocyclic compound that is frequently employed as a building block in medicinal chemistry. Remarkably, piperidine derivatives are considered “privileged scaffolds” in drug discovery as they are found in over 20 therapeutic classes, comprising anticancer drugs, antipsychotic drugs, etc., emphasizing their significant influence on contemporary medicine [21]. Piperidine‐based compounds exhibit anticancer activity through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, which regulates cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, and apoptosis [22]. The development of novel analogues through the molecular hybridization technique relies on the strategic combination of specific moieties. Thus, researchers attempt to increase biological activity by altering or merging functional groups within existing or novel chemical entities [28]. In this study, the rational design, synthesis, and biological assessment of a series of novel piperidine–embedded isoxazol–triazole conjugates is described. These conjugates 6a‐6o were evaluated for their antiproliferative activity against MCF‐7, a breast cancer cell line, using the SRB assay. The MCF‐7 cell line was selected as the in vitro model since it is broadly used in cytotoxicity screening due to its significance in hormone receptor‐positive breast cancer. The results of this study offer valuable insights into the potential of these conjugates as anticancer candidates, with some conjugates exhibiting significant cytotoxic activity.

Experimental

Materials

All chemicals used for these experiments were of commercial or analytical grade and were employed without additional purification, unless otherwise stated. All materials employed for the current study were procured from SRL, Finar, Merck, Sigma‐Aldrich, BLD Pharma, and Loba and were utilized as procured. The instruments and materials used for data acquisition and analysis are described below. Infrared (IR) spectra of compounds were recorded using the JASCO model spectrophotometer, and the values are given in cm− 1. 1H, 13C, and 19F NMR spectra were obtained using a 500 MHz Bruker spectrometer on a JEOL JNM/ECZ 500R/S1. Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) was applied to monitor reaction progression on pre‐coated Merck 60 F254 silica gel sheets, visualized under UV light and an iodine chamber. High‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) measurements were performed using a Waters Xevo G2‐XS QToF4k system with electrospray ionization (ESI) technique.

Methods

Synthesis of 5‐Fluoro‐3‐(1‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yl)piperidin‐4‐yl)benzo[d]isoxazole (2)

The intermediate 2 was synthesized using a well‐known nucleophilic substitution reaction, where the compound 5‐fluoro‐3‐(piperidin‐4‐yl)benzo[d]isoxazole (1) reacted with propargyl bromide. The process for the synthesis of compound 2 was thoroughly optimized by screening different reaction parameters, including catalyst, temperature, solvent, and reaction time. The outcomes of these optimizations are summarized in Table 1. Initially, 5 mmol of compound 1 was dissolved in 50 mL acetone, and then 10 mmol of potassium tert‐butoxide as a catalyst (KOtBu) was added with constant stirring at room temperature. After that, the reaction mixture was constantly stirred while propargyl bromide (6 mmol, 80% solution in toluene) was being added dropwise. The reaction mixture was then allowed to continue running at 60°C for 45 min. Reaction monitoring was carried out by TLC analysis and confirmed to be a successful conversion to the intermediate 2. Once it was finished, the resulting mixture was allowed to cool and then added to cold water. Intermediate 2 was then extracted thrice using ethyl acetate solvent. To remove inorganic contaminants, the organic layer was carefully washed with water. The organic layer was separated and dried with the help of anhydrous sodium sulfate. Then, after the solvent was removed, the resulting intermediate 2 was dried and collected as a pale‐yellow solid. The intermediate 2 was purified using column chromatography.

Synthesis of 2‑Chloro‐N‐phenylacetamide Intermediates (4a‐4o)

The intermediates 4a‐4o were successfully synthesized using a nucleophilic substitution reaction, where the aniline derivatives reacted with chloroacetyl chloride. For that, ten mmol of aniline derivatives (3a‐3o) were dissolved in 50 mL acetone. Then 15 mmol of chloroacetyl chloride was added dropwise in an ice bath, and was allowed to proceed further at room temperature for 3–5 h. Reaction progress was monitored by TLC analysis, confirming successful conversion to the 2‑chloro‐N‐phenylacetamide intermediate. After completion, the precipitate of intermediate 4a‐4o was collected by vacuum filtration, washed carefully with water, and dried at 40°C, which were then used directly in the further reaction without additional purification.

Synthesis of 2‐Azido‐N‐phenylacetamide Intermediates (5a‐5o)

The azide derivatives 5a‐5o were synthesized through a nucleophilic substitution reaction by treating the intermediate 4a‐4o with sodium azide. For that, 10 mmol of intermediates 4a‐4o were reacted with 30 mmol of sodium azide in dry 15 mL DMF at room temperature for 24 h. Progression in reaction was monitored by TLC analysis, which confirmed complete conversion to the azide intermediates. Upon completion, the resulting mixture was poured onto ice‐cold water, and the obtained solids of intermediate 5a‐5o were filtered under vacuum and used directly in subsequent reactions without additional purification.

Synthesis of Piperidine Embedded Isoxazol–Triazole Conjugates (6a‐6o)

Piperidine‐embedded isoxazol–triazole conjugates (6a‐6o) were synthesized via the copper(I)‐catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC), also known as the click reaction. Initially, 0.4 mmol of intermediate 2 was added to a reaction vessel having 5 mL of DMF: n‐Butanol: H2O in a ratio of 1:1:1. After that, sodium ascorbate and copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O) were added to this solution in catalytic amounts. At ambient temperature, 0.4 mmol of intermediate 4a‐4o was introduced into the reaction system. It was continuously stirred at ambient temperature for 24–30 h. Progress in the reaction was monitored by TLC analysis, which confirmed complete conversion to the final product. Once the reaction process was finished, the conjugates 6a‐6o were precipitated by pouring the reaction mixture onto ice‐cold water and quenched with an aqueous ammonium solution. The resulting solid compounds were filtered, extensively washed using water and hexane to remove any unwanted contaminants, and then subsequently dried to yield pure compounds 6a–6o. The obtained final conjugates 6a–6o were further purified by silica gel column chromatography to make the conjugates in a highly pure form for further analysis.

Molecular Docking

The synthesized conjugates 6a–6o were evaluated for their anti‐breast cancer potential through molecular docking analysis against the human estrogen receptor alpha (ERα, PDB ID: 3ERT) to examine their potential binding interactions. The 3D structure of the ERα protein was initially taken from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [29, 30]. This docking simulation was carried out using AutoDock Vina 1.5.7 software, which predicted the binding affinity of synthesized conjugates to the target proteins, providing their capability to suppress estrogen signaling and tumor growth in ER+ breast cancer [31]. Initially, all water molecules as well as heteroatoms were removed from the structure of the protein using AutoDock before the docking simulation. In addition, the addition of polar hydrogen atoms to the protein structure enabled refining the accuracy of the interaction. After that, Kollman charges as well as torsion angles were introduced to the structure of the protein. The ligands were then prepared using Gaussian and AutoDock Vina. Structures of all fifteen synthesized compounds were converted to .mol files. The energy minimization of structures was done using Gaussian prior to molecular docking studies to ensure their accuracy for simulation. The obtained structures of ligands were then converted into the PDB format using GaussView 6.0.16. PDB files of the protein and ligands were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock. The docking was carried out with an exhaustiveness level of 24. To carry out site‐specific docking at the active site, the dimensions of the grid box were set to 40 Å × 40 Å × 40 Å, with center coordinates including X = 27.432317, Y = −2.033175, and Z = 26.269143. The conformers that bound best were taken. Discovery Studio Visualizer 2025 was used to display the visualization of protein‐ligand interactions.

Cytotoxicity Assay

The in vitro cytotoxic activity of the synthesized conjugates 6a‐6o against MCF‐7 human breast adenocarcinoma cell line (RRID: CVCL_0031) was estimated using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) colorimetric assay [37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. The cell culture and subsequent experimental analyses were carried out at the Anti‐Cancer Drug Screening Facility, Tata Memorial Centre, [Mumbai, India], following the standard protocols established by the facility. The cell lines were grown in a suitable medium having two mM L‐glutamine and 10% fetal bovine serum. 100 µL of 5000 cells/well were injected onto 96‐well microtiter plates for this screening experiment. Prior to addition of synthesized hybrid, the microtiter plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C, 95% air, 5% CO2, and 100% relative humidity following cell inoculation. The experimental compounds were dissolved in the proper solvent at a concentration of 100 mg/mL, diluted with water to 1 mg/mL, and then stored frozen prior to use. An aliquot of the frozen concentrate (1 mg/mL) was thawed and then diluted in complete medium containing the test substance at the time of compound addition to final concentrations of 100, 200, 400, and 800 µg/mL. The relevant microtiter wells containing 90 µL of medium were then filled with aliquots of 10 µL of these various compound dilutions. This gave the necessary final compound concentrations, which were 10 µg/mL, 20, 40, and 80 µg/mL. After the compounds were added, the plates were incubated under standard conditions for 48 h, and the assay was terminated with the adding cold trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Cells were fixed in situ by gently adding 50 µl of cold 30 % (w/v) TCA (10 % TCA, final concentration). The plates were then incubated at 4°C for 1 h. After incubation, the supernatant was carefully discarded, and the plates were washed five times using tap water and then air‐dried. Next, 50 µL of a 0.4% w/v sulforhodamine B (SRB) solution, prepared in 1% acetic acid, was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 20 min at room temperature to allow staining. Excess dye was then removed, and the wells were washed five times with 1% acetic acid to eliminate any unbound dye. The plates were air‐dried. The bound stain was eluted with trizma base (10 mM), and the absorbance was measured on a plate reader at a 540 nm wavelength, using 690 nm as the reference wavelength.

The percentage growth for test wells compared to control wells was computed plate‐by‐plate. The percentage growth was calculated as the test wells average absorbance divided by the control wells average absorbance *100. Using six absorbance measurements—namely, the baseline measurement at time zero (Tz), control cell growth without drug exposure (C), and test cell growth in the presence of the drug at four distinct concentrations (Ti). A growth inhibition of 50% (GI50) drug concentration causes a 50% decrease in the net protein growth in control cells throughout the drug incubation period, as shown by SRB staining. If the activity level was reached, the values were computed for each of these three parameters; if the effect was not attained or exceeded, the values for that parameter were stated as either higher or lower than the lowest or highest concentration tested. The images of the sample tested (the highest drug concentration used) were captured using a Nikon Ti‐S Inverted Research Microscope with a magnification of × 20 and Eclipse Image Processing software NIS‐Elements.

To further evaluate the anticancer potential and selectivity of the synthesized conjugates, the most active compounds, particularly 6m and 6j, were also evaluated against a noncancerous human cell line, human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293, RRID: CVCL_0045, [42]), at CellTech Life Sciences, [Surat, India], following the standard protocols established by the facility. The GI50 values obtained for the cancerous MCF‐7 cells were compared with those obtained for the normal HEK293 cells. The Selectivity Index (SI) was calculated as the ratio of the GI50 value against the normal cell line to that against the cancer cell line [SI = GI50 (normal cells)/GI50 (cancer cells)]. A higher SI value indicates preferential growth inhibition of cancer cells over normal cells. These results provide additional evidence regarding the selectivity and potential safety profile of the synthesized conjugates.

Results and Discussion

Rational Design of Compounds

Triazole motifs are found in several drug molecules and are considered a good strategy to enhance their effectiveness compared to their parent molecules. It is highly stable in physiological conditions, show useful interactions with targets, and have low toxicity [43]. Whereas, isoxazole‐containing molecules are found in several FDA‐approved drugs, giving the isoxazole motif importance in modern medicinal chemistry [44]. Another, piperidine‐based drugs are showing strong promise in cancer treatment due to their ability to enhance the effectiveness, stability, and selectivity of small‐molecule therapeutics. These compounds help drugs better target cancer cells while minimizing harm to healthy tissues. Since 2017, ten such drugs have received FDA approval, with many more in development. Their success highlights the growing importance of piperidine as a core structure in next‐generation anticancer therapies [45]. Piperidine‐containing drug raloxifene, shown in Figure 1, is approved to prevent and treat osteoporosis, and it shows potential to prevent breast cancer [46]. Deepika Dwarakanath et al. synthesized an isoxazole‐ piperidine‐tethered derivative. Out of them, the compound containing a nitro group at the para position showed IC50 = 35.57 µM against MCF‑7 cells compared to a standard doxorubicin having IC50 = 0.93 µM after 72 h of incubation, highlighting the importance of the piperidine and isoxazole moieties in imparting considerable anticancer activity [47]. Incorporation of the amide group has also become an important feature in modern anticancer drug design due to its ability to enhance the overall therapeutic behavior of small‐molecule compounds. Their flexible structure enables them to participate in key interactions with biological targets, often improving both affinity and selectivity. However, amides also contribute to the adjustment of physicochemical characteristics, such as metabolic stability, molecular rigidity, and solubility, which are essential for achieving adequate drug levels in the body. Consequently, the majority of recently developed anticancer agents containing amide linkages reflect their value as a reliable structural constituent in contemporary anticancer molecule design [48, 49]. The literature has also highlighted the use of compounds bearing benzoisoxazole, amide bonds, and fluorine substitutions, all of which can impact both the chemical properties and biological activity of compounds. The incorporation of fluorine as a hydrogen bioisostere can alter the electronic and physicochemical properties of the molecule, consequently influencing its biological activity [50, 51]. Based on the well‐known pharmacological relevance of isoxazole, triazole, and piperidine scaffolds, integrating these three motifs into a single hybrid molecular framework containing an amide linkage, as shown in Figure 2, can improve overall biological activity.

Chemistry

The synthesis of novel conjugates 6a–6o was carried out through a meticulously strategic sequence of designed reactions to explore their potential anticancer properties, as shown in Scheme 1. The process began with the intermediate 2 formation, having triple bond functionality required for the synthesis of the triazole nucleus. This was done using the reaction of compound 1 with propargyl bromide to yield intermediate 2 by a nucleophilic substitution process. This synthesis was optimized for reaction parameters, including different bases, solvents, and reaction times, as outlined in Table 1. The incorporation of KOtBu as a base, along with the acetone solvent, afforded the preferred product in good yield, at 89 %. The FT‐IR spectrum shows a characteristic peak of alkynes at 2123 cm− 1, while the 1H NMR signal at 2.28 ppm (t, alkynes) and the 1 3C NMR signal at 73.39 and 78.83 ppm (alkynes) confirmed the successful synthesis of intermediate 2. Furthermore, the HRMS spectrum of intermediate 2 shows an [M+H]+ ion peak at m/z 259.1259, further confirming the successful synthesis of intermediate 2.

The synthesis pathway for a series of novel piperidine–embedded isoxazol–triazole conjugates 6a‐6o.

The synthesis of the final conjugates 6a‐6o was successfully achieved through the CuAAC reaction, a widely recognized click reaction as shown in Scheme 1. For that, the required intermediates 4a‐4o and 5a‐5o were synthesized using a nucleophilic substitution reaction. These intermediates 5a‐5o were then reacted with intermediate 2 using the CuAAC approach, assisted by sodium ascorbate and a copper catalyst. The structures of conjugates 6a‐6o were thoroughly confirmed using various analytical techniques, including FT‐IR, mass spectrometry, and 1H, 1 3C, and 19F NMR, all of which provided clear evidence of their successful conversion. In the FT‐IR spectra, the complete disappearance of the alkynes stretching band near 2123 cm− 1 confirmed full consumption of intermediate 2. The 1H NMR spectrum displayed a singlet at around δ 10.44 and δ 8.01 ppm, corresponding to the N‐H and C‐H (triazole) protons, respectively. The 1H NMR also exhibited signals around δ 5.28 ppm, indicative of ─CH2─ groups linking the triazole to the acetamide portions, with corresponding peaks in the 1 3C NMR at δ 53.32 ppm. Further aliphatic and aromatic protons were aligned with the expected substitution pattern, supporting the structural integrity of the synthesized molecules. The 1 3C NMR spectra showed the triazole carbon signals at δ 143.83 ppm and 126.05 ppm. HRMS spectrometry further validated the successful synthesis of the conjugates 6a‐6o. Collectively, the FT‐IR, mass spectrometry, and 1H, 13C, and 19F NMR data validated the successful synthesis and structural integrity of novel conjugates 6a‐6o.

Molecular Docking Analysis

Pattanayak et al. reported that incorporation of a CF3 group into an isoxazole scaffold enhanced ERα binding through favorable hydrophobic and hydrogen‐bonding interactions, resulting in improved docking and binding energies compared with the non‐CF3 analog. These findings highlight the role of electronic substituent effects in modulating ligand–receptor interactions and binding affinity, ultimately contributing to improved anticancer activity against MCF‐7 cells [52]. The molecular docking analysis of the synthesized hybrid compounds 6a–6o, which contain fluorine as a hydrogen bioisostere and adriamycin (doxorubicin, DOX), was carried out against ERα (PDB ID: 3ERT). It revealed consistently strong binding affinities which surpassed the reference drug DOX (−8.7 kcal/mol), suggesting strong potential for anti‐breast cancer activity. In the active sites, the compounds 6a–6o showed strong binding interactions, including van der Waals, hydrogen bonds, halogen, Pi‐anion, Pi‐Sigma, Pi‐Sulfur, etc. The 2D graphical visualizations of binding interactions of the compounds 6a‐6j and DOX with the protein are presented in Figures 3 and 4. As summarized in Table 2, the majority of compounds clustered around docking scores between −9.1 and −10.2, reflecting a consistent optimization of the pharmacophore scaffold and highlighting the robustness of the design strategy to enhance ligand–receptor interactions. Conjugates including 6i (−10.2 kcal/mol), 6g (−10.1 kcal/mol), 6l (−10.0 kcal/mol), and 6a, 6m, and 6n (−9.8 kcal/mol) appeared as the most potent binders, signifying that their structural features conferred superior stabilization within the active site through favorable interactions. The most potent conjugate 6i was stabilized through multiple binding interactions, including hydrogen bonds with TRP383 and ASP351, Pi‐Anion bonds with GLU353, Pi‐Sigma bonds with LEU525, Pi‐Sulfur bonds with MET522, MET343, Amide‐Pi Stacked bonds with LEU346, and alkyl and Pi‐Alkyl bonds with LEU387, LEU349, LEU391, and ALA350. In contrast, conjugates including 6b (−9.1 kcal/mol), 6f (−9.3 kcal/mol), and 6o (−9.2 kcal/mol) displayed slightly weaker binding, which may be attributed to less favorable electronic distribution, thereby dropping their interactions with the binding pocket. The higher docking scores of 6a‐6o suggest that these conjugates could display improved cytotoxicity. Overall, the results highlight the promise of hybrid design in structure‐based drug discovery, offering a path to develop more potent and selective anticancer agents and reinforcing the importance of integrating in silico docking with in vitro cytotoxicity assays to accelerate the identification of effective therapeutic candidates. Additional experimental validation, including in vitro studies, is necessary to confirm their anticancer activity and therapeutic potential for further studies.

The TPSA values of the synthesized conjugates 6a‐6o ranged from 89.08 to 134.90 Å2, indicating differences in molecular polarity arising from structural modification. Compounds 6h‐6j and 6o exhibited the highest TPSA having values 134.90 Å2 and 116.77 Å2, respectively, which can be attributed to the presence of polar functionalities, particularly the nitro group and tri‐methoxy group, whose electronegative oxygen atoms increase the polar surface area. In contrast, compounds 6a‐6g and 6k‐6n showed lower TPSA values of 89.08 Å2, suggesting comparatively reduced polarity that may favor passive membrane permeation. The compounds exhibited moderate lipophilicity (MLogP 1.61‐3.45), which, together with their TPSA and hydrogen‐bonding characteristics, may support passive membrane permeation. All conjugates displayed good GI absorption, whereas the NO2─containing conjugates 6h‐6j, which may be attributed to their higher TPSA (134.9 Å2). The conjugates 6a‐6o have a significantly better capacity to penetrate the epidermal layer, as smaller negative logKp values are associated with higher skin permeability compared to DOX. Additionally, all conjugates except 6j and 6o showed a significantly higher bioavailability score (0.55) than DOX (0.17), suggesting improved systemic exposure and better oral drug potential. In silico toxicity prediction was also performed to assess the toxicity of the conjugates 6a‐6o. The toxicity of conjugates 6a‐6o and DOX is denoted by +Ve (for active toxicity) and −Ve (for inactive toxicity) in Table 5. The results showed that the majority of the compounds were inactive against hepatotoxicity, indicating a safety profile comparable to that of the reference drug DOX. However, an exception was observed in four molecules bearing a nitro (─NO2) and trimethoxy substituent, which showed predicted hepatotoxicity. It was observed that the synthesized molecules are inactive against cardiotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, cytotoxicity, and immunotoxicity, whereas DOX showed activity toward these endpoints. However, the synthesized conjugates were predicted to have neurotoxic, respiratory toxic, and mutagenic activity similar to DOX. The predicted LD50 value for all synthesized conjugates was 65 mg/kg, while one conjugate 6o, exhibited a higher LD50 of 205 mg/kg, which is similar to the reference drug DOX. All synthesized conjugates 6a‐6o, including the standard DOX, were classified under toxicity class III, suggesting moderate acute toxicity. Overall, the in silico toxicity predictions suggest that the synthesized conjugates 6a‐6o possess a reasonably acceptable safety profile comparable to the reference drug DOX, highlighting their potential for further pharmacological development.

Cytotoxicity Assay

The synthesized compounds 6a–6o were tested for their cytotoxic potential against MCF‐7 cell line using the sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay. Among the synthesized compounds, the most active compounds 6j and 6m were further evaluated against the human noncancerous HEK293 cell line. The half‐maximal growth inhibitory concentrations (GI50) are summarized in Table 6. Among the series, compound 6m demonstrated the highest potency, with a GI50 value below 10 µg/mL (SI > 5), comparable to the reference drug DOX. Notably, the favorable SI value (>5) highlights the promising selectivity of 6m toward MCF‐7 cancer cells, suggesting a potentially improved therapeutic potential. This suggests that at comparatively low concentrations, 6m has great potential to stop MCF‐7 cell proliferation. Compounds 6j, 6c, and 6f exhibited moderate cytotoxic activity, with GI50 values of 12, 35, and 38 µg/mL, respectively, indicating that these derivatives also have considerable anticancer potential, albeit at somewhat higher concentrations. Several compounds, including 6g, 6d, 6i, 6k, and 6b, showed weak to moderate growth inhibitory effects, with GI50 values ranging from 40 to 70 µg/mL. Although these conjugates might inhibit cell growth, their effect was not as strong as the potent conjugates.

Microscopic visualization (at × 20 magnification, Figure 7) of untreated MCF‐7 cells, compounds 6a‐6o‐treated, and DOX treated was carried out, as it offers a clear visual indication of conjugates 6a‐6o‐induced morphological alterations that support and validate the quantitative anticancer findings. Microscopic observations of the MCF‐7 cancer cells displayed clear differences between the control, the conjugates 6a‐6o‐treated, and the DOX‐treated groups. The cells in the microscopic image of the control appeared healthy, dense with intact cellular morphology that displayed unrestricted cell proliferation. The conjugates, including 6m (GI50 < 10), 6j (12), 6c (35), 6f (38), 6g (40), and 6d (43 µg/mL), triggered noteworthy cytotoxic effects in the images, including rounding, cell shrinkage, loss of adherence, and the formation of apoptotic bodies, which are the indicators of apoptosis and cell death. Out of them, conjugate 6m was the most damaging, closely resembling the disturbing effects detected in DOX. However, moderately active conjugates, including 6i (63), 6k (62), and 6b (70 µg/mL), displayed milder but noticeable signs of cell stress. In contrast, the least active compounds (6a, 6h, 6l, 6n, and 6o), having GI50 more than 80 µg/mL, displayed pictures that were almost identical to the control group, representing low cytotoxicity. The microscopic images showed a clear correlation with the GI50 values: substances with lower GI50 induced morphological alterations that were more dramatic and scientifically relevant, indicating their higher potential for anticancer. In summary, the SRB assay results show that some of the synthesized compounds 6a‐6o have promising cytotoxic qualities against MCF‐7 cells, with 6m emerging as the most effective, comparable with the conventional chemotherapeutic drug. These results provide a basis for additional research on the most active substances.

Structure‐Activity Relationship (SAR)

The structure–activity relationship (SAR, Figure 8) analysis of the synthesized conjugates 6a‐6o revealed that variations in the aromatic substituents substantially influenced their cytotoxic activity against MCF‐7 cells. The combination of electron‐rich heteroatoms in the isoxazole, triazole, and amide moieties provides sites for electronic delocalization and hydrogen‐bond formation, while substituents on the aromatic rings can modulate the electron density of the conjugated system. These changes in charge distribution may affect molecular reactivity and ligand–target interactions, thereby contributing to the differences in cytotoxic activity observed among the synthesized derivatives.

Interpretation of structure–activity relationships shows how alterations in the chemical structures of the synthesized compounds 6a‐6o affect their anticancer activity.

It was observed that the unsubstituted ring, the electron‐donating groups (EDGs) substituted ring, and the NO2‐substituted ring were found to be the least potent or inactive for their anticancer activity against the MCF‐7 cell line. Among the substitution patterns evaluated, the 2,5‐dichloro substituted arrangement showed distinctly potent activity compared to mono‐substituted at the 2‐, 3‐, or 4‐positions chloro analogs, indicating that the presence of dual halogens at 2 and 5 positions creates an optimal electronic and steric environment for interaction with the biological target. Furthermore, the presence of 2‐NO2 with chloro substitution at the fourth position exhibits promising anticancer activity, surpassing compounds with mono‐substitution of nitro groups. This suggests that a synergistic combination of strong electron‐withdrawing groups (EWGs), such as NO2 or Cl at the ortho position, maximizes both π‐electron withdrawal effects and hence refines anticancer activity.

In contrast, the introduction of electron‐donating groups (EDGs) such as CH3 and OCH3 resulted in a considerable loss of activity as compared to the presence of EWGs, confirming that electron‐rich aromatic rings lessen binding affinity with the target. However, it was observed that the optimal anticancer activity can be achieved when the aromatic ring is attached to EWGs combinations. These results highlight the critical role of electron‐withdrawing strength and substituent positioning effects in leading anticancer activity and provide a rational basis for future lead optimization and anticancer drug design.

Conclusion

In this study, a series of piperidine–embedded isoxazol–triazole conjugates 6a‐6o were designed, synthesized, and biologically evaluated. Preliminary in silico docking studies showed potential interactions with the estrogen receptor site (ER+). Synthesized compounds 6a‐6o were screened against the MCF‐7 and HEK293 cell lines. Among the compounds 6a‐6o, compound 6m appeared as the most potent analog, having a GI50 value less than 10 µg/mL (SI > 5), remaining the strongest candidate to be an anticancer agent. However, compound 6j also demonstrated strong inhibitory activity, having a GI50 value of 12 µg/mL, but having a selective index of less than one. Microscopic analysis of treated and untreated MCF‐7 cells also provided qualitative support for the SRB assay findings. Cells exposed to active conjugates displayed noticeable morphological alterations. Whereas, cells treated with weakly active conjugates retained a normal morphology. Overall, this study shows that the piperidine‐embedded‐isoxazol–triazole conjugates serve as promising lead candidates for the novel anticancer agent developments. The greater activity of conjugate 6m recognizes it as a strong lead candidate for further development.
