The Unfolding Mystery of Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis in Pregnancy: A Case Report
Authors: Sebastian Asteguieta, Silvana Torselli, Carlos Diaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare, life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by uncontrolled immune activation and excessive cytokine release. Pregnancy-associated HLH is particularly uncommon and presents a significant diagnostic challenge because its clinical manifestations frequently overlap with obstetric conditions such as sepsis, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome, and acute fatty liver of pregnancy.We report the case of an 18-year-old primigravid woman at 34 weeks of gestation who presented with persistent fever, fatigue, and progressive clinical deterioration despite antibiotic therapy. Following cesarean delivery, she developed hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice, pancytopenia, hypertriglyceridemia, coagulopathy, and persistent systemic inflammation. Bone marrow aspiration demonstrated hemophagocytosis, fulfilling diagnostic criteria for secondary HLH. The patient required intensive supportive care, including blood product transfusions, before initiating etoposide-based therapy according to the HLH-94/HLH-2004 protocol, with subsequent clinical stabilization and outpatient hematology follow-up.This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for HLH in pregnant or postpartum patients presenting with persistent fever, cytopenias, liver dysfunction, and hyperinflammatory laboratory findings. Early recognition, prompt multidisciplinary evaluation, and timely initiation of immunosuppressive therapy are essential to reduce morbidity and mortality in this rare but potentially fatal condition.

Introduction

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare but potentially fatal hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages, resulting in excessive cytokine production, hemophagocytosis, and progressive multiorgan dysfunction. Secondary HLH accounts for most adult cases and is commonly associated with infections, malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and, less frequently, pregnancy. Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, HLH continues to be associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly when recognition is delayed [1-4].

Pregnancy-associated HLH is exceptionally uncommon and represents one of the most challenging diagnostic entities in maternal medicine. Physiological immune adaptations during pregnancy may predispose susceptible individuals to an exaggerated inflammatory response, although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Its clinical manifestations-including persistent fever, cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, liver dysfunction, hyperferritinemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and coagulopathy-overlap considerably with more common obstetric conditions such as sepsis, hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count (HELLP) syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, and severe preeclampsia, often delaying diagnosis and treatment [2,3,5].

The diagnosis of HLH is based on the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria or the HScore, integrating clinical findings, laboratory abnormalities, and evidence of hemophagocytosis. However, the application of these diagnostic tools during pregnancy remains challenging because several physiological and pathological changes of gestation may mimic the laboratory profile observed in HLH. Consequently, maintaining a high index of suspicion is essential, particularly in patients with persistent fever and unexplained cytopenias after exclusion of infectious and obstetric etiologies [3,6,7].

Early recognition is crucial because adult HLH is associated with mortality rates approaching 40-60% despite modern intensive care and immunosuppressive therapy. Current treatment strategies emphasize prompt suppression of the hyperinflammatory response using corticosteroids, etoposide-based regimens, and management of the underlying trigger whenever possible. Owing to the rarity of pregnancy-associated HLH, current evidence relies primarily on case reports and small case series, underscoring the need to increase awareness of this uncommon but life-threatening condition [2,4,8-11].

Herein, we present the case of an 18-year-old primigravid woman who developed secondary HLH during the third trimester of pregnancy, highlighting the diagnostic challenges, multidisciplinary management, and favorable clinical outcome following early recognition and treatment.

Case presentation

We present the case of an 18-year-old female from Guatemala City, originally from Villa Nueva, at 34 weeks of gestation. She presented to the emergency department with a seven-day history of agitation, fever (38.7ºC), and fatigue. Before this, she had been evaluated by a private physician, who conducted laboratory tests, including a complete blood count and metabolic panel, both of which were normal. Despite receiving antibiotic treatment, her symptoms persisted, prompting her to seek further medical care. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no chronic diseases, surgeries, trauma, or allergies. Gynecologically, she had no previous pregnancies, experienced menarche at age 13, and was receiving prenatal care at a private clinic. On physical examination, she presented with a distended abdomen consistent with advanced pregnancy.

The fetus was in a longitudinal lie, with a cephalic presentation and a fetal heart rate of 130 bpm, as determined by Leopold’s maneuvers. At 35 weeks of gestation, a few days after admission, the patient underwent a cesarean delivery and delivered a healthy infant. During the postpartum period, she developed persistent fever, profound fatigue, generalized weakness, nausea, vomiting, and jaundice, accompanied by electrolyte disturbances, including hyponatremia and hypokalemia. She was subsequently referred to the internal medicine department, and hospital discharge was deferred because of her deteriorating clinical condition. Physical examination revealed hepatosplenomegaly, while abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated moderate hepatic steatosis. Initial laboratory results revealed leukopenia, severe anemia, thrombocytopenia, hypertriglyceridemia, and elevated aspartate aminotransferase levels, raising suspicion for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. As her cytopenias progressively worsened, a bone marrow aspiration was performed, revealing normocellular marrow with a slight decrease in the granulocytic series. She remained hospitalized for 29 days for further diagnostic evaluation and treatment.

A week later, a fluorescent antinuclear antibody test (FANA) was performed, which was negative, ruling out autoimmune diseases and mixed connective tissue disease. The patient received fresh frozen plasma (FFP) and packed red blood cells (pRBCs) to manage hemorrhagic shock and coagulopathy. Further bone marrow aspiration showed 70% cellularity with a mild decrease in the granulocytic series and a myeloid-erythroid ratio of 2 to 4, consistent with HLH. Hemophagocytosis was observed, where macrophages engulfed blood cells, a hallmark of HLH (Figures 1, 2, 3). The patient was treated with etoposide, an antineoplastic drug that inhibits topoisomerase II, commonly used for cancers and severe inflammatory conditions. Outpatient hematology-oncology consultation was arranged, and treatment followed HLH-94 or HLH-2004 protocols for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis.

Discussion

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a rare but potentially fatal hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by uncontrolled activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages, resulting in excessive cytokine release and multiorgan dysfunction. HLH is broadly classified as primary (familial), caused by inherited defects in cytotoxic lymphocyte function, and secondary (acquired), which is associated with infections, malignancies, autoimmune disorders, and, less commonly, pregnancy [4,5,7].

Pregnancy-associated HLH is exceptionally uncommon, with fewer than 100 cases reported in the literature. Physiological immune adaptations during pregnancy may predispose susceptible individuals to immune dysregulation, particularly in the second and third trimesters, although cases have also been reported during the postpartum period. Recent systematic reviews have shown that infection remains the most common trigger, followed by autoimmune diseases and malignancies, while pregnancy itself may act as an independent precipitating factor in susceptible patients [2,3,6].

The diagnosis of HLH remains particularly challenging because its clinical presentation overlaps with other obstetric emergencies, including severe sepsis, HELLP syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, and thrombotic microangiopathies. Consequently, delayed diagnosis is common and contributes to the high maternal mortality reported in published series. Current recommendations continue to support the HLH-2004 diagnostic criteria and the HScore as valuable tools to facilitate early recognition, especially in adults with unexplained persistent fever, cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, and multiorgan dysfunction [4,8]. In our patient, persistent fever, pancytopenia, markedly elevated ferritin levels, hypertriglyceridemia, and bone marrow hemophagocytosis fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for secondary HLH.

Compared with previously reported pregnancy-associated cases, our patient demonstrated a similarly fulminant clinical course characterized by rapid progression to systemic inflammatory response and multiple organ dysfunction. However, unlike many reported patients in whom Epstein-Barr virus infection, autoimmune diseases, or hematologic malignancies were identified as triggering factors, no definitive alternative trigger was demonstrated, suggesting that pregnancy-related immune dysregulation played a major role in disease development [2,3,6]. This highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion even in the absence of an obvious precipitating condition.

Current management recommendations emphasize prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy once HLH is suspected, as treatment delays are strongly associated with increased mortality. Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of therapy, while etoposide continues to be recommended for severe or progressive disease despite concerns regarding fetal toxicity, as maternal survival remains the priority. Recent adult guidelines further recommend individualized treatment according to disease severity and the underlying trigger [5].

Overall, this case reinforces the importance of considering HLH in pregnant patients presenting with persistent fever, unexplained cytopenias, hyperferritinemia, and progressive multiorgan dysfunction. Early recognition, prompt application of standardized diagnostic criteria, and timely initiation of HLH-directed therapy are essential to improve maternal outcomes. Given the rarity of pregnancy-associated HLH, reporting additional cases remains crucial to improve understanding of its presentation, optimize diagnostic strategies, and refine therapeutic approaches.

Conclusions

Pregnancy-associated hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis is an uncommon but potentially fatal condition that remains difficult to recognize because of its nonspecific presentation and significant overlap with other obstetric and inflammatory disorders. This case highlights the importance of considering HLH in pregnant or postpartum patients presenting with persistent fever, unexplained cytopenias, liver dysfunction, hyperferritinemia, and progressive clinical deterioration despite conventional treatment. Early application of established diagnostic criteria, prompt multidisciplinary evaluation, and timely initiation of HLH-directed therapy are essential to improve maternal outcomes and prevent irreversible organ damage. Given the rarity of this condition, additional well-documented case reports are needed to expand current knowledge, improve diagnostic awareness, and guide evidence-based management strategies for pregnancy-associated HLH.
