A pilot study of peripheral magnetic stimulation combined with physical therapy for motor function in early acute stroke survivors with sensory ataxic hemiparesis
Authors: Shigeru Obayashi, Rina Takahashi, Kazue Taguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with sensory ataxic hemiparesis face a poorer recovery prognosis than those with pure motor deficits. We explored the feasibility and efficacy of repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) combined with physical therapy (PT) for improving upper extremity (UE) function in early acute stroke survivors. Eighteen Participants were randomized to rPMS group (20 min rPMS + 20 min PT) or conventional PT group (40 min PT). Outcomes (the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT), the UE motor section of the Fugl-Meyer Motor Assessment Scale (FMA-UE), and the Thumb Localizing Test (TLT)) were primarily analyzed using duration-corrected gain (gain normalized by sessions) to account for a significant dose imbalance (rPMS mean 6.5 vs. Control mean 12.8 sessions). A robustness ANCOVA was performed on the FMA-UE Post-score, adjusting for baseline scores and dose. The rPMS group received fewer total sessions but demonstrated significantly greater gains in WMFT and FMA-UE after dose correction. The ANCOVA confirmed a robust and statistically significant treatment effect on FMA-UE Post-score (F(1,11) = 5.984, p = 0.032). TLT showed a moderate effect size (r = 0.44). rPMS combined with PT is feasible and significantly enhances the rate of UE motor recovery, even after rigorous statistical adjustment for dose imbalance. Future studies in larger cohorts are warranted.Trial registration: University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (February 1, 2019) https//www.umin.ac.jp/ctr/. Introduciton

Proprioceptive deficits in the upper extremities impair activity of the daily living ability such as skillful movement of hands and manipulation of objects. Proprioceptive deficits following stroke occur in 27 to 54% of patients1,2 and are consistently correlated with length of hospitalization and mortality3–5. The severity of impaired proprioception can also predict poor motor ability, functional ability, and dexterity of the affected upper extremity (UE) during the chronic phase of stroke6–8. This is particularly true for sensory ataxic hemiparesis (proprioceptive deficits accompanied by motor paresis), a condition specifically associated with a poorer prognosis for motor recovery compared to pure motor deficits9–11. Consequently, achieving substantial motor recovery in acute stroke survivors with sensory ataxic hemiparesis is exceptionally challenging.

It is well established that the first month after stroke onset offers the highest recovery rate, defining a critical period for maximizing treatment effectiveness12. Given this narrow window, here is an urgent need to establish effective strategies to augment post-stroke recovery. This necessity is compounded by external factors, particularly in settings like Japan. The medical service circumstances of stroke survivors are unfavorable for investigating the advantages of early intervention in the acute phase. Rehabilitation intervention in acute care hospitals is often suspended within 2–4 weeks of onset, and patients are subsequently followed at restorative rehabilitation care units in other hospitals or nursing homes. The transfer timing mainly depended upon bed control, indicating that the patient might abruptly suspended. It would therefore be difficult to design clinical studies or follow-up motor recovery over a longer period. In situations where patients are receiving a limited amount of conventional intervention during the critical period, we need to strive to establish an effective strategy to augment post-stroke recovery.

Neuromodulators are promising candidates expected to enhance post-stroke motor recovery. The efficacy of top-down neuromodulators (e.g., repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, rTMS) follow a phase-dependent hierarchy (acute > subacute > chronic)13. Similarly, bottom-up neuromodulators, such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) and repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) are expected to become intervention devices that facilitate motor recovery after stroke14–16, are expected to facilitate recovery, with NMES showing faster motor recovery when applied in the acute phase17.

rPMS is particularly advantageous due to its ability to penetrate deeper tissue without causing pain, generate greater muscle torque, and its suitability for pediatric applications. However, technical drawbacks include potential coil overheating and non-specific stimulation from increased intensity18. Compounding this, previous rPMS efficacy studies for pure UE paresis have yielded mixed results (beneficial14,18 vs. no effect19,20. We attribute this discrepancy primarily to two factors: treatment timing (effective studies commenced in the acute phase, ineffective ones in the chronic phase), and the use of a vibratory sham control21,22. A vibratory sham risks masking true beneficial effects by providing an active therapeutic agent (muscle vibration) itself.

Given our recent findings supporting acute-phase effectiveness14, we hypothesize that the adjuvant application of rPMS will be effective for improving sensory ataxic hemiparesis, with efficacy expected to be greater in the acute phase than in the chronic phase.

Our primary aim was to evaluate the feasibility, safety and preliminary efficacy of applying rPMS combined with physical therapy to the affected UE in the early acute phase of stroke to augment recovery from sensory ataxic hemiparesis, specifically in terms of motor function and proprioception.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A CONSORT flow diagram explaining the allocation of participants randomized throughout the study is presented in Fig. 1. Eighteen subjects met the study criteria and were randomly allocated to the rPMS group (n = 9) or the control group (n = 9), stratified by age and sex (Tables 1 and 2). Demographic characteristics (age, sex, lesion side, stroke subtype, and time since stroke onset) were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 2). At baseline, no significant differences were observed in upper extremity motor function, except for FMA wrist scores. However, the total duration of intervention was significantly incongruent between the groups (p = 0.031), highlighting an unavoidable dose imbalance inherent to the acute setting. (Table 2). The Median (IQR) number of sessions was 7.0 (4.0–8.0) for the rPMS group and 9.0 (7.5–20.0) for the control group. The Mean number of sessions showed an even greater disparity (rPMS: 6.6 sessions vs. Control: 12.9 sessions), which is indicative of significant positive skewness in the Control group’s data. Therefore, the duration-corrected gains (the change score (Δscore) divided by the total number of rehabilitation sessions (Δscore/session) were calculated to justify group comparisons by assessing the rate of improvement rather than the absolute score change.

Primary efficacy analysis

The primary efficacy analysis utilized the Mann–Whitney U test to compare the duration-corrected gain between the rPMS and control groups. The WMFT-FAS scores did not significantly differ between the pre- and post-treatment groups. However, the corrected improvements in motor recovery according to the WMFT-FAS were significantly different (U = 61, Z = 2.406, p = 0.015). rPMS had a powerful effect on the WMFT-FAS score (r = 0.58), indicating that the WMFT-FAS score is highly clinically significance (Fig. 2).

Comparison of the FMA-UE function between the pre- and posttreatment revealed no significant differences. However, the corrected gains in motor recovery according to the FMA-UE total scores showed significant differences between the groups, i.e., 2.10 for the rPMS group and 0.88 for the control group (U test; U = 63, z = 1.991, p = 0.05). rPMS had a medium effect (r = 0.47) on FMA-UE, indicating that FMA-UE had moderate clinical significance. In the sub-analysis of each component of the FMA, the corrected gains of the FMA-shoulder, fingers, and coordination/speed were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 3). However, the rPMS group showed a medium effect size on FMA-fingers (r = 0.30) and FMA-coordination/speed (r = 0.35), suggesting moderate clinical significance of FMA-fingers and FMA coordination/speed.

Sensitivity analysis (ANCOVA)

A sensitivity analysis was performed using Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) on the FMA-UE Total Post-score, adjusting for age, sex, FMA-Wrist baseline score, total number of intervention sessions, and FMA-UE Total baseline score. The ANCOVA revealed a statistically significant difference between the rPMS and control groups after accounting for these covariates (F(1,11) = 5.984, p = 0.032, η2p​= 0.35). This finding supports the robustness of the beneficial effect of rPMS on motor function, independent of known baseline imbalances and differences in intervention dosage. FMA-UE Total Post-treatment: rPMS Group: Adjusted Mean = 48.1 (95% CI: 43.2 − 53.0); Control Group: Adjusted Mean = 39.3 (95% CI: 34.4 − 44.2).

Feasibility and safety of rPMS

The rPMS protocol demonstrated excellent safety and feasibility.

Safety and tolerance

No adverse events—including pain or discomfort during the intervention, or any complications during the follow-up period—were recorded. Furthermore, the protocol was highly tolerable: the rate of muscle fatigue throughout the rPMS session was confirmed to be minimal (11.1%), supporting the strategic sequence of rPMS immediately preceding the physical therapy (PT) sessions.

Feasibility metrics

Patient engagement was high, with participants expressing enthusiasm for utilizing rPMS as an adjuvant to rehabilitation. This high compliance is supported by key metrics: the Recruitment Rate was 94.7% of eligible patients enrolled; the Adherence Rate was 100% of scheduled sessions completed; and the Dropout Rate was 0% due to non-medical reasons, further supporting the excellent feasibility of our protocol.

Pragmatic protocol

To mitigate the potential loss of post-intervention data resulting from unavoidable administrative transfers, we adopted a pragmatic protocol: motor function and proprioception were evaluated as the post-score on the day immediately preceding the patient’s transfer or discharge date.

Discussion

The proposed rPMS protocol demonstrated excellent feasibility, safety, and tolerability in the challenging environment of the acute stroke care setting, with zero reported adverse events and 100% adherence. Despite the rPMS group receiving significantly fewer intervention sessions (mean 6.5) than the control group (mean 12.8)—a limitation inherent to acute care—Mann–Whitney U tests on the duration-corrected gain (gain per session) showed that both WMFT and FMA-UE gains were significantly greater in the rPMS group. These results represent the first report to support the contribution of rPMS in accelerating the motor recovery of sensory ataxic UE paresis in the early acute phase of stroke, expanding upon previous work on pure motor deficits14,18.

We acknowledge that the significant disparity in the total number of intervention sessions (mean 6.5 vs. 12.8 sessions) was an unavoidable limitation inherent to the acute care setting of this pilot trial. To rigorously address this major confounder, we employed a sensitivity Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) on the FMA-UE Post-score. The model was fully adjusted for all known covariates, including baseline FMA-UE Total score, FMA-Wrist score, total intervention sessions, age, and sex. This analysis confirmed a robust and statistically significant treatment effect on FMA-UE Post-score (F(1,11) = 5.984, p = 0.032, η2p = 0.35​). This finding unequivocally indicates that the observed improvement is not merely due to baseline differences or variations in rehabilitation dose, but rather reflects a genuine, accelerated functional gain attributable to the rPMS intervention.

We rigorously addressed the major confounder of dose imbalance by employing a sensitivity Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) on the FMA-UE Post-score. This analysis, fully adjusted for all known covariates including the total intervention sessions, confirmed a robust and statistically significant treatment effect on FMA-UE Post-score (F(1,11) = 5.984, P = 0.032, η2p​ = 0.35). This finding unequivocally indicates that the observed improvement reflects a genuine, accelerated functional gain attributable to the rPMS intervention, independent of rehabilitation dose variations. Furthermore, the significant ANCOVA result serves as a powerful validation of the clinical signal detected by our more conservative, non-parametric analysis (Median and IQR), justifying its priority due to the non-normality of the gain data.

We recognize that the absence of a true, fully inert sham control represents a methodological limitation inherent to many peripheral magnetic stimulation studies. However, we opted for a conventional physical therapy control rather than a low-intensity sham condition (e.g., 5% output of maximal stimulator output) due to a critical confounding factor. This approach was necessitated by the suspicion that a low-intensity sham pulse, which generates localized muscle vibration, is itself a physiologically active intervention. Given robust evidence demonstrating the therapeutic benefit of localized muscle vibration on post-stroke motor recovery21,22,24, comparing the active rPMS against a vibratory sham would have likely masked the genuine, magnetic-field-mediated therapeutic effects we aimed to isolate. Furthermore, our active rPMS intervention is mechanistically distinct from simple localized vibration, as it includes the crucial component of repetitive voluntary contractions of the target muscle, which are synchronized with the magnetic pulse, alongside the stimulation and vibration components. Therefore, we postulate that the observed positive results are partly attribute to the Pathleader®’s unique specificity, which mitigates the general rPMS drawback of non-selective stimulation. This precise targeting, combined with the voluntary contraction component, may explain why our study found a positive outcome, whereas previous studies using adapted rTMS coils (designed for broader brain stimulation) yielded mixed results.

rPMS, as a bottom-up neuromodulator, generates cortical plastic changes in conjunction with motor recovery. Peripheral stimulation, similar to NMES, is known to generate massive proprioceptive flows to the affected hemisphere25, potentially enhancing synaptic connectivity26 and inducing reorganization of the motor map17,23,27–30. While conventional PT utilizes top-down cognitive and corticomotor pathways, rPMS - by stimulating peripheral afferent nerves - induces widespread bottom-up sensory input that facilitates an acute increase in somatosensory and motor cortical excitability, a mechanism generally shared with other peripheral stimulation techniques like NMES. However, the observed therapeutic effect is hypothesized to stem from the unique specificity of the rPMS intervention used here: it was timed to coincide with the volitional contraction of each weakened muscle. We propose that the synergy of rPMS providing cortical priming (bottom-up) immediately before task-specific practice (PT, top-down) maximizes the transient window of neuroplasticity, thus accelerating the recovery observed in the acute phase. This precise, synchronized targeting may explain why our study found a positive outcome, whereas previous studies yielded mixed results.

To date, various measures are used to assess proprioceptive function31. TLT and Joint position sense (JPS) tests has often been used for the assessment of proprioceptive function32–38, and several reports have discussed the validation and reliability of TLT and compared it with other relevant measures33,39. The association between proprioception and UE motor recovery is still unclear because of mixed results among the relevant studies. The difference may in part depend on the proprioception measures used40. TLT showed a notable trend toward improvement in the rPMS group (moderate effect size, r = 0.44), suggesting a clinically meaningful signal of sensory recovery. Although not statistically significant, this lack of significance is likely attributable to the limited statistical power of this pilot study. While we acknowledge that age-related proprioceptive decline is a potential confounder, the non-significant age difference between the groups (64 vs. 70 years) was negligible and could not explain the superior rate of change. This improvement suggests that rPMS may counteract central proprioceptive deficits, which have been speculated to involve damage to the ipsilesional uncrossed descending corticospinal pathways41 or disconnection of interhemispheric transcallosal transfer42. Given the TLT’s ordinal scale possesses limited discriminative sensitivity and the lack of statistical significance due to the pilot study’s small power, future adequately powered trials must incorporate highly sensitive continuous indicators (such as JPS) to accurately quantify the magnitude of rPMS’s effect on sensory recovery.

This study has several limitations. First and foremost is the small sample size inherent to a pilot trial, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the significant dose imbalance (6.5 vs. 12.8 sessions) between groups, although addressed by the robust ANCOVA, necessitates validation in a dose-standardized randomized controlled trial to rigorously isolate the true therapeutic effect. Third, the short intervention duration leaves the long-term clinical benefits, and whether the accelerated recovery is sustained, unclear. To address these issues, future confirmatory trials are imperative to optimize the stimulation parameters (e.g., frequency, intensity, duration, and optimal sequencing). Furthermore, translational studies utilizing neuroimaging (fMRI) or neurophysiological measures (TMS-evoked potentials) are necessary to investigate the cortical mechanisms underpinning rPMS’s bottom-up effects, thereby bridging the gap between clinical findings and neurobiological understanding. Despite these limitations, the present findings provide valuable insights into the potential effects of rPMS on sensory ataxic UE motor dysfunction.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrated that the rPMS protocol possesses excellent safety, tolerability, and feasibility, evidenced by zero adverse events and a 100% adherence rate. Clinically, rPMS combined with PT significantly enhances the rate of upper extremity motor recovery in acute stroke survivors with sensory ataxic hemiparesis. Given its safety and ability to accelerate functional gain in a traditionally challenging patient population, rPMS represents a feasible and promising adjunctive strategy for early acute rehabilitation.

Methods

Participants

Patient with stroke admitted to an academic medical center between May 2019 and February 2021 were recruited. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) medically stable condition; (2) inpatients within 2 weeks of stroke onset; (3) aged 40–90 years; (4) intact skin on the affected UE; (5) adequate mental state to participate (stable consciousness); (6) first-ever UE paresis; and 7) proprioceptive impairment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of damage from stroke, brain injury, or brain tumor; (2) metal implant in the head or within the stimulation area; (3) aphasia; (4) history of cardiac arrhythmia with hemodynamic instability; (5) comorbidity with neurodegenerative diseases and mental disorders; (6) uncontrolled seizures; (7) impaired consciousness; and (8) implanted stimulators (such as cardiac pacemakers or defibrillators).

Design

This was a pilot and feasibility study, which, by nature, is not powered to test efficacy definitively. Therefore, a formal power calculation for efficacy was not performed. The sample size of N = 9 per group (N = 18 total) was determined based on feasibility constraints (e.g., resources, time, and patient availability in the acute setting) and was considered sufficient to estimate the recruitment rate, adherence, and variance in the outcome measures (effect size) for planning a future confirmatory trial.

A preliminary, randomized controlled case series to explore the feasibility of combining rPMS with conventional physical therapy was conducted between March 2020 and February 2021 in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at the Dokkyo Medical University Saitama Medical Center, Saitama, Japan. This study was designed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving an explanation of the study. The participants were randomly allocated to two groups: the rPMS group and the control group.

Randomization

Participants were randomly allocated to either the intervention (rPMS and subsequent Physical Therapy) or control (Physical Therapy only) group following consent, stratified by age and sex. The random sequence was generated by an independent researcher and preserved in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). A research coordinator, who was not involved in recruitment or baseline assessments, stored the SNOSE in a locked cabinet. Allocation concealment was strictly maintained: envelopes were only opened after the participant completed all baseline measures and was deemed eligible. Due to the nature of the intervention, participants and personnel delivering the intervention could not be blinded. However, outcome assessors and data analysis personnel remained blinded to group allocation.

Intervention protocol

The rPMS group received five sessions of treatment per week, alternating 20 min of rPMS and subsequent 20 min of physical therapy (PT) with 40 min of PT every other day. rPMS was applied to paretic muscles and timed to coincide with the volitional contraction of each weakened muscle. The effort is fairly severe for most of patients and may generate sore muscle and fatigue. Thus, the frequency of rPMS sessions was limited to every other day. The control group received five sessions of treatment/week, consisting of 40 min of PT per day, as depicted in Fig. 3. Participants were consistently positioned with their forearms resting on a desk during the intervention.

rPMS conditions

We used a peripheral magnetic stimulator (Pathleader, IFG, Sendai, Japan) for rPMS treatment. This stimulator is uniquely characterized by its accurate generation of a selective and exclusive contraction of the target muscle. This device was specifically chosen because it mitigates the known general drawbacks of rPMS, such as non-specific muscle stimulation. This round coil stimulator generated biphasic 350 µsec with magnetic gradients of up to 15 kT/s, thereby generating repetitive contraction–relaxation cycles that amplify proprioceptive input from target muscles. We adopted an intermittent ON/OFF stimulation protocol consisting of three 10-consecutive stimulations for each muscle per session, where the duration of each stimulation (ON) was 2 s, with a frequency of 30 Hz, a total of 1800 pulses, and an intensity of 70% MSO (maximal stimulator output, corresponding to 0.65 Tesla) at each stimulation site. The stimulation intervals (OFF period) were then set at 2 s. The stimulation was administered to the belly of the target muscles while the subjects was in a sitting position. The stimuli were timed to coincide with the generation of voluntary motor commands for the target muscle.

This process proceeded sequentially (Fig. 4b) as follows: (1) identification of the motor point (Fig. 4a) and (2) repeated stimuli (Fig. 4b). Motor points were identified as the most sensitive by stimulating the target muscle point-by-point. The target was changed from proximal to distal, and the same stimuli were used for each muscle (Fig. 4c and d). The deltoid, triceps brachii, ECR, and EDC are mandatory target muscles for rPMS. The optimal application of rPMS to the flexor muscles (biceps brachii and FDS) was conducted only in patients with flaccid muscle tone (those with a stiffness less than 3 in the knee-mouth test and/or finger function test of SIAS).

Conventional physical therapy as a control

All participants received PT, including range of motion exercise, affected arm muscle tone normalization, passive and active-assisted mobilization, progressive neuromuscular facilitation training, and task-oriented training, which was conducted by a trained therapist.

Outcome assessment

The post-intervention assessment was performed immediately upon the completion of the 4-week protocol or at the time of the patient’s transfer. Specifically, to mitigate the loss of critical post-intervention data due to unavoidable administrative transfers to other rehabilitation facilities, motor function and proprioception were evaluated as the post-score on the day immediately preceding the patient’s scheduled transfer or discharge date. This ensured the integrity of our primary outcome measures. To account for the observed difference in the total number of intervention sessions between groups, the primary efficacy outcomes were analyzed using a duration-corrected gain value. This was calculated as the change in outcome score (Δscore) divided by the total number of rehabilitation sessions (Δscore/session), allowing for the comparison of the rate of recovery per unit of rehabilitation dose.

To assess the feasibility of using rPMS, data on the ability to use rPMS during the sessions according to the protocol, user acceptance, and adverse events were analyzed.

Primary outcome measures included the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) functional ability scale (FAS)43 and the upper-extremity (UE) motor section of the Fugl-Meyer Motor Assessment Scale (FMA)44. Assessments were performed both before and after the end of the intervention. In the WMFT assessment sessions, all tasks were performed as quickly as possible and were truncated at 120 s. Tasks 1–6 of the WMFT consist of timed joint segment movements, and tasks 7–15 involve timed integrative functional movements43. When scoring the FMA motor Sects. (0–66 points), each patient was required to perform 33 movements, including shoulder, wrist, finger, and coordination/speed movements44. The performance of each movement was scored on a 3-point ordinal scale (0, cannot perform; 1, partially perform; 2, fully perform). To estimate the coordination/speed, a finger-to-nose test was used. When the subject was sitting with eyes closed, he or she was instructed to move the tip of his or her index finger from the knee to the nose 5 times as fast as possible. The following parameters were assessed: Tremor: 0, marked tremor; 1, slight tremor; and 2, no tremor. Dysmetria: 0: pronounced dysmetria; 1: slight and systematic dysmetria; 2: no dysmetria. Speed: swiftness of motion compared relative to that of the unaffected arm. 0: the finger-to-nose test repeated 5 times with the affected arm at least 6 s slower than the unaffected arm; 1: 2–5 s slower than the affected arm; 2: less than 2 s difference. The three estimated details yielded a maximum sum of 6 points.

The secondary outcome measure was the thumb localizing test (TLT), which is the most commonly used test for the quantitative assessment of UE proprioceptive function32,39,45. The examiner begins by confirming normal proprioception in the unaffected arm by enabling patients to touch their nose with the unaffected arm, with eyes closed. After the participant’s affected arm was moved passively to four different locations in space, the participant was required to precisely grasp his or her affected thumb with his or her unaffected hand in the eyes-closed condition. The scoring is based on the estimated distance that each patient misses their affected thumb in space and discriminates between 4 grades: no difficulty (grade 0: locates the affected thumb accurately and grasps it precisely), mild (grade 1: just misses to grasp the thumb by no more than 3 inches and locates correctly within 5 s), moderate (grade 2: is able to find the arm and then uses the arm to find the thumb), or severe proprioception deficits (grade 3: cannot climb up the affected arm to locate his/her thumb). The final TLT score was determined according to the worst performance.

Gains in UE motor recovery

To determine whether the delivery of rPMS before PT is more effective for motor recovery than conventional PT alone, improvements in UE function were compared. Because intersubject differences in sessions during intervention depend on the timing of hospital discharge, gains must be corrected for comparisons between the two groups. Thus, the duration-corrected gains in motor recovery or TLT are expressed as ⊿UE function or ⊿TLT per session, namely ⊿UE function scores divided by number of sessions and then compared between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical data and the Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric data using SPSS version 26. The results were considered statistically significant at p ≦ 0.05. Normality of the outcome measures was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Given the small sample size and the confirmed non-normality of the primary outcome measures (WMFT-FAS, TLT, and multiple FMA-UE subscales, with p values ranging from 0.002 to 0.045 for the non-normal variables), non-parametric methods were prioritized. Accordingly, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for group comparisons.

Descriptive statistics and consistency

Based on the small sample size of this pilot study and the confirmed non-normality in the primary outcome measures (WMFT-FAS and TLT), Median and Interquartile Range (IQR) were uniformly adopted for the descriptive statistics of all continuous and scale variables used in this study, with the exception of the Mean of intervention duration used for context.

Primary efficacy analysis

The primary efficacy analysis utilized the Mann–Whitney U test to compare the duration-corrected gain between the rPMS and control groups.

Sensitivity analysis (ANCOVA)

To test the robustness of the primary findings against potential confounding factors, an Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) was performed on the FMA-UE Total Post-score. The model included Group (rPMS vs. control) as a fixed factor, and age, sex, baseline FMA-UE score, baseline FMA-wrist score, and total number of intervention sessions as covariates.

Effect size reporting

Acknowledging that p values are heavily dependent on sample size, the effect size (r value) was calculated for the Mann–Whitney U test to measure the magnitude of the treatment effect: r = Z/√N​, where N is the total number of samples and Z is derived from the Mann‒Whitney U test46,47. Values of r = 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5 were interpreted as small, moderate, and large effect sizes, respectively.
