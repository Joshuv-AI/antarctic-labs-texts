Berberine inhibits colorectal liver metastasis via modulation of TGF-β in a cecum transplant mouse model
Authors: Yong-Hwi Kang, Jing-Hua Wang, Jin-Seok Lee, Seung-Ju Hwang, Nam-Hun Lee, Chang-Gue Son
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic metastasis is the primary cause of colorectal cancer (CRC)-induced death. Our previous results showed the anti-metastatic effects of Coptidis rhizoma using in vitro model.AimThe present study aimed to investigate whether berberine, the main active compound of C. rhizoma, inhibits colon–liver metastasis in an animal model, and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsMurine colon carcinoma (CT26) tumor tissue was implanted into the cecum of balb/c mice with/without oral administration of berberine (100 mg/kg) for 21 days, after which liver metastasis was evaluated. In addition, the pharmacological actions of berberine were explored using 5-fluorouracil-resistant human colon cancer cells (HCT116/R).ResultThe administration of berberine significantly decreased the number of tumor nodules in the liver, while significant activation of E-cadherin (an epithelial marker), and suppression of vimentin, Snail and TGF-β (mesenchymal markers) were observed in primary colon tumor tissues. Berberine treatment also notably lowered the levels of inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, TNF- α, IL-6 and IL-1β) in the blood. In HCT116/R cells, berberine significantly inhibited migration and invasion and modulated the expression of TGF-β and representative molecules related to its pathway. The results obtained with a TGF-β inhibitor (SB431542) and a TGF-β siRNA, strongly suggest that the mechanism of action of berberine is linked to TGF-β signaling.ConclusionIn conclusion, berberine evidently possess an anti-colon–liver metastatic effect, and its underlying mechanisms involve the inhibition of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the TGF-β signaling pathway. Thus, we cautiously propose the pharmacological potential of berberine in drug research studies targeting hepatic metastasis from CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02122-w.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Metastasis, 5-Fluorouracil, EMT, Berberine, TGF-β, Drug resistance

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) has emerged as the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. In 2020, there were approximately 1.9 million new CRC diagnoses, with 935,000 reported deaths attributed to the disease [2]. Favorable clinical outcomes rely on early diagnosis and preemptive treatment, however, this is difficult for patients with CRC because of absence of distinct phenotypic symptoms [3]. Approximately 10–25% of CRC cases exhibit notably high metastatic potential at the time of initial diagnosis, and almost 70% of CRC cases are prone to metastasize to distant sites during the entire treatment process, especially the liver, owing to the blood flow system of the portal vein [4]. CRC patients who are diagnosed with hepatic metastasis have a radically poor prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate that dramatically decreases to 10–20%, in stark contrast to the 80–90% survival rate observed in patients with localized CRC [5].

Metastasis is a multi-step process that involves primary tumor cell intravasation, movement through the circulation, extravasation, and final colonization in distant organs [6]. One pivotal phenomenon of this cascade is known as epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), which plays a critical role in allowing tumor cells to enter the initial step of metastasis [7]. During EMT, tumor cells undergo dissolution of tight junctions via the loss of epithelial markers, such as E-cadherin [8]. This process disrupts apical–basal polarity and triggers a reconfiguration of the cytoskeletal architecture through the expression of mesenchymal markers such as vimentin, which enables cells to develop an invasive phenotype [9]. Extracellular stimuli derived from microenvironmental signals, such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, or inflammatory cytokines, abnormally regulate EMT in cancer cells [10]. During metastasis, TGF-β induces EMT in cancer cells and modify the tumor microenvironment into a metastasis-favorable environment [11]. One clinical study reported that 78% of colorectal cancer patients with liver metastasis had a higher level of TGF-β than the baseline limit in plasma [12].

As pharmaceutical resources for anti-metastasis, some medicinal herbs such as Coptidis rhizoma (C. rhizoma), Tripterygium wilfordii, and Ginseng panax have been studied, especially for their ability to inhibit EMT [13–15]. We previously demonstrated the anti-metastatic properties of C. rhizoma in 5-FU-resistant HCT116 cells (HCT116/R) characterized by EMT [16]. C. rhizoma has been shown to have anti-tumor effects on liver and colorectal cancers in both in vivo and in vitro models [17, 18]. Berberine, a prominent compound in C. rhizoma, is well-known for its diverse therapeutic effects against inflammation, infections, diabetes, and cancers [19–21]. One study also demonstrated the anti-metastatic efficacy of berberine for colon cancer to liver metastasis in a splenic injection model. This study showed the modulation of mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET) in liver tissue [22].

Herein we investigated the pharmaceutical effect of berberine on the tumor microenvironment, with a focus on inhibiting colon–liver metastasis. We employed a cecum-transplantation model, which serves as a spontaneous metastatic model, to examine the impacts of berberine on EMT-related processes.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

RPMI1640 medium, Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), fetal bovine serum (FBS), trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and penicillin–streptomycin solution were obtained from WELGENE (Daegu, Korea); E-cadherin, vimentin, Snail, p-Akt, Akt, TGF-β and p38 were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); p-p38 was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA); α-tubulin was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA); water-soluble tetrazolium salt (WST)-8 cell viability assay kit was purchased from DoGen (Seoul, Korea); SB431542 and berberine were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA); bovine serum albumin was obtained from GenDEPOT (Katy, TX, USA); n-butanol was purchased from JT Baker (Mexico City, Mexico). Secondary horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibodies were purchased from GeneTex, Inc. (Irvine, CA, USA); TGF-β siRNA and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA); TGF-β1, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β ELISA kits were obtained (R&D system MN, USA and BD Biosciences for human cells, CA, USA).

Animals

Specific pathogen-free balb/c mice (male, 6 weeks old, 19–21 g) were purchased from a commercial animal breeder (Daehan-Biolink, Choong-buk, South Korea) and adapted for 1 week before use. The animals were housed in an environmentally maintained at 22 ± 2 °C under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. Mouse were freely fed pallet food (AgriPurina; Cargill, Gyeonggido, Republic of Korea) with tap water. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Daejeon University (DJUARB 2022-002) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals prepared by the US National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Orthotopic mouse model of hepatic metastasis

CT26 cells (5 × 106/200 μl) were injected into the subcutaneous layers of the flanks of the two balb/c mice. Once the tumor size exceeded 1 cm, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were removed and stored in Earle’s minimum essential medium at 4 °C. Before implantation, each tumor was washed twice with antibiotic-containing Earle’s minimum essential medium for at least 10 min to prevent contamination and infection. After the removal of non-cancerous and necrotic tissues from the tumor, the remaining cancer tissues were divided into pieces of approximately 20 mg. In total 10 mice were randomly divided into two groups (each with five mice). A piece of tumor tissue was sutured into the cecum of each of the 10 mice via a 6–0 suture under anesthetization using a ketamine/xylazine mixture (90/10 mg/kg for each mouse). After tumor implantation, the abdominal wall was closed in two layers with a 4–0 sized suture. The groups of mice were intragastrically administered either berberine (100 mg/kg) or distilled water daily. After 21 days, the animals were euthanized in a CO2 chamber and the number of metastatic tumors was calculated. All the primary tumor tissues were separated and weighed.

Histological analysis

To evaluate histology, liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin solution. The formalin in the tissue was removed by storing it in 10, 20, and 30% sucrose solutions in a stepwise manner, and then cryosections of the tissue were performed using a Leica CM3050 cryostat (Leica Microsystems). The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Sigma Aldrich, MO, United States). The staining slides were visualized using microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at 100× (H&E) magnifications, respectively.

ELISA assay

Blood was collected following the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Serum was collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The concentration level of inflammatory cytokines TGF-β (DY1679), TNF-α (DY410), IL-6 (DY406) and IL-1β (DY401) were determined in mouse serum using ELISA kits (R&D Systems). The entire process followed the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a UV spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices).

Immunofluorescence assay

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as follows. Briefly, the primary tumors were gradually cryoprotected in 10, 20, and 30% sucrose for 24 h each and embedded subsequently in an optimal cutting temperature compound (Leica Microsystems, Bensheim, Germany) in liquid nitrogen. They were cut into frozen coronal sections (8 µm) using a cryostat (CM3050_S, Leica) and stored free floating in the buffer. After being washed with ice-cold PBS, parallel free-floating sections were treated with blocking buffer (5% normal chicken serum in PBS and 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h at 4 °C) and incubated with anti-mouse E-cadherin monoclonal (1:200, 14472S, and Cell Signaling), anti-rabbit vimentin monoclonal (1:200, 5741S, and Cell Signaling) primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with ice-cold PBS, the sections were incubated with a goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (1:400; Alexa Fluor 488, ab150113), goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (1:400; Alexa Fluor 594, ab150080), and goat anti-mouse (1:400; Alexa Fluor 594, ab150116) secondary antibodies for 2 h at 4 °C. After washing the sections were treated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:1000; D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) to stain the cell nuclei. HCT116/WT and HCT116/R cells were plated in 12-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 cells per well and treated with berberine (5 μg/ml) for 48 h. The cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and blocked with chicken serum for 15 min. Then, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to the following: E-cadherin (1:200, 14472S, and Cell Signaling), vimentin (1:200, 5741S, and Cell Signaling), and TGF-β (1:200, 3711S, and Cell Signaling). After three-time washes with DPBS, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies following: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated rabbit (1:400, Ab150077, and Abcam) and anti-mouse (1:400, ab150113, and Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were observed using an Olympus IX71 microscope with an Olympus DP74 digital camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell culture and establishment of 5-FU-resistant cells

The human colon carcinoma cell line HCT116 was incubated in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. To construct a 5-FU resistant cell line (HCT116/R), we referred to the protocol described in a previous study. Briefly, HCT116 (HCT116/WT) cells, a wild-type cell line, were treated with 5-FU repeatedly for 10 months, and the degree of 5-FU resistance of the HCT116/R cells was evaluated by WST-8 assay. According to a previous study, when the survival rate of the HCT116 cells was less than 80%, the dose of 5-FU was maintained. Otherwise, the dose of 5-FU was gradually increased from 2 to 40 µM. Before use in the experiments, HCT116/R cells were maintained in medium without 5-FU for one week. Finally, RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5% FBS was used in all in vitro experiments except for the 3D invasion assay.

Transfection of cells with siRNA targeting TGF-β

The TGF-β siRNA were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). The following TGF-β siRNA primer sequences: F;5′-CCCACAACGAAAUCUAUGATT-3′, R;3′-UCAUAGAUUUCGUUGUGGGTT-5′. When the HCT116/R cells were grown to 80% confluence, cells were transfected with TGF-β siRNA (10 nM) of the indicated oligonucleotide using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 24 h. After 48 h, the HCT116/R cells were used for wound healing and 3D invasion assays.

Cell viability assay

HCT116/R cells were plated in 96-well microplates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well and treated with various concentrations of berberine (1, 2.5, 5, and 10 µg/mL) for 48 h. Then, the cytotoxicity of berberine was assessed using a WST-8 assay. The absorbance was determined at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (Molecular Merck Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Western blotting analysis

HCT116/WT, HCT116/R and knock-downed HCT116/R for TGF-β cells were plated in 60 pi cell culture dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and were cultured with/without berberine (5 µg/mL) and SB431542 (10 µM, a TGF-β inhibitor) for 48 h. The inhibitory efficacies of SB431542 and TGF-β siRNA were evaluated by western blotting (Figure S3). Total protein was obtained by ProPrep™ protein extraction solution (iNtRON Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea), and the protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The proteins were separated by 7.5 and 12% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After 1 h of blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific for the following proteins: E-cadherin (1:1000, 14472S, Cell Signaling), vimentin (1:1000, 5741S, Cell Signaling), Akt (1:1000, 9272S, Cell Signaling), p-Akt (1:1000, 9271S, Cell Signaling), Snail (1:1000S, 3789S, Cell Signaling), TGF-β (1:1000, 3711S, Cell Signaling), p38 (1:1000, 9212S, Cell Signaling), p-p38 (1:1000, SC166182, Santa Cruz) and α-tubulin (1:1000, ab7291, Abcam). After three-time washes, the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-mouse (GeneTex, GTX213111-01) or anti-rabbit (GeneTex, GTX213110-01) secondary antibody (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. The results were detected with an advanced enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit, and the band intensities were quantified using Image J (Version 1.46, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Gene expression was qualified by real-time PCR. HCT116/WT or HCT116/R cells were plated at 3 × 105 cells per 60 pi cell culture dish and then cultured with berberine (5 µg/mL) for 24 h. Total mRNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH, USA), and cDNA was then synthesized using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The primers sequences were as follows: E-cadherin (forward: 5′- CGA GAG CTA CAC GTT CAC GG-3′), reverse: 5′-GGG TGT CGA GGG AAA AAT AGG-3′), vimentin (forward: 5′-TCT GGA TTC ACT CCC TCT GGT-3′), reverse: 5′-CGT GAT GCT GAG AAG TTT CGT-3′), and GAPDH (forward: 5′- CAT GGC CTT CCG TGT TCC T′), reverse: 5′-CCT GCT TCA CCA CCT TCT TGA-3′). Rotor gene Q software from QIAGEN (Hilden, Germany). The expression of E-cadherin and vimentin was normalized to GAPDH.

Wound healing assay

HCT116/WT, HCT116/R and knock-downed HCT116/R for TGF-β cells were plated in 12-well plates at a density 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated until they reached confluence. The cell monolayer was scratched by a sterile 20-μL pipette tip. Then, the cell debris was washed with DPBS two-times. After treatment with berberine (5 µg/mL) with/without SB431542 (10 µM) for 24 h, image collection was conducted using an Olympus IX71 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at a magnification of 100×. The observations were quantified using ImageJ.

3D invasion assay

HCT116/WT, HCT116/R and knock-downed HCT116/R for TGF-β were labeled with 5 μL of DiI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The cells were subsequently plated on Matrigel (BD Biosciences) in 200 μL of RPMI 1640 without FBS in Transwell plates (8 μm pore size, Corning, Acton, MA) at a density 3 × 104 cells. Additionally, 800 μL of RPMI1640 with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 12 h of incubation, berberine (5 μg/mL) with/without SB431542 (10 µM) was added to the Transwell. After 48 h, the gels were sectioned and the numbers of invasive cells by were analyzed via an Olympus IX71 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

In the present study, all the data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were statistically analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey test using SPSS statistics software (version 20.0 SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences were considered statistically significant when the p value was less than 0.05 or 0.01.

Results

Berberine inhibits hepatic metastasis in an orthotopic CRC mouse model

The anti-hepatic metastasis effect of berberine was determined by measuring the number of metastatic tumors in the liver of an orthotopic CRC mouse model. Compared with the vehicle, berberine (100 mg/kg) significantly reduced the number of metastatic tumors (p < 0.05, Fig. 1A–C). Moreover, the levels of four inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) in mouse blood serum were notably decreased (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01, Fig. 1D–G).

Anti-metastatic effects of berberine in an orthotopic CRC mouse model. After the implantation of CT26 tumor tissue in the cecum (20 mg), berberine (100 mg/kg, from 3 to 21 days) was orally administered. Naked-eye photos (A) and histological analysis (B) of liver tissue are displayed. The number of tumors that metastasized to the liver was counted (C). The levels of four inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β) were determined using ELISA kits (D–G). The data are expressed as the means ± SD (n = 5, n = 3). *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01 indicate statistical significance compared with the vehicle group

Berberine inhibits epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition of hepatic metastasis

The mechanisms underlying the anti-hepatic and metastatic effects of berberine were determined in primary tumors implanted in the cecum. Compared with the vehicle, berberine (100 mg/kg) resulted in high protein expression of E-cadherin but lower expression of vimentin, TGF-β and Snail in primary tumor tissue (p < 0.01, Fig. 2A, B). In addition, immunofluorescence was used to evaluate the protein expression levels of E-cadherin and vimentin (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C–E).

EMT-related molecules in an orthotopic CRC mouse model. The protein levels of E-cadherin, vimentin, TGF-β and Snail in primary cecum tissue were analyzed using western blotting (A) and quantified by ImageJ software (B). The expression levels of E-cadherin and vimentin were visualized by fluorescence microscopy (C, D) and data were quantified (E). The data are expressed as the means ± SD (n = 3). **p < 0.01 indicates statistical significance compared with the vehicle group

Berberine inhibits EMT characteristics of HCT116/R cells

Treatment with berberine (up to 5 µg/mL) did not reduce the proliferation of HCT116/R cells (p < 0.01, Figure S1). At the 24 h time point of the wound-healing assay, HCT116/R cells migrated to fill 86.5% of the initial gap (0 h), and this migration distance was significantly greater than that covered by HCT116/WT cells, which filled 17.9% of the initial gap at (0 h). This accelerated migration of HCT116/R cells was significantly inhibited by berberine (44.5%, 5 µg/mL) compared with that of non-berberine treated cells (p < 0.01, Fig. 3A, B). In addition, compared with no treatment, berberine (5 µg/mL) treatment also suppressed the invasion of HCT116/R cells in the 3D invasion assay (p < 0.01, Fig. 3C, D).

Metastatic behaviors and EMT of HCT116/R cells. The effects of berberine (5 µg/mL) on migration in a wound-healing assay (A, B) and invasion of HCT116/R cells were assessed by a 3D invasion assay (C, D). EMT-related protein levels (E, F) and gene expression levels (E-cadherin and vimentin) were measured (G) with their immunofluorescence finding (H, I). The data are expressed as the means ± SD value (n = 3). ##p < 0.01, compared with HCT116/WT cells. *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01 compared with HCT116/R cells

The mechanisms of anti-metastatic effect of berberine were confirmed in vitro. Compared with HCT116/WT cells, HCT116/R cells showed lower E-cadherin expression but higher vimentin and Snail expression (p < 0.01, Fig. 3E, F). Similar patterns of E-cadherin and vimentin expression were observed when their mRNA expression was measured (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01, Fig. 3G) and when immunofluorescence was used to assess their protein expression levels (Fig. 3H, I).

Berberine inhibits EMT via the TGF-β signaling pathway in HCT116/R cells

Compared with those in HCT116/WT cells, the protein levels of TGF-β and the phosphorylation of Akt and p38 were notably increased in HCT116/R cells. In contrast, treatment with berberine (5 µg/mL) significantly suppressed their protein levels (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A, B). In addition, the intensity of the immunofluorescence analysis of TGF-β in HCT116/R cells was reduced by treatment with berberine (5 µg/mL) than after treatment with the vehicle (Fig. 4C).

Confirmation of the effect of berberine on TGF-β signaling. Protein expression of major molecules involved in TGF-β signaling in HCT116 cells (A, B) along with TGF-β expression using immunofluorescence (C). Effects of berberine (5 µg/mL) and SB431542 (10 µM) or knockdown of TGF-β on cell migration and invasion (D, E) and quantification of these data (F, G). The data are expressed as the means ± SD value (n = 3). ##p < 0.01, compared with HCT116/WT cells. **p < 0.01 compared with HCT116/R cells

The inhibitory effects of berberine (5 µg/mL) on the migration and invasion of HCT116/R cells were consistently observed following treatment with SB431542 (10 µM) or TGF-β siRNA. Moreover, co-treatment with SB431542 or TGF-β siRNA did not significantly enhance the effects of berberine, further supporting the role of TGF-β signaling in the mechanism of action of berberine (p < 0.01, Fig. 4D–G).

Discussion

In our previous study, we reported partial observations indicating that C. rhizoma exhibits anti-EMT properties by inhibiting the TGF-β pathway in the colon cell line, HCT116/R [16]. In this study, we further investigated whether berberine, the main active compound of C. rhizoma, inhibits colon–liver metastasis in an animal model. We employed a surgical orthotopic cecum-implanted design using CT26 tumor tissue. This experimental model mimics the full steps of EMT and metastasis in human CRC [23]. The blood flow between GI-tract organs and the liver through the portal vein, leads to liver metastasis from colon–rectal tumors in approximately 50% of colorectal cancer patients worldwide [24, 25]. One clinical study showed that 35% of colon cancer patients who underwent a complete resection experienced liver metastasis within 40 months [26].

All the mice in the vehicle group had metastatic nodules in the liver 3 weeks after implantation, while the administration of berberine significantly reduced the number of metastatic tumor nodules in the liver (Fig. 1A–C). Unexpectedly, berberine treatment did not affect the weight of primary tumors in the cecum (Figure S2), indicating that the anti-metastatic effect of berberine may not be attributed to a reduction in primary tumor growth but rather to its influence on the specific progression and microenvironment of metastasis. Cancer metastasis is a multi-step process that includes invasion, migration, intravasation, circulation, extravasation, and tumor colonization [6]. In this process, EMT plays a critical role, especially in the initial step of metastasis such as local dissemination and intravasation [27]. We found that cecum tumor tissues treated with berberine presented notable changes in the expression of both E-cadherin and vimentin (Fig. 2A, B). These two proteins are the classical molecular parameters for indicating EMT [28]. The actions of berberine to control EMT was also demonstrated via both gene and protein expression and immunofluorescence assay in HCT116/R cells, in which both E-cadherin and vimentin were inversely affected by berberine treatment (Fig. 3E, F).

Furthermore, the administration of berberine significantly lowered the levels of TGF-β in both blood and primary tumors implanted in the cecum. In addition, as a downstream molecule of the TGF-β signal, the Snail molecule, a key transcription factor that targets E-cadherin and vimentin [29]. TGF-β is an important cytokine that accelerates tumor angiogenesis, growth and metastasis [30]. A clinical meta-analysis reported that a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.68 was calculated between TGF-β expression and overall survival in CRC patients [31]. TGF-β induces EMT in cancer cells and modifies the tumor microenvironment into a metastasis-favorable circumstances [32–34]. TGFβ plays a role as one of the critical regulators in the inflammatory circumstance that orchestrates the tumor microenvironment and metastasis [35, 36]. As expected, the administration of berberine significantly lowered the levels of three representative proinflammatory cytokines in the blood, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig. 1D–G). Increased concentrations of serum TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β are closely associated with liver metastasis and survival in patients with CRC [37, 38]. From the current data, we anticipate that berberine has a pharmaceutical activity to manipulate the EMT process in tumors and metastasis-related microenvironments, mainly via the modulation of TGF-β expression. The action of berberine on TGF-β-mediated EMT and metastasis was verified from the metastasis-related behavior assays (migration and invasion) using a human colon cancer cell line with 5-FU resistance (HCT116/R) (Fig. 3A–D). Chemoresistant tumor exhibit increased expression of TGF-β and increased metastatic behaviors in CRC and other cancers [39, 40]. We previously confirmed the shifting behavior of HCT116/R cells toward EMT and metastasis compared with that of HCT116/WT cells [16, 41]. In the present study, berberine treatment suppressed the expression of TGF-β and its downstream molecules in HCT116/R cells (Fig. 4A–C). Furthermore, we confirmed the pharmacological effect of berberine via the modulation of TGF-β using both a TGF-β inhibitor (SB431542) and a siRNA against TGF-β (Fig. 4D–G). Currently, many agents that target TGF-β such as vactosertib and galunisertib, have achieved satisfactory clinical efficacy [42].

Berberine is one of the major active compounds in C. rhizoma. In fact, in our previous study, when we compared the anti-drug resistance activities of berberine with those of three other major ingredients of C. rhizome namely, coptisine, jatrorrhizine, and palmatine, we found that berberine had the most effective activity in CRC cell lines [41]. Based on these findings, we selected berberine for this study. Berberine has been studied extensively as an herb-derived resource for treating CRC in various animal and cell-derived models. Its therapeutic targets involve the modulation of angiogenesis [43], apoptosis [44], migration and invasion [45]. Similarly, a recent cell-model study demonstrated the anti-metastatic efficacy of berberine through the TGF-β/Smad and NF-κB pathways in colon cancer cell lines [46]. However, no studies have investigated the role of berberine in inhibiting EMT and, consequently, its impact on hepatic metastasis from CRC in vivo.

Our present data provide strong support for berberine as a candidate with the potential to inhibit the metastatic behavior of CRC, at least colon–liver metastasis. One clinical study showed the positive efficacy of berberine against radiation-induced lung fibrosis in patients with lung cancer, along with a lowered level of TGF-β in the blood [47]. TGF-β is well known as a typical profibrogenic cytokine in various tissues, including the liver, lung, and skin [48, 49]. These data strongly support that the main action of berberine is linked to modulation of TGF-β. However, our present study has several limitations, such as the use of only one cell line in an animal model and insufficient information regarding the mechanism by which berberine interacts with TGF-β. To address these limitations, we plan further experiments, which will encompass multiple animal studies employing human tumor-derived models, in addition to an in-depth investigation into the interaction between berberine and TGF-β. Furthermore, it is crucial to explore the role of berberine in mesenchymal–epithelial transition (MET), which represents another facet of the metastatic process.

Conclusions

Taken together, we revealed that berberine evidently possesses the potential to inhibit colon–liver metastasis via the suppression of EMT in an orthotopic mouse model. The underlying mechanisms involve the modulation of EMT via of the TGF-β pathway. We hopefully believe that berberine can be a pharmaceutical candidate for reducing the risk of colon–liver metastasis in the future.
