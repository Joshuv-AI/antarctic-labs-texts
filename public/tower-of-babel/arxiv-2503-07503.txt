Think Before You Segment: High-Quality Reasoning Segmentation with GPT Chain of Thoughts
Shiu-hong Kao, Yu-Wing Tai, Chi-Keung Tang

Abstract
Reasoning segmentation is a challenging vision-language task that aims to output the segmentation mask with respect to a complex, implicit, and even non-visual query text. Previous works incorporated multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) with segmentation models to approach the difficult problem. However, their segmentation quality often falls short in complex cases, particularly when dealing with out-of-domain objects with intricate structures, blurry boundaries, occlusions, or high similarity with surroundings. In this paper, we introduce ThinkFirst , a training-free reasoning segmentation framework that leverages GPT’s chain of thought to address these challenging cases. Our approach allows GPT-4o or other powerful MLLMs to generate a detailed, chain-of-thought description of an image. This summarized description is then passed to a language-instructed segmentation assistant to aid the segmentation process. Our framework allows users to easily interact with the segmentation agent using multimodal inputs, such as easy text and image scribbles, for successive refinement or communication. We evaluate the performance of ThinkFirst on diverse objects. Extensive experiments show that, this zero-shot-CoT approach significantly improves the vanilla reasoning segmentation agent, both qualitatively and quantitatively, while being less sensitive or critical to user-supplied prompts after Thinking First. Code will be released at github.com/DanielSHKao/ThinkFirst .

1
Introduction

Reasoning segmentation is an emerging vision-language approach that requires the pertinent model to generate precise image segmentation masks of objects of interest, given an implicit text queries like
“What is the largest object in the image? Please segment it.”
Unlike traditional vision-language tasks that directly associate visual data with textual descriptions, reasoning segmentation requires advanced cognitive capabilities. This is because the implicit nature of the queries often demands models to interpret nuanced instructions and engage in higher-order reasoning to accurately delineate relevant regions within the image.

Figure 3
:
ThinkFirst produces accurate results (right), while LISA fails to generate reasonable masks (middle) when given little information, especially for camouflaged objects or underwater images with severe color shift and blurry object boundaries.

In this paper, we propose
ThinkFirst
, a simple yet effective framework that utilizes chain-of-thought (CoT) to engage in such higher-level reasoning, leading to higher-quality segmentation.
Instead of directly feeding the user query and the image to a reasoning segmentation model, ThinkFirst
first

thoroughly analyzes the image with
chain of thoughts
using carefully designed prompts. Then, based on the CoT responses, a detailed summary of the image is generated, which is combined with the user query to guide the reasoning segmentation model (See Figure
1
).
To our knowledge, ThinkFirst is one of the first to use CoTs for general, zero-shot reasoning segmentation, given text and/or image scribbles queries. This zero-shot-CoT approach is training-free and is compatible with any reasoning segmentation modules, which may be applied as a plug-in to further improve their results.

Recently, Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs)
[
1
,
24
,
55
,
60
]
have been widely used to solve reasoning segmentation due to their excellence in image understanding
[
28
]
, complex reasoning
[
8
]
and instruction following
[
41
]
. These models have shown remarkable capabilities in interpreting and processing visual data alongside textual inputs, making them ideal for tasks that require both visual and linguistic understanding. To generate an image-based output, previous works often directly connect an MLLM with the auto-decoder architecture of pre-trained visual backbone, e.g., SAM
[
19
]
for reasoning segmentation. The representative work LISA
[
23
]
expands the vocabulary of MLLM with a segmentation-specific token and directly sends its latent features
to a mask decoder to guide the decoder with cross-attention mechanism,
which can be regarded as a strong MLLM + SAM baseline without CoT reasoning.

Despite impressive results to date,
they mainly focus on images with a primary foreground or objects with clear boundaries, while still struggling to output reasonable masks for complex images with multiple objects, especially when the target has similar colors to its surroundings, with ambiguous boundaries or boundaries blending into the background, see Figures
2
to
5
, indicating a higher level of visual understanding is necessary for resolving ambiguities and delineating subtle yet important features.

To improve the accuracy of reasoning segmentation, a concurrent work Seg-Zero
[
30
]
leverages a reinforcement learning approach to incorporate CoT reasoning into the multimodal LLM model. However, due to the requirement of a bounding box prior, Seg-Zero fails to generate a mask for multiple target objects (Refer to Figure
4
). Additionally, the notorious time cost for RL-based approaches makes the Seg-Zero less favorable for downstream applications. In this context, we claim that a training-free framework for high-quality and zero-shot reasoning segmentation is still needed to address the limitations of existing methods.

To validate our CoT approach showcasing ThinkFirst is a clear winner in challenging cases
not adequately covered by conventional datasets,
we qualitatively and quantitatively compare the segmentation quality in difficult camouflaged images using COD-10K
[
12
]
and underwater images using Marine Video Kit
[
45
]
without fine-tuning.
We also evaluate the generalization ability of ThinkFirst on diverse out-of-domain data, from modern architecture, mid-century furniture with bold design, to application on “Where’s Waldo” images. Results show that ThinkFirst indeed significantly improves the reasoning segmentation quality in difficult scenarios and given highly implicit user prompts.

Multimodal Large Language Model

Inspired by the remarkable reasoning ability of Large Language Models (LLM), Multimodal LLMs (MLLM) aim to incorporate visual modules for multimodal perception and reasoning, typically used to generate captions or answer questions for visual inputs. Training-free approaches

.

The unprecedented reasoning ability of MLLM has been examined on diverse vision-language tasks
[

,

]
. By connecting with a suitable vision decoder, MLLM can also be applied to traditional vision-centric tasks, such as image segmentation
[

,

,

, object detection

2.2
Image Segmentation

Semantic segmentation is an important computer vision task that assigns a class label to every pixel of a given image. Early methods primarily relied on graphical models like conditional random fields (CRFs)
[
5
,
22
]
and region growing
[
10
]
. Over the past decade, various model architectures have been proposed to effectively extract the semantic information and improve the state-of-the-art performance, such as encoder-decoder structure
[
2
]
, dilated
convolution
[
57
]
, pyramid pooling module
[
63
]
, and non-local operator
[
29
]
. Follow-up works on instance segmentation
[
7
,
14
]
and panoptic
segmentation
[
6
,
18
]
further advanced the instance-level image segmentation.

Recently, the Segment Anything Model (SAM)
[
19
]
has revolutionized image segmentation. By training with billions of images and masks, SAM serves as a foundation segmentation model, enabling promptable segmentation with multimodal inputs like points and bounding boxes. This inspires researchers to contribute to numerous works of reasoning segmentation. By incorporating SAM with multimodal LLM, subsequent works
[
23
,
52
,
59
,
13
]
showcase strong capabilities of generating the mask with respect to a complex or implicit text description. However, while SAM has shown outstanding generalization ability in diverse scenarios, it still often fails in challenging images with intricate structures, blurry boundaries, occlusions, or high similarity with surroundings
[
12
]
without careful or additional prompt considerations. By leveraging chain of thoughts from MLLMs, we found that the robustness of SAM can be substantially improved especially in challenging cases. Specifically, we propose to let LLM “think” autonomously before making decisions, instead of directly generating segmentation tokens to interact with SAM.

2.3
Chain of Thoughts

CoT aims at improving the reasoning performance of LLMs via a series of intermediate reasoning steps
[
50
,
48
]
. Existing CoT methods typically focus on inference with progressive reasoning demonstrations
[
61
,
50
,
35
]
or explicit step-by-step prompts
[
21
]
. However, in the context of multimodal LLMs, these approaches often fail to yield significant improvements due to model capacity constraints. Multimodal LLMs usually acquire CoT capabilities through fine-tuning on specially constructed multimodal CoT datasets
[
39
,
62
,
32
]
or integrating complex intermediate representations like graphs
[
38
]
and code
[
44
]
, thereby limiting their accessibility and scalability. In this work, we aim to bypasses these limitations by approaching training-free CoT prompts of multimodal LLM for reasoning segmentation.

Multimodal LLMs with CoT are recently integrated for reasoning segmentation. CoReS
[
3
]
attempts to solve reasoning segmentation with CoT, while the MLLM framework fails to generate a detailed chain of thoughts for deep comprehension. Rather, it focused on decomposing the segmentation problem into two successive tasks: localization and mask decoding. Therefore, it still requires a detailed user text prompt to achieve high segmentation quality. A concurrent work Seg-Zero
[
30
]
utilizes a reinforcement learning-based approach to train the reasoning model. While achieving impressive reasoning capability, Seg-zero requires a long training process and an intermediate bounding box, which limits the model from segmenting multiple objects within an image. In contrast, our proposed ThinkFirst is training-free, utilizing zero-shot understanding and reasoning capability from GPT-4o.

3.1
Preliminary: Reasoning Segmentation Agent

Given an image
I
and a textual query
q
, a reasoning segmentation agent aims to output the binary mask
\hat{\mathrm{M}}(I,q)
corresponding to the best object instances that
q
refers to.

A reasoning segmentation agent usually consists of three modules: a vision encoder
E
, a mask decoder
\mathcal{D}
, and a vision-language model
\mathcal{F}
. During each inference, the image
I
will be first encoded by the visual encoder and generate a visual-based feature
\mathbf{f}
.
Simultaneously, the image
I
is combined with the prompt
q
and fed into the model
\mathcal{F}
for a segmentation-purposed representation
\mathbf{h}
. Finally, the visual-based feature
\mathbf{f}
and the segmentation-purposed representation
\mathbf{h}
are fed to the mask decoder to generate outputs via a series of attention-based operations. The entire process can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=E(I),

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}=\mathcal{F}(I,q),

(2)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathrm{M}}=\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{f}).

(3)

In practice, the vision encoder
E
and mask decoder

\mathcal{D}
usually inherit the same architecture from SAM
[
65
]
, where the vision encoder is frozen and the mask decoder requires further fine-tuning. The vision-language model
\mathcal{F}
is fine-tuned with LoRA
[
15
]
.

3.2
ThinkFirst Overall Framework

Instead of directly feeding the input image
I
and the user prompt
q
to the reasoning segmentation agent, we first utilize GPT-4o
[
16
]
for CoT pre-processing. The prompts for GPT-4o consists of two parts: environment prompt
P_{e}
and task-specific prompt
P_{t}
, where environment prompt is universal and task-specific prompt varies by the problem settings. These prompts are combined and fed to GPT-4o for a long chain of thoughts
\{(Q_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In specific, for the first pair of question and answer, the generative process can be formulated as

\begin{split}Q_{1}&=\mathrm{GPT}(P_{e},P_{t},I),\\
A_{1}&=\mathrm{GPT}(P_{e},P_{t},I|Q_{1}).\end{split}

(4)

The follow-up chain of thoughts can be formulated as

\begin{split}Q_{k}&=\mathrm{GPT}(P_{e},P_{t},I|Q_{1},A_{1},...,Q_{k-1},A_{k-1}),\\
A_{k}&=\mathrm{GPT}(P_{e},P_{t},I|Q_{1},A_{1},...,Q_{k-1},A_{k-1},Q_{k}),\end{split}

(5)

where
k\in\{2,3,...,n\}
. Notably, the actual value of
n
is sampled from a probabilistic distribution. After generating the chain of thoughts, GPT-4o will generate a short paragraph of informative summary
S
. This process can be written as

S=\mathrm{GPT}\left(P_{e},P_{t},I|Q_{1},A_{1},...,Q_{n},A_{n}\right)

(6)

Finally, we concatenate the summary
S
with the user query
q
, generating the predictive binary mask
\hat{\mathrm{M}}(I,S\oplus q)
with reasoning segmentation agent such as LISA
[
23
]
.

3.3
ThinkFirst Prompt Details

GPT-4o setup.
To maintain the stability of ThinkFirst among all inferences, we set the temperature of GPT-4o to 0.5, and use the following context
“You are a helpful assistant that answers question as simple as possible.”
. We limit the maximal number of output tokens to 2,000, such that GPT-4o does not generate redundant descriptions.

Environment prompt.
The environment prompt
P_{e}
aims to guide the behaviors of the GPT-4o. We use the following prompt to set up the environment for GPT-4o:

“You will serve as an agent for language-based image segmentation model. During each inference, your task is to describe a given image with chain of thoughts. You need to provide as many details as possible to help the segmentation model understand the image better. The target objects may contain multiple layers, be blocked by other objects, or be seamlessly embedded in their surroundings. Your description will be later sent to the segmentation as prompt. For example, if given an image, you need to describe what can be seen in the image, the number of objects for each categories, the position of each object, the structure of the object, the number of layers of the object, etc. The actual description depends on the given image.

For the output, you need to follow the format:

-
<
Question 1
>
:
<
Answer 1
>
.

-
<
Question 2
>
:
<
Answer 2
>
.

…, etc, where each pair of prompt and answer implies the chain of thoughts, i.e., different levels or different part of the image understanding. For example, the first prompt can be related to the overall style or background of the image. Finally, you need to summarize the description based on your generated prompts and answers with the format:

- Summary:
<
your summary
>
.”

Task-specific prompt.

The task-specific prompt
P_{t}
aims to provide simple and high-level instructions for GPT-4o, which are sent along with the image. In simple cases, we formulate it as
“Please describe the image.”
In more difficult cases, users may provide additional information for specific goals. For example, we use the task-specific prompt:
“Please describe the image and find the camouflaged objects if any.”
when tackling camouflaged object detection with implicit queries.

3.4
Casual Image-based Controls

ThinkFirst can process diverse image-based controls in addition to text, such as points, bounding boxes, scribbles casually drawon on query images. This input setting is more flexible than SAM
[
65
]
and allows the user to further refine the object of interests after text-based segmentation. Given an image
I
, a control image
I_{ann}
with annotations, and a query
q
. We first define the task-specific prompt
P_{t}
as follows:

“Note that your task is slightly different for this input image. A particular part of the object is manually annotated with a circle, a star point, or a bounding box. Please describe the image with chain of thoughts and identify which part it is. Your summary needs to include the description for the target part, so that the segmentation model know how to output the mask. After summarizing the description, please generate a prompt in the format of ‘The image features a …. Please segment the xxx in the image.’, where xxx implies what the annotated part is. Do not mention what the annotation type is in the prompt.”

Then, we feed
I_{ann},P_{e},P_{t}
to GPT-4o, where
P_{e}
is identical as mentioned in Section
3.3
. This will generate a chain of thoughts
\{(Q_{i},A_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, a summary
S
, and an output prompt
\widetilde{P}
. Finally, we use
\widetilde{P}
to segment the original image
I
for the output mask
\hat{\mathrm{M}}(I,\widetilde{P})
with reasoning segmentation agent to achieve segmentation with casual cues marked on the image.

4.1
Experimental Settings

Dataset.
We focus on evaluating ThinkFirst on camouflaged images in COD10K
[
12
]
and underwater images in MVK
[
45
]
. The COD10K dataset contains 69 camouflaged classes and 5,066 camouflaged images (3,040 in train set and 2,026 in test set), where objects are “seamlessly” embedded in their surroundings. The MVK dataset is a video dataset for underwater scenes, where we sample random frames for qualitative evaluation. Additionally, we use the ReasonSeg
[
23
]
dataset as evaluation for standard images without special distortion or coloring conditions, where each image is queried with three different user prompts.

Task.
We use both the explicit and implicit queries to define
q
for ThinkFirst evaluation. The explicit query follows the form
“Please segment the
<OBJ>
in the image.”
, where
<OBJ>
refers to the object class. The implicit query is
“What is the camouflaged object in the image that can move like an animal? Please segment it.”
.

Model.
We select the open-sourced
LISA-13B-Llama2
as our reasoning segmentation backbone. When testing the ThinkFirst results, we use fp16 with 8-bit quantization to prevent out-of-memory issues, which may slightly harm the performance compared to the full model.

Evaluation metric.
We follow the previous works of reasoning segmentation and use cIoU and gIoU to measure the segmentation quality. gIoU is defined by the average of all per-image Intersection-over-Unions (IoUs), while cIoU is defined by the cumulative intersection over the cumulative union.

Application: Where is Waldo?

“Where’s Waldo?”
, also referred to as
Where’s Wally?
, is a visual search puzzle game that challenges players to locate a character, Waldo, among densely populated and intricately detailed illustrations, where Waldo is a boy in a red and white striped shirt. Previous methods generally struggle to find the location of Waldo due to the complex scenes, i.e., there is no obvious foreground and focus in the image.

We ask GPT-4o to think with chains of thoughts and eventually find the location of Waldo by slightly adjusting the environment and task-specific prompts. For this game-playing application, please refer to the prompting details in the appendix: Given a game image
I

Limitations.
ThinkFirst shows little improvement, while still being comparable, when testing on traditional datasets such as RefCOCO
[
17
]
, as shown in Table
3
. We do not use the three common datasets (RefCOCO, RefCOCO+
[
17
]
, and RefCOCOg
[
37
]
) for evaluation due to the potential data leakage issue. Specifically, segmentation models like LISA
[
23
]
are often pretrained on the MSCOCO
[
26
]
dataset, while these three datasets are all subsets of the train division in MSCOCO. To uphold fairness of comparison, we mainly tested ThinkFirst on out-of-domain datasets with a substantial difference from MSCOCO. To enhance the capability for ThinkFirst on traditional datasets requires further exploration of other GPT-4o prompts.

Concluding remarks
This paper introduces ThinkFirst, a novel framework for reasoning segmentation that leverages the chain-of-thought (CoT) capabilities of GPT-4o to enhance the robustness of reasoning segmentation outputs. Our approach demonstrates significant improvements over existing methods, particularly in challenging scenarios involving camouflaged objects, ambiguous boundaries, and out-of-domain instances. Besides the exceptional reasoning capability, ThinkFirst accepts multimodal image controls, thereby enhancing accessibility and usability for a broader audience. We validate the effectiveness of our framework across diverse datasets, including COD10K
[
12
]
, MVK
[
45
]
, and the “Where’s Waldo?” challenge, both qualitatively and quantitatively. The results indicate that our CoT-based approach not only improves segmentation quality but also makes it a powerful tool for visual understanding tasks. Future work will explore refinements to the framework and the applications in other vision-language challenges using GPT-4o CoT.

Appendix C
Concurrent Work

We discuss the difference between our work and the concurrent work Seg-Zero
[
30
]
. To our best knowledge, Seg-Zero introduces a robust and novel way to train multimodal LLM on outputing “numbers”. In specific, if we simply ask GPT-4o to generate a bounding box or a point coordinate, the result is often inaccurate and discrete (e.g. with values like 100, 150, 200, etc). This is because existing MLLMs are good at text outputs instead of precision numbers. By using reinforcement learning (RL) with IoU rewards, Seg-Zero achieves impressive visual results on complex reasoning segmentation datasets. However, as shown in Figure
4
, Seg-Zero fails to process simple cases for multiple objects of interest, due to the limitation of their RL dataset and rewarding mechanisms, thereby largely reducing the usability and applications of their framework. On the other hand, ThinkFirst demonstrates clearly better flexibilities, which are zero-shot without additional learning, much less the notorious RL training process that possibly complicates the problem when CoT can be achieved at inference time with zero-shot capabilities.
