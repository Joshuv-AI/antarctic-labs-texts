Association of Post-Amputation Stump Infection with Persistent Residual Limb Pain: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Kateryna Ksenchyna, Oleh Ksenchyn, Oleksandr Nazarchuk, Dmytro Dmytriiev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent residual limb pain (RLP) is a common and disabling complication following limb amputation. Although nerve injury and neuroma formation are well-established contributors, the potential association between stump infection and long-term pain outcomes has not been fully elucidated.AimTo evaluate the association between post-amputation stump infection and persistent residual limb pain, with particular emphasis on infection severity, microbiological profile, and antimicrobial resistance.MethodsThis prospective observational cohort study included 430 adult patients who underwent upper or lower limb amputation. Stump infection was classified as superficial infection, deep infection, or osteomyelitis and diagnosed using standardized clinical criteria with microbiological confirmation when available. The primary outcome was persistent residual limb pain at 6 months, defined as pain lasting ≥3 months with a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score ≥3. Secondary outcomes included pain intensity, neuropathic pain features assessed using the Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4) questionnaire, and moderate-to-severe pain (NRS ≥5). Associations between stump infection and persistent RLP were evaluated using unadjusted and multivariable logistic regression analyses.ResultsStump infection was identified in 118 of 430 patients (27.4%). At 6 months, persistent RLP was more frequent among patients with stump infection than among those without infection. Increasing infection severity was associated with worse pain outcomes, including higher pain intensity and a greater prevalence of neuropathic pain features. Patients with polymicrobial infections, Gram-negative pathogens, and multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO) demonstrated less favorable pain outcomes than those with uncomplicated microbiological profiles. After adjustment for age, sex, severe soft tissue injury, and time to wound closure, stump infection remained independently associated with persistent residual limb pain.ConclusionPost-amputation stump infection was independently associated with persistent residual limb pain in this prospective cohort. Greater infection severity, increased microbiological complexity, and antimicrobial resistance were also associated with less favorable pain outcomes. These findings support the potential clinical importance of early infection recognition and management while highlighting the need for further multicenter studies to clarify the underlying mechanisms and confirm these associations.Keywords: stump infection, residual limb pain, antibiotic resistance, multidrug-resistant organisms

Introduction

Persistent residual limb pain (RLP) is one of the most common and disabling long-term complications following limb amputation, adversely affecting rehabilitation, prosthetic use, functional recovery, and quality of life. Despite advances in surgical techniques, perioperative care, and rehabilitation strategies, chronic pain continues to affect a substantial proportion of amputees and remains a major clinical challenge.1 Nerve injury, neuroma formation, and maladaptive peripheral and central nervous system plasticity are recognized as key mechanisms underlying the development and persistence of post-amputation pain.2,3

The pathophysiology of persistent post-amputation pain is complex and multifactorial. Peripheral nerve injury may induce ectopic neuronal activity, whereas central sensitization contributes to amplification and maintenance of pain signaling.4,5 Increasing evidence also highlights the importance of neuroimmune interactions in chronic pain, with activation of immune cells, glial cells, and pro-inflammatory mediators promoting both peripheral and central sensitization.6,7 Together, these mechanisms provide a biologically plausible framework linking persistent inflammation to chronic pain after tissue injury.

Postoperative stump infection is a frequent complication following limb amputation, particularly after traumatic injuries associated with extensive soft tissue damage. In addition to impairing wound healing and prolonging hospitalization, stump infection often necessitates repeated surgical procedures and prolonged antimicrobial therapy. Although infection has traditionally been considered a local postoperative complication, its potential association with long-term pain outcomes has received relatively little attention.

Several biological mechanisms may explain this association. Bacterial pathogens, particularly Gram-negative organisms, activate innate immune pathways through pathogen-associated molecular patterns, including lipopolysaccharides, leading to sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines.8 These inflammatory mediators may increase nociceptor excitability, enhance neuroimmune signaling, and promote both peripheral and central sensitization. Persistent inflammation may also interact with nerve injury and neuroma formation, providing a biologically plausible explanation for the persistence of residual limb pain.9

Despite these mechanistic considerations, clinical evidence evaluating the relationship between stump infection and chronic residual limb pain remains limited. Most previous studies have focused primarily on surgical technique, nerve injury, neuroma formation, or prosthetic factors, whereas the potential contribution of infection severity, microbiological characteristics, and antimicrobial resistance has been insufficiently investigated.

Therefore, the present prospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between post-amputation stump infection and persistent residual limb pain. In addition, we investigated whether infection severity, microbiological profile, and the presence of multidrug-resistant organisms were associated with long-term pain outcomes following limb amputation.10,11

Materials and Methods

This prospective observational cohort study evaluated the association between stump infection and persistent residual limb pain (RLP) following limb amputation and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07438795), with approval from the local ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 430 consecutive adult patients undergoing upper or lower limb amputation were prospectively enrolled at a specialized trauma and rehabilitation center. Inclusion criteria comprised age ≥ 18 years and traumatic or surgical amputation with available follow-up data, while patients with pre-existing unrelated chronic pain, severe cognitive impairment, or incomplete data were excluded.

Clinical and demographic variables, including injury mechanism, amputation level, severity of soft tissue injury, and time to wound closure, were prospectively recorded. Geographic information regarding the region of injury and subsequent medical evacuation was also collected. Most patients originated from Eastern and Central Ukraine, including the Kharkiv, Dnipro, and Zaporizhzhia regions.

Stump infection was initially suspected based on standardized clinical criteria, including local signs of inflammation (erythema, edema, increased local temperature, purulent wound discharge, wound dehiscence, and delayed wound healing), supported by laboratory findings when appropriate. All patients with clinically suspected stump infection underwent microbiological investigation as part of routine clinical care. Infection was microbiologically confirmed in all 118 patients by positive wound culture.

Because multiple wound specimens could be collected from the same patient during the course of treatment and individual specimens could yield more than one bacterial species, a total of 501 bacterial isolates were recovered from the 118 patients with confirmed stump infection. Consequently, microbiological analyses describing pathogen distribution and antimicrobial resistance were performed at the isolate level, whereas analyses of clinical outcomes were conducted at the patient level.

Patients were assigned to mutually exclusive microbiological categories according to the overall microbiological profile of their infection. Patients with a single bacterial isolate were classified as having either Gram-positive or Gram-negative infection, whereas those with isolation of two or more bacterial species were classified as having polymicrobial infection. Multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO) were defined as isolates demonstrating resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent in three or more antimicrobial classes.

For exploratory analysis, the geographic distribution of microbiological profiles was evaluated, and results were visualized using a regional mapping approach, where circle size reflected the number of patients and color coding represented microbiological categories (Figure 1).

The primary outcome was persistent RLP at 6 months, defined as pain lasting ≥ 3 months with intensity ≥ 3 on the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). Secondary outcomes included pain intensity (NRS 0–10), neuropathic pain assessed using the DN4 questionnaire (≥ 4), and the proportion of moderate-to-severe pain (NRS ≥ 5).

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables and χ2-test for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was used to assess the independent association between stump infection and persistent RLP. The unadjusted model included stump infection as the sole independent variable: persistent RLP ~ stump infection. To account for potential confounding, an adjusted multivariable logistic regression model was constructed including covariates selected a priori based on previous literature and clinical judgment: persistent RLP ~ stump infection + age + sex + severe soft tissue injury + time to wound closure, with results reported as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Figure 2).

Results

A total of 430 patients undergoing limb amputation were prospectively included in the study. Stump infection was confirmed microbiologically in 118 patients (27.4%), whereas 312 patients (72.6%) had no evidence of stump infection.

Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Overall, the two groups were comparable in terms of demographic variables and baseline pain intensity. There were no significant differences in age, sex distribution, or proportion of major amputations between patients with and without stump infection. However, clinically relevant differences were observed in injury-related factors. Patients who developed stump infection more frequently presented with severe soft tissue injury and experienced a longer time to wound closure, suggesting a more complex and prolonged postoperative course. These findings suggest that patients with stump infection had a more severe initial injury profile and delayed wound healing. Baseline pain intensity was similar between groups, indicating that differences in long-term pain outcomes were unlikely to be explained by initial pain severity alone (Table 1).

Persistent Residual Limb Pain and Pain Phenotype

In addition to a higher prevalence of chronic pain, patients with infection also demonstrated greater pain intensity and a higher proportion of moderate-to-severe pain.

Importantly, neuropathic pain features were significantly more prevalent in the infection group. Patients with stump infection had higher DN4 scores and a greater likelihood of meeting criteria for neuropathic pain, suggesting a shift in pain phenotype rather than a simple increase in nociceptive pain.

These findings indicate that infection is associated not only with the presence and severity of persistent pain, but also with its neuropathic characteristics. When stratified by infection severity, clear differences in pain outcomes were observed across categories. In the univariable logistic regression analysis, stump infection was associated with persistent residual limb pain at 6 months. This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for age, sex, severe soft tissue injury, and time to wound closure in the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3).

Patients without infection had the lowest proportion of persistent residual limb pain (RLP), whereas progressively higher rates were seen in patients with superficial and deep infections, as well as osteomyelitis. In addition to increased prevalence, greater infection severity was associated with higher pain intensity and a higher proportion of neuropathic pain features. In multivariable analysis, the strength of association increased across severity categories, supporting a graded relationship between infection severity and chronic pain outcomes.

These findings suggest a potential dose–response relationship, with more severe infection associated not only with a higher likelihood of persistent pain but also with a shift toward a more severe and neuropathic pain phenotype. The consistency of findings across prevalence, intensity, and neuropathic features supports the robustness of this association. Among patients with documented stump infection, microbiological analysis revealed a heterogeneous spectrum of pathogens, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, as well as a substantial proportion of polymicrobial infections (Table 4).

Staphylococcus aureus was the most frequently isolated pathogen, while Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella species were also commonly identified.

A subset of infections involved multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO), identified in approximately one-fifth of cases. These included methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Gram-negative bacteria.

The presence of MDROs was indicative of a more complex infectious profile, likely reflecting more severe tissue injury, prolonged wound exposure, or prior antimicrobial therapy. Such infections may be associated with delayed wound healing and sustained inflammatory responses.

From a clinical perspective, these findings suggest that microbiological characteristics – particularly antimicrobial resistance – may contribute to the persistence of nociceptive input and could be relevant for understanding variability in long-term pain outcomes.

Pain outcomes varied according to both microbiological composition and antimicrobial resistance profile (Table 5).

Infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus served as the reference category and were associated with intermediate rates of persistent pain and moderate pain intensity.

In contrast, infections involving Gram-negative organisms were associated with higher rates of persistent residual limb pain and greater pain intensity. These infections also demonstrated a higher proportion of multidrug-resistant organisms, suggesting a more complex infectious profile.

The most pronounced differences were observed in polymicrobial infections, which showed the highest prevalence of persistent pain and the greatest pain intensity. This group was also characterized by the highest proportion of multidrug-resistant organisms, reflecting a complex wound environment with multiple interacting pathogens.

Overall, these findings indicate that increasing microbiological complexity and higher prevalence of antimicrobial resistance are associated with worse pain outcomes.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort of 430 patients, stump infection was significantly associated with persistent residual limb pain (RLP). This association remained statistically significant after adjustment for prespecified confounding variables. A graded relationship between infection severity and pain outcomes was observed, with more severe infections associated with higher pain intensity and a greater prevalence of neuropathic features. In addition, infections characterized by higher microbiological complexity, including polymicrobial composition and multidrug-resistant organisms (MDRO), were consistently associated with worse pain outcomes.

These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that infection-related inflammation may be associated with biological processes involved in the development of chronic pain rather than merely reflecting injury severity. Sustained inflammation following tissue injury is known to play a central role in nociceptor sensitization and the transition to chronic pain states. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 modulate nociceptive pathways, lower activation thresholds, and promote peripheral sensitization.12 In the context of amputation, this inflammatory environment may interact with nerve injury and neuroma formation, amplifying pain signaling and facilitating persistence of symptoms.13

A key observation in this study is the graded increase in pain outcomes with increasing infection severity. Patients with deep infection and osteomyelitis demonstrated the highest rates of persistent pain and neuropathic features. This graded association is consistent with biological plausibility of the association, as persistent or intense nociceptive input is known to drive central sensitization and amplification of pain signals.4,14 These findings suggest that infection severity may represent a clinically relevant characteristic associated with less favorable pain outcomes.

Microbiological characteristics were also associated with differences in pain outcomes. In particular, Gram-negative and polymicrobial infections were linked to higher pain intensity and increased likelihood of neuropathic pain. These findings may reflect differences in host–pathogen interactions, as Gram-negative bacteria activate innate immune pathways via lipopolysaccharide signaling and toll-like receptor activation, leading to strong pro-inflammatory responses.15 Polymicrobial infections may further amplify inflammation through microbial synergy and biofilm formation, contributing to prolonged immune activation and delayed healing.

An additional important observation is the association between antimicrobial resistance and pain outcomes. Infections involving MDRO were more prevalent in groups with worse pain outcomes. These infections are often associated with prolonged treatment, repeated interventions, and sustained inflammatory activity, which may contribute to ongoing nociceptive input and facilitate both peripheral and central sensitization. Although causality cannot be definitively established, these findings suggest that antimicrobial resistance may represent a clinically relevant modifier of chronic pain risk.

Patients with stump infection also demonstrated a higher prevalence of neuropathic pain features, as reflected by DN4 scores. This supports the hypothesis that infection-related inflammation may contribute not only to nociceptive pain but also to neuropathic mechanisms. Following amputation, nerve injury and neuroma formation predispose patients to neuropathic pain, and inflammatory mediators may exacerbate this process by increasing ectopic neural activity and altering neuronal excitability.16,17 These results are consistent with a model in which infection may be associated with a greater prevalence of neuropathic pain features.

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of early recognition and appropriate management of stump infection. Although this observational study cannot establish whether infection treatment reduces the risk of persistent residual limb pain, timely infection control may improve postoperative recovery and warrants further investigation in prospective interventional studies. Microbiological profiling may also help identify patients with more complex infections who could benefit from closer clinical follow-up. Overall, these findings support a multidisciplinary approach integrating surgical care, infection management, and pain assessment in patients undergoing limb amputation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the study was prospective, causal relationships cannot be definitively established. First, despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding related to injury severity and treatment characteristics cannot be completely excluded. Second, subgroup analyses, particularly those involving patients with osteomyelitis, were limited by relatively small sample sizes and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Finally, because the study population consisted predominantly of combat-related amputees treated in specialized trauma centers in Ukraine, the generalizability of these findings to civilian populations, including patients with dysvascular or diabetic amputations, should be interpreted cautiously.

In conclusion, stump infection was associated with persistent residual limb pain following limb amputation, and this association remained statistically significant after adjustment for prespecified confounders. Greater infection severity, increased microbiological complexity, and antimicrobial resistance were associated with less favorable pain outcomes. Although these findings highlight the potential clinical importance of early infection recognition and management, further multicenter studies with more comprehensive adjustment for injury severity are required to confirm these associations and clarify potential causal mechanisms.
