Computation of stresses in jammed packings modeled with Tresca friction
Frédéric Marazzato and Shankar Venkataramani 2 1 Department of Mathematical Sciences, University of Nevada Las Vegas, Las Vegas, NV 89154-4020, USA 2 Department of Mathematics, The University of Arizona, Tucson, AZ 85721-0089, USA email: frederic.marazzato@unlv.edu , shankar@arizona.edu

Abstract
This paper is interested in the computation of stresses within jammed packings of rigid polygonal cells. The cells are considered to follow a Tresca friction law. First, a constrained minimization problem is introduced where the friction energy is minimized while enforcing the non-interpenetration of neighboring cells as inequality constraint. The corresponding dual maximization problem is then deduced and its solution provides normal stresses at the interface between cells. Finally, lowest order Raviart–Thomas finite elements are used to reconstruct a consistent stress field by solving local problems. Numerical results are presented to showcase the consistency and robustness of the proposed methodology.

Introduction

Jammed particulate systems, such as granular materials, or masonry structures, exhibit complex mechanical behaviors arising from a disordered microstructure and a network of inter-particle contacts.
When subjected to external loads, these systems support forces through a self-organized arrangement of contact stresses that ensure mechanical equilibrium.
Understanding and accurately computing the stress fields within such jammed configurations is essential for predicting macroscopic mechanical response and failure mechanisms.
Typical methods to compute stresses within granular material include Discrete Element Methods (DEM) as in
[
5
,
12
]
.

Granular materials inherently contain a lot of voids, which is an extra difficulty on its own.
This paper is concerned with jammed packings that do not have such voids e.g. brick walls or vaults, see Figure
1
.

a domain of interest (the jammed packing).
We will consider that a packing is defined by a Voronoi mesh, denoted by
\mathcal{M}

Throughout this paper, we will refer to each element of the packing as a cell (of the mesh
\mathcal{M}
).
In Figure
2
, the blue dots represent the Voronoi centers of each cell.
The interface between cells will then be the internal edges of the mesh
\mathcal{M}
.

For certain engineering applications (civil engineering in the case of Figure
1
), the range of external loads that jammed packings are subjected to does not lead to a significant elastic deformation of the cells.
Therefore, one can consider that the cells are fully rigid (i.e. they do not deform elastically).
When the packing is submitted to external loads, even though it might not deform elastically, internal stresses are still present within
\Omega
.
Under certain conditions, masonry structures
[
14
]
fall into that category.
The stresses within such jammed packings can then be determined exclusively by friction forces.
As
[
16
]
puts it, masonry structures are not accurately modeled by elasticity.
We then decide to model the physics of jammed packings by the rigid friction of neighboring cells with a Tresca friction criterion.
Plasticity in the joint and other physical phenomena
[
14
]
should be taken into account to produce a more physically realistic model.
This work proposes a simplified model that allows to deduce stresses in jammed packings only from friction, without elastic effects.

Since
[
5
]
, many DEM have computed friction forces by penalizing interpenetration.
The friction forces are then deduced linearly from the interpenetration.
Plasticity can also be taken into account in
[
5
]
.
More recently,
[
13
]
has proposed an approach, using DEM, that does not allow interpenetration of neighboring cells and does not use elastic parameters.
Within appropriate loading regimes, we believe this approach better captures the underlying physics of jammed packings.
The main difference with our work is that
[
13
]
models large displacements whereas we are interested in the stresses of jammed packings.

This paper will first give a short reminder on Tresca friction.
It will then present the reconstructions used to obtain a primal energy based on the Tresca criterion.
The existence of minimizers of the primal energy is shown under appropriate hypotheses.
Assuming the jammed packing is stable, the displacement of each cell should be zero (up to machine error).
Thus, only solving the primal problem is not useful for the task at hand.
The dual program is then introduced and classical results relating the primal and dual problem are recalled.
The output of solving the dual problem is a collection of normal stresses at the edges of the mesh
\mathcal{M}
.
Using this data, a consistent reconstruction of the stresses, is then presented.
The reconstruction takes advantage of the fact that the lowest-order Raviart–Thomas elements
\mathbb{RT}_{0}
are
H(\mathrm{div})
conforming and that the associated degrees of freedom (dofs) are the normal stresses at the barycenter of the edges of the mesh.
The idea is then to reconstruct in each cell
c\in\mathcal{M}
a stress
\sigma_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\text{sym}}
, the space of symmetric square tensors of size
d
, such that
\mathrm{div}(\sigma_{c})=0
(in the absence of volume loads).
Finally, numerical results are presented to show the consistency of the proposed methodology as well as its efficiency in computing the stresses for domains with numerous cells.

For simplicity, we consider in the rest of this paper that
d=2
.
Extending the following methodology to
d=3
is straightforward.

1.1
Tresca Friction

Let us illustrate the Tresca friction law in a very simple case.
One can refer to the reference
[
3
]
for further detail.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open bounded domain.
We assume that the boundary of
\Omega
, written
\partial\Omega
, is Lipschitz and write as
\Gamma_{c}\subset\partial\Omega
the contact surface.
We write as
\bm{n}
the outward unit normal to
\partial\Omega
and as
\bm{t}=R(\frac{\pi}{2})\bm{n}
the unit tangent to
\partial\Omega
, where
R(\frac{\pi}{2})
is the 2d rotation matrix by an angle
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
We write as
\bm{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
the displacement field and
\sigma:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{\text{sym}}
the stress tensor.
We consider that the material in
\Omega
is potentially in contact at
\Gamma_{c}
with a fully rigid material (it does not deform).
See figure
3
for a sketch of the setup.

\Omega
Rigid domain
\Gamma_{c}

Sketch of the contact between a rigid domain and
\Omega

Let us decompose the displacement and normal stress on
\Gamma_{c}
as
\mathbf{u}=u_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{t}}
and
\sigma\mathbf{n}=\sigma_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbf{n}+\bm{\sigma}_{\mathbf{t}}
.
The kinematic contact conditions at
\Gamma_{c}
are:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{\bm{n}}\leq 0,\\
&\sigma_{\bm{n}}\leq 0,\\
&\sigma_{\bm{n}}u_{\bm{n}}=0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

The first equation in (
1
) prevents the penetration of
\Omega
into the rigid domain, whereas the second describes that contact forces can only be repulsive.
The third equation describes that two situations are possible at any point on
\bm{x}\in\Gamma_{c}
:

•

if the domain
\Omega
is detached from the rigid support
u_{\bm{n}}(\bm{x})<0
, then necessarily
\sigma_{\bm{n}}=0

(there cannot be a contact force),

•

if there is a negative contact force
\sigma_{\bm{n}}<0
, then necessarily the elastic body must stick to the support
u_{\bm{n}}=0
.

Note that (
1
) are sometimes called KKT conditions in the context of constrained optimization and in that context the normal stress
\sigma_{\bm{n}}
is the Lagrange multiplier of the contact constraint.
The equations in (
1
) are common to all contact laws and not specific to the Tresca friction law.
The Tresca friction law writes on
\Gamma_{c}
as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &|\bm{\sigma}_{\mathbf{t}}|\leq s_{T}&\text{if }\mathbf{u}_{\bm{t}}=\bm{0},\\
&\bm{\sigma}_{\bm{t}}=-s_{T}\frac{\bm{u}_{\bm{t}}}{|\bm{u}_{\bm{t}}|}&\text{otherwise},\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
s_{T}>0
is the Tresca friction coefficient.
The energy dissipation due to the Tresca friction writes as

\int_{\Gamma_{c}}s_{T}|\bm{u}_{\bm{t}}|.

(3)

Discrete Model

We consider an open bounded polyhedral domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

2.1
Setup

Let
\mathcal{C}

Let us write as
\mathcal{E}

The edges are partitioned as
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}^{\partial}\cup\mathcal{E}^{i}

\mathcal{E}^{\partial}
are the external edges and
\mathcal{E}^{i}
are the internal edges.
For a cell

\bm{x}_{c}

Each cell
c\in\mathcal{C}
is composed of a collection of edges written
\mathcal{E}_{c}

The cells are considered to be rigid, and thus we take the current position
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{P}^{0}(\mathcal{C})^{2}=:V

The initial position of the cells is also represented by a piecewise constant functions
\bm{x}\in V

The displacement
\bm{u}_{c}

is defined as
\bm{u}_{c}:=\bm{z}_{c}-\bm{x}_{c}

The collection of cell displacements is then stored in

Since the cells are considered as rigid bodies, a complete description of the kinematics of cell

would require a translation of the centroid,
\bm{u}_{c}
and a rotation
\theta_{c}
, about that point.
But, since in the present context, we consider jammed packings, the cells cannot rotate and we therefore do not consider any rotational degree of freedom.
As a consequence, only force balance is imposed on each cell, while moment balance is not enforced.

2.2
Facet values

In order to write the primal energy that we shall minimize, we need facet values computed from cell values.
For a boundary edge
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\partial}
, we write the facet reconstruction operator as
\bm{R}_{e}(\bm{u})
.
Since
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\partial}
, there exists a unique
c\in\mathcal{M}
such that
e\subset\partial c
.
The idea of the reconstruction is to simply use the displacement of the cell
c
that contains the boundary facet
e
.
Since the packing is jammed, cells cannot spin.
Also, we consider the cells to be fully rigid, which makes this reconstruction consistent.
One thus has

\bm{R}_{e}(\bm{u}):=\bm{u}_{c}.

(4)

2.3
Primal problem

We consider that the cells interact through Tresca friction forces, and that there can be no inter-penetration between the cells.
Let us first describe the energy
E_{e}
between the two neighboring cells
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
of Figure
4
.
We write the energy of the Tresca friction law (compare to (
3
)) for
e
as

E_{e}(\bm{u}):=s_{T}|e||[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{t}_{e}|,

(5)

In order to forbid inter-penetration between cells, we add the following constraint:

\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}\geq 0.

(6)

We also add a confinement term in order to keep the granular material compressed.
It materializes as a Neumann boundary condition
\sigma\bm{n}=\bm{g}
on
\partial\Omega
.
Assume that
\bm{g}\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)^{2}
and let
e\in\mathcal{E}^{\partial}
.
We define
\bm{g}_{e}:=|e|^{-1}\int_{e}\bm{g}
as the average of
\bm{g}
over
e
.
Therefore, the work of external loads is defined as

E_{\text{ext}}(\bm{u}):=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{\partial}}|e|\bm{g}_{e}\cdot\bm{R}_{e}(\bm{u}).

(7)

The primal energy
E
is then defined as

E(\bm{u}):=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{i}}E_{e}(\bm{u})-E_{\text{ext}}(\bm{u}).

(8)

Finally, since (
8
) has a translation invariance, we add the following equality constraint:

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}=0.

(9)

We therefore define the primal problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{u}\in V}

\displaystyle E(\bm{u})

(10)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}=0.

2.4
Dual problem

The problem (
10
) is physically relevant but does not contain internal stresses.
The normal stresses will appear as dual variables in the dual problem.
The constrained problem (
10
) can be rewritten as an unconstrained minimization of a convex functional.
Let us write as
I
the indicator function defined as

I(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&\text{ if }x\geq 0,\\
&+\infty&\text{ if }x<0.\end{aligned}\right.

We can now define the proper convex function
\mathtt{E}:V\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
as

\mathtt{E}(\bm{u})=E(\bm{u})+\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{i}}I([\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}).

(11)

Note that for simplicity, we neglect the equality constraint from (
10
) in the exposition of the dual problem.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\bm{g}
is such that
E(\bm{u})\to+\infty
when
\|\bm{u}\|_{V}\to+\infty
.
Then, the functional
\mathtt{E}
defined in (
11
) admits a minimizer.

Remark 2
.

Note that typical external loads
\bm{g}
verifying the hypothesis of Proposition
1
contain compressive loads.

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Let us define
\mathcal{V}:=\{\bm{v}\in V;\ \forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}\geq 0\}
.

\mathcal{V}
is a closed subspace of

and thus is a vector space.
Note that over
\mathcal{V}

and
\mathtt{E}
is therefore continuous over
\mathcal{V}
.
Using the hypothesis and the fact that

is finite-dimensional, once can deduce that
\mathtt{E}
admits a global minimizer over
\mathcal{V}
.
∎

Let
W:=\mathbb{P}^{0}(\mathcal{E}^{i})
.
We write dual variables as
\bm{f}\in W^{2}
.
The dual energy
\mathtt{E}^{*}
is then defined as the convex conjugate of
\mathtt{E}
by

\mathtt{E}^{*}(\bm{f})=\sup_{\bm{u}\in V}\left\{\langle\bm{f},\bm{u}\rangle-\mathtt{E}(\bm{u})\right\}.

(12)

The dual problem is then defined as

\max_{\bm{f}\in W^{2}}\mathtt{E}^{*}(\bm{f})

(13)

Note that, because
\mathtt{E}
has the physical dimension of an energy, then
\bm{f}
is interpreted as a collection of forces associated to each internal edge, with the corresponding physical dimension.
Let us recall the following classical result.

Proposition 3

(Weak Duality)
.

If
\bm{u}\in V
is feasible for (
10
) and
\bm{f}
is feasible for (
13
), then

\mathtt{E}(\bm{u})\geq\mathtt{E}^{*}(\bm{f}).

Note that Proposition
3
shows that, if Proposition
1
applies, then (
13
) admits a solution.

Remark 4
.

Since
\mathtt{E}
is not strictly convex, one cannot expect to have uniqueness of minimizers.
A similar reasoning applies to
\mathtt{E}^{*}
and (
13
).

Let
e\in\mathcal{E}^{i}
, we write
\bm{f}_{e}=(f^{n}_{e},f^{t}_{e})
.
Assuming (
13
) has a feasible solution, the normal stress for a cell
c\in\mathcal{C}
and an internal edge

is thus defined as

\bm{\lambda}_{e,c}:=|e|^{-1}(\bm{n}_{c}\cdot\bm{n}_{e})\left(f_{e}^{n}\bm{n}_{e}+f_{e}^{t}\bm{t}_{e}\right).

(14)

Remark 5
.

The principle of action-reaction is intrinsically taken into account here since for

and

, one has
\bm{\lambda}_{e,c}=-\bm{\lambda}_{e,c^{\prime}}

That is due to the fact that one has, for instance,
\bm{n}_{c}=\bm{n}_{e}
and
\bm{n}_{c^{\prime}}=-\bm{n}_{e}

For

\displaystyle b(\sigma,q)=0,

H_{0}(\mathrm{div};c)^{2}:=\{\sigma\in H(\mathrm{div};c)^{2};\sigma\bm{n}=\bm{0}\text{ on }\partial\Omega\}

\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(c)}\leq C\|\mathrm{div}(\sigma)\|_{L^{2}(c)}.

Let us reason by density.
Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(c)^{2\times 2}

We now consider a sequence
(\varphi_{n})_{n}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(c)^{2\times 2}

\mathrm{curl}(\varphi_{n})=\bm{0}
and
\sigma\in Z

\|\varphi_{n}-\sigma\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}\to 0

Thus, as

One has

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{div}(\sigma)\|_{L^{2}(c)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathrm{div}(\varphi_{n})|_{L^{2}(c)}+\|\mathrm{div}(\varphi_{n}-\sigma)|_{L^{2}(c)},

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(c)}+\|\mathrm{div}(\varphi_{n}-\sigma)|_{L^{2}(c)}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{C}\|\sigma\|_{L^{2}(c)}.

Therefore,

\|\sigma\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}^{2}=\|\mathrm{div}(\sigma)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(c)}+\|\sigma\|^{2}_{L^{2}(c)}\leq\left(1+C^{2}\right)\|\mathrm{div}(\sigma)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(c)}.

Thus, one has

a(\sigma,\sigma)=\|\mathrm{div}(\sigma)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(c)}\geq\frac{1}{1+C^{2}}\|\sigma\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}^{2},

which shows the coercivity over
Z
.
To finish, we note that
\mathfrak{V}_{0}
is conforming in
Z
in the sense that
\mathfrak{V}_{0}\subset Z
.

In order to apply the BNB theorem for mixed formulation
[
6
, Theorem 2.34]
, it remains to show the second condition of the theorem.
Let
q\in Q
and
\tau\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}
.
One has

\displaystyle b(\tau,q)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}_{c}}\int_{T}\tau:j(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}_{c}}|T|\left<\tau\right>_{T}:j(q),

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}_{c}}|T|\left|\left<\tau\right>_{T}\right|\sqrt{2}|q|,

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}_{c}}|T|\left|\left<\tau\right>_{T}\right|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{T\in\mathcal{M}_{c}}|T||q|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}\|\left<\tau\right>_{T}\chi_{T}\|_{L^{2}(c)}\|q\|_{L^{2}(c)}

where
\left<\tau\right>_{T}
is the average over
T
and
\chi_{T}
is the indicator function over
T
.
Considering
\tau\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}\cap\mathbb{P}^{0}(\mathcal{M}_{c})^{2\times 2}
, one has
\|\tau\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}=\|\tau\|_{L^{2}(c)}
, and thus

\frac{b(\tau,q)}{\|\tau\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}}=\sqrt{2}\|q\|_{L^{2}(c)}.

The supremum over
\tau\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}
is thus achieved and one has

\inf_{q\in Q}\sup_{\tau\in\mathfrak{V}_{0}}\frac{b(\tau,q)}{\|\tau\|_{H(\mathrm{div};c)}\|q\|_{L^{2}(c)}}\geq\sqrt{2},

which finishes the proof.
∎

4.1
Linear program

Let us rewrite (
10
) as a linear program.
This allows us to use very efficient linear solvers.
For each internal edge
e\in\mathcal{E}^{i}
, we add a new variable
v_{e}
such that
v_{e}\geq|[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{t}_{e}|
.
Note that this inequality constraint can be written with two linear constraints.
Let us write
v:=(v_{e})_{e}\in W
.
Therefore, the linear program is now

\displaystyle\min_{(\bm{u},v)\in V\times W}

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}^{i}}s_{T}|e|v_{e}-E_{\text{ext}}(\bm{u})

(16)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{n}_{e}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad v_{e}\geq[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{t}_{e},

\displaystyle\forall e\in\mathcal{E}^{i},\quad v_{e}\geq-[\bm{u}]_{e}\cdot\bm{t}_{e},

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}=0.

Upon recomputing the forces

Note that some stress concentration is observed in Figure

Finally, even though the boundary condition is a homogeneous compression, we observe that several cells are in tension.
We believe that even though this result sounds counter-intuitive, it is logical since the friction between neighboring cells can lead to having cells under local tension.
This is a well documented phenomena.
Local tension is required for a crack to open which itself is required for a crack to propagate.
In

10
