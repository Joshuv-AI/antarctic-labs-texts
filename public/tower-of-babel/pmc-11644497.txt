The biochemical mechanisms of plastic biodegradation
Authors: Ethan G Gates, Nathan Crook
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSince the invention of the first synthetic plastic, an estimated 12 billion metric tons of plastics have been manufactured, 70% of which was produced in the last 20 years. Plastic waste is placing new selective pressures on humans and the organisms we depend on, yet it also places new pressures on microorganisms as they compete to exploit this new and growing source of carbon. The limited efficacy of traditional recycling methods on plastic waste, which can leach into the environment at low purity and concentration, indicates the utility of this evolving metabolic activity. This review will categorize and discuss the probable metabolic routes for each industrially relevant plastic, rank the most effective biodegraders for each plastic by harmonizing and reinterpreting prior literature, and explain the experimental techniques most often used in plastic biodegradation research, thus providing a comprehensive resource for researchers investigating and engineering plastic biodegradation.

Introduction

Starting in the mid-nineteenth century, the development of plastics and other synthetic polymers aimed to combat a significant environmental problem at the time: poaching. In the wake of the industrial revolution, demand for materials such as tortoiseshell and ivory increased to unsustainable levels as more people had expendable income for goods like combs and billiard balls. This led to the development of semisynthetic polymers such as cellulose nitrate, which was used as a substitute for ivory in the 1860s. While celluloid could be considered the first plastic, Bakelite, a thermosetting resin made from the condensation of phenol and formaldehyde, was the first fully synthetic plastic invented in 1909 (Freinkel 2011). Following the commercial success of Bakelite, polymer research and production greatly expanded and by the 1950s, all the currently most commonly used plastics were produced industrially. Since that time, humanity has produced an estimated 12 billion metric tons of plastic (Fig. 1), 70% of which was produced between 2000 and 2023 (Geyer et al. 2017, OECD Environment Statistics 2022). The production and use of plastics has become a defining feature of human existence, so much so that the presence of “Plastiglomerates”—buried amalgamations of melted plastic and sediment, will serve as a marker of our current geologic epoch, the Anthropocene, for millennia (Trinastic 2015).

An estimated 12 billion tons of plastic have been produced.

Plastics have been specifically formulated for long-term stability and resistance to microbial degradation, but ironically some of the most resistant plastics are often used in short term, disposable applications, with 40%–45% of plastics used as single-use food packaging (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022). Despite their short intended life span, recycling, which has increased dramatically since 2006, still only accounts for about 18% of plastic disposal, though this number is uncertain and varies greatly by region. For example, according to PlasticsEurope, about 30% of European postconsumer plastic was recycled in 2019, while according to OECD statistics for the same year, recycling rates are as low as 12%. These figures stand in contrast to the USA, where as little as 4%–9% of plastic waste is recycled and over 70% is landfilled (OECD Environment Statistics 2022). In addition to recycling or landfill disposal, plastic waste is incinerated or pyrolyzed to generate heat or electricity. However, on balance, the reduced landfill use and electricity generated by plastic incineration are outweighed by its negative environmental impacts (Demetrious and Crossin 2019). Even with proper scrubbing in place, incineration emits persistent organic pollutants like polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins and dibenzofurans. This problem is caused by the heterogenous nature of plastic waste, resulting in unstable and incomplete combustion (Ágnes and Rajmund 2016, Li et al. 2019b).

Microplastics (particles from 1 µm to 5 mm in size) and nanoplastics (<1 µm in size) are heterogenous in both size and composition, and are often an amalgam of two or more different polymers. Micronanoplastics (MP/NPs) have been found in nearly every environment researchers have looked for them, including pristine Antarctic snow (Aves et al. 2022), airborne dust (Trainic et al. 2020), human infant stool (Zhang et al. 2021), and bottled water (Qian et al. 2024). Inhalation and ingestion are the most common routes of entry for MP/NPs in humans (Krug and Wick 2011). The morphology and physiochemical properties of nanoplastics allow them to cross biological barriers, and nanoplastics have been shown to accumulate in plant tissue (Luo et al. 2022), particularly in root systems. Nanoplastics can accumulate in rat placental tissue leading to low birth weight (Fournier et al. 2020), negatively impact the reproduction efficiency of crustaceans (Jaikumar et al. 2019), and cross the blood–brain and alveolar barriers in rats and mice, leading to neurodegenerative effects (Wang et al. 2022). Accumulation of polystyrene microplastics results in reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydrogen peroxide (Sun et al. 2020). Overall, oxidative stress appears to be a leading factor in the inflammatory and neurodegenerative effects of micronanoplastics, and antioxidants have been shown to alleviate these effects (Mitrano et al. 2021). Taken together, microplastics present a growing and difficult-to-tackle consequence of the poor containment of plastic waste.

The ballooning amounts of plastic waste, coupled with their heterogeneity and low concentration, indicate the utility of a biological degradation approach. Compared to traditional recycling methods, biodegradation requires much less energy and eliminates toxic compounds generated by incineration, instead converting plastics into CO2 and biomass. Poly(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) can even be converted back to pure monomers or value-added products (Sadler and Wallace 2021, Sullivan et al. 2022).

Since the earliest reports on plastic biodegradation in the late 1960s and early 1970s, hundreds of “plastic-degrading” microbes have been described. In this review alone, over 200 different strains are mentioned (Supplementary Excel File S1), but publications on plastic biodegradation are estimated to number in the thousands (Buchholz et al. 2022). Despite this, detailed descriptions of the enzymes involved are few, and many reports may be misleading due to extremely slow degradation rates, improper abiotic controls, confusion over whether the polymer backbone or additives are being utilized, inability of the tested microbe to utilize the plastic as a sole carbon source, or partial breakdown rather than complete mineralization. This confusion is especially dire when certain blended polymers are marketed as biodegradable when in reality, microplastics will still be left behind (Iwamoto and Tokiwa 1994, Goldberg 1995, Ali et al. 2014a). To combat this, we endorse a definition of biodegradability that refers to the complete mineralization of plastic into CO2 or CH4 and H2O. According to this definition, at least 90% of the carbon in the plastic should be converted to CO2 or CH4 within 180 days in an industrial composting facility or at least 30% conversion within 180 days at 30°C in marine environments (Amaral-Zettler et al. 2020).

To provide a comprehensive resource for researchers investigating plastic biodegradation, this review will discuss the metabolic pathways involved in biodegradation, some of which are not yet fully understood. While the main text focuses on the biochemical evidence for plastic biodegradation, the Supplementary Text provides additional details on specific studies that most closely align with the above definition of biodegradability, while adapting it to include organisms that can reduce the weight of a plastic sample by the same amount in the specified timeframe (30% weight loss within 180 days), since percentage weight loss is much more commonly reported and easier to interpret than the CO2 production method, [ISO 14855 (International Organization for Standardization 2012), invoked in Amaral-Zettler’s definition]. In Supplementary Data File S1, we provide a quantitative comparison of these organisms. Finally, we will describe the rationale, procedure, and common pitfalls encountered in the experimental techniques most used in biodegradation studies in the Supplementary Text.

Bioavailability as a critical prerequisite for biodegradation

Hydrophobicity has a considerable influence on the efficacy of biodegradation. Polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) are completely insoluble in water, as one might expect from their extremely hydrophobic structure. Other polymers, like polyethylene glycol (PEG) are readily soluble in water below a certain length (Ensing et al. 2019). Still others, such as PET and Nylon-6 have slightly water-soluble monomers (Yuan et al. 2022, BioCrick 2024) and are susceptible to acid-catalysed hydrolysis, but are themselves insoluble at neutral pH and room temperature (Wang et al. 2017, Yang et al. 2021b). By definition, microplastic particles are also insoluble (Kye et al. 2023). Plastic-degrading microbes have several ways to cope with this bioavailability challenge. Most notably, the first catabolic step of either oxidation or hydrolysis serves to increase the plastic surface hydrophilicity by increasing the number of hydrogen bonds that can be formed with the solvent. These plastic-degradative enzymes typically have hydrophobic binding domains (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1999) and are secreted or expressed on the cell’s surface (Yoshida et al. 2016, Li et al. 2023), while later metabolic steps are carried out in the periplasm or cytosol. Cell surface hydrophobicity (Sivan 2011) and the secretion of biosurfactants (Arkatkar et al. 2010), amphipathic molecules akin to detergents, are also characteristics of some plastic-degrading organisms. With these characteristics, biofilm formation arises as an enabling precursor to plastic biodegradation, but there are many examples of biofilm formation on plastic that do not confer appreciable degradation, particularly in marine environments (Amaral-Zettler et al. 2020, Philippe et al. 2024).

The historical and evolutionary origins of plastic biodegradation

The ability of microbes to break down plastics is best understood in the light of other more common biological polymers. Some synthetic polymers, such as polystyrene and Bakelite, share structural similarity to the plant polymer lignin, the primary component of wood. Other plastics, specifically polyesters and polyamides, are similar to cutin, the waxy barrier of plant leaves (Fig. 2) (Barr and Aust 1994b, Nawrath 2002). At least one biodegradation study has been carried out on Bakelite using Phanerochaete chrysosporium, and its degradation mechanism is an often-repeated motif (Gusse et al. 2006). The biodegradation mechanism seen in P. chrysosporium is due to three ligninolytic enzymes: lignin peroxidase, Mn-dependent peroxidase (MnP), and laccase, a multicopper oxidase. These enzymes are expressed as a result of carbon and nitrogen starvation and break down lignin as well as xenobiotic substances (Barr and Aust 1994b). Lignin peroxidases and MnPs are both heme-proteins associated with biodegradation (Twala et al. 2020). They catalyse the one-electron oxidation of chemicals to free radicals, which in turn generate alcohols, ketones, and unsaturated compounds. The ferriperoxidase catalytic cycle proceeds by oxidation of the catalytic iron by H2O2, forming Compound I, an iron (IV)-oxo radical cation of heme. This highly reactive form of the enzyme is then reduced by a substrate, oxidizing it in turn and releasing water in the process. The MnPs operate slightly differently, with MnP compound I oxidizing Mn(II) to Mn(III), which in turn is reduced by a substrate (Cai and Tien 1993, Barr and Aust 1994b). Laccase is a multicopper oxidase that catalyses one-electron oxidation of a broad range of phenolic substrates while reducing molecular oxygen to water (Messerschmidt and Huber 1990). Multiple laccases have been found in nearly every fungal strain in which a laccase was identified, particularly in white rot fungi that degrade lignin (Mayer and Staples 2002). The usage of lignin-degrading enzymes for biodegradation of the first synthetic plastic foreshadows the resourcefulness that microbes would exhibit to degrade subsequently developed plastics. We propose that the plastic-degrading phenotype has evolved in part from ligninolytic and cutinolytic behaviors, enabling microbes to metabolize recalcitrant polymers that only share small similarity to these natural polymers, such as polyolefins.

Two major routes toward biodegradation of plastics

From a metabolic perspective, we categorize plastic into two types: oxidizable, where the first metabolic step involves the addition of oxygen to the carbon backbone, and hydrolyzable, whose first metabolic step involves hydrolysis of an ester or amide bond. So-called oxidizable plastics include PE, PP, and polystyrene (PS), while hydrolyzable plastics include polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), and polyurethane (PUR) (Table 1). An important caveat is that while there is a mounting body of evidence to support oxidative metabolism of “oxidizable” polymers, the pathways for these plastics are still hypothetical and have yet to be explicitly proven. Alternatively, it would be accurate to describe “oxidizable” plastics as “non-hydrolyzable,” as oxidative methods of degradation have also been documented for several of the hydrolyzable plastics (Fujisawa et al. 2001, Romero et al. 2007, Magnin et al. 2021). For simplicity, these “non-hydrolyzable” plastics will be referred to as “oxidizable plastics” for the remainder of the text. We begin our discussion with oxidizable plastics.

Oxidizable plastics

Plastics in the “oxidizable” set require an oxidation step to initiate further biodegradation. Many biodegradation studies of oxidizable plastics, especially PE, have included a UV pretreatment step that serves to partially oxidize the polymer and increase its surface hydrophilicity for easier microbial breakdown. This initial oxidation event may also disrupt intermolecular forces within the polymer that maintain its crystallinity, thus further enhancing enzyme access to the polymer. This pretreatment step has allowed scientists to hypothesize that microbes also encode oxidases that perform this initial step, generating intermediates that are similar to photooxidation products (Fig. 3) (Singh and Sharma 2008, Palacios et al. 2013, Gewert et al. 2015). Because the oxidized molecules are much more readily consumed than their precursors, it is thought that primary oxidation of polyolefins is the rate-limiting step in their biodegradation, an idea that is supported by the strength of C–H and C–C bonds relative to C–O. Recent biodegradation studies of oxidizable plastics omit UV pretreatment in order to better isolate biological biodegradation mechanisms (Zampolli et al. 2023). Oxidizable plastics include PE, PP, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), PS, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and PEG. Because insect larvae are commonly used to degrade oxidizable plastics, a dedicated section is also included.

PE

PE is arguably the simplest type of plastic, consisting of repeating units of -CH2-. PE is primarily categorized as either low density (LDPE), an amorphous polymer owing to steric hinderance from long branched chains, or high density (HDPE), which is more linear and thus able to pack together more tightly, leading to higher crystallinity. Combined, the two PE types account for 28%–30% of all plastics produced, making PE the most used plastic in the world. As such, there is significant interest in understanding routes for its biodegradation. PE biodegradation is generally either initiated by alkane mono-oxygenase or peroxidase/laccase. Pioneering studies of PE biodegradation proposed β-oxidation as the major route of mineralization (Albertsson et al. 1987), a hypothesis that would be built upon in many subsequent efforts. Alkane monooxygenases in the context of PE biodegradation

As early as 1974, Haines and Alexander noted that the trend of lower consumption rates as paraffin chain length increased could explain the “yet unexplained persistence in nature of polyethylenes” (Haines and Alexander 1974). Thus, alkane metabolism seemed to offer an enzymatic route to PE mineralization. Alkane monooxygenase (AlkB) is a family of enzymes that catalyse the terminal oxidation of medium-length alkanes (∼C8–C20). The AlkB or alkane hydroxylase system consists of a nonheme diiron (Fe2+) integral membrane monooxygenase (AlkB), whose catalytic domain lies on the cytosolic side; rubredoxin (AlkG), a soluble electron transfer protein with a single nonheme iron; and rubredoxin reductase (AlkT), a flavoprotein that catalyses electron transport to rubredoxin and ultimately the substrate via NADH (van Beilen et al. 1994, Guo et al. 2023). alkL probably encodes an outer membrane transport protein, and alkS is a putative LuxR-type regulator (Liang et al. 2016).

Untreated low molecular weight PE (LMWPE), much heavier than the range of n-alkanes thought to be utilized by bacteria (Haines and Alexander 1974), was used to enrich and isolate Pseudomonas sp. E4 from oil-contaminated beach soil. An AlkB with 86% sequence homology to other known Pseudomonas alkBs was amplified and cloned into Escherichia coli BL21, which mineralized 19.3% of the carbon in LMWPE to CO2 in 80 days (Yoon et al. 2012, Jeon and Kim 2015). This result suggests that AlkB is a primary step in PE biodegradation, and the fact that E. coli BL21 could biodegrade LMWPE with AlkB alone strengthens the hypothesis that AlkB-derived products are further metabolized via β-oxidation without the need for specific alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenases (van Beilen et al. 1994).

Soluble cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP153) with specificity for shorter (∼C5–C11) alkanes are sometimes found alongside or in lieu of integral-membrane alkane hydroxylases (AlkB) (Funhoff et al. 2006, van Beilen et al. 2006). The CYP153 family is a class I P450, requiring ferredoxin and ferredoxin reductase for electron transport and heme as a cofactor (Maier et al. 2001); these usually appear as a single operon downstream of an AraC-type activator (Liang et al. 2016). A third family of terminal alkane hydroxylases (AlmA), are secreted flavoproteins that act on long chain n-alkanes (up to ∼C40) (Throne-Holst et al. 2006, 2007, Feng et al. 2007).

In general, alkane-degrading microorganisms tend to have more than one type of AlkB, allowing a wider range of substrates to be utilized. Alcanivorax sp. 24 isolated from marine plastic waste possesses two CYP153 genes, three alkB homologs, two almA homologs, six alkane transporters, 17 peroxidases, 13 esterases, and a putative laccase, along with ROS-generating NADH oxidase and superoxide dismutase (Zadjelovic et al. 2020, 2022). Rhodococcus ruber C208 also possesses several AlkBs (Gravouil et al. 2017). Expression of alkane hydroxylase and CYP153 systems has been shown to be induced by both n-alkanes and primary alcohols (Benson and Shapiro 1975, Grund et al. 1975). It is most likely that AlkBs are involved in the utilization of secondary PE metabolites, as there is no feasible way for these enzymes to cause chain scission. Still, it is possible that secreted long-chain monooxygenases (AlmA) play a minor role in initial PE oxidation.

PE oxidation by peroxidases and laccase-Like enzymes

Fungi and bacteria also degrade PE via the use of peroxidases and laccases. In their natural habitat, fungi encounter lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose. Lignin is a recalcitrant natural polymer with a complex, irregular, highly cross-linked structure of aromatic compounds and ketones (Fig. 2A). When fungi are starved of carbon and nitrogen, they enter a “ligninolytic” state, secreting peroxidases and laccases (Barr and Aust 1994b). HDPE films treated with MnP from P. chrysosporium exhibited significant reductions in tensile strength. (Iiyoshi et al. 1998). Instead of oxidizing substrates directly, laccases can oxidize mediator molecules, such as 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HBT), which then oxidize the substrate of interest, including nonphenolic compounds. For example, purified T. versicolor IFO 6482 laccase incubated with HBT significantly decreased tensile strength, relative elongation, and molecular weight distribution of a PE membrane over the course of 3 days (Iiyoshi et al. 1998, Fujisawa et al. 2001).

Bacteria also use laccases or laccase homologues to degrade PE. For example, R. ruber C208 isolated from soil contaminated with agricultural PE film formed a uniform biofilm on UV-pretreated LDPE film provided as the sole carbon source and reduced its weight by 4% in 30 days. Further studies conducted on R. ruber confirmed that it produces laccase, which reduced the molecular weight of PE films when supplied as a crude extract (Santo et al. 2013). Through differential gene expression analysis, it was shown that culturing the strain on PE induced expression of its β-oxidation pathway (Gravouil et al. 2017). Two efficient laccase-like multicopper oxidases (LMCO2 and LMCO3) for PE biodegradation were recently discovered in Rhodococcus opacus R7. LMCOs from this strain had low amino acid sequence homology to the LMCO of R. ruber C208 (<50%). Both enzymes are thermostable up to 60°C. Strikingly, purified enzymes catalysed oxidation and chain scission of commercial LDPE powder in just 1 day without UV pretreatment or laccase mediators. In agreement with previously discussed results, carboxylic acids, esters, ketones, alcohols, and alkanes constituted the products of PE oxidation (Zampolli et al. 2023). The stringently curated Plastics-Active Enzyme Database (PAZy) considers these LMCOs to be the only true examples of PE-degrading enzymes (Buchholz et al. 2022). However, benzoate-derived additives were also present in the LDPE sample, as evidenced by a minor carboxylate IR signal in the untreated plastic and the detection of benzoic acid as a degradation product; the possibility of this additive acting as a laccase mediator cannot presently be ruled out.

Studies have probed the gut microbiota of insect larvae to find PE degraders. Aspergillus flavus PEDX3 isolated from Galleria mellonella (greater wax moth) larvae was able to degrade HDPE by about 4% in 28 days. Two laccase-like multicopper enzymes were shown to be upregulated via RT-PCR (Zhang et al. 2020). Similarly, a laccase-like multicopper oxidase purified from Acinetobacter baumannii Rd-H2, isolated from the gut of Rhizopertha dominica larvae (wheat weevil) was shown to reduce the molecular weight distribution of PE film, and its activity was increased with the addition of copper (II) ions (Zhang et al. 2022a). The fungus Meyerozyma guilliermondii ZJC1 and bacterium Serratia marcescens ZJC2 were isolated from the gut of Plodia interpunctella larvae. Strain ZJC1 was able to reduce the weight of UV-irradiated PE film by 13.9%, while ZJC2 exhibited just 3.57% weight reduction. RT-PCR revealed upregulation of multicopper oxidase, catalase peroxidase, esterase, and alkene reductase in ZJC2 and ZJC2 + ZJC1 cultures. Mass spectroscopy of metabolites revealed long chain oxygenated hydrocarbons (Lou et al. 2022).

Outlook for PE biodegradation

Collectively, it appears that PE biodegradation is dependent upon an initial oxidation event, either via radiation or enzymes, the products of which can be further metabolized in an analogous manner as long-chain alkanes. The PE-biodegradation phenotype can be found in alkane-rich soils and the gut of insects that feed on waxy substrates or lignocellulosic plant biomass. Accordingly, peroxidases, laccases, and AlkBs have been strongly linked with PE biodegradation. It is interesting that while MnP has never been explicitly shown in PE- or PP-degrading bacteria, the inclusion of Mn in PE or PP samples appears to increase the extent of bacterial degradation (Albertsson et al. 1995, 1998, Fontanella et al. 2013, Auta et al. 2018) compared to other pro-oxidant metal ions (Fontanella et al. 2013). It would also appear that bacterial laccases are more suitable for PE and PP biodegradation than fungal laccases (Iiyoshi et al. 1998, Arkatkar et al. 2010, Jeyakumar et al. 2013, Santo et al. 2013, Zampolli et al. 2023). While AlkB has been the most extensively studied of the AlkBs in the context of PE biodegradation, it is likely that CYP153s, AlmAs, and subterminal monooxygenases play an equally significant role. Baeyer–Villiger monooxygenases (BVMO), flavoproteins that convert ketones to the corresponding ester (Fraaije et al. 2002, Minerdi et al. 2012, Fürst et al. 2019), probably account for esterase and lipase activities occasionally observed in biodegradation of additive-free PE (Lou et al. 2022, Jayan et al. 2023). Given AlkB’s strong preference for medium chain alkanes, it is surprising that AlkB alone was able to metabolize LMWPE (Yoon et al. 2012, Jeon and Kim 2015). Additionally, it remains unclear how LMWPE was able to access inner-membrane-bound AlkB in this study. While examples of alkane transporters are known (van Beilen et al. 1994, Gregson et al. 2018, Zadjelovic et al. 2020) and some have been shown to be upregulated in the presence of PE (Gravouil et al. 2017, Zadjelovic et al. 2022), better understanding of these transporters is needed to enable efficient metabolism. Taken together, the continued discovery of more efficient PE-oxidizing enzymes, coupled with the development of prolific fatty acid consumers (Walker et al. 2023), points to promising routes for biodegradation of this common plastic.

PP

PP, invented in 1951, accounts for ~20% of all plastics produced, making it the second-most widely used plastic. The primary application of PP is in packaging. PP also finds significant use as fibers, plastic toys, caps on soda bottles, and in the automotive industry (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022). In contrast to PE, a likely mechanism of PP utilization is not yet known, and fewer studies have been published.

Summary of reports on PP biodegradation

Fungal studies have mostly used Engyodontium album, P. chrysosporium, or Aspergillus sp. to degrade PP blended with other materials, such as starch or metal, using a variety of strategies to try to increase utilization (Jeyakumar et al. 2013, Sheik et al. 2015, Samat et al. 2023). PP-degrading bacterial consortia have primarily been isolated from plastic-contaminated soils and waste management facilities (Cacciari et al. 1993, Arkatkar et al. 2008, 2010, Skariyachan et al. 2022), but PP degraders have even been found in mangrove sediments (Auta et al. 2018) and Antarctic soils (Habib et al. 2020). Due to their structural similarity and co-occurrence, biodegradation of PE-PP blends has been studied (Alariqi et al. 2006, Fontanella et al. 2013), or the same organisms have been assessed on both polymers (Jeon and Kim 2016, Skariyachan et al. 2018).

Polyisoprene metabolism as context for PP biodegradation

As with other plastics, comparisons to natural polymers may reveal details about the mechanism and evolutionary origins of PP degradation. Unvulcanized poly(cis-1,4-isoprene) [natural rubber (NR)] and poly(trans-1,4-isoprene) [gutta-percha (GP)], are rubbers that share some structural similarity to PP, except that these rubbers contain C=C double bonds within their structure. NR metabolism has been discovered in many actinomycetes (Rose and Steinbüchel 2005) and Gram-negative bacteria (Tsuchii and Takeda 1990). Conversely, very few GP-degrading bacteria have been described, all in the genus Nocardia and also capable of degrading NR (Warneke et al. 2007), but the reverse is not applicable (Tsuchii and Takeda 1990). A possible explanation for this discrepancy is that far fewer plant species produce GP than NR, along with the widespread human use of NR leading to greater presence in the environment. Vulcanized rubber and styrene butadiene rubber are also biodegradable to a lesser extent (Tsuchii et al. 1985, Aboelkheir et al. 2019), but chloroprene rubber appears recalcitrant to microbial attack (Tsuchii and Takeda 1990). (B) Initial oxidation of GP has only been demonstrated in Gram-positive Nocardia sp., putatively indicating activity of Lcp (Warneke et al. 2007). Subsequent metabolism of NR oligomers proceeds by oxygenation of a terminal aldehyde by a molybdenum-dependent oxygenase (OxiAB) (Rose et al. 2005). Following this, two cycles of β-oxidation occur per isoprene unit, involving four novel phases (Banh et al. 2005); (i) rearrangement of double bonds via 2,4-dienoyl-CoA reductase and enoyl-CoA isomerase generates a suitable substrate for enoyl-CoA hydratase. (ii) 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase and thiolase release acetyl-CoA. (iii) acyl-CoA dehydrogenase and enoyl-CoA hydratase generate ⍺-methylacyl CoA racemase (Mcr) ensures that the methyl group is in the required (S) configuration for 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase. (iv) β-oxidation releases propionyl-CoA. This pathway is thought to be the same in GP-degrading bacteria (Luo et al. 2013). A detailed membrane structure is not shown because both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria are depicted.

Degradation of NR through occurs through oxidative cleavage of the double bond (Tsuchii and Takeda 1990, Linos and Steinbüchel 2005), resulting in intermediates with terminal aldehyde and methyl keto groups. Two mutually exclusive growth habits have been documented: obligate adhesion to the polymer surface and formation of clear zones on latex agar (Tsuchii et al. 1996, Rose et al. 2005). In general, the former is exhibited by Gram-negative strains while the latter is observed in Gram-positive strains. The primary NR-degrading enzyme in Gram-positive bacteria is known as latex clearing protein (lcp) (Rose et al. 2005), while in Xanthomonas (Steroidobacter) cummioxidans 35Y and other Gram-negative strains, the primary NR-degrading enzymes are rubber oxidase (roxA and roxB). All known rubber oxygenases are secreted heme proteins; RoxA/B possesses two covalently bound heme groups while Lcp possesses only one noncovalently bound heme. RoxA catalyses progressive exo-cleavage of NR, while Lcp and RoxB cleave NR in a random endo-wise fashion. (Jendrossek and Birke 2019). In Streptomyces, a heterodimeric molybdenum hydroxylase is encoded downstream of lcp, denoted oxiAB. This enzyme catalyses the oxidation of terminal aldehydes to ω-keto carboxylic acids (Rose et al. 2005). It is suggested that further metabolism of NR proceeds via β-oxidation (Rose and Steinbüchel 2005, Sharma et al. 2018). In particular, transposon mutagenesis of α-methylacyl coenzyme A racemase (mcr) in Gordonia polyisoprenivorans resulted in a loss of rubber degrading ability. This enzyme is vital in the metabolism of branched alkanes, as the desaturation step of β-oxidation is stereospecific for the (S) configuration (Sakai et al. 2004, Banh et al. 2005). This mechanism of β-oxidation undergoes two cycles, alternatively releasing acetyl- and propionyl-CoA. Presumably, homologs of these enzymes are also present in Gram-negative bacteria (Sharma et al. 2018).

As the structure of PP is also similar to GP, an understanding of GP biodegradation is desired. Unfortunately, studies of GP biodegradation are few, and detailed information about the primary oxidation of GP is sparse (Warneke et al. 2007). When GP degrading bacterium Nocardia nova SH22a was subjected to transposon mutagenesis, 66 mutants exhibited impaired growth on both NR and GP, compared to just 10 mutants deficient in GP utilization alone. Two possible explanations for this were offered: (i) the GP degradation pathway is more complex and harbors more specific steps, or (ii) pathways are almost identical, but fewer enzymes with greater substrate specificity are employed. As with NR, α-methylacyl coenzyme A racemase (Mcr) was detected, implying β-oxidation of GP oligomers (Luo et al. 2013).

Outlook for PP biodegradation

Further work is required to elucidate the mechanisms by which the products of PP breakdown contribute to microbial growth, as there does not seem to be a generally accepted mechanism for PP biodegradation. While biodegradation could feasibly be initiated via laccase-catalysed oxidation of the tertiary carbon leading to chain scission and ultimate mineralization, laccase activity does not appear to positively correlate with PP weight loss (Arkatkar et al. 2010, Jeyakumar et al. 2013, Sheik et al. 2015). The parallels between PP and polyisoprenes are promising, especially when considering that some of the PP metabolites observed by Jeyakumar et al. (2013) are similar to the substrates in polyisoprene β-oxidation, but the structural differences of the two polymers are important. In particular, saturated C–C bonds are not electrophilic, and methyl groups appear twice as frequently in PP as in polyisoprenes. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies investigating the applicability of polyisoprene-degrading bacteria for PP biodegradation. Another possible mechanism for PP biodegradation is α-oxidation, a method of branched hydrocarbon and fatty acid biodegradation in which one carbon is removed from the carboxyl end of the chain as CO2 when the 3-carbon is blocked by a methyl group (Miura 2013). If the biodegradation pathway of PP indeed mirrors that of polyisoprene, α-oxidation will additionally be utilized. However, this pathway does not generate high energy phosphates, limiting the utility of α-oxidation alone for microbial growth. Looking forward, further bioprospecting or engineering of pathways related to PE or polyisoprene biodegradation seem to be the most promising for development of PP-degrading microbes.

PVC

PVC is commonly used in construction and food packaging. It is the third most abundantly produced plastic after PE and PP, accounting for about 12% of all plastic production (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022). Incineration is of particular concern for PVC due to the release of toxic gases like Cl2, HCl, chlorinated alkanes, and dioxins (Ali et al. 2014b). Thus, biological routes to PVC disposal are of interest.

Summary of reports on plasticized PVC biodegradation

Pure PVC is very brittle, so most commercial samples of PVC are heavily plasticized, containing up to 70 wt% plasticizers and stabilizers, such as phthalate esters (PAEs), citrates, adipates, polyadipates, epoxidized soybean oil, or Zn/Ca stearate (Bueno-Ferrer et al. 2010, Giacomucci et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2022b). Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all commercial samples of PVC can be assumed to contain plasticizing additives. As such, weight loss and CO2 release measurements on plasticized PVC are unreliable and may overrepresent the biodegradability of PVC samples. Nevertheless, a wide array of fungi have been identified to biodegrade additives present in plasticized PVC (Novotný et al. 2022), including Emericella nidulans MZ103 (Webb et al. 2000), P. chrysosporium PV1 (Ali et al. 2014a, b), Cochliobolus sp. (Sumathi et al. 2016), and other white rot fungi (Kırbaş et al. 1999).

Bacterial strains have also been reported to degrade plasticized PVC, including Micrococcus, Pseudomonas, Achromobacter, Acanthopleurobacter, Bacillus, Klebsiella, Staphylococcus, and E. coli (Das et al. 2012, Patil and Bagde 2012, Anwar et al. 2016, Kumar et al. 2017). However, for these studies, FTIR results do not conclusively indicate whether PVC or additives were degraded (Sandt et al. 2021). On the other hand, Bacillus flexus and Pseudomonas citronellolis reduced the Mn of additive-containing PVC film, potentially indicating true PVC degradation (Giacomucci et al. 2019). As a rare example of biodegradation under anaerobic conditions, which is what might be expected in unmixed matrices, an anaerobic marine consortium was able to reduce the weight of a plasticized PVC film. Among the bacteria detected in the consortia, several were similar to facultative organohalide respirators Desulfovibrio dechloracetivorans and Fusibacter sp., along with Acetobacterium, some species of which were reported to dechlorinate 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane and mineralize tetrachloromethane (Giacomucci et al. 2020).

As PAEs account for 65% of all plasticizing additives (Hu et al. 2021), some studies have attempted to elaborate on their observed utilization in PVC. For example, Mycobacterium sp. NK0301 enriched from garden soil had the ability to grow on bis(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) as its sole carbon source. When cultivated on PVC sheets with DEHP additives, the bacterium removed 90% of the DEHP in 3 days. Major products identified by GC–MS were 2-ethylhexanol and 1,2-benzenedicarboxylic acid and the reaction was catalysed by a lipase-like enzyme (Nakamiya et al. 2005). An extensive review of PAE metabolism is provided by Hu et al. (2021), which describes 60 bacterial strains in diverse phyla with reported PAE degradative abilities. Both aerobic and anaerobic PAE metabolisms have been described, and aerobic metabolisms further differ between Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains. The efficiency of PAE biodegradation and substrate range varies greatly among the described species, however low molecular weight PAE metabolism has been described more often than high molecular weight. Vinyl chloride metabolism

Despite its apparent absence as a biodegradation product of PVC, it is worth mentioning that vinyl chloride monomer has been shown to be effectively utilized by several strains of Pseudomonas, Mycobacterium, and Ochrobactrum. This compound was found to be metabolized via oxidation to vinyl chloride epoxide by a monooxygenase. The chlorinated epoxide is predicted to be dechlorinated and utilized in the citric acid cycle (Verce et al. 2000, Danko et al. 2004).

The mechanism of PVC dechlorination represents a gap in current knowledge

It is interesting to note that PVC is quite unlike any of the natural polymers (lignin, polyisoprene, and cutin) we have discussed as potential evolutionary origins of plastic metabolism. Anaerobic metabolism may prove a promising avenue to explore for PVC biodegradation, since anaerobic organochlorine metabolisms appear more widespread (Atashgahi et al. 2018, Giacomucci et al. 2020), but it is not understood how biological dechlorination of PVC occurs, and the lack of compelling evidence for authentic PVC biodegradation is a pressing issue.

A few studies of PVC biodegradation by insect larvae and their associated bacterial symbiotes have used verifiably additive-free PVC to present putative evidence of dechlorination. For example, pure PVC-fed Tenebrio molitor (mealworm) larvae demonstrated some evidence of dechlorination, as FTIR spectroscopy of residual PVC in the frass (fecal waste) showed a reduction in the intensity of C–Cl signals, while 1H NMR revealed the formation of double bonds. Furthermore, gel permeation chromatography results showed a reduction in molecular weight of the residual PVC, indicating deconstruction. The gut microbes associated with PVC diet were Lactococcus sp., Spiroplasma sp., Clostridiaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae (Peng et al. 2020).

A strain denoted Klebsiella sp. EMBL-1 isolated from the gut microbiome of Spodoptera frugiperda (fall armyworm) was able to grow on PVC film, reducing its weight by 19.57% in 90 days. Through full genome sequencing and multi-omic studies, laccases, AlkBs, catalase-peroxidase, enolase, aldehyde dehydrogenase, and dihydroxy-acid dehydratase were confirmed to be present in the strain and associated with PVC degradation. Additionally, two groups of genes were identified to potentially encode dehalogenases. As expected, the FTIR spectra of the Klebsiella-degraded film showed hydroxyl groups and double bonds, indicating oxidation. Efforts were made to grow the isolate on additives present in the initial PVC sample, but it was not able to effectively utilize them, suggesting that biodegradation products originated from the PVC backbone. An activity assay utilizing pure PVC and purified catalase–peroxidase seemed to suggest oxidation of PVC powder (Zhang et al. 2022b). However, the results presented in this study are controversial (Stepnov et al. 2024, Supplementary Material). Despite these controversies, the proposed biodegradation pathway of PVC by Klebsiella sp. PS

PS accounts for about 6%–7% of annual plastic production (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022). PS typically appears as a foam-like solid (Styrofoam), often referred to as expanded polystyrene, extruded polystyrene, or high-impact polystyrene. Compared to PE, fewer studies have been conducted on the biodegradability of PS, but promising candidates for biodegradation have been isolated and enough evidence has been presented to corroborate a likely picture of PS metabolism, aided by the relative absence of obfuscating additives (Mohan et al. 2016) and the common appearance of PS-specific metabolites (Sielicki et al. 1978, Santos et al. 2000, Mohan et al. 2016, Shi et al. 2022), shown below in Fig. 8.

Summary of PS biodegradation efforts

As with other polymers, PS degrading organisms have primarily been discovered in contaminated soils (Sielicki et al. 1978, Nakamiya et al. 1997, Mohan et al. 2016, Syranidou et al. 2017) and the guts of insect larvae (Wang and Shao 2012, Yang et al. 2015a, 2018, Peng et al. 2019, Lou et al. 2020, Przemieniecki et al. 2020, Woo et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2020a, Arunrattiyakorn et al. 2022, Shi et al. 2022, Mamtimin et al. 2023). In this second category, strong evidence has been presented for the widespread ability of larval gut microbiota (particularly in T. molitor; Yang et al. 2018) to effectively utilize PS compared to other polymers. This may not be coincidental, as mealworms subsist on lignin-rich plant material and these two polymers have somewhat similar structures (Fig. 2, Table 1). In fact, a recent study found similar gut microbiomes among T. molitor fed only lignin-heavy straw or PS (Mamtimin et al. 2023).

In contrast, despite the potent lignin degradation abilities of fungi, many fungal studies have yielded limited evidence of PS biodegradation (Murali and Umamaheswari 2013, Chaudhary and Vijayakumar 2020, Chaudhary et al. 2021, Oviedo-Anchundia et al. 2021, Bhanot et al. 2024, Karimah et al. 2024). While such studies have typically reported low weight loss percentages, a Pestalotiopsis strain conferred a weight loss of 74.4% in 30 days, but potential issues in experimental techniques may suggest that this data is inaccurate (Yanto et al. 2019). Still, the exuberant growth on PS-agar compared to the other fungi and bacteria tested certainly warrants further investigation. At least one example of a PS-degrading fungal laccase has been documented, reported to reduce the weight and molecular weight distribution of PS without the addition of mediators (Zerva et al. 2023). Likewise, bacterial peroxidases and laccases have been documented in the context of PS biodegradation, for example in lignin-degrading bacterium Azotobacter beijerinckii (Nakamiya et al. 1997) and the metatranscriptome of T. molitor gut microbiota (Mamtimin et al. 2023).

Stable isotope tracing experiments have conclusively proven that aromatic side chains do not persist throughout biodegradation, with most of the carbon being mineralized as CO2 and a limited fraction being incorporated into biomass as cell membrane components (Sielicki et al. 1978, Yang et al. 2015a, b, Tian et al. 2017, Rohrbach et al. 2024), supported by the suppression of aromatic double bond signals (Mohan et al. 2016, Syranidou et al. 2017, Yanto et al. 2019) and formation of vinyl double bonds (Syranidou et al. 2017) seen in FTIR.

Probable mechanisms of PS biodegradation

In contrast to the other oxidizable plastics discussed thus far, serine hydrolases such as lipases and esterases appear much more frequently in the context of PS metabolism (Mohan et al. 2016, Przemieniecki et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2020a, Mamtimin et al. 2023). We posit that peroxidases and laccases are primarily responsible for oxidation at the α- or β-carbon of the PS chain, and that lipases and esterases play an indispensable role in chain scission, as evidenced by the fact that inhibition of these enzymes resulted in Pseudomonas sp. isolated from the gut of Zophobas atratus (superworms) being completely unable to degrade PS (Kim et al. 2020a). On the other hand, the detection of long-chain fatty acids in the mass spectrometry results of Sielicki et al. (1978) cannot be ignored; these seem to suggest that oxygenation and fission of the aromatic ring via a dioxygenase enzyme also represents a route of metabolism, though they admit that this may not represent the major route of PS catabolism. This observation is supported by upregulation of ring-opening enzyme alkylglycerol monooxygenase in the metatranscriptome of T. molitor guts (Mamtimin et al. 2023).

To the best of our knowledge, alkane monooxygenase (alkB) has only been reported once in the context of PS biodegradation (Syranidou et al. 2017). As there is only one terminal methyl group per PS chain, this enzyme is most likely involved in the oxidation of aromatic monomers like ethylbenzene (Fukuda et al. 1989). Furthermore, researchers have mined the genomes of reported PS-degrading microbes or their nearest neighbors for hydrolases and monooxygenases, demonstrating that many such enzymes are present in species commonly associated with PS biodegradation (Hou and Majumder 2021), additionally suggesting that cytochrome P450s could play a role (van Beilen et al. 2005, Pham et al. 2012). A PE-degrading strain of R. ruber C208 showed little effect when cultured on PS flakes (Mor and Sivan 2008), despite forming a biofilm and possessing both laccase and AlkB. Perhaps expression of these genes was not induced in the presence of PS or the lack of UV pretreatment in this study precluded the ability of laccase to cleave PS chains, leading to a diminished presence of suitable monooxygenase substrates.

The appearance of styrene monomer (Shi et al. 2022) and upregulation of styrene-catabolic genes in PS biodegradation studies (Syranidou et al. 2017, Wang et al. 2020) suggests that this well-understood pathway (comprising styrene monooxygenase, styrene oxide isomerase, and phenylacetaldehyde dehydrogenase to convert styrene to phenylacetic acid) is vital in PS biodegradation (Sielicki et al. 1978, Santos et al. 2000, Mohan et al. 2016, Shi et al. 2022). Phenylacetate in turn is metabolized via phenylacetyl-coenzyme A ligase. Thus, a wide variety of interesting chemicals could theoretically be produced from these aromatic biodegradation intermediates, thus incentivizing the development of PS upcycling processes (Sullivan et al. 2022).

PVA

PVA is a water-soluble synthetic polymer. PVA represents a small fraction of total plastic production, only 1250 kt in 2007 (Kawai 2010a). Nonetheless, the degradation pathway is well characterized, and it could offer insights into the biodegradation mechanisms of other plastics such as PE and PVC, with PVA representing a highly oxidized form of PE or alternatively, PVC with hydroxyl moieties instead of C–Cl bonds.

Biodegradation of PVA in Pseudomonas spp. has been known since 1973 (Suzuki et al. 1973, Shimao et al. 1983, Sakai et al. 1986), and several novel mechanisms have been discovered. For example, secondary alcohol oxidase from a PVA-degrading Pseudomonas strain was shown to oxidize secondary alcohols and β-ketols to β-diketones, while β-diketone hydrolase was shown to hydrolyze β-diketones to carboxylic acids and methyl ketones (Sakai et al. 1986). This is in contrast to Pseudomonas sp. VM15C, whose membrane-bound, PVA dehydrogenase (PvaA) is dependent on the presence of pyrroloquinoline quinone (PQQ) (Shimao et al. 1983, Masayuki et al. 1996). From the amino acid sequence, a Ca2+ site associated with PQQ binding and a heme c binding site were deduced (Masayuki et al. 1996). Subsequently, oxidized PVA hydrolase (PvaB) from the strain was found to be similarly membrane-bound and contain the lipase consensus sequence Gly–X–Ser–X–Gly, confirmed by the loss of ketone-hydrolysis activity upon treatment with serine hydrolase inhibitor. Escherichia coli transformed with the pvaBA operon showed similar PVA utilization efficiency to wild-type VM15C. At the time this study was published, there were no proteins reported to have high similarity to oxidized PvaB (Shimao et al. 2000). A 2010 review of PVA biodegradation reports previously unpublished data on Sphingopyxis sp. 113P3, which possessed a pva operon encoding for PVA dehydrogenase, oxidized PVA hydrolase, and cytochrome c, which acts as an electron acceptor for PVA dehydrogenase in the strain (Kawai 2010a). Yet another mechanism of PVA utilization is found in Alcaligenes faecalis KK314, which possesses a single enzyme with dehydrogenase activity in the presence of PQQ, forming β-hydroxyketones, and aldolase activity in the absence of PQQ, converting β-hydroxyketones into ketones and aldehydes (Matsumura et al. 1999). PEG

PEG is a water-soluble polymer widely used in detergents and medical applications. In water, PEG forms a “random coil” shape where terminal alcohols are randomly folded into the interior of the structure, taking up much more space than the linear polymer alone and making it difficult for enzymes to attack the termini (Kawai 2010a). Like PVA, PEG comprises a small percentage of the overall plastics market, but its biodegradation may offer insight into the biodegradation of highly recalcitrant polyether PUR, a copolymer containing di- or polyethylene glycol segments.

The river isolate Pseudomonas stutzeri JA1001 is able to efficiently grow on PEG of molecular weights from 200 Da to 10 kDa, with 83% of the PEG degradation ability in the periplasmic fraction. Further study revealed PEG dehydrogenase activity, generating aldehydes from terminal hydroxyls with glyoxylic acid as a product. Biodegradation was inhibited at high substrate concentration. The isolate was able to grow on glyoxylate as a sole carbon source, and malate synthase activity was detected, indicating that the strain utilizes PEG through the citric acid cycle (Obradors and Aguilar 1991). Purified PEG dehydrogenase from Sphingomonas terrae was characterized as a membrane-bound homodimer, with each subunit binding one flavin adenine dinucleotide. When expressed in E. coli, activity was lower due to the recombinant enzyme being expressed in the cytoplasm rather than the membrane. Compared to benzyl alcohol, PEG 400 and 1-heptanol were shown as the best substrates for PEG dehydrogenase. This enzyme also oxidizes the corresponding aldehydes, though the rates are lower (Sugimoto et al. 2001, Ohta et al. 2006). Subsequently, the entire PEG degradation operon from various Sphingomonads (Ohta et al. 2005, Tani et al. 2008) was characterized. Breakdown of oxidizable plastics by insect larvae

In 2015, Yang et al. (2015b) published a study that would start a new trend in the field of plastic biodegradation: the use of insect larvae and their associated gut microbiomes to biodegrade plastics. Given the relative infancy of larval-mediated plastic biodegradation, it is necessary to dedicate a section to discussing the unique results and challenges presented in these studies.

Larvae fed solely with plastics frequently show a lower survival rate compared to control diets, but this varies greatly depending on the larvae and substrate used. For example, while the survival rates of bran-fed Z. atratus (superworm) and T. molitor (mealworm) were similar, PP-fed Z. atratus showed a lower survival rate than starvation conditions, in contrast with T. molitor, which had similar survival rates on bran and PP (Yang et al. 2021a). Utilization of PS by T. molitor larvae appears robust, frequently showing similar survival rates on PS and control diets (Yang et al. 2015b, Peng et al. 2019, Mamtimin et al. 2023), though the same cannot be said for G. mellonella (greater wax moth), which show lower survival rates than starvation conditions when fed PS or PE (Lou et al. 2020). Similarly, lower survival rates of T. molitor and S. frugiperda (fall armyworm) were observed on PVC diets than control diets (Peng et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2022b). The significant detection of CO2 in T. molitor fed with α13C- or β13C-labeled PS supports the claim that this larvae can effectively utilize this polymer, but a limited fraction of the carbon in the polymer substrate was incorporated into larval biomass in the form of lipids (Yang et al. 2015b). pH of the frass and ion-exchange chromatography indicated limited PVC mineralization by T. molitor with most of the Cl ingested being present as residual PVC or chlorinated intermediates. To this end, the larvae-fed PVC lost 3% of their weight over the 16-day period (Peng et al. 2020). Therefore, the ability of larvae to utilize polymers as a legitimate source of nutrients may be overstated. In an extended trial of PS consumption by T. molitor, the initially high survival rate dropped to a lower figure than that of the starvation conditions, and larvae were observed to engage in cannibalism (Yang et al. 2018). Part of this effect is likely due to the lack of nitrogen in synthetic polymers, in contrast to the heterogenous nature of plant sources.

Also present is the problem of weight loss being overrepresented due to the presence of additives. For example, an FTIR peak at 1000 cm−1 in the untreated PE sample used to isolate A. flavus PEDX3 from G. mellonella implies that the sample was not pure, containing C–O bonds (Zhang et al. 2020). Similarly, Bacillus sp. PELW2042 from the gut of T. molitor larvae was reported to reduce the weight of UV-treated HDPE film by 17.36% in 42 days, but FTIR results indicate the untreated PE film again appeared to be impure (Zhang et al. 2023). FTIR results may be misinterpreted even if pure polymer is used. In the case of pure LDPE biodegradation by the bacterial consortium NyZ450 + NyZ451 isolated from T. molitor larvae, IR evidence of double bond and hydroxyl formation after biotreatment (Yin et al. 2020) cannot be distinguished from residual protein contamination, as what is claimed as a double bond peak is remarkably similar to the amide I (C = O stretching) and amide II (C–N stretching, N–H bending) signals characteristic of peptide bonds (Bunaciu et al. 2014, Lozano et al. 2017, Sandt et al. 2021). Protein contamination would also account for the increase in PE film weight seen after incubation with either NyZ450 or NyZ451. Taken together, reports of larval plastic degradation should be reviewed rigorously. We provide additional discussion of controversial studies in the Supplementary Material.

Hydrolyzable plastics

Hydrolyzable plastics are polyesters, polyamides, polyurethanes (PURs), or polycarbonates (PCs). In general, hydrolyzable plastic biodegradation mechanisms are better understood than oxidizable plastics. While oxidation by laccases has been documented for some hydrolyzable plastics (Fujisawa et al. 2001, Romero et al. 2007, Magnin et al. 2021), the complex mixture of intermediates released in this metabolism is often undesirable compared to the predictable compounds produced by biological hydrolysis.

PET

PET is a thermoplastic manufactured from the polycondensation of ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid. It is the fourth most widely produced plastic, after PE, PP, and PVC. It is used extensively in packaging, and its most common uses are as disposable plastic bottles and fibers (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022).

Examples of PET biodegradation in nature

The biodegradation mechanism of PET is well known and characterized, thanks to a 2016 publication from Yoshida et al. (2016), who identified the novel PET-hydrolytic soil bacterium Ideonella sakaiensis 201-F6. This strain was isolated from a microbial consortium in one sediment sample out of 250 contaminated soil, wastewater, and activated sludge samples from a PET recycling plant. PETase, the enzyme responsible for primary PET hydrolysis, was characterized and found to have 51% sequence homology to a known hydrolase with activity on PET from Thermobifida fusca (Müller et al. 2005). However, this new PETase had high specificity for PET, showing extremely low activity in an esterase assay compared to previously known enzymes but a high kcat when incubated on PET film or BHET monomer. Another hydrolase (MHETase) was identified with high specificity for MHET, the major hydrolysis product of PETase. Prior to this study, a few other enzymes with PET-hydrolyzing activity had been discovered, including cutinase from thermophilic T. fusca (Müller et al. 2005, Ribitsch et al. 2012), cutinase from fungal strains Fusarium oxysporum LCH I and F. solani (Nimchua et al. 2007, 2008), and a cutinase from a leaf-branch compost community (LCC) (Sulaiman et al. 2012), but none had as high reaction rate at room temperature or substrate specificity as PETase and MHETase discovered in I. sakaiensis. Transcript levels of the terephthalic acid degradation pathway were measured and found to be strongly upregulated when cultures were grown on PET film, BHET particles, or TPA.

Biochemistry of PET biodegradation

As one of the best examples of synthetic polymer biodegradation, the metabolic pathways and individual enzymes involved in PET degradation are well characterized. Both PETase and MHETase are secreted and related to the α/β hydrolase proteins, including cutinase and lipase, which hydrolyze cutin and lipids. The lid domain of MHETase plays a crucial role in catalytic activity, as this enzyme loses function when the lid is deleted (Knott et al. 2020). PETase cleavage occurs in two phases, first generating smaller oligomers, then cleaving terminal moieties, generating MHET as the major product and small amounts of BHET, TPA, and EG (Joo et al. 2018). PETase has been shown to be active on both amorphous and crystalline samples. Additionally, PETase from I. sakaiensis was shown to hydrolyze PET analog polyethylene-2,5-furandicarboxylate, but MHETase did not have any activity on the resulting mono(2-hydroxyethyl) furanoate or the PET copolymer mono-(2-hydroxyethyl) isophthalate (Knott et al. 2020).

Terephthalate metabolism has been known for some time (Schläfli et al. 1994); protocatechuate (PCA), the major intermediate of TPA metabolism, can be cleaved in multiple locations, branching off into different metabolic pathways. Yoshida et al. (2016) reported a PCA-3,4 dioxygenase, which utilizes β-ketoadipate metabolic pathway to ultimately shuttle material to the TCA cycle (Harwood and Parales 1996). On the other hand, ethylene glycol metabolism has been shown to proceed in I. sakaiensis in a similar fashion to how it occurs in P. putida and E. coli, with I. sakaiensis possessing calcium/lanthanide-dependent alcohol and aldehyde dehydrogenases. Ethylene glycol is oxidized to glycolaldehyde, glycolate, and finally glyoxylate. From there it is incorporated into the TCA cycle as malate (Pandit et al. 2021, Hachisuka et al. 2022). Metabolic engineering of PET biodegradation

Unlike the “oxidizable” plastics discussed in the previous sections, whose metabolisms are not yet fully known and suffer from low biodegradation rates, enzymatic PET degradation reliably produces PET monomers and is efficient (I. sakaiensis was able to reduce the weight of thin PET film by >90% in 40 days, corresponding to a half-life of just 12 days). This has allowed researchers to begin seriously investigating PET waste as a feedstock to biologically produce chemicals of interest, an approach sometimes called “bio-upcycling”. For example, PET waste has been used to produce vanillin (Sadler and Wallace 2021) and adipic acid (Valenzuela-Ortega et al. 2023) in engineered strains of E. coli, utilizing an improved and purified version of LCC (Tournier et al. 2020) for the initial depolymerization of PET waste.

Protocatechuate, an intermediate in the metabolism of PET, is a key metabolite in many aromatic metabolism pathways (Johnson and Beckham 2015). On the other hand, since ethylene glycol is also generated from the hydrolysis of PET, strains can be engineered to utilize ethylene glycol as a carbon source, increasing the yield of aromatic compounds (Franden et al. 2018, Li et al. 2019a). Alternatively, valuable chemicals could instead be produced from ethylene glycol, utilizing terephthalate as a carbon source (Pandit et al. 2021, Kalathil et al. 2022).

Terephthalate monomer has been used as a sole carbon source to produce polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), a type of biodegradable plastic, in several Pseudomonas strains (Kenny et al. 2008). In another study, β-ketoadipate was biologically produced from PET waste (Werner et al. 2021). Efficient production of β-ketoadipate and PHAs from mixed plastic waste including PET has been achieved in engineered strains of P. putida by utilizing chemical oxidation to generate soluble monomers suitable for bioconversion (Sullivan et al. 2022).

Through a combination of machine learning and site-directed mutagenesis, improved versions of IsPETase and LCC have been created (Austin et al. 2018, Lu et al. 2022). Notably, a computationally derived FAST-PETase is able to near-perfectly degrade postconsumer PET products in as little as 1 week (Lu et al. 2022). Many additional PETases have also been identified in nature since the discovery of IsPETase (Danso et al. 2018, White and Wallace 2023, Bergeson et al. 2024), including some PUR esterases with activity on PET (Molitor et al. 2020, de Witt et al. 2024a). Recently, a membrane-bound chimera of PETase and MHETase has been successfully displayed on the surface of the marine bacterium Vibrio natriegens, with implications for ocean bioremediation (Li et al. 2023). Thanks to its easily hydrolyzable ester moieties and synthetically valuable degradation products, PET has become a promising and hopeful model to reference when studying other plastics.

Biodegradable polyesters

Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) are a class of naturally occurring polyesters produced by some bacteria as stores of carbon and energy, forming aggregates in the cytosol that can account for up to 90% of a cell’s dry weight (Jendrossek et al. 1996). PHAs are comprised of repeating units of 3-hydroxy fatty acids of length 3–15.

Crystalline PHAs are found outside the cell left behind by dead bacteria. Consequently, extracellular PHA depolymerases exist to take advantage of this resource. Intracellular PHA is highly amorphous due to the binding of peptide and phospholipid ligands. Intracellular PHA depolymerase can only act on this type (Jendrossek and Handrick 2002). In carbon-limited conditions, PHA synthesis is inhibited, and degradation occurs (Senior and Dawes 1973). PHA depolymerases are serine hydrolases and are inhibited by propylfluorophosphate and acylsulfonyl compounds. They are highly specific for a certain chain length, with polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) and polyhydroxyvalerate (PHV) depolymerases unable to hydrolyze a bond between 3-hydroxybutyrate and 3-hydroxyhexanoate. PHA depolymerases have been shown to catalyse hydrolysis 2–3 ester bonds away from the hydroxyl end of the chain. The PHA depolymerase active site contains the serine hydrolase consensus sequence Gly–X–Ser–X–Gly. PHA depolymerases are discussed at length in excellent reviews by Jendrossek et al. (1996) and Jendrossek and Handrick (2002) and the sources referenced within.

Several synthetic polymers structurally similar to PHA are also biodegradable, namely polycaprolactone (PCL) and poly[lactic acid] (PLA). PCL depolymerase expression is induced by cutin monomers, implying that it is a cutinase, a type of serine hydrolase (Shimao 2001). The biodegradability of PCL was discussed by Goldberg (1995), showing over 40% biodegradability in 60 days, assessed with the compost burial method ISO 14855 (International Organization for Standardization 2012). Enzymatic hydrolysis of PCL produces 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid, an intermediate in ω-oxidation (Nakasaki et al. 2006). It is likely that adipic acid is formed via ω-oxidation (Miura 2013), then converted to succinyl-CoA and acetyl-CoA via β-oxidation. It is worth noting that β-oxidation of adipic acid and other dicarboxylates requires a specific set of enzymes located near the protocatechuate locus in Acinetobacter (Parke et al. 2001) and Pseudomonas (Ackermann et al. 2021), or distributed throughout the genome in Halopseudomonas (Kruse et al. 2024).

PLA is a type of polyester that is slowly hydrolyzed in aqueous conditions. PLA is used in medical applications and is the most common plastic filament in 3D printing. Several esterases, proteases, and organisms have been identified that biodegrade PLA (Pranamuda et al. 1997, Tokiwa and Calabia 2006, Kim and Park 2010). PLA is optically active and the stereochemistry affects the biodegradation mechanism (Kawai 2010a). For example, poly[(l)-lactic acid] was shown to be biodegradable by Bromelain, Pronase, and Proteinase K (Williams 1981). On the other hand, cutinase-like PLA hydrolytic enzymes have been found with activity on racemic PLA and preferential activity on d-PLA (Kawai 2010b). Other examples of PLA utilization are detailed in (Kawai 2010a) and the sources referenced within. For further reading on biodegradation of polyesters (Tokiwa and Calabia 2007, Kawai 2010a), polyester blends (Shah et al. 2014), blends of biodegradable and traditional plastics (Iwamoto and Tokiwa 1994, Martins-Franchetti et al. 2010), or polyorganosiloxanes (silicone) (Rościszewski et al. 1998, Rücker et al. 2023), we urge the reader to refer to these excellent reviews, as there is much more that can be said on these topics. Nylon

Polyamides, known by the propriety eponym nylons, are a widely used class of synthetic fibers. Polycaprolactam (Nylon-6) is synthesized via ring-opening polymerization of caprolactam, while Nylon-66, a copolymer of 1,6-hexanediamine and adipic acid, is manufactured via polycondensation of its constituent monomers. Several instances of Nylon-6, biodegradation have been reported. Flavobacterium (Arthrobacter) sp. KI72 was discovered with the ability to grow on cyclic dimers of ε-aminocaproic acid, along with nylon oligomers (Kinoshita et al. 1975). This enzyme was classified as 6-aminohexanoic acid cyclic dimer hydrolase (NylA), which did not show any activity on oligopeptides, which are also linked by amide bonds (Kinoshita et al. 1977). From the same strain, another serine hydrolase (NylB) with activity on 6-aminohexanoic acid oligomers was discovered (Kinoshita et al. 1981). Both enzymes are encoded on a single plasmid, pOAD2 (Kanagawa et al. 1989), and strains cured of this plasmid lost all nylonase activity (Negoro et al. 1992). An additional hydrolytic enzyme with activity on nylon oligomers (NylC) as well as N-carbobenzoxy derivatives was discovered in pOAD2 in Flavobacterium sp. KI725, an ability not shared by the previously described enzymes (Negoro et al. 1992). This enzyme was classified as Endo-Type 6-Aminohexanoate oligomer hydrolase, which is an N-terminal nucleophile threonine hydrolase that undergoes post-translational autocatalysis to generate alpha and beta subunits, which assemble into a tetrameric quaternary structure (Kakudo et al. 1993, Negoro et al. 2012, 2018). In contrast, 6-aminohexanoate dimer hydrolase (NylB) is a serine hydrolase with a two domain structure similar to β-lactamases (Negoro et al. 2005, 2007). 6-aminohexanoate cyclic dimer hydrolase (NylA) is a single peptide in the amidase signature superfamily with a Ser–Ser–Lys catalytic triad (Yasuhira et al. 2010).

Nylon biodegradation was hypothesized to have evolved in response to the presence of nylon oligomers and monomers in chemical plant wastewater. Supporting this hypothesis, a Pseudomonas strain (Kanagawa et al. 1989), an Agromyces strain, and a Kocuria strain (Yasuhira et al. 2007) were isolated from factory wastewater with similar abilities. Of the two nylon hydrolases identified in the Pseudomonas strain, one was homologous to nylA in Flavobacterium sp. KI72, but the second had low homology to the previously discovered nylB. Additionally, the genes for nylon degradation in Pseudomonas NK87 are located on two different plasmids (Kanagawa et al. 1989). The Agromyces and Kocuria nylonases were suggested to be highly homologous to those of Flavobacterium sp. KI72, but amino acid sequence differences in the Kocuria sp. NylC conferred greater thermostability (Yasuhira et al. 2007).

Enzymes responsible for nylon degradation appear to be secreted, based on the formation of clear zones around cultures grown on nylon-containing solid media (Negoro et al. 1992, Yasuhira et al. 2007). Furthermore, oligopeptide permeases (oppA-F) discovered on the pOAD2 plasmid likely enable 6-aminohexanoate transport into the cell (Kato et al. 1995). Once inside, 6-aminohexanoate (Ahx) monomers and pyruvate are first converted to adipate semialdehyde and alanine, respectively, by Ahx aminotransferase (NylD), a homolog of GABA aminotransferases that requires pyridoxal 5′-phosphate as a cofactor. Then adipate semialdehyde is oxidized to adipic acid by adipate semialdehyde oxidoreductase (NylE). Over 90% of 6-aminohexanoate monomer is converted to adipate, which is likely utilized in β-oxidation and the TCA cycle (Takehara et al. 2018, Negoro et al. 2021). The most effective nylon-degrading enzymes were revealed to be NylC and NylB discovered in Kocuria sp. This accessibility could be improved by conducting reactions near the glass transition temperature; for this task a thermostable mutant of NylC was constructed with activity at temperatures as high as 80°C (Bell et al. 2024). Still more polyamidases by comparing known NylC sequences to available databases, elucidating several novel enzymes with activity on both Nylon-6 and polyester amides (de Witt et al. 2024b).

In contrast to Nylon-6, the biodegradation of Nylon-66, has been less frequently studied. Oxidative biodegradation has been demonstrated to some extent by MnP or laccase purified from T. versicolor ISO 6482 in the presence of HBT (Fujisawa et al. 2001). Powdered Nylon-66 was shown to be biodegradable by NylC, though the extent of degradation was only 60% of what was observed for Nylon-6 powders (Nagai et al. 2014). Limited information is available on the biodegradation of 1,6-hexanediamine (Kim et al. 2002).

PUR

Polyurethanes (PURs) are the sixth most widely produced plastic, accounting for about 5%–8% of the market (PlasticsEurope 2019, 2021, 2022). PUR differs from the other plastics discussed previously in two key ways. First, while other plastics usually have a single repeating unit that is common across every sample of the plastic, PUR can be made of either aromatic or aliphatic monomers, and regardless of the diisocyanate used to form carbamate (urethane) bonds, PURs are further classified as polyether or polyester PUR depending on whether dicarboxylate or polyol comonomers are used. For this reason, the terms “polyurethanes” and “polyurethane” are used somewhat interchangeably. The second key difference is that most PURs are thermosetting resins whose morphology cannot be changed through heating once synthesized. This is in contrast to PE, PS, PVC, and PET, which are thermoplastic resins that can be reheated and molded into new shapes. Although the polymerization reaction is between a C=O group and an OH group, this is not considered a polycondensation reaction because water is not produced as a biproduct.

Biodegradation of synthesized PURs

The earliest report on PUR biodegradation in 1968 involved the synthesis of 84 polyether and 12 polyester PURs with molecular weights ranging from 1130 to 6740 Da from the monomers shown below in Fig. 14. In their report, they found that the fungi tested grew best on the polyester PURs formulated with aromatic diisocyanates. In contrast, polyether PURs were much less susceptible to biodegradation, with only those made with straight chain diols and aromatic diisocyanates seeing any significant biodegradation while those made with bisphenols showed no growth at all (Darby and Kaplan 1968). Based on this early result, three polyester PURs and three polyether PURs consisting of toluene-2,4-diisocyanate and either polypropylene glycol or poly(diethylene glycol adipate) were used to grow soil isolate Comamonas (Delftia) acidovorans TB-35. The strain was able to utilize poly(diethylene glycol adipate) and associated polyester PURs as its sole carbon source, with adipate derived from polyester segments acting as its primary source of nutrition. The microbe was able to utilize about 50 mg of polyester PUR in 7 days, but was unable to grow on polyether PUR (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1995). The cell surface-bound PUR esterase (PudA) was characterized and shown to have a Ser–Glu–His catalytic triad close to a hydrophobic binding domain (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1997, 1999). The PUR esterase did not exhibit lipase activity or PHA depolymerase activity but did show a limited ability to hydrolyze low molecular weight PLA (Akutsu et al. 1998). Biodegradation of commercial PURs

Another PUR-degrading bacterium, Pseudomonas chlororaphis, was identified with the ability to grow on Impranil™ DLN, a proprietary polyester PUR (Howard et al. 1999). This degradation ability was later found to be due to two extracellular enzymes: a 63 kDa protein exhibiting both esterase and protease activities and a 31 kDa protein with esterase activity (Ruiz et al. 1999). Both enzymes were similar in size to a few previously reported PUR-degrading enzymes (Crabbe et al. 1994, Akutsu et al. 1998, Howard and Blake 1998), suggesting similarity in structure and function. Sequencing of secreted polyester PURases from P. chloraphis (Stern and Howard 2000) and P. fluorescens (Vega et al. 1999) showed significant homology to lipases, while the membrane-bound PudA from C. acidovorans is more closely related to acetylcholine esterases (Nomura et al. 1998). A strain of P. aeruginosa isolated from plastic-contaminated soil showed the ability to degrade PUR-diol and Impranil™ DLN by 32% in 10 days due to an esterase (Mukherjee et al. 2011). Esterase from P. fluorescens, Pseudomonas sp. lipase, and Bacillus sp. protease were reacted with Impranil™ DLN. Pseudomonas sp. lipase showed the greatest hydrolysis of Impranil™ DLN, attacking ester bonds (Biffinger et al. 2015).

Two endophytic fungi, Pestalotiopsis microspora E2712A, and P. microspora E3317B, had the ability to grow on Impranil™ DLN both aerobically and anaerobically, an ability not previously observed. A 29 kDa serine hydrolase was partially purified from the organisms, consistent with previous results. Interestingly, P. microspora has been shown to engage in horizontal gene transfer, implying that the PUR degradation ability, which is not universal within the genus, could have been inherited from another source (Russell et al. 2011). Recently, two strains of yeast were isolated with the ability to grow on Impranil™ DLN as its sole carbon source. Both strains showed high esterase and protease activity in standard assays (Giyahchi and Moghimi 2023).

Due proprietary nature of Impranil™ DLN, its exact composition and structure are not explicitly known to the wider scientific community. This secrecy leads to conflicting reports of Impranil™ DLN structure in the literature (Howard et al. 2012, Biffinger et al. 2015), a problem considering that Impranil™ DLN is becoming more widely used as a substrate to screen for polyester hydrolysis (Molitor et al. 2020). The hydrolysis of Impranil™ DLN and two other proprietary PURs was investigated in Halopseudomonas formosensis FZJ by de Witt et al. (2024a) (Fig. 16). In the case of Impranil™ DLN, adipic acid, neopentyl glycol (NPG), 1,6-hexanediol, and a dimer of NPG and glycolic acid (GA) were detected as the major products of a secreted hydrolase; deletion of this gene revealed the involvement of other hydrolases in biodegradation by this strain. Consistent with previously discussed results, the strain derived its carbon and energy from adipic acid, lacking the ability to grow on 1,6-hexane diol, NPG, or aliphatic diamino constituents of Impranil™ DLN. 4-hydroxybutanoate was detected in the culture supernatant, but not in purified enzyme reactions, suggesting that this was a dead-end oxidation product of 1,6-hexanediol (de Witt et al. 2024a).

Outlook for PUR biodegradation

Polyester PUR-degrading cultures are unable to achieve significant growth when PUR is supplied as the sole nitrogen source (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1995), and the absence of nitrogen containing compounds in chromatography methods is likely a result of carbamate polymer sections being too large or insoluble to be detected rather than being suggestive of utilization (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1997, Mukherjee et al. 2011, de Witt et al. 2024a). In polyester PUR hydrolysis, adipic acid is identified as the major degradation product and nutrient source (Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1995, de Witt et al. 2024a). Many of the identified polyester “PURases” have been defined as esterases or lipases (Nomura et al. 1998, Vega et al. 1999, Stern and Howard 2000, Howard et al. 2012), but the significance of protease activity demonstrated by a few of these enzymes remains unclear (Howard and Blake 1998, Ruiz et al. 1999, Biffinger et al. 2015, Vargas-Suárez et al. 2019, Giyahchi and Moghimi 2023). Coupled with the fact that these polyester “PURases” have occasionally been shown to hydrolyze PET (Molitor et al. 2020, de Witt et al. 2024a), it would be more accurate to classify them as polyesterases. Still, enzymatic polyester hydrolysis of PURs is valuable in the greater context of plastic bioremediation as these compounds are often present as coatings on other types of plastic.

In contrast, the polyether class of PUR appears much more recalcitrant (Darby and Kaplan 1968, Nakajima-Kambe et al. 1995, Álvarez-Barragán et al. 2016). Most studies of polyether PUR biodegradation involve the use of polyether PURs formulated with additives like PCL (Magnin et al. 2019) or urea (Phua et al. 1987), and yield limited biochemical insight beyond what has already been presented for the polyester type (that is, PUR-degradative activity is ascribed to esterases). The most compelling example of polyether PUR biodegradation comes from Gaytán et al. (2020), who provide fairly strong metagenomic, chromatographic, and spectroscopic evidence of toluene diisocyanate release and polyol metabolism. Oxidative degradation by laccase of both polyether and polyester PURs has also been investigated; a laccase mediator and UV pretreatment were both used (Magnin et al. 2021). This oxidative mechanism is expected to release a wide array of different metabolites, making bioconversion into valuable chemicals more challenging. However, due to the difficulty of biodegrading polyether PUR, this strategy may warrant further investigation.

Therefore, despite many reports of so called “PURases,” there is very little conclusive evidence that carbamate bonds of PUR are broken, with the few reports of enzymatic carbamate bond hydrolysis being limited to agricultural chemicals (Marty and Vouges 1987, Pohlenz et al. 1992) or low molecular weight dicarbamate compounds (Akutsu-Shigeno et al. 2006, Utomo et al. 2020). Recently, however, three urethanases with activity on low-molecular weight dicarbamates and polyester PUR samples alike (Mw = 100 000) were discovered in a soil metagenome library exposed to PUR waste (Branson et al. 2023, Bayer et al. 2024). Based on amino acid sequence and structural homology, these enzymes were categorized as amidase signature family enzymes with a Ser–Ser–Lys catalytic triad (Bayer et al. 2024). This classification was supported by their apparent activity on Nylon-6 (Bayer et al. 2024, Bell et al. 2024). In another notable exception, a polyamidase from Nocardia farcinia fused with a PHA depolymerase binding domain from A. faecalis was able to hydrolyze both ester and carbamate bonds of two PUR samples, though ester hydrolysis appeared to proceed more readily. Compared to polyamidase, the fusion protein showed poor activity on a carbamate model substrate, but a much greater ability to hydrolyze the PUR samples, emphasizing the importance of substrate binding in plastic biodegradation (Gamerith et al. 2016).

PC

Polycarbonate (PC) is an “engineering plastic,” praised for its resistance to deformation under high temperatures or sudden compressive forces. According to one source, 2.7 million tons of PC are produced annually, and some everyday examples include CDs, reusable water bottles, receipt paper, and office water cooler jugs (Valco Group 2021). PC refers to the type of bond between the monomers, and thus there are different types of PCs (Fig. 18). Preeminent among these is Bisphenol A PC, traditionally made by reacting phosgene and Bisphenol A (BPA) or a sodium salt of BPA under basic conditions (Carey and Giuliano 2017) or via transesterification of diphenyl carbonate (Valco Group 2021). BPA has been shown to leach out of PC and have numerous negative health and environmental impacts (Alasalmi 2023), and is being phased out in favor of other bisphenols less susceptible to hydrolysis (Glausiusz 2014), such as bisphenol S (BPS), or alternative plastics, such as Eastman’s Tritan™. A 2020 review of health-related studies on bisphenols suggests that BPS and other bisphenols are likely to have similar health impacts as BPA (Kim et al. 2020b).

PC has characteristics of both oxidative and hydrolytic metabolisms

Comparatively few studies have been conducted on the biodegradation of PC. Fungus Geotrichum candidum was accidentally discovered growing on a CD (Bosch 2001, Garcia-Guinea et al. 2001). Closer study showed that the strain could not only degrade the PC but also solubilize and mobilize the aluminum used in CDs. Enzymatic assays found aryl-alcohol oxidase (AAO), esterase, and MnP activity but did not detect lignin peroxidase or laccase activity. AAO plays a role in the production of H2O2 required as a substrate for peroxidase (Romero et al. 2007).

Another study published in 2009 examined the degradation of BPA PC under field seawater and controlled laboratory conditions. After 12 months of incubation on BPA- PC in sterile seawater, bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and plated on Zoebell’s marine agar before being incubated at 30°C for 2 days. Pseudomonas sp. BP2 was identified as the most abundant hydrolase-producing strain. The number average molecular weight (Mn) of the seawater and lab samples was both slightly reduced. Low esterase activity was detected in the lab samples (Artham and Doble 2009). Following this, the team tested two fungal strains isolated from a plastic dumpsite: E. album MTP091 (SF1) and Penicillium spp. MTP093 (SF3), as well as commercial strain Phanerochaete chrysosoporium NCIM 1170 (SF2) on their ability to biodegrade UV-treated, thermally treated, or untreated PC. The highest weight loss observed was UV-pretreated PC incubated with SF2, at 5.4% in 12 months. The decrease in methyl index indicated oxidation of the methyl groups of BPA (Artham and Doble 2010). Recently, a strain of Pseudoxanthomonas with the ability to degrade BPA-PC was isolated from activated sludge at a wastewater treatment plant in Suzhou, China. The bacterium reduced the intensity of the carbonate FTIR peak characteristic of BPA-PC, indicating hydrolysis of this bond. A new peak at 3300 cm−1 indicated the emergence of a hydroxyl group in biotreated BPA-PC. GC–MS results showed exohydrolysis of the BPA-PC chain by the presence of chain terminating monophenol 4-cumylphenol (Yue et al. 2021). The low weight loss observed was probably a result of BPA toxicity, disrupting the bacteria’s ability to synthesize lipids and form a cell membrane (Zaborowska et al. 2020). Steps toward bisphenol bioremediation

Due to the toxicity of BPA, PC bioremediation strategies will need to ensure its rapid degradation as well. Fortunately, efforts have already been made in characterizing BPA metabolism and its ultimate fate in the environment, discussed in an excellent review by Im and Löffler (2016). Most examples of plant and algal BPA metabolism are glycosylations, and the ultimate fate of BPA-glycosides is unclear. Fungal and bacterial examples of BPA metabolism involve enzymes discussed previously in this review, including cytochrome P450, monooxygenases, laccases, p-hydroxybenzoate hydroxylase, Mn peroxidase, and laccase (Fukuda et al. 2001). Major intermediates of BPA biodegradation are 4-hydroxybenzoate/benzaldehyde, 4-hydroxyacetophenone, 4-hydroxycumyl alcohol, and 4-isopropenylphenol. However, this does not represent the full diversity of BPA metabolisms in bacteria or fungi, with some fugal strains forming oligomers of BPA and some bacteria forming nitrated or o-methylated derivatives. The pathways and mechanisms of BPA metabolism are still not fully understood.

Conclusions

When considering the characteristics of plastic-degrading organisms, several themes repeatedly appear. Plastic-degrading fungi are often white rot basidiomycetes, such as P. chrysosporium or E. album, which express peroxidases and laccases during ligninolytic conditions when the organism is starved of carbon and nitrogen to break down the tough plant polymer lignin (Barr and Aust 1994b). While this could be an effect of selection bias, fungi with other growth habits, such as plant-symbiotic mycorrhizal or commensal endophytic fungi, do not possess the necessary machinery to degrade recalcitrant plant polymers (Miyauchi et al. 2020). It is important to remember that fungal plastic biodegradation is a starvation response and is not specific to the presence of plastic. Indeed, ligninolytic activity is involved in the degradation of a number of unrelated environmental pollutants (Barr and Aust 1994a). Penicillium and Aspergillus spp., well-known ascomycetes with a cosmopolitan distribution and a wide range of biotechnological applications, are also commonly implicated. Many of the plastic-degrading bacteria discussed in this paper have been of the genera Pseudomonas and Bacillus. It would, however, be reductionist to suggest that these large, cosmopolitan genera with well documented propensity for horizontal gene transfer are somehow uniquely capable of plastic biodegradation, as some of the most compelling evidence for plastic biodegradation presented in this review come from bacteria in diverse genera (Yasuhira et al. 2007, Yoshida et al. 2016, Yue et al. 2021, Branson et al. 2023, Zampolli et al. 2023). These findings are supported by several recent publications, which attempt to describe the phylogeny of all known plastic-degrading microbes and categorize verified plastic-active enzymes (Gan and Zhang 2019, Gambarini et al. 2021, 2022, Buchholz et al. 2022). Insect larvae such as waxworms (G. mellonella and P. interpunctella) and mealworms (T. molitor and Z. atratus) have shown plastic biodegradation ability, the most effective combination being T. molitor and PS. This ability is related to their natural diets: beeswax or plant material rich in lignin and cutin, and is heavily dependent on the activity of gut microbiota, with larvae losing plastic-degrading abilities when supplied with antibiotic (Yang et al. 2015a, b, 2018, Mamtimin et al. 2023). Reports of insect salivary enzymatic action on plastics is controversial (Sanluis-Verdes et al. 2022, Stepnov et al. 2024), and the symbiotic nature of this bioremediation strategy remains to be discovered.

Biofilm formation is an important step in bacterial plastic biodegradation (Shah et al. 2008), and as such plastic-degrading bacteria often have a hydrophobic cell wall or the ability to produce biosurfactants (Sivan 2011). Accordingly, plastic degrading enzymes are secreted from the cells or anchored to the cell surface (Howard 2002, Yoshida et al. 2016); secreted enzymes possess hydrophobic binding domains to mediate interactions with hydrophobic polymers. As is the case with PEG and alkanes, the presence of the plastic sometimes induces the expression of plastic degrading enzymes in bacteria, a feature not seen in fungi (Benson and Shapiro 1975, Grund et al. 1975, Kawai 2010a).

As we have discussed, plastics can be categorized as oxidizable or hydrolyzable based on their first step in biodegradation. Biological oxidation can be the result of alkane monooxygenase/alkane hydroxylase (AlkB, AlmA) (Yoon et al. 2012, Gregson et al. 2018) generating primary alcohols or the result of lignin and MnPs (LiP and MnP) and laccases (LMCOs), which utilize H2O2 and O2 to nonspecifically oxidize random positions in a polymer chain, resulting in a diverse array of products giving rise to chain scission (Iiyoshi et al. 1998). Laccase was previously thought to only act on phenolic compounds unless a mediator is present. Despite much study, it remains unclear if LMCOs oxidize plastic directly or indirectly through a phenolic mediator (Zampolli et al. 2023, Zerva et al. 2023), though the latter seems more likely (Niku-Paavola and Viikari 2000). Mediator candidates in the context of plastic biodegradation are phthalic acid generated by hydrolysis of PAEs (Sanluis-Verdes et al. 2022), PQQ (Reid and Fewson 1994), which acts as an electron acceptor in PVA biodegradation (Masayuki et al. 1996), or hydroquinone derivatives, which drive nonenzymatic biological fenton chemistry in fungi (Krueger et al. 2015). Even in the case of LMCOs from R. opacus R7, which used purified enzymes and no mediators, benzoate was present as a LDPE additive (Zampolli et al. 2023). In this model of laccase mediation, primary alcohols generated from hydrolytic cleavage of aromatic esters could also serve to induce expression of alkane hydroxylase genes. Significant work has gone into developing more efficient laccase mediators (Obleser et al. 2022). Importantly, bacterial laccases appear much better suited for plastic oxidation than fungal laccases (Iiyoshi et al. 1998, Santo et al. 2013, Zampolli et al. 2023).

In the case of PVC, dechlorination is a necessary step in complete mineralization. A proposed candidate for this activity is nonheme chloroperoxidase (Zhang et al. 2022b), though this may not be true as these enzymes typically catalyse chlorination rather than dechlorination (Atashgahi et al. 2018, Schuiten et al. 2021). Another possibility is oxidative dechlorination via an epoxide intermediate (Danko et al. 2004). Biodegradation of PS appears to proceed via laccase/peroxidase oxidation of β-carbons, followed by hydrolytic cleavage via esterase-like enzymes (Nakamiya et al. 1997, Mohan et al. 2016, Przemieniecki et al. 2020, Kim et al. 2020a, Mamtimin et al. 2023). Oxidation and ring cleavage of aromatic PS sidechains by monooxygenases and dioxygenases forming fatty acids has also been documented (Sielicki et al. 1978, Mamtimin et al. 2023). Further metabolism of PE, PP, and PS oxo-intermediates occurs via β-oxidation (Albertsson et al. 1987, Gravouil et al. 2017), though the mechanism is still not fully known for PP and PS. In PVA, which shares structural similarity to PE, PP, and PVC, hydroxyl groups already present in the chain are oxidized to ketones via a secondary alcohol oxidase or a PVA-specific dehydrogenase, using PQQ as a cofactor. PVA is further metabolized by serine hydrolases to form carboxylates or aldehydes and ketones (Kawai 2010a). In the case of PEG, terminal hydroxy groups are oxidized until a glyoxylate is cleaved off.

Hydrolyzable plastics are mostly polyesters and are hydrolyzed by serine hydrolases. In the case of PET, the aromatic terephthalic acid is further metabolized by dioxygenases resulting in ring cleavage (Knott et al. 2020). Nylon-6 is degraded by serine or threonine hydrolases, an ability which appears to have evolved in response to polyamide in the environment (Negoro et al. 1992). While hydrolysis of PUR carbamate bonds is rare (Gamerith et al. 2016, Branson et al. 2023), polyester PURases with protease, esterase, or amidase activity have been reported (Howard 2002, de Witt et al. 2024a), and protease-like enzymes catalyse the hydrolysis of poly[(l)-lactate] (PLLA) (Williams 1981), while esterase-like enzymes catalyse the hydrolysis of the other enantiomer (Kawai 2010b). PCL is hydrolyzed by cutinase-like enzymes (Shimao 2001). PHAs are synthesized by bacteria and are metabolized by hydrolytic PHA depolymerases that are specific for one or two types of PHA (Jendrossek et al. 1996). Again, metabolites of polyester hydrolysis are utilized in β-oxidation (except in the case of PLA and PET), though the common occurrence of dicarboxylates requires specific enzymes not ubiquitously expressed (Parke et al. 2001, Ackermann et al. 2021, Kruse et al. 2024).

PC is a case where both oxidative degradation of bisphenol methyl groups and hydrolytic degradation of the carbonate moiety can occur (Artham and Doble 2009, 2010). Further study is needed in this area as biodegradation of PC has the potential to release toxic BPA, which must also be metabolized for efficient bioremediation (Im and Löffler 2016). Similar gaps exist in PUR biodegradation, in which proprietary PURs with unclear structures are used and aromatic diisocyanate derivatives are not found to be efficiently metabolized (Gaytán et al. 2020). For PP biodegradation, no widely agreed upon mechanism exists (though parallels to polyisoprene utilization may provide useful clues; Rose and Steinbüchel 2005, Sharma et al. 2018), while in the case of PVC biodegradation, many reported “PVC degrading” organisms are simply utilizing the plasticizing additives (Sumathi et al. 2016) and the dechlorinating mechanism is not understood. Ultimately, biodegradation products are utilized in the TCA cycle.

From an applied perspective, PS, PUR, and PC could serve as feedstocks for the bioproduction of novel aromatic compounds, as has been done for PET (Sadler and Wallace 2021, Valenzuela-Ortega et al. 2023). Presently, tandem chemical and biological approaches show promise in efficiently dealing with plastic waste (Sullivan et al. 2022, Branson et al. 2023, Walker et al. 2023), and could be applied to such bioupcycling goals. The use of chemical depolymerization for the first step in these processes highlights the challenges and immediate future research goals of plastic bioremediation: the poor solubility and substrate accessibility of plastics leads to rate-limiting primary enzymatic deconstruction. Therefore, discovery of new plastic-degrading enzymes or engineering of existing enzymes will enable pure bioupcycling of plastics. Purely biological approaches can be preferable to current chemical–biological methods for two reasons: (i) bioprocesses can be carried out at much lower temperatures than chemical deconstruction methods, and (ii) chemical deconstruction methods often involve the use of toxic chemicals. Amorphous plastics are thought to be more readily biodegraded due to the disruption of stabilizing intermolecular forces. Therefore, thermostable mutants of plastic-degrading enzymes could be employed to catalyse the initial depolymerization step near glass transition temperatures (Branson et al. 2023, Zampolli et al. 2023, Bell et al. 2024).

If the goal of efficient enzymatic depolymerization can be achieved, and the appropriate biocontainment safeguards are put in place, it would enable environmental plastic bioremediation. Engineered microbes with the ability to metabolize multiple types of plastic could be used in wastewater treatment plants (Mitrano et al. 2019), or as soil amendments to remove MP/NPs. Such strategies have been applied with some success for the removal of PAEs from these environments (Liao et al. 2010, Zhao et al. 2017, 2019, Zhang et al. 2018). However, the results in Liao et al. (2010) indicated that native soil microbes eventually outcompeted bioaugmentative additions. To overcome this, the metabolic capabilities of native soil microbes could be engineered to be dependent on plastics, thereby creating a durable yet ultimately self-limiting treatment. A number of strategies have also been developed for biocontainment of genetically engineered microbes, including CRISPR/Cas9 systems triggered by temperature or other environmental signals (Rottinghaus et al. 2022), synthetic auxotrophy employing nonstandard amino acids (Xie et al. 2007, Mandell et al. 2015, Kunjapur et al. 2021), or genetic circuits consisting of tunable transcription regulators (Chan et al. 2016). While the latter two could be used in industrial bioupcycling or wastewater treatment, the former appears most applicable for environmental bioremediation, as the addition of nonstandard amino acids or antibiotics to large areas of soil or ocean is undesirable. Additionally, for environmental release, the presence of environmental analogs to target substrates can lead to leaky expression of inserted genes. For example, utilizing an alkS regulator upstream of an operon containing degradation genes for multiple types of plastic could allow soil or ocean microbes to effectively remove MP/NPs containing PE, but it would also be induced by alkanes, potentially wasting cellular resources. Thus, the development of plastic-specific transcription factors would also be very useful.

From an environmental and economic perspective, engineering bioremedial or industrial strains is only half of the story. Although few engineered bioremediation strains have seen widespread deployment to date (Chemla et al. 2024), plastic biodegradation ironically comes with an environmental cost due to emissions; both aerobic and anerobic plastic metabolism release greenhouse gases (CO2 and CH4, respectively). Without accounting for its sequestration in biomass, we estimate that all the manufactured PE, PP, PVC, PET, PS, and PUR (assuming TDI-co-DEG/Adipate as a model PUR) would release 20.6 gigatons of CO2 if completely mineralized. For context, global CO2 emissions amounted to 37.55 gigatons in 2023 (Tiseo 2024), leading to an increase in atmospheric CO2 at a rate of 2.57 ppm yr−1 (Rasmussen 2024). From this, we can estimate that the complete conversion of these plastics to CO2 would result in an increase of 1.41 ppm atmospheric CO2. We believe this is an upper bound, because although we did not consider the 35% of plastics that are of other types, we also did not limit ourselves to waste plastics, nor did we consider carbon sequestration via biomass production. Still, these environmental costs are less than incineration, which additionally releases POPs. We therefore envision that carbon capture and biological CO2 fixation must be employed in tandem with plastic bioremediation strategies (Schulz-Mirbach et al. 2023). In the scenario of soil bioremediation, the various additives in plastics must also be accounted for. Because these can be persistent toxicants, greater transparency in the plastics industry and efforts to reduce the production and use of plastics are absolutely critical. In the context of industrial bioupcycling, downstream purification processes must be able to remove residual MP/NPs and cell debris, especially if the derived products are to be used in food or pharmaceutical applications. The safety of such plastic-derived products must also be assessed. Additionally, the regulatory implications of releasing transgenic microbes into the environment must be considered (Chemla et al. 2024). A promising outlook is the bacterial production of PHB and PHV from other plastics (Sullivan et al. 2022) or CO2, leading to a circular plastic economy.

Taken together, these studies indicate that even for recently invented, highly synthetic materials, nature is poised to harness their free energy for its own goals. Though inspiring, it is possible that evolution in response to ever-increasing concentrations of plastic waste accounts for the trend towards more efficient plastic-degrading microbes being discovered in recent years. For plastic and other types of persistent pollutants (e.g. PFAS), let us hope that the biological ingenuity that can be brought to bear on the biodegradation challenge from the research community (via continuing advances in protein and cell modeling, engineering, and evolution) can obviate the need for an increasingly polluted environment to find a solution for us.
