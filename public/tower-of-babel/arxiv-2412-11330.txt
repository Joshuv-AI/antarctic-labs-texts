Exact Verification of First-Order Methods via Mixed-Integer Linear Programming
Vinit Ranjan, Jisun Park, Stefano Gualandi, Andrea Lodi, Bartolomeo Stellato

Abstract
We present exact mixed-integer linear programming formulations for verifying the performance of first-order methods for parametric quadratic optimization. We formulate the verification problem as a mixed-integer linear program where the objective is to maximize the infinity norm of the fixed-point residual after a given number of iterations. Our approach captures a wide range of gradient, projection, proximal iterations through affine or piecewise affine constraints. We derive tight polyhedral convex hull formulations of the constraints representing the algorithm iterations. To improve the scalability, we develop a custom bound tightening technique combining interval propagation, operator theory, and optimization-based bound tightening. Numerical examples, including linear and quadratic programs from network optimization, sparse coding using Lasso, and optimal control, show that our method provides several orders of magnitude reductions in the worst-case fixed-point residuals, closely matching the true worst-case performance.

In many such applications, solving the optimization problem is part of a wider system, imposing strict computational requirements on the algorithm used.

First-order optimization algorithms have attracted an increasing interest over the last decade due to their low per-iteration cost and memory requirements, and their warm-start capabilities.
These properties are particularly useful, on the one hand, in large-scale optimization problems arising in data-science and machine learning, and, on the other hand, in embedded optimization problems arising in engineering and optimal control.
First-order methods date back to the 1950s and were first applied to QPs for the development of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm

In this paper, we introduce an exact and scalable Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulation of the problem of analyzing the performance of first-order methods in LP and QP.

1.1
The problem

We are interested in solving QPs of the form

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&(1/2)z^{T}Pz+q(x)^{T}z\\
\text{subject to}&Az+r=b(x)\\
&r\in C_{1}\times C_{2}\times\dots\times C_{L},\end{array}

(1)

with variables
z\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n}
and

, and instance parameters

The sets

{\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}^{n_{i}}

We denote the objective as

The constraints are defined by matrix

If

1

We focus on settings where we solve several instances of problem (
1

Problems where

[

]
(

[

[

Our goal is to solve problem (
1

of the form

s^{k+1}=T(s^{k},x),\quad k=0,1,\dots,

(2)

where

that maps the (primal-dual) iterate

For prescribed iteration-dependent schedules, we also allow

below as

For any convergent first-order method and instance

can be constructed such that the fixed-points of

correspond to the optimal solution of the given instance.
We assume the operator to be
L

and, therefore, single-valued
[
70
, Sec. 2.1]
.
Operators of this form encode a wide range of algorithms, including gradient descent, proximal gradient descent, and operator splitting algorithms
[
70
]
. We denote the initial iterate set as the polyhedron
S\subset{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}
.
For cold-started algorithms, we have
{S=\{0\}}
.
For a given

correspond to the (primal-dual) optimal solutions of problem (
1
) (

).
We define the

As

, averaged or contractive

[

Furthermore, it is often the case that the fixed-point residual with proper scaling can model the optimality conditions of (

In Section

Therefore, the fixed-point residual is an easily computable measure of suboptimality and infeasibility.

However, for any convergent first-order method, the residual will still converge to 0 as

We are interested in numerically verifying that the worst-case magnitude of the fixed-point residual after

at most

Specifically, we would like to verify the following condition:

\|H(T^{K}(s^{0},x)-T^{K-1}(s^{0},x))\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon,\quad\forall x\in X,\;s^{0}\in S,

(3)

Note that the

Verifying condition (

\delta_{K}=\begin{array}[t]{ll}\text{maximize}&\|H(s^{K}-s^{K-1})\|_{\infty}\\
\text{subject to}&s^{k+1}=T(s^{k},x),\quad k=0,1\dots,K-1,\\
&s^{0}\in S,\quad x\in X,\end{array}

(
{\rm VP}
)

In the rest of the paper, we will describe scalable ways to formulate and solve this problem as a MILP.
We refer to (

1.2
Related works

There has been much interest in both analyzing theoretical convergence properties of first-order methods and verifying their practical properties in different applications.

Performance estimation

The optimization community has recently devoted significant attention on computer-assisted tools to analyze the worst-case finite step performance of first-order optimization algorithms.
Notable examples include the performance estimation problem (PEP)
[
34
,
76
,
77
]
approach that formulates the worst-case analysis as a semidefinite program (SDP), and the integral quadratic constraints (IQCs) framework that models optimization algorithms as dynamical systems, analyzing their performance using control theory
[
55
]
.
Interestingly, solutions to IQCs can be viewed as feasible solutions of a specific PEP formulation searching for optimal linear convergence rate using Lyapunov functions
[
79
]
.
By defining the appropriate constraints (
i.e.
, interpolation conditions), the PEP framework can model complex methods, including operator splitting
[
28
,
69
,
87
]
, linear operators
[
16
]
, and stochastic optimization algorithms
[
78
]
.
Thanks to user-friendly toolboxes such as PEPit
[
40
]
, PEP has promoted numerous discoveries, including the design of optimal algorithms
[
33
]
, by constructing custom inequality constraints
[
64
]
or by formulating the design problem as bilevel optimization where PEP serves as the subproblem
[
32
]
.
We provide a detailed discussion of our work and how it differs from the PEP framework in Section
1.3
.

Verification for real-time optimization

In many embedded optimization problems from engineering or optimal control, it is important to guarantee an approximately accurate solution in real-time, which corresponds to a tight iteration limit
[
51
,
61
,
74
]
.
This motivates our verification problem as the convergence behavior in the short-term (
i.e.
for a small, fixed number of iterations) can behave very differently from the long-term convergence rate
[
68
, Section VII]
[
75
]
.
Due to their widespread applicability, many works have focused on convergence verification and complexity estimates for convex QPs
[
4
,
5
,
66
]
.
Critically, these methods use the parametric QP structure by looking at box constraints
[
26
]
or the active set of constraints at optimality
[

]
.
In optimal control, model predictive control problems are of particular interest as the individual parametric QPs are often not large problems, but it is imperative that they can be approximately solved in a small amount of time

Model predictive control is particularly important among the examples we consider in this paper, as it satisfies all properties we desire: it gives a small to medium family of parametric convex QPs for which we aim to verify the performance of first-order methods within a small number of iterations.

Neural network verification

Our approach closely connects with the neural network verification literature. The main goal in this area is to verify that, for every allowable input (
e.g.
, all permissible pixel perturbations of training images), the output of a trained neural network satisfies certain properties (
e.g.
, correctly classifies the images). This ensures that there are
no adversarial examples
that are misclassified.
Similarly, in this paper, we verify that at a given iteration, for every allowable problem instance, the norm of the residual remains below a precision threshold
\epsilon
, guaranteeing that there are
no adversarial problem instances
that are not solved to the desired precision.
The neural network verification problem is inherently challenging and nonconvex, and various approaches frame it as a MILP
[
21
,
35
,
82
]
by encoding ReLU activation functions with discrete variables. Lifted representations of these activation functions can yield tighter problem relaxations and have been shown to reduce solution times
[
1
,
81
,
83
]
.
However, successfully verifying large, general neural network architectures requires specialized branch-and-bound algorithms
[

,
73
]
featuring specialized bound tightening
[
48
]
, GPU-based bound propagation
[
86
]
and custom cutting planes
[
88
]
. Despite recent progress, the complex structure of trained neural networks still makes it challenging to verify architectures with tens of layers.
While also relying on MILP formulations, our work exploits the specific convergence behavior of first-order methods to verify algorithm performance over tens of iterations.
In addition, we model nonconvex iteration functions (
e.g.
, soft thresholding).

1.3
Relation to Performance Estimation Problem (PEP)

It is important to highlight some key differences between our proposed framework and the existing PEP framework.
They are complementary tools since, at their core, both frameworks aim to answer a similar question:
what is the worst-case performance of a given first-order method on some family of optimization problems?

In PEP, the answer is provided as a solution to the following optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{maximize}&\mathcal{P}(f,\{s^{k}\})&\text{(Performance metric)}\\
\text{subject to}&f\in\mathcal{F}&\text{(Optimization problem)}\\
&\{s^{k}\}=\mathcal{A}(s^{0},f),\quad s^{0}\in S&\text{(First-order method)}\end{array}

(4)

where
\mathcal{P}
is a performance metric,
\mathcal{F}
is a family of optimization problem represented by a function or operator
f
,
S
is a set where the initial iterate
s^{0}
resides, and
\mathcal{A}
is a given first-order method. The solution
(f^{\star},s^{0\star})
of (
4
) encodes the problem instance that achieves the worst-case performance.
The key difference between the PEP framework and this paper comes from the encodings of
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
s^{0}\in S
.

The PEP framework represents
f\in\mathcal{F}
by imposing interpolation conditions on algorithm iterates
\{s^{k}\}\subseteq{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}
.
Since such conditions are quadratic, the above problem becomes a non-convex Quadratically-constrained Quadratic Program (QCQP), and we denote this problem as (QCQP-PEP).
Notably, if the formulation depends only on the inner products of
\{s^{k}\}
, then we can exactly reformulate QCQP-PEP as a tractable convex SDP (SDP-PEP)
[
34
, Section 3.2]
,
[
77
, Theorem 5]
.
The SDP-PEP enjoys favorable properties such as dimension independence and provides direct convergence proofs by analyzing its dual
[
34
, Section 3.2]
,
[
77
, Section 3.4]
.
However, applying the SDP-PEP formulation to (
1
) can result in a pessimistic,
i.e.
, non-tight, worst-case result.
First of all, the problem class
\mathcal{F}
of (
1
) cannot be represented in terms of the interpolation conditions of SDP-PEP.
This is due to the fact that the worst-case instance of SDP-PEP is invariant under orthogonal transformation, whereas it is not in our setup as observed in prior work
[
67
, Section 3.1]
.
Also, SDP-PEP only allows for
S
that can be represented by inner product values of iterates such as
S=\{s^{0}\mid\|s^{0}-s^{\star}\|_{2}\leq R\}
.
Therefore, initialization schemes, such as warm-starting techniques, where
S
depends on constant vectors in
{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}
, cannot be encoded by SDP-PEP.
In the same context, it is not possible in SDP-PEP to represent the performance metric of termination criteria using the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm because this norm cannot be represented in terms of the inner products of the iterates
\{s^{k}\}
.
In contrast to the SDP-PEP formulation, our proposed verification problem (VP) can successfully mitigate the aforementioned pessimism by explicitly representing parametric QPs as
f
, as well as
\ell_{\infty}
-norms and warm-starts using MILP.

We would like to clarify that, even though the SDP-PEP limitations mentioned earlier do not apply to QCQP-PEP, solving the latter can be numerically challenging, as non-convex QCQPs are NP-hard
[
85
]
.
Furthermore, algorithms for non-convex QCQPs, such as spatial branch-and-bound, are underdeveloped compared to algorithms and technology for MILP solvers
[
44
]
.
We do not present this work as an intended replacement for PEP; instead, we answer the same question with an approach that is neither an extension nor a special case to the prior PEP-based framework.

1.4
Our contributions

In this paper, we introduce a new MILP framework to exactly verify the performance of first-order optimization algorithms for QPs.

•

We formulate the performance verification problem as a MILP where the objective is to maximize the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the convergence residual after a given number of iterations.
We model common gradient, projection, and proximal steps as either affine or piecewise affine constraints in the verification problem.

•

We construct convex hull formulations of piecewise affine steps of the form of the soft-thresholding operator, the rectified linear unit, and the saturated linear unit, and show that the corresponding separation problems can be solved in polynomial time.

•

We introduce various techniques to enhance the scalability of the MILP. Specifically, we develop a custom bound tightening technique combining interval propagation, convergence properties from operator theory, and optimization-based bound tightening. Furthermore, we devise a sequential technique to solve the verification problem by iteratively increasing the number of steps until we reach the desired number of iterations.

•

We compare our method against state-of-the-art computer assisted performance estimation techniques, on LP instances in network optimization and QP instances in sparse coding using Lasso as well as optimal control, obtaining multiple orders of magnitude reduction on worst-case norm of the scaled fixed-point residual.

Mixed-Integer Linear Programming formulation

The remainder of this paper focuses on modeling (

VP

) using MILP techniques along with our many problem-specific improvements to speed up the solving procedure.

For this construction, we assume finite valid component bounds on all intermediate variables over the prescribed horizon.

For a general overview of MILP, we refer to
[
29
]
.
Since algorithms often involve multiple steps for each iteration, we use the following notation to represent each iteration
k
:

s^{k+1}=T(s^{k},x)\quad\iff\quad\begin{array}[]{ll}v^{1}&=\phi_{1}(s^{k},x)\\
v^{2}&=\phi_{2}(v^{1},x)\\
&\;\vdots\\
s^{k+1}&=\phi_{l}(v^{l-1},x),\\
\end{array}

(5)

where
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{l}
are the intermediate step mappings.
In the intermediate steps, proximal algorithms rely on evaluating the proximal operator of a function
g
, defined as
{\bf prox}_{g}(v)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y}\;(g(y)+(1/2)\|y-v\|_{2}^{2})

[
9
,
63
]
.
Most intermediate steps arising in LP and QP can be computed as either
affine
or
piecewise affine
steps
[
63
, Sec. 6]
, which we express as MILP constraints.

2.1
Objective formulation

To model the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm in the objective of problem (

VP

), we define
\delta_{K}=\|H(s^{K}-s^{K-1})\|_{\infty}=\|t\|_{\infty}
, with
t=H(s^{K}-s^{K-1})\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{h}
being the scaled residual at the last step.
Using the lower bounds
\text{\@text@baccent{$s$}}^{K-1},\text{\@text@baccent{$s$}}^{K}
and the upper bounds,
\bar{s}^{K-1}
and
\bar{s}^{K}
, of the last two iterates
s^{K-1}
and
s^{K}
, we can derive the lower and upper bounds on the residual
t
, denoted
$̱t$
and
\bar{t}
, respectively
[
42
, Sec. 3]
.
We provide the explicit bound formulas in Section
3.1
.

Let
M_{t}=\max_{1\leq i\leq h}\max\{\bar{t}_{i},-\text{\@text@baccent{$t$}}_{i}\}
, where
h\geq 1
.

Then, by writing
t
as the difference of two nonnegative variables
t^{+}
and
t^{-}
,
i.e.
,
t=t^{+}-t^{-}
, we can represent the absolute value of its components as
t^{+}+t^{-}
and write
\delta_{K}
as the following constraints:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&t=t^{+}-t^{-},\quad t^{+}\leq\bar{t}\odot\omega,\quad t^{-}\leq-\text{\@text@baccent{$t$}}\odot(\mathbf{1}-\omega)\\
&t^{+}+t^{-}\leq\delta_{K}\mathbf{1}\leq t^{+}+t^{-}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{t}(\mathbf{1}-\gamma)}\\
&\mathbf{1}^{T}\gamma=1,\quad t^{+}\geq 0,\quad t^{-}\geq 0,\end{array}

(6)

where
\odot
is the elementwise product.
Here, we introduced variable
\omega\in\{0,1\}^{h}
to represent the absolute values of the components of
t
, and variable
\gamma\in\{0,1\}^{h}
to represent the maximum inside the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm.
We remark that the lower bound constraints
t^{+}+t^{-}\leq\delta_{K}\mathbf{1}
provide a complete description of
\delta_{K}
, but they are not strictly necessary due to the maximization.

2.2
Affine steps

Consider affine steps of the following form:

\phi(v,x)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid Mw=By,\quad y=(v,x)\},

(7)

where
M\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d\times d}
is an invertible matrix and
B\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d\times(d+p)}
.
We directly express these iterations as linear equality constraints for the verification problem (

VP

).

2.2.1
Explicit steps

When
M=I
, affine steps represent explicit iterations.

Gradient step

A gradient step of size
\eta>0
for a quadratic function
f(v,x)=(1/2)v^{T}Pv+x^{T}v
can be written as

\phi(v,x)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=v-\eta\nabla f(v,x)=(I-\eta P)v-\eta x\},

(8)

where
\nabla f(v,x)=Pv+x
is the gradient with respect to the first argument of
f
.
Therefore, it is an affine step with
M=I
and
B=[I\!-\!\eta P\;-\!\eta I]
.

Momentum step

For a momentum step
[
59
]
[
67
, Sec. 2.2]
, an operator is applied to a linear combination of iterates at both iteration
k
and iteration
k-1
.
Let
g
denote the intermediate step variables that momentum is applied to, and
v=(g^{k},g^{k-1})
is the concatenation of intermediate step variables from iteration
k
and
k-1
.
Given
\beta^{k}>0
, momentum updates are defined by:

\displaystyle\tilde{v}

\displaystyle=g^{k}+\beta^{k}(g^{k}-g^{k-1})

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}-\eta\nabla f(\tilde{v},x),

and so can be written in our framework via substitution as:

\phi(v,x)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=(1+\beta^{k})(I\!-\!\eta P)g^{k}-\!\beta^{k}(I\!-\!\eta P)g^{k-1}-\eta x\}.

Therefore, it is an affine step with
M=I
and
B=[(1+\beta^{k})(I\!-\!\eta P)\;-\!\beta^{k}(I\!-\!\eta P)\;-\!\eta I]
.

Shrinkage operator

The shrinkage operator represents the proximal operator of the squared
\ell_{2}
-norm function
f(v)=(1/2)\|v\|^{2}

[
63
, Sec. 6.1.1]
.
Given
\lambda>0
, we have

\phi(v,x)={\bf prox}_{\lambda f}(v)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=(1+\lambda)^{-1}v\},

which is an affine step with
M=I
and
B=[(1\!+\!\lambda)^{-1}\!I\;0]
.

Fixed-schedule, average-based restarts

Similar to the momentum steps, fixed-schedule restarts consider a history of
h
previous iterates, where
h
is a predefined lookback window
[
2
,
3
]
.
This scheme averages the previous
h
iterates so that
w=(1/h)\sum_{j=k-h+1}^{k}g^{j}
.
So, the restart can be written as

\phi(v,x)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=(1/h)(\mathbf{1}^{T}\otimes I)v\},

where
v=(g^{k},g^{k-1},\dots g^{k-h+1})
.
The operator
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product and so
\mathbf{1}^{T}\otimes I
is the horizontal stack of
h
copies of the identity matrix.
This is an affine step with
M=I
, and
B=(1/h)(\mathbf{1}^{T}\otimes I)
.

Halpern anchor

Halpern mechanisms are also known as
anchoring
based methods
[
45
,
65
]
.
The anchoring step computes a weighted average with the
initial
iterate
s^{0}
.
So, an anchored gradient step is, given
\beta^{k}\in(0,1)
, written as

w=\beta^{k}(g^{k}-\eta\nabla f(g^{k},x))+(1-\beta^{k})s^{0}.

By substituting in the gradient step (
8
) and letting
v=(g^{k},s^{0})
, we can write

\phi(v,x)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=\beta^{k}((I-\eta P)g^{k}-\eta x)+(1-\beta^{k})s^{0}\},

which is an affine step with
M=I
and
B=[\beta^{k}(I\!-\!\eta P)\;\;(1\!-\!\beta^{k})I\;-\!\beta^{k}\eta I]
.

2.2.2
Implicit steps

When
M\neq I
, affine steps represent implicit iterate computations that require linear system solutions with left-hand side matrix
M
.

Proximal operator of a constrained quadratic function

The proximal operator with
\lambda>0
of a convex quadratic function subject to linear equality constraints defined by
A\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m\times d}
,
b\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
, is given by

{\bf prox}_{\lambda f(\cdot,x)}(v)=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\{Az=b\}}~\left\{(1/2)z^{T}Pz+x^{T}z+1/(2\lambda)\|z-v\|^{2}\right\}.

(9)

We assume that the equalities are consistent and remove redundant rows so that
A
has full row rank.

The solution of (
9

[
18

These include steps in common algorithms such as OSQP
[
75
]
and SCS
[

[
63

By introducing dual variable
\nu\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m}
, and defining

, the KKT conditions corresponding to the
prox

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\phi(v,x)=\left\{(w,\nu)\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d+m}\;\middle|\;\begin{bmatrix}P+\lambda^{-1}I&A^{T}\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}w\\
\nu\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda^{-1}I&-I&0\\
0&0&I\end{bmatrix}y\right\}},

and therefore can be written as an affine step with the corresponding matrices.

Proximal operator of an unconstrained quadratic function

The proximal operator of a convex quadratic function is a special case of (
9
) without the affine constraints and dual variables.
As such, the constraints simplify to
[
63
, Sec. 6.1.1]

\phi(v,x)={\bf prox}_{\lambda f(\cdot,x)}(v)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid(P+\lambda^{-1}I)w=\lambda^{-1}v-x\},

which corresponds to an affine step with
M=P+\lambda^{-1}I
and
B=[\lambda^{-1}I\;-\!I]
.

Projection onto an affine subspace

The orthogonal projection onto the affine subspace defined by

is also a special case of (
9
) with
P=0
,
x=0
.
We assume that
A
is a wide matrix (
m<d

By rearranging the terms, this can also be written as an explicit affine step of the form

\phi(v,x)={\bf prox}_{\lambda f(\cdot,x)}(v)=\{w\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d}\mid w=(I-A^{\dagger}A)v+A^{\dagger}b\},

where
A^{\dagger}=A^{T}(AA^{T})^{-1}
is the pseudoinverse of
A
.
Thus it also corresponds to an affine step with
M=I
and
B=[I\!-\!A^{\dagger}\!A\;A^{\dagger}]

Soft-thresholding

We consider the composition of an affine function and the

operator with the form

\!\!\!\!(\phi(v,x))_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)=\begin{cases}a^{T}y-\lambda&{a}^{T}{y}>\lambda\\

By introducing binary variables

\displaystyle a^{T}y-\lambda\leq w

\displaystyle\leq a^{T}y+\lambda

\displaystyle a^{T}y+\lambda-2\lambda(1-\zeta)\leq w

\displaystyle\leq a^{T}y-\lambda+2\lambda(1-\omega)

\displaystyle(M^{-}+\lambda)\zeta\leq w

\displaystyle\leq(M^{+}-\lambda)\omega

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda+(M^{+}+\lambda)(1-\zeta).

Theorem 2.2

(Convex hull of soft-thresholding operator)
.

Define
\ell^{0},u^{0},\ell_{I},J
as done in Theorem
2.1
.
Additionally, given an index set
I
, define the upper bounds
u_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}u^{0}_{i}+\sum_{i\notin I}a_{i}\ell^{0}_{i}
.
Note that
u_{\emptyset}
corresponds to the lower bound of
a^{T}y
over
[\text{\@text@baccent{$y$}},\bar{y}]
and by definition
u_{\emptyset}=\ell_{J}
and
u_{J}=\ell_{\emptyset}
.
Also, define the sets of indices
\mathcal{I}^{+}=\{(I,o)\in 2^{J}\times J\mid\ell_{I}\geq\lambda,\;\ell_{I\cup\{o\}}<\lambda\}
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}=\{(I,o)\in 2^{J}\times J\mid u_{I}\leq-\lambda,\;u_{I\cup\{o\}}>-\lambda\}
.

Finally, define the families of index sets
\mathcal{J}^{+}=\{I\subseteq J\mid\ell_{I}\geq\lambda\}
and
\mathcal{J}^{-}=\{I\subseteq J\mid u_{I}\leq-\lambda\}
.

Consider the region
\Phi=\{(y,w)\in[\text{\@text@baccent{$y$}},\bar{y}]\times{\mbox{\bf R}}\mid w=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)\}
. We can write its convex hull as

{\bf conv}(\Phi)=\left\{(y,w)\in[\text{\@text@baccent{$y$}},\bar{y}]\times{\mbox{\bf R}}\;\middle|\;\begin{cases}w=a^{T}y-\lambda&\ell_{J}>\lambda\\
w=a^{T}y+\lambda&u_{J}<-\lambda\\
w=0&-\lambda\leq u_{\emptyset}\leq\ell_{\emptyset}\leq\lambda\\
(y,w)\in Q&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\right\},

(16)

where

Q=\!\left\{(y,w)\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{d+p+1}\,\middle|\!\!\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}&a^{T}y-\lambda\leq w\leq a^{T}y+\lambda\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w\leq 0\;\text{ if }u_{J}\leq\lambda,\qquad w\geq 0\;\text{ if }\ell_{J}\geq-\lambda}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w\leq\frac{\ell_{I}-\lambda}{\ell_{I}+\lambda}(a^{T}y+\lambda)+\frac{2\lambda}{\ell_{I}+\lambda}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(y_{i}-\ell^{0}_{i}),\;\forall I\in\mathcal{J}^{+}}\\
&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w\geq\frac{u_{I}+\lambda}{u_{I}-\lambda}(a^{T}y-\lambda)+\frac{2\lambda}{\lambda-u_{I}}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(y_{i}-u^{0}_{i}),\;\forall I\in\mathcal{J}^{-}}\\

&w\geq\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(y_{i}-u^{0}_{i})+\frac{u_{I}+\lambda}{\ell^{0}_{o}-u^{0}_{o}}(y_{o}-u^{0}_{o}),\;\forall(I,o)\in\mathcal{I}^{-}\end{array}\right\}.

Proof.

We consider the last case in
{\bf conv}(\Phi)
, since
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)
is affine over the box in the first three.
Let
\theta=a^{T}y
and
\delta_{I}(y)=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}(y_{i}-\ell^{0}_{i})\geq 0
.
For
I\in\mathcal{J}^{+}
, let
\rho_{I}=(\ell_{I}-\lambda)/(\ell_{I}+\lambda)\in[0,1)
, so that the right-hand side of line 3 of
Q
is
R_{I}(y)=\rho_{I}(\theta+\lambda)+(1-\rho_{I})\delta_{I}(y)
.
The set
{\bf conv}(\Phi)
consists of the points
(y,w)
with
y\in[\text{\@text@baccent{$y$}},\bar{y}]
and
w
between the convex and the concave envelopes of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)
over the box
[
81
, Appendix A.1]
.
We show that the concave envelope is the minimum of the upper bounds in lines 1, 2, 3, and 5 of
Q
, and the lower bounds follow by applying the same argument to
-w=\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(-a^{T}y)
(lines 1, 2, 4, and 6 of
Q
).

To show validity of the formulation, first observe that lines 1, 2, and 5 of
Q
hold because
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\theta)\leq\theta+\lambda
and
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\theta)\leq\mathop{\bf ReLU}(\theta-\lambda)
, using Theorem
2.1
applied to
\mathop{\bf ReLU}(a^{T}y-\lambda)
.
For line 3, write
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(\theta)=\max\{\theta-\lambda,\min\{\theta+\lambda,0\}\}
.
We have
R_{I}(y)\geq\rho_{I}(\theta+\lambda)\geq\min\{\theta+\lambda,0\}
and, because
\lambda(1+\rho_{I})=(1-\rho_{I})\ell_{I}
, also
R_{I}(y)-(\theta-\lambda)=(1-\rho_{I})(\ell_{I}-\theta+\delta_{I}(y))\geq 0
, where the last inequality holds because
\theta-\delta_{I}(y)\leq\ell_{I}
over the box.

For sufficiency of the formulation, recall that the concave envelope is the infimum of the affine upper bounds
L
on
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)
over the box.
An affine function
L
satisfies
L\geq\min\{\theta+\lambda,0\}
over the box if and only if there exists a
\rho\in[0,1]
such that
L\geq\rho(\theta+\lambda)
over the box.
To justify this step, write
u(y)=\theta+\lambda
and choose
\rho=\sup(\{0\}\cup\{L(y)/u(y)\mid y\in[\text{\@text@baccent{$y$}},\bar{y}],\ u(y)<0\})
.
The assumed bound gives
0\leq\rho\leq 1
.
For points
y,z
with
u(y)<0<u(z)

has
L\geq 0
, which implies
L(y)/u(y)\leq L(z)/u(z)

Thus
\rho u\leq L
also where

, and the cases

follow directly.
The converse follows from
\min\{u,0\}\leq\rho u
.
Therefore, the concave envelope of
\mathcal{T}_{\lambda}(a^{T}y)
is the infimum over
\rho\in[0,1]
of the concave envelope of

.
We have
f_{1}=\theta+\lambda
(line 1 of
Q
) and, for
\rho<1

\sigma_{\rho}=\lambda(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)\geq\lambda
.
Applying Theorem
2.1
to
\mathop{\bf ReLU}(a^{T}y-\sigma_{\rho})
shows that the concave envelope of
f_{\rho}
is
\rho(\theta+\lambda)
if
u_{J}<\sigma_{\rho}
, it is
f_{\rho}=\theta-\lambda=R_{J}(y)
if
\ell_{J}\geq\sigma_{\rho}
, and otherwise it is the minimum of
\rho(\theta+\lambda)+(1-\rho)\delta_{I}(y)+(1-\rho)(\ell_{I}-\sigma_{\rho})(y_{o}-\ell_{o}^{0})/(u_{o}^{0}-\ell_{o}^{0})
over the pairs
(I,o)
with
\ell_{I}\geq\sigma_{\rho}>\ell_{I\cup\{o\}}
.
For fixed

(1-\rho)\sigma_{\rho}=\lambda(1+\rho)

The endpoint

\rho=0

\sigma_{\rho}=\ell_{I}

\sigma_{\rho}=\ell_{I\cup\{o\}}

\ell_{I}-\ell_{I\cup\{o\}}=a_{o}(u_{o}^{0}-\ell_{o}^{0})

Therefore, the concave envelope is at least the minimum of the upper bounds in lines 1, 2, 3, and 5 of

, and is equal to it by our validity argument.

Even though the constraints in
Q
define exponentially many inequalities, we can define an efficiently solvable separation problem: given a point
(\hat{y},\hat{w})

Proposition 2.1
.

The separation problem for
{\bf conv}(\Phi)
is solvable in
O(d+p)
​time.

Proof.

First check the box constraints, the applicable affine cases, and lines 1 and 2 of
Q
.
Separate the families
\mathcal{I}^{+}
and
\mathcal{I}^{-}
in
O(d+p)
time by the fractional-knapsack procedure of
[
81
, Prop. 2]
.
It remains to check
\mathcal{J}^{+}
and
\mathcal{J}^{-}
.
For the upper family, write
d_{i}=a_{i}(u_{i}^{0}-\ell_{i}^{0})>0
,
x_{i}=(\hat{y}_{i}-\ell_{i}^{0})/(u_{i}^{0}-\ell_{i}^{0})\in[0,1]
,
U=u_{J}
, and
\theta=a^{T}\hat{y}
.
If
U<\lambda
, this family is empty.
Otherwise set
C=U-\lambda
and
A=(U-\lambda)(\theta+\lambda)-\hat{w}(U+\lambda)
.
For
I\in\mathcal{J}^{+}
, multiplying the slack of its inequality by
\ell_{I}+\lambda>0
gives

A+\sum_{i\in I}d_{i}\bigl(2\lambda x_{i}-(\theta+\lambda-\hat{w})\bigr),\qquad\sum_{i\in I}d_{i}\leq C.

The set
I_{*}=\{i\in J\mid 2\lambda x_{i}<\theta+\lambda-\hat{w}\}
minimizes this expression without the constraint on the sum.
If
I_{*}
is admissible, its inequality therefore detects any violation in
\mathcal{J}^{+}
.
If
I_{*}
is inadmissible, interpolate any feasible
z\in[0,1]^{J}
with the indicator of
I_{*}
until
\sum_{i}d_{i}z_{i}=C
.
The cleared slack cannot increase, because the indicator of
I_{*}
minimizes it over
[0,1]^{J}
.
At equality its objective is

2\lambda\left(\sum_{i}d_{i}x_{i}z_{i}-\hat{w}\right).

The already checked ReLU upper bounds give
\hat{w}\leq\min\{\sum_{i}d_{i}x_{i}z_{i}\mid 0\leq z\leq 1,\ \sum_{i}d_{i}z_{i}=C\}
by the same fractional-knapsack characterization.
Consequently, no inequality in
\mathcal{J}^{+}
is violated in this case.
Reflection
(a,\hat{w})\mapsto(-a,-\hat{w})
handles
\mathcal{J}^{-}
.
Each additional check takes one pass through the coordinates, so the total time is
O(d+p)
.
If a check fails, it supplies a violated inequality.
∎

Saturated linear unit

Given two scalars
b,c\in{\mbox{\bf R}}

b\leq c

\!\!\!\!(\phi(v,x))_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{[b,c]}(a^{T}y)=\begin{cases}b&{a}^{T}{y}<b\\
a^{T}y&b\leq a^{T}y\leq c\\
c&{a}^{T}{y}>c\end{cases}\quad\text{with}\quad y=(v,x).

(18)

If
b=c
, the operator is constant and its convex hull is simply
w=b
over the box.

For the big-
M

arising from the three cases, and assume that the constants from (

M^{-}<b
and
M^{+}>c

Otherwise, replace wholly saturated cases by

We can write (

\displaystyle b\leq w

\displaystyle\leq c

\displaystyle c-(c-b)(1-\omega)\leq w

\displaystyle\leq b+(c-b)(1-\zeta)

3
Scalability improvements

In this section, we introduce scalability improvements that allow us to solve (

VP

) for larger values of
K
.
Modern MILP solvers implement a branch-and-bound (B&B) algorithm
[
14
,
44
,
50
]
with cutting planes,
i.e.
, branch-and-cut.
The B&B algorithm systematically partitions the feasible region into sub-regions and obtains bounds via the solution of the associated LP relaxations.
By tracking the best known primal and dual bounds, the B&B algorithm can prune out suboptimal regions of the feasible space.
The practical efficiency of B&B solvers is heavily reliant on the tightness of the LP relaxations.
These can be strengthened by tightening bounds on the continuous variables, such as the big-
M
constraints introduced in Section
2.3
, or by adding valid linear inequalities,
i.e.
, cutting planes.

We now introduce various improvements that include a combination of bound tightening techniques using standard interval propagation, optimization-based bound tightening, and operator-theory bounds specific to our setting.
Furthermore, we make use of the convex hull inequalities from Section
2.3
to tighten the relaxations at the root node of the B&B procedure.
Finally, we describe how we solve (

VP

)
sequentially
from iteration
1
to
K
, by iteratively exploiting the solutions at previous iterations.

3.1
Bound tightening

Interval propagation

Consider an affine step of the form (
7

Using the factorization of

Given lower and upper bounds

For the piecewise affine steps considered above, first bound the affine input

Chaining bounds for all iterations (

\text{\@text@baccent{$s$}}_{\rm ip}^{K}\leq s^{K}\leq\bar{s}_{\rm ip}^{K}

Operator theory based bounds

For a convergent operator

, the fixed-point residual is bounded by a decreasing sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
,
i.e.

\|s^{k}-s^{k-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{k}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\|s^{k}-s^{k-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{k}
and
\text{\@text@baccent{$s$}}^{k-1}\leq s^{k-1}\leq\bar{s}^{k-1}
. Then,
\text{\@text@baccent{$s$}}^{k-1}-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}\leq s^{k}\leq\bar{s}^{k-1}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{1}
.

Proof.

Since
\|s^{k}-s^{k-1}\|_{\infty}\leq\alpha_{k}
, we have
|s_{i}^{k}-s_{i}^{k-1}|\leq\alpha_{k}
.
The absolute value implies
s_{i}^{k}-s_{i}^{k-1}\leq\alpha_{k}

Replacing

by its upper and lower bounds, respectively, completes the proof.
∎

In theoretical analysis of first-order methods, the bounding sequence

depends on the worst-case initial distance to optimality that we denote by

\begin{array}[]{lll}R^{2}=&\text{maximize}&\|s^{0}-s^{\star}\|^{2}_{2}\\

where

We use operator theory bounds only when the initial radius has a finite certified upper bound and the rate constants hold uniformly over the parameter and initial sets.

Consider a

Consider now an averaged operator

for any

In this case, the residual is bounded by

]

Using the fact that any vector

3.2
Warm-starting between consecutive iterations

In order for the B&B algorithm to efficiently prune out suboptimal regions of the feasible space, it needs to exploit quality primal heuristics, which are schemes that aim to quickly provide a feasible solution
[
11
]
[
29
, Chapter 9.2]
[
72
, Section 3.1]
.
In our setting, after solving the MILP at iteration
K
, we can extract the worst-case instance sequence of iterates
\{s^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
and parameter
x
that achieves the objective value.
We find a quality heuristic solution for the next iterate
K+1
by applying one extra step of operator
T(\cdot,x)
and inferring the binary variable values from it.
In practice, we found this procedure allowed the MILP to quickly find the optimal lower bound in the B&B procedure.

3.3
Cutting planes at the root node

The theorems from Section
2.3
provide exponentially many inequalities to exactly describe the convex hulls.
However, Proposition
2.1
gives an efficient procedure by which we separate from the hulls, giving a cutting plane.
We tighten the root node continuous relaxation with these cutting planes by running the separation procedure for each output component of the piecewise affine steps.
In our implementation, we add only the most violated cutting plane, if any exists, to avoid overly increasing the size of the LP relaxations, which may slow down the B&B execution
[
72
]
.

4.2
Network optimization

We consider a minimum cost network flow problem
[
12
]
with varying node demands.
Given a set of
n_{s}
supply nodes and
n_{d}
demand nodes, consider a bipartite graph on the node groups with capacitated directed edges from supply to demand nodes.
Let
A_{s},A_{d}
be submatrices of rows corresponding to supply nodes and demand nodes, respectively.
For each edge
i
, let
c_{i}
be the flow capacity,
\mu_{i}
be the cost of sending a unit of flow, and
f_{i}
be the variable representing the amount of flow sent.
Lastly, let
b_{s}\geq 0
be the vector of supply amounts available at each supply node and
x\leq 0
be the amount of demand requested at each demand node.
The minimum cost network flow problem becomes

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&\mu^{T}f\\
\text{subject to}&A_{s}f\leq b_{s},\quad A_{d}f=x,\quad 0\leq f\leq c,\\
\end{array}

(27)

i.e.
problem (
1
) with

P=0\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{e}\times n_{e}}
,

A=[-\!I\;I\;A^{T}_{s}\;A^{T}_{d}]^{T}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{(n_{s}+n_{d}+2n_{e})\times n_{e}}
,

q(x)=\mu\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{e}}
,
b(x)=(0,c,b_{s},x)\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{s}+n_{d}+2n_{e}}
, and
C={\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}^{n_{s}+2n_{e}}\times\{0\}^{n_{d}}
.

Algorithms

We solve the problem using the primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) algorithm
[
22
]
that operates on a primal and dual iterate
s^{k}=(z^{k},v^{k},w^{k})
.
Note that
(v^{k},w^{k})
are the separate components of the dual vector that correspond to the primal inequality and equality constraints, respectively.
We consider both the standard PDHG
[
22
]
with fixed step size
\eta>0
adapted to LPs
[
2
, Equation (3)]
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}z^{k+1}&=\mathcal{S}_{[0,c]}(z^{k}-\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k}))\\
v^{k+1}&=\max\{v^{k}+\eta(-b_{s}+A_{s}(2z^{k+1}-z^{k})),0\}\\
w^{k+1}&=w^{k}+\eta(x-A_{d}(2z^{k+1}-z^{k})).\end{array}

(28)

We also consider a version with momentum in the primal variable:

\begin{array}[]{ll}z^{k+1}&=\mathcal{S}_{[0,c]}(z^{k}-\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k}))\\
\tilde{z}^{k+1}&=z^{k+1}+\xi_{k}(z^{k+1}-z^{k})\\
v^{k+1}&=\max\{v^{k}+\eta(-b_{s}+A_{s}(2\tilde{z}^{k+1}-z^{k})),0\}\\
w^{k+1}&=w^{k}+\eta(x-A_{d}(2\tilde{z}^{k+1}-z^{k})),\end{array}

(29)

where we determine the momentum coefficients with Nesterov’s update rule
\xi_{k}=k/(k+3)

[
59
]
.
For both algorithms, we choose the step size as
\eta=0.5/\left\|[-\!A^{T}_{s}\;A^{T}_{d}]\right\|_{2}
.

Performance metric

The optimality condition of problem (
27
) is given by

0\in\begin{bmatrix}\mu+A_{s}^{T}v-A_{d}^{T}w+{\mathop{N}}_{[0,c]}(z)\\
-(A_{s}z-b_{s})+{\mathop{N}}_{\geq 0}(v)\\
A_{d}z-x\end{bmatrix},

where
{\mathop{N}}_{[0,c]}
and
{\mathop{N}}_{\geq 0}
refer to the normal cone of sets
[0,c]
and nonnegative orthant
{\mbox{\bf R}}_{+}^{m_{1}}
, respectively.
The right-hand side of the optimality condition at
s=s^{K}
for
s^{k}=(z^{k},v^{k},w^{k})
of momentum PDHG (
29
)
contains

H(s^{K}-s^{K-1})
with

H=-\begin{bmatrix}\eta^{-1}I&-A_{s}^{T}&A_{d}^{T}\\
-(1+2\xi_{K-1})A_{s}&\eta^{-1}I&0\\
(1+2\xi_{K-1})A_{d}&0&\eta^{-1}I\end{bmatrix}.

We recover a similar result for standard PDHG (
28
) when
\xi_{k}\equiv 0
.

Problem setup

We generate a random bipartite graph on 15 supply nodes and 10 demand nodes by adding each edge with probability 0.5.
After generating the random graph and forming the LP, we obtain
A\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m\times n}
with
n_{s}=15,n_{d}=10,n_{e}=63
.
For this experiment, we keep a fixed supply
b_{s}=10
for each node and a fixed capacity
c=5
for each edge, and we sample the costs i.i.d. from a uniform distribution
\mu_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}[5,10]
.
We parameterize only the demand as a unit hypercube
X=[-7,-6]^{n_{d}}
.
We choose
s^{0}
to be the primal-dual solution to the problem instance where all demand nodes have maximum demand.
If this instance has a solution, then all instances in the family are feasible.
For the operator theory bounds, we apply
[
56
, Theorem 1]
, which says that the fixed-point residual for both algorithms converges at rate
O(K^{-1/2})
.

4.3
Optimal control

We consider the problem of controlling a constrained time-invariant linear dynamical system with
n_{s}
states and
n_{u}
inputs, over a finite time horizon indexed as
t=1,\dots,T

[
15
, Chapter 11.3]
.
Given dynamics matrices
A^{\text{dyn}}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{s}\times n_{s}}
and
B^{\text{dyn}}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{s}\times n_{u}}
, along with positive semidefinite state cost
matrices
Q,Q_{T}\in{\mbox{\bf S}}_{+}^{n_{s}\times n_{s}}
, positive definite input cost matrix
R\in{\mbox{\bf S}}_{++}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
, we aim to solve

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&s_{T}^{T}Q_{T}s_{T}+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}s_{t}^{T}Qs_{t}+u_{t}^{T}Ru_{t}\\
\text{subject to}&s_{t+1}=A^{\text{dyn}}s_{t}+B^{\text{dyn}}u_{t},\quad t=1,\dots,T-1\\
&s_{\text{min}}\leq s_{t}\leq s_{\text{max}},\quad t=1,\dots,T\\
&u_{\text{min}}\leq u_{t}\leq u_{\text{max}},\quad t=1,\dots,T-1\\
&s_{1}=s^{\rm init},\end{array}\!\!\!\!\!

(30)

where
s^{\rm init}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{n_{s}}
is the initial state.
By defining a new variable
z=(u_{1},\dots u_{T-1})\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{(T-1)n_{u}}
, problem (
30
) can be written as function of inputs only
[
15
, Section 11.3]
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\text{minimize}&(1/2)z^{T}Pz+q(x)^{T}z\\
\text{subject to}&l(x)\leq Mz\leq u(x),\end{array}

(31)

where the parameter
x=s^{\rm init}
,
m_{c}=Tn_{s}+(T-1)n_{u}
, and
l(x),\;u(x)\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m_{c}}}
,
P\in{\mbox{\bf S}}_{++}^{(T-1)n_{u}\times(T-1)n_{u}}
, and
M\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mbox{\bf R}}^{m_{c}\times(T-1)n_{u}}}
.
Problem (
31
) can be written in the form (
1
), with
A=[-M^{T}\;M^{T}]^{T}\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2m_{c}\times(T-1)n_{u}}
,
q(x)\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{(T-1)n_{u}}
,
b(x)=(-l(x),u(x))\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{2m_{c}}
, and
C={\mbox{\bf R}}^{2m_{c}}_{+}
.

Algorithms

We solve problem (
31
) using the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) from the OSQP solver
[
6
,
75
]
given
\rho,\sigma>0
and primal-dual iterate
(z^{0},v^{0})
:

\begin{array}[]{l}w^{k+1}=\mathcal{S}_{[l(x),u(x)]}(v^{k})\\
\text{Solve }(P+\sigma I+\rho M^{T}M)z^{k+1}=\sigma z^{k}-q(x)+\rho M^{T}(2w^{k+1}-v^{k})\\
v^{k+1}=v^{k}+Mz^{k+1}-w^{k+1}.\end{array}

(32)

Performance metric

By defining
y^{k}=\rho(v^{k-1}-w^{k})
and by construction of algorithm (
32
), the pair
(w^{k},y^{k})
can be shown to satisfy the relevant normal cone conditions of (
30
) at every iteration
[
6
, Section 4.1]
.
So, the only optimality conditions of problem (
31
) that we need to measure are primal and dual feasibility:

0=\begin{bmatrix}Pz+M^{T}y+q(x)\\
Mz-w\end{bmatrix}.

The right-hand side at
s=s^{K}
for
s^{k}=(z^{k},v^{k})
is equivalent to
H(s^{K}-s^{K-1})
with

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}H=\begin{bmatrix}-\sigma I&-\rho M^{T}\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}}.

Problem setup

We consider the dynamics of a linearized quadcopter from
[
54
]
and a time horizon
T=5
.
The input and state cost matrices are
Q=Q_{T}=\mathop{\bf diag}(0,0,10,10,10,10,0,0,0,5,5,5)
, and
R=0.1I
.
We consider a setting with only input constraints
u_{\rm min}=(-0.99)\mathbf{1}
and
u_{\rm max}=(2.41)\mathbf{1}
(
i.e.
,
s_{\rm min}=-\infty\mathbf{1}
and
s_{\rm max}=+\infty\mathbf{1}
).
We allow the initial state
x=s_{\rm init}
to live in

X=\{x\in{\mbox{\bf R}}^{12}\mid-\pi/6\leq x_{1},x_{2}\leq\pi/6,\;0\leq x_{3}\leq 1,x_{i}=0,\;i=4,\dots,12\}
,
i.e.

and

We compare three versions of algorithm (
32
) with
\rho=0.1,1,10
and fixed
\sigma=10^{-4}
, and we use operator theory bounds from
[
38

This bound says that the convergence rate of the fixed-point residual decreases at rate
O(\tau^{K})

5
Conclusion

We presented a new MILP framework to exactly verify the performance of first-order optimization algorithms for LPs and QPs.
We formulated the verification problem as an MILP by maximizing the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm of the convergence residual after a fixed number of iterations over a given parametric family of problems and set of initial iterates.
By constructing convex hull formulations, we represented a variety of piecewise affine steps in addition to directly embedding affine steps into the MILP.
We also incorporated many techniques to enhance the scalability of the MILP, specifically through a custom bound tightening procedure combining interval propagation, operator theory, and optimization-based bound tightening.
Using different examples, we benchmark our method against state-of-the-art computer-assisted performance estimation techniques.
While our analysis was restricted to QPs in this work, we believe it motivates similar studies in parametric convex settings.

Lemma B.1

(Proposition 16.44 of

]
)

Suppose

Then,
x-p=(I-{\bf prox}_{g})(x)\in\partial g(p)
for any

and

B.1
Sparse coding

For notational simplicity, let
g(u)=\lambda\|u\|_{1}
.
Then, the FISTA update (

) with Lemma
B.1
gives

(I-\eta D^{T}D)(w^{k}-u^{k+1})\in\eta\partial g(u^{k+1})+\eta D^{T}(Du^{k+1}-x).

Combining this with
u^{k}-u^{k+1}=(u^{k}-w^{k})+(w^{k}-u^{k+1})
, we get

\displaystyle(I-\eta D^{T}D)(u^{k}-u^{k+1})-\frac{\beta_{k-1}-1}{\beta_{k}}(I-\eta D^{T}D)(u^{k-1}-u^{k})

\displaystyle\in\eta\partial g(u^{k+1})+\eta D^{T}(Du^{k+1}-x).

The result for ISTA also directly follows from taking

B.2
Network optimization

From the momentum PDHG update (

),

\displaystyle z^{k}-z^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(I-\Pi_{[0,c]})\big(z^{k}-\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k})\big)+\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k})

\displaystyle\in{\mathop{N}}_{[0,c]}(z^{k+1})+\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k})

\displaystyle={\mathop{N}}_{[0,c]}(z^{k+1})+\eta(\mu+A_{s}^{T}v^{k+1}-A_{d}^{T}w^{k+1})+\eta A_{s}^{T}(v^{k}-v^{k+1})-\eta A_{d}^{T}(w^{k}-w^{k+1}),

and the third inclusion holds from Lemma

.
Similarly, we get

\displaystyle v^{k}-v^{k+1}

\displaystyle w^{k+1}=\Pi_{[l(x),u(x)]}(v^{k})
