Closed-Form Statistical Relations Between Projected Separation, Semimajor Axis, Companion Mass, and Host Acceleration
Timothy D. Brandt 1

Abstract
I derive the statistical relationship between a radial velocity or astrometric acceleration (a trend), a companion’s mass, and the projected separation of the companion. These relationships, expressed as probability density functions, are analytic and independent of all Keplerian orbital elements so long as orbits are randomly oriented in space. I also derive a closed-form expression for the probability distribution of the ratio of the projected separation to the semimajor axis at fixed eccentricity. This expression can be numerically integrated over eccentricity for an arbitrary distribution of eccentricities. I verify my results with empirical comparisons to equivalent but more complex expressions in the literature based on the equations of Keplerian orbits. The closed-formed expressions derived here would be especially useful for any calculation that requires derivatives, e.g., Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. I also provide a Jupyter notebook including all figures and calculations at https://github.com/t-brandt/analytic-orbit-calculations .

Introduction

Stars with widely separated companions undergo orbital motion, but the periods involved can be much longer than available observational baselines. In this case we see only a short orbital arc. For the host star, this may appear as an acceleration. A radial acceleration is observed in radial velocities
(
9
;
12
, RVs,)
; a sky-plane acceleration may be detected using absolute astrometry
(
1
)
. These accelerations, especially in RV, are also known as trends. We may also observe the companion itself, enabling the measurement of a projected separation
(
4
;
5
;
2
)
, and in some cases the relative sky-plane motion of the primary and secondary may be available. This is increasingly the case with data from the Gaia spacecraft
(
7
)
.

15
, hereafter
15

Using Monte Carlo, they computed the probability distributions of companion mass as a function of projected separation for a measured acceleration of the host star in either RV or astrometry.
15
also computed probability distributions of the ratio of projected separation to semimajor axis given a few different underlying distributions of eccentricity. These distributions were also calculated using Monte Carlo.
14
developed an analytic framework for computing distributions of true and projected separations, while
8
applied the results of
14
to direct imaging surveys in the case of zero eccentricity.

In this paper I derive closed-form expressions equivalent to those computed in
15
. For RV or astrometric accelerations, these expressions may be obtained using Newton’s Law of Gravity and assuming the separation vector to be randomly oriented, with no dependence on the orbital elements. Section
2
derives analytic expressions for the probability distributions of mass as a function of measured acceleration and projected separation in both the astrometric and the RV case. Sections
3

4

15
. Section
5
derives a closed-form expression for the probability distribution of the ratio of the projected separation to semimajor axis at fixed eccentricity. This must, in general, be integrated numerically over a distribution of eccentricities. I conclude with Section
6

2
Analytic Expressions for Trends

I first derive analytic expressions for the relation between the mass of a companion to a star, the star’s observed acceleration, and the companion’s projected separation. Denoting the mass of a faint companion tugging a star as
M_{B}
, the RV and astrometric accelerations may be derived from Newton’s Law of Gravity as

a_{\rm RV}=\frac{d{\rm RV}}{dt}=\frac{GM_{B}}{r^{2}}\cos\varphi

(1)

and

a_{\rm ast}=\frac{GM_{B}}{r^{2}}\sin\varphi

(2)

where
\varphi
is the angle between the line-of-sight and the separation vector
{\bf r}
between the star and its companion. A value of
\varphi=0
indicates a star with a companion displaced along the line-of-sight. This is geometrically less likely than a separation vector in the sky plane (with
\varphi=\pi/2
). Throughout this paper I will consider only the absolute values of accelerations, of
\sin\varphi
, and of
\cos\varphi
, so that it is sufficient to take
\varphi\in[0,\pi/2]
. Equation (
2
) assumes that only the acceleration of the primary star is measured, i.e., that there is no light from the companion contaminating the measurement. The projected separation, the product of the distance
D
from Earth to the star and the angular separation in radians
\rho
, is

D\rho=r\sin\varphi.

(3)

I treat the astrometric case first. I combine Equations (
2
) and (
3
) to get
M_{B}
as a function of astrometric acceleration, projected separation
D\rho
, and
\varphi
, as

M_{B}=\frac{1}{G}a_{\rm ast}(D\rho)^{2}\Phi_{\rm ast}(\varphi)

(4)

with

\Phi_{\rm ast}=\frac{1}{\sin^{3}\varphi}.

(5)

Assuming the orientation of the separation vector to be random on the unit sphere, the probability distribution of
\varphi
is given by

\frac{dp}{d\varphi}=\sin\varphi

(6)

or, equivalently,

\frac{dp}{d\cos\varphi}=1

(7)

for
\varphi\in[0,\pi/2]
. Next, I use Equations (
5
) and (
6
) together with the chain rule to derive the probability distribution
dp/d\Phi_{\rm ast}
of
\Phi_{\rm ast}(\varphi)
. After a few algebraic steps, I have

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\Phi_{\rm ast}}=\bigg|\frac{dp}{d\varphi}\left(\frac{d\Phi_{\rm ast}}{d\varphi}\right)^{-1}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3\sqrt{\Phi_{\rm ast}^{10/3}-\Phi_{\rm ast}^{8/3}}}.

(8)

The domain of
\Phi_{\rm ast}
runs from one to infinity. The cumulative distribution function of
\Phi_{\rm ast}
is

{\rm cdf}(\Phi_{\rm ast})=\sqrt{1-\Phi_{\rm ast}^{-2/3}}

(9)

with a median value of
(4/3)^{3/2}\approx 1.54
.

I now turn to the RV case,

M_{B}=\frac{1}{G}a_{\rm RV}(D\rho)^{2}\Phi_{\rm RV}(\varphi)

(10)

with

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm RV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cos\varphi\sin^{2}\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cos\varphi-\cos^{3}\varphi}.

(11)

I differentiate
\Phi_{\rm RV}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\Phi_{\rm RV}}{d\cos\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\cos^{2}\varphi-1}{\left(\cos\varphi-\cos^{3}\varphi\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\rm RV}^{2}\left(3\cos^{2}\varphi-1\right).

(12)

This time, I need to compute
\cos\varphi
from
\Phi_{\rm RV}
. Unfortunately,
\Phi_{\rm RV}(\cos\varphi)
has a cubic denominator, and its inverse is multiply valued. The two (real) solutions of

\cos\varphi-\cos^{3}\varphi=\frac{1}{\Phi_{\rm RV}}

(13)

that apply between
\varphi=0
and
\varphi=\pi/2
are

\displaystyle\cos\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\cos\psi

(14)

and

\cos\varphi_{2}=\sin\psi-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\cos\psi

(15)

with

\psi=\frac{1}{3}\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{27}}{2\Phi_{\rm RV}}\right).

(16)

Each of the equations for
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
applies over a different range of
\varphi
values: Equation (
13
) applies between
\varphi=0
and
\varphi=\cos^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
, and Equation (
15
) applies between
\varphi=\cos^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
and
\varphi=\pi/2
. When I compute a probability density via the chain rule, the expression
dp/d\varphi
arises. To treat this properly as a probability density, it must be normalized by the size of the relevant domain of
\varphi
values. Then, when adding the two probability densities from
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
together, each is multiplied by the same domain size that was previously used to normalize
dp/d\varphi
. I finally have

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\Phi_{\rm RV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi_{\rm RV}^{2}}{|3\cos^{2}\varphi_{1}-1|}+\frac{\Phi_{\rm RV}^{2}}{|3\cos^{2}\varphi_{2}-1|}.

(17)

The limits on
\Phi_{\rm RV}
run from
\sqrt{27}/2\approx 2.6

(
11
)
to infinity. Unfortunately, the more complex form of Equation (
17
) precludes a simple, analytic expression for the cumulative distribution function, though numerical integration is straightforward. Neither
dp/d\Phi_{\rm RV}
nor
dp/d\Phi_{\rm ast}
depend on the orbital elements, so long as the orientation of the separation vector between the star and its companion is random.

3
Equivalence to a Keplerian Derivation in RV

In this section I demonstrate the equivalence of Equation (
11
) to the corresponding expression of
15
. The expressions of
15
are derived from the equations describing a Keplerian orbit, and the resulting probability distributions must be evaluated by Monte Carlo. I derive the explicit equivalence for the RV case up to the definition of
\Phi_{\rm RV}
. I defer a full numerical validation of both the RV and astrometric results to the following section. From
15
, I have

\frac{M_{B}}{M_{\odot}}=5.341\times 10^{-6}\left(\frac{D\rho}{\rm AU}\right)^{2}\bigg|\frac{d{\rm RV}}{dt}\bigg|\Phi_{\rm RV}\left[i,e,\omega,\phi\right]

(18)

with

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm RV}\left[i,e,\omega,\phi\right]=(

\displaystyle 1-e)(1+\cos E)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\times\big[(1-e\cos E)\left(1-\sin^{2}(\nu+\omega)\sin^{2}i\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\sin(\nu+\omega)(1+\cos\nu)\sin i\big]^{-1}.

(19)

In Equation (
19
),
e
is the eccentricity,
E
is the eccentric anomaly,
\nu
is the true anomaly,
i
is the inclination,
\omega
is the argument of periastron, and
\phi
is the phase (the mean anomaly), which is implicit in both the eccentric anomaly and the true anomaly.

The equation simplifies considerably if I expand the term
1+\cos\nu
. The cosine of the true anomaly
\cos\nu
is related to the eccentric anomaly
E
by

\displaystyle\cos\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos E-e}{1-e\cos E}.

(20)

The phase function calls for
1+\cos\nu
, which, after factoring, may be written as

\displaystyle 1+\cos\nu

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-e)(1+\cos E)}{1-e\cos E}.

(21)

Plugging this into Equation (
19
) gives

\Phi_{\rm RV}\left[i,e,\omega,\phi\right]=\frac{1}{\sin(\omega+\nu)\sin i-\sin^{3}(\omega+\nu)\sin^{3}i}.

(22)

In Equation (
22
), the only remaining dependence on eccentricity is that implicit in the true anomaly
\nu
.

The standard choice of prior is one that is uniform in the argument of periastron
\omega
; this is a consequence of assuming the orientation of the angular momentum vector to be random on the unit sphere. In that case, Equation (
22
) simplifies further. If
\omega
is uniformly distributed, then
\nu+\omega
is also uniformly distributed for any well-defined distribution of
\nu
. This is because phase-wrapping the sum of any quantity and a uniformly distributed variable at the limits of the uniform distribution gives a uniformly distributed result. Defining the argument of latitude
u=\nu+\omega
, the phase function then simplifies to

\Phi_{\rm RV}\left[i,u\right]=\frac{1}{\sin u\sin i-\sin^{3}u\sin^{3}i}

(23)

for a uniform distribution of
u
and an arbitrary prior on
i
. Assuming a uniform prior on
u
and a
\sin i
prior on inclination, the distribution of
z=\sin u\sin i
is, in fact, uniform: it is the same as the distribution of
\cos i
under random orbital orientations. This can be proved using the conditional probability integral for
z=xy
:

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dz}=\int\left(\frac{dp}{dx}\right)\left(\frac{dx}{|x|}\right)\left(\frac{dp}{dy}\bigg|_{y=z/x}\right).

(24)

I take
x=\sin u
and
y=\sin i
and limit myself to the case where
\sin u
and
\sin i
are positive for simplicity. I have

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dz}=\int_{z}^{1}\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dx}{x\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\frac{z/x}{\sqrt{1-(z/x)^{2}}}=\frac{1}{4}.

(25)

Another factor of 2 comes from both sines being negative, while the situation is symmetric for
z<0
. I thus have
dp/dz=\frac{1}{2}={\rm constant}
for
z\in[-1,1]
, as I wished to show. Equation (
23
) is then equivalent to Equation (
11
); there is no remaining dependence on any orbital elements.

\Phi_{\rm RV}

\Phi_{\rm ast}
in Equations (
17
) and (
8

15

\Omega

The actual prior on eccentricity does not matter; my results would be the same for another distribution. 5
Ratio of Projected Separation to Semimajor Axis

I next address the probability distribution of the ratio of the projected separation to the semimajor axis. This probability distribution depends on the distribution of orbital eccentricities and is commonly calculated by Monte Carlo
(
15
;
3
;
6
)
. I begin with the probability distribution of the true separation divided by the semimajor axis,

\displaystyle y\equiv\frac{r}{a}=1-e\cos E

(26)

where
e
is the eccentricity and
E
is the eccentric anomaly. To obtain a probability distribution of
y
, I use the chain rule and Kepler’s equation to write

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dy}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{dp}{dE}\left(\frac{dE}{d\theta}\right)

(27)

where
\theta\in U(0,2\pi)
is the orbital phase. I have, after substitutions,

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{dy}=\frac{y}{\sqrt{e^{2}-\left(y-1\right)^{2}}}.

(28)

Next, I need to account for the unknown geometric orientation to convert
y
into the ratio of the projected separation to semimajor axis; I will designate this ratio by
\psi
. I have

\psi=y\sin\varphi

(29)

in direct analogy to the astrometric case in Section
2
. I only consider positive values of
\sin\varphi
since I am not distinguishing the cases where the companion is in front of or behind its host. The domain of
y
runs from
1-e
to
1+e
, and
\psi
can take on values from zero to
1+e
.

To obtain the probability distribution of
\psi
, I again apply the formula for the probability distribution of a product, Equation (
24
). I have

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\psi}

\displaystyle=\int\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{e^{2}-(y-1)^{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{dy}{y}\right)\left(\frac{\psi/y}{\pi\sqrt{1-\psi^{2}/y^{2}}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{\psi dy}{\sqrt{\left(e^{2}-(y-1)^{2}\right)\left(y^{2}-\psi^{2}\right)}}

(31)

where the factor of
\pi
normalizes
dp/d\varphi
. The limits of integration depend on the value of
\psi
. If
\psi<1-e
, then the integral runs from
y=1-e
to
y=1+e
. If
1-e<\psi<1+e
, then the lower limit of integration is
\psi
. If
\psi>1+e
, then the probability density is zero (this case is unphysical). Equation (
31
) is similar to Equation (43) of
14
, but without those authors’ integrals over eccentricity and semimajor axis.

Equation (
31
) describes an elliptic integral. For convenience, I define

\alpha=\frac{4e\psi}{1-\left(e-\psi\right)^{2}}.

(32)

The solution for
\psi<1-e
, i.e.
\alpha<1
, is

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\psi}\bigg|_{\psi<1-e}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\psi}{\pi^{2}e}}K(\alpha)

(33)

and the solution for
1-e<\psi<1+e
, i.e.
\alpha>1
, is

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\psi}\bigg|_{|\psi-1|<e}=\sqrt{\frac{\psi}{\pi^{2}e}}K(1/\alpha)

(34)

where
K
is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind.
dp/d\psi
diverges at
\psi=1-e
, which presents a modest difficulty when integrating
dp/d\psi
over
\psi
or over
e
(the divergence is integrable). Equation (
33
) is not valid for
e=0
as written, but I can take the limit to obtain

\frac{dp}{d\psi}\bigg|_{e=0}=\frac{\psi}{\sqrt{1-\psi^{2}}}.

(35)

Equations (
33
) and (
34
) give the probability density
\partial p/\partial\psi
at fixed
e
. This probability density is normalized, with

\int_{0}^{1+e}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\psi}\right)\bigg|_{e}d\psi=1.

(36)

To obtain
dp/d\psi
for a distribution of eccentricities, I need to calculate

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\psi}=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\psi}\right)\bigg|_{e}p(e)\,de.

(37)

This integral must, in general, be computed numerically.

6
Discussion and Conclusions

In this paper, I have derived analytic probability density functions for the dimensionless ratio of a companion’s true mass to the mass that would be inferred using a measured acceleration of the host star, either astrometric or radial, and a measured projected separation. These functions may be used to statistically relate measured accelerations of stars with very long-period companions to the projected offsets of those companions from their host stars. I have demonstrated the equivalence of these analytic expressions to probability distributions computed by Monte Carlo from the equations of Keplerian orbits. The probability distributions of the dimensionless mass ratio given an RV or astrometric acceleration measurement are independent of all Keplerian orbital elements so long as the orientations of orbits are random.

I have also derived closed-form solutions for the probability distribution of the ratio of the projected separation to the semimajor axis. These expressions at fixed eccentricity may be written in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. They may then be numerically integrated over an arbitrary distribution of eccentricities. At fixed eccentricity, the probability distribution of this ratio has an integrable singularity in the interior of the domain. Future work could implement efficient approaches to the numerical quadrature.
