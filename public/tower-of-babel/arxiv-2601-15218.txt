Some reverse inequality in optimal mass transportation
Luigi De Pascale, Igor Pinheiro

Abstract
Controlling the 𝒲 ∞ \mathcal{W}_{\infty} Wasserstein distance by the 𝒲 p \mathcal{W}_{p} Wasserstein distance is interesting both for theorical and numerical applications. A first paper on this problem was written several years ago [ 3 ] . Some year later [ 14 ] framed it in the same inequality for more general costs which increase with the distance. In this paper, we prove this type of inequality for optimal transport problems with pointwise cost which is a decreasing function of the distance. We show, in particular, that there is a general framework that encompasses all the cases above.



1
Description of the problem and of the results

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be two probability measures. Let
c:X\times X\to[0,+\infty]
be a ”pointwise transportation cost” function. Denote, as usual in this domain, by

{\mathcal{T}}(\mu,\nu):=\{T:X\to X\ :\ T_{\sharp}\mu=\nu\},

where
T
is a Borel map and
♯
denotes the push-forward of measures. The Monge or optimal transport problem related to the cost
c
consists of

\min_{\mathcal{T}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}c(x,T(x))d\mu.

It is, by now, well known that the Monge’s problem is difficult and may have no solutions so one resorts to its Kantorovich relaxation. We refer, for these questions, to any of the many books on optimal transport, for example
[
19
,
20
,
17
]
. The Kantorovich relaxation is

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\lambda\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}c(x,y)d\lambda,

(1)

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the space of probability measures
\lambda
on
X\times X
such that
\pi^{1}_{\sharp}\lambda=\mu
and

\pi^{2}_{\sharp}\lambda=\nu
. There is a natural identification between
T\in{\mathcal{T}(\mu,\nu)}
and
(id\times T)_{\sharp}\mu\in\Pi(\mu,\nu).

Existence of at least one optimizer for (
1
) holds under lower semi-continuity assumption on
c
and some mild integrability conditions.
We will mostly focus on the Kantorovich version of the problem and we will point, when of interest, to the consequence for the minimizers of the Monge version, if any. We also introduce the
sup
, or supremal, version of the problem.

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\lambda\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\lambda-ess\sup c(x,y),

(2)

which is, by now, well known in the literature.
The monotonicity of the integral implies that

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)\leq\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\mu,\nu).

(3)

This paper will be focused on inequalities that
“reverse”
(
3
) in the sense that allows to control
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
by
\mathcal{C}
. We will focus on the case of repulsive costs and, in a subsection, we will describe the literature for the attractive case and we will add some original remarks on the topic.
Since the costs we consider are repulsive, they make sense also in the case
\mu=\nu=\rho
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
h:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
be a non-increasing function and
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. We define the following transport costs,

\mathcal{C}(\rho):=\inf_{\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)}\int_{X\times X}h(d(x,y))\,\textup{d}\lambda(x,y).

(4)

and

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho):=\inf_{\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)}\lambda-\textup{ess sup}\,h(d(x,y)).

(5)

Remark 1.2
.

The model case for the definition above is the so called Coulomb interaction: The ambient space is
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the Euclidean metric and
h(t)=\frac{1}{t}
. We note that, in this case,
h
is not defined at
t=0
, but instead we have
h(0^{+}):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}h(t)=+\infty
. The key instance in which
h(0^{+})=+\infty
plays a role is the finiteness of
\mathcal{C}(\rho)
as noted in
[
4
]
and then studied in
[
7
,
2
]
(see Prop.
2.15
below). Regardless, our results hold either if
h(0^{+})<\infty
or
h(0^{+})=\infty
.

The type of reverse inequality that we will study is the following: there exists a function
\omega:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that,
\omega(t)>0
for
t>0
and

\omega(\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho))\leq\mathcal{C}(\rho),

(6)

where, of course,
\omega
should be explicit.
This formulation of the problem was first given in
[
14
]
. The function
\omega
, should allow situations as the one of the next example and this will need some study, later on, in this paper. The next example illustrate the need of some caution in the study of this problem.

Example 1.3
.

Let
h(t)=t^{-1}
and
\rho_{\epsilon}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
given by
\rho_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\epsilon\delta_{1}+(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)\delta_{\frac{1}{\epsilon}}
. Then,
\mathcal{C}(\rho_{\epsilon})=2\epsilon(\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
, but
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho_{\epsilon})=1
.

1.1
The case of Wassertein distances

When

the cost
(\min_{\lambda\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X}d^{p}(x,y)d\lambda)^{1/p}:={\mathcal{W}_{p}}(\mu,\nu)

defines the so called
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
. This is, certainly, one of the most significative cases.
In
[
3
]
the authors prove that, if
\Omega
is a bounded, connected, open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz boundary
then for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{ac}(\Omega)
, every
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\overline{\Omega})
and every
p>1
,

({\mathcal{W}_{\infty}}(\mu,\nu))^{p+d}\leq C_{p,d}(\Omega)\|1/f\|_{\infty}\ {\mathcal{W}_{p}}^{p}(\mu,\nu),

where the constant
C_{p,d}(\Omega)
only depends on
\Omega
and
f=\frac{d\mu}{dx}
is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

So, if we consider the class of
\mu
such that
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\geq a
for a certain positive constant
a
the inequality become

\frac{a({\mathcal{W}_{\infty}}(\mu,\nu))^{p+d}}{C_{p,d}}\leq{\mathcal{W}_{p}}(\mu,\nu)^{p}.

(7)

This last inequality is a form of (
6
) for a specific attractive cost.
We will discuss later on the different parameters in this inequality.
The paper
[
3
]
contains some open problems and several other results, among which an
L^{\infty}
estimate for the
p
-optimal transport map.

Inequality (
7
) played a key role in several contexts ranging from regularity theory to PDEs. For instance, in
[
10
]
the authors used (
7
) to improve the
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-convergence rate between stationary state solutions of the Porous Media Equation; in
[
18
]
the authors obtained uniform estimates on the
L^{\infty}
norm of the gradient of the Kantorovich potential corresponding to a JKO step; in
[
15
]
the authors developed local versions of (
7
), extended in
[
13
]
to more general costs, to obtain a purely variational approach to the partial regularity of the optimal transportation map which was later generalized in
[
16
]
to more generic settings than

and in
[
12
]
to the Coulomb cost.

The exponent
p+d
in estimate (
7
) is related to the Lipschitz boundary of
\Omega
, in fact, if
\Omega
is not a Lipschitz domain, one could still have for a similar estimate, but with exponent

2
Main theorem

In what follows, we consider
(X,d)
a complete and separable metric space, i.e. a Polish space,
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
a probability measure on
X
and a continuous and decreasing function
h:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty]
. For
N\geq 1
fixed we also consider the cost function
c:X^{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
given by

c(x_{1},\dots,x_{N}):=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}h(d(x_{i},x_{j})).

And also the functionals
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}_{\infty}:\mathcal{P}(X^{N})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
given by

\mathcal{C}(\lambda)=\int c(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\,\textup{d}\lambda(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\lambda)=\lambda-\textup{ess sup}\,c(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}).

\mathcal{C}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)
will denote, respectivelly, the infimum of each functional over all couplings
\lambda
in the set
\Pi(\rho)=\{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}(X^{N})\,:\,\pi^{i}_{\sharp}\lambda=\rho,1\leq i\leq N\}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\alpha>0
. The concentration of
\rho
at level
\alpha
is given by the quantity

\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)=\sup_{x\in X}\rho(\overline{B}(x,\alpha)).

The pointwise concentration of
\rho
is given by

\kappa(\rho)=\sup_{x\in X}\rho(\{x\}).

Remark 2.2
.

The pointwise concentration,
\kappa(\rho)
, first appeared in
[
11
]
where, in the study of optimal transport with Coulomb cost and
N
marginal constraints, it was observed that if
\kappa(\rho)<C(N)
then the integral transport cost is finite and the optimal transport plan has support at positive distance from the diagonal. For the same reason it was used in
[
4
]
. In
[
2
,
7
]
it was showed that
C(N)=\frac{1}{N}
is the sharp costant. In particular, Th. 1.1 in
[
7
]
and Th.1.1 in
[
2
]
prove that the condition
\kappa(\rho)<\frac{1}{N}
implies the existence of a coupling
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
which has support a positive distance from the set
\{x\in X^{N}\,|\,x_{i}=x_{j}\text{ for some }i\neq j\}
.

In the borderline case
\kappa(\rho)=\frac{1}{N}
nothing can be said in general and one can give examples in which both
\mathcal{C}(\rho)=+\infty
and
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)=+\infty
or both are finite of only
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)=+\infty

In our case, if

is not relevant for the finiteness of
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)

Assumption (A)

Definition 2.7

Let

Remark 2.8

Note that, for the cost defined as

\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}h(d(x_{i},x_{j}))

Moreover,
\mathcal{B}^{\binom{N}{2}h(\beta)}\subset\mathcal{B}_{\beta}
since

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N}h(d(x_{i},x_{j}))>\binom{N}{2}h(\beta)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\max_{i<j}h(d(x_{i},x_{j}))>h(\beta)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\min_{i<j}d(x_{i},x_{j})<\beta.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. If
\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)>\frac{1}{N}
, then for every
\lambda\in\Pi_{N}(\rho)
we have
\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})>0
.

Proof.

Assume that
\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})=0
and fix a point
z\in X
. In particular, for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
with
i\neq j
, we have

\lambda(X^{N-2}\times_{ij}B(z,\alpha))=0.

Thus
\rho(B(z,\alpha))=\lambda(X^{N-1}\times_{i}B(z,\alpha))=\lambda(B^{c}(z,\alpha)^{N-1}\times_{i}B(z,\alpha))
where the family
\{B^{c}(z,\alpha)^{N-1}\times_{i}B(z,\alpha)\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
is a family of disjoint sets. Therefore,

N\rho(B(z,\alpha))=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda(B^{c}(z,\alpha)^{N-1}\times_{i}B(z,\alpha))=\lambda\left(\bigcup^{N}_{i=1}B^{c}(z,\alpha)^{N-1}\times_{i}B(z,\alpha)\right)\leq 1

and taking the supremum over all

proves the contrapositive of the Lemma.

Theorem 2.10
.

Given
\lambda\in\Pi_{N}(\rho)
and
\alpha>0
, then

\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})\geq\frac{N\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)-1}{N(N-1)}

(10)

Proof.

For fixed
\alpha>0
and
x\in X
denote
B\coloneq{B}(x,\alpha)
and define
D_{B}\coloneq\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\,:\,x_{i},x_{j}\in B,\text{ for some }i\neq j\}.
Clearly,
D_{B}\subset\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha}
and we have

N\rho(B)=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda(X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)=\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda((X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\cap D_{B})+\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda((X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\setminus D_{B}).

First we study the second summation. Given
(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})\in(X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\setminus D_{B}
, then
x_{i}\in B
and
x_{j}\not\in B
for all
j\neq i
. Hence, for each
i
,

(X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\setminus D_{B}=(B^{c})^{N-1}\times_{i}B

moreover, for
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
,
[(B^{c})^{N-1}\times_{i_{1}}B]\cap[(B^{c})^{N-1}\times_{i_{2}}B]=\emptyset
therefore

\sum^{N}_{i=1}\lambda((X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\setminus D_{B})=\lambda\left(\bigcup^{N}_{i=1}(B^{c})^{N-1}\times_{i}B\right)\leq 1.

Now, we handle the first summation. Note that
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})\in(X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\cap D_{B}
means that
x_{i}\in B
and
x_{j}\in B
for some
j\neq i
. We have, for
i
fixed,

\displaystyle\lambda\left((X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\cap D_{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left([X^{N-1}\times_{i}B]\cap\bigcup_{j\neq i}[X^{N-1}\times_{j}B]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda([X^{N-1}\times_{i}B]\cap[X^{N-1}\times_{j}B])

\displaystyle\leq(N-1)\lambda(D_{B})

for any

, where in the last inequality we used

\sum^{N}_{i=i}\lambda\left((X^{N-1}\times_{i}B)\cap D_{B}\right)\leq\sum^{N}_{i=1}(N-1)\lambda(D_{B})=N(N-1)\lambda(D_{B}).

Putting everything together and recalling that
D_{B}\subset\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha}
leads to

N\rho(B)\leq N(N-1)\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})+1\Rightarrow\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})\geq\frac{N\rho(B)-1}{N(N-1)}

recalling that

The case of two marginals

In this section we restrict out attention to the case of two marginals and we provide simpler proofs and sharper conclusions of the results presented in the previous section. One of the key reasons for these improvements is that, for two marginals, the costly sets
\mathcal{B}^{B}
and

in Definition

Theorem 3.1

(Fréchet Bounds)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a polish space and
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. Given
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
, for any measurable set
A\subset X
it holds that

\max\{0,2\rho(A)-1\}\leq\lambda(A\times A)\leq\rho(A)

Proof.

The right-hand side inequality is easy:
\lambda(A\times A)\leq\lambda(A\times X)\leq\rho(A)
.
For the left-hand side inequality, if
\rho(A)\leq\frac{1}{2}
then
2\rho(A)-1\leq 0
, and since
\lambda
is a positive measure, we still get the inequality. If
\rho(A)>\frac{1}{2}
then, by Lemma
2.9
,
\lambda(A\times A)>0
and

\lambda(A\times A)=\lambda(A\times X)-\lambda(A\times A^{c})\geq\lambda(A\times X)-\lambda(X\times A^{c})=\rho(A)-\rho(A^{c})=2\rho(A)-1

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a Polish space and
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. Given
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
, for every
\alpha>0
the following inequality holds

\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})\geq 2\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)-1

(13)

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
. If
\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then the inequality is trivially true, as
\lambda
is a positive measure.
Assume
\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)>\frac{1}{2}
. Then, there exists
z\in X
such that
\rho(B(z,\alpha))>\frac{1}{2}
and by the proof of Lemma
2.9
we have that
\lambda(B(z,\alpha)\times B(z,\alpha))>0
. By the Fréchet bounds it follows that

\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{2\alpha})\geq\lambda(B(z,\alpha)\times B(z,\alpha))\geq 2\rho(B(z,\alpha))-1

and taking the supremum over all
z\in X
we obtain the result.

∎

4
Classes of probability measures

The function
t\mapsto\mathfrak{m}(t)=\frac{N\kappa_{\rho}(h^{-1}(\frac{t}{4}))-1}{N(N-1)}
is decreasing and plays a fundamental role in Theorem
2.3
. Examples
1.3
and
3.8
show that it is not possible to obtain a constant
C>0
(independent of
\rho
) such that

C\,\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{C}(\rho).

(16)

Throughout this section we provide two classes of probability measures in which it is possible to control
\mathfrak{m}
from below and then to obtain a constant
C>0
depending only on the class, and not on the particular probability measure, which gives (
16
).

The key idea is as follows. Observe that, for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)=\min_{\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)}h\big(\sup\left\{\alpha\,:\,\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{\alpha})=0\right\}\big)

(17)

thus, for any
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
,

h^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho))\geq\sup\{\alpha\,:\,\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{\alpha})=0\}.

(18)

If, for
\tilde{\alpha}>0
fixed,
\mathfrak{R}_{\tilde{\alpha}}\subset\mathcal{P}(X)
is a collection of measures such that, for each
\rho\in\mathfrak{R}_{\tilde{\alpha}}
there exists
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
such that
\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{\tilde{\alpha}})=0
, it follows from (
18
) that
h^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho))\geq\tilde{\alpha}>0
. If one can guarantee that, for such
\tilde{\alpha}
the quantity
\mathfrak{m}(\tilde{\alpha})
is strictly positive, then the inequality in Theorem
2.3
becomes, for
\rho\in\mathfrak{R}_{\tilde{\alpha}}
,

\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq C_{\tilde{\alpha}}\,h\left(2h^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)^{-1}}{N(N-1)}\right)\right).

(19)

where
C_{\tilde{\alpha}}=\mathfrak{m}(\tilde{\alpha})
.

In this section we will provide two classes
\mathfrak{R}_{\tilde{\alpha}}
: The first will be a collection of unimodal isotropic probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the second will be the collection of discrete measures. To avoid obscuring the meaning of the results by notational heaviness, we will restrict ourselves to the case of two marginals and to
h(s)=s^{-1}
, but the results can be naturally posed for an arbitrary number marginals and more general decreasing functions
h
.

We start with a generic condition that allows one to obtain the constant
C_{\tilde{\alpha}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
h:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be given by
h(s)=s^{-1}
. Define
r_{\rho}=\sup\{r>0\,|\,\kappa_{\rho}(r)\leq 1/2\}
. Let
0<\delta<\frac{1}{2}
. Then there exists
C=C(\delta)>0
(not depending on
\rho
) such that, if

\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(B^{c}(x,2r_{\rho}))\leq\delta<\frac{1}{2},

(20)

then

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\leq C\,\mathcal{C}(\rho).

Proof.

By Theorem
3.4
with
\alpha=r_{\rho}
we have
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\leq\frac{1}{r_{\rho}}
, from which we derive

\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)}\geq 2r_{\rho}

(21)

From
\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(B^{c}(x,2r_{\rho}))\leq\delta
it follows that
\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})\geq 1-\delta>\frac{1}{2}
. From (
21
) we obtain

2\kappa_{\rho}\Big(\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}}\Big)-1\geq 2\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})-1>1-2\delta,

which implies that
\mathfrak{m}(\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)})>1-2\delta
where
\mathfrak{m}
is the function in Theorem
3.6
. We conclude by taking
C:=\frac{1-2\delta}{4}>0
.
∎

4.1
Unimodal isotropic probability measures

Several classes of measures satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
. We will analyze the case of unimodal isotropic probability distributions which includes the Cauchy distribution, the Gaussian distribution, the Laplace distribution and the Logistic distribution, to cite a few. We will also show that for absolutely continuous probability measures which are unimodal and isotropic, the quantity
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}
can be fully characterized as inverse of the diameter of ball (Proposition

) which allows us to give a better expression of
\mathfrak{m}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\geq\sup_{\alpha}\left\{\frac{1}{2\alpha}\,:\,\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}>0\right\}

Proof.

Fix
\alpha>0
such that
\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)>\frac{1}{2}
. Let
z\in\textup{supp}(\rho)
be such that
\rho(\bar{B}(z,\alpha))>\frac{1}{2}
. By the proof of Lemma
2.9
for every
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
we have that
\lambda(\bar{B}(z,\alpha)\times\bar{B}(z,\alpha))>0
, that is, there exists a pair
(x,y)\in\textup{supp}(\lambda)
such that
|x-y|\leq 2\alpha
which implies that

\lambda-\textup{ess sup}\frac{1}{|x-y|}\geq\frac{1}{2\alpha},\,\,\,\,\forall\,\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)

taking supremum over all
\alpha
such that
\kappa(\alpha)>1/2
, and then infimum over all
\lambda\in\Pi(\rho)
leads to

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\geq\sup\left\{\frac{1}{2\alpha}\,:\,\kappa_{\rho}(\alpha)-\frac{1}{2}>0\right\}.

∎

Proposition 4.5

Let

\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)=\sup_{r}\left\{\frac{1}{2r}\,:\,\kappa_{\rho}(r)-\frac{1}{2}>0\right\}

Let

Let

This can be interpreted as the 68–95–99.7 rule in Statistics which states that, in a normal distribution, 68% of the values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two and 99.7% within three standard deviations. Our example highlights that, in a normal distribution, 96.87% of the data falls within two median radius of the mean, where the median radius is the

we can compute
\kappa_{\rho}
explicitly as
\kappa_{\rho}(t)=1-e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

thus

2\kappa_{\rho}(t)-1=1-2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}\text{ and }r_{\rho}=(2\sigma^{2}\ln 2)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Therefore,
\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{15}{32}.

We conclude this section with an application of Theorem
4.1
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be an absolutely continuous probability measure with density of the form
\rho(x)=Ke^{-g(|x|)}
where
K>0
is a normalization constant and
g:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is a positive and increasing convex function. For all measures of this form

\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{4}\,\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho).

Proof.

Let
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}([0,+\infty))
be the probability distribution with density given by
\tilde{\rho}(t)=K\exp(-g(t))t^{d-1}d\omega_{d}
for
t\in[0,+\infty)
. Observe that
\tilde{\rho}
is logarithmically concave, as
g
is convex. Therefore,

S(t)=\int^{+\infty}_{t}\tilde{\rho}(t)\,\textup{d}t

is also logarithmically concave.
Let
r_{\rho}
be the real number which solves the equation
2\kappa_{\rho}(r_{\rho})=1
, then
r_{\rho}
is also a solution for
2\kappa_{\tilde{\rho}}(r_{\rho})=1
and we have

\frac{\ln(S(2r_{\rho}))-\ln(S(r_{\rho}))}{2r_{\rho}-r_{\rho}}\leq\frac{\ln(S(r_{\rho}))-\ln(S(0))}{r_{\rho}-0}\Rightarrow S(2r_{\rho}))\leq\frac{1}{4}

(23)

where the implication comes from noting that
S(0)=1
and
S(r_{\rho})=\frac{1}{2}
. Observing that
S(2r_{\rho}))=\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(B^{c}(x,2r_{\rho}))
it follows from (
23
) and Theorem
4.1
with
\delta=\frac{1}{4}
that

\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)

∎

Example 4.10
.

Consider a probability measure in
\mathbb{R}
distributed as

\rho_{\nu}(x)=K(\nu)\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{|x|^{2}}{\nu}\right)^{\frac{\nu+1}{2}}}

where
\nu\geq 1
and
K(\nu)
is a normalization constant. Notice that this is the radially symmetric Student-t distribution and for
\nu=1
one recovers the Cauchy distribution, however as
\nu\to+\infty
one obtains the normal distribution. For
\nu=1
one may compute

\rho_{\nu}(B^{c}(0,2r_{\rho}))=1-2\frac{\arctan(2)}{\pi}>\frac{1}{4}

while, for
\nu
large enough,

\rho_{\nu}(B^{c}(0,2r_{\rho}))\leq\frac{1}{4}.

4.2
The case of discrete measures

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a discrete measure, i.e.

\rho(x)=\sum^{M}_{i=1}\rho_{x_{i}}\delta_{x_{i}}
where
x_{1},\dots,x_{M}
are distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\rho_{x_{i}}
are positive real numbers satisfying

\sum^{M}_{i=1}\rho_{x_{i}}=1
and assume that
\rho_{x_{i}}<1/2
for every
1\leq i\leq M
. For every
\delta>0
such that

\min\limits_{1\leq i\leq M}\rho_{x_{i}}\geq\delta,

(24)

then

\mathcal{C}(\rho)\geq\frac{\delta}{2}\,\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho).

Proof.

Define
r_{\rho}=\sup\{r>0\,:\,\kappa_{\rho}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
and
x_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the point realizing the supremum of
\kappa_{\rho}(r_{\rho})
. By Theorem
3.4
we have
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)\leq\frac{1}{r_{\rho}}
and hence

\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)}\geq 2r_{\rho}.

Since
r_{\rho}
is the largest radius satisfying
\kappa_{\rho}(r_{\rho})\leq\frac{1}{2}
, it follows that
\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})>\frac{1}{2}
. That is,

0<2\kappa(2r_{\rho})-1=2\rho(\bar{B}(x_{\rho},2r_{\rho})\setminus\bar{B}(x_{\rho},r_{\rho}))

which means that, for some
i\in\{1,\dots,M\}
we have
x_{i}\in\bar{B}(x_{\rho},2r_{\rho})\setminus\bar{B}(x_{\rho},r_{\rho})
, thus

2\kappa(2r_{\rho})-1\geq 2\min_{1\leq i\leq M}\rho_{x_{i}}\geq 2\delta.

Therefore,
\mathfrak{m}(\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)})\geq 2\delta
where
\mathfrak{m}
is the function defined in Theorem
3.6
and we conclude by taking
C:=\frac{\delta}{2}
.
∎

Remark 4.12
.

We highlight that the Theorem
4.11
is a Corollary of
4.1
. In fact, condition (
20
) implies that
\rho(B(x_{\rho},2r_{\rho})\setminus B(x_{\rho},r_{\rho}))\geq\delta-\frac{1}{2}
which implies a lower bound on
\{\rho_{x_{i}}\}^{M}_{i=1}
. However, we preferred to keep it as an independent result.

In
[
1
]
the authors discuss estimates in the same fashion as
[
3
]
and
[
14
]
for general cost functions
c:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
,
X
a Polish space, but restricted to marginals
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(X)
being discrete measures. Their strategy is as follows: Consider the set
\Pi_{\textup{opt}}(\mu,\nu)
of couplings
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(X\times X)
between
\mu
and
\nu
which are optimal for the cost function
c
. Among these we call
trim
the one with the smallest cardinality, i.e. the
trim
plan
\pi^{*}
satisfies

\#\textup{supp}(\pi^{*})\leq\#\textup{supp}(\pi),\,\,\forall\,\pi\in\Pi_{\textup{opt}}(\mu,\nu).

For a
trim
plan it is possible to find (
[
1
]
, Theorem 3) a decomposition
(\mu^{(d)},\mu^{(c)})
of
\mu
and a decomposition
(\nu^{(d)},\nu^{(c)})
of
\nu
, and a pair of functions
(h^{(1)},h^{(2)})
such that

\mu=\mu^{(d)}+\mu^{(c)}\text{ and }\nu=\nu^{(d)}+\nu^{(c)},

(25)

\pi^{*}=(\textup{Id},h^{(1)})_{\sharp}\mu^{(d)}+(h^{(2)},\textup{Id})_{\sharp}\nu^{(d)}.

(26)

Such decomposition is called
diffusive model
associated with the
trim
minimizer
\pi^{*}
and a consequence of the possibility of this decomposition is the following

Theorem 4.13

(
[
1
]
, Corollary 4)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be positive discrete measure and
c:X\times X
a cost function. If
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\textup{opt}}
is trim, then

\int_{X\times X}c(x,y)\,\textup{d}\pi^{*}\geq\alpha\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}||c(x,y)||_{L_{\pi}^{\infty}(X\times X)}

where

\alpha=\min_{x\in\textup{supp}(\mu^{(d)}),\,y\in\textup{supp}(\nu^{(d)})}\{\mu_{x}^{(d)},\nu^{(d)}_{y}\}

In the next proposition we show that on a Polish space
X
,
\mu=\nu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,
X
and
c(x,y)=d(x,y)^{-1}
our function
\mathfrak{m}
obtained in Theorem

is bigger than

.

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(X)
be a positive and discrete probability measure. Let
(\bar{\rho}^{(d)},\bar{\rho}^{(c)})
and
(\underline{\rho}^{(d)},\underline{\rho}^{(c)})
be any diffusive system associated with a trim solution
\pi
, then

\mathfrak{m}\left(\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}(\rho)}\right)\geq\alpha

where

\alpha=\min_{x\in\textup{supp}(\mu^{(d)}),\,y\in\textup{supp}(\nu^{(d)})}\{\mu_{x}^{(d)},\nu^{(d)}_{y}\}

Proof.

We have

\rho=\underline{\rho}^{(d)}+\underline{\rho}^{(c)}\text{ and }\rho=\bar{\rho}^{(d)}+\bar{\rho}^{(c)}

(27)

and
(h^{(1)},h^{(2)})
such that

\pi=(\textup{Id},h^{(1)})_{\sharp}\underline{\rho}^{(d)}+(h^{(2)},\textup{Id})_{\sharp}\bar{\rho}^{(d)}.

As a consequence the decomposition (
27
) we have

\min_{z\in\textup{supp}(\rho)}\rho_{z}\geq\min_{x\in\textup{supp}(\underline{\rho}^{(d)})}(\underline{\rho}^{(d)})_{x}\,\text{ and }\min_{z\in\textup{supp}(\rho)}\rho_{z}\geq\min_{y\in\textup{supp}(\bar{\rho}^{(d)})}(\bar{\rho}^{(d)})_{y}.

(28)

Let
r_{\rho}=\sup\{r>0\,|\,\kappa_{\rho}(r)\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
, then
\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})>\frac{1}{2}
which implies that there exists

\rho(B(x_{\rho},2r_{\rho})\setminus B(x_{\rho},r_{\rho}))\geq\rho_{z}
where
x_{\rho}
is the point in
X
realizing the supremum in
\kappa_{\rho}(r_{\rho})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{m}\left(\frac{2}{\mathcal{C}_{\infty}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\kappa_{\rho}(2r_{\rho})-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle=\rho(\bar{B}(x_{\rho},2r_{\rho})\setminus\bar{B}(x_{\rho},r_{\rho}))

\displaystyle\geq\min_{z\in\textup{supp}(\rho)}\rho_{z}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in\textup{supp}(\underline{\rho}^{(d)}),\,y\in\textup{supp}(\bar{\rho}^{(d)})}\{(\underline{\rho}^{(d)})_{x},(\bar{\rho}^{(d)})_{y}\}=\alpha

∎
