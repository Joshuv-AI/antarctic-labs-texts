An Overview of the Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Fever Outbreak in Iraq
Authors: Jeza Muhamad Abdul Aziz, Marcel Alied, Abdelrahman M Makram, Rohat Muhyadeen Abdullah, Nguyen Tien Huy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is currently implicated as a public health threat in Iraq. This perspective study explored the increasing rate of CCHF cases during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as climate change and human-animal conflict during festivities. CCHF mainly spreads through contact with infected humans/animals and by Hyalomma ticks. One health approach takes into account humans and animals as well as environmental science and economics should be established. Rural health care, vector control, and public awareness must be strengthened. It is important to continue cross-border cooperation and research about diagnosis, treatment, and vaccine development to control CCHF in Iraq and the region.

As of July 2022, Iraq was battling 4 concurrent epidemics according to the Ministry of Health (MOH): the fifth wave of the COVID-19 pandemic, a serious cholera outbreak, an increasing number of rabies cases, and an unparalleled outbreak of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever (CCHF). The MOH was bracing itself for another mpox epidemic. Iraq has become an endemic area for CCHF since the first serologically confirmed human case in the country was identified in 1979 [1]. Before 2022, the maximum number of CCHF cases in Iraq was 48 in 1996; zero cases were reported in 2005, 2006, 2008, 2014, 2016, and 2017; and only 1 confirmed case was reported in 2020. However, the increasing disease burden raised alarms in 2021, 2022, and 2023, with reported CCHF cases of 19, 389, and 587, respectively [2]. This increase was concurrent with the disruption of tick control campaigns targeting livestock during the COVID-19 pandemic [3]. Districts were running low on laboratory kits for the diagnosis of CCHF, which was a concerning issue. When a misdiagnosis or postponement of therapy occurs, it may result in a rise in the number of fatal incidents, and health care professionals working in hospitals are at risk, as they may contract disease from an undiagnosed patient without proper precautions [4]. Elevated temperatures and the expansion of grassy wetlands have potentially created favorable conditions for tick proliferation and activity, particularly in the southern provinces where the majority of cases were recorded [5], with cross-boundary movement of animals and trading possibly contributing to the spread of ticks.

Informal slaughtering of animals during Eid al-Adha might have increased the exposure risk among individuals whose occupations involve close contact with animals [6]. However, the confirmed cases from 2022 to 2023 had a history of exposure to 1 or 2 of the following risk factors: 328 cases had a history of exposure to fresh raw meat, 287 had a slaughtering history, 282 had a history of the presence of animals at home, 43 had a history of tick bite, and 15 had a history of contact with suspected or confirmed cases [2]. Currently, CCHF is being resurrected in Iraq. CCHF is considered a priority disease for accelerated research and development by the World Health Organization (WHO) because of its ability to cause public health emergencies and the lack of effective drugs [7]. The Iraqi Ministry of Agriculture initiated an annual tick control program in 1996 that focused mainly on spraying barns with insecticides and dipping the livestock of herders, who enjoyed this almost free service [8]. This may have played a pivotal role in maintaining an annual number of confirmed CCHF cases. Yet, this program was severely disrupted during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2021), when lockdowns and logistical barriers led to suspension of acaricide treatments coinciding with a surge in tick infestations and a major CCHF outbreak from April to June 2022 [2]. Nonetheless, the government has taken several steps to overcome this problem. In early May 2022, Iraqi MOH teams toured governorates with the highest CCHF cases to follow up on the situation [4]. The WHO implemented a One Health strategy to promote collaboration among agricultural, environmental, and veterinary agencies for long-term control of CCHF, also collaborating with the MOH regarding the availability of diagnostic kits and genomic sequencing [4]. In addition, regular teleconferences were conducted to assess the situation and provide guidance for outbreak responses [4]. The MOH deployed epidemiologic units to the governorate and examined outbreaks through house calls and contact tracing, and vector control teams sprayed acaricides in the affected zones and veterinary facilities [9].

The WHO report noted that a looming source of concern for health authorities was an increase in cases during the Ramadan and Eid al-Adha celebrations, which increased human-animal interaction due to animal sacrifice and livestock trading [9]. The upsurge of CCHF cases during Ramadan and Eid al-Adha festivities is a well-recognized epidemiologic phenomenon associated with religious activity leading to closer human-animal contacts. This increase is confirmed by studies such as the 2023 study by Baghdadi et al, which reported on growth in laboratory-confirmed cases during Eid al-Adha celebration period. Case counts in the weeks prior to, during, and following the Eid were 63, 118, and 23, respectively, showing that there was a marked impact of having the festival [10]. A set of strict measures and recommendations regarding the slaughtering process should be followed during Eid in endemic countries to halt its spread [11].

DISCOVERY, TRANSMISSION, AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

In the mid-1940s, CCHF was discovered after a significant outbreak among farmworkers in Crimea, called the Crimean hemorrhagic fever virus; however, a virus from patients in the Congo matched the Crimean virus, which led to its current name: the Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) [3]. Researchers reported 3 CCHFV strains circulating in Iraq, with evolutionary ties to Oman/Turkey (neighbors) and China (distant) [12].

CCHFV is a lipid-enveloped RNA virus that depends mainly on nonhuman host ticks, and humans are accidentally infected with this virus [13]. However, CCHFV is a very different and unique virus, as it can spread through >30 species of ticks that can remain on a host for weeks [13]. In Iraq the virus is usually transmitted to humans through infected ticks. While Hyalomma marginatum is the primary species, other Hyalomma ticks (eg, H anatolicum, H excavatum) may play a role in transmission of CCHFV in Iraq and neighboring regions [14]. Tick also serves as a reservoir [14]. The most common mode of transmission in 2023 was animal/possible animal contact (67%), followed by tick/possible tick bite (32.2%) and human contact (0.9%) through close contact with infected patients’ blood, organs, secretions, or other body fluids [15]. Akinci et al hypothesized that the virus has either a direct or indirect interaction with epithelial cells, leading to mass activation of proinflammatory cytokines that lead to the typical features of viral hemorrhagic fever [16].

CLINICAL FEATURES

The clinical presentation in Iraqi patients encompasses a broad spectrum of manifestations, ranging from nonspecific febrile illness to severe hemorrhagic syndrome with potentially fatal outcomes [17]. The onset of symptoms is characteristically abrupt, with patients experiencing sudden high-grade fever exceeding 38 °C, as reported by 97% of confirmed cases (n = 986) in Iraq during the 2021–2023 epidemic [17], which is consistent with global reports where fever is nearly universal [18]. Initial symptoms include severe headache, back pain, joint pain (myalgia), stomach pain, and vomiting, and Iraqi patients commonly present with anorexia, nausea, dizziness, and neck pain and stiffness [17, 18].

Hemorrhagic manifestations occurred in 52.1% of Iraqi patients. The most common bleeding presentations were bleeding from injection sites (26.6%), ecchymosis (24.8%), oral bleeding (16.9%), and epistaxis (13.8%), while gastrointestinal bleeding (4.3%) was uncommon [17]. This contrasts with some global reports that emphasize gastrointestinal, epistaxis, and other internal bleeding as more prominent features [19]. Conjunctival hyperemia was newly documented in 2023 in Iraq (7.3%), and conjunctival injection was noted in 6.6% overall [17]. Globally, petechial rashes on mucous membranes and conjunctiva are described, making this finding consistent but possibly underreported in earlier Iraqi data [17, 19]. Hemorrhagic symptoms of mucous membranes and skin range from petechiae to massive hematomas. Cerebral hemorrhage has been documented, as well as bleeding in the vaginal area, gingiva, nose, gastrointestinal system, genitourinary tract, and respiratory tract [20]. The case fatality rate in Iraq has fluctuated in recent years, from 19% to 14% in 2022 and 2023 [2, 21], but is notably higher than the global average of 11.7% [22].

DIAGNOSIS, TREATMENT, AND PREVENTION

Iraq faces significant diagnostic infrastructure challenges for CCHF. While the Central Public Health Laboratory in Baghdad has reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction testing capabilities using commercial kits (RealStar CCHFV RT-PCR Kit 1.0), there is a critical shortage of laboratory diagnostic kits, especially at district and regional levels [23]. Test results can take ≥3 days to become available, and this diagnostic delay can lead to increased case fatalities or misdiagnosis [23]. The rapid diagnostic tests for CCHF antigens were evaluated on stored samples at the Central Public Health Laboratory in 2023. These tests show 71.7% sensitivity and 92.5% specificity and the potential for point-of-care diagnostics in Iraq [24]. Between 2018 and 2022, the beneficial effects of early administration of ribavirin among CCHF cases in Iraq were highlighted. The study strongly recommended early ribavirin use for treatment and postexposure prophylaxis, particularly among health care workers at medium to high risk [25]. The COVID-19 pandemic has put significant strain on the Iraqi health care system, which has recorded >2.4 million confirmed cases. The simultaneous impact of several outbreaks has strained underfunded health infrastructure. Additionally, insufficient awareness of early CCHF symptoms among primary care providers can lead to misdiagnosis and inadequate infection control measures [26].

The principal preventive measures are to avoid tick bites, use personal protective equipment and gloves when dealing with raw meat and animals, and use acaricides for animals [11]. People at risk ought to be well informed regarding recommended precautions through education, while stringent infection control protocols must be rigorously enforced within all health care settings to curb potential exposure [27]. Unrestrained cross-border animal movements between endemic countries might help spread new variants of the virus; therefore, animal movement should be controlled by official legislation [11].

THE IMPACT OF CLIMATE CHANGE ON CCHF

Climate change is thought to be one of the factors that have made it easier for ticks to reproduce, leading to an increase in the incidence of CCHF caused by ticks. H marginatum [28] ticks are typically activated by rising temperatures in the spring, especially in April or May, and those in the immature stages are active in the summer between May and September [18].

Datasets confirmed that Iraq's average temperatures have significantly increased in recent decades, with a mean annual rise of approximately 2 °C since the 1950s and 1.9 °C between 1982 and 2020 [29]. Climate models project that Iraq could see future temperature anomalies of +2.1 °C or more by 2040 to 2060 and even higher under high-emission scenarios, with heat waves accelerating and becoming longer, especially in southern provinces such as Basra, Missan, Thi-Qar, and Muthanna [30]. In general, CCHF outbreaks have occurred against a backdrop of favorable environmental changes and climatic variables that have helped Hyalomma spp ticks survive in high numbers in their immature and adult stages, as well as the hosts that they feed on [18].

CITIZEN SCIENCE FOR TICK MONITORING

Community-based campaigns have proven to be beneficial tools for better monitoring of ticks and tick-borne diseases. Numerous initiatives that depend on citizens’ sighting and reporting of ticks or collecting and sending them to researchers have been described and shown to be very effective. In one initiative in Hungary, volunteers were asked through a large campaign to search for ticks and send them to researchers. Among the different species, one H marginatum and another H rufipes were found, thus emphasizing the significance of such community efforts in monitoring CCHF in Hungary [31]. For Iraq, adapting this model could involve training farmers and shepherds to identify Hyalomma ticks and submit specimens via local health centers.

In the Netherlands, a smartphone application made it possible for pet owners to identify ticks found on their animals and test them for pathogens at no cost, while harnessing information concerning tick location in research [32]. Furthermore, mobile technology through dedicated apps, SMS-based reporting, and integration with national surveillance systems can empower citizens across Iraq to report CCHF cases, tick sightings, and animal health events in real time. Also, early detection of hemorrhagic fever symptoms in humans via community health networks could reduce delays in diagnosis and isolation. Butchers, farmers, and slaughterhouse workers are high-risk groups. Training these stakeholders to report suspected animal infections or tick exposures could identify outbreak precursors.

CONCLUSION

CCHF has reemerged as a significant public health threat in Iraq, with cases increasing dramatically in recent years. Several factors have contributed to this resurgence, including disruptions to tick control programs during the COVID-19 pandemic, climate changes favoring tick proliferation, and increased human-animal contact during religious festivals. The virus is primarily transmitted through infected Hyalomma ticks or contact with blood/tissues of infected animals or humans. Iraq's geographic and climatic conditions make it particularly vulnerable to the spread of tick-borne diseases. Addressing this growing threat requires a comprehensive strategy that integrates human health, veterinary services, and environmental management under the One Health framework. Additionally, cross-border cooperation with neighboring countries is essential for regulating livestock movement and minimizing the spread of infected animals. Continued research into the virus's epidemiology, more effective treatments, and potential vaccines is also needed to better manage this reemerging threat to public health in Iraq and the wider region.
