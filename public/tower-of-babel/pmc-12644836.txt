Incidence of postoperative pharyngeal complications after using a new laryngeal mask airway supreme insertion method
Authors: Jie Zhou, Yu Zhang, Qing Wang, Wei Zhouv, Bing-wei Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, a new insertion method was used to reduce the intracuff pressure of laryngeal mask airway (LMA), and its impact on pharyngeal complications was compared with that of patients under routine care. Sixty women who underwent general anesthesia with LMA for hysteroscopic surgery were randomly allocated into routine care and intervention groups. In the routine care group, the LMA was inserted according to the LMA practice manual. In the intervention group, after insertion with standard manoeuvre, the LMA was withdrawn several millimeters to reduce hypopharynx compression, and the intracuff pressure was then fine-tuned to the minimum effective pressure through volume titration. Pharyngeal complications were assessed at 1, 2, 6 and 24 h postoperatively. Composite pharyngeal complications, pharyngeal complications at different time points and LMA intracuff pressures were compared. The study ultimately included 57 patients. The incidence of composite pharyngeal complications in 24 h was lower in the intervention group than in the routine care group (10.3% vs. 32.1%, P = 0.044). The differences in the incidence of pharyngeal complications at different time points between and within groups were marginally significant (P = 0.052 and 0.060, respectively). Mean LMA intracuff pressure was lower in the intervention group versus the routine care group (11.28 ± 4.22 vs. 68.89 ± 22.53 cmH2O, P < 0.001). Introduction

As a safe and effective airway management device, the laryngeal mask airway (LMA) has been rapidly and routinely used in clinical anesthesia. The LMA Supreme is a novel LMA featuring a preformed, rigid airway tube with an anatomical curvature1. In elective ambulatory surgery, the LMA Supreme is a safe and effective supraglottic airway device, demonstrating a higher first-attempt insertion success rate compared to the LMA ProSeal2. The most common complications of LMA are adverse pharyngeal outcomes, such as sore throat, dysphagia and dysphonia3–6. Other more serious complications, including arytenoid cartilage dislocation, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypoglossal nerve injury and lingual nerve injury, have also been reported7–10. A questionnaire of 355 day surgery patients revealed that sore throat was an important concern11. Notably, the overinflation and high intracuff pressure of the LMA are of paramount important factor in sore throat12. The high intracuff pressure of the LMA might reduce pharyngeal mucosal perfusion13. A previous randomized controlled study showed that limiting the LMA intracuff pressure to less than 60 cmH2O decreased the occurrence of pharyngeal complications14. Burgard et al. reported that reducing the intracuff pressure to the minimum required for an effective seal reduced the incidence of sore throat15. A prospective audit demonstrated that higher intracuff pressure of LMA was also related to sore throat in children16. Therefore, reducing the intracuff pressure could represent a clear opportunity for significant improvement in patient satisfaction and safety.

At present, the use of a manometer to limit intracuff pressure after LMA insertion is not a routine practice. The intracuff pressure was controlled empirically by limiting the cuff volume. In clinical practice, anesthesiologists choose the LMA size on the basis of the patient’s body weight according to the manufacturer’s instructions. However, the size of variable airway anatomy is not always proportional to body weight due to individual anatomical variation. For each patient, the reduction in intracuff pressure should be more individualized and precise.

Based on the concept of precision anesthesia, a new insertion method was developed to limit the intracuff pressure of LMA. We aimed to compare the incidence of pharyngeal complications in hysteroscopic surgical patients managed with this method with that in patients under the routine care of LMA insertion.

Methods

Ethics and study design

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongde Hospital of Zhejiang Province (No: ZTD-2023S-012), and written informed consent was obtained from all the subjects who participated in the trial. The trial was registered prior to patient enrolment at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=202373, Registration number: ChiCTR2300078350, Registration date: 06/12/2023). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Consents were obtained from all female patients scheduled to receive general anesthesia with the LMA for hysteroscopic surgeries.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were patients aged 18–70 yr with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status I – III undergoing elective hysteroscopic surgery with general anesthesia. The exclusion criteria were patients with a recent history of upper respiratory tract infection, contraindications to the use of LMA such as severe gastroesophageal reflux disease, hoarseness or dysphagia, symptomatic hiatus hernia, and a body mass index > 40 kg·m-2.

Randomisation and allocation

Using computer-generated numbers, patients were divided into routine care group and intervention group, with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The anesthesiologists who performed the insertion of LMA were not blinded. The patients and research assistants performing postoperative pharyngeal complications assessment after LMA removal were blinded to their group allocation. Group assignments were concealed until the end of the trial, and the last patient completed 24 h of the study from the day of anesthesia.

Anesthesia and study interventions

When patients were admitted to the operating room, routine monitoring, such as electrocardiography, pulse oximetry and noninvasive blood pressure, was applied. We chose a size 3 LMA Supreme™ (Teleflex Medical Co., Ltd., Ireland) for patients weighing 30–50 kg, a size of 4 LMA for those weighing 50–70 kg and a size of 5 LMA for those weighing greater than 70 kg. Anesthesia was induced by intravenous injection of propofol 2 mg·kg−1 and sufentanil 0.2 μg·kg−1. After patients lost the eyelash reflex and the jaw relaxed, the LMA was inserted by experienced anesthesiologists (over 5 yr of experience) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The standard insertion procedure was performed as follows: A. Apply generous lubrication to the surface of LMA just prior to insertion; B. Stand at the head of the bed, behind the patient’s head; C. Position patient’s head in the sniffing position (head extended and neck flexed) ; E. Press the cuff further into the mouth, maintaining pressure against the palate; F. Slide the device inward with a circular motion, pressing against the contours of the hard and the soft palate; G. Advance the device into the hypopharynx until resistance is felt. The ease of insertion was categorized by the attending anesthesiologist as easy, moderate, or difficult2.

In the routine care group, half of the maximum volume recommended by manufacturers was used to inflate the cuff. And then, the LMA was connected to an anesthesia machine (GE-Carestation 620, USA). If necessary, additional air can be reinflated 5 ml each time until no audible leakage occurs. Mechanical ventilation was instituted with volume-controlled ventilation at 8 ml·kg−1 and a respiratory rate of 10 to 15 breaths min−1. Once regular mechanical ventilation was achieved, the LMA intracuff pressure was assessed by a hand-held pressure gauge (Hi-Lo hand pressure gauge manometer, Covidien, USA).

In the intervention group, the LMA cuff was inflated using a method consistent with that used in the routine care group. The mechanical ventilation parameters were identical between the two groups. Thereafter, the LMA was withdrawn several millimeters slowly until the caudal LMA cuff was against the base of the tongue and there was significant resistance. Following withdrawal of LMA, the ventilator settings remained unchanged. Concurrently, tidal volume, oxygen saturation, and end-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂) were closely monitored. The LMA was repositioned if a significant air leak was detected. Finally, the LMA cuff was deflated gradually using a syringe. Until slight leakage occurred, the LMA cuff was reinflated with 2 ml air again for an effective audible seal. The detailed procedural demonstration is accessible via the accompanying operation video (provided in the supplementary materials). When regular mechanical ventilation was achieved, the LMA intracuff pressure was measured. The incisor position was marked before and after LMA withdrawal, and the withdrawal distance was measured using a vernier caliper after LMA removal.

Successful insertion was defined as the achievement of effective mechanical ventilation, including the following: I. normal bilateral chest movement; II. no air leakage; Ⅲ. a stable square capnographic waveform; IV. volume difference of inspiratory and expiratory tidal volume < 30 ml 17,18.

General anesthesia was maintained by intravenous infusion of propofol (50–100 μg·kg−1·min−1) with remifentanil (0.1–0.2 μg·kg−1·min−1), which was titrated to maintain bispectra index values between 40–60. Volume controlled ventilation with volume 8 ml·kg−1 and no positive-end expiratory pressure was applied. The respiratory rate was adjusted to keep the end-tidal carbon dioxide tension between 30–40 mmHg. Because no muscle relaxant was administered, when patients begin breathing spontaneously, the depth of anesthesia increased. At the end of surgery, when patients awoke and were able to cooperate on verbal command, the LMA was removed by anesthesiologists. Pharyngeal suctioning was performed routinely. The criterion for pharyngeal mucosa injury was the presence of one of the following conditions: I. blood stain on the LMA surface; II. blood stain during pharyngeal secretion. Postoperative pain was treated with intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg. The patients were monitored in post anesthesia care for 20 min.

The research assistants assessed postoperative pharyngeal complications, including sore throat, dysphonia and dysphagia, at 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, and 24 h after LMA removal. Sore throat was defined as “pharyngalgia or pain independent of swallowing”. Dysphonia was defined as “pain speaking or difficulty speaking”. Dysphagia was defined as “pain or difficulty provoked by swallowing”4. A home telephone interview was accomplished at the time point of 24 h for the assessment of pharyngeal complications.

Study outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was the incidence of composite pharyngeal complications within 24 h post-surgery. It was defined as the occurrence of any single postoperative pharyngeal complication of sore throat, dysphagia, or dysphonia at any time point of 1 h (T1), 2 h (T2), 6 h (T3), or 24 h (T4). The secondary clinical outcomes were the LMA intracuff pressure, the incidence of pharyngeal complications at different time points, the incidence of pharyngeal mucous membrane injury and the LMA withdrawal distance in the intervention group.

Sample size calculation

Based on existing data, the incidence of postoperative pharyngeal complications of LMA was 45.6% for routine care and 13.4% for intracuff pressure-limiting14. Given our assumption that the strategy of individualized minimum effective intracuff pressure is equally efficient, with a power of 0.8 and an alpha error of 0.05, a total sample size of 56 patients was determined to be necessary for calculation using PASS. Considering a possible 5% loss to follow-up, 60 patients were included in this study.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), and the graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8. Normally distributed data was presented as the mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD), skewed data was reported as median (M) and interquartile range (IQR), and count data was presented as the number and percentage (%). As a normality test for continuous data, the Shapiro‒Wilk test was used. Continuous data between the two groups were compared using independent samples t test. And categorical data were analyzed using chi-squared test or Fischer’s exact test. Generalized estimated equation (GEE)19 was used to analyze repeated measurement data to compare the risk of postoperative pharyngeal adverse events at different time points after surgery. P value < 0.05 (two-sided) indicated a statistically significant difference.

Secondary outcomes

Incidence of pharyngeal complications at different time points

GEE was employed to examine the within- and between-group disparities in the occurrence of pharyngeal complications at each designated time interval. The effect sizes of the RR values for the group variable (Wald χ2 = 3.791, P = 0.052) and time variable (Wald χ2 = 7.392, P = 0.060) were calculated using the Poisson loglinear model. In general, the marginally significant of p value are not scientifically meaningful; investigators could simply report effect sizes, P values, and confidence intervals to the discerning reader to infer the clinical applicability and importance20. Hence, the RR for different subgroups and time intervals were reported to the discerning reader to infer the clinical applicability and importance. The risk of pharyngeal complications in the intervention group was reduced by 72.4% (RR = 0.276, 95%CI 0.075–1.009, Wald χ2 = 3.791, P = 0.052); the risk of pharyngeal complications at T2 was 1.5-fold of that at T1 (RR = 1.5, 95%CI 0.852–2.641, Wald χ 2 = 1.973, P = 0.160); the risk of pharyngeal complications at T3 was 50.0% of that at T1 (RR = 0.5, 95%CI 0.125–1.999, Wald χ2 = 0.961, P = 0.327); and the risk of pharyngeal complications at T4 was 1.5-fold of that at T1 (RR = 1.5, 95%CI 0.423–5.315, Wald χ2 = 0.395, P = 0.530) (Fig. 2). Discussion

Our study demonstrated that using this insertion method of LMA, the postoperative pharyngeal complications were reduced by 45.7% (RR = 0.563, 95%CI 0.351–0.903). The unique distinction of our study is that when a manometer is unavailable, this method can be used to reduce the LMA intracuff pressure to the individualized and minimum pressure. We name this the two-step pressure reduction method. In step 1, after insertion and inflation, we withdrew the LMA several millimeters until the caudal LMA cuff was against the base of the tongue and there was significant resistance. In step 2, to achieve individualized minimum effective intracuff pressure, the LMA cuff was deflated gradually. Until slight air leakage occurred, the LMA cuff was reinflated with a small amount of air again for a minimum effective volume for an audible seal.

At present, LMA is inserted via “blind” placement. When the LMA is inserted too deep, the LMA tip can exert a significant substantial squeezing effect on the upper esophagus sphincter, resulting in high hypopharyngeal mucosal pressure21,22. A study of 3D modelling and finite element analysis indicated that sore throat caused by high mucosal pressure mostly occurred in the hypopharynx under high cuff pressure, especially in the area where the cricoid cartilage compressed the mucosa22. Therefore, reducing compression on the hypopharynx could be extremely important. The first step of this method was devised to prevent overly deep insertion of the LMA. Our study confirmed that the LMA could be withdrawn 5–10 mm along the axial direction in the absence of air leakage. This result suggested that when the LMA cuff was completely inflated, there was still a gap in the axial direction. Even a small withdrawal of the LMA can relieve compression of the LMA tip on the hypopharynx. Therefore, the correct position of LMA is important for the safety of patients. Additional improvements in multicamera technology have been suggested to obtain a panoramic view of the supraglottic airway device correctly placed in the hypopharynx accurately23. Visually guided insertion techniques may be a future direction of development.

The high intracuff pressure of the LMA might cause high pressure upon the pharyngeal mucosa 24. When the pressure on the pharyngeal mucosa exceeds the critical perfusion pressure, mucosal trauma is potentiated13. Brimacombe et al. reported that pharyngeal mucosal perfusion gradually decreased when the mucosal pressure increased from 34 to 80 cmH2O25,26. The intracuff pressure of the LMA is an underestimated issue. Under general anesthesia, the majority of surgical patients are exposed to excessive intracuff pressure. A randomized study reported that an extremely high LMA intracuff pressure of mean 180 cmH2O was recorded at the start of anesthesia in 63 patients27. Another study reported that 50% of patients who underwent general anesthesia were provided with too high of laryngeal mask cuff pressure28. In recent years, many previous studies have compared the effects of high and low cuff pressures on pharyngeal complications. Most studies have shown that patients with low intracuff pressure are less prone to adverse pharyngeal events. Seet et al. found that reducing the LMA intracuff pressure to less than 44 mmHg lowered the incidence of pharyngeal complications compared with routine care (13.4% vs. 45.6%, P < 0.01)14. G Chantzara et al. showed that, irrespective of the ventilation mode, maintenance of a constantly low intracuff LMA pressure resulted in a fourfold decrease in pharyngeal discomfort compared with the nonintervention approach 29. David et al. reported that, compared with classic LMA without intracuff pressure guidance, the use of a new supraglottic airway, which has a built-in intracuff pressure indicator, reduced postoperative pharyngeal complications (26% vs 49%; P = 0.002)30. Based on those studies, it was reasonable to assume that there was a positive dose‒response relationship between pharyngeal tissue trauma and mucosal pressure. Therefore, the second step of this method uses a titration method to achieve individualized minimum effective intracuff pressure. After cuff inflation, the LMA was gradually deflated using a syringe. When slight leakage occurred, a small amount of air inflation was then titrated until the minimum effective intracuff pressure was reached. Our study indicated that the intracuff pressure was much lower with the two-step pressure reduction method than with the routine care method (11.28 ± 4.22 vs. 68.89 ± 22.53 cmH2O, P < 0.001), even than many previous studies14,31,32.

Compared to directly pressure titration after insertion, our approach of first performing a slight withdrawal of LMA before titration pressure may mitigate both the pressure caused by deep insertion on the hypopharynx and the pressure from cuff inflation on the pharyngeal mucosa, thereby potentially reducing the incidence of pharyngeal complications. The two-step pressure reduction method allows for further individualized achievement of the minimal effective intracuff pressure beyond direct titration, specifically accounting for anatomical variations among patients. This represents a distinct advantage of the two-step pressure reduction method.

Although GEE had a marginally significant p value (P = 0.060), owing to the limited sample size, this study did not have sufficient statistical power to analyze the effects of time variables on the incidence of pharyngeal complications. The limited number of positive cases showed that the incidence of pharyngeal complications was lower in the intervention group than in the routine care group at each time point, and the incidence of pharyngeal complications was significantly lower at 6 h after LMA removal than at 1 h, 2 h and 24 h after LMA removal in both groups. This result seemed reasonable because a single intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg (half-life of 5.8 h) for analgesia was not sufficient to provide analgesia throughout the postoperative period. Even 24 h post-surgery, the incidence of pharyngeal complications in the two groups were 17.9% and 3.4%, respectively. Therefore, we recommend that postoperative analgesia prescriptions should be covered for a minimum of 24 h after surgery. Further studies may need longer follow-up times and intensive sampling to explore the temporal characteristics of postoperative pharyngeal complications.

One of the limitations of our study is that although the research assistant who assessed postoperative pharyngeal complications was blinded to the intervention, the anesthesiologist who performed the insertion of the LMA was not blinded. The single-blind design may have led to bias, as the unblinded anesthesiologists’ awareness of the technique used could have subconsciously influenced their insertion manner of LMA—potentially leading to gentler manipulation in intervention groups. This might decrease the incidence of postoperative pharyngeal complications in the intervention group, thereby overestimated the true comparative effect between the two methods. Second, this study specifically enrolled female patients undergoing hysteroscopy without the use of muscle relaxants. Consequently, the results may not be generalizable to surgical populations requiring neuromuscular blocking agents. Because the administration of such agents may provide enhanced muscle relaxation and reduce respiratory resistance, potentially resulting in a lower minimal effective intracuff pressure for LMA. Third, with the two-step pressure reduction method as a pioneering approach, a smaller sample size was used to reduce the occurrence of potentially unknown risks in accordance with medical ethical guidelines. This resulted in insufficient statistical power for some secondary clinical outcomes and difficulty in assessing the occurrence of rare complications such as nerve injuries.

Conclusion

Compared with routine care for LMA insertion, using two-step pressure reduction method which precisely achieved an individualized minimum effective intracuff pressure for LMA was superior in terms of the reduced incidence of postoperative pharyngeal complications.
