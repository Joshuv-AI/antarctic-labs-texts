Evidence‐Based Interventions for Young Dual Language Learners With or at Risk of Language Impairment: A Scoping Review
Authors: Yanling Song, Sarah Ohi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEarly intervention professionals, including speech‐language pathologists, teachers, and policymakers, face significant challenges in understanding and supporting young dual language learners who have or are at risk of language impairment. The existing research evidence base is fragmented and relatively sparse, and the overall extent and depth of interventions available for different language pairings (the home language and English) remains largely unknown.PurposeThis scoping review maps language interventions for dual language learners aged 3 to 9 years with, or at risk of, language impairment, with attention to intervention type, cultural responsiveness, and evidence of cross‐language transfer.MethodGuided by PRISMA‐SCR, four key databases were searched, yielding 29 eligible articles for full‐text review.ResultsThe included articles were comprised of 15 child‐based language intervention studies, four caregiver‐based intervention studies, six information and communication technology‐based intervention studies, and six research reviews. Twenty‐one studies focused on Spanish‐English dual language learners in the United States. Ten studies incorporated cultural considerations, including home language use, family values, familiar materials, personal narratives, and caregiving routines, while nine reported evidence of cross‐language transfer.ConclusionsTransfer was most consistent for vocabulary, cognates, semantic knowledge, and narrative skills, particularly when interventions incorporated children's first language or targeted shared linguistic features across languages. In contrast, English‐only interventions showed limited evidence of spontaneous transfer to children's home languages. The findings identify promising principles related to bilingual intervention, caregiver involvement, culturally involvement, culturally responsive practice, and cross‐language transfer, while highlighting an urgent need for language‐specific intervention research relevant to Australian multilingual contexts.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on this subject A review of prior research demonstrated the clear lack of intervention research focused on young dual language learners with learning difficulties. English and Spanish were the most commonly studied language pairings. The overall extent and depth of existing interventions for common home languages in Australia (Mandarin, Arabic, Vietnamese, Cantonese, and Italian) and English dual language learners remain unknown. What this paper adds to the existing knowledge This scoping review provides a map of existing interventions for 3‐to‐9‐year‐old dual language learners who are with, or at risk of language impairment, highlighting cultural considerations and cross‐language transfer in language interventions. The search revealed that there were no interventions targeting bilingual children whose language pairings are common home languages in Australia and English for that age‐group across the period 2012–2025. What are the clinical implications of this study? This study identified three types of interventions that can serve as a valuable reference for educators and speech‐language pathologists working with Spanish‐English dual language learners with or at risk of language impairment. Collectively, the evidence underscores the value of culturally responsive intervention and bilingual delivery; suggests that language pairings be carefully considered in intervention design, and that languages with a high degree of lexical overlap can benefit from the use of cognates in intervention. Future research should extend this work by systematically incorporating culturally responsive elements across a wider range of language pairings to better reflect population diversity.Keywords: cross‐language transfer, culturally responsive practice, dual language learners, home language, language impairment, language intervention, scoping review

Introduction

Background

Australia is culturally and linguistically diverse (Lampe et al. 2023) with 5.8 million people (22.8% of the population) reporting use of a language other than English at home (Australian Bureau of Statistics [ABS] 2022). In Australian education, more than 25% of children in the early childhood sector and 31% of primary and secondary school students come from a language background other than English (Australian Curriculum, Assessment, and Reporting Authority [ACARA] n.d.; Department of Education, Skills, and Employment 2022). Many dual language learners (DLLs) in Australia, face the dual challenge of developing English proficiency while maintaining their home language (L1) (ABS 2022). Some of these children experience language difficulties due to constrained exposure, limited supports, and individual varied circumstances. In Australia, demand for interventions to strengthen young DLLs’ language and literacy development is high (ACARA n.d.). At a global level there are calls to broaden multilingual intervention, highlighting the importance of cultural specificity in intervention design (Crowe et al. 2021).

This scoping review initially aimed to map interventions involving language pairings between English and the most commonly spoken Australian L1s including Mandarin (2.7% of the population), Arabic (1.4%), Vietnamese (1.3%), Cantonese (1.2%), and Italian (0.9%) (ABS 2022). These languages, hereafter referred to as MAVCI, are used to describe this group throughout the paper. Despite identical percentages, Italian language was prioritised over Punjabi (0.9%) and Greek (0.9%), due to longstanding immigration and marked growth in second generation speakers (ABS 2022). The absence of eligible studies using these language pairings warranted an expanded scope.

Notably, previous research literature in this area is dominated by Spanish and English language pairings (Crowe et al. 2021; Durán et al. 2016), highlighting the need to examine other language combinations. Spanish–English bilingualism represents one of the most extensively researched language pairings in the DLLs and developmental language disorder (DLD) literature. As such, this evidence base provides a useful empirical foundation for examining intervention design, delivery conditions, language‐of‐intervention decisions, and evidence of cross‐language generalisation in young bilingual children with, or at risk of, language impairment (LI).

The relevance of Spanish–English studies does not rest on the assumption that findings are directly transferable to all other language pairings. Rather, their value lies in the broader principles they illuminate about bilingual language learning and intervention. Theories of linguistic interdependence propose that children's developing languages are not entirely separate systems and that conceptual, metalinguistic, and academic language skills may transfer across languages under appropriate conditions (Cummins 1979). This theoretical position is reflected in intervention literature that distinguishes between bilingual intervention, which targets skills shared across languages, and cross‐linguistic intervention, which explicitly addresses language‐specific features (Kohnert 2010). Spanish–English intervention studies can inform the design of interventions for other English‐plus‐home‐language pairings by identifying broadly adaptable components (e.g., vocabulary depth, narrative structure, caregiver involvement, dialogic interaction, culturally responsive practices) and those requiring language‐specific modification (e.g., morphology, phonology, syntax, orthography, cognates).

DLLs

Children learning two languages simultaneously are classified as simultaneous bilinguals, whereas those acquiring a second language after an established first are sequential bilinguals (De Houwer 1995). In this scoping review, the term DLLs is used to describe children who speak more than one language as a result of growing up in a bilingual environment or whose home language is the minority language in their country of residence, while English is the primary, dominant language (Durán et al. 2016; Guiberson and Ferris 2019). Language pairing refers to the combination of two specific languages that an individual uses or is exposed to: a first language (L1), often the home language, heritage language, and a second language (L2), often the community language, school language, or instructional language. For example, in Australia, one common language pairing for DLLs is Mandarin (L1) and English (L2). In this study, L1 refers to the child's home language (first language other than English), and L2 refers to English. The studies reviewed may include simultaneous bilinguals, sequential bilinguals, or both.

Espinosa (2015) argued that bilingualism contributes positively to social skills, language development, and cognitive growth. For children from diverse backgrounds, the home language is often deeply tied to family relationships, emotions, and cultural identity. Maintaining L1 supports intergenerational relationships (particularly with elders who speak only the heritage language), and it supports participation in global communities (Hay et al. 2021). Over time, L1 maintenance underpins dual language development, strengthening the rationale for programs targeting L1 skills (Dam et al. 2020). Importantly, maintaining the L1 does not hinder English (L2) learning but rather strengthens overall linguistic and cognitive development (Genesee 2009).

Language Impairment in Bilingual Children

Specific language impairment (SLI) is a genetically complex disorder affecting children's spoken language (Stavrakaki 2015), previously a diagnosis for children with significant language difficulties, with otherwise normal development. In this scoping review, children with or at risk of LI include those with DLD, difficulties or delays in language and reading comprehension, and reading disabilities.

A major challenge for DLLs aged 3–9 years is the accurate identification of LI, which is frequently complicated by the overlap between disorder and typical bilingual development. Few assessments effectively distinguish LI from typical bilingual patterns (Kan et al. 2020). Bilingual language development often presents uneven patterns, such as mixing both languages, uneven vocabulary use across the two languages, or slower progress in the school L2 language. These are typical bilingual patterns but are frequently misidentified as LI (Peña et al. 2020). This contributes to both over and under‐identification of language‐related disorders. A recent study by Ortiz and Chow (2026) also found that many DLLs with DLD are identified later than their monolingual peers, often only after DLLs struggle with academic learning and rates of LI identification increase as children progress through the early elementary grades. Key features of typical bilingual development (i.e., cross‐linguistic transfer, temporary deceleration) are often misconstrued as indicators of LI, complicating identification (Marecka et al. 2020). Although bilingualism and DLD can both involve reduced lexical retrieval, bilingualism itself does not exacerbate DLD‐related difficulties (Degani et al. 2019). Evidence also suggests that bilingual children typically do not show phonological deceleration in their L1, particularly after the age of 4.7 years, and phonological transfers following typical developmental patterns. Bilingualism can contribute to both overdiagnosis of typical bilingual development as a disorder and underdiagnosis and neglect of bilingual children with genuine difficulties in language development (Marecka et al. 2020).

Bilingual children with LI typically show impairments in both L1 and L2, with severity varying across languages (Kohnert 2010). Distributed exposure to L1 and L2 supports learning more effectively than L2‐only exposure (Kaushanskaya 2023), and DLLs at risk often demonstrate weaker vocabulary in both languages than typically developing peers (Kan et al. 2020). Altman et al. (2024) found that 5‐6‐year‐old bilingual children with DLD produced fewer word types and graphic symbols than typically developing peers, and experienced difficulty articulating and using emotional language. Interestingly, inputting code‐switching practices into interventions that mirror natural code‐switching scenarios can facilitate children's word learning (Kaushanskaya 2023).

The Importance of Home Language and Culture

Cultural characteristics in language interventions include the values, practices, languages, communication styles, and caregiving approaches that shape culturally responsive support for children's learning and development. L1 and the sociocultural learning context shape how educators and Speech‐language pathologists (SLPs) support DLLs’ children's L1 learning, and how the L1 influences L2. Linguistically and culturally grounded measures are essential for determining whether young DLLs have LI (Kan et al. 2020). A systematic review by Larson et al. (2020) reported that culturally adapted interventions incorporating community values and practices across all stages are effective. Furthermore, community involvement, such as consulting families and cultural representatives during intervention design, can further enhance culturally responsive support for DLL programs. In summary, Larson et al. (2020) found that interventions considered both linguistically and culturally responsive (24%, n = 10) were effective in 100% of cases, regardless of the language measured (English and L1). Linguistically responsive and culturally appropriate interventions can enhance DLLs’ identities and family connections (Guiberson and Ferris 2019; Larson et al. 2020). McLeod et al. (2023) argued that interventions can strengthen L1 maintenance while supporting L2 (English) development and are particularly successful when the interventions align with L1 practices. For example, mothers’ speech and interactional engagement with their children at home can have a tremendous impact on their children's language development.

In addition, Sanabria et al. (2023) highlighted that oral fine‐motor abilities correlated with bilingual children's L1 development, both with and without DLD. Stronger speech motor skills predicted better vocabulary and grammar outcomes in the L1 (Spanish), but not in English. This suggests that the L1 environment plays an important role in linking oral motor skills with lexical and grammatical development in young DLLs.

Language Intervention

In this scoping review, language intervention refers to targeted strategies and therapies designed to directly or indirectly support and improve children's language skills.

Early language interventions are critical for young DLLs with LI. Evidence‐based language interventions can improve language and early literacy outcomes for DLLs with or at risk of LI, yet further research is required in this area (Crowe et al. 2021; Durán et al. 2016; Guiberson and Ferris 2019). Mastery of the community language and literacy (L2) is strongly associated with school readiness, social competence and later academic achievement in (Larson et al. 2020). In addition, Pihko et al. (2007) found that bilingual language‐impaired children aged 6–7 years old gained plastic changes in the brain activity of the left and right auditory cortices and improved speech sound discrimination after an 8‐week phonological intervention. Similarly, Ghosh and Banerjee (2025) highlighted the urgent need for early language intervention research that focuses on young DLLs at risk of developmental delays.

Intervention delivery ordinarily involves referral, assessment, and intervention, each with distinct challenges (Verdon et al. 2014). At referral, educators and caregivers may struggle to identify a child's language‐learning needs and appropriate support because early language trajectories are heterogenous and rapidly changing, limiting access to current information (Archibald and Kuyvenhoven 2024; Verdon et al. 2014). Assessment commonly targets the core language skills of phonology, vocabulary, morphosyntax, pragmatics, narrative skills and literacy (Nair et al. 2023), however, multilingual children's language development is frequently over or under‐diagnosed as disorders due to cultural and linguistic variations (Archibald and Kuyvenhoven 2024; Crowe et al. 2021). For example, emergent bilingual children in New York were identified with LI at twice the rate of monolingual English‐speaking peers (Peña et al. 2023). Comprehensive and culturally responsive assessments are in increasing demand (Crowe et al. 2021). During intervention, key development goals include a focus on receptive and expressive language, narrative skills, and phonological awareness, which are associated with early literacy learning (Crowe et al. 2021; Larson et al. 2020). At the intervention stage, SLPs and DLLs face the challenge of the mismatches between their own languages and those spoken by the children and their families (Verdon et al. 2014). There is a persistent shortage of SLPs who are proficient in two or more languages (Thordardottir 2010). Many educators and SLPs lack bilingual proficiency and knowledge of children's L1, which can hinder engagement with families and the provision of linguistically and culturally responsive services.

Theoretical Framework

This scoping review is about bilingual language learning and resonates with the systems framework of bilingual language acquisition (Byers‐Heinlein et al. 2025). This framework conceptualizes bilingual language acquisition as the result of dynamic and ongoing interactions across four nested levels: individual factors (e.g., cognitive and maturational factors), language experience and input, interpersonal contexts (family, intervention, and culturally responsive practices), and broader societal influences, these interactions unfold over developmental time (Byers‐Heinlein et al. 2025). The purpose of this scoping review was to assess the scope, diversity, and nature of existing research on evidence‐based early interventions for young DLLs (aged 3 to 9) with, or at risk of LI in order to understand the cultural adaptations of these interventions and Cross‐language transfer (CLT) in bilingual language development. We aimed to clarify key concepts and map intervention characteristics while charting and synthesising the evidence, rather than evaluating study quality (Arksey and O'Malley 2005; Peters et al. 2024).

The Research Questions

This scoping review followed the JBI methodology for scoping reviews (Aromataris et al. 2024; Peters et al. 2024). The JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis offers internationally recognised best‐practice guidance for conducting scoping reviews. It recommends using the PCC framework (Population, Concept, and Context) to clearly develop the research question for a scoping review. The main research question was: What evidence‐based language interventions currently exist for young DLLs with or at risk of language impairment (LI)? Three sub‐questions further refined the scope of inquiry:

Q1:What effective language interventions have been examined to support the language development of bilingual children aged 3 to 9 years with or at risk of language impairment (LI)?

Methods

The scoping review is a valid methodology for exploring literature in emergent fields and public health interventions as a basis for further research (Hanneke et al. 2017; Peters et al. 2021). At the time of this study, a preliminary search revealed that the scope of language interventions based on language pairings of MAVCI (L1) and English (L2) had not been previously investigated or reported by scholars. The Arksey and O'Malley scoping review framework (Arksey and O'Malley 2005) was adopted, which includes five stages: (1) defining the research question, (2) identifying relevant literature, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting results. This was integrated with the updated framework by Peters et al. (2021) and reported in accordance with PRISMA‐ScR (Tricco et al. 2018). The PRISMA‐ScR Checklist is recorded in Supplemental Materials 2.1. This scoping review protocol was developed in Sep 2024 and officially registered with the Open Science Framework on Sep 8, 2025. The protocol and its associated project are openly accessible and indexed within the Open Science Framework Registries without an embargo.

Inclusion Criteria

The eligibility criteria were guided by JBI's Population, Concept, and Context framework, and Types of evidence sources for scoping review (Peters et al. 2024). Studies were included if: (a) (Population) participants were DLLs aged 3–9 years with, or at risk of LI, for all language pairings that include English served as the community language and a non‐English language served as the home language; (b) (Concepts) studies focused on evidence‐based language interventions and early interventions; (c) (Context) studies were conducted in any country; (d) (Types of evidence sources) this scoping review included peer‐reviewed studies retrieved from four databases: Education Resources Information Centre (ERIC) via EBSCOhost, Education Source via EBSCOhost, Education Database via ProQuest, and Scopus; (e) (Additional criteria) studies were published from January 2012 to January 2025 and studies were published in English. To elaborate, although initially limited to the past decade, studies from 2012–2013 were retained due to the reduced volume of intervention studies reported during the Covid‐19 pandemic period (2020–2022); the final window was January 2012 to January 2025.

The study participants were limited to DLLs in the early childhood to early primary school period (ages 3–9 years), reflecting a fundamental phase of language development. However, studies were excluded if: (a) they involved children with autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, hearing impairment, cerebral palsy, or Down syndrome, to minimize confounding influences on language outcomes; (b) they were editorials, commentaries, letters, and conference abstracts.

Search Strategy

The screening process was undertaken by two reviewers, and the systematic review process was overseen by a scholarly service librarian with expertise in scoping reviews. The search terms were applied using ERIC (via EBSCOhost), which served as the primary database. Final searches in the ERIC (via EBSCOhost) database were completed on February 13th, 2025. The same search terms were applied to searches in the other three databases.

Screening was managed in Covidence (covidence.org). In total, of the 1294 records imported into Covidence via Zotero, 234 duplicates were removed. Following title and abstract screening of 1060 articles, and discussion of discrepancies, 29 articles were retained for full‐text screening. Full texts of the 29 eligible articles were uploaded and independently screened using Covidence. The process was iterative rather than linear, with two reviewers deliberately and repeatedly, reflecting on each step to ensure comprehensive coverage (Arksey and O'Malley 2005).

Data Extraction

Study selection and data extraction were conducted independently by two reviewers. Any discrepancies were resolved through regular discussions and consensus, with resolutions clearly documented. Consistency was ensured through this independent dual charting process and iterative consensus meetings.

A standardized data charting form was developed in Microsoft Word, based on the research sub‐research questions and refined during the process. Data charting followed a predefined set of variables, including bibliographic details (author and year), study aims, methodology and design, participant characteristics, intervention characteristics, and outcomes.

The data was summarized through a descriptive synthesis approach and organised into five tables to clarify and provide an Overview of the Studies (Table 2), details about the study participants (Table 3), and lastly, specific characteristics of the interventions (Tables 4, 5 and 6). Results

This review identified 29 studies that met the eligibility criteria. These interventions were categorized into three types by focussing on the delivery agent; how the intervention was delivered: caregiver‐based interventions (n = 4), Information and Communication Technology (ICT)‐based interventions (n = 6), and direct child‐based language interventions (n = 15). Ten studies reported cultural considerations in interventions, and 13 studies found that CLT occurred, while Bedore et al. (2020) and Fiestas et al. (2021) reported evidence of both cultural considerations and CLT.

Study Designs of Selected Studies

As listed in Table 2, the identified types of study designs were qualitative designs (n = 2), quantitative designs (n = 21), and systematic reviews and literature reviews (n = 6). Both qualitative studies were single participant case studies. Among the 21 quantitative studies, seven were single case studies, including multiple baseline designs and a single case experimental design. There were six randomized controlled trials. Eight studies used quasi‐experimental and other designs, including pre‐ and post‐designs, within‐subject designs, and comparisons between‐groups and school designs. The review studies are detailed at the bottom of Table 3, consisting of four systematic reviews and two literature reviews.

Participant Characteristics

Participant details of the selected studies are presented in Table 3. Apart from the participants in the included literature reviews (n = 6), the remaining 23 articles described a total of 1394 participants. Of the included literature reviews, only Harvey et al. (2021) reported 681 participants, while the other five did not specify the number of participants. The participants in these studies were predominantly Hispanic or Latinx.

Across the selected studies, participants were children aged from 2 years 8 months, to 13 years 8 months, with the majority of studies focusing on preschool and early primary school aged children (most commonly 3–5 years and 3–6 years). Several studies included children aged 4–7 years and 6–8 years, while a few extended into middle childhood (7–11 years) or early adolescence (up to 8–13 years). Participants in the six literature reviews were young children with ages ranging from birth to 12 years 5 months.

Eighteen articles included participants with LI. Four articles reported participants at risk of LI. Seven articles included participants with conditions that may place them at risk of LI, such as language delay and risks for comprehension, learning, language and communication, and reading difficulties.

Overview of the Interventions

Question 1: What effective language interventions have been examined to support the language development of bilingual children aged 3 to 9 with, or at risk of language impairment?

Overview of the Interventions

Details of each intervention are reported in Table 2. Caregiver‐based interventions were usually conducted at home (n = 4), whereas other types of interventions were conducted at school (n = 17) or in a clinic (n = 1). Those interventions lasted 1–14 weeks and were delivered in 2–48 sessions. The treatment sessions individually lasted 15–75 min.

Similar to previous language intervention studies, 72% of the selected studies (21 of 29) reported Spanish as participants’ L1. Other L1 included Somali, Dutch, Bulgarian and Cypriot Greek.

Regarding the language of instruction, six studies used Spanish (L1) only, seven studies implemented bilingual treatment, and nine studies investigated interventions conducted in English (L2) only. Only four studies targeted participants’ L1, while 12 studies targeted both L1 and L2, including four systematic reviews. Additionally, nine studies examined intervention outcomes in L2.

Types of Intervention

Four articles explored caregiver‐based intervention(s), all of them were conducted in the United States with Spanish‐speaking mothers and showed positive effects on supporting their children's L1 (Spanish) development. Caregiver‐based intervention(s) studies incorporated general strategies, language‐based intervention model, The Teach‐Model‐Coach‐Review instructional approach, and three language‐facilitation strategies, all of which demonstrated positive results to support Hispanic families and their bilingual children.

Six ICT‐based intervention(s) studies demonstrated efficacy in enhancing specific language skills, mostly among older participants aged over 6 years. Only one study examined an ICT‐based caregiver intervention that included children aged 3–5 years (Rivera Pérez et al. 2022), and one study was classified as an ICT‐based child language intervention (Pham et al. 2015). The intervention called, Enhanced Moved by Reading to Accelerate Comprehension in English (EMBRACE) was administered in English L2 and targeted English language skills. EMBRACE appeared to be more effective for DLLs with lower initial English reading abilities (decoding and comprehension) than for those with stronger reading skills (Sanabria et al. 2022).

Vocabulary interventions (n = 7) and narrative interventions (n = 3) accounted for a large proportion of direct child‐based language intervention(s) (n = 15). Vocabulary interventions included cognate‐based interventions (n = 4), Language and Literacy Together (n = 2) and word learning intervention (n = 1). These four studies on cognate‐based interventions demonstrated clear CLT (Dam et al. 2020; Kambanaros et al. 2017; Nair et al. 2023; Tribushinina et al. 2023). Fiestas et al. (2021) examined Language and Literacy Together, targeting semantic and narrative skills in Spanish, and found that bilingual children's Spanish narrative comprehension and English narrative production improved after the intervention. An enhanced version of Language and Literacy Together was studied by Bedore et al. (2020), showing that children's productivity in their narratives in Spanish and English increased, as reflected by the mean length of utterances in words, although there was little impact on grammaticality. A literature review of word learning experiments found that code‐switched contexts resembling naturalistic code‐switching behaviours enhanced learning in Spanish‐English bilingual children (Kaushanskaya 2023). Story Champs is a multi‐tiered narrative language intervention program. Both Risueño et al. (2024) and Weddle et al. (2016) described cultural considerations in the Story Champs intervention, which targeted story grammar and storytelling skills, both of which improved after the intervention. Petersen et al. (2016) indicated that typically developing children showed significantly greater cross‐linguistic transfer of causal subordination and story grammar than children with LI after the Story Champs intervention.

Cultural Characteristics

Question 2: What cultural characteristics are incorporated into interventions tailored to children's linguistic and cultural backgrounds?

The term ‘cultural characteristics’ refers to the values, beliefs, worldviews, practices, rituals, languages and communication styles, symbols and materials, and child‐rearing approaches of different cultural groups. Australia's Early Years Learning Framework (Australian Children's Education and Care Quality Authority 2022) emphasizes the importance of educators developing culturally responsive practices to support children's successful lifelong learning.

Ten studies reported culturally responsive interventions, shown in Table 4. Notably, all caregiver‐based interventions incorporated key Hispanic family cultural elements, including Spanish language use, family values, culturally relevant materials and symbols, and child‐rearing practices, to strengthen caregivers’ confidence in implementing language‐support activities at home (Kummerer 2012; Núñez and Hughes 2021; Peredo et al. 2018; Rivera Pérez et al. 2022). For example, Peredo et al. (2018) used the Teach‐Model‐Coach‐Review instructional approach with caregivers, alongside EMT en Español strategies for children. These interventions drew on utilizing familiar household and play‐based materials, such as toys, books, colouring materials, sensory‐play containers, bubbles, balloons, balls, shape sorters, and blocks. Following intervention, caregivers demonstrated increased use of targeted strategies, while children showed growth in spontaneous L1 word production.

Several studies also incorporated Spanish dialects, vocabulary, personal narratives, and cognates to support instruction, including where interventions were delivered primarily in English (Fiestas et al. 2021; Rivera Pérez et al. 2022; Sanabria et al. 2022). Child‐focused interventions further drew on participants’ individual interests and experiences as culturally responsive variables within intervention design (Kambanaros et al. 2017; Restrepo et al. 2013; Risueño et al. 2024; Weddle et al. 2016). More broadly, culturally responsive practices were reflected in efforts to build collaborative relationships with families through values commonly emphasised in Hispanic communities, including respect, relationality, approachability, and personal interest (Kummerer 2012). By incorporating these cultural dimensions, language‐based interventions sought not only to enhance children's language outcomes but also to build Latinx caregivers’ confidence and capacity to generalise language‐support strategies within everyday family routines and practices (Núñez and Hughes 2021).

CLT

Question 3: What evidence of Cross‐language transfer has been reported in these interventions?

CLT is a phenomenon in which skills or knowledge learned in one language transfer and leads to improvements in another language, without direct teaching in that second language. Furthermore, the acquisition of one language having an influence on the acquisition of another is referred to as ‘cross‐linguistic transfer’. This phenomenon is thought to occur because of common underlying cognitive processes associated with language development (Harvey et al. 2021). In the context of intervention, CLT refers to the generalization of treatment gains from the treated to the untreated language (Nair et al. 2023).

Evidence of CLT was identified in 13 studies (see Table 5), including seven child‐based language intervention studies, two ICT‐based intervention studies, three systematic reviews, and one literature review.

Overall, the findings suggest that bilingual or L1‐supported interventions are more likely than L2‐only to promote gains in children's L1, with transfer occurring more consistently from L1 to L2 than from L2 to L1 (Durán et al. 2016; Harvey et al. 2021; Pham et al. 2015). Several studies reported limited or no L1 gains following English‐only intervention among children with LI, although some evidence indicated cross‐language benefits through cognate‐based approaches and treatment of shared linguistic features (Bedore et al. 2020; Dam et al. 2020; Kambanaros et al. 2017; Tribushinina et al. 2023). For example, cognate‐focused interventions were associated with vocabulary gains across languages and, in some cases, maintained effects beyond the intervention period. However, evidence of bidirectional transfer was inconsistent, particularly for children with LI, suggesting that stronger, longer, and more explicitly bilingual interventions, alongside appropriate control groups, may be required to support development across both languages (Bedore et al. 2020).

Studies Related to MAVCI (L1s)

Although the review initially aimed to map interventions involving MAVCI‐English pairings, no eligible studies were identified. Supplemental Material 1.9 listed 22 MAVCI‐related studies (from the 1060 screened) that did not meet the inclusion criteria.

The 22 studies examined Mandarin (n = 2), Vietnamese (n = 3), Cantonese (n = 4), Italian (n = 4) and Arabic (n = 9), focussed on MAVCI language characteristics in typically developing bilingual and monolingual children with, or at risk of LI, instead of interventions. Findings on the language characteristics of children who speak MAVCI, may serve as important indicators for diagnosis and intervention in these populations. Discussion

Types of Interventions

The review of the 29 studies identified three types of interventions for educators and SLPs to support young DLLs with or at risk of LI: caregiver‐based intervention, ICT‐based intervention, and direct child‐based language intervention. Regarding direct child‐based language interventions (see Figure 2), vocabulary interventions and narrative interventions were more frequently investigated and indicated positive effects on children's bilingual language development. Vocabulary interventions included cognate‐based interventions, Language and Literacy Together and word learning interventions. Consistent with previous research (Crowe et al. 2021; Durán et al. 2016), the most commonly studied language pairings were Spanish (L1) and English (L2).

Child‐based language interventions.

Cultural Characteristics

Ten of the 29 studies specifically designed interventions that considered and incorporated cultural characteristics. Among these ten studies, nine were Spanish‐English language pairings. Seven of these nine studies used Spanish (L1) in the intervention: six studies used Spanish directly as the language of instruction (Fiestas et al. 2021; Kummerer 2012; Núñez and Hughes 2021; Peredo et al. 2018; Rivera Pérez et al. 2022; Restrepo et al. 2013), while the study by Sanabria et al. (2022) incorporated Spanish as additional language support. These interventions reported positive outcomes, including improved understanding and confidence among Latinx mothers, increased use of language facilitation strategies, gains in children's Spanish spontaneous words, narrative comprehension and production, vocabulary development (Spanish and conceptual), and CLT.

Risueño et al. (2024) and Weddle et al. (2016) both examined DLLs with Spanish–English language pairings using the Story Champs intervention. Although neither study directly incorporated Spanish (L1) within the intervention, both embedded participants’ personal interests and narratives, an approach that can be viewed as culturally responsive and broadly aligned with Spanish‐speaking children's cultural practices and experiences. Weddle et al. (2016) reported overall positive gains in children's storytelling abilities. In contrast, Risueño et al. (2024) reported more varied outcomes, with most participants demonstrating improvements in story grammar during retell tasks, but showing more limited progress across other language and narrative measures.

In addition, the participants in the study by Kambanaros et al. (2017) were multilingual children whose L1 was Bulgarian and whose dominant language was Greek. The intervention used English cognates and did not involve Spanish at all. Both the native language (Bulgarian) and the dominant language (Greek) benefitted equally from the treatment of cognates in English. Generalization to non‐treatment words was evident, predominantly for English. Therefore, language pairings and features of paired languages are fundamental.

CLT

The majority of studies with CLT occurrence showed positive evidence of improvement in the target languages. Most studies reported observable CLT effects, particularly for vocabulary, cognates, semantics and narrative skills (Castilla‐Earls and Horne 2023; Dam et al. 2020; Danahy Ebert et al. 2014; Fiestas et al. 2021; Kambanaros et al. 2017; Nair et al. 2023; Tribushinina et al. 2023). Cognates play a significant role as evidence‐based facilitators of CLT. Improvements in the targeted language can also extend to the non‐targeted language, particularly when the intervention builds on similarities shared across the two languages. These cognate‐based language interventions promote vocabulary learning and broader skills (e.g., MLU) across languages (Dam et al. 2020; Kambanaros et al. 2017; Nair et al. 2023; Tribushinina et al. 2023). This is further supported by robust research designs: quasi‐experimental designs (Dam et al. 2020; Tribushinina et al. 2023) and systematic review (Nair et al. 2023). The Systematic review by Nair et al. (2023) summarized that CLT was evident mainly for vocabulary. Positive evidence of CLT was found for conditional adverbials and causal subordination (Castilla‐Earls and Horne 2023; Petersen et al. 2016).

Studies with negative or limited findings about CLT were fewer, but still present. Three studies targeted L2 (English) learning, and reported minimal or no transfer from their L2 to L1 (Spanish) (Danahy Ebert et al. 2014; Harvey et al. 2021; Pham et al. 2015), and interventions delivered solely in L2 (English) showed limited or no spontaneous transfer to L1 (Spanish) (Danahy Ebert et al. 2014; Pham et al. 2015). Petersen et al. (2016) concluded that strong CLT from L2 (English) to L1 (Spanish) was observed in typically developing children, but not among those with LI. There was limited evidence of CLT improving morphosyntax and grammatical structures (Bedore et al. 2020; Nair et al. 2023).

Bilingual intervention is generally advantageous for DLLs. Multiple studies in the selected 29 studies demonstrated that approaches incorporating both L1 and L2 promote positive outcomes in both languages, or at least help maintain L1 proficiency while supporting robust gains in L2 (Danahy Ebert et al. 2014; Durán et al. 2016; Harvey et al. 2021; Pham et al. 2015). CLT also tends to be asymmetric, occurring more readily from L1 to L2 than in the reverse direction (Harvey et al. 2021). Consequently, bilingual intervention often yields additive or interactive effects that surpass those observed with L2‐only intervention.

Non‐linguistic cognitive interventions can also support language gains. Targeting attention, processing speed, and other cognitive skills in conjunction with language intervention shows promise for enhancing language outcomes (Danahy Ebert et al. 2014; Nair et al. 2023; Pham et al. 2015).

Children with DLD or LI demonstrated CLT, although often with some constraints. These children can benefit from CLT, particularly through cognate‐based and bilingual interventions. However, their language gains tend to be smaller or less consistent in certain domains compared with those of typically developing children (Bedore et al. 2020; Dam et al. 2020; Petersen et al. 2016). Importantly, DLD does not appear to increase children's vulnerability to any negative effects of bilingual exposure itself (Kaushanskaya 2023).

Interventions Without Cultural or CLT Components

Eight studies did not incorporate explicit cultural characteristics or demonstrate CLT but were included because their interventions were not dependent on specific language pairings and have applicability across diverse language backgrounds.

Although some studies delivered interventions in children's L1 through bilingual delivery approaches (Gutiérrez‐Clellen et al. 2012; Simon‐Cereijido and Gutiérrez‐Clellen 2014), these interventions as a whole, focused on transferable skills such as inferencing (McMaster et al. 2024), vocabulary development (Simon‐Cereijido and Gutiérrez‐Clellen 2014), phonological awareness (Li et al. 2023), narrative organization (Miller et al. 2018), and receptive/expressive language (Lee and Pring 2016), which can yield benefits for multilingual children with LI or at risk, often targeting L2 (English) in linguistically diverse groups.

Notably, two of the studies were review articles; one systematic review and one literature review. The systematic review included additional L1s, such as Vietnamese (5%) and English (2%), although Spanish remained predominant (36%) (Crowe et al. 2021). Despite several high‐quality studies, evidence for specific interventions with preschool multilingual children was limited; only Nuestros Niños met criteria as an evidence‐based practice for early literacy and phonological awareness. The literature review explained reading comprehension strategies that promoted children's reading comprehension skills (Sarisahin 2020).

Practical Implications of This Study

Spanish‐English studies represented the most developed body of intervention research for young DLLs with, or at risk of LI across the set period. The interventions identified, can serve as valuable references for educators and SLPs working with bilingual children, particularly whose language pairings are Spanish and English.

Among the nine studies with cultural characteristics that had Spanish‐English language pairings, cultural characteristics such as Spanish family values, beliefs, practices, rituals, and symbols and materials were employed at different levels (see Table 4). The positive language outcomes observed in interventions incorporating Spanish cultural characteristics provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of linguistically and culturally responsive approaches for young DLLs with or at risk of LI. The findings from studies of Spanish–English bilingual populations further suggest that L1 use may play a critical role in supporting language development. Different language pairings reflect differences in home language and cultural characteristics, and the results therefore vary across language pairs, indicating that the language pairing should be carefully considered in intervention design. Building on this, in the Australian context, agencies such as the Department of Education and the Department of Health and Human Services, and professionals (e.g., SLPs and educators) should prioritize the development of high‐quality, evidence‐based interventions for young DLLs with or at risk of LI, particularly those that account for prevalent language pairings with English in the Australian context. Future research should extend this work by systematically incorporating culturally responsive elements across a wider range of language pairings, including MAVCI and English to better reflect population diversity. Existing syntheses, such as Supplemental Material 1.9 summarizing key concepts from MAVCI‐related studies, may provide a useful foundation for this work. Comparative research across other national contexts would further strengthen understanding how language pairing influences intervention effectiveness.

There is compelling evidence of the positive impact of language interventions incorporating cognates in Spanish–English bilingual populations. When language pairs share cognates, these can be leveraged in intervention design, suggesting that cognate‐based approaches may also be applicable to other language pairs with substantial lexical overlap, such as Italian–English. In addition, further research on CLT is needed to examine how transfer operates between L1 and L2, and to identify the specific types of linguistic transfer that occur in bilingual and multilingual children with LI. Bilingual interventions often produce additive or interactive effects that exceed those achieved through L2‐only interventions. This finding suggests potential benefits of bilingual interventions for MAVCI–English language pairings, and possibly for other languages.

The interventions without cultural or CLT components provided key information to inform future research that some core skill‐based approaches can be universally effective regardless of specific language pairings.

Limitations

Due to restrictions in capacity, this review had some limitations. Four databases were searched for articles written in English only, across January 2012‐January 2025, which may have contributed to the paucity of intervention studies found. Articles were included when participant ages intersected with the inclusion criteria age, possibly reducing the specificity of this scoping review. Furthermore, the included studies varied in intervention type, duration, sample size, and outcome measures, with many focusing only on immediate post‐intervention effects; this heterogeneity limits conclusions about which specific intervention components were most effective and the extent to which findings may be sustained over time. Conversely, these limitations can be considered when designing intervention related research.

Additionally, in relation to the MAVCI languages, it is important to note that this collection of languages was based on data available in 2025, however the next Census will be conducted in 2026 Australia. Future researchers should update language selection criteria using the latest Census data to ensure that intervention priorities reflect contemporary demographic trends.

Conclusion

It is important to support the language learning of young bilingual children with, or at risk of, LI. This scoping review provided an intervention map outlining caregiver‐based, ICT‐based, and direct child‐focused interventions that can support language development, with evidence largely concentrated in Spanish–English contexts. Also, it highlighted the effectiveness of culturally and linguistically responsive language intervention design, bilingual delivery, and cognate use. Advancing research in this area will require future studies across diverse language pairings relevant to multilingual contexts globally, including Australia (MAVCI and English). The interventions identified have practical implications for language policy, teaching, and practice.
