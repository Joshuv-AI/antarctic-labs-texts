Enhancing professional adaptability and clinical soft skills of new clinical pharmacists through a pre-training model in China: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Tianlin Wang, Jinmei Liang, Li Chen, Shu Li, Man Zhu, Yun Cai, Mengli Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile academic groups in China offer one-year standardized training for clinical pharmacists, these programs primarily emphasize clinical knowledge and may not adequately address the lack of job readiness and clinical soft skills among graduates without an academic background in clinical pharmacy.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a new pre-training model, the Clinical Pharmacist Occupational Adaptive Learning (CPOAL) program, in enhancing the professional adaptability and clinical soft skills of newly recruited pharmacists (NRPs) alongside the existing one-year clinical pharmacist training.MethodThe study included 20 NRPs without a clinical pharmacy background who were randomly divided into a CPOAL group and a control group. The CPOAL group underwent a three-month professional adaptability training program before the one-year standardized training. A questionnaire assessing career awareness, insight and independent thinking, cooperation, good character, execution ability, and stress resistance was given to both groups before and after the one-year training to analyze differences within and between the groups. In addition, after the one-year training, seven aspects of clinical soft skills, such as mental status, communication skills, resilience, comprehensive analysis, clinical thinking, patient privacy protection, and compassion for patients, were evaluated by three general medicine experts and three clinical pharmacy experts to determine the impact of CPOAL program on improving NRPs’ soft skills.ResultsCompared to the control group, the CPOAL group exhibited notable enhancements in career awareness, adaptability to management demands, and basic work content adaptability, along with improvements in clinical reasoning and interdisciplinary collaboration abilities. Additionally, the CPOAL group displayed notably superior scores in adaptability, comprehensive analysis, and humanitarian values.ConclusionsCPOAL program serves as a valuable supplement to the existing clinical pharmacy training system, greatly enhancing the professional adaptability and clinical soft skills of NRPs. This pre-training model offers valuable insights for enhancing professional capabilities and improving the quality of medical services, with the potential for widespread adoption and impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07951-9.

Background

Clinical pharmacy academic training in China focuses more on undergraduate education [1]– [2]. As of 2019, 52 Chinese institutions of higher education offered clinical pharmacy programs, typically lasting 4–5 years [3]. Program specialization, duration, and objectives lack uniformity across schools, with a greater emphasis on establishing a comprehensive curriculum system based on theoretical learning capabilities than Western clinical pharmacy education, which mainly aims to cultivate professional clinical pharmacists. At the graduate level, Chinese students’ graduation topics shift more towards basic pharmacy research, resulting in a lack of clinical problem-solving ability and opportunities [4–6].

To ensure an adequate number of appropriately educated and skilled clinical pharmacy professionals in the US, the ACCP has established an industry-recognized list of competencies for clinical pharmacists [7]. In addition to professional knowledge, these competencies include professionalism, patient care, and communication, among others. This complete set of competency statements lays the groundwork for the next step in developing evaluation/assessment tools for clinical pharmacists. In China, the proportion of in-service clinical pharmacists with a clinical pharmacy academic background is low, making it difficult for healthcare organizations to recruit graduates with a clinical pharmacy background. Results from two nationwide survey studies involving 625 and 403 clinical pharmacists, respectively, revealed that the majority of in-service clinical pharmacists are graduates with undergraduate degrees in pharmacy, while those with academic backgrounds in clinical pharmacy account for only 29.76% and 24.81% of the total [8]– [9]. Given that undergraduate pharmacy programs in China are structured around core courses in chemistry and pharmaceutical sciences [10], it is evident that the vast majority of clinical pharmacists lack adequate and systematic learning and training in the work content, job requirements, and competencies essential for clinical pharmacy practice prior to employment [2].

In order to address the significant shortage of clinical pharmacists, academic bodies have recognized the need for specialized training programs. These programs, such as the one-year ‘Standardized Training for Hospital Pharmacists’ or ‘Standardized Training for Clinical Pharmacists [11]– [12],’ aim to quickly bridge the gap in the number of qualified professionals. The standardized training is typically conducted in professional training institutions, such as hospitals, that have the necessary faculty and resources to deliver high-quality training focused on clinical skills. Additionally, these programs include continuous monitoring and evaluation of the trained pharmacists and their respective hospitals to ensure they are prepared for the role of clinical pharmacist. It is recommended that new pharmacists participate in these training programs, as they provide valuable exposure to clinical knowledge for those without an academic background in clinical pharmacy.

The standardized outbound training has provided new pharmacists with a foundational understanding of the work responsibilities of clinical pharmacists, equipping them with essential working methods, basic clinical thinking skills, and partially bridging the gap in clinical medical knowledge. However, upon returning to their respective hospitals after a year, many pharmacists still struggle to efficiently and independently carry out clinical duties. Some even seek to transfer out of the clinical pharmacist role. This challenge can be attributed to two main factors: firstly, a lack of awareness regarding the specific job requirements and career goals of clinical pharmacists, leading to a failure to recognize the core responsibilities of the role. Secondly, difficulties in adapting to the pace, intensity, and management expectations of clinical pharmacists resulting in excessive stress [13]. These issues are further compounded by a lack of understanding of their own non-clinical capabilities, as evidenced by an inability to align with the standardized training due to mismatches in personality, comprehension of the healthcare system’s complexity, patient communication skills, and interdisciplinary collaboration. This mismatch results in a lack of competence, with some pharmacists also demonstrating a deficiency in patient-centered humanitarian values such as compassion and empathy. A study by Xu, et al., confirms that among 403 clinical pharmacists, the average self-rated scores for their cooperation, communication skills, career awareness, and continuous learning ability range from 6.3 to 7.05 points (out of 10), with less than 30% scoring ≥ 8 points in teamwork. Additionally, 93.8% of pharmacists believe their professional knowledge needs to be strengthened, and 80.15% hope to have more training opportunities [9]. This result directly reflects the lack of job readiness and clinical soft skills among clinical pharmacists. We posit that the root cause of these challenges lies in the oversight of selecting and evaluating pharmacists prior to standardized training, leading to a disconnect between their understanding of clinical pharmacist roles and their actual capabilities.

Therefore, the primary objective of this research is to establish a CPOAL program and evaluate its effectiveness in addressing the aforementioned critical issues, with a specific focus on enhancing professional adaptability and clinical soft skills among NRPs without a clinical pharmacy background.

Methods

We conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate the effectiveness of the newly developed CPOAL program, which is designed to provide selective and adaptive pre-training for NRPs prior to their one-year standardized clinical pharmacy training. This program serves as a targeted supplement to the existing standard training curriculum, aiming to address gaps in professional adaptability and clinical soft skills among NRPs without a clinical pharmacy background.

Twenty NRPs without prior experience in clinical pharmacy were included in the study from January 2020 to December 2023. Participants were divided into a CPOAL group and a control group based on their academic qualifications (PhD or below) using a stratified randomised approach. The CPOAL group received a 3-month job-adaptive training for clinical pharmacists prior to their one-year normative training, while the control group underwent a direct one-year standardised training.

The training program of CPOAL group consists of two main stages. The first stage, spanning two weeks, focuses on job awareness training for clinical pharmacists. This includes covering the daily work schedule, personnel management system, basic work content, skill requirements (professional and communication skills), and assessment criteria. The second stage involves teaching basic clinical diagnosis and treatment processes, as well as the use of common medical software in affiliated hospitals. Clinical pharmacist instructors handle topics such as personnel management systems, pharmacy service workstations, medical prescription review, patient monitoring, medication education, pharmacy consultation, and clinic work. Meanwhile, clinician instructors teach basic clinical procedures like clinical room visits, history taking, diagnosis, disease treatment, and follow-up management. Both centralised lectures and on-site teaching are conducted by senior experts in clinical pharmacy or clinical medicine, with on-site teaching taking place in the general medicine wards of the hospital.

The evaluation of NRPs was conducted in two parts. The first part focused on assessing NRPs’ willingness to engage in the clinical pharmacist profession and their adaptability to the management requirements of the position. A Likert 5-point scale questionnaire was designed to evaluate career awareness, insight and independent thinking, cooperative ability, resilience, good character and execution ability. Each dimension included 5 specific questions (see Annex 1 for details), with response options ranging from “Determined to be (will be)” (scored as 5 points) to “Definitely not (won’t)” (scored as 1 point) for most items. For scenario-based questions (e.g., cooperative ability Q1-Q4, execution ability Q2-Q5), options were phrased to reflect varying degrees of behavioral inclination (e.g., “Lend it to him immediately” for cooperative ability Q1, scored as 5 points; “Outright refusal” scored as 1 point), ensuring alignment with the 5-point gradient of agreement or behavioral tendency. All items were reviewed by three clinical pharmacy specialists to ensure content validity and administered to NRPs before and after one year of standardized training. In the second phase, the NRPs were assessed for their clinical soft skills by three general medicine experts and three clinical pharmacy experts, each with over 10 years of experience. They evaluated the NRPs on seven key areas: mental state, communication skills, adaptability, comprehensive analytical skills, clinical reasoning, protection of patient privacy, and compassion for patients (see Annex 2 for details). This evaluation was conducted by observing NRPs independently completing three tasks: pharmacy rounds, patient medication education, and case presentations. The assessment aimed to determine the NRPs’ ability to analyze patients’ medication situations comprehensively and holistically in the context of their conditions, exhibit clinical reasoning and empathy, and demonstrate proficiency in utilizing resources when faced with challenging problems. Pharmacy rounds and patient medication education were conducted in the ward, with the patients of the rounds and medication education selected by the NRPs themselves. The expert panel randomly followed the NRPs to observe and evaluate them on-site within two weeks. Case presentations were completed on a concentrated day, where the NRPs selected their own cases, delivered presentations in the form of slides in sequence, and then were evaluated by the expert panel. Each key area included 5 items out of 5 points, with 1 point deducted for 1 missing item. There were no question-and-answer sessions during the entire evaluation process, and experts submitted their scores after assessing all three tasks. These skills were expected to be evident to some extent in the occupational adaptive learning process, although they were not the primary focus of the one-year standardized training. (Fig 1)

In the initial phase of the study, we collected outcome scores from both the CPOAL group and the control group before and after one year of standardized training. We then utilized independent samples t-tests to compare the score differences between the two groups pre and post one year of standardized training. Depending on the data’s normal distribution, we employed either the paired t-test or the non-parametric Wilcoxon paired rank sum test to assess changes in scores within each group. Moving on to the subsequent phase of evaluation, we collected evaluators’ scores after one year of standardized training for both groups. The reliability of the evaluators was evaluated using the Kendall Concordance Coefficient. We compared score differences between the two groups pre and post one year of standardized training using independent samples t-tests, and paired t-tests for within-group comparisons. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0.

Validity

Our questionnaire and scoring system, developed based on clinical pharmacists’ identified weaknesses, draw on two authoritative competency standards: the ACCP’s six core competencies for clinical pharmacists [7] and the ACGME’s six core competencies for medical residents [14]. We have omitted ‘Medical Knowledge and Pharmacotherapy Knowledge’ from our assessment criteria because the retained dimensions (e.g., Patient Care, Interpersonal and Communication Skills, Professionalism) directly address the identified gaps in professional adaptability and soft skills critical for novice pharmacists’ transition into clinical roles. Hence, the emphasis of evaluation at both stages does not Lie in the mastery of specific medical and pharmaceutical knowledge in NRPs, which fundamentally differs from the assessment standards of a one-year standardized training program. Both assessments have been validated by 5 clinical pharmacists with a Chinese Clinical Pharmacist Educator Certification and over 10 years of clinical practice. For the Likert scale, the pharmacists independently reviewed each question and response option to evaluate content validity: they assessed whether the items accurately reflected the targeted constructs and rated the relevance of each item using a 4-point scale (1 = irrelevant, 4 = highly relevant). Items with an average relevance score < 3 were revised, and the final scale achieved a content validity index of 0.99, indicating high alignment with the evaluated dimensions. For the clinical soft skills evaluation, the pharmacists reviewed the observation criteria for the three tasks (pharmacy rounds, patient medication education, case presentations) and the 7 key areas. Through two rounds of independent review and group discussions, they reached a consensus on 97.1% of the indicators. Only one indicator was disputed, but this did not affect the core assessment logic. A clinical pharmacist suggested specifying “timely response” in adaptability as “response within 5 minutes” for better operability. This validation process ensured the assessments were scientific and well-aligned with newly recruited pharmacists’ job characteristics.

Results

None of the NRPs had academic backgrounds in clinical medicine or clinical pharmacy, nor had they led or participated in clinical research projects During their school years, or had internship experience in healthcare organizations. Following a 2-week job awareness training in the CPOAL group, two pharmacists initially considered withdrawing due to feeling ill-prepared for the job’s demands, including the strict management of clinical pharmacists, extensive job responsibilities, and high assessment standards. To address this, the lead pharmacist held individual discussions to clarify that withdrawal was permissible at any stage without penalty, and their concerns were documented to avoid implicit pressure. After confirming their voluntary consent to continue, they persevered and completed all assessment processes. Their data were retained in the final analysis to avoid selection bias. Prior to the one-year standardized training, there were no significant differences observed between the two groups of NRPs in clinical pharmacists’ career awareness, insight and independent thinking, cooperative ability, resilience, good character and execution ability (Table 2). However, following the one-year standardized training, significant differences emerged in certain topic scores between the two groups. Specifically, individuals who had undergone career-adapted learning displayed significantly higher scores in cooperative ability, resilience, and good character compared to the control group. Notably, the most substantial differences were observed in specific questions: Q2 (p < 0.001) and Q4 (p < 0.05) for cooperative ability. NRPs who had received occupational adaptive learning exhibited greater willingness to exchange or share in scenarios involving conflicts related to time off from work or sharing cutting-edge technology or information, demonstrating a more selfless and cooperative attitude. Similarly, for resilience, significant differences were found in Q2 (p < 0.05), Q4 (p < 0.05), and Q5 (p < 0.01). Moreover, in terms of good character, a statistically significant difference was observed in Q1 (p < 0.001), where NRPs who underwent occupational adaptive learning showed greater readiness to express their views and exhibited more confidence when conflicting with their superiors (Table 3). It is important to note that no statistical differences in scores on these specific questions were evident between the two groups prior to the one-year normative training. After one year of standardized training, significant differences were observed in the mean scores of clinical soft skills across seven areas between two groups of NRPs. The CPOAL group exhibited higher total soft skills scores compared to the control group (187.78 ± 8.57 vs. 171.33 ± 14.10, p = 0.009). Particularly notable differences were found in adaptability, comprehensive analytical skills, and clinical reasoning. While no statistical variance was noted in mental status between the two groups, the CPOAL group scored significantly higher (27.22) than the control group (23.78). In terms of communication skills, patient privacy protection, and compassion for patients, the scores of both groups were similar, with the CPOAL group slightly outperforming the control group (Table 4). The reliability of the scoring by the six experts was assessed using the Kendall Concordance Coefficient, which yielded a value of 0.887. The chi-square value was 79.875 with a significance level of p < 0.001. These results indicate a high level of agreement among the raters and validate the test outcomes.

Discussion

In China, in-service clinical pharmacists have a high proportion of bachelor’s degree holders and a low percentage with clinical pharmacy backgrounds. This is in contrast to the US Pharm.D and UK M.Pharm programs, which have a cohesive teaching structure aimed at cultivating professional clinical pharmacists [15–17]. Survey findings in China indicate that approximately 30% of clinical pharmacists hold postgraduate degrees, with only 10.1% possessing clinical pharmacy degrees [18]. Consequently, there are very few graduates who meet the criteria for employment in healthcare organizations, requiring a combination of clinical pharmacy education and advanced degrees. Due to research demands, many prominent healthcare organizations prefer candidates with extensive education in other pharmacy disciplines, resulting in a limited number of newly hired pharmacists with a clinical pharmacy academic background. This scarcity poses a challenge for the subsequent training of clinical pharmacists.

The six core competency models of ACCP and ACGME have been widely used globally to evaluate clinical pharmacists and medical residents [7, 15]. These models have been widely adopted globally. Competence evaluation is often compared to an iceberg, with ‘knowledge and skills’ representing what is visible above the surface, and vocational adaptability and clinical soft skills representing what lies beneath the surface [19]. The latter serves as the foundation for attaining the former and is a key differentiator between exceptional and mediocre practitioners. To excel as a clinical pharmacist, one must carefully evaluate personal attitudes and interest in pursuing the profession, while also being mindful of potential barriers that may deter one from choosing a clinical path. Surveys conducted among nurses and physicians have confirmed that vocational adaptability and clinical soft skills are key factors influencing career decisions [20]– [21]. The Chinese Hospital Association and the Chinese Medical Association have attempted to address the shortage of clinical pharmacists through programmes like the ‘Standardised Training for Hospital Pharmacists’ or ‘Standardised Training for Clinical Pharmacists’. However, these one-year training programmes have not fully addressed the professional adaptability and clinical soft skills of new pharmacists. Only 18.5% of clinical pharmacists in China chose the profession for personal or voluntary reasons [18]. To tackle this issue, the CPOAL was developed to provide targeted adaptive training for NRPs prior to standardized training, focusing on evaluating its impact on career adaptability and clinical soft skills, and also offering insights into whether this pre-training model can serve as a supplement to China’s current training system. A one-year follow-up evaluation showed a significant improvement in competencies related to cooperativeness, stress tolerance, and character among participating pharmacists. These results are in line with the core competency framework for clinical pharmacists, highlighting the importance of professionalism and communication skills in addition to clinical knowledge. Notably, the design of the CPOAL program did not draw on the training models for nurses, physicians, or clinical pharmacists from other countries, primarily due to the unique educational pathways of current clinical pharmacists in China. Both in China and other countries, nurses and physicians receive systematic clinical professional training during their academic education and internships, equipping them with clear role awareness. In contrast, most NRPs in China are graduates of basic pharmacy programs. They lack formal training in clinical medicine, clinical pharmacy, or clinical internships, resulting in limited understanding of clinical workflows, let al.one the specific roles and responsibilities of clinical pharmacists.

Before entering the second phase of training, two NRPs initially considered withdrawing, indicating that there is a subset of NRPs who may not fully comprehend or feel suitable for the role of clinical pharmacist. To preserve rare samples and reliable study outcomes, the lead pharmacist clarified their right to withdraw voluntarily. After obtaining their voluntary willingness to continue, we retained all NRPs following the initial training phase. Our aim was to observe the changes before and after occupational adaptive learning, both within the two groups and among the NRPs themselves. The study results validated our hypothesis that professional adaptive learning significantly contributes to pharmacists’ improved understanding of the clinical pharmacist role, adaptation to the clinical setting, increased willingness to collaborate, stronger self-confidence, and enhanced emotional regulation. Furthermore, it fostered teamwork, selflessness, optimism, and quicker adjustment to various stresses, thereby aiding pharmacists in better managing the pressures and challenges of clinical work. In terms of clinical soft skills, the group undergoing occupational adaptive learning demonstrated superior resilience, comprehensive analytical ability, and clinical thinking, highlighting the enhancement of NRPs’ ability to react promptly and problem-solve in unexpected scenarios. While there was no notable disparity in mental status scores between the two groups, the average score of the occupational adaptive learning group surpassed that of the control group, suggesting a potential positive impact of pre-training on NRPs’ psychological well-being.

Following the standardised training, a significant decrease in scores related to ‘Insight and independent thinking’ was observed in the CPOAL group. Specifically, there was a notable decline in Q1 ‘Do you ask or question your supervising pharmacist about questions about your work?’ (4.56 ± 0.73 vs. 3.78 ± 0.44, p = 0.01) and Q2 ‘Would you come up with three or more solutions to cure a patient?’ (4.56 ± 0.73 vs. 3.78 ± 0.44, p = 0.23). Similarly, in the control group, the ‘Resilience’ dimension showed significant decreases in Q4 ‘When feelings of low self-esteem arise due to the loss of a rescue job, you will’ (3.67 ± 0.71 vs. 2.67 ± 1.00, p = 0.001) and Q5 ‘When difficulties fall in your lap, you tend to’ (4.44 ± 0.73 vs. 3.33 ± 0.50, p < 0.009) after standardized training. Additionally, scores for Q2 (4.11 ± 0.78 vs. 3.56 ± 0.88 p = 0.32) and Q3 (4.11 ± 0.33 vs. 3.56 ± 0.88, p = 0.14) in this dimension, though not statistically significant, also trended downward. Interviews revealed that post-adaptive training, the CPOAL group exhibited a heightened awareness of the intricacies and challenges inherent in clinical work. This newfound understanding led them to consider factors such as timing and location when engaging with their supervising pharmacist, aspects not previously captured in our assessment. Despite undergoing a year of standardised training, the CPOAL group acknowledged their ongoing learning journey and the complexity of supporting rational treatment decisions in clinical practice. Consequently, they demonstrated a more thoughtful and cautious approach, resulting in a reduction in their questioning and initiative. In contrast, the control group indicated that, having entered standardized training directly without prior adaptive training for clinical pharmacy roles, they were more prone to frustration and self-reproach when facing difficulties in clinical practice, particularly in cases of rescue failure, and even questioned their suitability for the role. This phenomenon stems not only from the clinical challenges themselves but also from amplified anxiety about potential inadequacies in their skills. By comparison, the CPOAL group, having established foundational confidence through pre-training adaptation, could more clearly distinguish between “skill deficits” and “personal incompetence,” thereby demonstrating stronger psychological adjustment capabilities in similar situations. Interviews with some NRPs revealed that having prior knowledge of the management system, workflow, and work schedule is crucial for adjusting mental state, reducing stress, and enhancing mood. This, in turn, leads to stronger clinical thinking and humanitarianism in their work. Essentially, pharmacists who have received professional adaptive learning and have chosen to pursue a career as a clinical pharmacist tend to be happier and more relaxed. This not only boosts job satisfaction and patient care quality among pharmacists, but also offers valuable insights for healthcare organizations when selecting and training clinical pharmacists.

Despite the three-year duration of the study, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the focus was predominantly on short-term training outcomes, leaving the long-term impact on career development ambiguous. Although the sample of this study was derived from a single center, we believe the CPOAL program is likely applicable to most regions in China, as the core challenges it addresses - namely, NRPs’ extremely limited clinical background and difficulty in defining their professional roles - are prevalent across China [18]. The CPOAL program can be extended to countries or regions where clinical pharmacy is also an emerging discipline; incorporating training in career adaptability and clinical soft skills prior to professional skills development clearly offers added value. Conversely, in nations with mature clinical pharmacy education systems, the model can be adapted as a “role-specific refinement module” in clinical pharmacist training. Future research should expand the sample size and conduct extended follow-up to validate enduring effects and the wide-ranging relevance of the pre-training framework. In order to enhance training for clinical pharmacists, it is crucial to integrate occupational adaptive learning with other educational methods such as simulation training, case studies, and interdisciplinary collaboration. These approaches can bolster pharmacists’ comprehension of the clinical pharmacist role and improve their clinical soft skills.

Conclusions

This exploratory study in China is the first to investigate the complementary nature of the current training model for clinical pharmacists. It highlights the significance of soft skills and professional adaptability alongside professional knowledge. These findings supplement the existing training framework, provide replicable insights, and contribute to the ongoing refinement of clinical pharmacist education and training model in China.
