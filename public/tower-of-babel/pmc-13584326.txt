Chest Circumference, Stature and Body Mass Index as Determinants of 1918 Influenza Severity
Authors: Lauren E Steele, Maria Bekker‐Nielsen Dunbar, Taylor P van Doren, Lauren Church, Nathalie A J Verzele, Melanie Wu, Isabelle Hocking, Ellesandra C Noye, Jane E Sinclair, Kyle L Macauslane, Katina D Hulme, Conor J Bloxham, Keng Yih Chew, Georgina McCallum, Megan Airey, Sarah Shea, Agnes Carolin, Margarida Pereira, Svenn‐Erik Mamelund
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAnthropometric traits are established determinants of chronic disease, yet their role in shaping infectious disease outcomes during historical pandemics remains underexplored. During the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic, obesity emerged as a novel, independent risk factor for severe illness, raising questions about whether physique and nutritional state have consistently influenced pandemic outcomes across history.MethodsWe present the first large‐scale, systematic analysis of body size and influenza severity during the 1918 pandemic, using a newly constructed and validated cohort of World War 1 service men from the Australian Imperial Force (AIF), Canadian Expeditionary Force and New Zealand Expeditionary Force (N = 1444).ResultsMultivariable regression analyses revealed that larger chest circumference was associated with reduced odds of death (odds ratio [OR] 0.90 per 1‐cm increase, 95% CI 0.81–0.99). Body mass index (BMI) showed a non‐linear association with mortality, with BMI values below 22 kg/m2 significantly associated with lower odds of death. Length of hospital stay was shorter among members of the AIF (rate ratio [RR] 0.61, 95% CI 0.54–0.70), and increasing age and height exhibited non‐linear associations with longer hospitalisation. BMI was not associated with length of hospitalisation.ConclusionAnthropometric measures were associated with influenza outcomes during the 1918 pandemic, although effects were context dependent. These results highlight the continued need to integrate anthropometric and nutritional factors into modern pandemic preparedness frameworks, particularly as populations worldwide continue to face overlapping burdens of undernutrition and obesity.Keywords: 1918 influenza pandemic, anthropometry, body mass index, historical pandemics, military cohorts

Introduction

Influenza A viruses are major human pathogens, causing recurrent seasonal epidemics and occasional pandemics [1]. Four pandemics have occurred since 1900 (1918, 1957, 1968 and 2009), each differing in pathogenesis, mortality and epidemiology [2, 3, 4, 5]. Obesity emerged as an independent host risk factor during the 2009 pandemic [6], highlighting the potential relationship between anthropometric characteristics and influenza outcomes. Whether such associations existed in earlier pandemics or were unique to modern contexts of widespread obesity remains unclear.

The relationship between body mass and respiratory infection is complex. In community‐acquired pneumonia, individuals with obesity may exhibit improved short‐term survival despite higher rates of hospitalisation and comorbidity, in a phenomenon known as the ‘obesity paradox’ [7]. Secondary bacterial pneumonia was a major contributor to pandemic influenza mortality [8, 9, 10], including in 1918 [11], suggesting that excess body mass may have context‐dependent effects. Associations between anthropometric traits and pandemic influenza severity cannot therefore be assumed and require empirical investigation across historical and epidemiological contexts.

Research has centred on the 1918 and 2009 pandemics. The 1918 pandemic caused unprecedented mortality and generated extensive historical records [2, 12, 13]. In contrast, the 2009 pandemic occurred amid advances in molecular virology, sequencing and bioinformatics, allowing detailed study of viral dynamics [14], host immune responses [15] and epidemiology [16] in real time. Knowledge of obesity and pandemic influenza mainly derives from contemporary methods applied during the 2009 pandemic [17, 18], leaving gaps regarding historical patterns of body size and outcomes. Understanding whether anthropometric risk factors were consistent across pandemics is particularly relevant as global nutrition shifts, with undernutrition and overnutrition shaping population health risks [19].

Anthropometric measures provide indirect indicators of nutritional status [20], capturing long‐term nutritional, developmental and socio‐economic exposures that influence host resilience to infection. Undernutrition may manifest as wasting, stunting or being underweight [21], whereas overnutrition commonly manifests as obesity [22], typically defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2 [23]. BMI remains a widely used, though imperfect, population‐level measure [24]; height provides a proxy for early‐life nutrition and stunting [25, 26]; and chest circumference has historically assessed physique, lung capacity and overall health [27]. Waist circumference, widely used today as a marker of central adiposity [28], was rarely recorded in 20th‐century medical examinations and is largely absent from historical conscription records. Together, these anthropometric markers provide complementary perspectives on nutritional status and health risks.

Few historical studies have examined anthropometric traits in relation to 1918 influenza mortality. Analyses of New Zealand soldiers [29] and Alaskan civilians [30] reported isolated associations with chest circumference or BMI but were limited by sample size, cross‐sectional data or focus on individual measures. The overlap of the 1918 pandemic with World War 1 (WW1) produced rich, standardised military records, enabling detailed longitudinal analyses of height, BMI and chest circumference.

WW1 military cohorts comprised relatively healthy young adult men who underwent standardised medical assessments [31, 32]. Soldiers were predominantly 20–40 years old, an age group disproportionately affected during the pandemic [33], and experienced broadly similar exposure risks and treatment options [34, 35]. These features make military records uniquely valuable for assessing how anthropometric traits influenced influenza outcomes under comparable conditions, offering insights not available from civilian sources.

Considering the evidence linking both undernutrition and overnutrition to adverse outcomes following respiratory infections, we hypothesise that anthropometric markers reflecting extremes of body size would be associated with increased risk of severe influenza outcomes during the 1918 pandemic. Given the role of secondary bacterial pneumonia, higher BMI might also be associated with reduced disease severity, consistent with context‐dependent effects observed in other respiratory infections.

Here, we present the first systematic, large‐scale, longitudinal analysis of anthropometric characteristics as host risk markers for 1918 pandemic influenza, using a validated database of Australian Imperial Force (AIF), Canadian Expeditionary Force (CEF) and New Zealand Expeditionary Force (NZEF) military records from WW1. We identify evidence that chest size, height and BMI were associated with pandemic influenza severity in 1918.

Methods

General

All abbreviations in AIF, NZEF and CEF records were written in full following the Australian War Memorial glossary (https://www.awm.gov.au/learn/glossary). Population denominators were determined from the 1911 census or the closest preceding census (Data S1). Pandemic influenza‐like illness (ILI) was defined as any ILI recorded on or after 1 April 1918. Case inclusion had no fixed calendar end date; eligible soldiers had to experience their first ILI hospitalisation within 180 days of enlistment, ensuring BMI measurements at enlistment reflected their health status at the time of illness. Diagnoses classified as ILI included influenza, bronchitis, bronchopneumonia, la grippe, pleurisy and related terms (Data S2).

AIF

AIF soldiers were identified from the AIF Project (https://aif.adfa.edu.au/aif/) with records for ‘effective abroad’, ‘died of disease’, ‘discharged’ and a random sample of ‘returned to Australia’ soldiers (N = 86,494). Soldiers enlisting after October 1917 were retained (n = 4906) to ensure that ILI hospitalisation occurred within 6 months of enlistment. Personnel records were obtained from the National Archives of Australia (https://www.naa.gov.au/). Soldiers lacking weight at enlistment, without pandemic ILI hospitalisation before discharge or death or with first ILI hospitalisation over 180 days post‐enlistment were excluded, leaving n = 651 eligible (Figure 1).

Inclusion and screening of WW1 military records. Flow diagram showing records screened, excluded and retained for the AIF, CEF and NZEF. Included records had enlistment after October 1917, body weight and pandemic ILI within 180 days of enlistment. Final pre‐cleaning cohorts: 651 AIF, 783 CEF and 10 NZEF (N = 1444).

Demographics, enlistment data and anthropometrics were extracted from Attestation Papers, Medical History forms and Certificates of List Discharge, using the first written instance of each measurement. Height, weight and chest circumference were recorded from enlistment and discharge documents. Hospitalisation data, including admission/discharge dates, diagnoses and outcomes, were abstracted from Statement of Service, Medical History Table II and other related forms. Cause and date of death were obtained from all available archival sources. Full details including forms, variable definitions and transcription criteria are available in Data S3.

CEF

CEF hospitalisations were identified by screening district‐level admission and discharge books from Ontario (N ≈ 100,000 entries) for ILI from April 1918 onwards (n = 4572) and then cross‐referenced with personnel records at Library and Archives Canada (https://recherche‐collection‐search.bac‐lac.gc.ca/eng/help/pffww), yielding n = 2427 records. Records were excluded using the same criteria as the AIF, leaving n = 783 (Figure 1).

Demographics and enlistment information were obtained from Attestation Papers, Discharge Certificates and supplementary forms. Height, weight and chest circumference were obtained from Medical History Sheets at enlistment and discharge. Hospitalisations, outcomes and cause of death were abstracted from Casualty, Medical History and Clinical Chart forms using identical definitions to the AIF. Full details are available in Data S4.

NZEF

NZEF soldiers were identified from the NZEF Project (https://nzef.adfa.edu.au/search) by searching for individuals ‘effective abroad’, ‘died of disease’, ‘returned to New Zealand’ and ‘discharged’ (N = 3346). Soldiers enlisting from October 1917 onwards were retained (n = 512). Personnel records were obtained from Archives New Zealand (https://www.archives.govt.nz/). Exclusion criteria were the same as for the AIF, leaving n = 10 eligible (Figure 1).

Demographic and anthropometric data were recorded from the History Sheet, Attestation for General Service, Medical Examination and Medical History forms. Height, weight and chest circumference at enlistment and discharge were recorded, along with hospitalisation dates, diagnoses, outcomes and death information using the same criteria as the AIF and CEF. Full details are available in Data S5.

Data Handling

Height and weight recorded at enlistment were used to calculate BMI (kg/m2), and chest circumference was converted from inches to centimetres. Terminology for paired chest measurements varied across forces; values were harmonised as relaxed and expanded measurements, although their precise equivalence cannot be confirmed. Military units were grouped into functional categories as previously described [35], ranks collapsed into broad levels [36] and country of birth aggregated by continent. Place of birth for all individuals was classified as rural or urban using the Australian Rural, Remote and Metropolitan Areas Classification 1991 Census Edition (1994), with 10,000 inhabitants as the cut‐off. Civilian occupation at enlistment was coded to the Cambridge Social Interaction and Stratification (CAMSIS) scale. Categories with fewer than 20 observations were treated as missing for selected variables (force: NZEF; rank: junior/non‐commissioned/senior officer; unit: flying corps; and birth continent: Asia/Africa).

The database underwent systematic quality control: Duplicate entries and missing values were removed, and anomalies were identified. One record was excluded due to an implausible BMI. Transcription accuracy was assessed by re‐transcribing 100 randomly sampled records, yielding an estimated error rate of 0.2%, indicating minimal transcription error. Full details on variable derivation, coding and selection are provided in Data S6–S11.

Results

The final analytical cohort after cleaning and quality control comprised 1015 military personnel (mean age 23.6, standard deviation [SD] 5.8; mean BMI 21.3 kg/m2, SD 2.2; Table 1). Most served in the Canadian (62.3%) or Australian (36.9%) forces, primarily as infantrymen (61.7%), and nearly all entered at the enlisted rank (98.1%) (Table 1). Socio‐economic position, measured using CAMSIS, ranged from 38.6 to 99.0. During service, individuals experienced a mean of 1.7 hospitalisations (SD 1.0), with an average stay of 20.8 days (SD 26.9) (Table 1). Death during first pandemic ILI hospitalisation occurred in 54 individuals (5.3%) (Table 1). Key demographic and anthropometric distributions are presented in Data S12.

Multivariable logistic regression revealed that larger relaxed chest circumference was associated with reduced odds of death, with each 1‐cm increase corresponding to an odds ratio (OR) of 0.90 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.81–0.99, p = 0.037), indicating a 10% decrease in odds of death per 1‐cm increase in chest circumference (Figure 2A; Data S13). Unit type strongly predicted mortality, with service and support units exhibiting the highest risk (OR 8.89, 95% CI 3.71–21.28, p < 0.001), and railway, transport and forestry units were also associated with increased odds of death (OR 4.37, 95% CI 1.06–17.95, p = 0.041) (Figure 2A; Data S13). No clear associations were observed for age (OR 1.04 per year, 95% CI 0.98–1.09, p = 0.16), height (OR 1.7, 95% CI 0.01–363.15, p = 0.85) or force (AIF vs. CEF OR 1.21, 95% CI 0.52–2.79, p = 0.66) (Figure 2A; Data S13). CAMSIS showed weak evidence of association with mortality (OR 0.97 per unit, 95% CI 0.94–1.00, p = 0.063) (Figure 2A; Data S13). BMI demonstrated a non‐linear association with mortality risk (Figure 2B). Values below the reference of 22 kg/m2 were protective (OR < 1), whereas higher BMI values trended towards increased odds of death, though CIs widened and did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2B). ORs for medical and nursing, and light horse units were highly imprecise due to few deaths; estimates are retained but should be interpreted cautiously.

Analyses of ΔBMI across service revealed no difference between individuals hospitalised for pandemic ILI only and those with additional non‐pandemic infectious hospitalisations (Wilcoxon W = 27,229, p = 0.725) (Figure 4A). Discussion

This work examined whether anthropometric characteristics at enlistment were associated with severe influenza outcomes during the 1918 pandemic in WW1 military cohorts. Using longitudinal, individual‐level records, we identified associations between specific anthropometric measures and risk of death or prolonged hospitalisation during first pandemic ILI admission. Larger relaxed chest circumference was associated with reduced odds of death, BMI demonstrated a non‐linear relationship with mortality risk and unit type significantly predicted outcomes. These findings suggest that anthropometric measurements, as proxies for physique and nutritional status, may have influenced 1918 pandemic influenza severity, complementing literature linking age [37], comorbidities [38], socio‐economic status [39] and ethnicity [40] to outcomes.

Analyses of chest circumference, stature and BMI revealed distinct associations. Larger relaxed chest circumference consistently predicted lower odds of death, contrasting with Summers et al. (2014), who reported increased risk with maximum chest circumference [29]. Lung volumes scale with stature, and chest girth predicts vital capacity [41]; however, the relationship between historical chest measurements and respiratory capacity remains uncertain. Summers et al. (2014) used a case–control design comparing influenza deaths with general military controls, included NZEF personnel in the Northern Hemisphere with substantially broader durations of military service and analysed chest circumference categorically [29], whereas the present cohort comprised soldiers hospitalised with pandemic ILI within 180 days of enlistment and chest circumference was modelled continuously. Service duration may be particularly relevant, with longer service associated with substantially lower influenza mortality in the AIF [35] but increased mortality in the NZEF cohort [29], suggesting that service‐related effects may be complex and inconsistent. Differences in measurement, cohort composition, service duration and statistical approach may therefore contribute to the contrasting associations, and residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Height was associated with longer hospitalisation, but not mortality. Stature reflects lung volume and early‐life nutritional and disease environments [42], serving as a proxy for long‐term development rather than a direct physiological determinant. In some populations, shorter stature can reflect childhood infection or undernutrition, and taller stature can reflect more favourable developmental conditions [43, 44]. The boundary between ‘healthy’ and ‘stunted’ varies across populations, and shorter average heights in Arctic or Indigenous populations may reflect normal development rather than deprivation [45, 46]. In this cohort, the association between height and hospital stay was non‐linear, with greater uncertainty at distribution extremes.

BMI demonstrated a non‐linear association with mortality. Lower BMI values were associated with reduced odds of death, whereas higher BMI trended towards increased odds, consistent with evidence linking adiposity to inflammation and impaired antiviral responses [47], although upper‐end associations were not statistically significant. Obesity blunts both innate and adaptive immunity, leading to elevated viral loads and dysregulated cytokine production [48, 49]. Associations between BMI and adverse outcomes have also been reported for COVID‐19 and other respiratory tract infections [50, 51], suggesting that the relationship between body composition and infection outcomes may extend beyond influenza. Obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) in this cohort was rare, reflecting population norms and military fitness requirements [30]. Importantly, modern clinical cut‐offs may not apply to 1918 cohorts due to differences in body composition, early‐life nutrition, disease exposure and physical conditioning. BMI was treated continuously to allow assessment across the observed distribution without imposing anachronistic thresholds. Notably, BMI was not associated with LOS, highlighting its context‐dependent role rather than a simple linear risk factor. Protective associations at lower BMI contrast with famine‐associated excess mortality in India in 1918 [52], underscoring that nutritional risk is highly context dependent. Structured military environments, including rations and medical care, may have mitigated lethality, suggesting that observed associations could reflect chance, residual confounding or selection effects rather than direct biological mechanisms.

Unit type strongly predicted mortality, particularly among service and support roles. Unit classification reflects enlistment role at enlistment and not reassignment. Increased risk may reflect selection, with individuals in these roles possibly less physically robust or more likely to have prior illness, despite meeting fitness standards. Differences in exposure, living conditions or access to care may also contribute, warranting further investigation.

ΔBMI during service provides additional context for interpreting enlistment BMI. Individuals with multiple hospitalisations were more likely to exhibit net BMI gain, whereas ΔBMI was unrelated to cumulative LOS or pandemic ILI severity alone. Longer hospitalisations tended to occur near the end of service, limiting post‐illness recovery, whereas repeated early episodes may have allowed recuperation under structured conditions. Weight change likely reflects baseline body composition, illness timing, access to care and structured service rather than cumulative disease burden alone.

The unique characteristics of WW1 military cohorts enabled these insights. The large, standardised, multinational dataset captured baseline anthropometrics and subsequent disease outcomes in young adults under relatively homogeneous exposure risk, rations and medical care. Although limited to predominantly healthy males from developed countries, this selectivity allowed isolation of anthropometric influences in the most affected group. These cohorts provide a rare window into host‐level determinants of pandemic severity unavailable from civilian sources.

These analyses are observational and rely on historical records, which may contain inaccuracies or incomplete information. Death during ILI hospitalisation was rare, leading to wide CIs and increased chance findings. Some individuals enlisted before completing linear growth; stature (and BMI) may have increased modestly between enlistment and pandemic ILI hospitalisation, introducing potential non‐differential misclassification. Severe obesity (> 30 kg/m2) was rare, limiting statistical power at the upper BMI range. Anthropometric measures are crude proxies for nutrition and health compared with modern metabolic or body composition assessments [53, 54]. Weight may have changed during service due to training, recovery from illness or nutritional interventions, complicating BMI and ΔBMI interpretation. Disease outcome classification relied on broad historical terminology, potentially capturing non‐influenza respiratory infections and introducing misclassification. Findings cannot be generalised to women, children, older adults or civilian males. Residual confounding by pre‐existing disease, micronutrient deficiencies and prior illness cannot be excluded. Despite these limitations, the large, standardised, longitudinal military records provide a rare opportunity to examine host‐level determinants of pandemic influenza severity.

These findings demonstrate that specific anthropometric measurements were associated with severe influenza outcomes during the 1918 pandemic in WW1 military populations. Body size and physique contributed to heterogeneity in severity, with context‐dependent effects. Modern pandemic preparedness frameworks should consider overlapping vulnerabilities from undernutrition and overnutrition [19] alongside age, comorbidities and other host factors. Future work should extend analyses to civilian populations across age groups and multiple pandemics to determine whether anthropometric determinants recur under differing viral and nutritional contexts. Experimental models will be critical for defining mechanisms linking physique, nutrition and immunity.
