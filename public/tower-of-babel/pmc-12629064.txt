Biomarkers influence kidney function estimates more so than race among persons with HIV
Authors: Peggy-ita A Obeng-Nyarkoh, Amanda B Spence, Richard Teran, Christopher A Loffredo, Bruce Luxon, Joseph Timpone, Princy Kumar, Jason G Umans, Seble G Kassaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe sought to understand the results of using different estimating equations (with and without a “race” category) and the addition of cystatin C as a biomarker on Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) stage estimates among persons with HIV (PWH), for whom CKD is an important comorbidity.MethodsBiomarkers were measured in this cross-sectional single site U.S. clinic-based study from 2014 to 2016. Other laboratory and clinical data were abstracted from the electronic health record based on the last recorded value proximal to the study visit. Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) glomerular filtration estimating equations with and without cystatin C were applied to categorize CKD stage, and staging agreement was assessed using the difference of proportions test. Multivariable regression analyses evaluated factors associated with CKD stage, and the Breslow-Day test evaluated whether race served as an effect modifier.ResultsAmong 306 PWH, the median age was 48.2 years, 86 (28%) were female, 185 (61%) were Black, 91 (30%) Caucasian, 13 (4%) Latinx, and 46 (15%) had hepatitis C virus (HCV) co-infection. The median CD4 + T-lymphocyte count was 659/mm3, 299 (98%) were on ART, and 228 (75%) had HIV VL < 20 c/mL. Using the 2009 and 2012 CKD-EPI equations (including race), more individuals were categorized as having normal kidney function (Stage 1) with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (73% vs. 55%, p < 0.00001); fewer individuals were classified in CKD stages III-V using both cystatin C and creatinine than creatinine alone, though this did not meet statistical significance (8% vs. 12%, p = 0.14). Using 2021 equations (excluding race) a larger proportion were classified as normal kidney function with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (74% vs. 49%, p = 0.00001); fewer were categorized as CKD III-V with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (8% vs. 13%, p = 0.03). Multivariable linear regression identified age (β=-0.75, p < 0.0001) and tobacco use (β=-4.10, p = 0.03) as factors associated with kidney function. Race was not an effect modifier in our analyses based on the Tarone adjusted Breslow-Day test.ConclusionAmong PWH, cystatin C shifted estimates of kidney function towards normal and resulted in shifts in kidney function categorization much more so than the race effect. As some antiretrovirals raise creatinine without affecting GFR, cystatin C is an important biomarker to confirm diminished kidney function among persons with HIV.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-025-00812-x.

Background

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a public health concern with an estimated 14% prevalence in the United States (US) population in 2020 [1, 2]. In persons with HIV (PWH), the odds of developing kidney disease increase in those with a high HIV viral load or low CD4 count. CKD in PWH is also associated with higher morbidity, mortality, and cardiovascular disease incidence [3]. The risk of CKD among PWH is often multifactorial, and may result from direct effects of HIV infection, HIV-associated immune activation, drug toxicity related to ART use, and a high prevalence of traditional CKD risk factors such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes [4]. Impaired kidney function can affect drug toxicities and drug dosing [5]. Impaired renal function may require lower medication doses to prevent supratherapeutic/toxic drug levels or may require discontinuation completely.

Glomerular filtration rate (GFR), calculated using both plasma (or serum) and timed urinary excretion of an exogenous filtration marker, such as iohexol, is accepted as the best measure of overall kidney function and to classify CKD into stages [6, 7]. These gold standard urinary clearance approaches are impractical for daily clinical practice and screening. Therefore, estimated GFR (eGFR) calculations, using plasma or serum concentrations of the endogenous, renally-cleared, biomarkers creatinine and cystatin C are used in clinical practice [8, 9]. GFR estimation equations were revised in 2021 to exclude race [10, 11]. Creatinine is the most widely used biomarker routinely used in clinical practice; it is a byproduct of muscle metabolism, filtered by the glomerulus, and secreted by the proximal tubule with minimal reabsorption [12–14]. Cystatin C is a biomarker produced by nucleated cells and can be used independently or in conjunction with creatinine to estimate kidney function. Cystatin C is produced at a constant rate by all human nucleated cells, filtered by the glomerulus, and resorbed and catabolized in the proximal tubules [15, 16]. When compared to serum creatinine, serum cystatin C is less variable from person-to-person and unaffected by sex, age, malignancy, fever, diet, etc [17–20]. While measured serum creatinine levels are higher among Black populations – independent of age, sex, and GFR – serum cystatin C levels do not differ by race [21–23]. Additionally, cystatin C when used in conjunction with creatinine has been shown to be a better estimate of GFR than with creatinine alone [15, 18, 24]. Moreover, cystatin C in combination with creatinine is superior for detecting small changes in renal function, in populations with extremes in muscle mass, diet, or comorbidities [25].

Among PWH, certain antiretroviral medications can affect the measurement of creatinine. Commonly used medications such as rilpivirine, dolutegravir, raltegravir, and the pharmacokinetic booster cobicistat affect creatinine clearance through interference with tubular creatinine handling. This interference leads to an higher measured serum creatinine, but these medications are not nephrotoxic and do not alter the true GFR [26, 27]. Conversely, cystatin C levels remain unchanged following initiation of dolutegravir and cobicistat [28–30]. Systemic inflammation related to inflammatory conditions can affect serum cystatin C, but as viral replication is suppressed via antiretroviral therapies in PWH, serum cystatin C levels decrease [31] – however, they do not typically decrease to baseline levels seen in seronegative individuals [32–35].

Prior studies that focused on renal function in the setting of HIV have not fully considered the influence of race on eGFR assessment [4]. As such, the extent of the effect of race and the relative importance of different endogenous biomarkers, alone or in combination, on kidney function estimates in PWH is unknown. We sought to understand the differences in estimated CKD stage with or without the race term using cystatin C and creatinine biomarkers among PWH.

Methods

Recruitment strategy and study design

A convenience sample of consenting adults with HIV seeking care at the MedStar Georgetown University Hospital Infectious Diseases Clinic between November 2014 and May 2016 were enrolled into a cross-sectional study designed to explore the role of cystatin C in assessing renal function in this population. Potential participants were excluded if they declined to provide a blood sample or were under 18 years old.

Measures

At the baseline visit, all participants completed a blood draw for assessment of plasma cystatin C. Cystatin C measurements were performed at the MedStar Health Research Institute Biomarker, Biochemistry, and Biorepository Core, a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA) certified and College of American Pathologists (CAP) accredited research laboratory and at Quest Diagnostics Baltimore, a certified national commercial laboratory. Creatinine measurement was abstracted from the electronic health record (EHR) from the date of the study visit or, if not available, from the most proximal clinic visit. The creatinine measurements were performed at a national commercial laboratory where routine quality control measures were performed. The cystatin C measurements were performed at the Medstar Biorepository core. The laboratory used a turbidometric immunoassay (Gentian, Moss, Norway) standardized to the ERM-DA471/IFCC Cystatin C reference material. Additional demographic and clinical data such as age, self-reported race, sex, medical comorbidities, and antiretroviral treatment (ART) data were abstracted from the EHR.

The eGFR was calculated using equations with and without race as a category, and with and without cystatin C or creatinine as a biomarker. Thus the 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) creatinine (only) equation, 2012 CKD-EPI cystatin C and creatinine-cystatin C equations, and the recently adopted 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine and creatinine-cystatin C equations were used [10]. We calculated eGFR and categorized into groups: stage 1 ≥ 90, stage 2 60–89, stage 3 30–59, stage 4 15–29, stage 5 < 15 [12, 36].

Statistical analyses

Study population characteristics were described by univariate analyses. Percent agreement was computed, and Bland-Altman plots and scatterplots were generated to compare and assess the agreement across stages generated by selected equations. To examine the agreement when eGFR was measured using cystatin C instead of creatinine as is traditionally used, the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation was compared to the 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation. As it is now clinically accepted to drop race from estimating equations [11], the remaining comparisons assessed the effect of dropping race in addition to other outcomes. Finally, to isolate the effect of adding cystatin C as a second biomarker, the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation was compared to the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation.

Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with eGFR as determined by the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. Covariates that were associated with eGFR on bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model. Potential confounders and interactions were also assessed in the initial model. The chunk test was then performed to see which interactions terms should remain.

The Tarone adjusted Breslow-Day test was conducted in conjunction with the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel (CMH) to evaluate whether race served as an effect modifier; the CMH provided odds ratios across multiple strata and the Breslow-Day test assessed whether the generated odds ratios were homogenous. Race was reclassified as a binary variable inclusive of only Black and white participants with participants of other racial and ethnic identities being excluded as their number was very small and grouping them would have made a very heterogenous category. Age was reclassified as a binary variable with the cutoff of 48.2 years (median age). Tobacco use was reclassified as a binary variable with categories of “ever” – inclusive of current and previous smokers – and “never.” eGFR was reclassified as a binary variable with the cutoff of 60 mL/min/1.73 m² as indicative of kidney dysfunction [12].

All statistical analyses and equation modelling were performed in SAS® (Version 9.4 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

The study was approved by the Georgetown University Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB; number 2014 − 0556). All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Among 306 PWH, the median age was 48.2 years, 86 (28%) were female, 185 (61%) were Black, 91 (30%) Caucasian, 13 (4%) Latinx, and 46 (15%) had HCV co-infection. The median CD4 + T lymphocyte count was 659/mm3, 299 (98%) were on ART, and 228 (75%) had HIV VL < 20 c/mL. Most participants had creatinine measurements performed at enrollment concurrent with sample collection for cystatin C measurement, and only 15 (5%) had measurements that were performed at a later date ranging from 6 to 161 days with a median of 69 days (IQR 2, 85.5). Distributions of kidney function stages

The estimating equations were used to compute eGFRs that were then used to categorize CKD stage according to the Kidney Foundation’s Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (KDOQI) guidelines. As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1, most of the participants were either Stage 1 (n = 151) or Stage 2 (n = 113) when eGFR was determined with the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. A minority of participants were distributed to Stage 3a (n = 24), Stage 3b (n = 8), Stage 4 (n = 2), and Stage 5 (n = 8). With the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation – which served as the previous clinical standard until 2021 and includes an adjustment for race – more participants were found in Stage 1 (n = 167) and Stage 3b (n = 9); fewer participants were distributed to Stage 2 (n = 103) and Stage 3a (n = 19); there were no participants in Stage 4, and the number of participants (n = 8) distributed to Stage 5 remained the same with the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation as with the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. When cystatin C was used as the biomarker, most participants (n = 234) were classified as Stage 1. Fewer participants were classified as Stage 2 (n = 47), Stage 3a (n = 9), Stage 4 (n = 1), and Stage 5 (n = 7) when compared to the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation. In the combined 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation – which includes an adjustment on race – most participants (n = 224) were in Stage 1. In comparison to the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine equation, there were fewer participants classified as Stages 2–5 (n = 56 at Stage 2, n = 11 at Stage 3a, n = 7 at Stage 3b, n = 1 at Stage 4, and n = 7 at Stage 5).

Using the 2009 and 2012 equations (including race), more individuals were categorized as having normal kidney function (Stage 1) with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (73% vs. 55%, p < 0.00001). Fewer individuals were classified in CKD stage 2 when cystatin C was introduced than with creatinine alone (18% vs. 34%, p < 0.00001). Additionally, fewer individuals were classified in CKD stages 3–5 using cystatin C than creatinine alone, but this did not meet statistical significance (8% vs. 12%, p = 0.14). Using 2021 equations (excluding race) a larger proportion were classified as normal kidney function with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (74% vs. 49%, p = 0. 0.00001); fewer were categorized as CKD 3–5 with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (8% vs. 13%, p = 0.026).

Assessing agreement among selected equations

The eGFR values calculated with the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine equation were compared to those calculated with the 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation; both equations include race. In this analysis, more individuals were classified as having normal kidney function (Stage 1) with the introduction of cystatin C as opposed to using creatinine alone (73% vs. 55%, p < 0.00001). Similarly, fewer individuals were classified in CKD stages 3–5 with the addition of cystatin C than creatinine alone (8% vs. 12%, p = 0.14). Scatter and Bland-Altman plots assessing the 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine and 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equations showed high correlation, but at higher eGFRs, the 2012 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation had greater variation (Supplementary Fig. 1).

In comparisons of equations excluding race – specifically the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine vs. 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation – more individuals were classified as normal kidney function with inclusion of cystatin C vs. creatinine alone (74% vs. 49%, p = 0. 0.00001). Additionally, fewer individuals were categorized as CKD 3–5 with inclusion of cystatin C than creatinine alone (8% vs. 13%, p = 0.026). Here, the scatterplot and Bland-Altman plots of the eGFR values showed high correlation, but the 2021 CKD-EPI creatinine-cystatin C equation at higher eGFRs had greater variation (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this population of treated PWH, the measured prevalence of CKD ranged between 8% and 14% depending on which estimating equation was used. The availability of both creatinine and cystatin C allowed us to assess the effect of both biomarkers and race on classification of kidney function, and we noted significant differences in categorization of kidney disease stage with uses of creatinine alone versus creatinine with the addition of cystatin C. With the addition of cystatin C, fewer individuals were classified as having CKD with either set of equations (i.e., the earlier equations that included race and the more recent equations that were race neutral) demonstrating a bigger effect of the biomarker than the inclusion of race. These significant differences in classification of renal function in PWH highlight the importance in selecting appropriate biomarkers and estimating equations in this population. Inaccurate estimates can affect clinical care including medication dosing, referral to subspeciality care, or even consideration for renal transplantation [37].

The National Kidney Foundation and American Society of Nephrology (NKF-ASN) Task Force recommended dropping the “race correction” [11]. Historically, race was used an effect modifier in GFR estimating equations (e.g., 2012 and 2009 CKD-EPI creatinine based equations) [38] – an attempt to adjust for non-GFR determinants of plasma creatinine concentration. However, this has been a topic of significant debate. Opponents of using the race term argue that the inclusion of race overestimates eGFR in Black patients, with the same creatinine measurement suggesting higher eGFR among Black individuals compared to non-Black individuals [39–41]. This raised concerns of widening racial disparities related to CKD with potential delays in appropriate medical care [42]. Our analyses indicated that race was not an effect modifier in our study population and did not identify an association between eGFR whether or not race was included in the estimating equation. Our study population was limited to those with HIV and majority of participants were Black and it is possible that inclusion of participants taking antiretroviral medications that inhibit the portion of creatinine clearance due to renal tubular secretion without decreasing GFR may have influenced our outcomes.

Discussions have shifted towards assessing the efficacy of the endogenous filtration biomarkers (i.e., creatinine and cystatin C) in estimating GFR in specific populations. Although prior studies examined the effect of HIV on CKD and measurements of renal function [4, 43], they did consider possible contributions of effect of race and renal filtration biomarker selection among PWH. Recent studies demonstrated that omission of the race component in the CKD-EPI creatinine based estimating equation led to more accurate assessments of actual renal function in PWH. Other studies suggest that the performance of the CKD-EPI estimating equation was improved by the addition of cystatin C to creatinine but these studies did not necessarily use race free equations [35, 44, 45]. We were not able to assess kidney function using the iohexol or inulin clearance gold standard assessments [46]. However, the observed differences in our study and gaps in the literature call for systemic study of race-free estimating equations including cystatin C against a “gold standard” reference as the staging can be affected by unique HIV related factors such as direct viral effect and the antiretrovirals commonly used for management.

Importantly, we did not observe significant differential classification at the eGFR level that would change ART dosing, most of which would only be required when eGFR values fall below 50mL/min/1.73m2. For example, among the drugs used to treat HIV, tenofovir disoproxil fumarate – a nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitor – requires dose adjustment for eGFR below 50mL/min/1.73m2, and is associated with nephrotoxicity with cumulative exposure increasing CKD risk by 33–44% and ever exposure increasing CKD risk by 71–111% [5, 47]. As such, shift in stages from Stage 1 to Stage 2 are often not in the range to warrant medication dosing changes. However, reclassification of those in stages 3b, 4, and 5 to earlier stages could result in dosing decision changes that may provide opportunities for PWH to remain on single-tablet regimens that support improved long term adherence [48]. The study cohort was primarily comprised of people on HIV medications (98%). Our analyses did not adjust for the use of certain medications that could affect the outcomes such as dolutegravir and cobicistat, both agents that can raise measured creatinine without affecting GFR by decreasing tubular secretion of creatinine via inhibition of the organic cation transporter 2 [49, 50]. Future studies should determine the accuracy of race-free cystatin C and creatinine containing equations including in the setting of use of these agents.

There are some limitations to our study. While most participants had creatinine measurements performed at enrollment concurrent with sample collection for measurement of cystatin C, a minority had measurements that were performed at the most proximal clinic visit which could have introduced measurement bias. There were limited individuals with advanced kidney disease in this clinic-based convenience sample. We are unable to provide a prevalence estimate of kidney disease with a single eGFR measurement. However, this is not required to enable the comparison included in this analysis. Strengths of this study include a real-world clinical cohort – approximately 30% of clinic patients were successfully recruited in this effort – increasing the generalizability of the findings as the patient type is representative of those typically seen in clinical practice.

Conclusion

Although the race adjustment for measurement of eGFR has been dropped, more research needs to be done to determine the optimal balance and cost-efficiencies of the available biomarkers to measure kidney function among PWH. Use of cystatin C may help to identify clinically meaningful changes in kidney function of particular importance to the care of persons living with HIV.
