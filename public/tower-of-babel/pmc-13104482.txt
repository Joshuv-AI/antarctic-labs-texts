In silico and in vitro insights into the wound-healing potential of syringic acid: protective and regenerative effects on human fibroblasts
Authors: Ufuk Okkay, İlkin Kazimov, Irmak Ferah Okkay, Azizeh Shadidizaji, Mehmet Ali Yoruk, Murat Ozdemir, Fatih Burak Yildiz, Fatma Yesilyurt, Mustafa Ozkaraca, Ahmet Hacimuftuoglu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the wound-healing potential of syringic acid (SA) through integrated in silico and in vitro approaches using human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs). Network pharmacology analysis identified myeloperoxidase (MPO), a key enzyme involved in oxidative stress and inflammation, as a primary target of SA. Computational predictions also indicated favorable pharmacokinetic and ADMET properties. In vitro experiments were conducted using SA at concentrations of 50–400 µM. Cell viability was assessed by MTT and LDH assays, while antioxidant and inflammatory responses were evaluated by measuring SOD, CAT, MDA, TNF-α, IL-1β, TGF-β, iNOS, and hydroxyproline levels. SA treatment significantly enhanced fibroblast viability and exhibited strong antioxidant effects, as demonstrated by increased SOD and CAT activities and reduced MDA levels. Additionally, SA suppressed pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, and iNOS) while upregulating TGF-β expression. Importantly, SA promoted collagen synthesis, evidenced by increased hydroxyproline content. Among the tested doses, 100–200 µM showed the most pronounced effects. Overall, these findings demonstrate that SA facilitates wound healing by improving cellular viability, reducing oxidative stress and inflammation, and enhancing collagen production. This study highlights SA as a promising multi-target therapeutic candidate for wound repair, although further in vivo and safety studies are required to support its clinical application.

Introduction

Injury, defined as the disruption of normal anatomical integrity and function, extends beyond simple epithelial damage and may involve deeper structures such as connective tissue, vasculature, nerves, and parenchymal organs [1, 2]. Wound healing is a highly coordinated and dynamic biological process consisting of overlapping phases, including inflammation, proliferation, and tissue remodeling, all of which are essential for restoring tissue integrity [3, 4]. However, impaired regulation of these processes—commonly observed in aging, diabetes, and chronic inflammatory conditions—can lead to delayed or non-healing wounds, representing a significant clinical and socioeconomic burden worldwide [5, 6].

Among the key factors influencing wound healing, oxidative stress plays a central role. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated as part of the normal inflammatory response and contribute to host defense and cellular signaling [7, 8]. Nevertheless, excessive ROS production disrupts redox homeostasis, leading to lipid peroxidation, protein damage, and DNA instability, ultimately impairing cellular functions critical for tissue repair [9]. Recent studies have emphasized that persistent oxidative stress is one of the primary drivers of chronic wound pathology and delayed healing outcomes [10].

In addition to oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators and cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) are crucial regulators of the wound-healing cascade [11, 12]. While these mediators are necessary for initiating repair processes, their prolonged or excessive expression exacerbates tissue damage and disrupts collagen synthesis, thereby contributing to chronic wound formation [12, 13]. Conversely, growth factors such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) play a pivotal role in promoting fibroblast proliferation, extracellular matrix deposition, and collagen synthesis, which are essential for successful tissue remodeling [14, 15]. Given the critical involvement of oxidative stress and inflammation in wound healing, there has been increasing interest in natural compounds with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Polyphenolic compounds, particularly plant-derived phenolics, have attracted considerable attention due to their ability to modulate multiple biological pathways simultaneously [16–18].

Syringic acid (SA), a naturally occurring phenolic compound derived from benzoic acid, has been reported to exhibit a wide range of pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, hepatoprotective, and neuroprotective effects [19–21]. Recent studies have further demonstrated its capacity to modulate oxidative stress pathways and inflammatory signaling, highlighting its therapeutic potential in various disease models [22–24].

Despite these promising properties, the role of SA in wound healing remains insufficiently explored, particularly with respect to its molecular targets and multi-pathway mechanisms. In this context, computational approaches such as network pharmacology and molecular target prediction have emerged as powerful tools for elucidating complex biological interactions and predicting therapeutic mechanisms [25, 26]. In this context, myeloperoxidase (MPO), a neutrophil-derived enzyme involved in ROS generation and inflammatory responses, has emerged as a critical target due to its dual role in antimicrobial defense and tissue damage during excessive inflammation.

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the wound-healing potential of SA using an integrated in silico and in vitro approach. Specifically, we evaluated its effects on fibroblast viability, oxidative stress markers, inflammatory mediators, and collagen synthesis in human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells, while also exploring its potential molecular targets and pharmacokinetic properties through computational analyses. This combined approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of the therapeutic potential of SA in wound healing.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

This study utilized a HDF cell line obtained from the Department of Medical Pharmacology, Atatürk University (ATCC, USA) to establish an in vitro wound model. HDFs were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) and a mixture of penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B to ensure optimal cell growth and viability. The cells were maintained under incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 90% humidity. Standard cell culture techniques, including thawing, passaging, and cryopreservation, were employed for HDF maintenance. Following expansion, cells were seeded onto culture plates for subsequent experimental procedures. SA was prepared at various concentrations (50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM) and added to each well containing fibroblast cells. All experiments were conducted in triplicate. This study was approved by the Atatürk University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No.: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/727). Ethical approval was obtained in accordance with institutional research regulations. The study involves only in vitro experiments using a commercially available human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cell line and in silico analyses, and does not include any human or animal subjects.

Cell cytotoxicity assays

Cell viability was evaluated using two independent assays: the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay and the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay. For the MTT assay, cells were incubated with MTT solution (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following the designated incubation period, the formazan precipitate was resolved in 150 µl of DMSO, and absorbance values were detected using a spectrophotometer set at 490 nm (BioTek Instruments, USA). For the LDH assay, the release of LDH into the culture medium, an indicator of cell membrane damage, was quantified using an LDH cytotoxicity detection kit (Elabscience, United States) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Scratch wound healing assay

A scratch wound-healing assay was performed to evaluate fibroblast migration. HDF cells were cultured until 90–100% confluence, and a linear scratch was created using a sterile pipette tip. Cells were then treated with different concentrations of SA (50–400 µM). Images were captured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ software.

Ames mutagenicity test

The mutagenic potential of SA was evaluated using the Ames test. Salmonella typhimurium strains TA98 and TA100 were used to detect frame-shift and base-pair substitution mutations, respectively. The assay was performed both in the presence and absence of metabolic activation (S9 fraction). SA was tested at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 400 µM. Bacterial cultures were incubated with the test compound, and the number of revertant colonies was counted after incubation. Negative (vehicle) and positive controls were included in all experiments. Positive controls consisted of 4-nitro-o-phenylenediamine for TA98 (− S9), sodium azide for TA100 (− S9), and 2-aminoanthracene for both strains in the presence of S9. All experiments were performed in triplicate (n = 3).

Biochemical analysis

The concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydroxyproline, as well as the enzymatic activities of catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), present in the culture supernatant, were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, USA) following the protocols provided by the manufacturer. Absorbance measurements were obtained by reading each well in accordance with the respective kit’s protocol using an ELISA reader (BioTek Instruments, USA).

Immunofluorescence assay

The cells were initially fixed in methanol at -20 °C for a duration of 5 min. Following fixation, the cells underwent washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequent incubation in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After an additional washing step, the cells were subjected to incubation with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 60 min. Subsequently, the cells were allowed to incubate overnight at + 4 °C in the presence of primary antibodies targeting tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) (dilution 1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-133192), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) (dilution 1:200, Santa Cruz, sc-52012), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) (Santa Cruz, sc-130348), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Abcam, ab15323). Following the incubation period, the cells were treated with goat anti-mouse FITC secondary antibodies (dilution 1:50, Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-095-003; 111-095-003) in a dark environment at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequent to this, the cells were stained with DAPI and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The fluorescence intensity was quantified using Fiji Image J software.

In silico analysis

First, 3D structure of SA (PubChem CID 10742) were obtained from Pubchem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/10742). Second, pkcsm server was used to obtain Pharmacokinetic Properties of SA (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/prediction_single/adme_1767059760.81). Third, SA interaction -proteins STITCH server (https://stitch-db.org/) and for Protein-Protein interaction STRING database (https://string-db.org/) were used.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation (SD) were used to express the data. IBM SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Co., USA) was used for data analysis. As the data were normally distributed, post hoc Tukey’s test and one-way ANOVA test were applied (p < 0.05).

Results

Effect of SA on cell viability

While a noteworthy elevation in proliferation percentage was noted across all groups in comparison to the control group, displaying increments of 22.32%, 35.41%, 29.65%, and 18.75% for the respective groups, the augmentation was particularly pronounced in the 100 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM groups (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). LDH release was used as a marker of cell cytotoxicity. Notably, a significant decrease in LDH release (18.97%) was observed in the 100 µM group compared to the control group (p < 0.05), indicating reduced cell membrane damage. Although decreases in LDH release were also observed in the 50 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM groups, these changes did not reach statistical significance compared to the control group (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1).

Biochemical results

Hydroxyproline content increased in all treatment groups compared to the control, (Fig. 3). The observed increases were 28.57%, 63.2%, 50.97%, and 39.54% for the 50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM groups, respectively (p < 0.001). Similarly, SOD activity was significantly elevated in the 100 µM and 200 µM groups (Fig. 4). Although increases in SOD activity were noted across all groups, only the 100 µM and 200 µM groups showed significant enhancements, with increases of 22.65% and 20.85%, respectively (p < 0.05). The increases observed in the 50 µM and 400 µM groups, however, were not statistically significant compared to the control group. CAT activity increased significantly across all treatment groups (Fig. 4), with observed increases of 22.70%, 35.59%, 32.52%, and 23.19% in the 50 µM, 100 µM, 200 µM, and 400 µM groups, respectively. The 100 µM and 200 µM groups exhibited significant enhancements parallel to the pronounced increases in SOD levels (p < 0.001). Significant increases in CAT activity were also recorded in the 50 µM and 400 µM groups (p < 0.05). MDA levels, decreased across all treatment groups consistent with the SOD and CAT findings (Fig. 4). The most pronounced reductions were observed in the 100 µM and 200 µM groups, with decreases of 24.31% and 21.55%, respectively (p < 0.001).

The three-dimensional (3D) molecular structure of SA is presented in Fig. 9. The structure reveals a benzoic acid core substituted with two methoxy groups and one hydroxyl group, which are known to contribute to its biological activity. The presence of these functional groups provides multiple potential sites for hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with target proteins.

Notably, the hydroxyl and methoxy groups are associated with antioxidant activity, enabling free radical scavenging and stabilization of reactive species. In addition, the carboxylic acid moiety may facilitate interactions with enzyme active sites, including MPO, supporting the predicted inhibitory effects observed in network pharmacology analyses. These structural features collectively suggest that SA possesses a favorable chemical architecture for interacting with multiple molecular targets involved in oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby contributing to its potential wound-healing properties.

Pharmacokinetic properties of SA

Absorption

SA demonstrated moderate aqueous solubility (− 2.832 log mol/L) and high Caco-2 permeability (1.683 log Papp), indicating efficient passive diffusion across intestinal epithelial cells. The predicted human intestinal absorption (~ 58.9%) suggests moderate oral bioavailability. Importantly, the compound was not identified as a substrate or inhibitor of P-glycoprotein, indicating a low likelihood of efflux-related limitations or transporter-mediated drug interactions. Low skin permeability (− 2.735 log Kp) further suggests limited transdermal absorption.

Distribution

The predicted volume of distribution (VDss: 0.011 log L/kg) indicates that SA is primarily confined to the plasma compartment with limited tissue accumulation. A relatively high fraction unbound (0.636) suggests that a significant proportion remains pharmacologically active in circulation. Additionally, low blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and very low CNS penetration (log PS: −4.757) indicate minimal central nervous system exposure, which may reduce the risk of CNS-related adverse effects.

Metabolism

SA exhibited a favorable metabolic profile, with no predicted interaction with major cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4). This suggests a low potential for metabolic drug–drug interactions and supports predictable pharmacokinetic behavior.

Excretion

The predicted total clearance (− 4.587 log ml/min/kg) indicates relatively slow elimination, which may contribute to prolonged systemic exposure. Furthermore, the compound was not predicted to be a substrate of the renal transporter OCT2, suggesting a low risk of transporter-mediated renal interactions.

Toxicity

Overall, SA exhibits several desirable pharmacokinetic characteristics, including good permeability, low interaction potential, and limited CNS exposure. Nevertheless, its predicted genotoxicity highlights the need for further in vivo safety evaluation and potential structural optimization.

SA interaction network

A compound interacting with SA would probably act as a multifaceted antiproliferative and anti-inflammatory agent based on the specific protein targets, mainly through dual inhibition of the critical folate-dependent thymidylate pathway—targeting both dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) and thymidylate synthetase (TYMS) to disrupt DNA synthesis and stop rapidly dividing cells. This effect would be enhanced by concurrently modulating the immune response by suppressing chemokine CCL16 to reduce immune cell recruitment and MPO to limit oxidative tissue damage, with further interactions involving the ring finger proteins RNF113A/B potentially reflecting downstream effects on RNA processing and cellular stress responses, all of which together provide a coherent picture of a potential immunotherapeutic or chemotherapeutic drug intended to suppress inflammation and attack proliferation in concert (Fig. 10).

MPO interaction network

MPO functions within a crucial network of oxidative and antioxidative enzymes related to wound healing, according to the network given. At the location of a wound, active immune cells such as neutrophils release MPO, which produces strong microbicidal hypochlorous acid (HOCL). MPO-derived reactive species are crucial for preventing infection, but they can also seriously harm host tissues, such as proteins and lipids, and prolong inflammation, which can obstruct the fibroblast-driven proliferative phase of recovery. A variety of antioxidant enzymes, including as CAT, Glutathione Peroxidase 3 (GPX3), Peroxiredoxins (PRDX1, PRDX4), and Glutathione S-transferase A1 (GSTA1), are closely linked to MPO, according to the network. By detoxifying hydrogen peroxide and lipid peroxides, these molecules serve as an essential balancing that shields regenerated human fibroblasts from oxidative damage. The significance of glutathione metabolism in preserving cellular redox equilibrium in the inflammatory wound environment is further highlighted by the presence of enzymes like CHAC2 and GSTA1.

SA’s protective and regenerative actions on fibroblasts can be understood as a regulation of this specific MPO-centered network in relation to its potential for wound healing. By strengthening this natural antioxidant barrier, SA, a phenolic antioxidant, probably promotes fibroblast survival and function. It may enhance the cell’s ability to neutralize MPO-derived and other reactive oxygen species by upregulating or potentiating the activity of CAT, GPX3, and PRDX enzymes. By reducing inflammation and cellular damage, this oxidative stress mitigation would improve the milieu for fibroblast migration, proliferation, and collagen formation. Additionally, SA may assist reduce inflammation and change the balance from MPO-driven microbial killing to antioxidant-facilitated tissue regeneration by modulating regulators such as PTGS2 (COX-2), hastening the transition to the remodeling phase of wound repair (Fig. 11).

Oxidative stress response

When the body generates more reactive oxygen species than its natural antioxidant defenses can handle, oxidative stress results. Due to the disruption of the cellular environment caused by this imbalance, essential structures including DNA, proteins, and cell membranes may be harmed by free radicals and peroxides. But these reactive chemicals also play vital biological roles, like helping the immune system eliminate dangerous bacteria. Long-term oxidative stress is associated with a number of diseases, such as heart disease and neurological disorders, whereas short-term, controlled exposure may cause cells to develop advantageous adaptive responses (Fig. 12).

Biological process of MPO

In order to preserve cellular integrity, this group of biological processes focuses on the crucial control of ROS, especially hydrogen peroxide and superoxide radicals. These molecules’ catabolism and biosynthesis are essential for maintaining redox homeostasis by coordinating a precise cellular response to chemical and oxidative stress. Important physiological processes, such as defensive mechanisms like the respiratory burst, thyroid hormone production, and the metabolism of important signaling chemicals like prostaglandins and arachidonic acid, are directly supported by this regulatory network. In the end, these interrelated mechanisms influence more general biological outcomes such as aging, wound healing, and the general control of metabolism and programmed cell death (Fig. 13).

Several high-confidence interactions for SA with direct relevance to wound healing were identified through molecular target prediction analysis, particularly with multiple isoforms of the carbonic anhydrase (CA) enzyme family, including CA7, CA14, CA3, and CA6. Carbonic anhydrases play a critical role in regulating tissue pH, which is essential for maintaining an optimal wound microenvironment. Modulation of these enzymes may help reduce acidosis-induced inflammation and support fibroblast migration and proliferation during tissue repair.

In addition, the predicted interaction with the endoplasmic reticulum stress sensor IRE1 (ERN1) suggests a potential role in regulating cellular stress responses. Given that ER stress is closely associated with impaired wound healing and inflammatory signaling, targeting IRE1 may contribute to enhanced cellular survival under pathological conditions. SwissTargetPrediction analysis further indicated a high probability of interaction with lyase enzymes (80%), along with additional potential interactions with other enzyme classes such as general enzymes (13.3%) and transferases (6.7%) (Fig. 14). This strong association with lyases suggests a possible role in modulating key metabolic and redox-related pathways, which may contribute to the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of SA. Overall, these findings suggest that SA exerts its biological effects through a multi-target mechanism involving pH regulation, stress response modulation, and control of oxidative and inflammatory pathways, supporting its potential role in wound healing (Table 4).

Discussion

Non-healing wounds present a significant economic burden, requiring the development of novel therapeutic agents [27]. Wound healing is a complex process involving intricate interplay between various cellular and molecular mechanisms, including inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [28]. SA is known for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties, making it a promising compound for enhancing wound healing [29, 30]. This study investigated the potential of SA to modulate these processes and accelerate wound healing.

Fibroblasts are crucial in all stages of wound healing, including inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. Therefore, their viability, proliferation, and cytotoxicity directly linked to the healing process [31]. LDH is a cytoplasmic enzyme present in all cells. Exposure to toxic substances disrupts plasma membrane integrity, leading to LDH leakage into the cell medium [30]. LDH and MTT assays are widely used to evaluate the cytotoxic potential [32, 33]. Studies on hippocampal neurons have shown that SA improves cell viability in MTT assays and reduced LDH leakage, which is consistent with our findings [34]. Our data indicate that the application of SA significantly increases fibroblast count, particularly at the optimal dose of 100 µM, supporting wound healing. In addition, the scratch wound-healing assay performed in this study further confirmed the pro-healing effects of SA by demonstrating enhanced fibroblast migration in a dose-dependent manner. Since fibroblast migration is a critical step in wound closure and tissue regeneration, these findings provide direct functional evidence supporting the wound-healing potential of SA.

Oxidative stress is a critical factor in non-healing wounds, arising from an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants [35, 36]. Elevated ROS levels associated with inflammation, carcinogenesis, aging, and photobiological effects, can disrupt redox homeostasis and impede wound healing [35, 37, 38]. In wound healing, elevated ROS in response to inflammation or other factors act as significant regulators and lead to redox imbalances, which are the main cause of non-healing wounds [39]. SOD is an antioxidant enzyme that converts the oxidant superoxide into hydrogen peroxide, which is subsequently detoxified by CAT. Dysregulation of this system leads to increased oxidant levels, lipid peroxidation, and consequently, elevated levels of MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation [40]. Excessive oxidative stress during wound healing can suppress SOD and CAT activity while increasing MDA levels [41]. Various studies have demonstrated that SA reduces MDA levels and increases SOD and CAT activity under oxidative stress conditions [42–44]. For instance, a study on hepatic oxidative stress found that SA increased SOD and CAT activity while reducing MDA levels [42]. Similarly, in a study on hepatic encephalopathy, SA inhibited oxidative stress by reducing MDA levels and increasing SOD activity [45]. The results of our study align with these findings, suggesting that SA can prevent oxidative stress related to wound formation by inhibiting lipid peroxidation and activating antioxidant enzymes in response to inflammation and other factors.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of multi-target approaches in modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways involved in wound healing. In this context, enzymes such as MPO play a central role in reactive oxygen species production and amplification of inflammation. Excessive MPO activity has been associated with delayed wound healing and tissue damage [46]. Polyphenolic compounds structurally similar to SA have been shown to modulate MPO-related oxidative pathways, contributing to their anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects [47]. Furthermore, phenolic acids are known to regulate key signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, which are closely linked to oxidative stress, inflammation, and wound repair processes [48, 49]. Although direct experimental evidence for MPO inhibition by SA remains limited, its structural features and reported biological activities support the plausibility of such interactions.

During the inflammation phase of wound healing, cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α promote fibroblast proliferation and migration, leading to the production of abundant exudate and attracting leukocytes to the wound site [50–52]. However, excessive or prolonged cytokine release can exacerbate ROS levels, disrupt hydroxyproline-dependent collagen synthesis, and contribute to non-healing wounds [53, 54]. Studies on wound healing confirm the observation of increased cytokine production in non-healing wounds [27, 55, 56]. Previous studies on SA have demonstrated a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokine expression. In a model of ulcerative colitis, SA was found to have an anti-inflammatory effect by suppressing IL-1β and TNF-α expression [57]. Our findings are consistent with these reports, indicating that SA application reduces and modulates inflammation. SA applications at doses of 100 µM and 200 µM have demonstrated superior outcomes compared to other groups. Additionally, our results suggest a simultaneous reduction in inflammation and associated oxidative stress, consistent with the observed antioxidant and oxidant parameters.

Tissue remodeling involves TGF-β, which promotes collagen synthesis via hydroxyproline and facilitates wound contraction and angiogenesis [58, 59]. Furthermore, TGF-β is closely associated with angiogenesis, which is essential for wound healing [27]. Several studies indicate that delayed wound healing is often associated with reduced TGF-β and hydroxyproline activity [59, 60]. Research has shown that SA increases TGF-β expression. However, the impact of SA on hydroxyproline-dependent collagen accumulation is mixed. Our results indicate that SA enhances TGF-β expression and hydroxyproline-mediated collagen synthesis. These findings are consistent with reports of SA’s dual effects on hydroxyproline, increasing its levels in diabetic wound models while suppressing them in hepatic contexts [61–63].

Collectively, the in silico findings suggest that SA exerts its biological effects through a multi-target mechanism involving key regulators of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular homeostasis. The predicted interactions with enzymes such as myeloperoxidase and carbonic anhydrases, together with modulation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, indicate that SA may influence multiple interconnected pathways relevant to wound healing. This integrative mechanism highlights the potential of SA not only as an antioxidant compound but also as a regulator of the inflammatory microenvironment and tissue repair processes.

In a study investigating SA’s hepatoprotective effect, it was found to suppress hepatic hydroxyproline levels. Conversely, in a diabetic wound model, it increased hydroxyproline levels [61, 62]. The study shows that the application of SA improves the synthesis of TGF-β and hydroxyproline, which are responsible for collagen production.

Nitric oxide plays a crucial role in oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. iNOS is responsible for its release [64, 65]. Elevated expression of iNOS is well-established to be associated with delayed wound healing. Moreover, increased iNOS expression can suppress TGF-β, which is critical for collagen accumulation during the tissue remodeling phase, thereby inhibiting wound healing [64]. Several studies have demonstrated that SA can suppress iNOS, yielding positive effects [66, 67]. The immunofluorescent findings of iNOS in our study are consistent with these outcomes. The results show a decrease in iNOS expression that corresponds to the reduction in inflammatory and oxidative parameters, along with an increase in TGF-β expression. These effects were most pronounced at doses of 100 µM and 200 µM, where SA mitigated the suppressive effects of iNOS on inflammation, oxidative stress, apoptosis, and collagen synthesis.

SA’s potential as a topical wound-healing agent has a complicated but manageable profile according to ADMET forecasts. The substance is not a P-glycoprotein substrate and shows minimal intestinal absorption, indicating that it would have a low risk of interacting with efflux transporters if administered systemically. Its low skin permeability (− 2.735 log Kp) and poor water solubility, however, suggest that topical bioavailability would be restricted in the absence of formulation improvements. A substance must have sufficient skin penetration in order to function on human fibroblasts in wound beds. To provide efficient local concentrations, delivery methods such nanoencapsulation, gel-based carriers, or penetration enhancers can be required. SA has a favorable safety profile for a topical medication meant for patients with chronic or comorbid wounds because it does not inhibit important CYP enzymes and is not a hERG blocker, which lessens worries about systemic drug–drug interactions or cardiotoxicity if significant absorption occurs.

The Ames test results demonstrated that SA does not exhibit mutagenic activity under the tested conditions. This finding supports its safety profile and strengthens its potential as a bioactive compound for wound healing applications. The low maximum tolerated dose and evidence of aquatic toxicity point to a limited therapeutic window and environmental concerns, even though the lack of hepatotoxicity and skin sensitivity is advantageous. Despite being constrained by low skin permeability, systemic exposure could still result in accumulation given the extremely low anticipated clearance in the context of wound healing, when prolonged and potentially large-area application may occur. The in silico results give a solid mechanistic basis for the observed in vitro effects. Computational predictions identified MPO as a primary inhibitory target, with substantial interactions with carbonic anhydrase isoforms, implying that SA directly affects oxidative stress and tissue pH regulation. These findings, together with good expected pharmacokinetic features, justify its robust bioactivity and position it as a multi-target drug capable of intervening at critical stages of the wound-healing cascade.

Conclusion

In the present study, SA demonstrated significant wound-healing potential through its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-regenerative effects in human dermal fibroblasts. The integrated in silico and in vitro approach revealed that SA may act through a multi-target mechanism involving the modulation of oxidative stress, inflammatory pathways, and cellular repair processes. Importantly, the predicted interactions with key molecular targets, together with favorable pharmacokinetic properties, support its potential as a bioactive compound for wound management. From a practical perspective, these findings suggest that SA could be developed as a component of topical formulations, such as wound dressings or dermal therapeutic agents, aimed at enhancing tissue repair and reducing inflammation.

However, despite these promising results, further in vivo studies and detailed safety evaluations are required to validate its efficacy and clinical applicability. Future research should focus on formulation development, dose optimization, and mechanistic validation in relevant animal models. Overall, this study provides a scientific basis for the potential use of SA as a multi-target therapeutic candidate in wound healing and supports its further development toward clinical applications.
