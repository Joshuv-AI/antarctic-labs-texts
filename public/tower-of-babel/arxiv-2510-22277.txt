\displaystyle E=\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{\infty}}{\bigcup}B(b_{j},r_{j})\;\;\text{such}\;\text{that}\;\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}|b_{j}|=\infty\;\;\text{and}\;\;\sideset{}{{}_{j=1}^{\infty}}{\sum}r_{j}/|b_{j}|<\infty.

2.
Uniqueness problem of
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
sharing three values

In 1977, Rubel and Yang
[
26
]
were the first to consider the uniqueness of an entire function and its derivative. They proved the following result:

Theorem 2.A
.

[
26
]
Let
f
be a non-constant entire function and
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
such that
b\not=a
. If
f
and
f^{(1)}
share
a
and
b
CM, then
f\equiv f^{(1)}
.

In 2007, Bergweiler and Langley
[
4
]
established a relationship between
f^{(1)}(z)
and
\Delta f(z)=f(z+1)-f(z)
specifically showing that
f^{(1)}(z)\sim\Delta f(z)
,
r\rightarrow\infty
,
r\not\in E_{1}
, where
f(z)
is a transcendental meromorphic function with
\rho(f)<1
and
E_{1}
is an
\varepsilon
-set.

A natural question arises: What is the uniqueness result when
f^{(1)}
shares values with
\Delta_{c}f
for a finite-order meromorphic function
f
?

In response to this question, Qi et al.
[
25
]
obtained the following results.

Theorem 2.B
.

[
25
, Theorem 1.2]
Let
f
be a meromorphic function of finite order. Suppose
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta f
share
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}
IM, where
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}
are four distinct finite values. Then
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

Theorem 2.C
.

[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
Let
f
be a transcendental meromorphic function such that its order of growth is not an integer or infinite and
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
be two distinct finite values. If
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1},a_{2},\infty
CM, then
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

For further investigation, Qi et al.
[
25
]
proposed the following question in the same paper:

Question 2.A.
If we remove the condition that “
order of growth is not an integer or infinite
”, does Theorem 2.C remain valid?

In the paper, we note that the difference analogue of the logarithmic derivative lemma is crucial to the demonstration of Theorem 2.C. However, for the difference logarithmic derivative lemma for infinite order meromorphic functions, a limited work has been done (see
[
7
]
,
[
14
]
), as far as the authors are aware. Recently, the last two authors of the present paper
[
23
]
proved that Theorem 2.C holds for a finite-order meromorphic function and obtained the following result.

Theorem 2.D
.

[
23
, Theorem 2.1]
Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function such that
\rho(f)<\infty
and let
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
be two distinct finite values. If
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1},a_{2},\infty
CM, then
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

Therefore, the problem of investigating Theorem 2.D for an infinite-order meromorphic function appears to be worth considering. In this paper, we not only address this problem and provide a result that solves Question 2.A, but also improves Theorem 2.D. We now state our result:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function such that
\rho_{1}(f)<\infty
and let
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are two distinct finite values. If
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
and
\infty
CM, then
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

For entire function, we have the following result.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
f
be a non-constant entire function such that
\rho_{1}(f)<\infty
and let
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
be two distinct finite values. If
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
CM, then
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

Remark 2.1
.

The number of shared values cannot be reduced to two in Theorem
2.1
. For example,
f(z)=e^{z}
and
e^{c}=3
. Note that
\Delta_{c}f(z)=2e^{z}
. Clearly
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
0
and
\infty
CM, but
f^{(1)}\not\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

Obviously, the scope of Theorem 2.D is extended in Theorem
2.1
by considering a larger class of meromorphic function
f
such that

\rho_{1}(f)=\infty
and so Theorem
2.1
is an improvement of Theorem 2.D. But, our method does not work if
f
is a non-constant meromorphic function such that
\rho_{1}(f)=\infty
. Therefore we ask the following open question:

Question 2.1
.

If
f
is a non-constant meromorphic function such that
\rho_{1}(f)=\infty
, is it then possible to establish Theorem
2.1
?

We need the following lemmas for the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
8
, Theorem 3]
If
f
and
g
are two non-constant rational functions that share two values CM and one value IM, then
f\equiv g
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
24
]
Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function and
R(f)=\frac{P(f)}{Q(f)}
where
P(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{p}a_{k}f^{k}
and
Q(f)=\sum_{j=0}^{q}b_{j}f^{j}
are two mutually prime polynomial in
f
. If
a_{k},b_{j}\in S(f)
are such that
a_{p}\not\equiv 0
and
b_{q}\not\equiv 0
, then

T(r,R(f))=\max\{p,q\}\;T(r,f)+S(r,f).

Lemma 2.3
.

[
32
, Theorem 1.64]
Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be non-constant meromorphic functions and
f_{n+1},\ldots,f_{n+m}
be meromorphic functions such that
f_{k}\not\equiv 0\;\;(k=n+1,\ldots,n+m)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n+m}f_{i}\equiv A\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. If there exists a subset
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying
\text{mes}\;I=+\infty
such that

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n+m}N(r,0;f_{i})+(n+m-1)\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n+m}\overline{N}(r,f_{i})<(\lambda+o(1))T(r,f_{j})\;\;(r\to\infty,r\in I,j=1,2,\ldots,n),

where
\lambda<1
, then there exist
t_{i}\in\{0,1\}\;(i=1,2,\ldots,m)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}t_{i}f_{n+i}\equiv A
.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
10
, Lemma 6]
Let
f_{0},\ldots,f_{p}
be non-constant meromorphic functions such that
\Theta(\infty,f_{j})=1
,
j=0,\ldots,p
and
\sum_{j=0}^{p}\lambda_{j}f_{j}\equiv 1
, where
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

\sideset{}{{}_{j=0}^{p}}{\sum}\Theta(0,f_{j})\leq p+1-\frac{1}{p}.

Lemma 2.5
.

[
30
, Lemma 2.4]
Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function such that
\rho_{1}(f)<1
and
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. Then

T(r,f(z+c))=T(r,f)+S(r,f)\;\;\text{and}\;\;N(r,f(z+c))=N(r,f)+S(r,f).

Lemma 2.6
.

[
31
, Corollary 1]
Let
f
be a non-constant meromorphic function on
\mathbb{C}
, and let
a_{l},\ldots,a_{q}
be distinct meromorphic functions on
\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
a_{i}
are small functions with respect to
f
for all
i=1,\ldots,q
. Then we have the second main theorem,

(q-2-\varepsilon)\;T(r,f)\leq\sideset{}{{}_{i=1}^{q}}{\sum}\overline{N}(r,a_{i};f)+\varepsilon T(r,f),

for all
\varepsilon>0
and for all
r\not\in E\subset(0,+\infty)
such that
\int_{E}d\log\log r<+\infty.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

We know that
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1},a_{2},\infty
CM.
Now by the fundamental theorems, we get

\displaystyle T(r,f^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,f^{(1)})+\overline{N}(r,a_{1};f^{(1)})+\overline{N}(r,a_{2};f^{(1)})+S(r,f^{(1)})

(2.1)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\overline{N}(r,\Delta_{c}f)+\overline{N}(r,a_{1};\Delta_{c}f)+\overline{N}(r,a_{2};\Delta_{c}f)+S(r,f^{(1)})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3T(r,\Delta_{c}f)+S(r,f^{(1)}).

Similarly we have

\displaystyle T(r,\Delta_{c}f)\leq 3T(r,f^{(1)})+S(r,\Delta_{c}f).

(2.2)

Clearly from (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we get
S(r,\Delta_{c}f)=S(r,f^{(1)})
.

First we suppose
f
is a non-constant rational function. Then
f^{(1)}
is also a non-constant rational function.
Now from (
2.2
), we see that
\Delta_{c}f
is also a non-constant rational function. Then by Lemma
2.1
, we have
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
.

Next we suppose that
f
is a transcendental meromorphic function. Then
f^{(1)}
is also a transcendental meromorphic function. Now from (
2.1
), we see that
\Delta_{c}f
is also a transcendental meromorphic function.
Since
f^{(1)}
and
\Delta_{c}f
share
a_{1},a_{2},\infty
CM, there exist two entire functions
P
and
Q
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{c}f-a_{1}}{f^{(1)}-a_{1}}=e^{P}

(2.3)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta_{c}f-a_{2}}{f^{(1)}-a_{2}}=e^{Q}.

(2.4)

If
e^{P}\equiv e^{Q}
, then (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) give
\frac{\Delta_{c}f-a_{1}}{f^{(1)}-a_{1}}\equiv\frac{\Delta_{c}f-a_{2}}{f^{(1)}-a_{2}}
and so
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
. Henceforth we suppose
e^{P}\not\equiv e^{Q}
.
Now from (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we get

T(r,e^{P})\leq T(r,\Delta_{c}f)+T(r,f^{(1)})+O(1)\leq 4T(r,f^{(1)})+S(r,f^{(1)})\leq 8T(r,f)+S(r,f)

and so
\rho(P)=\rho_{1}(e^{P})\leq\rho_{1}(f)
. Since
\rho_{1}(f)<+\infty
, we have
\rho(P)=\rho_{1}(e^{P})<+\infty
. Similarly we have
\rho(Q)=\rho_{1}(e^{Q})<+\infty
.
We consider following three cases.

Case 1.
Let
P
be a polynomial and
Q
be a transcendental entire function. If
e^{P}\equiv 1
then from (
2.3
), we get
f^{(1)}\equiv\Delta_{c}f
. Hence we suppose
e^{P}\not\equiv 1
.
Note that
\rho(e^{P})=\deg(P)<+\infty
and
\rho(e^{Q})=+\infty
. Consequently
T(r,e^{P})=S(r,e^{Q})
. Note that

T(r,Q^{(1)})=m(r,Q^{(1)})=m\left(r,(e^{Q})^{(1)}/e^{Q}\right)=S(r,e^{Q}).

Now solving (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) for
f^{(1)}
, we get

\displaystyle f^{(1)}=\frac{a_{1}e^{P}-a_{2}e^{Q}+a_{2}-a_{1}}{e^{P}-e^{Q}}

and so

\displaystyle f^{(1)}(z+c)=\frac{a_{1}e^{P(z+c)}-a_{2}e^{Q(z+c)}+a_{2}-a_{1}}{e^{P(z+c)}-e^{Q(z+c)}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}f^{(1)}=(a_{2}-a_{1})\frac{e^{P}-e^{P+Q(z+c)}-e^{Q}+e^{P(z+c)+Q}-e^{P(z+c)}+e^{Q(z+c)}}{(e^{P}-e^{Q})(e^{P(z+c)}-e^{Q(z+c)})}.

(2.5)

Again from (
2.3
) and (
2.4
), we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}f=\frac{a_{2}e^{P}-a_{1}e^{Q}+(a_{1}-a_{2})e^{P+Q}}{e^{P}-e^{Q}}.

(2.6)

Differentiating (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{c}f^{(1)}=\frac{(a_{1}-a_{2})\left((P^{(1)}-Q^{(1)})e^{P+Q}+Q^{(1)}e^{2P+Q}-P^{(1)}e^{P+2Q}\right)}{\left(e^{P}-e^{Q}\right)^{2}}.

(2.7)

Let
g=e^{P}
. Then
g\not\equiv 1
and
T(r,g)=S(r,e^{Q})
.

Denote by
\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g\not=1)
the reduced counting function of those zeros of
e^{Q}-g
which are not the zeros of
g-1
. Also we denote by
\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g=1)
the reduced counting function of those zeros of
e^{Q}-g
which are the zeros of
g-1
.

Suppose
\infty
is not a Picard exceptional value of
f
.

Let
z_{p}
be a pole of
f^{(1)}
of multiplicity
p
such that
g(z_{p})\neq 1
. Then from (
3
), we see that
z_{p}
is a zero of
e^{Q}-g
of multiplicity
p
. Since

has no simple poles, it follows that

. Consequently

\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g\neq 1)\leq\frac{1}{2}N(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g\neq 1)\leq\frac{1}{2}N(r,g;e^{Q})\leq\frac{1}{2}T\left(r,e^{Q}\right)+S\left(r,e^{Q}\right).

On the other hand, we have

\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g=1)\leq N(r,1;g)\leq T(r,g)=S(r,e^{Q}).

Therefore

\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q})=\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g\neq 1)+\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q}\mid g=1)\leq\frac{1}{2}T\left(r,e^{Q}\right)+S\left(r,e^{Q}\right).

Now by Lemma
2.6
, we have

\displaystyle T(r,e^{Q})\leq\overline{N}(r,g;e^{Q})+S(r,e^{Q})\leq\frac{1}{2}T(r,e^{Q})+S(r,e^{Q}),

which is impossible.

Suppose
\infty
is a Picard exceptional value of
f
.

Let
z_{0}
be the zero of
e^{Q}-g
, i.e.,
e^{Q(z_{0})}=g(z_{0})
. Now from (
3

z_{0}
is a zero of

, which shows that

Now by Lemma
2.6
, we get
T(r,e^{Q})=S(r,e^{Q})
, which is impossible.

Case 2.
Let
P
is a transcendental entire function and
Q
be a polynomial. In this case also we get

, if we proceed in the same way as done in Case 1.

Case 3.
Let

and

Let
P
and
Q
be both constants. Set

) and (

\frac{\Delta_{c}f-a_{2}}{f^{(1)}-a_{2}}=d_{1}
.
If
c_{1}=d_{1}
, then

. Next let

. Clearly

(A)
Suppose first
P
and

Now from (

\displaystyle e^{P+Q}-e^{2Q}-e^{P+Q+Q(z+c)}+e^{P(z+c)+2Q(z)}-e^{P(z+c)+Q}+e^{Q(z+c)+Q}-e^{2P}+e^{P+Q}

\displaystyle+e^{2P+Q(z+c)}-e^{P(z+c)+P+Q}+e^{P+P(z+c)}-e^{Q(z+c)+P}

\displaystyle(P^{(1)}-Q^{(1)})e^{P(z+c)+P+Q}+Q^{(1)}e^{2P+Q+P(z+c)}-P^{(1)}e^{P+2Q+P(z+c)}

\displaystyle-(P^{(1)}-Q^{(1)})e^{Q(z+c)+P+Q}-Q^{(1)}e^{2P+Q+Q(z+c)}+P^{(1)}e^{P+2Q+Q(z+c)}

(2.9)

\displaystyle(b_{2}e^{-p_{1}}+1)e^{Q(z+c)}+(b_{1}+e^{-p_{1}})e^{-p_{1}}e^{P(z+c)}+(e^{-p_{1}}-1)(e^{-p_{1}(z+c)}-1)e^{P(z+c)-P(z)}

Fist suppose that

3.
Periodicity on a transcendental entire function

We know that, if
f(z)
is a periodic function with period
c
, then
f^{(k)}(z)
is also a periodic function with the same period
c
, but the converse is not true in general. As for example, let
f(z)=\sin z+z
. Clearly
f^{(1)}(z)
is a periodic function with period
2\pi
, but
f(z)
is not periodic with period
2\pi
. However if
f^{(k)}(z)
is a periodic function of period
c
, then
f(z)
can be expressed as
f(z)=\varphi(z)+p(z)
, where
\varphi(z)
is a periodic function of period
c
and
p(z)
is a polynomial such that
\deg(p)\leq k
. Related to the periodicity of entire functions, Wang and Hu
[
26
]
mentioned the following conjecture posed by Yang.

Yang’s Conjecture.
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and
k
be a positive integer. If
ff^{(k)}

is a periodic function, then
f
is also a periodic function.

In 2018, Wang and Hu
[
28
]
proved that
Yang’s Conjecture
is true for
k=1
by giving the following result.

Theorem 3.A
.

[
28
, Theorem 1.1]
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and
k
be a positive integer. If
(f^{2})^{(k)}

is a periodic function, then
f
is also a periodic function.

In 2019, Liu and Yu
[
15
]
further improved Theorem 3.A in a direction where
f^{2}
is replaced by a more general polynomial
a_{n}f^{n}+a_{n-1}f^{n-1}+\ldots+a_{1}f
,
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}(\neq 0)
are constants and
n\geq 2
. Now we recall their result.

Theorem 3.B
.

[
15
, Theorem 1.7]
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function such that
\rho_{1}(f)<1
and
N(r,0;f)=S(r,f)
and let
k
be a positive integer and
n\geq 2
. If
(a_{1}f+\ldots+a_{n}f^{n})^{(k)}
is a periodic function, then
f
is also a
periodic function, where
a_{n}\neq 0
.

Recently Wei et al.
[
29
]
proved that Theorem 3.B holds without the conditions “
\rho_{1}(f)<1
and
N(r,0;f)=S(r,f)
”.
Also in the same paper, Wei et al.
[
29
]
considered the problem of periodicity of
f^{n}(f^{m}-1)f^{(k)}
. Actually they obtained the following results.

Theorem 3.C
.

[
29
, Corollary 1.5]
Assume that
f
is a transcendental entire function. If

f^{n}(f^{m}-1)f^{(1)}
is a periodic function, then
f
is also a periodic function.

Theorem 3.D
.

[
29
, Theorem 1.6]
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and
n,\;m
and

k
be positive integers. If
f^{n}(f^{m}-1)f^{(k)}
is a periodic function with period
c
,
and one of following conditions is satisfied:

(i)

f(z)=e^{h(z)}
, where
h(z)
is an entire function;

(ii)

f(z)
has a non-zero Picard exceptional value and
f(z)
is of finite
order;

(iii)

f^{n}(f^{m}-1)f^{(k+1)}
is a periodic function with period
c
,

then
f(z)
is also a periodic function.

Motivated by
Yang’s Conjecture
and Theorem 3.D, Wei et al.
[
29
]
raised the following question for further research.

Question 3.A.
Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and
m,\;n
and

k
be positive integers. If
\frac{f^{(k)}}{f^{n}(f^{m}-1)}
is a periodic function with period
c
, is it true that
f
is
also a periodic function?

In the same paper, Wei et al.
[
29
]
proved that Question 3.A is solvable for the case
m=n=k=1
.

In the paper, inspired by Theorem 3.D we will try to give an affirmative answer of Question 3.A. Now we state our result.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f
be a transcendental entire function and
n,\;m
and

k
be positive integers. If
\frac{f^{(k)}}{f^{n}(f^{m}-1)}
is a periodic function with period
c
,
and one of following conditions is satisfied:

(I)

f(z)=e^{P(z)}
, where
P(z)
is a non-constant entire function;

(II)

f(z)
has a non-zero Picard exceptional value and
\rho_{1}(f)<+\infty
;

(III)

\frac{f^{(k+1)}}{f^{n}(f^{m}-1)}
is a periodic function with period
c
,

then
f(z)
is also a periodic function.

Following lemma is required in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
27
, Theorem 2]
If

is a non-constant polynomial and

is an entire
function which is not periodic, then
