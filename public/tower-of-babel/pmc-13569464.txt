Alcohol abstinence precipitates alcohol seeking and aversion-resistant intake in association with increased BNST activity
Authors: Marie A. Doyle, Hye Jean Yoon, Megan E. Altemus, Anika S. Park, Martha E. Troutman, Laura Grunenkovaite, Danielle N. Adank, Caitlyn M. Edwards, Nia A. Chetkovich, Sabrina D. Hallal, Louise Lantier, Cody A. Siciliano, Erin S. Calipari, Danny G. Winder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is defined by a common diagnostic framework, yet individuals show diverse drinking patterns and relapse vulnerabilities during abstinence, reflecting neurobiological heterogeneity. The bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) is well positioned to contribute to this variability. To understand how BNST activity co-segregates with individual alcohol behavior trajectories, we used the Structured Tracking of Alcohol Reinforcement (STAR) operant task to phenotype C57BL/6J mice as high, low, or aversion-resistant ethanol drinkers. During initial self-administration, dBNST cFos+ counts correlated with intake, linking dBNST activation to operant drinking. Using in vivo fiber photometry, we found that aversion-resistant drinkers displayed elevated dBNST calcium transients during drinking bouts despite similar intake across groups, consistent with heightened dBNST recruitment. Forced abstinence uncovered prominent phenotype-specific adaptations, where ethanol seeking during protracted, but not early, abstinence predicted aversion-resistant intake. High and low drinkers reduced seeking behavior across abstinence, whereas aversion-resistant drinkers persisted. Consistently, dBNST calcium transients increased during protracted abstinence seeking only in aversion-resistant drinkers, highlighting phenotype-specific plasticity. Comparing mice exposed to abstinence versus only operant training showed that abstinence itself potentiates aversion-resistant intake. Finally, these dBNST dynamics were ethanol-specific, as saccharin drinking more closely reflected activity for high drinkers. Together, these findings reveal that ethanol abstinence precipitates ethanol seeking and aversion-resistant intake, associated with phenotypic dBNST calcium dynamics. Because causality was not established, future studies are needed to define mechanistic contributions of dBNST activity to phenotypic behaviors. By uncovering how dBNST activity adapts in aversion-resistant drinkers, this work offers insight into AUD heterogeneity.

Introduction

Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is a chronic disease characterized by cyclic periods of alcohol intoxication, abstinence/withdrawal, and anticipation [1, 2]. Within the framework of diagnostic criteria, AUD is not uniformly experienced and, rather, diverse alcohol drinking patterns, withdrawal behaviors, and relapse vulnerabilities are observed in the clinical population [3–5]. This behavioral spectrum is recapitulated in rodent models, where individual differences in ethanol intake are associated with health and cognitive outcomes and distinct molecular signatures [6–12], underscoring the importance of animal models that capture individual variability to identify neural mechanisms contributing to AUD susceptibility and progression.

In the treatment-seeking population, periods of abstinence define a highly vulnerable portion of the AUD cycle. During this time individuals report heightened craving driven by the aversive and anxiogenic state of protracted abstinence [1, 13, 14]. The extended amygdala abstinence network is a critical regulator of abstinence-related behaviors [14]. Within this network, the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) is a key integrator of both negative valance and alcohol-related behaviors [15]. In early abstinence, altered BNST structural and functional connectivity is observed in individuals diagnosed with AUD compared to healthy controls [16, 17], including during unpredictable threat processing [18]. In rodent models of ethanol abstinence, altered BNST synaptic plasticity and transmission are observed following periods of protracted forced abstinence from ethanol [19, 20] and the BNST plays an important role in ethanol seeking [21–23]. Additionally, in rodents, heterogeneity in BNST cytoarchitecture has been linked with variability in behavior [24], positioning the BNST to participate in individual variability. Together, these findings identify the BNST as a candidate region for the expression of individual differences in ethanol-related behaviors.

In this study, we sought to determine whether BNST activity levels co-segregate with differential trajectories of alcohol intake and behavior. To identify individual differences in mouse ethanol drinking behaviors and abstinence-associated behavioral outcomes, we used an operant ethanol self-administration task known as Structured Tracking of Alcohol Reinforcement (STAR) to define subpopulations of male and female C57BL/6J mice displaying high, low, or aversion-resistant ethanol intake [8]. Given the known involvement of BNST in ethanol intake and abstinence behaviors [15, 25–28], we used in vivo fiber photometry to record calcium (GCaMP) transients as a method of assessing changes in dorsal BNST (dBNST) activity across operant ethanol intake and seeking sessions in association with behavioral phenotypes, where an abstinence period serves as the primary experimental manipulation. Collectively, our findings indicate that ethanol abstinence is associated with heightened ethanol seeking and aversion-resistant intake, accompanied by phenotype-specific dBNST calcium dynamics, highlighting BNST activity as a neural correlate of relapse vulnerability and biological diversity underlying AUD-related behaviors.

Methods

Additional methodological details are available in the supplemental materials.

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks of age) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (#000664). Mice were group housed on a reverse 12 h light-dark cycle at 22–25 °C with ad libitum water and food restricted to 90% of free feeding weight. Behavioral assays took place during the dark phase. All experiments were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and were carried out in accordance with the guidelines set in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health.

Operant oral ethanol self-administration

Operant chambers

Mouse operant behavior was run in MedAssociates chambers. Fluid levels on a retractable sipper were recorded at the beginning and end of the session to calculate consumption.

Training

Mice were trained to self-administer 20% ethanol in tap water (v/v) as described in Brown et al. [8], with the following exceptions: (1) Operant chambers contained two retractable levers, (2) 20% ethanol was used (15% ethanol was run in a single cohort, Supplemental Fig. 1), and (3) the fixed ratio (FR) schedule for the last phase of training was an FR3. Following training, responding for the active lever was highly accurate (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Ethanol exposure, progressive ratio, and forced abstinence

After training, mice self-administered 20% ethanol (v/v) in 1 h sessions on an FR5 for 14 days. A single progressive ratio session was run on day 11 [29, 30]. Next, mice began a 28 day forced abstinence period. During forced abstinence, mice remained in their home cage except for seeking tasks on abstinence (withdrawal) days 1 and 28. On these days, mice were placed back in the operant chambers and seeking was assessed with only tap water available in the sippers.

Structured Tracking of Alcohol Reinforcement (STAR) phenotyping

STAR behavioral phenotyping was performed with minor adaptations to the ethanol concentration and fixed ratio schedule [8]. Mice self-administered 20% ethanol (v/v) in 1 h sessions on an FR5 on STAR days 1–3 days before increasing concentrations of quinine (0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 mM), were added to the ethanol over the subsequent STAR days 4–7. Phenotypes were determined based on intake, as described in Brown et al. [8]. Male and female mice were combined for STAR phenotyping analysis.

Alternative STAR timeline

In one cohort, STAR was conducted immediately after training. Mice returned to self-administering 20% ethanol in a single, 1 h session. Ninety minutes after the start of this final ethanol session, mice were perfused for BNST cFos analysis.

Operant saccharin oral self-administration

Ethanol-experienced mice underwent a single 20 mM saccharin session after STAR testing. A separate cohort was trained and self-administered 2 mM saccharin under identical parameters to ethanol. Ninety minutes after the start of a saccharin session, mice were perfused for BNST cFos analysis.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and cell counts

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was run following established protocols [31]. 35 µm, fixed sections containing the BNST were incubated with Rabbit anti-cFos (Cell Signaling #2250S, 1:1000) then Cy3 Donkey anti-rabbit (Jackson #711-165-152, 1:500) and mounted with ProLong Gold. Images were acquired at 20× (Keyence BZX-800) and analyzed in ImageJ with identical settings. Cell counts were performed blind to group, averaging hemispheres per animal.

Viral-mediated gene transfer

Surgical procedure for virus injection and implant placement

Stereotaxic surgeries were performed following established procedures [19, 32–34]. A subset of mice received unilateral intra-dorsal BNST infusions (0.3 μL) of AAV9-hSyn-GCaMP7f-WPRE-SV40 (AddGene, 104488-AAV9) at established coordinates (from bregma: A/P +0.14, M/L ±0.88, D/V –4.24, 15.03° tilt). A 400 μm mono fiberoptic implant (Doric lenses) was placed 0.1 mm above the injection site and secured with dental adhesive. Mice were allowed to recover for at least 7 days before the start of operant training.

Targeting validation

At the completion of behavioral studies, GFP (GCaMP) labeling and cannula placement verified dorsal BNST targeting. Mice with off-target expression or placement were excluded from analysis.

In vivo fiber photometry

A TDT RZ5P fiber photometry system and Synapse software were used as previously described [31, 34, 35]. GCaMP transients were recorded during key operant sessions. Behavioral epochs (lever presses and individual licks) were collected as TTLs to align behavior with GCaMP transients. A drinking bout was defined as starting when a mouse licked the sipper 3 times in less than 1 s and ended when a 3 s pause occurred [8, 36]. Fiber photometry data were analyzed using custom Python scripts.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism and reported as mean ± SEM (Supplemental Table 1). Correlation analyses were specified a priori, where pearson correlations and linear regressions assessed relationships and multiple comparisons were accounted for using a False Discovery Rate (Benjamini and Hochberg). Group differences were tested with unpaired t-tests, one- or two-way (RM) ANOVAs with Tukey or Sidak post-hoc tests, and nested t-tests or ANOVAs for fiber photometry data with Tukey post-hoc tests, in which event-level measurements were treated as repeated observations nested within subjects. Post hoc statistical hypothesis testing accounted for multiple comparisons in these cases. Significance was defined as *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

cFos expression in the dorsal BNST is positively correlated with ethanol intake

To assess engagement of bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST) in operant oral ethanol self-administration, male and female C57BL/6J mice self-administered 20% ethanol via a retractable sipper [8] in a one hour session. Most mice reached binge-like blood ethanol concentrations (BECs) (>80 mg/dL) (Fig. 1A) and ethanol intake was significantly correlated to BECs (n = 12, Simple linear regression: F(1,10) = 30.66, p = 0.0002; Correlation: r = 0.87). Ninety minutes after the start of this session, mice were sacrificed and brains processed for immunohistochemistry to assess cFos+ cell counts as a marker for neuronal activation (Fig. 1B). In the dorsal BNST (dBNST), there was a positive correlation between cFos expression and ethanol intake (Fig. 1C), consistent with engagement in ethanol drinking behaviors. No correlation between cFos and ethanol intake was observed in the ventral BNST (vBNST) (Fig. 1D). Males and females did not differ in their ethanol intake during this session, with averages of 2.02 and 1.60 g/kg, respectively (Unpaired t-test, t = 0.82, df = 15, p = 0.43). Given the engagement of the dBNST during operant ethanol intake (Fig. 1), we next sought to assay dBNST activity in awake-behaving animals from an individual differences perspective. We used in vivo fiber photometry with the genetically encoded calcium indicator GCaMP7f to measure dBNST calcium transients as a proxy for neuronal activity aligned with behavioral events in a subset of mice. During the first binge session (day 1), calcium signal increased at the onset of ethanol drinking bouts (Nested t-test for pre-post event AUC, High: p = 0.003, Low: p = 0.004, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.007), consistent with dBNST activation during initiation of ethanol consumption across all STAR drinking phenotypes (Fig. 2E). Area under the curve (AUC) was greater for aversion-resistant drinkers compared to high drinkers (Fig. 2E). When calcium transients were recorded during the last binge day (day 14), aversion-resistant drinkers were the only phenotype to show a significant increase in AUC associated with the onset of an ethanol drinking bout (Nested t-test, High: p = 0.054, Low: p = 0.20, Aversion-resistant: p < 0.001), with peak height in aversion-resistant drinkers greater than high drinkers (Fig. 2F). Taken together, fiber photometry indicates that increased dBNST activity is associated with ethanol drinking across all drinking phenotypes, with aversion-resistant drinkers exhibiting persistently elevated calcium transients that distinguish them from high and low drinkers across binge sessions.

Seeking in protracted abstinence is associated with greater aversion-resistant drinking and phenotypic dBNST calcium transients

To assess changes in mouse behavior and dBNST dynamics across an abstinence period, mice underwent 28 days of forced abstinence. During this time mice remained in their home cages, except for ethanol seeking tasks on days 1 and 28 of abstinence (Fig. 3A). During seeking sessions, only tap water was available in the sipper, with lever presses and licks interpreted as seeking. Though lever pressing has traditionally defined seeking behavior in operant IV self-administration [39], the use of retractable sippers allowed us to also measure licks, more directly aligned to consummatory behaviors. When lever press counts in early abstinence (Withdrawal day 1, WD1) were correlated with average quinine-adulterated ethanol intake measured during post-abstinence STAR phenotyping, there was no correlation between seeking and future aversion-resistant drinking (Fig. 3B). However, when seeking was reassessed in protracted abstinence (Withdrawal day 28, WD28), a positive correlation between lever presses and aversion-resistant drinking emerged (Fig. 3B), demonstrating behavioral changes over the abstinence period. These correlation findings were similar but not mirrored in lick behaviors (Supplemental Fig. 4A-B), as only a trend was detected between seeking and future aversion-resistant intake. Interestingly, licks and lever presses were not correlated on WD1 but emerged as positively correlated on WD28 (Supplemental Fig. 4C), together highlighting a behavioral dissimilarity in seeking measures. Neither withdrawal day displayed a correlation between lever presses and ethanol intake measured during post-abstinence STAR (Supplemental Fig. 5B). Given the link between abstinence lever pressing and licking behaviors and quinine-adulterated ethanol drinking, we analyzed seeking data by STAR phenotype. When lever press data were analyzed by STAR phenotype, no differences were observed between phenotypes or over time (Fig. 3C). However, when lick counts were analyzed across abstinence, there was a main effect of withdrawal and an interaction of withdrawal x phenotype (Fig. 3D), again pointing to a divergence in seeking measures. Specifically, licks in high and low drinkers significantly decreased between early and protracted abstinence, with no change in aversion-resistant drinkers (Fig. 3D). These data highlight that aversion-resistant drinkers display persistent seeking behavior where the other phenotypes adapt to decrease responding. These data highlight the relevance for using licks as a measure of seeking in drinking paradigms, capturing subtle behavioral dynamics that are not detected through traditional lever-press metrics. Water intake during seeking sessions was significantly lower than prior ethanol intake (Supplemental Fig. 6A), suggesting licking was not driven by thirst alone. Finally, seeking behaviors were not different between male and female mice (Supplemental Fig. 6B-C).

Part of the extended amygdala abstinence network, the BNST undergoes synaptic plasticity during abstinence from ethanol exposure in association with behavioral changes [15]. Given the known role of the BNST in ethanol, heroin, and cocaine seeking behaviors [22, 23, 40–43], we assessed dBNST calcium transients aligned to ethanol seeking as measured by lick bouts. In early abstinence, high and aversion-resistant drinking phenotypes displayed increased dBNST activity at the onset of seeking (Nested t-test, High: p = 0.017, Low: p = 0.24, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.005), with no differences observed between groups (Fig. 3E). However, during protracted abstinence, only aversion-resistant drinkers showed dBNST activation at the onset of seeking bouts (Nested t-test, High: p = 0.71, Low: p = 0.48, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.013) and displayed significantly greater dBNST activity during seeking bouts compared to high and low drinkers in both AUC and peak height (Fig. 3F), consistent with a role for the dBNST in ethanol seeking during conditioned place preference assessment [22, 23] and highlighting phenotype-specific adaptations. Notably, these observations were not detected in dBNST activity time-locked to lever press behavior (Supplemental Fig. 5C-D), consistent with a lack of effect observed in lever pressing (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that abstinence time, used here as an experimental manipulation, is associated with phenotypic increases in dBNST activity during ethanol seeking, with elevated activity aligning with aversion-resistant drinking behavior.

STAR phenotyping reveals sex-associated distribution of drinking behaviors but similar dBNST responses across ethanol reinforcers

After forced abstinence, mice were returned to ethanol self-administration under the STAR protocol for behavioral phenotyping (Fig. 4A). STAR reliably segregates mice into high, low, or aversion-resistant drinkers, calculated as group averages of their average ethanol intake and average quinine-adulterated ethanol intake [8] (Fig. 4B). During STAR, mice phenotyped as high drinkers consumed significantly greater amounts of ethanol compared to low drinkers and when quinine was added to the ethanol, aversion-resistant mice drank significantly more ethanol compared to both high and low drinkers (Fig. 4B). We next analyzed the distribution of sexes within the STAR phenotypes and found that females largely grouped into high drinkers while males were primarily categorized as aversion-resistant drinkers (Fig. 4C). As increased dBNST calcium transients were previously observed during ethanol drinking, we measured GCaMP activity associated with the onset of an ethanol drinking bout (0 mM quinine), finding that all three drinking phenotypes displayed increased dBNST activity (Nested t-test, High: p = 0.015, Low: p = 0.012, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.046), with no statistical differences between groups (Fig. 4D). Because aversion-resistant drinking tracked with abstinent seeking and distinct dBNST dynamics (Fig. 3), we next analyzed dBNST activity associated with quinine-adulterated ethanol drinking bouts at both the lowest (0.25 mM quinine) and highest (1.0 mM quinine) doses of quinine. Interestingly, dBNST activity aligned with the onset of drinking bouts was largely not different from baseline (Nested t-test, 0.25 mM High: p = 0.068, Low: p = 0.0002, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.055; 1.0 mM High: p = 0.13, Low: p = 0.080, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.059), with no differences between groups (Fig. 4D). Together, these findings indicate that while STAR defines distinct behavioral phenotypes and reveals sex-related vulnerabilities to aversion-resistant drinking; however, the associated dBNST activity during quinine-adulterated ethanol consumption itself remains uniformly across groups rather than a characteristic of the aversion-resistant drinking phenotype.

Abstinence periods precipitate greater aversion-resistant drinking

Given the progression in seeking behavior and dBNST dynamics across abstinence periods (Fig. 3), we next asked whether abstinence reshaped ethanol consumption. We compared ethanol intake between mice exposed to forced abstinence and a separate cohort assessed immediately after operant training (Fig. 5A). In this cohort, overall behavioral effects were maintained, with high drinkers consuming more ethanol (0 mM quinine) than low drinkers, aversion-resistant drinkers consuming more quinine-adulterated ethanol, with no differences in acquisition (Fig. 5A-B). When ethanol intake was compared across the two cohorts, there was a main effect of phenotype but not abstinence experience (Fig. 5C), consistent with the presence of phenotypic drinking patterns but not indicative of abstinence-escalated intake. Similarly, no escalation was observed within the abstinence cohort when comparing ethanol intake before and after abstinence (Two-way ANOVA with RM, n = 9–10, Abstinence: F (1, 26) = 0.94, p = 0.34; Phenotype: F (2, 26) = 4.83, p = 0.016; Abstinence x phenotype: F (2, 26) = 1.59, p = 0.22). In contrast, quinine-adulterated ethanol intake was greater in abstinence-experienced mice (Fig. 5C), indicating that abstinence enhances vulnerability to aversion-resistant drinking. When intake in the training-only cohort was analyzed by sex, quinine-adulterated ethanol intake did not differ by sex, though females showed a trend toward greater intake at the lowest quinine dose (Supplemental Fig. 3B), consistent with prior reports. Saccharin drinking behaviors are associated with disparate dBNST activity patterns

As the dBNST has been implicated in broader ingestive behaviors, we next sought to determine if phenotypic dBNST activity during drinking bouts was specific to ethanol. A separate cohort of ethanol-naïve male and female C57BL/6J mice self-administered 2 mM saccharin in a one-hour session, with parameters identical to ethanol sessions. Ninety minutes after the start of this session, mice were sacrificed and brains were collected and processed for BNST cFos immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6A). Unlike ethanol intake (Fig. 1), saccharin intake did not correlate with cFos expression in either the dorsal or ventral BNST (Fig. 6B, C). To measure activity aligned to drinking bouts, we recorded in vivo dBNST GCaMP transients during saccharin drinking. Here, ethanol-experienced mice completed a single 20 mM saccharin session in the same context as previous ethanol sessions (Fig. 6D), with no differences in intake between phenotypes (One-way ANOVA, F(1,15) = 1.958, p = 0.87). Consistent with ethanol and quinine-adulterated ethanol intake (Figs. 2 and 4), we found increased calcium transients at the onset of saccharin drinking bouts (Nested t-test, High: p = 0.005, Low: p = 0.003, Aversion-resistant: p = 0.022). In contrast to ethanol data (Fig. 2E), high ethanol drinking mice emerged as a phenotype of interest, displaying a trend toward greater in dBNST activity during saccharin bouts (Fig. 6D). Discussion

The data presented in this study yield several important observations. First, they indicate that dBNST activity is associated with individual differences in ethanol consumption, with aversion-resistant drinkers showing heightened dBNST recruitment during initial ethanol exposure and ethanol seeking during a forced abstinence period. Second, they add to a literature demonstrating the importance of separately assessing reinforcer seeking and consumption, as we found that licks at the water-containing sipper during abstinence more accurately tracked with behavioral adaptions and dBNST dynamics compared to canonical lever pressing behavior. Third, they demonstrate that abstinence from ethanol intake can precipitate subsequent increases in aversion-resistant drinking and that aversion-resistant ethanol intake is not a predictor of heightened intake of other reinforcers. Taken together, these findings establish a link between ethanol drinking phenotypes and dynamic dBNST activity during abstinence, uncovering behavioral and dBNST activity adaptations in aversion-resistant drinkers and offering new insight into the biological diversity of AUD.

Ethanol drinking phenotypes reveal behavioral and neurobiological heterogeneity

Individuals can experience a wide range of symptoms under the an AUD diagnosis [4]. A recent human study tracked anxiety and depression trajectories during early abstinence in a treatment-seeking AUD population, identifying three subgroups of individuals with clinical implications for these classifications [3]. STAR leverages individual differences in ethanol behaviors to model the heterogeneity observed in the clinical population [8]. In the present study, we found that STAR subgroups display distinct patterns of ethanol consumption and correlated aversion-resistant ethanol drinking with greater ethanol seeking behaviors. Individual differences approaches have previously been used to highlight behavioral heterogeneity in preclinical AUD models. Sensitivity to aversive properties of ethanol are suggested to underly risk for AUD-like behavior [7], and behavioral flexibility [6] and anxiety [10] measures are predictive of future ethanol drinking. Further, aversion-resistant reward taking has been previously linked to greater seeking behaviors. In an operant social aggression model, mice categorized as compulsive aggressors displayed greater relapse-like behavior compared to typical aggressors [44]. Together, these findings underscore the value of individual differences approaches for capturing the behavioral heterogeneity that characterizes both clinical observations and preclinical models of AUD.

Individual differences in alcohol behaviors speak to the existence of underlying neurobiological heterogeneity. For example, high vs low ethanol preference was associated with VTA dopaminergic activity [11] and VTA dynamics were predictive of future drinking [12]. We focused on the BNST given it’s cytoarchitecture and ability to integrate a variety of inputs related to stress, reward, and negative affect [45–47], finding that drinking phenotypes associated with discrete patterns of dBNST activity. While informative, the use of bulk calcium signals and a global BNST viral strategy limits mechanistic specificity and interpretations by collapsing known subregional and cell-type heterogeneity, a limitation for the current study.

Distinct BNST subregions have been linked to behaviors [24], suggesting that key subnuclei or neuronal subpopulations may drive the observed outcomes. In the context of consummatory behaviors and negative valence signals, the oval nucleus of the dBNST (ovBNST) is of particular interest [48]. Signaling in the ovBNST, putative PKCδ-expressing neurons due to their tight localization here, contributes to compulsive sucrose intake [49] and aversive stimuli engages BNST PKCδ neurons [50]; however, their role in ethanol drinking remains unexplored. Other BNST neuronal subpopulations also modulate operant ethanol intake. Inhibition of serotonin 5HT2c receptor-expressing neurons increases ethanol intake without affecting sucrose intake and increases lever press responding during high quinine-adulteration (1.0 mM) sessions, consistent with increased ethanol seeking [51]. Corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) signaling similarly regulates operant ethanol self-administration, with knockdown of CRF decreasing ethanol intake and motivation to earn rewards without altering sucrose intake [52]. The absence of a causal relationship between dBNST activity and phenotypic behavior represents a central limitation to the present study. Establishing mechanistic relevance will require future experiments that directly manipulate dBNST neuronal subpopulations with temporal precision, such as cell type-specific chemogenetic modulation within an early abstinence critical window, to determine their contribution to the development and expression of seeking and aversion-resistant behaviors. Together, these findings highlight how the heterogeneity of the dBNST provides a future direction for investigation of neuronal populations through which the BNST can differentially shape individual patterns behavior.

Phenotype-associated dBNST activation during early ethanol intake but not quinine-resistant drinking

The dBNST is well known to participate in ingestive behaviors [49, 53–55]. In this study, we observed a positive correlation between operant ethanol intake and cFos expression in the dBNST, but not the vBNST. Ethanol bingeing has previously been associated with activity in the dBNST, where cFos expression was higher in mice that reached binge-like BECs compared to non-bingeing animals and water controls [37]. Similarly, increased BNST cFos following sucrose administration has been reported in rats [56, 57]. We did not observe a significant correlation between saccharin intake and BNST cFos in the current study; however, this may be due to a smaller sample size and a relatively narrow range of high intake across animals compared to ethanol correlation studies. Using in vivo fiber photometry, we also observed increased dBNST calcium transients aligned to ethanol, quinine-adulterated ethanol, and saccharin drinking bouts, paralleling previously reported home cage drinking dBNST calcium transients [28]. However, dBNST calcium transients were dynamic across conditions. While all STAR phenotypes showed dBNST engagement during initial ethanol intake, aversion-resistant drinkers displayed greater activity, relating heightened recruitment of this region to the aversion-resistant phenotype during early intake. However, during STAR phenotyping, no phenotype differences emerged across ethanol solutions containing 0, 0.25, or 1.0 mM quinine, indicating that dBNST activity does not scale phenotypically during aversion-resistant intake. In contrast, saccharin intake did not correlate with BNST cFos+ counts, though saccharin bouts elicited dBNST activation via GCaMP measurements, with a trend toward greater activity in high ethanol drinkers. Prior studies demonstrate that modulation of BNST neuronal activity is known to alter binge ethanol intake [25–27], operant ethanol self-administration [51], as well as saccharin and food intake [54, 58–60]. Together, these findings underscore the broad role of the dBNST in regulating ingestive behavior while reflecting phenotype-specific recruitment during early ethanol intake.

Forced abstinence precipitates phenotype-specific ethanol seeking linked to BNST activation

Abstinence following ethanol exposure represents a critical window for behavioral and neurophysiological changes observed in both humans and rodents [14, 15, 61]. In the present study we used this as the primary manipulation and found that abstinence following ethanol exposure enhanced aversion-resistant drinking when compared to mice with limited ethanol exposure. We also identified the development of a relationship between ethanol seeking and aversion-resistant ethanol intake across a forced abstinence period, with seeking on withdrawal day 28, but not day 1, predictive of future quinine-adulterated ethanol intake. Seeking behaviors measured as licks at the sipper containing water and lever presses reflected this correlation with future aversion-resistant ethanol intake, yet neither predicted future ethanol intake, underscoring behavioral specificity. Notably, when seeking was analyzed by STAR drinking phenotypes, only licking behavior displayed significant phenotype-specific behavioral changes over abstinence. These data highlight the utility of licks as a measure of seeking in operant paradigms employing sippers, in contrast to canonical use of lever pressing [39]. While operant IV self-administration (IVSA) has dominated the volitional drug-taking literature and home cage drinking has largely guided self-administration in the alcohol field, operant conditioning provides complementary advantages to home cage tasks, such as parsing appetitive versus consummatory processes, assessing motivation, and controlling drinking context [62], though, it should be noted that the development of open-source drinking and lickometer tools further extend these approaches to the home cage [36, 63–66]. Unlike lever presses, which are directly linked to drug consumption in IVSA procedures, licks immediately precede consummatory behaviors in this paradigm, positioning them as a more biologically-relevant measure of ethanol seeking. Licking during abstinence sessions should be interpreted with caution, as it may not exclusively reflect ethanol-seeking behavior. This measure could also be influenced by conditioned consummatory responses, habitual or perseverative licking patterns, or exploratory behaviors. Although licking was correlated with future aversion-resistant ethanol intake and exhibited phenotype-specific changes across abstinence, these associations do not definitively establish that the behavior reflects seeking. Together, these considerations highlight licking as a potential proxy for ethanol seeking, necessitating interpretation within the broader behavioral context.

Behavioral changes in forced abstinence periods are associated with adaptations in key brain regions [15]. In the context of dBNST activity, the present study found that mice categorized as aversion-resistant drinkers displayed persistent activation of the dBNST during ethanol seeking behaviors, with higher dBNST activity compared to low or high drinking mice. While this work provides correlational evidence linking dBNST activity to seeking behaviors, previous work has identified a causal role for BNST activity in seeking behaviors. Ethanol seeking increases dBNST cFos expression [23, 67, 68], and manipulation of dBNST activity via electrolytic lesion, pharmacological inactivation, or chemogenetic inactivation alters seeking behavior in a conditioned place preference task [22, 23]. Further, inhibition of the dBNST reduces stress-induced reinstatement of heroin and cocaine seeking [40–43]. While we did not see a correlation between cFos+ cell counts in the vBNST and ethanol intake, this region is known to play an important role in drug-seeking behaviors [21] and represents a future area of investigation. Together, our data highlight the BNST as a potentially neural correlate for relapse vulnerability in AUD.

Abstinence exposure shifts aversion-resistant drinking vulnerability

A benefit of operant self-administration is the ability to manipulate schedule and dose variables to achieve comparable intake between male and female mice, addressing documented sex differences in C57Bl/6J ethanol intake across home cage tasks [19, 20, 63, 69–72]. We modified the original STAR protocol [8] to achieve comparable ethanol intake across sexes, enabling the use of a pooled sex design for STAR behavioral testing. When data were analyzed by sex, forced abstinence precipitated greater aversion-resistant ethanol drinking in male mice, consistent with males comprising the majority of aversion-resistant STAR drinkers. This effect was not observed in training-only controls, with female mice displaying a trend toward greater aversion-resistant drinking at the lowest dose of quinine. While the literature shows greater baseline aversion-resistant ethanol drinking in female mice [73–77], our forced abstinence data parallel chronic or early life stress inducing aversion-resistant ethanol intake in males [78, 79], likening abstinence periods to a time of stress. These results are intriguing, but the detection of smaller or more subtle sex differences would require larger cohorts. Additionally, because males predominantly classify as aversion-resistant whereas females largely categorized as high drinkers, the observed phenotype-specific dBNST dynamics may reflect underlying sex differences, a limitation for interpretations. Consistent with clinical sex differences [80, 81], these findings suggest that forced abstinence may selectively enhance aversion-resistant ethanol drinking in male mice, highlighting a preliminary but intriguing sex-associated effect.

Conclusions

Through an individual variability framework, we uncovered maladaptive drinking patterns precipitated by abstinence. Using in vivo fiber photometry to measure dBNST GCaMP signals in freely moving mice, we tracked physiological dynamics throughout ethanol intake and seeking phases. Divergent dBNST calcium transients during ethanol seeking behaviors were associated with aversion-resistant drinkers. Collectively, our results link patterns of ethanol consumption with dynamic dBNST activity during abstinence, laying the groundwork for probing mechanisms of individual vulnerability in AUD.
