Abelian congruences and similarity in varieties with a weak difference term Thanks: The support of the Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council (NSERC) of Canada is gratefully acknowledged.
Ross Willard

Abstract
. This is the first of three papers motivated by the author’s desire to understand and explain “algebraically” one aspect of Dmitriy Zhuk’s proof of the CSP Dichotomy Theorem. In this paper we study abelian congruences in varieties having a weak difference term. Each class of the congruence supports an abelian group structure; if the congruence is minimal, each class supports the structure of a vector space over a division ring determined by the congruence. A construction due to J. Hagemann, C. Herrmann and R. Freese in the congruence modular setting extends to varieties with a weak difference term, and provides a “universal domain” for the abelian groups or vector spaces that arise from the classes of the congruence within a single class of the annihilator of the congruence. The construction also supports an extension of Freese’s similarity relation (between subdirectly irreducible algebras) from the congruence modular setting to varieties with a weak difference term.

1.
Introduction

Arguably the most important result in universal algebra in the last ten years is the
positive resolution to the Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) Dichotomy Conjecture,
announced independently in 2017 by Andrei Bulatov
[
2
]
and
Dmitriy Zhuk
[
36
,
37
]
.
One particular feature of Zhuk’s proof is his analysis of
“rectangular critical” subdirect products
R\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}_{1}\times\cdots\times{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
of
finite algebras
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{A}}_{n}
in certain locally finite idempotent Taylor varieties.
Zhuk showed that, in the varieties he considers,
such subdirect products generate derived relations, which he called “bridges,”
between certain meet-irreducible congruences
\rho_{i}\in\Con({\mathbf{A}}_{i})
which

R
determines.
Zhuk established a number of useful properties of his bridges and used them to
tease
out implicit linear equations in the CSP instances he considered.

Strikingly, Zhuk’s definition of “bridge” formally resembles some aspects of
Hagemann and Herrmann’s formulation
[
11
]
from 1979 of the centrality relation
on congruences of a single algebra.
Moreover, the “there-exists-a-bridge” relation, under suitable restrictions,
has an uncanny resemblance to the
“similarity” relation of Freese
[
5
]
from 1983; this latter relation is between
subdirectly irreducible algebras in a congruence modular variety, and can be viewed as an
amplification of a certain correspondence involving the centrality relation.
As well, Zhuk’s bridges arising in rectangular critical subdirect products in
locally finite idempotent Taylor varieties
resemble the “graphs of similarity” which Kearnes and Szendrei
proved arise in
rectangular critical relations of the form
R\leq{\mathbf{A}}^{n}

in congruence modular varieties

[
20
, Theorem 2.5(6)]
.

Our main goal in this paper and two companion papers
[
33
,
32
]

is to establish precise connections between Zhuk’s bridges,
centrality, and similarity, and then to apply these connections to the
study of rectangular critical relations.
In order to connect Zhuk’s bridges to similarity, we need to
first generalize the similarity relation from its original setting (congruence modular varieties)
to a broader class also containing all locally finite idempotent Taylor varieties.
For this purpose, we focus on the class of varieties “having a weak difference term.”
Here a “weak difference term” is an idempotent ternary term that specializes to a Maltsev
operation whenever it is restricted to a block of an abelian congruence.
Such terms are weak versions of terms, known as “difference terms,” that exist
and play an important role in congruence modular varieties
[
9
]
.
Weak difference terms arose organically in tame congruence theory in the 1980s, when
Hobby and McKenzie
[
13
]
showed that every locally finite Taylor has
such a term.
The property of a (general) variety having a weak difference term
was first explicitly identified in the 1990s in work of Lipparini
[
23
]
,
Kearnes
[
15
]
,
and Kearnes and Szendrei
[
19
]
,
who explored the extent to which centralizer properties in congruence modular varieties extend to
varieties with a weak difference term.
Recent work concerning varieties with a weak difference term is represented by

[
17
,
35
,
21
,
16
]
.

In this paper we show that Freese’s similarity relation
generalizes nicely to varieties with a weak difference term.
It turns out that in the abelian monolith case,
Freese’s original definition of similarity, as well as the reformulation
in
[
6
]
, does not generalize in a straightforward way.
Instead, we take as our starting point two equivalent
(in the congruence modular setting) characterizations
of similarity due to Freese –
one based on the construction from Hagemann and Herrmann’s seminal work,
the other a congruence lattice-theoretic formulation –
and show that both characterizations generalize to,
and are provably equivalent in, varieties with a
weak difference term (Theorems
4.6
and
7.8
).
We also give a third equivalent characterization using a variant
of Zhuk’s bridges, which we call “proper bridges” (Definition
7.3
).

Along the way,
we explore the abelian groups arising from the congruence classes of an abelian congruence

\theta
in an algebra in a variety having a weak difference term.
While the situation is not as well-behaved as in the congruence modular case
(see Example
8.4
), we discern some structure in Section
5
. In particular, we show that
the construction of Hagemann and Herrmann mentioned above
provides a sort of “universal domain” for the abelian groups arising from
the
\theta
-classes of
{\mathbf{A}}
within each
(0:\theta)
-class,
where
(0:\theta)
is the largest congruence centralizing

\theta
(Lemmas
5.2

and
5.4
(
3
) and Corollary
5.8
).

We also expend some energy explaining a folklore result: that
each class of an abelian congruence
\theta

(in an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
with a weak difference
term) naturally supports the structure of a left vector space over a
division ring
\mathbb{F}
, provided
\theta
is minimal. Here
\mathbb{F}
depends on

{\mathbf{A}}
and
\theta
but not on the particular
\theta
-class.
In particular, we show that if two subdirectly irreducible algebras in a
variety with a weak difference term have abelian monoliths and are similar,
then the division rings determined by their monoliths are isomorphic.

In the companion paper
[
33
]
,
we will develop Zhuk’s theory of bridges (in a slightly more general setting
than Zhuk did), and describe the precise relationships between
the objects in Zhuk’s theory, the centrality relation, similarity, and
proper bridges as defined here. In a third paper
[
32
]
, we will
study rectangular critical relations in locally finite Taylor varieties,
extending the work of Zhuk
[
37
]
and
connecting these relations to similarity (as was done by
Kearnes and Szendrei
[
20
]
in the congruence
modular setting). In particular, we will show explicitly how each such relation
can be understood as encoding
a family of “parallel” linear equations over a finite field.

A subset

\alpha
-class.

[n]
denotes
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra, an

-ary term

of

is

{\mathbf{A}}
satisfies the identity
t(x,\ldots,x)\approx x
,
and is a

satisfies an identity
of the form

t(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})\approx t(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})

and

is the variable

or

, and

\{u_{i},v_{i}\}=\{x,y\}
.
A particularly important example of a Taylor term is a

(WNU),
which is an
n
-ary idempotent term
w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with

which satisfies

w(y,x,x,\ldots,x)\approx w(x,y,x,\ldots,x)\approx w(x,x,y,\ldots,x)\approx\cdots\approx w(x,\ldots,x,y).

Another important example of a Taylor term is a

; this is a ternary
term
p(x,y,z)
satisfying the identities

(2.1)

p(x,x,y)\approx y\approx p(y,x,x).

The identities (

) are called the

. Any

(whether a term or not) satisfying them is called a

.

Before defining “weak difference term,” we recall the ternary centralizer relation on
congruences and the notion of
abelian congruences.
Given a non-empty set

, let
A^{2\times 2}
denote the set of all

2\times 2
matrics over
A
. If
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra, let

{\mathbf{A}}^{2\times 2}
denote the algebra with universe
A^{2\times 2}
which is
isomorphic to
{\mathbf{A}}^{4}
via the bijection

\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{3}\\
a_{2}&a_{4}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}).

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\varphi\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}(\theta,\varphi)
is the subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2\times 2}
consisting of
the matrices whose columns belong to
\theta
and rows belong to
\varphi
.

(2)

M(\theta,\varphi)
is the subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta,\varphi)
generated by the
set

X(\theta,\varphi):=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}c&c\\
d&d\end{pmatrix}:(c,d)\in\theta\right\}\cup\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
a&b\end{pmatrix}:(a,b)\in\varphi\right\}.

The matrices in
M(\theta,\varphi)
are called
(\theta,\varphi)
-
matrices
.

Definition 2.2
.

Suppose
\theta,\varphi,\delta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
. We say that

\varphi

centralizes
\theta
modulo
\delta
, and write

C(\varphi,\theta;\delta)
,
if any of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(1)

For every matrix in
M(\varphi,\theta)
, if one row is in
\delta
, then so is the other row.

(2)

For every matrix in
M(\theta,\varphi)
, if one column is in
\delta
, then so is the
other column.

(3)

For every term
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
and all pairs
(a_{i},b_{i})\in\varphi

(
i\in[m]
) and
(c_{j},d_{j})\in\theta
(

),

t(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}t(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{d})
, then

t(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\delta}}{{\equiv}}t(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{d})
.

More generally, if
S,T
are
tolerances
of
{\mathbf{A}}
(i.e., reflexive symmetric
subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
),
then we can define
{\mathbf{A}}(S,T)
,

,

, and the relation

in the same way; see
[
17
, §2.5]
.

Let
\theta,\delta
be congruences of an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
.
The
centralizer
(or

annihilator
)
of
\theta
modulo
\delta

We say that
\theta
is

if

holds; equivalently,

\theta\leq(0:\theta)
. More generally, if
\theta,\delta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

, then we say that

.
The following lemma expresses that centralizers behave nicely under
certain quotients and surjective

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}
are algebras in a common signature.

(1)

If
\delta^{\prime},\delta,\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta\leq\theta

(\delta/\delta^{\prime}:\theta/\delta^{\prime})=(\delta:\theta)/\delta^{\prime}

(2)

Suppose

\lambda,\mu\in\Con({\mathbf{B}})
with
\lambda\leq\mu

Then
f^{-1}((\lambda:\mu))=(f^{-1}(\lambda):f^{-1}(\mu))

Proof.

(

,

\theta=f^{-1}(\mu)

denote the isomorphism

\overline{f}:{\mathbf{A}}/\delta^{\prime}\cong{\mathbf{B}}

The corresponding isomorphism
\overline{f}:\Con({\mathbf{A}}/\delta^{\prime})\cong\Con({\mathbf{B}})

is then given explicitly by
\overline{f}(\psi/\delta^{\prime})=\varphi\iff\psi=f^{-1}(\varphi)
.
Thus

\displaystyle\overline{f}((\delta:\theta)/\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\overline{f}((\delta/\delta^{\prime}:\theta/\delta^{\prime}))

by (
1

\displaystyle=(\overline{f}(\delta/\delta^{\prime}):\overline{f}(\theta/\delta^{\prime}))

as
\overline{f}
is an isomorphism

\displaystyle=(\lambda:\mu),

which proves
(\delta:\theta)=f^{-1}((\lambda:\mu))
.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a variety,
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}

(1)

(a,b)\in\theta
.

(2)

is a

if it is a weak difference term for

.

Note in particular that if

is a weak difference term for

and

is
an abelian congruence,
then setting

in (

) gives that

to any

Because the definition of “weak difference term” is not given in terms of identities,
it can happen that an
algebra has a weak difference term, but does not belong to any
variety having a weak difference term.
Therefore,
we will take care to distinguish “having a weak difference term” from
the stronger “belonging to a variety with a weak difference term.”

At the level of varieties, the property of
having a weak difference term can be
characterized by the existence of idempotent terms satisfying a certain
pattern of identities, i.e., by an
idempotent Maltsev condition
,
as shown by Kearnes and Szendrei
[

, Theorem 4.8]
.
(See Theorem
8.1

for a slight improvement.)
In the context of locally finite varieties, the situation is simpler.

Theorem 2.5
.

For a locally finite variety
\mathcal{V}
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{V}
has a Taylor term.

(2)

\mathcal{V}
has a WNU term.

(3)

\mathcal{V}
has a weak difference term.

Proof.

This follows by combining
[
13
, Theorem 9.6]
,

[
30
, Corollary 5.3]
,
[

, Theorem 2.2]
,
and
[

, Theorem 4.8]
.
∎

Lemma

d(x,x,y)\approx y

The proof of Proposition

.
This is the requirement on a congruence

The converse implication is also true if

3.
Tools

In this section we gather some technical results
which will be used in later sections.
We start with results about algebras having a weak difference term.

The first result is an easily proved generalization of the
well-known fact (see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 4.65(1)]
)
that if an algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
has a Maltsev term, then
every reflexive subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
is a congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
(A proof can be easily extracted from
the proof of
[
17
, Corollary 6.22]
.)

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\rho
is a reflexive subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
.
Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
has a ternary
term
d(x,y,z)
such that for all
(a,b)\in\rho
we have

d(a,a,b)=b
and
d(a,b,b)=a
.
Then
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra with a weak difference term,
\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

is abelian, and
\theta
is minimal. Then for all
(a,b),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in\theta
,
if
a\neq b
then there exists a unary polynomial
f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{A}})

satisfying
f(a)=a^{\prime}
and
f(b)=b^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\rho=\{(f(a),f(b))\,:\,\text{$f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{A}})$}\}
. Then

\rho
is a reflexive
subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}

satisfying
0_{A}\neq\rho\subseteq\theta
.
Hence
\rho\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
by Lemma
3.1
, and thus
\rho=\theta

as
\theta
is minimal.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose the algebra
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term and
\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
. If
\theta
is abelian, then
\theta
satisfies the
two-term condition.

Proof.

See Lipparini’s proof of
[
23
, Theorem 3.1(a)]
.
His Theorem 3.1(a) assumes
that
{\mathbf{A}}
belongs to a variety with a difference term, but in fact all
the argument requires is that
{\mathbf{A}}
itself have a weak difference term.
∎

As a consequence, a well-known property of
difference terms in congruence modular varieties due to Gumm

[
9
]
partially extends to algebras with weak difference terms.

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra with a weak difference term
d(x,y,z)
, and

\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is an abelian congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Then for every polynomial operation
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and all

a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\in A

(1\leq i\leq n)
with
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}b_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}c_{i}
,

d(t(\mathbf{a}),t(\mathbf{b}),t(\mathbf{c}))=t(d(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}),\ldots,d(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n})).

Proof.

Obviously
t(\mathbf{a}),t(\mathbf{b}),t(\mathbf{c})
belong to a common
\theta
-class.
Define polynomial operations
r(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=d(t(\mathbf{a}),t(\mathbf{x}),t(\mathbf{y}))
and

s(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=t(d(a_{1},x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,d(a_{n},x_{n},y_{n}))
.
Then

\begin{pmatrix}r(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})&r(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})\\
r(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b})&r(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}t(\mathbf{b})&t(\mathbf{c})\\
t(\mathbf{a})&d(t(\mathbf{a}),t(\mathbf{b}),t(\mathbf{c}))\end{pmatrix}=:M_{1}

while

\begin{pmatrix}s(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})&s(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{c})\\
s(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b})&s(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}t(\mathbf{b})&t(\mathbf{c})\\
t(\mathbf{a})&t(d(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}),\ldots,d(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}))\end{pmatrix}=:M_{2}.

Obviously
M_{1},M_{2}
are
(\theta,\theta)
-matrices, and since they agree
at all positions except one, they form an instance of the two-term condition
for
\theta
. As
\theta
is abelian and
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term,

\theta
satisfies the two-term condition by
Lemma
3.3
.
Thus the lower-right entries of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are equal, as desired.
∎

for a short proof).
∎

While an algebra may have many different weak difference terms, they all
agree whenever restricted to a class of an abelian congruence.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta
is
an abelian congruence. If
d(x,y,z)
and
t(x,y,z)
are two weak difference
terms for
{\mathbf{A}}
, then for every
\theta
-class
B
we have
that
d(a,b,c)=t(a,b,c)
whenever
a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}c
.

Proof.

Again this is a well-known consequence of
(B,d{\upharpoonright}_{B^{3}})
being
an abelian Maltsev operation. For
a,b,c\in B
, Proposition
3.4

yields

d(t(a,b,b),t(b,b,b),t(b,b,c))=t(d(a,b,b),d(b,b,b),d(b,b,c))

which simplifies to
d(a,b,c)=t(a,b,c)
.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term,
\theta
is an abelian
congruence, and
e\in A
.
We let
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
denote the abelian group with universe
e/\theta
,
zero element
e
, and group operation
x+y:=d(x,e,y)
,
where
d
is a weak difference term.

The operation
+
in the previous definition does not depend
on the choice of the weak difference term, by Lemma
3.6
.
The notation
M(\theta,e)
has been used in
[
6
]
and
elsewhere for
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
.
In case
{\mathbf{A}}
is finite and
\theta
is a minimal congruence, more
can be said about the groups
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
. The following result
is possibly folklore; we include it here for completeness.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra with a weak difference term,

\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is abelian, and
0\prec\theta
.
There exists a prime
p
such that for all
e\in A
,

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
is an elementary abelian
p
-group.
Hence every
\theta
-class has cardinality
p^{k}
for some
k\geq 0

(where
k
can depend on the
\theta
-class).

Proof sketch.

This was proved by Freese
[
5
, §4]
in the congruence
modular case, and essentially the same proof works here. We recast
his argument using tools from
a recent paper of Mayr and Szendrei
[
25
]
. (See also
the arguments in Section
6
, where a generalization is proved.)

First note that we can
assume that
{\mathbf{A}}
has no nullary operations (by replacing each nullary operation with
a constant unary operation).
Let
{\mathbf{I}}={\mathbf{A}}/\theta
, and let
\nu_{\theta}:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{I}}
be the natural map.
The pair
({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
is called an
algebra over
{\mathbf{I}}
in

[
25
, Definition 2.1]
. By an old construction of Novotný

[
28
]
,
one can obtain a multisorted algebra
{\mathbf{M}}=\mathfrak{M}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})

whose sort domains are the
\theta
-classes of
{\mathbf{A}}
; then applying another old
construction of Gardner
[
7
]
, one can convert
{\mathbf{M}}
to a one-sorted
algebra whose universe is the Cartesian product of the

\theta
-classes of
{\mathbf{A}}
. Call this final algebra
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
.
For precise definitions, see
[
25
, Propositions 2.2 and 2.5]
.
Because
\theta
is minimal and abelian, it follows from

[
25
, Corollary 3.5 and Theorem 3.6]
that
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})

is simple and abelian. And because
{\mathbf{A}}
has a term
d(x,y,z)
whose restriction
to each
\theta
-class is a Maltsev operation, it follows from the definitions
and
[
25
, Remark 2.8]
that
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
has a Maltsev
term. In fact, writing the universe of
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
as

B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}
where

B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
is an enumeration of the
\theta
-classes,
then the Maltsev operation
m(x,y,z)
of
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})

is given coordinatewise by
d
.

Since
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
is simple, abelian, and has a Maltsev
term, it follows
that
\mathfrak{C}({\mathbf{A}},\nu_{\theta})
is polynomially equivalent to
a (finite) simple module by a theorem of Gumm
[
8
]

and Herrmann
[
12
]
.
If we fix a
k
-tuple
\mathbf{e}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})\in B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}
,
then we can assume that
\mathbf{e}
is the zero element of this module and
addition is given by
x+y=m(x,\mathbf{e},y)
. By Schur’s Lemma and finiteness,
the endomorphism ring of this module is a finite field
\mathbb{F}
,
say of cardinality
p^{m}
. It follows that the abelian group

(B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k},+,\mathbf{e})
inherits
the structure of a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
, which forces

px=\mathbf{e}
for all
x\in B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{k}
.
Interpreting this equation coordinatewise gives that each group

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e_{i})
satisfies

px=e_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,k
as required.
∎

Note that if
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,p
are as in the statement of Theorem
3.8
, then every
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
can be viewed as a vector space
over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. In fact,
Freese’s proof of Theorem
3.8
(in the congruence modular
case) actually showed that each
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e_{i})
carries the structure
of a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
. Freese proved something similar when
{\mathbf{A}}
is
infinite: there is a division
ring
\mathbb{F}
determined by
{\mathbf{A}}
and the abelian minimal congruence
\theta

such that every
\theta
-class inherits the structure of a left

\mathbb{F}
-vector space. We will show
in Section
6
that Freese’s proof carries through
assuming only that
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term.

The next lemma will be needed in Section
7
.
Its proof, in the congruence modular case,
essentially can be extracted from the proof of

[
6
, Theorem 10.11]
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an with a weak difference term,

\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
, and
\theta
is abelian.
Let
D_{1},D_{2}
be subuniverses of
{\mathbf{A}}
which are
transversals for
\theta
.
Then there exists an automorphism
\sigma
of
{\mathbf{A}}
satisfying

\sigma(D_{1})=D_{2}
and
\sigma(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}x
for all
x\in A
.

Proof.

For
i=1,2
let
\pi_{i}:A\rightarrow D_{i}
be the unique map given by

\pi_{i}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}x
for all
x\in A
, and note
that
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are retractions of
{\mathbf{A}}
with kernel
\theta
.
Let
d(x,y,z)
be a weak difference term for
\mathcal{V}
and
define
\sigma:A\rightarrow A
and
\tau:A\rightarrow A
by

\displaystyle\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=d(x,\pi_{1}(x),\pi_{2}(x))

\displaystyle\tau(x)

\displaystyle=d(x,\pi_{2}(x),\pi_{1}(x)).

Observe first that
\sigma(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}\tau(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}d(x,x,x)=x
for all
x\in A
.
Fix a
\theta
-class
B
; by construction, there exist
b,c\in B

such that
\pi_{1}(x)=b
and
\pi_{2}(x)=c
for all
x\in B
.
Pick an abelian group operation
+
on
B
such that
d(x,y,z)=x-y+z

for all
x,y,z\in B
. Then

\sigma(x)=x-b+c
and
\tau(x)=x-c+b
for all
x\in B
, and since this
is true for every
\theta
-class, we get
\sigma\circ\tau=\tau\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}_{A}
, proving
\sigma
is a bijection. Our aim is to prove
that
\sigma
is the desired automorphism.

Let
f
be an
n
-ary basic operation. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A
and
put
a=f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
; we need to prove

\sigma(a)=f(\sigma(a_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(a_{n}))
.
Let
b_{i}=\pi_{1}(a_{i})
and
c_{i}=\pi_{2}(a_{i})
, and note that
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
belong
to a common
\theta
-class
B_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. Let

b=f(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
and
c=f(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
, and note that
b\in D_{1}
and

c\in D_{2}
(since
D_{1},D_{2}\leq{\mathbf{A}}
), so
\pi_{1}(a)=b
and
\pi_{2}(a)=c
.
Thus what must be shown is

d(f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}),f(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}),f(c_{1}\ldots,c_{n}))=f(d(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1}),\ldots,d(a_{n},b_{n},c_{n}))

given that
a_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}b_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}c_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
.
This follows from Proposition
3.4
.
Hence
\sigma
is an automorphism of
{\mathbf{A}}
.

Finally, suppose
a\in D_{1}
. Then
\pi_{1}(a)=a
, so

\sigma(a)=d(a,a,\pi_{2}(a))=\pi_{2}(a)\in D_{2}
. This proves
\sigma(D_{1})\subseteq D_{2}
. A similar argument shows
\tau(D_{2})\subseteq D_{1}
,
so
\sigma(D_{1})=D_{2}
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
, and

.

(1)

, then

.

]
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,

, and

with

. If

is a modular lattice of permuting equivalence

Proof.

This is a special case of

17
, Theorem 6.16]
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
, and

\sigma,\tau,\delta,\varepsilon\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with

and

and

.

(1)

\tau/\sigma
is abelian if and only if

is abelian.

(2)

If

is abelian, then the map

given by

Proof.

(

) follows from
[

, Theorem 3.27]

]

, Theorem 6.24]
.
∎

Our last result in this section generalizes
[
14
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,

{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
, and
\delta,\varepsilon,\sigma,\tau\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

with
\delta\leq\varepsilon
and
\sigma\leq\tau
where

(\sigma,\tau)\nearrow(\delta,\varepsilon)
.

(1)

If
\varepsilon/\delta
is abelian, then

\varepsilon=\delta\circ\tau\circ\delta
and

(\delta:\varepsilon)=(\sigma:\tau)
.

(2)

If
\delta\prec\varepsilon
,
then
(\delta:\varepsilon)=(\sigma:\tau)
.

Proof.

(
1
)
We have
C(\varepsilon,\varepsilon;\delta)
by hypothesis, and hence

C(\tau,\tau;\delta)
by monotonicity.
Then
\varepsilon=\tau\vee\delta=\delta\circ\tau\circ\delta
by
Proposition
3.10
(
1
).

\delta\wedge\tau=\sigma
implies
C(\delta,\tau;\sigma)
by e.g.
[
17
, Theorem 2.19(8)]
, proving

(\sigma:\tau)\geq\delta
. Thus to prove

(\delta:\varepsilon)=(\sigma:\tau)
,
it is enough to show
C(\alpha,\varepsilon;\delta)\iff C(\alpha,\tau;\sigma)
for all
\alpha\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\alpha\geq\delta
.
We have
C(\alpha,\tau;\sigma)\iff C(\alpha,\tau;\delta)
by

[
17
, Theorem 2.19(4)]
, since
\tau\wedge\sigma=\tau\wedge\delta
.
Thus we only need to show
C(\alpha,\varepsilon;\delta)\iff C(\alpha,\tau;\delta)
.
The forward implication follows immediately by monotonicity.
Conversely, suppose
C(\alpha,\tau;\delta)
.
Then
C(\alpha,\delta\circ\tau\circ\delta;\delta)
by
[
17
, Theorem 2.19(3)]
, i.e.,

C(\alpha,\varepsilon;\delta)
.

(
2
)
If
\varepsilon/\delta
is abelian, then we can apply item (
1
).
Assume
\varepsilon/\delta
is nonabelian.
Then
\tau/\sigma
is also nonabelian by Proposition
3.12
(
1
).
As in the proof of (
1
), we only need to prove

C(\alpha,\tau;\delta)\implies C(\alpha,\varepsilon;\delta)
for all
\alpha\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

with
\alpha\geq\delta
.
Assume
C(\alpha,\tau;\delta)
. If
\alpha\geq\varepsilon
, then
C(\tau,\tau;\delta)

by monotonicity, which contradicts nonabelianness of
\tau/\sigma
. Thus

\alpha\ngeq\varepsilon
. Since
\alpha\geq\delta
and
\delta\prec\varepsilon
, we
get
\alpha\wedge\varepsilon=\delta
and hence
C(\alpha,\varepsilon;\delta)
as required.
∎

4.
Generalizing an old construction

In this section we revisit an old construction of
Hagemann and Herrmann
[
11
]
which they used to
characterize the centrality relation in congruence modular varieties.
Freese
[
5
]

adapted the construction to give a clean characterization of his similarity relation
in congruence modular varieties.
We will show that the construction
adapts nicely to varieties with a weak difference term, and we will use it in Section
7

to define similarity in that setting.

In the following definition, we follow the notation of

[
6
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\varphi\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})

with
\theta\leq\alpha
.

(1)

{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
denotes
\theta
viewed as a subalgebra of
{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
.

(2)

\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}
denotes
the congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
generated by
\{((a,a),(b,b))\,:\,\text{$(a,b)\in\varphi$}\}
.

The next notations depart from those in
[
6
]
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,\varphi
be as in Definition
4.1
.

(1)

\operatorname{pr}_{1},\operatorname{pr}_{2}:{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
denote the two projection homomorphisms.

(2)

\eta_{i}=\ker(\operatorname{pr}_{i})\in\Con({\mathbf{A}}(\theta))
for
i=1,2
.

(3)

If
\alpha\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
with
\alpha\geq\theta
, then
\overline{\alpha}
denotes

\overline{\alpha}=\{((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2}))\in A(\theta)\times A(\theta)\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},b_{1})\in\alpha$}\}.

Note that in the context of Definition
4.2
we have

\overline{\alpha}\in\Con({\mathbf{A}}(\theta))
and

\overline{\alpha}
is also given by

\overline{\alpha}=\{((a_{1},a_{2}),(b_{1},b_{2}))\in A(\theta)\times A(\theta)\,:\,\text{$(a_{2},b_{2})\in\alpha$}\}
.
Also observe that for each
i=1,2
,
\Con({\mathbf{A}})
is naturally isomorphic to
the interval
I[\eta_{i},1]
in
\Con({\mathbf{A}}(\theta))

via
\operatorname{pr}_{i}^{-1}
,
and
\alpha
maps to
\overline{\alpha}
under either isomorphism; see Figure
1
.

It will be useful to view
\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}
as a set of

(\theta,\varphi)
-matrices. Conversely, it will be useful to view

M(\theta,\varphi)
as a tolerance of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
.

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\varphi\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

(1)

Let
\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)
denote the subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta,\varphi)

“corresponding to”
\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}

\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}:((a,b),(c,d))\in\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}\right\}.

(2)

Let
M_{\theta,\varphi}
denote the subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\times{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
“corresponding to”
M(\theta,\varphi)
:

M_{\theta,\varphi}=\left\{((a,b),(c,d)):\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}\in M(\theta,\varphi)\right\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra and
\theta,\varphi\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
.

(1)

\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}
is the transitive closure of of
M_{\theta,\varphi}
.
Thus

\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)
is the “horizontal transitive closure of
M(\theta,\varphi)
.”

(2)

\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)
is “vertically symmetric.” I.e.,

\mbox{If~}\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi),~\mbox{then}~\begin{pmatrix}b&d\\
a&c\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi).\\

(3)

If
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term and
\varphi
is abelian,
then
\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)=M(\theta,\varphi)
.

(4)

If
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term and
\theta
is abelian, then

\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)
is “vertically transitive.” I.e.,

If

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
b&d\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}b&d\\
r&s\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi),~\mbox{then}~\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\
r&s\end{pmatrix}\in\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi).

Proof.

(
1
)

M_{\theta,\varphi}
is a reflexive, symmetric subuniverse of

{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\times{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
.

(
2
)
This follows from (
1
) and
the fact that the generating set
X(\theta,\varphi)
for

M(\theta,\varphi)
is vertically symmetric.

(
3
)
Let
\rho=M_{\theta,\varphi}
; then
\rho
is a reflexive
subuniverse of
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\times{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
.
If
\varphi
is abelian and
{\mathbf{A}}
has a weak difference term

d
, then

interpreted coordinatewise
in
{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)
satisfies the
hypothesis of Lemma
3.1
for
\rho
.
Thus
\rho
is transitive by Lemma
3.1
,
implying
\Delta_{\theta,\varphi}=M_{\theta,\varphi}
and
thus
\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)=M(\theta,\varphi)
.

(

)
We can consider
\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)
as a binary relation on
the set of its rows. As such, it is a reflexive subuniverse
of
{\mathbf{A}}(\varphi)\times{\mathbf{A}}(\varphi)
, and if

\theta
is abelian, a weak difference term for
{\mathbf{A}}
, interpreted
coordinatewise in
{\mathbf{A}}(\varphi)
, will
satisfy the hypothesis of
Lemma
3.1
for this relation.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Assuming the hypotheses of Lemma

(

\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\varphi)

generated by

The next theorem has its roots in
[

.

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,

{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\theta
is a nonzero abelian congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
,
and
\alpha=(0:\theta)
.
Let
\Delta=\Delta_{\theta,\alpha}
,
let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
be the kernels of the two projection maps

\operatorname{pr}_{1},\operatorname{pr}_{2}:{\mathbf{A}}(\theta)\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
, and let
\varepsilon=\overline{\theta}\wedge\Delta
.
Then:

(1)

For any
a\in A
,
(a,a)/\Delta=\{(b,b)\,:\,\text{$(a,b)\in\alpha$}\}
.

(2)

\Delta<\overline{\alpha}
and
(\Delta:\overline{\alpha})=\overline{\alpha}
.

(3)

\overline{\theta}
is abelian, and
the interval
I[0,\overline{\theta}]
in
\Con({\mathbf{A}}(\theta))
is a modular lattice
consisting of permuting congruences.

(4)

\{0,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\varepsilon,\overline{\theta}\}
is a sublattice
of
I[0,\overline{\theta}]
isomorphic to
{\mathbf{M}}_{3}
.

(5)

Each of
(\varepsilon,\overline{\theta}),(0,\eta_{1}),(0,\eta_{2})
transposes up
to
(\Delta,\overline{\alpha})
.

(6)

If
\theta
is minimal, i.e.,
0\prec\theta
, then
the interval
I[0,\overline{\theta}]
has height
2
,
\Delta\prec\overline{\alpha}
,
and
\Delta
is completely meet-irreducible.

Proof.

(
1
) follows from the fact that
\alpha
centralizes
\theta
.
In
\Con({\mathbf{A}}(\theta))
we have
\eta_{1}\wedge\eta_{2}=0

and
\eta_{i}<\overline{\theta}
for
i=1,2
.
Clearly
\Delta\leq\overline{\alpha}
.
Item (
1
) and
\theta\neq 0
imply
\overline{\theta}\nleq\Delta
and hence

\Delta<\overline{\alpha}
and
\varepsilon<\overline{\theta}
.
If
\varphi
is any congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
satisfying

\theta\leq\varphi\leq\alpha
, then we can show that

(\Delta\wedge\overline{\varphi})\vee\eta_{1}=(\Delta\wedge\overline{\varphi})\vee\eta_{2}=\overline{\varphi}
. Indeed, if

5.
The classes of an abelian congruence

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra in a variety
\mathcal{V}

with a weak difference term,

\theta
is an abelian congruence of
{\mathbf{A}}
, and
\alpha=(0:\theta)
.
Assuming
\mathcal{V}
is congruence modular,
a classical result of Gumm
[
9
, see 3.3]

states that
if
B,B^{\prime}
are two
\theta
-classes contained in a common
\alpha
-class,
then
|B|=|B^{\prime}|
and in fact

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
and
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e^{\prime})
are isomorphic for any

e\in B
and
e^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
.
This result extends to varieties with a difference term, as we shall see
in Corollary
5.3
below.
However it fails in general for varieties with a weak difference term, and in
Section
8
we will show just how badly it can fail there.

Nonetheless, we shall show in this section that the algebra

D({\mathbf{A}},\theta)
defined in the previous section, with its abelian congruence

\varphi=\overline{\alpha}/\Delta
satisfying
(0:\varphi)=\varphi
, has the following property:
for every
\alpha
-class
C
, the abelian group
\operatorname{Grp}_{D({\mathbf{A}},\theta)}(\varphi,0_{C})
forms a sort
of “universal domain” for all the groups
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
with
e\in C
.

Definition 5.1
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra in a variety
\mathcal{V}
with a weak difference term,

\theta
is an abelian congruence,
\alpha=(0:\theta)
,
\Delta=\Delta_{\theta,\alpha}
, and
\varphi=\overline{\alpha}/\Delta
.
Given
e\in A
, the map
\chi_{e}:e/\theta\rightarrow D({\mathbf{A}},\theta)
is defined by

\chi_{e}(x)=(x,e)/\Delta
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},\mathcal{V},\theta,\alpha,\Delta,\varphi
are as in Definition
5.1
.

(1)

For all
e\in A
, the map
\chi_{e}

is an embedding of

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
into
\operatorname{Grp}_{D({\mathbf{A}},\theta)}(\varphi,0_{e/\alpha})
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{V}
has a difference term, then
\chi_{e}
is an
isomorphism.

Proof.

(
1
)
Let
C=e/\alpha
.
Clearly
\chi_{e}(e)=0_{C}
. If
(x,e)/\Delta=(y,e)/\Delta
, then

x,y,e
all belong to a common
\theta
-class.
So

\displaystyle(x,e)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(y,e),\quad(y,e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(y,e),\quad(y,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(y,y).

Applying the weak difference term
d
to these pairs and using
(x,y),(y,e)\in\theta
gives

(x,y)=(d(x,y,y),d(e,e,y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(d(y,y,y),d(e,e,y))=(y,y).

By Theorem
4.6
(
1
), we get

x=y
. Thus
\chi_{e}

is injective. It is easily checked that
\chi_{e}
preserves
+
.

(
2
)
Suppose that
d(x,y,z)
is a difference term, i.e.,
\mathcal{V}\models d(x,x,y)\approx y
.
Given
(r,s)/\Delta\in 0_{C}/\varphi
, so
(r,s)\in\theta\cap C^{2}
,
define
a:=d(r,s,e)
. Then

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\theta}}{{\equiv}}d(r,r,e)=e
, so
a\in e/\theta
. Observe that

\begin{pmatrix}r&r\\
s&s\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}s&s\\
s&s\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}s&e\\
s&e\end{pmatrix}\in M(\theta,\alpha).

Also observe that
d(r,s,s)=r
as
(r,s)\in\theta
.
Thus applying
d(x,y,z)
to the above matrices gives

\begin{pmatrix}r&a\\
s&e\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}d(r,s,s)&d(r,s,e)\\
d(s,s,s)&d(s,s,e)\end{pmatrix}\in M(\theta,\alpha)\subseteq\Delta_{\mathsf{h}}(\theta,\alpha).

Hence

(r,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(a,e)
,
so
\chi_{e}(a)=(r,s)/\Delta
, proving
\chi_{e}
is surjective.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra in a variety with a difference term,

\theta

\alpha=(0:\theta)
, and
(e,e^{\prime})\in\alpha

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)\cong\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e^{\prime})

By Lemma

,
\chi_{e^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ\chi_{e}
is an isomorphism.

Lemma 5.4
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},\mathcal{V},\theta,\alpha,\Delta

.

(1)

(a,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(d(a,b,e),e)

(2)

(3)

Proof.

(
1
)
Let

d

\begin{pmatrix}x&a\\

(

) This is proved similarly to (

by applying

By (

3.2
,
there exists a unary polynomial
f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{B}})

with
f(b^{\prime})=u
and
f(e)=e
. Choose a
(1+n)
-ary term
t(x,\mathbf{y})
and
\mathbf{c}\in A^{n}

so that
f(x)=t(x,\mathbf{c})
. Let
u^{\prime}=t(b,0,\ldots,0)
. We have the following:

(b^{\prime},e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(b,0),\quad(c_{1},c_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(0,0),\quad\ldots,\quad(c_{n},c_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(0,0)

where the first congruence holds because
\chi_{e}(b^{\prime})=\chi_{0}(b)
while the
remaining congruences are because
(0:\theta)=1
.
Now applying
t
coordinatewise and using idempotency gives

(u,e)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{\equiv}}(u^{\prime},0)

which contradicts our choice of
u
.
∎

6.
Abelian minimal congruences

In this section we will explore further the groups
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)

where
{\mathbf{A}}
is an algebra with a weak
difference term,
\theta
is an abelian congruence, and

\theta
is
minimal
, i.e.,
0\prec\theta
.
Under this added assumption of minimality,
we explain in detail how to construct a division ring

\mathbb{F}
and equip each
\theta
-class with the structure of a
left vector space over
\mathbb{F}
.
The construction of
\mathbb{F}
was sketched by Freese
in
[
5
, Section 4]

assuming
{\mathbf{A}}
belongs to a congruence modular variety.
We describe the construction in somewhat more detail here, so that it will be
apparent that congruence modularity is not needed.
In case
{\mathbf{A}}

belongs to a variety with a weak difference term, we compare
\mathbb{F}
to
the division ring similarly obtained for

D({\mathbf{A}},\theta)
and its monolith, and show that they are isomorphic.
We will not need these results in Section
7
on similarity;
however, they will be used in an essential way in our subsequent paper on
rectangular critical relations
[
32
]
.

Notation 6.1
.

Throughout this section, let
{\mathbf{A}}
be an algebra with a weak difference term,
and let
\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{A}})
be minimal and abelian.
Let the
\theta
-classes be indexed by a set
I
via the notation

A/\theta=\{B_{i}\,:\,\text{$i\in I$}\}
. Let
T
be a transversal
for
\theta
. For each
i\in I
let
0_{i}
denote the unique element
in
B_{i}\cap T
.

(1)

For all
i,j\in I
,
R_{ij}
denotes the set of restrictions to
B_{j}
of
unary polynomials of
{\mathbf{A}}
which send
0_{j}
to
0_{i}
. (Thus
R_{ij}
is a set
of functions from
B_{j}
to
B_{i}
.)

(2)

\mathbb{F}
denotes the set of all indexed families
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}
such that

(a)

\lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{End}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))
for each
i\in I
; and

(b)

for all
i,j\in I
and
r\in R_{ij}
we have
\lambda_{i}\circ r=r\circ\lambda_{j}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,T,(B_{i})_{i\in I},(0_{i})_{i\in I},(R_{ij})_{i,j\in I}

and
\mathbb{F}
be as in Notation
6.1
.

(1)

For all
i,j\in I
,
R_{ij}
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{j}),\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))
.

(2)

For all
i,j,k\in I
, if
r\in R_{ij}
and
s\in R_{jk}
, then
r\circ s\in R_{ik}
.

(3)

For all
i\in I
,
R_{ii}
is a unital subring of
\operatorname{End}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))
.

(4)

\mathbb{F}
is a unital subring of
\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{End}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))

and is a division ring.

Proof sketch.

(
1
)
Let
d
be a weak difference term for
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Fix
i,j\in I
.
Let
+_{i}
and
+_{j}
denote the group operations of
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})
and

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{j})
respectively. Let
G
be the group

\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{j}),\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))
.
Let
O_{ij}
be the constant map
B_{j}\mapsto\{0_{i}\}
.
Clearly
O_{ij}\in R_{ij}
so
R_{ij}\neq\varnothing
.
Fix
r\in R_{ij}
. Then for all
x,y\in B_{j}
,

r(x+_{j}y)=r(d(x,0_{j},y))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{=}}d(r(x),r(0_{j}),r(y))=r(x)+_{i}r(y)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{=}}
is by Proposition
3.4
.
This proves
R_{ij}\subseteq G
.
Now suppose
r,s\in R_{ij}
and let
t=r+s
in the group
G
.
Then for all
x\in B_{j}
,
t(x)=d(r(x),0_{i},s(x))
, so
t
is the
restriction to
B_{j}
of a unary polynomial of
{\mathbf{A}}
, proving
t\in R_{ij}
.

(
2
) is obvious. (
3
) follows from
(
1
) and (
2
) and the fact that
\mathrm{id}_{B_{i}}\in R_{ii}
.

(
4
)
Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of
I\times I
matrices
(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
where

r_{ij}\in R_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
,
and for each
j\in I
,
r_{ij}=O_{ij}
for all but finitely many
i\in I
.
Because of items (
1
)–(
3
), we can turn
\mathcal{R}

into a unital ring in the usual “matrix” way: addition is defined
coordinatewise in the groups
(R_{ij},+)
, and multiplication is defined by

(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\cdot(s_{jk})_{j,k\in I}:=(t_{ik})_{i,k\in I}
where

t_{ik}:=\sum_{j\in I,\,s_{jk}\neq O_{jk}}r_{ij}\circ s_{jk}\quad\mbox{computed in $(R_{ik},+)$}.

Let
V
denote the direct sum of the abelian groups

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})
,
i\in I
,
realized as the subgroup of
\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

consisting
of those
(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in\prod_{i\in I}B_{i}
for which

a_{j}=0_{j}
for all but finitely many
j\in I
.

\mathcal{R}
acts in the usual “matrix” way on
V
:
given
M=(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in\mathcal{R}
and

\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in V
, define
M\mathbf{a}:=(b_{i})_{i\in I}

where

b_{i}=\sum_{j\in I,\,a_{j}\neq 0_{j}}r_{ij}(a_{j})\quad\mbox{computed in
$\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})$}.

Via this action we obtain a faithful left
\mathcal{R}
-module
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
.
It is well known (see e.g.
[
5
, p. 150]
) that minimality
of
\theta
implies that
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
is a simple module. Here is a direct
proof of this.
Let
\overline{0}=(0_{i})_{i\in I}\in V
.
The claim of simplicity reduces to proving the following:
if
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in V
with
\mathbf{a}\neq\overline{0}
,

\mathbf{b}=(b_{i})_{i\in I}\in V
,
k\in I
, and

b_{i}=0_{i}
for all
i\in I
with
i\neq k
, then
\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{R}\mathbf{a}
.
To prove it,
pick
\ell\in I
with
a_{\ell}\neq 0_{\ell}
. By minimality of
\theta
we have

(b_{k},0_{k})\in\cg(a_{\ell},0_{\ell})
. Thus by Corollary
3.2
, there
exists
f\in\Pol_{1}({\mathbf{A}})
with
f(a_{\ell})=b_{k}
and
f(0_{\ell})=0_{k}
.
Let
M=(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in\mathcal{R}
be the matrix given by

r_{k\ell}=f{\upharpoonright}_{B_{\ell}}
and
r_{ij}=O_{ij}
for
(i,j)\neq(k,\ell)
.
Then
M\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}
as required.

Since
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
is a simple faithful module, it follows by Schur’s Lemma
for modules that the endomorphism ring

\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
of
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
is a division ring.
For clarity, we consider both the endomorphisms of
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
and the
endomorphisms of
V
to act on
the left. Thus
\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
is the unital subring of
\operatorname{End}(V)

consisting of those
f\in\operatorname{End}(V)

for which
f(M\mathbf{a})=Mf(\mathbf{a})
for all
M\in\mathcal{R}
and all

\mathbf{a}\in V
.

Now let
{\mathbf{R}}
be the unital ring
\prod_{i\in I}\operatorname{End}(\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i}))
.
Define a unital ring embedding
\mathsf{L}:{\mathbf{R}}\hookrightarrow\operatorname{End}(V)
in
the obvious way: given

\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}\in R
and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in V
,
define
\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a}):=(\lambda_{i}(a_{i}))_{i\in I}
.
We claim that
\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)\subseteq\operatorname{ran}(\mathsf{L})
and

\mathbb{F}=\mathsf{L}^{-1}(\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V))
, which will prove (
4
).
The first claim can be deduced from the equation
f(M\mathbf{a})=Mf(\mathbf{a})

specialized to matrices
M
which, for some
k\in I
, have

r_{kk}=\mathrm{id}_{B_{k}}
and
r_{ij}=O_{ij}
for all
(i,j)\neq(k,k)
.
To prove the second claim,
for each
k,\ell\in I
and
r\in R_{k\ell}
let

E_{k\ell}^{r}\in\mathcal{R}
be the matrix

(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
where
r_{k\ell}=r
and
r_{ij}=O_{ij}
for all
(i,j)\neq(k,\ell)
.
For each
k\in I
let
\varepsilon_{k}:\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{k})\hookrightarrow V

be the obvious group embedding:
\varepsilon_{k}(a)=(a_{i})_{i\in I}
where

a_{k}=a
and
a_{i}=0_{i}
for all
i\neq k
.
Now fix
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}\in R
and let
f=\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}
.
If
f\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
, then
specializing the condition
f(M\mathbf{a})=Mf(\mathbf{a})
to
M=E_{k\ell}^{r}
and

\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{ran}(\varepsilon_{\ell})
, we get

\lambda_{k}(r(a))=r(\lambda_{\ell}(a))
for all

a\in B_{\ell}
. This proves

\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
.
Conversely, if
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
and
M=(r_{ij})_{i,j\in I}\in\mathcal{R}
,
then for all
j\in I
and
\mathbf{a}\in\operatorname{ran}(\varepsilon_{j})
we get

\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}(M\mathbf{a})=(\lambda_{i}(r_{ij}(a_{j})))_{i\in I}=(r_{ij}(\lambda_{j}(a_{j})))_{i\in I}=M\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a})
.
By linearity, we get
\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}(M\mathbf{a})=M\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}(\mathbf{a})
for
all
\mathbf{a}\in V
, proving
\mathsf{L}_{\lambda}\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
.
∎

Definition 6.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,T,(0_{i})_{i\in I}
, and
\mathbb{F}
be as in
Notation
6.1
.

(1)

We call
\mathbb{F}
the division ring
associated to

{\mathbf{A}}
,
\theta
and

T
.

(2)

Given
i\in I
, the
canonical action
of
\mathbb{F}
on

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

as follows: for
\lambda=(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{F}
and
a\in\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

.

The canonical action of
\mathbb{F}
on
\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

into a left
\mathbb{F}
-vector space, which we denote by
{}_{\mathbb{F}}\hskip-2.0pt\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})
.
Observe that
Proposition
6.2
(
1
) and
condition
6.1
(
2b
)
imply that
each
r\in R_{ij}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear transformation
from
{}_{\mathbb{F}}\hskip-2.0pt\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{j})
to
{}_{\mathbb{F}}\hskip-2.0pt\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})

If
i\in I
and

with

for

. Then

-vector space

The construction of

and its actions on the groups

for

.
Though we will not prove it here, it is not hard to show that if

is any other choice of transversal, the division ring

\mathbb{F}^{\prime}

is isomorphic to

and

induce the same actions on

\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,e)
.
(In the special case when

{\mathbf{A}}
belongs to a variety with a weak difference term,

6.15
.)

In addition to providing a vector space structure to each
\theta
-class,
the construction of the simple module

in the proof of

carries some useful information about the

Definition 6.4
.

Let

6.1
.
Let

Let

).

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,T,(B_{i})_{i\in I}
be as in Notation
6.1
.
Let
\mathbb{F}
be the division ring associated to
{\mathbf{A}},\theta,T
.
Fix
j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\in I
.

(1)

Every
restriction to
B_{i_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{i_{n}}
of an
n
-ary polynomial
of
{\mathbf{A}}

is
\mathbb{F}
-affine.

(2)

Conversely, every
\mathbb{F}
-affine function
f

with domain
B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}
is
finitely approximated by

n
-ary polynomials of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
That is, for every finite subset
X\subseteq B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}
there exists
f_{X}\in\Pol_{n}({\mathbf{A}})

such that
f(\mathbf{a})=f_{X}(\mathbf{a})
for all
\mathbf{a}\in X
.

In particular, if
B_{j_{1}},\ldots,B_{j_{n}}
are finite,
then

\{f{\upharpoonright}_{B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}}\,:\,\text{$f\in\Pol_{n}({\mathbf{A}})$}\}

is the set of all

\mathbb{F}
-affine functions with domain
B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}
.

Proof sketch.

(
1
)
This can be shown by an easy adaptation of known arguments such as
those found in
[
10
, p. 32]
. Given
f\in\Pol_{n}({\mathbf{A}})

and
i\in I
with
f(B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}})\subseteq B_{i}
,
define
g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=d(f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),f(0_{j_{1}},\ldots,0_{j_{n}}),0_{i})
.
Then
g(0_{j_{1}},\ldots,0_{j_{n}})=0_{i}
and it will suffice to show that

g{\upharpoonright}_{B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}}
is
\mathbb{F}
-linear. For each

t=1,\ldots,n
define
r_{t}:B_{j_{t}}\rightarrow B_{i}
by

r_{t}(x)=g(0_{j_{1}},\ldots,0_{j_{t-1}},x,0_{j_{t+1}},\ldots,0_{j_{n}})
.
Then for each
t
we have that
r_{t}
is in
R_{ij_{t}}
and so is

\mathbb{F}
-linear
by the comments following Definition
6.3
.
Furthermore, for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}

we have

\displaystyle g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=g(d(x_{1},0_{j_{1}},0_{j_{1}}),d(0_{j_{2}},0_{j_{2}},x_{2}),\ldots,d(0_{j_{n}},0_{j_{n}},x_{n}))

\displaystyle=d(g(x_{1},0_{j_{2}},\ldots,0_{j_{n}}),g(0_{j_{1}},0_{j_{2}},\ldots,0_{j_{n}}),g(0_{j_{1}},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}))

by Proposition
3.4
, which simplifies to

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=r_{1}(x_{1})+g(0_{j_{1}},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\quad\mbox{computed in
$\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})$}.

Iterating this argument eventually gives

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}r_{i}(x_{i})\quad\mbox{computed in $\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})$}

which implies that
g{\upharpoonright}_{B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}}
is
\mathbb{F}
-linear.

(
2
)
Recall the construction of the module
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
from the proof of
Proposition
6.2
(
4
).
As we saw there,
{}_{\mathcal{R}}V
is simple and
\mathsf{L}:\mathbb{F}\cong\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
.

V
naturally inherits the structure of a left
\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal{R}}(V)
-vector space
and thus
also the structure of a left
\mathbb{F}
-vector space via
\mathsf{L}
, the latter which
we denote by
{}_{\mathbb{F}}V
. Also for each
M\in\mathcal{R}
let

be
the group endomorphism given by
M
’s action:
\phi(M)(\mathbf{a}):=M\mathbf{a}
.
Then
\phi(\mathcal{R})
is a subring of
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}}(V)
, and

\phi(\mathcal{R})
is dense in
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}}(V)
.

We will first prove the claim in the following special case: if

i,j\in I
and

is
\mathbb{F}
-linear, then

f
is finitely approximated by members of
R_{ij}
. Fix such an

.
Let
X
be a finite
subset of
B_{j}
. Recall the embeddings
\varepsilon_{k}:\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{k})\hookrightarrow V
from the proof of Proposition

)
and note that each

is actually an
\mathbb{F}
-linear map

{}_{\mathbb{F}}\hskip-2.0pt\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{k})\hookrightarrow{}_{\mathbb{F}}V
, and

{}_{\mathbb{F}}V
is the direct sum of the spaces
\operatorname{ran}(\varepsilon_{k})
(

).
Let

be the
\mathbb{F}
-linear map determined by

for

and

h{\upharpoonright}_{\operatorname{ran}(\varepsilon_{j})}=\varepsilon_{i}\circ f\circ\varepsilon_{j}^{-1}
.
Because
\phi(\mathcal{R})
is dense in
\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{F}}(V)
,
there exists
M=(r_{k\ell})_{k,\ell\in I}\in\mathcal{R}
with
h{\upharpoonright}_{\varepsilon_{j}(X)}=\phi(M){\upharpoonright}_{\varepsilon_{j}(X)}
. Then

and

Now we prove the claim in general.
Suppose that
f:B_{j_{1}}\times\cdots\times B_{j_{n}}\rightarrow B_{i}
is

\mathbb{F}
-affine.
Then we can write

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{t=1}^{n}f_{t}(x_{t})+b\quad\mbox{computed in
$\operatorname{Grp}_{{\mathbf{A}}}(\theta,0_{i})$}

for some

Definition 6.15.2

For

Definition 6.15.3

Define

Claim 6.15.4

6.15.4
.

\lambda_{J}\circ\overline{r}=\overline{r}\circ\lambda_{K}

5.8

\lambda_{J}\circ\overline{r}{\upharpoonright}_{\operatorname{ran}(B_{k})}=\overline{r}\circ\lambda_{K}{\upharpoonright}_{\operatorname{ran}(B_{k})}

7.
Similarity

In a 1983 paper
[
5
]
, Freese introduced a relation
of
similarity
between subdirectly irreducible algebras
in a congruence modular variety. If the monoliths of the algebras
are nonabelian, then the algebras are similar if and only if they are
isomorphic. If the monoliths are abelian, then the relation is more
interesting. Freese’s original definition in the latter case, as well as
the equivalent formulation found in
[
6
]
, depend
on a certain “uniformity”
of the blocks of an abelian monolith
\mu
contained
in a common
(0:\mu)
-block, represented in part by
Corollary
5.3
. As we have claimed (and will prove in
Section
8
), this uniformity is lost in varieties with a weak difference
term.

Happily, Freese gave two other equivalent formulations of the similarity
relation in the abelian monolith case, one of which is based
on the construction in Section
4
. Both formulations
generalize nicely to varieties with a weak difference term.
We develop this generalization in this section.

The following definition, in the context of congruence modular varieties, is found in

[
6
, Definition 10.9]
.

Definition 7.1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term, and

{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{V}
is subdirectly irreducible with monolith
\mu
. The algebra
D({\mathbf{A}})
is
defined as follows:

(1)

If
\mu
is nonabelian, then
D({\mathbf{A}})={\mathbf{A}}
.

(2)

If
\mu
is abelian, then
D({\mathbf{A}})=D({\mathbf{A}},\mu)
as defined in Definition
4.7
.

Observe that, in the context of the previous definition,
D({\mathbf{A}})
is a subdirectly irreducible
algebra in
\mathsf{HS}({\mathbf{A}}^{2})
; furthermore, the monoliths of
{\mathbf{A}}
and
D({\mathbf{A}})

are either both abelian or both nonabelian.

Definition 7.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term, and

{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly irreducible. We say that
{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
are

similar
, and write
{\mathbf{A}}\sim{\mathbf{B}}
, if
D({\mathbf{A}})\cong D({\mathbf{B}})
.

By
[
5
, Theorem 5.1]
and
[
6
, Theorem 10.11]
,
our definition of similarity, when
restricted to the congruence modular setting, is equivalent to the original definition given
in
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
. It is in this sense that our definition
extends the original one.

If we let
\mu
and
\kappa
denote the respective monoliths of

{\mathbf{A}}
and
{\mathbf{B}}
, then
Definition
7.2
can be rephrased as follows:
{\mathbf{A}}\sim{\mathbf{B}}
if and only if one of the following holds:

(1)

\mu
and
\kappa
are both nonabelian and
{\mathbf{A}}\cong{\mathbf{B}}
; or

(2)

\mu
and
\kappa
are both abelian and
D({\mathbf{A}},\mu)\cong D({\mathbf{B}},\kappa)
.

Before stating the main theorem of this section, we introduce
a variant of a crucial construct due to Zhuk
[
37
, Section 6.6]
.

Definition 7.3
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}

are subdirectly irreducible algebras in a common signature with monoliths

\mu,\kappa
respectively. A
proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}

is a subuniverse
T\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
satisfying

(B1)

\operatorname{pr}_{1,2}(T)=\mu
and
\operatorname{pr}_{3,4}(T)=\kappa
.

(B2)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have
a_{1}=a_{2}
if and only if
b_{1}=b_{2}
.

(B3)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have
(a_{i},a_{i},b_{i},b_{i})\in T
for
i=1,2
.

If
T
is a proper bridge from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
, then we define its
trace

to be the set
\operatorname{tr}(T)=\{(a,b)\in A\times B\,:\,\text{$(a,a,b,b)\in T$}\}
, and
its
kernel
to be the set

\ker(T)=\{((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}))\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T$}\}.

Observe that for any proper bridge
T
we have

\operatorname{tr}(T)=\operatorname{pr}_{1,3}(T)=\operatorname{pr}_{2,4}(T)
by property
(B3)
,
and hence
\operatorname{tr}(T)\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
by property

(B1)
.

In the special case where one of the two monoliths in the previous definition is nonabelian,
the existence of a proper bridge is possible only in the most trivial way.

Proposition 7.4
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly irreducible algebras with monoliths

\mu,\kappa
respectively, and
T
is a proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
.
Assume that one of
\mu
or
\kappa
is nonabelian. Then so is the other,

\operatorname{tr}(T)
is the graph of an isomorphism
h:{\mathbf{A}}\cong{\mathbf{B}}
, and

T=\{(a,b,h(a),h(b))\,:\,\text{$(a,b)\in\mu$}\}
.

Proof.

Assume with no loss of generality that

\kappa
is nonabelian. We will first show that

\operatorname{tr}(T)
is the graph of a surjective homomorphism
h:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
.
Let

S=\{(b,c)\in B^{2}\,:\,\text{$\exists a\in A$ with $(a,b),(a,c)\in\operatorname{tr}(T)$}\}.

S
is a tolerance of
{\mathbf{B}}
. Let

Y=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}b&c\\
b^{\prime}&c^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in B^{2\times 2}~:~\mbox{$\exists\,(a,a^{\prime})\in\mu$ with $(a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}),(a,a^{\prime},c,c^{\prime})\in T$}\right\}.

One can easily show, using
(B1)
and the definitions of
S
and
Y
, that

X(\kappa,S)\subseteq Y

(see Definition
2.1
). Since
Y
is a subuniverse of
{\mathbf{B}}^{2\times 2}
, we
get
M(\kappa,S)\subseteq Y
. Then from
(B2)
and
Definition
2.2
(
2
) we can deduce
C(S,\kappa;0_{B})
.
Since
S
is a tolerance, we can improve this to
C(\cg(S),\kappa;0_{B})
by

[
17
, Theorem 2.19(2)]
. Now assume that
S\neq 0_{B}
; then
\cg(S)\geq\kappa

since
\kappa
is the monolith of
{\mathbf{B}}
; but then
\kappa
would be
abelian, contrary to our assumption. This proves
S=0_{B}
and hence
\operatorname{tr}(T)
is the graph
of a surjective homomorphism
h:{\mathbf{A}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
.

Assume that
h
is not injective. Then
\mu\subseteq\ker(h)
as
\mu
is the
monolith of
{\mathbf{A}}
.
Choose
(a,b)\in\mu
with
a\neq b
. By
(B1)
, there exists
(c,d)\in\kappa
with
(a,b,c,d)\in T
. Then
c\neq d
by
(B2)
. By
(B3)
,
we have
(a,c),(b,d)\in\operatorname{tr}(T)
, or equivalently,
c=h(a)
and
d=h(b)
.
But then
(a,b)\in\mu\subseteq\ker(h)
implies
c=d
, contradiction. Thus
h
is an isomorphism.

Finally, for every
(a,b)\in\mu
there is at least one pair
(c,d)\in\kappa
with

(a,b,c,d)\in T
by
(B1)
. On the other hand, the only pair

(c,d)
that can work is
(c,d)=(h(a),h(b))
by
(B3)
and the
definition of
h
. Hence
T=\{(a,b,h(a),h(b))\,:\,\text{$(a,b)\in\mu$}\}
.
∎

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly directly algebras with monoliths

\mu,\kappa
respectively, and
T
is a proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
. Let
{\mathbf{C}}=\operatorname{tr}(T)\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}

and
\tau=\ker(T)
. Then
\tau\in\Con({\mathbf{C}})
. Hence

(B4)

For all
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
we have
(a_{2},a_{1},b_{2},b_{1})\in T
.

(B5)

If
(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
and
(a_{2},a_{3},b_{2},b_{3})\in T
, then

(a_{1},a_{3},b_{1},b_{3})\in T
.

Proof.

Note that
\tau
is a reflexive subuniverse of
{\mathbf{C}}^{2}
by
(B3)
,
and that

(B4)
and
(B5)
simply express the symmetry and
transitivity of
\tau
. Hence all we need to prove is that
\tau
is symmetric
and transitive.

If
\mu
or
\kappa
is nonabelian, then
C
is the graph of an isomorphism

h:{\mathbf{A}}\cong{\mathbf{B}}
and
\tau=\{((a,h(a)),(b,h(b))\,:\,\text{$(a,b)\in\mu$}\}

by Proposition
7.4
. Clearly
\tau
is symmetric and transitive
in this case.

If
\mu
and
\kappa
are both abelian,
let
d(x,y,z)
be a weak difference term for
\mathcal{V}
. Let

\theta=\{((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}))\in C^{2}\,:\,\text{$(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mu$ and $(b_{1},b_{2})\in\kappa$}\}.

Clearly
\theta\in\Con({\mathbf{C}})
.
Because
\mu
and

\kappa
are abelian, the restriction of

to any
\theta
-class is a Maltsev
operation.
We have that
\tau
is a reflexive subuniverse of

{\mathbf{C}}^{2}
and
\tau\subseteq\theta
by
(B1)
.
Thus
\tau
is symmetric and transitive by Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly directly, and
T
is a proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
. Let
{\mathbf{C}}=\operatorname{tr}(T)\leq_{sd}{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}

and
\tau=\ker(T)
.
Also let

\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in\Con({\mathbf{C}})
be the kernels of the two projection
homomorphisms
\operatorname{pr}_{1}:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
and
\operatorname{pr}_{2}:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{B}}
, and let

\delta^{+}_{i}
be the unique upper cover of
\delta_{i}
in
\Con({\mathbf{C}})

for
i=1,2
.
Then

(\delta_{1},\delta^{+}_{1})\searrow(0,\tau)\nearrow(\delta_{2},\delta^{+}_{2})
.

Proof.

Clearly
\tau\leq\delta^{+}_{1}\wedge\delta^{+}_{2}
by
(B1)
.
Choose
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mu
with
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
.
Again by
(B1)
,
there exist
b_{1},b_{2}\in B
with

(a_{1},a_{2},b_{1},b_{2})\in T
and hence
(a_{1},b_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\equiv}}(a_{2},b_{2})
,
which proves
\tau\nleq\delta_{1}
.
A similar argument shows
\tau\nleq\delta_{2}
. This proves

\tau\vee\delta_{i}=\delta^{+}_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Finally, suppose
((a_{1},b_{1}),(a_{2},b_{2}))\in\tau\cap\delta_{1}
. Then

a_{1}=a_{2}
, so
b_{1}=b_{2}
by
(B2)
, so
(a_{1},b_{1})=(a_{2},b_{2})
.
This shows
\tau\wedge\delta_{1}=0
,
and a similar proof shows
\tau\wedge\delta_{2}=0
.
This
proves
(\delta_{1},\delta^{+}_{1})\searrow(0,\tau)\nearrow(\delta_{2},\delta^{+}_{2})
as required.
∎

In the next lemma,
we show that proper bridges between subdirectly irreducible algebras
with

Lemma 7.7
.

Suppose
\mathcal{V}
is a variety with a weak difference term,
{\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{V}
are subdirectly directly algebras with abelian monoliths

\mu,\kappa
respectively, and
T
is a proper bridge
from
{\mathbf{A}}
to
{\mathbf{B}}
. Let
\alpha=(0:\mu)
and

\Delta_{{\mathbf{A}}}=\Delta_{\mu,\alpha}
and

\Delta_{{\mathbf{B}}}=\Delta_{\kappa,\beta}
.

(1)

For all
(a,r),(b,s)\in\operatorname{tr}(T)
,
(a,b)\in\alpha\iff(r,s)\in\beta
.

(2)

For every
(\mu,\alpha)
-matrix

M=\begin{pmatrix}a&a^{\prime}\\
b&b^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
there exists a

(\kappa,\beta)
-matrix
\begin{pmatrix}r&r^{\prime}\\
s&s^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
with

(a,b,r,s),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in T
.

(3)

For all
(a,b,r,s),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},r^{\prime},s^{\prime})\in T

(a,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{{\mathbf{A}}}}}{{\equiv}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\iff(r,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta_{{\mathbf{B}}}}}{{\equiv}}(r^{\prime},s^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(
1
) Define
{\mathbf{C}}=\operatorname{tr}(T)\leq{\mathbf{A}}\times{\mathbf{B}}
and

\delta_{1},\delta^{+}_{1},\delta_{2},\delta^{+}_{2},\tau\in\Con({\mathbf{C}})
as in
Lemma

. Observe that
\delta^{+}_{i}/\delta_{i}
is abelian
for

, because
\mu
and
\kappa
are abelian. Since

(\delta_{1},\delta^{+}_{1})\searrow(0,\tau)\nearrow(\delta_{2},\delta^{+}_{2})

by Lemma

, we get
(\delta_{1}:\delta^{+}_{1})=(0:\tau)=(\delta_{2}:\delta^{+}_{2})
by Proposition
3.13
(
1
).
On the other hand, letting
\operatorname{pr}_{1}:{\mathbf{C}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{A}}
be the first projection
homomorphism, we have

(\delta_{1}:\delta^{+}_{1})=(\operatorname{pr}_{1}^{-1}(0):\operatorname{pr}_{1}^{-1}(\mu))=\operatorname{pr}_{1}^{-1}((0:\mu))=\operatorname{pr}_{1}^{-1}(\alpha)
by Lemma

(
2
), and similarly
(\delta_{2}:\delta^{+}_{2})=\operatorname{pr}_{2}^{-1}(\beta)
.
Together these facts prove
\operatorname{pr}_{1}^{-1}(\alpha)=\operatorname{pr}_{2}^{-1}(\beta)
, which
is equivalent to (
1
).

(
2

9.
Conclusion and questions

In this paper we have extended Freese’s analysis of abelian minimal
congruences, and his similarity relation between subdirectly irreducible
algebras, from the congruence modular setting to the setting of varieties
with a weak difference term. We also established a connection
between similarity and certain constructs which
we have called “proper bridges.” In the companion paper
[
33
]
, we
will compare proper bridges to Zhuk’s original bridges (which are superficially
weaker) and explain some related elements of Zhuk’s work in terms of centrality
and similarity. In a third paper
[
32
]
, we
will unite the analyses of
“rectangular critical relations” by Zhuk

[
37
, Lemma 8.21]
(in the idempotent locally finite Taylor case)
and by
Kearnes and Szendrei
[
20
]
(in the congruence
modular case), showing in particular the role of similarity, associated finite fields,
and linear equations.

In the congruence modular setting, Freese and McKenzie
[
6
]
established
some nontrivial results concerning the similarity relation,
such as bounding the number of similarity classes of
subdirectly irreducible algebras in finitely generated varieties.
One can ask whether any of these properties persist in varieties with a weak difference
term, or with a difference term. For example:

Problem 1
.

If
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite algebra and
\mathsf{HSP}({\mathbf{A}})
has
a weak difference term, does it follow that there are only finitely many similarity classes
of subdirectly irreducible algebras in
\mathsf{HSP}({\mathbf{A}})
with abelian monolith?

Because of their connection to the Constraint Satisfaction Dichotomy Theorem,
finite subdirectly irreducible algebras with abelian monoliths in an

idempotent

locally finite
Taylor variety are of particular interest. We have seen one result
(Lemma
5.9
) showing how the assumption of idempotency can simplify
our analysis. In what other ways does idempotency affect the analysis?
For example:

Problem 2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{A}}
is a finite idempotent subdirectly irreducible
algebra in a Taylor variety with abelian monolith
\mu
,

\alpha=(0:\mu)
