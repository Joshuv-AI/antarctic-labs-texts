Molecular exploration of mosquitoes and their vector-borne pathogens in urban water environments in Benin
Authors: Akihiko Hata, Saki Wakisaka, Olayide Spaiche Romanic Odjougbele, Kenshi Sankoda, Kevin Mael Patrick Zinsou Sintondji, Victorien Tamègnon Dougnon, Beatrix Amen Tigo, Blèkpé Cyr Raoul Akpassou, Akilou Socohou Amadou, Metogbe Belfrid Djihouessi, Mohamed Moukorab Arêmou Daouda, Keisuke Kuroda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria and dengue fever, both transmitted by mosquitoes, are major health concerns in (sub)tropical regions, with expanding endemic ranges. Monitoring mosquito populations and the pathogens they carry is crucial for effective vector control. Here, we explored molecular-based surveillance using environmental water samples as an alternative to conventional mosquito collection methods. A total of 154 samples were collected from eight environment types, including septic tanks, urban drainage, lake, groundwater, puddles, and tire tracks, in urban Benin. Polymerase chain reaction and high-throughput sequencing were used to detect gene fragments of mosquitoes and pathogens such as dengue virus and Plasmodium spp., a group of parasitic protozoa that cause malaria. The presence of DNA from several medically important mosquito species, including Aedes aegypti, Anopheles gambiae, Culex pipiens or Cx. quinquefasciatus, Cx. tritaeniorhynchus, and Mansonia africana, was identified in 12 samples from urban drainage, lake, groundwater, and tire tracks. However, neither dengue virus nor Plasmodium was found. These findings highlight both the potential and current limitations of environmental DNA for mosquito and pathogen surveillance. Improving primer specificity and refining sequencing protocols will be necessary to improve sensitivity and reliability. Despite these limitations, this approach offers a promising non-invasive approach that does not require mosquito capture and can complement traditional mosquito monitoring to strengthen vector control programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58135-4.Keywords: Dengue virus, Environmental DNA, Metabarcoding, Plasmodium, Tire track, West AfricaSubject terms: Diseases, Ecology, Ecology, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Mosquito species are important vectors of various microbial pathogens1. Malaria and dengue fever, caused by Plasmodium spp., a group of parasitic protozoa, and dengue virus (DenV), respectively, are among the most significant vector-borne diseases transmitted by mosquitoes. Vector-borne diseases account for approximately 17% of the global disease burden, with a particularly high impact in tropical and subtropical regions, including African countries1. Environmental factors such as ambient temperature and precipitation influence mosquito population dynamics, thereby affecting pathogen transmission2. Climate change has been linked to the expansion of mosquito habitats and the spread of vector-borne pathogens into new geographic regions3–6. Such expansion of mosquito habitats poses increasing challenges to current vector-borne disease control strategies, which rely heavily on insecticide use in indoor environments, agricultural fields, and breeding sites7–11.

To improve the control of vector-borne diseases, extensive research has been conducted on mosquito habitats, insecticide resistance, and pathogen carriage4,7–10,12–15. These studies primarily rely on the collection of live mosquitoes, which requires substantial effort, expertise, and technical skills. Various mosquito traps have been developed to collect adult mosquitoes; however, field workers involved in mosquito collection are exposed to mosquito bites and potential infections, necessitating ethical approval and risk mitigation13,16,17. Surveys targeting mosquito larvae and eggs typically involve analyzing water from breeding habitats and identifying species through morphological observations, a labor-intensive process requiring specialized expertise. Given these challenges, environmental DNA analysis has gained attention as a novel method for detecting mosquito presence6,17–20.

Environmental waters contain suspended DNA released from organisms that are or were previously present in or around it21. This enables the detection of target organisms even if they are not physically present in the collected water samples. Studies have demonstrated that environmental water samples can contain DNA not only from aquatic organisms but also from terrestrial animals and microorganisms22,23. At mosquito breeding sites, DNA from eggs, larvae, pupae, ovipositing adults, and dead individuals is expected to be detectable6,17–20. From this perspective, environmental DNA analysis offers a promising alternative to conventional mosquito collection methods, reducing fieldworker risks and requiring less specialized expertise and effort6,20.

Research on mosquito DNA detection in water samples remains in the validation stage, with most studies analyzing samples where mosquito presence was already confirmed6,17,19,20. To the best of our knowledge, only one study to date, conducted in European countries, has applied this method to samples with unknown mosquito presence18. Furthermore, investigations into vector-borne pathogens in water have recently gained recognition from an epidemiological standpoint, and their utility is just beginning to be recognized24–28. Previous studies have focused on pathogens in municipal wastewater, particularly those originating from infected individuals within the community24–28. It is also possible that mosquito species release not only their own DNA, but also the pathogens they carry, into the aquatic environment. In this respect, simultaneous investigation of mosquito DNA and vector-borne pathogens in water is potentially effective for locating pathogen-carrying mosquitoes, as an alternative to or complement to traditional capturing methods, and can contribute to improved control of vector-borne diseases.

For DNA analysis, two primary methods are commonly used: (1) real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), which targets specific species and enables quantitative evaluation, and (2) metabarcoding, which combines PCR with high-throughput sequencing (HTS) to comprehensively analyze a broad range of related species29,30. Metabarcoding, in particular, holds great potential for detecting newly introduced or unexpected species, allowing for broad biodiversity assessments with minimal effort. Considering that some mosquito species are strongly associated with specific pathogens1,2,11, the efficient species-level identification of mosquitoes via metabarcoding can facilitate pathogen detection in the sample.

Benin, located in West Africa, is one of the malaria-endemic countries, accounting for approximately 2% of global malaria cases and 1.7% of global malaria-related deaths11. Malaria transmission is particularly intense in densely populated urban areas such as Cotonou, the de facto capital of Benin10. Malaria is perennially endemic in Cotonou, with seasonal variation in case numbers, which increases during the rainy season10,31. A cohort study conducted in a peri-urban area around Cotonou reported a monthly malaria incidence rate of 84 per 1000 children31. Mosquito species belonging to the Anopheles gambiae complex, as well as other Anopheles species, serve as the primary vectors of Plasmodium spp.11 Among the An. gambiae complex, An. coluzzii has been reported to be the predominant species in Cotonou9,32. A mosquito collection study conducted in a neighboring region of Cotonou revealed that 9.63% of the collected An. gambiae mosquitoes were infected with Plasmodium spp.16 In that study, mosquitoes were collected by human landing catches, and An. gambiae accounted for less than 2% of the total mosquitoes collected16 highlighting the difficulty of tracking malaria vectors using conventional methods. Currently, the invasion of An. stephensi, a malaria vector native to Asia, is a major concern in African countries11. Its establishment and association with malaria cases have been reported mainly in East Africa11,33. In Benin, the presence of An. stephensi has not yet been reported; however, a recent study suggested that the probability of its invasion is relatively high in Cotonou and other regions with high densities of human and pigs34.

In contrast, DenV, transmitted by mosquito species such as Aedes aegypti and other Aedes species, has been linked only to sporadic dengue fever cases, primarily introduced by travelers in Benin35,36. Although Benin is not considered an endemic area for dengue, clinical surveillance is limited, and the true extent of dengue virus circulation remains unclear35,36. It is known that Aedes species are commonly present around Cotonou, as is An. gambiae16 suggesting that there is potential for DenV transmission in this region.

Cotonou and neighboring municipalities have a typical equatorial climate, characterized by two rainy seasons (March–June and September–November) and two dry seasons (July–August and December–February). Rainfall during the wet season creates numerous puddles that serve as ideal mosquito breeding sites. Additionally, urban drainage systems, designed for stormwater and wastewater management, may also function as mosquito hotspots4,7,8,10,37.

The present study aims to evaluate the utility of environmental DNA analysis for detecting mosquitoes and vector-borne pathogens in urban areas. To achieve this, environmental samples from various water matrices were collected in the urban areas of Benin, including Cotonou city, across different seasons (transition from wet to dry season, dry season, and rainy season), and the presence of mosquitoes, DenV, and Plasmodium spp. was assessed. For mosquito detection, metabarcoding was employed to enable comprehensive species identification.

Results

In the present study, 154 environmental water samples representing a variety of water matrices were examined for the presence of Plasmodium spp., DenV, and mosquito DNA. Although neither pathogen was detected in any of the samples, mosquito DNA, originating from several genera, was detected, particularly in tire track (during the dry and wet seasons) and in lake water (during the transition from the wet to the dry seasons).

Detection efficiencies of spiked phiX174 and murine norovirus

PhiX174 was spiked into the filtration unit used for sample filtration prior to DNA extraction, in order to evaluate the efficiency of the DNA extraction-qPCR process. Among the 154 samples, 23 (14.9%) exhibited low phiX174 detection efficiency (< 10%), in accordance with previous studies38,39. Of these, 19 (82.6%) showed no significant improvement upon dilution of the DNA extract, indicating that inefficient DNA extraction was the primary cause of low detection efficiency. Particularly, groundwater (4 of 6 samples) and lake water (6 of 20 samples) collected during February–March 2024 frequently exhibited low detection efficiency.

Murine norovirus (MNV) was spiked into the virus concentrate before RNA extraction to assess the efficiency of the RNA extraction-reverse transcription (RT)-qPCR process38. Initial testing of samples from the first sampling event (September 2023) without purification resulted in low detection efficiency (< 10%) in 16 of 34 samples. After applying the purification process to the 16 samples, only three samples remained below the 10% detection efficiency threshold. For samples collected in subsequent sampling events, purification was applied from the outset, and only one out of 120 tested samples exhibited low detection efficiency.

There were concerns that detection sensitivity and quantitative accuracy might be reduced for the samples showing low detection efficiency of process controls. However, because the possibility of obtaining positive results remained, all samples including those with low detection efficiency were retained for subsequent analyses.

Detection of vector-borne pathogens

The RT-qPCR assay for DenV was negative in all 154 samples. The qPCR and conventional PCR assays for Plasmodium spp. yielded positive signals in some samples. However, sequencing of the conventional PCR amplicons revealed that the obtained reads were associated with ribosomal RNA genes from non-target organisms, such as Amoebophrya spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: MK368186.1) and Cryptomonas spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: MG646388.1), and none were associated with Plasmodium spp.

Detection of mosquito DNA

Mosquito DNA was investigated in 154 samples using two conventional PCR assays targeting Anopheles spp. and Aedes spp. Both assays produced bands of expected amplicon sizes, along with additional bands. Sequencing analysis of the amplicons from the assay targeting Anopheles spp. did not yield any mosquito-related sequence reads. Instead, obtained sequence reads were associated with non-target organisms, such as Pseudomonas spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: CP044074.1) and Variovorax spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: CP091716.1).

The assay targeting Aedes spp. also yielded reads associated with non-target organisms, including Perkinsus spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: AY305327.1) and Exiguobacterium spp. (e.g., GenBank accession number: CP006866.1). However, sequence reads showed greater than 90% identity to mosquito DNA sequences in BLAST analysis, hereafter referred to as mosquito-like reads, were detected in 12 samples (Tables 1 and 2). Specifically, Aedes aegypti-like reads were found in three tire track samples collected in February and June 2024. Culex pipiens-, Cx. quinquefasciatus-, and Cx. tritaeniorhynchus-like reads were detected in a groundwater sample (September 2023), three lake water samples (September 2023), two urban drainage samples (February 2024), and two tire track samples (June 2024). An. gambiae- and Mansonia africana-like reads were detected in a tire track sample (June 2024) and an urban drainage sample (February 2024), respectively. In particular, both Ae. aegypti- and Cx. tritaeniorhynchus-like reads were simultaneously detected in one tire track sample (June 2024).

Discussion

This study evaluated the utility of environmental DNA analysis for detecting mosquitoes and mosquito-borne pathogens in urban aquatic environments in Benin. Mosquito-associated DNA was detected in 12 of 154 environmental water samples, representing several medically important genera. In contrast, neither DenV nor Plasmodium spp. DNA was detected. These findings indicate that environmental DNA metabarcoding can identify the presence and diversity of mosquito vectors in urban waters even when mosquito presence is not independently verified. However, the absence of pathogen detection suggests that environmental water-based surveillance of mosquito-borne pathogens remains technically challenging and requires further methodological refinement.

First attempt of environmental DNA-based mosquito surveillance in Africa

The present study represents the first attempt to identify vector mosquito DNA in urban aquatic environments in Africa using the environmental DNA approach. In particular, our metabarcoding, which primarily targets Aedes spp, revealed the presence of DNA associated with a broad range of mosquito genera, including Ae. aegypti, An. gambiae, Cx. pipiens or Cx. quinquefasciatus (not clearly distinguishable based on the obtained sequences), Cx. tritaeniorhynchus, and Man. africana, from tire track, groundwater, lake, and urban drainage samples in an urban area in Benin.

The species associated with the sequence reads are those typically found in Benin and other sub-Saharan African countries4,9,16. These species are known vectors of pathogens such as dengue virus, Plasmodium spp., West Nile virus, Wuchereria bancrofti, and Japanese encephalitis virus. In this regard, our study successfully identified traces of epidemiologically important mosquito species from environmental water samples using the environmental DNA approach. Mosquito habitat preference and seasonality are considered species-dependent. For instance, An. gambiae is typically associated with temporary, sunlit, and low-turbidity water bodies with minimal fecal contamination, such as puddles10. In contrast, Culex spp. are often found in more polluted environments, such as urban drains8. In the present study, some patterns of species-specific habitat use were observed. For instance, Ae. aegypti- and An. gambiae were detected exclusively in tire track samples (June), while Culex spp. were identified in lake water (September), urban drainage (February), and tire tracks (June). These findings suggest possible spatial and seasonal preferences. However, these trends should be interpreted with caution due to low numbers of positive samples.

Research on mosquito environmental DNA is still in the early stages of validation and optimization. Most existing studies have focused on detecting mosquito DNA in water inoculated with mosquito specimens - such as eggs, larvae, or pupae17,19,20- or in water where the presence of mosquitoes was confirmed.6 A previous study detected Aedes spp. DNA in water samples, even in the absence of visibly present mosquitoes18. That study analyzed 46 samples from seven European countries, primarily focusing on artificial containers such as cans, plastic buckets, and used tires that are known breeding habitats for Aedes mosquitoes in urban environments. In contrast, the present study investigated mosquito DNA in both municipal wastewater (septic tanks and urban drainage) and natural water bodies (lakes, groundwater, wetlands, puddles, and tire tracks) in Cotonou and neighboring municipalities. These sample matrices were selected based on previous studies identifying mosquito habitats in Africa10,37. As such, the present study demonstrates the effectiveness of the environmental DNA approach for efficiently identifying vector mosquito hotspots and species in urban aquatic environments.

Potential effects of targets recovery efficiency and (RT-) PCR inhibition

Process controls (phiX174 and MNV) indicated that some samples exhibited low DNA/RNA extraction efficiency and (RT-) PCR inhibition, even after the additional purification step. These challenging samples were nevertheless included in the mosquito and pathogen detection analyses and may therefore have resulted in false-negative outcomes. Most microbial and environmental DNA concentration methods, including the methods used in the present study, are known to be unable to completely eliminate impurities in water that adversely affect downstream molecular processes21,40. Such impurity can result not only in the false-negative results but also in underestimation of target concentrations. However, even under these circumstances, correcting quantitative values based on the detection efficiency of the control is not recommended. Detection efficiency of process controls reflects the potential for underestimation but does not necessarily correspond to the detection efficiency of target gene, as discussed previously38. From this perspective, there remains a need to further develop concentration and purification methods that maintain or enhance concentration factors while achieving consistently high detection efficiency.

Because the process controls were spiked during or after the concentration processes, potential loss of targets during the concentration processes themselves was not evaluated. Microfiltration-based concentration methods are well established for a wide range of targets, including macrobial environmental DNA and parasites21,41. Among them, the method employing Sterivex™ filtration unit (Merck, Germany), applied in the present study, is widely applied in studies on environmental DNA, including mosquito DNA6,42. Similarly, polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation method is a broadly accepted method for the recovery of viruses from water, including enveloped viruses such as DenV43,44. Accordingly, recovery efficiencies achieved using these concentration methods are generally considered to be high. However, membrane clogging can reduce recovery efficiency during microfiltration, and the performance of PEG precipitation can vary depending on the water quality40,45. In the present study, samples were carefully filtrated to minimize membrane clogging. Nevertheless, because a wide variety of water matrices were examined, it is possible that some samples experienced reduced recovery during the concentration processes, particularly during PEG precipitation. To ensure more robust evaluation of the entire detection workflow, the inclusion of controls spiked into, or inherently present in the original sample would be necessary38.

Necessity of improving the specificity of metabarcoding assays

The low detection rate of mosquito-DNA in the present study (12/154 samples) could also be attributed to the performance of the PCR assay. In our metabarcoding analysis, most samples were dominated by reads associated with non-target organisms, potentially leading to false-negatives and low sequence depth. This limitation may have prevented the formation of OTUs closely matching known mosquito sequences. The primer sequences used in the present study were derived from prior research5,46,47 and were designed to selectively detect target mosquito taxa, specifically Aedes spp. and An. gambiae complex. Their specificity was also verified in silico using BLAST. Nevertheless, the low abundance of mosquito DNA in environmental water samples might have facilitated the preferential amplification of non-target DNA. These primers were originally designed for species identification using isolated organisms, and to the best of our knowledge, they have not been previously applied to environmental samples. Unlike DNA from isolated organisms, environmental samples are highly complex and require careful optimization of PCR assays. In addition, the PCR assay designed for Aedes spp. unexpectedly detected a broader range of mosquito-like sequence reads. This outcome might be attributed to the low specificity of the assay. A previous study, in which a different PCR assay was applied for metabarcoding, similarly reported the dominance of sequences from non-target organisms18, suggesting that non-target detection is a common challenge in metabarcoding analysis of mosquito DNA in the environment. To improve metabarcoding for environmental DNA studies, enhancing primer specificity through the use of highly selective primers or nested PCR may be crucial.

Potential factors contributing to negative results for vector-borne pathogens

In addition to mosquito species, the present study also targeted DenV and Plasmodium spp., for which Aedes spp. and Anopheles spp. are known mosquito vectors, respectively. However, these two pathogens were not detected in any of the samples. Although the qPCR assay targeting Plasmodium spp. yielded positive signals in some samples, sequencing of the amplicons, obtained using the same primers as in the qPCR assay, did not reveal Plasmodium-like sequences. This suggests that the qPCR signals may have been false positives. We hypothesized that the mosquitoes carrying these pathogens would excrete both their DNA and the pathogens into water. These pathogens do not have an environmental stage in their life cycle. However, because they are transmitted through mosquito saliva, infected mosquitoes may release pathogens into the environment during water intake48. Simultaneous detection of mosquitoes and pathogens was expected to provide valuable epidemiological insights and contribute to disease intervention strategies. However, this hypothesis could not be confirmed in the present study, because the presence of the pathogens was not confirmed.

Recent studies in the U.S., Portugal, and Mozambique have reported the presence of arboviruses including DenV in wastewater25–27. Similarly, DenV has been investigated in wastewater samples in Nepal24. These previous studies fall within the framework of wastewater-based epidemiology, which focuses on pathogens excreted by infected individuals. As demonstrated by wastewater-based epidemiological studies on SARS-CoV-2, this approach can serve as an efficient method for assessing the prevalence of infectious diseases within human populations in a given area49. In contrast, our approach aims to identify the presence and hotspots of pathogen-carrying mosquitoes and thereby elucidate potential risks of vector-borne infections within a community. Accordingly, we focused primarily on environmental water bodies that are likely to serve as mosquito habitats, rather than on wastewater. In the present study, DenV was not detected, likely because the virus is not currently prevalent in Benin36.

Malaria remains a major public health concern in Benin, where it is highly prevalent50. To our knowledge, the presence of Plasmodium spp. in environmental water has not been documented. It is possible that the parasite is either present at extremely low concentrations or not present in environmental water, even in endemic regions as suggested by a recent study28. Alternatively, as discussed earlier regarding mosquito DNA detection, the PCR assay used in the present study may not have been optimal for environmental water samples, although it was designed to be Plasmodium genus-specific51. This observation highlights the need for further methodological refinement.

Future perspectives and summary

As highlighted by Monteiro et al. (2025)26, coordinating surveillance of mosquitoes and relevant vector-borne pathogens is crucial for effective infection control. Traditionally, studies on mosquitoes and their associated pathogens rely on direct mosquito collection. Collecting mosquito including their egg and larvae leads to risk of infection to workers, requires specialized knowledge and is labor intensive4–7,14,20. In this context, environmental DNA-based surveillance of both mosquitoes and vector-borne pathogens offers a non-invasive, efficient, and safe alternative. In the present study, mosquito DNA and vector-borne pathogen genes were investigated in environmental water samples. A diverse range of potential vector mosquito DNA sequences was detected across multiple water matrices. Further optimization of the methodology to improve assay selectivity and sensitivity will enhance the reliability and applicability of this approach, strengthening its role in mosquito and pathogen monitoring.

In summary, the present study demonstrated the feasibility of using metabarcoding-based environmental DNA analysis to detect mosquito DNA in urban water environments in Benin. By analyzing a diverse range of water matrices, we identified DNA traces from multiple medically important mosquito vector species. This highlights the potential of environmental DNA monitoring as a non-invasive tool for identifying mosquito habitats and understanding their distribution in urban settings. Despite the successful detection of mosquito DNA, the study also highlighted key methodological limitations. The high proportion of non-target sequence reads suggests that primer specificity and overall PCR assay design require optimization to improve detection sensitivity and taxonomic accuracy. Although we aimed to detect vector-borne pathogens alongside their mosquito hosts, no DenV or Plasmodium spp. were identified. These findings suggest that additional refinement of pathogen detection strategies in environmental water is necessary.

Overall, the present study provides valuable insights into the application of environmental DNA metabarcoding for mosquito surveillance in urban environments. This approach is potentially helpful for enhancing our understanding of vector ecology and contributing to more effective vector-borne disease control strategies. Future studies should integrate environmental DNA monitoring with conventional methods to validate effectiveness across ecological contexts and seasonal dynamics.

Methods

Sample collection and concentration

Water samples from Cotonou and neighboring municipalities were collected during three sampling events: September 2023 (transition from wet to dry season), February–March 2024 (dry season), and June 2024 (rainy season). In total, 154 samples were obtained from diverse aquatic environments, including urban drainage (15 sites, n = 45), Lake Nokoué (21 sites, n = 52), septic tanks (6 sites, n = 11), groundwater (8 sites, n = 15), puddles (8 sites, n = 8), tire tracks (7 sites, n = 7), wetlands (8 sites, n = 10), and tap water (6 sites, n = 6) (Fig. 2).

Map of the study area showing the points where the environmental water samples were collected. Diamonds, black circles, pentagons, and blue circles represent sampling sites for groundwater, lake water, urban drainage, and wetland samples, respectively. Septic tank and tap water samples were collected from households in the Cotonou and Abomey-Calavi regions. Puddle and tire track samples were collected from temporary water bodies formed in Cotonou. The map was created based on OpenStreetMap (open source; https://www.openstreetmap.org) with additional information added using QGIS 3.30 ‘s-Hertogenbosch (open source; https://qgis.org).

The recovery of mosquito DNA and Plasmodium spp. DNA was performed following a previous study52. Water samples were filtered through a 0.45 μm pore Sterivex™ filtration unit (type HV, Merck, Germany) using a pre-sterilized 50 mL syringe immediately after collection in pre-sterilized polyethylene bags (360 mL or 1,800 mL). Filtration continued until either 1 L of sample had passed through or membrane clogging prevented further filtration. Filtration volumes ranged from several tens of milliliters to 1 L, depending on suspended solid content. After filtration, with little residual liquid remaining in the filtration unit, 1 mL of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer was introduced into the filtration unit, which was then securely sealed with Luer fittings (ISIS Co., Ltd., Japan), stored on ice, and transported to a laboratory in Toyama Prefectural University, Imizu, Toyama, Japan.

For the recovery of DenV, PEG precipitation was performed in accordance with a previous study53. Briefly, 40 mL of each sample was directly collected in a 50 mL pre-sterilized conical tube containing 4.0 mg PEG 8000 and 2.3 mg sodium chloride. Tap water samples were first collected in a pre-sterilized polyethylene bag and dechlorinated with 1/10,000 volume of 1,000 g/L sodium thiosulfate before processing. After dissolving PEG 8000 and sodium chloride, the samples were kept cool overnight, centrifuged at 7,806 × g (the maximum speed of the ROTANTA 460 RF, Hettich, MA) for 30 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of phosphate buffer and recovered as a virus concentrate.

DNA and RNA extraction

Bacteriophage phiX174 and MNV were used as molecular process controls (MPCs) to assess potential underestimation of gene concentrations due to impurities affecting the DNA extraction–(q)PCR and RNA extraction–RT-qPCR processes, respectively.

For DNA extraction, 6.0 × 103 copies (5 µL of viral stock) of phiX174 were added to the cartridge filter unit containing TE buffer, which had been used for the sample filtration (see Sect. 4.1). The TE buffer was then removed by centrifugation. DNA retained in the filter unit was extracted following the protocol described by Miya et al. (2016),52 using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany), yielding a final DNA extract volume of 200 µL. A positive control for phiX174 was prepared by processing 200 µL of nuclease-free water in the same manner as the sample.

For RNA extraction, 140 µL of the virus concentrate obtained via PEG precipitation was spiked with 1.7 × 104 copies (5 µL of viral stock) of MNV. RNA was then extracted using the QIAamp® Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions, to obtain a 65 µL RNA extract. Samples with < 10% detection efficiency of the spiked MNV (described below) underwent another RNA extraction with purification. In this modified process, the sample was first mixed with 1.4 µL of 10% sodium deoxycholate (Wako, Japan) after lysis buffer (Buffer AVL) was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min45. The resulting RNA extract was further purified via gel filtration using a MicroSpin S-300 h column (GE Healthcare, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A positive control for MNV was prepared by processing 140 µL of nuclease-free in the same manner as the virus concentrate.

(Reverse transcription-) qPCR assays

TaqMan-based (RT-) qPCR assays were conducted for the detection of Plasmodium spp., DenV, and the spiked MPCs (phiX174 and MNV) using a qTOWER3 (Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany). The primer sets and TaqMan probes used in the present study are summarized in Table S151,54–56. For Plasmodium spp. detection, a previously established primer and TaqMan probe set targeting the 18 S rRNA gene of broad Plasmodium spp. was used with slight modifications51. For DenV detection, a primer and probe set designed to detect all DenV serotypes was employed55.

For detection of Plasmodium spp. and phiX174 DNA, qPCR reaction mixture (20 µL) was prepared by mixing 5 µL of DNA extract with 10 µL of a TaqMan™ Gene Expression Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Japan), 400 nM each of forward and reverse primers, 150 nM of a TaqMan probe, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions for qPCR were as follows: Enzyme activation and initial denaturation at 50 °C for 3 min and 95 °C for 10 min followed by 45 cycles of amplification with denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, and annealing and extension at assay-specific temperatures (Table S1) for 60 s.

For detection of DenV and MNV RNA, one step RT-qPCR assays were performed using the One Step PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit (Perfect Real Time) (TaKaRa, Japan). Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained 5 µL of RNA extract, 10 µL of 2×OneStep RT-PCR Buffer III, 0.4 µL of TaKaRa Ex Taq HS, 0.4 µL of PrimeScript™ RT enzyme Mix II, 400 nM each of forward and reverse primers, 150 nM of a TaqMan probe, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions for RT-qPCR were as follows: RT at 42 °C for 5 min, initial denaturation and RT enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 50 cycles of amplification with denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, and annealing and extension at assay-specific temperatures (Table S1) for 30 s.

To generate calibration curves, a 10-fold serial dilution of standard DNA (concentrations ranged from 1.0 × 100 to 1.0 × 105 copies/reaction) was subjected to (RT-)qPCR. DNA and RNA extracts obtained from nuclease-free water spiked with phiX174 and MNV, respectively, were used as positive controls and no-template controls for the spiked MPCs and other target genes. The efficiencies of the DNA extraction–(q)PCR and RNA extraction–RT-qPCR processes were estimated by comparing the measured gene concentrations of spiked phiX174 and MNV, respectively, in nuclease-free water with those in environmental samples.

Conventional PCR assays for mosquitoes and Plasmodium

Two primer sets were used for mosquito DNA amplification (Table S2). The first targeted Aedini mosquitoes (Aedes and Ochlerotatus spp.) using primers flanking the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) and 28S rRNA regions (Bang et al., 2021 with modifications), yielding ~ 450 bp amplicons5. The second primer set primarily targets the 3’ end of the 28 S rRNA gene in Anopheles spp. The forward primer (A0) was adapted from a previous study46 while the reverse primer was the reverse-complement of the UN primer47. This primer set was expected to generate an approximately 350 bp amplicon. For Plasmodium DNA amplification and sequencing, the same primer set used for qPCR was employed (Table S2). All mosquito and Plasmodium primers included sequences at the 5′ end that allow annealing of barcoding primers used in the second-round PCR.

PCR was performed using a TaKaRa PCR Thermal Cycler Dice® Touch (TaKaRa). The first-round PCR reaction mixture (20 µL) contained 5.0 µL of DNA extract, 10.0 µL of KAPA HiFi HS Ready Mix (NIPPON Genetics Co., Ltd., Japan), 400 nM each of forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions for the first-round PCR were as follows: DNA polymerase activation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of amplification, consisting of denaturation at 98 °C for 20 s, annealing at assay-specific temperatures (Table S2) for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min (Table S2). Following amplification, the amplicons were analyzed by gel electrophoresis using a 1.5% agarose gel. Regardless of band visibility, amplicons of the expected size were excised and purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (TaKaRa).

The purified amplicons underwent a second-round PCR to further amplify the target DNA and incorporate index sequences. The reaction mixture (20 µL) contained: 2.0 µL of purified amplicon, 10 µL of KAPA HiFi HS Ready Mix, 400 nM of barcode primers (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK), and nuclease-free water. Barcode primers were used to identify the sample origin of each amplicon. The second-round PCR was performed under the following thermal cycling conditions: DNA polymerase activation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of amplification, consisting of denaturation at 98 °C for 20 s, annealing at 65 °C for 15 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Following the second-round PCR, amplicons were subjected to gel electrophoresis as described above. Only amplicons that produced visible positive bands were excised and purified.

Nanopore sequencing

The purified second-round PCR amplicons were processed for library preparation with the Ligation Sequencing Kit V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies), followed by sequencing on a Flongle Flow Cell (R10.4.1) (Oxford Nanopore Technologies). For sequencing, 5.0 fmol of the prepared DNA library was loaded onto the flow cell. Basecalling was performed using a super-accurate basecalling model in Dorado (https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado). Adapter and primer sequences were trimmed using Porechop (v0.2.4) (https://github.com/rrwick/Porechop). The obtained reads were then subjected to BLAST analysis using BLAST+ (v.2.17, https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/executables/blast+/LATEST/) against reference sequences of Aedes spp., Anopheles spp., Mansonia spp., Culex spp., Ochlerotatus spp., and Plasmodium spp. A read was considered positive for a corresponding taxon if its BLAST score exceeded the expected second-round PCR amplicon size (bp) × 1, in accordance with a previous study57. To compensate for the relatively low sequence accuracy of Nanopore sequencing, OTUs were formed by clustering sequences with ≥ 95% identity using VSEARCH (https://github.com/torognes/vsearch). To avoid OTUs with unreliable sequence, only those represented by five or more sequence reads were retained. The obtained sequence reads and OTUs were aligned using the Clustal W2 program (v2.1) (http://www.clustal.org/clustal2/) with the reference sequences. The distances were calculated using Kimura’s two-parameter method, and phylogenetic dendrograms were generated from a bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates by the neighbor-joining method.
