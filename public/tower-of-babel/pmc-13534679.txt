Hierarchical organization of tau topography across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum integrates amyloid, connectome, and intrinsic molecular vulnerability
Authors: Zhi-Ming Zeng, Shu-Shu Han, Qin Liu, Cui-Die Zeng, Xin Jia, Jing Bi, Ling-Chen Liu, Teng-Ao Gao, Lei Liang, Fang-Xiao Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tau pathology defines the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum and is spatially heterogeneous, yet the roles of amyloid-β (Aβ), connectome architecture, and intrinsic molecular vulnerability remain unclear. Here we integrated Aβ- and tau-PET, diffusion MRI, resting-state fMRI, and transcriptomic maps across the AD continuum. We found that tau pathology followed a hierarchical spatial gradient that progressively stratified by Aβ and clinical stages. Across the AD continuum, Aβ covariance and structural and functional connectivity were associated with tau covariance, and their derived propagation signatures further recapitulated regional tau burden, explaining substantial spatial variance. A residual tau pattern not captured by the modeled propagation signatures was further identified and was associated with reproducible transcriptional gradients involving intracellular signaling, synaptic programs, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and post-transcriptional regulation, with stage-specific shifts in biology. Importantly, tau geometry related to epicenter segregation and perivascular diffusion and mediated their associations with clinical expression. These findings position tau topography as an integrative marker coupling Aβ, connectivity, and intrinsic molecular vulnerability, and indicate that tau geometry is related to the link between network organization and cognitive decline.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is increasingly viewed as a biological continuum in which pathophysiological changes begin decades before symptoms, spanning from clinically silent stages to overt dementia1,2. Conceptualizing AD along this continuum has fundamentally reshaped strategies for early diagnosis, disease staging, and therapeutic intervention3. At the core of this framework lie two hallmark proteins, amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau, whose temporal dynamics and spatial expansion jointly shape disease evolution4,5. Yet, the pathologies of Aβ and tau exhibit strikingly distinct spatiotemporal signatures. Spatially, tau propagates hierarchically from phylogenetically conserved regions toward the neocortex, whereas Aβ accumulates more diffusely across neocortical regions6,7. Temporally, early in the continuum, Aβ and tau co-accumulate in limbic regions, where synaptic interactions increase tau vulnerability to Aβ-related toxicity and hasten pretangle formation8–10. These observations support a tau-centered view of AD in which Aβ acts primarily as an upstream accelerator, underscoring the need to delineate stage-specific mechanisms that govern tau propagation.

Tau pathology is thought to progress hierarchically in a connectivity-dependent manner, propagating transsynaptically along axonal pathways in an activity-dependent fashion rather than diffusing passively into adjacent tissue11,12. Structural connectivity (SC) studies support a ‘prion-like’ mode of neuron-to-neuron transmission, and regional tau expansion can be predicted by interregional axonal connectivity13. In tau transgenic models, focal injection of pathological tau into the hippocampus induces the accumulation of filamentous tau in spatially remote yet anatomically connected regions14. At the functional level, neuronal activity has emerged as a critical modulator of tau seeding and propagation. Several in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that heightened neuronal activity promotes the release of filamentous tau, facilitating its uptake via micropinocytosis and subsequent aggregation in downstream neurons15–17. However, despite converging experimental evidence, it remains unclear in humans whether SC and functional synchrony directly relate to interregional similarity in tau deposition, particularly across different stages of the AD continuum.

Advances in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET) enable in vivo mapping of large-scale structural and functional connectivity (FC), alongside the spatial distribution of tau pathology18–20. Yet imaging-derived patterns cannot fully account for the pronounced regional heterogeneity of tau accumulation. Accumulating evidence suggests that regional susceptibility to tauopathy is, in part, shaped by the underlying genetic and molecular milieu21–23. Large-scale genetic studies have identified numerous AD risk loci, whose expression may confer vulnerability to pathogenic tau, for example, by accelerating its accumulation, or, conversely, may be protective by enhancing clearance mechanisms21. However, the cortical distribution of tau-vulnerable regions shows only limited overlap with the spatial expression profiles of AD-associated genes, and this dissociation between upstream molecular determinants and downstream biology remains a central unresolved question in neurodegeneration24,25. Previous neuroimaging-genetic studies have largely emphasized spatial correlations between regional gene expression and imaging-derived phenotypes, overlooking the Aβ and connectome context in which pathology unfolds22,26. How constitutive regional gene expression interfaces with large-scale network architecture to shape the hierarchical distribution of tau pathology remains poorly understood.

Tauopathy emerges from a limited set of focal epicenters and disseminates along interconnected brain systems27. The balancing segregation and integration of the brain exerts a profound influence on tau spread and cognitive impairment. Recent studies suggest that higher network segregation mitigates cognitive decline in AD, with individuals maintaining better cognition despite greater tau burden28,29. In parallel, the glymphatic system (GS) reshaped our understanding of brain-wide solute exchange and the clearance of neurotoxic proteins30. Diffusion along perivascular spaces, a key GS clearance pathway, has been linked to cognitive function but shows only weak associations with global pathological burden, implying a more regionally specific role31–33. We therefore hypothesized that network segregation and perivascular diffusion jointly shape hierarchical tauopathy across the AD continuum through effects on tau propagation and clearance, thereby influencing regional tau accumulation and cognitive decline.

Here, we integrated multimodal neuroimaging and transcriptomics to address the pivotal question of how tau topography is organized across the AD continuum. Specifically, we tested three hypotheses: (1) regional tau is constrained by Aβ covariation and by the structural and functional connectomes, and network-mediated propagation accounts for a substantial fraction of regional tau burden in a stage-dependent manner; (2) beyond the network-related propagation signatures, we identified a reproducible residual tau component that was associated with shared transcriptional gradients reflecting structural, synaptic, and metabolic biological processes; and (3) tau geometry provides a bridge from brain network architecture and perivascular diffusion to neurodegeneration. Results

Spatial distribution of abnormal tau across Aβ-stage groups

All group demographic and clinical characteristics and the directionality of group differences are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Across the AD continuum, tau-PET showed a hierarchical spatial pattern that was strongly stratified by Aβ status and clinical stage (Supplementary Fig 1A). In the CN Aβ− and MCI Aβ− groups, elevated tau was largely confined to the inferior lateral temporal cortex, with little spatial expansion and only sparse Aβ deposition. Tau burden increased stepwise from CN Aβ+ to MCI Aβ+ and AD, spreading from temporal regions into frontal association cortices in a Braak-like pattern, whereas Aβ deposition was widespread across the neocortex and was most pronounced in MCI Aβ+ and AD. Network- and group-level profiling of tau uptake revealed distinct, stage-consistent patterns (Supplementary Fig 1B): tau abnormalities involved multiple systems and increased with clinical severity from CN Aβ+ to MCI Aβ+ and AD, whereas CN Aβ− and MCI Aβ− showed comparable profiles.

Aβ covariance, SC, and FC associated with tau covariance

Tau covariance, Aβ covariance, SC, and FC matrices exhibited clear modular organization across the AD continuum (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Spatial regression revealed a robust correspondence between Aβ covariance and tau covariance, such that region pairs with stronger Aβ covariance exhibited higher tau covariance (β, 0.36–0.60; all P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Similar relationships were observed for the SC (β, 7.7-–15.2; all P < 0.001) and FC (β, 0.2–0.57; all P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). These relationships also persisted when controlling for Euclidean distance (all P < 0.001). The corresponding unadjusted (β1) and Euclidean distance–adjusted (β2) coefficients with 95% confidence intervals are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Propagation signatures are spatially associated with tau-PET uptake

Propagation signatures quantified the extent to which seed-to-target Aβ covariance, SC, or FC aligned with the distribution of tau, thereby indexing the component of regional tau topography statistically captured by network-related spatial organization. Across all subgroups, higher tau uptake was associated with stronger propagation signatures of Aβ covariance (r, 0.63–0.78; all Pspin < 0.005), SC (r, 0.69–0.76; all Pspin < 0.005), or FC (r, 0.71–0.83; Pspin < 0.005; Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 3A). Seed-extreme analyses further revealed a consistent bidirectional pattern: stronger Aβ covariance, SC, and FC from hot-spot were associated with higher target tau, whereas stronger Aβ covariance, SC, and FC from cold-spot were associated with lower target tau, indicating that strongly coupled regions tend to share similarly high or low tau levels (Supplementary Fig. 4). Across all subgroups, variance inflation factors (VIFs) for Aβ covariance, SC, and FC were all below 5 (Supplementary Table 4), indicating no substantial multicollinearity. Models regressing regional tau uptake on propagation signatures indicated that single-modality predictors explained a substantial proportion of regional variance (R², 0.41–0.70; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Model performance was generally higher in Aβ-positive CN/MCI than in Aβ-negative CN/MCI, whereas AD showed a plateau or modest attenuation, consistent with additional late-stage determinants beyond any single propagation channel. Multimodal integration improved and stabilized performance, with the combined Aβ + SC + FC model achieving the best fit (R², 0.64–0.72; Supplementary Fig. 5B). The model performance with 95% CIs is provided in Supplementary Table 5. Sensitivity analyses based on the Destrieux atlas demonstrated that the main propagation findings were largely preserved. The associations between propagation signatures and regional tau uptake remained significant across all groups for Aβ covariance, structural connectivity, and functional connectivity (Supplementary Table 6), and the overall pattern of model performance was similar to that observed with the Schaefer atlas, with multimodal predictors consistently showing the best performance (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Fig. 6). RMSE and MAE increased with clinical severity and were highest in AD, suggesting progressively greater contributions from non-propagation factors at later stages. After removing network-related propagation effects, residual tau retained a structured, stage-varying cortical topography (Fig. 3B) and remained strongly correlated with regional tau uptake across all groups (all Pspin < 0.005; Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. 3B), indicating a substantial independent component shaping tau distribution.

Gene expression profiles related to residual tau topography

PLS analyses in each group identified a reproducible transcriptional gradient that tracked residual tau topography beyond the modeled effects of Aβ covariance, SC, and FC. To assess the influence of donor-specific sampling on these findings, we conducted leave-one-donor-out (LODO) analyses. Across all subgroups, PLS1 gene weights remained highly consistent with those from the full-donor analysis, with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.906 to 0.943 in CN Aβ−, 0.850–0.905 in MCI Aβ−, 0.891–0.914 in CN Aβ+, 0.843–0.892 in MCI Aβ+, and 0.835–0.889 in AD (all P < 0.001; Supplementary Table 8). In the full-donor analysis, PLS1 explained 21.9%, 20.3%, 19.1%, 18.1%, and 17.7% of the variance in residual tau topography in CN Aβ−, MCI Aβ−, CN Aβ+, MCI Aβ+, and AD, respectively, with similar results observed in the LODO analyses (Supplementary Table 9).

Across the AD continuum, 475 genes were consistently identified in the intersection of significant PLS1 genes (PFDR < 0.05) across all five subgroups. Among these, 70 genes showed significant positive spatial correlations and 69 showed significant negative spatial correlations with tau uptake (Pspin < 0.05, Fig. 4A); the strongest positive association was observed for KIRREL3 (r = 0.582, Pspin < 0.001) and the strongest negative association for SYT6 (r = −0.560, Pspin < 0.001), with the expression gradient recapitulating the hierarchical pattern of tau. Significant genes associated with the residual tau pattern across the AD continuum are listed in Supplementary Table 10. These results suggest that shared genetic architecture contributes to tau spatial organization beyond network-related propagation across the AD continuum. Across the AD continuum, stage-specific enrichment and PPI analyses revealed a progressive shift in tau-associated biology (Supplementary Fig. 7): CN Aβ− signatures were dominated by mitochondrial respiration/ATP synthesis and basal metabolism, whereas MCI Aβ− and CN Aβ+ increasingly highlighted neurite/cytoskeletal remodeling and synaptic–GPCR/Ca²⁺-linked signaling. With advancing impairment (MCI Aβ+ to AD), enrichment broadened toward RNA processing/splicing, translation, chromatin remodeling, and cellular stress-response modules, forming denser PPI cores.

Epicenter segregation and perivascular diffusion associated with tau spatial gradient

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the tau spatial gradient across alternative atlases and parcellation resolutions, with strong correlations between Schaefer200-derived values and those from the Schaefer400 (rs = 0.956, P < 0.001) and Destrieux (rs = 0.938, P < 0.001) atlases (Supplementary Fig. 8). Tau geometry, epicenter segregation, and DTI-ALPS showed significant associations with cognitive performance (Supplementary Table 12). Group comparisons showed stage-dependent shifts in tau geometry, epicenter segregation, and DTI-ALPS (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. 9A): the tau spatial gradient and epicenter segregation were amplified in MCI Aβ+ and AD, whereas DTI-ALPS progressively declined with advancing disease stage. Mediation analyses indicated that the tau spatial gradient was associated with the links of epicenter segregation and DTI–ALPS to cognition, accounting for ~70% and ~40% of the total effects, respectively (all P < 0.05; Fig. 5C). In multiple-mediator models, epicenter segregation and DTI–ALPS were associated with cognition through a serial mediators in which they first shaped the tau spatial gradient, which in turn influenced global tau burden, yielding indirect-effect proportions of 53.5% and 35.6% (all P < 0.05), respectively (Fig. 5D). These findings were robust across alternative epicenter definitions and hemisphere-specific ALPS indices (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Discussion

The mechanisms underlying the heterogeneous spatial patterns of tau aggregation remain poorly understood. In this study, we integrated multimodal neuroimaging and transcriptomics to delineate how tau topography is organized across the AD continuum. Tau exhibited a hierarchical gradient that was sharply stratified by Aβ status, with Aβ− groups showing elevtions restricted to temporal region and Aβ+ groups and AD displaying a Braak-like pattern of progressive spread. Consistent with this stage-dependent topography, inter-regional tau covariance showed modular organization that closely tracked Aβ covariance as well as SC and FC, and propagation signatures derived from Aβ covariance, SC, and FC explained a substantial proportion of regional tau burden. Moreover, removing the modeled network-related propagation effects revealed a residual tau component that was reproducibly linked to a shared transcriptional gradient spanning intracellular signaling, synaptic/neurite programs, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and post-transcriptional regulation. We also found that tau geometry emerged as a candidate mediator linking epicenter segregation, perivascular diffusion, and clinical expression in the mediation analyses. To our knowledge, this study is the first to dissociate a residual tau component from the network-related propagation effects and to demonstrate that tau propagation geometry mediates links between physiology and clinical symptoms. This work advances understanding of the multiscale mechanisms that shape tau topography along the AD continuum.

Substantial progress has been made in clarifying the respective roles of Aβ and tau in AD, with converging evidence supporting a model in which Aβ acts as an upstream trigger that facilitates tau aggregation and spread, while tau pathology more directly tracks neurodegeneration and clinical symptoms10,34,35. Consistent with this framework, our results demonstrate that the hierarchical spatial gradient of tau is sharply stratified by Aβ status. Furthermore, widespread Aβ involvement in cognitively normal individuals, together with comparable tau levels observed between MCI Aβ− and CN Aβ− groups, underscores the need for integrative approaches that jointly consider molecular pathology and network context. Importantly, prior experimental work has shown that Aβ induces neuronal hyperactivity and hyperconnectivity, while tau spreads transsynaptically in an activity-dependent manner along connected circuits36,37. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that both Aβ and tau pathologies, as well as aging-related factors, are associated with white matter injury, which may in turn alter large-scale brain connectivity38–40. White matter lesion burden was associated with lower within- and greater between-network connectivity, and lower tract-specific functional connectivity41,42. In line with recent tau-PET studies, we confirm that regions embedded within highly connected network neighborhoods tend to exhibit similar tau levels43,44. However, a notable limitation of the majority of combined tau and connectivity analysis is that they overlook the variability in Aβ and disease stage45. We extend previous observations by showing that the similarity and strength of Aβ covariance, SC, and FC jointly account for a substantial proportion of regional tau variation across the AD continuum. The novelty of our current study is that it quantitatively links tau spatial organization to Aβ covariance, structural and functional connectivity through propagation signatures. We observed that the explanatory contribution of Aβ covariance to tau spatial patterns diminishes with advancing disease stage, whereas network-related propagation via SC and FC becomes increasingly dominant. Together, these findings are consistent with a stage-dependent shift from amyloid-conditioned vulnerability toward stronger connectivity-related spatial coupling in tau topography.

Removal of network-related propagation effects revealed that tau residuals retained a structured, stage-dependent cortical organization that closely mirrored the observed tau topography, indicating that hierarchical tau gradients cannot be explained by connectivity constraints alone. By integrating regional transcriptomic profiles with tau-PET residual maps, our findings provide evidence that constitutive molecular architecture contributes to tau accumulation beyond the influence of amyloid burden and large-scale network transmission. Previous genetic studies, including genome-wide association analyses implicating APOE and polygenic risk scores, have established links between genetic risk and global Aβ or tau burden46–49. However, these approaches largely rely on whole-brain measures and are limited in their ability to interrogate the spatial organization of tau pathology. More recent imaging-transcriptomic studies have attempted to relate regional gene expression to tau vulnerability, yet their conclusions have been inconsistent, likely reflecting methodological heterogeneity, and small PET sample sizes50–52. Critically, ignoring network-related propagation conflates intrinsic molecular susceptibility with transneuronal spread, limiting both interpretation and translational relevance22,53. Our analysis isolated a residual tau component not captured by Aβ covariance, SC, and FC propagation signatures and demonstrated that it is reproducibly anchored by a shared transcriptional gradient across disease stages. The genes contributing to this gradient were enriched for pathways governing intracellular signaling, synaptic and neurite organization, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and post-transcriptional regulation, implicating coordinated cellular processes that regulate tau phosphorylation, metabolic stress tolerance, and proteostatic control54–56. Furthermore, stage-specific enrichment revealed a progressive biological shift along the AD continuum, from metabolic and mitochondrial programs in CN Aβ−, toward synaptic remodeling and GPCR-linked signaling in prodromal stages, and ultimately to cellular stress-response pathways in AD. Together, these findings suggest that tau topography reflects not a single molecular vulnerability, but a dynamic hierarchy of intrinsic biological constraints that evolve with disease progression and operate in parallel with network-driven dissemination.

Distinct morphologies of tau propagation characterize disease biology57. Whereas prior work has mainly emphasized tau burden or region-specific involvement58,59, our findings highlight the tau spatial gradient as a compact descriptor of the hierarchical distribution of tau pathology across the cortex. This gradient captures the relative balance between epicenter-dominated tau accumulation and more widespread cortical involvement, thereby integrating information about both dissemination and containment. Within this framework, our results extend previous observations by showing that network segregation is not only associated with cognitive resilience but also actively shapes the spatial form of tau spread60,61. Complementing this network-related effect, perivascular diffusion exerted an opposing influence on tau geometry62. Rather than tracking global tau burden, DTI–ALPS in our data was related to a flatter tau gradient. This pattern supports a regionally specific role of perivascular diffusion, acting on the spatial redistribution of tau across the cortical mantle rather than on total burden alone63,64. Importantly, the mediation effects are consistent with a model in which network architecture and perivascular diffusion are jointly associated with a common geometric property of tau pathology, which in turn relates to neurodegeneration and symptoms. These findings position tau geometry as a candidate mediator linking connectome organization, perivascular diffusion, and cognitive decline across the AD continuum.

Several caveats should be considered when interpreting the results of the current study. First, the cross-sectional design limits temporal inference regarding stage-dependent shifts in network-related tau components and regional molecular susceptibility. Second, SC and FC provide imperfect proxies for actural synaptic routes: tractography is constrained by crossing fibers and distance biases, while rsfMRI reflects both direct and polysynaptic coupling and may be affected by pathological noise, which could blur the mechanistic interpretation of propagation estimates. Third, DTI–ALPS is an indirect index of glymphatic function and may be affected by underlying white matter microstructure65,66; multimodal biomarkers and physiological measures would strengthen inference. Fourth, the group-wise matrices were estimated from 200 regional variables within subgroups of modest size, and these matrices should be interpreted as descriptive large-scale spatial similarity structures rather than as precise edge-wise covariance estimates. Fifth, the subgroup-specific transcriptomic and enrichment analyses were performed on residual tau maps derived within relatively small subgroups; thus, the inferred stage-related biological shifts should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a progressive molecular sequence. Finally, transcriptomic anchoring relied on AHBA microarray maps from a small number of neurotypical adult donors. Although macroscale spatial expression patterns appear relatively stable, the limited sample may introduce uncertainty67; replication with larger, independent, disease-relevant spatial transcriptomic resources will be important.

In conclusion, this study provides an integrative framework for understanding how tau pathology is spatially organized across the AD continuum. Through joint quantification of network-related propagation and regional molecular vulnerability, tau topography emerges as an integrated phenotype that reflects the interplay among Aβ covariance, structural and functional connectivity, and molecular vulnerability, while linking brain network architecture and perivascular diffusion to clinical expression. These findings underscore the need to move beyond global burden measures toward geometry-informed markers of tau pathology, with important implications for disease stratification, longitudinal monitoring, and targeted intervention across the AD continuum.

Methods

Participants

We analyzed data from 192 participants in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) who had available Aβ-PET, tau-PET, T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI). Aβ status (Aβ+/−) was determined using PET-core–derived global SUVR cut-offs for ¹⁸F-florbetapir (standardized uptake value ratio, SUVR > 1.11) and ¹⁸F-florbetaben (SUVR > 1.08), as provided by the ADNI PET Core68. Clinical diagnosis was assigned by ADNI investigators as cognitively normal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or AD. The resulting Aβ/diagnostic-defined groups comprised 74 CN Aβ−, 26 MCI Aβ−, 45 CN Aβ+, 22 MCI Aβ + , and 25 AD participants. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Supplementary Method 1. All participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval from the individual Institutional Review Board (IRB; https://adni.loni.usc.edu/help-faqs/adni-documentation/) was granted to the investigators at each ADNI participating site. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Neuropsychological assessment included measures spanning global cognition, dementia severity, episodic memory, executive function, and daily functioning. Global cognition and dementia severity were indexed with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Clinical Dementia Rating Scale–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-11/13/Q4), and the modified Preclinical Alzheimer’s Cognitive Composite (mPACC). Episodic memory was assessed with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) and Logical Memory delayed recall (LDELTOTAL), executive function with Trail Making Test Part B time (TRABSCOR), and daily functioning with the Everyday Cognition scales completed by the participant and study partner (ECogPT and ECogSP) and the Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ); further details are provided in Supplementary Method 2.

PET and MRI acquisition

Aβ-PET was acquired using either ¹⁸F-florbetapir or ¹⁸F-florbetaben, and tau-PET using ¹⁸F-flortaucipir. All MRI data were collected on 3.0 T scanners. High-resolution structural imaging was performed with a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (3D MPRAGE) sequence. DTI was acquired using a spin-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence, and rsfMRI was obtained with a gradient-echo EPI sequence under eyes-open resting conditions. Full acquisition parameters are provided in Supplementary Method 3.

PET and MRI preprocessing

For Aβ- and tau-PET, images were motion corrected, registered to each participant’s structural space, and smoothed. The regional PET measures were extracted within the cortical parcellation.

DTI were preprocessed in MRtrix3 (v3.0.4; https://www.mrtrix.org) following the recommended pipeline69. Steps comprised brain masking, denoising, Gibbs-ringing removal, and correction for eddy-current distortions and head motion, after which diffusion data were aligned to the corresponding structural image. Tissue-type classification was performed to derive cortical and subcortical gray matter, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and lesion/pathological tissue compartments, and the white–gray matter interface was extracted for downstream analyses.

rsfMRI was preprocessed using AFNI (v25.1.03; https://afni.nimh.nih.gov). Preprocessing included removal of the first five volumes, slice-timing correction, motion correction, co-registration to the individual structural image, spatial normalization, and spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM). Linear trends, the 6 motion parameters, their 6 temporal derivatives, and nuisance signals from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid were regressed out. Time points with excessive motion ( > 0.3 mm), together with their neighboring time points, were censored. Finally, the data were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.10 Hz). Full preprocessing details are provided in Supplementary Method 4.

Cortical parcellation

We utilized the Schaefer (2018) parcellation to define 200 regions of interest (ROIs) for all analyses70. Each parcel was assigned to one of seven canonical functional systems: visual network (VN), somatomotor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), ventral attention network (VAN), limbic network (LN), frontoparietal control network (FPN), and default mode network (DMN).

Aβ and tau covariance

Cortical Aβ and tau SUVRs were computed using the whole cerebellum and the inferior cerebellar gray as the reference regions, repectively. In line with previous studies71,72, we first computed tracer uptake within each of the 200 Schaefer parcels for every participant, yielding a 200-element regional uptake vector per subject for quantifying inter-regional covariance of Aβ- and tau-PET. Within each Aβ/diagnostic-defined group, we then estimated ROI-to-ROI covariance as the across-subject Spearman correlation between regional SUVRs for every pair of parcels, generating a 200 × 200 covariance matrix for Aβ and for tau. Spearman rank correlations were used to reduce sensitivity to extreme PET values. The Aβ and tau covariance matrices were interpreted as group-level inter-regional similarity structures that summarize large-scale spatial organization across participants. Self-correlations were set to zero, and correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed to enable direct comparability with connectivity matrices.

Structural and functional connectivity

SC was reconstructed from preprocessed DTI. Diffusion tensors were fitted using weighted least squares with iteratively reweighted refinement to derive fractional anisotropy (FA) maps. Fiber orientation distributions were then estimated, and whole-brain probabilistic tractography was performed using second-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2). Streamlines were seeded within the white-matter mask and terminated upon reaching low anisotropy (FA < 0.2), excessive curvature (turning angle > 45°), or exiting the brain mask. Two parcels were deemed structurally connected if at least one streamline linked them; the resulting undirected SC matrix was weighted by streamline counts. Group-mean SC matrices were then derived by averaging individual matrices within each group.

For rsfMRI, parcel-wise blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) time series were obtained by averaging voxel signals within each region. FC was estimated as Pearson correlations between regional time series, with coefficients Fisher z-transformed and self-correlations set to zero. Group-mean FC matrices were then derived by averaging individual matrices within each group.

Tau epicenters and epicenter segregation

Participant-specific tau epicenters were identified using a Gaussian mixture model (GMM) fitted to regional tau-PET uptake73. For each individual, we derived the posterior probability that each region belonged to the right-most mixture component, interpreted as reflecting an abnormal tau signal. This probability provides a continuous, threshold-free estimate of tau positivity and reduces reliance on a priori thresholds. To further down-weight non-specific signal, regional SUVR values were multiplied by their corresponding GMM-derived probabilities, yielding probability-weighted SUVRs. Tau epicenters were then defined for each participant as the regions with the highest probability-weighted SUVR values.

Network determinants of tau propagation

Step 1: Coupling between tau covariance and Aβ covariance/SC/FC

Within each group, we tested whether inter-regional tau covariance was coupled to (i) Aβ covariance, (ii) SC, or (iii) FC using spatial regression. Specifically, the group-mean tau covariance served as the dependent variable, and the corresponding group-mean Aβ covariance, SC, or FC matrix served as the predictor. Statistical inference was performed using 2000 bootstrap resamples, recomputing group-average matrices in each resample to obtain a bootstrap distribution of regression coefficients, which was evaluated against zero using a one-sample t-test. To rule out confounding by spatial adjacency, we repeated the analysis while adjusting for inter-regional Euclidean distance. The Euclidean distance was determined as described in Supplementary Method 5.

Step 2: Seed-dependent propagation signatures

To evaluate whether the strength of Aβ covariance, SC, or FC reflects tau propagation, we conducted a seed-based regression analysis. For each seed ROI, we regressed tau-PET uptake across target ROIs on the corresponding seed-to-target Aβ covariance, SC, or FC, yielding a seed-dependent propagation signature that quantifies how strongly the connectivity/covariance profile is associated with the spatial distribution of tau across the brain. We next assessed whether tau burden at a seed region is associated with its propagation signature using Pearson correlation. For map-level association analyses, statistical significance was assessed using spin-test permutations (2000 rotations). We also analyzed the seed-to-target association patterns for tau “hot-spots” (highest tau) versus “cold-spots” (lowest tau).

Step 3: Variance in tau uptake accounted for by propagation signatures

To quantify the variance in regional tau burden accounted for by propagation signatures, we fitted linear models with tau uptake as the dependent variable and the corresponding propagation signatures as predictors. Separate models were estimated using ββ derived from Aβ covariance, SC, and FC individually, as well as from combined predictors (SC + FC and Aβ + SC + FC). To assess potential multicollinearity among propagation-signature predictors, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for Aβ covariance, SC, and FC within each subgroup. Model performance and stability were summarized using R², root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE), and were evaluated using repeated tenfold cross-validation (ten repeats) to assess out-of-sample generalizability. To assess the robustness of the findings to atlas selection, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the Destrieux atlas as an alternative cortical parcellation scheme. The propagation analyses, including estimation of Aβ and tau covariance, derivation of propagation signatures, association analyses with regional tau uptake, and prediction models for tau burden, were repeated under this alternative parcellation.

Transcriptomic anchoring of tau propagation

Regional gene-expression data were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA; https://human.brain-map.org), which provides microarray transcriptional profiles from multiple sampling sites. Transcriptional profiles were mapped to the Schaefer (2018) 200-parcel cortical atlas using the abagen toolbox. Donor sampling profiles and per-parcel donor coverage are summarized in Supplementary Method 6. To relate constitutive transcriptional patterns to tau processes beyond amyloid and connectome effects, we constructed residual tau maps for each subgroup by regressing tau uptake on propagation signatures of Aβ covariance, SC, and FC. Partial least squares (PLS) regression was then performed within each subgroup to identify multivariate transcriptional components that explained spatial variation in residual tau maps. The stability of gene weights for the first PLS component (PLS1) was quantified using bootstrap resampling of regions (2000 resamples with replacement), and genes with false discovery rate–corrected significance (PFDR < 0.05) on PLS1 were retained for downstream analyses. The genes were subsequently validated by correlating their spatial expression profiles with the average tau uptake, with significance assessed using spin-test permutations (2000 rotations). To evaluate the robustness of the transcriptomic findings to donor-specific effects, we performed a leave-one-donor-out (LODO) analysis. Specifically, AHBA mapping and PLS regression were repeated six times, each time excluding one donor. For each subgroup, PLS1 gene weights from each LODO analysis were correlated with those obtained from the full-donor analysis, and the variance explained by PLS1 was also recorded.

To delineate shared versus subgroup-specific molecular mechanisms across the AD continuum, we derived the intersection of significant genes across the AD continuum and, separately, group-unique gene sets. These gene lists were submitted to pathway and process enrichment analysis using Metascape (https://metascape.org), querying multiple knowledge bases including GO Biological Processes, KEGG, Reactome, Canonical Pathways, CORUM, WikiPathways, and PANTHER. Enriched terms were retained using Metascape’s default stringency and clustered based on term-gene membership similarity. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) enrichment was evaluated using STRING, BioGrid, OmniPath, and InWeb_IM, restricting to physical interactions (STRING physical score > 0.132 and BioGrid physical interactions). For networks comprising 3–500 proteins, densely connected modules were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) algorithm.

Tau spatial gradient

Participant-specific tau geometry was summarized using a tau spatial gradient, defined as the slope of the relationship between regional tau-PET uptake and its rank order across the cortex. To assess the robustness of the tau spatial gradient to atlas definition and parcellation resolution, we performed sensitivity analyses using two alternative cortical parcellation schemes: the Schaefer 400 atlas and the Destrieux atlas. Associations between cognitive measures and tau geometry, epicenter segregation, and DTI–ALPS was evaluated using Spearman rank correlations, and P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) method to control multiple testing.

Mediation analyses were then used to evaluate whether the tau geometry statistically links epicenter segregation or perivascular diffusion to global tau burden, specifying epicenter segregation or DTI–ALPS as the predictor, global tau burden as the outcome, and the tau spatial gradient as the mediator, with age and sex included as covariates. To determine whether these relationships extend to cognitive impairment through tau pathology, multiple-mediator models were fitted with clinical stage (reflecting the progression from CN to MCI and AD) as the outcome and both the tau spatial gradient and global tau burden entered simultaneously as mediators, again adjusting for age and sex. For all mediation analyses, statistical significance of indirect effects was evaluated using non-parametric bootstrapping with 2000 resamples.

Statistics and reproducibility

Analyses were conducted in MATLAB (version R2024a; MathWorks), SPSS (version 27.0; IBM), and R (version 4.4.1; http://www.R-project.org), with the corresponding code deposited in Zenodo [https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20278594]75. Continuous variables are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for approximately normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range, IQR) for skewed distributions. Differences across groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and compared using χ² tests. All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.
