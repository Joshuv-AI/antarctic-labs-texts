FrankenStat I: a New Approach to Pulsar Timing Array Data Combination
David Wright, Kalista Wayt, Jeffrey S. Hazboun, Xavier Siemens, Rutger van Haasteren, Levi Schult, Stephen R. Taylor

Abstract
In 2023, after more than two decades of searching, pulsar timing array (PTA) collaborations around the world announced evidence for a stochastic gravitational wave background. It was quickly followed by work from the International Pulsar Timing Array (IPTA), demonstrating that the results of regional collaborations were consistent with each other. The combination of these datasets is still ongoing and represents a significant investment of time and expertise. In that IPTA comparison, authors of this letter combined the separate datasets in the standard PTA optimal detection statistic for cross-correlations incoherently, that is, the data was combined without fitting a merged timing model across all PTA datasets, treating datasets of the same pulsar as independent, and neglecting the “same pulsar, different datasets” cross-correlations. This work refines that method by extending its core ideas beyond detection statistics and into a full, general data-combination method. We have demonstrated its efficacy and extreme efficiency on simulated data. This new method, FrankenStat , is very similar in sensitivity and parameter-constraining power to traditional data combination methods while completing the full data combination in just a few minutes.

III FrankenStat data combination

Typically, PTA data combination involves combining TOAs and fitting a single, merged TM.
The FrankenStat method of combining data, unlike the typical strategy, does not require a merged TM.
We instead opt to concatenate the residuals,

\boldsymbol{\delta t}=\bxmat[showtop=2,format=#1_{#2}]{\boldsymbol{\delta t}}{n}{1},

(6)

assuming that each PTA (denoted as subscripts) has adequately fit a TM to each pulsar.
After concatenating the residuals, we can proceed to build the likelihood.

For now, we limit the residuals to originate from one pulsar in possibly many different PTAs.
To correctly model our new, concatenated residuals we must also combine the TM perturbations
\boldsymbol{M\epsilon}
, Fourier series
\boldsymbol{Fa}
, and white noise covariance
\boldsymbol{N}
.

The Fourier design matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
is
{N_{TOA}\times 2N_{freq}}
, consisting of alternating sines and cosines evaluated at each TOA and modeled frequency where the frequencies are based on the total timing baseline of the pulsar.
The correct combination of
\boldsymbol{F}
is therefore just the concatenation along the first dimension,

\boldsymbol{F}=\bxmat[showtop=2,format=#1_{#2}]{\boldsymbol{F}}{n}{1},

(7)

and the coefficients
\boldsymbol{a}
are unaffected by this scheme.
Note that this concatenation, along with the following white noise covariance matrix combination, are exactly the same for a typical data combination as well.

The white noise covariance
\boldsymbol{N}
is modeled per receiver/back-end combination
(
19
;
4
)
.
To construct a combined
\boldsymbol{N}
, we build it as usual while including blocks for each receiver/back-end combination in the new concatenated set of residuals.
This will result in a block diagonal, square matrix:

\boldsymbol{N}=\bdiagmat[empty={}]{\boldsymbol{N}_{1},\boldsymbol{N}_{2},\ddots,\boldsymbol{N}_{n}}.

(8)

The biggest difference in this new analysis is that the TM Taylor expansion term,
\boldsymbol{M\epsilon}
, requires a different combination method.
The TM design matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
is
N_{TOA}\times N_{par}
, consisting of the first derivative of each TM component evaluated at the least-squares fit and each TOA for a given pulsar.
The second dimension of
\boldsymbol{M}
will typically not be the same for the same pulsar in different PTAs.
Furthermore, even if the dimension agreed, the least-squares fit may be different for each PTA and result in different first derivatives.
Thus, it is incorrect to simply concatenate the design matrices
\boldsymbol{M}
along the TOA dimension.
We will instead construct a
block diagonal
design matrix:

\boldsymbol{M\epsilon}=\bdiagmat[empty={}]{\boldsymbol{M}_{1},\boldsymbol{M}_{2},\ddots,\boldsymbol{M}_{n}}\bxmat[showtop=2,format=#1_{#2}]{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}{n}{1},

(9)

This is the simplest choice for treating the TM perturbations. It is also a conservative one. By ignoring that some of these parameters are the same physical process for a given pulsar, or that others are correlated, we add freedom/uncertainty to the model that can only lead to a loss of information. As we will show, this has
minimal
effect on the outcome of the analysis.

Note that some of these techniques were previously applied to single pulsar analyses of IPTA DR2. Known as the Borg method, it stitched constituent pulsars together with common intrinsic red noise, dispersion measure, and GWB parameters
28
. FrankenStat builds on this foundation, delivering a comprehensive approach to incoherent data combination.

These steps can be repeated per-pulsar, per-PTA.
The resulting concatenated and block-diagonal quantities can be used in all typical PTA likelihoods.

Before analyzing the detection statistics, we check that the FrankenStat and Combined PTAs recover the same estimated GWB parameters.
As seen in Figure

VI Conclusions

We have developed a PTA data combination method,
FrankenStat
, that is simple to implement and have demonstrated its efficacy.
Contrasting usual PTA data combination strategies that take years and considerable computational resources, FrankenStat pulsar combinations can be run on virtually any modern computer in minutes.

FrankenStat is not only fast and efficient---it also has similar sensitivity to gravitational waves as a traditionally combined dataset.
The implementation of FrankenStat used in this letter is available on GitHub
4
4

4

https://github.com/davecwright3/frankenstat-paper-1
.
For readers interested in using FrankenStat in PTA analyses, we recommend using the Python package
MetaPulsar

(
33
)
.

MetaPulsar
contains an implementation of FrankenStat, alongside other data-combination utilities and strategies.
Going forward, the reference FrankenStat implementation will be maintained there.

Late in the preparation of this work, an analysis of IPTA DR3 datasets was posted
39
which uses
MetaPulsar
in a different mode that has yet to be validated in publicly available work. One clear advantage of FrankenStat over the method used in
39
is that it sidesteps any questions about which dataset’s timing model parameters will be used. We also note that the anomalous WN parameters reported therein could be related to the challenges we point out with MAP-estimate convergence (see fn.
3
).

As an extension to the FrankenStat work shown here, we can treat datasets of the same pulsar as independent and neglect the “same pulsar, different datasets” cross-correlations in the calculation of the standard PTA optimal detection statistic. This approach to data combination, which is what was originally applied in the IPTA comparison paper
2
, is what we have named, “The Covariance Approach.” A detailed derivation that highlights corrections to the statistic’s variance that were neglected in the original IPTA comparison will be described here
37
.

We encourage the adoption of our method in PTA data analysis pipelines. Once constructed, FrankenPulsars can be used for any signal searches across multiple datasets.

Appendix: Proof of FrankenStat SNR upper bound for nested case

Here we prove that if the blocks of a block-diagonal FrankenStat TM design matrix comprise
exactly
the Combined, non-block diagonal TM design matrix, then the FrankenStat SNR is
less than or equal to
the Combined SNR.
In other words, this proof is valid if both the FrankenStat and Combined TM design matrix are produced from the
same TM fit
. This would be the case if the individual PTAs’ TM fits are the same as the Combined TM fit.

The proof proceeds by showing that, in the case described, the inverse-noise-weighted transmission function
\mathcal{N}_{I}^{-1}(f)

(
18
)

where
S_{h}(f)\equiv P_{h}(f)/\mathcal{R}(f)
,
P_{h}=\frac{A^{2}_{\textrm{gw}}}{12\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{f}{f_{\textrm{yr}}}\right)^{2\alpha}f^{-3}
is the one-side strain PSD, and
\mathcal{R}(f)=\left(12\pi^{2}f^{2}\right)^{-1}

Lemma 1

The trace of a projection matrix times a positive semidefinite matrix is greater than or equal to zero.

Proof.

A projection matrix is positive semidefinite.
It suffices to prove that the trace of the product of two positive semidefinite matrices is non-negative.
Let

We can take a unique matrix square root of either.
Proceed with the square root of

The trace becomes

\displaystyle\Tr\left[AX\right]
