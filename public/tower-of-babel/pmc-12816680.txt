A multi-dimensional lightweight attention-enhanced model for medical image segmentation
Authors: Mei Shang, Pyeoungkee Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Medical image segmentation is crucial in medical imaging analysis: based on grayscale, texture, and structural features, it precisely partitions an image into semantic subregions to enable accurate localization and analysis of lesions or anatomical structures. In recent years, deep learning methods—especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs)—have achieved remarkable progress in this field; however, fixed kernel sizes and limited receptive fields hinder adequate modeling of global dependencies. Although introducing multi-branch designs or global perception based on vision Transformers can partially alleviate this issue, they often increase computational complexity and are therefore limited in resource-constrained medical scenarios. To address this, we propose a multi-dimensional, lightweight, attention-enhanced medical image segmentation model. Our model integrates omni-dimensional dynamic convolution and mask attention: it performs adaptive modeling across spatial, channel, and kernel-number dimensions to significantly expand the effective receptive field, and employs binary masks to suppress background interference and focus on critical boundaries, with lower computational cost than global self-attention. Evaluated on three public benchmark datasets, our model achieves segmentation accuracy and inference efficiency that are superior to or comparable with current mainstream methods, demonstrating its potential applicability and practical value in clinical settings.

Introduction

Medical image segmentation is a fundamental task in medical imaging analysis, aiming to partition images into anatomically or pathologically meaningful regions to assist disease diagnosis, treatment planning, and postoperative assessment1. However, this task continues to face numerous challenges, such as limited sample sizes, complex morphological characteristics of lesions, and interference from artifacts and noise introduced during image acquisition2.

In recent years, rapid advances in deep learning have significantly propelled progress in medical image segmentation, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) playing a dominant role3. The U-Net architecture proposed by Ronneberger et al.4, employing skip connections to merge low and high-resolution feature maps, has greatly improved contextual understanding and detail capture. Although U-Net and its enhanced versions (e.g., UNet++, U-Net3+, Attention U-Net, and nnU-Net) have demonstrated effectiveness in feature representation and multi-scale information capture5–8, their fixed convolutional kernel sizes inherently limit the modeling of long-range dependencies. Consequently, these architectures struggle with multi-scale lesions, complex anatomical structures, and indistinct boundaries.

To overcome the limitations of static convolutions, Transformer architectures with self-attention have been introduced into medical image segmentation to enhance global context modeling9. The Vision Transformer (ViT) partitions images into patch sequences and performs self-attention across all patches, effectively capturing global dependencies but suffering from quadratic complexity with respect to sequence length, which limits its use in high-resolution medical imaging10. The Swin Transformer alleviates this issue via window-based and shifted window self-attention, reducing computational cost while improving multi-scale feature extraction, and Swin-UNet further integrates Swin blocks into a U-Net-like framework for multi-organ and cardiac segmentation11,12. However, these models remain suboptimal in precise local feature modeling and still incur nontrivial computational overhead. TransUNet combines high-resolution CNN features with Transformer-based global dependency modeling to better balance localization and context, yet its complex multi-module design leads to high computational cost and constrains deployment on resource-limited devices13. Overall, although Transformer-based methods significantly improve global information modeling, they still face challenges in efficient multi-scale fusion and fine-grained local representation under realistic computational constraints.

In parallel, recent work has introduced the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem into deep learning models14. By approximating high-dimensional mappings via compositions of low-dimensional operators, Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks (KANs) offer strong nonlinear expressiveness, interpretability, and parameter efficiency. UKAN, which embeds KAN modules into a U-Net encoder–decoder with skip connections, demonstrates effective feature reconstruction, stable convergence, and favorable performance in small-sample settings and is therefore adopted as the backbone in this study15. Nonetheless, two core limitations remain: (i) long-range dependencies in high-resolution images are still insufficiently modeled, limiting the capture of global semantics and distant structural relationships; and (ii) multi-scale representation and cross-scale fusion are relatively weak, as most KAN variants emphasize local enhancement, making them less robust for stable detection of small targets and fine-grained boundaries.

To address these limitations, this paper presents an efficient and lightweight medical image segmentation approach that performs joint adaptive modeling across multiple dimensions of the kernel space (kernel number, spatial scale, input/output channels), fundamentally alleviating the receptive-field insufficiency of fixed convolutions and the inadequate characterization of global relations. Meanwhile, a mask mechanism is introduced into standard attention computation, using adaptive binary masks to suppress background noise and highlight critical boundaries and fine structures, thereby enhancing cross-scale information fusion and boundary depiction without significantly increasing computational overhead. The overall design maintains model lightweightness while delivering effective performance gains, providing a more feasible segmentation solution for clinical applications in resource-constrained settings.

Related work

With the rapid development of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become mainstream methods for medical image segmentation, with U-Net and its variants as representative architectures5–8. Building on this foundation, researchers have further extended segmentation models to more complex clinical scenarios. Methods such as PPAF-net16 and VIS-Net17 employ parallel branches, attention-based feature fusion, and multimodal feature modeling to address tasks like radiotherapy target delineation and robot-assisted surgical instrument segmentation. Other networks adopt multi-branch, multi-scale, or lightweight designs to enhance the segmentation of anatomically complex organs and lesions under limited computational budgets. Overall, although these U-Net–based CNNs, equipped with attention mechanisms and multi-branch/multi-fusion strategies, have achieved strong performance across various clinical tasks, they remain fundamentally constrained by static convolutions with fixed receptive fields, making it difficult to fully capture long-range dependencies and complex cross-scale interactions.

To overcome the above limitations, researchers have introduced dynamic convolution as an adaptive mechanism to capture feature variations more flexibly. Li et al.18 proposed MAGIC, which models the correlations among spatial, channel, and global dimensions via a dimension reciprocal fusion module, thereby adaptively adjusting convolution kernels and enhancing multi-scale representation capability, while a kernel recalculation module further improves feature aggregation efficiency, leading to strong performance in segmentation tasks. Wang et al.19 proposed LightAWNet, a lightweight adaptive weighting network that employs an inverted residual structure with spatial attention and a dual-branch design in the encoder to extract and fuse detailed and spatial features, and replaces transposed convolutions with lightweight upsampling while introducing channel attention in the decoder, thereby significantly improving segmentation accuracy while maintaining high inference efficiency. Overall, such dynamic convolution methods effectively increase the flexibility of feature capture and reduce computational cost.

To address the challenge of capturing long-range dependencies, Transformer architectures leveraging self-attention mechanisms for global feature modeling have been introduced in medical image segmentation. Representative methods like TransUNet and Swin-UNet have achieved superior performance across various datasets12,13. In multimodal brain tumor segmentation, NestedFormer employs a modality-aware Transformer together with a modality-sensitive gating (MSG) mechanism to model intra- and inter-modality relationships among multimodal MRI sequences and to optimize cross-scale feature fusion20. Zhao et al.21 proposed an uncertainty-aware multi-dimensional mutual learning framework, in which 2D, 2.5D, and 3D CNNs are jointly trained and an uncertainty-gated mechanism selects reliable soft labels for mutual supervision, thereby improving robustness and cross-dataset generalization. Built upon DINO-v3, SegDINO provides a unified and efficient segmentation framework applicable to both medical and natural images, offering a new direction for general-purpose cross-domain segmentation22,23. However, these Transformer-based and multi-dimensional mutual learning approaches typically adopt complex architectures with high inference costs and rely heavily on large-scale annotated datasets and substantial computational resources, making deployment in resource-constrained clinical environments challenging.

To balance modeling capacity and efficiency, Gu et al.24 proposed the Mamba architecture based on the Selective State Space Model (SSM), which achieves attention-like computation with approximately linear complexity, significantly reducing computational cost while strengthening long-range dependency modeling. U-Mamba combines the local modeling ability of CNNs with the long-sequence modeling strength of SSM and shows strong performance in medical image segmentation25; SegMamba extends this paradigm to 3D volumetric data26, and Serp-Mamba targets high-resolution retinal vessel segmentation, using SSM to maintain fine-grained vascular representation while preserving inference efficiency27. Overall, these SSM/Mamba-based methods provide a promising technical route toward a more favorable trade-off between long-range modeling and computational efficiency.

In parallel, researchers have explored multilayer perceptron (MLP) and Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN) architectures to further enhance long-range dependency modeling and global feature capture. U-MLP28 and Rolling-Unet29 aggregate features along multiple directions, offering lightweight design and efficient parallel computation, but their nonlinear representation capacity remains limited. KAN, proposed by Liu et al.14, overcomes the linear constraints of conventional MLPs by introducing learnable nonlinear operators based on the Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem, thereby strengthening complex feature modeling. KAN-based variants such as UKAN, TransUKAN, KM-UNet, and ResU-KAN demonstrate strong feature representation and segmentation accuracy15,30–32. However, they still face practical challenges, including suboptimal long-range dependency modeling for high-resolution medical images, limited multi-scale information representation, and increased computational complexity from intricate module designs, which hinders deployment on resource-constrained devices.

In summary, existing methods are mainly limited in two respects: static convolutions struggle to effectively model long-range dependencies and multi-scale features, while global self-attention at high resolutions incurs prohibitive computational costs, making it difficult to balance accuracy and efficiency. To address this, we propose ODMA-UKAN as an enhanced framework that explicitly considers both performance and efficiency. In the encoder, we introduce an omni-dimensional dynamic convolution mechanism that jointly and adaptively adjusts kernel number, spatial scale, and input/output channels, thereby expanding the effective receptive field and strengthening cross-scale feature representation. Meanwhile, a data-driven binary mask is integrated into the attention computation to achieve sparse focusing and background suppression, highlighting fine structures and enhancing boundary delineation and multi-scale information aggregation. Under an overall lightweight network design, this synergistic combination of omni-dimensional dynamic convolution and mask-based attention effectively alleviates the limitations of conventional CNN/Transformer pipelines in global dependency modeling and fine-grained feature fusion, and constitutes the core innovation of this work.

Methods

To overcome these shortcomings, we propose the improved model ODMA-UKAN, which introduces structural enhancements for global modeling and fine-grained feature characterization while maintaining parameter efficiency and small-sample friendliness. Dynamic convolution

Omni-dimensional dynamic convolution

A static convolutional layer employs a single, fixed convolutional kernel for all input samples. However, in medical images, lesions and tissues often exhibit diverse grayscale intensities, textures, and morphological characteristics, making it difficult for a single kernel to comprehensively capture these subtle variations. To address this limitation, dynamic convolutional layers learn an n-way linear combination of multiple kernels and use attention mechanisms to weight them, enabling the convolution operation to adaptively adjust across spatial, channel, and other dimensions based on the input features. In other words, the weights of dynamic convolution kernels are conditioned on the specific texture and structural information of the current image, thereby significantly enhancing the model’s ability to represent medical image features. Mask attention

In medical image segmentation tasks, lesion regions typically occupy only a small portion of the input image. Conventional multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanisms, which distribute attention weights uniformly across the entire feature map, often result in valuable attention resources being spread over a large amount of irrelevant background. This not only increases computational overhead but also diminishes the model’s ability to focus on critical regions. To effectively address this issue, we introduce a learnable mask matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:M$$\end{document} into the standard scaled dot-product attention. This mask matrix dynamically modulates the attention allocation between each pair of spatial positions in the input feature map\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X$$\end{document}(reshaped as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X{\prime\:}\in\:{R}^{B\times\:H\times\:W\times\:C}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:B$$\end{document} is the batch size, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\times\:W$$\end{document} denotes the spatial dimensions, and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\:C$$\end{document} is the number of channels). By element-wise modulating the attention weights, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document}explicitly enhances the model’s focus on potential lesion and other key regions, while effectively suppressing interference from anatomical boundaries, vascular artifacts, and other background noise.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}MaskAttn\left(Q,K,V,M\right)=Softmax\left(\frac{Q{K}^{T}}{\sqrt{{d}_{k}}}+M\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q=\:X{\prime\:}{W}_{Q}$$\end{document},\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:K=X{\prime\:}{W}_{K}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:V=X{\prime\:}{W}_{V}\:$$\end{document}represent the query, key, and value matrices, respectively, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{Q}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{K}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{K}$$\end{document} are learnable linear projection parameters for the query, key, and value. These three projection matrices map the input features\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{X}^{{\prime\:}}$$\end{document}into different subspaces, facilitating the computation of spatial correlations among all positions in the attention module. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N=H\times\:W$$\end{document}denotes the total number of spatial positions, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{k}\:$$\end{document}is the dimension of the query and key vectors, used for scaling to prevent gradient explosion and to stabilize training. The introduced mask matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M\in\:{R}^{N\times\:N}$$\end{document}can be either learned through backpropagation or dynamically generated based on features. By assigning large negative values to the attention scores associated with background regions, the network’s expressive capacity is concentrated on potential lesion areas. Compared with conventional self-attention without masking, Mask Attention not only explicitly filters out structured noise and improves the detection rate of small lesions, but also reduces attention dispersion, enabling the network to more rapidly focus on key regions. Moreover, sparse masks, whether generated offline or dynamically, allow for the pruning of most background attention computations.

After obtaining the attention-weighted output \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:A$$\end{document}, we further enhance nonlinear modeling through a two-layer feed-forward network (FFN) with GELU activation, while preserving the original information via a residual connection:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{A}_{\mathrm{o}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{t}}=GELU\left(A{W}_{1}+{b}_{1}\right){W}_{2}+{b}_{2}+A\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{1}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{2}$$\end{document}, are the weight matrices, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{1}$$\end{document} and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:b}_{2}$$\end{document} are the biases of the feed-forward network (FFN), which further refine and enrich the representation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:A$$\end{document}. This produces the final output of the mask attention module, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{A}_{out}$$\end{document} which not only integrates both global context and salient regional features, but also preserves the original information through residual connections. This facilitates more precise target prediction in subsequent segmentation layers.

It is important to note that, while both mask attention and omni-dimensional dynamic convolution introduce attention mechanisms into the model, these two types of attention are not in conflict. The spatial attention in omni-dimensional dynamic convolution is applied within the convolutional kernel space, where the attention computation scope is determined by the kernel size. In contrast, mask attention operates over the entire feature map. As a result, these two complementary attention mechanisms effectively model both local and global features, thereby further enhancing the model’s ability to capture more informative and valuable representations.

In this work, the mask matrix is learned by the network rather than defined by hand-crafted rules or heuristic design. Specifically, the current-layer feature map\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\:F\in\:R}^{B\times\:C\times\:H\times\:W}$$\end{document}is fed into a lightweight mask-generation subnetwork composed of several \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:1$$\end{document} convolutional layers followed by nonlinear activations (e.g., ReLU or SiLU), producing mask logits of shape \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\times\:1\times\:H\times\:W$$\end{document}. These logits are passed through a sigmoid function to obtain a soft mask, which is then binarized using a fixed threshold (e.g., 0.5) to yield the mask used in attention computation. The binary mask is reshaped or downsampled according to the window size or sampling strategy so that its spatial resolution matches that of the attention weights, and positions corresponding to masked regions are suppressed by adding a large negative bias or directly setting their attention responses to zero. In this way, background activations are effectively attenuated while foreground and boundary structures are explicitly emphasized. The parameters of the mask branch are initialized together with the backbone (convolutional weights with He/Kaiming initialization and biases set to zero) and optimized end-to-end via standard backpropagation, so the mask matrix is fully learned in a data-driven and adaptive manner.

Tok-KAN block

To balance powerful nonlinear fitting capability with structural interpretability, ODMA-UKAN incorporates the Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN) module—based on the Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem into the core pathway of both the encoder and decoder. This module has been shown to provide efficient and interpretable feature learning in medical imaging contexts. KAN utilizes learnable spline functions to enhance the representation of nonlinear features, thereby enabling more accurate approximation of nonlinear functions as a replacement for traditional multilayer perceptrons (MLPs). In conventional MLPs, nonlinear functions are approximated by stacking layers of linear mappings and activation functions:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}MLP\left(X\right)=\left({W}_{n-1}\sigma\:\circ\:{W}_{n-2}\sigma\:\circ\:\cdots\:\circ\:{W}_{1}\sigma\:\circ\:{W}_{0}\right)\left(X\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:X\in\:{R}^{H\times\:W\times\:C}$$\end{document} denotes the input feature vector, which carries the original spatial structure and contextual information and serves as the foundation for subsequent nonlinear transformations. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{W}_{i}\in\:{R}^{{C}_{i+1}\times\:{C}_{i}}$$\end{document} r represents the linear transformation matrix, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} is the nonlinear activation function. In CNNs, feature extraction is performed through multiple layers of convolutional operations, and both CNNs and MLPs employ stacked nonlinear activation functions to approximate complex nonlinear features. However, while these black-box operations are effective, they do not explicitly reveal the specific functionality of each internal layer, thereby reducing the interpretability of the model. Analogous to MLPs, the KAN formulation at the n layer is given by:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}KAN\left(X\right)=\left({{\Phi\:}}_{n-1}\circ\:{{\Phi\:}}_{n-2}\circ\:\cdots\:\circ\:{{\Phi\:}}_{1}\circ\:{{\Phi\:}}_{0}\right)\left(X\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{{\Phi\:}}_{i}\in\:{R}^{{C}_{n+1}\times\:{C}_{n}}$$\end{document}is composed of several B-spline functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varphi\:}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}}$$\end{document}∈R, where:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}{{\Phi\:}}_{i}=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{\phi\:}_{i,\mathrm{1,1}}&\:{\phi\:}_{i,\mathrm{1,2}}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\phi\:}_{i,1,{n}_{i}}\\\:{\phi\:}_{i,\mathrm{2,1}}&\:{\phi\:}_{i,\mathrm{2,2}}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\phi\:}_{i,2,{n}_{i}}\\\:\vdots&\:\vdots&\:\ddots\:&\:\vdots\\\:{\phi\:}_{i,{C}_{i+1},1}&\:{\phi\:}_{i,{C}_{i+1},2}&\:\cdots\:&\:{\phi\:}_{i,{C}_{i+1},{n}_{i}}\end{array}\right],{\phi\:}_{i,a,b}\left(x\right)\\\:=\sum\:_{j=1}^{{n}_{j}}\:{d}_{j}{B}_{j}\left(x\right).\end{array} \end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\varphi\:}_{\mathrm{i},\mathrm{a},\mathrm{b}}\in\:\mathrm{R}$$\end{document} denotes the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:j$$\end{document} order B-spline basis function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{j}$$\end{document} are the learnable coefficients. By appropriately choosing the sampling points such as using 5 sampling points, spline order of 3, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}_{j}=8\:$$\end{document}, KAN can achieve a favorable balance between flexibility and computational efficiency. Owing to the local support property of B-spline basis functions, KAN is able to capture key features with greater precision when modeling blurred boundaries, noise artifacts, and multi-scale lesion contours in ultrasound images. Moreover, piecewise spline mapping offers more targeted representational power than an MLP with the same number of parameters.

In the implementation of the Tok-KAN block, the feature sequence \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:X$$\end{document}, flattened to a length of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N=H\times\:W$$\end{document}, is first normalized via LayerNorm, then passed through the aforementioned KAN layer, and finally, the original information is preserved via a residual connection:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{X}_{\mathrm{o}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{t}}=X+KAN\left(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{y}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{N}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{m}\left(X\right)\right) \end{array}$$\end{document}

The core advantage of KAN lies in the excellent local support and piecewise linear interpretability provided by B-spline basis functions. Each neuron’s output depends only on a small segment of its input, enabling fine-grained modeling of common features in ultrasound images, such as blurred boundaries, noise artifacts, and multi-scale structures. Compared to traditional MLPs with the same number of parameters, KAN offers more targeted fitting ability and greater capacity for feature decomposition with clearer physical interpretability. Furthermore, the Tok-KAN block, through its combination of normalization, spline-based mapping, and residual fusion, organically unifies global dependency modeling with the preservation of spatial details, thereby significantly improving both the accuracy and robustness of medical image segmentation tasks.

Experiments

Datasets

Datasets To comprehensively validate the feasibility and generalizability of the proposed model, we evaluated it on three medical image segmentation datasets with different modalities, structural characteristics, and clinical contexts: the Breast Ultrasound Image Dataset (BUSI)35, the Gland Segmentation Dataset (GlaS)36, and the Colon Polyp Segmentation Dataset (CVC-ClinicDB, hereafter CVC)37. BUSI contains 647 breast ultrasound images from subjects aged 25–75 years, covering three lesion categories (normal tissue, benign masses, and malignant tumors). All images are resized to 256 × 256 pixels, making this dataset suitable for assessing performance on complex lesion boundaries in ultrasound. GlaS comprises 165 H&E-stained histopathological images of colorectal adenocarcinoma, mainly at pathological stages T3 or T4 and collected from multiple laboratories. Images are standardized to 512 × 512 pixels to evaluate the model’s ability to handle structural variability and blurred gland boundaries. CVC-ClinicDB includes 612 images extracted from 29 colonoscopy video sequences and is widely used as a benchmark for colon polyp detection and segmentation. Owing to large variations in polyp morphology, location, and size, CVC is well-suited for testing robustness and generalization in complex gastrointestinal scenes, and all images are uniformly resized to 256 × 256 pixels.

Experimental details

Experimental setup

All experiments were performed in a unified hardware environment using PyTorch on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 GPU (16 GB). We trained the model for 400 epochs with the Adam optimizer, an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1\times\:{10}^{-4}$$\end{document}scheduled by cosine annealing, and a batch size of 8. The loss function is a weighted sum of a region-overlap loss and pixel-wise cross-entropy, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:=0.5$$\end{document}. To improve generalization and reduce overfitting, we applied random horizontal flipping, scaling, and mild translation/cropping, and all input images were intensity-normalized and resized to a fixed resolution. Each dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% validation, and all comparison methods used the same data partition and training settings. Every experiment was run multiple times, and we report results as “mean ± standard deviation” to ensure stability and reliability.

Loss function

To optimize segmentation performance, we adopt a hybrid loss that combines Binary Cross Entropy with Logits (BCE) and Dice loss, jointly accounting for pixel-wise classification and region overlap between prediction and ground truth. The BCE term, suitable for binary or single-channel segmentation, is defined as follows:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{L}}_{\mathrm{B}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{E}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum\:_{i=1}^{N}\:\mathrm{log}\left(\sigma\:\left(\widehat{{y}_{i}}{)}^{{y}_{i}}\right(1-\sigma\:\left(\widehat{{y}_{i}}\right){)}^{1-{y}_{i}}\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the ground truth label of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i\:$$\end{document}pixel, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the model’s predicted output (prior to Sigmoid activation), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:\left(\cdot \right)$$\end{document} is the Sigmoid function. Given the prevalent class imbalance between foreground and background in medical images, we further incorporate the Dice loss to enhance the model’s sensitivity to small target regions. The Dice loss is defined as follows:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{Dice}=1-\frac{2\sum\:_{i}\:{\widehat{y}}_{i}{y}_{i}+\varepsilon}{\sum\:_{i}\:{\widehat{y}}_{i}^{2}+\sum\:_{i}\:{y}_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon}\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\varepsilon\:$$\end{document} is a smoothing term to prevent division by zero. To fully leverage the advantages of both loss functions, we employ the following weighted composite loss as the final optimization objective:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}{L}_{Total}=\lambda\:{L}_{BCE}+\left(1-\lambda\:\right){L}_{Dice} \end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:\in\:\left[\mathrm{0,1}\right]\:$$\end{document}is a weighting parameter used to balance the contributions of the two loss components, and is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:=0.5$$\end{document} in our experiments. This composite strategy has been widely adopted in various medical image segmentation tasks and has proven effective in enhancing both the robustness of the model under complex backgrounds and the accuracy of region segmentation.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively and objectively assess the segmentation performance of the proposed model, we employ the following mainstream metrics: the Dice coefficient and Intersection over Union (IoU). The Dice coefficient is defined as:12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}Dice=\frac{2\left|P\cap\:G\right|}{\left|P\right|+\left|G\right|}\#\left(12\right)\end{array}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:P\:$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:G$$\end{document} denote the sets of pixels in the predicted and ground truth segmentation regions, respectively. The IoU is defined as:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\begin{array}{c}IoU=\frac{\left|P\cap\:G\right|}{\left|P\cup\:G\right|} \end{array}$$\end{document}

This metric reflects the overlap ratio between the predicted and ground truth regions. In addition, to more accurately assess boundary quality, we adopt two boundary-related metrics: Hausdorff Distance (HD) and Average Surface Distance (ASD). HD and ASD measure the maximum and mean deviations between predicted and ground-truth contours, and are widely used for medical image segmentation involving irregular or fine structures. In this study, HD95 and ASD are computed in pixel units, where smaller values indicate more precise boundary localization. To evaluate computational cost, we also report floating-point operations (FLOPs) and the number of parameters (Params), which denote the total multiply–add operations for a single forward pass and the total trainable weights, respectively. Combined with the overlap- and boundary-based metrics, these complexity measures provide a comprehensive assessment of both segmentation accuracy and efficiency, offering a quantitative basis for judging the model’s feasibility and scalability in practical deployment.

Comparative experiments

In this section, we compare the performance of the proposed ODMA-UKAN model against several mainstream approaches across multiple benchmark medical image segmentation datasets. The results are presented in Tables 1, 2 and 3. The comparison includes classical CNN-based architectures such as U-Net4 and its variants, attention-based models (e.g., Att-U-Net7, efficient Transformer frameworks (e.g., U-Mamba25, Serp-Mamba27, and SegDINO22, multilayer perceptron (MLP)-based segmentation networks like U-NeXt38 and Rolling-U-Net29, and Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN)-based models such as U-KAN15. Since KAN architectures are regarded as effective alternatives to MLP-based models, direct comparison with MLP-driven networks is particularly important. Moreover, U-KAN explicitly replaces the standard MLP blocks with KAN structures, making it a crucial baseline for evaluating our approach. To ensure fair comparison, all models are trained and evaluated under the same hardware environment and experimental settings.

Experimental results on the BUSI, GlaS, and CVC datasets show that ODMA-UKAN consistently outperforms the baseline UKAN and most comparison methods in both segmentation accuracy and boundary quality, with especially notable gains over the strong baseline SegDINO. On BUSI, ODMA-UKAN improves IoU by about 3.39% and 2.54% over UKAN and SegDINO, respectively, and raises F1 by 3.37% and 5.90%, while reducing HD95 by 4.16 and 8.74 and ASD by 0.37 and 3.19. Compared with U-Net, its attention variants, Serp-Mamba, and SegDINO, it better captures multi-scale lesion morphology and maintains high contour consistency even for blurred boundaries. On GlaS, ODMA-UKAN improves IoU over UKAN and SegDINO by roughly 1.58% and 1.27%, and F1 by 0.89% and 1.19%, with HD95 substantially lower than UKAN (≈ 8.73) and slightly higher than SegDINO. It more accurately separates thin gland branches and densely clustered regions, benefiting from omni-dimensional dynamic convolution with joint spatial–channel adaptive weighting. On CVC, ODMA-UKAN increases IoU by about 3.50% vs. UKAN and 2.33% vs. SegDINO, and improves F1 by 2.02% and 1.62%; HD95 is reduced by 3.95 relative to UKAN but slightly higher than SegDINO, while ASD is 1.04 lower than UKAN and about 0.84 higher than SegDINO. Given the large variation in polyp shape, size, and illumination, conventional CNN and pure MLP models struggle to balance receptive field coverage and detail preservation, whereas ODMA-UKAN expands the effective receptive field via omni-dimensional dynamic convolution and leverages mask attention to enhance key polyp regions, yielding higher overall accuracy and better boundary quality, particularly for low-contrast, small polyps.

Ablation experiments

To thoroughly evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of the ODMA-UKAN model across diverse scenarios, we conducted multiple ablation experiments using three heterogeneous medical image segmentation datasets. These experiments involved several distinct configurations: employing only the omni-dimensional dynamic convolution (ODConv) module, solely integrating the mask attention mechanism, and combining both modules. The objective was to examine both individual and synergistic contributions of these modules to the overall performance enhancement. Initially, dynamic convolution was introduced into medical image segmentation to overcome the limitations of static convolutions. The core concept involves replacing single fixed convolutional kernels with adaptive linear combinations of multiple kernels, dynamically weighted based on input feature conditions, thus enhancing representational capability across different image regions. Representative works include CondConv proposed by Yang et al.39 and DyConv by Chen et al.40, both significantly increasing feature modeling flexibility while maintaining computational efficiency. However, these methods predominantly focus on dynamically adjusting the number of convolution kernels, neglecting dynamic adaptations across multiple dimensions such as spatial size, input channels, and output channels, resulting in suboptimal performance when handling complex lesions. Although Li et al.41 introduced DCD dynamic convolution to mitigate this limitation via dynamic channel fusion, their approach still lacks comprehensive multi-dimensional adaptability. Our ablation experiments systematically compared various dynamic convolution modules within the same network architecture, as presented in Table 5. Although DyConv demonstrated slightly better segmentation accuracy compared to our proposed ODConv and DCD dynamic convolution, it significantly increased parameter complexity, rendering it impractical for deployment in resource-constrained scenarios. Considering both performance and complexity, we selected ODConv as our dynamic convolution module, achieving superior segmentation accuracy with minimized parameter overhead.

Next, we evaluated the individual effectiveness of ODConv by systematically replacing various encoding layers within the baseline U-KAN architecture with ODConv. The experimental outcomes across the three datasets are reported in Table 6. Results revealed that employing ODConv in four layers achieved optimal performance on the BUSI and GlaS datasets, whereas on the CVC dataset, ODConv performed best when integrated into five layers. This variance likely originates from dataset-specific characteristics: polyp boundaries in the CVC dataset are relatively clear and smooth, facilitating easier target delineation; conversely, BUSI and GlaS datasets contain more complex structures and ambiguous boundaries, necessitating more refined feature representations.

Discussion

Although experiments on the public BUSI, GlaS, and CVC datasets show that ODMA-UKAN has clear advantages in both segmentation performance and efficiency, the current evaluation is still confined to single-domain or mildly shifted scenarios. In real clinical practice, multi-center, multi-device, and multi-protocol acquisitions introduce substantial domain shifts in imaging parameters, populations, and annotation styles, often causing performance degradation in cross-center deployment. Enhancing the cross-domain generalization of ODMA-UKAN, especially when source-domain data are unavailable, is therefore an important direction for future work.

In this context, Source-Free Active Domain Adaptation (SFADA) is a promising solution: it assumes only a trained source model and relies on a small number of labeled and many unlabeled target samples, combined with active sampling and adaptive learning, to mitigate domain shift. SFADA has shown effectiveness in multi-center tumor and vessel segmentation and multi-rater medical image segmentation, and recent VLM-based variants further improve the balance between fairness and accuracy44–47. Building on the strong single-domain backbone of ODMA-UKAN—enabled by omni-dimensional dynamic convolution and mask attention—we plan to integrate SFADA with uncertainty- and structure-aware active sampling, incorporate self-supervised learning on unlabeled multi-center images, and introduce boundary-aware consistency constraints to support robust deployment of ODMA-UKAN in realistic multi-center clinical environments.

Conclusion

We propose ODMA-UKAN, a multi-dimensional, lightweight, attention-enhanced model for medical image segmentation. The encoder integrates omni-dimensional dynamic convolution with a mask-based attention mechanism, effectively improving multi-scale feature representation and long-range dependency modeling. Experimental results on three public medical image segmentation benchmark datasets show that ODMA-UKAN achieves higher IoU and F1 scores than current state-of-the-art methods, validating its advantages in both accuracy and inference efficiency.

It should be noted that the generalization ability of ODMA-UKAN on highly heterogeneous data, as well as its capability to delineate very small targets and blurred boundaries, still has room for improvement. In particular, the fusion between local details and global context remains insufficient in cases involving complex lesion structures. Future work will therefore focus on enhancing boundary sensitivity and improving the quality of multi-scale feature fusion, with the goal of further strengthening the applicability and robustness of the model in more challenging clinical scenarios.
