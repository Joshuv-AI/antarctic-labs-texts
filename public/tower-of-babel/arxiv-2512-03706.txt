Consistent Projection of Langevin Dynamics: Preserving Thermodynamics and Kinetics in Coarse-Grained Models Preprint: APS/123-QED
Vahid Nateghi, Lara Neureither, Selma Moqvist, Carsten Hartmann, Simon Olsson, Feliks Nüske

Abstract
Coarse graining (CG) is an important task for efficient modeling and simulation of complex multi-scale systems, such as the conformational dynamics of biomolecules. This work presents a projection-based coarse-graining formalism for general underdamped Langevin dynamics. Following the Zwanzig projection approach, we derive a closed-form expression for the coarse grained dynamics. In addition, we show how the generator Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (gEDMD) method, which was developed in the context of Koopman operator methods, can be used to model the CG dynamics and evaluate its kinetic properties, such as transition timescales. Finally, we combine our approach with thermodynamic interpolation (TI), a generative approach to transform samples between thermodynamic conditions, to extend the scope of the approach across thermodynamic states without repeated numerical simulations. Using a two-dimensional model system, we demonstrate that the proposed method allows to accurately capture the thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the full-space model.



I
Introduction

The task of finding a simpler representation for a complex dynamical system, while preserving essential features of the full system, is referred to as
coarse graining (CG)
or model reduction
7
;
5
;
3
. CG models are central across many scientific disciplines because they enable interpretability and simulation of large-scale systems at greatly reduced computational cost. In molecular dynamics, CG modeling has long been used to study biomolecular systems whose size and timescales are inaccessible to all-atom simulations
16
. Since simulations of biomolecules often exhibit rare events and meta stable states, constructing high-quality CG models that can reduce the computational burden is of particular importance.

The quality of a CG model depends both on the function that is used to map a full-state configuration to its reduced representation (CG map), as well as the dynamical model defined on the reduced space (CG dynamics). While numerous methods exist for defining effective CG maps
36
;
39
;
12
, the accurate construction of CG dynamics remains more challenging. Different metrics have been suggested to assess the quality of a CG model. For equilibrium systems, reproducing the marginal steady-state distribution is a common criterion of quality as formalized by the multi-scale CG method
30
and its machine-learning-based variants
41
;
4
. However, ensuring that the CG dynamics also reproduces the kinetic properties of the full model—such as transition rates and relaxation timescales—requires a more rigorous dynamical framework. The Mori-Zwanzig (MZ) projection formalism
26
;
48
provides an analytical foundation, offering a systematic way to derive reduced equations that retain essential dynamical information. Several variants of this approach have been developed, including the approach by Gyöngy
14
, the Zwanzig projection as formalized by Legoll and Lelièvre
21
;
45
, as well as hybrid formats
2
. For the Zwanzig projection which we consider in this paper, called effective dynamics in
21
, there exist various theoretical results for the error between the original and the CG dynamics
20
;
23
;
19
;
15
, see also
11
for results specific to the Langevin equation and affine CG maps. Importantly, it has been shown that the ability of a CG model to capture rare-event dynamics depends critically on how well the CG map resolves the leading eigenfunctions of the Fokker–Planck or Koopman operator
45
;
46
;
33
.

Over the course of the last decade, machine-learning approaches have emerged as powerful tools for constructing dynamically accurate CG models. These include spectral matching
6
;
31
, generator extended dynamic mode decomposition (gEDMD)
17
, which is based on the Koopman operator framework
18
;
24
, molecular latent space simulators
40
, implicit transfer operators
10
;
9
;
37
, and reaction coordinate flows
44
. Recently, the gEDMD method was used to directly approximate the coarse-grained dynamics implied by the Zwanzig projection for reversible systems. In the same study, a learning scheme for the parameters of the CG dynamics enabled direct validation of the learned model through rare-event transition timescales
28
. Here, we extend this approach to non-reversible underdamped Langevin dynamics—commonly used to model molecular systems in thermal equilibrium—and integrate it with recent advances in generative modeling, yielding a data-efficient framework for learning coarse-grained Langevin dynamics.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We derive, within the Zwanzig projection formalism, explicit analytical expressions for the coarse-grained (CG) dynamics corresponding to the underdamped Langevin equation. The resulting dynamics retain the structure of the full-space system but feature generalized forces and state-dependent diffusion. We further analyze the properties of the associated CG generator.

•

We show that thermodynamic interpolation (TI)
25
, a recently proposed generative modeling approach based on Boltzmann generators
29
;
8
;
13
and the stochastic interpolant framework
1
, can efficiently generate accurate position-space samples for numerically approximating the CG Langevin equation.

•

We employ the gEDMD method to learn models for the CG Langevin dynamics, and to analyze its dynamical properties, such as rare event transition timescales. Learning algorithms for the CG parameters are provided, and both the gEDMD models and parameters are trained on TI-generated data, removing the need for prohibitively long full-space simulations.

The structure of the paper is as follows: we introduce the underdamped Langevin dynamics and the key properties of its generator in Section
II
, along with the definition of the phase space CG map which is used throughout this work. The analysis of the CG Langevin dynamics and its parameters is given in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, we introduce TI as a sampling method, along with the gEDMD algorithm and the learning methods for the CG parameters. Using a two-dimensional model system, we demonstrate the capabilities of the proposed methods numerically, in Section
V
. Detailed proofs for our analytical results, as well as additional simulation and learning details are provided in the Appendix.

II
Langevin Equation and Coarse Graining Theory

II.1
Langevin Dynamics

Underdamped Langevin dynamics are a stochastic version of the Hamiltonian dynamics on full phase space coordinates
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. The dynamical equations are

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}_{t}&=\mathbf{p}_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t\\
\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}_{t}&=-\left[\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q}_{t})+\gamma\mathbf{p}_{t}\right]\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma\beta^{-1}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.\end{split}

(1)

Here,
\gamma,\beta>0
are constants (friction and inverse temperature), while
\mathbf{B}_{t}
is
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. We will frequently write (
1
) in the more compact form

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\,,\ \mathbf{W}_{t}=(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{t},\mathbf{B}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is
2d
-dimensional Brownian motion and the drift and diffusion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\
-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})-\gamma\mathbf{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\,,\quad\mathbf{A}:=\sigma\sigma^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{d\times d}&0_{d\times d}\\
0_{d\times d}&\gamma I_{d}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}.

Note that we can also write the drift field
\mathbf{b}
in the Hamiltonian form

\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{J}-\mathbf{A})\nabla H(\mathbf{x}),

where the canonical Hamiltonian and skew-symmetric matrix
\mathbf{J}
are given by:

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{x})=V(\mathbf{q})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{d\times d}&I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&0_{d\times d}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}.

The invariant distribution of the process (
1
) is the canonical distribution with density

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})&\propto\exp\left(-\beta H(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\\
&=\exp\left(-\beta(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}\|^{2}+V(\mathbf{q}))\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}\\
&=\mu_{\mathbf{p}}(\mathbf{p})\mu_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{q}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}.\end{split}

(3)

II.2
The Generator

An important tool for large parts of this paper is the evolution of observables under the Langevin dynamics. Observables are functions
\phi
of the phase space, which we require to be contained in the weighted space
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
:

L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})=\{\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}:\,|\,\int\phi^{2}(\mathbf{x})e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty\}.

(4)

The
generator
is a differential operator acting on observables
\phi\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}\phi&=\mathbf{b}\cdot\nabla\phi+\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}:\nabla^{2}\phi\\
&=\mathbf{J}\nabla H\cdot\nabla\phi-\mathbf{A}\nabla H\cdot\nabla\phi+\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}:\nabla^{2}\phi,\end{split}

(5)

where the notation
\mathbf{A}:\nabla^{2}\phi
denotes the Frobenius inner product between
\mathbf{A}
and the Hessian matrix of
\phi
:

\mathbf{A}:\nabla^{2}\phi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2d}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{x}_{i}\partial\mathbf{x}_{j}}\phi.

The first term in (
5
) is the Hamiltonian part of the generator

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}=\mathbf{J}\nabla H\cdot\nabla=\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}},

while the next two terms form the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck part of the generator

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OU}}=-\mathbf{A}\nabla H\cdot\nabla+\beta^{-1}\mathbf{A}:\nabla^{2}=-\gamma\mathbf{p}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\Delta_{\mathbf{p}}.

An equivalent way of decomposing the generator is into its anti-symmetric and symmetric parts:

\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\cdot\nabla+\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta H}\nabla\cdot\left(e^{-\beta H}\mathbf{A}\nabla\right),

where
\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is the stationary probability flow

\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\mathbf{b}-\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta H}\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{A}e^{-\beta H})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\
-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

It can be verified directly that
\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}=\mathbf{J}\nabla H
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{H}}=\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\cdot\nabla
coincides with the anti-symmetric part. Similarly, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck generator
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OU}}
coincides with the symmetric part. As a consequence,

\left\langle\mathcal{L}f,\,g\right\rangle_{\mu}=\left\langle\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\cdot\nabla f,\,g\right\rangle_{\mu}-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\langle\mathbf{A}\nabla f,\,\nabla g\right\rangle_{\mu},

(7)

where the rightmost term follows from integration by parts:

\left\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OU}}\phi,\,\psi\right\rangle_{\mu}=-\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{p}}\psi\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\langle\mathbf{A}\nabla f,\,\nabla g\right\rangle_{\mu}=\left\langle\phi,\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OU}}\psi\right\rangle_{\mu}.

Therefore, in order to approximate the generator, we can use numerical approximations that only require first order derivatives.

For systems exhibiting meta stable states and undergoing rare transitions, we are generally interested in eigenvalues of the generator close to zero. Thus, we expect to find a number of real eigenvalues
0=\kappa_{0}<\kappa_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\kappa_{M}
and eigenfunctions
\psi_{i},i=0,\ldots,M
, such that

-\mathcal{L}\psi_{i}=\kappa_{i}\psi_{i},

(8)

and
\kappa_{i}\ll 1
. These eigenvalues can be interpreted as rates of transition, their associated implied timescales are defined as
38
;
34

t_{i}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{i}}.

II.3
CG Map and Potential of Mean Force

Next, we consider coarse graining of the Langevin dynamics in Equation (
1
). We assume the CG map is defined based on a smooth mapping

\xi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}\,,\mathbf{q}\mapsto\xi(\mathbf{q}),\,k\leq d,

called the
spatial CG map
, which transforms the spatial degrees of freedom by mapping them to a lower-dimensional space. In order to arrive at a coarse grained dynamics which is again of Langevin type, we need to define CG momenta. Since formally
\frac{d}{dt}\xi(\mathbf{q})=\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}
, where
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}
is the Jacobian of the spatial CG map, it has been suggested
11
;
22
to define the coarse grained momenta analogously:

\mathbf{v}_{i}=\dot{\xi}_{i}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\xi_{i}(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p},

(9)

leading to an augmented
2k
-dimensional coarse graining map called the
phase-space CG map

\Xi(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{z}\\
\mathbf{v}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi(\mathbf{q})\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}.

(10)

From now on, we drop the variable
\mathbf{q}
in
\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\xi
as the function
\xi
is only a function of the position.
We use the following notation for its Jacobian determinant, assuming it has maximum rank almost everywhere (i.e. non-zero determinant for almost all
\mathbf{x}
):

\mathrm{Jac}(\mathbf{x})=\det\left[\nabla\Xi(\mathbf{x})\nabla\Xi^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\right].

To proceed further, we need to introduce some abstract concepts. It is often helpful to break up integrals over full phase space into an integral over all points that are assigned to the same point in CG space, and then integrate out over the CG variables. Formally, we write:

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}g(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}g(\mathbf{x})\delta(\mathbf{y}-\Xi(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

To make this notation more transparent, we consider the non-linear pre-image of a point
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{2k}
under the CG map as

\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\Xi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y}\right\}.

That is,
\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})
is the set of phase space elements which collapse to the same point
\mathbf{y}
in CG space. Writing
\sigma_{\mathbf{y}}
for the Hausdorff measure on the nonlinear manifold
\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})
, we introduce the weighted surface measure:

\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathrm{Jac}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}).

By means of the measure
\rho_{\mathbf{y}}
, we now re-write the previous integration formula by splitting up integrals along pre-images of the CG map, known as the
co-area formula
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}g(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\int_{\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}g(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{Jac}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.

(11)

We can now proceed to introduce the
free energy
or potential of mean force on the CG space:

F(\mathbf{y})=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left[\int_{\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})\right],

(12)

with associated probability measure
\nu(\mathbf{y})\sim e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}
. The co-area formula implies that for two functions
\phi,\psi\in L^{2}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})
, depending only on the CG variables, we have the following identity between inner products on full space and on CG space:

\displaystyle\left\langle\phi,\,\psi\right\rangle_{\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\phi(\mathbf{y})\psi(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\phi(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))\psi(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\left\langle\phi\circ\Xi,\,\psi\circ\Xi\right\rangle_{\mu}.

II.4
Projection Operators and CG Dynamics

To define dynamics on the CG space, given a phase-space CG map
\Xi
and the SDE in Equation (
2
), the starting point is usually to apply Ito’s Lemma to the observed process
\Xi(\mathbf{X}_{t})
:

\mathrm{d}\Xi(\mathbf{X}_{t})=\mathcal{L}\Xi(\mathbf{X}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\nabla\Xi(\mathbf{X}_{t})\cdot\sqrt{\mathbf{A}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t}.

This equation is not closed as it depends on the full state
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. A general procedure to close the equation is to apply a projection operator to the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}
. A linear orthogonal projection
\mathcal{P}
is a linear map from functions in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
to functions in the CG domain
L^{2}_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}^{2k})
, which is self-adjoint on
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, and satisfies
\mathcal{P}^{2}=\mathcal{P}
. A widely used choice for
\mathcal{P}
is
Zwanzig’s projection
operator
47
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}\phi(\mathbf{y})&=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi\,|\,\Xi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{y}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}}\int_{\Xi^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}\phi(\mathbf{x})e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

(13)

The Zwanzig projector represents the best approximation of
\phi
by a function that only depends on the CG variables
\mathbf{y}
. Following Ref.
45
, we choose
\mathcal{P}
according to Eq. (
13
), and define the generator in CG space by

\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}=\mathcal{P}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{P}.

(14)

It can be shown that
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
is indeed the generator of an effective SDE

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Y}_{t}=\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}

(15)

on the CG space
\mathbb{R}^{2k}

45
.

III
Analytical Structure of the Coarse Grained Langevin Dynamics

Here we report the main analytical results of the paper on the construction of a CG dynamics which preserve structural and dynamical properties of the full-space model.
Following Ref.
45
, the effective drift and diffusion of the coarse grained Langevin equation (
15
) are given by the abstract analytical expressions below:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}[\mathcal{L}\Xi](\mathbf{y}),

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}(\bar{\sigma})^{\top}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{P}[\nabla\Xi\mathbf{A}\nabla\Xi^{\top}](\mathbf{y}).

(16)

These parameters are spelled out in detail in the following result. To avoid over-cluttering the notation, we use the summation convention that repeated indices are being summed over, and the shorthand
\partial_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{q}_{i}},\ \partial_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\mathbf{q}_{i}\partial\mathbf{q}_{j}}
.

Proposition 1
.

The effective drift and effective diffusion of the coarse grained Langevin equation can be calculated explicitly as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})-\gamma\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{k\times k}&0_{k\times k}\\
0_{k\times k}&\gamma\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})\end{pmatrix},

(17)

where the effective force and diffusion fields are given by

\begin{split}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})&=\mathcal{P}\left(-\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{p}_{i}\partial_{ij}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}_{j}\right)(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v}),\\
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})&=\mathcal{P}\left(\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})^{\top}\right)(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v}).\end{split}

(18)

Proposition
1
states that the effective SDE (
15
) is again a Langevin dynamics, only with a modified force
\mathbf{f}
and a state-dependent diffusion field
\mathbf{M}
acting on the momentum variables. The CG force and diffusion fields in (
18
) essentially capture two effects: first, application of a non-linear change of variables, which manifests in the presence of derivatives of
\xi
. Second, averaging over all phase space points which are collapsed under the action of the CG map
\Xi
, manifest in the action of the projection operator
\mathcal{P}
.

The structure of the dynamical parameters in Eqs. (
17
-
18
) carries over to the coarse grained generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
and the invariant distribution of the CG Langevin dynamics:

Proposition 2
.

The effective dynamics in Equation (
15
) admits the invariant measure
e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}
. The associated stationary probability flow is

\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover, the projected generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
admits a decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}=\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{a}+\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{s},

with anti-symmetric and symmetric parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{a}\phi

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\phi,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{s}\phi=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\phi\right].

(19)

Proposition
2
shows that the invariant distribution of the CG dynamics is induced by the potential of mean force, ensuring thermodynamic consistency between the full-space and CG dynamics. As in the full-space setting, the stationary probability flow
\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}
associated to the invariant distribution determines the anti-symmetric part of the CG generator, as it accounts for the “Hamiltonian” part of the CG drift, consisting of the velocity and generalized force terms. The symmetric part is in turn determined by the potential of mean force and the CG diffusion field, and it accounts for the friction term and the generalized diffusion term acting on the CG momentum component. Since
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{s}
is also given in a divergence form, it admits an integration by parts formula analogous to (
7
), which can be exploited for numerical approximation of the CG generator.

In summary, Propositions
1
and
2
show that the coarse grained Langevin dynamics retains much of the structure of the full state dynamics (
1
). Detailed proofs of these results can be found in Appendix
A
.

IV
Methods

We now turn to the numerical approximation and analysis of the coarse grained Langevin equation (
15
). This entails three essential steps: first, generation of sufficient training data; second, analysis of the coarse grained generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
; third, learning of the parameters
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
of the CG dynamics.

IV.1
Data Generation

In order to infer properties of the coarse grained Langevin equation (
15
), we require samples from the coarse grained equilibrium distribution
\nu(\mathbf{y})
. As this distribution is the marginal of the full-state canonical distribution
\mu
, it is sufficient to generate data sampling from
\mu
and subsequently project them by means of the mapping
\Xi
. Finally, as the canonical distribution in full space factors into positional component
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
and momentum component
\mu_{\mathbf{p}}
, it is enough to generate samples from
\mu_{\mathbf{q}}
and complement them using samples from the multi-variate normal distribution
\mu_{\mathbf{p}}
. To obtain position space samples, we consider three methods:

IV.1.1
Traditional Methods

Rejection Sampling

Rejection sampling or acceptance-rejection sampling is a standard method to generate i.i.d. samples from a distribution with known density function (up to normalization constant). On a low-dimensional state space, it is usually an efficient approach, but it is not scalable to higher-dimensional spaces. We use rejection sampling only as a baseline to compare to the performance of other, more scalable methods.

Ergodic Integration of the Langevin Dyanmics

Another standard approach is to integrate the SDE (
1
) over a sufficiently long time horizon
T
, using an Euler scheme or higher-order integration method. By the ergodic theorem, all snapshots from the trajectory will then form an approximate (correlated) sample from the canonical distribution. As is well known, at high values of
\beta
(low temperature), a large time horizon
T
will be needed to explore the entire position space and obtain a near-equilibrated dataset. To circumvent these problems, we employ a generative modeling approach as outlined in the next section.

IV.1.2
Thermodynamic interpolation

Thermodynamic Interpolation
(TI) was proposed in Ref.
25
for equilibrium sampling of the Boltzmann distribution across different thermodynamic states. The method is based on flow models where we learn the velocity field
\mathbf{b}^{(\theta)}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of an ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(\tau)}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\mathbf{b}^{(\theta)}\left(\tau,\mathbf{x}(\tau)\right),\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0},

(20)

such that its solution is a flow map
\Phi_{\tau}^{(\theta)}
with a push-forward
\Phi_{1\,\#}^{(\theta)}\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}
. In other words, if we draw a sample
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
, and integrate Eq. (
20
) for
\tau
from
0
to
1
, we obtain a sample from
\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
. Here, we apply the ambient version of TI, meaning that the initial distribution is the canonical position space distribution
\rho_{0}=\mu_{\mathbf{q}}(\beta_{0})
at a high temperature
\beta_{0}
, while the target is the canonical distribution at a low temperature, i.e.
\rho_{1}=\mu_{\mathbf{q}}(\beta_{1})
.

Our implementation of TI is based on the two-sided interpolant
1

\mathbf{x}(\tau)=I(\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})+\gamma(\tau)\mathbf{z}

(21)

where
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\text{Id})
,
\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\rho_{0}
,
\mathbf{x}_{1}\sim\rho_{1}
. The function
I
is a deterministic interpolant that satisfies
\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{x}_{0}
,
\mathbf{x}(1)=\mathbf{x}_{1}
, and
\gamma(\tau)
is a smooth function controlling the noise level at intermediate times (see
1
for details.) We train the model according to the loss function

\begin{split}&\mathbf{L}\left[\mathbf{b}^{(\theta)}\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\tau,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1}}\left[\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{b}^{(\theta)}(\tau,\mathbf{x}(\tau);\beta_{0},\beta_{1})|^{2}-\mathbf{b}^{(\theta)}(\tau,\mathbf{x}(\tau);\beta_{0},\beta_{1})\left(\partial_{\tau}I(\tau,\mathbf{x}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{1})+\dot{\gamma}(\tau)\mathbf{z}\right)\right],\end{split}

(22)

employing antithetic sampling for smoother convergence, as previously described
1
.

Once the TI model has been trained, we can generate samples from the target distribution (lower temperature) given initial states from the initial distribution (higher temperature). We also compute the
importance weight
of each sample as proposed in Ref.
25
, in order to account for any bias or inaccuracy in the model output distribution. These weights are later used to correct towards the equilibrium expectation of mass and stiffness matrices
\mathbf{G}
and
\mathbf{A}
within the gEDMD algorithm (see next section), in the same fashion as in Ref.
29
. It is worth noting that the weights are computed with respect to the unnormalized distribution, as the normalization constant cancels out in the gEDMD algorithm.

IV.2
gEDMD

In order to verify if meta stable states, eigenvalues and implied timescales are preserved by the CG dynamics, we need to numerically approximate the coarse grained infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
in Equation (
14
). As shown in Ref.
17
,
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
can be approximated by a data-driven method called generator Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (gEDMD). Remarkably, the gEDMD algorithm can be applied directly on the full space data without the need to compute the coefficients
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
of the CG dynamics, see also Ref.
28
.

The algorithm requires
n
basis functions acting on the CG phase space variables
\mathbf{z},\,\mathbf{v}
:

\phi(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})=\{\phi_{1}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v}),...,\phi_{n}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})\},

along with position space samples
\{\mathbf{q}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{m}
, and the Jacobians of the CG map
\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q}_{l})
evaluated at each sample. We then augment these data by drawing momenta from the canonical momentum distribution, yielding phase space samples
\mathbf{x}_{l}=(\mathbf{q}_{l},\mathbf{p}_{l})
. We project these samples into CG phase space as

\mathbf{y}_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}_{l}\\
\mathbf{v}_{l}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\xi(\mathbf{q}_{l})\\
\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q}_{l})\mathbf{p}_{l}\end{pmatrix}.

Next, we build
n\times m
-dimensional data matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{1})&|&\cdots&|&\phi(\mathbf{z}_{m},\mathbf{v}_{m})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\mathcal{L}\phi](\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1})&|&\cdots&|&[\mathcal{L}\phi](\mathbf{q}_{m},\mathbf{p}_{m})\end{bmatrix}.

Note that to form the matrix
\mathcal{L}\mathbf{\Phi}
, we apply the full state generator
\mathcal{L}
, which requires derivatives of the spatial CG map. The final matrix approximation
\mathbf{L}
to the CG generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
is then given by

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}=\widehat{\mathbf{G}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{A}},

(23)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\phi(\mathbf{y}_{l})\otimes\phi(\mathbf{y}_{l}),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{A}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\phi(\mathbf{y}_{l})\otimes[\mathcal{L}\phi](\mathbf{x}_{l})

(24)

are called mass and stiffness matrices, respectively. To solve for
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}
numerically, we do not explicitly form
\widehat{\mathbf{G}}
, instead, we perform a whitening transformation by removing the small singular values of
\mathbf{\Phi}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}^{\top},

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{r}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbf{R},

(25)

where
r\leq n
. Slowest timescales
t_{i}
of the system, or dominant eigenvalues
\kappa_{i}
of the generator, can be computed by diagonalization of the reduced matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{r}
.

In this study, we use random Fourier features (RFFs)
35
as basis functions:

\phi(\mathbf{y})=\{\cos{(\omega^{\top}_{1}\mathbf{y})},\sin{(\omega^{\top}_{1}\mathbf{y})},\dots,\cos{(\omega^{\top}_{n}\mathbf{y})},\sin{(\omega^{\top}_{n}\mathbf{y})}\},

where
\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
are random frequency vectors drawn from a spectral distribution
\rho
. RFFs represent a low-rank approximation to a reproducing kernel function, e.g. associated to a Gaussian kernel
35
.

IV.3
Parameter Estimation for CG Dynamics

To produce simulations of the coarse grained Langevin SDE, we need to learn models for their diffusion and drift coefficients defined in Equation (
17
). Let us define the
local force

\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and
local diffusion

\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}
as follows:

\begin{split}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})&=-\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{p}_{i}\partial_{ij}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}_{j},\\
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})&=\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})^{\top}.\end{split}

(26)

These are the terms which are projected onto CG space in Eq. (
18
) to form the learnable parts of the effective drift (momentum component) and diffusion (lower right block). Using available training data, we can learn the effective drift and diffusion as best approximations of the local force and diffusion, by solving data-driven regression problems, similar to the one proposed in Ref.
28
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{M}_{m}&=\underset{\tilde{\mathbf{M}}=\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})}{\argmin}\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{l=1}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{z}_{l},\mathbf{v}_{l})-\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{q}_{l},\mathbf{p}_{l})\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
\mathbf{f}_{m}&=\underset{\tilde{\mathbf{f}}=\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})}{\argmin}\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{l=1}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{z}_{l},\mathbf{v}_{l})-\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbf{q}_{l},\mathbf{p}_{l})\right\|_{F}^{2},\end{split}

(27)

where the regression is carried out against
m
samples. Note that the learned effective force and diffusion are only functions of the CG variables. The complete effective drift and diffusion fields can then be constructed as

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{b}}_{m}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{f}_{m}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})-\gamma\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{A}}_{m}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0_{k\times k}&0_{k\times k}\\
0_{k\times k}&\gamma\mathbf{M}_{m}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})\end{pmatrix}.

In the numerical examples, we parametrize the effective force and diffusion either as a linear combination of random Fourier features, or as the output of a shallow neural network (NN). We found that the solution of the RFF regression tends to be accurate within data-rich regions, while the NN provides better extrapolation accuracy at the cost of increased computational cost. During simulations of the CG dynamics, we apply a hybrid method to leverage the strengths of both models by a switching rule: the RFF-based solution is used within data-rich regions, while the more accurate NN is used for extrapolation outside this region.

The summary of the numerical approximation of the CG dynamics is given in Algorithm

VI
Summary

We investigated coarse graining of the underdamped Langevin dynamics based on a phase space CG map which uses both a coarse grained position and momentum component. We showed that application of the Zwanzig projection leads to a coarse grained dynamics with a similar structure as the full Langevin equation. We have also shown that the CG generator can be decomposed into symmetric and anti-symmetric parts resembling the Hamiltonian and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck parts of the full generator. Moreover, we showed that thermodynamic interpolation, a generative modeling technique that interpolates in temperature space, can be used to produce accurate samples of the position space distribution after training on high-temperature simulations. These samples can be combined with data-driven learning techniques, in particular the gEDMD method, to analyze kinetic properties of the CG dynamics, and to estimate its parameters, without explicit integration of the coarse grained model. By means of a two-dimensional model potential, we demonstrated that the CG dynamics reproduce thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the full-space dynamics.

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L}\Xi)=\mathcal{P}(\nabla\Xi\mathbf{b}+0)

(29)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})&0_{k\times d}\\

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{P}\left(\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}\right)\\

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\ =\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{L}\Xi)(\mathbf{y})h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathcal{L}\Xi(\mathbf{x})h(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top},\,h\circ\Xi\right\rangle_{\mu}-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\langle\mathbf{A}\nabla\Xi^{\top},\,\nabla(h\circ\Xi)\right\rangle_{\mu}.

The gradient
\nabla(h\circ\Xi)
is taken with respect to the full phase space variables
(\mathbf{q},\,\mathbf{p})
and by the chain rule we have
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(h\circ\Xi)=\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}h\,\nabla\Xi
. Inserting this relation, and applying the co-area formula in the reverse direction, we re-express the above in terms of CG space integrals and projections:

\begin{split}&\left\langle\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top},\,h\circ\Xi\right\rangle_{\mu}-\frac{1}{\beta}\left\langle\mathbf{A}\nabla\Xi^{\top},\,\nabla(h\circ\Xi)\right\rangle_{\mu}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\mathcal{P}(\nabla\Xi\mathbf{A}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.\end{split}

It remains to apply a partial integration to the second term, which provides:

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}&\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})+\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\mathcal{P}(\nabla\Xi\mathbf{A}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\right]h(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.\end{split}

As the function
h
is arbitrary, and recognizing the definition (
16
) of the effective diffusion field, we have shown that

\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})+\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\bar{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right].

(31)

Using that only the lower right block of
\bar{\mathbf{A}}
is non-zero, the second term simplifies to:

\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\bar{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\end{pmatrix}.

This furnishes the final expression for the effective drift:

\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\end{pmatrix}.

(32)

A first decomposition of the effective generator

Using Eq. (
32
), we can start from the general definition of the CG generator
\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}
, and re-write it as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}&=\bar{\mathbf{b}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{1}{\beta}\bar{\mathbf{A}}:\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}\\
&=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\mathbf{M}:\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{v}}.\end{split}

To re-write the last term, we have used again that only the lower right block of
\mathbf{M}
is non-zero. The second and third term can be summarized as follows:

\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\right].

(33)

The decomposition (
33
) is intriguing as the second term is in divergence form, which implies that it is automatically symmetric with respect to the inner product defined by
e^{-\beta F}
. The first term remains as a candidate for the anti-symmetric part, which is going to be verified next.

Continuity Equation

Re-arranging Eq. (
31
), we see that

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})&=\bar{\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\bar{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right],\end{split}

which is exactly the definition of the
probability flow
associated to the Boltzmann factor
e^{-\beta F}
under the CG dynamics
45
. If we can show that
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})
satisfies the continuity equation

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]=0,

then it follows that
e^{-\beta F}
is the invariant distribution for the CG dynamics. The continuity equation follows from a similar sequence of arguments that led us to the decomposition (
32
). Again, we test against an arbitrary function
h
and integrate over CG space:

\begin{split}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}&\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\,h(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\,h(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\Xi^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}h(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},\end{split}

where we applied partial integration and the co-area formula. Using the chain rule and another partial integration, we arrive at the continuity equation in full space, which is zero as
\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is already the stationary probability for the full system:

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}&\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x})\nabla\Xi^{\top}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}h(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}h(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{x})e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{x})}\right]h(\Xi(\mathbf{x}))\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0.\end{split}

Indeed,
e^{-\beta F}
is the invariant distribution of the CG dynamics, and we can introduce the notation

\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{y}):=\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}\nabla\Xi^{\top})(\mathbf{y})

for its stationary probability flow. The continuity equation also implies directly that the first term in Eq. (
33
) is anti-symmetric:

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}&\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\phi(\mathbf{y})\,\psi(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\cdot\left[\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\right]\phi(\mathbf{y})\,\psi(\mathbf{y})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\psi(\mathbf{y})\,\phi(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2k}}\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\psi(\mathbf{y})\,\phi(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}.\end{split}

Summary of the Results

We have shown that Eq. (
33
) furnishes the decomposition of the CG generator into its symmetric and anti-symmetric parts, with invariant distribution
e^{-\beta F}
and stationary probability flow
\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{a}+\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{s},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{a}\phi

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\phi,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{\Xi}_{s}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}e^{\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\cdot\left[\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{y})e^{-\beta F(\mathbf{y})}\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\phi\right].

Lastly, we note that
\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}
indeed accounts for the Hamiltonian part of the CG drift:

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{J}}^{\mathrm{eq}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(\nabla\Xi\mathbf{J}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\mathcal{P}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})&0_{k\times d}\\
\partial_{ij}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}_{i}&\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}\\
-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q})\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{p}_{i}\partial_{ij}\xi(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{p}_{j}-\nabla\xi(\mathbf{q})\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q}))\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{f}\end{pmatrix}.
