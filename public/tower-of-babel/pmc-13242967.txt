Is a 32 mm glenosphere optimal in reverse shoulder arthroplasty for patients with small glenoid anatomy?
Authors: Hiroshi Kiyomatsu, Taketsugu Fujibuchi, Tatsuhiko Kutsuna, Tomofumi Kinoshita, Takashi Tsuda, Mizuaki Hayashi, Katsuhiro Shimono, Masaki Takao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIn reverse shoulder arthroplasty (RSA), the diameter and position of the glenosphere have a significant effect on impingement-free range of motion (ROM). This effect may be more pronounced in patients with small glenoid anatomy. We tested whether a 32 mm glenosphere provides greater ROM than 36 mm and 39 mm glenospheres across three inferior overhangs (IOs).MethodsAsian female patients undergoing RSA (mean age 75.5 years (SD 5.6; range 66 to 89); mean height 145.9 cm (SD 3.3; range 137.6 to 153.0)) were evaluated using 3D CT planning. Three diameters (32 mm, 36 mm, and 39 mm) and three IOs (1.5 mm, 3.0 mm, and 4.5 mm) were combined (nine simulations per patient). We recorded impingement-free ROM arcs from neutral (0°) to bony impingement (bone-to-bone contact) for extension–flexion (EF), abduction–adduction (AA), and external-internal rotation (RO). Postoperative outcomes were described for the 32 mm subset (n = 11). Repeated-measures analysis of variance assessed the main effects and interactions of diameter and overhang.ResultsBoth glenosphere diameter and inferior overhang had significant independent effects in each plane (all p < 0.001) without consistent interactions. The 32 mm glenosphere conferred broader arcs of motion in EF, AA, and RO, whereas the 39 mm diameter consistently restricted ROM. A higher IO (≥ 3.0 mm) further expanded motion.ConclusionIn this cohort with small glenoid anatomy, a 32 mm glenosphere and IO ≥ 3.0 mm maximized predicted bony impingement-free ROM, suggesting that downsizing with adequate inferior overhang may be advantageous in a small glenoid vault. Introduction

Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (RSA) restores elevation in cuff-deficient shoulders; however, early Grammont-style designs with a medialized centre of rotation (COR) were limited by scapular notching and suboptimal rotation, prompting later shifts towards glenoid lateralization and a 135° neck-shaft angle.1-3

Implant geometry and placement (glenosphere size, lateralization/inferiorization, inclination, and tray design), together determine impingement-free range of motion (ROM), deltoid loading, and intrinsic stability, entailing trade-offs between motion and stability.4-8

Asian shoulders, particularly in petite females, are smaller, increasing the risk of component mismatch and vault perforation with standard western baseplates; therefore, downsizing strategies may also be applicable to other patients with small glenoid anatomy.9,10

Glenosphere size shows heterogeneous results: larger heads may increase abduction and reduce notching, but raise joint/deltoid loads and reduce internal rotation; clinical series and registry data are inconsistent, and a randomized controlled trial showed no clinically relevant differences across common sizes.11-15

Inferior overhang and measured lateralization can expand ROM and reduce notching, whereas excessive lateralization increases deltoid load and acromial stress, underscoring a balanced configuration rather than maximizing a single parameter.4,5,8,16-19

Japanese populations (mean glenoid 31.5 × 23.1 mm) highlight the potential for implant-anatomy mismatch,9,10 while high-quality outcome data for 32 mm glenospheres remain limited.20,21 Accordingly, a mechanistic assessment using preoperative 3D planning may help inform implant selection in small-anatomy shoulders while prospective evidence accrues.

Given these controversies and the absence of data on truly small‐diameter glenospheres, individualized diameter selection based on patient skeletal morphology rather than height alone is essential to balance impingement‐free ROM and implant stability. Therefore, we hypothesized that, in patients with small glenoid anatomy, a 32 mm glenosphere would afford superior impingement‐free ROM compared with 36 mm and 39 mm diameters, independent of distalization effects. We tested this hypothesis using a cohort of petite Asian females undergoing RSA.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study enrolled 25 Asian females (mean age 75.5 years (SD 5.6; range 66 to 89); mean height 145.9 cm (SD 3.3; range 137.6 to 153.0)) (Table I) who had undergone 3D CT before RSA using the Medacta Shoulder System (Medacta, Switzerland). The inclusion criteria were aged ≥ 65 years, female sex, and the absence of significant bony defects precluding standard baseplate fixation. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board (approval no. 2505010), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.22 Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Imaging and glenoid morphological measurements

CT-derived 3D scapular models were generated in My Shoulder (Medacta, Switzerland) and aligned to the glenoid face. Glenoid height (maximum vertical diameter (MVD)) and width (maximum anteroposterior diameter (MAPD)) were measured, and the anteroposterior position ratio (APR) was defined as the distance from the MAPD line to the inferior rim divided by MVD (Figure 1).

Schematic illustration demonstrating how the maximum vertical diameter (MVD), maximum anteroposterior diameter (MAPD), and anteroposterior position (APP) are measured using a 3D glenoid model. The MVD is defined as the longest straight-line distance between the superior and inferior margins of the glenoid. The MAPD is determined by a line drawn perpendicular to the MVD from the anterior rim to the posterior rim. For the APP, the perpendicular distance from the MAPD line to the inferior rim of the glenoid is measured and normalized by the MVD to calculate the anteroposterior position ratio (APR).

Preoperative ROM simulations

All simulations involved the same humeral component and cup model. The baseplate was placed in a neutral orientation along the Friedman line and achieved contact with ≥ 80% of the glenoid surface. Three glenosphere diameters (32 mm, 36 mm, and 39 mm) and three IO (1.5 mm, 3.0 mm, and 4.5 mm) yielded nine simulations per patient (3 × 3). During each simulation, the humerus was articulated until contact of bony structures occurred, signifying the onset of impingement. Impingement was defined as bone-to-bone contact in this rigid model; soft-tissue impingement was not simulated (Figure 2).

Representative 3D simulations demonstrating the impingement‐free range of motion with different glenosphere diameters. a) A 32‐mm glenosphere (blue) permits greater extension of the humeral component (green) relative to the scapula (gray) before bony impingement occurs. b) In contrast, a 39 mm glenosphere (red) exhibits earlier contact with the scapular bone, thereby restricting motion.

Outcome measures

Maximal impingement-free ROM was recorded from neutral (0°) until bone-to-bone contact: EF (extension-flexion) with the humerus at 0° abduction/0° external rotation; AA (abduction-adduction) at 0° extension/0° external rotation; and RO (external-internal rotation) with the arm adducted at 0° extension/0° abduction. Combined arcs were calculated as the sum of the maximal angles in each direction. Direction-specific maxima are shown in the Supplementary Material.

Postoperative clinical outcomes (32 mm subset)

Postoperative outcomes were retrospectively reviewed for patients receiving a 32 mm glenosphere (n = 11) and are reported descriptively.

Statistical analysis

Two-factor repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) assessed the main effects and interaction of diameter (32 mm/36 mm/39 mm) and IO (1.5 mm/3.0 mm/4.5 mm) on EF, AA, and RO, with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons when appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed in Python v. 3.11 (Python Software Foundation, USA) using statsmodels; a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In each motion plane, larger IO led to higher mean ROM values, whereas smaller glenospheres yielded broader arcs (Table II).

RO: RO showed significant main effects of diameter and overhang without a significant interaction (Table III). RO increased with IO and was greatest with the 32 mm glenosphere (approximately 5° to 14° greater than 36/39 mm across IO levels) (Tables II, IV and V).

Diameter-based pairwise comparisons

Pairwise comparisons for diameter under each ROM measure (Table IV) showed that 32 mm compared with 39 mm reached significance in every plane (p < 0.01 after correction). Although the 32 mm to 36 mm and 36 mm to 39 mm differences were also often significant, they were sometimes less pronounced.

Overhang-based pairwise comparisons

Post-hoc comparisons for overhang changes (1.5 mm vs 3.0 mm, 1.5 mm vs 4.5 mm, 3.0 mm vs 4.5 mm) (Table V) demonstrated Bonferroni-adjusted p-values ≤ 0.033 across EF, AA, and RO. Even a small increase from 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm conferred a notable expansion of motion, particularly in EF and RO.

Postoperative clinical outcomes (32 mm subset)

A total of 11 patients received a 32 mm glenosphere (all female (n = 11); mean age 75.1 years (SD 6.7); follow-up 31.9 months (SD 12.3)). Indications were cuff tear arthropathy (n = 8) and other diagnoses (n = 3). The mean American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score was 81.5 (SD 13.9),23 University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) score 29.5 (SD 3.6),24 and Constant score 72.8 (SD 14.9).25 Complications included two dislocations and one radial nerve palsy; scapular notching occurred in one case. These data are presented descriptively to provide clinical context for the simulated ROM findings, as no comparative clinical cohort with larger glenosphere diameters was available.

Discussion

Our findings confirm that glenosphere diameter and IO independently affect impingement-free EF, AA, and RO in shoulders with small glenoid anatomy: the 32 mm glenosphere generally provides broader arcs, while IO ≥ 3.0 mm augments motion in all planes. Although our cohort comprised petite Asian females, these bony clearance mechanisms are likely relevant to other patients with similarly small glenoid vaults. These simulation findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted alongside the motion-stability trade-off of larger diameters.10,15,20,21,26-29

Impact of glenosphere diameter on impingement-free ROM

Our 3D-CT-based simulations in this small-anatomy cohort (mean height 145.9 cm (SD 3.3; range 137.6 to 153.0)) unequivocally demonstrate that glenosphere diameter independently governs impingement-free EF, AA, and RO. Because the mechanism is anatomically driven, these findings may be relevant to other patients with comparably small glenoid dimensions. Among the diameters tested, the 32 mm glenosphere consistently achieved the greatest ROM in all three planes, whereas the 39 mm device exhibited the most constrained kinematic envelope, with the 36 mm configuration intermediate.

Prior cadaveric and clinical studies in average-sized shoulders suggest that larger glenospheres can increase elevation/rotation but also increase deltoid/joint loads and scapular impingement risk.11,16,17,29 In contrast, our simulations in small glenoid vaults showed that 39 mm reduced EF, AA, and RO compared with 32 mm and 36 mm, while the 32 mm sphere delivered the broadest motion arcs in every plane. Although statistically significant, the absolute differences in combined arcs between 32 mm and 39 mm were modest in some configurations (e.g. approximately 8° to 23° in EF depending on inferior overhang) and accompanied by substantial inter-individual variability (Table II and Supplementary Material). Therefore, the clinical relevance of these mean differences likely depends on patient-specific anatomy and soft-tissue constraints, and glenosphere selection should be individualized using 3D planning and intraoperative stability assessment.

These findings suggest that peri-glenosphere clearance is a critical determinant of impingement-free ROM. In our cohort, the 32 mm glenosphere likely increased this clearance by reducing the humeral radius of rotation, thereby delaying bony impingement. Although soft tissues were not modelled, a smaller sphere may also reduce coracoid–conjoint tendon impingement during key motions in daily activities such as fastening clothes or reaching behind the back. Thus its mechanical benefit may not only contribute to the avoidance of osseous impingement but also potentially extend to the mitigation of subcoracoid soft-tissue impingement. From a clinical perspective, when preoperative 3D-CT planning predicts early bony impingement with a 36 mm or 39 mm glenosphere in a small glenoid vault (e.g. MVD 33.8 mm (± 2.8 mm) and MAPD 24.1 mm (± 2.1 mm) in the present cohort), a 32 mm glenosphere may be considered to increase bony clearance, provided intraoperative stability is satisfactory.

Clinical outcome data suggest that patient height alone does not determine the optimal glenosphere diameter, and reported differences across commonly used diameters are inconsistent.12,14,29 Randomized studies and clinical series in predominantly average-sized populations have not demonstrated a consistent functional advantage of larger diameters.12,29 Registry analyses suggest lower revision rates with 38 mm to 40 mm compared with < 38 mm, although truly small 32 mm spheres are under-represented in large cohorts.13 Prospective randomized studies further indicate that scapular notching and clinical outcomes can be comparable across diameters when inferior overhang or eccentricity is optimized.26,30 Taken together, these findings suggest that optimal diameter depends on anatomy rather than size alone. Pending additional randomized evidence in small-anatomy shoulders, our 3D-CT-based analysis provides a mechanistic rationale for when a 32 mm glenosphere may be advantageous in small glenoid vaults. Importantly, intrinsic stability is implant-dependent and varies with liner-derived constraint; Moroder et al31 demonstrated substantial variability across systems, suggesting that any instability risk with downsizing is system-specific.

Although our study focused primarily on inferior overhang, the biomechanical influence of lateralization in combination with a 32 mm glenosphere remains unclear. Because lateralization directly affects deltoid tension, joint stability, and acromial stress, future biomechanical and clinical investigations should evaluate whether a 32 mm sphere maintains sufficient stability and muscle balance when lateralized. Clarifying this interaction will be crucial for defining safe and effective configurations tailored to patients with small-anatomy shoulders. Intrinsic stability is also sensitive to component versioning: increasing humeral anteversion improves resistance to anterior dislocation, whereas glenoid retroversion beyond approximately 10° reduces the stability ratio in abducted positions.4,19 From a soft-tissue perspective, repairing the subscapularis decreases dislocation risk in medialized and lateralized designs, and when repair is not feasible, a lateralized COR mitigates instability relative to a medialized COR.

Impact of inferior overhang on impingement-free ROM

Our simulations also highlight that inferior overhang is a critical modulator of EF, AA, and RO. Across diameters, increasing inferior overhang from 1.5 mm to 3.0 mm increased impingement-free ROM by approximately 32° to 43° in EF, 8° to 14° in AA, and 37° to 41° in RO (Table II). Further increasing overhang to 4.5 mm provided smaller additional gains (3° to 7° in EF, 6° to 10° in AA, and 15° to 18° in RO), indicating diminishing returns beyond 3.0 mm. Thus, a minimum overhang of 3.0 mm appears essential to approach maximal kinematic freedom without excessively altering joint mechanics. Recent biomechanical evaluations report that inferior eccentric glenospheres improve moment arms and impingement-free motion more effectively than inferior tilt alone, and that adding inferior reaming to eccentricity further reduces scapular conflict.4,15 Clinically, a comparison demonstrated that small, eccentrically offset glenospheres can curb scapular notching as effectively as larger, centrally positioned spheres without sacrificing ROM.26 These convergent data support prioritizing adequate inferior overhang and selective eccentricity, particularly in small vaults, before upsizing the sphere to prevent unnecessary increases in joint and deltoid loads.17

3D modelling has shown that a 4.0 mm eccentric glenosphere significantly enhances the deltoid moment arm and reduces bony impingement compared with a neutral sphere or a 10° inferior tilt alone.4 Similarly, modest eccentric offset (2 mm) in a 38 mm sphere mitigates scapular notching as effectively as a standard 42 mm implant without sacrificing ROM.26 Finite-element stress analysis demonstrates that overhang shifts the COR inferiorly, raising impingement thresholds while maintaining balanced deltoid tension, whereas excessive overhang (> 4.5 mm) concentrates stress at the baseplate-bone interface, potentially jeopardizing fixation longevity.32

This study had some important limitations. We varied only the inferior overhang, keeping glenoid lateralization, humeral insert thickness, and component orientation (baseplate tilt and glenosphere version) constant. Lateralizing the glenoid by 3 mm to 5 mm increases deltoid and acromial stresses by up to 37%, predisposing to fatigue and acromial fractures.33 Variations in insert thickness shift the COR and impingement thresholds,16 and inferior baseplate tilt confers modest ROM gains at the cost of elevated von Mises interface stress compared with eccentric offsets.11 Particularly, maintaining the balance between lateralization and stability may be challenging when using a 32 mm glenosphere.

Our rigid-bone models did not account for soft-tissue constraints or dynamic muscle loading, factors shown to influence postoperative kinematics in cadaveric and simulation studies.4 Accordingly, our findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. The exclusive inclusion of petite Asian females limits generalizability to larger or male patients and to other glenoid morphologies, although the bony clearance mechanism is likely relevant to other patients with similarly small glenoid dimensions. Finally, clinical endpoints were available only for a limited 32 mm subset and were reported descriptively without a comparator group; therefore, we could not correlate the simulation findings with postoperative ROM, patient-reported outcomes, instability rates, or scapular notching across different glenosphere diameters.

Future prospective studies should evaluate diameter, overhang, lateralization, and component inclination in small-framed patients using comprehensive clinical and radiological endpoints. Integrating dynamic soft-tissue modelling or in vivo kinematic imaging, systematic implant stability assessments, and comprehensive clinical endpoints, including complication rates and long-term radiological follow-up, will be essential to refine implant parameter guidelines and optimize RSA outcomes across diverse anatomies.

In conclusion, for patients with small glenoid anatomy, a 32 mm glenosphere combined with an overhang ≥ 3.0 mm consistently achieved the widest arcs of impingement-free motion in EF, AA, and RO. Nevertheless, surgeons should balance this strategy against potential acromial stress and scapular notching risks. Our simulation-based results highlight the benefits of smaller glenospheres plus sufficient inferior overhang for smaller bony dimensions; however, clinical follow-up studies are required to confirm mid-term stability, scapular notching, fracture risk, and overall complication profiles in small-anatomy patients. This finding may guide implant selection for patients with small glenoid vaults to maximize shoulder function while minimizing mechanical stress.

Take home message

- A 32 mm glenosphere with inferior overhang ≥ 3.0 mm yielded the greatest predicted impingement-free range of motion, likely via increased peri-glenosphere clearance.

- Implant selection should be individualized using preoperative 3D planning and intraoperative stability assessment.
