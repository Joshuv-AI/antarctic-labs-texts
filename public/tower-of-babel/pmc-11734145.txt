Left Bundle Branch Area Pacing Lead Perforation Complicated by Left Ventricular Thrombus
Authors: Samuel Kim, Andrea Sit, Adam Perkovic, Sonia Chan, Linda Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 72-year-old woman underwent left bundle branch area pacing, and subsequent transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated potential septal lead perforation. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed an intracardiac mass, which resolved with anticoagulation. This case highlights left ventricular thrombus as a potential complication of septal lead perforation.Key Words: bradycardia, cardiac pacemaker, echocardiography, thrombus

A 72-year-old woman presented to our institution with presyncope and pulmonary edema on a background of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obstructive sleep apnea, and chronic kidney disease. She was found to have second-degree atrioventricular block and underwent successful left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) through septal lead placement of a Biotronik Solia S 60 active fixation lead with an impedance of 565 Ω at implant. The following day, routine transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated potential septal lead perforation into the left ventricle (LV) (Figure 1A), with no other significant findings. A subsequent device check demonstrated no change in bipolar impedance or unipolar signal.

To further characterize lead positioning, the patient underwent a transesophageal echocardiogram, which demonstrated the septal lead abutting the endocardial surface of the LV (Figure 1B). Incidentally, there was a 9-mm intracardiac mass along the aortomitral curtain in the LV outflow tract (Figure 1C, Video 1), with possible differential diagnoses including papillary fibroelastoma or an unusually located LV thrombus. The cardiothoracic surgery department was consulted, and the multidisciplinary consensus was to manage the intracardiac mass with apixaban 5 mg twice daily and reassess the need for surgical intervention in several weeks. Repeat transesophageal echocardiogram after 4 weeks demonstrated complete resolution of the mass (Figure 1D). On review of systems, there was no evidence of embolism, and cerebral magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated no embolic stroke. As such, it was theorized that the most likely diagnosis was LV thrombus involving the septal lead tip, which had subsequently embolized but remained contained within the LV outflow tract by the aortic valve. Given stable lead parameters, the patient’s septal lead was not repositioned. She was later found to have atrial fibrillation on device remote monitoring and was continued on apixaban long-term. She remained well, with no issues at the 2-year follow-up.

LBBAP is emerging as an attractive alternative to right ventricular pacing, offering advantages such as more physiologic pacing with a narrower QRS interval and improved clinical outcomes, with lower rates of heart failure hospitalizations and mortality.1 Complications recognized to be specific to LBBAP lead placement include LV cavity perforation, coronary arterial and venous fistulas, and acute coronary syndromes from coronary artery injury or spasm.2 In our case, we suspect that potential septal lead perforation acted as a nidus for LV thrombus formation, which was managed successfully with therapeutic anticoagulation. To our knowledge, this has not previously been reported and, if left untreated, may theoretically lead to thromboembolic complications such as stroke. Lead repositioning was also considered to reduce recurrent thrombus risk but was left alone, given stable lead parameters and need for anticoagulation ongoing. Our case, therefore, demonstrates how a multidisciplinary approach to an LV thrombus secondary to LBBAP lead perforation can successfully be managed conservatively with anticoagulation and the utility of echocardiography in making this diagnosis.
