Rewiring metabolic-immune circuitry in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC)
Authors: Xiaoyi Yin, Gang Jin
Published: 2026

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) continues to present a major clinical challenge. Despite ongoing advances in systemic therapy, the persistent treatment resistance and dismal prognosis underscore an urgent need to move beyond conventional paradigms. Rather than being merely a collection of malignant epithelial cells, PDAC constitutes a complex and dynamically adaptive ecosystem, where tumor-intrinsic adaptations and the immunosuppressive microenvironment interact to drive treatment resistance. The two reviews featured in this special issue, by Duttenhefner and Reindl1 on the mevalonate pathway and by Liu et al.2 on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), provide complementary and detailed insights into these complex mechanisms. Together, they highlight two key mechanisms of PDAC biology—metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion—and, more importantly, reveal their intimate crosstalk. This editorial contends that the future of PDAC therapy depends on targeting both pathways, demanding combined strategies that concurrently target tumor metabolism and the immunosuppressive microenvironment.3

The mevalonate pathway: a key metabolic regulator and therapeutic target

Duttenhefner and Reindl comprehensively review the mevalonate pathway as a key driver of PDAC’s metabolic plasticity.1 Far beyond its classical role in cholesterol synthesis, this pathway emerges as a central processor, converting metabolic flux into diverse oncogenic signals. Its products—sterols and isoprenoids—mediate a wide range of pro-tumor processes: they maintain redox homeostasis, support the membrane localization and activity of mutant kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) and other guanosine triphosphatases (GTPases), activate yes-associated protein (YAP)/transcriptional co-activator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) and Hedgehog signaling, facilitate protein glycosylation for receptor modulation, and enhance resistance to ferroptosis and pyroptosis.3 This pathway functions not merely as a passive metabolic supporter, but as an active oncogenic driver, closely linked to oncogenic KRAS signaling and upregulated by cues from the tumor microenvironment (TME), particularly hypoxia and acidosis.4

While the therapeutic implications are clear, translating these findings into the clinic remains challenging. As the review outlines, monotherapy with statins (3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-CoA [HMG-CoA] reductase inhibitors) has shown limited clinical success, hampered by dose-limiting toxicities and robust compensatory mechanisms.1 For instance, recent studies reveal that loss of the tumor suppressor mothers against decapentaplegic homolog 4 (SMAD4) can induce opposing metabolic programs, capable of enhancing both oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic upregulation.5 This metabolic flexibility likely directly influences how the tumor’s response to microenvironmental cues and shapes the immune landscape, underscoring the need for more refined targeting strategies beyond single-pathway inhibition. Therefore, combination approaches hold significant promise. Pre-clinical data demonstrate synergy when statins or bisphosphonates (farnesyl pyrophosphate [FPP] synthase inhibitors) are paired with chemotherapy (e.g., gemcitabine, 5-fluorouracil [5-FU]), radiation, KRAS-G12C/D inhibitors, or sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) inhibitors.1,6 These strategies aim to achieve multi-nodal inhibition of the pathway, overcoming metabolic redundancy. Furthermore, the development of dual farnesyl/geranylgeranyl transferase inhibitors and nanoparticle-conjugated agents represents a significant advancement towards enhancing efficacy and specificity.7

Therefore, we propose that the future of targeting lipid metabolism in PDAC lies not in generic systemic cholesterol reduction, but in the precise interception of specific mevalonate intermediates that govern tumor-stroma cross-talk. Simply repurposing conventional statins is insufficient; the field must pivot toward developing niche-specific metabolic modulators capable of overcoming intrinsic compensatory upregulation.

TAMs: key drivers of an immunosuppressive microenvironment

Alongside these metabolic adaptations, Liu and colleagues provide a comprehensive overview of the pivotal and multifaceted role of TAMs in shaping the PDAC immune landscape.2 They correctly note that the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy is oversimplified; single-cell studies reveal a spectrum of TAM subsets with distinct spatial locations, origins, and functions. These cells are actively reprogrammed by the tumor. Driver mutations like KRAS p.G12D, through the secretion of factors such as granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and cues from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and hypoxia, polarize TAMs towards immunosuppressive, pro-tumorigenic phenotypes.8

Functionally, TAMs secrete a diverse array of cytokines (interleukin-10 [IL-10], transforming growth factor β[TGF-β], CC chemokine ligand 2 [CCL2]) and express surface ligands (Programmed death ligand 1 [PD-L1], B7 family members, Siglec receptors) that directly inhibit cytotoxic T and natural killer (NK) cell function, promote the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and stimulate fibrosis and angiogenesis. Through these mechanisms, TAMs establish and sustain an immune-excluded, “cold” tumor microenvironment,2 which largely explains the failure of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) monotherapy in PDAC. Consequently, targeting TAMs is an essential component of any strategy aiming to restore anti-tumor immunity. Approaches range from blocking recruitment (chemokine receptors 2 [CCR2]/CCL2 axis),9 depleting specific subsets (CSF1-receptor [CSF1R] inhibitors), reprogramming their phenotype (cluster of differentiation 40 [CD40] agonists), or disrupting their immunosuppressive signaling (CD47/signal-regulatory protein α [SIRPα] blockade).10

We contend that the prevailing strategy of mere TAM depletion may be insufficient as a standalone strategy due to rapid stromal compensation and systemic toxicity. A more sustainable paradigm must focus on metabolic reprogramming—specifically disrupting lipid utilization programs that sustain immunosuppressive macrophage phenotypes to drive an anti-tumor phenotypic switch from within the harsh tumor microenvironment.

Metabolic-immune crosstalk in PDAC

The most significant implications arise when integrating the findings of these two reviews. The mevalonate pathway and TAM biology exhibit profound crosstalk, establishing a positive feedback loop that drives and sustains PDAC progression.

Tumor metabolism-mediated remodeling of the immune microenvironment

Tumor cell mevalonate activity directly shapes the immune microenvironment. Mevalonate-derived cholesterol is a key component of lipid rafts, essential for organizing immunosuppressive signaling platforms on both tumor and immune cells. Isoprenoids like geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) are required for the prenylation and function of small GTPases (e.g., Rho, Rac) in myeloid cells, influencing their migration, polarization, and cytokine secretion.1 The review by Duttenhefner and Reindl1 notes that the pathway supports hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) stabilization under hypoxia, a condition that also potently drives TAM recruitment and M2 polarization, as detailed by Liu et al.2 Beyond the mevalonate pathway, other metabolic adaptations also reshape the TME. For instance, tumor-derived lactate can induce histone lactylation to reprogram cholesterol metabolism, and nutrient scarcity activates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-δ (PPAR-δ) to drive metastasis. These dynamic metabolic rewirings consistently foster an immunosuppressive niche.

Metabolic reprogramming of TAMs and immune suppression

Within the TME, TAMs undergo distinct metabolic reprogramming that governs their functional polarization. Liu et al.2 highlight that TAMs in PDAC often exhibit a glycolytic and oxidative phosphorylation profile that supports their immunosuppressive role. Importantly, this metabolic dependency presents a targetable vulnerability. Modulating TAM metabolism—using agents like mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors or biguanides (e.g., metformin)—can reprogram these cells from a pro-tumor to an anti-tumor phenotype. Such interventions align with broader evidence demonstrating that targeting intrinsic metabolic vulnerabilities of immune cells can fundamentally reshape immune function and alleviate TME suppression.

Crosstalk effects in therapeutic interventions

Because of this intricate crosstalk, therapeutic targeting of one axis inevitably influences the other axis. Statins, as noted by Duttenhefner and Reindl,1 can downregulate PD-L1 expression and suppress YAP/TAZ, which is known to modulate cytokine secretion that recruits MDSCs and TAMs. This principle is exemplified by the finding that inhibiting the cholesterol metabolism regulator ubiquitin specific peptidase 20 (USP20) synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade,11 and parallel mechanisms exist where metabolic enzyme modification directly controls PD-L1 expression.4 Conversely, disrupting TAMs via CSF1R inhibition or CD40 agonism can alter the cytokine milieu, potentially affecting the tumor’s metabolic state and its susceptibility to metabolic inhibitors. Therefore, concurrently targeting tumor metabolism and immune compartments represents a highly synergistic strategy. Recent advances, such as nanoplatforms co-delivering chemotherapeutic and immunomodulatory agents and dual metabolic-immune blockade strategies, exemplify the practical implementation of this approach. Ultimately, preemptively targeting both axes is crucial to circumvent acquired resistance driven by compensatory shifts in the TME ecology.

Critically, the historical failure of metabolic monotherapies in PDAC trials should not be viewed as a biological dead end, but rather as a profound failure of patient selection. We propose that future clinical trial designs will likely require biomarker-guided stratification strategies and mandate metabolic or transcriptomic stratification to prevent the dilution of authentic efficacy signals.

Unresolved questions

While the reviews by Duttenhefner and Reindl1 and Liu et al.2 provide comprehensive overviews of their respective domains, certain limitations and broader knowledge gaps remain. First, regarding the limitations of the reviewed articles, Duttenhefner and Reindl1 comprehensively detail the preclinical rationale for targeting the mevalonate pathway; however, they leave the clinical paradox of statin therapy largely unresolved. Despite robust in vitro and murine data, statins have yielded conflicting or disappointing results in human PDAC trials, suggesting that compensatory lipid scavenging mechanisms or specific statin pharmacokinetics in the dense fibrotic stroma are not yet fully elucidated. Similarly, while Liu et al.2 thoroughly map the metabolic dependencies of TAMs, their review inevitably underrepresents the extreme spatiotemporal and ontogenetic heterogeneity of TAMs. For instance, tissue-resident macrophages versus monocyte-derived macrophages may exhibit fundamentally different metabolic requirements and responses to therapy, a nuance that complicates the simplified M1/M2 paradigm presented. Furthermore, the failure of TAM-depleting agents (e.g., CSF1R inhibitors) in human clinical trials indicates missing links in our understanding of TAM compensatory mechanisms that the review does not fully address.

Future directions: integrating precision and combinatorial therapies

Integrating these insights mandates a paradigm shift in PDAC therapeutic strategies, decisively moving beyond single-agent modalities toward rationally designed combinatorial regimens. Moving forward, the field must refocus on concrete targeted strategies and practical translational pathways.

Vertical and horizontal integration

To precipitate irreversible metabolic collapse within the malignant compartment, future interventions must employ robust vertical inhibition within core signaling cascades. For instance, overcoming compensatory upregulation requires advancing beyond statin monotherapy to dual blockade—such as combining sterol regulatory-element binding proteins (SREBP) inhibitors with statins, or pairing farnesyl/geranylgeranyl transferase inhibitors (farnesyltransferase inhibitors [FTIs]/geranylgeranyltransferase inhibitors [GGTIs]) with KRAS-targeted agents.1 A parallel vertical strategy must be applied to the stromal compartment to dismantle its defensive architecture while simultaneously intercepting TAM recruitment (e.g., via CCR2 antagonism),12 depleting resident populations (CSF1R inhibition), and neutralizing phagocytic checkpoints (anti-CD47).13

Beyond vertical blockade, the most compelling frontier lies in horizontal integration: concurrently targeting tumor metabolic dependencies and immune evasion mechanisms. The biological rationale for such cross-axis synergy is compelling. For example, utilizing bisphosphonates to disrupt both KRAS localization and TAM prenylation, while simultaneously blocking monocyte influx, presents a highly synergistic hypothesis. Disruption of the Siglec-10 checkpoint on macrophages enhances their phagocytic capacity and reverses local immune suppression.13 In parallel, interference with monocyte trafficking via CCR2 blockade effectively reprograms the immunosuppressive myeloid landscape within tumors.14 Similarly, lipid starvation via SREBP inhibition may proactively sensitize tumors to immune checkpoint blockade by restricting the local secretion of immunosuppressive metabolites. This is strongly supported by research showing that inhibition of the cholesterol synthesis regulator USP20 creates a favorable context for response to combined anti-PD-1 and anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4) therapy.11 The preliminary efficacy observed in combining statins with PD-1 blockade underscores the urgency of rigorously testing these cross-axis strategies in preclinical models.

Translational pathways for combined therapy

Ultimately, translating these highly complex regimens into the clinic necessitates stringent patient stratification driven by disease-specific precision medicine. Rather than relying on generic prognostic markers, future efforts must be strictly confined to biomarkers directly correlated with the metabolic-immune axis. First, the evaluation of TAM transcriptomic signatures (e.g., genes associated with lipid-scavenging or profound M2-polarization) will be crucial to identify patients who would benefit most from macrophage-reprogramming or depletion therapies. Second, the precise profiling of intermediate metabolites in the mevalonate pathway (such as farnesyl pyrophosphate and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate) within the TME or circulating plasma can reflect the real-time metabolic dependency of the tumor. Third, establishing predictive biomarkers for statin sensitivity—such as baseline HMG-CoA reductase (HMGCR) expression levels, specific circulating lipid profiles, or compensatory cholesterol-efflux markers—is urgently needed to overcome the highly variable clinical responses observed in historical statin trials. Integrating these specific metabolic and immune-phenotypic biomarkers will be essential to match patients with optimal combinatorial regimens. Furthermore, because these metabolic and inflammatory cascades are vital for systemic homeostasis, mitigating off-target toxicity is paramount. The development of advanced nanocarriers designed to selectively deliver reprogramming agents directly to the tumor niche will be critical for widening the therapeutic window. Promising examples include nanoparticles that co-deliver chemotherapeutic and immunomodulatory agents to simultaneously target tumor cells and the immune microenvironment, and sophisticated systems engineered to overcome stromal barriers and metabolic resistance.15 Even cellular therapies, such as engineered macrophages, are emerging as novel living delivery platforms to remodel the TME,10 thereby transitioning PDAC management from empirical protocols to precision, microenvironment-directed interventions.

Conclusion

In summary, the synthesis of these reviews underscores a critical paradigm in PDAC biology: the inextricable link between tumor-intrinsic metabolic reprogramming and stromal immune evasion. Rather than functioning in isolation, the mevalonate pathway and the TAM-dominated microenvironment form a reciprocal feedback loop—one that simultaneously sustains tumor viability and fortifies therapeutic resistance.

Consequently, dismantling this adaptive network necessitates a departure from unifocal treatments toward rationally designed, cross-axis combinatorial regimens. The conceptual frameworks presented in this special issue provide a mechanistically grounded rationale for concurrent intervention in both metabolic dependencies and immunosuppressive cascades. Ultimately, translating these foundational insights into precision clinical modalities represents the next essential frontier in overcoming the inherent recalcitrance of PDAC and meaningfully improving patient outcomes.
