Alternative Developmental Toxicity Screening Methods Using Chick Embryo‐Based Models
Authors: Jeong Hwan Kim, Cheoljin Park, Donghee Kim, Wonjun Jeong, Jinsoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNonclinical developmental toxicity studies are used to investigate the potential toxicities of drugs and chemical candidates on normal embryonic and fetal development during pregnancy, generally using mammalian laboratory animals such as rats and rabbits. Despite being well‐established, these models have ethical concerns, high costs, and long study durations. Consequently, alternative models, including in vitro human cell cultures, stem cell differentiation, 3D organoids, and zebrafish, are actively being studied; however, they have limitations in replicating whole‐body physiological complexity and homeostasis. Therefore, this study reviewed the use of chick embryo‐based models in developmental toxicity screening to overcome the limitations of other alternative models. Notably, chick embryos have been already utilized in cardiovascular development, cancer, and vaccine development. This study provides an overview of the advantages of chick embryo‐based models, including developmental relevance, cost‐effectiveness, and ethical alleviation, along with their limitations and future directions. Our review suggests that chick embryo‐based models can complement current mammalian laboratory animal‐based models and bridge the gap between in vitro and in vivo systems. However, continued research toward standardization of protocols, validation of methods, and technological integration is required to position chick embryo‐based models as a more practical and efficient model for nonclinical developmental toxicity studies.Keywords: 3Rs, fertilized egg, nonclinical developmental toxicity, relative replacement

Introduction

Nonclinical developmental toxicity studies investigate the potential toxicities of new drug and chemical candidates in normal development during pregnancy (DeSesso 2017). Laboratory animals such as rats and rabbits are most commonly used in such studies because of their comparable physiological complexity of development to humans (Piersma 2004b). Developmental toxicities are investigated by exposing new drug and chemical candidates during the major organogenesis period because major organs are formed at this critical stage (Adler et al. 2008; Martin and Weinbauer 2010). Particularly, developmental toxicities are determined by examining the normal development of fetuses, which are collected via caesarean section from pregnant animals on the day before parturition (Wise et al. 2009). These fetuses are then examined to investigate the developmental toxicity endpoints, including survival, morphological abnormalities, and growth alterations, during pregnancy (Hood 2012). The results collected during these procedures are used to determine developmental toxicities and establish the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) for human risk assessment (Hunt et al. 2008).

Various international regulatory test guidelines for nonclinical developmental toxicity studies, such as the International Council for Harmonization (ICH) for new drug candidates and the Organization for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) for new chemical candidates, have been established (Barrow 2009; Buschmann 2006). The ICH guidelines for developmental toxicities were first issued in 1993 following an agreement among regulatory agencies in the United States, Europe, and Japan. These guidelines provide comprehensive directions for developmental and reproductive toxicity studies, including specific recommendations on study design, selection of animal models, administration methods, timing, and dosage settings (Branch 2005). The ICH guidelines have played and continue to play a crucial role in improving the efficiency of the pharmaceutical industry through international harmonization and standardization of regulations related to new drug development (Barrow 2009). The OECD guidelines for developmental toxicities are standardized test methods developed by the OECD for new chemical candidates (OECD 2018). OECD member countries established standardized test guidelines to harmonize the evaluation methods for new chemical candidates, aiming to resolve inconsistencies and reliability issues caused by varying test procedures across various countries (Kitano 1992; Walker 1984). These guidelines facilitate the production of reliable and consistent data for protecting human health and the environment, contributing to the efficiency of chemical management worldwide (OECD 2018; Thomas 2006).

Despite the contribution of current regulatory test guidelines in providing reliable toxicity data to protect human health and the environment, these guidelines have limitations and require revision (Barrow 2016). For instance, the guidelines have a high reliance on laboratory animal testing; however, animal testing raises ethical concerns, incurs significant costs, and has long study durations (Doke and Dhawale 2015). This highlights the need for more efficient, affordable, and less ethically concerning alternative methods (Swaters et al. 2022). Additionally, the complexity and high cost of these guidelines can pose challenges for smaller research institutions or resource‐limited countries. Therefore, strategies to simplify developmental toxicity testing are needed (Meigs et al. 2018). Moreover, faster and more efficient methods for developmental toxicity screening are required to meet the growing demands of evaluating existing and new drug and chemical candidates (Krewski et al. 2011). This study presented the potential of chick embryo‐based models as practical alternatives for developmental toxicity testing. We summarize the current limitations of conventional animal models, recent advances in alternative approaches, the unique advantages and applications of chick embryos, and future directions for their standardization and validation.

Recent Alternative Methods for Nonclinical Developmental Toxicity Studies

Various alternative models to assess nonclinical developmental toxicity have been recently studied to overcome limitations of current mammalian laboratory animal‐based models (Fritsche et al. 2024; Ouedraogo et al. 2025; Yiğit et al. 2025). Among them, in vitro culture methods using human cells are recognized as one of the alternative methods for laboratory animal‐based models (Anadón et al. 2014; Engelhardt et al. 2025). These in vitro methods allow for analysis of toxic responses within specific human cells and provide insights into toxicity mechanisms at the cellular levels (Griesinger et al. 2016). Particularly, stem cells can be highly valuable in this context due to their ability to differentiate into various cell types (Tandon and Jyoti 2012). Furthermore, organoids as a 3D tissue model derived from differentiated stem cells can also mimic the physiological functions of organs (Park et al. 2024). However, these in vitro methods have inherent limitations, as their simplified cellular environments cannot fully replicate the homeostasis and complex systemic toxicity responses that arise from interorgan interactions in living organisms (Harding 2023; Park et al. 2024). Additionally, cellular‐level systems have limitations in accurately assessing toxicological effects resulting from prolonged exposure (Piersma 2006; Pleguezuelos‐Manzano et al. 2020).

In this regard, the zebrafish have gained significant attention as a promising lower phylogenetic laboratory animal to substitute for mammalian laboratory animals (Bauer et al. 2021; Song et al. 2025). The use of lower phylogenetic laboratory animals corresponds to the concept of relative replacement within the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement) framework (Council et al. 2010). This approach offers a means to mitigate the ethical concerns associated with animal experiments that predominantly rely on mammalian species (Strange 2016). Zebrafish have advantages such as a short developmental cycle and low cost for laboratory care over mammalian laboratory animals, enabling large‐scale experiments. However, zebrafish have more physiological differences with respect to humans compared to currently used mammalian species, which can lead to limitations in accurately predicting certain toxic responses (Sipes et al. 2011). For example, the structural and functional differences of the liver and kidneys in zebrafish can significantly impact the metabolic pathways and mechanisms of potential toxicological effects (Jerman and Sun 2017). These differences indicate the need for more careful consideration when extrapolating the zebrafish model results to humans when compared to currently used mammalian laboratory animals (He et al. 2014). Additionally, zebrafish enzymatic activities and metabolic rates may vary with incubation temperature, a concern that is not encountered in mammalian laboratory animals (Toni et al. 2023). Moreover, the extremely short developmental period of zebrafish embryos (approximately 2–3 days) can result in challenges in accurately assessing developmental toxicity (Lantz‐McPeak et al. 2015).

Additional lower phylogenetic models, including Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans) and frogs such as Xenopus species, have also been explored as complementary models for developmental toxicity assessment. Similar to zebrafish, these organisms provide practical advantages for early‐stage toxicity screening within the framework of relative replacement in the 3Rs concept. Xenopus embryos are relatively large, externally developing, and experimentally accessible, making them useful for studying embryogenesis and chemical‐induced developmental perturbations (Berg 2019; Hoke and Ankley 2005). C. elegans also provides several advantages, including a short life cycle, low maintenance cost, transparency, and well‐established genetic tools that facilitate mechanistic studies of toxicological responses (Boyd et al. 2012; Long et al. 2023). However, both models exhibit greater physiological divergence from mammals than other nonmammalian models, which may limit direct extrapolation to human developmental toxicity and restrict the evaluation of certain vertebrate‐specific developmental endpoints.

Although various alternative methods are currently under investigation, each method has its own advantages and disadvantages for substituting current mammalian laboratory animals (Table 1). Therefore, ongoing research aims to develop more accurate and reliable alternative methods to overcome the disadvantages of the abovementioned alternative methods. Consequently, these alternative methods are not yet regarded as comprehensive substitutes for mammalian laboratory animals; however, they may primarily be used as screening and complementary tools, as they are unlikely to fully replace animal experiments in the near future due to current technological and regulatory limitations. Accordingly, research efforts for alternative methods currently aim to minimize the current ethical issues and move toward more humane research methods (Kiani et al. 2022).

Chick Embryo‐Based Models: More Practical Alternative Methods to Complement Current Nonclinical Developmental Toxicity Studies

The chick embryo is one of the oldest and most extensively studied nonmammalian vertebrate models in experimental embryology, with documented scientific use dating back to the late 19th and early 20th centuries (Lillie 1919; Stern 2005). Classical embryologists employed the chick embryo to elucidate fundamental principles of external morphological development, including morphogenesis, organogenesis, and tissue differentiation. A major milestone in this historical progression was the establishment of the standardized developmental staging system by Hamburger and Hamilton, which provided a reproducible morphological framework that remains in use across developmental biology and toxicology (Hamburger and Hamilton 1951). Over decades, this model has enabled detailed investigations into neural tube formation, cardiovascular morphogenesis, and vascular development, generating a vast body of baseline developmental knowledge (Nagele et al. 1987; Wittig and Münsterberg 2016). Importantly, this long‐standing foundation in normal developmental biology has supported the extension of chick embryo applications into developmental toxicology.

Compared to other alternative methods, the use of chick embryos in nonclinical developmental toxicity studies can be more practical for screening and complementary purposes to support current standard methods. Current nonclinical developmental toxicity studies primarily use rodents or nonrodent species, which present ethical concerns, high costs, and long experimental periods. The use of chick embryos can overcome these issues, as they have reduced ethical concerns, lower costs, and shorter experimental periods compared to rodents or nonrodent species. Additionally, the developmental stages and structures of chick embryos are similar to those of human embryos, making them suitable for nonclinical developmental toxicity studies (Fraga et al. 2024). Moreover, the developmental stages of chick embryo and fetus are considerably well defined in previous studies (Hamburger and Hamilton 1951). Although further studies are needed to distinguish between normal morphology and birth defects, these existing studies can serve as valuable reference materials.

The reasonable developmental period of the chick embryo (approximately 21 days) is also advantageous for accurately evaluating developmental toxicity. This period overcomes the limitation of the excessively short embryonic period observed in other nonmammalian animal models (approximately 0.5–3 days), including zebrafish, C. elegans , and frogs. Another advantage of chick embryo‐based models is their ethical superiority over current mammalian laboratory animal‐based models. Chicken embryo‐based models allow access to developing fetuses without the need to sacrifice pregnant females, unlike current mammalian laboratory animal‐based models. In addition, the chicken is phylogenetically lower than current mammalian laboratory animals and is widely used as a food source.

Furthermore, the use of chick embryo‐based models reduces animal maintenance costs and allows for more efficient use of laboratory resources (Zosen et al. 2021). For example, unlike mammalian models that need extensive housing, chick embryos require only an incubator and minimal space, making them suitable even in resource‐limited settings (Vergara and Canto‐Soler 2012). In particular, chick embryos are readily available from commercial suppliers as fertilized chicken eggs, including under specific‐pathogen‐free conditions, which eliminates the need to maintain adult animals (Jungbäck and Motitschke 2008). These methods enable the screening of more compounds in a shorter time frame for large‐scale candidates screening purposes, allowing for more rapid decision‐making in research and industrial applications (Henshel et al. 2002; Vergara and Canto‐Soler 2012). These advantages make chick embryo‐based models more scientifically reliable and easier to handle compared to other alternative models, positioning them as a favorable alternative method for nonclinical developmental toxicity studies.

Utilization of Chick Embryo‐Based Models in Biomedical Research

Currently, chick embryos are used in various fields of biomedical research (Table 2), including neural and cardiovascular development research (Chen et al. 2022; Tahara et al. 2021). The results from studies using chick embryos can be translated to humans because their developmental processes closely mimic several key aspects of human organogenesis. In addition, chick embryos have proven valuable in cancer research, where they are used to study tumor growth and metastasis by introducing tumor cells into the vascular system (Javier and Butel 2008; Kain et al. 2014). Moreover, chick embryos are extensively employed in vaccine development and provide a cost‐effective platform for viral cultivation (Farzaneh et al. 2017; Javier and Butel 2008). Chick embryos are also valuable models in regenerative medicine research (Coleman 2008; Intarapat and Stern 2013; Rashidi and Sottile 2009). For instance, they have been used in stem cell studies to explore the mechanisms of tissue regeneration and repair (Intarapat and Stern 2013). Furthermore, chick embryos serve as models for studying gene function and expression during development, as gene editing techniques such as CRISPR can be directly applied to the chick embryos (Chojnacka‐Puchta and Sawicka 2020; Gandhi et al. 2017). These methods allow researchers to observe the effects of specific genetic modifications on organ development and disease pathways.

Several studies have also investigated toxicological effects on embryonic development using chick embryos (Table 3). For example, thiamethoxam exposure in chick embryos resulted in dose‐dependent growth retardation, with a distinct increase in the incidence of malformations, especially at higher doses (Zahoor et al. 2022). Similarly, embryos exposed to bisphenol analogs (bisphenol A, bisphenol S, bisphenol AF, and tetramethyl bisphenol F) showed a dose‐dependent increase in mortality, accompanied by specific malformations (Harnett et al. 2021). In addition, tris (2‐choloroisopropyl) phosphate negatively affected overall development, with the premature death of embryos, as well as delayed somatic and organ development and altered gene expression (Chigusa et al. 2023). Omega‐3 fatty acids resulted in low embryonic weight, microscopic lesions in tissues, and vascular defects (Salari et al. 2021). Bendiocarbamate resulted in sporadic malformations, slight weight loss, and growth retardation in chick embryos and its toxicity varied depending on the developmental stage and dose of exposure (Petrovova et al. 2009). Several other environmental toxins have also induced developmental toxicities in chick embryos, depending on the timing and dose of exposure (Ali et al. 2024; Buhr et al. 2021; Kishore et al. 2008).

These findings from chick embryo‐based studies show substantial similarities to developmental toxicity outcomes reported in mammalian models and also parallel developmental concerns described in human studies. For example, growth retardation and increased malformations observed following thiamethoxam exposure in chick embryos are in line with evidence that neonicotinoid insecticides can adversely affect mammalian developmental processes, particularly neurodevelopment (Cimino et al. 2016; Kimura‐Kuroda et al. 2012). Similarly, exposure to bisphenol analogs in chick embryos induced increased mortality and structural malformations, which are consistent with the developmental toxicity reported for bisphenol A in experimental mammals (Shelby 2008). In addition, cadmium exposure during chick embryogenesis has been associated with developmental abnormalities that parallel the embryotoxic and teratogenic effects described in mammalian studies (Thompson and Bannigan 2008). Epidemiological studies in humans have reported developmental effects associated with exposure to endocrine‐disrupting chemicals and toxic metals that resemble findings observed in chick embryo models (Cimino et al. 2016; Grandjean and Landrigan 2014).

In previous studies using chick embryo‐based models, the folllowing three main routes of administration were commonly used: air cell injection, yolk sac injection, and chorioallantoic membrane (CAM) injection (Figure 1 and Table 4). Air cell injection involves administration of test items to the air cells at the blunt end of the egg, which offers the advantage of gradual uptake that is well suited to assessing long‐term effects. This method is technically simple and minimizes the risk of mechanical eggshell damage; however, the slow absorption, reliance on material permeability, and potential impairment of gas exchange at high volume may limit its application (Mortola 2009; Peebles 2018). In contrast, yolk sac injections deliver test items directly to the yolk sac, which is the primary source of nutrition for embryos during early development. Although this method allows for efficient absorption and rapid systemic exposure, it may result in uneven diffusion. Additionally, a high injection volume may interfere with yolk metabolism and embryonic development (Acharya et al. 2024). Meanwhile, CAM injections are administered into the chorion, a vascularized extraembryonic structure. They allow for real‐time observation and rapid systemic delivery and are well suited for studies involving angiogenesis, inflammation, and tumorigenesis. However, CAM injections require partial removal of the eggshell, which increases the risk of infection and dehydration (Ribatti 2016).

Disadvantages and Limitations of Chick Embryo‐Based Developmental Toxicity Studies

Chick embryo‐based models have several advantages over other alternative models in nonclinical developmental toxicity studies and are already used in other research fields; however, they also have some disadvantages and limitations. For instance, chick embryo‐based developmental toxicity studies are based on avian embryos whose physiological characteristics do not fully align with those of mammals or humans. Although the majority of key mechanisms related to embryonic development are conserved across species, differences between avian and human systems still exist (Acharya et al. 2024). In addition, avian embryos develop in an external environment rather than within the uterus of the maternal body, unlike mammalian embryos. This difference in environmental conditions results in a lack of secondary effects caused by changes in maternal physiological status. Furthermore, chick embryos have limitations in terms of accurately assessing the toxicity of metabolites derived from the metabolism of maternal digestive systems (Zwadlo‐Klarwasser et al. 2001).

Technical limitations also present several challenges. Although chick embryo‐based models are relatively simple and involve straightforward administration routes, achieving precise dosing and control can be challenging. For example, when test items are administered to the air cell, yolk sac, or CAM, their distribution via each route varies, leading to variations in exposure (Ribatti 2017). Therefore, further research is required to identify the administration routes in chick embryos that correspond to the exposure and absorption scenarios of test items in humans. In addition, the anatomical structure and tissue composition of avian embryos differ from those of mammals, making detailed anatomical assessments more challenging and potentially limiting the extrapolation of findings to humans (Barnhill and Ryan 1983; Vargas et al. 2007).

Future Directions of Chick Embryos‐Based Developmental Toxicity Studies

Chick embryo‐based models must overcome several challenges to be considered a promising alternative and efficient screening method for developmental toxicity. For instance, standardization of experimental procedures is one of the essential challenges to be addressed. Rats, which are the most commonly used laboratory animals in developmental toxicity studies, have the same gestation period as the incubation period of chick embryos (approximately 21 days). Therefore, based on the established study designs and endpoints specified in the ICH and OECD test guidelines using rats, corresponding administration timing, methods, and evaluation endpoints must be developed for chick embryo‐based developmental toxicity studies.

A standardized experimental framework for chick embryo–based developmental toxicity studies could be developed by drawing on methodological approaches previously used to validate alternative embryotoxicity assays, such as whole embryo culture (WEC) (Piersma 2004a; Piersma et al. 2004). In WEC validation studies, toxicity assessment typically includes an initial range‐finding experiment followed by a main study evaluating multiple concentrations to characterize dose–response relationships. Range‐finding experiments generally use approximately three embryos per concentration with tenfold concentration intervals, whereas the main study evaluates about four dose levels with approximately seven embryos per group to assess developmental endpoints. Similar principles could be applied to chick embryo assays to enable systematic dose–response evaluation. In addition, accumulating results from chick embryo–based studies using well‐defined developmental toxicity positive controls, and comparing these findings with existing developmental toxicity data, may support the validation of this model and help define relevant endpoints. Furthermore, exposure timing should be aligned with defined developmental stages of the chick embryo. Previous studies indicate that chick developmental toxicity assessments can be conducted throughout the embryonic period from embryonic day (ED) 0 to ED18, with particular emphasis on the organogenesis stage occurring approximately between ED1 and ED7, when embryos are especially susceptible to teratogenic effects (Acharya et al. 2024). To ensure the robustness and regulatory relevance of such a framework, further validation studies will be required, including interlaboratory comparisons and evaluations using well‐established positive and negative controls to assess the sensitivity, specificity, and potential limitations of chick embryo–based developmental toxicity models.

Beyond standardization and validation efforts, recent advances in genomic technologies provide new opportunities to expand the use of chick embryo models in developmental toxicity studies (Kanda and Iwata 2024; Veeriah et al. 2017). The availability of a fully sequenced and annotated chicken genome, together with rapid progress in genomic sequencing and molecular profiling technologies, has greatly increased the biological resources available for avian models and enabled more precise investigation of developmental pathways (Consortium 2004; Mantri et al. 2021; Warren et al. 2017). In addition, gene editing approaches such as CRISPR/Cas systems have become increasingly applicable in chick embryos, allowing targeted manipulation of genes involved in embryonic development (Chojnacka‐Puchta and Sawicka 2020; Gandhi et al. 2017). These technological advances provide opportunities to investigate the mechanistic basis of developmental toxicity, including the identification of gene–environment interactions and toxicity pathways, thereby potentially improving the predictive value of chick embryo–based assays for future developmental hazard assessment. Furthermore, automated techniques incorporating micro‐dosing technology may enhance the efficiency and throughput of developmental toxicity screening, an approach already utilized in the poultry industry (Ricks et al. 1999). Collectively, the integration of these emerging technologies is expected to improve both the mechanistic insight and screening capacity of chick embryo–based developmental toxicity models.

Conclusions

This study presented an overview of the current status of nonclinical developmental toxicity studies, ethical issues, alternative methods under development, the advantages and limitations of using chick embryos, and future directions of chick embryo‐based developmental toxicity studies. Currently, nonanimal based models such as human cell lines, stem cells, and organoid methods have ethical advantages over animal models in nonclinical developmental toxicity studies; however, these methods face significant challenges in practically replicating the complex homeostasis of living organisms. Although chick embryo‐based developmental toxicity studies may not entirely replace the current mammalian laboratory animal‐based models, they represent the most practical and realistic method as screening tools, which also address ethical concerns. Further research addressing the challenges associated with chick embryo‐based developmental toxicity studies, standardization of experimental procedures, validation of test designs, and incorporation of new technical methodologies could enable chick embryos to be an effective alternative model to existing mammalian models in nonclinical developmental toxicity studies.
