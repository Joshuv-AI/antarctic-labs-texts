Adaptive Merit Framework: Merit-Anchored Fairness via SES Correction
Jung-Ah Lee

Abstract




Abstract

College admissions systems worldwide continue to face a structural tension between meritocracy and equity. Conventional fairness interventions—affirmative action, categorical quotas, and proxy-based targeting—often rely on coarse indicators (e.g., race or region), operate within fixed quotas that induce zero-sum trade-offs, and lack transparent decision rules. This paper introduces the Adaptive Merit Framework (AMF),
a policy-engineered mechanism that recognizes latent potential while preserving merit-based thresholds. AMF integrates three components: (1) a merit-anchored architecture in which conditional admits must exceed the same threshold as regular admits, (2) a dynamic threshold anchored to the raw score of the last regular admit, and (3) direct, continuous SES measurement verified through administrative data.

Empirical validation using the full PISA 2022 Korea dataset (N=6,377)
shows that AMF identifies 4, 6, and 9 additional admits under
\alpha
= 5,
10, and 15 respectively (0.06-0.14% of cohort). Population-weighted
estimates using OECD sampling weights suggest that the real-world scale
of conditional admits is modestly larger than the raw sample counts,
yielding approximately 491, 603, and 760 additional admits under

\alpha
= 5, 10, and 15. All conditional admits exceed the merit threshold
by 0.16 to 6.14 points, indicating that AMF recognizes suppressed
performance rather than relaxing standards.

Beyond SES-based corrections, AMF provides a design template for unified admissions architectures that replace fragmented equity tracks and support multi-dimensional evaluation frameworks. The correction rule
M_{i}^{*}=M_{i}+\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})
is compatible with any composite metric
M_{i}
, enabling gradual transitions from single-metric evaluations to more diversified, transparent assessment systems.

Keywords:
fairness in admissions, merit-based selection, socioeconomic equity, SES correction, mechanism design, algorithmic fairness, threshold-based selection, policy design

Introduction

1.1
From Inequality of Opportunity to Talent Recognition

Social mobility–the capacity for individuals to improve their socioeconomic position beyond that of their parents–is a foundational element of democratic legitimacy. Yet recent evidence indicates that mobility in Korea has become increasingly rigid. According to KRIVET
[
15
]
, intergenerational mobility has declined steadily over the past fifteen years. This trend aligns with Korea’s high intergenerational income elasticity (IGE = 0.60)
[
4
]
, which reflects the strong persistence of inequality across generations.

What makes this rigidity particularly striking is that it emerges despite Korea’s well-documented educational strength. Korea is among the top performers in the OECD’s
PISA 2022 Creative Thinking
assessment, and its compulsory education system shows high academic resilience: socioeconomic status (SES) explains only 6.4% of the variance in creative thinking scores, compared with the OECD average of 11.6%.

Analysis of the raw PISA 2022 dataset further reveals that although the ESCS gap between the 75th and 25th percentiles (2.09) is slightly smaller than the OECD average, the absolute performance gap remains substantial–over 100 points (481.6 vs. 584.1). Despite this disparity, a notable minority of low-SES students excel: 13.4% score at or above the top-quartile mean, and 9.2% reach Level 5+ proficiency. These students represent a population of “hidden excellence”–individuals who achieve high performance despite structural disadvantage.

However, this demonstrated
academic development does not translate into upward mobility
. KRIVET
[
15
]
reports a 22% decline in upward mobility (from 13.4% to 10.5%), with mathematics-based mobility now among the lowest in comparable economies. In effect, Korea has built an education system that successfully develops cognitive potential but does not consistently convert that potential into opportunity.
A strength in talent development becomes a weakness in talent recognition.

Although social mobility is influenced by broader economic structures, education remains the most immediate institutional lever for improving opportunity. The challenge today is therefore not whether fairness interventions are necessary, but
how they can be designed to correct structural bias while preserving meritocratic standards
.

1.2
The Fairness Paradox in College Admissions

College admissions systems around the world reveal a persistent tension between
meritocracy
and
equity
. Standardized test scores are often viewed as objective indicators of merit, yet they systematically reflect socioeconomic advantage. Conversely, policies designed to correct these disparities, such as affirmative action–face accusations of reverse discrimination and a lack of transparency, undermining public trust.

This tension reflects a deeper conceptual issue:
the purpose, or
telos
, of the admissions process.
As Sandel argues
[
23
]
, following Aristotle, judgments about fairness cannot be separated from the purpose of the institute. If admissions are designed to reward demonstrated performance, raw test scores are sufficient. But if the goal is to recognize latent potential–the ability to achieve under conditions of equal opportunity–then socioeconomic context must be incorporated. Modern admissions systems attempt to satisfy both aims simultaneously without clearly articulating their priorities, resulting in persistent controversy. MacIntyre described this as a dilemma
[
17
]
of modern institutions that pursue incompatible purposes without acknowledging the underlying trade-offs.

Recent events in Korea illustrate this dynamic. In 2025, Korea University’s multicultural admissions track sparked nationwide protests due to unclear selection criteria, polarizing public debate between advocates of inclusion and proponents of procedural fairness. Similar criticisms have been raised about the
regional balance
track used by Seoul National University and other institutions; despite its stated goal of promoting regional diversity, the proportion of admits from Seoul metropolitan area high schools increased from 51.6% (2020) to 61.5% (2024)
[
8
]
.

Comparable patterns appear internationally. In the United States, six decades of race-based affirmative action culminated in the Supreme Court’s 2023 decision (
Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard
), which ruled such practices unconstitutional. Chief Justice Roberts emphasized that race was an imprecise proxy for disadvantage, sometimes benefiting privileged minority students while excluding disadvantaged students of other races
[
25
]
. The College Board’s short-lived
Adversity Score (2019)
encountered similar criticism: its opaque calculation methods and reliance on neighborhood-level proxies limited its legitimacy
[
11
]
.

Together, these cases reveal three recurring structural challenges in existing fairness models.

•

Proxy dependence
: Group-based characteristics (such as race or region) are used as indirect measures of disadvantage, leading to inaccurate or politically vulnerable outcomes.

•

Zero-sum structure
: Providing additional opportunities to one group is often perceived as reducing opportunities for others.

•

Opacity
: Hidden or static thresholds weaken procedural legitimacy and public understanding.

The case of Seoul National University shows that even well-intentioned programs can misalign incentives: regional-balance admits achieved higher average graduation GPAs than regular admits (3.67 vs. 3.61)
[
8
]
, suggesting that
the issue lies not in the idea of fairness interventions but in their design
. If admissions explicitly adopt a potential-oriented purpose, the central question becomes
how to construct mechanisms that recognize potential without undermining merit
.

1.3
Beyond Zero-Sum: An Adaptive Merit Framework (AMF)

This paper introduces
Adaptive Merit Framework (AMF)
, a policy-engineered admissions mechanism designed to recognize latent potential while preserving merit-based standards. Rather than treating demonstrated ability and structural disadvantage as competing priorities, AMF integrates both through a transparent correction model anchored to a dynamic merit threshold. In doing so, it seeks to address three persistent limitations of existing fairness systems: proxy dependence, zero-sum redistribution, and procedural opacity.

1.3.1
Non-Displacement Structure

Traditional affirmative action redistributes opportunities within a fixed quota, creating the perception that gains for one group require losses for another. This zero-sum architecture is a central source of political resistance.

AMF removes this structural constraint through threshold-based selection:

•

Regular quota(N)
: Selected solely by raw merit.

•

Additional seats(+
\alpha
)
: Offered to applicants whose corrected scores exceed the raw score of the last regular admit.

This design ensures that no regular admit is displaced while expanding opportunities for underrepresented applicants. In effect, fairness becomes
additive rather than substitutive
, reframing equity as expansion rather than replacement.

1.3.2
Dynamic Threshold

Conventional admissions often rely on static cutoff scores that fail to respond to varying levels of competition and are perceived as arbitrary. AMF replaces static criteria with a
dynamic threshold
tied directly to merit:

threshold_{t}=raw\,score\,of\,the\,Nth\,admitted\,student

(1)

additional\,admit\Leftrightarrow corrected\,score>threshold_{t}

(2)

This mechanism provides three advantages:

•

Adaptation
: The threshold automatically adjusts to the applicant pool.

•

Transparency
: “As qualified as the last regular admit” is intuitively easier to justify.

•

Merit anchoring
: All additional admits meet a standard directly linked to observed performance.

By grounding fairness in real-time merit data, the dynamic threshold enhances both legitimacy and flexibility.

1.3.3
Direct Measurement, Not Proxies

Unlike group-based models that rely on race or region as indirect indicators of disadvantage, AMF uses
individual-level socioeconomic variables
drawn from verifiable administrative sources:

SES\,Index=f(family\,income,\,region,\,school\,quality,\,parental\,education)

The corrected score used in all subsequent sections follows the unified rule:

R_{i}^{*}=R_{i}+C_{i},\quad C_{i}=\alpha(\mu-S_{i}).

In percentile-normalized implementations (as in this study),

\mu=0.5
, yielding
C_{i}=\alpha(0.5-S_{i})
.

Lower-SES applicants receive modest, empirically calibrated adjustments consistent with observed effect sizes in large-scale educational datasets (e.g., PISA’s 47-point ESCS gap per SD). This approach avoids the pitfalls of proxy-based systems and provides a transparent, auditable basis for fairness.

AMF as a Mechanism, Not a Judgement

Taken together, these three components–non-displacement selection, dynamic thresholds, and direct SES-based correction–shift fairness from a moral debate to a
design problem
. AMF does not replace meritocracy; it seeks to refine its measurement by recognizing potential developed under structural disadvantage. By embedding fairness within adaptive, rule-based procedures, the framework aims to improve both equity and procedural legitimacy.

Theoretical Background: Why Existing Systems Fail

Fairness frameworks across policy, technology, and economics share a common limitation: they typically address inequality normatively or retrospectively rather than as a
design problem
that must operate within real institutional constraints. This section synthesizes insights from four areas–affirmative action policy, algorithmic fairness, education economics, and policy implementation–to clarify why existing systems fall short and to motivate the structural logic behind AMF.

2.1
Affirmative Action and the Proxy Problem

Affirmative action was originally conceived as a corrective mechanism for historical disadvantages through group-based preferences
[
24
]
. In the United States,
Regents of the University of California v. Bakke
held that diversity is a legitimate educational goal but ruled that racial quotas violated the Equal Protection Clause
[
21
]
. Subsequent cases–
Gratz v.Bollinger
and
Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard
–reaffirmed that fairness must rely on individualized evaluation rather than mechanical group bonuses
[
7
,
25
]
. These rulings highlight an enduring structural issue: when disadvantage is inferred through indirect group proxies such as race or geography, fairness mechanisms become politically unstable and legally vulnerable.

Korea’s experience mirrors this pattern. Programs such as “regional balance” and “multicultural admissions” have relied on group-based proxies, yet critics note that such categories do not reliably identify genuinely disadvantaged individuals. Data show that the share of metropolitan-area high school students in regional balance tracks has increased over time, illustrating that proxy-based systems can generate
symbolic inclusion rather than structural correction
[
8
]
.

Implication for AMF.

Two design conditions follow from these experiences:

(1)
direct measurement of disadvantage
through verifiable socioeconomic variables (income, parental education, school quality), and (2)
transparent linkage to merit-based criteria
. AMF incorporates both by replacing group proxies with a direct SES-based correction model grounded in auditable administrative data.

2.2
Algorithmic Fairness and the Limits of Statistical Definitions

Research in algorithmic fairness has proposed numerous statistical definitions–
demographic parity, equalized odds, individual fairness
[
9
,
6
]
. These frameworks are valuable descriptively but offer limited prescriptive guidance for how fair processes should be implemented
ex ante
.

Moreover, many fairness definitions are mathematically incompatible under realistic conditions
[
14
]
, meaning that no single statistical definition can fully resolve fairness trade-offs.

Attempts to apply machine-learning fairness frameworks to educational policy have faced similar challenges. Most assume closed datasets and well-defined protected attributes, whereas admissions involve institutional incentives, human discretion, and heterogeneous evaluation criteria
[
13
]
. The College Board’s short-lived “Adversity Score”(2019) demonstrated these limitations: its opaque formulas and proxy-based inputs drew criticism for lacking transparency and legitimacy
[
19
]
.

Algorithmic fairness frameworks such as equalized odds
[
9
]
and individual fairness
[
6
]
typically operate as
ex post
constraints: they adjust classifier outputs or impose statistical parity after predictions are made. In contrast, AMF functions as an
ex ante
mechanism design: it specifies how admissions decisions should be structured from the outset–through SES-based corrections, dynamic thresholds, and transparent rules–rather than retrofitting fairness onto existing systems. This distinction clarifies AMF’s theoretical position within the broader fairness literature: rather than imposing constraints on existing systems, AMF embeds fairness directly into procedural design while maintaining merit-based legitimacy.

Implication for AMF:

AMF departs from statistical parity models by embedding fairness into a
procedural algorithm
: SES-based corrections verified through administrative data and a merit-anchored dynamic threshold. Rather than optimizing an outcome, AMF defines
how
decisions are made, linking algorithmic transparency with institutional accountability.

. In Korea, PISA 2022 data show an SES-achievement correlation of

Korea presents a dual paradox:

Implication for AMF.

These patterns justify SES-based corrections that are continuous, empirically grounded, and modest in scale. By calibrating adjustments using large-scale educational data (e.g., PISA’s 47-point ESCS gap per SD), AMF aligns fairness with the statistical structure of inequality while avoiding deterministic or categorical assumptions.

Fairness mechanisms operate within strategic, incentive-driven institutional environments. Universities, administrators, and applicants adapt to policy rules, often producing unintended consequences. Traditional affirmative action frameworks seldom account for these dynamics.

Implementation Flexibility

Policy environments vary in fiscal and legal constraints. AMF supports both:

Methodology : An Adaptive Merit Framework

This section presents the Adaptive Merit Framework (AMF), a policy-engineered admissions mechanism designed to recognize latent potential while preserving merit-based standards.
Figure
1
provides a schematic overview of the four-step process: input measurement, correction calculation, adjusted score computation, and dual-track selection. In this study, we approximate such an administrative SES infrastructure using PISA’s ESCS index, which combines parental education, occupation, and household resources.

3.1
Correction Rule

The Adaptive Merit Framework(AMF) adjusts each applicant’s raw performance score using a linear SES-based correction.

The general specification is:

C_{i}=\alpha\cdot(\mu-S_{i}),

where,
S_{i}\in[0,1]
is the normalized SES index and
\mu
denotes the population mean of
S_{i}
.

This formulation preserves generality: when SES is measured on an arbitrary of non-percentile scale,
\mu
reflects the empirical center of the distribution and determines which portion of applicants are eligible for correction.

In this study, SES is percentile-normalized, yielding a uniform distribution on [0,1].

Consequently, its population mean satisfies:

\mu=\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]=0.5

Substituting this value into the general formulation yields the empirical correction rule used in the PISA-based analysis:

C_{i}=\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})

Thus, applicants with
S_{i}<0.5
receive a positive correction, while those at or above the empirical median receive none. This property is not a design choice but an immediate consequence of percentile normalization, which ensures that AMF focuses exclusively on structurally disadvantaged applicants.

Corrected performance is computed as:

R_{i}^{*}=R_{i}+C_{i}

and applicants qualify as additional admits when:

R_{i}^{*}\geq T

where,
T
is the raw-score threshold corresponding to the top-
k
positions. Because thresholds are determined solely from raw scores, AMF expands opportunity
without displacing regular admits.

3.2
SES Measurement and Normalization

SES is measured using PISA’s ESCS composite index.

To prevent distortion of the percentile transformation, extreme outliers are removed using a
1.5\times IQR
rule (14 cases removed out of 6,377).

Let
ESCS_{i}
denote the cleaned socioeconomic index.

The SES index used for correction is defined as:

S_{i}=Percentile\,Rank(ESCS_{i}).

This transformation yields an interpretable and scale-free SES measure, preserves relative disadvantage, and ensures that the empirical mean closely matches the uniform-distribution value of 0.5.

As a result, quartile-based analyses and cross-cohort comparisons remain stable and transparent.

Critical Note on Population-Level Normalization

In the PISA 2022 Korea dataset, the applicant pool (N=6,377) represents a nationally representative sample of 15-year-olds, ensuring that SES percentiles reflect population-level rather than self-selected distributions. In institutional implementations, SES percentiles should be calculated relative to the national population to prevent strategic manipulation and distributional bias. If percentiles were computed within a self-selected applicant pool (e.g., affluent families overrepresented), the empirical mean
\mu
would shift above 0.5, systematically under-correcting for genuine disadvantage. Anchoring percentiles to national distributions preserves the intended targeting of the correction function.

3.3
Threshold Construction

For each cohort, the raw-score threshold
T
is defined as the score of the
k
-th highest applicant (top 10% in this study). AMF does
not
modify the threshold; it evaluates whether an applicant’s corrected performance would have reached the same benchmark under equalized conditions.

3.4
Empirical Calibration of
\alpha

To ensure that AMF remains conservative and empirically grounded, the calibration of
alpha
is based on the SES-achievement gradient observed in the PISA 2022 Korea dataset.

A regression of mathematics scores on ESCS yields:

\beta=47.29\,\,\,\,(p<0.001),

indicating that a one-standard-deviation increase in ESCS corresponds to roughly:

1\,\,SD\,\,ESCS\approx 38.90points.

This gradient approximates the environmental effect of SES on performance.

Accordingly, we choose values of
\alpha
that represent modest fractions of this gradient:

•

\alpha=5\rightarrow
max correction
\approx 6\%
of the SES-achievement effect

•

\alpha=10\rightarrow\approx 13\%

•

\alpha=15\rightarrow\approx 19\%

These empirical fractions ensure that corrections remain proportionate and do not overstate structural disadvantage. In the PISA 2022 Korea sample, these values yield mean correction magnitudes among conditional admits of approximately 1.5 to 4.8 points (maximum: 2.5 to 7.5), confirming that AMF functions as a conservative adjustment mechanism–large enough to recover suppressed potential, yet far too small to shift the distribution wholesale or elevate weak performers above competitive thresholds.

2.

Obtain

3.

Select additional admits satisfying

4.

Analyze SES quartile distribution, threshold gap, and correction magnitude.

5.

Evaluate robustness: SES-noise perturbation, score-variance perturbation, and threshold-shift sensitivity.

5
Discussion

This study provides evidence that fairness can be embedded into admissions as a design property rather than redistributive trade-off. The simulation results using the full PISA 2022 Korea dataset reinforce the mechanism’s central premise: fairness can be expanded without displacing regular admits or relaxing merit-based thresholds.

This section interprets the implications of AMF’s structural features and empirical outcomes within broader policy and institutional contexts.

5.1
Equity Without Displacement

A central finding is that AMF increases opportunities for disadvantaged applicants while preserving the positions of regular admits. Because conditional admits must exceed a merit-based threshold, the mechanism avoids the main sources of resistance commonly seen in quota-based or proxy-based affirmative action, where fairness gains are often perceived as zero-sum.

Empirical validation using PISA data shows that AMF identifies
4, 6, and 9
students under
\alpha=5,10,15
, respectively—all of whom exceed the dynamic threshold determined by the last regular admit. The fact that all conditional admits surpass the benchmark by
0.16 to 6.14 points
(depending on
\alpha
) indicates that AMF does not lower standards but rather recognizes applicants whose performance is likely understated due to structural constraints.

5.2
Merit Anchoring and Academic Standards

A frequent concern in fairness interventions is whether adjustments dilute academic criteria. AMF avoids this issue by retaining an absolute merit anchor tied to the raw score of the marginal admit. Conditional admits exceed this benchmark even after correction, indicating that the mechanism identifies candidates whose observed performance reflects structural constraints rather than an absence of ability.

5.2.1
Why the Number of Additional Admits Remains Small

The empirical results show that AMF produces
only a modest number of additional admits
, even under progressive
\alpha
values. This reflects an inherent mathematical property of the threshold-anchored design: an applicant must simultaneously satisfy two conditions—(1) a raw score sufficiently close to the selective threshold, and (2) socioeconomic disadvantage large enough to generate meaningful correction. In high-competition environments, these conditions rarely coincide.

Using PISA 2022 Korea data, we find that all additional admits originate from the bottom 50% of the SES distribution, with 50-78% from Q1 (lowest quartile) depending on
\alpha
. Notably, no students from Q3 or Q4 ever qualify, even under
\alpha=15
. This pattern arises directly from the correction function

C_{i}=\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i}),

which ensures that only students below the median SES receive positive adjustments, effectively implementing an automatic filter that prevents high-SES applicants from gaining advantage.

This empirical scarcity echoes Chetty et al.’s findings on
“Lost Einsteins”
: high-potential low-SES students are rare but systematically hidden within competitive environments. AMF’s ability to isolate this small but meaningful subset underscores its role as a precision mechanism rather than a redistributive expansion.

A population-weighted analysis indicates that the aggregate scale of conditional admits under AMF is slightly larger than suggested by the unweighted sample. While this does not alter the qualitative behavior of the mechanism, it clarifies the practical magnitude of AMF when applied to population-level cohorts.

5.3
Institutional Transparency and Accountability

AMF’s operational logic can be fully disclosed:

•

SES variables rely on verifiable administrative records,

•

corrections follow a deterministic formula,

•

thresholds reflect observed cohort performance, and

•

audit trails can be generated automatically

This transparency aligns with principles of procedural justice and may help institutions explain admissions decisions more clearly. Unlike opaque indices such as the College Board’s short-lived “Adversity Score,” AMF specifies its inputs, rules, and thresholds in advance.

5.4
Policy Flexibility Across Systems

The mechanism is adaptable to varying institutional and legal constraints.

•

Budget-neutral contexts
may embed AMF within existing diversity or equity programs without altering regular seat allocation.

•

Expansion-oriented systems
can allocate new conditional seats based on corrected scores, allowing fairness policies to scale with public investment.

•

Test-optional or future multi-metric expansions
can incorporate AMF by applying corrections to composite metrics rather than test scores alone.

Because AMF adjusts automatically to cohort competitiveness, it can operate in diverse environments ranging from highly competitive Korean universities to U.S. institutions navigating post-
Students for Fair Admissions
legal constraints.

5.5
Ethical Considerations

A common critique of fairness interventions is that they assume disadvantaged students cannot compete without help-a form of condescension that reinforces deficit narratives. AMF does not claim that low-SES students lack ability; rather, it recognizes that they lack opportunities. This distinction is crucial. Consider two runners: one completes 100 meters in 11 seconds without weights, the other in 12 seconds while carrying a 5-kilogram load. Who is the better runner? Standard admissions systems select the first runner based on observed time. AMF’s correction represents counterfactual reasoning: it estimates what the second runner’s time would have been under equal conditions.

This claim is empirically testable. If AMF admits perform as well as or better than regular admits in college outcomes-retention rates, GPA, graduation-the correction is validated. If they underperform systematically, the correction parameter
\alpha
should be recalibrated downward. AMF is designed to be evidence-driven rather than ideological:
\alpha
is an adjustable policy lever informed by longitudinal outcome data, not a fixed commitment to a particular distributional goal.

A related question concerns institutional versus systemic responsibility: should universities correct for societal inequality, or should society address root causes first? AMF treats institutional and systemic reforms as complementary rather than mutually exclusive. Long-term systemic reforms–improving K-12 equity, reducing income inequality, expanding social safety nets–remain essential. However, these changes require decades to materialize. In the interim, universities can act within their institutional sphere to avoid losing talent while broader structural reforms unfold. AMF is designed as a short-term intervention whose necessity should diminish over time. Success is achieved not when AMF expands indefinitely, but when socioeconomic effects on academic performance become negligible and corrections approach zero. The ideal endpoint is a system where AMF is no longer needed because structural constraints no longer suppress performance.

5.7
Limitations and Future Work

Several limitations warrant further investigation.

First, SES measurement remains imperfect despite administrative verification; noise in socioeconomic variables could affect adjustments.

Second, empirical validation with real admissions datasets remains necessary; PISA-based simulations provide a rigorous testbed but not institutional behavior. Moreover, PISA samples the full 15-year-old cohort, whereas university applicants represent a self-selected subpopulation with different SES-achievement distributions.

Third, long-run analyses using dynamic Bayesian or panel-based models may offer insights into how small shifts in early opportunity influence broader patterns of social mobility.

Fourth, this study uses mathematics scores as the primary achievement metric. While mathematics represents a domain-general measure of cognitive ability with well-documented SES gradients, future work should validate AMF’s robustness across multiple subjects (reading, science) and composite metrics to ensure generalizability across diverse admissions contexts.

5.8
Summary

AMF’s contribution lies in demonstrating that fairness can be implemented procedurally—through transparent rules and dynamic thresholds—without reducing opportunities for high-performing applicants. This design-centered approach offers a practical pathway for institutions seeking to combine equity with legitimacy in competitive admissions.

Having established AMF’s empirical properties and structural limitations, it is equally important to situate the mechanism within the broader institutional and policy environment in which real admissions reforms unfold.

5.9
System-Level Considerations

Despite its conceptual simplicity, the Adaptive Merit Framework interacts with structural constraints that shape the feasibility, fairness, and scalability of any admissions reform. This subsection summarizes system-level considerations that clarify the practical boundaries of AMF and delineate avenues for responsible implementation. These points do not prescribe specific policies but provide a conceptual map for policymakers and institutions considering AMF-like mechanisms.

5.9.1
Resource and Capacity Constraints

AMF is designed to be non-displacement-based, yet any expansion of the admitted cohort is ultimately bounded by institutional capacity. Class-size literature suggests that marginal enrollment increases can influence peer environments, instructional load, and advising capacity. While AMF allows institutions to treat additional admits as a “micro-cohort,” facilities, faculty time, and per-student support costs create limits to unconstrained seat expansion. AMF can operate within two modes:

•

Hybrid Mode (Budget-Neutral)
: Additional admits are absorbed into existing equity or discretionary tracks, effectively reallocating a small portion of institutional flexibility. This mode has partial zero-sum characteristics due to finite support resources.

•

Expansion Mode
: Institutions create a small number of new seats for AMF-eligible students. This mode avoids displacement but requires per-seat marginal cost considerations (instruction, student services, advising), which vary widely across national contexts.

These constraints do not invalidate AMF but highlight that its non-displacement logic operates within institutional capacity limits rather than implying unlimited expansion.

5.9.2
Cost Structure of Additional Seats

Even in systems with strong public funding, marginal enrollment incurs costs. International data suggest that per-student instructional costs range significantly across systems, influenced by discipline mix, labor share, and support services. AMF’s design reduces administrative costs because it only requires a percentile-normalized SES measure and a fixed threshold. However:

•

Expansion-mode AMF requires per-seat budgeting

•

Budget-neutral implementation requires careful evaluation of student support load

•

Institutions must avoid tokenistic expansion that strains instructional quality

Future empirical work could explore cost-effectiveness relative to alternatives such as need-based aid, targeted retention programs, or K–12 pipeline interventions.

5.9.3
Institutional Stakeholders and Governance Dynamics

Universities are multi-actor systems. AMF’s adoption is influenced by the interaction of:

•

Admissions offices
: prioritizing procedural transparency and defensibility

•

Faculty governance bodies
: concerned with academic rigor and instructional load

•

Institutional research units
: evaluating data infrastructure and compliance risk

•

Finance and planning divisions
: assessing marginal seat costs

•

Students and student groups
: advocating for fairness, diversity, or status protection

Implementation is most feasible when AMF is framed not as a new “track” but as a calibration layer embedded into existing holistic processes. Consultation across units is essential to avoid perceptions of externally imposed reform.

5.9.4
National Data Infrastructure Differences

The feasibility of the SES percentile correction depends on data availability:

•

Korea
: Administrative SES measures (tax records, household income, welfare status) can be linked through government databases, enabling accurate percentile assignment.

•

United States
: FAFSA/EFC, Pell eligibility, and school-level SES proxies substitute for integrated administrative data. Lack of a unified SES database necessitates pragmatic, institution-specific approximations.

•

EU contexts
: National registries exist in some member states but vary substantially in accessibility and governance.

This variation does not preclude AMF; it indicates that the framework must be data-agnostic, relying on the best available proxy while acknowledging its limitations.

5.9.5
Anticipated Political and Public Responses

Admissions policies carry symbolic stakes far beyond their numerical impact. AMF’s emphasis on SES may attract both support and resistance:

•

Possible equity backlash, especially where “meritocracy” is framed narrowly

•

Perceptions of “affirmative action by another name” in polarized environments

•

Concerns over SES being a proxy for race in post-
SFFA
contexts

•

Media framing that can amplify or distort institutional intent

These risks suggest the need for careful communication emphasizing AMF’s transparency, minimal displacement logic, and restricted policy scope.

5.9.6
Intersectional Dimensions of Disadvantage

While AMF targets SES-related barriers in achievement, educational disadvantage often arises at intersections of class, race, geography, disability, or immigration background. The PISA-based simulation cannot fully reflect these interaction effects due to data limitations. However, acknowledging intersectional inequities is central for future applications:

•

U.S. contexts require race
\times
SES or immigrant
\times
SES analyses

•

Rural–urban gaps matter in Korea

•

Disability interacts with socioeconomic constraints in additive and multiplicative ways

AMF should therefore be understood as addressing only one axis of structural inequality, not as a comprehensive equity mechanism.

5.9.7
Practical Challenges in SES Percentile Construction

Finally, SES percentile estimation itself entails institutional constraints:

•

Short-term implementation may rely on imperfect proxies

•

Verification burdens must remain light to avoid discouraging applicants

•

SES disclosure should be voluntary and non-penalizing

•

Institutions may prefer coarse bins (quartiles/quintiles) over precise percentiles

•

Cross-institution alignment is ideal but not required

These constraints do not undermine AMF; they highlight its adaptability and the need for context-sensitive calibration.

This study proposes the Adaptive Merit Framework, a design-centered mechanism for increasing equity in competitive admissions without displacing regular admits or lowering merit-based standards.

By combining SES-based corrections with a transparent, threshold-anchored rule, AMF offers a procedural approach to fairness that is reproducible, auditable, and compatible with existing institutional structures.

6.1
Toward a Unified and Multi-Dimensional Framework

While this study focuses on SES-based corrections to test scores, the broader vision underlying AMF is a fundamental redesign of admissions systems toward two complementary goals:

Track Consolidation
. Current Korean admissions operate through fragmented categorical quotas–such as regional balance, rural students, income-based scholarships–each with separate criteria and administrative burdens. This fragmentation creates labeling effects, where students admitted through “special tracks” face stigma, and institutions struggle with coordination across multiple systems. AMF offers a unified framework that replaces categorical proxies with continuous, individual-level SES measurement. As demonstrated in
Appendix F.4.1
, this consolidation preserves seat counts while improving transparency and reducing administrative complexity. This correction rule
M_{i}^{*}=M_{i}+\alpha(0.5-S_{i})
extends naturally to any composite metric used in multi-dimensional evaluation systems.

Multi-Dimensional Evaluation
. Test scores capture cognitive performance but exclude dimensions where structurally disadvantaged students may demonstrate hidden strengths–creativity under resource constraints, persistence through adversity, and resilience in unstable environments, among other dimensions. AMF’s correction rule is designed to accommodate any composite metric
M_{i}
, enabling institutions to integrate multi-dimensional indicators once they become measurable and validated through redesigned evaluation systems. The current focus on test scores establishes the foundational architecture; future iterations can incorporate non-cognitive assessments as institutions develop transparent, SES-debiased evaluation protocols(
Appendix F.4.3
).

This vision directly addresses the “talent recognition” gap articulated in
Section 1.1
: Korea’s education system successfully develops cognitive potential but does not consistently convert it into opportunity. Unified frameworks with multi-dimensional evaluation offer a systematic path to bridge this gap. AMF is not merely a correction mechanism for test scores–it is a foundational step toward admissions systems that recognize multiple forms of merit while maintaining procedural transparency and merit-based standards.

Extensions Beyond Admissions

While this study addresses college admissions, AMF’s design principles–individual measurement, dynamic threshold, transparency, and non-displacement structure–extend naturally to other selection contexts requiring merit-equity balance. Extensions may incorporate diverse forms of structural disadvantage (regional disparities, caregiving burdens, family disruption) within the same rule-based architecture, provided measurements remain valid and ethical. These applications require domain-specific validation but illustrate AMF’s potential as a generalizable policy engineering framework beyond admissions.

Summary

In sum, AMF demonstrates that fairness need not be a redistributive or adversarial choice. When implemented as a transparent, merit-anchored procedural rule, fairness becomes
additive
–expanding opportunity while maintaining institutional legitimacy. This perspective provides a foundation for future research and policy experimentation on practical and implementable pathways to enhance educational mobility.

B.2
Empirical Calibration of the Policy Parameter
\alpha

This appendix provides the empirical rationale for selecting the policy intensity parameter
\alpha\in\{5,10,15\}
and documents the magnitude of corrections induced by these values within the PISA 2022 Korea dataset.

B.2.1
Estimating the SES-Achievement Gradient

We quantify the magnitude of structural disadvantage by regressing mathematics performance on the ESCS index.

Let
R_{i}
denote standardized math scores and let
ESCS_{i}
denote raw socioeconomic status.

R_{i}=\beta\cdot ESCS_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

For the 2022 Korea sample:

\beta=47.29,\,\,\,\,\,p<0.001

R^{2}=0.136

Thus, a one-standard-deviation increase in ESCS (
\sigma_{ESCS}
= 0.823) corresponds to:

\Delta\approx 47.29\times 0.823=38.90\,points

This figure provides an empirical benchmark for the “environmental effect” of socioeconomic status.

Note on ESCS Standard Deviation

Although ESCS is a standardized socioeconomic index (mean 0, SD 1) at the population level, the standard deviation can deviate from 1 when computed on a specific country’s sample without applying PISA sampling weights. In this study, we use the raw Korean PISA sample (N=6,377), whose empirical ESCS SD is 0.823. Using the sample SD is methodologically consistent with the regression coefficient
\beta
estimated on the same dataset.

Appendix C
Formal Properties, Threshold Geometry, and Selection Logic

C.1
Derivation of the Linear SES-Based Correction Rule

Let
R_{i}
denote applicant
i
’s raw performance score and
S_{i}\in[0,1]
denote the normalized SES index.

AMF adjusts performance using a linear function that preserves relative SES differences while ensuring a transparent and auditable mapping from SES to correction magnitudes.

C.1.1
General Formulation

We begin with the general correction rule:

C_{i}=\alpha\cdot(\mu-S_{i})

where,

•

\alpha>0
is the policy intensity parameter,

•

S_{i}
is the normalized SES index,

•

\mu=\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]
is the distributional center of SES.

This form ensures:

1.

Monotonicity
: lower SES yields weakly higher correction

2.

Upper bound
: corrections vanish at and above
\mu

3.

Scale invariance
: any affine transformation of SES is absorbed into
\mu

C.1.2
Percentile-Normalized SES

In the PISA analysis, SES is transformed via:

S_{i}=PercentileRank(ESCS_{i})

Because percentile ranks follow a uniform distribution on [0,1], we have:

\mu=\mathbb{E}[S_{i}]=\frac{1}{2}

Substituting into the general formulation yields the empirical rule:

C_{i}=\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})

C.1.3
Eligibility Boundary

An immediate consequence is that:

C_{i}>0\Leftrightarrow S_{i}<0.5

C_{i}=0\Leftrightarrow S_{i}\geq 0.5

Thus,
exactly 50% of applicants are eligible for non-zero correction
, a property induced by percentile-normalization–not by policy choice. This explains the empirical observation that
100% of additional admits in the PISA analysis originate from the bottom 50% of SES.

C.1.4
Corrected Performance and Threshold Geometry

Corrected performance is:

R_{i}^{*}=R_{i}+C_{i}

Let
T
denote the raw-score threshold corresponding to the top-
k
admits. Because
T
is determined solely from raw scores, regular admits cannot be displaced.

Applicants qualify as conditional admits when:

R_{i}^{*}\geq T\Leftrightarrow R_{i}+\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})\geq T

Rearranging yields the minimum raw score required for a given SES:

R_{i}\geq T-\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})

This establishes:

•

Linear threshold shift
: low-SES applicants require slightly lower raw scores, proportional to SES disadvantage

•

Binding constraint
: no applicant with
R_{i}<T-\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})
can be admitted

•

Merit preservation
: if
R_{i}
is far below threshold, no correction can elevate the applicant above
T

C.1.5
Bound on Maximum Correction

Given
S_{i}\in[0,1]
:

maxC_{i}=\alpha\cdot(0.5-0)=0.5\alpha

For the chosen policy intensities:

\alpha=5,10,15\Rightarrow max\,C_{i}=2.5,5.0,7.5

These bounds confirm that AMF operates within a narrow adjustment range, consistent with the empirical SES-achievement gradient observed in PISA.

C.1.6
Implication

The derivations above establish that:

•

The correction rule is a
distribution-aware linear compensator.

•

Median SES (
S_{i}=0.5)
constitutes a
structural eligibility boundary
, not policy-imposed cutoff.

•

Corrections cannot elevate low-performing applicants into competitive ranges.

•

Thresholds remain fixed, guaranteeing
no displacement
of regular admits.

A full characterization of threshold geometry and sensitivity analysis appears in
Appendix D
.

•

N_{app}=6,377

Formally:

C.2.2

AMF retains

used in existing admissions.

This design preserves:

•

non-displacement
of regular admits

•

merit anchoring

•

compatibility

Mathematically:

R_{i}^{*}=R_{i}+C_{i},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,but\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,T=fixed\,from\,raw\,data.

Thus AMF only asks a counterfactual question:

Define:

as the raw-score distance from the merit threshold.

Applicants eligible for AMF conditional admission must satisfy:

\alpha\cdot(0.5-S_{i})\geq T-R_{i}

Only applicant who are both

structurally disadvantaged

S_{i}

•

•

Low-SES applicants with very low raw scores cannot qualify.

•

Only the
intersection zone
(near-threshold
\times
low-SES) is eligible.

This is the mathematical reason PISA results show:

•

100% of additional admits originate from Q1-Q2

•

Additional admits always number
small (4-9 out of 6,377)

•

The model exhibits
selective, conservative expansion

•

The DBN is not part of the core contribution but provides an extensible framework for studying how small targeted corrections may propagate through multi-year educational trajectories.

Figure E.1:
DBN Long-term Trajectories

E.1
Model Overview

The DBN represents educational progression as a sequence of latent ability states:

X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{T}

where each
X_{t}
reflects an individual’s academic proficiency at time
t
.

Each student is associated with:

•

structural SES exposure
S_{i}

•

opportunity correction
C_{i}
(nonzero only once, at t=1 under AMF)

•

performance observation
R_{t}
(test scores or grade-level assessments

The DBN tracks how these quantities influence transitions from
X_{t}
to
X_{t+1}

E.2
State Space

Let
X_{t}\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
denote ordered latent ability tiers.

These tiers correspond approximately to quintile-level academic readiness but are not directly observable.

The observation model links latent states to performance indices:

R_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{X_{t}},\sigma_{X_{t}}^{2})

Parameter values may be calibrated from PISA/PIRLS/TIMSS cross-sectional distributions.

E.3
Transition Model

Transitions follow a first-order Markov structure:

P(X_{t+1}|X_{t},S_{i},C_{i})

We parameterize the transition kernel using three components:

(1)

Baseline mobility matrix

A row-stochastic matrix
M
reflecting average year-to-year mobility:

M_{ab}=P(X_{t+1}=b|X_{t}=\alpha)

(2)

SES exposure term

SES percentile
S_{i}
nudges transitions:

•

upward mobility probability decreases linearly in
S_{i}

•

downward mobility probability increases accordingly

Formally:

M_{ab}^{(SES)}=M_{ab}+\sigma(S_{i}-0.5)\gamma_{SES}

with small
|\gamma_{SES}|
.

(3)

AMF correction term

The correction
C_{i}
acts as an initial-condition shift at
t=1
:

•

it increases the probability of entering higher latent states

•

the effect decays over time (structural, not permanent)

One implementation:

M_{ab}^{(AMF)}=M_{ab}^{(SES)}+f(C_{i})\cdot 1(t=1,a<b)

where
f(\cdot)
is a bounded, increasing function (e.g., logistic or linear with saturation).

The combined transition kernel is normalized to maintain stochasticity.

E.4
Mobility Matrix Calibration

The transition matrices
M_{admit}
and
M_{not}
are stylized structures calibrated to reflect widely documented patterns in intergenerational mobility research: (a) higher persistence at top and bottom SES quartiles, and (b) modest upward mobility for middle groups.

The probabilities were constructed to satisfy four constraints:

1.

Row-stochasticity (each row sums to 1)

2.

Monotonic decline in upward mobility as SES increases

3.

Lower downward mobility for top-SES groups

4.

Qualitative alignment with Korea’s reported mobility trends (KRIVET, 2024)

The initial distribution
v_{0}=[0.35,0.30,0.20,0.15]
reflects a moderately bottom-heavy SES structure consistent with recent Korean demographic patterns. This distribution serves as a baseline state for long-run dynamic analysis; it is not used for statistical inference.

These matrices are calibrated to reflect qualitative patterns consistently
reported in the intergenerational mobility literature: higher persistence
at both tails of the SES distribution, lower downward mobility among
top-SES groups, and moderate upward mobility for middle-ranked groups.
These stylized features appear across multiple empirical contexts, including
U.S. administrative mobility studies
[
3
,
2
]
,
OECD’s cross-national mobility analyses
[
18
]
,
and recent Korean evidence
[
15
]
.

Appendix F
Policy Implementation Framework

This appendix formalizes how the Adaptive Merit Framework (AMF) can be operationalized within real institutional environments.
Its purpose is
not
to prescribe administrative procedures but to clarify how AMF’s
architectural properties—percentile-normalized SES measurement, calibrated corrections, and a threshold-preserving decision rule—interact with existing selection structures.
In doing so, this appendix extends the paper’s central argument: that fairness can function as a design property embedded directly into institutional procedures without displacing merit or introducing zero-sum redistribution.

F.1
Conceptual Positioning of Implementation

AMF is agnostic to specific institutional configurations. It provides a
generalizable design template
whose core components can be incorporated into multiple operational architectures.

Two structural properties make this possible:

•

Non-displacement
: regular admits remain entirely determined by raw performance.

•

Threshold anchoring
: corrections operative
relative to
an existing merit benchmark rather than redefining it.

These properties allow AMF to integrate with existing systems while maintaining institutional legitimacy.

F.2
Institutional Integration Models

F.2.1
Hybrid(Budget-Neutral) Model

In environments where enrollment caps or legal seat limits are fixed, AMF can allocate existing equity-oriented seats using corrected scores.

Key Properties

•

No change to the regular admit pool.

•

No expansion of cohort size required.

•

Suitable for systems that already maintain designated equity tracks (e.g., need-based or background-based tracks.

This model replaces categorical rules with continuous, transparent adjustments while preserving the underlying quota structure.

F.2.2
Expansion Model

Where institutional or governmental capacity allows additional seats, AMF identifies conditional admits strictly through threshold crossing.

Characteristics

•

Fully non-displacing.

•

Fully merit-compatible.

•

Scales with available budget or policy priority.

F.2.3
Choosing a Model

Institutions may select either model depending on:

•

fiscal capacity,

•

local regulatory constraints,

•

existing quota infrastructures,

•

tolerance for cohort expansion,

•

policy objectives (equity enhancement vs. fairness recalibration).

Both models remain consistent because each preserves AMF’s core procedural logic.

F.3
SES Disclosure, Data Requirements, and Safeguards

AMF requires only a minimal SES infrastructure:

F.3.1
Voluntary Disclosure (Opt-in SES Reporting) Model

AMF employs a voluntary disclosure model, in which SES information is submitted on an opt-in basis and never generates downside risk for applicants.

•

Applicants who opt in to SES reporting receive both raw and corrected evaluations.

•

Applicants who do not disclose SES are evaluated solely on raw performance.

•

Opt-in submission cannot disadvantage only.

This structure integrates SES-based corrections into a unified evaluation pathway while avoiding labeling effects or separate application tracks.

F.3.2
Data Requirements

A functional SES index should provide:

•

continuous percentile-based structure,

•

verified administrative inputs or validated composites,

•

temporal stability,

•

privacy-compliant data handling.

F.3.3
Data Infrastructure Considerations

AMF assumes the availability of a reasonably reliable SES infrastructure.
In settings where income or asset information is partially unobserved (e.g., informal income, under-reporting, or gaps in administrative linkage), corrections may imperfectly reflect true socioeconomic constraints.

This limitation is not specific to AMF but inherent to any data-driven equity mechanism.
In practice, robustness can be improved by using multi-indicator SES composites (e.g., parental education, regional deprivation indices, school-level resource measures) that are less sensitive to income misreporting and more stable across administrative contexts.

F.3.4
Procedural Safeguards

To ensure transparency and institutional defensibility:

•

\alpha
must be publicly announced and fixed for each cycle,

•

raw-score thresholds must remain unmodified,

•

R_{i},S_{i},C_{i}
and
R^{*}_{i}
must be auditable,

•

SES data must not be used for any purpose beyond corrective adjustment.

These safeguards align with procedural justice norms and reinforce institutional legitimacy.

F.4
Applicability Across Educational Systems

F.4.1
Systems With Quotas

AMF can replace fragmented, categorical quotas with a continuous SES-based adjustment mechanism. Seat counts remain unchanged; the allocation process becomes more transparent and better targeted. This enables
track consolidation
, reducing administrative burden and mitigating labeling effects.

For example, Korea’s regional balance and income-based tracks could be consolidated into a single AMF-based mechanism, reducing fragmentation while improving targeting precision.

F.4.2
Systems Without Quotas

Where quotas do not exist, AMF functions naturally as an expansion mechanism requiring no structural reconfiguration.

F.4.3
Holistic and Multi-Metric Systems

AMF is not intended to integrate directly with existing holistic admissions models that rely heavily on qualitative or portfolio-based evaluation (e.g., self-reported activities, essays, recommendation letters). Such components introduce substantial variability, are highly sensitive to differential access to information and private tutoring, and limit the transparency needed for merit-preserving corrections.

Instead, AMF is compatible with
future multi-metric systems
in which the evaluation structure itself is redesigned to be more measurable, diversified, and education-driven rather than portfolio-driven.

Example include:

•

standardized indicators of academic preparation (e.g., CSAT or equivalent),

•

creativity or problem-solving assessments administered under standardized conditions,

•

longitudinal measures of school-based engagement or persistence,

•

domain-general basic competency tests aligned with intended majors (kept at accessible difficulty levels),

•

optional bonus components for applicants with exceptional domain-specific talent (maintaining a separate talent track).

Such a framework reduces reliance on opaque, preparation-intensive components, encourages students to prepare along a unified track, and limits the influence of private tutoring markets. Within this redesigned evaluation space, AMF operates naturally on the composite merit index:

M_{i}^{*}=M_{i}+\alpha\cdot(\mu-S_{i})

providing transparent, continuous adjustments while preserving the integrity of the underlying metrics.

F.5
Cross-Domain Extensions

Although developed in the context of academic admissions, AMF’s architectural logic is domain-general: whenever merit-based evaluation interacts with structural constraints, the correction rule and dynamic threshold can be adapted without compromising performance standards.

F.5.1
Scholarships and Fellowships

Scholarship selection can incorporate SES-based adjustments to identify high-potential applicants whose measured performance may reflect resource constraints rather than underlying capability, allowing merit-based allocation to more accurately reflect latent potential.

F.5.2
Employment Selection

Structured assessments (aptitude tests, standardized evaluations) can integrate SES-informed corrections while preserving existing performance benchmarks, supporting fairer comparisons without altering hiring standards.

F.5.3
Government Examinations

High-stakes examinations can apply threshold anchoring to maintain meritocratic legitimacy while acknowledging contextual disadvantage, particularly in settings where uniform standards amplify structural inequities.

F.5.4
Barrier-Free Certification and Engineering Evaluations

AMF can extend to performance-based evaluations in which environmental or structural constraints systematically depress measurable outcomes. Current barrier-free certification frameworks apply uniform standards despite wide variation in site-specific limitations—including existing infrastructure, topographical conditions, historical preservation requirements, and budgetary constraints. A context-aware adaptation of the correction principle could provide proportional adjustments while maintaining accessibility benchmarks, mirroring how AMF recognizes socioeconomic disadvantage without lowering academic thresholds. Household-level resource constraints, such as disability-related costs, can be incorporated into SES measurement using analogous logic.

F.5.5
Research Funding and Grant Competitions

Grant evaluation can introduce modest adjustments to account for institutional resource disparities that shape preliminary outcomes. Across these domains, AMF functions not as a redistribution tool but as a
procedural design principle
for translating structural constraints into transparent, merit-preserving adjustments.

F.6
Pilot Pathways

AMF’s deployment can proceed along three structured phases:

Phase 1: Calibration and Limited Testing

•

Initial calibration using existing datasets (e.g., PISA, college entrance archives)

•

Limited-scale pilots within a single track or program

•

Measurement of threshold-gap behavior, SES composition of conditional admits, and correction distributions

Phase 2: Validation and Adjustment

•

Analysis of opt-in patterns and noise stability under repeated sampling

•

Refinement of
\alpha
based on observed threshold stability and fairness–merit trade-off patterns

•

Incorporation of stakeholder feedback (students, faculty, admissions officers)

Phase 3: Institutional Integration

•

Broader rollout contingent on empirical validation

•

Cross-institutional comparison if multiple pilots are conducted

•

Public transparency reporting (e.g., annual disclosures of conditional admits and SES composition)

These pathways demonstrate feasibility without imposing prescriptive administrative models.

F.7
Summary

AMF’s modular and threshold-preserving architecture enables integration within a wide range of institutional settings.

It can function as:

•

a budget-neutral fairness recalibration tool,

•

an expansion-based opportunity augmenter,

•

or a cross-domain adjustment mechanism in non-academic contexts

By maintaining merit anchoring while incorporating continuous, empirically grounded corrections, AMF offers an implementable framework that institutions can adopt without restructuring their core evaluation systems.
