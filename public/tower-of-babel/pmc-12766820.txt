Planetary health and environmentally sustainable healthcare: perceptions of primary care practitioners in Ireland – a qualitative study
Authors: Stephen James Walsh, Aisling O’Leary, Matthew Lynch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman and planetary health are deeply interconnected and endangered by the triple planetary crisis: climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution. Despite proactive measures being pursued to reduce healthcare’s overall environmental footprint, relatively few of these are directed at primary care, notwithstanding that it accounts for up to 25% of healthcare’s ecological impact. Within Ireland’s primary healthcare sector, despite a growing recognition of the importance of environmentally sustainable healthcare, little is known about the perspectives of those healthcare professionals (HCPs) working in the sector to delivering more environmentally sustainable primary care services.MethodsThis qualitative descriptive study explored the knowledge and attitudes of community pharmacists, dentists, general practitioners and practice nurses working in primary care in Ireland, toward planetary health and sustainable healthcare. Semistructured interviews were conducted between May and September 2024 and analysed using inductive qualitative content analysis.ResultsAnalysis of interviews (n=20) produced a broad range of insights which were consolidated into three higher order categories. Findings revealed that while HCPs recognised the importance of climate action, many felt unable to translate concern into practical change in primary care due to barriers such as a lack of knowledge, limited guidance and inadequate system-level supports. HCPs emphasised the need for targeted training, access to practical tools and clear leadership from the relevant stakeholders.ConclusionThis study highlights the urgent need to shift from reliance on individual motivation to practice sustainably, to a coordinated public health response in Ireland. Achieving this requires educational and systemic changes, including supportive policy, incentivisation and practical sustainable interventions, to embed environmental sustainability into routine care.

Introduction

Human health and well-being is dependent on planetary health and at risk due to the threat of the triple planetary crisis: climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution.14 Rising global temperatures, extreme weather events and environmental pollutants are exacerbating disease burdens, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations.5 6 What was once considered a distant environmental concern has now materialised into a pressing health crisis,7 8 compelling healthcare systems to address climate-related health effects while reducing their own environmental impact.911 Despite a growing urgency toward healthcare decarbonisation,12 13 efforts to date have largely focused on secondary care, while primary care has received comparatively less attention despite accounting for up to 25% of the sector’s environmental impact.14 15 This focus on hospitals reflects their status as the most resource-intensive sector within health systems, where interventions such as energy efficiency and waste reduction are easier to measure and where organisational control enables more streamlined implementation.16 17

In response to the climate crisis, Ireland has committed to halving emissions by 2030 and achieving net-zero emissions by 2050.18 The Irish Health Service Executive (HSE), the organisation responsible for healthcare delivery, has aligned with this agenda through its Climate Action Strategy 2023–2050, aiming to embed sustainability into care delivery and operational practices.19 In practice, however, much of the HSE’s mitigation efforts led by its National Office for Climate Action and Sustainability and Capital and Estates Sustainable Infrastructure Office has similarly prioritised secondary care-based interventions through initiatives such as the HSE’s Green Healthcare Programme.20 21 Achieving ambitious targets for 2030 and 2050, respectively, will require an urgent expansion of mitigating actions encompassing primary care. This is against a background of Ireland’s track record of achieving past climate goals being somewhat varied.22

In Ireland, primary care is delivered via a model in which community pharmacists (CPs), dentists (DTs), general practitioners (GPs) and practice nurses (PNs) operate within private practices and opt to provide care on behalf of the HSE under contract.23 The HSE’s long-term reform strategy, Sláintecare, seeks to achieve integration of primary care services and shift the focus of care into community settings to optimise resource use, improve patient outcomes and reduce preventable hospital admissions.24 25 Although this transformation aligns with principles of sustainable healthcare,26 environmental objectives currently appear not to incorporate the routine application of indicators and sustainable quality improvement frameworks needed to drive progress.2730

Previously published qualitative studies have explored various healthcare professionals’ (HCPs) perspectives on environmentally sustainable healthcare across primary care systems in countries such as Australia, Canada, Germany, Sweden and the UK, where integrated and formalised governance arrangements can better enable climate-conscious care.3135 However, little is known about HCPs’ knowledge, attitudes and practices in systems with less formal governance arrangements, where limited institutional or coordinated support may make environmental sustainability more challenging to embed.

Ireland’s primary care sector presents inherent challenges to coordinated environmental reform. In contrast to more integrated international models where multidisciplinary teams and shared data systems can enable collective sustainability action, Ireland’s decentralised structure offers fewer systemic levers for change. Combined with this, there is limited insight into how HCPs in a predominantly private, contract-based, fee-for-service systems navigate environmental challenges. This structure by default positions primary HCPs as key stakeholders in any transition to sustainable healthcare, given their influence on medicines utilisation, resource use and direct patient care.

Environmental sustainability has not yet presented as a meaningful priority within primary care service delivery in Ireland. The Irish College of General Practitioners (ICGP) has developed the GLAS Toolkit to support GPs to integrate sustainability into routine practice,36 but its impact has yet to be evaluated. While the Toolkit signals a growing awareness, it also underscores the absence of comparable resources for other primary care HCPs, leaving much of the sector without direction or support in advancing environmental sustainability. This raises a critical question: how can meaningful, system-wide sustainability be achieved in models of healthcare delivery that have evolved over time rather than being designed ab initio as integrated models of care delivery?

This study addresses an apparent gap in the international discourse by examining how HCPs in Ireland perceive the delivery of more environmentally sustainable care within their current practice. Ireland provides a particular situation where delivery of all facets of its primary healthcare is largely private and contract-based, with few systems-level supports currently in place to promote more sustainable practice. This stands in contrast to more integrated systems with sustainability agendas, such as the National Health Service in the UK or regionalised European models like Sweden, from which much of the existing evidence on sustainability in healthcare has emerged.3132 3739 By analysing the experiences of Irish primary care providers, this study further aims to generate a context-specific understanding of the organisational and institutional enablers and barriers to achieving sustainable healthcare delivery. In doing so, it contributes to the international evidence base by demonstrating how system design and its operation influences the feasibility, scope and pace of the reforms required to facilitate the provision of more environmentally sustainable care, and by identifying the conditions required to support this transition in less integrated primary care settings.

Methods

Design

This qualitative descriptive study examined HCPs’ knowledge and attitudes toward planetary health and environmentally sustainable practices within the Irish primary care context. Semistructured one-to-one interviews were conducted and analysed using qualitative content analysis.40 41 The reporting of the study’s findings adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research for transparency and rigour.42

Sampling strategy

A combination of purposive and snowball sampling was initially used to recruit eligible HCPs.43 HCPs were eligible if they were qualified CPs, DTs, GPs or PNs working in primary care in Ireland. Purposive sampling was undertaken where HCPs were invited to participate via email recruitment through two primary channels: (1) professional contacts of the researchers and (2) HCPs in practice who also hold part-time posts in academic departments within the academic institution. Snowball sampling was subsequently employed, whereby professional contacts and study participants were requested to share the study invitation among their networks. Interested HCPs contacted SJW via email and received a participant information leaflet and consent form (onlinesupplemental material 1 2). Interviews were scheduled following the return of signed consent forms. The sampling strategy supported a broad and diversely representative sample within the study’s time constraints.44

The sample size for the study was determined by a process of informational redundancy or data saturation. ‘Data saturation’ represents the point at which no new categories emerge during the data collection phase and is key to determining the sample size. Guest et al45 describe it as the number of interviews ‘needed to get a reliable sense of thematic exhaustion and variability within [their] data set’. Sample sizes in the range of 4–40 participants typically appear in published qualitative studies. Kaae and Traulsen46 note that for semistructured interviews, between 15 and 25 interviews are often necessary to achieve data saturation. To inform the determination of data saturation, data analysis was undertaken on an ongoing basis during the interviewing phase of the study. The authors were satisfied that data saturation was reached when 20 interviews were completed, and that further categories would not present in any additional interviews.

Data collection

One-to-one semistructured interviews were conducted using a topic guide (online supplemental material 3) addressing: (1) understanding of climate change and its impact, (2) awareness of healthcare’s environmental footprint and mitigation efforts and (3) perspectives on novel strategies for environmentally sustainable practice. The guide was informed by prior qualitative literature on HCPs’ views on environmental sustainability3134 and refined through a scoping review of environmental sustainability in primary care.15 Two pilot interviews informed final revisions.

Interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams and audio recordings transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were checked against recordings, pseudonymised and shared with participants for verification. Preliminary data analysis was carried out alongside data collection. Recruitment concluded once it became clear that no new categories were emerging from the interviews.47 48

Data analysis

Inductive qualitative content analysis was used to analyse the interview data, to identify and explore categories that emerge from the data. Elo and Kyngäs49 state that a qualitative content analysis is normally represented as three key phases as follows: preparing, organising and reporting. For the preparing phase, in-depth engagement with the data set enabled thorough familiariliarity with the data. In the organising phase, the data was categorised according to a process of open-coding undertaken by SJW and ML. This involved identifying notes and as many headings as were necessary from the interview transcripts to describe all aspects of the interview data.49 Following this, a number of categories were created, which were then grouped into a smaller number of higher order categories.

Open coding and subsequent categorisation were conducted by SJW and ML using NVivo (V.15). This was then reviewed by AO’L who validated both the coding and categorisation processes.

Reflexivity

The authors were careful to adopt a systematic and impartial approach to analysing participants’ responses, focusing strictly on the manifest content of the data. As registered pharmacists, the researchers were conscious of their professional backgrounds in analysing responses from HCPs from different disciplines in primary care, mindful that the analysis was to be and took care to prevent personal views, experiences or assumptions from influencing study design, data collection or interpretation. In particular, as pharmacists whose expertise is in medicines and their use, we were mindful of this during both the conduct of the interviews and the data analysis, to ensure that the sustainability of any particular aspect of primary healthcare practice including medicine usage and supply was not unduly focused on. Having variations to the interview topic guides that reflected the practice norms of the HCPs’ particular discipline being interviewed, further supported this. Throughout the process, the authors engaged in reflective discussion to maintain analytical rigour and to avoid subjective inference or overinterpretation of the data.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in this research study.

Results

A total of 20 one-to-one interviews were conducted with primary care HCPs, comprising CPs, DTs, GPs and PNs (table 1). HCPs had a mean of 15 years’ experience (range: 5–34 years) and included both employees and practice owners, with employees forming the majority. Recruitment was evenly split: 10 via the authors’ networks and academic departments (CP=6, DT=1, GP=2, PN=1), and 10 via snowball sampling (CP=1, DT=2, GP=4, PN=3). Three additional HCPs expressed interest (DT=2, GP=1) but were unavailable due to demanding work commitments. Interviews averaged 36 min (range: 18–61 min).

Current knowledge and practices

Understanding of climate crisis and health impacts

HCPs demonstrated an understanding of climate change and its potential negative health consequences, with one GP characterising it as ‘probably one of the biggest threats facing humans in today’s world’ (Quote(Q)1). Several HCPs associated climate change with rising global temperatures, extreme weather events and related health impacts, highlighting the link between temperature increases and mortality, ‘Greater heat puts more strain on the body and increases deaths’ (Q2). Changes to local climates were identified with some HCPs describing the impact their patients experienced, including a perceived ‘increased [incidence] of respiratory and skin issues’, attributed to environmental changes (Q3–5).

Awareness of sources of healthcare’s environmental footprint

Varying levels of awareness regarding the environmental impact of healthcare practices were demonstrated. Frequent use of ‘single-use medical items’ was highlighted, attributed to ‘guideline and infection control’ requirements (Q6,7). Other notable sources identified were paper use and energy consumption, although it was acknowledged ways ‘to [reduce their] energy consumption’ were seldom considered (Q8,9). HCPs who were generally familiar with sustainable healthcare highlighted pharmaceuticals, particularly inhalers and their propellants, as key sources of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Q10). Concerns were raised about the wholesale pharmaceutical supply chain, with pharmacists highlighting the frequency of medication deliveries as a source of unnecessary emissions, noting ‘we get multiple [medicine] deliveries a day, which could be reduced’ (Q11).

Lack of awareness and education regarding sustainable healthcare

A lack of awareness and education on sustainable healthcare was acknowledged across the various disciplines. Its absence from professional training and clinical guidance was noted, ‘I don’t think we’ve ever received any guidance or training’ (Q12–14). Difficulties in promoting sustainable practices without foundational knowledge were highlighted by a CP who stated, ‘I can’t educate someone without being educated first’ (Q15). One PN remarked that pollution and climate change rarely enter healthcare discussions, ‘it’s not something that has ever come up’ (Q16).

Established practices and norms

HCPs emphasised the need for ‘evidence-based’ care, but acknowledged that established practices contributed to unsustainable care (Q17). These included an over-reliance on medication with a ‘pill for every ill’ approach to care, increased demand for healthcare services which is ‘trending upwards’ (Q18,19) and shifts towards single-use items for cost, convenience and sterility reasons, with one PN noting ‘we used to sterilise equipment and re-use it’ but that now it was ‘easier to just use single-use items which then get thrown in the bin’ (Q20). Medication waste was widely reported, especially by CPs who highlighted the challenge of accepting medicines for disposal in the absence of funding for the provision the service, giving rise to the potential of improper disposal by patients. One CP noted, ‘We just take back our own patients’ medication, so many [unused patient medication] ends up being wasted or thrown in landfill’ (Q21). Withdrawal of funding for medicine disposal schemes was seen as a causative factor limiting CPs’ capacity to accept this waste to safely dispose of (Q22). Out of pocket payments by patients were also reported as a possible reason influencing medication trends. It was opined that patients paying out of pocket for prescriptions exhibited greater mindfulness in managing their medication supply, while those qualifying by means testing for full or part state-subsidised medicines often accumulated surpluses (Q23).

Attitudes and perceived barriers to change

A willingness to engage in sustainability efforts was apparent among all HCPs interviewed. However, not having ‘the tools to do it’ was a perceived deterrent, while others questioned the impact of individual actions, with one CP noting ‘it’s very hard on an individual level to feel like you’re making a difference’ (Q24,25). Some questioned how sustainability applied to their area of practice and believed sustainable actions must never compromise or ‘be a negative on patient care’ (Q26,27). It was noted that sustainability initiatives would only be widely adopted were they practical and aligned with the economic realities of running a business, whereby financial stability was prioritised over environmental sustainability out of necessity (Q28,29). This constraint is structurally reinforced by the fee-for-service model. Time constraints and workload demands were also described as major barriers to implementing environmentally sustainable practices, with one GP stating, ‘I’m just not going to do it unless it’s easy to be done’ (Q30), which would be particularly emphasised when perceived as an uncompensated burden, secondary to the core incentivised provision of care. HCPs reported resorting to familiar existing processes and ingrained workflows, whether sustainable or not, when faced with mounting workload pressures arising from the demanding nature of clinical practice (Q31). The cost of environmentally friendlier interventions ‘that might be costing the HSE or patient more money’ was percieved as a limiting factor in prescribing decisions (Q32).

Catalysing actions for sustainable transformation

Education

Education and training were identified as enablers of environmentally sustainable healthcare. One CP stressed the need for a foundational understanding before discussing sustainability with patients, stating ‘it starts with us getting the knowledge and then being able to disperse it’ (Q33). The importance of communication style was stressed, with a GP advocating for ‘positive information about sustainability’ over fear-based ‘catastrophising’ messaging (Q34). A PN noted how education about environmentally sustainable healthcare must not ‘just tell us what to do but also explain why it matters’ (Q35). Frustration was expressed with passive guidance, underlying the need for active learning approaches instead of merely offering instruction (Q36). Mandatory professional training was proposed to ensure all HCPs are made aware of environmentally sustainable healthcare, though it was suggested sustainability educational initiatives should be integrated into broader healthcare-related topics ‘like social care or migration’, rather than being treated as a stand-alone issue separate to routine clinical care (Q37,38).

Patient inclusion and enhanced self-care

Patient engagement was seen as vital to environmental sustainability, especially in self-care, medication use and eco-informed decisions. Active participation where ‘you have to engage with them and make them want to do it’ was emphasised as more effective for lasting behavioural change than passive instruction, as seen in smoking cessation approaches (Q39). Medication waste was highlighted as a major issue, prompting calls for greater public awareness about its environmental and financial consequences, with one CP opining that ‘more education for the public on medicines wastage would help’ (Q40). Patient buy-in, ‘getting patients on board….to see the value of these changes’ was seen as vital to the success of any environmental sustainability initiative (Q41). Some HCPs argued patients must take ‘some responsibility’ for reducing unnecessary prescriptions, while one CP proposed that educating patients about the environmental impact of certain treatments could encourage patients to select more sustainable, ‘environmentally friendly’ options (Q42,43).

Clinical optimisation

HCPs identified strategies to optimise care and improve environmental sustainability, particularly by reducing the use of potent GHG propellants in pressurised metered dose inhalers through greener prescribing practices, with some GPs already indicating that they ‘tend not to prescribe aerosol inhalers’ (Q44,45). Reducing low-value care and unnecessary interventions with an emphasis on ‘rationalising medicines and ensuring patients only take what’s necessary’ was perceived as an essential component of sustainable clinical care resulting in significant environmental and patient co-benefits, through rationalisation of prescriptions and elimination of ineffective treatments (Q46). In dentistry, minimising the use of nitrous oxide, a potent GHG, was perceived as an important consideration (Q47). In diabetes care, broadening access to continuous glucose monitoring for patients was seen as a favourable sustainability measure notwithstanding the increased associated costs, as it reduces ‘all of the lancets and test strips, [that] have to be single use’ (Q48). Regular medication reviews which ‘find things [ie, medicines] that patients are not using’ were identified as an important tool for discontinuing unnecessary prescriptions and reducing waste (Q49). The integration of CPs into general practice teams was proposed as a way of optimising medication use (Q50).

Leaner service delivery

Opportunities were identified for efficiency improvements in service delivery. One suggested strategy involved reducing packaging waste, while maintaining necessary infection control standards, with one DT reporting how ‘grouping instruments in packaging rather than individually wrapping’ together could minimise excess waste generation (Q51). Expanding the medium of service provision by leveraging telemedicine when appropriate ‘for results and follow-ups’ was also seen as a way to reduce unnecessary travel for patients, benefiting both the environment and accessibility (Q52). Transitioning towards paperless healthcare systems, optimising supply chain logistics and improvements in information technology infrastructure and communication between healthcare providers were seen as crucial to enable more efficient delivery of services (Q53–55).

Sustainable operational resource use

Improvements in operational resource utilisation were acknowledged, but often constrained by ageing infrastructure and inconsistent environmental sustainability practices, with one HCP noting ‘if we had the opportunity to build a new building obviously, we would make it as energy efficient as possible’ (Q56, 57). Recycling practices varied, and stronger mandates were proposed to improve uptake (Q58). Automated energy-saving measures were mentioned, including ‘sensors in the corridors and rooms that activate lights’ and machine shutoff protocols, though effective attempts remained limited (Q59). Most energy-saving efforts were reported as informal, led by individuals rather than institutional policy (Q60).

Embedding change and sustaining greener practices

Legislation and policy

Explicit policy and legislative support were perceived as essential for embedding environmental sustainability in primary care with one HCP indicating that ‘our hands are tied in what we can do without legislation changes to support us’ (Q61). Prioritising the reimbursement of environmentally preferable generics on State-funded medicine schemes was suggested to simplify decision-making and promote sustainable prescribing (Q62). Guidelines alone were viewed as insufficient without accompanying investments to ensure implementation (Q63). A combined approach of regulation and financial incentives was seen as most effective, as articulated by one GP, who felt that ‘regulation can set a standard and make certain practices mandatory, while incentives can encourage people to go beyond the minimum requirements’, together with long-term national planning with phased sector targets (Q64–66).

Ensuring feasibility in sustainability efforts

HCPs expressed openness to sustainability initiatives but emphasised that their success depends on practicality of implementation, clarity of guidance and financial viability. One GP highlighted that while there is a willingness to adopt environmentally sustainable practices, the challenge lies in ‘how easy they are to implement’ (Q67). Another GP emphasised that environmentally sustainable practices must be capable of seamless integration into existing workflows so they become routine ‘and the norm without conscious effort’ rather than additional burdens (Q68). HCPs suggested that integrating prompts for eco-friendly medicine choices and incorporating information on a medicine’s carbon footprint into prescribing and dispensing software would make the use of environmentally sustainable medicines more practical and achievable (Q69,70). An overall lack of a structured implementation plan was seen as a barrier, with HCPs advocating for clear, step-by-step guidance (Q71). Some HCPs noted that unclear environmental sustainability guidelines currently hinder implementation, with CPs highlighting the lack of ‘clear guidance’ (Q72). Financial viability remains a major concern, particularly for practice owners who must balance environmental sustainability with economic viability (Q73).

Data-informed decision making

There was a call for measurable data to underpin sustainable change, noting that efforts would be more effective if supported by visual metrics and relatable comparisons making the impact of adoption tangible. Measuring and tracking practice performance through a ‘grading system’ would encourage engagement and reinforce environmentally sustainable behaviours (Q74). Similarly, the potential of carbon footprint reports for prescribing patterns, similar to the ‘reports [we get] on our antibiotic prescribing’, was suggested as a way to highlight performance and provide feedback to support improvement (Q75). HCPs stressed the value of using relatable visuals, like ‘paper generation which is equivalent of half an acre of forest’ or ‘equivalent of driving from Dublin to Mayo’, to present environmental metrics, would help HCPs better appreciate the ecological impact of their actions (Q76, Q77).

Prioritising preventative care

Prevention was widely acknowledged as a critical strategy, offering multiple health co-benefits while reducing the need for resource-intensive interventions (Q78, 79). The need for greater access to and training in social prescribing was identified: ‘social prescribing is a big area… but not everyone has access [to it]’, which participants noted could offer several advantages (Q80). Several CPs expressed interest in broader roles in health promotion, provided adequate support is available which would ‘reduce the burden on the healthcare system and contribute to more sustainable practices’ (Q81). A PN emphasised the value of proactive management of chronic conditions to reduce hospital admissions (Q82). These findings point to support for a greater focus on preventative care, which would more optimally align with a model of environmentally sustainable care. However, while benefitting both the patient and the environment, such an approach is likely to have an adverse financial impact on primary care practices, particularly in pharmacy and dentistry, where revenue streams depend predominantly on treatment in the form of service provision rather than preventative care, thereby making more environmentally sustainable-friendly practices less financially rewarding.

Leadership and role of professional organisations

Leadership, at both practice and system levels, was viewed as essential to advance sustainable healthcare. Practice-level leadership ‘with the principal [setting] the tone for the whole practice’ was seen as a prerequisite to integrating sustainability into daily operations (Q83). Professional organisations were identified as important drivers in raising awareness and promoting action, particularly through the provision of accessible, practical guidance and coordinating with health agencies to tailor support for primary care (Q84–86). Reinforcing sustainability as a priority at conferences and forums organised by ‘professional organisations [which] make sustainability a theme and helps reinforce the message’ was also seen as important (Q87). It was perceived that this leadership needs to be reflected in, and integrated with, various educational programmes (Q88).

Discussion

The study findings reveal a disconnect between climate action aspirations and current implementation capabilities among HCPs in Irish primary care settings. Despite recognising the urgency of the climate crisis, many HCPs struggle to translate this awareness into actionable sustainable practices, as evidenced in other published work.335052 There is also evidence that environmental sustainability efforts in primary care are largely driven by individually motivated HCPs rather than being systemically supported.53 The HSE’s Climate Action Strategy 2030–2050 outlines broad environmental goals and provides welcome support to HSE-owned estates and facilities, predominantly in secondary care, to achieve sustainability objectives.19 However, it falls short in addressing the unique operational realities of primary care organisations, which are mostly privately-owned practices operating under contract from the HSE. The absence of a clear, actionable implementation plan tailored to primary care leaves primary care HCPs without the tools, guidance or supportive governance structures needed to embed environmental sustainability into routine practice.54 55 A key example was the burden placed on CPs, who appeared to be expected to provide a medicines waste disposal service in the absence of a funded national system for managing patient-generated medicines waste.56 57 However, a change to the community pharmacy contract announced in September 2025 provides for the introduction of such a scheme that was not previously funded.58 The recently published HSE Greener Models of Healthcare Framework represents a significant opportunity to bridge these gaps.59 Although the framework will address many aspects of the healthcare service, its focus on sustainable medicines use holds strong potential to equip primary care with the tools, guidance and governance supports necessary to translate sustainability ambitions into everyday practice.

A fundamental barrier to sustainable practice identified by HCPs was their limited knowledge of sustainable healthcare and the environmental impacts of clinical practice. This gap fosters a perception that sustainability is incompatible with clinical best practice. As one GP noted, clinical decisions must be ‘evidence-based’, yet sustainability measures are often seen as lacking sufficient evidence.33 Committed to ‘first do no harm’, HCPs tend to favour conventional approaches over sustainable alternatives when the supporting evidence is unclear.31 53 This gap may stem from many HCPs completing their education and training before recognition of the need to include sustainable healthcare in undergraduate and postgraduate curricula became apparent. Efforts to integrate these topics in undergraduate training are underway,6065 but ongoing professional development has only begun to address the lack of accessible guidance and clear pathways for embedding sustainable practices.54 55

Broadening the definition of clinical best practice to include environmental sustainability, alongside quality and safety, reflects a growing recognition that resource efficiency is central to excellence in care.6668 HCPs in this study consistently expressed a preference for practical, solution-oriented education rather than fear-driven messaging. As such, for sustainability to become embedded in routine practice, education must go beyond conveying facts and should foster a deeper understanding and appreciation of planetary health, as professionals are unlikely to prioritise what they do not fully value.69 Professional organisations and health systems have a pivotal role in developing tailored education and supportive frameworks trusted by HCPs.70 In Ireland, sustainable healthcare efforts in primary care have largely been limited to the ICGP’s GLAS Toolkit for GPs,36 leaving a significant gap for other primary care disciplines. In contrast, advancements internationally have demonstrated that integrating sustainability into healthcare delivery through resource efficiencies and elimination of low-value care is achievable without compromising the quality of patient care.71 72 Resources such as the Royal Pharmaceutical Society’s Greener Pharmacy Toolkit and initiatives by Health Care Without Harm, SEE Sustainability and the Centre for Sustainable Healthcare demonstrate progress in supporting HCPs to integrate planetary health into care delivery.7376 Ireland, however, lacks analogous multidisciplinary frameworks, underscoring the need for national leadership and coordinated guidance to integrate sustainability into primary care.

HCPs’ views in this study highlight that the transformation of primary care to sustainable practice requires a multifaceted approach that extends beyond educational interventions and calls for strong governmental leadership, structural reform and systemic support, as stated by Robinson et al.77 While education builds foundational awareness, it is insufficient on its own to drive the scale of change needed to address the climate-related health crisis, which echoes published sentiments.78 79 From the perspectives of those interviewed, a meaningful strategy for sustainable healthcare must fully leverage primary care’s unique capacity to deliver holistic, person-centred care. This means moving beyond an over-reliance on traditional biomedical models and embracing high-value, prevention-oriented approaches to integrate positive health behaviours via social prescribing and community-based supports.80 81 To unlock this potential, it is essential to address the structural misalignment of the fee-for-service model, which can financially reward overtreatment and in turn medicines usage, rather than less resource intensive sustainable healthcare. This can create significant barriers for HCPs in private practice, who face business pressures to maintain financial viability through increased throughput, and for employee HCPs, who may be incentivised to maintain and enhance throughput by their employers.82 Overcoming these financial drivers in the status quo is critical to empowering HCPs to lead in reshaping primary care as a system focused on prevention, well-being and long-term health outcomes. It was very clear that HCPs in this study felt that sustainable practices must not only be accessible and practical, but also economically viable. Further, enabling healthcare providers and patients to make environmentally friendly choices requires systemic redesigns that support, rather than hinder, sustainable behaviours. Improving environmental performance, such as reducing waste and energy use, can also yield cost savings. Both Eckelmann and Sherman and Afjal and Trivedi also suggest that targeted incentives can further reduce financial barriers while discouraging unsustainable practices.83 84

This multifaceted approach proposed by the study participants for sustainable healthcare transformation draws parallels with the historical development of patient safety in healthcare practice, yet diverges critically in required timelines.85 The analogous blueprint previously adopted by patient safety, which leverages highly effective systemic interventions, has transitioned from a peripheral concern in healthcare delivery to a fundamental principle through systematic interventions.86 However, unlike patient safety’s gradual evolution, sustainability interventions require an urgent response similar to pandemic-level mobilisation, compelling immediate and comprehensive systemic modifications.87

In relation to the study’s strengths and limitations, while the findings from qualitative studies cannot be considered to be generalisable, it is considered that these findings are transferable to HCPs working in less integrated models of primary care that exist in Ireland and elsewhere. The inclusion of HCPs from four different disciplines at various career stages underpins the shared perspectives found and supports the study’s transferability. The sample size of 20 participants aligns with acknowledged participants’ numbers in standard qualitative studies and achieved data saturation.46

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need to shift from individually-led climate action to a coordinated, public health-driven response to the climate-health crisis. HCPs identified the need for a dual strategy: equipping HCPs with practical tools and education, while driving system-level change through policy reform, infrastructure investment and financial incentivisation. There is a perceived need for educational and professional bodies to embed environmental sustainability into training, continuing professional development and clinical guidance to normalise environmentally conscious practice as part of routine, high-quality care. However, to support HCPs to make informed decisions, the environmental impact of medicines and healthcare interventions needs to be measured and reported. This evidence base is considered essential to developing meaningful performance targets and sustainability metrics within primary and other areas of healthcare delivery. Sustainability should be seamlessly integrated into everyday primary care, rather than seen as an added burden or source of extra workload. Seamless integration of environmentally conscious practices into primary care workflows which align with optimal, high-quality patient care can be achieved through contractual mechanisms that reward sustainable interventions, driving value-based improvements that prioritise public health and safeguard the well-being of both current and future generations.
