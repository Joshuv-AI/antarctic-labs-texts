Bronchial Artery Chemoembolization (BACE) Combined with PD-1 Blockade Versus Chemotherapy Combined with PD-1 Blockade for Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer in Older Adults
Authors: Jing Xiang, Jianfei Tu, Weiwei Gong, Panhang Chen, Yonghui Wang, Weiwen Li, Jieming Zhong, Jiansong Ji, Jianhui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystemic immunochemotherapy is the standard of care in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) without druggable driving mutations, yet many older patients cannot tolerate systemic chemotherapy. Bronchial arterial chemoembolization (BACE) represents an alternative to systemic chemotherapy for this population.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study included consecutive patients with stage IIIC–IV NSCLC over 65 years old who underwent BACE or systemic chemotherapy, plus a PD-1 inhibitor, in our hospital from August 2019 to October 2024. The objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and adverse events (AEs) were compared between the two groups.ResultsThe final analysis included 81 patients: 39 and 42 in the chemotherapy and BACE groups, respectively. In comparison to the chemotherapy group, the BACE group had older age (median age: 78 vs 71 years) and poorer Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS≥2: 47.6% vs 2.6%). The ORR was 73.8% in the BACE group vs 64.1% in the chemotherapy group. The median PFS was 5.4 months in the BACE group vs 5.2 months in the chemotherapy group (P=0.841). The median OS was 10.3 months in the BACE group vs 8.7 months in the chemotherapy group (P=0.449). Neither PFS nor OS differed significantly between the 2 groups in either Cox proportional hazards regression or inverse probability of treatment weighting analyses. The BACE group had lower rate of myelosuppression (28.6% vs 74.4%) but higher rate of dyspnea (16.7% vs 0%). No treatment-related serious AEs, eg, spinal artery injury due to ectopic embolism or venous thrombosis of the lower limb, were reported in the BACE group.ConclusionIn older patients with stage IIIC–IV NSCLC, BACE combined with PD-1 inhibitor showed no statistically inferior response or survival versus chemoimmunotherapy. These exploratory results require validation in large prospective cohorts.

Introduction

In China, lung cancer is the most common cause of both cancer incidence and death, especially among the older adults.1 After the age of 65 years, the incidence of lung cancer rises noticeably.2 Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancer cases.3 The management of older adults with NSCLC with no druggable driving mutations or failed treatment with targeted therapies represents a complicated challenge due to high rate of co-morbid diseases, reduced cardiovascular and hematologic reserves and often intolerance to chemotherapy.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have become the cornerstone in the treatment of advanced NSCLC with negative driving mutations.4 The IPSOS study reported improved overall survival (OS) with atezolizumab vs single-agent chemotherapy in NSCLC patients with Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) ≥2 or aged ≥70 years with comorbidities.5 However, among the patients 75 years of age or older in the NEJ057 trial, 86 patients (24.3%) in the ICI+ chemotherapy group had grade 3 or higher immune-related adverse events (irAEs), and 115 patients (32.5%) required steroid treatment for irAEs.6

Older patients, particularly those with frailty, impaired renal or hepatic function, or multiple comorbidities, are at substantially higher risk for chemotherapy‑related toxicities such as myelosuppression, infection, and gastrointestinal side effects. In bronchial arterial chemoembolization (BACE), chemotherapeutic drugs are infused into the bronchial arteries via a catheter and embolic materials are used to block blood supply to the lesions.7 In comparison to systemic chemotherapy, BACE achieves higher concentration in the active site with reduced systemic exposure and thus lower rate of adverse events (AEs), and represents a significant therapeutic option in patients who could not tolerate systemic chemotherapy.8 Following chemoembolization, the release of cell debris, proinflammatory cytokines, and damage-associated molecular patterns triggers anti-tumor immune responses by priming adaptive immunity via antigen-presenting cells.9 Adding BACE to PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors may enhance antitumor immunity by inducing immunogenic cell death and altering the tumor microenvironment, potentially leading to enhanced the antitumor effects,10 but only a limited number of studies have investigated the combination of BACE with immunotherapy in NSCLC. A retrospective study reported 12-month median progression-free survival (PFS) and 27-month OS in 27 NSCLC patients treated with BACE plus a programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor.10 However, most of these studies were conducted in mixed‑age populations with heterogeneous treatment protocols, and robust evidence specifically focused on older adults remains scarce. Furthermore, there is a lack of real-world data to directly compare BACE plus immunotherapy versus standard chemotherapy plus immunotherapy in older patients.

The current study compared survival outcomes and safety profiles of BACE vs systemic chemotherapy, plus immunotherapy, in a older adults of NSCLC patients.

Methods

Patient Characteristics

We conducted a retrospective study of older adults with stage IIIC–IVB NSCLC receiving treatment with PD-1 inhibitors, plus either BACE or systemic chemotherapy, between August 2019 and October 2024 at authors’ center. The study was approved by the institutional review board at authors’ center, and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived by the institutional review board due to the retrospective study design. Patients who met the following inclusion criteria were included in the study: aged ≥65 years; complete clinicopathological information, including age, sex, smoking history, disease stage and histological type of NSCLC; and a treatment regimen of immunotherapy plus BACE or systemic chemotherapy. NSCLC patients with positive driver genes and those who underwent immunotherapy alone were excluded.

Treatments

All BACE procedures were performed by interventional radiologists with >10 years of experience. Prior to the procedure, contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) was examined to delineate the tumor-feeding arteries and to exclude any common origin with the spinal artery. After administering local anesthesia under sterile conditions, vascular access was established via the Seldinger technique through the unilateral femoral artery, with insertion of a 5F sheath. A 5F RLG or Cobra catheter was then navigated toward the target vessel, after which a microcatheter was employed for super-selective catheterization to identify the arteries supplying the tumor. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was utilized to visualize potential feeding vessels, including the bronchial, intercostal, phrenic, subclavian, and internal thoracic arteries. Once the tumor-feeding artery was confirmed, a 1.9F microcatheter (ASAHI, Japan) was advanced for super-selective access. Following catheter positioning, chemotherapeutic agents were administered, and embolization of the feeding artery was subsequently performed using 300–500 μm microspheres (Embospheres Beads, Meriton Co., USA). The procedure was considered complete when angiographic stasis of the target vessel was achieved, with no residual tumor staining observed. The total dose of chemotherapeutic drugs delivered by BACE was 70% of the recommended dose for systemic chemotherapy. The choice of chemotherapeutic agents (type and dose) and ICI regimens was based on physician discretion, with reference to the recommendation by the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. Chemotherapy drugs included pemetrexed plus cisplatin or carboplatin for adenocarcinoma, and nab-paclitaxel plus cisplatin or carboplatin and gemcitabine plus cisplatin for squamous cell carcinoma. PD-1 inhibitors included pembrolizumab (200 mg), toripalimab (240 mg), tislelizumab (200 mg), and sintilimab (200 mg), and were given every 3 weeks until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity.

Treatment Response and Survival Outcomes

Treatment response was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST v1.1) every 6–8 weeks, and categorized into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD). The overall response rate (ORR) included CR and PR. The disease control rate (DCR) included CR, PR, and SD. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to disease progression. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to patient death or the last follow-up. AEs were retrospectively collected through comprehensive review of electronic medical records, including outpatient clinic notes, hospitalization records, and laboratory reports, and assessed using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate, and presented as number (percentage). Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, followed by the Log rank test. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis was conducted to adjust for the following confounding factors: sex, age, smoking history, ECOG PS, disease stage, pathologic type, distant metastases, PD-L1 expression, stroke, hypertension, type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The propensity scores were based on the logistic regression models using multiple covariates as independent variables and treatment group as the dependent variable. A covariate was considered balanced between the 2 groups if the standardized mean difference (SMD) was 0.1 or less after IPTW weighting. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression including these confounding factors. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), GraphPad Prism (version 9.0) and R (version 4.0).

Results

Safety and Toxicity Profile

The most common AEs were myelosuppression, nausea, and fatigue (Table 3). In comparison to the chemotherapy group, the BACE group had lower rate of myelosuppression (28.6% vs 74.4%), nausea (23.8% vs 69.2%), and fatigue (33.3% vs 59.0%). The irAE included skin reactions, pneumonia, nephritis and hypothyroidism. Five patients developed grade-3 irAEs (immune-related pneumonia in all cases): two in the BACE group and three in the chemotherapy group. No delayed immune toxicities occurred during the follow-up. No treatment-related serious AEs, eg, spinal artery injury due to ectopic embolism or venous thrombosis of the lower limb, were reported in the BACE group. Discussion

We compared the clinical outcomes and treatment safety of patients aged 65 years or older with NSCLC, who received immunotherapy combined with either systemic chemotherapy or BACE. In comparison to systemic chemotherapy plus immunotherapy, BACE+immunotherapy showed no statistically inferior treatment response (ORR of 73.8% vs 64.1%, DCR of 100% vs 92.3%) and survival outcomes (PFS of 5.4 vs 5.2 months, OS of 10.3 vs 8.7 months), despite of older age (78 vs 71 years) and poorer ECOG performance status (≥2: 47.6% vs 2.6%) in the BACE group. As expected, the BACE group had lower rate of myelosuppression (28.6% vs 74.4%), nausea (23.8% vs 69.2%), and fatigue (33.3% vs 59.0%). No treatment-related serious AEs were reported in the BACE group. Results from the current study should be viewed with considerable caution, given the inherent drawbacks of our retrospective single-center design, limited sample size, and unbalanced baseline characteristics at enrollment.

Chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy are the primary treatments for advanced NSCLC. Several Phase 3 trials demonstrated that adding pembrolizumab to platinum-based chemotherapy results in prolonged OS and PFS, regardless of PD-L1 expression level in the tumor.11,12 However, in a retrospective study by Tsukita et al, patients receiving immunotherapy plus chemotherapy had higher rate of grade ≥3 irAEs and irAEs requiring glucocorticoid treatment compared with the immunotherapy alone.13 A pooled analysis of eight randomized controlled trials14 reported no significant survival benefit of ICI combined with platinum-based chemotherapy vs immunotherapy alone in patients aged ≥75 years and with 1% to 49% programmed cell death ligand 1 tumor proportion score (PD-L1 TPS).

Reduced organ reserve and comorbidities in the older patients represents a major challenge in the use of systemic chemotherapy. Several previous studies suggested that BACE is an option in such a population.15,16 BACE works on the principle of infusing chemotherapeutic agents directly into the bronchial artery that supplies the tumor.17 The “first-pass” effect allows chemotherapeutic agents to be administered at a lower dose while still achieving a sufficient drug concentration in the tumor.18

The median PFS and OS in the BACE group, either before IPTW (5.4 and 10.3 months) or after IPTW (8.2 and 9.9 months), in the current study was shorter than that reported by a retrospective study by Li et al in 10 patients (mean age of 71.1 years) receiving sintilimab combined with bronchial arterial infusion (BAI) chemotherapy/drug-eluting embolic (DEE)-BACE (PFS and OS of 11.0 and 8.0 months, respectively),19 and a single-center cohort study by Xu et al (PFS and OS of 12 and 27 months, respectively).10 We believe that such a discrepancy could be partly attributed to the more advanced disease stage in the current study (stage IV in 83.3% of patients vs 40% and 48.1% in the studies by Li et al and Xu et al, respectively). Also, the median age in the current study was 78 years, whereas the median age of patients in the studies by Li et al and Xu et al was 71.1 and 65.3 years, respectively.

In a retrospective study of 41 NSCLC patients by Yu et al20 both PFS and OS were longer in patients treated with drug-eluting bead (DEB)-BACE than those treated with BACE. Improved ORR and PFS have also been reported with DEB-BACE vs BACE in patients with advanced NSCLC by Ye et al.21 Previous studies have also reported that DEB-BACE was more effective than systemic chemotherapy.16,22 It is noteworthy to point out that all patients in the current study received BACE rather than DEB-BACE, and this might have influenced the survival outcomes relative to previous studies. The median OS in the chemotherapy group in the current study (8.7 months) is markedly shorter than the expected benchmarks seen in pivotal trials for first-line Chemo+ICI in NSCLC (15–20 months).23–25 In our opinion, such a discrepancy could be attributed to the relatively older age (78 years vs 65 in the KEYNOTE-189 study, 64 in the IMpower132 study and 63 in the ASTRUM-004 study) and the poorer ECOG performance status (≥2 in 47.6% of the patients vs 0.2% in the KEYNOTE-189 study, 0% in the IMpower132 study and 0% in the ASTRUM-004 study).

Consistent with previous studies,26–28 the rate of bone marrow suppression was lower in the BACE group in the current study. The BACE group also had lower rate of other AEs known to be associated with the use of chemotherapeutic agents. This finding is expected due to the lower dosage of chemotherapeutic drugs in the BACE group vs the chemotherapy group. It is noteworthy to point out that dyspnea occurred in 16.7% of the patients in the BACE group and none in the chemotherapy group. We hypothesize that these events may be attributable to procedure-related factors such as transient diaphragmatic irritation, post-embolization syndrome, or the use of contrast media, rather than representing a systemic toxicity. Although dyspnea was grade 1 or 2 in all cases, and resolved with symptomatic management (eg, oxygen supplementation, steroids) within 24–48 hours, and no patient discontinued treatment due to dyspnea, cautions must be exercised. There was no treatment-related severe AEs (eg, spinal artery injury) in the BACE group, supporting the safety of BACE if conducted by experienced interventional oncologists. As for serious irAEs, only two patients (4.8%) developed grade 3 or higher irAEs (all pneumonia cases) in the BACE group. The incidence of severe pneumonitis was lower than reported by a meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials (43.4–61.4%).29

The current study has a number of important limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, the cause-effect relationship could not be established based on comparison of treatment responses and survival outcomes between the 2 groups. Second, lack of significant difference in ORR and survival outcomes of PFS and OS should not be interpreted as equivalence between the 2 treatment regimens. Third, baseline characteristics were clearly imbalanced between the 2 groups, particularly in age and ECOG performance status, indicating substantial treatment-selections. We attempted IPTW analysis to adjust the confounding, and obtained similar results (lack of difference in survival outcomes), adding support to the main findings. Nevertheless, due to the small sample size, patient-selection biases are unlikely to be fully controlled by IPTW analysis. Fourth, as a single-center retrospective study, the survival outcomes and the safety outcomes may be dependent on the expertise of the authors in BACE. Alternatively, the relatively low rate of AEs may reflect the fact that AEs were retrospectively collected based on medical records, rather than prospectively assessed. Fifth, variable chemotherapy regimens and different types of PD-1 inhibitors were used in the current study, resulting in significant heterogeneity that may have influenced the observed outcomes. And the small sample size prevented us from conducting subgroup analyses based on chemotherapeutic regimens and PD-1 inhibitors. Future studies are needed to examine the potential difference in the efficacy profiles and toxicity patterns of different chemotherapeutic regimens and PD-1 inhibitors. Last but not least, the follow-up was relatively short. Thus, long-term survival and external validity remain uncertain. Extended follow-up in future multicenter studies is warranted to determine whether the survival benefits observed in this analysis are durable over time.

Conclusion

Among older adults with stage III/IV NSCLC, BACE plus PD-1 inhibitors did not demonstrate statistically inferior response or survival outcomes versus systemic chemotherapy plus PD-1 inhibitors. These findings require verification by prospective studies with larger cohorts.
