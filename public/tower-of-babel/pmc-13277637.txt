Odor-induced sweetness enhancement: EEG evidence for olfactory and gustatory cross-modal interactions
Authors: Yuchao Yang, Yuchen Guo, Zhijie Hua, Qun Wang, Yan Shi, Hong Men
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reducing sugar content while maintaining perceived sweetness is a major challenge in beverage formulation. This study combined sensory evaluation and electroencephalography (EEG) to investigate how varying odor intensities influence sweetness perception in sucrose solutions. Sixteen participants evaluated solutions with matched sugar content but different odor levels while EEG signals were recorded. Behavioral results revealed an inverted U-shaped response: beverages with moderate odor intensity elicited the highest perceived sweetness, increasing ratings by 14.7% relative to the sucrose-only baseline (no added odor), whereas high odor intensity slightly reduced sweetness perception by 5.4%. EEG analysis showed that α-band functional connectivity exhibited the strongest and most concentration-dependent modulation, with source localization identifying the temporal lobe as the principal hub coordinating distributed cortical networks. Although changes in individual graph-theoretical metrics were minimal, integrating multiple EEG features through a random forest model enabled accurate classification of sweetness-related conditions, achieving an average accuracy of 84.5%. These findings indicate that sweetness perception arises from distributed, high-dimensional network coordination rather than isolated pathways. By combining sensory and neurophysiological measures, this approach provides actionable guidance for optimizing odor formulations, enabling sugar reduction while maintaining consumer-perceived sweetness.

Introduction

Food perception is a complex, multisensory integration process encompassing vision, olfaction, gustation, somatosensation, and audition, which not only determines product acceptance and preference but also profoundly shapes eating behavior and health outcomes (Spence, 2015). Among sensory modalities, sweetness-one of the five basic tastes-occupies a distinctive position in food perception. Sweetness is directly linked to energy intake and activates reward circuitry to elicit pleasure (De Araujo, 2011), making it one of the most consequential sensory attributes in the food industry. However, excessive sugar consumption is closely associated with the prevalence of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, prompting global public-health agencies to call for reductions in added sugars (Huang et al., 2023; World Health Organization, 2015). This tension renders the preservation of sweetness alongside sugar reduction a major challenge in food science. Conventional sugar-reduction strategies primarily rely on high-intensity sweeteners; however, these substitutes often exhibit off-notes such as lingering aftertaste, metallic notes, or bitterness, and thus fail to recapitulate sucrose's flavor profile (Sievenpiper et al., 2025).

In recent years, researchers have explored cross-modal interactions to enhance sweetness perception, with odor-taste integration mechanisms attracting particular attention (Tan and Pang, 2025). In practice, the everyday experience of “flavor” is not purely gustatory but the central integration of gustatory, olfactory, and other sensory inputs. A substantial psychophysical literature demonstrates that odors can modulate taste perception through cross-modal interactions (Auvray and Spence, 2008). However, these effects are odor-dependent and are most consistently observed when the odor is perceptually congruent with the target taste attribute. For example, sweet-congruent odors such as strawberry or vanilla can enhance perceived sweetness without altering sucrose concentration (Tahmouzi et al., 2025; Spence, 2022; Amano et al., 2022). This cross-modal enhancement offers a promising avenue for lower-sugar products with preserved sweetness: by adding appropriate odor compounds, manufacturers may reduce sugar while maintaining consumer-expected sweetness levels (Khorisantono et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

However, the neural mechanisms underlying odor-induced taste enhancement remain incompletely understood. Early neuroimaging studies, primarily using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), have identified the contributions of the insula, orbitofrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex to odor-taste integration (Santoyo-Zedillo et al., 2023; Small et al., 2004). Yet the second-scale temporal resolution of fMRI constrains sensitivity to the rapid neural dynamics that characterize multisensory integration. Moreover, most studies have emphasized regional activation magnitude while underappreciating functional connectivity and network-level coordination among areas (Farruggia et al., 2022; Noppeney, 2011). In reality, multisensory integration is not a local phenomenon of a single “integration center,” but instead it emerges from dynamic interactions within large-scale networks spanning sensory cortices and higher-order association regions (Bauer et al., 2020; Van Atteveldt et al., 2014). Accordingly, methods with high temporal resolution and explicit connectivity characterization are required to delineate the network basis of odor-sweet interactions.

Electroencephalography (EEG), with its millisecond-level temporal resolution and sensitivity to neural oscillatory activity, provides an ideal tool for investigating rapid sensory processing and cross-modal integration (Senkowski and Engel, 2024). Unlike hemodynamic techniques such as fMRI, EEG directly records electrical signals from multiple electrodes distributed across the scalp, enabling simultaneous measurement of neural activity over the entire head. This combination of high temporal resolution and broad spatial coverage makes it possible to characterize rapid neural dynamics and time-varying interactions among distributed brain regions. These characteristics render EEG ideally suited for connectivity- and network-based analyses designed to elucidate the large-scale neural architecture of odor-induced sweetness enhancement.

Methodologically, EEG-based functional connectivity approaches have advanced substantially in recent years. Unlike conventional event-related potential (ERP) analyses that focus on signal changes at single electrodes, connectivity methods quantify statistical dependence between electrodes/regions, thereby revealing how inter-regional coordination supports complex cognition (Nagy et al., 2024; Khaleghi et al., 2024). Multiple metrics, including the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), imaginary coherence (iCoh), and weighted phase-lag index (wPLI), have been developed to characterize neural synchrony from complementary perspectives and mitigate contamination by volume conduction (Chiarion et al., 2023). Building on this, graph-theoretical analysis represents brain connectivity as a mathematical graph (nodes = regions, edges = connections). By computing degree and betweenness centrality, clustering coefficient, and small-worldness, it quantifies network integration, segregation, and hub properties (Sporns, 2018; Ismail and Karwowski, 2020). This network perspective has been widely applied to elucidate the neural bases of cognitive processes, psychiatric conditions, and neurodegenerative disorders (Ortiz et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2023); however, its applications to food perception remain limited.

In addition to connectivity and graph-theoretical analyses, EEG source imaging can provide spatial interpretation of scalp-recorded responses by estimating their underlying cortical generators. Standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA) was developed as a distributed inverse solution for estimating intracerebral current density from scalp EEG recordings (Pascual-Marqui, 2002). Michel and Brunet further summarized the practical analysis steps of EEG source imaging and emphasized its utility for interpreting task-related cortical activity with both temporal and anatomical information (Michel and Brunet, 2019). In food-related sensory research, Iannilli et al. applied sLORETA to event-related responses elicited by food and non-food odors, showing that source imaging can help localize cortical responses associated with odor processing (Iannilli et al., 2015). Beyond spatial interpretation, feature-based machine learning provides a way to test whether extracted EEG features contain information sufficient to distinguish perceptual or experimental conditions. Bilucaglia et al. recently used boosted-tree regression models to predict coffee sensory attributes from EEG features, supporting the feasibility of EEG-based prediction in flavor perception research (Bilucaglia et al., 2025).

Collectively, these conceptual and methodological advances have established a rich analytical framework for investigating the neural basis of odor-induced sweetness enhancement; however, several important limitations remain. First, most studies employ simple odor-present versus odor-absent contrasts, with limited systematic evaluation of odor concentration effects. In reality, multisensory integration often follows a nonlinear dose-response, where overly strong or weak cues may fail to yield optimal integration. Second, existing work frequently targets single frequency bands or local regions, lacking a global account of how cross-band oscillations cooperate and how whole-brain networks reconfigure dynamically to support sweetness enhancement. Third, conventional statistics largely test mean differences between conditions, yet multisensory information may be encoded in high-dimensional, distributed network patterns. Single features are insufficient, necessitating multivariate pattern analyses and machine-learning approaches.

Building on these considerations, the present study employed EEG together with a multi-level analytical framework to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying odor concentration-dependent sweetness enhancement. Sixteen participants completed gustatory and olfactory tasks under four ethyl maltol concentration conditions, while perceived sweetness and whole-head EEG signals were recorded. Functional connectivity and graph-theoretical analyses were used as the primary framework to characterize concentration-dependent changes in large-scale brain network organization across canonical frequency bands. Source localization was further applied to provide anatomically informed interpretation of task-related neural responses, and feature-based machine learning was used to evaluate whether connectivity-derived EEG features could discriminate sweetness-related conditions. By integrating behavioral ratings, EEG connectivity, network topology, source-level interpretation, and multivariate classification, this study aimed to clarify how odor concentration modulates the neural network dynamics associated with sweetness enhancement and to provide electrophysiological evidence relevant to precision sugar-reduction strategies.

The experimental paradigm involved concurrent behavioral evaluations and continuous EEG recordings from 16 healthy adults exposed to varying concentrations of ethyl 2-methylbutyrate (EMB) in a sucrose solution. Dataset acquisition and data preprocessing

Participants

We initially recruited 20 healthy adults, and after screening for adequate gustatory perception, 16 right-handed participants (8 males, 8 females) were included in the final analysis. Participants were all Asian, aged 18-30 years. Body weight ranged from 62 to 101 kg for males and 48 to 65 kg for females. Handedness was assessed to ensure all participants were right-handed. Prior to enrollment, all participants completed a medical history screening and interview, reporting no rhinitis, no recent upper-respiratory infection, and no conditions affecting olfactory or gustatory perception. None had a psychiatric history or were taking medications that affect central nervous system function. To control EEG recording conditions and olfactory confounds, participants washed their hair on the day of the test. They abstained from smoking, consuming caffeine-containing beverages, and engaging in other activities that could alter neural excitability. All participants provided written informed consent after being fully apprised of the study purpose, procedures, and potential risks. The study adhered to the revised Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional ethical guidelines and received ethics approval from the Technical Safety Committee of Northeast Electric Power University.

Stimuli

All stimuli used purified water produced by Wahaha Group Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) as the solvent base. Sucrose and EMB were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd., China. Solutions were prepared at room temperature using calibrated analytical balances and piston pipettes, following weight-to-volume (w/v) and volume-to-volume (v/v) ratios. Five sucrose solutions (S1-S5) were prepared by dissolving 2, 4, 6, 8, or 10 g of sucrose in 100 mL of purified water, yielding ≈ 2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% (w/v). Using S3 as the stock, 1, 10, or 100 μL of EMB was added per 100 mL to obtain S3-O1, S3-O10, and S3-O100, corresponding to ≈10, 100, and 1000 ppm (v/v). During preparation, sucrose was fully dissolved by magnetic stirring. Afterward, EMB was added and gently inverted to mix. All samples were colorless and transparent to avoid visual cues.

Rationale for stimulus selection: EMB is a naturally occurring ester found in various fruits such as apples and strawberries, characterized by a fruity, sweet odor profile. The compound is recognized as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the Flavor and Extract Manufacturers Association (FEMA No. 2443). It has also been evaluated by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA No. 206) in 1998, supporting its safe use as a flavoring agent in food applications. The 6% (w/v) sucrose concentration was selected to approximate the sweetness level commonly found in commercial beverages and fruit juices, as sensory quantification studies typically employ sucrose reference solutions ranging from 2% to 10% by weight (Mao et al., 2022, 2025), with 6% representing a moderate sweetness level. Secondly, regarding the addition of EMB, its use in food products such as candies, non-alcoholic beverages, and baked goods typically ranges from 0.5 to 10 ppm, and previous studies have shown that EMB can significantly enhance sweetness perception at sucrose solution (Barba et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2024). Based on this, to further investigate the neural dimensions of sweet taste perception and explore whether higher EMB concentrations maintain the sweetness-enhancing effect, we selected a concentration gradient of approximately 10-1000 ppm in the present study.

Instrument

We employed an olfactory presentation and control system optimized for flavor-evoked EEG, paired with a high-stability EEG acquisition platform and a computer-controlled synchronization/trigger module to ensure millisecond-level alignment of stimulus timing, gas concentration, and physiological recording. Mass-flow controllers (MFCs) operated in closed-loop with solenoid valves to precisely regulate the pressure inside the sample vial. A touchscreen interface was used to set the stimulus duration and inter-stimulus interval, while monitoring software continuously checked the valve status and communication to maintain stable pressure and liquid delivery timing. The system utilized polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tubing throughout to minimize adsorption and residuals. For liquid delivery, a dedicated tube submerged in the sample solution was connected to the outlet; purified air was introduced into the sealed sample vial to generate positive pressure, which drove the solution through the submerged tube to the participant's mouth. An inline liquid flow meter was installed in the delivery tube to monitor the flow rate, ensuring consistent delivery of 1 mL of solution per stimulus.(2)EEG acquisition. EEG was recorded with the NCERP system (NCC Electronics Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at a sampling rate of 256 Hz. A 21-channel electrode cap (Greentek Pty. Ltd., China) included Fz, Cz, Pz, T3, T4, C3, C4, Fp1, Fp2, F7, F8, T5, T6, O1, O2, F3, F4, P3, P4, A1, and A2, with placement conforming to the international 10-20 system. The olfactory system wrote event markers to the EEG stream at stimulus onset, achieving millisecond synchronization with stimulus timing.

Sweetness perception training

Sensory training comprised two components: sweetness perception training and retronasal olfactory perception training. The sweetness training familiarized participants with graded sweetness levels to facilitate objective evaluation of the test solutions. To establish a quasi-objective reference, sweetness values for five sucrose solutions (S1-S5) were anchored at 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10, respectively. Per trial, 1 mL sucrose solution was delivered to the tongue midline over 4 s; participants held the solution for 10 s to fully perceive sweetness, after which they were informed of the assigned sweetness level. Before EEG recording, each concentration was tasted 10 times to consolidate familiarity with the sweetness scale. The subjects rinsed their mouths with purified water and rested for 3-5 min between two samplings to minimize residual flavors and sensory adaptation.

Retronasal training employed a sipping technique to maximize concurrent odor-taste perception. Using a liquid-delivery system, 1 mL sucrose plus EMB mixtures were delivered to the tongue midline over 4 s. While holding for 10 s, participants inhaled orally and exhaled nasally, allowing volatiles to traverse the oropharynx into the nasal cavity and evoke retronasal olfaction. Before EEG recording, each mixture was sampled ten times using the same breathing pattern to stabilize the combined gustatory-olfactory perception.

EEG experimental procedure

Pre-experimental preparation maintained the laboratory at 21 ± 2 °C, powered down non-essential devices, and ensured a quiet environment free of measurable electromagnetic interference. Four 100-mL test solutions (S3, S3-O1, S3-O10, S3-O100) were pre-prepared and held at 37.5 °C in a thermostatic water bath. Upon arrival, participants were fitted with an EEG cap and earplugs, then sat quietly for 3 min. A sample vial was randomly selected and connected to the liquid-delivery system; seat height was adjusted so that the outlet nozzle was positioned 0.25-0.5 cm above the tongue midline, after which EEG acquisition commenced.

At trial onset, the liquid-delivery and EEG systems were triggered synchronously to record 14 s of EEG, comprising 4 s of solution delivery and 10 s of intraoral holding. After stimulation, participants expectorated the solution and rinsed with purified water. Subsequently, participants rated sweetness using the S1-S5 reference scale, and these ratings served as labels for the corresponding EEG epochs. A 5-min inter-trial interval was provided, during which the liquid-delivery system was rinsed with purified water. The procedure was repeated to acquire EEG data for the remaining three vials. Each participant completed a total of eight experiments, with each experiment spaced one day apart.

EEG preprocessing

To ensure reliable EEG acquisition, the raw data underwent the following preprocessing pipeline. For each 14-s trial, the first 5 s were discarded to minimize interference caused by solution transfer. The remaining data were then filtered using a 0.2–50 Hz band-pass filter and a 49-51 Hz notch filter to attenuate low-frequency drift and power-line noise, respectively. The sampling rate was down-sampled from 256 Hz to 128 Hz to optimize computational efficiency. Subsequently, data were re-referenced to the common average reference (CAR) to reduce global background activity and reference bias, thereby improving spatial specificity. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then performed to identify and remove non-neural artifacts, including ocular (EOG) and myogenic (EMG) components. These components were automatically classified using the ICLabel plugin within EEGLAB with a ≥90% artifact probability threshold; classification decisions were further confirmed by visually inspecting scalp topographies, time courses, and spectral features prior to elimination. Following artifact removal, six-level wavelet-packet decomposition (Daubechies family) was applied to the clean signals, yielding five canonical frequency bands plus a broadband (0.2–49 Hz) for subsequent feature extraction.

Graph-theoretic and statistical analyses

Construction of functional connectivity matrices. To comprehensively characterize odor-evoked changes in brain connectivity from multiple perspectives, we employed three complementary connectivity metrics. PCC quantified linear dependence between electrode pairs. iCoh focuses on the imaginary part of the cross-spectrum to mitigate volume-conduction artifacts, indexing genuine inter-regional synchrony. The wPLI emphasizes consistent non-zero phase lags, thereby discounting spurious zero- or π-phase relationships. Together, these methods characterize network organization from the perspectives of correlation, spectral coherence, and phase synchrony across six frequency bands thus avoiding the limitations of any single measure.

Considering the temporal stationarity of functional connectivity, and following prior recommendations on optimal window length (Sinding et al., 2021), we used the full 9-s trial as the computational unit. For each 9-s EEG segment, the raw data matrix was used to compute pairwise connectivity across all electrode pairs, yielding a 21 × 21 symmetric connectivity matrix for each metric. Specifically, the PCC quantifies linear dependence via the normalized covariance between two electrode time series, given by:(1)PCij=∑t=1m(xi(t)−x‾i)(xj(t)−x‾j)∑t=1m(xi(t)−x‾i)2∑t=1m(xj(t)−x‾j)2where xi(t) and xj(t) denote the time series from electrodes i and j, respectively; x‾i and x‾j are their means; PCij∈[−1,1], with positive values indicating synchronous co-fluctuation and negative values indicating anti-phase (anticorrelated) variation.

ICoh suppresses instantaneous zero-phase-difference links arising from volume conduction and common reference by isolating the imaginary part of the frequency-domain cross-spectrum, thereby retaining only neural synchrony with genuine time delays. Its computation is given by:(2)iCohij(f)=Im(Sij(f))Sii(f)·Sjj(f)Here, Sij(f) denotes the cross-power spectral density between electrodes i and j at frequency. Sii(f) and Sjj(f) denote the auto-spectral densities, Im(·) represents the operation of taking the imaginary part. Band-specific iCoh values were averaged across frequency bins within each band to yield a single connectivity weight for each electrode pair. By definition iCohij(f)∈[0,1], larger values indicate stronger inter-electrode synchrony within the corresponding band.

The wPLI further strengthens the robustness of phase-relation estimates by computing a weighted average based on the sign consistency of the cross-spectrum's imaginary part, thereby down-weighting near-zero and π-phase couplings and enhancing sensitivity to true phase-lagged interactions. Its computation is given by:(3)wPLIij(f)=N−1∑t=1N|imag(S)|sign(imag(S))N−1∑t=1N|imag(S)|Here, N denotes the total number of EEG samples; imag(·) denotes the imaginary part of the cross-spectral density between the two electrodes; and sign(·) denotes the sign function. By definition, wPLIij(f)∈[0,1], larger values indicate stronger inter-electrode phase synchrony.

Based on the constructed connectivity matrices, brain-network topology was quantified using graph-theoretical analyses. All analyses were implemented in MATLAB R2021b using the GRETNA toolbox (Graph Theoretical Network Analysis, v2.0.0) (Wang et al., 2015). To reduce dependence on arbitrary single-threshold choices, sparsity-based thresholding was applied from 0.10 to 0.50 in 0.01 increments (retaining the top 10-50% of edges), yielding 41 sparsity levels. Only positive edges were retained, and weighted-graph analyses were performed to preserve continuous connection strengths. To evaluate topological optimization, each empirical network was compared with 100°-preserving, randomly rewired surrogates. Global network integration and segregation were assessed using characteristic path length (Lp), global efficiency (Eg), clustering coefficient (Cp), local efficiency (Eloc), and small-worldness (σ). Nodal metrics included degree centrality (Dc), betweenness centrality (Bc), nodal efficiency (Ne), nodal clustering coefficient (NCp), and nodal local efficiency (NLe). Metrics across sparsity levels were integrated using area under the curve (AUC) for robust comparisons.

Statistical testing and effect-size estimation. To evaluate differences in brain functional connectivity across sweetness ratios, we adopted a paired-samples t-test framework that properly accounts for the repeated-measures structure of our data. For each participant, connectivity metrics (global and nodal) were first averaged across the 8 repeated sessions within each odor condition, producing one representative value per participant per condition (n = 16). Using these session-averaged values, paired t-tests were performed to compare conditions: for global network metrics, tests were conducted for all pairwise comparisons among the four odor conditions (6 contrasts total); for nodal/network attributes, tests were performed at each electrode location (n = 21) for all condition pairs. To address multiple comparisons, false discovery rate (FDR) control was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. For global metrics, FDR correction spanned six pairwise contrasts per metric; for nodal metrics, correction covered all electrode-contrast combinations (21 electrodes × 6 contrasts = 126 tests). Statistical significance was set at FDR-adjusted q < 0.05.

To quantify effect size, we computed Cohen's d as a standardized measure:(4)d=MdiffSDdiffHere, Mdiff and SDdiff denote the mean and standard deviation of the paired differences, respectively. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks: small (|d| ≈ 0.2), medium (C≈0.2|d| ≈ 0.5), and large (C≈0.2|d| ≈ 0.8).

EEG source localization analysis framework

To investigate the neural sources of odor-sweetness interactions, we employed EEG source localization to reconstruct the cortical distribution of electrical activity. Source analysis comprised two key steps: forward modeling and inverse estimation. The forward model was built from high-resolution structural MRI, where image segmentation yielded a multilayer head model comprising scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and cortex. Tissue conductivities were set to 1 S/m (scalp), 0.0125 S/m (skull), and 1 S/m (brain). The source model used cortical surface dipoles with orientations constrained to the cortical normal (normal to cortex). Boundary element method (BEM) computations modeled field propagation from sources to sensors, yielding the lead-field matrix L that specifies the contribution of each putative source to each EEG electrode.

The inverse problem was formulated as a linear system Y=LX+N, where Y denotes the measured EEG data, L is the lead-field matrix, X represents the unknown source activity, and N represents the sensor noise. We first obtained an initial estimate using minimum-norm imaging (MNI), which penalizes the L2 norm of the source vector and is well-suited for distributed source localization. We then applied standardized low-resolution electromagnetic tomography for variance normalization of the minimum-norm solution. Specifically, sLORETA uses the resolution kernel to standardize depth- and location-dependent variance, thereby reducing spatial bias and improving anatomical specificity across cortical locations. Under idealized assumptions, sLORETA achieves zero localization error in theory; in practice, it yields unbiased peak localization and is appropriate for identifying odor-evoked cortical activations.

Using this source-localization framework, scalp EEG was projected back into cortical space to identify differentially activated regions across sweetness conditions, thereby providing an anatomical substrate for connectivity analyses and elucidating the neural circuitry underlying odor-sweetness interactions.

Feature selection and classification

To evaluate how graph-theoretical network metrics represent odor-sweetness interaction conditions, we systematically analyzed ten features, comprising five global and five nodal metrics. Each metric was computed across 41 sparsity levels and summarized using an area-under-the-curve (AUC) measure, integrating feature trajectories across sparsity. The AUC captures overall patterns and reduces dependence on any single threshold, and was computed via the trapezoidal rule:(5)AUC=∑i=14012(f(θi)+f(θi+1))×(θi+1−θi)Here, f(θi) denotes the feature value evaluated at sparsity threshold θi. AUC values were subjected to paired t-tests with FDR correction to evaluate discriminative capacity across conditions.

For machine-learning classification, only raw features at the 41 sparsity levels were used to avoid redundancy from cross-threshold aggregation. Four classifiers—random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and logistic regression (LR)—were applied, with all features z-scored to remove scale effects. A four-fold cross-validation scheme was implemented at the subject level: 16 participants were divided into four folds, with each fold using 12 subjects for training and 4 for testing, ensuring independence between training and test data. For each sparsity configuration, classifiers were trained and evaluated across all folds, and standard performance metrics were computed on held-out test data. To explore the effect of different sparsity ranges on classification performance, four threshold sampling schemes were defined: Top-5 corresponding to thresholds 0.1-0.14, Top-10 to 0.1-0.19, Top-20 to 0.1-0.29, and Top-41 to 0.1-0.5. In addition, to rigorously validate the statistical significance of the classification performance and mitigate potential overfitting risks associated with high-dimensional feature spaces, a permutation test was conducted. The experimental condition labels were randomly shuffled 1000 times. For each permutation, the best-performing classifier was retrained and evaluated using the identical subject-level four-fold cross-validation procedure to generate a null distribution of accuracies. The empirical p-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted accuracies that equaled or exceeded the true classification accuracy.

To visualize class separability, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) was applied to both raw and model-transformed feature representations, including RF leaf indices, SVM decision-function values, KNN distances, and LR class-probability scores. t-SNE parameters were set to perplexity 10, 750 iterations, and a random seed of 42. This approach allowed us to assess discriminative representations learned by classifiers, identify network metrics most sensitive to odor-sweetness interactions, and derive data-driven feature priorities for mechanistic interpretation.

Results

Psychophysical and statistical modeling of odor-induced sweetness enhancement

Sixteen participants rated the perceived sweetness of sucrose solutions under four odor conditions (S3, S3-O1, S3-O10, S3-O100). Mean ratings are presented in Fig. 2. The intermediate odor concentration (S3-O10) elicited the highest sweetness rating (6.95 ± 0.48), compared with S3 (6.06 ± 0.43), S3-O1 (6.40 ± 0.45), and S3-O100 (5.73 ± 0.44). Frequency-specific analysis revealed that alpha-band connectivity showed the largest modulation by odor concentration. Alpha-band mean connectivity increased from 0.368 (S3) to 0.446 (S3-O10) with iCoh (+21.20%) and from 0.558 to 0.607 with wPLI (+8.7%), then declined under S3-O100 to 0.320 (iCoh) and 0.436 (wPLI) (−13.0% and −21.86% vs. baseline). Other bands (delta, theta, beta, gamma) exhibited smaller changes.

Low-frequency bands (delta and theta) showed limited, locally confined connectivity changes, whereas beta and gamma connectivity changes were spatially restricted and generally weaker than alpha and broadband. Alpha-band connectivity dominated cross-modal coordination, consistent with behavioral sweetness enhancement, whereas excessive odor input (S3-O100) reduced connectivity across all bands, indicating disruption of interareal coordination.

These results suggest a frequency-specific, concentration-dependent modulation of functional connectivity, with alpha-band phase synchronization closely tracking the inverted-U behavioral pattern of odor-induced sweetness enhancement.

Characterization of network topology and topological hubs

To further delineate the topological networks supporting odor-sweetness integration, the top 5% strongest electrode pairs (by connectivity strength) were extracted for each condition. Their spatial distributions were visualized using circular connectograms and scalp topographies (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). This thresholding strategy focuses the analysis on the most robust functional couplings, enabling the identification of key inter-regional pathways that play a central role in cross-modal processing.Fig. 4PCC - based connectograms across bands and conditions.Fig. 5iCoh - based connectograms across bands and conditions.Fig. 6wPLI - based connectograms across bands and conditions.

Across all frequency bands and connectivity metrics, these top-tier connections were predominantly localized to temporal electrodes (T3, T4, T5, and T6) and central-parietal sites (C3, C4, P3, and P4). Beyond these intra-temporal and centro-parietal links, prominent long-range connections were observed, particularly fronto-occipital pairs (e.g., Fp1 to O1, Fp2 to O2), which consistently ranked within the top 5% across multiple frequency bands. These patterns identify the temporal region as a primary topological hub within the functional network during the task.

Longitudinal comparisons across the S3, S3-O1, and S3-O10 conditions revealed a concentration-dependent pattern of network reconfiguration. Functional coupling between temporal electrodes and frontal, occipital, and parietal sites strengthened progressively with increasing perceived sweetness, reaching a peak under the S3-O10 condition. This structural augmentation exhibited distributed consistency across metrics and frequency bands rather than crystallizing into a single, fixed electrode pair. For instance, within the alpha band, the most prominent temporal-to-frontal connection was identified at T3-Fp1 when measured by PCC, whereas it shifted to T3-Fz under iCoh analysis. Within each method and frequency band, at least one temporal-parietal and one temporal-occipital pair reliably appeared in the top 5% threshold and exhibited concentration-dependent modulation. Taken together, these results indicate that the observed enhancement involves the flexible recruitment of multiple temporal nodes that dynamically couple to distinct cortical targets.

sLORETA-based cortical source localization and activation profiles

Standardized low-resolution electromagnetic tomography was applied to estimate the spatial distribution and magnitude of cortical sources under the four stimulation conditions. To facilitate quantitative comparisons, a uniform amplitude threshold was set at 50% of the maximum activation, corresponding to a source-space current density of 7.5 × 10−10 A/m2. This thresholding strategy effectively suppressed background noise, isolating cortical regions that exceeded half-maximal activation.

As depicted in Fig. 7, the temporal cortices exhibited the strongest and most extensive activations across all four conditions, particularly within the bilateral inferior, middle, and superior temporal divisions. Frontal activations, primarily centered in the bilateral inferior frontal gyrus and adjacent areas, displayed the second-highest activation magnitudes. Additionally, significant but comparatively weaker activations were observed in the medial and superior parietal regions, lateral and medial occipital cortices, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.Fig. 7sLORETA source maps for a randomly selected participant under four conditions: (a) S3, (b) S3-O1, (c) S3-O10, (d) S3-O100.

Frequency-specific analyses indicated that alpha-band metrics displayed the most pronounced modulation. For instance, PCC alpha-band degree centrality rose from 1.353 to 1.437 (+6.2%) in S3-O10, whereas changes in delta (+3.1%), theta (+4.4%), and gamma (+2.4%) bands were weaker. To evaluate statistical significance, pairwise paired-samples t-tests were performed for each metric at every frequency band and sparsity threshold, applying FDR control for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Table S1). Only limited significant differences were observed, and no reproducible spatial patterns emerged. Across all frequency bands and 41 thresholds, certain contrasts met the FDR-adjusted significance level (p < 0.05); however, these significant effects were highly scattered and did not form contiguous spatial clusters. For example, the nodal clustering coefficient showed a significant difference between S3 and S3-O1 at node 20 (t = −2.67, p = 0.018, FDR-adjusted), whereas adjacent nodes and other contrasts lacked significance. Similar dispersion was evident across the other connectivity methods and frequency bands. Further inspection revealed a slightly higher incidence of significant findings at lower sparsity levels (10%-12%) compared to higher sparsity thresholds.

Multivariate discrimination and permutation validation

Although univariate statistical analyses revealed only sporadic and irregular significant differences, multivariate pattern analysis successfully discriminated among the four stimulation conditions. Fig. 9(c) presents the Random Forest classification accuracies across six frequency bands for four sparsity-sampling schemes under the wPLI approach. To verify that these high accuracies were not driven by overfitting, permutation testing was applied to the classification frameworks. Evaluating the models against 1000 label shuffles generated null distributions closely aligned with theoretical chance levels. Discussion

Psychophysical mechanisms of the non-monotonic dose-response pattern

The sensory evaluation revealed a non-monotonic, inverted-U relationship between odor intensity and perceived sweetness. The intermediate odor concentration (S3-O10) elicited the highest sweetness rating (6.95 ± 0.48), whereas lower (S3-O1, 6.40 ± 0.45) and higher (S3-O100, 5.73 ± 0.44) concentrations produced smaller increases or suppression. Linear mixed-effects modeling confirmed these differences, with S3-O10 significantly higher than the baseline (β = 0.889, z = 16.48, p < 0.001), S3-O1 also exceeding S3 (β = 0.345, z = 6.40, p < 0.001), and S3-O100 lower than S3 (β = −0.328, z = −6.08, p < 0.001) (Table 1). Pairwise contrasts further supported the concentration-dependent pattern (Table 2).

The observed enhancement at the intermediate concentration corresponds to a 14.7% increase relative to the baseline. This effect likely exceeds the just-noticeable difference for sucrose perception in typical psychophysical tests, indicating a perceptually meaningful enhancement. However, individual participants showed variability in sensitivity, suggesting that odor-induced sweetness enhancement may be modulated by subject-specific gustatory and olfactory thresholds (Valicente et al., 2023). Mapping these ratings to equivalent sucrose concentrations indicates that 100 ppm EMB could maintain perceived sweetness while allowing for a reduction of approximately 13.7% in sugar content. This quantitative representation highlights the practical significance of the effect for beverage formulation (Ge et al., 2024).

Mechanistically, this non-monotonic enhancement aligns with the optimal-range principle of multisensory integration. Moderate odor input may facilitate co-activation along gustatory-olfactory pathways, amplifying sweetness perception without exceeding attentional or neural processing limits, a mechanism reported in the anterior insula and orbitofrontal cortex (Ai and Han, 2022). Odor-induced taste enhancement is a robust cross-modal phenomenon whereby the perceived intensity of basic tastes can be augmented by specific odors (Brunert and Rothermel, 2020). Near-optimal cross-modal input levels have been shown to evoke superadditive neural responses—multisensory activity exceeding the arithmetic sum of unisensory responses—thereby sharpening perceptual clarity and shortening response latencies (Chandrasekaran, 2017).

Taken together, these findings indicate that the inverted-U dose-response pattern observed in the current study is consistent with previous reports of odor-taste interactions, confirming the generalizability of this non-monotonic effect across different stimuli and experimental settings (Wang et al., 2018).

Frequency-specific phase coordination in cross-modal flavor integration

Phase-based connectivity metrics (iCoh and wPLI) tracked odor-dependent modulation of functional networks more closely than amplitude-based PCC, which showed less alignment with behavioral sweetness enhancement. This indicates that precise temporal coordination across cortical regions underlies effective cross-modal odor-sweetness integration. Amplitude-based measures are more susceptible to non-specific co-fluctuations and volume conduction (Yoshinaga et al., 2020).

Alpha-band connectivity exhibited the strongest modulation, whereas delta/theta and beta/gamma bands showed smaller and more localized changes. This pattern suggests functional specialization of oscillatory bands in multisensory integration, with low-frequency bands supporting temporal scaffolding of unisensory inputs, higher-frequency bands supporting local feature binding, and alpha coordinating distributed network interactions (Keller et al., 2017). Alpha phase may provide a temporal gating window for cross-modal inputs, aligning cortical excitability with multisensory input (Cecere et al., 2015).

Connectivity changes followed an inverted-U pattern across odor concentrations, mirroring behavioral sweetness enhancement. Moderate odor input (S3-O10) maximally engaged phase-synchronized networks, whereas excessive odor input (S3-O100) reduced connectivity across all bands. This suggests that optimal levels of olfactory stimulation recruit distributed cortical networks, while excessive input may overtax attentional or inhibitory resources (Guo et al., 2024).

The convergence of multiple frequency bands and metrics in tracking behavioral enhancement suggests that odor-induced sweetness perception emerges from distributed interactions among temporal, frontal, parietal, and occipital regions. Alpha-band phase synchronization closely tracked behavioral enhancement, indicating its potential as a neural marker of successful cross-modal integration (Xia et al., 2025).

Cortical sources and network hubs of odor-sweetness integration

The topological network analysis and source localization provide converging evidence for the neural substrates mediating odor-induced sweetness enhancement. Topologically, the strongest functional couplings were consistently anchored at temporal and central-parietal electrodes, forming a flexible architecture that peaked under the optimal intermediate odor concentration (S3-O10). Concurrently, sLORETA identified the bilateral temporal cortices as the primary generators of this concentration-dependent activity, with secondary contributions from the inferior frontal gyrus.

These spatial patterns align with established neuroanatomical pathways for gustatory and olfactory processing. Neuroimaging meta-analyses indicate that gustatory stimulation reliably engages the bilateral insula and overlying opercula, which project to temporal and centro-parietal scalp sites (Klimesch et al., 2007). Furthermore, medial temporal structures, particularly the piriform cortex, are critical for olfactory processing (Liao et al., 2024). The prominent temporal activation observed in the present study likely reflects joint activity within this insula-piriform circuit. Additionally, the superior temporal sulcus (STS), located within the temporal lobe, is a well-documented multisensory hotspot capable of generating superadditive responses to cross-modal stimuli (Poo and Isaacson, 2009; Zhou et al., 2019). The synchronous engagement of these regions under S3-O10 suggests that moderate odor input optimally recruits this integrated network to facilitate gustatory-olfactory binding.

Beyond the temporal region, the engagement of fronto-occipital long-range connections and inferior frontal sources points to the recruitment of ventral attentional networks, which support multisensory salience detection and semantic matching (Das et al., 2026; Vossel et al., 2014). Furthermore, the relative stability of activations in frontal, parietal, and occipital cortices, contrasted with the marked concentration-dependent plasticity of the temporal regions, supports a hierarchical architecture of perceptual processing. Primary and secondary sensory integration regions exhibit high sensitivity to cross-modal stimulus intensity, whereas higher-order associative regions provide a more stable functional scaffold for cognitive appraisal (Wei et al., 2024).

While these source-level findings align robustly with established multisensory circuits, they should be interpreted within the inherent spatial constraints of scalp EEG. Specifically, the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem limits the precise spatial dissociation of superficial cortical activity from deep-structure generators (e.g., the insula and piriform cortex). Therefore, while the temporal region emerges as a primary functional node in the current analysis, its activation likely reflects an aggregate of these anatomically overlapping networks. Similarly, while phase-based connectivity metrics (iCoh, wPLI) were explicitly employed to effectively suppress spurious zero-lag synchrony, the inherent biophysics of EEG dictate that minor residual volume conduction cannot be entirely ruled out. Nevertheless, the robust concentration-dependent convergence across independent metrics strongly suggests that these large-scale topological dynamics reflect genuine cross-modal neural coordination rather than methodological artifacts.

Network topology dynamics and machine learning classification

Univariate statistical analyses revealed that individual graph-theoretic metrics exhibited modest concentration-dependent changes, typically remaining below 8%. This relatively small magnitude of change raises the critical question of whether these topological shifts reflect genuine functional network reconfiguration or merely fluctuate within the bounds of measurement noise. However, the multivariate pattern analysis provided compelling evidence for their functional significance. By integrating these high-dimensional topological features, the Random Forest classifier successfully discriminated the four odor-sweetness conditions with a peak accuracy of 87.5%. This striking contrast between univariate insensitivity and multivariate discriminability suggests that cross-modal integration is not driven by massive, global network reconfigurations or the isolated activation of a single hard-wired pathway. Instead, it emerges from subtle, finely tuned, and distributed coordination across multiple regions, frequency bands, and topological attributes (Kuntzelman et al., 2021). The distinct clustering observed in the t-SNE feature space further corroborates that these high-dimensional graph metrics collectively encode robust, condition-specific neural representations.

The classification performance across different sparsity schemes offers additional mechanistic insights. The model achieved optimal performance at lower sparsity thresholds (Top-5 scheme) and declined progressively as higher thresholds (Top-41) were enforced. In graph theory, higher thresholds retain only the most powerful, densely connected core links, while discarding medium-to-weak connections. The superiority of the low-sparsity scheme indicates that multisensory binding heavily relies on preserving these numerous medium-strength connections. Rather than solely depending on a few primary sensory pathways, effective odor-sweetness integration requires the flexible recruitment of secondary, cross-network interactions that are functionally meaningful despite their lower absolute connectivity weights (Santarnecchi et al., 2014).

While the machine-learning framework demonstrated high discriminative capacity, the application of high-dimensional feature spaces to a relatively small sample size (n = 16) inherently introduces the risk of overfitting. To rigorously address this limitation, a permutation testing protocol (1000 label shuffles) was incorporated alongside the subject-level cross-validation. The empirical results (all p < 0.001) confirmed that the observed classification accuracies significantly exceeded theoretical chance levels. This stringent statistical validation demonstrates that the models captured genuine neurophysiological boundaries rather than memorizing sample-specific noise, substantiating the reliability of the topological features as biomarkers for odor-induced sweetness enhancement (Valente et al., 2021).

The confusion matrix revealed that the highest misclassification rates occurred between the baseline (S3) and the highest odor concentration (S3-O100). Behaviorally, these two conditions correspond to the lowest perceived sweetness levels, suggesting that gustatory and olfactory inputs are integrated in a highly nonlinear manner, leading to overlapping perceptual and neural states at both extremes of the dose-response curve. Furthermore, it is crucial to acknowledge that the observed EEG signal modulations may not exclusively reflect pure sensory integration. Factors such as attention allocation, arousal, and expectancy are known to profoundly influence large-scale brain networks, particularly within the alpha band (Bacigalupo and Luck, 2022). For instance, the excessively strong odor in the S3-O100 condition may have acted as an unexpected or highly salient distractor, shifting spatial attention away from the gustatory stimulus and altering general arousal levels. Consequently, the distinct topological reconfiguration and reduced connectivity observed under S3-O100 likely represent a convolution of diminished multisensory binding and dynamic shifts in top-down attentional resources. Future paradigms employing continuous behavioral monitoring and autonomic arousal measures are necessary to disentangle the pure cross-modal sensory components from these higher-order cognitive confounds (Ahmed et al., 2023).

Limitations and potential applications

Limitations. While this study provides objective neurophysiological insights into concentration-dependent odor-induced sweetness enhancement, several methodological and experimental limitations must be explicitly acknowledged. First, regarding the neuroimaging methodology, the intrinsic spatial resolution of the 21-channel EEG system and the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem inherently restrict the precision of cortical source localization. Consequently, accurately dissociating deep-structure generators (e.g., the insula and piriform cortex) from superficial cortical activity remains challenging based solely on scalp EEG. Future research must incorporate multimodal approaches, such as concurrent EEG-fMRI, to provide millimeter-level spatial validation of these multisensory networks. Second, the relatively small sample size (n = 16) may limit the generalizability of the current findings. Subsequent studies should employ larger, demographically diverse cohorts to systematically account for individual variability in chemosensory sensitivity and cross-modal integration thresholds.

Third, the experimental parameters were restricted to a single odorant (EMB) and a fixed sucrose concentration (6% w/v). Additional investigations are necessary to map the cross-modal dose-response dynamics across a broader spectrum of sweet-congruent odors and varying tastant intensities. Finally, from an ecological validity perspective, the use of simple laboratory-based aqueous solutions does not adequately represent the complexity of real food matrices. Physicochemical factors such as viscosity, acidity, fat, and protein content can significantly alter flavor release and cross-modal perceptual interactions. Addressing these complex matrix effects is critical for safely translating these neuro-sensory findings to commercial food products.

Potential Applications. Despite these limitations, the current findings offer valuable quantitative benchmarks and methodological frameworks for food formulation. The identification of an optimal EMB concentration (100 ppm) that enhanced perceived sweetness by 14.7%-equivalent to a ∼13.7% reduction in sucrose content—establishes clear dose-response boundaries to maximize sensory impact while preventing counterproductive suppression. Furthermore, the robust modulation of alpha-band phase synchronization (+21.2% at optimal concentration) serves as an objective neural biomarker for this enhancement. When integrated with the multivariate machine-learning classification framework, this approach demonstrates the feasibility of developing rapid, consumer-independent neuro-evaluative tools for flavor optimization. Integrating such EEG-based biomarkers with traditional sensory profiling and longitudinal consumer acceptance studies could substantially advance the targeted development of reduced-sugar products, maintaining palatability while supporting global public health objectives.

Conclusion

This study presents an EEG-based integrated framework to evaluate and optimize odor-induced sweetness enhancement in food systems, providing a neurobiologically informed approach for sugar reduction. The main contributions are as follows:(1)Quantitative guidance for sugar reduction. EMB enhanced the perceived sweetness of a 6% sucrose solution by 14.7% at optimal concentrations (100 ppm), equivalent to a ∼13.7% reduction in sugar, while excessive concentrations suppressed sweetness. This establishes a practical concentration window for odor-sugar formulation, offering direct utility for product development.(2)Objective validation of flavor modulation. Functional connectivity and machine-learning analyses demonstrated that neural responses reliably track odor-induced sweetness changes, confirming that EEG-based metrics can serve as objective biomarkers for cross-modal flavor enhancement. This approach complements traditional sensory panels by providing a rapid, consumer-independent assessment of odor-sugar interactions.(3)Distributed network mechanisms inform odor-sugar optimization. The study identified coordinated patterns of brain activity underlying sweetness perception, indicating that effective odor-sugar optimization relies on distributed coordination across strongly coupled sensory networks rather than any single pathway. These findings underscore the importance of considering large-scale sensory integration, rather than isolated sensory cues, when designing reduced-sugar products.
