Bone dimensional stability with versus without a stent following apically repositioned flap and single-stage implant placement: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Ahmed Hamdy, Jazia A Alblowi, Dalia Ghalwash
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaintaining peri-implant bone and tissue health is essential for long-term implant success. The effectiveness of adjunctive stent stabilization is still unclear, though the apically repositioned flap (ARF) improves soft tissue conditions. Therefore, this study assessed whether the use of stents during single-stage placement improves peri-implant bone changes and clinical outcomes following ARF. This randomized clinical trial included twenty partially edentulous patients who were divided into two groups: ARF with stent stabilization (n = 10) and ARF using conventional suturing (n = 10). At baseline, three months, and six months, radiographic bone dimensions measured by cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) were recorded, along with clinical parameters including probing depth (PD), plaque index (PI), and bleeding index (BI). Appropriate statistical tests were used for intragroup and intergroup comparisons (α = 0.05). No statistically significant intergroup differences were observed for any clinical or radiographic outcomes (p > 0.05). Over the course of the trial, PD, PI, and BI remained stable in both groups. Neither group’s bone width changed significantly, according to CBCT data. The stent group’s bone height remained stable (p = 0.216), the control group’s bone height significantly decreased from baseline (p < 0.001), mostly in the early stages of healing. Within the limitations of this randomized clinical trial, ARF performed during single-stage implant placement produced anticipated early bone remodeling and stable peri-implant clinical conditions. Introduction

Long-term success of dental implant therapy extends beyond osseointegration to include establishing and maintaining the stability of peri-implant hard and soft tissues. In this context, the quality and quantity of peri-implant soft tissue, particularly the presence of adequate keratinized mucosa, have gained increasing attention. A growing body of research shows how crucial sufficient keratinized mucosa is for preserving peri-implant health, promoting plaque control, and improving patient comfort (Wennström and Derks 2012, Lin et al. 2013, Souza et al. 2016). Conversely, the association between inadequate keratinized tissue and an increased risk of peri-implant inflammation, mucosal recession, and patient-reported discomfort is further reinforced by more recent systematic reviews and consensus reports (Thoma et al. 2018, Tavelli et al. 2021, Monje and Blasi 2019, Avila-Ortiz et al. 2014). Although keratinized mucosa may not be technically necessary for implant life, its presence seems to be essential for long-term health and peri-implant tissue stability (Klinge et al. 2012, Derks and Tomasi 2015).

Peri-implant diseases, including mucositis and peri-implantitis, remain a major concern in implant dentistry and are strongly influenced by local tissue conditions and plaque control (Heitz-Mayfield and Salvi 2018, Schwarz et al. 2018, Figuero et al. 2014). The prevention of these conditions relies not only on effective maintenance protocols but also on the establishment of favorable peri-implant soft tissue architecture at the time of implant placement. In this context, soft tissue augmentation procedures have been widely advocated to optimize peri-implant conditions, with systematic reviews and studies demonstrating their positive impact on peri-implant health and tissue stability (Thoma et al. 2018, Hamdy and Ghalwash 2023, Zucchelli et al. 2019, Hamdy and Ghalwash 2022).

An established mucogingival surgical procedure called apically repositioned flap (ARF) is used to reposition the mucogingival junction apically in order to expand the width of keratinized tissue. Considering the convenience of application, predictability, and avoidance of donor site morbidity associated with grafting procedures, it has been employed extensively around both teeth and implants (Carnio and Camargo 2006, Wang et al. 2025). According to recent clinical trials, ARF can significantly and steadily increase the keratinized mucosa around implants, improving the stability of the peri-implant tissue (Tavelli et al. 2021, Zucchelli et al. 2019). Consequently, ARF represents a minimally invasive alternative to soft tissue grafting in selected clinical scenarios.

Maintaining flap stability throughout early healing is still a crucial aspect affecting clinical outcomes, notwithstanding its efficacy. Healing and tissue maturation may be hampered by micromovement, dead space generation, and insufficient flap adaptation. Periosteal sutures, compression techniques, and the use of prefabricated or customized stents are some of the methods that have been suggested to improve flap stabilization (Thoma et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2025). Among these methods, stents are designed to apply gentle pressure to the repositioned flap, reduce dead space, and maintain the ideal apical position during the early stages of healing. Despite their potential benefits, there is still little conflicting clinical data to support their regular usage. While some studies claim no additional advantage over traditional suturing procedures, others suggest better stabilization and patient comfort. Furthermore, high-quality evidence, particularly randomized clinical trials, evaluating their impact on peri-implant soft tissue and bone remodeling, is limited and inconclusive (Hamdy et al. 2021, Bouffleur et al. 2026, Sabri et al. 2026). Moreover, peri-implant bone remodeling after placing implants is affected by various factors, such as the method of surgery, the design of flaps, and the management of the soft tissue (Thoma et al. 2018, Tavelli et al. 2021, Sanz-Martín et al. 2018).

Simultaneously, peri-implant bone remodeling after the implant insertion is considered a multifactorial and dynamic process. Early crestal bone alterations can be regarded as physiological healing, but the reduction of unwarranted bone loss is a major goal in implant treatment (Sanz-Martín et al. 2018, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015). Randomized clinical studies and studies based on cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) have shown that the majority of bone remodeling takes place in the initial healing phase and is predetermined by such factors as surgical trauma, implant location, and the features of the peri-implant soft tissues (Sabri et al. 2026, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015, Taheri et al. 2020). This interaction of soft tissue management and bone stability further emphasizes the need to optimize surgical procedures.

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized clinical trial to specifically report CBCT-based peri-implant bone dimensional outcomes following apically repositioned flap surgery with adjunctive stent stabilization during single-stage implant placement. While a previous publication from the same randomized clinical trial reported the clinical outcomes of this intervention, the present study presents a distinct predefined radiographic analysis using CBCT to evaluate peri-implant bone dimensional changes, thereby providing complementary evidence regarding the biological effects and clinical value of adjunctive stent stabilization.

Given the limited and conflicting evidence regarding the role of stent-assisted stabilization, the current randomized clinical trial aimed to assess peri-implant clinical parameters and CBCT-based bone dimensional changes following ARF performed with or without a stent during single-stage implant placement. It was hypothesized that the use of an adjunctive stent would not provide any further clinical or radiological benefits when compared to traditional suturing.

Materials and methods

This study was designed as a two-arm, parallel-group superiority randomized controlled clinical trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The study involved 20 partially edentulous patients requiring restoration of missing teeth in the premolar-molar area of either arch. Patient recruitment was conducted from December 2016 to November 2018 at the outpatient clinic of the Oral Medicine and Periodontology Department, Faculty of Dentistry, Ain Shams University, and at the British University in Egypt located in Cairo, Egypt. All the participants were informed about the study protocol, and informed consent was signed in writing. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov in December 2016 (registration number: NCT03754894).

This manuscript reports predefined radiographic outcomes from the registered randomized clinical trial. Clinical outcomes related to peri-implant soft tissue augmentation using the same trial cohort have been reported previously (Hamdy et al. 2021), whereas the CBCT-based bone dimensional outcomes presented herein have not been previously published. This randomized clinical trial was designed and reported according to the CONSORT 2010 Statement. The primary outcome was peri-implant bone dimensional change (bone height and bone width) assessed by CBCT. Secondary outcomes included probing depth, plaque index, bleeding index, and postoperative complications.

Randomization

Eligible patients were randomly assigned to two equal groups with the help of a computer-generated randomization list using online platform (Randomizer.org). Serially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes were used to conceal allocation and were organized by an investigator who was not involved in the recruitment or treatment of the patients.

Participants were assigned to either the stent group (ARF with stent stabilization, n = 10) or the control group (ARF with conventional suturing, n = 10). In the stent group, after the implant placement, the ARF stabilized with a plastic stent and sutures, in the control group, with sutures only.

Blinding

Participants and the operator as well as the outcome assessor could not be blinded due to the nature of the surgical intervention. Nonetheless, the statistician was blinded to group allocation during data analysis.

The clinical assessment was conducted after 3 and 6 months of the operation, which entailed probing depth, plaque index, and bleeding index. CBCT was used to evaluate bone dimensions radiographically.

During the study period, the adverse events, as well as the unintended effects, such as postoperative infection, flap necrosis, delayed healing, and patient-reported complications, were observed and documented.

The inclusion criteria included healthy adults between the ages of 20 and 50 years with an attached gingival width of less than 3 mm. The exclusion criteria involved heavy smokers (> 10 cigarettes/day), pregnant women, those with poor oral hygiene, and those who are not willing to perform oral hygiene.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined a priori based on the primary outcome of peri-implant bone dimensional changes (bone height and bone width). The calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1, Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) for comparison of two independent means (two-tailed test).

The effect size was derived from a human CBCT-based randomized clinical trial by Puisys and Linkevičius (2020) (Puisys and Linkevicius 2015), which reported a mean difference (Δ) of approximately 0.8 mm in crestal bone changes with a standard deviation (σ) of 0.6 mm during early healing, corresponding to a standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of 1.33. Stent design

The stent was fabricated from polypropylene (Louis Button II; Dentis Co., Korea) and is commercially available in diameters of 3.8 and 4.8 mm to accommodate different healing abutment sizes. In the present study, the 4.8-mm stent was used. The stent incorporates buccal and lingual wings measuring 2 mm and 1 mm, respectively, which apply gentle pressure to stabilize the repositioned flap. The central cylindrical component (4.8 mm in diameter and 2 mm in height) fits securely over the stock healing abutment through a friction-fit mechanism, ensuring stable stent retention during healing.

CBCT imaging acquisition

CBCT imaging was performed using a Planmeca ProMax® 3D Mid (Planmeca Oy, Helsinki, Finland) with standardized exposure settings (90 kVp, 10 mA, 200-µm voxel size, 5 × 5 cm field of view).

Surgical procedures

Local anesthesia was achieved using a field block of 4% articaine hydrochloride with epinephrine (1:100,000), administered buccally and palatally at maxillary surgical sites, or via inferior alveolar nerve block supplemented with buccal infiltration at mandibular surgical sites.

The surgical approach involved a papilla-sparing para-crestal incision extending to bone to preserve and use the keratinized mucosa from the lingual/palatal aspect. A para-crestal incision was performed, leaving approximately 4 mm of keratinized tissue at the buccal flap. A split-thickness flap was then elevated, followed by a vertical releasing incision to facilitate maximum apical relocation of the keratinized mucosa. Following complete reflection of the full/partial-thickness flap, a dental implant of appropriate diameter and length (Jdental care – Italy), according to site requirements, was positioned in the correct prosthetically driven site. A healing abutment was then connected and secured using a screwdriver.

In the stent group, the ARF was stabilized around the abutment with a ready-made plastic stent of appropriate diameter (Louis button II – dentis co – korea), designed to fit snugly over it. Gentle vertical pressure was applied to seat the stent fully, thereby maintaining the position of the buccal flap and minimizing dead space.

In the control group, the ARF was secured around the abutment using simple interrupted sutures placed at the mesial and distal vertical incisions, or a combination of simple interrupted and periosteal sutures to ensure optimal flap stability (Fig. 1).

All patients received antibiotics for seven days postoperatively (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid 1 g twice daily) and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (ibuprofen twice daily). Patients were told to avoid rinsing during the first day, after which they began rinsing twice daily with 0.12% chlorhexidine Di gluconate mouthwash. Mechanical plaque control in the surgical area was avoided for 2 weeks, and patients were commanded to avoid brushing the surgical site for two weeks.

In the stent group, the stent was removed after 10 days, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. In the control group, suture removal was done after two weeks, and patients were instructed to resume tooth brushing using the Bass technique.

All patients were recalled after 3 months for taking records and final crown fabrication.

Examiner calibration and reliability

Every clinical and radiographic measure was standardized in a calibration exercise before the study. All the evaluations were done by one examiner to reduce variability and to make the results consistent, having been trained on a small sample of patients not part of the study sample. The most important measurements, such as probing depth, plaque and bleeding indexes, as well as bone dimensions based on CBCT, were measured twice in a period of one week. The consistency and reproducibility of the measurement protocol were confirmed by the intra-examiner reliability of both continuous (ICC > 0.85) and categorical (κ > 0.80) variables.

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation values were calculated for each group and each assessed variable. Data distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests. Probing depth, bleeding index, and plaque index demonstrated a non-parametric distribution, whereas bone width and bone height showed a parametric distribution.

For non-parametric data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two independent groups, while the Friedman test was applied for comparisons among multiple related measurements. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for pairwise comparisons within related samples. For parametric data, the independent samples t-test was used to compare two independent groups, repeated measures ANOVA was used for comparisons among multiple related measurements, and the paired samples t-test was used for comparisons between two related measurements. Statistical comparisons were performed using appropriate parametric and non-parametric tests according to data distribution. Results were interpreted considering both statistical significance and the magnitude of observed clinical differences. The significance level was set at P ≤ 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics Version 20 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Where applicable, absolute mean differences between groups were considered to facilitate interpretation of the magnitude of observed changes.

Results

A total of 25 participants were assessed for eligibility, of whom 5 were excluded (3 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 2 declined to participate), resulting in 20 participants who were randomly allocated into two equal groups (n = 10 each). All randomized participants received the assigned intervention, with no losses to follow-up or discontinuations reported, and all were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). The mean age of the overall sample was 36.1 ± 9.1 years, with no statistically significant difference between the stent group (36.1 ± 9.7 years) and the control group (36.1 ± 9.4 years) (p > 0.05). In terms of gender, 60% of the total sample was represented by females, with 40% being males. In the stent group, males were 60% and females were 40%, and in the control group, there were 20% males and 80% females. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of gender (p > 0.05). Overall, the demographic features of the baseline were similar in the study groups (Table 1).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial evaluated whether adjunctive stent stabilization provides additional benefit when an ARF is performed during single-stage implant placement, with an emphasis on clinical parameters and peri-implant bone dimensional changes as assessed by CBCT. The primary finding is that, following a 6-month follow-up, stent use did not produce better radiographic or clinical results than traditional suturing. These findings suggest that, under the conditions evaluated, peri-implant healing may be influenced more strongly by biological processes and surgical technique than by supplementary mechanical stabilization (Thoma et al. 2018, Zucchelli et al. 2019, Hamdy et al. 2021, Sanz-Martín et al. 2018).

Radiographically, CBCT analysis showed that peri-implant bone dimensions were generally stable in both groups, which agrees with the expected physiological remodeling following the placement of implants. More importantly, the change in bone height over time was statistically non-significant in the stent group (p = 0.216), although the observed change over time did not reach statistical significance. Although no significant difference was demonstrated between 3 and 6 months, the control group showed statistically significant bone height reduction from baseline (p < 0.001), suggesting that bone dimensional changes occurred predominantly during the early healing period. This intra-group reduction did not significantly affect intergroup comparisons at any of the time points (all p > 0.05), indicating that these changes were not causing a clinically significant benefit of stent use.

The comparable outcomes observed between the two groups may be explained by several biological and technical factors. Although the use of a stabilization stent is theoretically expected to enhance flap adaptation and minimize micromotion during early healing, meticulous suturing techniques may already provide sufficient flap immobilization and primary soft tissue closure, thereby reducing the potential additional benefit of a supplementary stabilization device. Furthermore, early peri-implant bone changes are primarily governed by physiological remodeling processes associated with implant placement, establishment of the peri-implant biological width, surgical trauma, and host healing response rather than by minor differences in mechanical stabilization. The presence of healing abutments in both groups may have contributed to maintaining peri-implant mucosal contours and supporting soft tissue stability during healing, potentially reducing differences attributable to stent use. The small sample size (10 participants per group) may also have limited the ability to detect small between-group differences, particularly for secondary outcomes. Accordingly, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation in larger, adequately powered trials.

Although statistical comparisons did not demonstrate significant differences between groups, the clinical interpretation of the observed dimensional changes should be approached cautiously. Early peri-implant bone remodeling is expected following implant placement and may reflect physiological adaptation associated with surgical trauma and establishment of the peri-implant biological width. The numerical difference in bone height between groups at 6 months was 1.61 mm; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Given the small sample size and the fact that the study was not designed or powered to establish clinical equivalence or non-inferiority, the clinical significance of this numerical difference cannot be determined from the present findings. Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the two treatment approaches. Rather, the findings indicate that no statistically significant additional benefit of stent stabilization in preserving bone height was demonstrated within the six-month observation period. Larger, adequately powered studies with longer follow-up are required to determine whether the observed numerical difference has meaningful clinical implications.

These results are consistent with new CBCT-based and randomized clinical evidence that demonstrates that early peri-implant bone remodeling is a multifactorial, largely physiological process (Thoma et al. 2018, Sanz-Martín et al. 2018, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015). This has been reinforced by recent systematic reviews and CBCT-based studies that biologic remodeling processes and implant-related factors, and not the surgical adjuncts, are the key factors that cause changes in crestal bone level (Taheri et al. 2020, Linkevicius et al. 2015, Costa et al. 2023).

Surgical trauma, peri-implant biologic width establishment, implant–abutment interface characteristics, and host-related factors are known to have an impact on early crestal bone alterations (Derks and Tomasi 2015, Schwarz et al. 2018, Sanz-Martín et al. 2018). Additionally, randomized clinical trials show that the majority of vertical bone changes take place in the first few months after implant placement, after which bone levels often stabilize (Sabri et al. 2026, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015, Urban et al. 2019). Peri-implant bone stability is mostly established during early healing and is stable afterward when favorable tissue conditions are maintained, according to recent randomized and prospective clinical studies (Thoma et al. 2018, Valles et al. 2022). Furthermore, whereas soft tissue thickness and phenotype may influence crestal bone stability, surgical and implant-related factors seem to have a greater impact (Tavelli et al. 2021, Thoma et al. 2014, Hamdy et al. 2024). The current results are consistent with this body of research, indicating that the dynamics of early bone remodeling are not considerably affected by adjunctive stabilization.

Clinically, peri-implant tissue health in both groups remained stable over the observation period, as evidenced by consistent values for the bleeding index, plaque index, and probing depth. Both groups maintained favorable peri-implant clinical parameters throughout the observation period, with no statistically significant intergroup differences. Clinically, both groups maintained favorable peri-implant tissue parameters throughout the observation period, with no significant intergroup differences in probing depth, plaque index, or bleeding index. These findings indicate comparable short-term clinical conditions under the standardized plaque-control and maintenance protocols used in the study (Klinge et al. 2012, Derks and Tomasi 2015, Heitz-Mayfield and Salvi 2018, Schwarz et al. 2018, Figuero et al. 2014).

The absence of significant clinical or radiographic differences suggests that, under the conditions evaluated, meticulous flap management and suturing may provide sufficient stabilization without an evident additional benefit from routine stent use. However, this interpretation should be considered exploratory given the small sample size.

Hamdy et al. (2021), in a randomized controlled clinical trial evaluating the effect of a plastic stent used with an apically repositioned flap for peri-implant soft tissue augmentation, reported comparable peri-implant soft tissue outcomes between stent-assisted and conventional approaches. These findings suggest that meticulous surgical handling and adequate soft tissue stabilization achieved through conventional techniques may provide predictable healing outcomes without necessarily requiring additional stabilization devices (Hamdy et al. 2021). Furthermore, evidence from clinical studies investigating peri-implant soft tissue management emphasizes the importance of tissue phenotype and biological characteristics in determining peri-implant stability. Thoma et al. (2018), in a systematic review and meta-analysis, demonstrated that soft tissue augmentation procedures can improve peri-implant soft tissue parameters, highlighting the influence of tissue thickness and volume on peri-implant health (Thoma et al. 2018). Similarly, a recent multicenter randomized controlled trial demonstrated that collagen matrix augmentation achieved comparable soft tissue outcomes to connective tissue grafts, further emphasizing that targeted modification of soft tissue characteristics, rather than mechanical stabilization alone, represents a biologically meaningful approach for improving peri-implant tissue conditions (Hämmerle et al. 2023).

In addition, long-term clinical evidence indicates that implant positioning, contour management, and biological adaptation during healing play major roles in maintaining peri-implant stability. Buser et al. (2013) demonstrated long-term stability of peri-implant hard and soft tissues following early implant placement with contour augmentation, emphasizing the importance of appropriate implant-related and biological factors in achieving predictable outcomes (Buser et al. 2013). Likewise, Zucchelli et al. (2019) highlighted the critical role of facial peri-implant soft tissue dimensions and deficiencies in determining esthetic and biological outcomes, supporting the concept that management of the peri-implant tissue phenotype may have a greater influence on clinical outcomes than adjunctive mechanical devices (Zucchelli et al. 2019).

Collectively, these findings suggest that once adequate flap adaptation and immobilization are achieved through conventional suturing, subsequent peri-implant healing is primarily governed by physiological remodeling, implant-related factors, soft tissue characteristics, and host response. Therefore, within the limitations of the present study, adjunctive stent stabilization did not demonstrate an additional clinical or radiographic benefit in selected, uncomplicated implant cases where adequate flap adaptation and suturing were achieved.

Within the limitations of this study, routine use of adjunctive stent stabilization may not provide additional clinical or radiographic benefit in selected, uncomplicated cases when meticulous flap adaptation and suturing are achieved. Avoiding routine stent use may simplify the surgical procedure and reduce additional treatment requirements; however, these potential advantages require confirmation in larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up. Stent stabilization may remain appropriate in selected situations involving compromised soft tissue conditions, complex anatomy, extensive flap mobilization, or anticipated difficulties in maintaining postoperative tissue stability (Hamdy and Ghalwash 2023, Puisys and Linkevicius 2015, Urban et al. 2019).

The present study has some limitations which should be taken into consideration when interpreting the findings. The relatively small sample size, with only 10 participants per group, may have limited the statistical power to detect small between-group differences, particularly for secondary outcomes. Accordingly, these analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and non-significant findings should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the treatment approaches. The relatively short follow-up period also limits conclusions regarding long-term peri-implant stability. The single-center design may also limit external validity and generalizability to other patient types, surgical techniques, and operators of different levels of experience. Although outcome assessor blinding was not feasible because of the nature of the intervention, measurement bias was minimized through examiner calibration, standardized CBCT acquisition and assessment protocols, repeated measurements, and excellent intra-examiner reliability (ICC > 0.85; κ > 0.80). Nevertheless, the absence of assessor blinding remains a potential source of measurement bias. Further, patient-reported outcomes such as postoperative pain, satisfaction, and treatment-related quality of life were not assessed and should be included in future studies. In addition, peri-implant soft tissue parameters, such as mucosal thickness and soft tissue volume changes, were not directly assessed, which further reduces the evaluation of the potential effect of stent stabilization on soft tissue parameters. Further multicenter randomized clinical trials with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, blinded outcome assessment, patient-centered outcomes, and sophisticated soft tissue evaluation techniques are warranted. Additionally, descriptive and inferential statistical analyses were conducted, but not confidence intervals nor standardized effect sizes were planned as part of the original analysis plan; future adequately powered studies should incorporate these measures to provide more precise estimates of treatment effects and facilitate interpretation of their clinical relevance.

Although participant recruitment and follow-up were completed between 2016 and 2018, the present manuscript reports a distinct predefined radiographic outcome from the same randomized clinical trial. An earlier publication from this trial focused on the clinical effectiveness of plastic stent-assisted peri-implant soft tissue augmentation (Hamdy et al. 2021). The interval before submission reflects the sequential publication of different predefined outcomes together with detailed CBCT image analysis, manuscript preparation, and peer review. Importantly, this interval did not affect the integrity of the study data or the validity of the reported findings. Radiographic bone dimensional changes were predefined secondary analyses in the registered clinical trial protocol and have not been previously reported.

Conclusion

An apically repositioned flap during single-stage implant placement demonstrated stable peri-implant clinical parameters and predictable bone remodeling patterns during early healing, within the constraints of this randomized clinical trial. Adjunctive stent use did not significantly improve clinical outcomes or CBCT-assessed bone dimensions. These findings support a simplified, technique-driven surgical approach that prioritizes surgical precision and careful flap management over the routine use of supplementary stabilization devices.
