Psychological distress, self-efficacy, and academic advising among Saudi nursing students during academic reform: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Sharifah Abdulmuttalib Alsayed, Ebtsam Aly Abou Hashish, Eman Zain Bajamal, Nouf Afit Aldhafeeri, Mona Alanazi, Shahrazad Mahmoud Timraz, Sohad Bahanshal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChanges in academic structure—such as the implementation of a trimester system—can impose considerable psychological strain on students. In nursing education, such reforms may elevate stress, anxiety, and depression while undermining self-efficacy. Academic advising can potentially buffer these effects, yet its role during transitional periods remains underexplored in the Saudi context.PurposeThis study investigates the levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy among nursing students following the adoption of a trimester academic system at King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS) while examining the mediating role of academic advising in this context.MethodsA mixed-methods design was employed. The quantitative phase included a convenience sample of 465 nursing students at KSAU-HS, who completed reliable and validated measures of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21), the General Self-Efficacy Scale, and a researcher-developed Academic Advising Scale. In the qualitative phase, six focus group interviews were conducted with a purposive sample of 30 students until data saturation to gather in-depth insights into students’ emotional experiences during the implementation of the trimester system. Quantitative data were analyzed through inferential statistics and path analysis. Qualitative data were examined via conventional content analysis.ResultsStudents reported moderate levels of psychological distress, with mean total DASS-21 score of 47.56 ± 28.95, including stress (17.29 ± 10.14), anxiety (15.20 ± 10.29), and depression (15.07 ± 11.5). Self-efficacy was moderately high (M = 30.92 ± 4.45), while academic advising satisfaction showed moderate variability (M = 30.06 ± 10.10). Significant negative correlations were found between psychological distress and self-efficacy (r = − 0.417, p < 0.01). Structural equation modeling confirmed that academic advising significantly mediated the relationship between psychological distress and self-efficacy (indirect effect β = 0.08, p = 0.02). Qualitative findings supported these results through themes of emotional turbulence, self-efficacy under pressure, advising gaps, and student-centered support.ConclusionAcademic reform contributes to elevated psychological distress among nursing students, though academic advising is a mediating resource in preserving students’ self-efficacy. Enhancing advising systems, training faculty in student-centered support, and involving students in academic reforms are critical to promoting psychological resilience in nursing education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08229-w.

Background

Higher education has been compelled to change due to global competition, advances in technology, and educational reforms to meet the needs of students and the labor market [1]. The aftermath of these changes usually affects the well-being of students, as many of them suffer from psychological distress [2, 3]. Psychological distress is a multidimensional mental health problem that mainly includes depression, anxiety, and stress. According to the literature, stress is known as a physiologic reaction by an individual secondary to uncomfortable physical or psychological stimulus that might cause physical, emotional, or psychological strain [4]. Stress can have a negative impact on our health and contribute to the appearance of mental disorders. Whereas moderate stress may improve performance, high levels of stress, if poorly managed, have the potential to cause anxiety, depression, and even physical illness. Anxiety and depression caused by stress also greatly impact students’ mental health, productivity, and overall well-being [5]. Stress and associated anxiety are thought to be prevalent problems among college student populations for a variety of reasons, including study overload, pressure of examinations, time-consuming assignments, high expectations, change in lifestyle, and uncertain future [6, 7].

An optimal level of stress is essential for success; however, it is also pertinent to understand that stress does not guarantee better performance from students. In fact, under stressful conditions, they will perceive the task as unchallenging and may become easily bored [8]. Although, certain levels of stress motivate students to perform at their best, when it is not managed effectively due to insufficient resources to deal with stress, it can have negative consequences for both the student and the institution. Excessive stress may become overwhelming and lead to chronic anxiety, depression, physical illness, and long-term physical and psychological health problems [6, 7].

Previous studies have shown a negative effect of stress on college students’ physical health, especially prolonged stress, because of its direct link with higher anxiety levels. Furthermore, higher anxiety levels are linked with long-lasting health effects, such as insomnia, mood disturbance, frequent sickness, chronic headache, and other symptoms [4]. From psychological and sociological perspectives, anxiety could be a normal response to stress; however, prolonged stress might lead to aggressive consequences. One of these extreme consequences, which is commonly reported in literature, is depression, which is a severe mood disorder that causes a persistent feeling of sadness and loss of interest that affect how individuals feel, think, and behave [8]. These forms of psychological distresses affect the students’ well-being and productivity [9].

Stress and self-efficacy are thus considered as interrelated concepts [10]. Self-efficacy functions as a coping resource that reduces anxiety [4]. Self-efficacy is a part of a person’s self-system that comprises a person’s attitudes, abilities, and cognitive skills, which play a significant role in how people perceive and respond to different situations [11]. In other words, self-efficacy helps to determine how people think, behave, and feel about themselves to decide whether they successfully achieve their goals. Thus, self-efficacy is particularly significant for college students to help them increase their goals and academic performance [12].

Another factor that is believed to reduce stress and anxiety as well as enhance self-efficacy among college students is academic advising and counseling services [11]. Academic advising service is believed to decrease stress and anxiety as well as increase self-efficacy among college students. Academic advisors not only guide and support students to overcome any academic problems, social or personal issues that may hinder their academic progress but also help in developing their capacities and potential to enhance their academic performance [11, 12].

Study context

Changes and challenges in higher education can significantly impact students’ well-being and their ability to adapt to stress. In addition to structural reforms, broader policy and curricular shifts in higher education may have also influenced students’ experiences and mental health outcomes [13]. While these changes aim to enhance educational quality and efficiency, they have also introduced new pressures, heightened expectations, and increased support needs within academic institutions. As a result, many students face increasingly complex academic environments that may contribute to psychological distress [13].

One notable example of structural change is the shift from a traditional two-semester system to a trimester academic structure, an educational reform widely associated with elevated stress and anxiety levels among students. This transition compresses academic content into shorter timeframes, accelerates the pace of instruction, and reduces vacation periods, all of which demand rapid adaptation and advanced time management skills [7].

Students often report feeling overwhelmed by the quick succession of new classes, assignments, and exams, with limited opportunities to rest or reflect between terms. The pressure of covering the same volume of academic material in a shorter period contributes to significant academic and emotional strain. Simultaneously, faculty members who play a pivotal role in realizing these educational reforms face their own challenges, including adapting to revised curricula, participating in collaborative lesson planning, and maintaining instructional quality under compressed timelines [14].

At King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS), this shift to a trimester system was implemented during the academic year 2022–2023. In the College of Nursing, this rapid transition has raised notable concerns among students and educators alike. Reports indicate a rise in anxiety and stress levels, often linked to poor adaptation, difficulties in time management, and increased pressure due to tight deadlines for assignments and examinations. Student preparation and support are critical factors in determining whether the transition to university is experienced positively or negatively. In higher education, students are expected to take full responsibility for their own learning and academic success. This autonomy, while empowering, also comes with the burden of managing time effectively across multiple academic and personal demands. Such responsibility, especially when students are inadequately prepared and supported, can lead to heightened stress and anxiety [15].

Understanding the psychological factors that influence both student well-being and academic performance is therefore essential. This study aims to assess levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy among nursing students and educators at KSAU-HS within the context of the trimester plan. It also explores the role of academic advising in preparing and supporting students during this significant academic transformation.

Theoretical and conceptual framework

This study is guided by Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [11], which emphasizes the role of cognitive processes in shaping human behavior. According to SCT, individuals are not passive recipients of experiences; rather, they actively interpret and respond to their environment. One of the key concepts in this theory is self-efficacy—a person’s belief in their ability to manage and succeed in specific situations. In the context of this study, SCT helps explain how students perceive and cope with the psychological impact of the new academic reform implemented in Saudi universities—specifically, the shift to the trimester system. This reform may increase academic pressure, contributing to heightened levels of stress, anxiety, and depression. At the same time, cognitive factors like self-efficacy may help students better manage these challenges [11].

Additionally, the study considers the mediating role academic advising and support services provided by educators. These services can enhance students’ self-efficacy, guide their adaptation to the new system, and ultimately reduce the negative psychological effects associated with the transition [11]. By applying SCT, this framework allows for a deeper understanding of how psychological and behavioral variables interact to influence student well-being and academic success during times of educational change.

Stress, depression, and anxiety among students are the most prevalent psychological distresses. Lovibond and Lovibond developed a manual to measure the emotional states of three dimensions: depression, anxiety, and stress, and entitled the DASS-21. Depression is characterized by feelings of hopelessness, helplessness, and worthlessness. Hence, the depression domain assesses dysphoria, hopelessness, devaluation of life, self-deprecation, lack of interest/involvement, anhedonia, and inertia [2, 16]. Anxiety is characterized by unpleasant interior sentiments followed by caution, complaints, meditation, anxiety, and worry. The anxiety domain assesses autonomic arousal, skeletal muscle effects, situational anxiety, and subjective experience of anxious affect. The stress domain is sensitive to levels of chronic non-specific arousal. It assesses difficulty relaxing, nervous arousal, and being easily upset/agitated, irritable/over-reactive and impatient. The assumption underlying the DASS-21 is that differences between depression, anxiety, and stress experienced by normal subjects and clinical populations are essentially differences of degree [16]. Psychological distress, including depression and anxiety, can impact a person’s behavior, academic performance, general health, sleep quality, eating habits, and well-being [9]. In addition, they could influence suicidal ideation among undergraduate and graduate students [17]. Therefore, it is essential to find strategies and factors to mitigate these psychological distresses among nursing students and improve their well-being.

A significant variable that can influence students’ psychological and behavioral responses is self-efficacy, which can be developed through training and knowledge building [11, 12]. Bandura [11] emphasized the concept of self-efficacy as the personal belief that a task can be successfully achieved. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to learn and perform actions at predetermined performance levels. Bandura identified self-efficacy as a cognitive factor that influences whether individuals engage in specified activities and the sustained performance of associated behaviors. Individuals with a prominent level of self-efficacy are more confident in their ability to achieve specified goals and take the required steps to ensure that these goals are attained. Likewise, self-efficacy was identified as a useful indicator of nursing students’ clinical performance [10]. Thus, self-efficacy is particularly significant for college students to help them increase their goals and academic performance [12]. It helps them feel competent in meeting the entry-level clinical field requirements and accept this challenging role. Therefore, the level of self-efficacy among nursing students must be understood to assist nurse educators in taking measures to develop self-efficacy beliefs in student nurses [18].

Moreover, academic advising is a fundamental component of the higher education system that plays a vital role in promoting students’ academic and professional success. It serves as a structured process in which institutional representatives support students by providing guidance on academic, social, and personal matters [19]. Effective advising helps students navigate academic requirements, understand institutional policies, access campus resources, and integrate into the college environment [19, 20]. Advisors are often the first point of contact for students, particularly during times of transition or academic reform, such as the shift to a trimester system. During such periods, students may face increased stress, anxiety, and uncertainty, making the advisor’s role even more essential. Advisors not only guide students through institutional procedures but also serve as advocates and referral agents connecting students with campus and community support services [21]. Beyond administrative support, academic advising fosters meaningful mentoring relationships that contribute to students’ psychological well-being. Research shows that advisor mentoring can enhance students’ productivity and self-efficacy, and act as a protective factor against anxiety and depression. Through regular interactions, advisors help students identify their academic goals, reflect on their progress, and build confidence in their ability to succeed [15, 22].

Effective academic advising is characterized by collaboration, trust, and personalized support. Advisors serve not only as institutional guides but also as mentors, coaches, and role models exemplifying accountability and lifelong learning [15, 22, 23]. Their influence can significantly shape students’ academic self-efficacy and career development, especially when students are encouraged to focus on their goals and areas for improvement through continuous assessment and feedback [19–22]. Measuring students’ satisfaction with academic advising is critical to ensure the effectiveness of these services. A positive advising experience not only aids students in overcoming academic challenges but also strengthens their motivation, resilience, and engagement in the learning process [15, 23].

Significance of the study

Students’ success and well-being are critical priorities for academic institutions. Preventing poor mental health and supporting positive mental wellbeing needs to be based on evidence informed understanding what factors influence the mental health of students [13]. Hence, understanding the psychological factors that influence students’ mental health and academic performance is essential for fostering a supportive and effective learning environment. Prior research has consistently demonstrated the negative impact of prolonged stress on college students’ physical and psychological health. Elevated stress levels are often linked to anxiety, which, if persistent, may result in long-term health complications such as insomnia, mood disturbances, weakened immunity, and chronic pain [4]. While anxiety may be a natural response to stress, unchecked psychological distress can lead to more severe conditions, including depression a mood disorder marked by persistent sadness, lack of motivation, and disruptions in thought, behavior, and emotional well-being [8]. These psychological challenges directly affect students’ well-being and academic productivity [9]. While many studies have investigated stress, anxiety, and depression either individually or collectively few have explored their relationship with self-efficacy and the role of academic advising, particularly in the context of recent educational reforms.

Academic advising is widely recognized as a cornerstone of student development and institutional success. It supports students in navigating academic challenges, understanding institutional expectations, and preparing for future careers [21]. Advisors serve not only as academic guides but also as motivators and mentors, especially during periods of transition or change. Their influence contributes to students’ sense of purpose and resilience in adapting to academic pressures [19].

Although psychological distress among university students has been widely studied, little is known about how nursing students in Saudi Arabia are affected by the recent shift to a trimester academic system. This rapid curricular reform may introduce distinct academic and emotional stressors that influence students’ mental well-being, self-confidence, and their dependence on academic advising for support. Understanding these dynamics is particularly critical in the nursing profession, where students must balance intensive clinical and academic responsibilities. The current investigation addresses this underexplored context by providing timely, evidence-based insights into the challenges faced by nursing students navigating institutional change. By doing so, it offers practical guidance for enhancing academic advising systems and student support mechanisms, contributing to both improved mental health outcomes and academic success during times of structural transformation.

Aim of the study

This study aims to investigate the levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy among nursing students following the adoption of the trimester academic system in a Saudi university. It further seeks to examine the role of academic advising in the relationship between psychological distress (stress, anxiety, and depression) and students’ self-efficacy during this academic transition.

Methods

Study setting and design

The study was conducted at the Colleges of Nursing affiliated with King Saud bin Abdulaziz University for Health Sciences (KSAU-HS), Saudi Arabia. The study employed a convergent mixed-methods design, allowing parallel collection and subsequent integration of quantitative and qualitative data. Integration was achieved through side-by-side comparison, joint displays, and narrative meta-inference [24]. This design integrates both quantitative and qualitative approaches to gain a comprehensive understanding of the psychological impact of the newly implemented trimester system. The quantitative component utilized a cross-sectional correlational design, with data collected through structured self-report surveys to assess levels of stress, anxiety, depression, self-efficacy, and perceptions of academic advising. Complementing this, the qualitative component adopted a descriptive approach through focus group interviews, which allowed participants to share in-depth perspectives and contextual insights regarding their experiences [24] with the trimester system and the support received through academic advising.

Participants and sample size

The study targeted undergraduate nursing students enrolled in CON, KSAU-HS during the academic year 2022–2023. For the quantitative phase, the sample size was estimated using MacKinnon et al. [25] model for mediation analysis, assuming small effect sizes (S = 0.14) for both the path from the independent variable to the mediator (a) and from the mediator to the dependent variable (b). Accounting for an expected 10% missing data rate, the required sample size was projected at 509 students (462 + 47). A total of 465 nursing students participated in the quantitative phase of the study.

For the qualitative phase, purposive sampling was employed to recruit nursing students for in-depth exploration. Participants were selected based on their current enrollment, experience with the trimester curriculum reform and voluntary willingness to participate, ensuring the relevance and richness of the data in relation to the study’s objectives. A total of six focus group sessions were conducted, comprising three primary groups, with each group meeting twice to allow for continuity, deeper reflection, and validation of shared themes. Each group included 10 participants, resulting in a qualitative sample of 30 nursing students. This structure enabled participants to revisit earlier discussions, elaborate on emerging ideas, and offer more thoughtful insights—particularly useful for understanding psychological adaptation and responses to curricular changes over time. Data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged during data collection, was achieved by the second session of each group. This confirmed the adequacy of the sample size for capturing the breadth and depth of student perspectives [26].

Data collection instrument

Data for this study were collected using five tools designed to assess demographic characteristics, psychological states, self-efficacy, perceptions of academic advising, and lived experiences through qualitative inquiry.

Demographic questionnaire

A structured demographic questionnaire was used to collect information about participants’ personal and academic characteristics. Variables included age, marital status, campus location, year in the nursing program, and cumulative GPA (CGPA). This data served to describe the sample and allowed for comparisons across subgroups.

Depression, anxiety, and stress Scale – 21 (DASS-21)

To assess participants’ emotional distress, the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale – 21 (DASS-21) was administered. Developed by Lovibond and Lovibond [16], the DASS-21 is a self-report instrument consisting of 21 items divided equally into three subscales: depression, anxiety, and stress, with seven items per subscale. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Did not apply to me at all”) to 3 (“Applied to me very much or most of the time”). The responses are totaled according to the Lovibond scoring method to produce an overall score that reflects the participant’s mental health status. This score places the individual along a spectrum from “Normal” to “Extremely Severe,” based on the criteria established by Lovibond and Lovibond [16].

General Self-Efficacy scale (GSE)

Self-efficacy was measured using the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE), developed by Jerusalem and Schwarzer [10]. The GSE consists of 10 items, each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Not at all true”) to 4 (“Exactly true”). The total score ranges from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived self-efficacy. The GSE has been widely validated across populations and has demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.76 to 0.90.

Student advising survey(SAS)

To evaluate perceptions of academic advising services, the researchers developed a Student Advising Survey based on an extensive review of the literature [8, 13, 19–23]. The survey was designed to capture three interrelated domains: (1) structural accessibility (availability of advisors and adequacy of meetings), (2) relational quality (advisor engagement, empathy, and responsiveness), and (3) developmental impact (guidance for stress management, self-efficacy, and academic progression), with specific focus on how advising supports students in managing the stress and anxiety associated with the trimester plan. The survey includes two categorical items: the first asks how many times students met their advisor per semester (with options: never, 1–2 times, or 3 or more), and the second asks whether the number of meetings was sufficient for their advising needs (yes or no). In addition, the scale includes eight Likert-type items rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly agree”). Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with advising services.

Focus group interview guide

For the qualitative phase, the researchers developed a semi-structured focus group interview guide to explore students’ lived experiences with the transition to the trimester system and their use of academic advising. The guide was informed by a comprehensive literature review [22, 23] and included two components. The first collected demographic information such as age and academic level. The second component consisted of open-ended questions focused on students’ emotional responses, challenges encountered, coping mechanisms, and perceptions of academic advising. Sample questions included: “How did you feel when you heard the university would implement the trimester plan?”, “Tell me about a situation where you felt distressed during the transition,” “How did you manage your feelings? Did you seek support from your academic advisor?”, “Do you think the advising plan is important for you, and why?”, and “Compared to the two-semester plan, do you feel more or less stressed under the trimester system?”. Validity and reliability

To ensure the validity and reliability of the instruments used in this study, academic experts validated the content of all tools in their original English format prior to data collection. A panel of five experts in nursing education and research reviewed the instruments for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness to establish content validity.

A pre-testing phase was conducted with 23 nursing students, representing 5% of the total study sample (N = 465), which aligns with standard recommendations for pilot testing in survey-based research. The pilot aimed to evaluate the clarity, readability, and completion time of the instruments. Findings from this phase indicated that no modifications were needed, confirming the appropriateness of the tools for the target population.

Psychometric properties of the current study tools

DASS-21 (Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale)

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) confirmed a three-factor structure (Depression, Anxiety, Stress) with salient loadings > 0.30 across items. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) value was 0.927, indicating sampling adequacy. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) showed acceptable model fit (CFI = 0.963, IFI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.075, χ²/df = 3.227, p < 0.001). Internal consistency was high: total DASS α = 0.902; subscales ranged from 0.804 to 0.884. Item-to-total correlations ranged from 0.518 to 0.805, indicating good item reliability (see Supplementary Tables S1–S3, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Self-efficacy scale

EFA revealed a strong one-factor model with KMO = 0.956. All items loaded above 0.78 on the main factor, with high communalities (> 0.60). CFA supported this structure with good model fit indices (CFI = 0.948, IFI = 0.956, RMSEA = 0.077, χ²/df = 3.939, p < 0.001). Cronbach’s alpha was 0.817, indicating strong internal consistency. All items showed moderate to high item-total correlations (r = 0.511–0.748) (see Supplementary Tables S4–S6, Supplementary Fig. 2).

Student advising survey

The Student Advising Survey (SAS) was developed by the research team to evaluate students’ perceptions of advising quality during the transition to the trimester system. Item generation was based on a comprehensive review of international and regional literature on academic advising and student support [8, 13, 19–23]. The initial item pool was reviewed by five experts in nursing education and higher education support services. Face validity was confirmed through student feedback during a pilot test with 23 participants, who assessed clarity and relevance of the items. Content validity was further established by the expert panel using the Content Validity Index (CVI); the scale-level CVI (S-CVI) was 0.92, indicating excellent agreement on item relevance. Psychometric testing demonstrated strong reliability and construct validity. These findings align with prior research emphasizing the value of systematic and rigorous content validation during instrument development. Polit and Beck [27] highlighted that strong expert consensus ensures conceptual clarity and operational relevance of measurement tools, while Zamanzadeh et al. [28] advocated for structured approaches to content validation to enhance scale credibility.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) supported a unidimensional structure (KMO = 0.986), with all items loading above 0.80 and communalities exceeding 0.89. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) confirmed the model fit (CFI = 0.973, IFI = 0.944, RMSEA = 0.089, χ²/df = 2.961, p < 0.001) which is good given the commonly accepted cutoff of ≤ 0.08 for adequate model fit [29]. Internal consistency was robust (Cronbach’s α = 0.869), with item-total correlations ranging from r = 0.503 to 0.877. The result is consistent with psychometric recommendations that corrected item–total correlations >0.40 indicating a satisfactory construct validity and reliability [30]. These results indicate that the SAS is a psychometrically sound tool for capturing students’ perceptions of academic advising as both a structural and psychosocial support resource in the context of academic reform. See Supplementary Tables S7–S9, and Supplementary Fig. 3).

For the qualitative component, the semi-structured interview guide was also evaluated for content validity by five academic experts, who assessed its alignment with the study’s objectives, thematic relevance, and question clarity. No modifications were suggested. Additionally, a pilot interview was conducted with two nursing students to assess the guide’s structure, flow, and ease of understanding. The guide was confirmed to be suitable for use in the focus group discussions without further revisions.

Data collection

Following Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC), data collection was carried out during the academic year 2022–2023 and was conducted in two phases: quantitative and qualitative.

Quantitative Data Collection

Nursing students at KSAU-HS were invited to participate via their university emails. A structured, self-administered questionnaire was created using Microsoft Forms. Upon accessing the link, students answered four screening questions to determine eligibility. Eligible students were then directed to complete the full questionnaire, which included five sections: demographic information, DASS-21, GSE, and the student advising survey. The questionnaire was in English and took approximately 10–20 min to complete. To ensure accessibility and convenience, both paper-based and electronic versions of the questionnaire were made available. Paper-based forms were distributed and collected during class sessions, while the electronic version was shared via email and WhatsApp, with a one-week deadline. Comprehensive instructions accompanied both formats to ensure clarity and consistency. To preserve data integrity and prevent duplicate submissions, a unique identifier was assigned to each student across both formats.

Qualitative Data Collection

Students who completed the questionnaire were invited to participate in focus group interviews through a follow-up link embedded at the end of the survey. Interested participants were contacted by the researchers to schedule interviews based on their availability and preference for in-person or virtual participation (via Microsoft Teams or Zoom). Three primary focus groups were conducted, with each group meeting twice resulting in six sessions total. Data collection continued until data saturation was reached by the second session of each group. Each group consisted of 10 participants, and each session lasted between 45 and 60 min. Saturation was reached after six focus group interviews across different campuses, when no new themes or subthemes emerged. This approach aligns with saturation principles in conventional content analysis [31]. Discussions were moderated by a trained facilitator and centered on students’ experiences with the new trimester plan, focusing on how it affected their levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and self-efficacy. Participants were briefed before each session on the study’s purpose, ethical considerations, and confidentiality measures, and written informed consent was obtained. With permission, sessions were audio-recorded, and detailed notes were taken. Transcriptions were prepared immediately after each session to support accurate and timely analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from KAIMRC under IRB Approval Number NRJ23J/025/03. The researchers adhered to both institutional and international ethical standards, strictly following the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant rights and data integrity were upheld at all stages of the study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles related to autonomy, confidentiality, and voluntary participation. Prior to data collection, the researchers clearly explained the study’s purpose, procedures, and participants’ rights, including the right to refuse participation or withdraw at any time without academic consequences. Informed consent was obtained in both written and electronic formats, depending on the mode of participation. For students completing the paper-based questionnaire or participating in in-person interviews, written consent was signed before participation. For those completing the online questionnaire or joining virtual focus groups, electronic consent was secured through digital confirmation prior to participation. To protect confidentiality, all data were anonymized and securely stored on password-protected computers, accessible only to the research team.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

Quantitative Data

Quantitative data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize participants' demographic characteristics and study variables, including levels of depression, anxiety, stress, self-efficacy, and perceptions of academic advising. Independent sample t-tests and ANOVA were used to examine differences in psychological outcomes across demographic groups. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationships among depression, anxiety, stress, self-efficacy, and student advising. In addition, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to determine the predictive effects of student advising and psychological variables (stress, anxiety, and depression) on self-efficacy. To examine the mediating role of academic advising between psychological distress (DASS scores) and self-efficacy, path analysis was conducted using a regression-based mediation model. The coefficient of determination (R²) was reported to indicate the proportion of variance explained by the model. A significance level of p ≤ 0.05 was adopted for all statistical tests.

Qualitative Data

Qualitative data were analyzed using conventional content analysis to explore nursing students’ experiences and perceptions of stress, anxiety, depression, and the support provided through academic advising during the transition to the trimester system. Conventional content analysis was used to analyze the qualitative data, guided by the approach of Hsieh and Shannon. This method allowed categories and themes to emerge inductively from the transcripts without imposing preconceived categories. The analysis involved immersion in the data, coding meaningful segments, clustering codes into categories, and identifying overarching themes grounded in students’ narratives [31]. Transcripts from six focus group sessions were reviewed independently by two researchers. Through repeated readings, initial codes were manually identified by highlighting key phrases and patterns in the responses.

Codes such as “tight deadlines,” “supportive advisor,” “lack of adaptation time,” and “emotional burden” were grouped into broader categories such as “academic challenges,” “coping resources,” and “role of advising.” An inductive approach was applied to generate themes that emerged directly from the students’ narratives, allowing a rich understanding of the psychological and academic transition they experienced. To ensure the trustworthiness of the qualitative findings, the study adhered to Guba and Lincoln’s criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [32]. Credibility was reinforced through peer debriefing and independent coding by two researchers, who later compared and discussed their interpretations to reach consensus on the final categories and themes. This collaborative approach enhanced the accuracy and minimized potential researcher bias in interpreting students’ lived experiences. Dependability was addressed by providing a clear and detailed account of the research process, allowing for replication. Confirmability was achieved through member checking, in which selected participants reviewed and validated the accuracy of the findings. Transferability was supported by offering thick, rich descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, and data collection procedures, enabling readers to determine the relevance of the findings to other contexts.

Results

Demographics characteristics

The final sample consisted of 465 nursing students. In terms of age, the majority (76.1%) were between 21 and 23 years and were single (88.0%). Regarding academic level, 65.4% were fourth-year students, and 34.0% were in their third year. As for academic performance, 31.2% of participants reported a CGPA between 4.10 and 4.50, and 28.2% had a CGPA between 4.60 and 5.00. Very few students had a CGPA of 3.00 or below. In relation to mental health care, 28.8% of students reported visiting a doctor due to depression or anxiety symptoms, and 11.6% indicated they were taking medication for such conditions (Table 1).

Correlations among variables

Table 4 indicated that both self-efficacy and student advising showed significant negative correlations with all psychological distress variables: anxiety (r = −0.310; r = −0.578), stress (r = −0.220; r = −0.535), and depression (r = −0.169; r = −0.596), with all p-values < 0.001. These findings suggest that higher levels of psychological distress are associated with lower self-efficacy and less perceived support from academic advising. This highlights the potential protective and supportive role of academic advising in mitigating the impact of psychological distress. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between self-efficacy and student advising and counseling (r = 0.268, p < 0.001), indicating that students who perceived greater academic advising support tended to report higher levels of self-efficacy.

The coefficient of determination (R²) values in Table 44 indicate the proportion of variance in self-efficacy explained by each predictor. Collectively, the total DASS score accounted for 6.5% (R² = 0.065) of the variance in self-efficacy. When examined individually, anxiety explained 9.6%, stress accounted for 9.7%, and depression contributed 11.7% of the variance. Notably, student advising explained the largest proportion at 13.5%, reinforcing its significant and positive role in predicting students’ self-efficacy levels. These findings suggest that while psychological distress variables have a meaningful impact, academic advising demonstrates a stronger predictive power in this model.

Predictors and mediation analysis

The regression analysis identified all psychological distress variables—anxiety (B = −0.30, p < 0.001), depression (B = −0.24, p < 0.001), and stress (B = −0.13, p = 0.04)—as significant negative predictors of self-efficacy. Among them, anxiety had the strongest effect. In contrast, student advising was a significant positive predictor (B = 0.08, p = 0.002), underscoring its role in enhancing students’ confidence in their academic abilities. The overall model explained 13.5% of the variance in self-efficacy.

Further mediation analysis presented in Table 5 and illustrated in Fig. 3 showed that anxiety, stress, and depression had significant negative direct effects on student advising: anxiety (β = −0.56, p < 0.001), stress (β = −0.28, p = 0.02), and depression (β = −0.48, p < 0.001). Importantly, indirect effects through student advising were also observed: anxiety (β = −0.04), stress (β = −0.02), and depression (β = −0.04), indicating partial mediation. The final path model showed acceptable fit. Figure 3 visualizes these relationships, confirming that student advising mediates the impact of emotional distress on self-efficacy. The positive path from advising to self-efficacy (β = 0.08, p = 0.002) further supports the protective role of institutional academic support.

Associations between Socio-Demographic characteristics and Psychological/Academic measures

Table 6 reveals several statistically significant associations between students’ demographic characteristics and their levels of anxiety, stress, depression, self-efficacy, and perceptions of student advising and counseling support. Students who reported visiting a doctor for symptoms of depression or anxiety had significantly higher levels of anxiety (12.03 ± 4.95), stress (12.22 ± 4.46), and depression (13.18 ± 5.10) compared to those who had not. They also reported significantly lower self-efficacy (29.11 ± 4.32) and perceived lower advising support (24.75 ± 9.91), with all differences being highly significant (p < 0.001). Similarly, those taking medication for depression or anxiety scored significantly higher on all three psychological distress measures and significantly lower on self-efficacy (p < 0.001), though no significant difference was observed in perceived advising support (p = 0.64).

Age was significantly associated with anxiety (p < 0.001) and student advising support (p < 0.001), with younger students (18–20 years) reporting higher anxiety and lower satisfaction with advising services. Academic year level was significantly related to stress (p = 0.014), depression (p = 0.002), and student advising (p = 0.017). Fourth-year students reported higher stress and depression than third-year students and perceived less support from advising services. Finally, GPA was significantly associated with all study variables. Students with the lowest GPA (≤ 2.49) reported the lowest self-efficacy and the highest levels of perceived advising support, while those with a GPA of 4.60–5.00 reported the highest anxiety (M = 9.48 ± 4.79) and stress (M = 11.09 ± 4.84) levels (p < 0.001), as well as lower self-efficacy (p = 0.022) and advising support (p < 0.001).

Themes and subthemes were developed to reflect and explain the psychological distress, advising patterns, and self-efficacy trends identified in the quantitative analysis. Summary of emerged themes and subthemes from qualitative data analysis

Theme 1: Emotional turbulence of transition

The majority of participants described a profound emotional impact resulting from the abrupt shift to the trimester system. This theme reflects the psychological unrest particularly stress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms—that emerged as students struggled to adapt to intensified academic demands and reduced timeframes. The emotional burden was especially pronounced during the early stages of implementation, when many students felt unprepared for the sudden change in structure and expectations. This theme was represented by three interconnected subthemes:

Subtheme 1.1: anticipatory Anxiety

Students expressed heightened anxiety and emotional discomfort upon first hearing about the transition to the trimester model. The uncertainty of what to expect, combined with fears of academic overload, triggered feelings of unease and worry even before the new system was implemented. Many participants reported experiencing stress and anxiety due to the numerous assignment deadlines and the limited time available for learning. According to them, managing their time and adapting to the rapid pace of the trimester system proved to be challenging. Some participants expressed feelings of self-blame for not being able to fully absorb the scientific content and complete projects within the required time frame, which contributed to a sense of being overwhelmed. For example, one of the participants said “It was feeling very anxious and overwhelmed, and I was blaming myself for not being able to finish the tasks on time as well as I was worried about my marks all the time”. Another added, “It felt like we were pushed into something we weren't ready for.” Further stated that “I was very worried when I heard about the change and once we began the semester, we felt that we are working like machines to finish everything in the deadline to not lose marks.

Subtheme 1.2: academic overload

The accelerated schedule imposed by the trimester system left many students feeling overwhelmed by the relentless academic demands. The continuous cycle of assignments, quizzes, and exams created a sense of pressure that affected both their academic performance and emotional well-being. Several students described struggling to keep up with coursework, leading to fatigue, frustration, and decreased motivation. One student stated, “There was no break between assignments, quizzes, and exams…I just couldn’t keep up.”Another added, “I was exhausted all the time and still felt behind.”“We have three full terms now, each with its own courses, exams, and deadlines. It feels like we never get a break—just one wave after another.”

Subtheme 1.3: clinical experience

Some students reported that the reduction in clinical rotations resulting from the implementation of the trimester system posed challenges in gaining sufficient clinical exposure. This limitation reduced the opportunity to observe a broader range of cases, ultimately impacting their overall clinical experience. For instance, one participant said “Instead of having 10 weeks in clinical placements, we only had 6 weeks which is very short time to see more cases and to perform different nursing procedures and I think this was the reason for having low clinical evaluation performance” Another participant said “I started to use the whole weekend to study and to finish my clinical assignments because there is no time during the weekdays to finish, so there is no time left for myself”.

Subtheme 1.4: social Disconnection

Increased academic workload also took a toll on students’ social and personal lives. Many reported sacrificing personal time and social interactions in order to meet academic obligations. Some participants noted that the condensed schedule made it difficult to form strong social bonds with peers, faculty, or mentors. This loss of balance contributed to emotional isolation and reduced opportunities for rest and recovery, further exacerbating stress and anxiety, which led to strained social and personal life. Additionally, several participants highlighted the lack of time for research and extracurricular activities, as the demanding nature of the program left little opportunity to engage in activities such as research, volunteer work, or participation in academic clubs. One student shared, “I stopped seeing friends or doing anything for myself… there was no time.” Another expressed, “I was too tired to talk to anyone after classes. It felt isolating.” A third participant noted, “Before the trimester system, I used to volunteer and attend events now I barely have time to sleep, let alone do anything extra.”

Theme 2: Self-Efficacy under pressure

Despite the challenges posed by the trimester system, students displayed varying degrees of self-efficacy some experienced a decline in confidence, while others adapted by developing coping strategies. This theme explores how the new academic environment tested students’ self-beliefs and highlights the factors that either hindered or supported their ability to adapt and perform effectively.

Subtheme 2.1: confidence challenges

The compressed timeline and increased workload led many students to question their academic competence. They described feeling inadequate, unprepared, or incapable of meeting the heightened expectations. One student stated, “I used to feel confident managing my time, but with three packed terms a year, I constantly feel like I’m falling behind.” “I started doubting myself. I wasn’t used to failing deadlines.” Another added, “Even when I tried hard, I felt like I wasn’t doing enough.”

Subtheme 2.2: coping strategies

While some students struggled, others reported actively developing adaptive strategies to better manage the academic pressure. These included using planners, collaborating with peers, and prioritizing tasks to regain control over their schedules. One student explained, “Using a planner helped me plan my week better… it saved me.” Another shared, “My friend group started sharing notes and it made a big difference.”

Subtheme 2.3: supportive boosts

Support from peers, faculty, or advisors helped some students regain a sense of control and confidence. Participants emphasized that being heard and emotionally supported made them feel more capable and confident of handling the demands of the trimester system. One student reflected, “My advisor did help once… after talking, I felt calmer and more capable.” Another said, “Knowing my peers were also struggling helped me feel less alone and more confident.”

Theme 3: gaps in advising support

This theme reflects students’ perceptions of inadequacies in academic advising during the transition to the trimester system. While academic advising was intended to support students through this change, many participants felt that advising was infrequent, procedural, or disconnected from their real academic and emotional struggles. These gaps in support contributed to increased uncertainty and reduced students’ ability to manage the pressures of the new academic environment.

Subtheme 3.1: limited engagement

Some students expressed disappointment in the limited availability and engagement with their academic advisors. Interactions were often described as transactional, with little opportunity for meaningful discussion or individualized support. One student stated, “My advisor just signed my forms… there was no real conversation because of time limited.” Another noted, “Time limitation prevent me to attend the advising session so I didn’t even get a follow-up”.

Subtheme 3.2: mismatched expectations

Two participants entered advising sessions hoping for emotional or academic guidance but left feeling unheard or misunderstood. Students reported that the advising process was not aligned with the psychological challenges they were experiencing. One student explained, “They asked if I was okay, but didn’t offer help when I said no.” Another added, “They didn’t understand how stressed we really were with everything.”

Theme 4: Student-Centered advising

Beyond sharing their struggles, participants offered thoughtful suggestions for improving academic advising and the educational experience under the trimester system. This theme captures students’ desire for institutional responsiveness, more empathetic advising, and opportunities to participate in shaping their academic environment.

Subtheme 4.1: proactive advising

Students emphasized the importance of having regular, scheduled advising sessions rather than only being contacted when issues arose. Proactive check-ins were seen as a way to catch problems early and offer reassurance. One student suggested, “Even a short monthly meeting would make me feel supported and less lost.” Another noted, “Sometimes I didn’t know I needed help until it was too late more check-ins could have changed that.”

Subtheme 4.2: personalized and empathetic approach

Many students expressed a strong desire for a more personalized, proactive, and empathetic advising experience. They valued advisors who took time to listen, understood their circumstances, and offered both academic and emotional guidance. One student shared, “Advising should be more than just scheduling. We needed someone to listen.” Another reflected, “I wish they checked on us more, not just for grades.”

Subtheme 4.3: advisor Training

Several students felt that advisors need to be more equipped to provide psychological and emotional support. They called for training that would help advisors better understand students’ mental health challenges and respond with empathy. One student stated, “They need to know how to talk to us when we’re stressed—not just about grades.” Another expressed, “An advisor should be someone who understands both the system and the student’s feelings.”

Subtheme 4.4: student voice and empowerment

Participants highlighted the importance of involving students in academic decision-making, especially when changes such as the trimester system are being implemented. They felt that student perspectives were overlooked and should be more valued. One student remarked, “Only a few students are okay with the trimester system, but no one asked us before changing it. We should’ve had a say.” We should have a say in how changes are implemented—it affects us the most.” Another added, “Students should always be part of any curriculum reform—it’s our education. Everyone has different needs and ways of coping.”

Qualitative insights were used to contextualize statistical associations and support meta-inferences regarding students’ psychological distress and academic functioning amid the trimester reform, as reflected in students lived experiences.

Correlation and regression analyses indicated that increased psychological distress was significantly associated with lower self-efficacy. Discussion

These findings highlight the presence of psychological distress among a considerable portion of the nursing student population, particularly with respect to anxiety and depression. At the same time, the results point to the potential mitigating effects of self-efficacy and academic advising in buffering against these mental health challenges.

The current study revealed moderate levels of psychological distress particularly stress and anxiety among nursing students following the implementation of the trimester academic plan. This elevated distress might be attributed to the accelerated pace of learning, compressed academic timelines, and frequent assessments characteristic of the trimester system. Students reported feeling overwhelmed by continuous academic demands, limited personal time, and reduced opportunities for social interaction and extracurricular involvement. These stressors likely intensified feelings of anxiety, stress, and, in some cases, depression. The qualitative data supported these findings through the first emergent theme, “Emotional Turbulence,” in which students described the trimester system as triggering anticipatory anxiety, academic overload, compromised clinical experience, and social disconnection. Many shared that they felt they were “working like machines” and constantly “worried about marks.”

These findings align with previous research suggesting that structural changes in academic programs and the increased demands they impose can significantly contribute to heightened psychological strain among students. Haque and Sharmin [14] found that students enrolled in trimester-based systems reported higher levels of stress and anxiety compared to those in traditional two-semester systems, negatively affecting their academic performance and emotional well-being. Similarly, Deng et al. [7] emphasized that academic stress is a major contributor to depression, which in turn impairs students’ learning outcomes and quality of life. Supporting this, Yang et al. [33] reported that students frequently cited continuous studying, preparing for exams, completing assignments, and uninspiring teaching approaches as daily academic stressors. The pressure to perform, competitive grading environments, and the expectation to master large volumes of content within short timeframes all emerged as key contributors to their psychological distress. Taken together, the current study reinforces that rapid academic transitions especially without adequate preparation and support can negatively impact student well-being. Lavoie-Tremblay et al. [34] reported that the majority of nursing students experienced mild levels of psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress, associated with their academic stressors. These results underscore the prevalence of psychological strain among students, even when most symptoms remain within the lower severity range. In Egyptian studies, Dogham et al. [35] reported that most nursing students experienced moderate levels of academic stress. Similarly, Ghattas and El-Ashry [36] found that 46.5% of students reported high levels of academic anxiety.

Similar challenges have been documented internationally. For instance, Campbell et al. [13] reported that UK-students experienced significant psychological distress during transitions to new curricula, while Lavoie-Tremblay et al. [34] found Canadian nursing students faced comparable stressors across all program years. These parallels highlight that the psychological impact of structural reforms is not unique to Saudi Arabia, but reflects a broader global trend. In this respect, several studies have recommended incorporating stress management, mentorship, and counseling programs to enhance student well-being and improve the quality of nursing education [35–37]. In addition, Abou Hashish et al. [37] and Asal et al. [38] emphasized the importance of fostering academic resilience as a positive construct that enables students to cope effectively with stressful learning situations.

Moreover, nursing students in the current study reported moderate to high levels of self-efficacy. These results provide theoretical support for Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory by illustrating that self-efficacy acts as a key cognitive mediator between emotional distress and academic adaptation [11]. In line with SCT, advising functioned as a source of social modeling and verbal persuasion, which bolstered students’ confidence despite elevated stress and anxiety. This highlights self-efficacy not only as an individual coping resource but also as a mechanism through which institutional support systems exert their effects. Such insights extend the theoretical application of SCT in nursing education by emphasizing the role of structured advising in shaping students’ cognitive appraisals and adaptive behaviors during periods of academic reform [37].

However, the reported self-efficacy was significantly and negatively influenced by elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and depression, as revealed by the correlation and regression analyses. Although students generally maintained a baseline confidence in their academic abilities, the psychological burden imposed by the trimester transition appeared to undermine their capacity to cope effectively. As stress and anxiety increased, students expressed growing doubts about their ability to meet academic demands reflected in the second qualitative theme, “Confidence Under Pressure,” which highlighted both confidence challenges and coping strategies. For instance, some students described feeling “exhausted all the time” and “blaming themselves” for not completing tasks on time, indicating a diminished sense of self-efficacy under the weight of academic pressure. Similarly, Haque and Sharmin [14] further emphasized that, under the trimester reform, Saudi students had insufficient time to rest between terms, resulting in chronic fatigue that adversely affected their performance in achievement tests. These findings are consistent with Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory [11], which posits that self-efficacy is influenced not only by prior accomplishments but also by emotional and psychological states.

International evidence reinforces this pattern. Studies in China by Zhang et al. [18] and the Netherlands by Broks et al. [39] similarly demonstrated that low self-efficacy magnifies exam-related anxiety and weakens coping capacity, confirming that the protective role of self-efficacy transcends cultural and institutional contexts. Broks et al. highlighted that academic stress and exam-related anxiety often coexist, particularly in students with weak self-regulation skills. Without effective coping strategies and strong self-efficacy, these students are more likely to experience heightened stress and anxiety, which negatively impacts both their mental health and academic performance [39]. In line with the findings of Abou Hashish & Bajbeir [12] and Abou Hashish et al. [37], these results underscore the importance of closely monitoring nursing students’ psychological well-being and implementing targeted interventions that promote emotional regulation and self-efficacy especially during periods of intensified academic stress. Strengthening students’ knowledge through structured learning and clinical experiences not only enhances their competence but also fosters greater confidence and a stronger sense of self-efficacy as key attributes for their future professional roles as nurses [23, 40].

Moreover, the findings revealed that students were moderately satisfied with academic advising, which also emerged as a significant positive predictor and mediator of self-efficacy. Path analysis confirmed the mediating role of academic advising in the relationship between psychological distress and self-efficacy, demonstrating that supportive advising buffered the negative effects of stress, anxiety, and depression on students’ academic confidence. However, some of the reported correlations and indirect effects were relatively small. These findings, while statistically significant, should be interpreted cautiously in terms of practical impact. Students who perceived advising as accessible, frequent, and meaningful reported higher confidence in managing academic demands. This result concurs with Abou Hashish et al. [37], who reported that nursing students highly valued academic advising for its role in enhancing the quality of their academic life, fostering resilience, and strengthening their self-confidence. Comparable findings have been reported across different educational systems. For example, Johnson et al. [41] in the US and Saiyad and Mahajan [21], in India emphasized that advisor communication and proactive mentoring were critical in enhancing students’ resilience and self-efficacy. Taken together, these studies suggest that advising systems can serve as universally relevant mechanisms for supporting students during academic transitions, though their design must be adapted to cultural and institutional contexts.

However, the moderate level of satisfaction with academic advising reported in the current study suggests a potential gap in the support systems designed to assist students in navigating academic challenges. Qualitative findings reinforced this relationship. In the third theme, students reported being negatively affected by “gaps in advising support”, limited engagement with their advisors, and mismatched expectations often attributed to advisors’ time constraints and workload. Conversely, the fourth theme reflected “student-centered recommendations”, where participants emphasized that academic advising should serve not only as an informational tool but also as an emotional and motivational anchor. They highlighted the emotional reassurance and academic clarity gained from effective advising, and called for a more proactive, personalized, and empathetic advising approach led by advisors trained to address both academic and emotional needs.

These results align with the social cognitive perspective, in which advising functions as a form of social modeling and verbal encouragement core components in the development of self-efficacy, as proposed by Bandura [11]. Likewise, the existing literature emphasizes academic advising as a critical factor in student adaptation, mental well-being, and self-efficacy, particularly during periods of academic transition [19–23]. Academic advising functions not only as a mechanism for delivering academic information but also as a form of interpersonal and emotional support that fosters students’ resilience and enhances their sense of control over their learning. Furthermore, Abdelrahman et al. [42] noted that resilience often supported through quality advising can protect students from the adverse effects of psychological distress. Their study showed that perceived advisor support significantly influenced student resilience, even more so than distress levels. Similarly, Johnson et al. [41] found that advisor communication, individualized support, and fostering a sense of community were key drivers in enhancing students’ self-efficacy. In a related context, Abou Hashish et al. [37] reported that nursing students frequently relied on academic advisors to manage stress and build coping strategies. Nurse educators, in particular, were valued for their mentoring roles, helping students navigate academic demands and improve psychological well-being.

A noticeable discrepancy appeared between the two strands of data: the survey results showed moderate satisfaction with advising, but the focus groups revealed strong dissatisfaction. This difference may be explained in several ways. First, self-report surveys are often affected by social desirability, where students may give more positive answers to appear agreeable or to reflect well on their program. Second, when all measures are collected from the same source at the same time, common-method bias can reduce variation and make results appear more consistent than they are. Third, cultural factors may also influence how students respond to rating scales, as some may avoid giving very negative scores but feel more comfortable expressing dissatisfaction in open discussions. These issues show why mixed-methods designs are valuable by combining surveys with qualitative data, the findings gain a fuller and more balanced understanding of students’ real experiences with advising [24].

It is worth mentioning that cultural factors in Saudi Arabia likely shaped students’ distress, help-seeking, and engagement with advising. Mental-health stigma both public and self-stigma remains a consistent barrier to acknowledging symptoms and seeking formal support among young adults and university students, which can suppress disclosure in academic settings and reduce uptake of advising or counseling referrals [43]. Given that the study was conducted in a female-only nursing college, concerns about privacy and confidentiality, family expectations regarding academic conduct, and a preference for women-only, gender-concordant support may further influence comfort with advising and referral. Evidence from Saudi samples shows strong preferences for same-gender providers in sensitive or personal discussions, underscoring the importance of gender-responsive, confidential, and women-cenetred advising models [44].

Furthermore, and in alignment with the fourth theme, the subtheme student voice and empowerment emphasized the importance of incorporating students’ perspectives into academic decision-making. These findings are consistent with previous study which underscored the significance of student involvement in academic planning. Students reflected that they can develop greater confidence in managing their academic environments when supported by consistent academic guidance and structured learning experiences provided through their nursing programs [45]. This aligns with Kahu and Nelson’s [46] framework on student engagement, which highlights the essential role of institutional support and emotional connection in fostering academic resilience. Similarly, Zheng [47] argued that involving students in curriculum development enhances their sense of ownership, satisfaction, and emotional well-being—elements that were strongly reflected in this subtheme.

Demographic characteristics and studied variables

The current study findings revealed that students who reported visiting a doctor or taking medication for depression or anxiety exhibited significantly higher levels of anxiety, stress, and depression, along with notably lower self-efficacy. Age, academic year, and GPA were also significantly associated with several study variables. Notably, students with lower GPAs and those in earlier academic years reported greater psychological distress and lower satisfaction with academic advising. These results highlight how both demographic and academic contexts are significantly associated with nursing students’ emotional well-being and their perceptions of institutional support.

These findings are in line with previous research. Abu Ruz et al. [48] indicated that nursing students experiencing persistent anxiety and depression are more likely to demonstrate lower academic achievement and higher absenteeism than their non-distressed peers. Similarly, Chen et al. [49] in a study of 700 nursing students, reported that depressive symptoms were strongly associated with GPA, where students with lower GPAs reported more depressive symptoms than those with higher academic performance. In a literature review, Mofatteh [5] also emphasized the connection between academic pressure and student mental health, noting that symptoms of stress, anxiety, and depression often reflect the burdens associated with transitions into demanding academic environments. These psychological symptoms were frequently linked to factors such as age, GPA, and academic level.

Moreover, academic advising has been consistently recognized as a vital support mechanism in enhancing both academic and personal development. Alsabhan et al. [20] found that academic advising plays a pivotal role in students’ academic progression and personal well-being. Positive perceptions of advisor engagement have been shown to contribute to better academic performance and increased student satisfaction. In light of these findings, several studies recommend that academic administrators identify sources of student distress and implement targeted strategies to address them. These include enhancing mental health support, promoting emotional regulation, and referring students who display early signs of psychological distress to trained psychosocial counselors. El-Ashry et al. [50], Alnajjar and Abou Hashish [23], and Abou Hashish et al. [37] also emphasized the importance of proactive advising systems and tailored educational support to meet the specific needs of at-risk student populations. Collectively, these efforts can foster a supportive academic environment and significantly improve nursing students’ mental health, academic performance, and future professional readiness.

Strengths and limitations

One of the key strengths of this study is its mixed-methods design, which enabled triangulation of quantitative and qualitative data, thereby deepening the understanding of nursing students’ psychological responses to the trimester academic transition. The use of validated instruments such as the DASS-21 and the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSE) alongside a custom-developed academic advising scale, enhanced the methodological rigor and robustness of the data collection. Moreover, the inclusion of a relatively large sample size (N = 465) strengthens the reliability of the findings and supports their potential generalizability to similar academic settings.

Another strength lies in the qualitative component, which captured rich, contextualized narratives of students’ lived experiences during a period of educational reform. The use of conventional content analysis enabled the identification of emotional, behavioral, and institutional factors affecting students, adding depth and nuance to the interpretation of the quantitative data.

However, several limitations should be noted. As a cross-sectional study, it does not allow for causal inferences or examination of changes over time. The reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly when discussing sensitive emotional or psychological issues. These factors may partly explain why survey results showed moderate satisfaction while qualitative findings highlighted dissatisfaction. Given the cross-sectional design, the mediation findings should be interpreted with caution. While statistical mediation was tested, the temporal order of variables and causality cannot be confirmed. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to establish causal pathways. Although the achieved sample (n = 465) was slightly below the initially targeted number (n = 509), the 91.4% target achievement rate lends credibility to the findings. However, the slight shortfall may have reduced the power to detect small but meaningful effects. Additionally, while qualitative data collection continued until saturation was reached, the sample excluded faculty and academic advisors, whose perspectives could have offered a more comprehensive understanding of the transition’s impact.

Additionally, the study was limited to nursing students from a single institution, which may reduce the transferability of the findings to other educational or cultural contexts. Expanding such research to diverse institutional settings could help validate and extend these findings more broadly [45, 51]. It is also important to note that some of the observed correlations and indirect effects were small in magnitude. While statistically significant, these modest effect sizes suggest limited practical significance and should therefore be interpreted with caution. Rather than overstating their impact, these results highlight nuanced but meaningful pathways, particularly the mediating role of advising, that warrant further investigation through longitudinal or experimental designs [25].

What this study adds

This study contributes important empirical evidence on the psychological impact of academic reform—specifically, the implementation of a trimester system—on undergraduate nursing students in Saudi Arabia. It underscores the critical role of academic advising not only as a predictor but also as a mediator of self-efficacy, a key psychological construct influencing students’ ability to adapt to institutional and curricular changes.

To our knowledge, this is among the first mixed-methods studies in the Middle East to examine the interconnected roles of emotional distress, academic advising, and self-efficacy during a rapid curricular transition in nursing education. By integrating both quantitative findings and students’ qualitative reflections, the study provides actionable insights for improving academic support systems. It also offers student-centered recommendations that can inform the development of more empathetic, proactive, and psychologically supportive advising models—especially during periods of educational change or reform in healthcare education programs.

Conclusion

This study highlights the psychological burden nursing students encounter when adjusting to academic reforms, particularly the shift to a trimester system. Elevated levels of stress, anxiety, and depression were significantly linked to lower self-efficacy, indicating a critical vulnerability in students’ capacity to cope with accelerated academic demands. Importantly, academic advising emerged as a key protective factor—positively influencing students’ confidence and buffering the detrimental effects of psychological distress.

The integration of quantitative results with qualitative narratives offered a nuanced understanding of students’ challenges and coping mechanisms. The findings underscore that academic advising, when proactive, empathetic, and student-centered, plays a pivotal role in supporting students’ psychological resilience during times of institutional transition. Furthermore, students’ voices emphasized the necessity for more individualized and emotionally responsive advising systems that account for their diverse experiences and evolving needs.

Implications for nursing education and future research

The findings of this study offer several practical implications for nursing education, particularly in contexts undergoing curricular transformation such as the transition to a trimester system. First, they underscore the urgent need for academic institutions to establish structured, accessible, and student-centered advising systems that go beyond administrative guidance to include emotional and psychological support. Academic advising should be reconceptualized as a frontline support mechanism that actively contributes to students’ academic success and emotional resilience.

To achieve this, nursing education institutions should:

Train academic advisors in foundational psychological first aid, empathetic communication, mental-health awareness, and brief screening/referral protocols (e.g., a focused annual workshop with simple role-play and a quick pre/post check).Establish scheduled, proactive advising sessions at critical transition points in the academic year (e.g., week-1 orientation, mid-term, pre-finals, and post-results debrief) to monitor adaptation and well-being.Implement early distress screening embedded within advising, using brief tools at weeks 2–3 and pre-midterm, with clear referral pathways and a prompt follow-up for positive screens (e.g., contact within a few days).Integrate mental-health support within advising systems by coordinating with counseling services (e.g., same-day “warm handoffs,” short skills sessions on time/stress management, and peer mentoring during peak stress periods).Foster a collaborative advising environment where students feel safe expressing academic and personal concerns without stigma or judgment (e.g., privacy-focused scheduling, brief confidentiality reminders).Integrate student feedback into curricular reform processes, ensuring that policy changes are informed by learners lived experiences (e.g., a short end-of-term survey and brief focus groups, with “you said–we did” summaries shared with students).
