Healthcare applications for elderly: a scoping review and a practical roadmap for developing health apps
Authors: Sorayya Rezayi, Leila Shahmoradi, Mahdieh Shabzendehdar, Erfan Esmaeeli, Roya Naemi, Fatemeh Sadat Mirmohammad Meigooni, Pegah Morovati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIn this scoping assessment, our primary goal was to investigate the uses of health apps for elder citizens. Original articles and a few relevant sources have been mined in this regard. A development roadmap for health apps has also been created.MethodThis scoping review is divided into three sections: 1. A thorough analysis of the pertinent literature and gray sources; 2. A qualitative study intended to produce a roadmap for the development of healthcare apps; 3. Grouping related concepts into descriptive themes. The study team looked through three distinct databases in order to conduct a scoping review. To obtain material published between January 1, 2015, and July 24 and 25, 2023, we used electronic search tactics from Medline (via PubMed), Web of Science (WoS), and Scopus.ResultsThere were 46 citations with studies meeting our criterion. Of the 46 studies that were analyzed, 30% (n = 14) were carried out in the United States, while 6% (n = 3) were carried out in Canada. The developed apps offer five primary advantages, with 46 reviewed publications highlighting improved physical health as the most significant outcome. Because of the technological development, people and governments are considering creating and developing various apps to increase physical stamina, which can lead to enhanced overall health and fitness levels among users. Twenty-eight (60%) of the developed apps demonstrated a positive impact on the metrics evaluated through urgent reasoning. The majority of these assessments have focused on usability or ease of use. A product’s usability can be assessed at several phases of development. Continual usability testing during the development phase improves the product’s responsiveness to user needs, ensuring that the final product aligns closely with user expectations and requirements. Without relying on statistical calculations, 12 apps, or 26%, demonstrated positive results. A useful roadmap for creating and implementing healthcare apps can be found, and it consists of 14 primary phases with accompanying sub-actions.ConclusionThe papers under investigation show that developed apps significantly improve the health of elderly adults, and we will highlight their numerous benefits. The literature review and thematic analysis revealed that the creation of health applications requires more than fundamental technological design; it necessitates a multi-phase, user-centered, and market-oriented approach within a specified timeframe.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03541-x.Keywords: Mobile application, Elderly, Aged, Roadmap

Introduction

As the world’s population is rapidly aging, the elderly population continues to grow in both developed and developing countries [1, 2]. In 2020, over a billion people in the world were 60 or older, representing 13.5% of the total population of 7.8 billion. This figure is projected to climb to 2.1 billion by 2050, meaning that one in five people would be old. This demographic transition is especially evident in developing nations like Iran [3]. According to reports from the Iranian Statistics Center, the number of elderly people in Iran has quadrupled between 1976 and 2016 and will reach well over 8 million in 2021 [4]. These trends underscore the increasing importance of addressing the health and well-being of older adults. As people age, their health needs become increasingly complex; common challenges include reduced physical performance, a higher prevalence of chronic diseases, disability, frequent hospitalizations, and polypharmacy [3].

Healthy aging involves preserving and enhancing physical and mental capacities, alongside optimizing living conditions and environmental interactions. For such improvements to happen, health systems need to be changed so that everyone can get integrated and complete healthcare services [5, 6]. In this pursuit, digital health—encompassing the creation and use of digital technologies—has emerged as a crucial facilitator. Digital health extends eHealth to smart and connected devices, advanced computing, big data analytics, and artificial intelligence [7, 8]. Mobile Health (mHealth) apps are a key area of digital health technologies [9]. They have some advantages over traditional computer-based apps. They make it possible to get health-related services on your phone or tablet [10].

For older adults, mHealth is particularly valuable as it enables continuous monitoring of health status. Features such as access to patient data, support for diagnosis and treatment, chronic disease management, drug reference tools, and integration with electronic health record systems are especially beneficial in addressing the complex healthcare needs of this population [11, 12]. These smartphone-based technologies can effectively boost the quality of life for seniors, especially in rural areas where access to medical care providers is limited [13].

Despite the vast potential of mHealth, its effective utilization by older adults is heavily dependent on their technological proficiency. Since new technologies are generally available online, using them requires digital skills and technological literacy. The substitution of traditional personal computers with smartphones has specifically highlighted the importance of digital literacy—the ability to use information online through digital devices [14].

Consequently, higher digital literacy is consistently associated with greater adoption and effective use of mHealth apps across society [15, 16]. As digital health continues to advance, mobile health-related technologies in various domains, such as diagnosis, treatment, clinical decision support, care management, and care delivery, are also widely embraced in the medicine section [17, 18]. Studies indicate that older adults have developed greater confidence in utilizing information technology and have enhanced their skills. People often have trouble using these apps because of technical problems, not because of a lack of knowledge. This shows how important it is to have a user-friendly design and strong functionality. Therefore, mHealth technologies have become an important part of daily life for many older adults, helping them age healthily and feel better overall [14, 19].

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section “Methods and material” presents the materials and methods, Section “Results” outlines the results, Section “Discussion” provides the discussion, and Section “Conclusion” concludes the study.

Motivation and aims

The following review studies have focused on mHealth apps for older adults. To assess the efficacy of mHealth for behavior change, Madison Milne-lves et al. conducted a systematic analysis of mobile apps for health behavior changes in food, physical activity, drug and alcohol use, and mental health. One of its limitations is the heterogeneity of studies, focusing on five health behaviors and considering randomized and controlled trials. This review demonstrated that companion health applications successfully modified health behavior in 25% of the reported outcomes. Companion health apps could improve some health behaviors, including physical activity, nutrition, and reducing sedentary behaviors [20].

Clemens Kruse et al. systematically reviewed the impact of telehealth on older adults, identifying both benefits and barriers. Telehealth was associated with improvements in activity levels, independence, cognitive function, and overall quality of life. However, limitations included the scarcity of analytical studies and potential biases, while key barriers to adoption were technical literacy, cost, lack of support, and privacy concerns [21].

In a distinctive narrative review, Nimmanterdwong et al. explore the application of Human-Centered Design (HCD) in mHealth for the elderly, revealing certain limitations such as the exclusion of the ACM digital collection and the incomplete examination of the HCD process due to its evolving nature. They highlight variations in study instruments and insufficient information quality stemming from vague quantitative data and sampling methods, which can lead to unreliable results and hinder the effectiveness of HCD applications in mHealth for the elderly. The study concludes that HCD can be implemented in four key phases: identifying the context of use, defining user needs, creating design solutions to meet those needs, and evaluating designs against requirements [22].

Abbaspourbehbahani et al. examined mHealth technologies for older adults during the COVID-19 pandemic. The study identified key factors influencing health outcomes, reporting improvements in treatment efficiency, service delivery, and safety for both the elderly and healthcare providers. Limitations included restricted database access and uncertainty about the generalizability of findings [23].

Paiva et al. conducted a systematic review of mobile applications for senior healthcare, focusing on how telemedicine software is utilized by medical providers and patients. The study highlighted various applications in telemedicine, health records, customer care, decision support, and medication management. It also discussed challenges such as user adoption, data mining, privacy, and quality assurance. Notably, the willingness to adopt mobile applications for senior healthcare increased significantly from 2014 to 2018 [24].

A review underscores the necessity for future investigations into artificial intelligence-driven personalization, longitudinal usability studies, and culturally inclusive mobile applications. By using age-friendly design concepts, coders and developers can augment digital inclusion, foster independence, and increase the general well-being of older individuals in an increasingly digital world [25].

Despite the provided evidence that older adults are increasingly willing to use technology, there is still a deep gap between “access to technology” and “effective use.” In Iran, given the rapid aging of the population and the limited healthcare infrastructure in rural and remote areas, identifying barriers to the adoption of these technologies has become even more urgent. Studies showed that many of the challenges faced by users are not due to unwillingness but rather arise from the complex interaction between the level of digital literacy and the functional characteristics of the applications.

This study aimed to systematically synthesize existing evidence and develop an evidence-informed practical roadmap for the design, development, and implementation of mHealth apps for older adults. In other words, the primary goal is to explore the use of mHealth applications in facilitating care, monitoring, and health management for older adults. To achieve this objective, relevant supplementary sources were analyzed, resulting in the formulation of an evidence-informed practical roadmap for the development of health applications targeted at the elderly, derived from the insights obtained.

Nevertheless, even with the important insights provided by earlier reviews outlined in Table 1, none have explicitly compiled evidence on the practical elements, developmental factors, and implementation aspects of mHealth applications aimed at older adults. The majority of previous reviews have concentrated on either broad digital health tools or interventions focused on specific diseases, thus highlighting a significant gap in the availability of a thorough roadmap and an integrated summary of mHealth app attributes designed for the elderly demographic.

This study proposed a comprehensive 14-phase practical guide to assist in the design, development, and implementation of effective, user-focused health applications aimed at elders. This research utilizes the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) along with other theories related to the adoption of digital health. These frameworks elucidate how a user’s perceptions of usefulness influence the ongoing utilization of digital health technologies. The roadmap created in this research is conceptually rooted in these models, aiming to convert the factors influencing mobile app acceptance into practical design and implementation stages for mHealth applications aimed at older adults.

Methods and material

Study design

This study was structured as a scoping review with two primary objectives (1): to systematically catalog and evaluate the current literature on the development and evaluation of mHealth applications for the elders, and (2) to integrate the findings to create a practical, evidence-based framework for researchers and developers.

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guideline, which provides methodological guidance for conducting scoping reviews. The following are the essential steps: Planning is the first step; identification, screening, eligibility/assessment, discussion, and results presentation follow. Additionally, the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension of the Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist, which consists of 20 required reporting elements and two optional ones, was used to standardize the reporting of scoping reviews [26, 27].

In the final stage, a four-step synthesis procedure was implemented to develop the practical roadmap and code the limitations identified in the literature. This procedure, guided by the principles of thematic analysis, involved: The procedure consisted of four steps: (1) reviewing all included studies and grey literature; (2) identifying significant phases and sub-actions; (3) organizing an expert panel session; and (4) mapping and synthesizing the findings into a coherent, practical framework. This synthesis process was guided by the principles of thematic analysis to systematically interpret the findings.

Data sources and search strategy

To conduct this scoping review, the research team searched three different databases. We used electronic search strategies in the Medline (via PubMed), Web of Science (WoS), and Scopus databases to find articles published between January 1, 2015, and July 24–25, 2023. The search strategy included a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to the concepts of “mobile health” and “elderly.” A comprehensive summary of all the phrases and keywords utilized in the search approach is given in Fig. 1. To complete the database search, a manual search was conducted by screening the reference lists of all selected articles and searching Google Scholar to identify any other relevant studies.

Review procedure

Based on the study’s inclusion and exclusion criteria, publications identified through the search strategy were imported from EndNote into the Rayyan online platform (https://rayyan.qcri.org/) for screening and selection. Initially, each of the four authors (FMM, PM, RN, and MSH) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles. Data extraction was then performed independently by two authors (FMM and PM) using a standardized form developed according to the study objectives. Full-text articles were subsequently obtained and independently evaluated by SR to identify studies eligible for inclusion. Disagreements regarding eligibility were resolved through discussion or by consulting two additional reviewers (SR and LSH). Interrater reliability was assessed by SR for 80% of the included articles and 10% of the excluded studies. The extracted data were systematically entered into an Excel spreadsheet, and data extraction forms were cross-checked among reviewers to ensure consistency.

Mapping concepts and suggesting the practical roadmap

To ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility, we used thematic synthesis, a validated qualitative analysis method, to extract the main themes and subthemes of reported limitations and to develop the action-oriented roadmap. This approach provides a transparent and auditable link between the evidence extracted from the included studies, grey resources, and the final output of the review (i.e., the roadmap and the coded limitation categories).

The process was conducted independently by three authors (SR, LSH, and FMM) and followed three structured phases. Furthermore, to enhance the credibility and confirmability of the extracted themes, one expert panel session with three domain experts was convened to review, refine, and validate the coding framework, descriptive themes, and analytical themes.

Quality assessment and critical appraisal

A two-stage quality appraisal was conducted to ensure the methodological robustness of the included literature. In the first stage, 153 full-text articles were screened using six preliminary evaluative queries developed by the research team. This initial assessment resulted in the exclusion of 102 studies that did not adequately meet the minimum reporting and methodological criteria. Consequently, 51 studies entered the second-stage appraisal.

Using the 10-item CASP qualitative checklist, each study was assessed across the following domains: clarity of research aims, appropriateness of qualitative methodology, suitability of study design, adequacy of the recruitment strategy, rigor of data collection, consideration of researcher–participant relationships, attention to ethical issues, rigor of data analysis, clarity of findings, and overall value of the research.

Across the 51 studies evaluated in the quality-assessment stage, most papers demonstrated acceptable methodological rigor. The majority of the studies provided a clear statement of aims and used an appropriate qualitative methodology (Questions 1–2). Over 80% of the studies adequately reported recruitment strategies and data-collection methods (Questions 4–5). Less consistently addressed were ethical considerations (Question 7) and the researcher-participant relationship (Question 6), which represent the most common methodological weaknesses across the included literature.

Of the 51 assessed studies, 41 met high-quality criteria, receiving scores of 10–12, while 5 studies scored between 8 and 9, indicating moderate quality. Only 5 studies scored ≤ 7, and these were excluded from the final synthesis. Overall, 46 studies were deemed methodologically robust and were included in the final analysis.

Various outcomes of apps developed in reviewed papers

The heterogeneity in the definition of the ‘elderly’ group across studies was a key methodological finding in this review. The age range in the 46 articles included varied. For example, inclusion criteria ranged from broad terms such as ‘elderly person’ to broad numerical ranges such as ‘50–86’ and ‘62–92’. Some studies used a minimum age threshold that was itself inconsistent, including “over 50 years,” “over 60 years,” or “over 65 years.”

Table 4 presents the main benefits of the developed apps. A key finding of this review is the overwhelming thematic focus on physical health. Of the 46 studies, 43 (93%) identified primarily physical health-related outcomes as their primary objective. Furthermore, enhancing social well-being through improved interactions and fostering mental health were recognized as secondary objectives. However, these areas have received far less attention than physical health, and this imbalance is a prominent feature of the current research landscape.

Four (8%) of the reviewed apps focused on improving the quality of life of older adults. In this context, improving physical and mental health and communicating are primary principles. Three (6%) of these apps were designed to improve physical health, and three (6%) were designed to prevent falls in older adults. In addition, three (6%) were developed to prevent muscle mass loss or sarcopenia.

Usability evaluation was identified as the most common measurement method in the studies. Most of the developed applications were evaluated in terms of different aspects of usability. In these evaluations, indicators of efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction were measured descriptively in some studies and qualitatively and analytically in others.

Twenty-eight studies (61%) reported a positive impact on the evaluated criteria, with most of these evaluations focusing on usability or ease of use. However, one critical finding regarding the quality of evidence emerged: 12 of these studies (equivalent to 26% of the total sample) reported positive effects without providing valid statistical calculations. This finding highlights a methodological weakness in the existing literature.

Analysis of the main objectives of the applications showed that the majority of the developed applications (n = 42, 91%) aimed to improve the physical health of the elderly. This was followed by increasing psychological and socio-behavioral capabilities as secondary objectives, which included enhancing social interaction, reducing feelings of loneliness, and promoting mental well-being among the elderly. This classification was done based on the objectives stated in the articles themselves, with a focus on user-centered design.

Of the 46 studies assessed, 22 (47%) were Android-based, ten (21%) were Android- and iOS-based, only four (89%) were iOS-based, nine (19%) did not specify an operating system, and only one was Amulet-based. The majority of the 46 extracted studies—28 apps, or 60%—were smartphone-based, while eight (17%) were tablet- and smartphone-based.

Discussion

Interpretation of results

In this scoping review, existing apps related to elderly health were investigated through a comprehensive search and then evaluated. First and foremost, we identified 46 articles in which apps were developed and evaluated purposely. This research utilizes the TAM along with other theories related to the adoption of digital health. These frameworks elucidate how a user’s perceptions of usefulness influence the ongoing utilization of digital health technologies. The roadmap created in this research is conceptually rooted in these models, aiming to convert the factors influencing mobile app acceptance into practical design and implementation stages for mHealth applications aimed at older adults. There is a helpful implementation and creation roadmap for healthcare apps that consists of 14 main phases and related sub-actions.

The literature review and thematic analysis revealed that developing health apps involves more than mere technical design; it requires a multi-stage, user-centered, and market-oriented approach. The extracted themes indicate that the development process begins with an initial phase of needs assessment and market analysis, followed by implementation, continuous evaluation, business management, and sustainable development.

A precise understanding of user needs, behavioral patterns, and target populations—including healthy individuals, patients with chronic diseases, and older adults—is essential for improving application adoption, usability, and effectiveness. This finding is consistent with previous studies in digital health system design, which emphasize the critical role of user-centered design in enhancing the acceptance and success of health applications [76, 77].

Users expect to receive a broad range of health services from both commercial and non-commercial applications. These services include nutrition management, sleep monitoring, stress management, disease status tracking, and communication with wearable devices. In addition, users increasingly expect applications to be intelligent and personalized according to their individual needs [78]. This finding suggests that health applications are evolving from single-purpose, one-dimensional tools into integrated and user-centered digital health platforms.

Our findings also highlight the critical importance of appropriate technical infrastructure, robust security measures, and the protection of user privacy. Selecting a suitable development framework, ensuring interoperability with Health Information Systems (HIS), integrating artificial intelligence technologies, complying with privacy regulations such as HIPAA, adopting communication protocols and standards, and enabling secure data exchange were all identified as key factors contributing to the success of health applications [9].

Another notable finding of this study was the simultaneous consideration of both managerial and business dimensions alongside technical and clinical aspects. This indicates that the effectiveness of health applications depends not only on sound design and implementation but also on strategic marketing, user acquisition and retention, maintenance, and continuous development.

The primary innovation of the proposed framework lies in its provision of a comprehensive, step-by-step roadmap for health application development that encompasses not only technical and clinical dimensions but also market-related considerations, design and usability aspects, resource management, sustainable development, and growth strategies. This framework could be a useful tool for people who work on digital health or do research in that area.

The use of apps by the elderly in developed countries is a growing trend [24]. For instance, the adoption rate of smartphones by older adults aged 55–91 years was 40–68% in 2019 [79], and due to recent advancements in mHealth, its amount is gradually increasing [80]. However, according to Xie et al.’s study, the adoption rates of mHealth among the elderly are still relatively low. To improve acceptance of mHealth interventions in this demographic, prior research has underscored the necessity of delivering high-quality information, intuitive interfaces, engaging functionalities, minimal storage demands, expansive display screens, and the participation of healthcare professionals [81]. In line with these findings, the present study identified several key barriers among older adults, including low digital literacy, limited technological experience, lack of smartphone ownership, and reluctance to use new technologies (Table 6). These findings highlight the existence of a digital divide among elderly populations and suggest that improving application design alone is insufficient. Instead, complementary educational and supportive interventions are necessary to facilitate effective adoption and sustained use of mHealth applications.

According to the study’s findings, the most significant barriers to the creation or assessment of health apps for the elderly are participant selection bias, small sample sizes, lack of generalizability, subpar app design, low levels of technology literacy, a lack of smartphones, and evaluation difficulties. Heuristic evaluation is the most often used method for assessing usability. Heuristic evaluation is one inspection technique that can be used to pinpoint the primary usability problems [82].

In a scoping review, Wang et al. demonstrated that financial limitations, functional constraints, and lack of access to health services pose barriers to healthcare app utilization by the elderly [79]. By and large, self-care mobile apps can help senior patients with self-awareness (i.e., self-help, self-education, self-regulation, and self-efficacy), self-management (i.e., self-medication, self-treatment, and self-examination), and self-testing (i.e., self-screening, self-diagnosis, and self-monitoring) [10].

The current review demonstrated that the apps in elderly health led to the improvement of physical, mental, and social-behavioral health; communication; and economic status, of which physical health accounted for the largest amount. A study conducted by Abbaspur-Behbahani et al. revealed that mhealth services for the elderly were mostly used for therapy, information provision, self-help, monitoring, and remote interventions during the COVID-19 outbreak [23].

In another study in 2022, the University of Michigan Institute for Healthcare Policy and Innovation conducted a poll that found nearly three in ten adults aged 50–80 currently use one or more mHealth apps, most commonly to track their physical activity, nutrition, and medication [28]. The findings of these studies are consistent with those of the current study. To discuss, a review was conducted by Awan et al. to demonstrate usability barriers for elderly users in smartphone app usage; ten considerable obstacles were reported [83]. In contrast, the main aim of this work was to identify crucial barrier themes for developed apps.

According to publications, technological advances have had a profound impact on society’s health and have led to the emergence of new methods to improve people’s health and quality of life [84]. From the studies reviewed, it can be concluded that cultural, social, economic, and infrastructural differences in different countries, i.e., developed or developing countries, can affect the acceptance and use of health apps by the elderly [28, 81, 85].

The development of online consultations allows seniors to benefit from health services without the need to visit a doctor in person [86]. On the other hand, the use of remote monitoring tools, such as digital blood pressure devices and health apps, allows seniors to continuously monitor their health status and provide doctors with accurate information about their condition [14]. Despite the promise of mobile-oriented technologies to improve population health, there are major concerns about the introduction of new technologies and health apps. A major challenge is that these technologies may exacerbate inequalities [10]. In the context of mHealth, the main challenge is that lower adoption rates in disadvantaged communities and poor countries may lead to inequitable improvements in health and widening inequalities [88]. Scientifically, in developed countries with strong infrastructure, technology adoption is high and literacy is high, while in countries with low economic levels and weak communication infrastructure, adoption of emerging technologies such as mobile apps is very poor [89].

Furthermore, the effectiveness of these apps remains unconfirmed, particularly in the elderly mental health domain. Liu et al., in a study, revealed that most mHealth apps available in the market do not carefully consider the unique needs, preferences, and capabilities of elderly users, resulting in low usability [90]. To overcome this issue, this review stated that it is crucial to create an app design roadmap that outlines the necessary phases of the app development process and their significance. Espay et al. conducted a study that developed a roadmap for the implementation of patient-centered digital outcome measures in Parkinson’s disease. Results indicated that it can harmonize the collaborative endeavor among stakeholders, improve assessments of individuals with Parkinson’s disease, tailor symptomatic therapy, and enhance healthcare outcomes [91]. The results of this study are consistent with those of the current study.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this review lies in its comprehensive literature search, which covered three major bibliographic databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) and was supplemented by the inclusion of relevant grey literature. The extensive volume of retrieved studies prompted the researchers to set a time frame to ensure the feasibility of the review process.

One notable limitation pertains to the varying age thresholds employed to delineate “elderly” across the studies included. Most articles lumped older adults together into one group, even though there was a lot of age-related diversity. This made it challenging to do a meaningful subgroup synthesis. Consequently, we present the findings and the proposed action roadmap at a broad and generalized level.

Although the search of medical databases is comprehensive, the exclusion of prominent computer science databases, e.g., ACM Digital Library and IEEE Xplore, is another limitation. Such a decision may have resulted in the loss of relevant studies focused on technological aspects or user interface design.

Conclusion

The main purpose of this scoping review was to investigate the use of health apps designed for the elderly. The findings indicate that the utilization of health apps has shown a promising trend recently, largely due to their effectiveness in rehabilitation and health monitoring, particularly in improving quality of life and promoting independence among elderly populations.

The results of the data extraction process also showed that the majority of the apps reported in the literature concentrated on social, psychological, and physical advancements. Furthermore, we noticed that the majority of the research included suggestions for improving the health of the elderly and that controlled experiments and usability evaluations were typically included in the validation process. Apps can assist elderly residents in their daily activities, enabling them to maintain their health and safety while living independently. Overall, the findings suggest that mHealth applications have a significant positive impact on the health and well-being of older adults, offering multiple functional and clinical benefits.

In future works, the research team could consider piloting the roadmap in a real-world setting and reporting on its effectiveness. The literature research and theme analysis indicated that the development of health applications necessitates more than basic technological design; it demands a multi-phase, user-centric, and market-focused methodology within a defined timeframe. The extracted themes show that the development process starts with an initial phase of needs assessment and market analysis. This is followed by implementation, ongoing evaluation, business management, and sustainable development.
