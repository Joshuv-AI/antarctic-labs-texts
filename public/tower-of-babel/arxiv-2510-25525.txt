Multiparameter Lévy white noise theory and applications
Olfa Draouil, Rahma Yasmina Moulay Hachemi & Bernt Øksendal

Abstract
We construct a white noise theory and white noise calculus for the (multi-parameter) Lévy sheet and its compensated Poisson random measures. The theory applies to stochastic partial differential equations subject to Lévy noise.

Introduction

The theory of white noise for Brownian motion was initiated by T.Hida
[
9
]
and further developed by him and coauthors Kuo, Potthoff and Streit in
[
10
]
. Subsequently an extension to (multiparameter) Brownian sheet was carried out and applied to stochastic partial differential equations by Holden, Ubøe, Øksendal and Zhang in
[
12
]
.

Later Di Nunno, Øksendal & Proske
[
4
]
and Øksendal & Proske
[
21
]
developed a white noise theory for Lévy processes and for compensated Poisson random measures, respectively. See the book
[
5
]
for a comprehensive coverage of this.

In view of the success of this white noise theory for the Brownian sheet and the Lévy process it is natural to ask if it is also possible to carry out a construction of white noise for the
(multiparameter) Lévy sheet
.

The theory of Lévy sheets was developed by Iafrate & Ricciutu in
[
13
]
and by Dalang & Walsh
[
3
]
. See also the earlier paper Adler et al
[
1
]
.
But, to the best of our knowledge, so far there has been no papers dealing with the white noise theory for Lévy sheets. The purpose of this paper is to provide such a construction and give some applications. This theory appears to be new. We obtain it by combining the results in the papers
[
13
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
21
]
above.

Multiparameter Lévy white noise and stochastic distributions

This is the main section, in which we first give a construction of multiparameter Lévy sheets, and then the associated white noise theory and calculus.

First we recall the theory of Lévy sheets, mainly based on
[
13
]
,
[
3
]
:

2.1
Multiparameter Lévy sheets

Definition 2.1

A
multiparameter Lévy sheet
is a stochastic process
L(x)=L(x,\omega);x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\omega\in\Omega
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and satisfying the following conditions:

•

L
(0)=0 a.s.

•

L
has stationary, independent increments with respect to the natural partial ordering on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

•

L
is continuous in probability and has cádlág paths a.s.

Remark 2.2

If
R=\Pi_{j=1}^{n}(x_{j},x^{\prime}_{j}]
is a box in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
then
\Delta_{R}L
denotes the increment of
L
over the box
R
. For example, if
n=2
then

\Delta_{R}L=L(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})-L(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2})-L(x_{1},x_{2}^{\prime})+L(x_{1},x_{2}).

Remark 2.3

If we also assume that
L
has continuous paths, then
L
becomes a
Brownian sheet
.

Definition 2.4

(i)

Let
R
denote a box in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and let
U
be a Borel subset of
\mathbb{R}_{0}:=\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
.
Define
the jump measure

N(R,U)
to be the number of jumps of
L
within the box
R
of jumps size in
U
. By stationarity the map
R\mapsto N(R,U);U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{0})
depends only on the volume of
R
and we can write the random measure generated by
(R,U)\mapsto N(R,U)
in differential form as
N(dx,du)
, where
dx
and
du
are the Lebesgue measure differentials corresponding to
R
and
U
, respectively.

(ii)

Put
R_{1}=[0,1]^{n}
. The measure
U\mapsto\nu(U):=\mathbb{E}[N(R_{1},U)];U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{0})
is called the Lévy measure of
L
.

(iii)

The corresponding
compensated jump measure
is defined by

\widetilde{N}(dx,du)=N(dx,du)-\nu(du)dx,

where
dx=dx_{1}dx_{2}...dx_{n}
is the volume differential at
x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the following we will for simplicity assume that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty.

(2.1)

Then by the Lévy-Khinchine theorem the Lévy sheet
L
can be given the following representation:

\displaystyle L(x)=\alpha\Pi_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}+\sigma B(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widetilde{N}(x,dz);\ x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

(2.2)

for some constants
\alpha,\sigma
, where
B(x)
is a Brownian sheet.

In particular, if we assume that
\alpha=\sigma=0
then we have the
pure jump Lévy sheet
, with representation

\displaystyle L(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z\widetilde{N}(x,dz)=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\widetilde{N}(d\xi,dz);\ x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}.

(2.3)

2.2
The Lévy white noise probability space and the pure jump Lévy sheet

In the following we let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing smooth functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
denote its dual, which is the space of tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the weak
∗
topology.

It is possible to construct a
pure jump Lévy sheet

L
corresponding to a given Lévy measure
\nu

as a process on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. The details are the following:

Let
\nu
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)<\infty.

(2.4)

By the Bochner-Minlos-Sazonov theorem (see
[
8
]
), there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{B}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
such that

\int_{\Omega}e^{i\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle}\mathbb{P}(d\omega)=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Psi(\varphi(x))dx\right);\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(2.5)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\langle\omega,\varphi\rangle
denotes the action of
\omega\in\Omega:=\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

\Psi(w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{i\,w\,z}-1-i\,w\,z\right)\nu(dz);w\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.6)

We let
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
and
Var[\cdot]=Var_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
denote expectation and variance, respectively, with respect to
\mathbb{P}
.

Note that by (
2.5
) we have, for all parameters
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|e^{i\langle\omega,a\varphi\rangle)}]=\exp\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\{e^{ia\varphi(x)z}-1-ia\varphi(x)z\}\nu(dz)dx\right)

(2.7)

Expanding the exponential function on both side of (
2.7
) and comparing the terms with the same power of
a
, we obtain the following result:

Lemma 2.5

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then we have

\mathbb{E}[\langle\cdot,\varphi\rangle]=0

(2.8)

and

Var[\langle\cdot,\varphi\rangle]:=\mathbb{E}[\langle\cdot,\varphi\rangle^{2}]=M\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi^{2}(x)dx,

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle M=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz).

(2.10)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\langle\omega,\varphi_{1}\rangle-\langle\omega,\varphi_{2}\rangle)^{2}]=M\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\varphi_{1}(x)-\varphi_{2}(x))^{2}dx=M\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\lambda)},

(2.11)

where
\lambda
is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Using this result we can for each
\omega\in\Omega
extend the definition of
\langle\omega,f\rangle
from
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to any
f\in L^{2}(\lambda)
as follows:

If
f\in L^{2}(\lambda)
, choose
f_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
f_{n}\rightarrow f
in
L^{2}(\lambda)
.
Then by Lemma
2.5
we see that
\{\langle\omega,f_{n}\rangle\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
and hence convergent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.
Moreover, the limit depends only on

f
and not the sequence
\{f_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
We denote this limit by
\langle\omega,f\rangle;\ \omega\in\Omega,f\in L^{2}(\lambda).

In particular, if we define

\widetilde{L}(x):=\widetilde{L}(x,\omega):=\langle\omega,\chi_{\Pi_{j=1}^{n}[0,x_{j}]}\rangle;\quad x=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

then we have the following result:

Theorem 2.6

The stochastic process
\widetilde{L}(x)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
has a càdlàg version, denoted by
L
.
This process
L(x)\ ;x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is a (pure jump) Lévy
sheet with Lévy measure
\nu
.

2.3
A chaos expansion

We now proceed to define the white noise of the Lévy sheet we constructed in the previous section. We will follow the presentation in
[
5
]
Section 13.2 closely, but with the necessary modifications due to the fact that we are now dealing with an
n
-parameter Lévy sheet, not a classical 1-parameter Lévy process. Here are the details:

From now on we assume that the Lévy measure
\nu
satisfies the
following condition:

For all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}\backslash(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}\exp(\lambda\left|z\right|)\nu(dz)<\infty

(2.12)

One can prove that if this holds, then
\nu
has finite moments of all orders
n\geq 2
.
For example, the condition clearly holds if
\nu
is supported on
[-C,C]
for some
C>0
.

From this condition one can prove that the polynomials are dense in
L^{2}(\rho)
,
where

\rho(dz)=z^{2}\nu(dz).

(2.13)

See
[
20
]
. We let
\left\{\eta_{m}\right\}_{m\geq 0}=\left\{\eta_{0}=1,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},...\right\}
be the orthogonolization of

\left\{1,z,z^{2},...\right\}
with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}(\rho)
.

Define

p_{j}(z):=\left\|\eta_{j-1}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{-1}z\eta_{j-1}(z);\text{ }j=1,2,...

(2.14)

and

m_{2}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}z^{2}\nu(dz)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left\|\eta_{0}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\left\|1\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

(2.15)

In particular,

p_{1}(z)=m_{2}^{-1}z\text{ or }z=m_{2}p_{1}(z).

(2.16)

Then
\left\{p_{j}(z)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
is an
orthonormal basis

for
L^{2}(\nu)
.

Recall that the
Hermite polynomials

h_{n}(x);x\in\mathbb{R}
are defined by

h_{n}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{{1}/{2}x^{2}}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(e^{-{1}/{2}x^{2}});\quad n=0,1,2,\cdots.

[formula]

Thus the first Hermite polynomials are

\displaystyle h_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=1,\;h_{1}(x)=x,\;h_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1,\;h_{3}(x)=x^{3}-3x,

(2.17)

\displaystyle h_{4}(x)

\displaystyle=x^{4}-6x^{2}+3,\;h_{5}(x)=x^{5}-10x^{3}+15x,\cdots.

(2.18)

The
Hermite functions

\xi_{n}(x)
are defined by

\xi_{n}(x)=\pi^{-{1}/{4}}((n-1)!)^{-{1}/{2}}e^{-{1}/{2}x^{2}}h_{n-1}(\sqrt{2}x);\quad n=1,2,\cdots.

We note the following useful properties of
\xi_{n}
:

•

\xi_{n}\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{for all}\;n.

•

The collection
\{\xi_{n}\}^{\infty}_{n=1}
constitutes an
orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}).

•

\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\xi_{n}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{-{1}/{12}}).

Proofs of these statements can be found in Hille and
Phillips (1957), Chapter 21.

We will use these functions to define an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
. In the following, we let

\beta=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})
denote
n
-dimensional multi-indices with

\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all natural numbers.. By the above it follows that the family of tensor
products

\xi_{\beta}:=\xi_{(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})}:=\xi_{\beta_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{\beta_{n}}\;;\;\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let

\beta^{(j)}=(\beta_{1}^{(j)},\beta_{2}^{(j)},\cdots,\beta_{n}^{(j)})
be
the
j
th multi-index in some fixed ordering of all
n
-dimensional
multi-indices
\beta=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. We
may assume that this ordering has the property that

i<j\Rightarrow\beta_{1}^{(i)}+\beta_{2}^{(i)}+\cdots+\beta_{n}^{(i)}\leq\beta_{1}^{(j)}+\beta_{2}^{(j)}+\cdots+\beta_{n}^{(j)},

i.e., that the
\{\beta^{(j)}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
occur in increasing order.

Next, define the bijection
\kappa:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{N}
by

\kappa(i,j)=j+(i+j-2)(i+j-1)/2;\quad i,j=1,2,...

(2.19)

As in Section 2.2.1 in
[
12
]
we let
\left\{e_{j}(x)\right\}_{j\geq 1}
be a fixed ordering of all
n
-times tensor products of the Hermite functions.
This family forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Put

\theta_{\kappa(j,k)}(x,z):=e_{j}(x)p_{k}(z);\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},z\in\mathbb{R}_{0}.

(2.20)

In the following we let
\mathcal{J}
denote the family of all finite multi-indices
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},...,\alpha_{m});\ m=1,2,...
of nonnegative integers
\alpha_{j}
.

If
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}
with
Index(\alpha)=j
and
\left|\alpha\right|=m
, we define
\theta^{\otimes\alpha}:(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\theta^{\otimes\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})

(2.21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{1}^{\otimes\alpha_{1}}\otimes...\otimes\theta_{j}^{\otimes\alpha_{j}}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{\alpha_{1}\text{ factors}}{\underbrace{\theta_{1}(x^{(1)},z_{1})\cdot...\cdot\theta_{1}(x^{(\alpha_{1})},z_{\alpha_{1}})}}\cdot...\cdot\underset{\alpha_{j}\text{ factors}}{\underbrace{\theta_{j}(x^{(m-\alpha_{j}+1)},z_{m-\alpha_{j}+1})\cdot...\cdot\theta_{j}(x^{(m)},z_{m})}}.

We define
\theta_{i}^{\otimes 0}=1.

Finally we let
\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}
denote the

symmetrized
tensor product of the
\theta_{k}

{}^{\prime}s:

\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})=\theta_{1}^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha_{1}}\otimes...\otimes\theta_{j}^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha_{j}}(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m}).

(2.22)

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{J}
define

K_{\alpha}:=I_{\left|\alpha\right|}\left(\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}\right),

(2.23)

where in general
I_{m}
is the symmetrized iterated integral with respect to
\widetilde{N}
, defined for symmetric functions
g:(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle I_{m}(g)=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{0})^{m}}g(x^{(1)},z_{1};x^{(2)},z_{2};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})\widetilde{N}^{\otimes m}(dx^{(1)}dz_{1};...,dx^{(m)}dz_{m}).

(2.24)

Example 2.7

For example, if
m=1
we get

\displaystyle I_{1}(g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(x,z)\widetilde{N}(dx,dz),

(2.25)

and if
m=2
we have

\displaystyle I_{2}(g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}g(x^{(1)},z_{1};x^{(2)},z_{2})\widetilde{N}(dx^{(2)},dz_{2})\Big)\widetilde{N}(dx^{(1)},dz_{1}).

(2.26)

Example 2.8

With
\varepsilon^{(k)}=(0,...,0,1,0,...)
with
1
on
k
th
place, we have, writing
\varepsilon^{(\kappa(i,j))}=\varepsilon^{(i,j)}
,

K_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}=I_{1}(\theta^{\otimes\varepsilon^{(i,k)}})=I_{1}\left(\theta_{\kappa(i,j)}\right)=I_{1}\left(e_{i}(x)p_{j}(z)\right).

(2.27)

As in the Brownian sheet case one can prove that
\{K_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}
are
orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
and

\|K_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P)}=\alpha!.

Note that if
|\alpha|=m
,

\alpha!=\|K_{\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(P)}=m!\|\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((\lambda\times\nu)^{m})},

where as before
\lambda
denotes Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
By our construction of
\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}
we know that
any
f\in\widetilde{L}^{2}((\lambda\times\nu)^{m})
can be written

f(x^{(1)},z_{1};...;x^{(m)},z_{m})=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=m}c_{\alpha}\theta^{\hat{\otimes}\alpha}(x^{(1)},z_{1},...,x^{(m)},z_{m}).

(2.28)

Hence

I_{m}(f_{m})=\sum_{\left|\alpha\right|=m}c_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}

(2.29)

This gives the following chaos expansion:

Theorem 2.9

(Chaos expansion)

Any
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})

has a unique expansion of the form

F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}c_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}.

(2.30)

with
c_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover,

\left\|F\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}\alpha!c_{\alpha}^{2}.

(2.31)

Example 2.10

Let
h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and define, for
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
,

\displaystyle F=\int_{0}^{x}h(\xi)dL(\xi).

(2.32)

By (
2.3
) this can be written

\displaystyle F=I_{1}(h(\xi)\chi_{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,x_{i}]}(\xi)\zeta).

(2.33)

Therefore
F
has the following expansion, writing
\chi_{\Pi_{i=1}^{n}[0,x_{i}]}=\chi_{[0,x]}
for short:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{1}(\sum_{i\geq 1}(h\chi_{[0,x]},e_{i})e_{i}(\xi)\zeta)=\sum_{i\geq 1}(h\chi_{[0,x]},e_{i})I_{1}(e_{i}(\xi)\zeta)

(2.34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}h(\xi)e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\right)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}m_{2}.

Recall that
z=m_{2}p_{1}(z);
see (
2.16
).
In particular, choosing
h=1
we get the following expansion for the Lévy sheet
L
:

L(x)=\sum_{i\geq 1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}e_{i}(\xi)d\xi\right)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}m_{2}.

(2.35)

In the following we put

(2\mathbb{N})^{k\alpha}=\prod_{j\geq 1}(2j)^{k\alpha_{j}},

(2.36)

if
\alpha=(\alpha_{1,}\alpha_{2,},...)\in\mathcal{J}
.

Definition 2.11

(The Lévy-Hida spaces)

(i)

Stochastic test functions
(\mathcal{S})
.

We define
(\mathcal{S})
to be the set of all
\varphi=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})

such that

\left\|\varphi\right\|_{k}^{2}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{k\alpha}<\infty\text{ for \emph{all}
}k\in\mathbb{N},

(2.37)

equipped with the projective topology.

(ii)

Stochastic distributions
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
.

We define
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
to be the set of all expansions
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}
such that

\left\|F\right\|_{-q}^{2}:=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{-q\alpha}<\infty\text{ for \emph{some} }q\in\mathbb{N}.

(2.38)

endowed with the inductive topology.

The space
(\mathcal{S})^{*}
is the
dual of
(\mathcal{S}).
If
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}b_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
and
\varphi=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S}),
then the action of
F

on
\varphi
is

\left\langle F,\varphi\right\rangle=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}b_{\alpha}\alpha!.

(2.39)

(iii)

Generalized expectation.

If
F=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}a_{\alpha}K_{\alpha}\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
, we define
the generalized expectation
\mathbb{E}[F]
of
F
by

\mathbb{E}[F]=a_{0}.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[K_{\alpha}]=0
for all
\alpha\neq 0
.
Therefore the generalized expectation coincides with the usual expectation if
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
.

2.4
White noise for Lévy sheets and their compensated Poisson random measures

Now we have the prerequisites to define the white noise of a Lévy sheet and its associated Poisson random measure. In view of (
2.35
) the following definition is natural:

Definition 2.12

(i)

The
Lévy white noise

\overset{\bullet}{L}(x):=V(x):=\frac{\partial^{n}L(x)}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}...,\partial x_{n}}

is defined by the expansion

\displaystyle V(x):

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)K_{\varepsilon^{(i,1)}}=m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}(e_{i}(x)p_{1}(z))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}(x)I_{1}(e_{i}(x)z).

(ii)

The
white noise

\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)
of the compensated random measure
\widetilde{N}(dx,dz)
is defined by the expansion

\overset{\bullet}{\widetilde{N}}(x,z)=\sum_{i,j\geq 1}e_{i}(x)p_{j}(z)K_{{}_{\varepsilon^{(i,j)}}}(\omega).

(2.40)

Remark 2.13

Note that in the case of the expansion for
\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)
we have

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{J}}c_{\alpha}^{2}\alpha!(2\mathbb{N})^{-q\alpha}=m_{2}^{2}\sum_{i\geq 1}e_{i}^{2}(x)2^{-q}\kappa(i,1)^{-q}<\infty\text{ }

for
q\geq 2.
Here we have used that
\kappa(i,1)=1+(i-1)i/2\geq i,
and the following
well-known estimate for the Hermite functions:

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left|e_{m}(x)\right|=\mathcal{O}(m^{-\frac{1}{12}}).

(2.41)

Therefore
\overset{\bullet}{L}(x)\in(\mathcal{S})^{*}
for all
x.
Similarly

3.1
Application to Tumor Cell Invasion in Human Tissues

Introduction

The dynamics of cancer invasion represent one of the complex and important problems in modern biology. It was stated by Murray in his seminal work on mathematical biology:

“The process of tumor invasion is not a simple, deterministic unfolding of a genetic program. It is a spatially structured, stochastic competition between the tumor and the host tissue, mediated by a myriad of biochemical and biomechanical signals. Capturing this requires models that can integrate deterministic drivers with intrinsic randomness and discrete, transformative events.”
[
19
]

The important question is; what is the role of the multi-parameter Lévy noise? A multi-parameter Lévy sheet
V(t,x)
allows us to model events that are localized in both space and time. A jump at a point
(t_{0},x_{0})
represents a sudden, disruptive event happening at a precise location
x_{0}
at the exact moment
t_{0}
. This is the strong point to simulating the spatially heterogeneous nature of real tumor.

Modeling the problem

Our model for tumor cell density
Y(t,x)
is:

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y(t,x)=\lambda\Delta Y(t,x)+\sigma W(t,x)+\gamma V(t,x)

(3.11)

In this application, we model the invasion of a solid tumor through a host tissue, such as breast or brain parenchyma.

The solution
Y(t,x)
represents the logarithm of the tumor cell density. The initial condition
Y(0,x)=\delta(x)
represents a localized cluster of tumor cells initiating the invasion process.

•

\frac{\partial^{\alpha}}{\partial t^{\alpha}}Y
:
Models the “anomalous diffusion” of cells. We know that cells move in a complex random motion with memory path as a result of interactions with the tissue structure.

- In the classical case, i. e. when
\alpha=1
, this equation models the normal diffusion of heat in a random or noisy medium, the noise being represented by the time-space white noises
W(t,x)
and
V(t,x)
.

- When
\alpha>1
the inclusion can be used to model
superdiffusion or enhanced diffusion
, where the particles spread faster than in regular diffusion. For example, this may occur in some biological systems.

- When
\alpha<1
the inclusion models
subdiffusion
, in which the times of travel of the particles are longer than in the standard case. Such a situation may occur in transport systems.

•

\lambda\Delta Y
:
Models the random motility of cancer cells through the tissue. The coefficient
\lambda
represents the cell motility rate, help to quantify how the active cells move and disperse from high.

•

\sigma W(t,x)
:
Represents random fluctuations in the tumor environment, such as microscopic variations in nutrients, constantly drive the cells, making their behavior unpredictable.

•

\gamma V(t,x)
:
models sudden events in the tumor microenvironment that can change the direction of invasion. See e.g.
[
7
]
.
