Case report: primary cardiac lymphoma presenting as acute coronary syndrome and atrioventricular block due to right coronary artery compression
Authors: Clara Belmonte Herrera, Mireia Ble Gimeno, Laia Belarte Tornero, María José Martínez Membrive, Miren Vicente Elcano, Doralisa Morrone, Youssef Mahmoud AbdElMoneim, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac tumours are exceedingly rare and often present with non-specific clinical manifestations, making diagnosis challenging. Primary cardiac lymphoma represents a small proportion of primary cardiac tumours and may occasionally mimic acute coronary syndromes due to mass effect on coronary arteries or the conduction system.Case summaryWe report the case of a 61-year-old woman admitted for syncope who subsequently developed chest pain, inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and complete atrioventricular block. Emergency coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary disease but demonstrated extrinsic compression and stiffness of the right coronary artery. Transthoracic echocardiography identified a large right ventricular mass with tricuspid valve involvement and right ventricular inflow obstruction, associated with pericardial and pleural effusion. Multimodality imaging with computed tomography and positron emission tomography showed a large hypermetabolic cardiac mass with mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Histological analysis from a computed tomography-guided biopsy confirmed primary cardiac high-grade B-cell lymphoma. Chemotherapy with rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone was initiated, leading to significant tumour reduction but complicated by transient chemotherapy-induced left ventricular dysfunction, which was managed through a multidisciplinary cardio-oncology approach. Despite initial clinical and cardiac improvement, the patient later developed central nervous system progression and died 10 months after diagnosis due to therapy-related neurotoxicity.DiscussionThis case highlights a rare presentation of primary cardiac lymphoma manifesting as acute coronary syndrome and atrioventricular block secondary to extrinsic coronary compression. It underscores the importance of multimodality imaging, histological confirmation, and adherence to European Society of Cardiology cardio-oncology guidelines. Early multidisciplinary management is essential to balance effective oncological treatment with cardiovascular safety.Keywords: Primary cardiac lymphoma, Cardiac tumour, Acute coronary syndrome, Atrioventricular block, Case report, Multimodality imaging, Cardio-oncology

Introduction

Cardiac tumours are rare and may present with a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations. Sometimes, they are detected incidentally through imaging studies.1,2 We present the case of a young woman presenting to the emergency department with acute coronary syndrome and complete atrioventricular (AV) block due to extrinsic compression of the right coronary artery (RCA) by a cardiac mass.

Case presentation

On 7 March 2024, a 61-year-old woman was admitted to our tertiary-level hospital due to syncope. Her medical history included long-term smoking but no other cardiovascular risk factors, previous diseases, or regular medication use.

Upon initial evaluation, the patient had arterial hypertension of 144/90 mmHg and a heart rate of 84 bpm. Peripheral arterial oxygen saturation was 95% on oxygen at 2 L/min with adequate peripheral perfusion.

The first electrocardiogram (ECG) showed a sinus rhythm of 84 bpm with diffuse T-wave inversions in the precordial leads. During observation in the emergency room, chest pain began, and a new ECG showed bradycardia due to complete AV block with a narrow QRS complex and ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads. AV conduction was restored after intravenous administration of atropine. Coronary angiography (Figure 1) was performed emergently, and no significant obstructive atherosclerotic coronary disease was identified apart from a dynamically compressed segment of the RCA. Laboratory analysis revealed an estimated glomerular filtration rate of 62 mL/min and peak troponin T levels of 214.2 ng/L (normal value <14). She was admitted to the cardiac critical care unit to complete the study.

Positron emission tomography-computed tomography image showing a large, known solid cardiac mass centred in the right atrium with intense and highly heterogeneous pathological uptake (SUVmax 31.99).

Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed a mass in the right ventricle measuring 74 × 60 mm, with tricuspid valve involvement and right ventricular inflow obstruction. Intravenous corticosteroids and intravenous fluids were started to reduce inflammation and reduce extrinsic compression of the right coronary artery. The patient remained haemodynamically stable, and a new ECG showed sinus rhythm and isoelectric ST segments.

A staging workup was performed with computed tomography (CT) and positron emission tomography (PET) scans, which revealed a large solid cardiac mass with pathological and heterogeneous uptake (SUVmax 31.99) and several mediastinal lymph nodes (see Supplementary material online, Video S1).

A CT-guided transthoracic biopsy of the cardiac mass suggested high-grade B-cell lymphoma. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the lymphoid cells were positive for CD20, CD19, and CD79a but negative for CD3 and CD5. The final diagnosis was primary cardiac high-grade lymphoma.

Chemotherapy was initiated with rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R-CHOP), with close follow-up by the Cardio-Oncology unit.

After R-CHOP initiation, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) showed a right-sided cardiac mass encasing the right coronary artery and involving the tricuspid valve apparatus. Biventricular systolic function was mildly reduced (left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) 41%, right ventricular ejection fraction 45%), with elevated native T1 and extracellular volume values.

After a multidisciplinary team (MDT) discussion involving cardio-oncologists and haematologists, the decision was made to continue R-CHOP with liposomal anthracyclines while initiating heart failure treatment and close monitoring of heart failure symptoms. After six cycles, a TTE revealed normal LVEF and a reduction in the size of the mass (43 × 42 mm), with no pericardial effusion. During follow-up, she remained asymptomatic, with no signs of heart failure or arrhythmias.

At diagnosis, the disease was predominantly confined to the heart. Despite an initial partial response to R-CHOP chemotherapy, the patient subsequently developed central nervous system (CNS) progression, requiring to be referred to a specialized centre for chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy. CNS involvement is a recognized adverse prognostic feature in aggressive B-cell lymphomas and reflects the challenging clinical course of primary cardiac lymphoma. According to the available clinical information, the patient was reported to have died 10 months after diagnosis due to treatment-related neurotoxicity. Further details regarding the neurological complications and their management were not available to the authors.

Discussion

Primary cardiac lymphoma (PCL) is rare, accounting for ∼1%–2% of the incidence of primary cardiac tumours.1

Clinical presentation may vary from incidental findings on imaging studies to a broad spectrum of symptoms. Symptoms may be related to systemic manifestations, cardiac symptoms (e.g. mass effect leading to arrhythmias or valvular obstruction), or embolic events.1

Multimodality imaging plays a central role in the evaluation of cardiac masses. Transthoracic echocardiography is often the first diagnostic modality, allowing detection and anatomical localization of the lesion. Cardiac magnetic resonance provides detailed tissue characterization, while CT and PET-CT contribute to the assessment of tumour extent, extracardiac involvement, and metabolic activity. In the present case, the combination of TTE, CT, PET-CT, and CMR enabled comprehensive characterization of the mass and guided further management. Nevertheless, tissue biopsy remained essential for establishing the definitive histopathological diagnosis. This diagnostic approach is consistent with current ESC cardio-oncology recommendations.2,3

Primary cardiac lymphoma is most frequently associated with B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. It is more commonly observed in males than in females, predominantly located in the right cardiac chambers. The most frequent clinical manifestation is dyspnoea, followed by chest pain, syncope, and constitutional symptoms. Arrhythmias such as AV block and supraventricular arrhythmias are a common presentation.4,5

We report a case of inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) complicated by an AV block. From a MINOCA perspective, the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease supports evaluation for alternative mechanisms of myocardial ischaemia. In this case, multimodality imaging supported extrinsic coronary compression as the most likely mechanism of myocardial ischaemia. The transient nature of the STEMI and its rapid resolution after corticosteroid therapy further support this hypothesis over alternative mechanisms such as coronary vasospasm. Given these findings, additional MINOCA workup was not pursued as it was unlikely to change the diagnostic conclusion.

Regarding the complete AV block, transient AV nodal ischaemia secondary to RCA compression is the most likely mechanism. The transient nature of the block, narrow QRS complex, prompt response to atropine, and the lack of imaging evidence of septal involvement make direct tumour infiltration less likely.

While this is not the most common presentation of cardiac lymphoma, it is not unprecedented. Similar cases have been described in the literature, including reports of PCL presenting as myocardial infarction6–8 and a case of previously undiagnosed metastatic lymphoma causing transmural myocardial infiltration and subsequent left ventricular free wall rupture.8

The recommended treatment for primary cardiac lymphoma is rituximab- and anthracycline-based chemotherapy, typically the R-CHOP regimen. Surgical intervention has primarily been reserved for the management of outflow tract obstruction; however, it has not demonstrated a survival benefit.5 Moreover, involvement of the left cardiac chambers has been identified as an adverse prognostic factor.4

Cardiotoxicity in the form of ventricular dysfunction associated with anthracycline use is well known and represents a significant challenge in the management of these patients. However, thanks to MDT meetings and the surveillance protocols established by ESC, early diagnosis and appropriate management are made possible through the timely initiation of protective treatment and serial monitoring of cardiac biomarkers and cardiac imaging, allowing continuation of chemotherapy, which has a prognostic impact.9
