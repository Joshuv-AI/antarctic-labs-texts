Gelatin-Based Hydrogel for Three-Dimensional Neuron Culture Application
Authors: Yu-Yun Gao, You-Ren Ji, Yi-Hua Hsu, Fang-Chun Liao, Nien-Tzu Kao, Abel Po-Hao Huang, Tai-Horng Young
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Gelatin is a biocompatible biomaterial composed of a variety of amino acids that provides a possibility to regulate the interaction between cationic amino acids and neural cells. Based on our first finding that the neuron viability was improved as the lysine on the gelatin was converted into a guanidine structure, a three-dimensional (3D) gelatin hydrogel composed of gelatin and poly(allylguanidine) (PAG) was prepared to investigate neural cell behaviors. As expected, improved neuron viability, neurite outgrowth, synaptogenesis, and inhibited glial cell growth were simultaneously observed in the gelatin cross-linked with the PAG hydrogel (G-PAG) but not in the gelatin hydrogel cross-linked with poly-d-lysine (PDL) or polyethylenimine (PEI). In addition, in vivo tests also illustrated that G-PAG could provide an environment for neural culture, with improving neuron viability and neurite outgrowth. Several hydrogel characteristics—including the swelling ratio, mechanical strength, and electric property—that theoretically can influence neural cell response showed no significant difference among them. Therefore, the guanidine structure of PAG was proposed to determine the behaviors of neural cells within the gelatin-polycation hydrogels, and we proposed that the neural cell behavior is regulated by a specific gelatin–neuron relationship. The information found in this study provides a concept to design and modify gelatin-based hydrogels for neural tissue engineering applications.

Introduction

The limited capacity of neural cells versus the formation of glial scars usually causes irreversible neural injury.1,2 During the past decade, two-dimensional (2D) substrates and three-dimensional (3D) scaffolds have been studied for neural engineering applications. The 3D scaffolds are more advantageous in mimicking in vivo environments to provide cell behaviors closer to the native situation.3−5 Gelatin is a biocompatible, biodegradable, low-antigenic, and Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved biomaterial6−8 that is an ideal alternative to collagen for 3D hydrogel construction. Several cross-linking techniques6,9,10 have been employed to make gelatin hydrogel with inner porous structure characteristics to ensure the exchange of nutrients and metabolites, increase the cell-matrix contact surface, and guide cell attachment and alignment.11−14 For neural engineering applications, gelatin hydrogels have been combined with other polymers,15 such as alginate,16,17 chitosan,17,18 and laminin,17,19 to improve neural stem cell differentiation, neuron activity, and neurite extension. However, the 3D hydrogel made of gelatin alone has not been reported to support neuron cell culture. It is speculated that its basic amino acid ratio is not enough to regulate neuronal cell behaviors.

A variety of amino acids in gelatin allow neural cells to interact with different pendant functional groups throughout the polymeric chain.20 It is known that the cationic polymer with a positive charge could enhance neural cell adhesion and differentiation.21,22 Typically, the main basic amino acids in gelatin are lysine and arginine, which might improve neural cell adhesion. Several studies have confirmed that lysine has a regulating role in neural cell behavior, but the relationship between the arginine group and neural cell behavior is rarely well-known. Moreover, our prior study found that, at the same applied concentration, PAG demonstrated a better impact on neural cells than PDL did, which gave rise to our interest in investigating whether an increase in arginine in gelatin could have a positive effect on neural cell behavior. Establishing a gelatin hydrogel with only a slightly increased amount of lysine or guanidine function groups instead of modifying other amino acid compositions provides a venue to explore the physical function of these two basic amino acids. Poly-d-lysine (PDL) has been used as a standard coating polymer for neuronal cell adhesion.23 According to our previous study, poly(allylguanidine) (PAG) possessed the ability to improve neuronal cell adhesion and viability.24 Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether altering the composition of lysine and arginine within the 3D gelatin hydrogel construction could guide neural cell response for further possible tissue engineering applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Poly(allylguanidine) (PAG) was synthesized as described in our previous study.24 Branched polyethylenimine (PEI) (P3143-100 ML), poly-d-lysine (PDL) (P6403), gelatin type A from porcine skin (gel strength ∼175 g bloom) (G2625-500G), N-(3 dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) (E7750-25G), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (130672-25G), morpholonoethanesulfonic acid (MES) (M2933-100G), and 1H-pyrazole-1-carboxamidine hydrochloride (PC) (H60631) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich.

Guanidilytation of Gelatin

Gelatin was guanidinylated by reacting with PC.25 Briefly, gelatin (2 g) was dissolved in deionized water (10 mL) at 60 °C and sterilized at 200 °C for 30 min. Different molar ratios to lysine in gelatin (1:2 and 1:10) of PC were added to the gelatin solution. The pH of the reacted solution was adjusted to over 9 with 5 N NaOH, and the guanidinylation reaction was performed at 37 °C for 5 days. After dialysis (molecular weight cutoff = 6000–8000) against deionized water for 3 days, the final products were collected and sterilized at 200 °C for 30 min.

Preparation of Gelatin-Based Hydrogels

Gelatin type A (200 mg) was dissolved in deionized water (1 mL) at 60 °C and sterilized at 200 °C for 30 min. The PEI, PAG, and PDL solutions were prepared by dissolving 4 mg of polymer powder in 1 mL of deionized water with adjustment of the pH value of PAG to 12.0.

EDC (48 mg) and NHS (6 mg) were dissolved in 0.05 M MES buffer (1 mL) and sterilized by filtration. The cross-linking agent and polymer solution were added to the gelatin solution sequentially to obtain the gelatin-based hydrogels at 37 °C. The hydrogels were subsequently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 3 days to remove the byproducts and residual unreactive agents.

Morphology of Gelatin-Based Hydrogel

The inner porous structure of gelatin-based hydrogel was visualized by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Hitachi Tabletop TM-3000) operated at 20 kV. The gelatin-based hydrogels were washed with PBS and freeze-dried for 3 days. Before SEM scanning, the sample was sputter-coated with gold. The ImageJ software was used for porosity analysis for a minimum of five SEM micrographs with at least 100 randomly measured pores. The data obtained were statistically analyzed and presented by a distribution histogram using the Prism software.

Neural Cell Culture

All experiments were conducted according to the guidelines of the National Taiwan University College of Medicine, Laboratory Animal Center. Seven day old Wistar rats were sterilized by wiping with 75% ethanol and then decapitated. The skull was removed to isolate the brain and the cerebellum.

Cerebellar Neurons

For the preparation of cerebellar neurons, the meninges as well as blood vessels or extraneous brain regions were removed under a dissecting microscope in the ice-cold Krebs–Ringer solution [120 mM NaCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 25 mM Na2HCO3, 5 mM KCl, 2.5 mM MgSO4, 13 mM glucose, and 0.3% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA)]. Each cerebellum was dissected and transferred to the 50 mL tube, and 0.25% w/v trypsin and 100 μL of DNase (2.5 mg/mL) were added. The tube was placed in the incubator (5% CO2, 37 °C) for 30 min. The digestion process was terminated by gently mixing with basal medium Eagle (BME) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS), and 25 mM KCl for 10 min. The cells were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The sediment was resuspended with the Krebs–Ringer solution, filtered through a 40 μm mesh, and then centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed carefully, and the cell pellet was resuspended with the fresh neuron medium and seeded on the gelatin-based hydrogels at a density of 1 × 106 cells per sample. After being cultured for 24 h, 10 μM cytosine arabinoside (Ara-C) was added to stop the proliferation of the glial cells for the near-pure neurons culture.26 For the coculture of neurons and glial cells, the same procedure excluding the addition of Ara-C was adopted.

Glial Cells

For the preparation of glial cells, the meninges of the brain were removed in an ice-cold PBS-EDTA solution followed by dissection and digestion with 0.25% w/v trypsin and 100 μL of DNase (2.5 mg/mL) in the incubator (5% CO2, 37 °C) for 30 min. The digestion process was terminated by the addition of Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) nutrient mixture F-12 (Gibco, US) [DMEM-F12, containing 10% FBS and 1% PS] and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. After the supernatant was carefully removed, the cell pellet was resuspended with the glia medium and seeded into a T75 flask, and the medium was changed every 3 days for a 7–10 day period of culture. To purify near-pure passage glial cells, the T75 flask was shaken at 180 rpm for 30 min on an orbital shaker. After replacing the supernatant with the glial medium, the T75 flask was shaken at 240 rpm for 6 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was removed, and the fresh DMEM-F12 was added to get the near-pure glial passage cells.

Near-pure passage glial cells were trypsinized and seeded on gelatin-based hydrogels at a density of 1 × 104 cells per sample.

Cell Viability Assay

Cellular Viability Test

For the cellular viability test, 250 μL of each gelatin-based hydrogel was cross-linked in a 24-well plate. After the hydrogel was completely cross-linked, two washing steps with both PBS and medium were performed. Neural cells were seeded for a 12 day period. The Alamar blue assay (Thermo Fisher, US) was adapted for cell viability. The Alamar blue reagent solution (100 μL) was added to each well and reacted for 4 h in an incubator (5% CO2, 37 °C). The measurement of Alamar blue fluorescence was determined by an ELISA reader (SpectraMax M2e; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), with the excitation at 560 nm and emission at 590 nm.

Live/Dead Assay

For the cellular viability qualitative test, 250 μL of each gelatin-based hydrogel was prepared in a μ-Plate 24 well (ibidi, Germany). After the hydrogel was completely cross-linked, two washing steps with both PBS and medium were performed. Neural cells were seeded for a 12 day period. The quantification of viable and dead cells was performed by the Live/Dead assay (Thermo Fisher, US). Gels were incubated in a medium supplemented with 2 μM calcein AM for living cells and 4 μM ethidium homodimer-1 for dead cells for 30 min. For washing, the medium was replaced with the fresh medium, and the cells were observed with a spinning disc confocal microscope (Zeiss Cell Observer SD).

Immunocytochemical Staining

For immunocytochemistry staining, 300 μL of each gelatin-based hydrogel was prepared in a three-well chamber slide (ibidi, Germany). After the hydrogel was completely cross-linked, two washing steps with both PBS and medium were performed. Neural cells were seeded and cultured for a 12 day period. Gelatin-based hydrogels were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with 1% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA, Hycells, Taiwan) for 1 h. The primary antibodies diluted in 1% w/v BSA in PBS were incubated for 3 days at room temperature. The primary antibodies used in this study included rabbit anti-growth-associated protein (anti-GAP43, 1:2500, ab75810; Abcam), mouse anti-GAP43 (1:2500, MO22170; Neuroscience), rabbit anti-synapsin I (1:1000, ab64581; Abcam), and mouse anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (anti-GFAP, 1:1000, ab190288; Abcam). After that, the primary antibodies were gently removed, and the PBS solution was added for the washing process. The secondary antibodies diluted in a PBS solution were incubated overnight at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used in this study included FITC-conjugated goat antirabbit or antimouse IgG (Chemicon, US), rhodamine-conjugated goat antirabbit or antimouse IgG (Chemicon, US), and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma, US). The secondary antibodies were washed for 1 h in PBS, and images were captured by a spinning disc confocal microscope (Zeiss Cell Observer SD). The quantification of the immunocytochemical image was analyzed by measuring the signal area by ImageJ.

PAG Hydrogel Implantation

Animal experiments were approved by the ethics committee of the Laboratory Animal Center at the National Taiwan University College of Medicine and conducted according to the relevant guidelines (IACUC Approval No. 20200105). Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 250 to 300 g were purchased from BioLASCO (Taipei City, Taiwan) and used for in vivo study.

The implant surgery was performed on normal rats.27 The rats were anesthetized with Forane (i.e., isoflurane; Aesica Queenborough, Queenborough, UK) and placed in a stereotaxic frame. A 23G needle was inserted through a burr hole to the striatum [1 mm anterior and 3.2 mm lateral to bregma, 5.0 mm back to 4.5 mm ventral to dura mater]. Six microliters of hydrogel loaded in a 25 μL syringe was injected at 1.0 μL/min by a microinfusion pump (#70-4507; Harvard Apparatus, MA, USA).

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

The in vivo images were repeatedly acquired 1 day and 7 days after surgery by using a 7 T MRI system (Biospec 70/30; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) was performed using a fast spin-echo sequence with the following parameters: field of view = 2.56 cm2, slice thickness = 1 mm, number of slices = 8, repetition time/echo time = 4000/80 ms, echo train length = 8, number of excitations = 4, and matrix size = 256 × 128 (zero filled to 256 × 256, resulting in an in-plane resolution of 100 × 100 μm.). Image analyses were performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, MD, USA).

Behavioral Tests

Three behavioral tests were introduced into this study, all frequently employed to assess neurological abnormalities in rats following damage to the striatum.28 The rotarod test was used to assess motor coordination and balance alterations in animals. During each trial, the duration for which the rats effectively walked on an accelerating rotating cylinder was measured across three iterations. The mean duration spent on the rotating cylinder prior to hydrogel implantation served as the baseline reference, denoted as 100% for each rat. Subsequent measurements were delineated as a percentage of the initial baseline value. The corner turn test was conducted to identify the sensorimotor and postural asymmetries. The rats were allowed to advance into a 30° corner and turn either left or right to exit. The test was repeated for 10 trials, and the percentage of the turning direction was calculated. The forelimb placement test was conducted to examine the reflexive motor ability of the contralateral forelimb. The rats were gently held by their trunk, maintaining alignment parallel to a tabletop’s upper surface. During the trials, the rats were allowed to graze the table’s surface with their vibrissae on one side. A total of 10 trials were performed to examine reflexive forelimb placements. The percentages of successful placing responses were determined.

Histology

After the second MRI, the rats were sacrificed by transcardial perfusion with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA) in phosphate-buffered saline. The brains were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 5 μm. Sections were collected to match the MR images and stained using immunostaining to study the interactions between the implanted hydrogel and brain tissue. Briefly, the sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and then boiled in citrate buffer (pH 6; Agilent, CA, USA) for 30 min at 98 °C for antigen retrieval. The boiled sections were treated with 0.3% H2O2 (Sigma-Aldrich) to inhibit endogenous peroxidase for 30 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating sections in 10% normal goat serum in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h. The obtained sections were incubated with primary antibodies in PBS overnight at room temperature and then with biotinylated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA) for 1 h. The primary antibodies included rabbit anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; ab7260; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for astrocytes, rabbit anti-NeuN (ab177487; Abcam) for neurons, rabbit antineurofilament (1:1000; ab8135; Abcam) for axons, rabbit anti-Iba1 (1:1000; 019-19741; FUJIFILM Wako, Japan) for microglia, and mouse anti-CD68 (1:1000; ab31630; Abcam) for monocytes and macrophages. For signal amplification, the sections were exposed to an avidin–biotin–peroxidase complex (Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 1 h. For visualization, the sections were treated with 0.025% 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Sigma-Aldrich) plus 0.024% H2O2 in PBS and counterstained with hematoxylin (Sigma-Aldrich). The stained sections were examined by using microscopy (BX51; OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan).

Characterization of Gelatin-Based Hydrogels

Swelling Ratio

After gentle mixing, gelatin-based hydrogels were formed in acrylate molds (with a diameter of 16 mm and a height of 0.5 mm). The sample was dried for 24 h and then weighed to obtain the weight of the dry hydrogel composites (Wd). The air-dried hydrogels were immersed in 1 mL of PBS (pH 7.0) for different time intervals before 48 h (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h). After removing PBS, the hydrogels were weighed (Ws). The swelling ratio of the gelatin-based hydrogels was determined by eq 1.1

Compression Testing

For compression testing, the gelatin-based hydrogels were cross-linked in the acrylate molds (with a diameter of 16 mm and height of 0.5 mm) and were swollen in PBS for 24 h. The compression modulus of hydrogels was measured by a dynamic mechanical analyzer (MTS Criterion Model 42) at room temperature. The hydrogels were placed between a parallel-plate compression clamp, and a maximum force of 5 N was applied at a crosshead speed of 1 mm min–1. The compression modulus was calculated as the slope of stress versus strain given by eq 2 within the strain in the range that appeared to be elastically linear.2

Electrical Properties

After the gelatin-based hydrogels were completely cross-linked, 250 μL of the hydrogels was dispersed in 1 mL of deionized water and sonicated for 10 min. The zeta potential of the hydrogels was determined by using a Zetasizer instrument (LITESIZER, Anton Paar).

Sakaguchi Test

The Sakaguchi test is a biochemical test for the detection of the presence of a guanidinium group by colorimetric reaction. After complete cross-linking, 0.1 g of wet-stated hydrogel was placed into the 1.5 mL tube, and 40% sodium hydroxide and 0.1% α-naphthol (dissolved in alcohol) were added to the hydrogel. After ice bathing for 2 min, sodium hypochlorite as the oxidizing agent and 40% urea to avoid the colorimetric degradation were added to the hydrogel. The measurement of colorimetric intensity was determined by an ELISA reader with the absorbance at 520 nm.

Statistical Analysis

Each experiment was performed in at least triplicate, and the results were obtained from at least three independent experiments with a different primary culture. Differences between multiple groups were calculated using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Bonferroni test (p-value < 0.05 was considered a significant difference; * represented a p-value < 0.1, ** represented a p-value < 0.05).

Results

Effect of Guanidinylation of Gelatin on the Neuron Viability

Compared to PDL, PAG has been demonstrated to exhibit a more significant effect on neuronal cell adhesion and viability in our previous publication.24 Therefore, this study aimed to alter the composition of lysine and arginine of gelatin construction to guide neural cell response for further possible tissue engineering applications. At first, gelatin was modified by the guanidinylation of lysine with PC in ratios of 1:2 and 1:10 of lysine to PC. These hydrogels were used for cultivation of the Ara-C-treated near-pure neuron culture for 12 days. Figure 1 confirms that the amount of guanidine in gelatin measured by the Sakaguchi test was increased by guanidinylation and exhibited a significant difference between unmodified gelatin and 1:10 modified gelatin (Figure 1D) (p < 0.05). A representative picture of neuron aggregates and separate neurons displayed outgrowth and branching of neurites on the coated gelatin modified with 1:10 of lysine to PC after 12 days of culture. In contrast, very limited neurite growth was seen on the unmodified gelatin or modification with a low ratio of 1:2 of lysine to PC (Figure 1B,C). Figure 1E also shows that neurons on the gelatin modified with 1:10 of lysine to PC had significantly higher neuron viability than unmodified gelatin (p < 0.05), consistent with morphological observation.

(B–D) The phase contrast images of neuron morphology after culturing cerebellar neurons on coated gelatin without or with modification for 12 days of culture. (E) Neuron viability was measured by Alamar blue assay after 12 days of culture. Gelatin Hydrogel Modification with Various Polycations

Based on the effect of guanidinylation of gelatin on the neuron viability, we proposed an easy method to modify 3D gelatin hydrogel by cross-linking with PAG instead of converting lysine to guanidine moieties for neural engineering applications. For comparison, PEI and PDL with different positively charged amino groups were also cross-linked with gelatin by EDC/NHS at 37 °C. Figure 2A shows that gelatin and various polycations could be used to form injectable hydrogels within 15 min. Under scanning electron microscope (SEM), these hydrogels exhibited a porous structure as shown in Figure 2B. Overall, the presence of polycation did not remarkably affect the porous structure, and the pore size histogram distribution ranged from 20 to 100 μm. In detail, gelatin hydrogels cross-linked with PEI (G-PEI) and PDL (G-PDL) displayed a smaller average pore size of about 40–45 μm. The average pore diameter of gelatin hydrogels without polycation (G) and cross-linked with PAG (G-PAG) was about 65–70 μm. As the size of cerebellar neuron soma is approximately 5 μm,29 the pore size range allowed the migration and distribution of neural cells.

By passage without Ara-C, glial cells of the near-pure glial culture were seeded on gelatin-based hydrogels for 7 days. Compared to G, Figure 3A shows that the addition of polycation could significantly inhibit glial cell viability by the Alamar blue assay (p < 0.05 or 0.01). The Live/Dead assay confirmed the viability assay with more live and less dead cells in G and decreasing green and increasing red fluorescent signals in gelatin-polycation hydrogels (Figure 3B).

Rat cerebellar neurons from a near-pure neuron culture were prepared in the presence of Ara-c and incubated on the gelatin and gelatin-modified hydrogels for 12 days (Figures 3C,D). Neurons exhibited significantly higher viability on G-PAG than on control G (p < 0.01). No significant difference in neuron viability was found between G and G-PEI or G-PDL. Simultaneously, both G and G-PAG displayed more live and less dead cells than G-PEI and G-PDL.

To observe the neuron proteins that are responsible for the formation of processes, the neurons of a near-pure neuron culture within the hydrogel were immunostained against growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43; elongating axon) and synapsin I (synaptic vesicle) after 12 days. As shown in Figure 4, GAP43 and synapsin I were brightly expressed around the neuron aggregate in G. Contrarily, the expression of synapsin I in G-PAG concentrated in high fluorescence puncta along the GAP43-positive neurites, showing a flat-like distribution. For G-PEI and G-PDL, no obvious GAP43-positive neurites and synapsin I expression were observed in the hydrogel.

These results indicated that the addition of PAG to the gelatin hydrogel could promote neuron viability and effectively inhibit the survival and adhesion of glial cells.

G-PAG Hydrogel In Vivo

Because G-PAG presented better neuron viability than G-PEI and G-PDL, its application was further evaluated using the rat brain model. The G-PAG hydrogel was injected into the striatum and was monitored using T2WI that is sensitive to water content (Figure 6A). The implanted G-PAG hydrogel with intact and elliptical hyperintensity could be clearly found in the striatum 1 and 7 days after surgery, and there were minor additional hyperintensive or hypointensive signals around the hydrogel, suggesting that the G-PAG hydrogel did not induce obvious foreign body reaction in the brain. Furthermore, three behavioral tests were performed to study whether the hydrogel injection into the striatum elicits neurological deficits (Figure 6B). Among them, only the corner turn test exhibited a significant turning bias on the first day following hydrogel implantation, which, however, recovered within a week. The rotarod test and forelimb placement test displayed minor behavioral alterations in rats on the first day after surgery, albeit without achieving statistical significance. These findings indicate that hydrogel implantation does not induce long-term behavioral abnormalities in rats.

Figure 6B shows the degradation ratio of the G-PAG hydrogel in vitro to the biodegradable property of the G-PAG hydrogel. The results show that within 14 days, the G-PAG hydrogel demonstrated a linear degradable pattern. In addition, the MR image also illustrated the biodegradable property. The hyperintensive volume (i.e., hydrogel size) on days 1 and 7 was 4.26 ± 0.52 and 1.47 ± 0.24 mm3, respectively, suggesting that G-PAG is biodegradable. The G-PAG hydrogel volume decreased by about 65% from 1 to 7 days of implantation.

The in vivo interactions between the hydrogel and the brain tissue were studied using immunostaining for the brain sections obtained 1 week after implantation. Figure 7A shows the ipsilateral tissue away from the implanted hydrogel, where neurons (NeuN-stained section) with a well-defined cell body, axons (neurofilament) with long and slender shape and their bundled fiber tracts, astrocytes (GFAP) with multiple radiating processes, and resting microglia (Iba-1) with ramified form were observed. No macrophages (CD68) were detected. In the tissue around the hydrogel (Figure 7B), a glial scar, approximately 150–200 μm thick, was formed. Whereas, in most neurological damages, scar is primarily composed of astrocytes,30 the scar surrounding our implant appeared to be primarily composed of ameboid microglia followed by reactive astrocytes and infiltrated macrophages. Figure 7C shows that no neurons or astrocytes were found within the hydrogel. Interestingly, there were axon-like fibers within the hydrogel and even fibers extended from the axonal bundles in the striatal tissue near the hydrogel. Inflammatory cells were found in the gel, suggesting that G-PAG allowed the migration of host cells. These data suggested that G-PAG hydrogel impeding reactive astrocytes may be applied to reconstruct the brain cavity that remained after a serious injury or surgical resection.

Swelling, Mechanical, and Electrical Properties of Gelatin-Based Hydrogels

The swelling ratio of the gelatin-based hydrogel depends on the cross-linking density and the cationic polymer ionization degree. Figure 8A shows the swelling ratio of prepared hydrogels by measuring the increasing weight of the hydrogel after immersing in PBS for 48 h. The swelling ratio of all gelatin-polycation hydrogels smoothly increased and reached equilibrium after 12–24 h. Compared to the control gelatin hydrogel, the addition of cationic polymer into the gelatin hydrogel slightly increased the water-absorbing capabilities, but there was no significant difference between them.

(B) The Young’s modulus of hydrogels was measured by a compression test (n = 21). (C) The amount of guanidine group (n = 5) of the gelatin-based hydrogels was measured by the Sakaguchi test. * represents a significant difference compared with G (p < 0.01).

The Young’s modulus of the gelatin-based hydrogel was measured by uniaxial and unconfined compression tests (Figure 8B). The addition of polycation resulted in a slightly softer hydrogel without a significant difference. Discussion

At the injury site, in addition to the loss of neuron cells, glial cells are activated and act as progenitor cells to proliferate and form the glial scars in response to damage. Although the formation of glial scars helps to restrict the injury cavity, it impedes axonal outgrowth and neuron regeneration. An appropriate approach that can inhibit the overproliferation of astrocytes and the formation of glial scars undoubtedly provides a potential possibility for nerve tissue engineering. Several treatments have been studied to reach this achievement, such as molecular inhibitors and drugs. In this study, we established a novel injectable hydrogel for nerve tissue engineering not only for supporting neuron survival and maturation but also for inhibiting the adhesion and proliferation of astrocytes without applying additional implements, increasing the efficiency and accessibility for clinical application.

Gelatin, which is a biocompatible biomaterial, is a good candidate for tissue engineering applications. It possesses abundant amino acids, which allow the biological role of amino acids in cell behavior regulation. Generally, positively charged biomaterials such as PDL improve neural cell adhesion. Reasonably, lysine and arginine, two basic amino acids in gelatin, are supposed to support neural cell adhesion and neurite outgrowth. This study first found the positive effect of converting ammonium of lysine into guanidine moieties on improving neuron viability (Figure 1). Therefore, a new 3D gelatin hydrogel for culturing neurons was designed by cross-linking gelatin and PAG to increase the amount of guanidine in the hydrogel structure. For comparison, PDL and PEI were also included in the cross-linked gelatin hydrogel to establish a 3D culture environment to investigate whether all positively charged materials possess the ability for neural cell behavior improvement.

It is known that the scaffold microarchitecture is highly related to the neural cell response for tissue engineering applications. Instead of establishing the underlying culture substrate, the pore size and the inner topographies could determine neural cell distribution and guide neuronal processes alignment.33 For cross-linking with various polycations, the gelatin-based hydrogels used in this study retained their porosity with a slight variation in average pore size (Figure 2). Such porous structure increases the contact area between cells and hydrogel, as well as the outgrowth of neural processes.34−36

The effect of gelatin-based hydrogels on neuron survival and maturation was evaluated by both in vitro cell culture (Figures 3–5) and in vivo animal study (Figures 6 and 7). The consistent in vitro results analyzed by Alamar blue and Live/Dead assay show that only the G-PAG hydrogel possessed the ability to enhance neuronal survival. Functional protein expression is another critical indicator for long-term regenerative capability validation. Several proteins have been reported to correlate with neuron function and neurite outgrowth. GAP43 is preferentially highly expressed in the growth cone and elongating axon of developing neurons.29 Synapsin I is a phosphoprotein present in the synaptic vesicles, representing synapse formation and synaptic transmission.37,38 The upregulated expression of GAP43 and synapsin I showed a flat-like distribution with neurites extending from neuron aggregate in the G-PAG hydrogel. In addition, GFAP, a major protein component of differentiated astrocytes for maintaining astrocyte’s mechanical strength,39 showed a decreasing expression in the G-PAG hydrogel. Therefore, the G-PAG hydrogel could not only enhance neuron behavior but also effectively inhibit the survival and adhesion of astrocytes. Furthermore, the in vivo study verified the efficiency of the G-PAG hydrogel on axon extension and astrocyte impediment. These results supported the potential of the G-PAG hydrogel for neural engineering application and indicated that not all polycations could be used to improve neural cell behavior in the gelatin hydrogel.

Some physical properties, including mechanical strength, swelling ratio, and electrical properties, of hydrogels have been thought to be essential to cell behavior regulation. Previous studies indicated that appropriate mechanical strength, which is similar to the native mechanical strength of the rat brain, around 0.25 kPa, allows selective neuron survival and axonal outgrowth38,40,41 and inhibits astrocyte proliferation.42−44 The positively charged hydrogel could improve neuronal cell attachment and differentiation.22,45 Thus, several properties of gelatin-based hydrogels were examined in this study (Figure 8). The addition of polycations led to increasing water-absorbing capabilities, softening hydrogel structure, and increasing surface potential when compared to the control gelatin. The decreasing mechanical strength of the gelatin-polycation hydrogel possibly resulted from a higher level of water content in the equilibrium hydrogels, but no significant difference was found among the various polycations in the gelatin-based hydrogels. In terms of electrical property, the zeta potential showed that the addition of the cationic polymers could increase the potential of the hydrogel but still did not show a significant difference between groups. Therefore, the variation in the survival of neural cells and neurite outgrowth might not be attributed to the physical properties of the hydrogels.

In addition to the physical properties, the chemical structure plays an important role in cell adhesion and processing. The different functional groups of amino acids might be a key factor in understanding the underlying regulated mechanisms. A significantly increasing amount of guanidine in G-PAG supported the hypothesis that the guanidine group plays an essential role in neuron behavior regulation, consistent with the studies that pointed out the function of the guanidine group in neuron development and differentiation.46,47 Therefore, the dissimilarity in biological results of gelatin-based hydrogels indicated that the neural cells’ behavior was dependent on the polycation used. The hydrophobic backbone structure of PAG forms a globule conformation, whereas the hydrophilic backbone structure of PDL and PEI retains the extended conformation in the high-water content environment. Because of this characteristic, G-PEI and G-PDL are more likely to induce protein–polymer interaction and activate an excess level of biological processes that cause neurotoxicity. The adhesion of TGF-β, which is a vital factor for neural development, has been shown to increase when the polymer undergoes a coil-to-globule transformation.24 However, an excess level of TGF-β might also activate inflammation. Moreover, reactive oxygen species (ROS)48 and mitochondrial toxicity49 have been reported to be induced by PEI, leading to cell death. Based on the structure difference between PAG and PEI or PDL, it is reasonable to consider the possibility that the globule conformation of PAG exposes a lower level of interactive proteins to neural cells, whereas G-PEI and G-PDL extend their structure and improve the adhesion of proteins to the hydrogels, which may have a rising risk of inducing more cell–protein interactions and result in cell death. The numerous branches and elongated neurites between neurons on 2D guanidine-modified gelatin or 3D PAG-modified gelatin hydrogel validated our assumption that the interaction between neural cells and gelatin is sensitively dependent on the guanidine moieties.

Conclusions

In conclusion, gelatin-based hydrogels with increasing 2% lysine or arginine composition by cross-linking gelatin with PDL or PAG were developed and investigated regarding the response of neural cells to the gelatin-polycation hydrogel. The results of the improved neuron viability, distribution, neurite outgrowth, synapse formation, and synaptic transmission on G-PAG compared to G-PDL and G-PEI, combined with the in vivo neural cell interactions after G-PAG hydrogel implantation, demonstrated that gelatin cross-linked with PAG could act as an ideal scaffold for neuron culture. Because there was no considerable difference among G-PAG, G-PDL, and G-PEI in swelling, mechanical, and electric properties that are generally thought to affect cell adhesion and proliferation, the role of various primary, secondary, and tertiary amino groups of PDL and PEI in regulating neuron cell behaviors could be ruled out by the absence of similar functional groups in PAG. The structural similarity, guanidine group, present in arginine and PAG repeating units might explain the peculiar properties of PAG as regard the ability to regulate neuron cell response in the cross-linked hydrogel scaffolds. Therefore, the specific basic gelatin–neuron relationship depends on a specific amino acid in a very sensitive manner, and the guanidine functional groups are a very important neurotrophic effect in the G-PAG hydrogel for neuron growth and migration. The information found in this study provides a concept for designing gelatin-based hydrogels for neural tissue engineering applications.
