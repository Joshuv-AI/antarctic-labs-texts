Estimation of the effective dose in torso 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET/CT: an analysis of real-world clinical data from 51,784 patient scans
Authors: Jeongryul Ryu, Jungsu S. Oh, Yong-il Kim, Sangwon Han, Jae Seung Kim, Kyung-Hyun Do, Jin-Sook Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLarge-scale estimation of effective dose (ED) for positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) remains limited. This study aimed to determine the ED of torso 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET/CT using extensive real-world clinical data from a high-volume centre. Differences in ED were also evaluated according to PET/CT scanner type, patient sex, and body size.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on PET/CT examinations from individuals aged ≥ 16 years and acquired between March 2019 and June 2022. Five PET/CT scanners were used during the study period. CT ED was calculated by multiplying the dose length product (DLP) by an ED/DLP conversion coefficient. PET ED was estimated using injected 18F-FDG activity and the conversion factor reported in International Commission on Radiological Protection Publication 106. Total PET/CT ED was computed as the sum of CT and PET ED values.ResultsA total of 51,784 examinations were analysed (mean age, 61.9 ± 12.6 years; 29,990 men). Mean CT ED was 3.7 ± 1.5 mSv in men and 3.7 ± 1.7 mSv in women. CT ED varied according to scanner type and protocol, with newer scanners and advanced image processing technologies associated with lower radiation exposure. A strong positive correlation was observed between volume CT dose index and body mass index across all scanners. Mean PET ED of PET was 6.6 ± 1.1 mSv in men and 5.6 ± 1.0 mSv in women. Mean total PET/CT ED was 10.2 ± 2.4 mSv in men and 9.3 ± 2.5 mSv in women.ConclusionsThe mean ED of torso 18F-FDG PET/CT derived from extensive real-world clinical data was comparable to that of conventional diagnostic CT. Both patient-related factors, including sex and body size, and scanner-specific characteristics, such as dose modulation algorithms, significantly influenced ED.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-026-00857-5.

Background

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) is a pivotal imaging modality for the diagnosis and monitoring of a wide range of diseases, particularly in oncology. The global increase in PET/CT utilisation reflects its high diagnostic accuracy and value in treatment planning, a trend expected to persist as clinical indications continue to expand. However, the growing reliance on diagnostic medical imaging raises concerns regarding the potential health effects of radiation exposure. During PET/CT examinations, patients are exposed to both external radiation from the CT component and internal radiation from the PET radiopharmaceutical. The effective dose (ED) is widely used to quantify radiation exposure, enabling comparison of relative doses across different medical procedures, institutions, countries, and imaging technologies used for similar indications [1].

Several studies have evaluated radiation exposure associated with PET/CT examinations. One study reported a mean ED of 8.2 mSv from injected 18F-FDG, whereas the mean ED from CT scans covering the skull base to mid-thigh for attenuation correction or diagnostic purposes was 6.5 mSv [2]. Another study reported a mean ED of 16.5 mSv for torso PET/CT, comprising 6.1 mSv from injected 18F-FDG and 10.3 mSv from low-dose CT [3]. A nationwide Japanese survey conducted in 2011 across 51 facilities reported a mean estimated total ED of 13.5 mSv per PET/CT examination [4]. In South Korea, Chong et al. [5] reported a mean total ED of 10.93 ± 3.14 mSv among 99 adults weighing 70 kg across 31 hospitals. These survey-based studies predominantly relied on questionnaire-derived data from multiple institutions and included limited participant-level information, with baseline characteristics such as patient sex, body size, scanner type, and image acquisition protocols often not accounted for.

Although some studies have reported the ED of PET using dynamic PET protocols [6] or the ED of CT using phantom-based measurements [7], these approaches are impractical for routine clinical use or large-scale analyses. To date, the ED of PET/CT has not been evaluated using extensive clinical data. Moreover, although recent advances in imaging technology may have reduced radiation exposure, the magnitude of this reduction has not been quantitatively assessed. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the ED of torso 18F-FDG PET/CT using extensive clinical data from a high-volume centre, stratified by PET/CT scanner type, as well as patient sex and body size.

Methods

Study participants

In this retrospective study, electronic imaging procedure records were reviewed for individuals who underwent torso 18F-FDG PET/CT between March 2019 and June 2022. Among 51,795 torso 18F-FDG PET/CT examinations performed in adult patients (aged ≥ 16 years), 51,784 scans were included in the analysis; 11 scans were excluded because of incomplete records (Fig. 1). For each examination, data were extracted on patient characteristics, including sex, age, height, and body weight; PET/CT scanner type; injected 18F-FDG activity; PET and CT acquisition parameters; volume CT dose index (CTDIvol); and dose length product (DLP). During the study period, 7,406 patients underwent two or more PET/CT scans, which were performed on the same or different scanners, and all examinations were included in the analysis. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center (IRB No. 2022–1299), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.Fig. 1Participant recruitment flow diagram. PET/CT imaging was performed according to standardised clinical protocols [8, 9]. Patients were required to fast for at least 6 h before 18F-FDG administration and were instructed to maintain adequate hydration (> 1 L). Blood glucose levels were measured before injection to confirm values < 150 mg/dL. Image acquisition began 60 min after 18F-FDG injection, with a standardised administered activity of 5.18 MBq/kg applied uniformly across all scanner types. Patients were positioned with the arms raised overhead when possible; otherwise, the arms were positioned alongside the body. The scan range typically extended from the skull base to the upper thigh, using a scout scan and low-dose CT for attenuation correction and anatomical localisation [10].

CT and PET acquisition and reconstruction parameters were adjusted according to scanner type to achieve comparable image quality, as detailed in Tables 2 and 3. Automatic exposure control (AEC) of the tube current was applied to all CT acquisitions. For PET acquisition, flow motion technology was used with the Biograph Vision 600, whereas the remaining scanners employed a conventional stop-and-go acquisition mode. Comprehensive quality control procedures were implemented to ensure imaging stability and dose accuracy. For PET systems, daily quality assurance included Quality Guard checks or normalisation, with quarterly assessments performed using the American College of Radiology PET phantom. Annual verification of standardised uptake value calibration was conducted using an 18F water-filled uniform phantom. Dose calibrators underwent daily constancy checks, quarterly linearity testing, and annual accuracy assessments. For CT systems, daily consistency checks, quarterly performance evaluations, and annual CTDI measurements were conducted according to standardised protocols. In addition, mandatory inspections were performed every three years in accordance with the Diagnostic Radiation Generating Device Safety Act in Korea, including CT phantom-based evaluations to confirm dose accuracy and compliance with national safety standards.

Estimation of the ED of 18F-FDG PET/CT

The total ED of 18F-FDG PET/CT was calculated as the sum of the estimated EDs from the CT and PET components. For the CT component, the ED was estimated using DLP values recorded in the scanner-generated dose reports for each CT acquisition. These DLP values were multiplied by ED/DLP conversion coefficients derived using CT-Expo software (version 2.8; SASCRAD) [11], based on the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) Publication 103. Standard adult male and female phantoms were used, representing adult males (> 18 years, 70 kg, 170 cm) and females (> 18 years, 60 kg, 160 cm), respectively.

In this analysis, a constant tube current (mA) was used instead of AEC-based tube current values. The constant mA was selected to yield a DLP equivalent to the average value for each patient demographic, accounting for factors such as pitch, collimation, tube voltage, and rotation time [12]. The corresponding constant mA values and ED/DLP conversion coefficients calculated for each scanner type and patient sex are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The ED from the PET component was estimated from the administered 18F-FDG activity using patient body weight and a conversion factor of 0.019 mSv/MBq, as recommended by ICRP Publication 106 [13]. Patients were categorised according to body mass index (BMI) [14] as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 ≤ BMI < 25.0 kg/m2), overweight (25.0 ≤ BMI < 30.0 kg/m2), or obese (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

The ED of PET/CT was analysed according to scanner type and patient sex. To evaluate the relationship between CTDIvol and BMI for each scanner, linear or non-linear regression models were selected based on goodness-of-fit metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2) and the Akaike Information Criterion. To reflect real-world clinical data distributions, the complete dataset was analysed, and patients who underwent two or more PET/CT examinations were handled using mixed-effects regression models with subject-specific random effects [15].

Differences between two groups with normally distributed data were assessed using Student's t-test, whereas one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey–Kramer post hoc testing, was used for multiple-group comparisons, particularly when sample sizes were unequal. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1; R Foundation). Density-weighted scatter plots were generated using Python (version 3.13.7; Python Software Foundation).

Results

Participant characteristics

The analysis included 51,784 PET/CT examinations, comprising 29,990 scans from men and 21,794 from women. The mean age of the study population was 61.9 ± 12.6 years, with a mean height of 163.1 ± 8.6 cm and a mean body weight of 62.7 ± 11.7 kg. The mean BMI was 23.5 ± 3.5 kg/m2. The distribution of scanner types was as follows: Biograph TruePoint 40 (N = 3,749), Discovery 690 Elite (N = 12,624), Discovery 710 (N = 15,340), Discovery 690 (N = 13,578), and Biograph Vision 600 (N = 6,493). The replacement of the Biograph TruePoint 40 with the Biograph Vision 600 during the study period resulted in fewer examinations performed using these systems.

Table 4 summarises patient characteristics stratified by scanner type and includes the number of patients who underwent two or more examinations on the same scanner. Body weight, height, and BMI were consistent across scanner groups in men, whereas slight variations were observed among women. The included scanner types differed substantially in underlying technology. For CT acquisition, GE scanners employed the Auto mA AEC algorithm, whereas Siemens scanners used Care Dose 4D. For PET acquisition, the Biograph Vision 600 was equipped with silicon photomultiplier detectors, whereas the remaining scanners used photomultiplier tube detectors. Discussion

Our comprehensive analysis of 51,784 torso 18F-FDG PET/CT examinations yielded a mean estimated ED of 9.8 mSv, which closely aligns with the generally recognised average ED for conventional diagnostic CT scans (8–10 mSv) [16]. This finding is consistent with previously reported PET/CT survey results from South Korea (10.93 ± 3.14 mSv) [5] and the United States (approximately 10 mSv) [16]. However, the estimated ED in our study was lower than that reported in earlier investigations [2, 3]. Li et al. [2] reported a higher mean ED of 8.2 mSv from injected 18F-FDG alone, exceeding the corresponding values observed in our cohort. This difference is likely attributable to higher administered 18F-FDG activities and differences in patient weight distributions in their study. In addition, Martí-Climent et al. [3] reported a mean CT ED of 10.3 mSv, which also exceeds our estimates. This discrepancy may reflect the use of AEC on Siemens scanners with higher reference mA settings than those applied in our protocols. The difference from the Japanese nationwide survey results [4] may be explained by their reliance on questionnaire-based data collected from multiple facilities using heterogeneous equipment and imaging protocols. In contrast, the present study used extensive real-world clinical data from a single high-volume centre, with a standardised 18F-FDG injection protocol and relatively homogenous CT acquisition parameters in accordance with established imaging guidelines. Notably, the Japanese survey reported a lower mean PET ED (4.4 mSv) but a higher total PET/CT ED, suggesting the use of higher-dose CT protocols in that cohort.

This high-volume, single-centre study provides a unique perspective through the analysis of extensive clinical data, enabling a detailed assessment of the influence of sex, body size, and scanner type on ED. In contrast to experimental phantom-based studies [7], which may not accurately reflect human anatomical variability or internal organ distribution, this investigation incorporates realistic patient profiles. Although some studies have sought to address these limitations by enrolling larger cohorts or conducting surveys across multiple institutions, such multicentre approaches often face challenges in evaluating the effects of patient characteristics and imaging system types because of substantial data heterogeneity and variations in imaging protocols.

Radiation exposure and its biological effects following CT examinations differ between men and women [17, 18]. When ICRP Publication 103 was applied in CT-Expo, CT conversion coefficients were higher for women than for men, likely reflecting differences in the distribution of radiosensitive organs. Across most scanner types, women exhibited higher CT EDs than men, both in the overall cohort and across BMI categories. The Discovery 690 was an exception, showing higher EDs in men within the normal-weight and overweight subgroups. These differences may be attributable to scanner-specific AEC behaviour or variability in patient positioning, such as differences in arm-down positioning or scan length. Despite this exception, substantial overlap existed between the ED distributions for men and women, and the upper bounds of observed EDs in women exceeded those in men in the overall cohort and in most BMI strata. Collectively, these findings suggest that, on average, women may receive higher CT-related EDs than men during PET/CT examinations.

CT ED varied significantly across subgroups, with higher values observed in patients with larger body size. Individuals classified as overweight or obese accounted for 31% (16,031/51,784) of the study population. Given the increasing global prevalence of obesity [19] and the growing emphasis on radiation dose optimisation in this population [20], further investigation is needed to refine dose optimisation strategies for patients with higher body mass. Across all BMI categories, the Biograph Vision 600 demonstrated the lowest CT ED, likely owing to two primary factors. First, manufacturer-specific differences in AEC algorithms contribute to dose variability. The Siemens Care Dose 4D algorithm dynamically modulates tube current based on patient size using both angular and longitudinal modulation relative to an operator-defined reference mA, whereas the GE Auto mA algorithm applies Z-axis modulation within operator-defined minimum and maximum mA limits [21]. Second, the Biograph Vision 600 operated at reference mA levels approximately 50% lower than those used for the Biograph TruePoint 40 and toward the lower end of the range applied across GE scanners.

Correlation analysis demonstrated a significant, scanner-specific relationship between CTDIvol and body size. Linear regression provided the best fit for Siemens scanners, whereas logistic sigmoid regression best described GE systems. These distinct patterns likely reflect differences in the design and implementation of AEC algorithms across scanner platforms. For GE scanners, the operator-defined reference mA range (30–210 mA) may have limited optimal modulation at the extremes of body size, leading to excessive tube current in severely underweight individuals or insufficient exposure in patients with severe obesity, thereby producing the observed sigmoid distribution. In contrast, Siemens scanners would have adjusted tube current proportionally to patient size, favouring a linear relationship between CTDIvol and body size.

Outliers were more frequently observed in Siemens scanners than in GE systems (Fig. 2), a pattern that was particularly pronounced among patients with obesity and most evident with the Biograph Vision 600. Although patient size-based modulation may yield optimal mA within an average BMI range, variations in anatomy (e.g., increased chest circumference) or positioning (e.g., arms-down posture) can lead to overestimation of axial patient diameter, particularly in individuals with obesity, thereby driving excessive tube current. Furthermore, Care Dose 4D relies on localiser scans to estimate patient attenuation, assuming accurate isocentre positioning. Vertical miscentring or incomplete Z-axis coverage during the scout scan can bias attenuation estimates and result in up‑modulation of tube current [22].

Supplementary Table 2 shows that Siemens scanners exhibited lower between-group intraclass correlation coefficients than GE scanners; for the Biograph Vision 600, residual variance contributed substantially in men, whereas between-subject random effects were more prominent in women. These findings suggest greater variability in Care Dose 4D responses to patient-specific factors such as patient size and positioning. In addition, the Biograph Vision 600 employed a narrower detector collimation than the other scanners (Table 2), which reduces geometric dose efficiency through increased overbeaming. In patients with high attenuation, the combined effect of higher required tube current and reduced collimation efficiency may inflate the upper tail of the CTDIvol distribution [23].

The number of patients with very high BMI (≥ 40 kg/m2) was limited across all scanner groups, particularly for the Biograph Vision 600; therefore, regression trends in this extreme BMI subgroup should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the occurrence of identical CTDIvol values in a subset of examinations (Fig. 2) was traced to technical configuration issues during the initial implementation phase of a scanner. Although this represents a limitation, it highlights the importance of precise system configuration and rigorous quality assurance in routine clinical practice to ensure effective radiation dose management.

With respect to the PET component, the high sensitivity of the silicon photomultiplier detectors in the Biograph Vision 600 digital PET/CT enables reductions in administered 18F-FDG activity while maintaining image quality compared with conventional PET systems [24]. In the present study, however, a uniform weight-based 18F-FDG injection protocol was applied across all PET/CT scanners. Although the enhanced sensitivity of digital PET/CT permitted shorter acquisition times, this capability was not leveraged to reduce the administered activity. Future investigations should evaluate tailored dosing strategies that allow individualised optimisation of 18F-FDG administration while preserving image quality and diagnostic performance. Moreover, as with CT, further research is needed to optimise 18F-FDG dosing and PET image reconstruction based on BMI rather than body weight alone [25], particularly in patients with overweight or obesity.

Although this study benefits from a large volume of real-world clinical data, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the single-centre design may limit the generalisability of the findings. Second, the analysis relied on electronic imaging procedure records rather than a systematic visual review of all 51,784 examinations. As a result, a detailed evaluation of factors such as patient positioning or motion artefacts for every individual outlier was not feasible. Third, iterative reconstruction techniques were not implemented for CT imaging at the study centre during the study period; future adoption of these techniques could allow additional dose reduction. Fourth, CT dose estimation using CT-Expo software [11] was based on constant mA values rather than AEC-modulated currents, which may not fully capture patient-specific exposure and could introduce estimation inaccuracies. In addition, CTDIvol, the metric used for dose estimation, is derived from standardised phantom measurements and does not account for individual anatomical factors such as body size or arm positioning [26]. Consequently, size-specific dose estimates [27], which may provide more individualised dose assessments, were not incorporated. Dose calculations were also not cross-validated using alternative commercial software packages, which may produce differing results because of algorithmic variability [28]. Fifth, the analysis focused on exposure levels rather than biological risk, as ED is not directly equivalent to organ-specific absorbed doses [29]. Therefore, extrapolations such as cancer risk estimates based on these values should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, some patients underwent repeated PET/CT examinations, potentially violating the independence assumption required for certain regression analyses. However, these analyses were exploratory and not central to the primary outcomes, and the overall impact is considered minor. Finally, the study population consisted exclusively of adults; future studies should include paediatric populations, who are more radiosensitive and require particular consideration regarding radiation exposure.

Conclusion

The mean ED of torso 18F-FDG PET/CT derived from extensive real-world clinical data was comparable to that of conventional diagnostic CT. Both patient-specific factors, including sex and body size, and scanner-specific characteristics, such as dose modulation algorithms, had a significant influence on ED. These findings highlight the importance of optimising imaging protocols and scanner technologies to maintain diagnostic accuracy while minimising radiation exposure.
