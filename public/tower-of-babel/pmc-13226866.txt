Work-Related Asthma: An Occupational Medicine Perspective
Authors: Yangwoo Kim, Inah Kim, Jaechul Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Work-related asthma (WRA), encompassing occupational asthma and work-exacerbated asthma, is among the most prevalent yet preventable occupational respiratory diseases, with population-based studies estimating that 10% to 25% of adult asthma cases are attributable to workplace exposure. This review examines epidemiology, causative agents, pathogenesis, clinical evaluation, management, and WRA prevention from an occupational medicine perspective, with emphasis on Korean surveillance data. The Korea Work-related Asthma Surveillance program (2004–2009) identified 236 cases with a crude incidence of 3.31 per million workers, revealing a 40- to 90-fold detection gap when compared to European population-based estimates of 250 to 300 per million workers annually. Korean cases showed a distinctive agent profile, with isocyanates accounting for 46.6%—a figure that far exceeds the 13% to 23% in Western surveillance systems—which reflects the prominence of the furniture manufacturing industry. Temporal trends demonstrated successful prevention, with isocyanate-related cases decreasing from 65.0% to 26.9% during the period 2004–2009. Despite treatment advances, the prognosis remains poor: only 32% of patients achieve symptomatic recovery following the complete avoidance of exposure, and continued exposure increases the symptom burden 10-fold. Workers' compensation data confirm persistent underdiagnosis, with only 0 to 5 cases annually approved during the period 2013–2023 despite expanding recognition of occupational diseases overall. The Korea Occupational Disease Surveillance Center, established in 2022, offers an opportunity to bridge the prolonged surveillance gap. Clinicians treating working adults should maintain a high index of suspicion for the occupational causes of asthma, pursue early diagnosis and exposure cessation, and integrate workplace assessments into their routine clinical evaluations.

INTRODUCTION

Work-related asthma (WRA) is one of the most prevalent yet preventable occupational respiratory diseases, with population-based studies estimating that 10% to 25% of adult asthma cases are attributable to workplace exposure.12 Despite clear opportunities for prevention through workplace interventions, WRA remains significantly under-recognized, particularly in rapidly industrialized economies. The substantial discrepancy between these population-based estimates and the lower rates reported by surveillance systems underscores critical challenges in case identification. The global reporting mechanisms remain inadequate.3

The International Labor Organization (ILO) provides the current international diagnostic standards defining WRA to include occupational asthma (OA), where workplace exposure induces new-onset disease, and work-exacerbated asthma (WEA), in which occupational factors exacerbate preexisting asthma.45 This framework serves as a guide for clinical diagnosis, epidemiological surveillance, and decisions regarding worker compensation. International surveillance systems report WRA incidence rates ranging from 18 to 175 cases per million workers annually, with the variation reflecting differences in both genuine differences in occupational exposures and detection methodologies.5

In contrast, the Korea Work-related Asthma Surveillance (KOWAS) program documented only 3.31 cases per million workers from 2004 to 2009, a rate significantly lower than those in most Western countries.6 However, this low official surveillance rate contrasts sharply with clinical observations. The Korean Severe Asthma Registry (KoSAR) identified WRA in 17.9% of adults with severe asthma.7 These contrasting findings—low surveillance detection yet substantial contribution to severe disease—suggest persistent under-recognition among Korean workers rather than a genuinely low occupational burden. Workers' compensation data corroborate this pattern, with few approved cases despite increasing overall recognition of occupational diseases (Fig. 1).8

This article presents a comprehensive overview of WRA for allergists and pulmonologists who treat working adults. The Korean cases, particularly the data from the KOWAS surveillance program, offers valuable insights when compared to international patterns. Although the KOWAS program concluded in 2009, the absence of a subsequent equivalent national surveillance system renders this dataset the sole available benchmark for characterizing Korea’s distinct Korean industrial exposure profile. Special emphasis is placed on practical aspects, specifically the integration of workplace assessment into clinical evaluation—a critical component frequently overlooked in routine asthma care.

WORKPLACE HAZARDS AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

Epidemiology of WRA

Accurate epidemiological data on WRA are difficult to obtain despite surveillance efforts. International surveillance systems report incidence rates of 18 to 175 cases per million workers annually, although population-based estimates suggest true rates of 250–300 per million.59 In Korea, official surveillance captures 3.31 cases per million workers, whereas severe asthma registries indicate that occupational factors affect nearly 1 in 5 patients with severe asthma.67 This discrepancy between surveillance and clinical reality underscores persistent underdiagnosis that compromises preventive efforts.

International surveillance programs vary considerably in their methodologies and incidence rates (Table 1).2691011 The Finnish mandatory reporting system identifies the highest global rate of 175 cases per million workers,9 while the French Observatoire National des Asthmes Professionnels network reports 24 to 41 cases per million.10 These differences reflect surveillance design—mandatory reporting outperforms voluntary systems, and programs with systematic testing identify more cases than clinical diagnosis alone.5 The European Community Respiratory Health Survey-II estimates the true OA incidence of 250 to 300 per million annually, with occupational exposures causing 10% to 25% of adult-onset asthma.2

Established in 2004, the KOWAS program documented 236 cases over a 6-year period (2004–2009) from multiple sources: occupational physicians (22.0%), allergy and chest physicians (52.5%), regional surveillance networks (24.2%), and workers' compensation claims (43.2%) (percentages exceed 100% because of multiple reporting sources per case).6 Workers' compensation claims served as a critical but variable source of cases throughout the surveillance period (Fig. 2). Case detection was highest during the years where the surveillance program actively coordinated with compensation adjudicators, demonstrating that effective surveillance requires sustained engagement with the workers' compensation infrastructure.6 The relatively high proportion of compensation-identified cases (43.2%) suggests that many WRA cases come to clinical attention only when workers seek legal recognition, indicating missed opportunities for early detection through routine clinical care.

The crude incidence of 3.31 per million workers ranks among the lowest internationally. Statistical capture-recapture analysis suggested the true incidence was approximately 6.28 per million workers (95% confidence interval [CI], 4.96–7.60), nearly double the observed rate but still below international benchmarks.6 The KoSAR demonstrated that 17.9% (65/364) of employed patients with severe asthma have work-related disease.7 This prevalence falls within the 10% to 25% population-attributable fraction in European populations,2 suggesting that Korean WRA burden aligns with international patterns. Korean studies support this finding: construction workers exhibit an elevated asthma risk,12 and WEA is common among employed patients.13

KOWAS data reveal distinct demographic and geographic patterns. Incidence peaks among workers aged 50 to 59 years (7.74 per million) and is higher in male workers (3.78 vs. 2.58 per million).6 The Gyeonggi/Incheon manufacturing corridor reported 8.50 cases per million, a 9-fold increase over Seoul, with furniture manufacturing demonstrating the highest industry-specific incidence (67.62 per million) due to isocyanate exposure.6

Several factors contribute to underdiagnosis in Korea: KOWAS excludes agricultural workers, small enterprise coverage is limited, and workers are reluctant to report illness because of job security concerns.614 Workers' compensation data reinforce this pattern—between 2013 and 2023, the Korea Workers' Compensation and Welfare Service (KCOMWEL) approved only 0 to 5 OA cases annually despite an 8-fold increase in approvals for occupational diseases overall (Fig. 1).8 Comparison of Korean surveillance data (3.31–6.28 per million) with European estimates (250–300 per million) reveals a 40–foid to 90-fold detection gap,26 indicating that most WRA cases remain unrecognized.

Causative agents and exposures

Over 400 workplace agents can cause WRA, with causative agents varying across countries, revealing distinct industrial profiles and exposure patterns. The ILO provides a framework for categorizing occupational respiratory sensitizers by molecular weight, immunological mechanisms, and chemical properties.4 This system provides a guide for surveillance, diagnosis, and prevention worldwide.

The ILO framework categorizes agents into 3 groups. High molecular weight (HMW) agents—proteins or glycoproteins over 10 kDa—induce immunoglobulin (Ig) E-mediated sensitization and include flour, animal proteins, latex, enzymes, and plant antigens.4 Low molecular weight (LMW) agents—chemicals of 10 kDa or less—can trigger both IgE- and non-IgE-mediated responses and include isocyanates, metals, wood dusts, dyes, and pharmaceuticals.4 Irritant agents cause reactive airways dysfunction syndrome (RADS) through direct toxic injury without prior sensitization.5

The KOWAS program, which documented 236 cases between 2004 and 2009, reveals causative agent patterns differing from international surveillance data (Table 2).56910111516171819 Isocyanates accounted for 46.6% of Korean cases, far higher than the 13% to 23% in Western surveillance systems in Canada, the United Kingdom, and France.6 This pattern likely reflects the prominence of furniture manufacturing in Korea, where workers are commonly exposed to polyurethane products containing toluene diisocyanate, methylene diphenyl diisocyanate, and hexamethylene diisocyanate.

Reactive dyes represented 5.1% of Korean cases, a pattern that is uncommon in Western surveillance systems and reflects common exposure in the domestic textile dyeing industry.615 Flour and grain dusts accounted for 8.5% of cases in bakery and food processing.6 Metals (5.9%) and wood dust (3.4%) occurred at frequencies comparable to those reported internationally (Table 2). Animal antigens were infrequently identified at 0.8%, compared to 5% to 10% in countries with laboratory animal facilities, indicating industrial differences between Korea and Western countries.9

The distinct Korean pattern—with isocyanates constituting 46.6% compared with 13.6% in Canada, 15% in the United Kingdom, and 23.1% in France101617—may partly reflect ascertainment bias, as Korean surveillance was particularly efficient in identifying isocyanate cases because of physician awareness and established compensation pathways.6 These differences highlight the importance of country-specific surveillance systems tailored to local industries and exposure contexts.

The KOWAS surveillance showed key temporal changes from 2004 to 2009. The incidence of isocyanate-related cases decreased from 65.0% to 26.9% (P = 0.002), indicating successful workplace prevention in furniture manufacturing.6 Flour- and grain-related cases increased from 0% to 25.9% by 2007 (P = 0.023), reflecting improved detection after bakery investigations.6 In addition, the KOWAS identified new agents such as azodicarbonamide and cyanoacrylate, demonstrating surveillance value in detecting previously unrecognized occupational hazards.6

Pathogenesis

WRA involves diverse pathophysiological mechanisms, including immunological sensitization, direct cellular toxicity, and genetic-environmental interaction. Distinguishing between immunological and non-immunological pathways guides clinical evaluation and workplace intervention.111 In Korea, the predominance of isocyanates and reactive dyes indicates that clinicians predominantly encounter non-IgE-mediated mechanisms, which affect diagnostic approaches.6

HMW agents induce OA through IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reactions, with specific IgE antibodies detectable in 80% to 90% of cases.1120

In contrast, LMW agents (≤ 10 kDa), including isocyanates and reactive dyes, acquire immunogenicity through haptenization—binding to carrier proteins to form complete antigens.1121 However, specific IgE antibodies are detected in only 5% to 30% of affected workers, indicating a predominance of non-IgE pathways, such as IgG-mediated responses and cell-mediated immunity.622 This mechanistic diversity complicates diagnosis within Korean surveillance systems, as conventional allergy testing yields negative results in most cases which necessitate alternative strategies such as peak flow monitoring and inhalation challenge testing.

Genetic factors, particularly polymorphisms in xenobiotic-metabolizing enzymes and HLA molecules, modestly modify susceptibility to LMW agents and may inform risk stratification in Korean industries with high isocyanate exposure.232425

RADS represents a distinct non-immunological pathway in which high-level irritant exposure causes epithelial injuries through oxidative stresses, producing immediate-onset asthma without prior sensitization.1426 Irritant-induced OA comprises 5.5% of KOWAS cases, a proportion that may reflect Korean manufacturing-focused industry and potential under-recognition.613

CLINICAL EVALUATION IN WORKERS

Clinical presentation

WRA cases present with symptoms identical to those of non-OA and requires active clinical investigation. Wheezing, dyspnea, cough, and chest tightness do not inherently indicate workplace involvement.527 Occupational etiology is instead inferred from the temporal relationship between symptoms and workplace exposure, although this association may be subtle with late-phase responses from LMW agents commonly observed in Korean industries.16

Upper respiratory symptoms often precede lower airway involvement. Allergic rhinitis and conjunctivitis frequently accompany asthma symptoms, particularly in cases caused by HMW sensitizers.528 The presence of work-related nasal symptoms increases the likelihood of sensitizer-induced OA.20 Immediate responses occur within minutes and resolve within 1 to 2 hours.27 Late responses appear 4 to 8 hours after exposure and peak in the evening.5 Workers exposed to LMW agents more commonly experience late responses,6 manifesting as evening cough rather than acute dyspnea during work hours.20

Evidence of work relatedness becomes apparent when symptoms improve during weekends or vacations.518 Serial peak flow monitoring provides objective confirmation when clinical history alone is inconclusive. Although symptom improvement away from work indicates sensitizer-induced OA, prolonged exposure may result in persistent asthma even after removal from the workplace.529

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of WRA requires objective confirmation beyond clinical assessment. History-based diagnosis has a false-positive rate of 34%.18 The 2022 ILO guidelines provide a diagnostic framework that integrates occupational history, lung-function testing, and exposure assessment (Fig. 3),4 establishing 3 evidence levels: sufficient evidence for a definite diagnosis, supportive evidence for a probable diagnosis, and insufficient evidence requiring further evaluation.4

Initial evaluation includes obtaining occupational and medical histories to identify potential exposure, followed by spirometry with bronchodilator testing to confirm asthma.5 In Korea, specific inhalation challenge testing is available at only 2 national centers, requiring most diagnoses to rely on serial peak flow monitoring combined with immunological testing.30

Serial peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) monitoring is the primary tool to establish work-relatedness: patients record measurements 4 times daily over 2-week periods at work and away from work.31 Positive findings include a diurnal variation of ≥ 20% on workdays or a ≥ 15% decline during workdays compared to off-work periods, achieving a sensitivity of 63.6%–86.7% and a specificity of 77.2%–90.0% (Table 3).3233 In Korea, compensation assessments mandate 2 weeks of PEFR monitoring both during work and away from work.30

Skin prick testing and specific IgE measurements have complementary roles that vary by agent class (Table 3). For HMW agents, skin prick testing demonstrates a sensitivity of 70%–100%, while these tests have limited value for LMW agents, which predominantly operate through non-IgE-mediated mechanisms.4633

The specific inhalation challenge test remains the definitive diagnostic tool, with a sensitivity exceeding 80% and a specificity surpassing 90% when standardized ILO-based protocols are followed.534 Workplace challenge testing provides a practical alternative when laboratory-based challenges are inaccessible, offering realistic exposure conditions.35 However, comprehensive industrial hygiene assessments remain limited in many Korean workplaces, particularly in small enterprises. Workers' compensation evaluation through the KCOMWEL requires rigorous documentation that satisfies criteria for sufficient diagnostic certainty.36

Regional occupational medicine centers provide specialized evaluation networks, while the Korea Occupational Safety and Health Agency (KOSHA) offers consultation for complex cases.37 Early diagnosis remains critical, as it influences long-term prognosis and eligibility for workers' compensation.

Treatment and management

Management of WRA requires integrated medical and workplace interventions. The 2022 ILO guidelines provide an evidence-based framework emphasizing early intervention and hierarchical workplace controls.4 Among available strategies, complete avoidance of exposure offers the strongest evidence to improve outcomes, although implementation presents significant challenges in occupational settings.

Medical treatment follows established asthma management principles, with controller therapy being the cornerstone.38 Inhaled corticosteroids, either alone or combined with long-acting beta-agonists, constitute the first-line therapy according to stepwise approaches adapted from the Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines.5 Treatment intensity depends on the severity of asthma at presentation, which often reflects cumulative exposure duration, underscoring the importance of early diagnosis.1

For severe or uncontrolled cases, biological therapies targeting IgE (omalizumab) or IL-5 pathways (mepolizumab and benralizumab) may be beneficial, particularly for HMW agent-induced asthma with demonstrated IgE sensitization.38 However, pharmacological therapy alone rarely achieves complete remission unless ongoing exposure is addressed; this is a critical management principle distinguishing OA from non-OA.5

Complete avoidance: evidence for workplace exposure intervention

Substantial evidence supports complete removal from causative exposure. Vandenplas et al. 39 demonstrated that continued exposure, even at reduced levels, is associated with worse outcomes, such as limited symptomatic improvement, persistent airway hyperresponsiveness, and lung function decline. Complete avoidance of exposure remains the most effective intervention for symptomatic recovery and the prevention of disease progression.40 Prognostic data indicate that continued exposure increases symptom burden 10-fold and airway deterioration nearly 6-fold compared to complete avoidance.

Among OA patients who achieve complete exposure avoidance, 32% attain symptomatic recovery, whereas nonspecific bronchial hyperresponsiveness normalizes in 27%.3940 Workers with symptoms lasting less than 6 months achieve a recovery rate of 36%, which decreases with longer exposure duration. These findings highlight early removal as a key determinant of long-term respiratory outcomes.

Korean manufacturing presents implementation challenges. For isocyanate-exposed workers, complete avoidance often entails job loss, forcing difficult trade-offs between health protection and economic security, particularly in small enterprises lacking alternative positions.30 Although KOSHA regulations mandate exposure control measures, enforcement gaps persist.37

When complete avoidance is not feasible, engineering controls become the primary intervention. Improvement in ventilation and process modification may reduce exposure levels.27 However, personal protective equipment provides inconsistent protection against vapors and gases; poor compliance further reduces its effectiveness in preventing sensitization or disease progression.127

Prognosis and outcomes

The prognosis of WRA remains poor despite advances in treatment, with complete recovery achieved in only one-third of all patients. Management decisions, particularly exposure avoidance, play an important role in determining long-term outcomes, including breathing capacity, employment status, and quality of life.

A systematic review of 39 studies demonstrates complete symptomatic recovery in only 32% of patients following exposure avoidance (95% CI, 26%–38%) (Table 4).15404142 In contrast, nonspecific bronchial hyperresponsiveness (NSBHR) persists in 73% (66%–79%) of patients despite exposure avoidance. This dissociation between symptoms and airway pathophysiology indicates ongoing inflammation, which may explain exercise intolerance and symptom recurrence following diagnosis.43

Recovery rates vary across study populations. Workplace surveillance cohorts show higher rates of symptomatic recovery (53%) compared to clinic-referred cases (30%, P = 0.053) (Table 4),40 likely reflecting earlier detection and referral bias.1

Four factors have been reported to predict outcomes (Table 4): duration of exposure before diagnosis, duration of exposure after symptom onset, age at diagnosis, and the degree of exposure avoidance.40 Workers with short exposure durations (< 6 months) achieve symptomatic recovery in 36% of cases, with recovery rates declining as exposure duration increases. Younger age independently predicts better recovery (P = 0.019), reflecting a greater physiological reserve and longer remaining working life.41

A meta-analysis demonstrated that reduced exposure, compared to complete avoidance, is associated with increased symptom burden (odds ratio [OR], 10.23; 95% CI, 2.97–35.28) and NSBHR deterioration (OR, 5.65; 95% CI, 1.11–28.82).39 Another study reports NSBHR normalization in 12.5% of workers following relocation, compared to 50% among those who underwent complete workplace changes.44

HMW agent-induced asthma demonstrates persistent NSBHR in 73% of cases, a rate significantly exceeding that in asthma induced by LMW agents (P = 0.006).40 In Korean workers, among whom isocyanates and other LMW agents account for most cases,6 this pattern suggests potentially better respiratory outcomes compared to HMW-dominant populations.

OA has a severe socioeconomic impact (Table 4). Job loss occurs in 50%–70% of affected workers, with 30%–50% of income reductions, and only 43% remain fully employed 2 years after diagnosis.1639 Functional limitations persist in many cases, with 65% reporting exercise intolerance and 60% requiring assistance with daily activities.44 Korean workers face additional challenges due to limited transferable skills and age discrimination,6 underscoring the need to emphasize prevention as the primary strategy.

OCCUPATIONAL HEALTH MANAGEMENT

Compensation and disability

The severe socioeconomic consequences of WRA—including job loss, income reduction, and functional limitations—establish it as a compensable occupational disease.3940 Unlike non-OA, its preventable, work-related nature creates a responsibility for employers for medical costs and income loss.429

In Korea, workers' compensation is administered by KCOMWEL under the Ministry of Employment and Labour, in accordance with the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Act.30 By 2009, the system covered 13.9 million workers, representing 86.2% of wage earners, making it one of Asia's most comprehensive mandatory occupational health insurance programs.30

Claim adjudication follows a multi-tiered process. Workers submit medical diagnoses to the local KCOMWEL office, which forwards unclear cases to the Committee on Occupational Diseases Judgment (CODJ), a multidisciplinary panel comprising physicians, lawyers, and labor representatives.30 For complex cases, KCOMWEL may request epidemiological investigations by the Occupational Safety and Health Research Institute (OSHRI) under KOSHA, which conducts workplace evaluations and provides expert opinions.37 Rejected claims may be appealed through the Industrial Accident Compensation Insurance Examination Committee and the Reexamination Committee, with final recourse to civil courts.

Despite the mandatory coverage, OA remains severely underreported in Korea. Between 1992 and 2006, KCOMWEL approved only 218 cases (a mean annual incidence of 1.6 per million workers), whereas the KOWAS program identified 236 cases during the period of 2004–2009 alone (3.31 per million workers).6 Recent KCOMWEL data (2013–2023) confirm that this pattern persists (Fig. 1).8 The number of annually approved cases ranged from 0 to 5, with no approvals in 2016 or 2018, despite the total number of occupational disease approvals increasing from approximately 1,000 in 2013 to over 9,000 in 2022. Notably, OA approvals demonstrated modest increases during periods of active surveillance (2021 and 2023; 5 cases each), suggesting that targeted surveillance efforts may enhance case detection. Persistent barriers include limited worker awareness, reluctance to file claims owing to job security concerns, and diagnostic resource constraints particularly in small and medium enterprises.

Compensation systems for international workers face similar challenges. Most of the industrialized countries require rigorous diagnostic standards with a “more probable than not” threshold (> 50% likelihood of work-related causation) and approval rates vary widely (30%–70%) across jurisdictions.545 Benefits typically include temporary and permanent disability payments with 50%–75% wage replacement and coverage of medical expenses.27

Clinicians bear a critical responsibility for securing appropriate compensation for Korean workers. Obtaining objective diagnostic evidence while patients remain employed increases the likelihood of successful claims, as retrospective diagnosis after job separation complicates exposure documentation.514 In the Korean context, physicians should coordinate with occupational health systems, support OSHRI’s epidemiological investigations when requested, and participate in the CODJ adjudication process to ensure an accurate determination of work relatedness.30

Prevention

Compensation systems address the consequences of WRA, whereas prevention targets its root causes. Because 10% to 25% of adult asthma cases are attributable to occupational factors and also because WRA is theoretically preventable, systematic prevention represents both clinical and public health imperatives.25 Prevention strategies follow a well-established framework: primary prevention eliminates hazards before disease onset, secondary prevention detects subclinical disease for early intervention, and tertiary prevention minimizes disability in established cases.14

Systematic surveillance of exposed worker populations enables early case detection before irreversible airway remodeling occurs.42 Surveillance programs, when rigorously implemented, demonstrate effectiveness. For instance, monitoring of bakery apprentices in Quebec detected sensitization in 13.5% of workers within 2 years, enabling early intervention.9 Optimal monitoring programs combine periodic symptom questionnaires, spirometry, and—for high-risk exposure cases—immunological testing for specific IgE antibodies.19

Secondary prevention extends to co-worker screening when sentinel cases emerge, recognizing that OA indicates hazardous workplace conditions that may affect others.45 Korean regulations mandate health examinations for coworkers following confirmed cases of occupational lung disease, although implementation varies widely across industries.30

Worker education constitutes another surveillance component, focusing on early symptom recognition and encouraging prompt reporting.45 However, reporting barriers persist, including fear of job loss, stigma, and complex compensation processes, necessitating multifaceted approaches that address both knowledge gaps and structural obstacles.19 For workers removed from causative exposures, return-to-work decisions require a careful risk assessment. Workers may safely return to modified jobs if exposure to the specific sensitizing agent is eliminated and adequate workplace controls are in place to prevent cross-contamination.39 Vocational rehabilitation programs that integrate occupational medicine, industrial hygiene, and vocational counseling have facilitated successful job transitions.5 In Korea, workers' compensation provides vocational training benefits, although utilization remains low because of limited program awareness and accessibility.30

Effective prevention requires coordination across multiple systems, including occupational medicine clinics for diagnosis and monitoring, KOSHA for workplace inspection and enforcement, and workers' compensation for financial support during job transitions.30 Korean occupational medicine networks, centered at Seoul National University and Gachon University (Incheon), provide diagnostic services and workplace consultation, although geographic accessibility challenges persist for workers outside metropolitan areas.6

Employer obligations extend beyond hazard control to include maintaining medical surveillance programs, facilitating workers' compensation claims, and providing job accommodations when feasible.30 However, the predominance of small enterprises, many of which lack dedicated occupational health resources, create implementation gaps that require strengthened regulatory oversight and institutional support.37

The discontinuation of disease-specific surveillance programs, including KOWAS (operational during the periods of 1998–2002 and 2004–2011), created detection gaps that persisted for over a decade.46 To address this fragmentation, the Korea Occupational Disease Surveillance Center (KODSC) was established in 2022 as a comprehensive hospital-based system with 10 regional centers nationwide. In 2023, KODSC reported 4,341 cases of suspected occupational disease, with respiratory diseases comprising 23.8% (1,034 cases), substantially exceeding annual KCOMWEL approvals.46 Unlike disease-specific programs, such as KOWAS, KODSC integrates emergency department reporting, partner hospital networks, and coordination with KOSHA for workplace investigations, enabling detection of acute poisoning and emerging respiratory hazards that periodic health examinations may miss. Although KODSC does not specifically target WRA, its hospital-based infrastructure, and emphasis on sentinel event detection may help bridge this surveillance gap. Respiratory cases identified through the KODSC can trigger KOSHA workplace investigations, potentially identifying additional affected workers and enabling preventive interventions, thereby fulfilling the sentinel health event function required for effective surveillance.5

CONCLUSION

WRA remains substantially underdiagnosed worldwide, with Korean surveillance data revealing a 40–fold to 90-fold detection gap compared to population-based estimates.26 Prevention programs targeting specific agents have achieved a 50%–80% reduction of incidence, demonstrating that systematic workplace intervention is effective. The establishment of the KODSC in 2022 offers an opportunity to bridge the prolonged surveillance gap following the KOWAS program,46 and early experience with hospital-based surveillance has demonstrated improved detection of occupational diseases.47 Clinicians treating working adults should maintain a high index of suspicion for occupational causes of asthma and pursue early diagnosis, as timely exposure avoidance remains the strongest determinant of favorable outcomes.
