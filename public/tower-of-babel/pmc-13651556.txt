Distinct facets of other-oriented social cognition show differential associations with internalizing symptoms in young women with low subjective social status
Authors: Victoria Guazzelli Williamson, Alex Rankine, Valerie Owusu-Hienno, Kathryn Celis Mills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial hierarchy exerts widespread influences on human health and cognition. Other-oriented social cognitive processes may be particularly relevant for individuals in low social status positions as they navigate their social environments, and some of these processes may also be associated with internalizing difficulties. Given gender differences in both internalizing difficulties and social cognition, it is important to examine these associations among young women of low social status. In this study, we assessed associations between multiple dimensions of other-oriented processing and internalizing difficulties in 300 young women. Greater uncertainty about mental states was associated with symptoms of generalized anxiety, social phobia, social interaction anxiety, and depression, and remained associated with all four outcomes after accounting for rumination and other significant predictors. Personal Distress was initially associated with all four outcomes but remained associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety in the final models. Hypermentalizing and a greater tendency to imagine oneself experiencing the feelings and actions of fictional characters were also associated with social phobia. Notably, visual perspective-taking and self-reported perspective-taking in everyday life were not associated with any internalizing symptom questionnaires. Together, these findings suggest that associations between other-oriented social cognition and internalizing symptoms depend on the specific cognitive or affective process assessed. These findings contribute to growing evidence that heightened internalizing risk among young women of low social status may be linked not to greater other-oriented processing broadly, but to specific dimensions of other-oriented cognition and affect.Keywords: Mentalizing, Perspective-taking, Social status, Internalizing, Anxiety, Depression, Young adult

Introduction

Social hierarchy exerts widespread influences on human health and cognition [1,2]. Individuals in low status positions in social hierarchies—and women in particular—are at greater risk for adverse mental and physical health outcomes such as depression and obesity compared to those in high social status positions [[3], [4], [5]]. Social status is also related to individual differences in cognitive strategies, with individuals in low status positions more likely to attend to environmental context, be attuned to others’ emotions and perspectives, and develop interdependent self-views [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. These differences in cognitive strategies might reflect differences in both experiences and individual needs when navigating the social environment. Despite elevated internalizing difficulties among individuals with low social status, as well as differences in other-oriented cognitive processing, the relationship between these processes remains relatively unexplored. In particular, it remains unclear whether specific dimensions of other-oriented social cognition are differentially associated with internalizing difficulties among individuals of low social status. As an initial step toward addressing this question, the present study examines associations between multiple dimensions of other-oriented processing and internalizing symptoms in young women with low subjective social status.

Lower social status is associated with greater other-oriented processing

Increases in other-oriented behavior and cognition have been robustly linked with low social status [7,11,12]. For example, individuals of lower social class have been shown to be more compassionate of others [13], display greater empathic accuracy, and perceive others’ emotions and perspectives more accurately [6,9]. Individuals of lower social status have also been shown to look more at others, both in the lab as participants viewed images of streets (tracked via eye movements) and in vivo as participants walked down a street (assessed via head movements). The authors of this study also found that individuals of lower social class more quickly detected changes in images of faces, but not objects, compared to their higher class counterparts [14]. Previous research has also found that individuals of lower status perform better on emotion perception tasks (i.e., accuracy of emotional judgements from pictures of eyes) and make fewer errors in a visual perspective-taking task (i.e., the Director Task) compared to their higher status counterparts [6]. Together, this behavioral work suggests that lower social status is associated with greater engagement in several forms of other-oriented processing.

Other-oriented social cognition may link lower social status and internalizing psychopathology

Internalizing disorders—including mood and anxiety disorders—are among the most common mental health problems. Internalizing disorders often begin in adolescence and are more common among girls and women [[15], [16], [17]]. There is evidence that underlying cognitive processes (e.g., greater rumination) may contribute to the higher incidence of internalizing disorders observed in women compared to men [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21]].

Internalizing symptoms, particularly depression, may be especially important to examine among individuals with low subjective social status, given that depression is characterized by low self-esteem and negative self-perceptions. Adolescents and young adults with lower subjective status report more depressive symptoms, poorer overall mental health, and greater psychological distress [22,23]. These associations extend beyond objective indicators of socioeconomic status, with meta-analytic and longitudinal evidence linking subjective social status particularly closely to psychological outcomes, including depression [3,5,24,25]. Accordingly, individuals with low subjective social status may be an especially important population in which to examine processes associated with internalizing symptoms.

Cognitive processes characteristic of depression and anxiety include negative attentional bias [[26], [27], [28]], self-focused attention [[29], [30], [31], [32]], hypervigilance [33], and rumination [20,[34], [35], [36]]. As previously mentioned, ruminative cognitive processes (which are more prevalent among women; [37]) may contribute to higher rates of internalizing psychopathology among women [20,38] and may represent one cognitive process relevant to internalizing symptoms among women experiencing low social status.

Individual differences in other-oriented social cognition may likewise be relevant to internalizing symptoms within this population. Findings relating mentalizing to depression are mixed. These mixed findings may be due, in part, to variability in Theory of Mind functioning by disorder as well as nuances in Theory of Mind effects (e.g., first versus second order Theory of Mind). Some studies suggest that individuals with diagnoses or symptoms of depression or mood disorders have decreased mentalizing or experience some Theory of Mind deficits [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]]. However, others find no significant associations between mentalizing and internalizing symptoms or disorders [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50]]. Some recent studies have suggested that perspective-taking strategies among individuals with social anxiety may look different from those of individuals with depression, with some studies finding excessive theory of mind, or hyper-mentalizing, in individuals with Social Anxiety Disorder compared to controls or compared to individuals with Major Depressive Disorder [51,52]. A recent study tested whether hypermentalizing for individuals with social anxiety is context-dependent and found that hypermentalizing was only associated with social anxiety in the self-referential social situation [53]. Thus, specific dimensions of other-oriented social cognition (e.g., propensity and degree) may be related to internalizing psychopathology, such as social anxiety and depression [54,55]. However, little research has measured multiple domains of mentalizing within the same study, or properly differentiated between the subtype of mentalizing they measure. Thus, additional research that acknowledges the multidimensional nature of other-oriented social cognition is needed to understand how specific cognitive and affective processes may relate to different internalizing symptoms [54,55].

Preregistered hypothesis and analytic plan

The present study examined associations between multiple dimensions of other-oriented processing and internalizing symptoms in young women with low subjective social status. Higher rates of internalizing disorders among women, along with evidence that low social status may relate more strongly to some health outcomes among women than men [56], motivated our focus on women. Additionally, because the onset of internalizing disorders frequently occurs during late adolescence and young adulthood [16,57,58], and subjective social status appears to be a particularly strong predictor of depression during this period, especially among women [22], our sample comprised young women aged 18–30 years.

Consistent with our preregistration, we generally hypothesized that higher levels of other-oriented mentalizing, including greater altercentrism, self-reported perspective-taking, and hypermentalizing, would be associated with higher levels of symptoms of depression, generalized anxiety, and social anxiety. We preregistered a three-stage analytic procedure in which we first tested each measure of other-oriented processing separately, then entered significant predictors of each outcome into a combined model, and finally added rumination and compared the fit of models with and without rumination. Because we assessed several cognitive and affective dimensions of other-oriented processing, these analyses also allowed us to determine whether support for the general directional hypothesis differed across the specific processes assessed.

Method

Women with low subjective social status, between the ages of 18–30, who were based in the United States were recruited via Prolific to complete our approximately hour-long study programmed in Gorilla. Eligibility was determined using participants’ Prolific prescreening information, which required participants to identify as women. Within the study, participants also completed a separate select-all-that-apply gender identity item for descriptive purposes; these responses were not used to determine eligibility or included in analyses. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated approximately $9 upon completion of the study. Participants were excluded if they had internet connection speeds < 5Mbps and did not have a self-rated United States subjective social status falling in the range of 1–3. We initially recruited participants who had rated their subjective social status between 1 and 3 when they signed up for Prolific, and we re-screened these participants at the start of the study to ensure their current subjective social status was in this range.

Six bot/attention checks were embedded throughout the study. Failure of the CAPTCHA bot check at the start of the study resulted in discontinuation from the study. Apart from the CAPTCHA, the participants needed to fail two or more of the other bot/attention checks to be rejected from the study. If they failed only one attention check (N = 24), their responses for the questionnaire within which the attention check was embedded were not used (see Supplemental Materials for information about these attention checks). After data cleaning, data from 300 young women with low subjective social status were retained.

Measures

Subjective Social Status. Subjective social status was measured via the adult version of the MacArthur Ladder of Subjective Social Status. This is a reliable and well-validated measure which asks participants to rank where they would fall on a ladder representing the United States of America [59,60]. At the top of the ladder are the people who are the best off, those “with the most money, the highest amount of schooling, and the jobs that bring the most respect” while the bottom of the ladder has those who are the worst off, those with “the least money, little or no education, no jobs or jobs that no one wants or respects” [3]. This measure has shown adequate test-retest reliability in other samples and has been shown to predict self-rated health in a multi-ethnic sample in the United States [61].

Indices of cognitive and affective other-oriented processing

Director Task. The Director Task is a measure of perspective-taking propensity. Unlike measures of perspective-taking propensity and accuracy, the Director Task assumes that most participants will perform reasonably well on the task and that variability in task performance will be largely due to individual perspective-taking propensity (including propensity to make egocentric or altercentric errors). Egocentric bias was calculated from the number of errors on the Director task where the participant chose the distractor object when the speaking Director was behind the shelf, and thus did not share the same visual perspective as the participant. Altercentric bias was calculated as the number of errors on the Director task where the participant chose the distractor object when the speaking Director was in front of the shelf, and thus shared the same visual perspective as the participant. Greater visual perspective-taking, which was hypothesized to be related to internalizing measures, in the Director Task would be reflected by greater altercentric bias and decreased egocentric bias. Please see the Supplemental Materials and Supplemental Figure 1 for additional information about the Director Task.

Interpersonal Reactivity Index. The Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) is a 28-item scale measuring individual differences in different facets of other-oriented processing, such as empathy and social cognition. It consists of four subscales. Each subscale contains seven items (two reverse-scored) that participants rate on a Likert scale ranging from 0 “does not describe me at all” to 4 “describes me very well.”

i. Perspective-Taking Subscale. The Perspective-Taking subscale of the IRI (IRI-PT) measures an individual’s “tendency to spontaneously adopt the point of view of others” [62]. The subscale has displayed good test-retest reliability (0.61 among males and 0.62 among females) and good internal consistency (alpha = 0.75 among males and 0.78 among females [63]. An example item for this subscale reads “I try to look at everybody's side of a disagreement before I make a decision.”

ii. Fantasy Scale. This subscale of the IRI measures the “respondents' tendencies to transpose themselves imaginatively into the feelings and actions of fictitious characters in books, movies, and plays” [62]. An example item from this scale includes, “When I am reading an interesting story or novel, I imagine how I would feel if the events in the story were happening to me.”

iii. Personal Distress. The personal distress subscale of the IRI (IRI-PD) “measures "self-oriented" feelings of personal anxiety and unease in tense interpersonal settings.” An example item from this subscale reads “When I see someone who badly needs help in an emergency, I go to pieces.”

iv. Empathic Concern. The empathic concern subscale of the IRI (IRI-EC) “assesses "other-oriented" feelings of sympathy and concern for unfortunate others.” An example item for this subscale includes “When I see someone being taken advantage of, I feel kind of protective towards them.”

Movie for Assessment of Social Cognition (MASC). The MASC is a computerized task that assesses mentalizing degree and accuracy [64]. Participants are asked to watch a movie about four characters attending a dinner party. At multiple timepoints, the movie is paused and participants are asked to answer questions about one of the character’s thoughts, feelings, or intentions at that moment. Strengths of the MASC include sensitivity in assessing several facets of mentalizing (e.g., degree and accuracy)—for both cognitive and affective mentalizing—in an ecologically valid manner [65]. The MASC assesses balanced mentalizing (a measure of mentalizing accuracy), or theory of mind, as well as various measures of degree of mentalizing: hypomentalizing, hypermentalizing, and lack of mentalizing. The hypomentalizing or “under-mentalizing” condition refers to responses whereby the participant inferred the mental states of others to a lesser degree or insufficiently. Hypermentalizing, or overmentalizing, responses measure excessive attribution of mental states to others—a high degree of mentalizing. Lack of mentalizing refers to responses where participants did not mentalize at all. Greater mentalizing degree in the MASC would be reflected in higher hyper-mentalizing scores, and lower hypo-mentalizing scores, both of which were hypothesized to be related to increased internalizing symptoms.

Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (RFQ). The eight-item RFQ (RFQ-8; [66]) is a brief self-report measure assessing individuals’ perceived understanding of their own and others’ mental states. Participants rated eight statements from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 7 (“strongly agree”). Although the RFQ-8 was originally scored using separate certainty and uncertainty subscales, subsequent psychometric work has supported a unidimensional scoring approach and raised concerns regarding the original double-scoring procedure [[67], [68], [69]]. Accordingly, we scored the RFQ-8 as a unidimensional measure of uncertainty about mental states by reverse-scoring Item 7 and averaging responses across all eight items. Higher scores therefore indicate greater uncertainty about mental states, or greater difficulties in reflective functioning.

Internalizing symptoms

Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression scale (CES-D). The CES-D is a well-validated 20-item scale which asks participants to rate the frequency of depression symptoms in the past week [70]. Possible scores range from zero to 60. The scale has good psychometric properties including high internal consistency, and adequate test-retest reliability and validity [70].

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 Item Scale (GAD-7). The GAD-7 is a seven-item scale used to assess generalized anxiety disorder [71]. Scores for each item range from 0 “not at all” to 3 “nearly every day”, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 21. The GAD-7 has good previously reported reliability, validity, specificity (82%), and sensitivity (89%; [71]).

Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS). The SIAS is a 20-item questionnaire that measures distress when meeting and talking with others. The SIAS is widely used in clinical settings and among researchers of social anxiety [72]. Participants respond to each item on a five-point Likert scale. The scale has good previously reported internal consistency (previously reported Cronbach's alpha = 0.88–0.94), validity, and test-retest reliability (4 and 12-week test-retest reliability = 0.92; [72]).

Social Phobia Scale (SPS). The SPS is a 20-item questionnaire that measures each item on a 5-point Likert scale [72]. The SPS measures fear of scrutiny by others during routine tasks (eating, drinking, writing, etc.) and is widely used in conjunction with the SIAS to assess for social anxiety disorder. The scale has good internal consistency (previously reported Cronbach's alpha = 0.89–0.94), validity, and test-retest reliability (4 weeks = 0.91; 12 weeks = 0.93; [72]).

Other cognitive processes

Ruminative Response Scale (RRS). Rumination was measured via the shortened, eight-item RRS which consists of two subscales that measure reflective self-regulation and depressive brooding [73]. Respondents are asked to rate how often they do each of eight items such as “I think about how sad I feel” and “I think about my failures” on a scale of 1 “almost never” to 4 “almost always.”

Analysis

Our hypotheses and general analytic strategy were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/mz8jg/?view_only=3364705f76e14addb30dd66208c66adc). The preregistration specified separate linear regressions relating each mentalizing variable to each internalizing outcome, followed by combined models containing the significant predictors of each outcome and comparisons of those models with corresponding models that included rumination.

We made two modifications to this preregistered procedure. First, although the preregistration specifically identified Item 1 of the Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (“People’s thoughts are a mystery to me”), we used the mean of all eight RFQ items as a unidimensional measure of uncertainty about mental states, in light of psychometric evidence supporting this scoring approach and raising concerns about the original two-subscale scoring procedure [[67], [68], [69]]. To evaluate the robustness of the findings to this scoring decision, we repeated the full analytic sequence using Item 1 in place of the full RFQ score. RFQ Item 1 coefficients and model-fit comparisons from these sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplemental Tables 5–7. Second, the preregistration did not specify a correction for multiple comparisons. However, to reduce the likelihood of false-positive selection, we opted to apply Benjamini–Hochberg corrections within each internalizing outcome to the initial individual regression models. Predictors surviving this correction were entered into the corresponding multiple regression model.

We then added rumination to each multiple regression model and compared models with and without rumination using Akaike Information Criterion, as specified in the preregistration. We additionally report Bayesian Information Criterion. Lower AIC and BIC values indicate better relative model fit. Coefficient-level p values in the subsequent multiple regression models were not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Models with rumination

Next, consistent with our preregistered analytic plan, we added rumination to each multiple linear regression model containing the measures of other-oriented processing that were significantly associated with the corresponding outcome. Models including rumination demonstrated better relative fit, as indicated by lower AIC and BIC values, than the corresponding models without rumination (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3). Rumination was significantly associated with all four internalizing outcomes. Greater uncertainty about mental states remained significantly associated with each of the four outcomes after accounting for rumination and the other predictors in each model. Other associations were more specific. Personal Distress remained significantly associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety, while Fantasy and hypermentalizing remained significantly associated with social phobia. In contrast, Personal Distress was no longer significantly associated with depressive symptoms (p = .059) or generalized anxiety symptoms (p = .065), and Fantasy was no longer significantly associated with generalized anxiety symptoms (p = .141). Thus, adding rumination improved model fit while several associations between specific dimensions of other-oriented processing and internalizing symptoms remained.

Sensitivity analyses using RFQ item 1

Analyses using the RFQ item identified in the preregistration partially reproduced the full-scale RFQ findings (Supplemental Tables 5–7). Greater uncertainty as assessed by Item 1 was associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety after correction for multiple comparisons and remained associated with both outcomes in the combined models. After adding rumination, Item 1 remained associated with social interaction anxiety, β = .166, 95% CI [.063,.268], p = .002, but was no longer significantly associated with social phobia, β = .106, 95% CI [−.002,.214], p = .054. Item 1 was not associated with generalized anxiety or depression in the individual models.

Discussion

The present study tested whether individual differences in other-oriented social cognition may be associated with internalizing symptoms among young women with low subjective social status. One motivation for this hypothesis was the possibility that greater attention to others’ perspectives and mental states may be useful for navigating low-status social environments, while also carrying psychological costs under some conditions. Support for this hypothesis was selective. Hypermentalizing, reflecting a greater degree or elaboration of mental-state inference, was associated with social phobia symptoms. In contrast, measures intended to capture a greater propensity to engage in perspective-taking, including self-reported perspective-taking and visual perspective-taking, were not associated with internalizing symptoms after correction for multiple comparisons.

Several other facets of other-oriented processing were associated with internalizing symptoms, although these should not be interpreted simply as reflecting greater mentalizing. Greater uncertainty about mental states was associated with all four internalizing outcomes and remained significant in each final model. Greater personal distress in interpersonal situations was also initially associated with all four outcomes but remained significantly associated only with social phobia and social interaction anxiety after accounting for rumination and other significant predictors. Fantasizing was also specifically associated with social phobia. Together, these findings suggest that the relationship between other-oriented social cognition and internalizing symptoms depends substantially on which facet of social cognition is being measured, rather than reflecting a general relationship between greater mentalizing and internalizing symptoms.

This pattern is consistent with a multidimensional account of social cognition in which propensity, degree, valence, accuracy, and related processes are conceptually distinct and may have different implications for psychopathology [55]. It may also help explain why prior studies relating mentalizing and other-oriented social cognition to internalizing psychopathology have produced positive, negative, and null associations: studies often use broad labels such as “mentalizing” for measures that capture meaningfully different processes.

Other-oriented cognitive and affective tendencies

Among measures capturing cognitive and affective tendencies in other-oriented processing, findings differed across constructs. personal distress and fantasy were the only Interpersonal Reactivity Index subscales associated with any internalizing symptom questionnaires, while perspective-taking and empathic concern were not associated with any outcome after controlling for multiple comparisons. Consistent with prior associations of personal distress with depression and anxiety, personal distress showed broad initial associations with internalizing symptoms, relating positively to all four symptom measures [74,75]. However, after accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors, personal distress remained significantly associated only with social phobia and social interaction anxiety, but not depressive or generalized anxiety symptoms. These findings suggest that its broader initial associations with depression and generalized anxiety may partly reflect processes shared with rumination and uncertainty about mental states, whereas its more robust associations with the two social anxiety measures may reflect the interpersonal distress captured by this subscale.

The initial significant associations between personal distress and internalizing symptoms align with previously reported positive associations between the personal distress subscale and internalizing processes [74,75]. Notably, prior work has found stronger associations between Personal Distress and internalizing outcomes than for other IRI subscales, including Empathic Concern [74,75]. In the present study, however, these associations became more specific after accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors: Personal distress remained significantly associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety, but not depressive or generalized anxiety symptoms. personal distress captures self-oriented emotion dysregulation and distress in response to interpersonal situations and may reflect “an inability to regulate negative empathic arousal” [74,75]. This interpersonal and affective focus may help explain why personal distress showed its most robust associations with the two social anxiety outcomes, both of which involve distress in situations characterized by social interaction or evaluation.

The fantasy subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index showed a more specific pattern. Greater tendency to imagine oneself experiencing the feelings and actions of fictional characters was associated with social phobia symptoms, and this association remained significant after accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors. Prior studies have also found relationships between the fantasy subscale and symptoms of social anxiety and neuroticism, noting that the fantasy subscale measures a combination of cognitive and affective dimensions of empathy [74]. Similarly, a meta-analysis using a different measure of fantasy proneness revealed significant associations between fantasy proneness and depression and anxiety [76]. The relationship between fantasy proneness and internalizing processes has been theorized to be due to difficulty distinguishing between real and fiction for individuals with very vivid mental imagery [77]. Scholars have proposed that such vivid imagination can increase an individual’s susceptibility to vividly recalling and embellishing negative events/experiences, even leading them to overfocus on the possible negative consequences of past events [78], although we were unable to directly test this interpretation in the present study.

We did not find significant relationships between perspective-taking and empathic concern subscales of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index and any of the internalizing symptom questionnaires after controlling for multiple comparisons. Notably, the perspective-taking subscale measures a cognitive aspect of empathy and recent meta-analyses have shown positive associations between affective empathy and social anxiety, but nonsignificant, very weak, and sometimes negative associations between cognitive empathy and anxiety symptoms [79,80]. In addition, while perspective-taking may be harmful in certain contexts [81], the type of everyday perspective-taking captured by the questionnaire may not accurately reflect this nuance or granularity. Thus, we did not find support for a general association between greater self-reported propensity to take others’ perspectives and internalizing symptoms.

Similarly, propensity to engage in visual perspective-taking (as measured via the Director Task) was not associated with internalizing symptoms. While a study with a small sample of adolescents found that adolescents with clinical levels of social anxiety symptoms at clinical threshold performed worse at taking the Director’s visual perspective into account [82], more recent studies with larger samples failed to find a relationship between performance on the Director Task and internalizing symptoms [45,83]. There are a number of possible explanations for these null associations. One may be the nature of the task: it simply may be difficult to follow and remember the instructions while completing the task, and thus the task may more closely measure constructs like working memory. Additionally, the task requires visual perspective-taking which may meaningfully differ from social perspective-taking and mentalizing that occurs in real-life situations. In other words, it is possible that the cognitive processes involved in visual perspective-taking as tapped by the Director Task, even if it does measure visual perspective-taking, are not the social perspective-taking strategies involved in in vivo social interactions.

Overall, we did not find evidence that a greater propensity to take others’ perspectives in everyday life or in a social communicative context [84] was broadly associated with internalizing symptoms. Instead, associations differed depending on the specific cognitive or affective tendency assessed. After accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors, personal distress remained associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety, while Fantasy remained associated with social phobia symptoms. Notably, the associations that remained for these cognitive and affective tendencies were concentrated among social anxiety outcomes, suggesting that these forms of other-oriented processing may be particularly relevant to symptoms involving interpersonal distress and social evaluation. Together, these findings highlight the importance of distinguishing among different forms of other-oriented processing rather than treating dispositional perspective-taking or empathy as unitary constructs.

Uncertainty about mental states

Greater uncertainty about mental states was consistently associated with internalizing symptoms. Greater uncertainty about mental states, assessed with the Reflective Functioning Questionnaire (RFQ), was associated with depressive symptoms, generalized anxiety, social phobia, and social interaction anxiety, and these associations remained significant after accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors in each model. Importantly, higher RFQ scores reflect greater uncertainty about one’s own and others’ mental states rather than greater mentalizing ability or accuracy. Thus, these findings suggest that uncertainty in understanding mental states may be broadly associated with internalizing difficulties.

This finding may help clarify some of the mixed literature relating reflective functioning and mentalizing to internalizing symptoms. Measures grouped under these broader constructs can capture meaningfully different processes, including confidence or uncertainty in understanding mental states, the tendency to engage in mentalizing, and accuracy in inferring others’ thoughts and feelings. Our findings suggest that greater subjective uncertainty about mental states may be particularly relevant to internalizing symptoms, underscoring the importance of considering the specific social-cognitive process captured by a given measure.

Mentalizing degree and accuracy

The Movie for the Assessment of Social Cognition (MASC) captured both mentalizing accuracy and degree. The only significant association between the MASC and internalizing symptoms was between hypermentalizing and social phobia. This association remained significant after accounting for rumination and the other significant predictors. Hypermentalizing reflects a tendency to make overly complex or unsupported inferences about others’ mental states, and therefore captures a greater degree or elaboration of mentalizing rather than simply poorer mentalizing ability. Previous research using the MASC has similarly shown greater hypermentalizing errors among individuals with diagnosed social anxiety disorders compared to control groups [51,52]. Excessive mentalizing among individuals with social anxiety has been theorized to reflect a tendency to read too much into the actions of others, which may contribute to misunderstandings of social situations and misinterpretations of others’ mental states [51,52].

Interestingly, in a study of young adults, social anxiety disorder was associated with greater hypermentalizing errors, whereas dimensional social anxiety and depressive symptoms were not [52]. Our significant association between hypermentalizing and social phobia symptoms may differ from these null dimensional findings because these processes operate differently among young women with low subjective social status or because our larger sample provided greater power to detect the association. In contrast, overall mentalizing accuracy, hypomentalizing, and lack of mentalizing were not significantly associated with internalizing symptoms after correction for multiple comparisons. Thus, our findings do not suggest that poorer mentalizing accuracy is broadly associated with internalizing symptoms. Instead, they point more specifically to excessive elaboration of others’ mental states as potentially relevant to social phobia.

Taken together with the findings for personal distress and fantasy, the association between hypermentalizing and social phobia suggests that particular forms of other-oriented processing may be especially relevant to social anxiety-related symptoms. Importantly, these associations were not evident for measures capturing a general propensity or ability to take another person’s perspective. Instead, the significant findings involved interpersonal distress, imaginative engagement with others’ experiences, and excessive elaboration of others’ mental states. This pattern may be particularly relevant to social anxiety, given its central focus on social evaluation and concern about how one is perceived by others.

Context, Adaptation, and Low Social Status These findings may be particularly meaningful in the context of the present sample. Greater attention to others’ thoughts, feelings, and evaluations may be useful for navigating environments in which social standing is uncertain or resources and influence are limited. From this perspective, social-cognitive tendencies associated with internalizing symptoms need not originate as deficits. Instead, some may reflect strategies that are adaptive within particular social contexts but carry psychological costs when they become generalized across situations or persist after the context that elicited them has passed [55]. For example, heightened attention to others’ mental states may help an individual anticipate rejection or interpersonal threat, while greater elaboration of ambiguous social cues may support cautious social decision-making. Yet these same tendencies may become costly when they contribute to persistent uncertainty, excessive inference, or difficulty disengaging from social evaluation.

The present study cannot directly test this developmental or causal account because all participants were selected for low subjective social status and data were cross-sectional. Nevertheless, the pattern of findings is consistent with the possibility that it is not greater other-oriented processing in itself that confers risk, but rather the particular form that processing takes and the contexts in which it is deployed. Longitudinal research spanning individuals with a wider range of social status will be needed to test whether social environments shape multidimensional profiles of other-oriented cognition and whether particular profiles subsequently contribute to internalizing symptoms.

Limitations and future directions

Our study is not without its limitations. Perhaps most obviously, our study was conducted in young women with low subjective social status, and thus it is unclear whether our findings extend to other populations. In addition, we did not recruit a clinical sample specifically comprised of individuals with known or diagnosed internalizing disorders. Thus, future studies may wish to test whether these relationships are qualitatively different when internalizing processes reach diagnostic levels or among other populations.

Given constraints on our study design, including cross-sectional data collection and a focus on individuals with low subjective social status, we also cannot determine the direction of the observed relationships or the contribution of social status to these associations. Longitudinal studies and experimental manipulations may be better equipped to test whether low-status social environments shape specific forms of other-oriented processing and whether these processes, in turn, contribute to internalizing symptoms. Importantly, our findings do not suggest that low subjective social status is associated with a general increase in mentalizing that subsequently confers risk. Future research should instead distinguish among specific cognitive and affective tendencies, uncertainty about mental states, degree of mentalizing, and mentalizing accuracy when testing how social status may shape social cognition and internalizing symptoms.

Future studies may also benefit from assessing multiple aspects of social cognition within the same study and across methods. Although the present study included both self-report and performance-based measures, these measures captured meaningfully different processes and should not necessarily be interpreted as interchangeable indicators of mentalizing. Future work using more ecologically-valid approaches may help clarify when these processes occur in everyday social interactions and whether their associations with internalizing symptoms depend on social context. This may be particularly important for social anxiety, given that several of the significant associations in the present study involved processes related to interpersonal distress, social evaluation, or excessive inference about others’ mental states.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that associations between other-oriented social cognition and internalizing symptoms vary across specific facets of social cognition among young women with low subjective social status. Greater uncertainty about mental states was associated with symptoms of depression, generalized anxiety, social phobia, and social interaction anxiety, and remained significant after accounting for rumination and other significant predictors. In contrast, associations with other forms of other-oriented processing were more specific. Personal distress remained associated with social phobia and social interaction anxiety, while fantasy and hypermentalizing were associated with social phobia. Notably, measures of self-reported and visual perspective-taking, as well as mentalizing accuracy, were not associated with internalizing symptoms after correction for multiple comparisons. Together, these findings suggest that relationships between other-oriented social cognition and internalizing symptoms depend on the specific cognitive or affective process being assessed rather than reflecting a general relationship between mentalizing and mental health. More broadly, they highlight the importance of considering multiple dimensions of social cognition when investigating how social-cognitive processes may contribute to internalizing difficulties in individuals navigating low-status social environments.
