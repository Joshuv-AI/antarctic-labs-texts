Alterations of regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in drug-naïve adolescents with nonsuicidal self-injury
Authors: Kaike Liao, Rui Yu, Yuwei Chen, Xinyue Chen, Xinyan Wu, Xiaohua Huang, Nian Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) is one of the major public health problems endangering adolescents. However, the neural mechanisms of NSSI is still unclear. The purpose of this study was to explore regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI using amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) combined with functional connectivity (FC) analysis. Thirty-two drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI (NSSI group) and 29 healthy controls matched for sex, age, and level of education (HC group) were enrolled in this study. ALFF and seed-based FC analyses were used to examine the alterations in regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits. The correlation between ALFF or FC values of aberrant brain regions and clinical characteristics were detected by Pearson correlation analysis. The NSSI group showed increased ALFF in the left inferior and middle occipital gyri, lingual gyrus, and fusiform gyrus; additionally, decreased ALFF in the right medial cingulate gyrus, left anterior cingulate gyrus, and left medial superior frontal gyrus compared to those in the HC group. With the left inferior occipital gyrus as seed, the NSSI group showed increased FC between the left inferior occipital gyrus and the bilateral superior parietal gyrus, right inferior parietal angular gyrus, right inferior frontal gyrus of the insular region, and left precuneus relative to that the HC group. With the left anterior cingulate gyrus as seed, the NSSI group showed increased FC between the left anterior cingulate gyrus and right dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus. With the left lingual gyrus as seed, the NSSI group showed increased FC between the left lingual gyrus and right middle frontal gyrus, and decreased FC between the left lingual gyrus and the left superior temporal gyrus, right supplementary motor area, and left rolandic operculum. With the left fusiform gyrus as seed, the NSSI group showed increased FC between the left fusiform gyrus and left middle and inferior temporal gyrus, and decreased FC between the left fusiform gyrus and the bilateral postcentral gyrus, right precentral gyrus, right lingual gyrus, and left inferior parietal angular gyrus. Moreover, the FC value between the left fusiform gyrus and left inferior temporal gyrus was positively correlated with suicidal ideations score. This study highlights alterations in regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in brain regions related to visual and emotional regulation functions in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI. These findings may facilitate better understand the underlying neural mechanisms of NSSI in adolescents.Keywords: Nonsuicidal self-injury, Adolescents, Amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations, Functional connectivity, Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imagingSubject terms: Brain imaging, Magnetic resonance imaging, Psychosis, Neural circuits, Striate cortex

Introduction

Nonsuicidal self-injury (NSSI) refers to intentional and direct injury to body tissues in a socially unacceptable way without suicidal intention1. NSSI often occurs in the context of psychiatric disorders, such as major depressive disorder (MDD) and borderline personality disorder2. In addition, it can occur also without accompanying psychopathology3. Self-injury behaviors are usually adopted by adolescents with repetitive self-injury as an emotion regulation strategy to relieve negative emotions such as anxiety, guilt, loneliness, alienation or self-hatred4,5. The most commonly recognized ways of self-injury include cutting and scratching the skin, hitting the body or a wall6. The lifetime prevalence of NSSI among adolescents has been reported to be 13–17%7, while the rate among adolescents in China has risen to 23%8. Adolescents who repeatedly self-injury are more likely to develop mental disorders or suicidal behavior9. With the rising prevalence of NSSI10, the disorder has been included in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th edition) draft diagnosis (DSM-5). Although NSSI has attracted attention due to its high incidence and potential risk of suicide, its neural mechanisms remain unclear.

In recent years, functional magnetic resonance (fMRI) has played an important role in the diagnosis of mental disorders due to its non-invasiveness and high spatial and temporal resolution11. Previous studies on NSSI were mostly based on task-fMRI12–15, which provided a considerable amount of evidence for the exploration of NSSI mechanisms. However, it is difficult to obtain consistent results due to differences in paradigms or experimental schemes used. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) as a relatively simple method of signal acquisition because it can examine alterations in brain function while participants remain at rest16. In addition, rs-fMRI avoids the superimposition of complex interactions between intrinsic brain activity and task performance due to the non-involvement of task11. Although previous studies have provided important clues to the neural mechanisms of NSSI, studies on the NSSI is still very limited, with only a few studies to date investigating resting-state brain function in adolescents with NSSI.

Previous rs-fMRI studies exhibited abnormalities in brain activity and functional connectivity (FC) in patients with NSSI16–19. However, the results are not entirely consistent. A study found that adolescents with MDD who engaged in NSSI showed enhanced amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) in the fusiform gyrus and median cingulate gyrus17. In another study, increased ALFF in the middle occipital gyrus and median cingulate gyrus were reported in similar samples18. Compared with adults with MDD alone, those who engaged in NSSI showed enhanced ALFF in the middle occipital gyrus, lingual gyrus, and superior frontal gyrus19. In addition, a study focusing on reward circuits found altered FC in the cingulate gyrus and nucleus accumbens in MDD adolescents with NSSI16. One of the reasons could be that the mixed clinical sample with MDD or borderline personality disorder confounded the results17–19. Antidepressant use can also interfere with the link between NSSI and brain functions19. Therefore, exploring the regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in drug-naïve NSSI adolescents without psychiatric comorbidities may facilitate better understand the neural mechanisms of NSSI.

ALFF and seed-based FC analyzes are two commonly used rs-fMRI analysis methods that have been used to examine the regional and network-related characteristics of low-frequency oscillations20. ALFF reflects the spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations (0.01 Hz–0.08 Hz) of the brain at rest state, and the function of the brain at baseline can be detected by measuring the amplitude of the fluctuation21. Moreover, FC is a statistical method that indirectly speculates co-activation between brain regions based on changes in blood oxygenation level dependent responses22. The higher FC between two regions may indicate that they are co-activated16. Especially, ALFF and seed-based FC methods have been combined to examine the alterations of brain function and networks in patients with depression20. To explore the alterations in the regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits, we used ALFF combined with seed-based FC analysis to examine the rs-fMRI data of drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI.

Materials and methods

Subjects

A total of 33 drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI were enrolled from the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College between November 2022 and April 2023, but one adolescent was excluded due to poor image quality. Finally, 32 adolescents with NSSI were included (30 females, age range: 13–19 years). According to self-report, 9 adolescents had suffered from life or academic failure, 8 had poor peer relationships, and 4 had experienced negative influence from their parents. No patients had been diagnosed with another psychiatric disorder or received any antipsychotic treatment before participating in the study. Thirty sex-, age-, and education-matched HC were recruited by advertising, excluding one person due to image quality problems. A total of 29 HC were included (24 females, age range: 14–16 years). This study conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College [2022ER406-1].

Adolescents with NSSI in this study were included by experienced psychiatrists based on the diagnostic criteria for NSSI in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (5th edition) Part III. The criteria for inclusion in the NSSI group were as follows: individuals who met the diagnostic criteria for NSSI in the DSM-5: a history of engaging 5 or more times of NSSI in the past year, with at least 1 episode occurring in the last month; were aged 10–19; were of Han nationality and right-handed; were not treated with psychotropic drugs; had no history of bipolar disorder, MDD, pervasive developmental disorders, or psychotic disorders.

The inclusion criteria for the HC were as follows: no history of neuropsychiatric illness after DSM-5 nonpatient version evaluation and screening; no history of mental disorders in HC; and no contraindication for MRI scanning.

The exclusion criteria for all subjects were as follows: had a history of serious organic disease or head trauma, had contraindications for MRI scans, or had a history of mental disorders in family members.

The NSSI and HC groups underwent MRI scans within 24 h after they agreed to participate in this study. All subjects or guardians signed informed consent before the study implementation.

Clinical assessment

We collected clinical data on adolescents with NSSI, including sex, age, education level, and age at first self-injury. To obtain self-injury information from the adolescents with NSSI as comprehensively as possible, the Ottawa Self-Injury Inventory23 was adopted to collect information on the causes, frequency, modes, and sites of self-injury. To assess the anxiety levels, the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)24 was used. In addition, the suicidal ideations level was evaluated in adolescents with NSSI by the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideations (BSSI)25. The higher the score, the stronger the suicidal ideations. For subjects under 18 years of age, diagnosis and evaluation were performed with the consent of the adolescent’s parent/guardian.

Acquisition of rs-fMRI data

All adolescents with NSSI and HC were evaluated with a GE Discovery MR750 3.0T magnetic resonance scanner and a 32-channel head coil. The subjects were equipped with noise-reduction earplugs before scanning to minimize the effect of machine noise. A suitable foam pad was used to stabilize the subject’s head to minimize head movement. The rs-fMRI sequence parameters were as follows: time-echo (TE)/time-repetition (TR), 28/2500 ms; flip angle (FA), 68°; section thickness/spacing, 3/0.6 mm; section number, 40; field of view (FOV), 192 × 192 mm and matrix, 64 × 64; Each functional imaging sequence produced 250 image volumes, and the total scanning time was 625 s. The three-dimensional T1-weighted imaging parameters used were as follows: TE/TR, 2.7/7.1 ms; FA, 9°; section thickness/spacing, 1/0 mm; FOV, 256 × 256 mm; matrix, 256 × 256; and total scanning time, 327 s.

Data preprocessing

Rs-fMRI data were pre-processed by DPABI software (DPABI_V5.4 version of https://rfmri.org/dpabi) based on MATLAB R2014a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) following the steps below: (1) The first 10 volumes were discarded to eliminate signal instability during the initial scan. (2) The slice timing was corrected through the interpolation method, and the 40th layer whole-brain image was regarded as the reference layer. (3) Head motion correction, head movement > 3.0 mm in any direction and rotation > 3.0° were excluded. (4) The DARTEL method was applied to standardize the corrected data into the Montreal Neurological Institute space. (5) Using a 4 mm FWHM (half of the maximum width) Gaussian filter to smooth. (6) Friston-24 model was used to regress various covariates such as white matter, cerebrospinal fluid and head movement parameters to minimize the impact of physiological noise on data analysis26. (7) Linear detrending and filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) were used to eliminate irrelevant signals and noise, extracting low-frequency signals that can truly reflect resting-state brain activity. (8) The head movement parameters and the image registration were checked. One adolescent with NSSI and one healthy control were excluded based on the eighth step. Finally, we obtained imaging data from 61 participants (including 32 adolescents with NSSI and 29 healthy controls).

ALFF analysis

After the sixth preprocessing step, we used DPABI software to carry out ALFF analysis. As one of the indicators of rs-fMRI analysis, ALFF is commonly used to measure the characteristics of local brain regions. Based on the Fourier transform, the power spectrum is obtained by converting the time domain signals of each voxel in the whole brain into frequency domain signals. The average square root of the area under the 0.01–0.08 Hz frequency spectrum curve for the voxels was subsequently calculated as the ALFF measurement. Finally, we converted ALFF to zALFF using Fisher’s z transformation to weaken the inter-individual variability.

FC analysis

After the ALFF analysis, the seed-based FC analysis was used27. First, we obtained 4 clusters with significant differences between the NSSI and HC groups, including 7 brain regions. Second, we plotted a sphere with a radius of 6 mm centered on the peak voxel of each cluster as the seed. We ended up with 4 seeds. Third, we used DPABI software to calculate the association between the time series of all voxels in each seed and that of other voxels to obtain the correlation map. Finally, Fisher’s z transformation was utilized to transform FC cluster correlation maps into z-scored FC maps before statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Demographic/clinical characteristics were undertaken using the SPSS software (26.0 version, SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the continuous variables were analyzed by the Shapiro‒Wilk tests. The age and education level were analyzed by the Mann–Whitney U tests. The SAS and BSSI scores were examined by two-sample t-tests, and the sex data were examined by Chi-square test. The significance threshold was set as p < 0.05.

We used DPABI software to perform two-sample t-tests on ALFF/FC between the two groups. Sex, age, and mean frame-wise displacement were regressed as covariates to eliminate the potential effects of sex, age, and head movement on the results. To control the false positive results caused by multiple comparisons, the Gaussian random field was utilized to adjust the alpha level in the DPABI software. The significance threshold was set as: cluster-wise p < 0.05 and voxel-wise p < 0.001 (two-tailed). The DPABI software was used to perform Pearson’s correlation analysis at the voxel level to explore the correlation between the average ALFF/FC values of abnormal regions and clinical symptom scores. The significance threshold was set as p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristic

No significant differences were observed in age, sex, or level of education between the two groups. A significant difference in clinical symptoms was found between the two groups, and the NSSI group had higher SAS scores relative to the HC group (p < 0.001). The Ottawa Self-Injury Inventory exhibited that the frequency of self-injury in the NSSI group was mostly 1–5 times (71. 88%) in the past month. The modes of self-injury included cutting, stabbing the skin, hair pulling, hitting and head banging, and the most commonly adopted mode was cutting (93. 75%) (see Table 1).

Discussion

In current study, we have demonstrated the alterations in brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI by ALFF and seed-based FC analyses. Specifically, the adolescents with NSSI showed increased ALFF in the left IOG, MOG, LG, and FFG, and decreased ALFF in the left SFGmed, left ACG, and right MCG compared to the HC. Furthermore, the adolescents with NSSI also exhibited FC alterations mainly between the left occipital lobe and bilateral dorsolateral parietal lobe, the right medial frontal lobe, and left dorsolateral temporal lobe relative to the HC. In addition, the FC values between the left FFG and left inferior temporal gyrus was positively linked to the level of suicidal ideations. The present study highlights the alterations in brain activities in brain regions associated with visual and emotional regulation functions in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI, which may provide new insights into the neural mechanisms of NSSI.

Our results showed that the ALFF of left IOG, MOG, LG, and FFG in adolescent with NSSI were significantly higher than those in the HC. The current results was consistent with some previous studies which reported that MDD patients who engaged in NSSI exhibited increased ALFF in the occipital cortex17–19. The IOG and MOG are the main anatomical areas of the visual regions and play a key role in facial emotion perception and visual information processing28,29. The lingual gyrus, located in the medial occipital lobe, is mainly involved in visual memory encoding30–32. The fusiform gyrus, commonly referred to as the "fusiform facial area", is considered to be a key area for facial detection and recognition33,34. Some researchers have reported that the occipital cortex of patients with NSSI showed abnormal activation in emotion recognition and processing12,35. Combined with our findings, we speculate that the increased ALFF in left occipital cortex may be associated with emotion perception and emotion recognition, and that impaired emotional feedback may lead adolescents to adopt NSSI behaviors as a maladaptive strategy. However, there was no significant correlation between ALFF values in these brain regions and the severity of NSSI. Further researches are required to explore the link between the brain function of occipital cortex and NSSI in the future.

We found that adolescents with NSSI exhibited a significant decrease in ALFF in the left SFGmed, left ACG, and right MCG. Previous studies are not entirely consistent with our results17,19. A recent study also found a decrease in ALFF in the superior frontal gyrus of patients with NSSI and that ALFF values were associated with the frequency of NSSI behaviors19. However, another study reported that MDD patients who participated in NSSI showed increased ALFF in the MCG17. The inconsistent results may be due to the interference of antipsychotic drugs or sample differences. The addictive hypothesis of self-harming suggested that repetitive NSSI behaviors may be associated with the midbrain dopamine reward system36. The midbrain dopamine reward system, a dopaminergic pathway, is associated with the processing of pleasure and motivational stimuli37. Prefrontal cortex has been shown to serve as a crucial target area for midbrain-cortical pathways38. The SFGmed, a part region of the frontal cortex, is involved in reward and decision making assessment39,40. The ACG and MCG as key components of the cingulate gyrus, are responsible for receiving and integrating reward stimuli and value assessment information from the prefrontal cortex for guiding actions41. Together with our results, the reduced ALFF in the left ACG and SFGmed and in the right MCG may be related to impaired reward processing and behavioral decision making, which may increase the risk of NSSI behaviors in adolescents.

Our results demonstrated increased FC between the left IOG and the bilateral superior parietal gyrus, and right inferior parietal angular gyrus in adolescents with NSSI. Prior research supported our results that adolescents with NSSI exhibited increased FC between the occipital cortex and the amygdala, suggesting that the occipital cortex may be involved in negative emotion transmission42. The occipital cortex is the main anatomical area of the visual region, which plays an important role in facial emotion perception and visual information processing28. The parietal cortex is confirmed to be activated during processing emotional faces and episodic memory43,44. Individuals who engaged in NSSI have been reported to often experience difficulties with emotion regulation, which may be related to their abnormal emotion recognition14. Seymour et al. have demonstrated deficits in facal emotion recognition in adolescent inpatients who engaged in NSSI45. According to the emotional intelligence theory, emotion recognition is a precursor to emotion regulation14. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that the increased FC between the left IOG and bilateral parietal lobes may be related to emotion recognition and emotion information transfer. The impaired emotion recognition and information feedback may lead to abnormal emotion regulation in adolescents, which exposes them to a higher risk of engaging in NSSI behaviors5,46. However, no correlation was found between FC values and NSSI behaviors. Thus, we speculate that FC alterations between the left IOG and bilateral parietal cortices may indirectly contribute to NSSI behaviors by affecting emotion regulation.

We demonstrated that reduced FC between the left LG and right supplementary motor area in adolescents with NSSI. Previous rs-fMRI study supported our results which reported that FC changes between the supplementary motor area and amygdala in adolescents with NSSI42. The LG is thought to play a role in visual memory encoding and visual memory storage32. Studies have found that the surface area of LG mediates inhibitory function and divergent thinking47. The supplementary motor area is related to the negative motor network of the brain, which is responsible for performing inhibition during movements48. Individuals who engaged in the NSSI have been reported to exhibit greater impulsivity than those who do not engage in NSSI49. Recent research suggested that individuals who are impulsive may be particularly prone to reckless behavior in the context of negative emotions49. Given that NSSI behaviors has proven to be an effective way to regulate emotions, individuals with high impulsivity at greater risk for NSSI behaviors50. Combined with our results, the decreased FC between the left LG and right supplementary motor area may be an underlying neural mechanism for the impaired inhibitory control of adolescents with NSSI, which increases the impulsivity of adolescents to engage in self-injury.

In addition, our results showed that the increased FC between the left FFG and left inferior temporal gyrus, and decreased FC between the left FFG and the right precentral gyrus, and bilateral postcentral gyri relative to the HC group. A recent study is partially consistent with our results, they observed decreased FC between the right postcentral gyrus and right medial cingulate gyrus in patients with MDD who engaged in NSSI17. The FFG is a key area for facial recognition33. The inferior temporal gyrus is confirmed to be related to self-reference processing51 and emotional stimulation52. Enhanced FC between this circuits may be related to the emotional perceptions of adolescents with NSSI. Intriguingly, we observed that FC between the left FFG and the left inferior temporal gyrus in adolescents with NSSI was positively associated with suicidal ideations. Thus, it can be inferred that the higher the FC value of the circuit, the stronger the suicidal ideation and the more likely the NSSI behavior is to occur. The precentral gyrus participates in the execution of autonomic movement as part of the primary motor cortex53. The postcentral gyrus primarily engaged in somatic sensation and pain processing54,55. Individuals who engaged in NSSI exhibit greater pain tolerance has been reported by previous study56. An underlying neurobiological mechanism for greater pain tolerance is aberrant levels of endogenous opioids in individuals with NSSI, which leads to a reduction in the effort involved in initiating NSSI57. Another theoretical model suggested that NSSI behaviors may temporarily increase endogenous opioid levels, which allows the system to return to normal briefly and improve the experience of pain58. However, over-release of opioids may eventually create tolerance and addiction in susceptible individuals36. Taken together with the previous findings, our results suggest that the decreased FC between the left FFG and the right precentral gyrus and bilateral postcentral gyrus may be related to abnormal behavioral control and pain tolerance in adolescents with NSSI, which further increases the likelihood of NSSI behaviors in adolescents.

Limitations

Several limitations in current study that deserve attention. First, the size of the sample was relatively small. However, these findings can still provide some insights into the neural mechanisms of NSSI in adolescents. The sample size needs to be further expanded in the future. Second, there were more female than male patients in the NSSI group, which led to sex imbalance. However, the HC group was sex-matched with the NSSI group and there was no significant difference between the two groups. In addition, we included sex as a covariate to control for potential confounding effects on the results. Future studies need to add more male samples to balance sex factors. Third, this was a study of cross-sectional design, we can not confirm whether these ALFF or FC results were a continuous state in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed for thorough exploration. Finally, most of the brain regions with significant differences obtained in this study were located in the left hemisphere. The reason is probably the small sample size, or the lateralization or incoordination of the left and right brain functions caused by the disorder itself, which deserves further exploration in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study highlights alterations in regional brain activity and corresponding brain circuits in brain regions related to visual and emotional regulation functions in drug-naïve adolescents with NSSI. These findings may facilitate better understand the underlying neural mechanisms of NSSI in adolescents.
