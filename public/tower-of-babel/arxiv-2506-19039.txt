Abstract
Geological data show that, early in its history, the Earth had a large-scale magnetic field with an amplitude comparable to that of the present geomagnetic field. However, its origin remains enigmatic and various mechanisms have been proposed to explain the Earth’s field over geological time scales. Here, we critically evaluate whether tidal forcing could explain the early Earth’s geodynamo, by combining constraints from geophysical models of the Earth–Moon system and predictions from rotating turbulence studies. We show that the Moon’s tides could be strong enough before − 3.25 -3.25 Gy to trigger turbulence in the Earth’s core, and possibly dynamo action in the mean time. Then, we propose new scaling laws for the magnetic field amplitude B B . We show that B ∝ β 4 / 3 B\propto\beta^{4/3} , where β \beta is the typical equatorial ellipticity of the liquid core, if the turbulence involves weak interactions of three-dimensional inertial waves. Otherwise, we would expect B ∝ β B\propto\beta if the amplitude of tidal forcing were strong enough. When extrapolated to the Earth’s core, it suggests that tidal forcing alone was too weak to possibly explain the ancient paleomagnetic field. Therefore, our study indirectly favours another origin for the early Earth’s geodynamo on long time scales (e.g. the exsolution of light elements atop the core, or thermal convection due to secular cooling).

Introduction

This research article concludes a series published in the
Comptes Rendus

(
Deguen et al., 2024
;
Nataf and Schaeffer, 2024
;
Vidal et al., 2024
;
Plunian et al., 2025
)
, sparked by the Ampère Prize awarded in
2021
by the French Academy of Sciences to the
géodynamo
team.
The latter was originally formed to study convective flows within the Earth’s core and the geomagnetic field.
Indeed, more than a century after the seminal idea of
Larmor, 1919

(
Roberts and King, 2013
;
Dormy, 2025
, e.g.)
.
Moreover, paleomagnetism indubitably shows that the Earth has hosted a large-scale magnetic field for billions of years
(
Macouin et al., 2004
, e.g.)
.
Therefore, the dynamics of the liquid core over geological time scales can be indirectly probed by studying the geomagnetic field.
For example, geomagnetic data allows reconstructing surface core flows
(
Istas et al., 2023
;
Rogers et al., 2025
, e.g.)
, which may enhance future predictions of geomagnetic variations
(
Madsen et al., 2025
, e.g.)
, or anchoring models aimed at understanding the origin of magnetic reversals
(
Driscoll and Olson, 2009
;
Frasson et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.
New research interests have also emerged among the
géodynamo
’s members and collaborators for some years.
In particular, understanding the origin of the Earth’s dynamo is recognised as being of paramount importance, since it would provide invaluable insights into the long-term evolution of the Earth since its accretion
(
Halliday and Canup, 2023
)
.

1.2
Non-consensual dynamo scenarios

Finding a convincing scenario to explain the ancient Earth’s magnetic field is a long-term goal in geophysical modelling
(
Landeau et al., 2022
)
, and similarly for the Moon
(
Wieczorek et al., 2023
)
.
To this end, dynamo action is believed to predominantly operates in the planet’s liquid core
(
Braginsky and Roberts, 1995
, e.g.)
, as the latter is surrounded by a silicate mantle having a weaker electrical conductivity (compared to that of liquid iron at core conditions) in both its upper and lower regions
(e.g.
Yoshino, 2010
;
Jault, 2015
, for the Earth)
.
As such, the mantle is often considered as an electrical insulator on long time scales for dynamo modelling.
Note that dynamo action may be possible in a (basal) magma oceans if the electrical conductivity of molten silicate rocks is high enough
(
Ziegler and Stegman, 2013
;
Scheinberg et al., 2018
;
Stixrude et al., 2020
;
Lherm et al., 2024
, e.g.)
, but this scenario is out of the scope of the present study.

To assess the viability of each candidate dynamo scenario, we should strive to reproduce the main characteristics of the recorded field over geological time scales.
In particular, we can consider the typical amplitude of the large-scale field in the dynamo region, which can be extrapolated from the data.
Indeed, for an electrically insulating mantle, the amplitude
B_{\mathrm{cmb}}
of the largest-scale dipolar field at the core-mantle boundary (
cmb
) is related to that at the planet’s surface
B_{s}
by
(
Moffatt and Dormy, 2019
, e.g.)

B_{\mathrm{cmb}}\simeq B_{s}\left(\frac{R_{s}}{R_{\mathrm{cmb}}}\right)^{3},

(1)

where
R_{\mathrm{cmb}}
is a mean core radius, and
R_{s}
is the mean surface radius.
Typical values are
R_{\mathrm{cmb}}\approx 3480
km for the Earth and
R_{\mathrm{cmb}}\approx 200-380
km for the Moon
(
Wieczorek et al., 2023
)
, which then yield from Figure
1
the estimate
B_{\mathrm{cmb}}\sim 10^{-2}-10^{-1}
mT
for the early Earth’s field before
-3.25
Gy, and
B_{\mathrm{cmb}}\sim 10-70
mT
for the early Moon’s field during the high-field epoch (where dynamo action is more probable).
The strength of the magnetic field could even be larger than that predicted by formula (
) inside the core, as for instance reported for the current Earth
(
Gillet et al., 2010
, with a hidden toroidal field of a few mT, e.g.)
.
Note it is unclear whether these ancient dynamos have operated in a strong-field regime (as currently in the core) or not, that is a dynamo regime in which the magnetic energy dominates over the kinetic energy of the core flow
(
Moffatt and Dormy, 2019
, e.g.)
.
If so, a strong-field behaviour would put another strong constraint to assess the viability of candidate dynamo models.

Now, do we have some ancient dynamo scenarios meeting the above constraints ?
We start with the Earth’s case, from which we can get physical insight into the past by looking at the present geodynamo.
Currently, the main driver of the Earth’s core flows is inner-core crystallisation
(
Buffett et al., 1996
, e.g.)
.
Indeed, this scenario has proven successful in reproducing the main characteristics of the current geomagnetic field using numerical simulations
(
Schaeffer et al., 2017
;
Aubert, 2023
, e.g.)
.
However, inner-core crystallisation was missing in a distant past.
The exact chronology remains disputed, but a nucleation starting
1\pm 0.5
Gy ago seems reasonable from recent thermal-evolution models
(
Labrosse, 2015
, e.g.)
or paleomagnetism
(
Biggin et al., 2015
;
Bono et al., 2019
;
Zhou et al., 2022
;
Li et al., 2023
)
.
Prior to inner-core growth, it remains unclear which mechanism could have sustained a large-scale magnetic field.
For instance, the recent estimates of the thermal conductivity of liquid iron at core conditions, which do not show any consensus yet between experimental and computational values
(e.g.
Williams, 2018
;
Ohta and Hirose, 2021
, for recent reviews)
, might suggest that secular cooling was energetically less efficient than initially thought to sustain dynamo action in the Earth’s core.
In addition to thermal convection alone
(
Aubert et al., 2009
;
Burmann et al., 2025
, e.g.)
, various scenarios could be invoked to cope with this situation
(
Landeau et al., 2022
, see a recent review in)
, such as flows driven by the exsolution of light elements in the core
(
Monville et al., 2019
, e.g.)
or more exotic ones driven by tides
(
Le Bars et al., 2015
, e.g.)
.

Finally, we point out that the situation is even worse for the early Moon than for the early Earth.
Indeed, any viable dynamo scenario should be capable of generating a magnetic field that is
10
larger in the Moon’s core than in the Earth’s one, despite its dynamo region being about
10
times thinner.
This clearly challenges the dynamo hypothesis in the early Moon, for which different scenarios have been proposed
(
Wieczorek et al., 2023
, e.g. see a review in)
.

1.3
Outline of the manuscript

Motivated by the puzzling paleomagnetic data shown in Figure
1
, we aim to thoroughly assess whether tidal forcing could have sustained the early Earth’s magnetic field.

Landeau et al., 2022
provided preliminary estimates for tidal forcing in their proof-of-concept study but, as we will see below, applying this scenario to Earth is still intricate.
The extrapolation is underpinned by some arguments that must be quantitatively revisited in the light of recent geophysical models and fluid-dynamics studies.
This manuscript is thus also intended for non-experts in the field, with the aim of explaining how the community models tidally driven flows and turbulence in planetary cores.

For instance, to the best of our knowledge, there is no self-consistent numerical code to efficiently explore the dynamo capability of orbitally driven flows in realistic core geometries (i.e. with a weakly non-spherical
cmb
).
This is a noticeable difference with convection-driven dynamos, for which efficient numerical strategies have been developed for decades
(
Christensen et al., 2001
;
Schaeffer, 2013
, e.g.)
.
Therefore, we must currently rely on scaling arguments to extrapolate the few available results to the early Earth.
However, planetary extrapolation is not easy task as it requires a strong understanding of the turbulence properties, which are still debated in the fluid-dynamics community
(
Vidal et al., 2024
, e.g. as recently reviewed in)
.
Similarly, a universal scaling law that could be applied to dynamo action in any set-ups is unlikely to exist.

With the aforementioned goals in mind, the manuscript is divided as follows.
We introduce the basic fluid-dynamics ingredients for core flows driven by orbital forcings (e.g. tides or precession) in §
2
, as they may not be familiar to non-expert readers.
We then focus on the tidal forcing in §
3
, combining constraints from geophysical models, hydrodynamic studies and new dynamo scaling laws, to extrapolate our findings to the early Earth.
We discuss the results in
4
, and we end the manuscript in §
5
.

2
Modelling of orbitally driven flows

In this section, we will present the minimal fluid-dynamics ingredients needed to model the dynamics of planetary liquid cores subject to orbital mechanical forcings (such as tides or precession).
As the prospective readers may not be familiar to these features, we will try to make this presentation as pedagogical as possible.
First, we introduce in §
2.1
an idealised common description of orbitally driven core flows.
Then, we briefly outline in §
2.2
the different steps of the (expected) flow response of a planetary liquid core to an orbital mechanical forcing, which will be revisited in §
3
to carry out the planetary extrapolation to the early Earth.

2.1
Fluid-dynamics equations

We want to model the core dynamics of a planetary body prior to the nucleation of an inner core.
Thus, we consider a liquid core of volume
V
with no inner core, which is surrounded by a rigid and electrically insulating mantle.
The
cmb
is denoted by
\partial V
below.
The core geometry is generally assumed to be spherical in most geodynamo models, as it is sufficient for convection-driven studies
(
Burmann et al., 2025
, e.g.)
and allows developing very efficient numerical methods
(
Christensen et al., 2001
;
Schaeffer, 2013
, e.g.)
.
However, it is important to take the small departures from a spherical
cmb
into account for tidal and precession forcings
(
Le Bars et al., 2015
, e.g.)
.
For simplicity, we assume below that
V
is an ellipsoid, which agrees with the mathematical theory of equilibrium figures for a rotating fluid mass with an orbital partner
(
Chandrasekhar, 1987
, e.g.)
.
Then, it is customary to work in the frame rotating with the ellipsoidal distortion, which is rotating at the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}
.
Note that the latter does not need to be the rotation of the fluid, which will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{s}
below.
Working in the frame rotating at
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}
will ease the computations, since the
cmb
will be steady in that frame and can be written as
({x}/{a})^{2}+({y}/{b})^{2}+({z}/{c})^{2}=1
,
where
[a,b,c]
are the ellipsoidal semi-axes and
(x,y,z)
are the Cartesian coordinates.
Note that dynamical pressure (due to core flows) is expected to be much weaker than hydrostatic pressure on long time scales.
Consequently, in practice, the flow dynamics is usually explored in rigid ellipsoids by considering prescribed values of
[a,b,c]
.
Planetary values can be estimated from tidal theory
(
Farhat et al., 2022
, e.g.)
and the theory of planetary figures
(
Chambat et al., 2010
, e.g.)
.
Note that the
z-
axis is usually chosen along the rotation of the planet, such that it is customary to introduce the two parameters given by

\beta=\frac{|a^{2}-b^{2}|}{a^{2}+b^{2}},\quad f=\frac{a-c}{a},

(2a,b)

where
0\leq\beta\ll 1
is the (equatorial) ellipticity and
0\leq f\ll 1
is the (polar) flattening.

The core is filled with an electrically conducting liquid, which is assumed to have a constant kinematic viscosity
\nu
and magnetic diffusivity
\eta
.
The ratio of these two quantities yields the magnetic Prandtl number
Pm=\nu/\eta
, whose typical value is
Pm\sim 10^{-6}
in a planetary liquid core when adopting the typical core values
\nu\simeq 10^{-6}
m
2
.s
-1
and
\eta\simeq 1
m
2
.s
-1

(
Nataf and Schaeffer, 2024
, e.g.)
.
As a consequence, we expect magnetic dissipation to be much larger than viscous one in a dynamo regime.
Note that we neglect density effects below, to focus on incompressible fluids with constant density.
Indeed, most fluid-dynamics studies consider orbitally driven flows without buoyancy effects (see a rationale in §
4
).
In the frame rotating at
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}
, the fluid velocity
\boldsymbol{v}
is then governed by the incompressible momentum equations given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{v}+2\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}\times\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=-\nabla P+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{v}+\frac{1}{\mu}\nabla\boldsymbol{B}\times\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{f}_{P},

(3a)

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=0

(3b)

where
\mathrm{d}_{t}=\partial_{t}+\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla
is the material derivative,
\boldsymbol{f}_{P}=-\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}_{\epsilon}\times\boldsymbol{r}
is the Poincaré force with the position vector
\boldsymbol{r}
, and
\mu
is the magnetic permeability of the fluid (
\approx\mu_{0}
, where
\mu_{0}
is the magnetic permeability of vacuum in practice).
The momentum equation is coupled, through the Lorentz force, to the magnetic field equations given by
(
Moffatt and Dormy, 2019
, e.g.)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=\nabla\times(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})+\eta\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{B},

(4a)

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle=0.

(4b)

Finally, the above equations must be supplemented with appropriate boundary conditions (BCs) at the
cmb
.
The mantle being supposed to be an electrical insulator, the magnetic field in the core must match at the
cmb
the field in the mantle, which is given by

\boldsymbol{B}=\nabla\Phi,\quad\Phi\to 0\ \text{when}\ |\boldsymbol{r}|\to+\infty.

(5a,b)

Next, because the mantle is supposed to be rigid, the velocity must satisfy the no-penetration BC given by

\left.\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right|_{\partial V}=0

for any forcing, where
\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
is the (outward) unit vector normal to the boundary.
Some BCs must also be enforced on the tangential velocity components, but they are forcing-dependent.

Finally, it is customary to write down the mathematical problem using dimensionless variables.
In particular, we introduce the Ekman number
E
and the Rossby number
Ro
given by

E=\frac{\nu}{\Omega_{s}R_{\mathrm{cmb}}^{2}},\quad Ro=\frac{\mathcal{U}}{\Omega_{s}R_{\mathrm{cmb}}},

(6a,b)

where
\Omega_{s}=|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{s}|
is the mean angular velocity of the fluid (e.g.
\Omega_{s}\simeq 7.3\times 10^{-5}
rad.s
-1
currently in the Earth’s core),
R_{\mathrm{cmb}}
is the mean core radius, and
\mathcal{U}
is a typical amplitude of the core flows.
We will show in §
3
that both
E
and
Ro
will be key for the planetary extrapolation of the results.
Indeed, despite
E
is a very small quantity in planetary cores (e.g.
E\sim 10^{-15}
in the Earth and
E\sim 10^{-12}
in the Moon), it will mainly control the growth of turbulent flows.
Similarly, the strength of the rotating turbulence (and hence that of the dynamo magnetic field) will be influenced by the value of
Ro
.

2.2
Towards dynamo magnetic fields

This may take the non-expert reader aback but, even with the strong physical assumptions we have employed, the model presented in §
2.1
is extremely difficult to solve.
One of the reasons is that, currently, there is no numerical code that can efficiently account for magnetic BC (
5
) in ellipsoidal core geometries.
Nonetheless, previous numerical and experimental studies have allowed the identification of a rather general pattern for the flow response to orbital forcings
(e.g.
Vidal et al., 2024
, for a recent review)
.
We briefly describe it below, as it will guide us for the extrapolation of tidal forcing in §
3
.

We first aim to understand the transition from a non-dynamo regime (when magnetic effects were initially negligible) to a dynamo one.
Prior to the establishment of the geomagnetic field, an orbital forcing drives a large-scale flow
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
in an ellipsoid, which is governed by

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\boldsymbol{U}_{0}+2\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}\times\boldsymbol{U}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\nabla P_{0}+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{U}_{0}+\boldsymbol{f}_{P},

(7a)

\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{U}_{0}

\displaystyle=0.

(7b)

This is a laminar flow, sometimes referred to as a Poincaré flow for precession
(
Roberts and Wu, 2011
, e.g.)
, which has a nearly uniform vorticity and can be written in the bulk as
(
Noir and Cébron, 2013
)

\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\simeq\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\epsilon}\times\boldsymbol{r}+\nabla\Psi_{\epsilon},\quad\left.\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\right|_{\partial V}=0,

(8a,b)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\epsilon}
is the fluid rotation vector in the
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}-
frame, and
\nabla\Psi_{\epsilon}
is a shear flow that is non-vanishing only when
a\neq b
,
a\neq c
or
b\neq c
(i.e. when the
cmb
is not spherical).
Note that orbital forcings also sustain other laminar flows, such as mean flows due to weak nonlinear interactions in the boundary layer below the
cmb

(
Busse, 1968
;
Cébron et al., 2021
, e.g.)
.

It turns out that the forced laminar flows are unlikely to sustain dynamo action, because their spatial structures are generally too simple
(e.g.
Tilgner, 1998
, for the Poincaré flow driven by precession)
.
However, turbulent flows departing from
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
could be viable candidates for dynamo action.
Indeed, if we write down the velocity as

\boldsymbol{v}\simeq\boldsymbol{U}_{0}+\boldsymbol{u},\quad|\boldsymbol{u}|\ll|\boldsymbol{U}_{0}|,

(9a,b)

it often exists unstable perturbations
\boldsymbol{u}
that can grow upon
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
with an amplitude
|\boldsymbol{u}|\propto\exp(\sigma t)
in the linear regime (i.e. when
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}
remains negligible), where
\sigma>0
is the growth rate of the unstable flows.
Physically, such hydrodynamic instabilities can result from parametric couplings between the shear component
\nabla\Psi_{\epsilon}
in equation (
8
a) and free waves existing in the core (e.g. inertial waves, whose restoring force is the global rotation of the core).
The growth rate
\sigma
of these unstable flows can be written as

\sigma\simeq\sigma^{i}-\sigma^{d},

(10)

where
\sigma^{i}>0
is the diffusionless growth rate (i.e. when
\nu=\eta=0
), and
\sigma^{d}>0
is the diffusive damping term.
Prior to the existence of a strong planetary magnetic field, the main dissipation mechanism is due to viscous dissipation in the Ekman boundary layer below the
cmb
, such that
\sigma^{d}/\Omega_{s}\sim\mathcal{O}(E^{1/2})
with a numerical pre-factor depending on the spatial complexity of
\boldsymbol{u}

(
Greenspan, 1968
, e.g.)
.
A first necessary condition to reach a dynamo regime is thus given by

\frac{\sigma^{i}}{\Omega_{s}}>\mathcal{O}(E^{1/2}).

(11)

Condition (
) will allow us to estimate when unstable flows could have been triggered by tidal forcing in the Earth’s core.

After their exponential growth, these flows will saturate in amplitude due to a non-vanishing nonlinear term
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}
, and become turbulent.
As outlined in
Vidal et al., 2024
, various regimes of turbulent flows can be expected, leading to different scaling laws for the velocity amplitude
\mathcal{U}\sim|\boldsymbol{u}|
as a function of the control parameters (e.g.
E
and
Ro
).
Therefore, we need to determine the correct scaling law for the turbulence amplitude.

Once this is done, we can move on to dynamo action.
In induction equation (
4
a), the magnetic field can growth over time if the production term
\nabla\times(\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{B})
is much larger than the dissipation term
\eta\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol{B}
.
Using orders of magnitude, this yields the condition that the magnetic Reynolds number
Rm
, given by

Rm=\frac{\mathcal{U}R_{\text{cmb}}}{\eta}

(12)

where
\eta\simeq 1
m
2
.s
-1
is the typical value in the Earth’s core, must be larger than some threshold value to have a growth of the magnetic energy.
In practice, a typical condition
Rm>40-100
is often assumed from convection-driven or precession-driven dynamos in spherical geometries
(
Christensen and Aubert, 2006
;
Cébron et al., 2019
)
, in good agreement with theoretical studies
(
Chen et al., 2018
;
Holdenried-Chernoff et al., 2019
;
Vidal and Cébron, 2021
)
.
Yet, larger onset values have also been reported for orbitally driven dynamos in preliminary simulations
(
Reddy et al., 2018
)
.

Finally, the nonlinear regime of orbitally driven dynamos remains unknown.
Indeed, direct numerical simulations (
dns
) of dynamos in ellipsoids have only be performed using either unrealistic (ad-hoc) numerical approximations that strongly hamper the planetary extrapolation
(
Cébron and Hollerbach, 2014
;
Vidal et al., 2018
)
, or were limited to exponential growth regimes
(
Ivers, 2017
;
Reddy et al., 2018
)
.
Therefore, we must rely on scaling-law arguments to estimate the possible strength of dynamo fields in planetary interiors.
However, there is no consensual dynamo scaling law in the literature for tidal forcing
(
Le Bars et al., 2011
;
Barker and Lithwick, 2014
;
Vidal et al., 2018
)
or precession
(
Cébron et al., 2019
)
.

Finally, it is worth noting that a dynamo scenario can fail at any stage.
For instance, condition (
) may not be fulfilled on long enough time scales to yield turbulent flows
\boldsymbol{u}
, or the resulting turbulence may have not remained vigorous enough to maintain a
Rm
value above the dynamo onset over time.
Similarly, the dynamo magnetic field may be too weak to match the paleomagnetic estimates given in Figure
1
.
Therefore, we will now critically revisit the above steps for tidal forcing in §
3
.

3
Results for tidal forcing

As customary in long-term evolution scenarios for the Earth-Moon system
(
Farhat et al., 2022
, e.g.)
, we consider a simplified model neglecting the effects of Earth’s obliquity, of the Moon’s orbital eccentricity and inclination, and of the small phase lag between the tidal bulge of the Earth and the Moon.
Hence, the Earth’s core is supposed to be instantaneously deformed by the tidal potential into an ellipsoid whose equatorial semi-axes can be written as

\frac{a}{R_{\text{cmb}}}=\sqrt{1+\beta},\quad\frac{b}{R_{\text{cmb}}}=\sqrt{1-\beta},

(13a,b)

where is
R_{\text{cmb}}
the mean radius of the liquid core.
We work in the frame rotating with the Moon at the angular velocity
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\epsilon}=\Omega_{\text{orb}}\boldsymbol{1}_{z}
, and assume that the liquid core is co-rotating with the planet at the angular velocity
\Omega_{s}\boldsymbol{1}_{z}
with respect to the inertial frame.
In fluid-dynamics studies, it is tacitly assumed that the orbital parameters evolve on a time scale (denoted by
\tau
below) that is much longer than that of the turbulent flows.
Consequently,
[\Omega_{\text{orb}}
,
\Omega_{s}]
and the ellipsoidal geometry are always supposed to be constant for the flow dynamics.
This allows performing parametric studies as a function of the different parameters over geological time scales, and obtaining scaling laws for extrapolation to the Earth.

To assess the validity of the tidal scenario, we first estimate the amplitude of
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
in §

Then, we investigate whether this flow can sustain flow instabilities and turbulence over geological time scales by using constraints from hydrodynamics studies in §

Finally, we discuss possible dynamo effects in §

Estimates from geophysical models

3.2.1
Onset of turbulent flows

As outlined in §
2.2
, unstable tidal flows can possibly grow upon the flow
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
with an exponentially increasing amplitude
\propto\exp(\sigma t)
in the initial stage.
The underlying mechanism is that of a sub-harmonic (parametric) instability, known as the elliptical instability
(
Kerswell, 2002
;
Le Bars et al., 2015
)
.
This instability results from couplings between some normal modes
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
sustained by the global rotation of the core, called inertial modes and oscillating with an angular frequency
\omega_{i}
, and the shear component of the forced flow
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
through the linearised term
(\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)\boldsymbol{U}_{0}+(\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
in the momentum equation.

For this instability to exist, the modes and the tidal forcing must satisfy a resonance condition in time given by
(
Vidal and Cébron, 2017
, e.g.)

\omega_{i}\simeq\pm|\Omega_{s}-\Omega_{\text{orb}}|.

(19)

Imperfect resonances can only occur in condition (
) for finite values of the ellipticity
\beta
or the Ekman number
E
.
Hence, the latter should be negligible in the early Earth’s core with
\beta\ll 1
and
E\to 0
.
Moreover, we emphasise that equation (
) is only a necessary condition, because some resonance conditions must also be fulfilled in space to have a non-zero growth rate
\sigma
(not shown here).
Then, theory predicts that
\sigma
is of the form
(
), in which the diffusionless part
\sigma^{i}
is given by
(
Vidal et al., 2019
, e.g.)

\frac{\sigma^{i}}{\Omega_{s}}\lesssim\frac{(2\tilde{\Omega}+3)^{2}}{16(1+\tilde{\Omega})^{2}}|1-\Omega_{0}|\beta

(20)

with
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{\mathrm{orb}}/\Omega_{s}
and
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega_{0}/(1-\Omega_{0})
.
Note that the upper bound in equation (
) is reached when the unstable flows have a large enough spatial complexity
(
Vidal and Cébron, 2017
)
.
Moreover, in the absence of strong magnetic fields, we can also simply estimate the damping term
\sigma^{d}
from the viscous damping
\sigma^{\nu}
of the inertial modes involved in resonance condition (
).
Boundary-layer theory shows that the viscous damping of inertial modes in a rotating ellipsoid is given by

\sigma^{\nu}\simeq\Omega_{s}\left(\sigma^{\nu}_{1/2}\,E^{1/2}+\sigma^{\nu}_{1}\,E\right),

(21)

where
\sigma^{\nu}_{1/2}>0
results from the viscous friction in the Ekman layer below the
cmb

(
Greenspan, 1968
, e.g.)
, and
\sigma^{\nu}_{1}\geq 0
is a bulk contribution
(
Liao et al., 2001
;
Lemasquerier et al., 2017
, e.g.)
.

Equation (
) is illustrated in Figure
6
(a).
Note that the inertial modes can be expressed in terms of polynomial functions of degree
\leq n
in rotating ellipsoids
(
Backus and Rieutord, 2017
;
Colin de Verdière and Vidal, 2025
, e.g.)
, such that they can be computed using dedicated numerical methods
(
Vidal and Colin de Verdière, 2024
, e.g.)
.
We have only shown the modes for which we could expect resonances from condition (
), that is with

|\omega_{i}|\sim 0.96\pm 0.02
for the early Earth according to Figure
5
.
Interestingly, we deduce that no modes of degree
n<7
could be triggered in the early Earth’s core.
Indeed, the first modes that possibly satisfy resonance condition (
) in the early Earth are the two
n=7
modes whose angular frequencies are

\frac{\omega_{i}}{\Omega_{s}}\approx\pm 0.965.

(22)

Among all modes satisfying the resonance condition, the latter
n=7
modes also have the lowest surface and bulk damping contributions given by

\frac{\sigma^{\nu}_{1/2}}{\Omega_{s}}\approx 5.73,\quad\frac{\sigma^{\nu}_{1}}{\Omega_{s}}\approx 261.

(23)

Hence, we can conclude that the damping term had a lower bound in the early Earth given by

{\sigma^{d}}/{\Omega_{s}}\geq 5.73\,E^{1/2}+261\,E.

(24)

This is an improvement with respect to the prior lower bound
{\sigma^{d}}/{\Omega_{s}}\geq 2.62\,E^{1/2}

(
Landeau et al., 2022
, e.g.)
, which corresponds to the damping term of the
n=1

\omega_{i}/\Omega_{s}\approx\pm 1

(
Greenspan, 1968
)
.
However, theory shows that such modes cannot yield an elliptical instability for the
cmb
geometry with
a\geq b\geq c

(
Cébron et al., 2010
)
.
The damping term in equation (
) was thus at least two times larger than previously thought for the early Earth.
Moreover, given the observed trends for
\sigma^{\nu}_{1/2}
and
\sigma^{\nu}_{1}
when
n
is large, we would require
n\gg 1000
to have a bulk contribution larger than the surface one.

Next, we compare
\sigma^{i}
given by equation (
) and
\sigma^{\nu}
in Figure

We have estimated
\sigma^{\nu}
from Figure

(

\frac{\ell}{R_{\text{cmb}}}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\frac{\pi}{n+1/2},

(25)

where

First, we see that small-scale flows (e.g. with

Therefore, only some large-scale flows may have become unstable in the early Earth’s core.
Moreover, we see that the largest-scale modes with

-3.25

These results provides a strong constraint on the age of a possible tidally driven dynamo in the core.
Indeed, we can assert that paleomagnetic measurements younger than

Yet, it remains possible so far that older points could evidence an ancient tidally driven geodynamo because tides may have injected energy into some large-scale unstable flows with

-4.25

-3.25

Linear analysis yields predictions for the time window where turbulent flows may be triggered by elliptical instabilities sustained by tidal forcing.
Now, we have to estimate a typical vigour of the turbulence (denoted by
\mathcal{U}
before), as this quantity will play an important role in the planetary extrapolation.
To do so, we can have a look at simulations of tidally driven flows without magnetic fields.
Actually, several turbulence regimes can be expected.
Weakly nonlinear analysis shows that the normal form of the elliptical instability is that of a supercritical Hopf bifurcation
(
Knobloch et al., 1994
;
Kerswell, 2002
)
.
Hence, the saturation amplitude should scale as
\mathcal{U}\propto\sqrt{\beta-\beta_{c}}
near the onset, where
\beta_{c}
is the critical ellipticity (i.e. such that
\sigma=0
).
On the contrary, we expect the nonlinear amplitude to vary as
\mathcal{U}\propto\beta-\beta_{c}\sim\beta
far enough from the onset according to phenomenological arguments
(
Barker and Lithwick, 2014
)
.

Since we have
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{1}_{z}=0
for tidal forcing, we can estimate
\mathcal{U}
from the axial component
u_{z}
of the velocity.
Indeed, any departure from zero will be associated to tidally driven turbulence and mixing.
We show in Figure

u_{z}
obtained from simulations in ellipsoids with a rigid boundary at
E=1.5\times 10^{-5}
and
|1-\Omega_{\text{orb}}/\Omega_{s}|\approx 1.98

(
Grannan et al., 2017
)
.
We do recover the two expected regimes in the simulations.
The transition is believed to occur when
\beta-\beta_{c}\sim\mathcal{O}(E)
from theory
(
Kerswell, 2002
)
.
This is again in broad agreement with the simulations, but we report here a quite large numerical pre-factor since it occurs at
\beta-\beta_{c}\sim 600\,E
.
The second regime with
\beta\gg\beta_{c}
can be more efficiently probed by relaxing the no-penetration BC in the model.
This can be achieved by using a free-surface condition in an ellipsoid
(
Barker, 2016a
)

\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
in Cartesian periodic boxes
(
Barker and Lithwick, 2013
;
Barker and Lithwick, 2014
)
.
Such simulations, performed for different values of

Although they have been performed for very different parameters, almost all simulations are well reproduced by the linear scaling law
u_{z}\propto(0.25\pm 0.15)\ \beta
in the second regime (see the inset in Figure

To apply these results to the early Earth, we need to estimate how supercritical the early Earth’s core was before
-3.25
Gy.
Going back to Figure
6
(b), we see that super-criticality is never very large (i.e.
\sigma^{i}/\sigma^{\nu}\ll 10
).
We can estimate a lower bound for the critical value of the ellipticity at the onset from equations (
) and (

This yields

\beta_{c}\gtrsim\frac{5.73\,E^{1/2}}{|1-\Omega_{\text{orb}}/\Omega_{s}|}\frac{16(1+\tilde{\Omega})^{2}}{(2\tilde{\Omega}+3)^{2}},

(26)

from which we obtain the typical value
\beta_{c}\geq 2.5\times 10^{-7}
before
-3.25
Gy.
Hence, we deduce that we had at most
\beta/\beta_{c}\lesssim 2
after
-4

Yet, if the transition between the two regimes when

is lowered remains as reported in Figure

\beta-\beta_{c}\gg 600\,E

Therefore, to the best of current knowledge, we assume that the tidally driven velocity amplitude is given in planetary cores by

\mathcal{U}\simeq\alpha_{1}\beta|\Omega_{s}-\Omega_{\text{orb}}|R_{\text{cmb}},\quad\alpha_{1}=0.25\pm 0.15,

(27a,b)

as deduced from the simulations above.

Finally, note that scaling law (

Indeed, the properties of tidally driven turbulence remains largely disputed for planetary conditions
(

In the following, we focus on dynamo action during the late Hadean and early Archean periods, when tidal forcing was stronger to potentially explain the limited paleomagnetic measurements.

Since state-of-the-art

cannot properly investigate dynamo action for realistic

We assume that
Rm-Rm_{c}
was large enough at that time, to render the proposed scaling laws for dynamo action in the vicinity of the onset invalid
(
Fauve and Pétrélis, 2007
, e.g.)
.
A fruitful approach in dynamo theory has been to consider power-based scaling laws
(
Christensen and Tilgner, 2004
;
Christensen and Aubert, 2006
;
Davidson, 2013
)
.
Such laws have been tested against to numerical models (regardless of the parameters), and have provided useful insight into planetary extrapolation.
In such models, it is assumed that the saturated magnetic energy density per unit of mass, which is given by

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{B})=\frac{1}{\rho_{f}V}\int_{V}\frac{\boldsymbol{B}^{2}}{2\mu}\,\mathrm{d}V\sim\frac{1}{2}\frac{B^{2}}{\rho_{f}\mu}

(28)

where
\rho_{f}
is the mean fluid density and
B
is a typical magnetic field strength, should be controlled by the available power per unit of mass for dynamo action
\mathcal{P}_{M}=\mathcal{P}/(\rho_{f}V)
, where
\mathcal{P}
is the mean energy production rate (in W).
The Joule dissipation per unit of mass given by

\epsilon_{\eta}=\frac{1}{\rho_{f}V}\int_{V}\frac{\eta}{\mu}|\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}V\sim\frac{\eta}{\rho_{f}\mu}\frac{B^{2}}{\ell_{B}^{2}},

(29)

where
\ell_{B}\sim 2\eta\,\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{B})/\epsilon_{\eta}
is a magnetic dissipation length scale, is expected to dominate over the viscous dissipation
\epsilon_{\nu}
for a turbulent dynamo at low
Pm
numbers.
Thus, we expect the magnetic energy to stop increasing when the injected power would be statistically balanced by Joule heating (i.e.
\mathcal{P}_{M}\sim\epsilon_{\eta}
).
This simple reasoning yields the power-based scaling law
(
Christensen and Tilgner, 2004
)

B\propto\sqrt{\rho_{f}\mu}\,\left(\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}}{\eta}\epsilon_{\eta}\right)^{1/2}.

(30)

The latter law was found to agree quite well with numerical results
(
Christensen, 2010
;
Oruba and Dormy, 2014
, e.g.)
, but the extrapolation to planets is not easy because
\ell_{B}
is largely unknown for core conditions.
It is often assumed to be some fraction of the core radius
(
Starchenko and Jones, 2002
, e.g.)
, while it could also be non-constant and regime-dependent.
To overcome such a difficulty,
Davidson, 2013
proposed that, in the low-
Ro
turbulence regime characterising the Earth’s core, the magnetic field should solely scale with
\mathcal{P}_{M}
and
R_{\mathrm{cmb}}
.
If so, we obtain from dimensional analysis that

B\propto\sqrt{\rho_{f}\mu}\,\left(R_{\text{cmb}}\mathcal{P}_{M}\right)^{1/3}.

(31)

Plugging typical values for the current Earth’s liquid core (i.e.
\rho_{f}\sim 10^{4}
kg.m
-3
,
\mathcal{P}\sim 1-10
TW) into equation (
) yields
B\sim 1-3
mT, which is a satisfactory upper bound for the current magnetic field amplitude atop the core
(
Gillet et al., 2010
, e.g.)
.
This law generalises previous laws for buoyancy-driven dynamos
(
Christensen et al., 2009
, e.g.)
, which assume that
B
varies with the advected energy flux to the power
1/3
.
As such, scaling law (
) is often a cornerstone for extrapolation to natural dynamos.
Finally, note that only upper bounds can be obtained from scaling laws (
) and (
) for the comparison with paleomagnetic data, since only a fraction of the dynamo field has a dipolar morphology at the
cmb
(the pre-factor is here set to
1
without further knowledge).

Next, could we faithfully use any power-based scaling law for tidally driven dynamos ?
Since there are no dynamo simulations of tidal flows in ellipsoids with which we can compare theoretical predictions, we cannot be assertive.
However, we expect the above power-based arguments to remain largely valid for orbitally driven flows.
To get insight into this, we have re-analysed in Figure

the dynamo simulations of precession-driven flows in a sphere reported in
Cébron et al., 2019

The simulations show that
B\propto\epsilon_{\eta}^{1/2}
when

Moreover, a transition towards between (

\epsilon_{\eta}/(\epsilon_{\eta}+\epsilon_{\nu})\to 1

We have also re-analysed in Figure

(

A good agreement is found with scaling law (

\mathcal{P}_{M}\sim\epsilon_{\nu}+\epsilon_{\eta}
(it was not possible to separate

On the one hand, tidal forcing could sustain a regime of inertial-wave turbulence when
Ro\ll 1
,
(
Le Reun et al., 2017
;
Le Reun et al., 2019
;
Le Reun et al., 2021
)
.
This is a regime of weak turbulence, characterised by weakly nonlinear interactions of three-dimensional inertial waves.
Like in Kolmogorov turbulence, inertial-wave turbulence involves energy being injected at some large scale.
This energy is then transmitted to smaller scales via a direct cascade, in which the input power is balanced by dissipation at every scale, until energy is finally dissipated at sufficiently small scales.
Inertial wave turbulence is described by an anisotropic energy spectrum
(
Galtier, 2003
;
Galtier, 2023
)
, from which we estimate the effective dissipation as

\epsilon_{Ro\ll 1}\sim\frac{\ell_{\parallel}}{\ell_{\perp}}\frac{u_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}^{4}}{\Omega_{s}\ell_{\perp}^{2}}

(32)

with an expected
\mathcal{O}(1)
pre-factor from theory
(
Zhou, 1995
)
, where
u_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}
is the velocity amplitude at length scales
\ell_{\parallel}\geq\ell_{\perp}
(where
\ell_{\parallel}
is the length scale parallel to the rotation axis, and
\ell_{\perp}
is the one perpendicular to it).
Since the dissipation is a constant in the theory, it can be estimated from the knowledge of
u_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}
at some length scales.

Figure
6
(b) shows that tidal forcing can can only inject energy at rather large scales (i.e. at fraction of the core radius denoted by
\alpha_{2}
below), for which we may assume
\ell_{\perp}\sim\ell_{\parallel}\sim\ell
.
Moreover, scaling law (
27

u_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}\lesssim\mathcal{U}
at large scales.
Altogether, this allows us to estimate the mean dissipation in a regime of inertial-wave turbulence as

\epsilon_{\mathrm{tides},\,Ro\ll 1}\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{U}^{4}}{\Omega_{s}\ell^{2}}

(33)

for tidal forcing, with
\ell=\alpha_{2}R_{\mathrm{cmb}}
and
\alpha_{2}\simeq 0.01-1
.
Formula (
) is compatible with an energy spectrum scaling as
\ell^{2}
at large scales
(
Galtier, 2003
)
, which is consistent with prior findings in rotating turbulence
(
Baroud et al., 2002
;
Thiele and Müller, 2009
, e.g.)
.

Note that another predictive law ought to be used for tidally driven turbulence with moderately small values
Ro\lesssim 1
.
The latter range of values is a priori not directly relevant for the early Earth’s core, whose typical value is
Ro\sim 10^{-7}
at
-4

However, tidal forcing can be much stronger with
\beta\to 10^{-2}
in short-period Hot Jupiter
(

(

If so, the turbulence usually exhibits strong nearly two-dimensional (geostrophic) flows
(

;
Le Reun et al., 2020

(

;

)

\epsilon_{Ro\lesssim 1}\sim\frac{u^{3}_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}}{\ell_{\perp}}\quad\text{or}\quad\epsilon_{Ro\lesssim 1}\sim\frac{u^{3}_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}}{\ell_{\parallel}}.

(34a,b)

We see that we recover from the two formulas the usual Kolmogorov prediction
\epsilon\sim u^{3}_{\ell}/\ell
for homogeneous isotropic turbulence when
l_{\perp}=l_{\parallel}=\ell
.
Using the same reasoning as before, we assume that
u_{\ell_{\parallel},\ell_{\perp}}\lesssim\mathcal{U}
and
\ell_{\perp}\sim\ell_{\parallel}\sim\ell
at large scales, such that the two laws should reduce at large scales to

\epsilon_{\mathrm{tides},\,Ro\lesssim 1}\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{U}^{3}}{\ell},

(35)

with
\ell=\alpha_{2}R_{\mathrm{cmb}}
and
\alpha_{2}=0.01-1
.
This dissipation law is compatible with simulations performed at
Ro\gtrsim 10^{-2}

(

Barker, 2016a
)
, and has also been proposed for some stellar applications
(

;
Vidal et al., 2019
)
.

Finally, we combine equations (

B\propto\sqrt{\rho_{f}\mu}\begin{cases}\alpha_{2}^{-1/3}\,\mathcal{U}&\text{if}\quad Ro\lesssim 1\\

\end{cases}

(36)

for tidal forcing in the early core.
Note that the magnetic field amplitude is expected to be

smaller when

\end{cases}.

(37)

Hence, only weak-field dynamos with

4
Geophysical discussion

As summarised in Figure
11
, tidal forcing was probably not strong enough to generate a dynamo magnetic field with an amplitude matching the (scarce) Hadean and Archean paleomagnetic measurements, at least for flows in a regime of weak wave turbulence.
Note that several modelling assumptions were made to arrive at this rather pessimistic conclusion.
We discuss them below, in order to assess how our main results could be modified or not.

4.1
Scaling-law uncertainties

The tidal scenario heavily relies on different scaling laws, which must be used for planetary extrapolation.
We have done our best to constrain the various scaling laws as much as possible, by uniquely combining Earth-Moon evolution scenarios, theoretical results, and re-analyses of the most up-to-date numerical simulations of tidally driven turbulent flows.
This has allowed us to find that the preliminary predictions presented in
Landeau et al., 2022
are actually overestimated (as we have used the same modelling assumptions here).
This mainly results from the chosen numerical pre-factors in the different extrapolation laws, which were set to unity for a first proof-of-concept study.

In particular, we have shown that
\alpha_{1}=1
in velocity scaling law (
27
) disagrees with the available numerical results gathered in Figure
7
, suggesting instead that
\alpha_{1}\simeq 0.25\pm 0.15
.
The latter values should thus be used for quantitative planetary extrapolation from now on, but additional research work may shed new light on this question.
Indeed, whether the value of
\alpha_{1}
could be increased or not when
E
is lowered (and similarly
\beta
) remains unknown but, heuristically, we always expect
\alpha_{1}<1
to sustain tidally driven turbulence in the core over time.
Otherwise, tidally driven turbulent flows would become of the same amplitude as the shear component of the forced flow
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
when
\alpha_{1}\to 1
, which would stop the energy injection responsible for the turbulence.
This would echo some peculiar regimes that are sometimes observed in rotating turbulence
(
McEwan, 1970
;
Malkus, 1989
, involving growth and collapse phases, e.g.)
, which occur when the forcing amplitude is so large than the rotation of the fluid can be (locally) cancelled out by some unstable flows.

Other modelling uncertainties also call the dynamo extrapolation into question.
In particular, the dissipation law is a flaw of the scenario.
We have assumed that tidal forcing establishes a regime of inertial-wave turbulence, as often postulated after
Le Reun et al., 2017
;
Le Reun et al., 2019
;
Le Reun et al., 2021
.
However, inertial-wave turbulence is a research topic that is far from being well understood, especially because it is very challenging to obtain in simulations or in laboratory experiments.
As such, current investigations still strive observing predictions of wave-turbulence theory in set-ups mimicking as much as possible the theoretical model
(
Yarom and Sharon, 2014
;
Monsalve et al., 2020
, e.g.)
.
Therefore, it is still unclear whether the quantitative predictions of wave turbulence theory could be directly applied to rotating turbulent flows in bounded geometries at
Ro\ll 1

(
Vidal et al., 2024
, see a discussion in)
, such as in the early Earth’s core with
Ro\sim 10^{-7}
.
For instance, rotating turbulence at
Ro\ll 1
may be instead in a regime of geostrophic turbulence characterised by the presence nearly two-dimensional (geostrophic) flows, which would challenge the applicability of wave-turbulence theory when
Ro\ll 1

(
Gallet, 2015
, e.g.)
.
On the contrary, simulations at moderate values
Ro\sim 10^{-2}

(
Barker and Lithwick, 2013
;
Barker and Lithwick, 2014
;
Barker, 2016a
)
seem broadly compatible with other dissipation law (
), which may yield a much stronger amplitude if it were valid for the Earth.

Obviously, the lack of numerical methods capable of simulating self-sustained dynamos in ellipsoids hampers our ability to assess the validity of dynamo scaling law (
).
For instance, tidally driven dynamos may not operate not in a regime of inertial-wave turbulence but, instead, in a regime of magnetostrophy when
Ro\ll 1
.
In such a scenario, the Coriolis force could balance the Lorentz force such that
B
would be given by
(
Christensen, 2010
, e.g.)

B\propto\sqrt{\rho_{f}\mu}\sqrt{l_{B}\Omega_{s}\mathcal{U}}.

(38)

Formula (
) would then give the field estimate
B\lesssim 5
mT in the early Earth’s core at
-4
Gy (using the numerical values given in Table
1
).
A magnetostrophic regime would therefore seem to be a better candidate than inertial-wave turbulence to yield large field values but, so far, there is no evidence that magnetostrophy could be achieved with tidal forcing.

4.2
Archean background magnetic field ?

We have explored the possibility of explaining the onset of dynamo magnetic fields in the core from hydrodynamic flows driven by tidal forcing.
This is a valid assumption during the Hadean era, since it is likely that the Earth was not subject to a strong ambient magnetic field before dynamo action began in the core.
However, the paleomagnetic measurements clearly show that the Earth’s core had a magnetic field since at least
-3.5
Gy (whatever its dynamical origin).
Hence, could the presence of a background magnetic field (possibly of different origin) alter the behaviour of tidally driven flows within the core during the Archean era ?
Actually, ambient magnetic fields can have competing effects on tidally driven flows.

First, the elliptical instability mechanism is formally unchanged in the presence of a background field
(
Kerswell, 1994
;
Vidal et al., 2019
, e.g.)
, but the waves involved in resonance condition (
) may be modified or of a different nature.
To quantify magnetic effects on wave structures in the core, we introduce the Lehnert number defined as

Le=\frac{B_{\mathrm{cmb}}}{\sqrt{\rho_{f}\mu}\,\Omega_{s}R_{\mathrm{cmb}}}.

(39)

A typical value is
Le\sim 10^{-4}-10^{-3}
in the early core, which is similar to the current value in the Earth’s core.
As sketched in Figure
12
, the wave spectrum in an ellipsoidal core is separated in several parts when
Le\ll 1
.
On the one hand, high-frequency waves remain almost inertial in nature, because they are barely affected by the magnetic field in the absence of an inner core.
This is a noticeable difference with the shell geometry, in which even the high-frequency waves can be modified by magnetic effects
(
Lin and Ogilvie, 2018
)
.
On the other hand, the originally low-frequency inertial waves morph into various low-frequency waves shaped by the magnetic field, such as torsional Alfvén waves
(
Luo and Jackson, 2022
, e.g.)
and magneto-Coriolis waves
(
Luo et al., 2022
;
Gerick and Livermore, 2024
, e.g.)
.
Given the expected frequency
|\Omega_{s}-\Omega_{\mathrm{orb}}|
of tidal forcing in the early core, the injection of energy can only come from resonances with high-frequency inertial waves in resonance condition (
).
Then, the diffusionless growth rate
\sigma^{i}
of the elliptical instability, given in equation (
), will remained unchanged at the leading order in
Le
.
However, Ohmic diffusion below the
cmb
will provide an additional magnetic damping term
\sigma^{\eta}
in growth rate equation (
).

Kerswell, 1994
showed that the magnetic damping
\sigma^{\eta}
and the viscous one
\sigma^{\nu}
are probably of comparable order of magnitude for plausible core fields.
Therefore, the effective damping term in Figure
6
may increase by a factor of (up to) two.
If an ambient field of a different origin was present in the early Earth’s core, tidal forcing may not have been strong enough throughout most of the Archean period to sustain turbulence.

Next, could magnetic effects also modify the tidally driven turbulence in the core ?
Much less is known about the possible nonlinear interactions between magnetic fields and tidally driven turbulence.
Since high-frequency inertial waves are barely affected by magnetic fields, we could expect inertial-wave turbulence to persist in the presence of an ambient magnetic field.
This may qualitatively agree with pioneering magnetohydrodynamics simulations for tides
(
Barker and Lithwick, 2014
;
Vidal et al., 2018
)
and precession
(
Barker, 2016b
;
Kumar et al., 2024
)
, showing that the obtained turbulence can be largely unchanged if magnetic effects are rather weak.
Yet, for small
Pm
values, the dissipation would occur on a diffusive magnetic length scale in law (
), and the width of the wave-turbulence range would also be reduced compared to the case without a background field.
However, a regime of geostrophic turbulence when
Ro\ll 1
would not survive in the presence of magnetic field, as it should morph into a magnetostrophic regime (as discussed above).

4.3
Rheological uncertainties

The viscosity value appears to be important for determining the age of tidally driven turbulence in Figure
6
, because it controls the viscous damping
\sigma^{\nu}
.
We have chosen the standard value
\nu=10^{-6}
m
2
.s
-1
, but the core value may be between
10^{-7}
m
2
.s
-1
and
3\times 10^{-6}
m
2
.s
-1

(
Mineev and Funtikov, 2004
)
.
Thus, the viscous damping may be decreased by a factor of
3
with the lowest value, or increased by almost a factor of
2
with the largest one.
Similar effects could be obtained if the the liquid core were rotating in the bulk faster or slower than the mantle.
We have here assumed that the liquid core is co-rotating with the mantle at the angular velocity
\Omega_{s}
, but the core may be rotating a bit faster than the mantle according to some tidal models
(
Wahr et al., 1981
, e.g.)
.
Our model would then remain largely unchanged, except that the value of
E
would be smaller as it must be based on the fluid rotation
(
Greenspan, 1968
, see in)
.

The rheology of the overlying mantle could also play an important role in the core dissipation.
We have assumed that the mantle remained rigid over time, which is reasonable throughout most the Earth’s history.
Yet, the Earth’s mantle was probably molten after the giant impact that formed the Moon
(
Nakajima and Stevenson, 2015
, at least partially, e.g.)
, and a crystallising basal magma ocean (
bmo
) probably survived in the aftermath for millions of years to a billion of years
(
Labrosse et al., 2007
;
Boukaré et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.
How such a
bmo
could have interacted with tidally driven core flows remains unknown.
A crude extrapolation of old fluid-dynamics experiments on the spin-up of rotating immiscible fluids
(
Pedlosky, 1967
;
O’Donnell and Linden, 1992
)
might suggest that
cmb
dissipation is not significantly reduced in the presence of a low-viscosity
bmo
(due to interfacial friction with the core, and Ekman friction with the solid mantle above).
However, further work of the fluid-dynamics community is needed to elucidate this question.

Finally, we have also neglected possible interactions between tidal forcing and buoyancy effects in the core, such as with either convective flows or density stratification.
Convection would essentially sustain slowly varying flows in the core, whereas tidal forcing may mainly trigger high-frequency motions with inertial-wave turbulence.
Because of this separation of time scales, strong interactions are not expected between both flows

(
de Vries et al., 2023a
;
de Vries et al., 2023b
, unless convective flows could locally cancel out the rotation of the core, e.g.)
.
Convection would then provide an additional damping, but the latter might be rather weak for fast tidal forcing
(
Duguid et al., 2020a
;
Duguid et al., 2020b
;
Vidal and Barker, 2020a
;
Vidal and Barker, 2020b
, e.g.)
.
Note that the early core may have been instead (at least partially) stably stratified in density, according to thermal evolution modelling
(
Labrosse, 2015
, only for large values of the thermal conductivity of liquid iron at core conditions, e.g.)
or if the early core had been insufficiently mixed after giant impacts
(
Landeau et al., 2016
)
.
In this case, density stratification would not modify the largest growth rate of the elliptical instability
\sigma^{i}

(
Vidal et al., 2019
)
.
Similarly, boundary-layer theory suggests that the value of the damping rate
\sigma^{\nu}
would not change much with stratification
(
Friedlander, 1989
)
, such that the predictions shown in Figure
6
(b) may remain largely valid.
However, preliminary simulations show that density stratification could significantly weaken the strength of radial flows and mixing
(
Vidal et al., 2018
)
.
We may thus expect dynamo action to be less favourable with stratification, but further work is needed to carefully investigate the interplay with density stratification.

5
Concluding remarks

5.1
Summary

We have thoroughly explored the capability of tidal forcing to explain the ancient geodynamo.
We have combined geophysical constraints from Earth-Moon evolution models, theoretical predictions, and re-analyses of recent results on tidally driven turbulent flows.
This has allowed us to show that tidal forcing was likely strong enough to sustain turbulence prior to
-3.25
Gy, and possibly dynamo action.
However, the self-sustained magnetic field was certainly too weak to explain paleomagnetic measurements if tides were solely sustaining an inertial-wave turbulence regime in the ancient core.
We hope that our results will guide future studies of tidally driven core flows, to possibly strive beyond the limits we have identified.
For example, we have outlined that magnetostrophic turbulence driven by tidal forcing might produce a strong-field dynamo regime consistent with the observations. However, strong evidence that tides could effectively sustain such a regime remains to be found.

Alternatively, other mechanisms have been proposed to explain the ancient geodynamo
(
Landeau et al., 2022
, see in)
, notably thermal convection alone (depending of the value of thermal conductivity of liquid iron at core conditions, which is very disputed) or double-diffusive convection driven by exsolution (or precipitation) of light elements below the
cmb
.
Yet, a difficulty with such mechanisms may be to obtain vigorous enough turbulence
(
Monville et al., 2019
, e.g.)
, or to sustain magnetic fields with a dipolar morphology when starting from almost hydrodynamic flows
(
Burmann et al., 2025
, e.g.)
.
Actually, a weak tidally driven dynamo might have been important in providing an optimal magnetic seed of finite amplitude to kickstart an efficient convection-driven geodynamo
(
Cattaneo and Hughes, 2022
)
.

5.2
Towards precession in the Moon ?

To finish with, we come back to another geological motivation of our work, that is the puzzling origin of early Moon’s dynamo.
Indeed, the core field amplitudes inferred from paleomagnetic measurements in Figure

(b) put very tough constraints on any dynamo scenario (given the small size of the lunar core).
It has been proposed that the ancient Moon’s dynamo could result from precession-driven flows
(
Dwyer et al., 2011
, e.g.)
, which have many similarities with tidal flows
(
Vidal et al., 2024
, e.g.)
.
The
géodynamo
team and its collaborators have worked on precession for a long time, making pioneering contributions to the hydrodynamics
(
Noir et al., 2001
;
Lin et al., 2015
, e.g.)
and magnetohydrodynamics
(
Lin et al., 2016
;
Cébron et al., 2019
)
of such flows.
The present work, at the crossroad of the
géodynamo
’s research activities, may thus also guide future studies of precession-driven flows.

Different phenomenological laws have been proposed for the velocity amplitude
(
Barker, 2016b
;
Vidal and Cébron, 2023
)
.
Yet, as for tidal forcing, the characteristics of turbulent flows and dynamos are still largely unknown for precession.
For instance, we have not been able to explain how
\epsilon_{\nu}
and
\epsilon_{\eta}
vary with the control parameters in the simulations shown in Figure
9
.
Further work is thus needed to understand the characteristics of precession-driven turbulent flows, even without dynamo action.
Indeed, extrapolation to the Moon suggests that precession may be vigorous enough to sustain dynamo action billion years ago, with a magnetic Reynolds number
Re\geq 100

(
Vidal and Cébron, 2023
)
.
We should thus revisit these predictions in the light of new simulations and recent constraints on lunar precession, before we can estimate a typical field strength.
