Clinical Outcomes of Rh(D)-Incompatible Allogeneic Haematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: A Single-Centre Retrospective Case Series with a Narrative Literature Review
Authors: Miaomiao Liu, Min Lin, Weijia Fu, Ziwei Wang, Lili Xu, Jie Chen, Jianmin Yang, Haihui Gu, Xiong Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRh(D)-negative individuals are relatively rare in the Chinese population, and clinical experience with Rh(D)-incompatible allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) remains limited. Compared with ABO incompatibility, the immunohaematological consequences of Rh(D) mismatch in allo-HSCT are less well defined. Herein, we report a single-centre retrospective case series of four patients receiving Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT at our institution, including two Rh(D)-negative recipients with Rh(D)-positive donors and two Rh(D)-positive recipients with Rh(D)-negative donors. We systematically evaluated post-transplant transfusion strategies, haematological recovery, anti-D alloantibody seroconversion, haemolysis or graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), and long-term survival outcomes. During a median follow-up of 36 months (range, 24–48 months), none of the patients developed detectable anti-D alloantibodies. Only one patient exhibited laboratory evidence of subclinical haemolysis responsive to low-dose prednisone, and another patient developed grade II acute gastrointestinal GVHD controlled by immunosuppressive agents. Nevertheless, all patients achieved sustained haematological engraftment without transplant-related mortalities. Combined with data from a narrative literature review, our single-centre observations are consistent with prior studies showing low risks of clinically significant haemolysis and anti-D alloimmunisation after Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT. Rh(D) mismatch does not appear to be associated with severe transplant complications in our limited cohort, but these findings cannot be generalised broadly given the extremely small sample size. Rigorous peri-transplant transfusion management and regular post-transplant serological monitoring are still mandatory for Rh(D)-mismatched recipients.

Introduction

The ABO and Rh blood group antigens are inherited independently of the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system.1 Although with respect to ABO and Rh antigens, compatibility between donor and recipient is not essential for allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT),2 mismatches in blood group antigens are common in allo-HSCT and may lead to immunohaematological complications, including haemolytic anaemia, delayed red blood cell engraftment, and pure red cell aplasia.3 However, whereas extensive research has contributed to establishing the clinical implications of ABO incompatibility in allo-HSCT, the effects of Rh(D) incompatibility remain less well defined.3–5 Globally, the incidence of Rh(D)-negative phenotype varies drastically by ethnicity; only 0.2% to 2.5% of Chinese Han individuals carry Rh(D)-negative blood type, rendering Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT an uncommon clinical phenomenon in China.6 Consequently, there is currently limited clinical experience and outcome data regarding such transplantations.

Allo-HSCT is a definitive curative therapy for a wide range of malignant and non-malignant haematological disorders, with more than 80,000 procedures performed each year globally and over 20,000 annual cases registered with the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT).7 In recent years, China has become the world’s largest allo-HSCT-performing country, with a rapidly expanding transplant volume dominated by haploidentical donor grafts, which substantially increases the probability of unplanned donor–recipient RBC antigen mismatches.8,9 Notably, the distribution of the Rh(D)-negative phenotype exhibits striking ethnic disparity: only 0.2–2.5% of Chinese Han individuals carry the Rh(D)-negative blood type, in sharp contrast to 15–17% of Caucasian populations.10,11 This low prevalence renders Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT a rare clinical scenario in China, resulting in extremely limited single-centre experience, scarce real-world outcome data, and a lack of population-specific transfusion optimisation strategies for this patient subgroup.12 Rh(D) mismatches in allo-HSCT are classified into two clinically distinct categories based on antigen disparity direction: major Rh(D) mismatch, defined as RhD-negative recipients receiving RhD-positive donor grafts, and minor Rh(D) mismatch, defined as RhD-positive recipients receiving RhD-negative donor grafts.13 Unlike naturally occurring anti-A/anti-B isoagglutinins, anti-D alloantibodies only arise after sensitisation by exogenous Rh(D)-positive red blood cells via transfusion or pregnancy, with long-term persistence after seroconversion. This unique immunological mechanism creates distinct clinical risks compared with ABO incompatibility, requiring separate clinical monitoring algorithms.

Despite these unique clinical risks, current international allo-HSCT transfusion guidelines (including EBMT and BCSH guidelines) primarily focus on standardised management for ABO-mismatched transplantation, with only generalised, low-evidence recommendations for Rh(D) antigen incompatibility, lacking targeted, stratified strategies for major versus minor Rh(D) mismatch.14,15 Existing published studies report highly inconsistent incidences of de novo anti-D alloimmunisation in Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT recipients: previous Western cohorts have documented anti-D seroconversion rates ranging from 10% to 15%, particularly among patients receiving reduced-intensity conditioning, while other comparable studies have reported near-zero anti-D conversion rates under similar immunosuppressive backgrounds.16 Critically, these conflicting Western data cannot be directly extrapolated to Chinese populations due to ethnic differences in Rh antigen distribution, disparities in blood resource availability, and distinct domestic allo-HSCT graft patterns and conditioning regimens. Furthermore, no consistent Chinese real-world evidence has clarified the actual incidence of anti-D alloimmunisation, haemolytic complication risks, and long-term transplant outcomes in Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT patients.17,18 Additionally, few studies have systematically stratified clinical outcomes and transfusion strategies according to the two directional types of Rh(D) mismatch, creating a critical knowledge gap in targeted clinical guidance for domestic practice.

Herein, we comprehensively describe the clinical courses, transfusion management regimens, serological changes, and transplant prognoses of four patients with Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT, integrated with a systematic literature review. Our findings aim to supplement the real-world evidence for Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT in China, resolve existing clinical controversies regarding anti-D alloimmunisation risk and transfusion decision-making, and provide practical, population-specific references for optimising transfusion support and monitoring strategies in domestic Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT practice.

Patients and Transplant Characteristics

Study design, case identification, and literature review methodology.

This is a single-centre retrospective descriptive case series that enrolled consecutive patients who underwent Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT at Changhai Hospital, Shanghai, between January 2018 and December 2022.

Case inclusion criteria: (1) allo-HSCT with confirmed donor-recipient Rh(D) antigen mismatch; (2) complete clinical, laboratory and transfusion records available. Exclusion criteria: incomplete serological follow-up data, lost to follow-up within 12 months post-transplant. No formal inferential statistical analysis was performed due to the tiny sample size and descriptive nature of the study. For the narrative literature review referenced in this manuscript: electronic databases including PubMed, Embase, CNKI and Wanfang were searched from database inception to March 2025. Search terms included combinations: “Rh(D) incompatible”, “D mismatch”, “allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation”, “anti-D alloantibody”, “haemolysis”, “graft-versus-host disease”. Inclusion criteria: original human clinical studies reporting Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT outcomes; full-text available. Exclusion criteria: animal experiments, single-patient case reports, review articles without original data, studies focusing solely on ABO incompatibility without Rh(D) subgroup analysis. All clinical procedures performed in this study were strictly in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent for transplantation was obtained from all enrolled patients. Separate written consent for the publication of anonymised clinical data was acquired before manuscript preparation. In accordance with the regulations of the local institutional review board, this study was a retrospective analysis of de-identified case series without any interventional modifications to routine clinical practice, and therefore formal approval from the ethics committee was waived. All identifiable patient information was removed before data analysis to fully protect patient privacy and information security.

We retrospectively analysed the cases of four patients who underwent Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT at Changhai Hospital, Shanghai, China, between January 2018 and December 2022. Among them, there were two Rh(D)-negative recipients with Rh(D)-positive donors and two Rh(D)-positive recipients with Rh(D)-negative donors. All procedures followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all included patients. Additionally, written consent for publication of the related clinical data was obtained from each patient, and this report adheres to the CARE guidelines.

For the screening of anti-D antigen antibodies, indirect agglutination test (IAT) is conducted monthly within the first 12 months after transplantation, and then once every 3 months until the end of the follow-up. Direct agglutination test (DAT) is carried out simultaneously with each antibody screening. Routine laboratory tests for haemolysis monitoring include haemoglobin, reticulocyte percentage, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total bilirubin, and globulin. These tests are conducted once a week in the first three months after transplantation, and then changed to once a month thereafter. Subclinical haemolysis is defined as persistent anaemia accompanied by elevated reticulocytes and elevated LDH, but without clinical jaundice or organ dysfunction.

Rh(D)-Positive Donors and Rh(D)-Negative Recipients

Case 1

A 46-year-old woman was diagnosed with severe aplastic anaemia and had responded poorly to immunosuppressive therapy with cyclosporin A and supportive transfusions. She underwent allo-HSCT following a conditioning regimen of fludarabine, cyclophosphamide, and anti-thymocyte globulin. She was blood group A Rh(D)-negative and received peripheral blood stem cells from her HLA-identical brother [blood group A Rh(D)-positive]. GVHD prophylaxis consisted of cyclosporin A and short-course methotrexate. Post-transplantation transfusion support included 9 units of leukoreduced Rh(D)-negative red blood cells and 3 units of leukoreduced apheresis platelets. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment were achieved on day +17. Persistent severe anaemia (nadir haemoglobin 40 g/L, reference range 110–150 g/L) accompanied by elevated reticulocytes 4.2% (reference range 0.5–1.5%) and increased LDH supported the diagnosis of subclinical haemolysis. Low-dose oral prednisone was initiated, with gradual haemoglobin recovery observed over 4 weeks. Bone marrow examinations at 90, 180, and 360 days post-transplantation revealed a sustained haematopoietic recovery. During a 48-month follow-up period, serial IAT and DAT testing remained negative for anti-D antibodies, with no episodes of clinical haemolysis.

Case 2

A 21-year-old man with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia in first complete remission underwent myeloablative allo-HSCT using a busulfan and cyclophosphamide conditioning regimen. He was blood group B Rh(D)-negative and received peripheral blood stem cells from his HLA-identical brother [blood group B Rh(D)-positive]. GVHD prophylaxis included cyclosporin A, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil. Haematopoietic engraftment was achieved without complications. Only 3 units of apheresis platelets were transfused for transient thrombocytopenia. No acute or chronic GVHD developed, and leukaemia complete remission was maintained until last follow-up.

Rh(D)-Negative Donors and Rh(D)-Positive Recipients

Case 3

A 46-year-old male acute myeloid leukaemia patient with CEBPA, NRAS, and TET2 mutations achieved complete remission after induction and consolidation chemotherapy. He subsequently underwent allo-HSCT following a modified busulfan and cyclophosphamide conditioning regimen. He was blood group A Rh(D)-positive and received combined bone marrow and peripheral blood stem cells from his HLA-haploidentical sister [5/10 match, blood group A Rh(D)-negative]. GVHD prophylaxis comprised cyclosporin A, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil. Post-transplantation transfusion support included two units of Rh(D)-positive leukoreduced red blood cells and three units of platelets. Haematopoietic recovery was uneventful, and bone marrow evaluations on days 30, 60, and 90 post-transplantation confirmed a sustained complete remission. Over 24 months of follow-up, all anti-D screening and haemolysis markers were within normal limits.

Case 4

A 45-year-old female acute myeloid leukaemia patient with NRAS, DNMT3A, IDH1, BCOP, and STAG2 mutations achieved morphological complete remission with persistent low-level pre-transplant minimal residual disease. Pre-transplant desensitisation with plasma exchange and rituximab was implemented due to detectable donor-specific HLA antibodies, followed by modified busulfan-cyclophosphamide conditioning. The patient was blood group O Rh(D)-positive and received combined bone marrow and peripheral blood stem cells from her HLA-haploidentical daughter [6/10 match, blood group O Rh(D)-negative]. GVHD prophylaxis included cyclosporin A, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil. Post-transplantation transfusion support consisted of four units of leukoreduced Rh(D)-positive red blood cells and three units of leukoreduced platelets. Neutrophil and platelet engraftment occurred on day +13. The patient developed grade II upper gastrointestinal acute GVHD, fully resolved with high-dose corticosteroids plus tacrolimus switching. Bone marrow examination confirmed persistent leukaemia remission. Thirty-six months of continuous follow-up documented negative anti-D serology and normal haemolysis biomarkers at all timepoints.

Discussion

Rh(D) incompatibility in allo-HSCT is immunologically distinct from ABO incompatibility, primarily with respect to the fact that anti-D alloantibodies are not naturally occurring and require preliminary immunisation via exposure to Rh(D)-positive red blood cells (eg, through transfusion or pregnancy).1,19,20 Having developed, these anti-D antibodies tend to persist and rarely disappear spontaneously.21,22 Accordingly, Rh(D) incompatibility in allo-HSCT differs from ABO incompatibility in terms of both immunohaematological mechanisms and clinical consequences.23,24

Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT can be divided into two immunologically distinct subgroups with divergent alloimmunisation risks. In the setting of a D+ donor to a D- recipient, recipient plasma lacks pre-existing anti-D antibodies; the primary risk is transient subclinical haemolysis from pre-transplant recipient sensitisation, with minimal risk of de novo donor-derived anti-D production. For a D- donor to a D+ recipient, donor mature B lymphocytes may mount anti-D responses against residual recipient RhD-positive red blood cells, identified as the higher-risk subgroup in prior cohorts with reported anti-D conversion rates of 10%–15%, especially after reduced-intensity conditioning regimens. However, multiple other retrospective studies have reported extremely low or zero anti-D seroconversion in this subgroup, consistent with our four-patient series.16,19,24–27

None of our four patients developed detectable anti-D alloantibodies during long-term follow-up, even among the two high-risk D- donor → D+ recipient cases. We hypothesise two key mechanisms suppressing anti-D alloimmunisation in this cohort: first, intensive pre-transplant myeloablative/reduced-intensity conditioning induces profound lymphodepletion, eliminating recipient residual memory B cells; second, standard post-transplant triple immunosuppressive prophylaxis (cyclosporin A, methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil) persistently inhibits donor B-cell activation and antibody secretion against recipient RhD antigens, Only Case 1 (D+ donor → D- recipient) presented mild subclinical haemolysis, fully resolved with low-dose corticosteroids, without any severe clinical haemolytic anaemia across all patients. This single-centre observation aligns with global literature reporting a low incidence of clinically significant haemolysis after Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT. In our limited case series, all patients achieved durable full donor engraftment, only one grade II acute gastrointestinal GVHD occurred, and zero transplant-related deaths were recorded. Importantly, we emphasise that these favourable safety outcomes are only observed within our four patients and cannot be generalised to all Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT populations; broader multicentre data are required to validate prognostic effects of Rh(D) disparity.

Current clinical guidelines regarding transfusion management focus primarily on ABO incompatibility in allo-HSCT, with limited specific recommendations for cases of Rh(D) mismatch.

Combined with our laboratory monitoring and transfusion experience, we put forward recommended peri-transplant strategies for Chinese Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT recipients: (1) Stem cell graft manipulation: Removal of red blood cells or plasma from donor grafts is unnecessary given the lack of natural anti-D antibodies. (2) Red blood cell transfusion stratification:D-negative recipients receiving D-positive grafts should receive RhD-negative red cell transfusions until complete donor blood group conversion; D-positive recipients receiving D-negative grafts should preferably receive RhD-negative red blood cells, and RhD-positive red blood cells may be administered under emergency circumstances with weekly haemolysis biomarker surveillance. (3) Platelet support: RhD-matched platelet products are not mandatory, with regular haemolysis laboratory monitoring sufficient for risk control.12,13,28

The present study does have certain limitations, notably its small sample size (four cases) and single-centre retrospective design; the observed zero anti-D conversion rate cannot be extrapolated to all Chinese Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT recipients. The study is also limited to a certain extent by the lack of long-term dynamic monitoring of anti-D antibody titres, and future studies should ideally incorporate this procedure to ensure a more comprehensive evaluation of transplant outcomes. Heterogeneous confounding factors exist across cases, including variable conditioning intensity, sibling-matched versus haploidentical HLA compatibility, and different primary haematological malignancies; subgroup statistical comparison is impossible due to limited case numbers. The narrative literature review did not conduct formal quantitative meta-analysis, limiting pooled effect size estimation of anti-D alloimmunisation incidence globally.

Future large-scale, multicentre prospective Chinese cohorts are warranted to clarify the prognostic impact of Rh(D) incompatibility and optimise transfusion support strategies for recipients undergoing Rh(D)-mismatched allo-HSCT.

Our single-centre retrospective four-case series combined with a structured narrative literature review shows that Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT is well tolerated within this small Chinese patient cohort. No anti-D alloantibodies were detected during long-term follow-up, and only one mild self-limited subclinical haemolytic episode occurred, with no transplant-related mortality. These observations are consistent with previously published international data indicating low risks of severe haemolysis and anti-D alloimmunisation after Rh(D)-mismatched transplantation. However, given the extremely limited sample size, we cannot conclude that Rh(D) incompatibility universally exerts no adverse impact on transplant outcomes. Strict standardised transfusion support and scheduled serial anti-D antibody and haemolysis laboratory monitoring remain essential clinical practice for all Rh(D)-incompatible allo-HSCT recipients.
