Ramsey-Theoretic Characterizations of Classically Non-Ramseyian Problems
Bryce Alan Christopherson

Abstract
In this paper, we will develop a significantly more general notion of classical Ramsey numbers (extending most other graph-theoretic generalizations) and make some preliminary characterizations of these new Ramsey numbers using simple algebraic tools. Throughout, we make a case arguing that, while our access to specific values of Ramsey numbers (or, in general, precise numerical solutions to Ramsey-theoretic problems) may be limited, the interplay between and overall structure of Ramseyian objects is likely tractable. To support the relevancy of this perspective, we conclude by demonstrating that the Green-Tao Theorem, the Twin Prime conjecture, Zhang’s bounded prime gap theorem, and Polignac’s conjecture can be viewed as statements about Ramsey numbers.

Introduction

Ramsey theory addresses the mathematical phenomenon which seems to dictate that the
local
structure of certain ordered collections of objects often necessarily becomes more regular beyond a certain bifurcation point, provided there is a suitable degree of
global
structure present in the ordered collection of objects themselves. Usually, a generic Ramsey-theoretic problem seeks to determine the precise value at which this bifurcation occurs. There are many notions of Ramsey properties (see, e.g.,
[

Definition 1
(Classical Ramsey Numbers)

Let

denote the undirected complete graph on

be positive integers. The Ramsey number

is the smallest

There are a number of different notions extending and generalizing classical Ramsey numbers

This generalization, though moderately cumbersome to work with and perhaps not ideal in its technical formulation, does offer the useful ability to ‘see everything at once’ in a single context. Instead of proposing this generalization as a good way to approach these problems, its purpose is instead to allow us a better vantage to take stock of them, see their essential structure, and gain some insight as to what a ‘correct’ overarching formulation might be.

We will approach things using some basic tools to convert the general problem into a digestible algebraic one that more clearly allows us to ascertain the degree and nature of the difficulties that can arise. The approach, started in

2
Preliminary Work

In this section, we will develop an alternative formulation of classical Ramsey numbers, as well as a generalization of classical Ramsey numbers which emerges naturally in this context. To begin, we define a graph coloring.

Definition 2
(Graph Coloring)

Let
G
be a finite, undirected graph and let
E(G)
denote the edge set of
G
. Given a set
A
of size
m

\rho:E(G)\rightarrow A

m
-edge-coloring of

m
-edge-colorings of

the constant mapping
a_{G}(e)=a
for all

We will require the following definition which will be used heavily throughout the paper. If we say a graph

is isomorphic to a graph

are (resp. are not) adjacent in

\pi_{*}:E(G)\rightarrow E(H)
denote the map induced by

which sends the edge

incident to vertices
v
and

to the edge
\pi_{*}(e)\in E(H)
incident to
\pi(v)
and
\pi(w)
.

Definition 3
(Isomorphism Set)

Let

and

be finite, undirected graphs. Write

if

is a subgraph of

if

The isomorphism set of a graph

is isomorphic to

subgraphs of

Example 1

Let
G
be a finite, undirected graph. Then,
G/G=\big\{\pi_{*}:Y\leq G,Y\cong_{\pi}G\big\}
by definition. So,
G/G=\big\{\pi_{*}:\pi\in\textrm{Aut}(G)\big\}
. For a less trivial example, the number of elements belonging to

can be determined by noticing that there are
\binom{4}{3}=4
distinct subsets of
3

K_{4}
and
|S_{3}|=3!=6
automorphisms of each subgraph induced by such a subset. So,
|K_{4}/K_{3}|=4!=24
.

For the remainder of the paper, we will do away with the asterisk subscript and simply write
\pi\in G/X
. Using the two definitions given above, we are now ready to provide a somewhat more symbolically nuanced (though, ultimately, equivalent) reformulation of classical Ramsey numbers.

Definition 4
(Ramsey Numbers)

Let
A
be an
m
-set and let
\big\{z_{j}\big\}_{j\in A}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. The Ramsey number
R(z_{a}:a\in A)
is the smallest
n
such that for any
t\geq n
and any
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}(K_{t},A)
, there exists some
a\in A
and
\pi\in K_{t}/K_{z_{a}}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(K_{z_{a}})}}}=a_{K_{z_{a}}}\circ\pi
.

Of course, it is natural to ask when or if it is possible to guarantee that some suitably ordered sequence of sufficiently ’nice’ graphs eventually possesses a similar property. Namely, given two index sets
I
and
J
with some sufficiently amicable ordering and two sequences of graph
\big\{G_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
and
\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
, when is it the case that there exists
n\in I
such that, for any
k\geq n
, any
global

m
-edge-coloring of
G_{k}
has a specified (possibly non-constant)
local
restriction to a subgraph of
G_{k}
isomorphic to
X_{j}
for some
j\in J
?

Naturally, for the question to be answered in the affirmative, it seems apparent that at least one of the graphs belonging to
\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
must be isomorphic to a subgraph of
G_{i}
for all indices
i\in I
beyond a certain point. So, for this reason, we give a definition that will simplify things considerably. In what follows, we will use
I
to denote our index set and will assume it has a strict well-ordering–that is,
I
has a strict total order and every nonempty subset of
I
has a unique minimal element with regard to the restriction of the order to that subset. Given such a strictly well-ordered set
I
, we will let let
0
denote the unique minimal element of
I
, and let
k
denote the unique minimal element of
I\setminus\big\{t\in I:0\leq t<k\big\}
for appropriately sized
k
, writing
I_{\geq k}=\big\{t\in I:k\leq t\big\}
. That is,
I_{\geq k}
is the subset of
I
obtained by removing the
k
elements of lowest order from
I
. As a shorthand, if
T
is a set indexed by
I
, we write
T_{\geq k}
to denote the subset of
T
with indices in
I_{\geq k}
.

Definition 5
(Constituent, Hereditary)

Let
I
be a strictly well-ordered set and let
\mathbb{G}=\big\{G_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
and
\mathbb{X}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
be collections of finite, undirected graphs. Then, if
G_{i}/X_{j}
is nonempty for all
i\in I
and all
j\in J
, we say
\mathbb{X}
is constituent in
\mathbb{G}
and write
\mathbb{X}\unlhd\mathbb{G}
. If
\big(\mathbb{G}\setminus\mathbb{G}_{{\geq i}}\big)\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
for every
0<i\leq k
, we say that
\mathbb{G}
is hereditary.

Broadly speaking, a set of graphs
\mathbb{G}
indexed by a strict well-ordered set
I
is hereditary if the graphs belonging to
\mathbb{G}
induce an order via containment that agrees with the order on
I
. That is, heredity is a
global
property belonging to
\mathbb{G}
. To contrast, a set of graphs
\mathbb{X}
is constituent in a set of graphs
\mathbb{G}
indexed by strict well-ordered set if
each
element of
\mathbb{G}
contains a subgraph isomorphic to
each
element of
\mathbb{X}
. That is, constituency is a
local
property of the elements of
\mathbb{G}
relative to the set
\mathbb{X}
. It is also worth mentioning that, in general, we will not require the entirety of a set
\mathbb{X}
to be constituent in
\mathbb{G}
in most situations where such a pair will arise. Since it is easier to do so, however, we present their definitions together for the sake of clarity.

We introduce two final definitions before proceeding to our generalization of Ramsey numbers.

Definition 6
(Ramsey base)

Let
\mathbb{G}=\big\{G_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of undirected graphs and let
\mathcal{S}=\big\{S_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of subsets
S_{i}\subseteq\textrm{Hom}(G_{i},A_{i})
for some sets
A_{i}
. If
\mathbb{G}
is hereditary, we say the pair
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
is a Ramsey base.

Ramsey bases will serve as the ’basic’ graphs we color and check for certain subcolorings. The counterpart to this consists of what we’re checking for.

Definition 7
(Ramsey Symbol)

Let
\mathbb{X}=\big\{\mathbb{X}_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of sets
\mathbb{X}_{i}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{{j\in J_{i}}}
of finite, undirected graphs indexed by a finite set
J_{i}
for each
i\in I
, and let
\mathcal{C}=\big\{C_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
be a collection of sets
C_{i}=\big\{\psi_{j}\big\}_{{j\in J_{i}}}
of
A_{i}
-valued edge colorings
\psi_{j}\in\textrm{Hom}(X_{j},A_{i})
for all
j\in J_{i}
. If
\big\{\coprod_{{j\in J_{i}}}X_{j}\big\}_{i\in I}
is hereditary, we say the set
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
is a Ramsey symbol.

Broadly, viewing a Ramsey number with a given Ramsey base as a function, Ramsey symbols will serve as the
argument
of these functions. For the remainder of the paper, given a Ramsey symbol
\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
, we will implicitly take
I
as the index set of
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathcal{C}
, and we will likewise take
J_{i}
as the index set of
\mathbb{X}_{i}
and
C_{i}
. With these definitions in hand, we now have the framework to produce the generalization of Ramsey numbers previously described.

Definition 8
(Ramsey Numbers)

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
be a Ramsey base and let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a Ramsey symbol. The Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is the smallest
n\in I
, if such an
n
exists, such that, for any
t\geq n
and any
A_{t}
-valued edge-coloring
\rho\in S_{t}\subseteq\textrm{Hom}(G_{t},A_{t})
, there exists some
j\in J_{t}
and
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
. If such an
n
does not exist, then we say the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
does not exist.

The above definition is given in what will be, for practical purposes, too much generality. Throughout a substantial portion of the body of this paper, this generality will exceed the scope of what is useful. All the same, it is useful to state in this overly general form, since it more readily highlights the restrictions that need to be imposed to reach cases that can be dealt with. That is, we will frequently require our Ramsey base and Ramsey symbol to have certain desirable properties in order to make conclusions about the existence or nonexistence of a corresponding Ramsey numbers. After proving some results for these more restrictive cases in early sections, we will return to a more general case closer to the definition above by using certain decompositions and embeddings to convert our previously obtained results to this broader context. The following definitions make precise the nature of the constraints we will initially require.

Definition 9
(Locally Finite, Finite, Maximal)

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
be a Ramsey base, where
\mathbb{G}=\big\{G_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
and
\mathcal{S}=\big\{S_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
with
S_{i}\subseteq\textrm{Hom}(G_{i},A_{i})
. Then:

1.

If
|A_{i}|<\infty
for all
i\in I
, we say it is of locally finite type.

1.a.

If, additionally,
A_{i}=A_{j}
and all
i,j\in I
, we say it is of finite type.

2.

If
S_{i}=\textrm{Hom}(G_{i},A_{i})
for all
i\in I
, we say it is maximal.

Definition 10
(Locally Finite, Finite, Uniform)

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a Ramsey symbol, where
\mathbb{X}=\big\{X_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
,
X_{i}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J_{i}}
, and
\mathcal{C}=\big\{C_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
with
C_{i}=\big\{\psi_{j}\big\}_{j\in J_{i}}
for some
\psi_{j}\in\textrm{Hom}(X_{j},A_{i})
. Then:

1.

If
|A_{i}|<\infty
for all
i\in I
, we say it is of locally finite type.

1.a.

If, additionally,
A_{i}=A_{j}
for all
i,j\in I
, we say it is of finite type.

2.

If
A_{i}\subseteq A_{t}
and
(\mathbb{X}_{i},C_{i})=(\mathbb{X}_{t},\mathcal{C}_{t})
for all
t\geq i
(where the equality is understood as factoring through an inclusion if
A_{i}\neq A_{t}
), we say it is uniform.

As a natural shorthand, if the Ramsey base
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
is maximal, we write
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
instead of
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
for simplicity. Similarly, if the Ramsey symbol
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
is uniform, we will omit reference to the index set
I
for
\mathbb{X}
and
\mathcal{C}
and write
\mathbb{X}=\left\{X_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
and
\mathcal{C}=\left\{\psi_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
instead.

Notice the minor asymmetry between the constraints placed on Ramsey symbols and Ramsey bases in item
(2)
of each of the above definitions. This is mainly due to the fact that, while we
could
certainly define maximal Ramsey symbols or uniform Ramsey bases in the same fashion, it is straightforward to see that they would be (in general) of relatively little interest, since maximal Ramsey symbols would, essentially, measure only subgraph inclusion and uniform Ramsey bases would allow for only a trivial form of heredity.

Using the above definitions, notice that setting
\mathbb{G}=\left\{K_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and letting
\mathbb{X}=\left\{K_{z_{j}}\right\}_{{j\in[m]}}
,
\mathcal{C}=\left\{j_{K_{z_{j}}}\right\}_{{j\in[m]}}
with
[m]=\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
yields
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=R\left(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\right)
. So, the Ramsey numbers we describe are a generalization of classical Ramsey numbers. Likewise, the generalized Ramsey numbers
R(H_{1},\ldots,H_{m})
for graphs
H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
–as described in; e.g.,
[
2
]
–can be produced from our Ramsey numbers in the same way by simply replacing
\mathbb{X}=\left\{H_{j}\right\}_{{j\in[m]}}
and
\mathcal{C}=\left\{j_{H_{j}}\right\}_{j\in[m]}
.

Indeed, as a Ramsey number of the form we describe in Definition
8
, both the classical and generalized Ramsey numbers previously studied are of a particularly convenient form: they have maximal bases of finite type and a uniform symbols of finite type. Let’s look at some further examples to familiarize ourselves with these constraints.

Example 2

Let
\mathbb{G}=\left\{K_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and suppose
\mathcal{S}=\left\{S_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

, the set

consists of the

-valued edge colorings of

, such that no two edges of

are assigned the same element in

. Then, since

is hereditary and

is a Ramsey base of locally finite type. However, since

for

, the Ramsey base

is not of finite type.

Example 3

Let
\mathbb{X}=\left\{\mathbb{X}_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathbb{X}_{i}=\left\{K_{2j}\right\}{j\in\mathbb{N}}
for all

, and let
\mathcal{C}=\left\{C_{i}\right\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

and for all

. Then,
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
is not a Ramsey symbol, since the index set

As mentioned previously, certain cases of Ramsey numbers will prove to be more or less amicable to study. Among those cases, two particular ones are worth distinguishing with names.

Definition 11
(Exact, Galois Type)

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
be a Ramsey base and let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a uniform Ramsey symbol. If
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}
for some nonempty subset
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
, we say that the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is exact.

If the Ramsey base
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
is of finite type, the Ramsey symbol
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
is uniform and of finite type, and
|A|=p^{k}
for a prime
p
and some positive integer
k
, we say that the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is of Galois type.

For an example of a classical Ramsey number of Galois type, consider any one where the number of colors used is a prime power. We will address this case more later.

To start, however, the remainder of this section is dedicated to illustrating the general approach we will employ, which is to show that complicated Ramsey numbers can be converted into simpler ones. We use the exact Ramsey numbers for this, since they are particularly simple. We will do this by building relatively simple ‘tools’ to relate Ramsey numbers with different bases and symbols. The first example of such a tool is very straightforward and will allow us to remove unnecessary portions of a Ramsey base.

Definition 12
(Resolutions)

Let
\mathbb{G}=\big\{G_{i}\big\}_{i\in I}
be hereditary and let
\mathbb{X}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
be a collection of finite, undirected graphs. We say
\mathbb{G}
admits a
\mathbb{X}
-resolution if there is a minimum value
k
such that
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
for some
\mathbb{X}\supseteq\mathbb{V}\neq\emptyset
. If
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution, we say
\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
is the
\mathbb{X}
-resolution of
\mathbb{G}
and write
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
.

As a shorthand, if
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
and
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
is a Ramsey symbol, we will write
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S}):=(\mathbb{G}_{\geq k},\mathcal{S}_{\geq k})
to avoid explicit reference to the value
k
and the unnecessary introduction of additional variables. Directly by construction, it is not particularly difficult to see that if
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is a Ramsey number with a uniform Ramsey symbol, then
R_{\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is exact.

Example 4

Let
\mathbb{G}=\big\{K_{n}\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and let
\mathbb{X}=\big\{K_{6},K_{40}\big\}
. Then,
\mathbb{X}
is not constituent in
\mathbb{G}
, as
K_{t}/K_{6}=\emptyset
for
0<t<6
and
K_{t}/K_{40}=\emptyset
for
0<t<40
. However,
\mathbb{G}
does admit an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution, as the subsets
\mathbb{G}_{\geq 6}=\big\{K_{n}\big\}_{6\leq n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{V}=\big\{K_{6}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
satisfy
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq 6}
. Since it is straightforward to see that this is the least value
k
such that there is a nonempty subset

of
\mathbb{X}
satisfying
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
, we have
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq 6}
.

\mathbb{X}
-resolutions give us a method to turn non-exact Ramsey numbers into exact ones.

Theorem 2.1

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
be a Ramsey base and let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a uniform Ramsey symbol. Then, if
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution,

R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)=R_{\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big).

Proof

If
R_{\mathbb{G}}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
does not exist, then we are done, as it is clear that
R_{\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
cannot exist either. So, suppose
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution and suppose
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
exists. Then, by definition,
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
for some
0\leq k<\infty
and, by the uniformity of the Ramsey symbol,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
is the least
n\in I
such that, for any
t\geq n
and any edge-coloring
\rho\in S_{t}
of
G_{t}
, there exists
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
for some
j\in J
. As
\mathbb{G}
is hereditary by assumption, then
G_{m}/X_{j}=\emptyset
for every
j\in J
if
m<k
by definition, as
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq m}
must hold for some
\big\{X_{j}\big\}=\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
if
G_{m}/X_{j}
is nonempty. Hence,
R_{\mathbb{G}}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)\geq k
.

That is to say, the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
with uniform Ramsey symbol for which
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution remains invariant under
\mathbb{X}
-resolutions of the Ramsey base. In fact, a nice partial converse can be shown as well.

Lemma 1

Let
(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})
be a Ramsey base and let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a uniform Ramsey symbol. Then, if the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
exists,
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution.

Proof

If the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
exists and has a uniform symbol then, by definition,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
is the least
n\in I
such that, for any
t\geq n
and any edge-coloring
\rho\in S_{t}
of
G_{t}
, there exists
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
for some
j\in J
. As
\mathbb{G}
is hereditary by assumption, then
G_{m}/X_{j}=\emptyset
for every
j\in J
if
G_{m}\in\mathbb{G}\setminus\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})
by definition, as
\mathbb{V}\unlhd\mathbb{G}_{\geq m}
must hold for some
\big\{X_{j}\big\}=\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathbb{X}
if
G_{m}/X_{j}
is nonempty. As the existence of
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
requires
G_{k}/X_{j}
to be nonempty for some
j\in J
and every
k\geq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
, we are done.

So, studying exact Ramsey numbers that exist is equivalent to studying Ramsey numbers with a uniform symbol that exist and admit
\mathbb{X}
-resolutions. Though this is not necessarily a particularly interesting fact by itself, this is a good illustration of the type of approach we will employ in this paper, which can be summarized as follows:

1.

Choose a class of Ramsey numbers that is sufficiently constrained so as to be amicable to study in some sense, or for which some results are known.

2.

Create a device which allows a less constrained class of Ramsey numbers to be converted to or related to the constrained class.

3.

Prove that a property–such as existence or the value–of the less constrained class of Ramsey numbers is preserved under this conversion or relation.

A slightly less trivial instance of this is presented next.

3
Indicator Polynomials for Ramsey Numbers of Galois Type

In this section, we will consider Ramsey numbers of Galois type and observe a way to understand them via the zero sets of certain polynomials over finite fields. This will prove instrumental in the later sections of the paper, and will allow us (after applying some conversions and embeddings, which we will develop later on) a basic sort of characterization of the locally-finite type Ramsey numbers.

In the following definition, we will set ourselves up to exploit the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-vector space structure we can impose on
\textrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{F}_{q})
by defining a certain useful class of polynomial and considering the points in the free vector space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G))}:=\oplus_{e\in E(G)}\mathbb{F}_{q}
where they vanish. Since
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{F}_{q})
produces
v=\oplus_{e\in E(G)}\rho(e)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G))}
, we will treat vectors
v
as colorings
\rho
and vice versa.

Definition 13
(Subgraph Coloring Indicator Polynomials)

Let
G
,
X
be finite undirected graphs and let
q
be a prime power. If
\psi\in\textrm{Hom}(X,\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then the
(X,\psi)
-indicator polynomial of
G
is the polynomial
p[G,X,\psi]\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{e}:e\in E(G)]
given by

p[G,X,\psi](x)=\prod_{\pi\in G/X}\left(1-\prod_{e\in\pi^{-1}(X)}\left(1-\big(x_{e}-\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)\big)^{q-1}\right)\right).

Recalling that
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a unique factorization domain and that
g^{q-1}=1
for any nonzero element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, it follows that

\prod_{e\in\pi^{-1}(X)}\left(1-\big(x_{e}-\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)\big)^{q-1}\right)\neq 0

if and only if
x_{e}=\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)
for every
e\in E\big(\pi^{-1}(X)\big)
. That is, indicator polynomials are just a (computationally explicit) way to write an Iverson bracket. We will use this fact to our advantage in the following theorem.

Lemma 2

Let
G
,
X
be finite undirected graphs and let
q
be a prime power. If
\psi\in\textrm{Hom}(X,\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
p[G,X,\psi](v)=0
for
v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G))}
corresponding to a coloring
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{F}_{q})
if and only if there exists
\pi\in G/X
satisfying

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X)}}}=\psi\circ\pi.

Proof

As
q
is a prime power,
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a field, and is a unique factorization domain. So,
p[G,X,\psi](\rho)=0
if and only if there is some
\pi\in G/X
such that

\prod_{e\in\pi^{-1}(X)}\left(1-\big(\rho(e)-\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)\big)^{q-1}\right)=1.

But, since every nonzero element
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
satisfies
a^{q-1}=1
, this then implies that
\rho(e)-\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)=0
must hold for every
e\in\pi^{-1}(X)
, which is to say that
\rho(e)=\psi\big(\pi(e)\big)
must hold for every
e\in\pi^{-1}(X)
. So, this says
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X)}}}=\psi\circ\pi
.

If
\mathbb{X}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
is a finite collection (i.e
|J|<\infty
) of finite, undirected graphs, and
\mathcal{C}=\big\{\psi_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
is a collection of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-valued
q
-edge-colorings
\psi_{j}\in\textrm{Hom}(X_{j},\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then we write
p[G,\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
to denote the product

p[G,\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]:=\prod_{j\in J}p[G,X_{j},\psi_{j}].

This will allow us to use the indicator polynomials of
G
to discern whether a given
q
-coloring of
G
contains a colored subgraph isomorphic to a member of a given
family
of colored graphs.

Lemma 3

Let
G
be a finite undirected graph and let
q
be a prime power. If
\mathbb{X}=\big\{X_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
is a finite collection of finite undirected graphs and
\mathcal{C}=\big\{\psi_{j}\big\}_{j\in J}
is a collection of colorings
\psi_{j}\in\textrm{Hom}(X_{j},\mathbb{F}_{q})
, then
p[G,\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
is identically zero as a polynomial function if and only if for every
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}(G,\mathbb{F}_{q})
, there exists
\pi\in G/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
for some
j\in J
.

Proof

Since
p[G,\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](\rho)=0

if and only if
p[G,X_{j},\psi_{j}](\rho)=0
for some
j\in J
, Lemma
2
delivers the result.

Example 5

Consider
K_{4}
with edges numbered as below:

1
0
2
3
5
4

Let
\pi:H\rightarrow K_{3}
denote any graph isomorphism which sends the subgraph
H
of
K_{4}
induced by the edge set
\left\{1,3,5\right\}
to
K_{3}
. Then, the polynomial

\big(1-x_{1}x_{3}x_{5}\big)\big(1-(1-x_{1})(1-x_{3})(1-x_{5})\big)

in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{e}:e\in K_{4}]
has a zero at any value where
x_{1}=x_{3}=x_{5}=0
or
x_{1}=x_{3}=x_{5}=1
. That is, the zeros of this polynomial corresponds to the
2
-edge-colorings of
K_{4}
that contains
H
as a monochromatic subgraph. Considering the other subgraphs of
K_{4}
isomorphic to
K_{3}
and taking
\mathbb{X}=\left\{K_{3},K_{3}\right\}
,
\mathcal{C}=\left\{0_{K_{3}},1_{K_{3}}\right\}
, we may proceed to produce (after some slight algebra) the polynomial

\displaystyle p[K_{4},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{2}}\prod_{\pi\in K_{4}/K_{3}}\left(1-\prod_{e\in\pi^{-1}(K_{3})}\left(1-\big(x_{e}-a\big)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\big(x_{0}(1-x_{3})+x_{3}(1-x_{4})+x_{4}(1-x_{0})\big)^{6}

\displaystyle\quad\big(x_{1}(1-x_{3})+x_{3}(1-x_{5})+x_{5}(1-x_{1})\big)^{6}

\displaystyle\quad\big(x_{2}(1-x_{4})+x_{4}(1-x_{5})+x_{5}(1-x_{2})\big)^{6}

\displaystyle\quad\big(x_{0}(1-x_{1})+x_{1}(1-x_{2})+x_{2}(1-x_{0})\big)^{6}

Here,
p[K_{4},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](\rho)=0
if and only if

is a 2-coloring of

that contains a monochromatic

.

The above makes it clear that studying the algebraic sets where the polynomials
p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
vanish may help us to discern properties related to the existence and value of a Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
of Galois type with maximal base. We will show that this is precisely the case in the remainder of this section, and will prove that this holds more generally in Section
5
.

Remark 1

It is worth pausing here to stress a distinction between polynomials and polynomial functions. Given a field
\mathbb{F}
, let
\mathbb{F}[x]
denote the ring of univariate polynomials in the variable
x
over
\mathbb{F}
and let
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F})
denote the polynomial functions
p:\mathbb{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
. Naturally, both
\mathbb{F}[x]
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F})
are algebras over
\mathbb{F}
, and we can certainly map any
p\in\mathbb{F}[x]
to
q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F})
by the algebra homomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{F}[x]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F})
defined by the
\mathbb{F}
-action
\Phi(p)(x)=q(x),\enskip\forall x\in\mathbb{F}
. It is routine to verify that we have
\mathbb{F}[x]/\textrm{ker}(\Phi)\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{F},\mathbb{F})
. So, for example, the polynomial
x^{q}-x
is not the zero polynomial in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
, but is identically the zero polynomial in
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
since
x^{q}-x
factors as
\prod_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\big(x-a\big)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]
. More succinctly,
x^{q}-x\in\textrm{ker}(\Phi)
. Naturally, an analog of the same holds in the multivariate case as well (see, e.g.
[
6
]
). This distinction should be kept in mind to avoid mistakes. For clarity, we treat polynomials as polynomials, not polynomial functions, unless explicitly stated.

Remark 2

Lemma
3
also makes some sense of the choice to use the term ’
\mathbb{X}
-resolution’ in Definition
12
. Namely, for a hereditary set
\mathbb{G}
of finite undirected graphs, a finite set
\mathbb{X}=\left\{X_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of finite undirected graphs, and a set
\mathcal{C}=\left\{\psi_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of functions
\psi_{j}\in\textrm{Hom}(X_{j},\mathbb{F}_{q})
, if
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
, then it follows from the heredity of
\mathbb{G}
that there are (not necessarily unique) surjective projection homomorphisms

P_{t}:\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t-1}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)

and quotient homomorphisms

q_{t}:\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)/\left\langle p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\right\rangle

which induce, for any

, the exact sequence of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-modules

\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{k+r}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)\xrightarrow{P_{k+1}\circ\ldots\circ P_{k+r}}\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{k}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)\xrightarrow{q_{k}}0.

Exactness follows from the surjectivity of the projections and the fact that
p[G_{m},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]=1
if and only if

, yielding
q_{k}\big(\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{k}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)\big)=0
. So, an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution of
\mathbb{G}
induces a corresponding resolution of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-module
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{k}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
in the traditional sense.

Remark 3

The
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomials of a hereditary set of graphs
\mathbb{G}
allow for certain convenient applications of
\mathbb{X}
-resolutions. Namely, by the construction of the indicator polynomials, note that if
t
is such that
G_{t}/X_{j}=\emptyset
for all
X_{j}\in\mathbb{X}
, then the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomial of
G_{t}
is a constant function equal to
1
. Using the heredity of
\mathbb{G}
, it is somewhat intuitive to see that if
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution, then the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomials of
G_{i}\in\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})
may be used to factor the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomials of
G_{t}\in\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})
whenever
t>i
. Of course, the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomials of
G_{t}\in\mathbb{G}\setminus\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})
are also factors of all other indicator polynomials as well, but this is of little interest since these polynomials are constant and identically equal to
1
.

Theorem 3.1

Suppose the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is of Galois type and suppose
\mathbb{G}
admits an
\mathbb{X}
-resolution. If
\textrm{res}_{\mathbb{X}}(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{G}_{\geq k}
, then for all
n>k
and all
\pi\in G_{n}/G_{k}
, the polynomial

p[\pi(G_{k}),\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\big(x_{\pi^{-1}(G_{k})}\big)

is a factor of
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](x)
.

Proof

By the heredity of
\mathbb{G}
, it follows that
G_{n}/G_{k}\neq\emptyset
for all
n>k
. So, if
\sigma\in G_{n}/G_{k}
, then for any
j\in J
and any
\eta\in G_{k}/X_{j}
, it follows that
\big(\sigma\circ\eta\big)\in G_{n}/X_{j}
. So, for any fixed
\pi\in G_{n}/G_{k}
, the indicator
p[G_{n},X_{j},\psi_{j}](x)
is divisible by

\prod_{\eta\in G_{k}/X_{j}}\left(1-\prod_{e\in\pi^{-1}\big(\eta^{-1}(X_{j})\big)}\left(1-\big(x_{e}-\psi_{j}\big((\pi\circ\eta)(e)\big)\big)^{q-1}\right)\right).

This may equivalently be written as
p[\pi(G_{k}),X_{j},\psi_{j}]\big(x_{\pi^{-1}(G_{k})}\big)
, and by Definition
13
, we may conclude that this is also a factor of
p[G_{n},X_{j},\psi_{j}](x)
. To produce our desired result, we simply take the product over
j\in J
:

\prod_{j\in J}p[\sigma(G_{k}),X_{j},\psi_{j}]\big(x_{\sigma^{-1}(G_{k})}\big)=p[\sigma(G_{k}),\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\big(x_{\sigma^{-1}(G_{k})}\big).

It is worth noting that this holds for each fixed
\pi\in G_{n}/G_{k}
, but it need not be the case that
\prod_{\pi\in G_{n}/G_{k}}p[\pi(G_{k}),\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\big(x_{\pi^{-1}(G_{k})}\big)
divides
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](x)
. That is, if
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in G_{n}/G_{k}
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in G_{k}/X_{j}
, while it is true that
\pi_{1}\circ\eta_{1}
and
\pi_{2}\circ\eta_{2}
are embeddings of
X_{j}
in
G_{n}
, it is possible to have
\pi_{1}\circ\eta_{1}=\pi_{2}\circ\eta_{2}
while
\sigma_{1}\neq\sigma_{2}
and
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}
. That is, some factors may appear too many times when the product is taken over
G_{n}/G_{k}
, analogously to how
(2)(3)=6
and
(3)(5)=15
both divide
(2)(3)(5)=30
, but
(6)(15)=90
does not.

Returning to the main focus of the section, in the next theorem, we will show that it is not difficult to use the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicators of
\mathbb{G}
to produce a sort of characterization of Ramsey numbers
R_{\mathbb{G}}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\big)
of Galois type with maximal base. This class of Ramsey numbers, as previously noted, contains all of the classical Ramsey numbers with a prime power number of arguments.

Theorem 3.2

Suppose the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
exists and is of Galois type. Treating
p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
as an element of
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{i}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
and writing
i_{m}=\textrm{max}\left\{i\in I:p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\neq 0\right\}
and
i_{k}=\textrm{min}\left\{i\in I:p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]=0\right\}
, we have
i_{m+1}=R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=i_{k}
.

Proof

As
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is of Galois type, let
q
be the prime power number of colors. By Definition, since the base of
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is maximal, the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
if
n\in I
is the lowest order element of
I
such that, for
t\geq n
and any
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-valued
m
-edge-coloring
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}\big(G_{t},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
of
G_{t}
, there exists
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
for some
X_{j}\in\mathbb{X}
. But, as
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
is identically the zero polynomial in
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
if and only if for every
\rho\in\textrm{Hom}(G_{t},\mathbb{F}_{q})
there exists
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
for some
j\in J
, it follows that that
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)\geq i_{m+1}
if
p[G_{i_{m}},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
is not identically the zero polynomial in
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{i_{m}}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
. In the reverse, it follows similarly that
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
is identically the zero polynomial in
\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{t}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
for every
t\geq n
and
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)\leq i_{k}
. So,
i_{m+1}\leq R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)\leq i_{k}
. The result follows from the assumption that
I
is well-ordered by noting that
i_{m+1}=i_{k}
.

As a corollary, we get the following:

Corollary 1

Suppose the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is of Galois type with maximal base and suppose
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
. Then,
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\in\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal generated by the polynomials
\big\{x_{e}^{q}-x_{e}:e\in E(G_{n})\big\}
.

Proof

If
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
exists and is of Galois type with maximal base and
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
, then
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{n}))}
by Theorem
3.2
. So, since
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]
is not the zero polynomial but is identically zero as a polynomial function, it must lie in the kernel of the evaluation map
\Phi
, i.e. the algebra homomorphism
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\left[x_{e}:e\in E(G_{n})]\right]\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\big(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{n}))},\mathbb{F}_{q}\big)
that sends a polynomial to the corresponding polynomial function of minimal degree with which it agrees point-wise (see, e.g.
[
6
]
). But, since
\textrm{ker}(\Phi)=\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the ideal generated by the polynomials
\big\{x_{e}^{q}-x_{e}:e\in E(G_{n})\big\}
, this then says that
p[G_{n},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\in\mathcal{I}
.

Put another way, if the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
exists and is of Galois type and
\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle
is the ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{e}:e\in E(G_{i})]
generated by the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomial of
G_{i}
, then the algebraic set

V\big(\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle\big)=\big\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{i}))}:p(x)=0,\enskip\forall p\in\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle\big\}

must satisfy
V\big(\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle\big)=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(E(G_{i}))}
for all values of
i\geq R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
. This makes it clear that everything we have done so far can be extended to Ramsey numbers of Galois type without maximal base without much effort: we need only check that
V\big(\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle\big)
contain all admissible colorings
S_{i}
. We can state this precisely by adopt the usual convention of writing
V\big(\mathcal{I}\big)
to denote the algebraic set on which the members of an ideal
\mathcal{I}
vanish and, for an algebraic set

to denote the ideal of all polynomials that vanish on
U
.

Corollary 2

Suppose the Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
exists and is of Galois type. Then,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
if and only if
n
is the least value such that

S_{n}\subseteq V\left(\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle\right)

or, equivalently, such that

I(S_{n})\supseteq\langle p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\rangle.

Example 6

Consider the indicator polynomial
p\left[K_{4},\left\{K_{3},K_{3}\right\},\left\{0_{K_{3}},1_{K_{3}}\right\}\right]
given in Example
5
. It is routine to check that the coloring
\rho=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&1&0&0&1&0\end{array}\right]
yields
p\left[K_{4},\left\{K_{3},K_{3}\right\},\left\{0_{K_{3}},1_{K_{3}}\right\}\right](\rho)=1
. So,

V\big(\langle p\left[K_{4},\left\{K_{3},K_{3}\right\},\left\{0_{K_{3}},1_{K_{3}}\right\}\right]\rangle\big)\not\supset F_{2}^{(E(K_{4}))}

and we may conclude

.

Via Theorem
3.2
and Corollary
2
, we know that studying Ramsey numbers of Galois type without maximal base is not, on a conceptual level, any harder than studying Ramsey numbers of Galois type with maximal base. This is mainly due to the fact that the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomials do not depend on the set of admissible colorings
\mathcal{S}
. We can basically use the same trick to show that any Ramsey numbers with bases of finite type and uniform symbols of finite type can be converted to an equivalent Ramsey number of Galois type, or–more generally–that any Ramsey number with a base and symbol of locally finite type is ‘locally’ like a Ramsey number of Galois type. In the next section, we do this.

4
Indicator Polynomials for Ramsey Numbers of Locally Finite Type

We begin by adapting the locally finite case to a local analog of the Galois case. Among other things, this allows any Ramsey numbers with bases of finite type and uniform symbols of finite type to be converted to an equivalent Ramsey number of Galois type. In either case, the conversion is simple and just requires choosing a few injections.

Theorem 4.1

Let
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a Ramsey number with a base and symbol of locally finite type and let
f_{i}:A_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
be any sequence of injections, where
q_{i}
is any appropriately large prime power. Then, setting
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=(S_{i}^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=(C_{i}^{\prime})
, where
S_{i}\prime=\left\{f_{i}\circ\rho:\rho\in S_{i}\right\}
and
C_{i}^{\prime}=\left\{f_{i}\circ\psi:\psi\in C_{i}\right\}
, we have
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})=R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
.

Proof

By Definition
8
, we know that
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=n
if and only if
n
is the minimum element of
I
such that for every
t\geq n
and any
\phi S_{t}^{\prime}\subseteq\textrm{Hom}\big(G_{t},\mathbb{F}_{q_{t}}\big)
, there is some
j\in J_{t}
such that there exists
\pi\in G_{t}/X_{j}
satisfying, for the corresponding coloring
\eta_{j}\in C_{t}^{\prime}
,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\eta_{j}\circ\pi
. But, by definition, if
\phi\in S_{t}^{\prime}
and
\eta_{j}\in C_{t}^{\prime}
, then
\phi=f_{t}\circ\rho
for some
\rho\in S_{t}
and
\eta_{j}=f_{t}\circ\psi_{j}
for
\psi_{j}\in C_{t}
. So, we then have
{\left.\kern-1.2ptf_{t}\circ\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=f_{t}\circ\psi_{j}\circ\pi
. As
f_{t}
is an injection, it has a left inverse which we may apply to produce
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\rho\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(X_{j})}}}=\psi_{j}\circ\pi
. This is precisely the condition required for
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})=n
as well.

Notice that, for Ramsey numbers with bases and symbols of finite type, the sequence of injections can be chosen to be constant, rendering
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
a Ramsey number of Galois type and facilitating the conversion mentioned before the theorem. More generally, Theorem
4.1
tells us that it is possible to apply everything we have done so far to the
much larger
class of Ramsey numbers with a base and symbol of locally finite type. The requirement that a Ramsey number be of Galois type imposed in the last section largely served only as an illustration of how this process should work in a simpler case.

In light of Theorem
4.1
, we can show that Corollary
1
holds more generally.

Theorem 4.2

Suppose the Ramsey number
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)
is of locally finite type with maximal base and suppose
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
. Then, for all
t\geq n
,
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{I}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
is as in Theorem
4.1
and
\mathcal{I}_{t}
is the ideal in
\mathbb{F}_{q_{t}}[x_{e}:e\in E(G_{t})]
generated by the polynomials
\big\{x_{e}^{q_{t}}-x_{e}:e\in E(G_{t})\big\}
.

Proof

Note that
R_{\mathbb{G}}\left(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}\right)=n
if and only if
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}](z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q_{t}}^{(E(G_{t}))}
,

, as shown in the proof of Theorem

. So, since

is not the zero polynomial but is identically zero as a polynomial function, it must lie in the kernel of the evaluation map

that sends a polynomial to the corresponding polynomial function of minimal degree with which it agrees point-wise. But, since
\textrm{ker}(\Phi_{t})=\mathcal{I}_{t}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{t}
is the ideal generated by the polynomials
\big\{x_{e}^{q_{t}}-x_{e}:e\in E(G_{t})\big\}
, this then says that
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}]\in\mathcal{I}_{t}
.

Analogously to Corollary
2
, we then reach the following result immediately:

Theorem 4.3

Let
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a generalized Ramsey number with a base of locally finite type and symbol of locally finite type and let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be as in Theorem

. Then,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})=n
if and only if

is the least value such that

or, equivalently, such that

I\big(S_{t}^{\prime}\big)\supseteq\big\langle p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},C_{t}^{\prime}]\big\rangle.

The above result says that–locally at each level
i\in I
, at least–Ramsey numbers of locally finite type are, really, no harder to deal with conceptually than those of Galois type.

5
Some Structural Results for Ramsey Numbers

In this section, we will provide some structural results regarding the manipulation of Ramsey numbers. This will highlight a connection that ties in to the previous results nicely. The next two lemmas regard the extension and restriction of components of either the symbol or base of a Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type.

Lemma 4

Let
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a Ramsey number with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type. Suppose that
(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
is a uniform Ramsey symbol of finite type and that
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})\subseteq(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
. Then if
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
exists and

R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})\leq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}).

Proof

As both
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
are Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type, we may treat them as Ramsey numbers of Galois type with colorings taking values over the same finite field via Theorem
4.1
. So, as
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})\subseteq(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
and
|(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})|\leq|(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})|
, the
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
-indicator polynomial of
G_{i}
is a factor of the
(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
-indicator polynomial of
G_{i}
. More precisely, if
J_{1}
is the index set of
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
and
J_{2}
is the index set of
(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
we may assume, without loss of generality, that
J_{1}\subseteq J_{2}
. So, writing
\mathbb{Y}=\big\{Y_{j}\big\}_{j\in J_{2}}
and
\mathcal{W}=\big\{\phi_{j}\big\}_{j\in J_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle p[G_{i},\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W}](x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j\in J_{2}}p[G_{i},Y_{j},\phi_{j}](x)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j\in J_{1}}p[G_{i},Y_{j},\phi_{j}](x)\right)\left(\prod_{j\in J_{2}\setminus J_{1}}p[G_{i},Y_{j},\phi_{j}](x)\right)

\displaystyle=p[G_{i},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](x)\left(\prod_{j\in J_{2}\setminus J_{1}}p[G_{i},Y_{j},W_{j}](x)\right).

If
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists, then by Corollary
2
, there is a value
n\in I
such that for any
t\in I
satisfying
t\geq n
, we have
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}]\big(\rho\big)=0
for any
\rho\in S_{t}
. By the above partial factorization, this then says that
p[G_{t},\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W}]\big(\rho\big)=0
for any
\rho\in S_{t}
as well. So,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
exists and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})\leq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
.

In particular, Lemma
4
tells us that if
Q=(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
is the symbol of a Ramsey number with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type and
\emptyset\neq Y\subseteq Q
is a uniform Ramsey symbol of finite type, then

R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(Q)\leq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(Q\cap Y).

That is, Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type
grow larger
under
intersections
. In the next result, we show that the opposite happens when changes are made to a portion of the base, i.e. Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type grow smaller under intersections of admissible coloring sets.

Lemma 5

Let
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
be a Ramsey number with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type. Suppose
L_{i}\subseteq S_{i}
for all
i\in I
. Then, if
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists,
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{L})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists and

R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{L})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})\leq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}).

Proof

As both
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{L})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
are Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type, we may treat them as Ramsey numbers of Galois type with colorings taking values over the same finite field by Theorem
4.1
. So, note that if
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists, then by Corollary
2
, there is a value
n\in I
such that for any
t\in I
satisfying
t\geq n
, we have
p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](\rho)=0
for any
\rho\in S_{t}
. Since both
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{W})
are Ramsey numbers of Galois type with the same Ramsey symbol, and as

p[G_{t},\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C}](\rho)=0

exists and
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{L})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})\leq R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
.

Together, the above lemmas tell us that Ramsey numbers with a base of finite type and uniform symbol of finite type behave somewhat intuitively, in the sense that

the Ramsey symbol or

the admissible coloring functions in the Ramsey base

the value of a given Ramsey number, provided it exists to begin with. That is,

is monotonic with respect to inclusion and

is monotonic with respect to reverse inclusion.

This suggests that Ramsey numbers share more than a superficial similarity to the classic algebraic geometry ideal-variety interplay described in the previous section. That is, Ramsey numbers are somewhat like

\leq

\Leftrightarrow

\leq

\psi_{j}\in C_{n}\end{bmatrix}

\subseteq

\Leftrightarrow

We conjecture that developing this correspondence more fully could give insights into the ’correct’ formulation of a general theory of Ramseyian phenomena. In the next section, we will try to offer some evidence that doing so might be interesting and worthwhile.

6
Ramseyian Formulations of Classically Non-Ramseyian Problems

Now, some applications. We consider some familiar problems from other mathematical disciplines. In particular, problems which involve the assignment of distinct values belonging to some common set to pairs of a finite set of objects generally admit some form of a coloring interpretation. Most notably,
distances
between pairs of objects may be regarded in this manner by attaching data to the vertex set of a graph.

Definition 14
(Metrical Colorings)

G

be a metric space, let

A useful, somewhat unexpected consequence of allowing the admissible coloring set

to be smaller than the set

Let’s illustrate this by re-framing a well-known number-theoretic result in Ramseyian form. For notation, we will say a set

The Green-Tao Theorem is equivalent to the existence of a family of Ramsey numbers.
First, an intermediate Ramsey-theoretic statement:

Proof

To begin, note that for any choice of

\eta\in L_{i}(m)
that satisfies

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\eta\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{E(K_{i+1})\setminus\sigma_{i}^{-1}(P_{i})}}}={\left.\kern-1.2ptr_{K_{i+1}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{E(K_{i+1})\setminus\sigma_{i}^{-1}(P_{i})}}}

for any
r\in A_{(i,m)}
. If
A_{(i,m)}=\left\{2t\right\}
, then we are done. If not, then by choosing
r\neq 2t
, it follows that if there exists
\pi\in K_{i+1}/K_{2}
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\eta\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\pi^{-1}(K_{2})}}}=(2t)_{K_{2}}\circ\pi
, then
\pi^{-1}(K_{2})\subseteq\sigma_{i}^{-1}(P_{i})
. So, since
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\eta\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{{\sigma_{i}^{-1}(P_{i})}}}=d_{\Theta[i,m]}
, the desired conclusion follows immediately.

It’s obvious that other related conjectures can also be posed in a Ramseyain form with only a small degree of effort. Per Theorem
6.5
, it’s easy to see that Zhang’s result is no exception, and is equivalent to the following Ramsey-theoretic statement:

Theorem 6.6

For some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the Ramsey numbers
R_{\big(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{L}(m)\big)}\big(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{W}(N)\big)
as in Theorem
6.5
exist for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

On one hand, this demonstrates that determining whether or not a Ramsey number
R_{(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{S})}(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{C})
exists is, at least, as hard as the Twin Prime conjecture or Polignac’s conjecture. On the other hand, it is not entirely outlandish to imagine that there might be at least a slight possibility that some useful insight into problems of this nature could be gained by this sort of a conversion–perhaps by generating upper bounds on some transformed Ramseyian formulation of the problem. Regardless, it certainly shows that the version of Ramsey numbers addressed in this paper capture more than the classical or generalized forms of Ramsey numbers that are often studied. Complicated, highly nontrivial nonclassical Ramsey numbers do indeed exist.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we have developed a significantly more general notion of classical Ramsey numbers (extending most other generalizations) and performed some basic characterizations of them using a few simple algebraic tools. This generalization allowed a better vantage to take stock of various Ramseyian problems and notice some similarities to Galois connections that hint that a better, less-cumbersome formulation might be possible. To conclude, it was demonstrated that the Green-Tao Theorem, the Twin Prime conjecture, Zhang’s bounded prime gap theorem, and Polignac’s conjecture can be viewed as statements about Ramsey numbers. This may offer potential avenues to explore Ramsey-theoretic interpretations of classical non-Ramseyian problems, and at least shows that the generalization of Ramsey numbers introduced captures a larger range of phenomena than one might initially expect.
