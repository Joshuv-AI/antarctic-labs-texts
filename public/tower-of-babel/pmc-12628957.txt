Nitidine chloride inhibits colorectal cancer by targeting BUB1: mechanistic insights from molecular dynamics simulation, spatial transcriptomics, and single-cell RNA sequencing
Authors: Ke-Jun Wu, Jin-Cheng Li, Li-Min Liu, Xiao-Jue Huang, Liu-Hui Mo, Jing-Wen Ling, Kun-Hua Xiong, Wei Zhang, Rong-Quan He, Hui Li, Gang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignancy with the efficacy of current treatments limited. Nitidine chloride (NC), a natural alkaloid, exhibits antitumor potential, but its mechanism in CRC remains unclear.ObjectiveTo investigate the antitumor effects and mechanisms of NC in CRC for clinical therapeutic strategies.MethodsA Cell Counting Kit-8 was used to assess the viability of HCT116 cells. Xenograft mice received NC (2, 4, 8 mg/kg, intraperitoneal, once every other day for 2 weeks). Transmission electron microscopy and hematoxylin-eosin staining analyzed tumor ultrastructure and pathology. RNA sequencing, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) examined gene expression and NC-budding uninhibited by benzimidazoles 1 (NC-BUB1) interaction. Single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, immunohistochemistry, and chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing explored BUB1 expression and RAD21 regulation. Immune correlation analysis was conducted using TISIDB, TIMER2.0, and deconvolution algorithms (CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, ESTIMATE, MCPcounter, quanTIseq, TIMER, xCell) to evaluate associations between BUB1 and immune factors or infiltration.ResultsNC inhibited HCT116 cells with time- and concentration-dependent half-maximal inhibitory concentration reduction. NC-treated tumors displayed apoptosis and reduced angiogenesis. Transcriptomics identified BUB1 as a core downregulated gene. Multi-omics confirmed BUB1 overexpression in CRC (1293 vs. 2299 samples; standardized mean difference = 1.77, area under the curve = 0.94) in proliferative/malignant regions. Immune analyses revealed that high BUB1 expression correlated negatively with antigen presentation molecules, chemokines/receptors, and immune cell infiltration (B cells, M2 macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts, endothelial cells), suggesting immune microenvironment suppression. BUB1 knockout inhibited CRC cells; NC reduced BUB1 expression. MDS showed a stable NC-BUB1 complex (− 8.8 kcal/mol). The RAD21–BUB1 axis exerted key regulatory roles.ConclusionNC exerts anti-CRC effects by downregulating BUB1, disrupting the RAD21–BUB1 axis, and alleviating BUB1-mediated immune suppression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04423-8.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Nitidine chloride, RAD21–BUB1 axis, Spatial transcriptomics, Single-cell sequencing, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. Although treatment methods such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy are widely used, their efficacy remains limited by factors such as tumor heterogeneity, drug resistance, and metastasis. Therefore, exploring new and effective treatment strategies is crucial. In this context, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with its thousands of years of history and rich clinical experience, has gradually attracted the attention of modern medicine. Many components of TCM have been shown to possess significant anticancer effects and exert antitumor effects through multi-target, comprehensive mechanisms. Nitidine chloride (NC) is one such widely studied active ingredient in TCM. Studies have indicated that NC can inhibit proliferation, induce apoptosis, and suppress metastasis in various tumor cells [2–4].

Budding uninhibited by benzimidazoles 1 (BUB1) is a key molecule in the cell cycle checkpoint, responsible for ensuring correct chromosome segregation during mitosis [5]. Overexpression of BUB1 is closely related to rapid proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells [6, 7]. Recent research has shown that BUB1 plays a central role in the occurrence and progression of CRC [8]. In addition, elevated BUB1 expression is strongly associated with poor clinical prognosis and advanced tumor stages, indicating its role as a driver of aggressive cancer behavior [7, 9]. Therefore, targeting BUB1 may offer a new therapeutic direction for CRC.

With the continuous advancement of TCM research, the role of traditional Chinese herbal medicines in cancer treatment has gradually been recognized, especially in regulating tumor cell cycles and promoting cell apoptosis. As an alkaloid natural product, NC has demonstrated its antitumor potential in several studies; however, its specific mechanism of action in CRC remains incompletely understood. In particular, the potential targets of NC still require further verification and exploration.

Therefore, we investigated the impact of NC on BUB1 expression and conducted an in-depth analysis of its possible mechanism of action by integrating multi-omics data. Through this research, we aimed to provide new insights and potential therapeutic strategies for CRC treatment while investigating the application of TCM in modern tumor therapy.

Methods

Detection of cell proliferation activity by CCK8 assay

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin, counted, and adjusted to a density of 5 × 10³ cells/well. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 100 µL per well and incubated at 37℃ in a 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. After the culture medium was discarded, 100 µL of medium containing drugs at different concentrations were added to each well. A blank control group, a solvent control group, and drug-treated groups with different concentrations were set up, with six replicate wells in each group. The cells were further cultured for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. Four hours before the end of culture, 10 µL of Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent were added to each well, followed by incubation at 37℃ in the dark for 4 h. The absorbance values of each well were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader, with 630 nm as the reference wavelength.

Anesthesia and euthanasia

All invasive procedures, including tumor cell inoculation and terminal tissue collection, were performed under appropriate anesthesia to minimize pain and distress in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines. Inhalational anesthesia was achieved with isoflurane (3%–4% in oxygen) for induction and maintained at 1.0–2.0% via nose cone or chamber, with surgical depth confirmed by loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex and absence of purposeful response. Core body temperature was maintained with a warming pad. Injectable anesthesia, when required for longer procedures, consisted of ketamine (80–100 mg/kg) combined with xylazine (5–10 mg/kg), administered intraperitoneally (IP) or intramuscularly. Terminal euthanasia was performed under deep isoflurane anesthesia by intraperitoneal overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150–200 mg/kg, IP) or another IACUC-approved equivalent. Cessation of respiration and cardiac activity was observed for at least 5 min, and a secondary method (thoracotomy or cervical dislocation) was applied to confirm death. All procedures were carried out by trained personnel and documented in animal procedure records.

Evaluation of in vivo antitumor activity

HCT116 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin-EDTA solution and prepared into a cell suspension of 5 × 10⁷ cells/mL with phosphate buffer saline (PBS). After mixing, 0.2 mL of the suspension was subcutaneously injected into the dorsal region (near the axilla) of 5–6-week-old nude mice. Approximately 7–10 days later, when the volume of subcutaneous tumors reached 100–150 mm³, the nude mice were randomly divided into groups (five mice per group), which were intraperitoneally injected with normal saline (control group), 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) at 5 mg/kg/week (positive control group), and NC at 2, 4, and 8 mg/kg once every 2 days (NC low-, medium-, and high-dose groups, respectively). The administration cycle lasted for 2 weeks. The longest and shortest diameters of subcutaneous xenografts were measured with vernier calipers every 5 days, and the body weight of nude mice was recorded. The general status of nude mice (hair color, mental state, food intake, water intake, and activity) was observed. On day 15 after the last dosing, all mice were humanely euthanized under deep anesthesia. Tumor tissues were dissected, rinsed with normal saline, weighed, and photographed. The tumor blocks were then aliquoted for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining and transmission electron microscopy analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

After the completion of drug administration, nude mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. Tumor tissues were dissected and immediately rinsed in physiological saline. Tumor tissues were cut into 4 mm³ pieces, quickly immersed in 2 mL centrifuge tubes containing glutaraldehyde, and fixed in a 4℃ refrigerator for 2 h. After sample preparation, the tissues were observed and photographed under a transmission electron microscope.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining

After the completion of drug administration, nude mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. Tumor tissues were dissected and immediately rinsed in physiological saline. Appropriate-sized tumor tissues were cut and immediately fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde solution for more than 24 h. Gradient dehydration was performed at room temperature (75% → 80% → 95% → 100% ethanol, 30 min each), followed by xylene clearing (30 min × 2 times). Tissues were immersed in paraffin at 52 ℃–56 ℃ for 3 h, with paraffin replaced every hour. When the tissues became transparent, they were embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned using a microtome at a thickness of 4 μm. Before dewaxing, sections were placed in a 37℃ oven for 5 min then dewaxed to hydration with xylene: xylene (Ⅰ) for 10 min → xylene (Ⅱ) for 10 min → gradient ethanol hydration (100% → 95% → 80% → 75%, 5 min each) → rinsed with distilled water for 10 min. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed with ultrapure water, differentiated with 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol for 20 s, and then repeatedly rinsed with ultrapure water. Sections were then stained with eosin for 2 min, followed by gradient dehydration (75% → 80% → 95% → 100% ethanol, 1 min each). After xylene clearing, sections were air-dried. A drop of neutral balsam was added to the center of the slide, and a coverslip was gently placed to avoid bubbles. The prepared sections were observed, photographed, and saved under a microscope.

Gene expression change analysis

Divide HCT116 cells into two groups: control group (0.1% Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO)) and NC group (6 µmol/L NC), with three replicate wells per group. After collecting logarithmic growth phase cells, adjust cell suspension concentration and seed into six-well plates. When cell confluence reaches approximately 60%, replace with culture medium containing NC or DMSO and continue culturing at 5% CO₂, 37℃ for 48 h. Send cell samples to HAIYI ERATM for whole genome sequencing. Extract total RNA using an AxyPrepTM Multisource Total RNA Miniprep Kit (Axygen, China), and construct mRNA sequencing library through mRNA purification, fragmentation, reverse transcription, adapter ligation, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. Subsequently, use the STRING (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins) database) for protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis and construction.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The three-dimensional structure of BUB1 came from the PDB database (ID: 5dmz), while the three-dimensional structure of NC came from the PubChem database (Compound CID: CID_4501). Molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) used the Gromacs2022 program [10], where small molecules used the GAFF force field, proteins used the AMBER14SB force field and the TIP3P water model [11], and protein and small molecule ligand files were merged to construct composite simulation system. The simulation was performed under constant temperature and pressure and periodic boundary conditions, and all calculations involving hydrogen bonds were constrained using the LINCS algorithm [12], step size set to 2 fs. Electrostatic interactions were calculated through the particle mesh Ewald method [13], cutoff value 1.2 nm, non-bonded interaction cutoff value 10 Å, updated every 10 steps. During simulation, the V-rescale method [14] was used to control temperature at 298 K and the Berendsen method [15] to control pressure at 1 bar. The simulation included 100 ps NVT and NPT equilibration simulation, followed by 100 ns MD simulation, saving conformations every 10 ps. After simulation completion, VMD and pymol [16] were used to analyze trajectories, combined with the g_mmpbsa program [17] for molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MMPBSA) [18] binding free energy analysis.

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) was used to measure differences between molecular structures, reflecting the degree of molecular change relative to initial structure during simulation [19]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to extract main modes of molecular motion and simplify high-dimensional data [20]. Surface electrostatic potential reflects the distribution of charges on molecular surfaces, affecting intermolecular interactions [21]. Radius of gyration (Rg) measures molecular compactness; the smaller the radius of gyration, the more compact the molecule [22]. Free energy landscape in molecular simulation depicts the free energy distribution of molecular systems under different conformations, showing conformational stability. Root mean square fluctuation helps identify flexible and rigid regions in molecules [23]. Center of mass evolution analysis was used to study binding states between small molecules and proteins [24]. Buried solvent accessible surface area (buried SASA) evaluates the strength of intermolecular interactions [22]. Hydrogen bond number and frequency analysis reflects the interaction strength of protein-ligand binding. Electrostatic and van der Waals force interaction analysis calculates the binding energy between small molecules and proteins in complexes [25]. Binding energy analysis calculates binding energy through the MM-PBSA method [26] and analyzes amino acid residue contributions to binding energy.

scRNA-seq analysis of BUB1 expression in CRC

Use Seurat to analyze scRNA-seq data from GSM6061702-GSM6061704, and remove genes expressed in < 3 cells and cells expressing fewer than 50 genes. Retain cells with > 200 genes and mitochondrial gene expression below 25%, use top 3000 highly variable genes for PCA and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering, and use marker genes to define cell types. Apply CellChat, monocle, and scMetabolism for communication and metabolic pathway analysis.

mRNA analysis of BUB1 expression in CRC

For analysis and comparison of BUB1 mRNA expression in CRC and non-CRC tissue samples, expression data came from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), International Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC), Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx), Sequence Read Archive (SRA), The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), PubMed, and ArrayExpress databases. The keywords for database searches were “colorectal cancer,” “colorectal carcinoma,” and “CRC.” Screening criteria were as follows: (1) only datasets containing human primary CRC tissue samples; (2) the experimental group must include CRC diagnostic tissue samples, and the control group normal colorectal tissue samples; and (3) experimental and control groups had to contain at least three samples each. Datasets that do not report BUB1 expression or are metastatic/recurrent CRC tissue samples were excluded. Datasets from the same GEO platform were merged into a single comprehensive matrix, and Log2(x + 1) transformation on BUB1 mRNA expression levels was performed for standardization processing. The limma and sva R packages were used to correct batch effects and the meta package (version 4.18-2.18.18) to calculate the standardized mean difference (SMD) for CRC genes, thereby revealing potential pathogenic roles of BUB1 in CRC development. Genes highly expressed in CRC tissues were screened. Screening criteria included: (1) the gene appeared in at least three independent studies; (2) the SMD value was greater than 0; and (3) the 95% confidence interval (95%CI) did not include 0. Through Spearman correlation analysis, genes co-expressed with BUB1 were screened; the screening conditions: the gene was co-expressed with BUB1 in at least 10 studies (Spearman correlation coefficient r > 0.30, P value < 0.05), and the gene set enrichment analysis package was used for enrichment analysis.

Spatial transcriptome analysis of BUB1 expression

This study used spatial transcriptome technology to analyze BUB1 expression distribution, adopting CRC tissue sections from literature [27] and performing data processing with the SpaCET software package. First, use the SpaCET package to create spatial transcriptome data objects, set minimum gene expression threshold to 1, and visualize key quality control indicators such as unique molecular identifier counts and gene numbers. Subsequently, perform cell type deconvolution analysis to identify and locate the spatial distribution of malignant regions. Finally, perform spatial expression visualization of the BUB1 gene, showing its expression intensity and distribution patterns in tissue sections, and conduct spatial communication analysis.

Methods for analyzing the correlation between BUB1 and the immune microenvironment

Transcriptome data and clinical information of CRC patients were obtained from the TCGA data portal, and additional immune-related CRC datasets were retrieved from the GEO database. Datasets with sufficient sample size and complete transcriptomic/clinical information were included, and all data were normalized and batch-corrected to build a large-scale CRC expression matrix.For immune regulatory factors, TISIDB and TIMER2.0 were used to collect immunostimulatory genes, immunosuppressive genes, antigen presentation molecules (MHC-I/II), chemokines, and their receptors. Correlations between BUB1 expression and these genes were assessed using Spearman correlation and visualized with heatmaps. For immune cell infiltration, multiple algorithms were applied, including CIBERSORT/CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, ESTIMATE, MCPcounter, quanTIseq, TIMER, and xCell. These methods collectively estimate immune and stromal cell proportions from bulk transcriptome data by using deconvolution or enrichment approaches. Correlations between BUB1 expression and immune cell abundance, ImmuneScore, and StromalScore were calculated with Spearman correlation and presented in heatmaps and scatter plots.

BUB1 expression and CRISPR gene knockout effects on CRC cell lines

Evaluate BUB1 expression levels in different CRC cell lines and the effects of CRISPR (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats) gene knockout on cell growth by using the Dependency Map (DEPMap) database. DEPMap provides gene expression, mutation, and CRISPR inhibition data for tumor cell lines. Through CRISPR gene knockout screening, use the CERES algorithm to calculate dependency scores to evaluate the importance of BUB1 in CRC cell lines. Negative dependency scores indicate that BUB1 knockout inhibits cell proliferation, suggesting a key role in cell growth, while positive dependency scores indicate that knockout promotes cell growth, suggesting it may have inhibitory effects.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Establish the control group (0.1% DMSO) and NC treatment group (6µmol/L), with three replicate wells per group. Extract total RNA according to the above methods, use Nanodrop 2000 to determine RNA concentration and purity, and store in a − 80℃ refrigerator for subsequent experiments. GAPDH serves as internal reference gene. BUB1 primer sequences: forward primer GAAAGCATGAGCAATGGGTAAA, reverse primer CCACCTGATGCAACTTCTTATG. Use primers synthesized by Takara Bio, Inc. (Otsu, Japan). Use a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with a gDNA Eraser Kit (TaKaRa, RR047A) to reverse transcribe total RNA into cDNA. Subsequently, perform PCR amplification according to Power Up™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix instructions. Experiment independently repeated three times; the average of three results provides the quantitative cycle number (CT) value. Finally, calculate the relative mRNA expression of the target gene using following formula: relative mRNA expression = 2^(−ΔΔCT).

Immunohistochemical staining

According to manufacturer instructions, evaluate protein expression levels of key target genes after NC treatment through immunohistochemistry staining. In the above CRC nude mouse xenograft experiment, collect tumor tissues treated with high, medium, or low concentrations of NC and untreated NC and 5-FU (positive control) treated tumor tissues. Embed the tissues in paraffin, perform sectioning and deparaffinization, and incubate with 3% H2O2 at room temperature for 15 min to remove endogenous peroxidase activity. Rinse with distilled water, and soak in PBS for 5 min, repeat twice. Block with 5%–10% goat serum (diluted 20-fold in PBS), incubate at room temperature for 10 min, then discard the serum. Add primary antibody solution, and incubate at 37℃ for 1–2 h. Rinse with PBS for 5 min thrice. Then add rabbit BUB1 polyclonal antibody (1:50, #13330, ProteinTech Group, Inc, Wuhan, China), and incubate at 37℃ for 30 min. Rinse again with PBS for 5 min thrice, add DAB staining solution, perform 3–15 min color development reaction, thoroughly rinse with tap water, and perform counterstaining, dehydration, clearing and mounting. Use a microphotography system (×200; DM18, Leica, Germany) to capture images, perform triple replication experiments, and ensure photography results under double-blind conditions. Finally, use Image J software to calculate the average optical density (OD) values of positive cells to analyze the protein expression levels of target genes.

ChIP-Seq data supporting RAD21 regulation of BUB1

ChIP-Seq data from the Cistrome data browser were analyzed to study RAD21 and its related epigenetic modification markers expression in CRC samples, especially the expression in the HCT116 cell line.

Statistical analysis

To evaluate BUB1 expression differences in CRC, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for inter-group comparisons (statistical significance level P < 0.05). In meta-analysis, when the heterogeneity was low (I² < 50%), the fixed effects model was used, while when heterogeneity was high, the random effects model was applied. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated with the pROC package, and STATA 18.0 drew the synthetic receiver operating characteristic (sROC) curve. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to evaluate BUB1 expression discriminatory ability; higher AUC values indicate greater expression differences. Begg’s test was adopted to evaluate publication bias; P > 0.05 indicated no significant bias.

NC can inhibit the growth of CRC

With the extension of treatment time, the IC₅₀ values of the drug on HCT116 cells showed a time- and concentration-dependent decrease (Fig. 2A). HE staining results are shown in Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. In the control group, cells had intact morphology and uniform size, with large, round, dense blue-stained nuclei; cell membranes were intact and clearly visible; cytoplasm was uniform in texture; numerous newly formed blood vessels and microvascular tissues were observed in the tissues, with flattened vascular endothelial cell nuclei visible in the vessel walls and abundant red blood cells in the lumen. In the NC group, cells showed abnormal morphology and poor integrity, with broken or absent cell membranes; large necrotic areas with vacuolation were present; and nuclei were significantly pycnotic, condensed into small blue granules. Numerous scattered cell debris were observed, and few blood vessels, microvessels, and red blood cells were found in the tissues (Fig. 2B–C). In the control group, tumor cells exhibited normal morphology and intact structure, with intact cell membranes without damage and abundant microvilli on the membrane surface. Abundant and intact organelles were present in the cytoplasm; mitochondria had intact structures with distinct cristae, the nucleus was intact with a clear nuclear membrane, and chromatin in the nucleus was uniformly and densely distributed. Scattered microvessels and blood cells were observed in tumor tissues, with intact vascular morphology. In the NC group, cell membranes showed abnormal morphology with an uneven surface, presenting shrinkage or even damage, and microvilli were reduced or even absent. Organelle structures in the cytoplasm were unclear; mitochondria had incomplete cristae, showing swelling and hypertrophy, and numerous vacuoles were present in the cytoplasm. The nuclear membrane was damaged, with sparsely distributed chromatin in the nucleus. In late apoptotic cells, organelle swelling and vacuolation were observed, and apoptotic bodies enclosed by nuclear membranes were common. Eventually, cells lost their intact morphology, with broken membrane surfaces, disrupted and disappeared organelles, condensed chromatin leakage, and cell death. Few microvessels and blood cells were observed in tumor tissues.

BUB1 is a potential target of NC acting on HCT116

Regarding the protein interaction network of downregulated genes after NC acts on HCT116, BUB1 is located at the center of the network, with close interactions with key cell division proteins such as BUB1, MKI67, and BUB1 (Fig. 3A). Downregulated genes after NC acts on HCT116 are enriched in pathways such as “cell cycle,” “DNA replication” and “microtubule binding,” and “chromosome condensation” and “mitotic sister chromatid separation” are closely related to cell cycle regulation (Fig. 3B). To further explore the relationship between BUB1 and NC, the study conducted MDS. The NC-BUB1 complex showed stability: RMSD gradually stabilized (Fig. 4A), and Rg remained stable (Fig. 4B). The distance between ligand and protein center and binding site remained stable (Fig. 4C–D), and buried SASA was constant (Fig. 4E). The ligand had high conformational retention (Fig. 4F). VDW and ELE in the complex were basically stable (Fig. 4G). LEU-793, ILE-945, etc. were key binding amino acids (Fig. 4H), with H-bond numbers fluctuating between 0 and 2 with the protein (Fig. 4I). VAL-801, GLY-872, LEU-793, ILE-924, and VAL-819 in the protein formed pi-sulfur, pi-sigma, and pi-alkyl hydrophobic interactions with the small molecule, while amino acids such as HIE-791, GLY-794, and LYS-821 formed van der Waals force interactions with the small molecule. The molecular docking binding energy was − 8.8 kcal/mol (Fig. 4J).

Continuing exploration revealed that the conformational distribution of the small molecule was relatively concentrated, with one conformation appearing with high frequency (Fig. 5A). The complex had a lower energy state with an overall stable structure (Fig. 5B). The small molecule bound to the internal region of the protein, with the binding site surface mainly showing negative charge (Fig. 5C). The amino acids around the small molecule had low flexibility; therefore, the binding between the small molecule and protein was relatively stable (Fig. 5D). Additionally, the hydrogen bond stability between the small molecule and protein was relatively low (Fig. 5E). It was noted that in the complex, the van der Waals force interaction energy ΔEvdw was higher than the electrostatic interaction ΔEele, and both were higher than the hydrophobic interaction ΔEnonpol. Therefore, in the composition of binding energy, van der Waals force interactions play the main role, electrostatic interactions play a secondary role, and hydrophobic interactions play a supplementary role. The ΔEMMPBSA of the small molecule and protein was − 138.478 ± 2.6 kJ/mol, with high binding energy and affinity between the two (Table 1).

Multi-omics confirms BUB1 high expression in CRC

In focusing on BUB1 at the single cell level in CRC, the study first used UMAP to perform dimensionality reduction clustering on CRC samples, identifying nine major cell types: B cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, myeloid cells, stem-like cells, and T cells (Fig. 6A). Cell type composition was similar among the three samples, with epithelial cells having the highest proportion, followed by fibroblasts and various immune cells (Fig. 6B). BUB1 had high expression in specific cell populations, with density distribution results showing it was mainly concentrated in some epithelial cells and proliferation-related cell subpopulations (Fig. 6C). Different cell types have specific molecular markers, with the top three marker genes for each cell type, such as EPCAM, KRT19 (epithelial cells), COL1A1, ACTA2 (fibroblasts), CD3D, and CD3E (T cells) highly expressed in corresponding cells (Fig. 6D). Cell communication analysis results indicated strong interactions between epithelial cells and fibroblasts, and endothelial cells and close connections between fibroblasts and immune cells (such as T cells and dendritic cells; Fig. 6E–F). Single-cell metabolic analysis results revealed significant metabolic heterogeneity among different cell types (Fig. 6G), where epithelial cells and stromal cells exhibited significantly increased activity in energy metabolism pathways such as glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, while immune cells (such as T cells and B cells) were more active in amino acid and nucleotide metabolism-related pathways. The TCA cycle, glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis and pentose phosphate pathway showed heterogeneous active distribution among different cell types (Fig. 6H).

Further exploring BUB1 mRNA levels, the study included data from 21 platforms with non-CRC: 1293 samples; CRC: 2299 samples (Fig. 7A). BUB1 was highly expressed in CRC: SMD = 1.77 CL(1.33–2.22), AUC = 0.94 CL(0.92–0.96). Begg’s test indicated no publication bias (P > 0.05), and funnel plots showed no heterogeneity (Fig. 7B). BUB1 co-expressed genes (R > 0.5, P < 0.05) were significantly enriched in cell cycle-related pathways, including mitotic spindle checkpoint, cell cycle checkpoint, sister chromatid cohesion separation, and cell cycle mitosis, with NES values all > 4.1 (all P < 0.001; Fig. 8A). The pathways significantly enriched by BUB1 co-expressed genes displayed highly interconnected network structures, forming functional modules centered on “Cell Cycle Mitotic” and “Cell Cycle.” Among them, “Cell Cycle” and “Cell Cycle Mitotic” gene sets were the largest, indicating the involvement of more genes; close connections could be seen between pathways such as cell cycle checkpoint, spindle checkpoint, sister chromatid cohesion dissolution, and M phase (Fig. 8B).

Tissue-level spatial transcriptome analysis showed that HE staining (Fig. 9A) clearly displayed CRC tissue structural characteristics, and after deconvolution analysis, tumor malignant regions were mainly concentrated in red-yellow marked areas (Fig. 9B). Importantly, BUB1 expression was spatially enriched in these malignant regions, with expression scores approaching 1.0 (Fig. 9C), highlighting its close association with proliferative tumor fronts and invasive niches. Spatial clustering analysis divided tissues into seven functional cluster regions (Fig. 9D), and intercellular signaling pathway interaction analysis was conducted. Results showed that in the MK signaling pathway, there were more interactions between Cluster1, Cluster2, and Cluster5, with high interaction intensity (Fig. 9E–H); in the MIF signaling pathway, connections between Cluster4, Cluster6 and other cluster regions were relatively frequent (Fig. 9I–K). Importance analysis results indicated that different clusters play differentiated roles in signal transmission, such as some clusters serving as main “senders” or “influencers” in pathways, suggesting BUB1 may participate in functional coordination between tumor cells and tumor microenvironment remodeling by regulating specific signaling pathways.

With regard to the relationship between BUB1 and the immune microenvironment in CRC, high BUB1 expression was predominantly associated with negative correlations. At the level of immune regulatory factors, BUB1 expression was negatively correlated with multiple antigen presentation molecules (HLA-A/B/C/E/F/G); some immunosuppressive factors, such as TGFBR1 and CD276, also showed negative correlations; in addition, several chemokines (e.g., CCL14 and CCL15) and their receptors (CCR and CXCR families) were negatively correlated with BUB1 expression (Fig. 10A). Regarding immune cell infiltration, integration of multiple algorithms (CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, ESTIMATE, MCPcounter, quanTIseq, TIMER, and xCell) revealed that high BUB1 expression was negatively correlated with both ImmuneScore and StromalScore. In determining whether BUB1 promotes CRC cell growth, CRISPR technology was used. BUB1 was highly expressed in multiple CRC cell lines. In the HCT116 cell line, BUB1’s CRISPR score was approximately − 1.0, suggesting its important role in cell survival, and knockout would significantly inhibit cell proliferation. Among all CRC-related cell lines, the three with the most significant inhibitory effects were CCK81, SNUC5, and SNU81, with CRISPR scores approaching − 1.5 (Fig. 11).

NC can downregulate BUB1 expression

Transcriptome sequencing results showed that BUB1 mRNA expression levels decreased in HCT116 cells after NC treatment (Fig. 12A, P < 0.05). Further qPCR validation results indicated that after 48 h treatment with 6 µmol/L NC, BUB1 mRNA expression was reduced (Fig. 12B, ***P < 0.001). In vivo immunohistochemical detection results (Fig. 12C) showed that NC could downregulate BUB1 protein expression in a dose-dependent manner, with the high-dose group (NC-H) showing inhibitory effects comparable to the positive control 5-Fu group. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that compared to the control group, the average OD values of all NC dose groups and the 5-Fu group were significantly reduced (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

NC may exert effects through the RAD21–BUB1 axis

Multi-omics comprehensive analysis suggests that NC may exert inhibitory effects on CRC by targeting the RAD21–BUB1 axis, with a binding score of 1.022 (Fig. 13). Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 13A) showed that in the intersection of BUB1-PEG (positive co-expression genes, BUB1 positively correlated co-expressed genes) with HCT116 transcription factor regulatory gene set, CRC SMD > 0 highly expressed gene set, and NC treatment downregulated genes, RAD21 was located at the core position. Epigenetic maps (Fig. 13B) suggest that RAD21 has high epigenetic regulatory signals at specific loci in the genome. Multi-dataset correlation analysis (Fig. 13C) indicated that in multiple independent CRC datasets, RAD21 and BUB1 expression exhibited stable and significant positive correlations (R values generally > 0.25, P values significant). The transcriptome volcano plot (Fig. 13D) showed that RAD21 was significantly downregulated after NC treatment (located in the lower left quadrant, P < 0.05). SMD analysis of RAD21 (Fig. 13E) further confirmed that RAD21 expression in CRC was significantly higher than in normal tissues (pooled SMD > 0, 95% CI does not cross 0).

Discussion

This study systematically demonstrates that the natural alkaloid NC exerts significant anti-CRC activity both in vitro and in vivo, and it further clarifies BUB1 as its core molecular target. Consistent with previous reports on other tumors, NC exerts antitumor effects by inhibiting the cell cycle and promoting apoptosis. The unique contribution of this work lies in integrating multi-omics approaches to reveal the pivotal role of BUB1 in CRC and to validate that NC suppresses tumor growth by downregulating BUB1, thereby interfering with cell cycle and survival signals.

At the single-cell and spatial transcriptomic level, BUB1 was highly expressed in proliferative cancer cell subsets and malignant regions. Similarly, other studies have shown that BUB1 is highly co-expressed with cell cycle genes such as MKI67 [28, 29] and TOP2A [30, 31] and is tightly associated with highly proliferative tumor populations. Furthermore, immune analyses indicated that high BUB1 expression was negatively correlated with antigen presentation molecules (e.g., HLA-A/B/C), chemokine pathways, and immune cell infiltration. The findings suggest that BUB1 not only drives tumor proliferation but also actively contributes to immune evasion by suppressing antigen presentation and limiting immune infiltration. This aligns with studies reporting reduced immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint upregulation in BUB1-high tumors [5], suggesting that BUB1 may contribute to immune evasion. Combined with CRISPR knockout experiments, our data further confirmed the essential role of BUB1 in CRC cell growth: deletion of this gene markedly impaired cell viability, indicating that BUB1 is not merely a biomarker but a functional driver.

At the mechanistic level, this study proposes that NC may act through modulation of the RAD21–BUB1 axis. RAD21, a key component of the cohesin complex, is indispensable for chromosome segregation and DNA repair, and its overexpression is associated with poor prognosis in bladder cancer [32] and breast cancer [33]. BUB1 and RAD21 have been implicated in cooperative roles during spindle assembly and mitotic regulation. NC intervention may disrupt this stable interaction, thereby perturbing critical checkpoints of the cell cycle. This hypothesis resonates with recent studies suggesting that targeting chromosome segregation complexes can effectively prevent rapid tumor cell proliferation, which provides a novel mechanistic perspective on how natural compounds may act. Similarly, studies have found that exosomal circRNAs play a crucial role in chemotherapy resistance for various cancers and influence tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis. Additionally, programmed cell death mechanisms, such as ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, are closely associated with tumor progression in gastrointestinal cancers, offering potential new therapeutic targets [34, 35]. Meanwhile, the application of artificial intelligence in oncology has been increasingly emphasized, with a growing need for global collaboration. It is expected to enhance diagnostic precision and help optimize therapeutic strategies [36].

In conclusion, this study not only uncovers NC’s antitumor mechanism in CRC via targeting BUB1 but also highlights its importance in cell cycle regulation and immune microenvironment remodeling. The findings deepen our understanding of the oncogenic role of BUB1 and provide a theoretical foundation for the development of novel NC-based therapeutic strategies. Future research should explore the potential of combining NC with chemotherapy or immune checkpoint inhibitors and conduct larger scale preclinical studies to evaluate safety and efficacy for accelerated clinical translation.

Limitations​

This study has several limitations that need to be addressed. First, the in vitro experiments were primarily conducted using the HCT116 cell line, lacking validation in other CRC cell lines with different genetic backgrounds, which may limit the generalizability of the findings regarding NC’s antitumor effects. Second, although the xenograft mouse model confirmed NC’s in vivo efficacy, the model does not fully recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment of human CRC, such as immune cell infiltration and stromal interactions. Third, the molecular mechanism studies focused on the RAD21–BUB1 axis, and other potential targets or signaling pathways involved in NC’s anti-CRC activity remain unexplored. Additionally, the pharmacokinetic properties of NC, such as its bioavailability, metabolism, and toxicity in vivo, were not evaluated, but they are critical for future clinical translation. Finally, the sample size of multi-omics analyses, while comprehensive, may be influenced by batch effects across different datasets, despite efforts to correct for such biases.​.

Conclusion​

In summary, this study demonstrates that NC exerts potent anti-CRC effects both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, NC downregulates BUB1 and disrupts the RAD21–BUB1 axis, thereby suppressing proliferation and inducing apoptosis. Multi-omics analyses confirm that BUB1 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and proliferative subsets, and MDS validates stable NC-BUB1 binding. Immune analyses additionally indicate that BUB1 is associated with impaired antigen presentation and reduced immune infiltration, supporting its role as a malignant driver. Together, the findings highlight NC as a promising natural alkaloid targeting BUB1 and thus a potential therapeutic strategy for CRC.
