The Association Between Maternal Smoking During Pregnancy and Neonatal Intensive Care Unit Admission Among Full-Term Infants: A Finnish National Cohort Study
Authors: Kalle Korhonen, Lotta S Holopainen, Mika Gissler, Mikael O Ekblad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMaternal smoking during pregnancy (SDP) is associated with adverse neonatal outcomes, but the specific conditions contributing to morbidity among term infants are poorly characterized. We examined whether maternal SDP increases the risk of neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission and antibiotic treatment during the first week of life, and identified NICU diagnoses more common among exposed infants.MethodsThis register-based cohort study included all full-term (≥ 37+0 weeks’ gestation) singleton births in Finland from 2006 to 2018, using data from the Finnish Medical Birth Register and Hospital Discharge Register (n = 795,836; 95.2% of singleton births). Maternal smoking was categorized as no smoking, quit smoking during the first trimester (early smoking), or continued smoking after the first trimester. Primary outcomes were NICU admission and antibiotic treatment during the first week of life. Secondary outcomes included neonatal diagnoses and perinatal mortality. Stillbirths and early neonatal deaths were excluded from the analyses of treatments and diagnoses. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) were estimated using logistic regression, adjusting for maternal age, parity, prepregnancy BMI, mode of delivery, and year of delivery.ResultsContinued smoking was associated with increased odds of NICU admission (aOR 1.43) and antibiotic treatment (aOR 1.22), while early smoking was associated with more modest increases (aORs 1.17 and 1.19, respectively), compared with no exposure. Both smoking exposures were associated with an increased risk of bacterial sepsis or congenital pneumonia. Neonatal hypoglycemia was increased only with continued smoking. Continued smoking was also associated with an increased the risk stillbirth (aOR, 1.49) whereas early smoking was not associated with perinatal mortality.ConclusionMaternal smoking during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of infections and metabolic disturbances requiring intensive care among term infants. Continued smoking also increased the risk of stillbirth. These findings further support the importance of smoking cessation during pregnancy.

Introduction

The prevalence of smoking during pregnancy (SDP) varies significantly across countries and regions and is influenced by factors such as socioeconomic status, education, and access to health care services.1,2 Although the prevalence of SDP has declined in Finland, 6.3% of pregnant women reported smoking in 2024.3 Smoking during pregnancy remains a significant public health concern, with approximately 6–10% of pregnant women in the Americas and Europe continuing to smoke despite well-established health risks.1

Maternal smoking during pregnancy is associated with adverse effects on both the mother and the developing fetus. Carbon monoxide and nicotine in tobacco smoke reduce fetal oxygen delivery and may impair fetal growth and development.4–6 Prenatal tobacco exposure has been linked to low birth weight, preterm birth, neonatal morbidity, and congenital anomalies.7–9 In addition, in utero exposure to maternal smoking has been associated with an increased risk of sudden infant death syndrome, respiratory disease, neurodevelopmental impairments, and obesity and metabolic dysfunction later in life.8–10

Approximately 10% to 15% of newborns are admitted to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).11,12 In addition to prematurity and congenital anomalies, common indications for NICU admission include respiratory disorders, infections, and transitional adaptation problems such as hypoglycemia. NICU admission is associated with substantial parental distress,13,14 and even milder neonatal morbidity may have long-term consequences.15 Previous studies have reported an association between prenatal smoking exposure and increased NICU admission rates.16,17 Most previous studies have focused on preterm infants or mixed populations, leaving full-term infants relatively understudied. Consequently, the specific clinical conditions leading to NICU admission among term infants remain incompletely characterized.

In this study, we examined the association between maternal smoking during pregnancy and neonatal morbidity among term infants. We hypothesized that prenatal tobacco exposure is associated with an increased risk of NICU admission for selected neonatal conditions and therefore focused our analyses on specific admission diagnoses.

Materials and Methods

Data Sources

This study utilized data from the Finnish Medical Birth Register and the Finnish Hospital Discharge Register. The current register controller, the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare (THL), conducted an ethical evaluation and granted permission to use the confidential health register data, as required by national data protection legislation. Pregnant women’s unique, encrypted personal identification numbers were used to link data across registers. The data linkages were performed by the national statistical authorities, and only unidentifiable data were provided to the researchers.

The Medical Birth Register includes all live births and stillbirths of fetuses with a gestational age of 22 weeks or more, or a birth weight of at least 500 grams. Data are collected from all delivery hospitals and, in the rare case of home births, from the attending healthcare personnel. The register contains information on maternal and infant identification numbers, maternal background characteristics, healthcare and interventions during pregnancy and delivery, and the newborn’s health status up to 7 days of age. The Medical Birth Register is considered a complete and comprehensive record of all births and newborns in Finland. Data quality studies have shown that most of the register’s content corresponds well or satisfactorily with hospital records.18,19

The Hospital Discharge Register includes data on all inpatient episodes (including hospitalizations requiring at least one overnight stay) in both public and private hospitals since 1967, and on outpatient visits to public hospitals since 1998. The register contains information on the patient demographics, hospitalization periods, procedures, and diagnoses coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD). The tenth revision of ICD-10, in use in Finland since 1996, was applied consistently throughout the study period. A systematic review has shown that the completeness and accuracy of the register are generally rated as satisfactory to very good.20

Study Sample

The study flow diagram is shown in Figure 1. The study population consisted of newborns from all singleton pregnancies (n=835,551) in Finland between 2006 and 2018. Preterm infants born before 37+0 gestational weeks were excluded (n=39,715). The final study population included 795,836 full-term newborns, representing 95.2% of all singleton births during the study period. Perinatal deaths (n=1127), including stillbirths (n=793) and deaths within the first week of life (n=334), as documented in the Medical Birth Register, were excluded from analyses of treatments and diagnoses.Figure 1Study flow diagram.A flowchart of a study population from singleton pregnancies in Finland, detailing exclusions and final numbers.

Study flow diagram.

Information on smoking during pregnancy and other maternal and neonatal background factors was obtained from the Finnish Medical Birth Register. Data on smoking were collected from the pregnant women during antenatal care visits by midwives. SDP was categorized into three groups: (1) no smoking during pregnancy, (2) quit smoking during the first trimester (early smoking), and (3) continued smoking after the first trimester (continued smoking).

Neonatal Outcome Data

Information on neonatal intensive care unit admission, antibiotic treatment within the first week of life, and perinatal mortality was obtained from the Medical Birth Register. Neonatal diagnoses for NICU admission were obtained from both the Hospital Discharge Register and the Medical Birth Register.

Statistical Analysis

Logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between SDP and neonatal outcomes. Smoking status was included as the independent categorical variable, and each neonatal outcome was treated as a dependent categorical variable. First, unadjusted models were calculated, followed by adjusted models. The adjusted models included maternal age as a continuous covariate and parity, prepregnancy BMI, mode of delivery and year of delivery as categorical covariates. Adjustment for socioeconomic factors was not possible, as these data were not available in the registers. Adjusted analyses were conducted as complete-case analyses including only participants with non-missing data on all covariates included in the models (n = 760,032 for the main analyses on NICU admission, antibiotic treatment, and diagnosis-specific NICU admissions, and n = 761,074 for the analyses on mortality).

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analyses of maternal smoking in relation to NICU admission, antibiotic treatment, and diagnosis-specific NICU admissions among newborns delivered vaginally. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed, and no evidence of multicollinearity was detected. Formal goodness-of-fit testing was not emphasized because the very large sample size may render the Hosmer–Lemeshow test overly sensitive to minor deviations from model fit.

The data analysis was performed using commercially available software (SAS, version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina). Differences were calculated using 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Associations were considered statistically significant if the 95% CI did not include the null value.

The study population included 795,836 term newborns. Among mothers, 82.8% did not smoke during pregnancy, 5.4% quit smoking during the first trimester, and 9.1% continued smoking beyond the first trimester. Maternal smoking status was unknown for 21,657 newborns (2.7%).

Women who did not smoke had the highest mean age (30.2 years [SD, 5.2]), while those who continued smoking were youngest (27.0 years [SD, 5.9]). The proportion of primiparous women was greatest among those who quit smoking during the first trimester (58.9%). A normal prepregnancy BMI (20–24.9 kg/m2) was most common among non-smokers (51.2%), whereas underweight and obesity were more prevalent among women who smoked.

Newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking had lower mean birth weight and head circumference (3425 g [SD, 471.2] and 34.7 cm [SD, 1.4], respectively) compared with newborns unexposed to smoking (3593 g [SD, 463.6] and 35.1 cm [SD, 1.4]).

The prevalence of NICU admission was highest among newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking (119.9 per 1000 births) and lowest among those unexposed (77.9 per 1000 births). Compared with newborns unexposed to smoking, the odds of NICU admission were 43% higher among those exposed to continued maternal smoking and 17% higher among those exposed to early maternal smoking.

Need for Antibiotic Treatment

The incidence of antibiotic treatment was highest among newborns exposed to early maternal smoking (56.0 per 1000 births) and lowest among those unexposed (41.0 per 1000 births). Compared with the unexposed group, the odds of requiring antibiotic treatment were 22% higher among newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking and 20% higher among those exposed to early maternal smoking.

The risk of bacterial sepsis or congenital pneumonia was highest among newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking (aOR, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.24–1.39]), followed by those exposed to early maternal smoking (aOR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.14–1.30]) compared with the unexposed group.

For respiratory distress of newborn (ICD-10 code P22), early maternal smoking was associated with a slightly increased risk (aOR, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.02–1.16]), whereas continued smoking was not significantly associated (aOR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.93–1.04]).

The incidence on neonatal hypoglycemia was highest among newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking (28.4 per 1000 births; aOR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.46–1.61]) compared with the unexposed.

Neonatal withdrawal symptoms were markedly more common among newborns exposed to continued smoking (aOR, 90.18 [95% CI, 65.45–124.27]) and moderately more common among those exposed to early maternal smoking (aOR, 6.34 [95% CI, 3.65–11.00]).

The overall incidence of any neonatal diagnosis was highest among newborns exposed to continued maternal smoking (81.4 per 1000 births), followed by those exposed to early maternal smoking (76.7 per 1000 births), and lowest among the unexposed newborns (65.7 per 1000 births). Continued maternal smoking was associated with a significantly increased risk of any neonatal diagnosis (aOR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.23–1.31]) compared with no exposure.

The results for NICU admission, antibiotic treatment during the first week of life, and diagnosis-specific NICU admissions did not differ between newborns with unknown smoking exposure and those with no smoking exposure.

In sensitivity analyses restricted to vaginal deliveries, the results for NICU admission, antibiotic treatment during the first week of life, and diagnosis-specific NICU admissions were consistent with those of the primary analyses (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

In this nationwide register-based cohort study, maternal smoking during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of neonatal intensive care unit admission among term infants. Specifically, infants born to women who smoked were at higher risk of bacterial sepsis or congenital pneumonia and neonatal hypoglycemia.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting higher NICU admission rates among infants exposed to maternal smoking during pregnancy.16,17 Prematurity itself is an indication for NICU admission, and infants born before 35+0 weeks’ gestation are generally observed for apnea even in the absence of other clinical concerns. Because preterm births were excluded from the present study, the observed increase in NICU admissions among term infants more accurately reflects early neonatal morbidity associated with prenatal tobacco exposure.

Maternal smoking was associated with an increased need for antibiotic treatment and a higher incidence of neonatal infections, including sepsis or pneumonia. These findings align with earlier reports16,17 and support the hypothesis that in utero tobacco exposure may adversely affect neonatal immune function. Smoking is a well-established risk factor for preterm birth, and prematurity itself increases the risk for neonatal sepsis.21 However, our findings among term infants emphasize a possible direct link between smoking and neonatal sepsis. Tobacco smoke contains numerous toxic compounds that cross the placenta and may impair fetal immune system development. Supporting this mechanism, Noakes et al reported attenuated innate immune responses among infants exposed to maternal smoking, evidenced by altered cytokine profiles in cord blood.22 More recent evidence suggests that smoking-related alterations in the neonatal microbiome may further contribute to increased infection susceptibility.23,24

Maternal smoking during pregnancy is not an established independent risk factor for respiratory distress in term newborns. Indeed, some studies have reported lower rates of respiratory distress syndrome among preterm neonates exposed to maternal smoking.25,26 In the present study, maternal smoking was associated with a modest increase in the risk of early neonatal respiratory distress. However, this association was observed only among newborns exposed to smoking in early pregnancy and not among those with continued exposure throughout gestation. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as residual confounding cannot be excluded in this register-based study despite adjustment for key perinatal covariates.

A strong association was observed between maternal smoking during pregnancy and an increased risk of neonatal withdrawal symptoms requiring NICU admission. Although data on other maternal substance use were not available in the registers, this association is likely attributable to the high co-occurrence of smoking among mothers who use substances associated with neonatal withdrawal, particularly opioids.27

An increased risk of neonatal hypoglycemia was also observed among infants exposed to maternal smoking during pregnancy. Although a direct causal relationship cannot be determined, several indirect mechanisms may contribute. Maternal smoking increases the risk of intrauterine growth restriction, a well-established risk factor for neonatal hypoglycemia.28,29 Some studies have also reported an association between maternal smoking and gestational diabetes mellitus, although findings have been inconsistent.30,31 In addition, maternal smoking has been linked to lower rates of breastfeeding initiation and exclusivity,32,33 which may further contribute to an increased risk of early neonatal hypoglycemia.

Consistent with previous literature, maternal smoking during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of stillbirth.16,34 However, unlike some previous studies,35,36 we observed no increased risk of early neonatal death. This finding may reflect Finland’s low neonatal mortality rate37 and the country’s comprehensive, high-quality perinatal care system.

A major strength of this study is the use of extensive, high-quality national registry data covering approximately 95% of singleton pregnancies in Finland over a 13-year period. Finland’s publicly funded and universally accessible health care system minimizes socioeconomic selection bias and enhances the generalizability of the findings. In addition, the registries provide systematic documentation of clinical diagnoses, supporting the reliability of the results.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. Smoking status was based on maternal self-report and may therefore be subject to reporting bias. In addition, data on partner smoking and the use of alternative nicotine products were not available, although both may influence neonatal outcomes. The registers also lacked information on potentially important confounders, including maternal nutrition, alcohol use, and socioeconomic status. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment for available covariates.

A notable strength of the study is the ability to distinguish smoking limited to early pregnancy from continued smoking throughout pregnancy, rather than relying on a binary exposure classification. Finally, the generalizability of these findings to countries with different healthcare systems, population characteristics, or smoking prevalence rates may be limited.

Conclusion

In summary, maternal smoking during pregnancy was associated with increased risks of NICU admission, neonatal infections, and hypoglycemia among term infants. Although smoking was linked to a higher risk of stillbirth, no increase in early neonatal mortality was observed. These findings highlight the importance of sustained public health efforts to prevent tobacco use during pregnancy to improve neonatal health outcomes. However, the results of the present study should be replicated in other settings before widespread generalization.
