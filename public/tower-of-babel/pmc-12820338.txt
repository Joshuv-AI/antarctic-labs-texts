Evolution of chromosomes and nuclear architectures of amniotes
Authors: Jing Liu, Qi Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chromosomes of mammals and reptiles have diverged extensively in sequence and three-dimensional (3D) nuclear organization since their last common amniote ancestor. The fine-scale evolutionary patterns and mechanisms underlying this divergence and the relationship between chromosome rearrangements and 3D genome architecture remain unclear. Here we reconstruct ancestral linkage groups (ALGs) of the amniote ancestor and its descendent nodes using chromosome-level assemblies from 36 species, including two newly generated genomes. We infer that most microchromosomes originated in the jawed vertebrate ancestor were retained as distinct ALGs across all ancestral amniote nodes. They were mainly involved in fusions with each other in reptiles, but underwent frequent translocations with the larger ALGs (macro-ALGs) during the mammalian evolution. Despite extensive rearrangements, sequences derived from micro-ALGs consistently occupy nuclear interior positions in both reptiles and mammals, likely enriched near nuclear speckles, supported by published lamina-positioning data and our lamin-B1 ChIP-seq data. In contrast, mammals independently evolved interchromosomal interactions associated with lineage specific transposable elements, reflecting local innovations in nuclear architecture. Together, these results reveal contrasting rearrangement regimes in mammals and reptiles, yet a conserved higher-order nuclear organization linked to ancient micro- versus macro-ALG chromatin segregation, suggesting deep constraints on chromosome evolution.

Introduction

Chromosomes are folded at hierarchical levels and can occupy individual ‘territories’1 within the confined three-dimensional (3D) space of interphase nuclei2–4. Studying how chromatin is spatially arranged comprises one of the most fundamental tasks of molecular biology in understanding the principles of genome replication and long-range transcription regulation. It becomes clear now that according to the genome-wide interaction patterns charted by high throughput chromatin conformation capture (e.g., Hi-C) techniques, the genome can be partitioned into transcriptionally active or inactive (termed A or B) nuclear compartments5 roughly corresponding to euchromatin or heterochromatin domains, or it can be further divided into subcompartments (A1, A2, B1, B2 and B3) that associate with respective nuclear bodies6,7. At a finer scale, the genome is folded into topologically associated domains (TADs)6,8 through active loop extrusion or self-organization mechanisms9.

Compared to chromatin folding, less is known about how chromatin is spatially positioned along the nuclear radius. By fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), the group of Thomas Cremer first provided unequivocal evidence for a separate chromosome territory (CT) occupied by individual chromosomes through labeling each human chromosome by different colors10. His team also proposed that the radial positions of chromosomes are correlated with their sizes11. This is later supported by the pattern that the short and gene/GC-rich microchromosomes of reptiles are found to be distributed in the nuclear interior, and other large and gene-poor macrochromosomes near the nuclear periphery12,13. However, human chromosome 18 (chr18) and chr19, despite their similar sizes, occupy distinct positions respectively toward the nuclear periphery and in the nuclear interior, which is probably associated with their different gene densities14. Therefore, both factors of chromosome size and gene density seem to contribute to the divergent nuclear organization of reptiles, including birds, from that of mammals15, although it remains unclear whether the spatially segregated chromosomal distribution of reptiles reflects an ancestral or a derived state.

Parallel to the efforts by molecular cell biologists, evolutionary biologists have been long interested at reconstructing the trajectories of chromosome rearrangements that shaped the linear genomes of different phyla. Now such studies have also been transiting from the cytogenetic16–18 into the genomic era19–24. Many reconstruction works were initiated using scaffold-level draft genomes19,20 before the recent emergence of large numbers of chromosome-level genomes, facilitated by another application of Hi-C in providing linkage information5. It is established that various types of chromosome rearrangements are associated with both speciation25 and many malignant and congenital diseases26. It turns out the hypothesis proposed by H. J. Muller back in the 1940s that gene content within the same chromosomal element (later termed “Muller element”) remain mostly conserved27 during evolution of Drosophila species can be well extrapolated to many animal phyla28–31, including the amniotes to be focused in this work. Such ancestral chromosomal elements (termed hereafter ancestral linkage groups, or ALGs) exhibit strong conservation of gene content and genomic sequences within the same linkage group across most of the descendant lineages. A prominent example is the microchromosomes that are widely present and conserved among extant reptile (including birds) species but absent in mammals12,13,32–36. Many groups including us inferred that microchromosomes have an ancient origin dated to the ancestor of jawed vertebrates13,30,37,38. In addition, It is also well documented that in most reptiles, especially birds, microchromosomes show strong interchromosomal interactions and central nuclear positioning13,32,39–41. Previous cytogenetic and genomic studies suggested more extensive chromosome rearrangements, e.g., intrachromosomal inversions, interchromosomal fusions and translocations occurred during the karyotype evolution of mammals18,19,23,36,42–44. But it remains unclear how quantitatively different the tempo and mode of chromosome evolution within and between the reptiles and mammals are, since their divergence from the amniote ancestor. What’s more, lineage-specific genome reshuffling has been shown to generate distinct chromatin configurations, underscoring the interplay between chromosome evolution and nuclear organization in different mammalian lineages45,46. These findings highlight the need for broader investigation into how diverse evolutionary trajectories of chromosomes are connected to nuclear architecture, including their spatial positioning within the nucleus. So far only limited cytogenetic comparison suggested that human chr18 and 19 have a conserved nuclear position across primates during their 20-30 MY (million years) of divergence47,48. And many other works, for example, between Drosophila49 or mammalian species50–53, focused on characterizing the interspecific conservation of TAD boundaries rather than the chromosome-level nuclear architectures.

In this work, we aim to provide insights into the important question of how chromosome rearrangements have shaped the evolution of nuclear organization in amniotes. This is motivated by the functional significance of chromosome nuclear organization10,54, as well as our recent accomplishments of the high-quality chromosome-level genomes of the key early-branching species of mammals and birds, i.e., platypus22 and emu55, that allow inference of their respective ancestors. By incorporating newly generated genome assemblies and other published amniote chromosomal genomes with their Hi-C data, we reconstruct the ALGs spanning the amniote phylogeny and also the process of how they evolved into the reported divergent nuclear architectures represented by human and emu. We show that mammals and reptiles undergo markedly different chromosome-rearrangement regimes, yet micro-ALGs derived sequences retain a conserved nuclear interior positioning and interaction pattern. This nuclear segregation of euchromatin and heterochromatin may, in turn, constrain chromosome evolution across distant lineages.

Results

Reconstructing the ancestral linkage groups of major amniote phylogenetic nodes

To trace the stepwise evolution of chromosomal sequences since the amniote ancestor, we set out to reconstruct the genomes of major amniote phylogenetic nodes including the ancestors of Amniota, Mammalia, Reptilia, as well as all descendent nodes including those of Archelosauria, Archosauria, Eutheira, Boreoeutheria. Although cross-species comparison of chromosomal genomes of a few representative amniotes13, or reconstruction of ALGs focusing on their evolutionary breakpoint regions (EBRs) using scaffold genomes19,20,22,56 were conducted for some nodes, our study encompasses a much broader sampling of amniote diversity than these previous studies, including 20 mammals, 16 reptiles, and one amphibian outgroup (Fig. 1a). We chose these species after taking the phylogenetic representation, karyotypic diversity, and genome assembly quality of these species into account. To our knowledge, there was no systematic estimation of the rate of karyotype evolution within and between amniote classes using whole genome sequences, the only readily available karyotypic diversity feature that we can take references to is the chromosome number. Therefore, we included species with chromosomal genomes that have either small (e.g., Tasmanian devil, 2n = 14; Aardvark, 2n = 20) or large (e.g., Australian echidna, 2n = 64; Horse, 2n = 66) chromosome numbers, in addition to others with a typical chromosome number of each amniote class. This design enabled us to compare variations of chromosome numbers, which necessarily reflect extensive fission or fusion events, and to evaluate their implications for genome organization. Notably, large-scale reshuffling of this kind has been shown to substantially reshape 3D chromatin folding (e.g., in marsupials)45, further justifying the inclusion of variable karyotypes. To address the current under-representation of high-quality genomes for Testudines and Crocodilia lineages of Archelosauria, both of which were reported to have relatively conserved within-lineage karyotypes57–60, we generated de novo chromosome-level assemblies (Methods) for the Chinese softshell turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis, 2n = 66) and the Siamese crocodile (Crocodylus siamensis, 2n = 30) (Supplementary Fig. 4). There are currently both gene-based reference-free reconstruction21,24 and reference genome-based reconstruction methods available62. In order to include the intergenic regions, we chose the human (T2T-CHM13v2.063) and chicken (GGswu41), whose genome quality is among the best in mammals and birds, and performed reference-based reconstruction. We first used the DESCHRAMBLER pipeline19,20,23 to identify syntenic regions close to each other that can be connected to the larger homologous syntenic blocks (HSBs) conserved across the studied species, without taking their residing chromosomal information into account. These HSBs were assembled into larger reconstructed ancestral chromosome fragments (RACFs). Following the approach of Damas et al.23, we manually orient and connect the RACFs into the ancestral linkage groups (ALGs) for the amniote, mammalian, and reptilian ancestors, by examining their shared synteny with the extant homologous chromosomes across multiple species, after correcting for any potential bias of using one reference genome for each class (Methods). Between 72% to 89% of the analyzed species’ genomes are included in the multi-species alignments and have been used for reconstructing the ALGs of respective ancestral nodes (Supplementary Table 2). Our reconstruction indicated a reducing number of ALGs from the amniote ancestor (n = 44) to the descendant nodes of mammalian (n = 32) or reptilian (n = 36) ancestor, and the Boreoeutherian (n = 26) or Archosaurian (n = 38) ancestor (Fig. 1a). To better capture the size spectrum of ALGs, we specifically divided the AALGs into macro-, medium- (that means ‘intermediate-sized’ in this work), and micro-AALGs ranked by their sizes (Methods; Supplementary Data 1). We found that micro-AALGs remain predominantly intact in the ancestral nodes of reptiles and mammals (Fig. 1b), and only become fused/translocated into the extant larger chromosomes after the divergence between mammals (e.g., in humans; Fig. 1c). The ancestor of reptiles or birds shows a higher level of conservation of synteny with AALGs than mammals, when we projected the AALG onto these nodes or extant karyotypes. For example, the extant macrochromosomes of chicken can be inferred to be the product of six AALG fusions, and its extant microchromosomes experienced even fewer (n = 1) fusions. This is consistent with our and others’ previous works13,41,55 suggesting that the avian karyotype better recapitulates the proto-karyotype of jawed vertebrate ancestor, and confirmed a largely stable karyotype during the evolution from the ancestor of jawed vertebrates to that of amniotes or birds21,30,37.

Our reconstruction results (Supplementary Table 3) are mostly consistent with, and have expanded or revised the previous cytogenetic and genomic reconstructions (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). We have recovered all of the 14 reported associations between segments of two extant human chromosomes that were inferred to be next to each other in the amniote and mammalian ancestors by comparative fluorescence in situ hybridization using flow-sorted human chromosomes18,64 (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 2). We consistently identified the homologous avian chromosomes that comprise 10 previously inferred amniote ancestral macrochromosomes using individual genes as probes33 (Supplementary Fig. 4). In addition, we report one new fusion between two amniote ALGs (AALG 10 and 19; Fig. 1b) in the Reptilia ancestor that was missed in Uno et al.33, but can be confirmed across all investigated reptile species in this work (Supplementary Fig. 2). Previous genomic reconstructions have inferred the haploid chromosome number of the ancestor of amniotes21,24 and mammals22,23 (Supplementary Table 3). The difference in the inferred AALG numbers between this (n = 44) and Sacerdot’s (n = 49) is mainly because the latter used scaffold-level genomes that can be disconnected at common genomic fragile sites, possibly leading to a slightly higher number of estimated AALGs. Our difference with another more recent reconstruction22. We manually inspected all of the inconsistent AALG cases with these previous works, and confirmed that our reconstruction is supported by consistent linkage or breakage of AALGs among all or most studied extant mammalian and reptilian chromosome sequences (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Mammals and reptiles have distinct rates and patterns of chromosome evolution

The reconstructed genomes of major amniote phylogenetic nodes allow us to next trace the stepwise changes of ALGs along each lineage that gave birth to the extant divergent karyotypes between the studied species. By aligning the AALGs to the ALGs of all descendant nodes, we uncovered that only a few chromosome fusions and translocations have occurred on lineages leading to the reptile ancestor from the amniote ancestor, in contrast with much higher numbers of rearrangements (one-sided t-test, t = 16.901, df = 24.973, p value = 3.515e−15), particularly translocations in the mammalian ancestors, although the gross composition of mammalian ancestor ALGs is not very different from that of AALGs (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Translocations had continued to dominate the chromosome rearrangements during divergence between mammals, but not between reptiles, producing a pattern of average significantly (one-sided t-test, t = 4.2802, df = 33.85, p value = 0.000145; Supplementary Fig. 6) more fragments from different AALGs comprising the extant mammalian chromosomes (i.e., can be ‘painted’ with more different colors of AALGs’ alignments, e.g., human; Fig. 1c) than those of reptiles (e.g., chicken).

To gain a more detailed picture of different types of chromosomal rearrangements between mammals vs. reptiles, we divided them into intra- (i.e., inversions) and inter-chromosomal (including fusions, fissions, and translocations) rearrangements, and estimate their respective rates as per AALG per million years (MY), along each amniote lineage (Methods). Mammals have an average over 3-fold higher chromosome inversion rate during their divergence than those of reptiles (3.30 vs. 0.94 events/MY), with the highest estimated rate on the descendent branch of Atlantogenata (elephants, anteaters, etc.) followed by that of Laurasiatheria (bat, dog, pig, and whale, etc.) (Fig. 2a). For interchromosomal rearrangements, chromosomes of reptiles, particularly those of crocodiles (Fig. 2b), are mainly products of fusions between AALGs. Birds and turtles have among all amniote lineages the lowest rates of inter- and intra-chromosomal rearrangements, consistent with the idea that their extant karyotypes best represent that of the amniote ancestor13. By contrast, fissions/translocations are much more prevalent throughout evolution of mammalian chromosomes (Fig. 2c). This can be exemplified by the extant chromosomes of platypus and crocodile composed by AALGs (Supplementary Fig. 4), with those of the former consisting of fragments from on average 4.3 AALGs per extant chromosome resulted from massive translocations22, while the latter only consisting of fragments from on average 2.7 AALGs. The interchromosomal rearrangements also involve different lengths of AALGs between mammals and reptiles. Those of the mammalian ancestors derived mammalian lineages much more frequently (one-sided t-test, t = 3.3283, df = 9.2676, p value = 0.008473) involve rearrangements between micro- and macro-sized AALGs compared to that of reptiles (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 7). In mammals, macro-AALGs have a clear tendency to have undergone translocations/fissions (6.3 vs. 1.5; Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 3414.5, p value = 5.934e−15), while micro-AALGs are more likely to have undergone fusions (0.53 vs. 0.87; Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 720.5, p value = 1.023e−08) (Fig. 2b, c). The rearranged homologous syntenic blocks (HSBs) are demarcated by EBRs that have been studied in reptiles56,65 and mammals19,23,66, for their different gene and repeat contents between the EBRs and HSBs. But how the EBRs emerged at the respective phylogenetic nodes (e.g., ancestral vs. species-specific) with different evolutionary ages, difference in these sequence features and genomic-wide distribution remains an interesting question to be investigated. With the very broad sampling of species in this study, we divided the EBRs into those that are relatively old and shared across all mammals or reptiles (‘Class-level’), those shared only in one of the four lineages of mammals or reptiles (‘Order-level’), and those specific to one species (‘Family-level’) (Methods). We found that extant chromosomes of eutherian lineages are mainly shaped by class-level EBRs both within (Supplementary Fig. 8) and between AALGs that correspond to fusions and translocations/fissions (Fig. 2e), while those of monotreme and reptilian lineages are mainly composed by order-level EBRs. Consistent with the previous results67–69, we found substantial numbers (12% to 19%) of EBRs independently involved in rearrangements across eutherian lineages (termed ‘reused’ in previous works) but not in reptiles except for the squamate lineage (Fig. 2e). Many but not all of the reused EBRs have significantly higher GC content than the HSBs and other types of EBRs. Since GC-rich regions were shown previously to be more likely to have double-strand breaks70–72. The pattern of reused EBRs may lend some support to the existence of genomic fragile sites, consistent with previous studies67,73. Among all the major mammalian and reptilian lineages (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 9), most family-level EBRs tend to have a significantly (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 17,092,258, p value < 2.2e−16) higher GC and repeat content, but a significantly lower (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 9,705,670, p value < 2.2e−16) insulation score, i.e., more likely overlap with the TAD borders, than the HSBs and class-level or order-level EBRs. These properties likely reflect the recent TE insertions that contributed to local 3D organization. The different types of TE insertions may account for the inconsistency of the GC content pattern between lineages. For example, family-level EBRs of reptile lineages are enriched for GC-rich LINEs (e.g., CR1, L2), while Euarchontoglires EBRs contain more AT-rich L1 elements and fewer Alu repeats, resulting in lower GC content (Supplementary Fig. 10). Notably, Euarchontoglires EBRs specific to the lineage leading to human, but not HSBs or class-level EBRs, are enriched in segmental duplication regions, many of which are associated with psychiatric and cognitive disorders in human (Supplementary Fig. 11), suggesting that these regions represent lineage-recent hotspots of instability. By contrast, order-level and reused EBRs do not exhibit consistent trends across species. For instance, reused EBRs showed elevated GC content in some mammalian lineages, but not others (Supplementary Fig. 9), and the repeat densities of order-level EBRs varied substantially between taxa. These results suggest that, unlike family-level EBRs, which bear signatures of recent TE-associated fragility and structural impact, older or recurrently used breakpoints are shaped by more diverse molecular mechanisms without a shared sequence feature.

Ancestral microchromosomes exhibit high evolutionary stability in their nuclear architecture

The difference in chromosome rearrangements could have shaped the nuclear architecture of chromosomes differently between reptiles and mammals. Most extant microchromosomes of reptiles exhibit strong pairwise trans-interactions (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 13), likely because of their clustered distribution in the nuclear interior13,32,39,41,55. While the majority of small-sized mammalian chromosomes do not show such patterns. This can be because many of the small-sized mammalian chromosomes are products of fissions or translocations of macro- or medium-AALGs rather than being derived from micro-AALGs (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 12). We next asked across all the studied species either the micro-AALG composition of an extant chromosome (micro-AALG%) or the extant chromosome size can better predict the extant chromosome’s trans-interaction strengths with other chromosomes, potentially reflecting more interior nuclear positions. We found overall the extant chromosome size has a negative association (Pearson’s correlation test, R = −0.57, t = −19.213, df = 749, p value < 2.2e−16) with the trans-interaction strength. Among chromosomes of similarly small sizes (<100 Mb), extant chromosomes with mainly micro-AALG origins (micro-AALG% > 50%) show significantly higher (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 102,615, p value = 6.42e−16) trans-interaction strengths than others (Supplementary Fig. 14). And micro-AALG compositions exhibit a much stronger (correlation coefficient ρ: 0.35 vs. −0.0012, multiple linear regression) positive association (Pearsons’ correlation test, R = 0.59, t = 19.952, df = 749, p value < 2.2e−16) than the extant chromosome size, with the trans-interaction strengths (Fig. 3b). This suggests that the micro-AALG composition of extant chromosome can better predict its nuclear position than the chromosome size, and the high trans-interaction feature, or the nuclear interior position of micro-AALGs remain conserved during evolution.

To test this hypothesis, we examined the ChIP-seq data targeting the nuclear periphery protein lamin-B1 across multiple tissues of human and emu (Supplementary Fig. 15). We expected that both the extant microchromosomes and micro-AALG-derived genomic regions to be clustered within the nuclear interior and have a much weaker interaction strength with the nuclear periphery compared to other longer chromosomes or other AALG-derived regions. Indeed, fused genomic regions that are derived from micro-AALGs in human, or extant microchromosomes of emu exhibit a higher (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 1426, p value = 9.55e−05) trans-interaction strength, but a lower (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 28,625,854, p value < 2.2e−16) normalized lamin-B1 ChIP-seq intensity than other chromosomes (Fig. 3c, d). These results together indicate evolutionary constraints from higher-order nuclear organization, which preserves the trans-interactions and the interior nuclear positions of micro-AALGs during their subsequent evolution, to some extent against the chromosome rearrangements or sequence changes that they have undergone.

We nevertheless found some chromosomes derived from macro-AALGs have increased trans-interactions due to their much reduced extant length after fissions (e.g., human chr21 and platypus chr19), whereas some large chromosomes predominantly derived from micro-AALG fusions (e.g., leopard gecko chr4 and platypus chr3) have weakened trans-interactions. These outliers represent possible species-specific shifts of chromosome nuclear positions against their micro- or macro- AALG origins, which can be caused by chromosome-specific turnovers of euchromatin/heterochromatin compositions that are associated with nuclear interior/periphery (Supplementary Fig. 16). Indeed, we found that some but not all the micro-AALG derived chromosomes that have decreased trans-interactions, or possibly shifted towards the nuclear periphery, have significantly higher repeat content (e.g., in platypus; Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 569,450, p value < 2.2e−16) and lower GC content (e.g., in leopard gecko; Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 3,832,834, p value < 2.2e−16) than other micro-AALG derived chromosomes of the same species (Fig. 3e).

Lineage-specific repeats locally tune the evolutionarily conserved nuclear architecture in mammals

We inferred above that the homologous regions of micro-AALGs in mammals tend to be positioned in the nuclear interior by their conserved strong trans-interactions with those of birds and weaker interactions with the nuclear periphery protein lamin-B1 (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18a). Previously published GPSeq (genomic loci positioning by sequencing) nuclear positioning data74 of human HAP1 cells allow us to inspect genomic regions of different AALG origins in a more precise and quantitative manner. The higher the GPSeq score is, the more distant from the nuclear periphery, and the more interiorly located the focal genomic locus is (Supplementary Fig. 17). After excluding the translocated chromosomes (chr9 and 22) in HAP1 cells, we confirmed that any two human genomic fragments with high GPSeq scores indeed exhibit significantly stronger (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 28,209, p value = 4.517e−11) trans-interactions than those with lower scores (Fig. 4a). Across the human genome, the GPSeq scores show a stratified pattern (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Data 3) between chromosome regions originated from very different size classes of AALGs. Such a clear contrast can also be seen at the chromosome-wide level between the classic case of chr18 vs. chr19, which have a similar size but have been known to occupy different nuclear positions (Supplementary Fig. 18a and Fig. 4c). Previous studies in humans and other primates10,14,47 attributed their distinct nuclear positions to the two chromosomes’ very different gene density. Here, we uncover another explanation from the perspective of their evolutionary origin, and reveal that micro-AALG-derived genomic regions or chromosomes (e.g., chr19 and chr17; Fig. 4c) are more likely to have preserved their nuclear interior positions dated back to the amniote ancestor. As expected, they exhibit a significantly higher (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 28,222,445, p value < 2.2e−16; Supplementary Fig. 18b) GPSeq score than medium- or macro-AALG derived regions or chromosomes (e.g., chr18). Epigenomic data further suggests they exhibit different sequence compositions and chromatin features, as evidenced by significantly (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 216,354, p value < 2.2e−16) different levels of euchromatin (measured by H3K4me3) and heterochromatin (H3K9me3) modifications, consistently in human (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 19) and emu (Supplementary Fig. 20). This suggests their different sequence compositions and epigenomic states already present in the amniote ancestor. In addition, micro-AALG derived human genomic regions are significantly (Chi-square test, χ² = 4699.20, df = 8, p value < 2.2e−16) enriched for speckle associated A1 subcompartments in the interior6,7,75, while macro-AALG derived regions are characterized with nuclear lamina associated B3 subcompartments (Supplementary Fig. 21). Interestingly, we find ‘stripes’ of trans-interactions conserved across different cell types or developmental stages (Supplementary Fig. 22) between specific regions of chr19 and chr7 (Fig. 4d), as well as between those of many other chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 23) where short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) Alu, but not other types of repetitive elements like long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs) L1, are concentrated, regardless whether the interacting regions are derived from micro-AALGs or not (Supplementary Fig. 23). Consistent with the correlation between the nuclear position and the trans-interaction intensity (Fig. 4a), there is a genome-wide significant positive correlation (Pearsons’ correlation test, R = 0.452, t = 70.617, df = 19,412, p value < 2.2e−16) between the GPSeq score vs. the abundance of Alu elements per genomic window. And the correlation is the strongest in micro-AALG derived regions (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 24). A closer examination shows that only the most abundant AluSx subfamily (Supplementary Fig. 25) that specifically propagated in higher primates76, but no other Alu subfamilies or repetitive elements exhibit such significant trans-interactions with the same subfamily members (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 26), though AluSx elements also exhibit weak trans-interactions with other subfamilies, e.g., AluSz and AluY (Supplementary Fig. 27). These trans-interactions of AluSx elements could either reflect or enhance the ancestral trans-interactions between micro-AALGs derived regions where AluSx is significantly (Chi-square test, χ² = 645.44, df = 2, p value < 2.2e−16) enriched (Supplementary Fig. 28), or emerged de novo after the propagation of AluSx elements specifically in primates. Two lines of evidence suggest that the latter is more likely: first, some macro- or medium-AALG derived regions that are supposed to have few ancestral trans-interactions, also exhibit strong trans-interactions specifically at the AluSx-enriched loci (Supplementary Fig. 26). Second, the homologous regions of the unique flanking regions of these Alu elements in emu, non-primate mammals like platypus and elephant show no or few trans-interactions (Supplementary Fig. 29). Genes nearby high trans-interacting AluSx (±2 Kb) exhibit a significantly (1.72 vs. 0.54; Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 6,572,715, p value = 1.436e−10) higher binding level of housekeeping gene histone modification mark H3K36me3 than other genes. Consistent with this, these flanking genes are enriched for gene ontology categories related to various fundamental processes of DNA/RNA metabolic processes, DNA damage response and chromatin organization, etc. (Supplementary Fig. 30 and Supplementary Data 4). More importantly, genomic regions that contain high levels of Alu copies (Supplementary Data 5) are also more likely to be characterized with speckle associated A1 subcompartment, and less likely to be characterized with lamina associated B3 subcompartment (Fig. 4g). To summarize, the generally conserved nuclear architecture is locally tuned by Alu elements in human, which may have attracted different nearby housekeeping genes of different chromosomes around the speckles through their homotypic interactions77.

Interestingly, the B1, B2 and B4 subfamilies of murine SINEs (Supplementary Fig. 27), and MIR SINEs of the Asian elephant, but not other types of repeats also exhibit significant trans-interactions (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 31). Since these SINEs were independently propagated and inserted at different genomic loci in different lineages, their trans-interactions probably comprise an important mechanism that underlies the more recent divergence of nuclear architectures between mammalian species. Supporting this, human and mouse only share less than 10% of the orthologous genes nearby the interacting Alu and B1 elements (Supplementary Fig. 32).

Discussion

The spatial nuclear architectures of chromosomes have profound influence on genome replication and transcription regulation3,54, therefore comprise a fundamental but largely unexplored aspect for understanding the mechanisms of functional divergence between species. Here we study the patterns and the underlying mechanisms of the conservation and divergence of linear and spatial organization of chromosomes during evolution of reptiles and mammals since their amniote ancestor. Our recent comparison between the genomes of vertebrate outgroup amphioxus vs. chicken suggested origination of microchromosomes in the vertebrate ancestor over 500 MY ago through asymmetric loss between products of whole genome duplications37. In this work our chromosome reconstruction further concludes that these ancient ‘building block’ chromosomes13 were preserved in the ancestor of amniotes, reptiles and mammals, as well as in most extant reptiles, but became integrated into larger chromosomes in extant mammals due to recent fusions and translocations. Compared to previous cytogenetic studies of karyotypic evolution in reptiles or mammals18,33,34,42,44,78, the direct comparison of reconstructed AALG vs. the chromosomal genome of extant species enabled us to quantitatively delineate the different tempo and mode of chromosome evolution between reptiles and mammals. We estimate in this work that mammals have an on average 3-fold higher chromosomal inversion rate than reptiles after their divergence from the amniote ancestor. Mammals also have more frequently experienced translocations/fissions that disrupted the AALGs into smaller chromosome segments, while reptiles mainly experienced fusions of AALGs that mostly preserved the AALG unit (Fig. 2). These lineage-specific rearrangements can also be viewed through the lens of evolutionary breakpoint regions (EBRs), which disrupt synteny and often recur across distantly related species23,66. Previous studies have shown that EBRs tend to cluster in fragile genomic regions enriched for repetitive elements, such as tandem repeats and transposable elements67,69,73. Our observation of reused EBRs in both reptiles and mammals is consistent with these findings and supports the idea that recurrently fragile sites have shaped amniote chromosome evolution. Importantly, the recurrent usage of EBRs is probably related to the spatial proximity of chromosomal breakpoints79, corroborating with the conserved nuclear positions of AALG sequences across species revealed in this study.

Despite massive and different chromosome reshufflings with other AALGs in reptiles and mammals, micro-AALGs exhibit a striking level of conservation (with some exceptions) of nuclear positions in the interior that is predicted to be enriched around the speckles in human. Previous cytogenetic works indicated that reptile microchromosomes13,15,80, or the GC-rich euchromatin regions of amphibians12 are also concentrated in the nuclear interior. In addition, microchromosomes of early-branching vertebrates little skate81 and lungfish82 also exhibit strong trans-interactions like many of their homologous chromosomes in birds. These results together suggest that one of the most commonly found nuclear organizations in mammalian cells where euchromatin and heterochromatin are respectively segregated toward the nuclear interior and the periphery (so-called ‘conventional nuclei’)4 possibly originated before, and remain conserved during the radiation of vertebrates (Fig. 5). Supporting this idea, we found that the middle parts of amphioxus chromosomes, which are characterized with A compartments and high gene densities, thus likely euchromatic regions, exhibit strong trans-interactions with each other (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 33). A similar pattern has also been reported in C. elegans83, but not in Drosophila84. Conservation and divergence of nuclear architecture.

We hypothesize that the conventional nuclear architecture widely reported in mammalian cells originated before vertebrate radiation, and exists in the vertebrate outgroup species amphioxus. The gene and euchromatin rich microchromosomes originated in the bony vertebrate ancestor 450–460 MYA, and were preserved in the ancestors of amniotes, reptiles and mammals. Their nuclear positions in the interior and consequential trans-interactions are highly conserved likely due to strong selection from the conventional nuclear architecture, despite undergoing fusions in the reptilian lineages, or translocations in the mammalian lineages. On top of that, lineage-specific propagation of SINE elements, e.g., Alu in human and B1 in mouse, may tune the nuclear architecture and bring the nearby genomic sequences toward the nuclear interior, and surround the nuclear speckles. This contributes to the divergence of nuclear organization between species.

Nevertheless, we found the conserved nuclear architecture can be altered through lineage specific changes of chromosome size or certain repeat elements. Fusions or translocations with much larger macro-AALGs do not necessarily attract the entire micro-AALG from the interior toward the periphery, as exemplified by the stratified pattern of GPSeq scores on human chr1 and chr12 (Supplementary Fig. 16). That is, micro-AALG derived regions within these larger extant chromosomes mostly manage to preserve the interior positions. However, we found in reptiles some cases of large chromosomes mostly composed of micro-AALGs that have largely weakened trans-interactions (Fig. 3e), or possibly shifted toward the nuclear periphery, as well as some macro-AALG derived small human chromosomes (e.g., chr21) that have shifted toward the nuclear interior (Supplementary Fig. 15). This can be explained by the corresponding changes of recombination rate associated with those of chromosome size85,86, that in turn affect individual chromosomes’ repeat and heterochromatin content87. This is consistent with the observation regarding the interplay between chromatin organization and genome reshuffling, e.g., extensive chromosome fusions leading to distinct chromosome folding in marsupials45. Another mechanism that differentiates the nuclear architecture between species, besides certain chromosome rearrangements, are lineage-specific repetitive elements that may locally alter the interactions of flanking sequences through their homotypic attractions. Micro-AALG originated regions are gene/euchromatin rich and susceptible for Alu/B1 insertions in human/mouse88 (Supplementary Figs. 24 and S28). Previous studies suggest that clustering of L1 LINE elements and Alu/B1 SINE elements compartmentalize the genome respectively into heterochromatin and euchromatin4,89, and we further found only subfamilies of AluSx and B1 may play an important role, possibly through interactions with nuclear speckles. One prominent example is human chr21, which is almost entirely derived from macro-AALG, but also has specifically accumulated considerable amounts of highly trans-interacting AluSx elements (Supplementary Fig. 28). It is likely a case where the two mechanisms of nuclear architecture evolution have worked interactively: after the possible increase of recombination rate associated with origination of chr21 as a truncated fragment from a macro-AALG, it started to accumulate the AluSx elements that increased its trans-interactions (Fig. 3b), or brought it toward the nuclear interior, as shown by its GPSeq score (Fig. 4b). While our results highlight the strong nuclear interactions of specific SINE subfamilies (e.g., AluSx and B1), it needs to be noted that differential TE accumulation and contact frequency could partly reflect lineage-specific bursts and the intrinsic mapping ability of recent insertions. Although we filtered for uniquely mapped Hi-C reads to reduce such artifacts, potential inflation of contact signals due to mapping ambiguity in highly repetitive regions cannot be fully excluded. Nevertheless, we observed marked differences even among similarly aged or similarly abundant TE subfamilies, suggesting that the observed patterns likely reflect functional or chromatin-contextual properties beyond mapping bias alone. Another question is that how exactly the trans-interacting Alu or B1 copies differentiate the nuclear architecture and gene functions through possible interactions with nuclear speckles between human and mouse? One clue is a 42-nt long GC-rich sequence element called SINE-derived nuclear RNA localization (SIRLOIN) encompassed in the AluSx elements that was reported to facilitate nuclear accumulation of long non-coding RNAs and some mRNAs through interacting with heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNPK)90. SIRLOIN can be found in different Alu subfamilies and B1 elements, with variations in their consensus sequences per element (Supplementary Fig. 34). Future precise excision of the trans-interacting Alu/B1 elements identified in this work (Supplementary Data 6) and their SIRLOIN motifs is expected to provide detailed functional insights into this.

Previous studies have shown the microchromosomes conservation of sequence and spatial interaction in birds and other reptiles13,32,39,40,91, while mammalian chromosomes have undergone extensive rearrangements18,19,23,42,43,52. Here, this work significantly advances our understanding on amniote chromosome evolution in a three-dimensional perspective. By reconstructing ancestral chromosomes in broad taxonomic with spatial chromatin data, we reveal despite lineage-specific rearrangement trajectories across reptilian and mammalian species, much of the ancestral spatial organization is preserved, likely tuning by TE insertions such as Alu and B1, thus providing new insights on how genome structure and 3D organization evolve in parallel under constraints. Future studies combining imaging-based validation and functional perturbation will be essential to test the causality of TE insertions in shaping nuclear positioning and to directly confirm the predicted conservation of microchromosome-derived regions in nuclear space.

De novo assemblies of Pelodiscus sinensis and Crocodylus siamensis

We newly generated the chromosome-level assemblies for a male Chinese softshell turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis) and a female Siamese crocodile (Crocodylus siamensis). All samples were derived from the farms in the Zhejiang and Hainan provinces of China, respectively. We extracted the high molecular weight (HMW) DNA from the liver and generated 100x Nanopore GridION long reads for P. sinensis and 17x Pcabio HiFi Sequell CCS reads for C. siamensis. We then produced the contig sequences using software Flye v2.8116 for P. sinensis, and software wtdbg2.5117 for C. siamensis. The contigs of P. sinensis were further scaffolded with 10x linked reads using Scaff10X (https://github.com/wtsi-hpag/Scaff10X). Then both assemblies’ contigs were polished by several rounds of Racon118 using 100x Illumina PE150 reads and scaffolded into pseudo-chromosomes by Hi-C reads (Enzyme: Mbol) using Juicer119 and 3D-DNA101, followed by manual adjustments with Juicer_tools120. The chromosome numbers are 33 and 15, with contig N50 of 15 Mb and 25.9 Mb for the turtle and crocodile, respectively. The raw data and assembly statistics for both species are available in Supplementary Table 1.

Chromatin data collection and analysis

For the emu, all female samples were derived from emu farms in the Zhejiang provinces of China. We generated Hi-C sequencing data (liver, ovary, and brain), ChIP-seq data for H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 histone marks (ovary and brain), and CUT&Tag sequencing data for the nuclear lamin-B1 protein (ovary and brain). The Hi-C experiments were performed following the design in Rao et al.6 using MboI as the digestion enzyme. Briefly, cells were crosslinked, permeabilized, digested, end-filled with biotin, ligated in situ, de-crosslinked, sheared, and biotinylated junctions were isolated with streptavidin beads. Libraries were then prepared using standard Illumina protocols with T4 DNA polymerase, Klenow fragment, and T4 polynucleotide kinase for end repair and A-tailing, followed by Illumina adapter ligation, PCR amplification, size selection, and sequencing on HiSeq2500 platforms. Native ChIP-seq of H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 histone marks was performed following the protocol described in Saleh et al.121. The CUT&Tag experiments were performed using the Hyperactive Universal Kit (Vazyme, TD-903) with Anti-Lamin-B1 antibody (Abcam, ab16048). Cells were bound to ConA beads, incubated with the antibody, and treated with pA/G-Tn5 transposase followed by fragmentation. Libraries were prepared and indexed using the TruePrep Index Kit (Vazyme, TD-202), then sequenced on the NovaSeq 2500 (Illumina, Novogene) with paired-end 150 bp reads.

After adapter and quality trimming using Trimmomatic v0.33122, the clean reads were mapped to the emu genome (ZJU2.0)55 using bwa-mem123 with minimal mapping quality of 20. Sorting and indexing of bam files were performed by using samtools123. Then the alignment bam files were converted into IP/IN ratio files under 10Kb resolution with the RPKM normalization by bamCompare124. The human GPSeq data (HAP1 cell lines) for both 100Kb and 1 Mb resolution were downloaded from GSE13588274. The Hi-C matrix and sub-compartment profiles for three cell lines: GM12878, IMR90 and K562 were downloaded from GSE635256. The human epigenetic histone modification profile (H3K4me3 and H3K9me3 ChIP-seq) was obtained from the ENCODE project for the GM12878 cell line125 and coordinate-transformed into T2T-CHM13v2.0 through UCSC liftover. The lamin-B1 ChIP-seq data were obtained for the hESC, epicardium and liver cell from GSM5669226126.

Ancestral chromosome reconstruction

We selected human and chicken as the reference genomes for the reconstruction of the amniota/mammalian and reptilian ancestors, respectively, as DESCHRAMBLER19 requires the reference to be a descendant species for the reconstructed ancestor. All input genomes were firstly masked for repeats by RepeatMasker (version 4.1.2)127 and Tandem Repeat Finder128. We removed the unanchored scaffolds of input genomes before whole-genome alignment using LastZ (version 1.04.00, H = 2000 Y = 3400 L = 6000 K = 2200) with two reference genomes, human and chicken, respectively. The alignments were further converted into UCSC chain and net formats by lavToPsl, axtChain, chainMergeSort, chainPreNet and chainNet, all with default parameters129 and merged with Multiz (version 10.0)130. The fourfold degenerate synonymous sites based on the human gene annotations were extracted to infer the phylogenetic tree using IQ-Tree (version 2.0-rc1)131 with settings: -m MFP -bb 1000. The tree was further trimmed and visualized by ITOL (version 6.4.1)132. The divergence time of the species tree was extracted from TimeTree133.

With the “chainnet” alignments and phylogenetic tree, the DESCHRAMBLER software was used to generate SFs (Syntenic fragments), so called HSBs (Homologous syntenic blocks), among all the species at the resolution of 300Kb23 and produce the RACFs (Reconstruct ancestral chromosome fragments) which are oriented combinations of SFs in the interested ancestor nodes, under the default parameter. More specifically, the algorithm builds RACFs using a chromosome evolution model to calculate the probabilities of SF (Syntenic fragment) pairs being adjacent in a target ancestor, based on their order and orientation in descendants and outgroup species19. These SFs and their adjacency are then represented as a graph, which is used to estimate the most likely paths, reflecting the HSB order and orientation in the target ancestor19. Adjacency score is the value to judge whether two HSBs should be connected. Higher score suggests the connection of two HSBs is supported in more descendent species. For the RACFs of amniote ancestor, we combined the reconstruction results from human- and chicken-based ways to attenuate the potential reference bias62. To further anchor the resulting RACFs into longer ALGs (Ancestral linkage groups), we applied a stricter criterion compared to previous mammalian chromosome reconstruction23: two RACFs are manually connected into one ALG if they are connected on extant chromosomes at least in: i) one outgroup, four reptiles (4/16) and five mammals (5/20), or ii) zero outgroup, eight reptiles (8/16) and ten mammals (10/20). The different criteria could have caused significant differences for the final number of mammalian ALGs. That is, Damas et al., initially reconstructed 76 RACFs which were further anchored into 20 ALGs as long as two RACFs were connected in at least one outgroup and one descendent species. In this study, we have produced an initial 71 RACFs for the mammalian ancestor, and anchored into 32 ALGs (Supplementary Table 2). The genome coverages of reconstructed ancestors in this work (67.0%-89.4%) are a bit lower compared to previous analysis (69.4%–94.1%)19,23,62. This could be caused by two main factors: (1) the high-quality genomes have largely completed the highly repetitive regions compared with previous versions, leading to elevated percentages of unalignable regions, e.g., 8% in human63 and 5% in chicken41; (2) more species with greater phylogenetically distance included in this reconstruction would decrease the syntenic length of HSBs (Supplementary Table 2). To better depict the size spectrum of AALGs (Amniote ancestral linkage groups), we artificially divided them into three categories: macro- (A1-8, >90 Mb when using human sequence-based reconstruction), medium- (A9-15, 40–90 Mb) and micro-AALGs (A16-44, <40 Mb).

We defined the HSBs as macro-/medium-/micro- HSBs according to the AALG origins, and the junction regions between HSBs are defined as EBRs. For the EBR classification, we first merged the adjacent HSBs within 50Kb distance to avoid the putative false small EBRs due to alignment or assembly errors. Then, the remaining regions excluding merge HSBs were defined as putative EBRs after the largest 5% EBRs which were composed of complex regions, e.g., pericentromeric regions, as did in Damas et al.23. We then compared the EBR sets across descendent species, and defined the class-level, reused or family-level EBRs according to the coordinate overlaps following the phylogenetic tree. Specifically, the class-level EBRs were defined to have an occurrence rate over 80% in all investigated amniote lineages: Monotremata, Atlantogenata, Laurasiatheria, and Euarchontoglires; Squamata, Pantestudines, Crocodilia and Aves. The order-level EBRs were defined to have a rate over 80% in the certain lineage but lower than 20% in other lineages; family-level EBRs were unique in one investigated species, and the non-specific EBRs were defined as reused EBRs. If one EBR is located between two different AALG regions, we defined it as an inter-AALG EBR.

Characterizing chromosome rearrangements

The syntenic orders of AALG were compared with those of all the amniote internal ancestral nodes and extant species to infer the genomic rearrangements including inversions, translocations/fissions, and fusions based on the orientation and syntenic position of HSBs with GRIMM software134, followed by manual curations. To infer the rearrangement rates for the internal branches, we followed the principle that, if any rearrangement was shared by two closely related species, that is, the overlap length of their influenced regions was larger than 80%, it was likely present in their common ancestor. This tracing process was performed iteratively until we reached the ancestral node of all amniotes. The rearrangement rate for each branch was then divided by the divergence times extracted from timetree133. The number of inversions is scaled by reconstructed sizes for comparison between AALGs. Regarding the fraction calculation of each type of AALGs involved in inter- chromosomal rearrangements, one chromosome of certain focal lineage or species usually contains more than one type of AALG (macro-, micro- or medium) indicative of lineage/species specific translocations. We first designated each chromosome of that lineage/species as one of the three types of AALG based on the most abundant AALG constituting that chromosome on the x-axis. Then we calculated the percentage of other AALGs assigned into one of the three types that reside on the same chromosome. The longest alignment of each AALG for each extant chromosome in the extant species was recorded. If more than 50% of any two (or more than two) AALGs (A and B) were included in an extant chromosome (C), we defined a fusion event of ALG_A and B on chr_C of that species. Then any other AALGs that included within this extant chromosome were defined as translocations (or fissions) between two adjacent AALGs along the coordinate of extant chromosomes.

3D chromatin interaction analysis

Raw Hi-C reads of each species were firstly trimmed for adapter and quality by Trimmomatic v0.32122 and then mapped to genome assemblies using Bowtie2135, with only uniquely mapped reads (MAPQ > = 20) were kept for downstream analysis. The uniquely mapped reads were then assigned to a restriction enzyme fragment, and invalid ligation products were discarded, following by the binning and normalization using HiC-Pro (v2.10.0)136 with default parameters at 25Kb, 50Kb, 100Kb, 500Kb, and 1 Mb resolutions under ICE-normalization137. The trans-interaction contact frequency between each pairwise chromosome was determined by comparing the Observed vs. Expected (O/E) contacts between the two chromosomes following Zhang et al.138. We identified the A/B compartments using the pca.hic function from the HiTC package139 using gene density as correction track. TADs were called using ‘hicCallTADs’ with HiCExplorer140 using the following parameters: ‘--correctForMultipleTesting fdr --numberOfProcessors 30 --minBoundaryDistance 5000 --thresholdComparisons 0.01 --delta 0.05 --step 5000’, so that we obtained the TAD boundary positions and genome-wide TAD insulation scores. Heatmaps of O/E contact matrices were generated by Juicerbox and then visualized by Juicer_tools120. The trans-interaction interaction (O/E) matrix was extracted for each pair of different chromosomes using the ‘dump’ module of Juicer_tools under 100Kb resolution. We extracted 5Mb-long bins of upstream and downstream for each pair of repeats (e.g., Alu, B1, L1, etc.) from different AALGs and calculated the average trans-O/E value. The interaction aggregation was conducted for macro- and micro-AALG derived regions, respectively. To obtain the high trans-interaction (O/E) regions, all the O/E values were ranked, then we counted the number of bins whose trans-interaction is among the top 5%. The bins with the top 5% of the interaction number are defined as high trans-interaction bins. To detect high trans-interaction AluSx copies, we calculated the amount of interaction reads between two AluSx elements which are from different chromosomes. The AluSx copies with trans-reads more than 40 were defined as high trans-interaction AluSx (Supplementary Data 6). The FIMO software141 was used to detect the SIRLOIN motif in different Alu/B1 families and the visualization was achieved with ggseqlogo142.
