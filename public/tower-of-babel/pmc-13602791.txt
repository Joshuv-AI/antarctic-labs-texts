From metabolic disorder to autoimmunity: lupus-like immune dysregulation in children with SLC7A7-associated lysinuric protein intolerance
Authors: Xiaohua Tan, Li Li, Caifeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo characterize the clinical and immunological features of lupus-like immune dysregulation in children with lysinuric protein intolerance (LPI) through case analysis and literature review.MethodsThis study retrospectively analyzed the clinical records, genetic testing results, and immunological data of two LPI cases treated at Beijing Children’s Hospital. A literature review was simultaneously performed to contextualize our findings.ResultsThe proband (Patient 1), a 1.5-year-old girl with homozygous SLC7A7 (c.625 + 1G > A) mutation, presented with growth retardation and multisystem involvement (hematologic, digestive, respiratory). Laboratory tests showed ANA positivity and hypocomplementemia. While initial therapy with glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants and dietary protein restriction showed benefit, treatment non-adherence resulted in disease progression culminating in fatal respiratory failure. Patient 2 (the proband’s sister), carrying the same mutation, exhibited milder manifestations including growth retardation, interstitial lung disease, ANA positivity, hypocomplementemia, and proteinuria. With treatment, her condition stabilized, showing partial resolution of lung lesions and improved growth. Combining our two cases with 18 previously reported cases (total n = 20), common features included growth retardation (88.9%), renal involvement (72.2%), respiratory disease, and hematologic abnormalities. Rheumatologic markers included ANA positivity (90%), hypocomplementemia (85%), and anti-dsDNA antibodies (45%). Treatment primarily involved glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants, whereas plasma exchange and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation were used only rarely.ConclusionLPI-associated lupus-like immune dysregulation is rare and clinically heterogeneous. Accurate diagnosis requires an integrated assessment of genetic, metabolic, and immunologic data. Immunomodulatory therapy may be effective, but vigilance is essential due to infection risk and disease flares. Early recognition, management, and long-term follow-up are critical to improving prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07467-1.

Background

Lysinuric protein intolerance (LPI) is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by mutations in the SLC7A7 gene, leading to dysfunction of the amino acid transporter 1 (y+LAT1). This defect impairs the transport of cationic amino acids (lysine, arginine, and ornithine) in renal tubular and intestinal epithelial cells, resulting in characteristic manifestations such as hyperammonemia, protein aversion, vomiting, growth retardation, and osteoporosis [1, 2]. Notably, LPI exhibits multisystem involvement with potential complications including pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (PAP) and hemophagocytic lymph histiocytosis (HLH) [3, 4]. Emerging evidence has suggested an association between LPI and immune dysregulation [5, 6]. Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), a prototypical multisystem autoimmune disease, results from complex interactions between genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysfunction [7]. Recent studies have underscored the important role of immunometabolic dysregulation in the pathogenesis of SLE. Altered metabolic profiles in immune cell subsets, along with metabolic reprogramming of T and B lymphocytes, contribute to autoantibody production, activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, and subsequent organ damage [8]. Intriguingly, some LPI patients develop lupus-spectrum immune manifestations including autoantibody positivity, hypocomplementemia, and hemocytopenia [9]. Mechanistic studies indicate that LPI-related amino acid metabolic disturbances may modulate T-cell and macrophage functionality, potentially contributing to immune imbalance [10, 11].

This study describes two siblings from a single pedigree with genetically confirmed LPI who exhibited lupus-like clinical and immunological features despite substantial phenotypic heterogeneity. Additionally, we present a comprehensive literature review on the association between LPI and SLE, with the aim of enhancing understanding of this disease constellation.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical data from two pediatric LPI patients admitted to Beijing Children’s Hospital. A literature review was performed using PubMed and Google Scholar databases, employing the keywords “LPI,” “autoimmune disease,” and “childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus”. English-language reports of pediatric patients with confirmed LPI and either a reported diagnosis of SLE or lupus-like immune dysregulation were reviewed and summarized. Our study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institution Review Board of Beijing Children’s Hospital (Approval No: IEC-C-006-A04-V.07.2). Written informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of both participants, who were younger than 16 years of age.

Results

Patient 1 (Fig. 1): The proband, a female infant, presented at 1 year and 5 months of age in June 2022 with recurrent high-grade fever and respiratory infections. She exhibited significant growth retardation, with both height (z-score: -2) and weight (z-score: -2.09) below the 3rd percentile for age- and sex-matched references. Physical examination revealed scanty subcutaneous fat, hepatosplenomegaly, and livedo reticularis of the lower extremities. Laboratory investigations demonstrated pericardial effusion, anemia (hemoglobin 96 g/L [reference 104–143 g/l]), thrombocytopenia (PLT 120 × 10⁹/L [191–516 × 10⁹/L]), hypoalbuminemia (35.1 g/L [39–54 g/l]), hepatic dysfunction (AST 117.6 U/L [22–59 U/L], ALT 85.3 U/L [8–42 U/L]), elevated LDH (1815 U/L [110–295 U/L]), and hyperferritinemia (3433 ng/mL [6-159 ng/ml]). Immunological workup revealed positive ANA (1:160) with strong anti-SSA and anti-SSA-52 reactivity, hypocomplementemia, elevated CRP (13 mg/L) and ESR (45 mm/h), though anti-dsDNA was negative. The patient reached a total score of 14(serositis, hematological involvement, immunological markers, and ANA positivity) according to the 2019 EULAR/ACR SLE classification criteria.

Initial management included prednisone (15 mg/day, 2 mg/kg/day) and broad-spectrum antibiotics (meropenem/linezolid), achieving fever resolution. On day 12, she developed lethargy, tachypnea, generalized tonic-clonic seizures, and hyperammonemia (78 umol/L). Brain MRI showed only subtle periventricular white-matter FLAIR hyperintensities, without typical inflammatory or vascular lesions. Based on the acute neurological presentation, elevated blood ammonia level, and nonspecific MRI findings, the seizure was considered more likely related to an underlying urea-cycle or inherited metabolic disorder. Given the early disease onset, multisystem involvement, and a family history of similar manifestations in her elder sister, the possibility of an inherited metabolic disorder was further considered. Further analysis of blood amino acids revealed elevated levels of glutamate and methionine. Urinary metabolites were analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), with hex deuterated adipic acid (IS-HDA) as an internal standard for relative quantification. The results indicated a significant increase in urinary levels of lysine, lactate, uric acid, and histidine, while urea levels were markedly decreased (Table 1).

Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous splice-site variant in SLC7A7 (c.625 + 1G > A) in patient, and segregation analysis confirmed that both parents were heterozygous carriers (Fig. 2). Contrast-enhanced chest CT (Fig. 3) demonstrated diffusely reduced pulmonary transparency with parenchymal and interstitial abnormalities, accompanied by a consolidative necrotic lesion in the right upper lobe and multiple enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes. Microbiological studies and bone marrow aspiration showed no abnormalities. The final diagnoses were LPI with lupus-like immune dysregulation and interstitial lung disease (ILD). Therapy was escalated with tofacitinib (2.5 mg/day) and protein-restricted diet (1 g/kg/day), alongside tapering prednisone. Over the subsequent two years, the patient demonstrated poor outpatient adherence with unsupervised steroid tapering. In January 2025, she developed Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia with high fever and respiratory symptoms, followed by spontaneous discontinuation of tofacitinib. This precipitated rapid clinical deterioration featuring pancytopenia, bone marrow suppression, progressive interstitial lung disease, and invasive mucormycosis (Fig. 3). Despite aggressive interventions—including methylprednisolone pulse therapy (20 mg/kg/day), intravenous infusion of immunoglobulin (IVIG, 500 mg/kg/day), cyclosporine (3 mg/kg/day), antimicrobials (meropenem/linezolid/amphotericin B), hematopoietic growth factors (G-CSF/TPO), blood product transfusions, and mechanical ventilation—the disease progressed to Acute respiratory distress syndrome(ARDS), pleural effusions, and coagulopathy. Care was ultimately withdrawn per parental request, resulting in fatal outcome.

Patient 2: The elder sister presented at 3 years and 10 months (July 2022) with recurrent cough. Anthropometric measurements revealed growth retardation (height 93 cm, z-score: -2; weight 11 kg, z-score: -2.35). Physical examination demonstrated livedo reticularis of the lower extremities and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory investigations showed elevated LDH (712 U/L) and ferritin (377 ng/mL), hypocomplementemia, positive ANA (1:160) with negative anti-dsDNA, and proteinuria, with a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 530.82 mg/g Cr. Renal biopsy was not performed because the family was unable to afford the procedure. Chest CT revealed diffuse interstitial lung disease (Fig. 3). Notably absent were hematologic or neurologic involvement, with normal ammonia levels. Amino acid analysis revealed elevated plasma glycine and citrulline/arginine ratio, along with increased urinary lysine. Urinary metabolites were quantified by GC-MS using IS-HAD as an internal standard, confirming elevated urinary lysine levels (Table 1). Whole-exome sequencing confirmed the same SLC7A7 homozygous mutation as patient 1. The patient was diagnosed with LPI with lupus-like immune dysregulation and ILD, managed with prednisone (1 mg/kg/day), tofacitinib (2.5 mg/day), and protein restriction. Follow-up demonstrated: Catch-up growth (height/weight reaching 3rd percentile); Normalization of complement levels and ANA reduction to 1:80; Radiographic improvement on repeat chest CT.

Results of the literature review

A total of 20 pediatric patients with LPI and either a reported diagnosis of SLE or lupus-like immune dysregulation, including our two patients, were summarized [9, 11–24]. Demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Of the 20 patients, 11 were female (11/20, 55%) and 9 were male (9/20, 45%). Age at onset ranged from 1.25 to 16 years (mean ± SD: 6.12 ± 5.45 years). Nine patients had a definitive family history, and 16 patients exhibited growth retardation. Renal involvement was observed in 13 patients, primarily presenting as proteinuria and hematuria. Five patients underwent renal biopsy: one showed membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and improved after treatment with prednisone acetate (2 mg/kg/day) and a low-protein diet; three were diagnosed with membranous nephropathy; and one displayed immune complex deposition consistent with early-stage lupus nephritis. Respiratory system involvement occurred in 12 patients, including 4 cases of PAP. Hematologic involvement was noted in 14 patients, with 4 meeting diagnostic criteria for HLH. ANA positivity was present in 18 patients, hypocomplementemia in 17, anti-dsDNA antibodies in 9, and ENA antibodies in 12. Fifteen patients received glucocorticoids; three also received cyclophosphamide, and two each received azathioprine or cyclosporine. The patient reported by Contreras [17] initially underwent plasma exchange, blood purification, and eculizumab therapy for acute renal insufficiency and hemolytic anemia with thrombotic microangiopathy. One patient achieved symptom remission after initial glucocorticoid and cyclophosphamide treatment but relapsed with severe HLH after one year. Subsequent treatment with methylprednisolone pulse therapy, mycophenolate mofetil, and IVIG showed limited efficacy, ultimately requiring hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) for clinical remission [22]. Patients at our center additionally received tofacitinib for immunotherapy.

Discussion

LPI, a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by SLC7A7 mutations, manifests with growth retardation, hyperammonemia, and multiorgan dysfunction, particularly involving renal and pulmonary systems [25]. Notably, a subset of LPI patients develops immune-mediated complications including SLE and HLH [17]. The two siblings reported here had genetically confirmed LPI and shared autoantibody positivity, hypocomplementemia, and multiorgan involvement, but showed marked phenotypic heterogeneity and divergent outcomes. The literature review similarly identified frequent renal, respiratory, and hematologic involvement, together with ANA positivity and hypocomplementemia. These findings support a broad LPI-associated immune phenotype rather than a uniform diagnosis of classic childhood-onset SLE.

SLE represents a prototypical multisystem autoimmune disorder characterized by polyclonal autoantibody production (notably ANAs), immune complex deposition, and chronic inflammation, ultimately leading to progressive multiorgan damage and potential life-threatening complications [26]. Approximately 15–20% of cases manifest before age 18 (childhood-onset SLE, cSLE), which demonstrates more aggressive disease progression than adult-onset SLE, with higher frequencies of renal (lupus nephritis), hematologic (cytopenia), and neurologic involvement, along with higher disease activity and poorer outcomes [27]. The pathogenesis of SLE involves dysregulation of both innate and adaptive immunity, leading to the loss of immune tolerance to nucleic acid components and aberrant activation of the interferon (IFN) system. These mechanisms promote autoantibody production, immune complex formation, and subsequent deposition, ultimately causing multi-organ damage [28–30]. Accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal role of immunometabolism dysregulation and disrupted immune homeostasis in SLE pathogenesis [31–35]. Immune cells—including monocytes/macrophages, low-density neutrophils (LDNs), and CD4 + T cells—undergo type I interferon-driven metabolic reprogramming, manifesting as enhanced glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation alongside aberrant fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. These metabolic alterations exacerbate inflammatory responses at the immune-vascular interface and endothelial injury, thereby contributing to organ damage [34]. Recent advances in whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing have identified monogenic variants associated with SLE, giving rise to the concept of monogenic lupus [36, 37]. To date, over 50 pathogenic genes linked to monogenic lupus or lupus-like phenotypes have been reported, implicating pathways such as complement deficiency, type I interferon signaling, RAS signaling, and immune tolerance [38–44]. Notably, SLC7A7 gene mutations have been detected in patients with early-onset lupus, who frequently present with growth retardation and aversion to protein-rich foods [13, 15]. However, whether SLC7A7 functions as a definitive SLE-causative gene remains unclear and requires further investigation.

The SLC7A7 gene encodes the y + LAT-1 protein, which functions as the light chain subunit of the dibasic amino acid transporter system. Predominantly expressed in the intestinal mucosa, renal tubules, and liver, this transporter facilitates the basolateral membrane transport of basic amino acids (lysine, arginine, and ornithine) across intestinal and renal epithelial cells, thereby enabling systemic amino acid absorption and utilization for protein synthesis [45]. The present case involves a SLC7A7 (c.625 + 1G > A) mutation, which induces aberrant mRNA splicing and consequent loss of y⁺LAT-1 transporter function [46, 47]. This defect impairs lysine/arginine transport, disrupts urea cycle dynamics, and perturbs amino acid metabolism, ultimately manifesting as protein malnutrition, anemia, and osteoporosis [48]. Current hypotheses regarding between LPI and immune dysregulation suggest impaired y+LAT1 function in non-polarized cells (e.g., macrophages and lymphocytes), which hinders arginine efflux and increases intracellular arginine levels. Under the catalysis of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), excessive nitric oxide (NO) production activates CD8 + T cells, triggering cytokine overproduction and ectopic migration of leukocyte [10, 17, 49]. This cascade contributes to clinical manifestations such as hepatosplenomegaly, HLH, and other autoimmune disorders. In LPI patients with chronic kidney disease, arginine accumulation in renal tubular cells strongly activates nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), a key regulator of inflammatory and immune-related gene transcription [49]. Recent model studies suggest that monocytes in LPI patients adopt an inflammatory phenotype through an arginine-independent pathway. Downregulation of y+LAT1 in monocytes directly elevates cytokine levels, which act on hyperreactive airway epithelial cells, recruiting more dysfunctional monocytes and establishing a self-perpetuating inflammatory feedback loop. This mechanism may extend to other organ systems, explaining LPI’s multisystem involvement and suggesting that LPI could be classified as an autoinflammatory disease [49]. The model also provides a plausible explanation for the high incidence of HLH and SLE-like symptoms in LPI. In vitro studies have revealed significantly reduced system y+L activity in LPI monocytes and alveolar macrophages, severely impairing phagocytic function. This leads to defective clearance of apoptotic cells, triggering aberrant inflammatory and immune responses [2]. Additionally, prior studies have reported dysfunction of tolls-like receptor (TLR) in LPI macrophages, which may further contribute to immune dysregulation [17]. Reports of LPI complicated by SLE remain scarce, and clinical data are insufficient to establish clear diagnostic criteria. The phenotypic overlap between LPI and childhood-onset SLE, particularly in clinical and immunological features, may contribute to diagnostic delay or misclassification. For patients with early-onset SLE presenting with metabolic abnormalities or an inadequate response to conventional therapy, comprehensive evaluation that integrates genetic, immunological, and metabolic assessments should be considered. Such an approach may improve diagnostic accuracy and facilitate timely recognition of this rare overlap condition.

The treatment of LPI involves managing hyperammonemia, providing nutritional support, and preventing complications. In cases complicated by ILD, HLH, or lupus-like immune dysregulation, glucocorticoids combined with immunosuppressive agents may be administered [1]. Furthermore, biologics and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation have been reported in individual cases of LPI with SLE [17, 22], although their therapeutic efficacy requires further investigation. To date, tofacitinib has not been reported in LPI-associated lupus-like immune dysregulation. Both patients developed interstitial lung disease and responded poorly to conventional therapy. Although mycophenolate mofetil, cyclophosphamide, and cyclosporine are commonly used for autoimmune disease-associated interstitial lung disease [50, 51], no established immunosuppressive regimen exists for LPI-related pulmonary disease, which may involve macrophage dysfunction and a distinct pattern of immune dysregulation. Given the potential involvement of type I interferon–JAK/STAT signaling and emerging evidence for JAK inhibitors in childhood SLE and autoinflammatory disorders to facitinib was introduced as an exploratory adjunctive therapy [52, 53]. One patient showed clinical improvement, suggesting that JAK inhibition may be a potential option for refractory pulmonary involvement. Larger studies are warranted to clarify the potential role of JAK inhibitors in this rare overlap condition.

This study has several limitations. First, only two patients with SLC7A7 variants were included, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, the literature search was restricted to PubMed and Google Scholar and included only English-language publications; therefore, relevant cases may have been missed. In addition, publication bias toward rare or severe presentations, together with heterogeneity in data completeness and terminology across reports, may have influenced the results.

Conclusion

LPI is a rare metabolic disorder with broad clinical variability that may mimic or overlap with childhood-onset SLE, resulting in diagnostic delay. The presence of growth retardation, hyperammonemia, and protein aversion in pediatric SLE patients should raise suspicion for LPI. Integrating genetic, metabolic, and immunologic evaluation is essential for accurate diagnosis. Standardized treatment strategies, timely immunomodulatory therapy, and long-term multidisciplinary follow-up are crucial for improving survival and quality of life.
