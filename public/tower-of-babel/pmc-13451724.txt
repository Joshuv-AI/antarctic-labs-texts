Association of Mental Health, Quality of Life, and Sport Specialization Using the Wisconsin Sport Specialization Questionnaire Among Adolescent Athletes
Authors: Rachel Meyers, Sarah Petersen, Veronica Hogg-Cornejo, Emily Sweeney, Aubrey Armento, David Howell, Gregory Walker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Higher sport specialization levels have been associated with increased mental health issues. A recently validated questionnaire, the Wisconsin Sport Specialization Questionnaire (WISSQ), may provide clarity about the degree of sport specialization to investigate associations with mental health/quality of life (QoL) among adolescent athletes.Purpose:To examine the association of sports specialization, as measured by the WISSQ, and mental health/QoL outcomes among uninjured adolescent athletes, as well as the effect of age on the interaction between mental health/QoL outcomes and sport specialization.Study Design:Cross-sectional study; Level of evidence, 2.Methods:During the preseason assessment, performed in July and August from 2023 to 2025, participants completed the WISSQ and the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Global 25 (Version 1.1). A multiple linear regression model was constructed that evaluated the association between 6 PROMIS domains and WISSQ ratings, adjusting for age, sex, and self-reported physical activity volume. To address the secondary purpose, linear regression models with an interaction term were used to examine the association between each PROMIS domain and age with sport specialization.Results:A total of 251 uninjured adolescent athletes completed the study (mean ± SD age, 15.9 ± 1.4 years). The mean WISSQ rating was 54.1 (SD, 15.4). Results from the multivariable linear regression model indicated that higher depressive symptoms (β = 0.46; 95% CI, 0.16-0.76; P = .003) and pain interference ratings (β = 0.30; 95% CI, 0.02-0.57; P = .03) were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings, after adjusting for baseline physical activity, age, sex, and history of anxiety or depression diagnosis. For the secondary purpose, there was a significant interaction between fatigue and age group on sport specialization (β coefficient = 0.49; 95% CI, 0.02-0.95; P = .04). The relationship between fatigue and sport specialization differed by age: a stronger association occurred among older adolescents than younger adolescents. There were no other age × QoL interactions.Conclusion:This study demonstrated that higher depressive symptoms and pain interference ratings were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings. In addition, there was a significant interaction between fatigue and age group, with a stronger association identified among older adolescents. Higher specialized and older adolescent athletes may be more at risk for developing mental health/QoL concerns and may benefit from more targeted screening.Keywords: WISSQ, sport specialization, mental health, adolescence

In the United States, roughly 27 million individuals between the ages of 6 and 17 years participate in organized sports. 1 However, the culture of youth sports has evolved over recent years: it is now less common for adolescent athletes to participate in multiple sports or unstructured free play. 6 Instead, adolescent athletes may choose to specialize in a single sport year-round at the exclusion of other sports (sport specialization). 6 Although participating in sports has many benefits, such as improved quality of life (QoL), physical health, and self-esteem,14,29,36 it comes with inherent risks, including sport-related injuries, fatigue, or burnout. 8 Multiple studies have found that burnout, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and attrition are increased in youth athletes who specialize in their sport at early ages,5,7,20,28 as compared with those who specialize later (at 15 or 16 years) or are multisport athletes. 16 Higher sport specialization levels have also been associated with worse mental health.11,22,41 A recently developed validated sport specialization questionnaire, the Wisconsin Sport Specialization Questionnaire (WISSQ), 23 may provide additional clarity about the varying degrees of sport specialization that can be used to investigate associations with mental health and QoL among adolescent athletes.

Anxiety and depressive symptoms are common among adolescents in the United States. In 2020, the lifetime prevalence of anxiety symptoms among adolescents was >30%, 44 while the lifetime prevalence for depression in adolescents in 2021 was roughly 11%. 31 Although it is not uncommon for adolescents to experience anxiety or depression, studies are mixed on whether sport participation during high school confers a protective effect against mental health conditions as compared with nonathletes.18,33,35,38,39 Some studies have found that participating in sports is protective against mental health conditions,2,3,18,35 noting that athletes reported less psychological stress 38 and better mental, emotional, and physical well-being than nonathletes. 39 However, others have noted that the prevalence of anxiety or depression was as high as 34% among elite adolescent athletes, which was higher than the general population. 33 Previous studies have found that youth athletes who specialize in their sport are more likely to experience anxiety, depression, or burnout than adolescent athletes who are multisport athletes.5,7,16,20,28 Despite growing scientific and clinical interest in the potential consequences of sport specialization, limited research has examined its relationship with mental health among healthy adolescent athletes using the WISSQ.

Although previous studies have used sport specialization scales such as the Jayanthi scale,10,19,21 the low, moderate, and high grouping nature of this approach may reduce the ability to identify more specific relationships between the degree of sport specialization and clinically relevant outcomes. Additionally, prior research indicates that the Jayanthi scale may misclassify athletes who have never played other sports as moderately specialized instead of highly specialized. 32 Previous studies reported that sport specialization among adolescent athletes has been associated with higher rates of depression, anxiety, and burnout.5,7,16,20,28 In addition, athletes who specialized early tended to withdraw from their sports owing to burnout.7,43 Studies have found that in addition to depression, anxiety, and burnout, adolescent athletes who are pressured to perform to high standards are likely to internalize athletic failures and feelings of shame. 17 These feelings may lead to disordered eating behaviors or other harmful behaviors that result in a decline in performance, physical health, and overall QoL. 17 In addition to anxiety and depressive symptoms, evaluating the relationship between sport specialization and outcomes such as fatigue, peer relationships, and pain interference in adolescent athletes is important, as these factors are key indicators of physical and psychosocial well-being. Poor ratings may signal early risks associated with intensive single-sport focus.7,8,26 Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, current evidence does not clearly demonstrate that mental health outcomes and QoL measures are worse in older specialized adolescent athletes as compared with younger adolescent athletes; however, studies have found that early sport specialization (at <12 years of age) has been associated with overuse injury, psychological fatigue, and burnout.7,37,43 Thus, clarifying the relationships among sport specialization, mental health, and QoL, in addition to age, is important to advance understanding of adolescent athlete health outcomes.

The primary purpose of this study was to examine the WISSQ, a recently developed validated sport specialization tool, and its associations with mental health and QoL outcomes (ie, mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, fatigue, peer relationships, and/or pain interference) among healthy adolescent athletes. We hypothesized that a higher degree of sports specialization would be associated with worsened outcomes in each domain (ie, mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, fatigue, peer relationships, and/or pain interference). Secondarily, we sought to examine whether the interaction among mental health, QoL ratings, and sport specialization was dependent on age (older vs younger adolescents: ≥16 vs <16 years). We hypothesized that adolescents ≥16 years old would report worse mental health and QoL ratings as compared with adolescents <16 years old.

Methods

Study Participants and Design

We conducted a cross-sectional study of adolescents who were participating in a season of organized high school sports in a single metro area between July 2023 and August 2025. Potential participants were included if they were active on an organized sports team at a local high school of any level (ie, junior varsity or varsity) and had no injury that limited physical activity participation at the time of enrollment. Study participants were recruited by high school coaches and local community networks through distribution of study flyers. Participants enrolled and underwent an initial preseason assessment in July and August (prior to the start of any high school organized sport participation).

The study evaluation was performed at local recreational centers or sports centers or via online questionnaire completion. Participants completed electronic assessments via iPads (or paper/pen backups when device connectivity issues occurred) detailing demographics, sport specialization, self-reported typical physical activity volume (hours/week), mental health/QoL primary outcome variables (mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, fatigue, peer relationships, and/or pain interference), and self-reported anxiety symptoms and/or depression based on physician diagnosis. In total, the WISSQ and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Global 25 (Version 1.1) contained 49 questions (n = 24 and n = 25, respectively). The study protocol (23-1018) was approved by the local institutional review board before commencement, and all participants and their parents (if <18 years of age) provided informed consent/assent before completing any procedures.

Grouping Variable: Sport Specialization via the WISSQ

During the preseason assessment, participants were asked to complete the WISSQ. The WISSQ is a validated patient-reported outcome tool for adolescents 14 to 18 years old. 23 In our sample, some participants (10%) were 13 years old and were included in accordance with the broader age range (13-18 years) defined by the parent study. The WISSQ consists of 25 questions across 3 domains: physical, motivation, and social. A 3-point response option (yes, sometimes, no) was used for the questions in the physical domain, while a 5-point Likert scale (not at all like me, a little like me, somewhat like me, quite a bit like me, or a lot like me) was used for the motivation and social domains. Questions included “I play certain sports because others think I'm really good at those sports” or “I participate in multiple different sports at the same time.” The WISSQ yielded an overall rating that ranged from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating higher sport specialization level. 23

Outcome Measures: Mobility, Anxiety, Depressive Symptoms, Fatigue, Peer Relationships, and Pain Interference

Participants answered a series of questions from the PROMIS in the following domains: fatigue, pain interference, mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and peer relationships. A T score was then calculated, where a value of 50 represents the mean for the reference population with a standard deviation of 10. 15 A score <40 is less than average and >60 more than average (eg, a higher severity of the domain being measured).25,27,40

To assess mobility, participants ranked 4 questions on a 5-point Likert scale, measuring mobility capabilities such as ability to get up from a chair or ability to participate in running. The total sum of answers created raw scores, converted to T scores, with a higher score indicating greater mobility/physical function. For pediatric mobility, reporting no problems with physical function had a cut point >30, mild problems demonstrate scores 25 to 30, moderate problems 20 to 24, and severe problems <20. 9 This domain has established acceptable test-retest reliability among adolescents. 24

For anxiety and depressive symptoms, participants ranked questions on a 5-point Likert scale with the following answer options: never, rarely, sometimes, often, and always. Anxiety questions measure fear, anxious misery, and hyperarousal, while depression questions measure negative mood, views of self, social cognition, and decreased positive affect and engagement. T scores <50 indicate normal, 50 to 60 mild symptoms, 60 to 70 moderate, and >70 severe distress. 9 This tool has established acceptable test-retest reliability among adolescents for these domains. 15

Fatigue was assessed in the same manner as the other domains. Participants ranked questions on a 5-point Likert scale with the following answer options: never, rarely, sometimes, often, and always. Questions include “Being tired made it hard for me to keep up with my schoolwork,”“I got tired easily,”“I was too tired to do sports or exercise,”“I was too tired to enjoy the things I like to do,”“I felt weak,” and “I had trouble finishing things because I was too tired.” The total sum of answers created raw scores, which were converted to T scores, with a higher score indicating more fatigue.

To assess peer relationships, participants ranked 4 questions on a 5-point Likert scale, reflecting the frequency of perceptions around peer relationships. The total sum of answers created raw scores, which were converted to T scores, with a higher score indicating stronger peer relationships and support. Among uninjured adolescents, 50 is the average rating with a score between 45 and 55 being considered normal.

To assess pain interference, participants answered 4 questions, each ranked on a 5-point Likert scale. Questions measure how pain affects a person's daily life with answer options as follows: not at all, a little bit, somewhat, quite a bit, or very much. Examples include “In the past 7 days, how much did pain interfere with your day-to-day activities” and “In the past 7 days, how often did pain keep you from socializing with others?” The scoring system was the same as previously described for fatigue.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are descriptively presented as mean (standard deviation) and categorical variables as number (percentage within group). We first evaluated the bivariate correlation between WISSQ ratings and each PROMIS domain using scatterplots and Pearson R correlation coefficients, interpreted as weak (0.20-0.39), moderate (0.40-0.59), strong (0.60-0.79), or very strong (≥0.80). We then constructed a multiple linear regression model that evaluated the association between each of the 6 PROMIS domains (predictors) and WISSQ ratings (outcome), adjusting for the effect of age, sex, and self-reported typical physical activity volume (hours/week). All linear regression assumptions were evaluated and met. Residuals were examined for normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity, and no violations were detected. Multicollinearity was assessed by variance inflation factors, all of which were within acceptable limits. Influence diagnostics, including leverage and Cook's distance, indicated no undue influence from individual observations. Accordingly, the regression results reflect a model that satisfies standard assumptions for multiple linear regression. To address our secondary purpose, linear regression models with an interaction term were used to examine the association between each PROMIS domain and sport specialization, with age group (<16 vs ≥16 years) included as an interaction term with each PROMIS domain across different models. Age was dichotomized at 16 years to reflect developmental and sport-participation differences between younger and older high school athletes (eg, increased training demands) and because this threshold aligned with the central tendency of our sample (median age, 15.9 years). Interaction terms were tested, and predicted values with 95% confidence intervals were estimated and visualized through marginal effects plots. Statistical significance for the interpretation of multivariable models was defined a priori as P < .05 or a confidence interval for the adjusted odds ratio that did not cross zero, and all tests were 2-sided. Statistical analysis was performed in Stata Statistical Software (Version 19; StataCorp, LLC).

Results

A total of 316 athletes were enrolled, of whom 251 (79%) completed the entire WISSQ battery and were included in the final analysis (65 athletes did not complete the full set of questionnaires and were excluded). Thus, a total of 251 uninjured adolescent athletes were included in the study (mean ± SD age, 15.9 ± 1.4 years; 72% female; Table 1). A small proportion self-reported a prior anxiety (12%) or depression (5%) diagnosis. The mean WISSQ rating across the entire sample was 54.1 (SD, 15.4), with a median value of 54.2 (IQR, 43.8-66.7). Upon bivariate correlation analysis, higher ratings for depressive symptoms (Figure 1C), fatigue (Figure 1D), and pain interference (Figure 1F) were significantly but weakly associated with WISSQ ratings, while mobility, anxiety, and peer relationships were not significantly correlated with WISSQ ratings. Results from the multivariable linear regression model indicated that higher depressive symptoms and pain interference ratings were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings (Table 2), after adjusting for baseline physical activity, age, sex, and history of anxiety or depression diagnosis.

For our secondary purpose, we found that there was a significant interaction between fatigue and age group on sport specialization (β coefficient = 0.49; 95% CI, 0.02-0.95; P = .04), indicating that the relationship between fatigue and sport specialization differs by age with a stronger association identified among older adolescents than younger adolescents (Figure 2). In a sensitivity analysis modeling age as a continuous variable, the fatigue × age interaction was significant (β = 0.16; 95% CI, 0.001-0.31; P = .05), demonstrating consistency with the primary analysis. We did not identify any other interactions between age group and other PROMIS domains: mobility (P = .07), anxiety (P = .94), depressive symptoms (P = .82), peer relationships (P = .71), or pain interference (P = .06).

Discussion

The major findings of our study showed that higher depressive symptoms and pain interference ratings were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings, after adjusting for baseline physical activity, age, sex, and history of anxiety or depression diagnosis. We did not find any significant associations for the other PROMIS domains (mobility, anxiety, fatigue, or peer relationships). For our secondary purpose, our hypothesis was also partially supported as there was a significant age × sport specialization interaction for fatigue, demonstrating a stronger association between fatigue and sport specialization among older adolescents than younger adolescents. We did not identify any other interactions between age group and other PROMIS domains, including mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, peer relationships, or pain interference.

Previous literature is mixed on whether higher sport specialization is associated with worse mental health outcomes.12,41,45,46 Our study finding that higher WISSQ ratings were associated with higher depressive symptoms and pain interference aligns with prior work indicating that higher sport specialization is associated with worse mental health/QoL outcomes.16,41,42 Among 186 adolescent athletes, highly specialized athletes reported higher levels of depressive symptoms as compared with low and moderate specialized athletes. 41 However, in other studies, high sport specialization was not associated with increased depressive symptoms scores among adolescent softball players, 46 and there was no difference in any PROMIS domain (depressive symptoms, anxiety, pain interference, peer relationships, or fatigue) across sport specialization groups (low, moderate, or high) among adolescent athletes. 12 Among adolescent volleyball players, high sport-specialized athletes reported worse QoL measures as compared with low and moderate sport-specialized athletes, but the differences in QoL measures were no longer significant among specialization groups after adjusting for grade in school and prior injury. 45 Higher depressive symptoms and pain interference ratings were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings in our study, indicating the additional clarity about the degree of sport specialization that the WISSQ provides. Although we asked our participants about their concussion history, we did not ask them about musculoskeletal injury history, frequency, or severity. As highly specialized athletes are more likely to experience overuse injuries as compared with low and moderate specialized athletes,4,19,30 it is possible that injury history affects QoL more than specialization level itself. More research is needed to investigate whether injury history is associated with worse mental health/QoL outcomes and sport specialization rating using the WISSQ.

For our secondary purpose, we found that the relationship between fatigue and sport specialization differs by age with a stronger association identified among older adolescents (≥16 years) than younger adolescents (<16 years). Contrary to our findings, previous work suggests that those who specialize at earlier ages (<16 years) tended to withdraw from their sports owing to burnout or psychological fatigue.7,43 However, our study did not classify sport specialization as specialized versus nonspecialized athletes. Instead, we looked at the spectrum of severity via the WISSQ for older and younger adolescents. Additionally, studies have found that early sport specialization (<12 years of age) has been associated with overuse injury, psychological fatigue, and burnout.7,37,43 The WISSQ has been validated among adolescents between the ages of 14 and 18 years, 23 and future tools that are similar to the WISSQ in length/scoring should become validated among younger adolescents (<14 years). Future work is needed on the association of fatigue and sport specialization particularly among older versus younger adolescents.

Although we did not assess athletes aged <14 years, we did find that older adolescents (≥16 years) may experience more fatigue if they are more specialized in their sport than younger athletes (<16 years). Older adolescents typically engage in higher training loads and more intense competition, especially as specialization intensifies.13,21,26 This greater exposure increases cumulative physical stress and risk for overtraining and fatigue.13,21,26 Older adolescents may also face greater expectations and pressure to perform in their sport, which can outpace recovery and lead to fatigue.13,30 When compared with younger athletes, older adolescents report lower self-esteem and global physical self-perception and higher social physique anxiety, which are associated with increased burnout symptoms, including fatigue. 34 Older adolescents also experience more external pressures from coaches, parents, and peers, as well as internal pressures related to identity, perfectionism, and fear of failure.7,8,34 These factors are strongly linked to burnout and fatigue, especially when sport becomes central to self-identity.7,8,34 Last, the multidimensional nature of the WISSQ captures not just physical but also motivational and social aspects of specialization, 23 which may be more pronounced and potentially detrimental in older adolescents as their commitment and investment in sport increase. Future research should examine the different variables that influence fatigue in specialized athletes to allow for more targeted intervention.

Our study had several limitations. We assessed patient-reported outcomes through the well-established and validated PROMIS questionnaires, and while these are useful ratings-based tools, they were not intended to provide any sort of diagnosis, such as what one would receive from a qualified health care provider. The majority of our sample was White females, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other populations. We also did not assess injury history, aside from concussion history, which could have affected the relationships that we examined. We did not assess sleep quality, which might have affected fatigue among older versus younger specialized adolescents. Additionally, we did not assess specific sport type, club participation, training intensity, or individual versus team sports, which may have influenced the associations between mental health/QoL outcomes and sport specialization. However, strengths of our study include our large sample size of 251 adolescent athletes, as well as being the first study to examine the association of mental health/QoL measures and sport specialization as measured by the WISSQ.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that higher depressive symptoms and pain interference ratings were significantly associated with higher WISSQ ratings. We also found a significant interaction between fatigue and age group, with a stronger association identified among older adolescents. We did not identify any other interactions between age and other PROMIS domains on sport specialization, including mobility, anxiety, depressive symptoms, peer relationships, or pain interference. Higher specialized and older adolescent athletes may be more at risk for developing mental health/QoL concerns and may benefit from more targeted screening. Screening for depressive symptoms, pain interference, and fatigue preseason in these athletes may allow for early identification and help athletes seek appropriate medical care to improve overall QoL and encourage long-term sport participation.
