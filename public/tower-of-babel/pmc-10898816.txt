Internal ribosomal entry site-mediated translational activity of nitric oxide synthase 2
Authors: Kyung-Ha Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe internal ribosome entry site (IRES) is a unique structure found in the 5’ untranslated region (5'-UTR) of specific messenger RNAs (mRNAs) that allows ribosomes to bind and initiate translation without the need for a cap structure. In this study, we investigated the presence and functional properties of the IRES activity of nitric oxide synthase 2 (NOS2) mRNA, which encodes an enzyme that produces nitric oxide in response to various stimuli such as inflammation. Nitric oxide is a signaling molecule that plays a crucial role in various physiological processes, including immune responses and neuronal signaling. Our results showed the existence of IRES activity in the 5'-UTR of Nos2 mRNA in various cell types. IRES-mediated translation of NOS2 mRNA was higher in neuronal cells and its activity increased in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Despite inhibition of cap-dependent translation, nitrite production was partially maintained. These results demonstrate the presence of IRES activity in the 5'-UTR of NOS2 mRNA and suggest that IRES-mediated translation plays a key role in controlling nitric oxide production in response to LPS, an inflammatory stimulus.

Introduction

Nitric oxide synthase 2 (NOS2), also known as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), is an enzyme that generates nitric oxide (NO) in response to certain stimuli, such as inflammation, infection, or injury (Nathan and Xie 1994; Okamoto et al. 2004; Lechner et al. 2005). This signaling molecule plays a crucial role in various physiological processes, including blood vessel regulation, immune responses, oxidative stress control, and neuronal signaling (Esplugues 2002; Tuteja et al. 2004; Zhao et al. 2015; Lee et al. 2021; Jung et al. 2022). NOS2 also transforms L-arginine into NO and L-citrulline. There are three types of NOS: NOS-1 (neuronal NOS), NOS2 (inducible NOS), and NOS-3 (endothelial NOS). NOS-1 and NOS-3 are constitutively expressed and dependent on calcium/calmodulin, producing NO in picomolar to nanomolar quantities (Mattila and Thomas 2014). However, NOS2 is activated by cellular stress as well as specific cytokines and growth factors such as TNFα, IL-1, IFNγ, endotoxins, and lipopolysaccharides (LPS). NOS2 also generates large quantities of NO at the micromolar level for extended periods (Heinecke et al. 2014). NOS2 levels indicate the state of inflammation. Excessive NOS2 production is associated with various pathological conditions, such as sepsis, shock, chronic inflammation, and tumorigenesis (Wang et al. 2018). However, the translational regulation of NOS2 remains poorly understood as most studies have thus far focused on its transcriptional regulation.

The internal ribosome entry site (IRES) is a unique structure found in the 5’ untranslated region (UTR) of specific messenger RNAs (mRNAs) that allows ribosomes to bind and initiate translation without the need for a cap structure (Komar and Hatzoglou 2011). This means that IRES-mediated translation is a mechanism of protein synthesis where translation initiation occurs without the cap structure on the 5’ end of mRNA (Jang et al. 1988; Pelletier and Sonenberg 1988). This alternative mode of regulation enables cells to produce specific proteins in response to different stimuli such as stress, developmental signals, viral infection, and circadian rhythm (Lee et al. 2012; Torres et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2020). IRES-mediated translation plays a crucial role in various biological processes, and its disruption can lead to the development of various diseases, including cancer, neurological disorders, and viral infections (Dobson et al. 2013; Ji et al. 2017; Kapur et al. 2017; Martinez-Salas et al. 2017; Yang and Wang 2019; Fan et al. 2022). Understanding IRES-mediated translation and regulation has significant implications for exploring its role in various diseases and conditions. The 5’ UTR of mRNA plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression by controlling translational efficiency, and cap-independent translation initiation, also known as IRES activity, is one of the mechanisms that regulate translational efficiency. This study aims to investigate the presence and functional properties of IRES activity in the 5’ UTR of NOS2 mRNA.

Results

IRES activity exists in NOS2 mRNA

IRES-dependent translation is mainly modulated by 5′-UTRs. To investigate the existence of an IRES in NOS2 mRNA, we inserted the 5′-UTR of NOS2 mRNA into a bicistronic reporter vector (Figure 1A) (Lee et al. 2012). The vector contained a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter for direct transcription of bicistronic RNA encoding Renilla luciferase (Rluc) in the first cistron and firefly luciferase (Fluc) in the second cistron. The translation of Rluc from the first cistron is cap-dependent, whereas the translation of Fluc reflects the IRES activity of the inserted intergenic sequences. This system is considered to be the gold standard for identifying IRES elements. The 5′-UTR enhanced the translation of Fluc more than 4-fold when compared to the control vector pR/F (Figure 1B). The difference between the mean values of the two luciferase activities depending on the presence or absence of NOS2 5′-UTR was > 3 (Figure 1C). However, the different translational activities of the second cistron, Fluc, did not originate from the difference in mRNA levels. The mRNA levels of pR/F and pR/NOS2-5U/F were not significantly different (Figure 1D). These results suggest that NOS2 mRNA may contain an IRES element within its 5′-UTR. Confirmation of IRES activity in Nos2 mRNA

The validity of experiments investigating IRES-mediated translation of eukaryotic mRNA has recently been questioned due to issues with the methods used to identify IRES elements (Kozak 2001; Wang et al. 2005). The data shown in Figure 1 indicates that the 5'-UTR of NOS2 mRNA may contain either IRES elements that enhance the translation of Fluc from the bicistronic mRNA or a cryptic promoter that creates a monocistronic transcript of Fluc (Han and Zhang 2002; van den Akker et al. 2021). To rule out any cryptic promoter activity in the 5'-UTR of NOS2 mRNA, the CMV promoter was removed from the bicistronic reporter constructs (Figure 2A). The expression levels of Rluc and Fluc were almost zero in the absence of the CMV promoter (Figure 2C and D, ΔCMV-R/NOS2-5U/F). The activities of RLUC and FLUC were also nearly non-existent as they were measured from cells transfected with promoter-less bicistronic constructs (Figure 2E and F, ΔCMV-R/NOS2-5U/F). This confirms that the 5'-UTR of NOS2 mRNA does not contain a cryptic promoter. To determine if the effect of the NOS2 mRNA 5'-UTR on the translation of the second cistron was due to ribosome reinitiation (Fitzgerald and Semler 2009), a synthetic hairpin loop was inserted upstream of Rluc in pHR/NOS2-5U/F (Figure 2B). Insertion of the hairpin loop reduced the expression of the first cistron, Rluc, by 50% when compared to that in pR/NOS2-5U/F, but the activity of the second cistron, Fluc, produced by pHR/NOS2-5U/F, was not affected (Figure 2E and F, pHR/NOS2-5U/F). This argues against ribosome reinitiation as a possible mechanism. The knockdown of bicistronic reporter RNA by Rluc or Fluc targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA) led to a similar decrease in both luciferase activities of pR/NOS2-5U/F system (Figure 2G). These results further support the exclusion of any cryptic promoter activities. Therefore, we concluded that the 5'-UTR of Nos2 mRNA contains an IRES that can directly initiate translation. Neuronal cells display greater NOS2 IRES activity

Although the expression pattern of NOS2 is controversial, it has been shown to be highly inducible in neuronal cells (Bechade et al. 2014). To examine the differences in NOS2 IRES activity between different cell lines, IRES activity of the 5′-UTR of NOS2 mRNA was measured in several cell lines, including HEK 293A, NIH/3T3, Raw 264.7, SCN2.2, and Neuro-2a (Figure 3A-3). We observed enhanced IRES activity in the immortalized human embryonic kidney cell HEK 293A (approximately 5-fold), mouse embryonic fibroblast NIH/3T3 (approximately 5-fold), macrophage cell line Raw 264.7 (approximately 3-fold), rat suprachiasmatic nucleus cell line SCN2.2 (approximately 9-fold), and neuroblastoma cell line Neuro-2a (approximately 10-fold) cells in comparison to in the control vector pR/F. LPS increases NOS2 IRES activity and nitrite levels

The expression of NOS2 can be induced by inflammatory stimuli, including the bacterial compound LPS (Nathan and Xie 1994; Okamoto et al. 2004). When LPS was introduced into the neuroblastoma cell line Neuro-2a, nitrite levels, the oxidative form of the NOS2 product nitric oxide, increased (Figure 4A). Inhibition of transcription by actinomycin D (ActD) treatment dramatically decreased LPS-induced nitrite levels; whereas treatment with rapamycin (Rapa), which blocks the phosphorylation of 4E-BP1 and inhibits cap-dependent translation, reduced LPS-induced nitrite levels by approximately 35% relative to in the LPS- and vehicle (veh)-treated conditions. Treatment with cycloheximide (CHX), which blocks both cap-dependent and independent translation by preventing translation elongation, significantly reduced nitrite levels comparable to that in the RNA synthesis-blocked conditions of ActD-treated samples. These results suggest that cap-independent translation is necessary for LPS-induced nitrite production. The IRES activity of the NOS2 5′-UTR was also investigated in response to LPS treatment. The NOS2 5′-UTR showed approximately 10-fold higher IRES activity compared to that of the control, and LPS treatment significantly increased the IRES activity of the NOS2 5′-UTR relative to that of the control DMSO-treated samples (Figure 4B). These results indicate that IRES-mediated translation is necessary for nitrite production and that IRES NOS2 5′-UTR activity and nitrite production are increased in response to LPS treatment. Discussion

The results of this study demonstrate the presence of IRES activity in the 5′-UTR of NOS2 mRNA. The increased FLUC/RLUC activity in the bicistronic plasmid system containing the 5’-UTR of NOS2 suggests that this region can induce cap-independent translation. Further experiments using hairpin loop insertion and promoter deletion confirmed that IRES activity was not due to cryptic promoter activity or ribosome reinitiation. The IRES activity of NOS2 was greater in neuronal cells than in other cell types. Moreover, LPS treatment increases IRES activity and nitrite production in the neuronal cell line Neuro-2a. These findings highlight the importance of IRES-mediated translation in nitrite production and the role of the 5′-UTR of NOS2 in regulating this process.

IRES-mediated translation is modulated by mRNA and RNA-binding proteins. IRES activity-controlling proteins are known as IRES-transacting factors (ITAFs) (Vagner et al. 2001; Komar and Hatzoglou 2011; Godet et al. 2019). In this study, we found that the NOS2 5′-UTR displayed differing levels of IRES activity according to cell type. The greater NOS2 IRES activity of neuronal cells compared with other cell types further highlights the potential physiological importance of this mechanism. Neuronal cell-specific ITAFs may modulate the IRES activity of the NOS2 5′-UTR in neuronal cells. However, RNA secondary structures could differ among cell types, which could change the context of bound RNA-binding proteins (Sanchez de Groot et al. 2019). RNA binding proteins with altered binding can exhibit different IRES activities on the same mRNA. To clarify the reasons for differing NOS2 IRES activity among cell types, cell type-specific NOS2 5′-UTR binding proteins and their functions need to be investigated.

In a previous study, the presence of IRES activity in the human 5′-UTR of NOS2 was investigated, and it was reported that the human NOS2 5′-UTR does not possess IRES activity (Gather et al. 2019). However, in our research, we utilized the 5′-UTR of mouse NOS2, not the human counterpart. Although there is some sequence similarity between the human and mouse NOS2 5′-UTR sequences, even minor differences, such as small deletions or point mutations, can potentially impact the IRES activity of the 5′-UTR (Chappell et al. 2000; Kim et al. 2007; Grover et al. 2011; Lee et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2012). Additionally, it's important to note that the RNA secondary structure of the human and mouse 5′-UTRs of NOS2 also differs. The difference in sequences and secondary structures between human and mouse NOS2 5′-UTR could recruit different RNA-binding proteins, and it might lead to different IRES activity.

Many mammalian genes containing IRES activity exhibit regulation of their translation through both cap-dependent and IRES-mediated mechanisms. In this research, we observed that rapamycin partially inhibited nitrite production (Figure 4A). This suggests that the translation of NOS2 is regulated through a cap-independent mechanism, but not exclusively. Some degree of NOS2 translation is also mediated by cap-dependent mechanisms. The balance between these two modes of translation in the mouse NOS2 5’-UTR may vary under different cellular physiological conditions.

These results suggest that the IRES activity of NOS2 plays a role in regulating nitrite production in response to LPS, an inflammatory stimulus. LPS-induced ITAFs of the NOS2 5′-UTR or the binding pattern of RNA binding proteins to the NOS2 5′-UTR according to LPS treatment may be important in determining the exact mechanism of LPS-induced IRES-mediated translation of NOS2. Further studies are required to determine the exact mechanism by which the IRES activity of NOS2 is regulated and its role in nitric oxide production in response to different stimuli. Overall, these findings contribute to our understanding of the regulation of NOS2 expression and nitrite production, which has implications for our understanding of cellular signaling and gene expression mechanisms in inflammation.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

Mouse NOS2 5′-UTR (NM_010927.3) was amplified from cDNA of SCN2.2 using Pfu polymerase (NEB) and confirmed by sequencing. The resulting products were cloned into the SalI/SmaI site of the intercistronic region of the pR/F bicistronic vector containing Rluc in the first cistron and Fluc in the second cistron (Lee et al. 2012; Kim et al. 2020). We used pR/F, pHR/F, and ΔCMV-R/F vector backbones (Lee et al. 2012).

Cell culture and drug treatment

HEK 293A, NIH/3T3, RAW 264.7, and Neuro-2a cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich). SCN2.2 cells were cultured in minimum essential Eagle’s medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% GlutamaxTM supplement (GibcoTM), 1% MEM non-essential amino acids (GibcoTM), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Merck).

To block the transcription system, NIH 3T3 cells were treated with 5 μg/mL of ActD (Sigma-Aldrich) (Bensaude 2011; Lee et al. 2014). To block the translation system, NIH 3T3 cells were treated with 20 nM Rapa (Sigma-Aldrich) or 100 μg/mL CHX (Sigma-Aldrich), and then harvested at the indicated times (Lee et al. 2012). For LPS treatment, 1 μg/mL of LPS was administered to cells, which were then incubated for 12 h.

Transient transfection

For the expression of the reporter constructs, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well 1 d prior to transfection. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (InvitrogenTM) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were harvested and subjected to other assays.

For siRNA transfection, the following sense sequences of siRNAs were used: Fluc_si – CUUACGCUGAGUACUUCGAdTdT and Rluc_si – GGCCUUUCACUACUCCUACdTdT.

Nitrite measurement

Nitrite levels in the LPS-treated cell culture medium were measured using the Griess Reagent System following the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega).

RNA quantification

Total RNA was extracted from the cells using TRIzolTM reagent (InvitrogenTM). RNA was reverse transcribed using GoScriptTM Reverse Transcriptase (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mRNA levels of endogenous or reporter plasmids were detected by quantitative real-time PCR using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR Instrument (Applied Biosystems) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primer sequences used are described below.

Fluc, 5′-GAGGTTCCATCTGCCAGGTA-3′ and 5′-CACACAGTTCGCCTCTTTGA-3′; Rluc, 5′-GTAACGCTGCCTCCAGCTAC-3′ and 5′-CCAAGCGGTGAGGTACTTGT-3′; Nos2, 5′-CACCTTGGAGTTCACCCAGT-3′ and 5′-TGGTGGTGACAAGCACATTT-3′

Statistical analysis

Statistical parameters, including the definitions and exact values of n (the number of biological repeats), distributions, and deviations, are reported in the figures and their corresponding legends. All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons between more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance was used with Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software.
