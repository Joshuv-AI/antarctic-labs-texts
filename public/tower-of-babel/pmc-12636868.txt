Does preoperative prehabilitation improve lower limb motor function in patients with anterior cruciate ligament rupture? A protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Kai Wan, Qiaojun Zhang, Yumeng Xu, Libo Li, Ben Ma, Xiaohui Lei, Yiying Mi, Jing Jiang, Huali Shang, Hongfei Qiao, Bin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPostoperative rehabilitation after anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is crucial for functional recovery. Prehabilitation, performed before surgery, is considered to optimise the patient’s condition and improve postoperative outcomes. However, the evidence supporting the effectiveness of standardised prehabilitation protocols remains insufficient, particularly in comparison to conventional home rehabilitation programmes. This study aims to evaluate the impact of a 2-week standardised preoperative prehabilitation programme versus home rehabilitation training on perioperative functional outcomes and medium-term to long-term recovery in ACLR patients through a randomised controlled trial, providing evidence-based guidance for optimising rehabilitation pathways.Methods and analysisThis study is a single-centre, prospective, assessor-blinded, randomised controlled trial. Eligible participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the experimental group (receiving 2 weeks of standardised preoperative rehabilitation) or the control group (receiving 2 weeks of home-based rehabilitation training). Both groups will undergo 12 weeks of standardised rehabilitation postoperatively. The outcome measures include International Knee Documentation Committee Score (IKDC), Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score, Visual Analogue Scale, muscle strength, joint range of motion, anterior cruciate ligament-Return to Sport after Injury Index and Tegner score. The assessment time points are as follows: baseline (T0), 1–3 days preoperatively (T1), postoperative day 1 (T2), 30 days (T3), 90 days (T4), 180 days (T5) and 360 days (T6). All assessments will be performed at fixed time intervals within the same department of rehabilitation medicine, with the testing order randomised. The primary outcome (IKDC) will be analysed using a linear mixed model (LMM), including time, group and their interaction as fixed effects, with individuals as random effects. Secondary outcomes will be analysed using LMM, repeated measures analysis of variance or χ² tests. Missing data will be handled using the multiple imputation method, under the assumption that data are missing completely at random and effect sizes will be evaluated using Cohen’s d (α=0.05).Ethics and disseminationThis study has received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Number: 2025012). All participants will provide written informed consent after reviewing documentation containing comprehensive details regarding the study procedures, risks and benefits, enabling informed decision-making about participation. The findings generated from this research will be disseminated via publication in an open-access, peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant scientific conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500097293.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tears are a common knee injury, particularly prevalent among young athletes, with an annual incidence of approximately 68.6 cases per 100 000 people.1 These injuries not only cause acute pain, swelling and joint instability but may also lead to secondary meniscal tears, cartilage damage and ultimately increase the risk of developing osteoarthritis (OA) of the knee.2 Statistics show that around 50% of ACL tear patients develop OA within 10 years of the injury, significantly impacting their quality of life.3 Therefore, an ACL tear is not only an acute structural injury but also poses long-term functional impairment and degenerative changes, necessitating timely and effective clinical management. For patients with ACL tears who engage in sports, ACL reconstruction (ACLR) is the standard treatment to restore knee stability and return to preinjury activity levels. Approximately 2 million ACLR surgeries are performed globally each year.4 However, around 65% of patients successfully return to their preinjury sports level within 2 years after surgery, with this figure being even lower among non-elite athletes.5 Even with postoperative rehabilitation, the intensity of return to sport 1 year after surgery remains suboptimal. A study involving 675 ACLR patients found that although 91% of patients expected to return to sport before surgery, only 24% achieved a return to their preinjury activity level within 1 year after surgery.6 This gap is attributed to factors such as neuromuscular deficits, functional impairments and psychological barriers,79 particularly fear of reinjury and lack of confidence, which are key obstacles to returning to sport postsurgery.10 11 The significant gap between patient expectations and actual functional recovery highlights the limitations of current ACL injury management strategies. Relying solely on ‘surgical reconstruction and post-operative rehabilitation’ is increasingly insufficient to restore optimal function, with some patients failing to return to desired activity levels even after successful surgery. Recent evidence further suggests that even with the introduction of rehabilitation programmes incorporating differential training and visuomotor training post-ACL surgery, athletes still face challenges in functional recovery.12 These findings collectively highlight the need to shift the intervention focus to the preoperative phase. By optimising the preoperative condition through prehabilitation, we can address the limitations of postoperative rehabilitation, thereby improving long-term prognosis. Although traditional ACL rehabilitation has focused on postoperative interventions, an increasing body of evidence suggests that prehabilitation may help optimise treatment outcomes. Prehabilitation aims to improve both the physical and psychological condition of the patient, thereby providing a better foundation for postoperative recovery.13 Studies have shown that prehabilitation, through early physical therapy, aims to restore full knee extension and a good range of motion (ROM) in flexion, improve quadriceps contraction function and correct gait abnormalities and knee proprioceptive deficits prior to surgery.14 Additionally, prehabilitation training aids in psychological preparation. By engaging in preoperative exercises, patients enhance their sense of control over knee function, rebuild their confidence and reduce anxiety and fear related to the surgery and postoperative rehabilitation.15

Some studies have shown that prehabilitation can improve early postoperative function. For example, Shaarani et al’s research demonstrated that the prehabilitation group had significantly better single-leg jump distance and knee joint scores at 12 weeks postsurgery compared with the control group.16 Similarly, Eitzen et al observed that patients who underwent preoperative quadriceps strengthening exercises had better knee function scores and dynamic stability postsurgery, while those who did not undergo preoperative training had poorer recovery.17 However, not all studies have reached the same conclusion. Some small-scale studies failed to show significant effects of prehabilitation on pain or function. Shaarani et al’s study reported that preoperative rehabilitation did not significantly improve pain or subjective function scores at 3 months postsurgery.16 A 2020 systematic review also pointed out that while prehabilitation led to small improvements in quadriceps strength and single-leg jump, it had no significant impact on pain or general function.18 These discrepancies highlight the uncertainty in the field, and further high-quality research is needed to design the optimal prehabilitation protocol. In clinical practice, there are differing opinions on whether prehabilitation should be incorporated as a standard step. On the one hand, many orthopaedic and sports medicine specialists advocate for preoperative rehabilitation in patients scheduled for ACL reconstruction to optimise the knee joint environment and improve surgical outcomes. On the other hand, some clinicians are concerned that an extended preoperative waiting period may lead to joint instability, increasing the risk of secondary injuries, such as meniscal tears. Therefore, the decision of ‘immediate surgery or a period of prehabilitation” should be personalised based on the specific circumstances of each patient. Currently, there is a lack of large-scale randomised controlled trials on this issue, leading to significant variation in clinical practice. In conclusion, although prehabilitation is supported by a certain evidence base, its optimal implementation and clinical benefits remain contentious and require further high-quality research to clarify. A survey by Alshehri found that 73.7% of patients awaiting ACLR did not receive prehabilitation, with the main barriers being lack of recommendations from the orthopaedic team and lack of awareness.19

In conclusion, although traditional treatment relies on surgical reconstruction and postoperative rehabilitation, long-term recovery outcomes remain suboptimal, highlighting the urgent need to explore new interventions. Preoperative prehabilitation, as a proactive strategy, has both theoretical and clinical support and may have a positive impact on postoperative recovery. Against this backdrop, we propose a prospective randomised controlled trial aimed at establishing the first evidence-based expert consensus on ACL prehabilitation in China, addressing the gap between clinical practice and evidence translation. As van Melick stated, ‘The key to optimising ACL rehabilitation outcomes is to shift the focus of interventions to the preoperative phase, rather than concentrating solely on postoperative interventions’.20 The results of this trial will provide high-level evidence for the standardised implementation of prehabilitation, with the ultimate goal of improving the long-term functional recovery and quality of life for patients with ACL injuries.

Study aims and objectives

Primary objective

This study will aim to evaluate the impact of a standardised 2-week preoperative prehabilitation programme on postoperative lower extremity functional recovery in patients with ACL tears. The primary effectiveness of this intervention will be assessed by comparing changes in knee joint function between the intervention group and the control group.

Secondary objective

The secondary objectives will be to investigate the effects of preoperative rehabilitation on ROM, muscle strength, pain control, psychological readiness for return to sport (ACL-Return to Sport after Injury (ACL-RSI)) and daily activity ability (Tegner score). Additionally, the safety and patient adherence to the intervention will be assessed.

Research hypothesis

We hypothesise that a 2-week standardised prehabilitation programme for patients with ACL tears will more effectively improve postoperative lower limb functional movement, patient-reported outcomes and psychological readiness to return to sport, compared with traditional home-based rehabilitation.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

This study will be a single-centre, prospective, single-blind (blinded to the assessors), randomised controlled trial. This trial was prospectively registered prior to the enrolment of the first participant (Trial registration number: ChiCTR2500097293). Eligible participants will be randomly assigned to the experimental group and the control group in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated random sequence. The control group will undergo home-based rehabilitation training, while the experimental group will receive a standardised preoperative rehabilitation programme for 2 weeks. All participants in both groups will receive a standardised postoperative rehabilitation programme for 12 weeks. On enrolment, participants will undergo an initial baseline assessment, including the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score, Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score, muscle strength, knee joint ROM, ACL-RSI index and Tegner activity scale. Evaluations will take place 1–3 days before surgery and will be followed up on postoperative day 1, day 30, day 90, day 180 and day 360. Figure 1 will show the flowchart of this clinical trial. The research protocol will have been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval number: 2025012) and will strictly adhere to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants will sign an informed consent form, and any adverse events will be recorded and reported (see online supplemental appendix A). The study protocol was written following the PREPARE(Practice guideline for the Planning and Reporting of Evidence-based clinical trials And REsearch) trial guidelines21 and the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials 2013 checklist22 (see online supplemental appendix B).

Interventions

This study comprises an experimental group (receiving a standardised 2-week prehabilitation programme) and a control group (receiving a 2-week home-based rehabilitation training regimen). Both groups of patients will then undergo a standardised, uniform 12-week postoperative rehabilitation protocol. This protocol specifies in detail the objectives, specific exercises, frequency and intensity for each rehabilitation phase. All physiotherapists involved in the study will receive uniform training on the protocol to ensure adherence to its delivery. The same team of physiotherapists will supervise the rehabilitation process for both groups; however, they will strictly adhere to the group-specific protocols and avoid any cross-application of the two interventions. Adherence to the rehabilitation process will be recorded and monitored through treatment logs. The detailed description of the intervention follows the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist23 to ensure the clarity and transparency of the intervention, and the complete TIDieR checklist will be submitted (see onlinesupplemental appendices CE).

Prehabilitation protocol (experimental group)

The experimental group will undergo a 2-week prehabilitation programme before undergoing ACL reconstruction surgery. The programme mainly includes neuromuscular electrical stimulation, muscle strengthening exercises, balance training and cryotherapy for swelling reduction.

Pain management and swelling control

Cryotherapy

Apply an ice pack wrapped in a thin towel to cover the knee joint and areas of noticeable swelling. Apply for 15–20 min per session, with a 2–3 hours interval between applications, especially after physical activities or training.

Leg elevation

While lying on the back, elevate the entire lower limb (ankle above the knee, knee above the heart) using cushions. Elevate for at least 30 min during rest, sleep or after training sessions.

Knee flexion and extension exercises

The patient should perform knee flexion and extension while seated or lying down. The flexion angle can gradually increase. Both active and passive knee flexion–extension exercises are performed to restore the ROM. Perform 10–15 repetitions per set, with 10 sets, 3 days a week.

Neuromuscular electrical stimulation

Electrodes will be placed on key muscles such as the quadriceps and hamstrings, ensuring that electrical stimulation effectively targets the muscle groups. The treatment will last for 20 min, once per day, three times a week.

Muscle strengthening exercises

Phase 1: Isometric quadriceps contractions. The patient lies supine, placing a rolled towel under the knee. The patient pushes down to tighten the back of the knee, then slowly relaxes. Perform 15 s per contraction, 10 repetitions per set, for five sets. Phase 2: Straight leg raises. The patient lies supine with both legs extended. Slowly raise one leg to approximately a 30–45 degree angle from the bed, hold for 5–10 s and then slowly lower it. Perform 10–15 repetitions per set, alternating legs for five sets. Phase 3: Dynamic training for quadriceps and hamstrings. Perform knee extension and flexion exercises in both seated and supine positions. The intensity should gradually increase but not exceed the preoperative pain threshold. Perform 10 repetitions per set, for 10 sets, once per day, three times a week.

Functional training

Functional exercises include single-leg standing, balance board exercises, walking and lateral step movements. The therapist will adjust the exercises based on the patient’s condition. Each session lasts 20 min, performed once per day, three times a week.

During training, maintain low-to-moderate intensity to avoid excessive fatigue or pain. The intensity should be within the tolerable range of the participant, focusing on improving ROM and muscle strength. As the knee joint’s flexibility and strength gradually improve, the intensity and frequency of training may be increased, but avoid rushing progress.

Home-based rehabilitation protocol (control group)

The control group will receive a standardised home-based self-rehabilitation training video prior to surgery. The video includes activation exercises for the knee joint surrounding muscles, ROM recovery exercises and recommended training frequency and intensity (at least three times per week, each session lasting no less than 30 min). To enhance adherence, participants are required to record their training time, content, intensity, subjective feelings and any discomfort during each session. Additionally, the research team will follow-up weekly via phone, WeChat or video calls to verify adherence and provide feedback and encouragement. The video includes safety precautions and error correction tips to ensure safe and correct execution of exercises.

Standard postoperative rehabilitation protocol

Early postoperative phase (0–2 weeks)

Cryotherapy, leg elevation and straight leg raise: In accordance with the preoperative regimen for the experimental group.

Ankle pump exercises: In a supine position, keep the leg extended and perform dorsiflexion (pointing toes upward) and plantarflexion (pointing toes downward) for 20–30 repetitions per set. Perform one to two sets per hour to promote blood circulation and reduce swelling.

Muscle strengthening (1): Isometric quadriceps contractions: with the knee extended, contract the quadriceps (the muscle at the front of the thigh), tightening the muscles at the back of the knee. Hold for 5–10 s before relaxing. Repeat 20–30 times per set, performing three to four sets per day. This exercise strengthens the quadriceps without adding load to the joint.

Mid-postoperative phase (2–6 weeks)

Muscle strengthening: (1) Continue isometric quadriceps contractions as in the early phase but with increased intensity and holding time. Perform 30–50 repetitions per set, four to five sets per day. (2) Progressive straight leg raises: Add resistance by placing a 1–2 kg sandbag on the ankle during the straight leg raise, or perform side-lying leg raises to target the outer thigh and prone leg raises to target the back thigh. Perform 10–15 repetitions per set, three to four sets per day.

Knee ROM training: Continue to increase knee flexion and extension range. Perform active-assisted knee flexion and extension exercises by sitting on the edge of the bed with the unaffected leg assisting the affected leg. Assistive devices, such as pulley systems, can also be used. By 6 weeks postsurgery, the knee ROM should reach approximately 0–120 degrees.

Closed kinetic chain exercises: Begin partial closed-chain exercises, such as wall squats. With feet shoulder-width apart and back against the wall, squat slowly to a 30–45 degree knee flexion and hold for 30–60 s. Perform 5–10 repetitions per set, three to four sets per day. Additionally, begin fixed bicycle training, adjusting seat height and resistance. Ride for 10–15 min per session, two to three times per day to enhance knee stability, lower limb muscle strength and proprioception.

Gait training: Begin weight-bearing exercises with the aid of parallel bars or a walker. Start with partial weight-bearing and gradually progress to full weight-bearing. Focus on even weight distribution when standing and maintain a stable and balanced gait. As gait improves, reduce the use of assistive devices.

Late postoperative phase (6–12 weeks)

Strengthening training: (1) Deep squats: stand with feet shoulder-width apart or slightly wider. Squat slowly to a 90-degree knee flexion and then rise slowly. Perform 10–15 repetitions per set, three to four sets per day. Gradually increase squat depth and weight while maintaining proper form and avoiding incorrect knee movements. (2) Single-leg squats: perform single-leg squats, lowering the body to 30–45 degrees of knee flexion and then stand up. Alternate legs for 8–10 repetitions per set, three to four sets per day, to enhance unilateral strength and stability. (3) Hamstring training: perform prone knee flexion or standing leg curls using resistance bands or dumbbells. Perform 10–15 repetitions per set, three to four sets per day to strengthen the hamstrings, balancing the quadriceps-to-hamstring strength ratio and protecting the knee joint.

Proprioceptive training: (1) Balance board training: stand on a balance board, performing front-back and side-to-side movements or single-leg standing exercises. Perform each session for 5–10 min, two to three times per day to enhance knee proprioception and balance control. (2) Eyes-closed standing or walking: perform standing or short-distance walking exercises with eyes closed on a flat surface. The goal is to rely on proprioception to maintain balance. Gradually increase the duration of each exercise, two to three times per day.

Flexibility training: Perform active knee flexion and extension exercises, such as kneeling and supine knee hugs, to increase the knee’s ROM and flexibility. Low-intensity aerobic exercises like skipping rope or light jogging can also be incorporated but should be controlled to avoid excessive fatigue or injury.

Functional recovery training: Focus on activities of daily living (ADL) training, such as stair climbing, squatting, bending to pick up objects, to simulate real-life scenarios and improve the patient’s functional independence and quality of life.

Outcome measurements

The assessments will be conducted at baseline (T0) when the participants are enrolled, preoperatively 1–3 days before surgery (T1), on postoperative day 1 (T2), at 30 days postoperative (T3), at 90 days postoperative (T4), at 180 days postoperative (T5) and at 360 days postoperative (T6). All tests will be performed at the same time of day, and all measurements will be completed in the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine, Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. All tests will be randomised to avoid any biases caused by patients becoming familiar with or learning the tests. Online supplemental appendix F shows the outcome measures and measurement time points of this trial. To maximise retention rates for long-term follow-up, a comprehensive set of strategies will be implemented, including: (1) collecting detailed contact information from participants and multiple alternative contacts during enrolment; (2) sending personalised reminders via telephone, text message or email prior to each scheduled follow-up point; (3) offering modest travel subsidies or small financial incentives to patients who complete all follow-up assessments; and (4) establishing proactive communication channels to regularly engage participants with health information or seasonal greetings throughout the study period, thereby maintaining a positive participant relationship and strong contact engagement.

Primary outcome measurement

International Knee Documentation Committee Score

The IKDC score is a validated and standardised assessment tool used to comprehensively evaluate functional recovery following knee joint structural damage and reconstruction. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better knee function and fewer symptoms. It includes three dimensions: subjective symptoms (eg, pain, swelling, giving way), activity levels and clinical examination findings. It is widely applied in clinical rehabilitation and sports medicine research. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the IKDC score after ACLR is 10 points, which represents the smallest perceptible improvement in subjective knee function and psychological readiness for recovery.24

Secondary outcome measurements

Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score

The KOOS is a self-administered questionnaire designed to assess the impact of knee-related conditions (injury or OA) on knee function and quality of life. It includes five subscales: pain, symptoms, ADL, sports/recreation and quality of life, each scored from 0 (extreme problems) to 100 (no problems). Although the minimal perceptible clinical improvement for KOOS has not been formally established, a 10-point change is commonly adopted as the clinically meaningful threshold, as KOOS incorporates the full Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index(WOMAC) index.25

Visual Analogue Scale

The VAS uses a 10 cm line anchored by ‘0’ (no pain) and ‘10’ (worst pain) to measure subjective pain intensity. Patients mark their current level of pain on the line, which is then measured in centimetres. The VAS demonstrates high reproducibility and sensitivity to clinical change. The MCID for postoperative VAS pain scores is 1.4 points, reflecting the smallest perceptible and clinically acceptable reduction in pain intensity.26

Muscle strength

Quadriceps and hamstring muscle strength will be measured using a handheld digital dynamometer (Lafayette Instrument, USA). Participants will perform maximal voluntary contractions in a standardised sitting position (knee flexed at 60°). Three trials will be performed, and the mean value will be recorded. No universal MCID has been defined for lower limb muscle strength after ACLR. Therefore, the minimal detectable change is adopted. A 10–15% improvement in strength from baseline is considered clinically meaningful.27

Knee range of motion

Knee flexion and extension will be assessed using a standard goniometer (Changzhou Qianjing, China). Three consecutive measurements will be taken to calculate the average. There is no established MCID for postoperative knee ROM. However, a ≥10° increase in flexion is generally regarded as clinically significant in early rehabilitation phases.

Anterior cruciate ligament-Return to Sport after Injury

The ACL-RSI scale assesses psychological readiness to return to sport, including confidence, motivation and fear of reinjury. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater psychological readiness. The MCID for the ACL-RSI is 15.3 points, representing the smallest clinically important improvement associated with substantial benefit in psychological recovery.28

Reinjury rate

The reinjury rate records the incidence of ACL reinjury during the study period to assess the long-term stability of postoperative recovery.

Tegner score

The Tegner Activity Scale assesses physical activity and sport participation levels on a scale from 0 (disability due to knee injury) to 10 (competitive sports at an elite level). A 1-point change on the Tegner scale represents the MCID in perceived physical activity level.29

Treatment satisfaction

Treatment satisfaction will be assessed using a self-reported questionnaire to evaluate the patients’ satisfaction with the entire treatment and rehabilitation process. A 5-point scale or VAS will be used to collect subjective feedback, reflecting the patient’s overall feelings about the treatment outcomes.

Sample size and power considerations

The sample size for this study was calculated using G*Power V.3.1.9.7. The primary outcome measure was the IKDC score. Based on literature reporting that the MCID for the IKDC score after ACL reconstruction is 10 points, with a SD of 15 points, the effect size was calculated as d=0.67.24 30 This study employed a two-tailed test with a significance level of α=0.05 and a statistical power of 80% (β=0.20). Thus, a minimum of 36 patients per group was required. Considering an approximate 20% dropout rate, each group ultimately required the inclusion of 45 patients, totalling 90 patients.

Randomisation and blinding

The randomisation process will be carried out by an independent statistician who is not involved in subsequent subject assessments, data collection or data analysis. Eligible ACL tear patients will be numbered from 1 to 90 based on the order of their inclusion. A random number table will be used to generate a random number sequence, and the subjects numbered 1 to 90 will be ordered according to the generated random numbers, creating corresponding treatment groups (experimental group or control group). Based on the random group allocation provided by the software, participants will be assigned to the appropriate experimental or control group, and the corresponding intervention measures will be implemented. The generated random group allocation information will be strictly recorded and kept confidential and will only be accessible to the independent statistician responsible for randomisation, who will not be involved in the subsequent trial process.

Due to the significant differences in the nature of the interventions between the experimental and control groups, double-blinding of the treating practitioners (eg, therapists) and participants will not be feasible in this study. Following random allocation, participants will be informed of the specific intervention they will receive but will remain unaware of the details of the other group’s protocol. To minimise bias to the greatest extent possible, blinding will be implemented for data collectors (assessors) and statistical analysts. Throughout the data collection and analysis process, the assessors will remain blinded to group allocation. They must strictly adhere to predefined assessment criteria and procedures and are prohibited from communicating with any individual’s privy to group assignment details (such as therapists, participants or the statistician responsible for randomisation) regarding any information that could potentially unblind them. Once all data collection is complete and the final statistical analysis phase has concluded, an independent party who has maintained the random allocation sequence will perform the unblinding to facilitate the correct interpretation of the analysis results.

To mitigate the potential for performance bias arising from the inability to blind patients and therapists, the following supplementary measures will be adopted: (1) Standardised communication scripts will be used to introduce all participants to the study information and rehabilitation principles, avoiding any details that might reveal intergroup differences; (2) An independent research coordinator, who will be blinded to patient group allocation, will be tasked with outcome assessment and data collection; (3) In-depth discussions between patients and therapists beyond those stipulated in the study protocol, which could potentially influence the study outcomes, will be discouraged. Furthermore, a treatment fidelity checklist will be developed. This checklist will be used by the research coordinator for periodic audits to ensure therapists’ strict adherence to the prescribed rehabilitation and intervention protocols.

Study duration

Patient recruitment commenced on 27 February 2025 and is expected to conclude on 30 June 2026. The preoperative and postoperative rehabilitation interventions and follow-up will last approximately 13 months. The study end date is defined as the time point when the last participant completes the final data collection. Subsequently, approximately 11 months are allocated for data analysis and manuscript writing. Therefore, the total study duration from participant enrolment to the completion of the final research outcomes is approximately 40 months, with an anticipated completion date of 30 June 2028.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes

All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Descriptive statistics will be performed for baseline characteristics (such as age, sex, weight, height), including means, SD, minimum values and maximum values. Categorical variables (such as sex, smoking status, additional treatments) will be presented as frequencies and percentages. The analysis of the primary outcome measure (IKDC score) will be performed using linear mixed models (LMMs) to assess the differences between the intervention and control groups. In these models, time (follow-up points) will be treated as a fixed effect, group (intervention vs control) will be treated as a fixed effect, individual differences (repeated measurements) will be treated as a random effect and the group×time interaction will be included to evaluate the effect of the intervention. Secondary outcome measures, such as KOOS score, VAS score, muscle strength, knee ROM, reinjury rate, Tegner score and treatment satisfaction, will be analysed using LMMs, repeated measures analysis of variance or χ² tests, as appropriate. For missing data, multiple imputation will be used under the assumption that data are missing completely at random. Effect sizes will be evaluated using Cohen’s d to assess clinical differences between the intervention and control groups. All analyses will be conducted using R statistical software, with a significance level set at α=0.05 for two-tailed tests.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

The economic evaluation will be conducted from a societal perspective, encompassing direct medical costs, direct non-medical costs and indirect costs. Direct medical costs will include, but are not limited to, professional fees for preoperative rehabilitation training, equipment and venue usage, and costs of subsequent medical services. These costs will be quantified primarily based on the national ‘Standardised Fee Schedule for Medical Services’ and provincial-level healthcare insurance reimbursement rates. Direct non-medical costs will cover transportation expenses and the opportunity cost of time for patients and their families, collected via a structured patient cost questionnaire. Indirect costs, representing productivity losses due to the disease and its treatment, will be assessed by evaluating both absenteeism (work days lost) and presenteeism (reduced work performance). The human capital approach will be employed, valuing productivity losses using the locally published average daily wage rate.

Health outcomes will be measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Clinical efficacy, assessed through a battery of validated instruments including the IKDC score, OOS, VAS, muscle strength recovery, ROM improvement, ACL-RSI and Tegner score, will be used to inform the estimation of health utility. Health-related quality of life will be directly measured using the Chinese version of the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level(EQ-5D-5L) instrument at baseline and at predefined follow-up intervals. The resulting health states will be converted into utility scores using the Chinese value set. QALYs accrued over the trial period will be calculated for each patient using the area-under-the-curve method, with linear interpolation assumed between measurement points.

The primary outcome for the cost-effectiveness analysis is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which will be calculated as the difference in total costs between the experimental and control groups divided by the difference in total QALYs gained: ICER = (Cost Experimental − Cost Control)/(QALY Experimental – QALY Control). This ICER will indicate the additional cost required to gain one additional QALY under the new intervention protocol.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis will be used to assess the impact of key parameter variations on ICER. This will be done through one-way sensitivity analysis, multiway sensitivity analysis and probabilistic sensitivity analysis to test the impact of changes in key cost and benefit variables on the stability of the results, and to determine the robustness of the research conclusions.

Data collection and management

Data collection will be carried out by research personnel who have undergone uniform training to ensure the standardisation, consistency and reliability of the assessment process. The evaluation data for all participants will be recorded on standardised case report forms (CRFs). The CRF must be completed accurately and thoroughly, with no unauthorised alterations. If changes are made, they must be signed and dated. During data entry, a double-entry method will be employed, and after the data is entered, consistency checks will be performed by a third party. Data entry and verification will use a specially designed electronic database. All raw data and electronic records will be securely stored and regularly backed up to prevent data loss or damage. Data access will be strictly controlled, with only designated members of the research team allowed access, ensuring the confidentiality of the data. Quality control of the data will be continuously carried out during the research process, with random checks of case records conducted monthly to assess the accuracy and completeness of the data and to promptly correct any issues discovered.

Data monitoring

To ensure participant safety and the integrity of the research, an independent data monitoring committee (DMC) will be established, composed of clinical medical, statistical and ethical experts who are not involved in the trial. The DMC will be responsible for regularly reviewing trial data (every 3 months), including participant safety data, adverse event reports, trial protocol adherence and data quality. In the event of any serious adverse events or unexpected situations, researchers must report them to the DMC within 24 hours. The DMC has the authority to recommend modifying the trial protocol, suspending or terminating the trial to protect participants’ safety. Researchers must ensure the complete preservation of all relevant documents (including ethical approval documents, informed consent forms, data collection forms, original records, monitoring reports and audit documents) for auditing and inspection by regulatory authorities and ethical review committees.

Safety and adverse event monitoring

To ensure participant safety and systematically assess the risks of the intervention, a structured adverse event monitoring and reporting mechanism will be established in this study. Adverse events are defined as any unfavourable medical signs (including unexpected symptoms, signs or diseases) occurring during the trial, regardless of whether they are related to the intervention. Serious adverse events are those that result in death, are life-threatening, require hospitalisation or prolongation of hospital stay, lead to persistent or significant disability or involve other medical events that could potentially harm the participant’s health. All events will be assessed for causality by the principal investigator and an independent clinician. The monitoring period will cover the entire process, from signing the informed consent form to the final postoperative follow-up. Researchers will not only actively inquire during visits but will also require participants to immediately report any discomfort or unusual situations to the research team at any time during the study. All event details, including name, timing, severity, management measures and outcomes, will be recorded. In the event of a serious adverse event, the research team will submit a written report to the ethics committee within 24 hours, suspend the intervention for the affected participant and provide necessary medical support. If an adverse event (such as a mild muscle strain) is assessed as not likely to worsen the participant’s condition and does not affect the subsequent intervention or evaluation process after appropriate rest and treatment, the participant will not be required to withdraw from the study.

Ethics and dissemination

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (approval number: 2025012). The research will strictly adhere to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Before enrolment, all participants will be fully informed about the study’s objectives, process, potential risks and benefits, and will sign a written informed consent form. All researchers participating in the trial will undergo training on the study protocol. Any changes to the protocol will be reported to the Ethics Committee and updated promptly on the trial registration website.

Throughout the study, the research team will maintain ongoing attention to ethical issues, ensuring strict protection of participants’ privacy. Any information that may identify participants will be concealed when results are published or disseminated. The study findings will be disseminated in a scientific and ethical manner, including through academic journals, academic conferences and patient education activities, ensuring that both the scientific community and society receive timely access to the research outcomes.
