Body mass index (BMI) associates with frequency of arterial hypertension and type 2 diabetes in obese patients candidates for bariatric surgery
Authors: Lucia La Sala, Luca Busetto, Valentina Carlini, Carmela Asteria, Andrea Peri, Alessandro Giovanelli, Silvia Magnani, Andrea Trabucchi, Michela Ravelli, Antonio E. Pontiroli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArterial Hypertension (AH) and Type 2 Diabetes (T2DM) are common in obesity. However, there is no data on the influence of different degrees of obesity on the frequency of the two diseases.Patients and methodsIn this cross-sectional study 3,486 patients (867 men and 2,619 women, aged 42.6 ± 11.48 years, minimum BMI 30.0 kg/m2, mean BMI 41.9 ± 6.62 kg/m2), were evaluated at presentation for bariatric surgery (BS). BMI was expressed as a continuous variable, as BMI quartiles (BMIQ), as full obesity class (I-III), as reduced obesity class (II-III), Frequency of AH and T2DM, and of a comorbidity score (CS: AH + T2DM) was recorded, and univariable and multivariable stepwise regression analysis was employed to evaluate the association of BMI indexes with AH, T2DM, and CS.ResultsAH, T2DM and CS showed increasing frequency with increasing BMI quartiles (p < 0.001), and with age (p < 0.001). AH and T2DM were frequently associated. At multivariable analysis, age and BMIQ were associated with AH (p < 0.001), T2DM (p < 0.001), and with CS (p < 0.001), more than BMI (NS for CS) full obesity class and reduced obesity class and BMI (NS for T2DM and for CS). The same results were observed at receiver operating curves (ROC), with greater area (AUC) for BMIQ than for BMI and obesity classes.ConclusionThe frequency of AH and T2DM increases with increasing BMI levels. BMIQ outperforms BMI and obesity classes in modelling. This finding supports the use of fine stratification of BMI in clinical risk assessment. More specific studies are required to fully understand the impact of different BMI thresholds in determining the health status of obese patients, and to reduce the risks of BS in patients with extreme obesity.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02989-x.

Introduction

Obesity is frequently accompanied by diabetes (T2DM) and arterial hypertension (AH) [1, 2]. The association between obesity and AH was shown prospectively in the Framingham Heart Study in the 1960s, when peripheral insulin resistance was seen in obese hypertensive patients compared with normotensive obese patients [3]. Many factors are implicated in the link of central obesity with pathogenesis of AH and diabetes (T2DM) [4–6], and these co-morbidities are probably inter-related, due to hyperleptinemia and leptin resistance, oxidative stress, sub-clinical inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, sympathetic activation, insulin resistance (IR), overactivation of the renin angiotensin-aldosterone system [RAAS, 7], and the interplay between glucose tolerance and RAAS in obesity [8–11]. The prevalence of obesity and of severe obesity is changing, as severe obesity is going to be the prevalent form of obesity in the next decades [12, 13]. Therefore, it would be of great interest to delineate a comprehensive assessment of the risk connected to AH and to T2DM in patients with severe obesity. Trajectory studies (mostly performed in China) have clearly shown that increase of body mass index (BMI) is associated with increased frequency of AH [14–17] and of T2DM [16, 17], but a systematic evaluation of frequency of T2DM and AH in connection with different degrees of obesity has never been performed. Obesity has most been considered as present or absent, without any discrimination as to degrees of obesity [18–22]; in contrast, BMI intervals and obesity classes have been considered in studies relating obesity to mortality, showing a continuum in the increase of mortality due to different degrees of obesity [23–25]. The aim of the present study is to depict frequency of AH and T2DM in relationship with their BMI in patients presenting for bariatric surgery (BS) at various BS centers, and to compare the performance of different BMI stratifications through multivariable regression analysis and through ROC curve analysis.

Patients and methods

Four Centers routinely performing bariatric surgery (BS) participated to this evaluation of body mass index (BMI) and of associated arterial hypertension (AH) and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) at presentation., in the period 1995 to 2023. 3,486 obese patients (867 men and 2,619 women, aged 42.6 ± 11.48 years, baseline BMI 41.9 ± 6.62 kg/m2), candidates for BS, were included in the study. Only patients with a BMI > 30 kg/m2 were considered; all patients gave written informed consent following the common protocols already described [26] approved by the local Ethics Committees and were studied under standardized conditions. All evaluations were performed at 30–45 days before intervention. Height, weight and waist circumference were measured in all patients. Diagnosis of T2DM was done by known presence of T2DM, or by oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) on fasting and/or 2-h plasma glucose [26]. Hypertension was defined by current treatment with anti-hypertensive drugs, or by systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg [27]. Secondary hypertension was excluded based on clinical and instrumental diagnosis; blood pressure was measured with an appropriate cuff three times in resting conditions in supine position, and the average of the second two readings was recorded [11, 26]. In this study, patients undergoing BS and patients not undergoing BS (controls, patients referring to the Centers, usually in the waiting list for BS) were analyzed. The databases of the Centers had to include: body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), date of surgery or of presentation to the Center, age, sex, and presence of AH and of T2DM. From the available data, the term of Comorbidity Score (CS) was also generated, meaning the sum of obesity, AH, and T2DM, when present, i.e. with a value of 1 to 3. Data were anonymized before merging. Patients were then subdivided into BMI quartiles and obesity classes and considered for comparisons.

Ethical approval

The Ethics Committee of IRCCS MultiMedica notified the Authors that because of the nature of the study (non-interventional retrospective analysis of anonymized data), the study could be approved without further analysis by the Ethics Committee.

Statistical analysis

Data were first expressed as mean ± SD for age and BMI, and as frequencies for AH, T2DM and CS in the whole cohort of patient. Data of patients in study were analyzed first as being part of BMI quartiles. One way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess significant differences between data of patients in the different BMI quartiles. Chi-square analysis was employed to assess distribution of patients within each BMI quartile with T2DM and AH. Then, a univariate regression analysis was performed to assess association between each independent variable (variables listed in Table 1) and presence of T2DM and of AH. Sensitivity analyses were also performed to assess the role of age, sex, sample size, and co-morbidities. Finally, a stepwise regression was used to assess the association of T2DM and of AH connected with independent variables statistically significant at univariate analysis. Various models of stepwise regression were used, with prevalent AH and T2DM as the dependent variable (Table 2); age, sex, BMI quartile were introduced as independent variables. p levels < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To make possible comparisons with other data reported in other settings, obesity class were also computed, i.e. obesity class I, II, and III; since patients of class I were underrepresented, patients of class I and class II were pooled, so that only two classes were considered. Finally, the models were compared through stepwise regression and confirmed through logistic analysis, and through ROC curves, using the Stata software that provides a test for the equality of the area under the curves, using an algorithm suggested by DeLong, DeLong, and Clarke-Pearson [28]. Since Centers involved were four, modelling was also performed by excluding one Center at a time. Sensitivity analyses

Sex differences

Despite similar age (p = 0.124), BMI was usually higher in men than in women (p < 0.001); also, frequency of CS (p < 0.001), of T2DM (p < 0.001), and of AH (p = 0.031) was higher in men than in women. The association between BMI quartiles and frequency of AH, T2DM was statistically significant in women (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively), not in men (p = 0.127 and p = 0.759, respectively), possibly reflecting sample size limitations, while the association between BMI quartiles and CS was significant in both men (p = 0.011) and women (p < 0.001). To investigate the possible role of sampling size, a simulation was made in which the number of men was doubled; the association between BMI quartiles and AH (p = 0.009) and CS (p < 0.001) became statistically significant, while the association with T2DM remained not significant (p = 0.495).

Age differences

When only patients aged > 50 years where considered, the presence of AH increased from 26.8% to 41.9% (p < 0.001), the presence of T2DM from 21.0% to 35.1% (p < 0.001), and the presence of CS from 9.6% to 19.0% (p < 0.001). These changes were seen in women (T2DM from 18.9% to 32.7% (p < 0.001), AH from 25.9% to 40.7% (p < 0.001), CS from 8.3% to 16.8%) (p < 0.001), and in men for CS (from 13.4% to 26.6% (p < 0.001), for AH (from 29.1% to 45.9%) (p < 0.001), and for T2DM (from 27.4% to 43.7%) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Frequency of arterial hypertension (AH), of diabets (T2DM), and of Co-morbidity score (CS) in patients of the quartiles of body mass index (BMI quartiles). Co-morbidities

The presence of T2DM was significantly associated with the presence of AH (21.0% vs 35.6%, p < 0.001), both in men (p < 0.001) and in women (p < 0.001).

Additional examinations

All data were also expressed as referred to obesity classes. Differences were less evident (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2); in addition, calculation of patients with age > 50 years did not change in significance. To compare results obtained through the various models, multivariable analysis was performed and showed a better performance of BMI quartiles over obesity classes and BMI for AH (p = 0.001), for T2DM (p = 0.001), and for CS (p = 0.001).

Finally, ROC curve analysis was performed to compare the discriminative ability of the three BMI-based stratification schemes (BMI quartiles, BMI, obesity class I- III, obesity class II-III) together with age. For AH, the AUC 0.5604 using BMI quartiles versus 0.5497 for BMI, and 0.5322 and 0.5296 using obesity class I-III and obesity class II-III, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1A). For T2DM, AUCs were 0.5553, 0.5361, 0.5237 and 0.5230, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1B). For CS, AUCs were 0.5610, 0.5322, 0.5136, and 0,5153, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1C). In all cases, AUC of age was the highest among other items, being 0.661, 0.6834, 0.7065, for AH, T2DM, and CS, respectively. These results confirm the superior performance of BMI quartiles over broader obesity categories (Supplementary Fig. 1A–C). All statistical analyses were performed also after exclusion of one Center at a time, and results were superimposable (not shown).

Discussion

Despite the known association between obesity and AH or T2DM, a systematic evaluation of the association between AH and T2DM and different degrees of obesity has never been performed. In this study, by dividing BMI into quartiles, we found that the frequency of AH and of T2DM increase with BMI quartiles in patients seeking BS with no major differences among men and women, with the only aggravating effect of age > 50 years. These results were not obtained when BMI or obesity classes were employed instead of BMI quartiles, and this speaks in favour of BMI quartiles as opposed to BMI or obesity classes, as more sensible than obesity classes. This is reflected by the analysis performed through stepwise regression analysis showing a better performance of BMI quartiles in comparison with obesity classes, confirmed through logistic regression and through ROC curve analysis.

Several large-scale epidemiological studies have demonstrated the cumulative burden of obesity-related comorbidities with advancing age. For instance, the PURE study highlighted how the prevalence of hypertension increases exponentially in obese individuals aged > 50, irrespective of geography or income level [29]. Similarly, the Global BMI Mortality Collaboration showed that mortality associated with increasing BMI is particularly evident in older adults [24]. In our population, age emerged as a powerful effect modifier: in patients over 50 years of age, the prevalence of AH and T2DM increased by 15–20%, a finding that is clinically meaningful and statistically robust. Aging is associated with increased arterial stiffness, vascular inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance, all of which synergize with obesity to drive cardiometabolic risk. It is important to underline that, although BMI remains a crude marker of adiposity and does not account for fat distribution, our results suggest that even a simple stratification by quartiles may provide clinically relevant information. Waist circumference, body fat percentage, or visceral adiposity indices may offer further insight, but are not always available in large clinical cohorts. Nonetheless, our results mirror previous findings that finer stratification of obesity is more informative than categorical classification. In the NHANES data, stratified BMI percentiles were more predictive of incident metabolic syndrome than broad WHO categories [30].

The discriminative power of BMI quartiles, although modestly greater than obesity classes in absolute terms, confirms the multifactorial nature of cardiometabolic risk. The low-to-moderate absolute values are consistent with other population-based studies that tested anthropometric indices alone to predict metabolic disease [31]. This emphasizes the need to integrate BMI with biochemical, clinical, and possibly genetic or microbiome data to improve predictive models.

Our regression analysis further supports these findings. In univariate models, both BMI quartile and age were significantly associated with the presence of AH and T2DM. When included in multivariable logistic regression, age remained the strongest independent factor associated with both conditions (p < 0.0001), followed by BMI quartile (p < 0.001).

Interestingly, when obesity class was used instead of BMI quartile, the models were less discriminative, with high p-values and lower explanatory power. This reinforces the superiority of a continuous or semi-continuous approach to BMI in stratifying co-morbidities. The effect of age was robust even in models stratified by sex, since it maintained statistical significance. This consistency across subgroups highlights age as a central, non-modifiable risk determinant that should be routinely considered in obesity-related risk stratification.

These data are of great interest in view of the possible predictive role of health conditions in light of prognosis and of possible treatments of obesity; small studies have indicated that heavier obese patients may have greater 30-day mortality than lighter obese patients [32, 33], but data on the possible role of higher BMI on long term mortality are not yet present in the literature. Another indication of the risk associated with extreme obesity comes from studies on patients undergoing bariatric surgery (BS), showing that heavier patients were at higher risk of short-term complications [34], so that pre-surgery treatment with various drugs, or bridge interventions are recommended in such patients [35].

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, it must be considered a pilot study because of the small sample size and of the few data examined. Second, these data were obtained in a selected group of patients, and with limited ethnic variability, and cannot be generalized to other people, and sex distribution was in line with the known sex distribution of BS patients, as women are usually more represented than men [36], and as such is not representative of the general population, but only of obese patients seeking BS. More, we have no data at follow-up, so that we cannot predict the role of BMI quartiles in the development of complications, especially of the cardiovascular system. However, our data agree with the results of the “all of us” study, performed in 2025 in a multi-cultural and multi-ethnic study on the general population in the US [36]. One should also wander why obesity classes are less discriminative than BMI quartiles. The first answer might be that obesity classes as they stand now are highly heterogeneous; for instance, obesity classes I and II are restricted in their BMI limits (from 30 to 34.9 kg/m2 and from 35 to 40 kg/m2), class III spans from 40 to over 80 kg/m2). In the next future, other obesity classes might be introduced (i.e. IV, V, and so on) to better discriminate BMI of patients and their metabolic risk.

In conclusion, more specific studies are required to fully understand the impact of different BMI thresholds in determining the health status of obese patients, and in reducing the risks of BS in patients with extreme obesity.
