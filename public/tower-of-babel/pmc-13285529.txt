Developmental trajectories of frailty among Chinese elderly COPD patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Qian Xu, Xiaoli Guo, Lijun Chen, Chong Chen, Yajing Liang, Yan Shi, Chunbo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe trajectory of frailty in patients with COPD and its predictors have yet to be determined. Therefore, this study aims to explore the development trajectory and predictors of frailty in patients with COPD.MethodsThis study is a prospective cohort study, ultimately including 378 participants. Among the 378 enrolled participants, 307 (81.2%) were male, with a mean age of 73 ± 8 years. Based on GOLD Classification, patients were distributed as follows: Group A (n = 104), Group B (n = 155), and Groups C/D (n = 119) .The Frailty Phenotypic Assessment Scale was employed to measure the frailty status of patients at admission, as well as at 1,6 and 12 months post-admission. Group-based trajectory modeling was utilized to analyze the frailty trajectories of COPD patients, and further analysis was conducted to identify the factors influencing these frailty trajectories.ResultsThe study identified distinct trends in the frailty of elderly patients with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). The group trajectory model revealed three classifications: Group 1, Pre-frail Rapidly Developing Group” (28.87%); Group 2, Pre-frail Sustainable Group” (41.23%); and Group 3, High Frailty Persistent Group” (29.90%). The results of the disordered multi-categorical logistic analysis indicated that, when comparing the Group1 and Group 2, a higher number of hospitalizations for acute exacerbations (≥ 2) was associated with older age, elevated IL-6 levels which increased the likelihood of a greater trajectory in the Pre-frail Sustainable Group (P < 0.05). In both the Group2 and Group3, a higher number of hospitalizations for acute exacerbations of COPD (≥ 2) correlated with lower frequency of social activities, older age, increased eosinophil percentage (EOS%), a smaller FEV1/FVC ratio, and higher IL-6 levels, all of which contributed to a greater likelihood of a High Frailty Persistent trajectory (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe observed frailty progression underscores the need for early screening and targeted interventions in high-risk COPD patients, particularly those with recurrent exacerbations or systemic inflammation. Future studies should explore whether attenuating frailty progression improves long-term outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03929-x.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), Frailty, Influencing factors, Longitudinal studies

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), also known as chronic obstructive lung disease, is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterized by progressive airflow limitation. Its main symptoms include cough, expectoration, and progressively worsening dyspnea [1]. This disease has high morbidity and mortality rates [2]. According to estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) in 2019 [3], COPD caused 1.04 million deaths in China, ranking third among causes of death. In China, the prevalence of COPD is relatively high among individuals aged 60 and above, with some data indicating that the prevalence rate exceeds 25% in this population [4]. COPD poses a serious threat to human health as a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways, and comorbidities are significant contributors to acute exacerbations of COPD and lead to patient visits, hospitalizations, and deaths [5]. Due to various complications caused by COPD, such as chronic inflammation, inadequate oxygen supply, and muscle degeneration, COPD patients often suffer from frailty, which has become an inevitable and significant comorbidity that COPD patients ultimately face. The identification of frailty status is crucial for monitoring the health condition of COPD patients in clinical nursing.

Frailty is a nonspecific condition characterized by reduced physiological reserve, increased susceptibility, and decreased resistance to stress [6, 7], and is recognized as a clinical syndrome. It serves as an independent risk factor for exacerbations and progression in COPD patients, leading to a series of adverse health outcomes [8, 9]. Although prior studies like Galizia [10] established the mortality risk associated with frailty stages, their reliance on Single-time assessments overlooks the nonlinear progression of frailty in COPD. For instance, the reported 35-fold rehospitalization risk in frail patients [11] may underestimate cases with rapid frailty escalation post-exacerbation. The meta-analysis by [12] pooled global data but lacked stratification by COPD severity—a key modifier of frailty dynamics. Notably, none of these studies examined how trajectory patterns correlate with exacerbation frequency or inflammatory biomarkers, which our study addresses. Frailty is a dynamic process, and current research on frailty measurement is often based on cross-sectional studies, lacking dynamic changes. Moreover, frailty is a condition that can be reversed and intervened upon, but most current studies focus on a single time point, hindering our understanding of the trends in frailty among COPD patients and the ability to quickly and effectively identify high-risk frail patients in clinical decision-making. By early identification and intervention to reduce the occurrence of frailty in COPD, the risk of adverse clinical outcomes for patients can be effectively lowered, and their quality of life improved.

While frailty in COPD patients has been studied [13–15], existing research predominantly relies on cross-sectional assessments during hospitalization. This approach fails to capture the dynamic progression of frailty - a critical gap because: (1) COPD-specific mechanisms (e.g., chronic hypoxia, systemic inflammation) may accelerate frailty progression differently than other chronic conditions; (2) Identifying trajectory patterns (e.g., rapid vs. slow progression) could enable personalized interventions timed to disease stages.

Current frailty assessments fail to capture its temporal dynamics. To address this, our study employs: (1) High-frequency longitudinal design: Measurements at admission, 1/6/12 months to identify non-linear trajectories; (2) Trajectory modeling: Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) to classify subgroups by progression rate. Through longitudinal follow-up research, we aim to observe the trends in frailty among elderly COPD patients. Additionally, we will analyze the impact of demographic factors and disease-related variables on frailty, identifying distinct features among subgroups that influence the heterogeneous progression of frailty, and providing a basis for clinical interventions.

While frailty is a common geriatric syndrome, its trajectory in COPD patients holds unique clinical significance. The persistent systemic inflammation and recurrent exacerbations in COPD accelerate physiological decline, making frailty progression distinct from other chronic conditions. Specifically, the interaction between lung function deterioration (e.g., FEV1 decline) and frailty may form a vicious cycle—frailty limits physical activity, further worsening pulmonary rehabilitation, while hypoxia exacerbates muscle catabolism. Understanding these COPD-specific trajectories is critical to breaking this cycle through timed interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study aimed to describe the trends of frailty in elderly patients with COPD. This study is a longitudinal analysis and has been reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The convenience sampling method was employed to select patients who were hospitalized in the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at a tertiary hospital in Ningbo from September 2021 to February 2022.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) met the diagnostic criteria for COPD established by the Chinese Medical Association [16] (ii) Age ≥ 60 years old (iii)able to communicate without barriers, walk independently (with the use of assistive devices permitted), and cooperate with the study procedures. Additionally, both patients and their families had to agree to voluntarily participate in the study and be capable of completing the evaluation tests. To facilitate follow-up, the majority of study subjects were residents of two communities under the jurisdiction of a hospital in Ningbo. They had lived in the study community for more than one year and had no plans to relocate within the next two years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i)Elderly individuals with cognitive impairments or psychiatric disorders; those with severe heart, liver, kidney, or other physical or mental illnesses, such as advanced heart failure (ii) individuals who have been bedridden for an extended period or whose condition has significantly deteriorated, rendering them unable to complete the assessment. The exit criteria were as follows: During the study period, if a respondent experiences serious complications or a severe deterioration of their condition, they will be unable to continue as a participant. Additionally, if respondents are unwilling to accept or continue with the study due to personal reasons during the survey period, they will also be excluded.

Sample size calculations

In this study, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the factors influencing frailty in patients with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). The statistical requirement stipulates that the sample size should generally be 10 to 15 times the number of covariates. In this analysis, the maximum number of covariates considered is 20. Based on the 15-fold guideline and accounting for a 10% follow-up loss rate, the estimated sample size required is 334 cases. Ultimately, the final sample size included in the frailty trajectory analysis was 348 cases, which satisfies the sample size requirements.

Measurements

Measurement of frailty

Frailty was assessed using the Frailty Phenotypic Assessment Scale [17], which includes five components: (1) Weight Loss: In the past year, a body weight loss of greater than 4.5 kg or more than 5.0% of body weight; (2) Fatigue: Respondents were asked two questions: (a) feel that I need to work hard to do everything, and (b) can’t get up to do things. respondents indicated a frequency of any one of these questions over the past week; (3) Low Grip Strength: Grip strength was measured twice using the dominant hand, and the maximum value was recorded. According to the Asian Sarcopenia Working Group, the recommended muscle strength standards are 25 kg for men and 18 kg for women; (4) Slowing of Gait: The time taken to walk 4.57 m was measured, and slowing of gait was assessed based on the corresponding height and gender; (5) Low Physical Activity: This was evaluated using the International Physical Activity Short Questionnaire, with a physical activity level considered low if it is less than 383 kcal/week for men and 270 kcal/week for women. For each of the five components that are met, one point is scored, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 5 points. A score of 0 indicates no frailty, 1 to 2 points indicates the early stage of frailty, and 3 points or more indicates frailty. In this study, weight loss, fatigue and physical activity were assessed through questionnaires. Grip strength was measured using a hand dynamometer (Xiangshan Hand Dynamometer EH101). The grip spacing was adjusted according to the size of the patient’s palm. The patient stood upright with arms naturally hanging down, gripped the instrument for measurement, and the process was repeated three times, with the maximum value being recorded. In the community service center, a designated area for measuring gait speed is set up, where team members use stopwatches for timing. Firstly, participants are informed about the test procedure. Once they are ready, the time taken by the participants to walk to the finish line is recorded. Based on their height and gender, their gait speed is then assessed to determine if it is slowed.

Measurement of nutritional status

Nutritional status was assessed using the Short-form Mini-Nutritional Assessment (MNA-SF), a six-item scale developed by Rubenstein based on the original MNA scale [18, 19]. This assessment evaluates six aspects: calf circumference or BMI, recent weight changes, acute illness or significant psychological changes, mobility, neuropsychiatric disorders, and food intake. A score of 11 or higher out of 14 indicates normal nutritional status, while a score below 11 suggests malnutrition.

Measurement of sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse in 1989 in the United States and later translated into Chinese by Liu in 1996 [20, 21], was utilized to assess sleep quality. The PSQI consists of 18 items, each scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with a maximum total score of 21 points; higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality, with a cut-off value of 7 points.

Measurement of depression

The Simplified Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), designed by Sheikh [22], was employed to evaluate depression. This 15-item scale requires participants to respond with “yes” or with “yes” assigned 1 point and “no” assigned 0 points. The total score ranges from 0 to 15 points, with scores of 6 or higher indicating the presence of depressive symptoms; higher scores reflect more severe depressive symptoms.

Measurement of COPD assessment test (CAT)

The COPD Assessment Test (CAT), designed by Jones [23], was developed for the assessment of quality of life in patients with COPD. The CAT score ranges from 0 to 40. Numbers 0–5 indicate severity. The interpretation of the scores is as follows: 0–10: Mild impact; 11–20: Moderate impact; 21–30: Severe impact; 31–40: Very severe impact.

Measurement of modified medical research council(mMRC)

The mMRC (modified Medical Research Council) scale is derived from the original dyspnea scale of the Medical Research Council (MRC) in the UK, which has undergone subsequent modifications [24]. It assesses patients based on the degree of dyspnea experienced during daily activities and is divided into five grades (0–4).

Measurement of barthel index (BI)

The Measurement of Barthel Index (BI) is used to assess patients’ ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs). It quantifies their functional status by evaluating their independence in 10 daily activities [25]. Each activity is scored based on the level of assistance the patient requires, with a total score ranging from 0 to 100. A higher score indicates greater independence.

Additionally, the sociodemographic information and health behaviors of the patients were collected through questionnaires (In this study, the section regarding basic information and blood indicators in the questionnaire was designed by us, while other parts, including measurements of frailty, sleep, depression, nutritional status, patients’ self-care ability, quality of life for COPD patients, and assessment of dyspnea, utilize well-established scales. The detail of the questionnaires in Supplementary material II), including gender, age, education level, marital status, residence status, occupation, type of medical insurance, the number of acute exacerbations of COPD in the past year, COPD classification, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, physical exercise, and social activities. Health-related variables encompassed self-reported and anthropometric data, as well as relevant laboratory test metrics, various blood indices including WBC, RBC, LYMPH%, NEUT%, EOS%, Hb, HCT, PLT, PA, IL-6 and CRP were collected during patient admission.

Blood sample collection and processing: Fasting peripheral venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from patients on the second hospital day after an 8-hour overnight fast, using standardized vacuum blood collection tubes (EDTA tubes for complete blood count analysis and serum separator tubes for cytokine testing). All samples were delivered to the hospital clinical laboratory within 2 h of collection. Serum was separated by centrifugation and subsequently analyzed using automated biochemical analyzers.

Statistical analysis

The identification of frailty score trajectories in elderly COPD patients by Group-based trajectory modelling (GBTM), described by Nagin in 1999, is a semi-parametric modelling approach which is used to identify clusters/classes in a population with similar developmental trajectories. The GBTM model cannot determine the ideal grouping of the study population in advance, and the optimal number of trajectory groups and the shape of trajectory group curves need to be determined based on model evaluation criteria and professional interpretability [26].The model evaluation criteria include: (1) using the maximum a posteriori probability assignment rule to calculate the average posterior probability (Avepp) assigned to each group of individuals, the closer to 1 the better, and generally the Avepp of each trajectory group needs to be greater than 0.7. (2) Compare correspondence of posterior probability of group membership (Pj) trajectory distribution proportions and group Probability of group membership (πj) trajectory distribution proportions, the closer the two distribution proportions, the better. (3) Bayesian information criterion (BIC): the closer to 0, the better. (4) △BIC: the difference between the complex model BIC and the empty model BIC, the larger the difference the more complex model is accepted. (5) Odds of Correct Classification (OCC): measures the ratio of the probability of correct classification to each group, the larger the OCC the better. (6) Relative entropy (Relative entropy): a measure of classification accuracy, the closer to 1, the higher the accuracy of classification [27].

In this study, considering the frailty score is quantitative information, the estimation fits the model based on Censored normal distribution, and for patients with less than 3 measurements during the follow-up period are not included in the trajectory modelling. Initially, we conducted linear, quadratic, and cubic fitting on 2, 3, 4, and 5 groups of trajectory models, respectively. We compared the fitting metrics of different models and the interpretability of the trajectory morphology, model selection process was shown in supplementary Table 1. In the end, we chose 3 trajectory models with linear, linear and quadratic trajectory orders, respectively.

Categorical variables are presented as n (%), and intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test. For normally distributed data, the mean and standard deviation (±s) are reported, and the t test was used for comparisons between groups. For the data that were not normally distributed, the median and interquartile interval (M (P25, P75)) are reported, and the rank-sum test was used for comparisons between groups. The process steps of “proc traj” were used for group trajectory model analysis. Multicategorical logistic regression model was used for the analysis of factors influencing the trajectory group. The relationship between trajectory group and readmission was analysed using a binary logistic regression model. Unless otherwise specified, the inspection level α was set to 0.05. The abovementioned data management and statistical analysis procedures were implemented in SAS 9.4 (version 9.4 for Windows, SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NC, USA).

Frailty in elderly patients with COPD

In this study, a total of 378 respondents were enrolled during hospitalization. Fourteen participants were lost to follow-up at the one-month mark, 16 at the six-month mark, and 22 at the twelve-month mark. Since at least two follow-up data points were required for trajectory measurement, a total of 30 individuals were excluded from the analyses at the one- and six-month follow-ups, resulting in 348 respondents included in the frailty trajectory analysis. The frailty scores of elderly patients with COPD were 2.07 ± 1.55, 2.05 ± 1.38, 2.27 ± 1.13, and 2.28 ± 1.07 during admission, 1 month follow-up, 6months follow-up, and 12 months follow-up, respectively. The incidences of frailty during hospitalization (T0), 1 month of follow-up (T1), 6 months of follow-up (T2), and12 months of follow-up (T3) were 49.20%, 44.5%, 44.25%, and 44.78%, respectively.

Taking the frailty scores of elderly COPD patients at 4 different time points as the observation index, considering that at least 3 time points are required for the fitting of the GBTM model, the trajectory modeling analysis part excludes the study subjects (30 cases) with only 2 or fewer time points and includes 348 eligible study subjects. According to GBTM model, we finally selected the group trajectory model with three trajectory groups. There were 97 cases (28.87%) in group 1, 151 cases (41.23%) in group 2, and 100 cases (29.90%) in group 3. Combined with the screening cut-off value of the frailty phenotype score scale, group 1 (blue) named " Pre-frail Rapidly Developing Group”, Group 2 (red) named the " Pre-frail Sustainable Group and Group 3 (green) named the " High Frailty Persistent Group.” See Tables 2 and Figs. 1-2 for details.

Analysis of influencing factors of different trajectories

By disordered multi-categorical logistic analysis model of the trajectory group, suggesting that the number of hospitalizations due to acute exacerbation, age, EOS% and IL-6 were the influencing factors affecting the frailty trajectory in the comparison between the Group1 and the Group2. The probability of continuous development of COPD in patients with ≥ two hospitalizations due to acute exacerbation was 10.942 times higher than that of the rapid development group (95%CI = 2.97 ~ 40.26, P < 0.001), and the higher the age (95%CI = 1.00 ~ 1.256, P = 0.049) and the higher the IL-6 level, the greater the possibility of continuous development trajectory (95%CI = 1.235 ~ 4.252, P = 0.009). The higher the EOS%, the greater the possibility of a frailty trajectory (95%CI = 0.181 ~ 0.586, P < 0.001). In Group1 and Group3, the number of hospitalizations due to acute exacerbations, social activities, age, EOS%, FEV1/FVC, and IL-6 were the influencing factors affecting the frailty trajectory. Patients with COPD who were hospitalized ≥ twice for acute exacerbations were 15.262 times more likely to have a high level of frailty than those in the rapid development group (95%CI = 4.543 ~ 51.232, P < 0.001). The older the age, the greater the likelihood of frailty and high-level development (95%CI = 1.080 ~ 1.376, P = 0.001). The higher the FEV1/FVC ratio, the less likely it was to develop a high level of frailty (95%CI = 0.930 ~ 1.585, P = 0.035). The higher the IL-6, the greater the possibility of a frailty trajectory (95%CI = 2.711 ~ 12.173, P < 0.001) (Table 4). The higher the EOS%, the greater the possibility of a frailty trajectory (95%CI = 0.233 ~ 0.930, P = 0.003) (Table 4 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, three types of frailty trajectories in elderly COPD patients were analyzed using trajectories, indicating that there was population heterogeneity in the frailty changes in this population. Group 1 (Rapidly progressing pre-frailty), although demonstrating the lowest 12-month rehospitalization rate (7.22% vs. 36.73% in Group 3, p < 0.001), multivariable-adjusted analysis (Model 3) revealed a 3.80-fold increased risk of progression to Group 3 (95% CI: 1.35–10.67). These findings suggest that despite favorable short-term outcomes, the rapid frailty progression warrants vigilance for long-term adverse consequences. We recommend quarterly frailty screening and propose that initiating preventive pulmonary rehabilitation for patients with FEV1/FVC < 0.6 may be clinically beneficial. Group 2 (Persistently progressing frailty), this group showed higher mortality than Group 1, though multivariable analysis did not demonstrate independent risk significance. This underscores the critical importance of comorbidity management-targeted interventions such as calcium/vitamin D supplementation for osteoporosis may prove more effective in reducing rehospitalizations than respiratory-focused therapies alone. Group 3 (Persistent high-level frailty), even after adjusting for confounders, this group maintained a 3.80-fold higher rehospitalization risk versus Group 1 (95% CI: 1.35–10.67, p = 0.011) and accounted for 81.25% of cohort mortality (13.13%). These results strongly suggest that these patients should be prioritized for palliative care, with therapeutic focus shifted to symptom control (e.g., opioid therapy for dyspnea relief) rather than frailty reversal.

In this study, the incidence of frailty was 49.2% and that Tian Gang [28] also used the FP frailty phenotype scale to evaluate COPD, and the incidence of frailty was 44.7%, respectively. Wang [29] conducted a systematic review of 20 studies and found that the prevalence of COPD patients was 32.07%, and the results of each survey were different. This variation may stem from assessment heterogeneity: The differential prevalence of frailty based on the frailty tool used. Our study used longitudinal FP assessments (capturing dynamic changes), whereas 50% studies in Wang’s review relied on Single-timepoint tools like FRAIL scales and other scales. Furthermore, heterogeneity in disease severity across populations represents an important determinant of the observed disparities in incidence rates. Our cohort had higher GOLD 3–4 patients, consistent with known frailty-COPD severity gradients [30]. Moreover, regional variations significantly contribute to these disparities: Asian COPD patients show higher sarcopenia prevalence [31].

FEV1/FVC is an important factor influencing the progression trajectory of frailty in COPD patients, likely through its association with disease severity, systemic inflammation, and respiratory limitations. A lower FEV1/FVC ratio reflects greater airflow obstruction, which is characteristic of advanced COPD. This obstruction leads to reduced gas exchange, increased work of breathing, and chronic hypoxemia, all of which may contribute to muscle wasting, metabolic dysfunction, and systemic inflammation—key pathways in the development of frailty [32, 33]. Additionally, severe airflow limitation (as indicated by low FEV1/FVC) often results in physical inactivity due to dyspnea, further accelerating sarcopenia and functional decline [34].

The risk of frailty was 4-fold higher in patients with severe or higher COPD (GOLD grade 3–4) compared to those with mild or moderate COPD (GOLD grade 1–2) [35]. Qian et al. [36] observed that frailty was less prevalent in mild COPD (GOLD grade 1–2), whereas its incidence escalated to 30.0% (15/50) in GOLD grade 3 and 52.9% (18/34) in GOLD grade 4, underscoring a dose-response relationship between COPD severity and frailty risk. The findings of this study align with these reports, demonstrating that a higher FEV1/FVC ratio—indicating better-preserved lung function—is associated with a lower likelihood of developing high-level frailty.

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is a key intervention to reverse frailty syndrome in COPD patients. Many frailty-related factors—such as physical deconditioning, malnutrition, and systemic inflammation—can be addressed through PR, including tailored exercise programs and nutritional support. Therefore, early frailty screening and timely interventions are crucial to prevent or delay functional decline in COPD patients.

Age significantly influences frailty progression in COPD. Consistent with prior studies [36, 37], our findings confirm that older age predicts higher frailty risk, with elderly patients more likely to belong to the persistent frailty or high-level frailty groups compared to the rapid pre-frailty group.

Biomarkers such as eosinophil percentage (EOS%) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) may help predict frailty trends. Elevated blood EOS% is linked to airway eosinophilic inflammation in COPD [38] and a higher risk of acute exacerbations [39]. Similarly, IL-6, a known frailty-associated cytokine [40], worsens airway obstruction by impairing bronchial smooth muscle function [10]. In contrast, CRP, BMI, PLT, and Hb did not show significant predictive value in our study, possibly due to limited sample size.

Hospitalization history further stratifies frailty risk. Frail COPD patients face a 1.6-fold higher hospitalization risk than non-frail patients [6], partly due to accelerated lung function decline and reduced resilience to exacerbations. Multimorbidity (56.31% of our cohort had ≥ 3 chronic conditions) and aging synergistically increase readmission rates [41], likely via amplified inflammatory cascades and acute health events.

Limitations

First, due to the limitations of research funding, research environment, and researchers, patients with COPD and frailty were followed up for 12 months, and the trend of frailty in patients over a longer period of time was not investigated. In the future, long-term frailty follow-up studies can be carried out on patients with COPD to explore the changes and influencing factors of long-term frailty in patients with COPD, so as to provide reference for patient frailty intervention. Second, the sample size was only selected from a tertiary hospital in Ningbo for longitudinal investigation, so the sample size and scope of the study were limited. In the later stage, the sample size can be expanded to multiple hospitals, communities or elderly care institutions, and the longitudinal survey of long-term random sampling can further verify the results. Third, In the analysis of the impact of different trajectories on fatal outcomes, due to the excessively low number of deceased samples and the presence of a zero in one of the crossover cells, it is not suitable to conduct Logistic regression analysis. Further research is awaited with long-term follow-up in the future.

Conclusions

Frailty in elderly COPD patients showed an upward trend over time, and there were three different trajectories. Different trajectory groups have an impact on patients’ rehospitalization and mortality rates. The number of hospitalizations due to acute exacerbations, age, EOS%, IL-6 and FEV1/FVC are important characteristic factors of the frailty trajectory of COPD in the elderly.
