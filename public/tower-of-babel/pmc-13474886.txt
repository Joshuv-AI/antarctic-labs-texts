Incidence and associated factors of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hui-Qin Zhang, La-Mei Liu, Xiao-Xuan Wang, Qi-Han Zhang, Tong-Yao Xu, Yan Zhang, Peng Wang, Qiu-Fang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition is prevalent among older people in nursing homes, and assessing associated factors of malnutrition is critical for successful malnutrition prevention interventions. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the incidence and associated factors of malnutrition in older people living in nursing homes.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, China Biological Medicine (CBM), Wan Fang, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were comprehensively searched for observational studies investigating the incidence and associated factors of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes from inception to September 20, 2024. Two investigators independently selected the literature, evaluated their quality, and extracted relevant data.ResultsA total of 25 studies comprising 51,915 cases were included. The prevalence of malnutrition in nursing homes was 22% (95% CI: 0.19–0.25). Sociodemographic-associated factors of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes included age (OR = 2.39, 95%CI: 1.87–3.07), female (OR = 1.63, 95%CI: 1.34–1.99), marital status (OR = 1.37, 95%CI: 1.11–1.69), high cultural literacy (OR = 0.1, 95%CI: 0.02–0.49), denture (OR = 2.3, 95%CI: 1.17–4.51), and lack of exercise (OR = 1.25, 95%CI: 1.09–1.43). Physical and physiological function factors included care dependence (OR = 2.44, 95%CI: 1.99-3.00), disability for activities of daily living (OR = 3.37, 95%CI: 1.90–5.96), health self-assessment (OR = 2.05, 95%CI: 1.51–2.77), chewing function (OR = 1.77, 95%CI: 1.05–2.99), swallowing function (OR = 3.12, 95%CI: 2.21–4.41), intake (OR = 3.68, 95%CI: 1.72–7.86), appetite (OR = 7.59, 95%CI: 4.19–13.76), high BMI (OR = 0.29, 95%CI: 0.11–0.77), and pressure ulcer (OR = 1.74, 95%CI: 1.52-2.00). Disease-related associated factors included dementia (OR = 1.63, 95%CI: 1.54–1.74), depression (OR = 2.31, 95%CI: 1.24–4.29), frailty (OR = 3.37, 95%CI: 1.22–9.29), 1 < illness < 3 (OR = 3.86, 95%CI: 2.14–6.97), illness ≥ 3 (OR = 5.76, 95%CI: 2.81–11.79), diseases of the digestive system (OR = 1.31, 95%CI: 1.20–1.44), and cancer (OR = 1.26, 95%CI: 1.11–1.41). However, it remained unknown whether the use of medication (OR = 3.14, 95%CI: 0.38–25.77), diabetes (OR = 0.67, 95%CI: 0.49–0.93), and diseases of the respiratory system (OR = 1.55, 95%CI: 0.77-3,15) were associated with malnutrition.ConclusionsMalnutrition is a widespread problem among older people residing in nursing homes due to various physiological, psychological, social, and lifestyle factors. Future research should focus on developing risk prediction models based on these influencing factors, creating intervention programs, and conducting large-scale randomized controlled trials across multiple centers.Trial registrationThe study was registered in the International Database of Prospectively Registered Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO): CRD42023477611.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07704-3.Keywords: Nursing homes, Older people, Malnutrition, Incidence, Risk factors, Meta-analysis

Background

The worldwide population is aging rapidly due to declining fertility rates and increasing life expectancy. According to the United Nations’ World Population Prospects 2019 report, the projected global population is anticipated to reach 9.8 billion by the year 2050, with over 1.5 billion individuals aged 65 or older making up 16% of the total population. Consequently, the health of senior citizens is becoming increasingly vital for aging societies globally [1]. With the global aging trend, a growing demand for long-term care for older people is growing. The reliance on nursing care has surged, especially as traditional “421” family structures have diminished. This transition has reduced families’ role in providing care for older people. Consequently, older people’s nursing homes have become the preferred choice for both seniors and their families [2, 3].

Malnutrition is a common geriatric syndrome in clinical practice, referring to insufficient, excessive, or imbalanced energy and nutrient intake. This includes undernourishment (wasting, stunting, and being underweight), malnutrition associated with micronutrient deficiencies or excesses, as well as issues like overweight, obesity, and non-communicable diseases related to diet [4]. Malnutrition is a widespread problem among seniors in nursing homes [5]. The prevalence of malnutrition in this demographic can significantly differ, ranging from 1.5% to 66.5%, depending on the diagnostic and assessment criteria employed [6, 7]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that malnutrition leads to physical impairment, prolonged treatment durations, elevated treatment costs, an increased risk of depression, and higher mortality rates among older adults living in nursing homes [8–11]. Moreover, debility and malnutrition are robust predictors of mortality among older adults in nursing homes [12].

Hence, it is crucial to ascertain the prevalence and potential factors contributing to malnutrition in older adults at nursing homes to enable the early implementation of effective interventions for reducing malnutrition and its associated adverse outcomes. However, existing studies show considerable variations in malnutrition and its influencing factors among older people in nursing homes. For instance, Cai [13] found that 20.7% of older people were malnourished. In contrast, Zhu [14] discovered that only 6.33% suffered from malnutrition, indicating a considerable discrepancy. Castaldo [15] identified women as a factor influencing malnutrition among older people in nursing homes, while Yuan [16] found that gender was not a contributing factor. In addition, there is a lack of evidence-based information regarding the current status and influencing factors of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes.

Aims

This study aims to conduct a meta-analysis to determine the incidence and influencing factors of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes. It will provide evidence-based insights for early identification and prevention of malnutrition and the development of targeted interventions.

Methods

Our analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement guidelines. The detailed study protocol is available on the PROSPERO website under the registration number CRD42023477611.

Search strategy

Two researchers autonomously conducted searches on the following English and Chinese electronic databases from their inception until September 20, 2024: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, China Biological Medicine (CBM) databases, Wan Fang, VIP databases, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). The search involved combining search terms with subject words and free words. The search terms were: nursing homes, old-age care institution*, homes for the aged, old folk’s home*, homes for the older people, retirement home*, nursing home resident*, rest home*, institutionalized older people, aged, elder*, geriatric, senior, old*, Frail Elder*, old adult*, aging, Senescence, Frail Older Adult*, Functionally-Impaired Older people, malnutrition, Nutritional Deficienc*, Malnourishment*, Undernutrition*, undernutrition*, nutrition disorder*, Undernourish*, risk factor*, relevant factor*, predictor*, associate factor*. Additionally, we explored grey literature and conducted a hand search of references to full-text articles. The search strategies for each retrieval database are detailed in Supplementary Material 1.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for this review were as follows: (1) participants: Older people aged 60 years and above in nursing homes, with no restrictions on gender, race, or nationality; (2) exposure: the presence of at least one sociodemographic, behavioral, or disease-related factor associated with malnutrition reported; (3) outcomes: reporting the prevalence and associated factors of malnutrition and providing either the relative risk (RR) or standardized mean difference (SMD) along with the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the related factors, or the ability to obtain RR/SMD values and 95% CI through transformation; (4) study design: cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies; (5) articles in both Chinese and English.

The exclusion criteria for this review were as follows: (1) inability to access the original text, duplicate publications, or incomplete data from literature; (2) studies assessed as having low methodological quality; (3) Review; (4) Conference paper.

Study selection

Two researchers carried out literature screening and data extraction, with any disagreements resolved through mutual discussion or consultation with a third researcher, as per the inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently used Microsoft Excel 2019 to extract data from the included studies. The process involved collecting the first author’s name, publication year, country, sample size, malnutrition risk, number of malnourished individuals, study type, malnutrition assessment tools, diagnostic methods, and influencing factors.

Quality appraisal

Two independent researchers assessed the quality of the literature using risk-of-bias assessment tools. Cross-sectional studies underwent evaluation based on the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [17] criteria, featuring 11 entries with responses of “yes,” “no,” or “unclear.” A perfect score of 11 out of 11 indicated high-quality literature; the scores ranging from 0 to 3, 4–7, and 8 were categorized as low, medium, and high quality, respectively. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [18] was employed for cohort studies, encompassing three components: selection of study subjects, comparability between groups, and assessment of exposure factors. High-quality literature was defined as scoring 9 out of 9 points and a total score of ≥ 6.

Data synthesis

We summarized the characteristics of the included studies in tables. We used Stata14 software for Meta-analysis of the extracted data and heterogeneity testing on the literature meeting inclusion criteria. If P ≥ 0.1 and I2 ≤ 50%, indicating homogeneity among study groups, a fixed-effects model was employed for Meta-analysis. Conversely, if P<0.1 and I2 > 50%, signifying heterogeneity, Q-tests, I2-tests, or sub-group analyses were utilized to explore its source. If heterogeneity persisted, a random-effects model was applied. The study assessed the prevalence of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes and the impact of various factors on this population. Dichotomous variables were represented as combined effect sizes in odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). We compared continuous variables as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% CI, using funnel plots, Egger’s tests, and Begg’s tests to assess publication bias. We descriptively evaluated studies with insufficient impact factor data and set statistical significance at P < 0.05.

Results

Search results

We performed an initial literature search and identified 1406 documents. Of these, 194 were from Chinese databases, and 1212 were from English databases: (1) 280 articles were excluded due to duplication. (2) After reading the titles and abstracts, 1051 articles unrelated to the research content were excluded. (3) After reading the complete text, 50 articles that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded, with detailed reasons for exclusion provided in Supplementary Material 2. After the selection process, 25 studies were ultimately included, including 11 in Chinese and 14 in English. The detailed selection process is presented in Fig. 1.

Meta-analysis of the prevalence of malnutrition in nursing homes

A total of 25 studies reported the prevalence of malnutrition; however, as one study utilized two assessment methods, we included a total of 26 datasets for the heterogeneity test and subgroup analysis. And heterogeneity analysis indicated significant variation among the studies (I2 = 98.8%, P < 0.001), so the random-effects model was applied to consolidate the effect values, revealing a malnutrition prevalence of 22% [95% CI (0.19–0.25), P < 0.001]. Forest plots regarding the prevalence of malnutrition in nursing homes are detailed in Supplementary Material 3.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression

We performed subgroup analyses on malnutrition measurement instruments, regions, developed or developing countries, and the year of the study to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. The details are presented in Table 2. We performed a meta-regression analysis on the prevalence rates from outcomes with ten or more included studies. In this analysis, the variables ‘Instruments’, ‘Regions’, ‘Developed or developing countries’, and ‘The year of the study’ were dummy-coded and used as covariates. The meta-regression results showed no statistical significance (P > 0.05), and no sources of heterogeneity were identified. The results of the meta-regression analysis are presented in Supplementary Material 3.

Meta-analysis of the associated factors of malnutrition in nursing homes

Our meta-analysis of 25 influencing factors revealed that various elements, including age, gender, marital status, cultural literacy, prosthetics, physical activity, care dependency, health self-assessment, ability to perform daily activities, chewing function, swallowing function, appetite, intake, BMI, pressure ulcers, dementia, depression, debility, number of illnesses, digestive disorders, diabetes mellitus, and cancer were significantly associated with malnutrition in older adults residing in nursing homes(P < 0.5). Additional research is warranted to explore the relationship between medication use, respiratory diseases, and malnutrition in older people in nursing homes (P > 0.5). Thirteen factors exhibited homogeneity across studies and were subjected to analysis using a fixed-effects model. Twelve factors displayed heterogeneity, prompting additional sensitivity analyses to exclude literature with pronounced heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis revealed that only the heterogeneity of the age factor showed a significant change. After removing two studies causing heterogeneity, the remaining six showed no heterogeneity (P = 0.633 > 0.1, I2 = 0%), and a fixed-effects model was used for analysis. Additionally, we found that the assessment tools for disability for activities of daily living and depression factors varied, so we reclassified them and conducted a Meta-analysis. Variability in assessment tool utilization can introduce heterogeneity. Consequently, daily living abilities and depression were re-categorized and subjected to a meta-analysis. Among the 10 studies examining the impact of disability on activities of daily living and malnutrition in older adults in nursing homes, there were differences in the tools employed to measure these abilities. For instance, while Wang [24] did not specify the tool used, Madeira [30] utilized the Instrumental Daily Living Activities (IADL) scale, and the remaining 8 studies employed the Barthel Index (BI) scale. A meta-analysis was conducted on these 8 studies, revealing significant heterogeneity (P < 0, I2 = 94.3%). However, sensitivity analysis did not identify significant heterogeneity, leading to choosing a random-effects model for the meta-analysis. Five studies have investigated the relationship between depression and malnutrition in older adults residing in nursing homes. However, different depression measurement tools were employed in the studies. For instance, Zhao [20] utilized the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4) for screening, while Yuan [16], Wang [24], Cankurtaran [27], and Madeira [30] used the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). Consequently, a meta-analysis was performed on the four studies mentioned above, revealing a substantial degree of heterogeneity between them (P < 0.1, I2 = 84.8%), and a random-effects model was chosen for the Meta-Analysis. Descriptive analysis

We identified several factors influencing the occurrence of malnutrition among older people individuals in nursing homes, including the number of children [19], place of residence [13], sleep quality [24], health insurance [25], type of institution [26], recent admission [36], receipt of cafeteria-supplemented meals [14], percentage of weight change within three months [38], and fall [39]. Nevertheless, more data is needed to quantify these factors’ effect sizes. Therefore, additional research is imperative to evaluate the extent to which these factors contribute to malnutrition in older people residing in nursing homes.

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis results showed that with the exclusion of any one literature, the combined Incidence did not change significantly, thus indicating good stability of the meta-analysis results. By conducting a publication bias test on the included malnutrition Incidence rates, the results showed that the scatter in the funnel plot was not uniform and symmetrical, so the publication bias was further tested using Egger’s test and Begg’s test (t = 0.98, P = 0.335; Z = 2.34, P = 0.019). It indicated a possible latent publication bias. For detailed results of publication bias and sensitivity analyses, please refer to Supplementary Material 5.

Discussion

The current state of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes

Malnutrition was a prevalent issue among the older people in nursing homes. The results of this study indicated that the incidence of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes was 20%, which was relatively high and similar to the findings of Duan Chuancai [23]. Subgroup analysis in this study revealed differences in the incidence of malnutrition as measured by different assessment tools, with the ESPEN criteria showing a higher Incidence, which may have reflected the superiority of the ESPEN criteria in capturing the state of malnutrition. However, there currently needs to be more comparison of the sensitivity of different assessment tools for malnutrition in the same group of older people in nursing homes. Therefore, we do not consider them entirely comparable [40]. The choice of assessment tools significantly impacted the epidemiological study and clinical diagnosis of malnutrition, and further exploration was needed on the differences in measuring malnutrition among older people in nursing homes using various assessment tools. Subgroup analysis by region showed that the incidence of malnutrition was higher in the Americas, followed by Asia and Europe. However, they were only partially comparable because only two studies from the Americas were included, one from North America and one from South America. Our subgroup analysis by national development status showed that malnutrition in older people in nursing homes in developing countries was slightly higher than in developed countries. The reason may have been that developed countries entered the aging society earlier and had accumulated more experience and resources in the long-term development of nursing homes, possibly leading to more mature nutrition support, health management, and service quality. In contrast, developing countries may still be in the early stages of dealing with aging issues, and nursing homes’ resource allocation and service levels need to be improved [41]. Additionally, our subgroup analysis based on the publication year revealed that malnutrition among older people in nursing homes was higher from 2000 to 2009, followed by the period from 2020 onwards, and was the lowest from 2010 to 2019. The possible reasons for this trend could be that, compared to the period from 2000 to 2009, there was a significant improvement in the nutritional management and service quality of nursing homes from 2010 to 2019, along with an increasing societal focus and investment in the nutritional health of older people. From 2020 onwards, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic led to acute inflammatory responses in older people infected with the novel coronavirus, resulting in increased levels of specific inflammatory markers such as CRP, ferritin, TNF-α, and IL-6. This inflammatory response consumes the body’s albumin and muscle protein, leading to weight loss and poor nutritional status in older patients with COVID-19 [42]. In summary, the incidence of malnutrition in older people in nursing homes was high, and nursing homes should have paid attention to the nutritional management of the older people, early identified those at high risk of malnutrition, and actively intervened to improve their nutritional status.

Sociodemographic factors contributing to malnutrition among older people in nursing homes

This study showed that aging is closely related to malnutrition, which may be associated with age-related physiological factors such as the decline in olfactory and taste functions, decreased basal metabolism, and reduced daily activity ability [43], leading to malnutrition. The results of this study indicated that the incidence of malnutrition was higher among older women in nursing homes, which may be related to poor appetite, insufficient food intake, and reduced physical activity during menopause, thus increasing the risk of malnutrition [44]. A meta-analysis [45] on sociodemographic factors and malnutrition in older people showed that older people in nursing homes who were single, widowed, or divorced and had low education levels were prone to malnutrition, consistent with the findings of this study. This may be due to reduced social interaction after widowhood, poor eating habits, less food consumption, and lack of cooking experience [45]. Older people with low education levels may have poor nutritional status due to lower nutritional literacy, limited access to information, and poor compliance with dietary guidance, leading to difficulties in making healthy food choices [46]. Additionally, our study found that older people wearing dentures were more prone to malnutrition, which may be because denture wearers have reduced masticatory and crushing abilities for solid food, making it difficult to eat solid food, potentially leading to changes in food composition, affecting appetite, and resulting in malnutrition [47]. BMI is one of the phenotypic indicators directly measuring the body’s nutritional status; a lower BMI indicates insufficient intake and storage of body fat, leading to nutritional deficiencies and a higher risk of malnutrition [48]. Therefore, nursing homes should strengthen nutritional education for older people, especially those who are older, female, widowed, or divorced, prepare food suitable for denture wearers, reinforce correct nutritional concepts, improve nutritional literacy, guide them to establish active nutritional behaviors, improve the current situation of nutritional management in nursing homes, and encourage them to engage in physical exercise based on adequate nutritional support.

Physical and physiological factors leading to malnutrition in older people in nursing homes

This study found that the lack of physical exercise among older people in nursing homes increases the Incidence of malnutrition. The possible reason is that older people, due to the decline in their physiological functions, have slower basal metabolism and decreased muscle strength, which reduces the frequency of physical exercise, further accelerating the loss of muscle protein and exacerbating malnutrition [49]. Our study found that older people individuals in nursing homes with higher levels of care dependency and poor daily living abilities are more prone to malnutrition. It may be because they often have physical disabilities and long-term bed rest, leading to decreased mobility, reduced gastrointestinal digestion, slowed intestinal peristalsis, and increased nutrient loss [50]. This study also showed that poor self-rated health is one of the factors leading to malnutrition among older people in nursing homes. Studies [51] have shown that older people with good self-rated health are often optimistic, have better appetite and sleep, experience fewer physical discomforts, and are relatively active and proactive in maintaining and promoting their health. They can consciously use effective self-protection methods to feel better about their nutrition and health. This study also showed that impaired chewing and swallowing functions, reduced intake, and low appetite can lead to decreased nutrient intake and reduced bioavailability of nutrients, causing related nutrient deficiencies and increasing the risk of malnutrition [52]. Additionally, this study showed that older people individuals in nursing homes with frailty are more prone to malnutrition. Frailty is a common syndrome in older people. In nursing homes, frail older adults with co-existing cognitive impairment or depressive symptoms face an increased risk of malnutrition, which in turn elevates the likelihood of functional decline and mortality [53]. In conclusion, nursing homes should strengthen the screening and assessment of malnutrition in older people, detect related physical and physiological factors early, pay attention to dietary guidance and intake management, improve the intake and quality of meals for older people in nursing homes, adjust the texture and shape of food according to different physical and physiological states of older people, establish a multidisciplinary nutrition support team, and carry out comprehensive nutrition management and intervention to prevent the risk of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes.

Disease factors contributing to malnutrition in older people in nursing homes

The results of this study showed that older people in nursing homes with pressure ulcers are more prone to malnutrition. The healing of pressure injuries requires energy supply, and proteins are crucial for tissue repair, granulation growth, and infection resistance. Moreover, older people patients with pressure injuries often have underlying diseases, physiological aging, and prolonged negative nitrogen balance, leading to continuous consumption of proteins and the common occurrence of malnutrition [54]. We also found that older people with mental illnesses such as dementia and depression are more likely to suffer from malnutrition. The reason may be that although these diseases do not activate the inflammatory response, they can lead to reduced food intake, difficulties in eating and swallowing, and increased risk of aspiration, causing aspiration pneumonia. This results in decreased body resistance, increased protein consumption, and impaired nutrient absorption, leading to malnutrition [55]. Additionally, this study showed that older people in nursing homes with comorbidities, digestive system diseases, and cancer have a higher incidence of malnutrition. The analysis suggests that digestive system diseases, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer can lead to persistent inflammation in the central nervous system, induce skeletal muscle catabolism, inhibit gastric emptying, and hinder the action of appetite control hormones, thereby increasing the risk of malnutrition [56]. However, this study found that diabetes is a protective factor against malnutrition in older people in nursing homes (OR = 0.67), and respiratory diseases are not associated with malnutrition, which is inconsistent with the findings of multiple studies [57, 58]. This discrepancy may be due to the small number of included studies and differences in sample sizes between groups. Therefore, further research is needed to determine whether diabetes and respiratory diseases affect malnutrition in older people in nursing homes. Nursing homes should comprehensively assess the health conditions of older people, reduce the risk of malnutrition caused by diseases and complications, and select appropriate nutritional preparations, suitable routes of nutritional support, and administration methods to determine individualized nutritional interventions and improve the nutritional status of older people with illnesses in nursing homes.

Strengths and limitations of the review

This study boasts several notable strengths. Firstly, it is the inaugural systematic review and meta-analysis with a specialized focus on the incidence and factors associated with malnutrition among older people in nursing homes. Secondly, a meticulous literature search was executed across eight databases, and a comprehensive review of pertinent literature was conducted to mitigate the risk of overlooking relevant studies. Thirdly, the majority of studies incorporated into the review exhibited high quality, ensuring the generation of reliable results.

However, this study has certain limitations, including disparities in sample sizes, malnutrition assessment methods, and diagnostic techniques employed across different studies, contributing to heterogeneity within the dataset. Moreover, all the included literature was cross-sectional, precluding an analysis of the enduring factors influencing malnutrition among older people in nursing homes. Despite the implementation of subgroup analysis, which did not markedly alleviate the heterogeneity, the small number of patients included underscores the need for additional validation to substantiate the findings.

Implications to clinical practice

Conducting a meta-analysis on the Incidence of malnutrition and its influencing factors among older people in nursing homes holds significant clinical relevance and extensive social impact on clinical practice and health policy formulation. The clinical significance of this study lies in its ability to accurately quantify the Incidence of malnutrition in older people and identify and assess key influencing factors, thereby providing targeted intervention guidance for medical staff and optimizing nutritional support plans for older people. Regarding social impact, the study helps enhance the service quality of nursing homes, promote the health and well-being of older people, and provide data support for government departments in formulating relevant health policies. It promotes the improvement of older people’s nutrition and health management system, ultimately achieving the healthy and sustainable development of an aging society.

Conclusions

This study found that older people in nursing homes have poor nutritional status, and various factors influenced it, including physiological, psychological, social, and lifestyle aspects. Nursing staff can develop targeted nutritional intervention measures based on risk factors to reduce the incidence of malnutrition among older people in nursing homes. Nevertheless, the correlation between medication use, diabetes, respiratory diseases, and malnutrition among older people in nursing homes remains ambiguous. Rigorous multicenter and prospective cohort studies with substantial sample sizes are imperative for further elucidating these associations and comprehending the dynamics of malnutrition. Future research endeavors should consider many variables associated with malnutrition in older adults within nursing homes, facilitating proactive measures for prevention and early intervention to mitigate the risk and incidence of malnutrition.
