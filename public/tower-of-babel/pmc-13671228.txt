Assessing the impact of parental linear gene normalization on the performance of statistical models for circular RNA differential expression analysis
Authors: Erda Qorri, Valentin Varga, Katalin Priskin, Dóra Latinovics, Bertalan Takács, Emese Pekker, Gábor Jaksa, Bernadett Csányi, László Torday, Ali Bassam, Zsuzsanna Kahán, Lajos Pintér, Lajos Haracska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCircular RNAs (circRNAs) have emerged as promising non-invasive cancer biomarkers due to their stability, abundance in body fluids, and regulatory potential. However, circRNA differential expression analysis (DEA) remains challenging, owing to a lack of consensus on important preprocessing strategies such as filtering and normalization. While well-established bulk RNA-sequencing frameworks are commonly applied to circRNA data, newer approaches such as CIRI-DE (part of CIRI3 suite) integrate linear and circular transcript information to improve detection. Despite developments, an assessment of these integrative strategies is lacking, and the critical impact of filtering on DEA model performance has not been comprehensively evaluated.ResultsIn this study, we evaluated the impact of multiple normalization and filtering strategies on circRNA DEA using experimental datasets, including two in-house blood platelet sets and semi-parametric simulated in silico datasets. Our results emphasize the importance of selecting an appropriate filtering threshold, as overly lenient filtering can reduce model performance across datasets. We found edgeR’s filterByExpr() strategy particularly effective in handling zero counts in circRNA data, while also generating the most reliable results across most datasets.Furthermore, by incorporating linear and circular information as described in CIRI-DE, most methods identified a higher number of DE circRNAs compared to circular counts alone. Notably, circRNAs identified by both CIRI-DE and the modified bulk RNA-sequencing pipelines showed substantial overlap.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that automated filtering combined with linear-aware normalization significantly enhances the sensitivity and reproducibility of circRNA DEA, providing a standardized framework for more reliable biomarker discovery in transcriptomic research.Keywords: circRNA, DEA, platelets, biomarkers, benchmarking, liquid biopsy

Background

Next-generation sequencing has become a substantial tool in biomedical research and clinical diagnostics, enabling large-scale analysis of genetic material. Its application at a transcriptomics level has shed new insights into the diversity of RNA molecules with direct clinical utility. Among these, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have emerged as key players in cancer and other diseases [1]. Nevertheless, non-invasive cancer detection remains challenging due to the scarcity of stable biomarkers and the low abundance as well as rapid degradation of tumor-derived nucleic acids in liquid biopsy samples [2, 3]. However, the use of platelets presents a promising approach to overcome these limitations, as they are enriched in linear and circRNAs at much higher rates compared to other cell types [4].

circRNAs are a special class of RNA molecules formed when the linear splicing of pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) is replaced by a back-splicing event, resulting in a covalently closed, continuous loop. This unique topology provides resistance to exonuclease degradation, enabling independent regulation and function within the cell. Their exceptional stability, abundance in body fluids and extracellular vesicles position them as robust biomarkers for cancer diagnostics and monitoring, including early tumorigenic events. Given the subtle expression changes characteristic of early-stage cancer, highly reliable statistical models are essential to distinguish biological signal from noise and optimize diagnostic accuracy.

In RNA-based analyses, accurate detection of transcript abundance changes depends on precise quantification and well-defined definition of parameters throughout the experimental and analytical workflows. Moreover, the reliability of differential expression analysis (DEA) hinges on minimizing technical and biological biases and the careful selection and validation of normalization strategies and reference standards. The emergence of circRNA as a transcriptomic feature has introduced a new layer of bioinformatic difficulty, as standard linear RNA analysis tools are insufficient [5, 6].

Numerous tools are available for detecting circRNAs from total or Ribo-depleted RNA libraries [7–10]. These tools rely primarily on the identification of chimeric reads corresponding to back-splice junctions (BSJs), a hallmark characteristic of circRNAs [11]. However, circRNA data analysis often remains limited by the lack of standardized approaches for their identification, filtering, and quantification, with bulk RNA-sequencing methodologies still widely applied despite the distinct properties of circRNA data. Moreover, deep learning and advanced machine learning algorithms such as CircDC, CirRNAPL, and circDeep, which learn from sequence-derived information, have expanded computational approaches for circRNA classification by enabling more accurate discrimination of circRNAs and providing an additional layer of evidence that can help prioritize predictions and increase confidence in circRNA annotation [12–14].

Another important consideration is the normalization strategy, typically based on BSJ-spanning reads, which defines circRNA expression solely from circular read counts. This overlooks the unique expression dynamics of circRNAs relative to their host genes and evidence that circRNA can be regulated independently of its parental linear RNA [12]. New experimental strategies such as synthetic circRNA spike-ins (SynCRS), which introduces synthetic circRNA spike-ins, and Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI)-guided workflows have begun to address these limitations by improving quantitative calibration and reducing library preparation and amplification-related bias, facilitating comparisons of circRNA abundance across different libraries [13, 14]. In parallel, computational approaches have sought to address these challenges by incorporating linear RNA expression when estimating circRNA abundance [15, 16]. One such approach, implemented in CIRI3, derives normalization factors from linear RNAs to normalize circRNA expression, following the strategy originally described in CIRIquant [10].

However, the effectiveness of these novel normalization methods has not been evaluated in comprehensive benchmarks, representing a key gap in the field.

To address these issues, we benchmarked commonly utilized DEA methods alongside circRNA-tailored approaches such as circMeta [15] and CIRI-differentially expressed (DE), on simulated and experimental data from non-enriched total RNA-sequencing datasets, including two in-house platelet datasets from early breast cancer patients. In addition, we characterized the sparse and zero-inflated nature of circRNA counts and explored the impact of three filtering strategies on the performance of widely utilized DEA methods. Furthermore, we also explored the benefits of incorporating information derived from linear RNAs into circRNA DEA. Collectively, our findings highlight the limitations of treating circRNAs in isolation from their linear counterparts and support the development of circRNA DEA methods tailored to their unique nature.

Results

Characteristics of circRNAs in different non-circRNA-enriched datasets

To conduct this study, we used a combination of publicly available datasets, simulated data, and our own liquid biopsy samples. Public datasets were selected from peer-reviewed studies according to previously described criteria [17] and processed following the workflow shown in Fig. 1. An additional key selection criterion was the inclusion of datasets generated from total RNA libraries.

Workflow of the benchmarking steps.

Emphasis was placed on total RNA libraries, as most recent circRNA quantification studies rely on total RNA isolation rather than circRNA-specific enrichment [18]. Based on the abovementioned criteria, four public datasets comprising 94 samples were selected (Supplementary Table S1 and Supplementary Fig. S1).

Simulated datasets were generated following previously reported strategies described in Buratin et al. [17]. In addition, we analyzed an in-house dataset consisting of blood platelets isolated from healthy individuals and patients with early-stage breast cancer (EBC). Owing to the high circular-to-linear RNA ratio in platelets, this dataset was considered a natural model of circRNA-enriched libraries.

To identify BSJ and forward-spliced junction (FSJ) events across all datasets, we employed three well-established circRNA-detection algorithms: CIRI3 [10], CircExplorer2 (ce2) [9], and circtools2 (CT2) [19] to minimize algorithm-specific biases and increase the reliability of identified circRNAs [20–22].

The number of identified circRNAs varied substantially across datasets (Fig. 2A), which can be attributed to differences in sample size and sequencing depth. ce2 identified more circRNAs than both CIRI3 and CT2, with CT2 consistently reporting the lowest counts across most datasets. The lower number of circRNA detected by CT2 prior to filtering may be due to its additional filtering steps [19]. However, post-filtering, the number of confidently identified circRNAs by all algorithms converged to a comparable range (Fig. 2A). To ensure concordance between the three algorithms, we assessed the overlap of retained circRNAs. This analysis revealed a substantial overlap supported by high Jaccard similarity indices (Fig. 2B).

Comparison of circRNA identification and tool agreement across filtering strategies; (A) total number of circRNAs identified with CIRI3, ce2, and CT2 pre- and post-filtering across all datasets; (B) Jaccard similarity index between CIRI3, ce2, and CT2 across the filtering strategies; BC: Breast cancer; HCC: Hepatocellular carcinoma; PBMC: Peripheral blood mononuclear cell; EBC: Early-stage breast cancer; SCLC: Small cell lung cancer.

Next, we evaluated the percentage of zero counts of the identified circRNAs in each dataset. A high percentage of zero counts was observed across all datasets, independent of the library preparation strategy. Notably, EBC1 showed lower proportions than the other datasets, reaching 63.05% and 67.59% for CIRI3 and ce2, respectively (Supplementary Table S2).

To address the zero-inflated nature of circRNA datasets and avoid biases from arbitrarily defined thresholds in the absence of standardized filtering strategies, we adopted the automated filtering approach implemented in edgeR’s filterByExpr().

As expected, this filtering step substantially reduced the proportion of zero counts across all datasets as reported by CIRI3 (Supplementary Table S2). By contrast, less stringent filtering (Min5 and Min1) retained higher proportions of zero counts and low-abundance circRNAs.

Selecting an appropriate filtering strategy strongly affected Jaccard similarity across datasets (Fig. 2B). Auto-filtering increased similarity between methods, suggesting that removing low-abundance circRNAs reduced stochastic variability and improved concordance. Similar results were obtained with ce2 and CT2 (Fig. 2B).

We next examined the distribution of BSJ-supporting read counts across datasets. Under auto-filtering, the median ranged from 6 to 63 reads per sample across the datasets, compared with 0 in the unfiltered data, but decreased consistently under Min1 and Min5 filtering. These patterns were consistent for all three algorithms across all filtering strategies, highlighting the influence of filtering on BSJ support levels. Additionally, filtering markedly reduced differences among all algorithms (Fig. 3A). Notably, platelet libraries showed higher median BSJ read counts than the other datasets across all filtering strategies (Supplementary Table S2), consistent with the reported enrichment of circRNAs in platelets.

Median BSJ counts across filtering thresholds and DEA method usage in the literature; (A) median read number supporting BSJ events detected by CIRI3, ce2, and CT2; (B) relative usage of DEA tools for circRNA studies in the literature; BC: Breast cancer; HCC: Hepatocellular carcinoma; PBMC: Peripheral blood mononuclear cell; EBC: Early-stage breast cancer; SCLC: Small cell lung cancer.

To confirm that within-condition differences were sufficient for circRNA DEA, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on BSJ and FSJ counts separately and combined. The analyses showed tight within-condition clustering, supported by moderate-to-high inter-sample correlations (Supplementary Fig. S2) and acceptable separation between conditions across all datasets, regardless of read count origin. These results indicate that transcriptional differences were consistently captured by both circular and linear RNA signals, supporting their suitability for DEA. BSJ log2CPM density distributions across normalization approaches are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3.

Differential expression analysis method selection

Prompted by the strong impact of filtering strategies, we evaluated how these thresholds affect the performance of DEA methods in identifying DE circRNAs. DEA methods were selected based on their usage frequency in the literature, as determined by recent citations. We found that while most studies relied on three well-established bulk RNA-sequencing pipelines: DESeq2 [23], edgeR [24], and limma-voom [25] (Fig. 3B), many employed custom DEA strategies or combinations of existing pipelines, highlighting the lack of uniformity in the field. Linear RNA-aware approaches such as CIRI-DE [10] and circRNA-specific algorithms such as circMeta [15] were among the least utilized methods. Furthermore, we assessed the performance of CIRI-DE, circMeta, and RNA-seq DEA methods across different filtering thresholds and parameter settings. As circMeta was specifically developed for circRNA DEA and uses a distinct statistical framework, its inclusion enabled direct comparisons between circRNA-specific and conventional DEA methods with and without linear transcript information. We also assessed if incorporating linear transcript information in traditional bulk RNA-seq methods improves DE circRNA detection.

Type I error control

The ability of DEA methods to control Type I error was evaluated across six simulated datasets without DE circRNAs, where circRNAs incorrectly identified as DE were automatically considered false positives. For each method, the false-positive rate (FPR) was calculated at three significance thresholds (0.01, 0.05, and 0.10) applied to the unadjusted P-values.

Notably, the voomLmFit Trimmed Mean of M-values (TMM) and TMMwsp configurations consistently displayed inflated FPR values across all datasets and filtering thresholds, especially under Min1 filtering (Supplementary Data S1 and S2). The remaining limma-voom implementations showed median FPR values below the nominal level, indicating conservative Type I error control. This trend was more pronounced under the lenient filtering thresholds and was particularly consistent in the platelet datasets.

DESeq2 configurations showed minimal sensitivity to the filtering thresholds, maintaining near-nominal FPR under auto-filtering and Min5, while Min1 filtering resulted in more conservative Type I error control, with FPR values consistently below the nominal 0.05. Similar patterns were observed for the tested edgeR configurations.

Overall, auto-filtering provided the most balanced performance across methods, with FPR values close to the nominal 0.05 and comparatively low inter-dataset variability. Although Min5 filtering produced similar results, it displayed greater dispersion in some datasets (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Performance evaluation on the DE-Signal-10 dataset: true-positive rate, false discovery rate, F1-score, and area under the precision–recall curve

True-positive rate (TPR) varied notably across filtering thresholds for all the evaluated DEA methods. Auto-filtering consistently resulted in the highest sensitivity with median values across all datasets ranging from 0.91 in DESeq2-LRT to a perfect score in LV-Def-TMMwsp, LV-DefRBT-TMMwsp, and LV-voomLmFit-TMMwsp.

By contrast, under Min1 filtering, all methods displayed lower median TPR values and a higher inter-dataset variability. This effect was particularly pronounced for the DESeq2 configurations and edgeR (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, DESeq2 implementations were the most sensitive to the applied filtering strategy, displaying TPR values below <0.5 while exhibiting a more dataset-dependent performance under Min5. Although their performance improved under auto-filtering, inter-dataset variability remained high compared with the other methods.

Performance of DEA method configurations at α = 0.05; performance of different DEA method configurations in the DE-Signal-10 dataset at an α = 0.05; (A) median TPR distribution; (B) FPR; (C) F1-score distribution.

Limma implementations exhibited a stable performance under auto-filtering, characterized by high median sensitivity values. All methods demonstrated reduced sensitivity and increased variability in performance in the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)-peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) dataset and the other two platelet-derived datasets, especially under Min1 and Min5 filtering (Fig. 4A), highlighting the influence of biological input origin on method performance [26]. Overall, reducing filtering stringency increased performance variability for several methods, particularly DESeq2 and edgeR. In contrast, auto-filtering emerged as a plausible strategy that maximizes sensitivity while stabilizing performance.

A reliable DEA method should enable robust identification of truly expressed circRNAs, while limiting false-positive calls. To evaluate false-positive control, we calculated the median false discovery rate (FDR). FDR values varied considerably across datasets, with the impact of the filtering strategy depending on both the method and the dataset (Supplementary Fig. S5). DESeq2 showed the largest increase in FDR under auto-filtering, driven by elevated false-positive calls across all datasets except EBC2. Comparably high FDR values were obtained under Min5, whereas Min1 filtering generally resulted in lower and more stable FDR (Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, edgeR and limma-voom displayed the opposite trend. All limma-voom configurations demonstrated lower FDR than both DESeq2 and edgeR, although their performance was more sensitive to lenient filtering, as reflected by increased FDR under Min1 (Supplementary Data S3 and S4). Although method performance remained largely dataset dependent, auto-filtering generally resulted in lower median FDR and reduced inter-dataset variability compared with Min1 and Min5.

To obtain a more robust evaluation of their performance, we calculated F1-scores across all datasets and filtering thresholds. All methods achieved higher F1-score values under auto-filtering, ranging from ~0.91 for DESeq2 versions to ~0.99 for the different limma-based configurations, indicating consistently strong performance regardless of parameter settings (Fig. 4C). Auto-filtering consistently outperformed Min1 and Min5 across datasets, including HCC-PBMC, where most methods achieved F1-scores >0.80 only under auto-filtering (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Notably, DESeq2 and edgeR were more sensitive to lenient filtering, whereas limma-voom configurations maintained relatively stable F1-score distributions across datasets, even under Min1 and Min5. Overall, these results suggest that limma-voom configurations provide a more balanced and stable trade-off between sensitivity and precision across filtering conditions and heterogeneous circRNA datasets derived from different biological sources, including liquid biopsies and tissues.

Further evaluation using area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), showed the better performance of DEA methods under auto-filtering, with values ranging from 0.89 to 1.0 as opposed to Min5 and Min1 (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Notably, limma-voom pipelines achieved the highest and most stable scores across datasets, especially in the platelet datasets, HCC-Tissue, and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). Overall, these configurations demonstrated consistent performance regardless of the applied filtering threshold.

Similar patterns were observed for the two edgeR pipelines, which displayed modest sensitivity to filtering, with median AUPRC values >0.85. However, their inter-dataset variability increased compared with limma-voom configurations, largely due to reduced performance in the HCC datasets (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Overall, our findings suggest that limma-voom and edgeR more reliably distinguish true from false positives across diverse biological contexts and are less sensitive to the applied filtering strategy.

Reproducibility and tool ranking on the DE-Signal-10 dataset

To determine the consistency of the significantly expressed circRNAs reported at an FDR of 0.05, we calculated the average Jaccard similarity index across all replicates for each dataset, method, and filtering level. Among the evaluated methods, the highest similarity was observed for the limma-voom pipelines with both TMM and TMMwsp normalization achieving Jaccard indices >0.85 under auto-filtering in the SCLC dataset set (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Consistent with our previous results, all methods displayed low similarity in both HCC datasets, particularly under Min1 and Min5. Interestingly, across the BC, HCC-PBMC, HCC-Tissue, and SCLC datasets, the DESeq2 pipelines displayed the highest consistency in the reported circRNA sets.

When evaluating the impact of filtering, all methods showed higher similarity indices under auto-filtering compared to Min1 and Min5 strategies, suggesting that removing low-count circRNAs prior to DEA reduces background noise and leads to more reproducible calls across replicates.

Analyzing the effect of linear normalization on circRNA expression

To evaluate whether incorporating linear RNA information improves the detection of DE circRNAs compared to BSJ-only approaches, we performed a second benchmarking analysis using six experimental datasets (Supplementary Table S1). The selected DEA methods, including CIRI-DE and circMeta, were assessed under three scenarios (see Methods section Statistical analysis).

The performance of DEA methods varied across datasets and significance thresholds. Most methods reported few or no DE circRNAs under the BSJ-only strategy, particularly at α = 0.01, where they identified either none or fewer than 10 DE circRNAs. In contrast, circMeta’s JuncDE-pois.ztest module consistently identified higher numbers of DE circRNAs, up to 18.25-fold more at an FDR of 0.01, across all datasets combined, compared to the JuncDE-edgeR module and other BSJ-only DEA methods. This trend persisted at the less stringent FDR thresholds of 0.05 and 0.10, with the majority of circRNAs originating from the EBC1 and EBC2 datasets (Figs 5A and B).

Comparison of DEA methods for circRNA detection; (A and B) significantly DE circRNAs reported in the platelet datasets; (C) overlapping DE circRNAs across different tool-normalization combinations in the EBC1 dataset.

Next, our results suggest that incorporating FSJ and linear RNA information increases the number of identified DE circRNAs across datasets (Supplementary Figs S9–S13). This effect was particularly evident for CIRI-DE and the limma-voom pipelines, which consistently identified more DE circRNAs, compared to none under the BSJ-only scenario. This trend became more pronounced at less stringent significance thresholds (0.05 and 0.10), with most methods detecting more DE circRNAs after incorporating linear RNA information, particularly in the platelet datasets (Fig. 5A and B).

However, the response to linear-aware normalization differed across methods. edgeR-glmQLFit (TMM and TMMwsp) and DESeq2 (BetaPrior and LRT) displayed more conservative behavior compared with limma-voom pipelines and CIRI-DE, which consistently identified the largest numbers of DE circRNAs across datasets. Notably, when incorporating FSJ-derived information, TMM-normalized CIRI-DE identified approximately twice as many DE circRNAs as edgeR-QLFit-TMM. A similar trend was observed when incorporating total linear RNA information. However, despite the increase in detected DE circRNAs following the incorporation of linear RNA information, the total number remained lower than that identified by JuncDE()-pois-z-test.

Lastly, most methods failed to identify DE circRNAs in the HCC-PBMC dataset under the tested scenarios, suggesting high within-group variability and minimal circRNA differences between conditions. This observation is consistent with the simulation results, where most methods performed poorly on this dataset, with even JuncDE-pois.ztest identifying comparatively few DE circRNAs relative to the other datasets (Supplementary Fig. S11).

Furthermore, we assessed whether incorporating linear information affected the directionality of circRNA expression. circRNAs identified across both scenarios were collected, and the consistency of their log fold changes was evaluated using CIRI-DE’s predictions as a baseline. Although fold-change magnitudes varied across methods, the direction of regulation was largely consistent across all traditional DEA approaches and remained concordant with the defined baseline (Supplementary Figs S14 and S15). Interestingly, this pattern was not observed for the shared DE circRNAs identified by circJuncDE-pois.ztest. Although some overlap was detected in the expression of DE circRNAs identified in the Breast Cancer (BC) and predominantly the SCLC dataset, the direction of expression change was often contradictory (Supplementary Figs S14 and S15). Moreover, in cases of concordance, the magnitude of expression change was generally lower than that reported by the other DEA methods.

Inter-method concordance across differential expression analysis methods

Subsequently, we examined the concordance between the circRNAs identified by CIRI-DE and those detected by the other DEA pipelines when including linear information. circRNAs identified as significantly DE by both FSJ- and BSJ-based versions of each method were collected at three FDR thresholds, forming the set used to assess overlap between methods. Directionality of expression was not considered at this stage. For consistency with our previous analyses, we focused on results obtained at FDR <0.05.

In the BC dataset from a total of 20 reported circRNAs, six were jointly identified by both methods and only three were shared with the limma-voom configurations. Notably, all circRNAs detected by edgeR-quasi-likelihood F-test (edgeR-QLF) were present within the broader set identified by CIRI-DE, which consistently reported more circRNAs than the DEA methods.

Given the relatively low number of significantly DE circRNAs detected in the BC dataset, we assessed whether this trend persisted in higher-signal datasets such as EBC1 and SCLC (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S16). In both datasets, ˃30 circRNAs overlapped between CIRI-DE and edgeR-QLF, as opposed to limma-voom and DESeq2. By contrast, only five circRNAs were jointly identified by DESeq2, edgeR-QLF, and CIRI-DE. Similar concordance patterns were observed at FDR thresholds of 0.01 and 0.10 (Supplementary Figs S16–S20).

We further examined the concordance between DE circRNAs identified by circJuncDE and the other DEA methods. Although the majority of the DE circRNAs were identified exclusively by circMeta, the greatest overlap was consistently observed between CIRI-DE and circMeta, with >50 and >100 shared circRNAs identified in the EBC1 and SCLC datasets, respectively, followed by edgeR- and limma-based approaches.

Discussion

In this two-part benchmarking study, we systematically evaluated the performance of four well-established DEA methods for circRNA analysis. In the first part, we assessed the effect of three pre-filtering strategies across 12 configurations of edgeR, DESeq2, and limma-voom, using semi-parametric simulated datasets. In the second part, we expanded the analysis to include circRNA-specialized DEA methods and investigated the impact of incorporating linear RNA and FSJ information into circRNA DEA, an approach that remains widely unexplored in the field. Benchmarking was performed across six datasets, including two in-house platelet-derived ones. Platelets were selected due to their naturally elevated circRNA content compared to nucleated cells [27]. This characteristic allowed us to evaluate the DEA method performance in a physiologically circRNA-rich system while avoiding biases introduced by exonuclease treatment in the abundance of linear RNA transcripts. Consistent with previous reports, the utilized detection algorithms, especially CIRI3 and ce2, identified large numbers of circRNAs while also reporting high proportions of zero counts across all datasets [17]. Despite the absence of circRNA enrichment, platelet-derived datasets contained the highest number of circRNAs post-filtering, supporting platelets as a naturally enriched source of circRNAs even in early breast cancer, where transcriptomic changes are less pronounced than in late-stage disease [26], highlighting their potential as input material for biomarker discovery.

Moreover, the abundance of zero- and low-count circRNAs reduced inter-tool concordance reflected by low Jaccard similarity indices, which improved only after stringent filtering. These properties of circRNA data reinforce the importance of carefully defining filtering thresholds prior to DEA [28–30]. In zero-inflated circRNA count matrices, filtering is particularly critical, as it directly affects the ability to distinguish biological signal from technical noise, algorithm-specific artifacts, and low-abundance circRNA transcripts.

While the impact of filtering strategies has been investigated in single-cell sequencing data [31, 32], their implications in circRNA data remain largely unknown. Filtering criteria applied to circRNA count matrices vary widely across studies, reflecting a lack of consensus on appropriate thresholds. We encountered similar methodological uncertainty in our research, which motivated us to explore how different filtering strategies affect the performance of well-established DEA methods. We evaluated three filtering strategies: the automated filterByExpr() approach and two lenient thresholds: Min1 and Min5. Although studies on low-count bulk sequencing data suggest that careful model calibration alone may reduce the need for filtering [33], the applicability of these findings to circRNA data remains unclear.

Our results show that auto-filtering effectively reduces zero inflation, particularly in CIRI3 and ce2 results, leading to higher median BSJ counts and improved concordance among circRNAs identified by all three methods. By contrast, CT2 reported a considerably lower number of circRNAs, likely due to its additional filtering steps that remove ribosomal RNA (rRNA) reads and circRNA candidates originating from repetitive regions of the genome. Nevertheless, its results were largely concordant with the other two algorithms as reflected by the high Jaccard similarity index.

Min1 and Min5 filtering retained higher proportions of zero counts, which reduces inter-tool concordance, suggesting that these thresholds are not efficient in removing unreliably detected circRNAs. Similar observations were reported by Digby et al., when testing a commonly used filtering threshold (≥2 BSJ-supporting reads) in circRNA datasets. Together, these findings suggest that auto-filtering provides a robust strategy for increasing confidence in circRNA candidates; however, further evaluation with additional detection tools is required to confirm its robustness.

When evaluating the impact of filtering thresholds on DEA method performance, most methods achieved lower FPR and FDR, alongside high sensitivity and high F1-scores under auto-filtering—a stark contrast to Min1 filtering, under which method performance decreased substantially. This performance decline may be partly explained by the higher sparsity of Min1 count matrices, which contained, on average, 6.1-fold more zero counts than the auto-filtered ones.

Moreover, the applied filtering strategy strongly affected the ability of DEA methods to control Type I error across datasets. This effect was particularly evident in DESeq2 and edgeR, which maintained near-nominal FPR under auto-filtering and Min5 but exhibited conservative behavior with Min1-filtered matrices containing up to 28% zero counts. Consistent with previous RNA-sequencing studies, these findings suggest that retaining weakly supported BSJ counts affects dispersion estimation in negative binomial models, ultimately reducing their power to identify truly DE circRNAs [34]. This observation aligns with prior circRNA and other studies reporting the conservative behavior of DESeq2 in zero-inflated datasets [17, 35–37].

Our findings demonstrate that LV-Def and limma-voom-DefRBT maintain a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and precision across filtering conditions and heterogeneous circRNA datasets, including the challenging HCC-PBMC dataset. This was reflected in their stable performance under auto-filtering, with consistently high F1-scores and AUPRC values. These observations align with prior studies reporting stable FDR control and sensitivity for limma-voom in circRNA and low-count RNA-seq data [17, 37, 38], supporting its reliability for circRNA DEA. Another notable observation was the decline in DEA performance in the HCC-PBMC dataset, especially under lenient filtering thresholds.

The poor performance in this dataset can be attributed to a combination of biological and technical factors. Biologically, PBMCs contain a naturally lower abundance of circRNAs compared to nucleated blood cells and are characterized by greater cellular heterogeneity [38], which can mask condition-specific signals [39]. In addition, non-standardized protocols for sample collection, handling, and storage represent potential sources of technical variation, which are known to influence blood transcriptomes and may further increase inter-sample variability [40–43].

Collectively, these factors can result in a low-signal, high-variance scenario, limiting the ability of DEA methods to distinguish true biological signal from noise. Our findings highlight the challenging nature of liquid biopsy-based biomarker discovery and further emphasize the importance of standardized sample collection and processing protocols to minimize technical variability. Moreover, having shown the impact of these technical factors in blood-derived datasets and the inherently heterogenous nature of these samples, the performance and results of DEA methods on such datasets ought to be interpreted with caution. Given the challenges associated with heterogeneous datasets and complex input materials, methods that account for the unique properties of circRNA are necessary to address these issues.

Several approaches have attempted to incorporate circRNA-specific information into DEA, including the use of linear-to-circular ratios at the BSJ sites such as differentially expressed back-splicing (DEBKS) [44], CircMeta-CLRDE [15], and CIRI-DE [10].

As CIRI-DE is among the more widely adopted approaches in the literature, we provide, to the best of our knowledge, the first independent benchmark evaluating whether incorporating FSJ-derived information improves circRNA detection compared to conventional DEA pipelines that omit such information. In addition, we further compare its performance and that of traditional DEA methods to circMeta’s circDE-JuncDE model, specifically developed to quantify circRNA expression.

Our results showed an increase in detected DE circRNAs when information from corresponding FSJs or linear transcripts was included alongside the BSJ counts, suggesting the presence of shared signal between circular and linear transcripts that may be overlooked when circRNAs are analyzed in isolation.

This behavior is likely driven by how FSJ-derived information is incorporated during the modeling of BSJ counts. Specifically, library sizes and normalization factors used to construct the BSJ count object are derived from the FSJ counts, and the resulting normalized BSJ counts serve as input for dispersion estimation and Generalized Linear Model (GLM) fitting. Considering the sparse nature of circRNA counts relative to linear transcripts, the integration of this additional information likely provides more stable and reliable sample-level scaling prior to dispersion estimation and model fitting, resulting in the identification of more DE circRNAs, compared to approaches that rely solely on BSJ counts.

Notably, the edgeR-glmLRT approach used by CIRI-DE identified more DE circRNAs than the edgeR-glmQLFit pipeline. However, this difference likely reflects the stricter error control of the quasi-likelihood framework rather than a methodological advantage of CIRI-DE [45, 46]. Despite this, the sets of identified circRNAs showed substantial overlap between approaches.

Next, we evaluated the circJuncDE module, a circRNA-specific differential abundance method. circJuncDE reported more DE circRNAs than conventional DEA methods across all datasets and tested scenarios. This performance may be attributed to its distinct statistical framework, which fits a Poisson model to BSJ counts and utilizes Poisson-based z-test statistics, in contrast to the Negative Binomial (NB) models commonly applied in traditional DEA methods [15]. Such an approach may be better suited for the sparse BSJ count distributions, as was the case in our datasets, reaching up to 84% zero counts in HCC-Tissue.

Despite the notable differences in the number of DE circRNAs identified, the overlap observed between circJuncDE and CIRI-DE suggests that both methods capture a common underlying biological signal albeit with differing sensitivity and statistical power. Nevertheless, Poisson-based approaches are not without limitations. A recent benchmarking study reported low F1 scores for circJuncDE, highlighting the potential trade-off between increased sensitivity and elevated false-positive instances [17]. However, additional benchmarking studies with diverse circRNA count distributions and sparsity levels are needed to fully evaluate the generalizability of these approaches.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that incorporating linear transcript information prior to DEA can increase circRNA detection, even when using statistical models developed for bulk RNA-sequencing data. A potential caveat of FSJ-based normalization strategies arises from the dynamic nature of circRNA expression, which can be regulated independently of parental gene expression [12, 47–50]. Consequently, normalization approaches based on linear reads may influence circRNA differential expression estimates, particularly when FSJ information is applied in a per-locus manner. In the CIRI-DE approach, however, linear gene expression levels are used to estimate normalization factors prior to testing circRNA expression changes, rather than computing a per-locus circular-to-linear ratio [16]. Nonetheless, comprehensive research across multiple datasets, disease types, and biological conditions is required to evaluate the impact of these linear-based normalization approaches on circRNA differential expression estimates. Specifically, simulation studies that evaluate different regulatory scenarios: (i) circRNAs and their parental genes change in the same direction; (ii) circRNA expression changes, while parental gene expression shows no significant change; and (iii) circRNAs and parental genes change in opposite directions. Our findings highlight the potential value of incorporating parental linear RNA information into circRNA DEA analysis, while also emphasizing that its suitability may depend on the underlying regulatory context.

From a practical perspective, we recommend assessing the extent of host gene-independent circRNA regulation within a dataset before selecting a normalization strategy for DEA, particularly given that different cancer types exhibit distinct circRNA landscapes [51]. Lastly, although circular and linear RNA transcripts are often subject to independent regulation, their parallel analysis remains important for the robust identification of features that would otherwise remain undetected [12]. We also emphasize the need for further studies, encouraging investigating the relationship between circRNA expression and that of their parental linear genes.

Simultaneously, careful processing, especially the selection of appropriate filtering, is essential for reliable circRNA analysis. While we recommend dataset-driven filtering, automated approaches like filterByExpr() greatly mitigate the impact of zero inflation in circRNA data, providing a robust alternative to arbitrary thresholds. Notably, limma-voom configurations showed the most stable performance across filtering strategies and heterogeneous datasets.

Overall, our results highlight important limitations of applying conventional RNA-sequencing DEA methods to circRNA data and emphasize the need for novel circRNA-centered analytical models that also account for the dynamic and potentially parental-gene-independent regulation of circRNA expression. Such approaches may ultimately improve the reliability and clinical utility of circRNA-based biomarkers in non-invasive cancer diagnostics.

Methods

Differential expression analysis method literature evaluation

The most prevalent DEA methods utilized in circRNA studies were identified via a literature search performed in Google Scholar using a predefined set of criteria (Supplementary Table S3). This resulted in 100 research articles, which were retained for comparative assessment. Combinatory approaches unique to each article were reported under the “Other” category.

Online dataset selection

Six publicly available datasets comprising 94 liquid and tissue biopsy samples from different cancer types were analyzed (Supplementary Table S1).

Dataset selection followed strict criteria: (a) a minimum of four biological replicates per condition; (b) sequencing depth ≥20 million reads per sample; (c) availability of raw sequencing data, and (d) clear separation at circRNA level between conditions. The selected datasets were retrieved and processed as described in Supplementary Material S1. For the two in-house datasets, EBC1 and EBC2, platelet isolation and RNA extraction were performed according to the ThromboSeq protocol described by Best et al., while library preparation and sequencing steps are detailed in Supplementary Material S2. The raw data are available at National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject ID PRJNA1429817.

Statistical analysis

Inter-sample similarity within each condition was assessed by Spearman correlation using the stats R package [52]. Post-filtering concordance between CIRI3, ce2, and CT2-retained circRNAs was quantified using the Jaccard similarity index (Jaccard v0.1.2) [53].

Differences in circRNA expression between conditions were evaluated by PCA under three scenarios: (1) BSJ counts only, (2) FSJ counts only, and (3) a combination of BSJ and FSJ counts (Supplementary Material S1).

Simulation of datasets

Using the selected datasets, we generated two in silico datasets to benchmark DEA tool performance, a “zero set” dataset containing no DE circRNAs to assess Type I error control, and datasets containing 10% DE circRNAs to evaluate their performance using different performance metrics that require the ground truth set.

Simulations were generated using SPsimSeq, a semi-parametric simulation framework that preserves key characteristics of the input data [54]. The original BSJ feature count matrices were filtered as follows: (i) automatic filtering using edgeR’s “filterByExpr()” with default parameters, (ii) min.count set to 1, and (iii) min.count set to 5. Detailed simulation settings and parameter configurations are provided in Supplementary Material S3. Outlier samples within each biological group were identified using the Interquartile Range (IQR) outlier detection method and removed to reduce bias and control intra-condition variability. A summary of the removed samples is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Selection of differential expression analysis methods

To evaluate BSJ-only and combined FSJ–BSJ approaches, we centered our analysis on assessing the performance of four widely utilized DEA methods: edgeR, limma-voom, DESeq2, CIRI-DE, as well as the circRNA-specific DEA algorithm circMeta.

Though most studies surveyed utilized these methods with default parameters, we evaluated their performance at the default as well as with adjusted parameter settings to account for the zero-heavy nature of circRNA data, including two normalization strategies, TMM and TMMwsp (Supplementary Table S5) [55]. This resulted in 12 different method configurations.

Evaluating the impact of linear RNA normalization in circRNA differential expression

To evaluate the effect of employing gene-level normalization from predicted FSJ counts, we included CIRIquant (CIRI-DE), which normalizes circRNA counts using gene-level information derived from linear transcript expression. CircMeta, a circRNA-specific DEA framework, was included via the circJuncDE module for the BSJ-only DEA (Supplementary Material S4).

In addition, we assessed the effect of using the total linear RNA count output from STAR, and evaluated CIRI-DE performance using the original glmFit model as well as the quasi-likelihood F-test [46]. Overall, this step resulted in 12 configurations (Supplementary Table S6). The analysis was performed on six experimental datasets following a standardized preprocessing pipeline, starting with filtering, normalization, and DGEList construction. Tool implementations and parameter descriptions are found in Supplementary Material S4. In the absence of a ground-truth DE set available for the experimental datasets, we focused on comparing the total number of significantly DE circRNAs (FDR <0.05), the overlap between the significantly DE circRNAs across methods, and P-value distributions.

Type I error control

Type I error control was evaluated on the null datasets (ZeroSets), under the hypothesis that any circRNA identified as DE represents a false positive. FPR was defined as the proportion of false positives over the total number of tested circRNAs and was calculated using unadjusted P-values at three nominal significance thresholds: [α ∈ (0.01, 0.05, 0.10)], across three different filtering strategies.

Performance evaluation on the DE-Signal-10 dataset

For each DEA method, we computed TPR, FDR, F1-score, and AUPRC across 660 simulated datasets. These calculations were performed at three significance levels and filtering strategies. Per method, medians of all metrics were calculated and reported across datasets to quantify their central tendency and per-dataset variability. All analyses were performed in R v4.5.0 [52], and the AUPRC analysis was carried out using the PRROC v1.4 package [56].

The consistency and reproducibility of differential expression calls across simulated replicates per dataset were examined by quantifying the overlap of significantly DE circRNAs within each dataset using the Jaccard similarity index [53, 57] calculated as described in Supplementary Material S5.

Run time analysis

The computational efficiency of the tested DEA tools on both dataset types was assessed by measuring the wall-clock time required to complete an end-to-end DEA analysis using the Sys.time() function in R (v4.5.0). Analyses were performed on a dedicated workstation (Supplementary Table S7).
