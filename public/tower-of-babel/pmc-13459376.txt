Plasma metabolomic signatures associated with inhaled corticosteroid requirements in clinically stable asthma: a prospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Hyun Woo Lee, Young Jin Pyung, Sohee Oh, Jung-Kyu Lee, Eun Young Heo, Cheol-Heui Yun, Deog Kyeom Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity, asthma severity is often classified by treatment intensity, which may inadequately reflect underlying pathobiology. We aimed to characterize plasma metabolomic profiles associated with treatment-defined severity among patients with clinically well-controlled asthma receiving guidance-based inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapy.MethodsIn this prospective cross-sectional study, patients with well-controlled asthma, defined by Asthma Control Test scores > 20 and consistent ICS use without recent exacerbations, were enrolled. Plasma samples were analyzed using proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. After asthma severity was defined by ICS dose requirement, metabolomic differences were evaluated between severe and non-severe asthma. The significant metabolites were identified through elastic net–assisted variable selection and Firth’s penalized logistic regression adjusting for clinical covariates. Probabilistic relationships among these metabolites and severity were further explored using Bayesian network modeling.ResultsFifty-five patients were included. Lactate and pyruvate levels were significantly elevated in the severe asthma group. In multivariable analyses adjusting for clinical covariates, pyruvate (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = 10.239 [95% CI = 1.320–19480.301], P-value = 0.025) and dimethylamine (aOR = 12.693 [95% CI = 1.292–628.175], P-value = 0.028) were associated with treatment-defined severity. Bayesian network analysis further supported the direct probabilistic associations of these metabolites with asthma severity. Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent trends across clinical strata, suggesting that distinct metabolic states may underlie higher ICS requirements despite stable symptom control.ConclusionIn clinically stable asthma, elevated pyruvate and dimethylamine were associated with greater ICS requirements, suggesting that metabolomic profiling may aid in refining endotypes and personalizing anti-inflammatory therapy beyond symptom-based assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-026-03737-9.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous respiratory disease characterized by variable symptoms and expiratory airflow limitation, with clinical severity ranging from intermittent mild disease to forms requiring sustained intensive therapy. In clinical practice, asthma severity is often determined from the treatment intensity required to achieve and maintain symptom control [1, 2]. While this treatment-based classification of asthma severity is clinically practical, its association with underlying disease pathobiology remains less clearly established [1].

Treatment intensity alone does not always reflect the underlying inflammatory activity, and some patients may be overtreated or undertreated relative to their actual disease burden [3].

Moreover, accurate assessment of asthma severity frequently requires longitudinal observation and dose titration, which can delay definitive classification [4].

Current approaches to asthma phenotyping have included demographic characteristics, lung function parameters, and surrogate biomarkers of type 2 inflammation such as blood eosinophil counts and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [5]. Transcriptomic clustering of peripheral blood has identified distinct asthma endotypes characterized by heterogeneous clinical severity and immune profiles, including both type 2 and non-type 2 pattern [6]. Although these indicators provide valuable clinical insight, they reflect only a portion of the complex molecular processes that underlie asthma pathophysiology [7]. Increasing evidence suggests that immune dysregulation, oxidative stress, and altered cellular metabolism contribute meaningfully to disease heterogeneity and may account for variations in treatment responsiveness [8].

Metabolomics has emerged as a promising tool to investigate asthma heterogeneity at the molecular level. Metabolite profiles reflect dynamic and interrelated processes including immune modulation, redox balance, and energy metabolism, all of which are closely linked to asthma pathogenesis and treatment response [9, 10]. While previous studies have reported metabolomic differences across asthma severity groups, few have examined intra-disease variation within a clinically homogeneous population characterized by well-controlled condition but differing in the level of inhaled corticosteroid therapy required to sustain that control [11, 12].

Therefore, we aimed to compare metabolomic profiles between severe and non-severe asthma among the patients with clinically well-controlled disease.

Methods

The reporting of this study adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, with additional reference to STROBE-ME (Molecular Epidemiology) recommendations regarding biospecimen-based molecular analyses [13].

Study design and setting

This prospective cross-sectional study enrolled patients who visited the respiratory outpatient clinic for asthma management between July 2023 and August 2024. At the initial visit, participants underwent screening to verify the asthma diagnosis, confirm ongoing use of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), and assess eligibility for further evaluation based on treatment response (Fig. 1). Patients were included in the subsequent analyses only if they demonstrated adequate adherence to ICS therapy and achieved well-controlled asthma at the one-month follow-up. At the three-month visit, patients were reassessed to confirm continued use of the same inhaled treatment regimen over the preceding three months, sustained adherence to ICS therapy, and maintenance of well-controlled asthma. Following this confirmation, a comprehensive asthma assessment and blood sampling were conducted after obtaining written informed consent.

Participants and eligibility criteria

Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) a physician-confirmed diagnosis of asthma (2), patients whose treatment response allowed for the assessment of asthma severity (3), prescription of the same inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) regimen for at least three months prior to enrollment (4), evidence of adequate treatment adherence and device technique (5), well-controlled asthma, indicated by an Asthma Control Test (ACT) score greater than 20, and (6) no history of exacerbation within the preceding 12 weeks. Asthma was diagnosed based on the presence of variable respiratory symptoms and objective evidence of variable expiratory airflow limitation, in accordance with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) report. Adherence to inhaled therapy was objectively quantified using the MPR, calculated as the number of days of medication supplied divided by the number of days in the observation period.

Adequate adherence was defined as a medication possession ratio (MPR) greater than 80%. Inhaler technique was assessed at each clinic visit by trained healthcare professionals using a standardized device-specific checklist. Adequate inhaler technique was defined as the absence of any critical errors, consistent with the ERS/ISAM consensus statement [14].

Patients were excluded if they had any clinically significant pulmonary disease other than asthma, including chronic respiratory infections, COPD, bronchiectasis, pulmonary fibrosis, cystic fibrosis, lung cancer, or rare conditions such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency or primary ciliary dyskinesia. Conditions associated with peripheral eosinophilia, including allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, Churg–Strauss syndrome, and hypereosinophilic syndrome, were also exclusionary. Additional exclusions included systemic comorbidities that could influence blood biomarkers, such as acute infections, cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, endocrine, autoimmune, hematologic, or malignant diseases. Patients receiving medications known to affect metabolic or inflammatory markers, including antidiabetic drugs, lipid-lowering agents, systemic corticosteroids, or immunosuppressants, were also excluded. Additionally, patients receiving biologic therapies for asthma, including anti-IgE, anti-IL-5, anti-IL-5Rα, and anti-IL-4/IL-13 agents, were not included in this study.

Enrolled patients were classified into two groups based on asthma severity. Severe asthma was defined as asthma requiring high-dose ICS/LABA to achieve and maintain disease control, despite good adherence and adequate inhaler technique. High-dose ICS was defined according to the GINA 2025 report as a daily dose equivalent to budesonide > 800 µg/day (or equivalent doses of other ICS formulations). All other patients were classified as having non-severe asthma.

Clinical data collection

Demographic information including age, sex, and body mass index was collected. Smoking history was recorded and categorized as never smoker, ex-smoker, or current smoker, and cumulative exposure was quantified in pack-years. Comorbid conditions were evaluated with the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), where a score of one or more indicated the presence of clinically relevant comorbidities. Pulmonary function was evaluated using post-bronchodilator spirometry, including measurements of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC). These values were reported both in absolute terms and as percentages of predicted values, calculated using the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) 2022 reference equations [15]. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide was measured concurrently with spirometry. Blood tests included total white blood cell count, absolute eosinophil count, and eosinophil percentage. Additional laboratory assessments comprised serum protein, albumin, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Biospecimen collection and handling

A 10 milliliter sample of peripheral venous blood was collected into pre-labeled, EDTA-treated vacutainer tubes. Immediately following collection, samples were securely sealed and placed in ice-packed transport containers. On the day of sampling, the specimens were transferred under temperature-controlled conditions by trained study personnel to the Seoul National University Gwanak Campus for immediate processing. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 12,000 × g at 4°C for 15 min and stored at − 80°C until metabolomic analysis.

Metabolomic profiling

1H-NMR spectra were obtained at 298 K using a Bruker Avance III HD 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with an BBO prodigy probe (Bruker Biospin, Germany). Plasma samples were reconstituted in D2O (pH 7.0) with an internal standard of 1 mM 3-(trimethylsilyl) propionic-2,2,3,3-d4 acid (TSP). NMR spectra of all the plasma content samples were acquired using the first increment of cpmg pulse sequence with the relaxation delay of 4s. The 90° pulse length was adjusted to about 10 ms for each sample, resulting in 64 k data points per spectrum with spectral width of 20 ppm. NMR data processing was conducted using TopSpin Version 4.4 (Bruker Biospin, Germany). The chemical shifts (δ) of 1H-NMR spectra were calibrated relative to the TSP resonance, and manual adjustments were made for baseline corrections. To validate the results obtained from the profiling, a 2D 1H-13C heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) and correlated spectroscopy (COSY) NMR experiment was employed [16]. Identification was performed using the Human Metabolome Database (www.hmdb.ca). The 1H-NMR spectra were analyzed using Chenomx NMR Suite (Chenomx, Canada) for the identification and quantification of the metabolites. Additionally, spiking experiments were conducted to validate the overlapping or marginally shifted peaks. The list of identified metabolites is provided in Additional file 1.

While NMR analysis enabled partial quantification of various plasma metabolites, quantification of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) was specifically performed using targeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MSMS). LC–MSMS analysis was performed using an integrated system composed of Nexera X2 and LCMS-8050 (both from Shimadzu, Japan). Then, 1 µL of reaction mixture containing GT-labeled SCFAs was injected into CORTECS C18 column (90Å, 1.6 μm, 2.1 mm X 150 mm, Waters, USA). Solvent A comprised of water with containing 0.1% formic acid, while solvent B composed 100% acetonitrile. GT-labeled SCFAs were separated on the analytical column at a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min. An elution profile begins at 20% B, followed by 20% B (0 to 1 min), 50% B (1 to 12.15 min), 50 to 99% B (12.15 to 12.35 min), 99% B (12.35 to 14.85 min), and finally 99 to 120% B (15 to 18 min). The mass spectrometer was operated in the positive ion mode through electrospray ionization at unit resolution. Optimized LC-MSMS experiments were conducted using nebulizing gas flow of 3 L/min, heating gas flow of 10 L/min, interface temperature of 300°C, DL temperature of 250°C, heater block temperature of 400°C, and drying gas flow of 10 L/min.

Statistics

Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges and compared using independent samples t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using Fisher’s exact test or chi-squared test. Plasma metabolite levels between non-severe and severe asthma groups were first compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. To identify metabolites associated with asthma severity, univariable logistic regression analyses were initially performed for each metabolite.

To account for the limited sample size and potential separation issues in binary outcome modeling, multivariable analyses were conducted using Firth’s penalized logistic regression to account for small sample size and potential separation [17]. Metabolite variables were standardized prior to model fitting. For multivariable analysis, candidate variables were selected based on the univariable results (P-value < 0.1) and clinical relevance. To account for potential multicollinearity and high-dimensionality, elastic net–regularized logistic regression was conducted to refine the selection of variables. The elastic net method combines L1 (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO]) and L2 (ridge) penalties, allowing for both variable selection and coefficient shrinkage. The mixing parameter (α) was set to 0.5 to balance the contributions of L1 and L2 penalties, and the optimal regularization parameter (λ) was selected via five-fold cross-validation. Variables with non-zero coefficients at the optimal λ were subsequently included in a Firth’s logistic regression model to obtain interpretable effect estimates and corresponding P-values. Selected clinical and metabolite variables were subsequently entered into Firth’s penalized logistic regression model, which was used as the primary multivariable analytic approach [18].

As a secondary exploratory analysis, a Bayesian network model was constructed using the ‘bnlearn’ package in R to evaluate probabilistic relationships among selected metabolites and asthma severity [19, 20]. No prior structural assumptions were imposed during model construction, and the hill-climbing algorithm was allowed to freely explore network configurations based solely on the observed data. In this model, directed edges represent conditional dependencies based on learned data structures, providing insights into interrelations without assuming causal directions. Subgroup analyses were conducted across predefined clinical strata, including age, blood eosinophil count, FeNO, FEV1/FVC ratio, and FEV1 percent predicted.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0, and P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 55 patients with asthma who met the eligibility criteria at both the screening and follow-up visits were enrolled in the study, of whom 46 were classified as having non-severe asthma and 9 as having severe asthma. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Of the baseline variables, the mean post-bronchodilator FEV1 (% of predicted) was significantly lower in the severe asthma group than in the non-severe group (79.8% vs. 96.3%, P-value = 0.028). Furthermore, white blood cell count was significantly higher in patients with severe asthma compared to those with non-severe asthma (8,354/µL vs. 6,619/µL, P-value = 0.026). In univariable logistic regression analyses, a lower post-bronchodilator FEV1 (% of predicted) was significantly associated with severe asthma (unadjusted odds ratio [uOR], 0.956; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.914–0.994; P-value = 0.034) (Additional file 2). Although a trend toward higher odds of severe asthma was observed in current smokers, it did not reach statistical significance.

In univariable analyses, pyruvate (uOR = 12.341 [95% CI, 2.260–67.375], P = 0.004) and lactate (uOR = 1.177 [95% CI, 1.051–1.318], P = 0.005) were each significantly associated with severe asthma (Table 3). Discussion

In this prospective metabolomic study of patients with clinically well-controlled asthma, we investigated biological differences associated with treatment-defined severity. By focusing on a population receiving a consistent ICS regimen over time with sustained symptom control, we were able to minimize clinical heterogeneity and isolate metabolomic signals potentially linked to underlying disease biology. Plasma metabolomic profiling revealed that levels of pyruvate and dimethylamine were significantly elevated in patients classified as having severe asthma based on high-dose ICS requirements. These associations remained robust after adjustment for important clinical variables, including smoking status, lung function, and systemic inflammatory markers, in multivariable analyses using Firth’s penalized logistic regression. To refine variable selection and account for potential multicollinearity, an elastic net–regularized approach was applied prior to the multivariable modeling. Furthermore, exploratory Bayesian network analysis supported the probabilistic dependencies between these metabolites and asthma severity, reinforcing the biological relevance of the identified metabolic differences. Importantly, these findings were observed in a population without recent exacerbations or systemic corticosteroid exposure, suggesting that the identified metabolic differences may reflect intrinsic disease characteristics rather than transient clinical instability. Taken together, our results suggest that even among patients with comparable symptom control, distinct underlying metabolic states may reflect differential ICS requirements linked to asthma severity.

Previous metabolomic studies in asthma have provided valuable insights into systemic biochemical profiles associated with disease severity, but their interpretability has often been limited by methodological and clinical heterogeneity. In previous studies, patient cohorts included a wide range of treatment backgrounds and clinical states, including recent exacerbations or ongoing systemic corticosteroid use, which can substantially influence metabolomic profiles independent of baseline disease characteristics [11, 21, 22]. Consequently, it is often unclear whether observed metabolic differences reflected underlying disease mechanisms or transient effects of inflammation and pharmacologic intervention. In contrast, our study focused on a clinically homogeneous population with stable disease control, as defined by ACT scores and consistent use of ICS therapy, thereby providing a more uniform framework for biological interpretation. Within this stable clinical context, we compared patients according to the intensity of ICS therapy required to maintain control, offering a more refined perspective on metabolic profiles potentially linked to intrinsic disease mechanisms rather than reactive physiological states.

Among the identified metabolites, pyruvate and dimethylamine were elevated in patients with severe asthma. Pyruvate is a central intermediate in glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, playing a critical role in cellular energy metabolism and redox regulation [23, 24]. Increased serum pyruvate levels may reflect enhanced glycolytic flux coupled with impaired mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, a metabolic adaptation that has been linked to chronic airway inflammation and structural remodeling in asthma [25–27]. Furthermore, elevated pyruvate has been associated with hypoxic stress and oxidative imbalance within inflamed airway microenvironments, potentially contributing to corticosteroid insensitivity and persistent inflammation despite adequate symptom control [28–30]. Dimethylamine is a byproduct of choline and asymmetric dimethylarginine metabolism, and has been implicated in nitric oxide homeostasis and systemic inflammatory responses [31]. Elevated levels of dimethylamine have been observed in various inflammatory conditions and may reflect alterations in methylation pathways or dysregulation of nitrogen-related oxidative processes [32–34]. In the context of asthma, increased dimethylamine may indicate perturbations in endothelial function and immune-metabolic interactions, both of which are increasingly recognized as contributors to disease persistence and suboptimal treatment response [8]. Especially, dimethylamine was significantly associated with small airway dysfunction parameters including R5-R20, AX, Fres and AX in multivariable analyses [35]. Of note, while dietary sources can acutely influence circulating dimethylamine levels, the predominant determinants of its plasma concentrations are these endogenous metabolic pathways rather than acute nutritional intake. Furthermore, given that non-fasting sampling conditions introduce biological noise that would be expected to attenuate rather than amplify metabolomic signals, the emergence of dimethylamine as a significant and independent predictor of asthma severity under such heterogeneous conditions may itself reflect the robustness of the observed association. These findings suggest that distinct underlying metabolic states may contribute to differential ICS requirements even among patients with comparable symptom control. The concurrent elevation of pyruvate and dimethylamine points to interconnected perturbations in energy metabolism, redox balance, and endothelial function, supporting the concept that certain patients classified as well-controlled by conventional measures may still harbor persistent pathobiologic processes that necessitate higher anti-inflammatory dosing.

In subgroup analyses, dimethylamine levels were significantly elevated in patients aged less than 65 years, those with higher fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO > 50 ppb), and those with preserved lung function (FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.7 and FEV1 ≥ 80%). These findings suggest that metabolic perturbations associated with dimethylamine may be particularly prominent in relatively younger patients with active type 2 airway inflammation and minimal ventilatory impairment. Given that elevated FeNO reflects ongoing type 2 inflammation and that preserved spirometric indices imply limited structural airway damage, the increased dimethylamine levels observed in these subgroups may reflect early endothelial dysfunction or systemic inflammatory priming. This pattern suggests the potential role of dimethylamine as a biomarker for subclinical disease activity in patients who appear clinically stable yet harbor persistent inflammatory or metabolic dysregulation. In contrast, pyruvate levels did not show significant variation across clinical subgroups, suggesting that its association with asthma severity may represent a more generalized metabolic alteration, independent of specific clinical phenotypes.

In clinical practice, ICS dose decisions are largely empirical, guided by stepwise adjustment based on symptom scores and lung function. However, this approach may inadequately capture the underlying biological disease burden, exposing patients to periods of under- or over-treatment [4]. The present findings offer a complementary biological perspective on ICS dose requirements. Elevated pyruvate, reflecting enhanced glycolytic flux and mitochondrial dysfunction, may indicate a state of heightened inflammatory activity and oxidative stress that intrinsically demands greater anti-inflammatory suppression. Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress have been mechanistically linked to corticosteroid insensitivity through impairment of histone deacetylase-2 activity, a critical mediator of ICS anti-inflammatory action [28–30]. Under this framework, elevated pyruvate may not merely reflect greater disease activity but may also signal relative ICS insensitivity, providing a biological rationale for higher dose requirements. Similarly, elevated dimethylamine, through its associations with asymmetric dimethylarginine metabolism and endothelial dysfunction, may identify patients with subclinical inflammatory burden that is inadequately captured by conventional symptom-based metrics [36]. Collectively, these metabolic signals may help identify patients whose biological disease activity remains disproportionately elevated relative to their symptomatic presentation, informing more individualized decisions regarding ICS dose escalation or the consideration of add-on therapies. Early optimization of ICS dosing guided by such biological signals, with the goal of achieving clinical remission, may translate into improved long-term outcomes in asthma [37]. If validated in larger prospective cohorts, metabolomic profiling could serve as an adjunctive tool in precision asthma management, guiding therapeutic decisions beyond what symptom scores and lung function alone can provide.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the study sample was relatively small, and the number of patients with severe asthma was particularly limited, which may constrain statistical power, the stability of effect estimates, and the generalizability of the observed associations. Although robust analytical strategies including penalized regression and Bayesian network modeling were applied to mitigate overfitting and multicollinearity, further accumulation of cases remains essential. While smoking status was incorporated as a covariate in all multivariable models, the numerically greater proportions of male patients and current smokers in the severe asthma group introduce the possibility of residual confounding that larger, sex-balanced cohorts would be better positioned to address. Second, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inference or dynamic assessment of metabolite fluctuations in response to changes in treatment or disease state. Longitudinal metabolomic profiling could offer additional insight into the temporal stability of the observed metabolic differences and their relationship with clinical outcomes. Third, although our cohort was intentionally restricted to patients with well-controlled asthma, unmeasured confounding related to diet, microbiome composition, or unrecorded comorbidities may have influenced metabolite levels. Also, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that differences in ICS dose or the specific pharmacological properties of individual agents may have contributed to the observed metabolomic differences. Finally, while our focus on treatment-defined severity offers clinical relevance, it remains an indirect surrogate for biological activity. Integrating multi-omics approaches such as transcriptomics or proteomics may improve the resolution of disease endotyping in future work.

In conclusion, our metabolomic study of patients with clinically well-controlled asthma identified elevated plasma levels of pyruvate and dimethylamine as metabolic markers associated with higher ICS requirements, independent of smoking status, lung function, and systemic inflammatory markers. These findings highlight the presence of persistent biological activity despite stable clinical symptoms and support the potential utility of metabolomic profiling in refining asthma endotyping and informing personalized anti-inflammatory treatment strategies.
