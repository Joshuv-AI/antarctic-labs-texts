Abstract
. The study of the evolving phenomena in a domain helps to understand the relationships between entities at different points in time and predict future trends. These phenomena, often complex, can be represented using knowledge graphs, which have the capability to model heterogeneous data from multiple sources. Nowadays, a considerable amount of sources delivering periodic updates to knowledge graphs in various domains is openly available. The evolution of data is of interest to knowledge graph management systems, and therefore it is crucial to organize these constantly evolving data to make them easily accessible and exploitable for analyzes. In this article, we will present and formalize the condensed representation of these evolving graphs.

1.
Introduction and motivations

1.1.
Data evolution and versioning

Time is a fundamental aspect of data and the need for temporal data management
(
Jensen and
Snodgrass 2009
)
has been recognized for a long time. Temporal snapshots of data help to understand the evolution of data over time and is essential for various applications, such as historical data analysis, trend analysis, and data quality assessment.

Our requirement emerged from urban data management, where temporal data management is highly significant. Researchers have used it to represent and study both historical data and future urban projects
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
. Thanks to the growing availability of open urban data, often released periodically, scientists can study and understand complex urban phenomena with incremental changes
(
Chaturvedi et al. 2017
;
Kutzner
et al. 2020
;
Samuel
et al. 2020
;
Eriksson
et al. 2021
)
. A comprehensive understanding of dynamic elements like population growth, infrastructure development, and transportation patterns is crucial for effective urban management. Such evolving open data empowers urban planners, policymakers, and researchers to gain valuable insights into these dynamics. This in turn facilitates improved planning and decision-making processes.

In our work, focusing on urban data management, where periodical data snapshots are available, we propose the use of versioning to refer to data at different points or periods of time. The versioning of scientific data, including urban data is of great interest as it enables the exploitation and reproducibility of experiments and analyzes
(
Klump et al. 2021
)
. Versioning captures the state of the data at a given point or period in time. Without versioning, it becomes difficult to reproduce results at a later date if the data changes. It helps protect the integrity of the data by allowing for the restoration of previous versions in case of errors. Furthermore, scientists often need to experiment with different analytical approaches, and versioning allows for easy switching between different versions of the data for these experiments.

Several ways exist for urban data management. Knowledge graphs are now being recently explored for the representation of urban phenomena and evolution
(
Vinasco-Alvarez et al. 2021
)
. By incorporating versioning into these knowledge graphs, it also becomes possible to capture concurrent viewpoints of urban evolution
(
Samuel
et al. 2020
)
. It enables the representation of different states of the urban environment over time, providing a comprehensive view of the city’s evolution. Thus, in our work, we propose to make use of knowledge graphs available from heterogeneous sources (concurrent viewpoints of urban data) at different periods of time (hereafter, referred to as versions).

1.2.
Improving the comprehension of data evolution

In our context of urban evolution, where urban data is continuously evolving as cities grow and change over time. Buildings are constructed and demolished and infrastructure is modified to meet changing needs. This constant evolution creates challenges for data management systems that need to track and analyze these changes effectively. Additionally, the growing volume of urban data being collected, from multiple sources and at different temporal granularities, adds complexity to the task of managing and understanding urban evolution.

To achieve our goals of achieving effective evolving urban data management and reproducibility of experiments and analyzes, we identified the following requirements for our system:

•

R1: Managing and querying multiple graphs and their different versions
: Our system should allow users to query multiple knowledge graphs coming from one or more sources and access their different versions simultaneously. This capability will enable comprehensive analysis and comparison of urban evolution over time.

•

R2: Linking versioned data with metadata
: It is crucial to establish a connection between versioned data and their associated metadata. This linking will provide valuable context and additional information about the data, enhancing its usability and interpretability. For example, when tracking changes to a building’s height over time, metadata could include the source of the measurement, the measurement method used, and the date of observation - allowing users to better understand and validate the data’s provenance and reliability. Furthermore, one can also associate information related to scenarios of urban evolution from different viewpoints
(
Samuel
et al. 2018
)
.

•

R3: Isolating and querying individual graph versions
: Our system must provide a mechanism to isolate and query specific versions of a knowledge graph independently. This capability allows users to focus their analysis on particular versions without interference from other versions, enabling precise examination of the data at specific snapshots.

By addressing these requirements, the goal of our system is to empower urban planners, policymakers, and researchers to gain deeper insights into urban dynamics, facilitate informed decision-making, and ensure the reproducibility and reliability of scientific experiments and analyzes. Although our focus in this article is on urban data management, we propose a generic framework for evolving data management that can be applied to other domains as well.

In this article, for attaining the above requirements, we will present the formalization of flat and condensed representations and querying of evolving knowledge graphs.
The state of the art in entity versioning is detailed in Section

. Section

presents the necessary preliminaries and notations. Section
4
presents the contributions of our work: datasets using flat and condensed models, algebra for handling query operators and the resulting data in flat and condensed representations.
Section
5

2.
State of the art - Versioning

Resources evolve over time and hence the statements associated to describe them.
Version control systems
(
Zolkifli
et al. 2018
)
, for example, handle this evolution of data (or code) by associating a tag and a timestamp to a snapshot of the data at a given instant of time.
This supplementary information associated to a snapshot, referred to as a version can be used to not only represent the state of the data at any given point of time, but also to understand the evolution of data over a period of time.
As mentioned by
Bayoudhi
et al. 2020
, versioning is a critical aspect of management in a project, allowing developers to track changes to the elements, manage different versions, and collaborate with other team members.
For the sake of familiarity, we will use the word
versioning
to this process of adding information like timestamp, tag to snapshots of one or more resources and their associated statements at any given moment of time.
In this section, we will take a look at different approaches of versioning. Table
1
summarizes these results.

2.1.
Code versioning

Version control systems
(
Zolkifli
et al. 2018
)
such as Git, Mercurial, Subversion, CVS, and Bazaar are commonly used for managing the evolution of source code. However, these solutions are not specifically designed for data versioning, especially for structured data
(
Sveen 2020
)
and have certain limitations for efficient querying
(
Bulteau
et al. 2023
;
Yilmaz and
Dittrich 2025
;
Samuel
et al. 2018
)
. Apart from some exceptions, like Git LFS, they don’t provide adequate support for large files and versioned data queries. Therefore, they may not be the ideal choice for managing data versioning in a project.

2.2.
Dataset versioning

2.2.1.
Snapshot-based versioning

There are several versioning tools available for managing data in a project. Some Git-based tools include Qri
(
team 2024b
)
, QuitStore
(
Arndt 2020
)
, GeoGig
(
team 2024a
)
, and UrbanCo2Fab
(
Samuel
et al. 2018
)
. These tools provide version control capabilities specifically designed for data versioning. However, one limitation of these tools is that in order to query a version, it needs to be checked out (i.e., materialize virtually or physically the complete state of the version before making any query request). This limits the capacity of such systems to answer queries across multiple versions at the same time. Despite this limitation, database versioning tools provide valuable functionality for managing data in a project.
Furthermore, there are also machine learning tools like
DVC
,
DagsHub
and
Weights and Biases
that offer advanced features for managing data versions. These tools are particularly useful for structured data and provide functionalities such as tracking changes, managing different versions, and collaborating with team members. However, these tools are not suitable for our use case, as they do not support efficient querying across multiple snapshots at the same time.

2.2.2.
Interval-based versioning

Another approach to data versioning involves using temporal tables or bitemporal tables
(
Kulkarni and
Michels 2012
)
to represent the validity of data over time. These tables store the start and end timestamps for each version of the data, allowing for querying the data at specific points in time or in a specific time interval. However, this approach has limitations, in particular the lack of support for finding data belonging to concurrent periods of time, commonly seen as branches or forks in version control systems.

2.2.3.
Delta-based versioning

Delta-based versioning systems like Delta Lake and Apache Hudi store changes made to data as deltas, allowing for efficient querying at different versions. OSTRICH (Offset-enabled TRIple store for CHangesets)
(
Taelman et al. 2019
)
is another delta-based system specifically designed for RDF (Resource Description Framework) data. While OSTRICH provides efficient time-based querying through its delta encoding, we did not use it because it only supports linear version history (no branching/forking). Our use case requires supporting concurrent versions to represent different viewpoints of urban evolution. Additionally, OSTRICH focuses on temporal versioning with timestamps, whereas we need a more general versioning model that can handle arbitrary version identifiers and relationships between versions.

This work takes into account certain aspects of the design, development and formalization of version control systems
(

;

;
Zolkifli
et al. 2018

)
, and extends it to knowledge graph. We propose an alternative representation of these graphs called "condensed representation" for eventual querying using SPARQL. This representation is based on the idea of representing the graph as a set of triples that are associated to the versions where they are valid. Our main contributions are the formalization of the condensed representation of evolving knowledge graphs and the algebra for handling query operators and the resulting data in flat and condensed representations.

3.
Preliminaries and notations

In this section, we will present the key concepts and notations related to representing knowledge graphs in the form of RDF and querying them using the SPARQL query language.

3.1.
RDF context representation

For the sake of simplicity, we considered the following RDF context representation based on the W3C definitions
(
W3C 2024a
)
:

3.1.1.
Literal

A literal denotes resources of the real world.

•

l,l_{1},l_{2}\dots
a literal

•

{L},{L}_{1},{L}_{2}\dots
a finite set of literals

•

\mathcal{{L}}
the infinite set of all literals

3.1.2.
IRI

Internationalized Resource Identifier (IRI) is a Unicode string, analogous to Uniform Resource Identifiers (URI), and used to identify resources.

•

i,i_{1},i_{2}\dots
an IRI

•

{I},{I}_{1},{I}_{2}\dots
a finite set of IRI

•

\mathcal{{I}}
the infinite set of all IRIs

3.1.3.
Blank Node

Not all resources and the relationships can be explicitly named and are represented as blank nodes indicating the existence of something. To handle blank nodes, we use Skolemization
(
Hogan
et al. 2014
)
, a technique that replaces blank nodes with generated IRIs. This allows us to treat blank nodes as regular resources with globally unique identifiers, enabling better integration and querying of RDF data.

3.1.4.
Terms

Terms are used to refer to IRIs or literals.

•

t,t_{1},t_{2}\dots
a term

•

{T},{T}_{1},{T}_{2}\dots
a finite set of terms

•

\mathcal{{T}}
the infinite set of all terms

•

\mathcal{{T}}=\{\mathcal{{L}},\mathcal{{I}}\}
where:

–

\mathcal{{L}}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{{T}}

–

\mathcal{{I}}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{{T}}

–

\mathcal{{L}}\cap\mathcal{{I}}=\emptyset

3.1.5.
Triples

An RDF triple is a statement about a resource that consists of three parts: a subject, an object, and a predicate. The subject is an IRI that represents the resource being described. The object can be an IRI or a literal that represents the value of the property. The predicate is an IRI that represents the relationship between the subject and the object.

•

{TR},{TR}_{1},{TR}_{2}\dots
a finite set of triples

•

\mathcal{{TR}}
the infinite set of all triples

3.1.6.
Graphs

An RDF graph is a set of RDF triples. It represents a collection of resources and the relationships between them. An RDF graph can be named or unnamed by a resource. An unnamed graph is called the default graph.

3.1.7.
Quads

A quad is a statement that consists of four parts: all triple elements (the subject, the predicate, the object), and the graph name. The graph name is an IRI that represents the context in which the triple is defined.

t_{g}\in\mathcal{{T}}_{g}

3.1.8.
Datasets

A dataset
d
is a collection of graphs. A dataset can contain zero or more named graphs, each of which contains a set of RDF triples. A dataset can also contain a default graph, which is a set of triples that are not associated with any specific graph name.

3.1.9.
Versioning

A version is a snapshot of the RDF data at a given point in time. We define the following versioning representation:

•

v,v_{1},v_{2}\dots
a version

•

{V},{V}_{1},{V}_{2}\dots
a finite set of versions

•

\mathcal{{V}}
the infinite set of all versions

Because the RDF data model is atemporal as explained in the "RDF and Change over Time" paragraph of the W3C RDF 1.1 Concepts and Abstract Syntax
(
W3C 2024a
)
, we consider the versioning of RDF data as a set of versions.

3.2.
SPARQL queries

SPARQL
(
W3C 2024b
)
is a declarative programming language used to query data stores containing a set of triples or quads.

3.2.1.
Variables

A SPARQL variable is a placeholder for a value that is not known beforehand. It allows querying for information without having to specify the exact values the user is looking for. Variables are denoted by a question mark "?" or a dollar sign "$", immediately followed by a string without any white space characters.

•

var,var_{1},var_{2}\dots
a SPARQL variable

•

{VAR},{VAR}_{1},{VAR}_{2}\dots
a finite set of SPARQL variables

•

\mathcal{{VAR}}
the infinite set of all SPARQL variables

3.2.2.
Query

A SPARQL query is a formal language expression used to retrieve and manipulate data stored in RDF format, according to the W3C definition
(
W3C 2024b
)
.

•

qu,qu_{1},qu_{2}\dots
a SPARQL query

•

{QU},{QU}_{1},{QU}_{2}\dots
a finite set of SPARQL queries

•

\mathcal{{QU}}
the infinite set of all SPARQL queries

SPARQL expression

A SPARQL expression refers to a part of a SPARQL query that computes or filters values within the query. We note:

•

e,e_{1},e_{2}\dots
a SPARQL expression

•

{E},{E}_{1},{E}_{2}\dots
a finite set of SPARQL expressions

•

\mathcal{{E}}
the infinite set of all SPARQL expressions

SPARQL expression can be:

•

A term
t
(e.g.,
10
) is an expression if
t\in\mathcal{{T}}

•

A variable
var
(e.g.,
?x
) is an expression if
var\in{VAR}

•

A function
f(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
is an expression, if
f
is a function and
\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\},e_{i}
is a SPARQL expression (e.g.,
AVG(e)
where
e
is a SPARQL expression)

•

e_{1}\neq e_{2}
is an expression if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are expressions

SPARQL expressions are used to:

•

Combine values from different RDF triples to form more complex patterns (e.g., triple patterns, basic graph patterns, or subqueries)

•

Filter results based on conditions (e.g., using the
FILTER
keyword)

•

Transform data using functions like mathematical operations, string manipulation, or logical operators (e.g.,
CONCAT
,
STRLEN
…)

Solution mapping

A solution mapping is a mapping between variables and RDF terms. It is used to represent the results of a SPARQL query. We note
m:\mathcal{{VAR}}\to\mathcal{{T}}
a solution mapping that maps a set of variables to a set of RDF terms and
\mathcal{{M}}
an infinite set of solution mappings.

Solution sequence

A solution sequence is a sequence of solution mappings. We denote a solution sequence as
seq=[m_{1},\dots,m_{n}]
where each
m_{i}
is a solution mapping.

Let

{{V}{I}},{{V}{I}}_{1},{{V}{I}}_{2}\dots

Metadata plays a crucial role in our approach, particularly for managing concurrent snapshots or concurrent viewpoints. By associating a constant version
v:0
to all metadata, we ensure that version information and relationships between versions remain consistent and accessible across the entire dataset. This is especially important when dealing with branching version histories or multiple concurrent viewpoints, as the metadata helps track and organize these complex relationships. While this means metadata is not condensed and can only be represented in the flat model, this design choice provides a stable foundation for managing versioned data across different temporal and conceptual dimensions.

4.2.3.
Model relationships

Definition 4.4 (Condensation).

Consider a flat dataset
d_{F}=\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{T}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{V}}
. We can condense the dataset
d_{F}
into the dataset
d_{C}=\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{T}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\to 2^{\mathcal{{V}}}
. The condensation function
C
takes a quad and returns the set of versions in which that quad exists. This function is defined as:

C=(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\mapsto\{v\mid(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in d_{F}\}

The domain of
C
is the set of quads in the dataset
d_{F}
where the quad occurs at a set of versions such that:
\mathcal{D}(C)=\{(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\mid\exists{v}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in d_{F}\}
.

Definition 4.5 (Flattening).

Let
d_{C}=\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{T}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\to 2^{\mathcal{{V}}}
be a dataset. We can flatten the dataset
d_{C}
into the dataset:
d_{F}=\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{T}}\times\mathcal{{I}}\times\mathcal{{V}}
. The flattening function
F
is defined as:

F=\{(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\mid v\in d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\}

The domain of
F
is the set of versioned quads in the dataset
d_{C}
where the quad exists in each version according to the condensed model:
\mathcal{D}(F)=\{(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\mid\exists{v}\in d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\}
.

Theorem 4.6 (
C\circ F(d_{F})=d_{F}
).

Given a flat model
d_{F}
, its image by the composition of the
C
and the
F
functions is equal to the flat model.

Proof.

We want to prove the following equivalence:
C\circ F(d_{F})=d_{F}
.
This means we need to prove:

•

On the one hand, that
C\circ F(d_{F})\subseteq d_{F}
;

•

On the other hand, that
d_{F}\subseteq C\circ F(d_{F})
.

In other words, we need to show that every element of
C\circ F(d_{F})
belongs to
d_{F}
, and conversely, every element of
d_{F}
belongs to
C\circ F(d_{F})
.

Let the versioned quad
(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in d_{F}
such that

v\in C(d_{F})(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})
, then
(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in F(C(d_{F}))
.

Let
(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in F(C(d_{F}))
, then
v\in C(d_{F})(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})=\{v^{\prime}\mid(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v^{\prime})\in d_{F}\}
. This proves that
C\circ F(d_{F})=d_{F}
.
∎

Theorem 4.7 (
F\circ C(d_{C})=d_{C}
).

Given a condensed model
d_{C}
, its image by the composition of the
F
and the
C
functions is equal to the condensed model.

Proof.

We want to prove the following equivalence:
F\circ C(d_{C})=d_{C}
.
This means we must prove:

•

For every element of
F\circ C(d_{C})
, there exists a corresponding element in
d_{C}
;

•

For every element of
d_{C}
, there exists a corresponding element in
F\circ C(d_{C})
.

In other words, we show equality by proving both inclusions.

Let
{V}^{C}=C(F(d_{C}))(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})
, so
{V}^{C}=\{v\mid(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in F(d_{C})\}=\{v\mid v\in d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\}=d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})
.
∎

4.3.
SPARQL algebra

A SPARQL query algebra provides a formal framework for representing SPARQL queries
(
Cyganiak 2005
)
. It also defines the execution semantics by translating the high-level query syntax into a series of operations that an RDF query engine can process. We use
\mathcal{A}
to denote the SPARQL algebras set. This paper will present two types of algebra: a condensed algebra and a flat algebra that will be respectively noted as
\mathcal{A}_{C}
and
\mathcal{A}_{F}
. Based on the SPARQL algebra proposed by
(
Cyganiak 2005
, Cyganiak, Richard, A relational algebra for SPARQL)
, we modified the SPARQL Algebra to take into account graph versioning to allow us to query them. An initial proposition was made by
(
Cuevas and Hogan 2020
, Cuevas, Hogan)
to represent queries over RDF Archives. We have taken this proposition as a basis to define what we call
the flat SPARQL algebra
.

4.3.1.
Flat SPARQL algebra

The flat SPARQL algebra contains operators that define its expressive power. For example, the quad pattern
QP_{F}(?s,?p,?o,?g)
retrieves triples matching the triple pattern
TP_{F}(?s,?p,?o)
within a versioned graph
?g
, and is considered syntactic sugar for the graph operator, as shown by
(
Cuevas and Hogan 2020
, Cuevas, Hogan)
. The
JOIN
operator
\bowtie_{F}(e_{1},e_{2})
combines results from two expressions based on common variable bindings, while the
FILTER
operator
\sigma_{F}(\mathcal{{E}},e)
restricts results according to filter functions over variable bindings. The
UNION
operator
\cup_{F}(e_{1},e_{2})
merges results from two expressions into a single set. The
DIFF
operator
\setminus_{F}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
computes the difference between two sets of solutions, conditioned by an additional expression, and is essential for defining the
OPTIONAL
operator. The
OPTIONAL
operator
\mathbin{\rule[0.75348pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\rule[3.65973pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}}\mkern-6.25mu\bowtie}_{F}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
extends the join by including all results from the first expression and, where possible, matching results from the second, returning nulls otherwise. Finally, the aggregation operator groups results based on variables and supports operations such as count, sum, average, min, and max, typically used with the
GROUP BY
clause in SPARQL queries.

4.3.2.
Condensed algebra

The condensed algebra provides operators tailored for querying over all versions of a graph in a compact representation. The quad pattern
QP_{C}(?s,?p,?o,?g)
retrieves triples matching the triple pattern
TP_{F}(?s,?p,?o)
across all versioned graphs
?g
. The condensed algebra includes optimized aggregation operators that can process data across versions. For example, aggregation operators can leverage optimizations such as count-views (as defined by Sarah Cohen
(
Cohen
et al. 2006
)
) to efficiently compute aggregate values over versioned data without having to materialize each version separately.

4.3.3.
Variable representation

SPARQL queries are composed of operators and contain variables whose representations need to be defined. The variable representation is used to determine its behavior in the query and how it is processed in the algebra.

It is important to note that in our current formalization, we do not handle cases where there are variable clashes. Variable clashes occur when two variables with the same name but different scopes or contexts are used within the same query, leading to ambiguity and potential errors in query evaluation. By not addressing these clashes, we assume that all variables are uniquely named within their respective scopes. This simplification allows us to focus on the core aspects of our model and its algebraic semantics without the added complexity of variable disambiguation. However, in a more robust formalization, mechanisms to detect and resolve such clashes would be necessary to ensure the correctness and reliability of query results.

A variable representation is a type that is assigned to a variable in a SPARQL query.

4.3.6.
Operators translation

As shown in Figure
4
, the dataset is stored in a condensed model. In order to evaluate a SPARQL query on this condensed dataset, we need to translate the input SPARQL query to another query language in order to make it compatible with the condensed model. For this purpose, we will now define the translation functions for the SPARQL operators of the flat algebra to the condensed algebra. The translation of these operators are done with the function
\tau
and is defined as:

The translation of the flat quad pattern to the condensed quad pattern is straightforward. It doesn’t require any variable type transformation.

(15)

\begin{split}\tau(QP_{F}(s,p,o,g))=&QP_{C}(s,p,o,g)\end{split}

The translation of the flat join operator is done by promoting the variables to the lowest common representation between the two expressions.

(16)

\begin{split}\tau(\bowtie_{F}(e_{1},e_{2}))=&\bowtie_{C}(\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{1}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{1})),\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{2}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{2})))\\
&\text{where }\Gamma={R}^{*}(\bowtie_{F}(e_{1},e_{2}))\end{split}

The translation of the flat filter operator is straightforward and doesn’t require any variable type transformation because they are already in the lowest common representation in the flat algebra.

(17)

\tau(\sigma_{F}(\mathcal{{E}},e))=\sigma_{C}(\mathcal{{E}},\tau(e))

The translation of the flat optional operator is done by promoting the variables to the lowest common representation between the two joined expressions and the filter expression.

(18)

\begin{split}\tau(\mathbin{\rule[0.75348pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\rule[3.65973pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}}\mkern-6.25mu\bowtie}_{F}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}))=&\mathbin{\rule[0.75348pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\rule[3.65973pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}}\mkern-6.25mu\bowtie}_{C}(\\
&\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{1}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{1})),\\
&\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{2}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{2})),\\
&\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{3}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{3})))\\
&\text{where }\Gamma={R}^{*}(\mathbin{\rule[0.75348pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\rule[3.65973pt]{2.5pt}{0.5pt}}\mkern-6.25mu\bowtie}_{F}(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}))\end{split}

The translation of the flat union operator is done by promoting the variables to the lowest common representation such that the variables are of the same type.

(19)

\begin{split}\tau(\cup_{F}(e_{1},e_{2}))=&\cup_{C}(\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{1}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{1})),\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{2}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{2})))\\
&\text{where }\Gamma={R}^{*}(\cup_{F}(e_{1},e_{2}))\end{split}

The translation of the flat group operator requires promoting the grouping variables to their lowest common representation, ensuring compatibility across all group expressions. The translation is defined as follows:

(20)

\begin{split}\tau(Gr_{F}(\mathcal{{E}}_{n},e))=&Gr_{C}(\\
&\{\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{1}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{1})),\dots,\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{n}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{n}))\},\\
&\tau(e))\end{split}

The translation of the flat aggregation operator involves promoting each aggregated expression to the appropriate representation in the target environment. The translation is defined as follows:

(21)

\begin{split}\tau(Agg_{F}(\mathcal{{E}}_{n},AggFun_{F},\mathcal{S},Gr_{F}))=&Agg_{C}([\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{1}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{1})),\\
&\dots,\downarrow_{r}({R}^{*}(e_{n}),\Gamma)(\tau(e_{n}))],\\
&\tau(AggFun_{F}),\mathcal{S},\tau(Gr_{F}))\end{split}

The translation of the flat aggregate join operator is performed by translating each aggregated expression individually and then combining them using the condensed aggregate join operator. The translation is defined as follows:

(22)

\begin{split}\tau(AggJ_{F}(Agg_{F1},\dots,Agg_{Fn}))=AggJ_{C}(\tau(Agg_{F1}),\dots,\tau(Agg_{Fn}))\end{split}

Lemma 4.47 (Canonical representation of an evaluation).

If
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
then
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(e))
.

Canonical representation of an evaluation.

Let’s use the condensed algebra
A_{C}=\tau(A_{F})
be the condensed algebra expression derived from a flat algebra expression
A_{F}
after its translation. The lemma states: If
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},A_{C})
then
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(A_{C}))
.

The proof proceeds by induction on the number of elementary variable representation transformations required to get from
\Gamma
to
\Gamma^{\prime}
. An elementary transformation changes the representation of a single variable to the next representation in the defined order
\psi<_{r}\zeta<_{r}\iota<_{r}\nu
.

Base Case:

\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}
.
In this case, the number of transformations is 0. The environment transformation function
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
is the identity function when
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}
, because
\{var\mid\Gamma(var)\neq\Gamma^{\prime}(var)\}=\emptyset
. Thus,
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(e)=e
.
The lemma statement becomes: If
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
then
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
, which is trivially true.

Inductive Hypothesis (IH):
Assume that for any pair of environments
(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
such that
\Gamma<_{r}\Gamma^{\prime}
and the transformation
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
involves
k
elementary steps, the lemma holds. That is, if
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
, then
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(e))
.

Inductive Step:
Consider a transformation
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})
that involves
k+1
elementary steps, where
\Gamma<_{r}\Gamma^{\prime}
.
We can decompose this transformation by identifying an intermediate environment
\Gamma_{1}
such that
\Gamma<_{r}\Gamma_{1}\leq_{r}\Gamma^{\prime}
, where
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})
represents a single elementary transformation (e.g., changing the representation of the variable
var_{0}
from
\Gamma(var_{0})
to
\Gamma_{1}(var_{0})
), and
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})
involves
k
elementary steps.
Thus,
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(e)=\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})(\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})(e))
.

Let
e_{1}=\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})(e)
.
The evaluation
eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
produces a solution sequence that is equivalent to applying the type downgrade transformation
\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma\to\Gamma_{1}}
to the solution sequence resulting from
eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
.
Let
seq_{C}=eval_{C}(d_{C},e)
and
seq_{C1}=eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
.
By definition of the operator
\downarrow_{r}
on expressions,
seq_{C1}
is equivalent to
\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma\to\Gamma_{1}}(seq_{C})
and
\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma\to\Gamma_{1}}(seq_{C})=\bigcirc_{m\in seq_{C}}(\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma\to\Gamma_{1}}(m))
.

We get
seq_{F}\cong seq_{C}
. By Definition
4.45
(Flattening) and Definition
4.46
(Permutation), this means
seq_{F}
is permutation-equivalent to
\bigcup_{m\in seq_{C}}\Xi(m)
.

We need to show that
seq_{C}\cong seq_{C1}
in terms of their flat representation equivalence to
seq_{F}
. Here we use the fact that flattening a
m
match gives the same set of flattened matches as if we first applied type reduction to
m(var_{0})
to get a set of (potentially multiple) mappings
\{m^{\prime}\}
(one for each value in
\tau_{\Gamma(var_{0})\leadsto\Gamma_{1}(var_{0})}(m(var_{0}))
), and then flattening each
m^{\prime}
and taking their union.

That is:
\Xi(m)=\bigcup_{m^{\prime}\in\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma\to\Gamma_{1}}(m)}\Xi(m^{\prime})
.
Therefore,

\bigcup_{m\in seq_{C}}\Xi(m)=\bigcup_{m\in seq_{C}}\left(\bigcup_{m^{\prime}\in\downarrow^{var_{0}}_{\Gamma(var_{0})\to\Gamma_{1}(var_{0})}(m)}\Xi(m^{\prime})\right)

This means
seq_{F}\cong seq_{C1}
. So,
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
.

We have
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
, and
e_{1}=\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})(e)
. The remaining part of the transformation is
\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})
, which involves
k
elementary steps.

By the IH (applied to
e_{1}
with environments
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
): if
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
, then
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})(e_{1}))
.

Since we have established that
seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},e_{1})
, we can conclude:

seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})(e_{1}))
.
Substituting
e_{1}=\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})(e)
, we get:

seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma^{\prime})(\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma_{1})(e)))
.
By the compositional nature of
\downarrow_{r}
, this is equivalent to:

seq_{F}\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\downarrow_{r}(\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime})(e))
.
This completes the inductive step.

Therefore, by the principle of induction, the lemma holds for any transformation

where
\Gamma<_{r}\Gamma^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 4.48 (Evaluation equivalence).

Let a flat algebra
A_{F}
, a condensed algebra
A_{C}
such that
A_{C}=\tau(A_{F})
and a dataset
d
and its associated representations
d_{F}
and
d_{C}
then
eval_{F}(d_{F},A_{F})\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\tau(A_{F}))
.

Evaluation equivalence.

We want to prove
eval_{F}(d_{F},A_{F})\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\tau(A_{F}))
.

Let’s proceed with the proof by induction on the structure of SPARQL algebra operators.

Base Case:
we want to show that

eval_{F}(d_{F},QP_{F}(s,p,o,g))\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\tau(QP_{F}(s,p,o,g)))

We rewrite this formula by applying the
\tau
function:

eval_{F}(d_{F},QP_{F}(s,p,o,g))\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},QP_{C}(s,p,o,g))

Let
m_{F}\in eval_{F}(d_{F},QP_{F}(s,p,o,g))
, by definition, there exists
(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g},v)\in d_{F}
such that:

m_{F}(s)=t_{s},m_{F}(p)=t_{p},m_{F}(o)=t_{o},m_{F}(g)={{V}{I}}(v,t_{g})
. We have
v\in d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})
then
(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})\in\mathcal{D}(d_{C})
. This means that
m\in eval_{C}(d_{C},QP_{C}(s,p,o,g))
such that
m^{\iota}(s)=t_{s},m^{\iota}(p)=t_{p},m^{\iota}(o)=t_{o}
and
m^{\zeta}(g)=(t_{g},{V})
with
{V}=d_{C}(t_{s},t_{p},t_{o},t_{g})
.

Let
var\in\mathcal{D}(m_{F})
then:

•

if
var\in\{s,p,o\},m_{F}(var)=m^{\iota}(var)\land m^{\iota}(var)\in\nu_{m}(var)
.

•

if
var=g,m_{F}={{V}{I}}(v,t_{g}),v\in{V}\land m^{\zeta}(var)=(t_{g},{V})
then
m_{F}(var)\in\nu_{m}(var)
in addition,
\mathcal{D}(m_{F})=\mathcal{D}(m^{\nu})\cup\mathcal{D}(m^{\iota})\cup\mathcal{D}(m^{\zeta})\cup\mathcal{D}(m^{\psi})
. This means that
m_{F}\in\Xi(m)
.

IH:
we now assume that the theorem holds for subexpressions and prove it for each SPARQL algebra operator.

Inductive Step:
we want to show that

eval_{F}(d_{F},e)\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\tau(e))

for each operator
e
in the SPARQL algebra. We will show that the theorem holds for each operator in the SPARQL algebra.

It is given that

m_{F}\in\Xi(m)

The Group operator partitions the solution sequence based on the values of the grouping expressions
\mathcal{{E}}_{n}

\Omega_{F}

\Omega_{C}

G_{C}=Gr_{C}([\tau(e_{1}),\dots,\tau(e_{n})),\Omega_{C}]

4.30

m_{F}

G_{F}

G_{C}

Thus, the grouped structures are equivalent, and the theorem holds for the Group operator.

(44)

\begin{split}&eval_{F}(d_{F},Agg_{F}(\mathcal{{E}}_{n},AggFun_{F},\mathcal{S},Gr_{F}))\cong\\
&eval_{C}(d_{C},Agg_{C}(\mathcal{{E}}_{n},AggFun_{C},\mathcal{S},Gr_{C}))\end{split}

From the IH for the
Group
operator, we know that the grouped structures are equivalent:

eval_{F}(d_{F},Gr_{F})\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},Gr_{C})

Let
G_{F}
and
G_{C}
be the grouped solution sequences for the flat and condensed models, respectively. By the inductive hypothesis, for each group
g_{F}\in G_{F}
defined by a key
k
, there is a corresponding group
g_{C}\in G_{C}
for the same key
k
, such that
\bigcup_{m\in g_{C}}\Xi(m)
is a permutation of
g_{F}
.

The flat aggregation function
AggFun_{F}
is applied to each group
g_{F}
. The condensed aggregation function
AggFun_{C}
is applied to each group
g_{C}
. We need to show that for each group, the result of the aggregation is the same.

For the
COUNT
aggregation, the flat model computes the number of mappings in
g_{F}
. In the condensed model, for each mapping
m\in g_{C}
, we compute
eval_{e}(card,m)
, which represents the number of flat mappings encoded by
m
. Summing
eval_{e}(card,m)
over all
m\in g_{C}
yields the same total as counting the mappings in
g_{F}
, ensuring equivalence between the two models.

For other aggregations like
SUM
,
AVG
,
MIN
,
MAX
, the values are extracted from the variables. Since the
\Xi
function preserves the values of the variables, the set of values passed to the aggregation function is the same for both the flat and condensed models. For example, for each mapping
m_{F}\in g_{F}
, there’s a corresponding
m\in g_{C}
such that
m_{F}\in\Xi(m)
and the values of the relevant variables are the same.

This equivalence is formally supported by the principles of rewriting queries with aggregation functions using views, as established in the paper by Sarah Cohen
(
Cohen
et al. 2006
)
, which shows that aggregations over base data can be equivalently computed over pre-aggregated views.

Therefore, for any given group key, the aggregation results will be identical. Since this holds for all groups, the entire result of the aggregation operation is equivalent.

Aggregate join operator:
We must demonstrate that

eval_{F}(d_{F},AggJ_{F}(\mathcal{AE}_{n}))\cong eval_{C}(d_{C},\tau(AggJ_{F}(\mathcal{AE}_{n})))

This can be rewritten as:

(45)

\begin{split}&eval_{F}(d_{F},AggJ_{F}(Agg_{F1},\dots,Agg_{Fk}))\cong\\
&eval_{C}(d_{C},AggJ_{C}(\tau(Agg_{F1}),\dots,\tau(Agg_{Fk})))\end{split}

The
AggregateJoin
operator, as per Definitions
4.35
and
4.41
, combines the results from multiple aggregation operations based on their common group keys.
From the proof for the
Aggregation
operator above, we have established that the result of each flat aggregation
Agg_{Fi}
is equivalent to the result of its translated condensed counterpart
\tau(Agg_{Fi})=Agg_{Ci}
.
This means that for each aggregation, the set of
key\to val
pairs produced by the flat evaluation is identical to the set produced by the condensed evaluation.
Since both
AggJ_{F}(\mathcal{AE}_{n})
and
AggJ_{C}(\mathcal{AE}_{n})
operate on an identical set of input pairs and perform the same function of joining these pairs by their keys, their final output must also be equivalent.
Therefore, the theorem holds for the
Aggregate Join
operator.
∎

5.
Discussion

Our formalization provides a solid foundation for understanding and handling RDF versioning in a condensed representation. However, several theoretical limitations need to be addressed in future works.

5.1.
Benchmarking and performance evaluation

A thorough performance evaluation is essential to empirically validate the advantages of the condensed representation. An initial implementation of the condensed algebra, named
ConVerG
(Concurrent Versioning of knowledge Graphs), has been developed and described in
(
Gil
et al. 2024
, Puget Gil et al.)
, with the source code available on GitHub.

The architecture includes a SPARQL algebra translator that converts standard SPARQL queries into their condensed algebraic forms for execution over data stored in the condensed model. Key operators such as quad pattern, join, union, filter, and aggregation have been implemented and tested.

Future work should focus on comprehensive benchmarking to compare the performance of the condensed model against the traditional flat model. Important metrics include query execution time for various versioned queries, memory usage during data import and query processing, and storage requirements. Such benchmarks will help quantify the trade-offs of the condensed approach and identify scenarios where it provides the greatest benefits, thereby complementing the theoretical contributions with empirical evidence.

5.2.
RDF standards and expressiveness

5.2.1.
Integration with Existing RDF Standards

Our formalization has not been fully integrated with existing RDF standards and tools. This would involve aligning our versioning ontology with standards like PROV
(
W3C 2025
)
and implementing our model in popular RDF query engines.

5.2.2.
Handling of Variable Clashes

Our current formalization does not address variable clashes, which can lead to ambiguity and errors in query evaluation. A more robust approach would involve mechanisms to detect and resolve such clashes.

5.2.3.
Blank node handling

Our formalization does not address the handling of blank nodes, which are a common feature of RDF data. A more comprehensive model would need to include mechanisms for dealing with blank nodes in versioned data.

5.2.4.
Limited Expressiveness of the Versioning Model

Our model is based on a snapshot-based versioning approach, which may not be suitable for all use cases. For example, it cannot handle continuous changes or temporal intervals effectively.

5.3.
Solution Sequences and modifiers needs

Projection modifier has been considered in our formalization. However, other modifiers such as Offset, Limit, Order, Distinct, and Reduced are not yet supported. These modifiers are essential for many query scenarios and will be considered in future work.

5.4.
Condensed representation limitations

5.4.1.
Complexity of the Condensed Representation

While the condensed representation offers potential storage and query optimization benefits, it also introduces complexity in terms of model manipulation and query translation.

5.4.2.
Abstraction of the level of compression

Finite set of compression levels

Our formalization uses a finite set of compression levels. This means that we have defined a limited number of compression levels that our system can handle. While this approach is sufficient for many use cases, it has certain limitations.

A formalization that generalizes the typing of variables for any representation could be more advantageous. By allowing more flexible typing, we could support a wider range of compression levels, including intermediate levels or levels defined dynamically based on the specific needs of the application.

From a bounded total order to a bounded lattice

Our formalization uses a bounded total order to represent the variable representations. While this approach is sufficient for many use cases, it has certain limitations.

A formalization that uses a bounded lattice could be more advantageous. By allowing more flexible typing, we could support a wider range of compression levels, including intermediate levels or levels defined dynamically based on the specific needs of the application.
