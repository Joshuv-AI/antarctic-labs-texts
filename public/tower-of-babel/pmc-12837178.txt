Exogenous rutin ameliorates NaCl‑induced inhibition of Fagopyrum esculentum germination and early growth
Authors: Jia Yang, Luping Ma, Shuoyu Di, Zhanyu Wang, Muriel Quinet, Yu Meng, Jiadong He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil salinity is a pervasive threat to global agriculture, severely impairing the germination of salt-sensitive crops like common buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum). While natural flavonoids such as rutin are recognized for their potent antioxidant properties, their application as stress mitigators often relies on an incomplete mechanistic understanding. The literature currently lacks a systematic, quantitative assessment of how exogenous rutin orchestrates the interplay between antioxidant defenses, osmotic adjustment, and membrane stability to protect buckwheat during its most vulnerable germination stage, a gap that limits the development of effective, nature-based agricultural strategies. Here, we address this gap using a comprehensive physiological approach.ResultsSeeds from three buckwheat varieties were subjected to 200 mM NaCl stress with or without 1.5 mM rutin pretreatment. Rutin application dramatically reversed salt-induced germination inhibition, boosting the germination rate by up to 258%. Mechanistically, rutin significantly amplified the plant’s antioxidant enzyme machinery to effectively scavenge key reactive oxygen species like superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, while concurrently promoting the accumulation of protective osmolytes. This culminated in markedly reduced lipid peroxidation, measured as malondialdehyde content, and preserved cell membrane integrity.ConclusionsWe conclude that exogenous rutin operates not through a single mode of action, but by orchestrating a synergistic enhancement of the plant’s intrinsic antioxidant and osmotic defense systems. This integrated defense converges to maintain membrane stability, providing a robust mechanistic framework that validates rutin’s use as an effective biostimulant to enhance crop resilience in saline environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-08002-7.Keywords: Common buckwheat, Salt stress, Flavonoids, Oxidative stress, Osmotic adjustment, Seedling establishment

Introduction

Soil salinization is a pervasive abiotic stress threatening global food production and the sustainable development of agriculture [1]. The global area affected by salinity has been estimated at ~ 950 million hectares, representing roughly 10–20% of arable land [2, 3]. Salinity impairs seedling establishment primarily through osmotic stress and ion toxicity: elevated Na⁺/Cl− disrupt cellular ion homeostasis and inhibit key metabolic enzymes, while lowered soil water potential reduces root water uptake, inducing “physiological drought” [3]. Secondary oxidative stress further aggravates germination arrest, seedling vigor loss, and yield penalties, and can alter product quality traits in some crops [4].

Common buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum Moench) is valued for both ornamental appeal and nutritional uses, with applications in landscape design, leisure agriculture, and multifunctional farmlands. However, F. esculentum is particularly sensitive to salinity during germination and early growth. NaCl stress can depress germination percentage and inhibit radicle-plumule elongation, compromising stand establishment and landscape performance [5]. Thus, identifying low‑toxicity, plant‑derived molecules that promote buckwheat germination and early growth under salinity is of both scientific and practical interest.

Previous work indicates that exogenous osmolytes, signaling molecules, and seed‑priming approaches can enhance antioxidant defenses and help maintain ion/water homeostasis under abiotic stresses such as salinity, drought, and high temperature, thereby improving early-stage performance [4, 6, 7]. In buckwheat and related crops, for example, low‑dose hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) priming has been shown to alleviate NaCl toxicity, elevate photosynthetic and antioxidant enzyme activities, and reduce membrane lipid peroxidation [8]. Within this broader mitigation space, flavonoids are attractive candidates because of their well‑documented roles in reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, metal chelation, and membrane protection. Rutin (quercetin‑3‑O‑rutinoside; rutoside) is a widely distributed dietary flavonol glycoside [9] with robust antioxidant and metal‑chelating activities: it scavenges ROS, augments superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and chelates Fe2+ to dampen Fenton chemistry and lipid peroxidation [10–12]. In several non‑buckwheat systems, exogenous rutin has been reported to mitigate NaCl‑induced oxidative damage, partially restore germination and root elongation, and reduce H2O2 and superoxide (O2•−) accumulation [13–16]. These observations position rutin as a plausible, plant‑derived regulator for enhancing salt tolerance during the most vulnerable developmental window.

Despite the prominence of rutin in buckwheat tissues and its mechanistic plausibility, studies still lack a systematic evaluation of whether exogenous rutin confers additive protection, beyond endogenous defenses, during the germination and early seedling stages of F. esculentum under salinity. Specifically, there has been no integrated, quantitative assessment linking phenotypic rescue (germination kinetics, early growth) with coordinated shifts across the antioxidation-osmotic regulation-membrane stability module, nor a clear estimate of the relative contribution of each component to overall protection. This gap limits the translation of rutin into low‑toxicity, edible seed‑treatment strategies for salt‑affected settings and constrains its landscape/ornamental application potential.

Here, we address this gap by testing the hypothesis that exogenous rutin ameliorates NaCl‑induced inhibition of buckwheat germination and early growth through coordinated enhancement of antioxidant capacity, osmotic adjustment, and membrane stability. We (i) quantify germination performance and early growth under controlled salinity with and without rutin; (ii) measure oxidative damage markers and antioxidant enzyme activities to evaluate ROS detoxification; (iii) assess compatible solutes and related indices of osmotic regulation; and (iv) evaluate membrane integrity via lipid peroxidation and leakage‑based metrics. By coupling phenotype‑level readouts with mechanistic indicators, this study aims to provide (a) additional physiological evidence for salt‑stress responses in buckwheat, (b) a data basis for cost‑effective seed treatments (e.g., extraction‑based or chemical seed dressing) for ornamental and other salt‑sensitive crops in saline soils, and (c) quantitative guidance on the relative contributions and potential coordination of antioxidation, osmotic regulation, and membrane protection in rutin‑mediated salt‑stress alleviation.

Materials and methods

Plant material and reagents

Experiments were conducted in the College of Landscape and Tourism laboratory, Hebei Agricultural University. Three sweet buckwheat varieties were used: ‘kbtq’ (Kangbaotianqiao, a local landrace known for stress tolerance), ‘fhth’ (Fenghuangtehong, a cultivated variety), and ‘xn9976’ (an elite breeding line). Seeds were supplied by the Buckwheat Genetic Resources Innovation Research Group, Institute of Crop Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The NaCl and rutin (≥ 95% purity; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were used. Unless otherwise stated, all reagents were of analytical grade and solutions were prepared with deionized water.

Optimization of exogenous Rutin concentration and experimental treatments

Seeds of uniform size, with plump grains and intact embryos, were surface-sterilized by immersion in 95% ethanol followed by 3% hypochlorous acid for 10 min, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and air-dried under sterile conditions.

To identify an effective priming concentration, dried seeds were soaked for 14 h in aqueous rutin at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, or 2.5 mM. After priming, 30 seeds per treatment were placed in Petri dishes (five replicates per treatment) and incubated for 7 d in a light incubator (25 °C; light intensity, 150 µmol·m− 2·s− 1). Distilled water served as the blank control. Germination was recorded daily; germination potential (day 3) and germination rate (day 7) were calculated (Figure S1). The concentration that most enhanced germination (1.5 mM) was selected for subsequent experiments. Similarly, a preliminary NaCl gradient test was conducted to determine the optimal stress intensity. Based on the germination performance (Figure S1A-B), 200 mM NaCl was selected as it imposed significant salt stress (approximating a moderate salinity level) while maintaining sufficient seed viability for potential rescue.

Four treatments were then established: water immersion + 0 mM rutin (CK); water immersion + 1.5 mM rutin (rutin); water immersion + 200 mM NaCl (NaCl); 1.5 mM rutin + 200 mM NaCl (rutin + NaCl).

On days 2, 4, and 6 after the start of germination, ~ 0.5 g of whole seedlings (including roots, hypocotyls, and cotyledons; excluding ungerminated seeds) per treatment was sampled and stored at −80 °C for physiological and biochemical analyses, including relative electrical conductivity (REC), antioxidant enzyme activities, malondialdehyde (MDA), and H2O2 content.

Reaction mixtures contained 1.5 mL 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.3 mL methionine, 0.3 mL NBT, 0.3 mL EDTA-Na2, 0.25 mL distilled water, 0.05 mL enzyme extract, and 0.3 mL riboflavin. Control tubes substituted buffer for the enzyme extract; one control remained in the dark. Tubes were illuminated at ~ 4000 lx (25 °C; incubator light level 6) for 20 min, then immediately covered to terminate the reaction. Absorbance at 560 nm was recorded using the dark control as blank. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing 50% inhibition of NBT reduction under assay conditions.

Results

Effects of exogenous Rutin on seed germination

Effects on germination rate and germination potential

As shown in Fig. 1 A-B, exogenous rutin treatment did not significantly affect the germination rate of seeds from the three sweet buckwheat varieties (‘kbtq’, ‘fhth’, and ‘xn9976’) relative to the control (CK). Numerical increases in germination rate were observed, amounting to 5%, 1%, and 5% for ‘kbtq’, ‘fhth’, and ‘xn9976’, respectively.

NaCl treatment significantly decreased the germination rate and germination potential compared to CK in all varieties. The reductions were most pronounced in ‘xn9976’, with decreases of 78% in germination rate and 81% in germination potential.

Compared to NaCl treatment alone, rutin + NaCl treatment significantly increased the germination rate and germination potential in ‘kbtq’, ‘fhth’, and ‘xn9976’ by 77%, 32%, and 258%, respectively. Fresh weight under rutin + NaCl also increased significantly by 186%, 100%, and 433% relative to NaCl alone.

Effects on root length and fresh weight during germination

As presented in Table S1, exogenous rutin treatment significantly increased root length during germination in ‘kbtq’ and ‘fhth’ by 10% and 6%, respectively, compared to CK. In ‘xn9976’, root length increased numerically by 3% but not significantly.

NaCl treatment significantly reduced root length and fresh weight relative to CK across all varieties. The largest reductions occurred in ‘kbtq’, with decreases of 97% in root length and 76% in fresh weight.

Relative to NaCl alone, rutin + NaCl treatment significantly increased root length in ‘kbtq’, ‘fhth’, and ‘xn9976’ by 404%, 357%, and 371%, respectively. Fresh weight increased significantly by 62%, 35%, and 28%, respectively.

Discussion

Salt stress poses a multifaceted challenge to sweet buckwheat seed germination, as evidenced by the pronounced reductions in germination rate, germination potential, root length, and fresh weight observed under NaCl treatment across the three varieties (‘kbtq’, ‘fhth’, and ‘xn9976’). These phenotypic impairments align with the hypothesis that salinity inhibits early growth through coordinated disruptions in cellular homeostasis, including oxidative damage, osmotic imbalance, and membrane instability [34]. Exogenous rutin pretreatment effectively mitigated these effects, restoring germination parameters with notable increases in germination rate (up to 258% in ‘xn9976’ relative to NaCl alone), germination potential, root length (up to 404% in ‘kbtq’), and fresh weight (up to 433% in ‘xn9976’). This alleviation supports the proposed mechanism wherein rutin enhances salt tolerance by bolstering antioxidant capacity, promoting osmotic adjustment, and preserving membrane integrity.

The narrative begins at the cellular level, where NaCl-induced osmotic stress and ion toxicity trigger excessive ROS production, including O2•− and H2O2 [1, 35]. This ROS surge initiates a cascade of oxidative damage, manifesting as heightened lipid peroxidation (elevated MDA content, up to 56.90% higher than control in ‘fhth’ on day 2) and compromised membrane permeability (increased REC) [9, 36]. Such damage directly correlates with the observed germination inhibition, as PCA revealed strong positive loadings of H2O2 (0.385) and MDA (0.328) on PC1, which accounted for 50.84% of variance. Correlation analysis further substantiated this link, showing robust positive associations between MDA, O2•−, H2O2, and REC (r = 0.82, 0.63, and 0.81, respectively), underscoring how unchecked ROS accumulation exacerbates membrane disruption.

In response, sweet buckwheat seeds activated endogenous defense mechanisms, with NaCl treatment inducing elevations in antioxidant enzyme activities, SOD, POD, CAT, APX, and DHAR, an adaptive response commonly observed in plants under salinity [37]. However, these intrinsic responses were insufficient. Exogenous rutin amplified this antioxidant machinery, yielding further increases in enzyme activities (e.g., SOD with additional boosts of 25.82% under rutin + NaCl relative to NaCl in ‘xn9976’). This enhancement extended to the ascorbate-glutathione cycle, with rutin elevating APX (up to 78.03% in ‘fhth’ on day 4) and DHAR (up to 80.54% in ‘xn9976’ on day 2). The resulting reductions in O2•− (up to 92.42% in ‘fhth’ on day 2 under rutin) and H2O2 (up to 38.33% under rutin + NaCl relative to NaCl) directly curbed lipid peroxidation. This coordinated antioxidant response echoes findings in other crops, where rutin was shown to significantly improve germination in onion [16] and enhance antioxidant gene expression in maize [38]. It is also worth noting that rutin, as a flavonoid compound, possesses strong intrinsic non-enzymatic antioxidant capacity due to its phenolic hydroxyl groups. Therefore, the mitigation of oxidative stress observed in this study likely results from a dual mechanism: the direct scavenging of ROS by exogenous rutin and the rutin-induced upregulation of the endogenous antioxidant enzyme system. This synergistic effect contributes to maintaining cellular redox homeostasis under salt stress. PLS-SEM delineated the pathway: antioxidant system I (dominated by SOD, loading 0.943) generates H2O2 intermediates, but system II (CAT-dominated, loading 0.954) clears them effectively (negative path coefficient between systems I and II), reducing membrane damage indicators (negative path from system II to membrane stability, −0.793, R2 = 0.68).

Building on this ROS mitigation, rutin concurrently fortified osmotic adjustment, addressing the “physiological drought” imposed by salinity. NaCl alone prompted accumulations of Pro, Sp, and Ss, a key self-protection strategy [39]. Exogenous rutin augmented these levels further, with significant increases in Pro (up to 47.23% under rutin + NaCl relative to NaCl in ‘kbtq’ on day 4), Sp (up to 25.32% in ‘xn9976’ on day 4), and Ss (up to 57.54% in ‘xn9976’ on day 4). PCA confirmed the centrality of these solutes, with negative loadings of Pro (−0.318) and Sp (−0.320) on PC1 (opposing ROS markers) and positive contributions to PC2 (Ss 0.361, Pro 0.340), which explained 24.05% of variance. Correlation analysis revealed a strong synergy between Sp and Pro (r = 0.96), suggesting rutin activates shared biosynthetic pathways [40]. Grey relational analysis ranked Pro and Ss as the most comprehensive indicators of rutin’s efficacy. In PLS-SEM, the osmotic regulation module (Pro loading 0.986) exhibited a strong negative path from antioxidant system I (−0.851, R2 = 0.73), indicating a potential early prioritization of ROS scavenging, yet rutin’s dual action ensures both systems operate synergistically.

The interplay between these mechanisms culminates in preserved membrane stability. By curbing ROS and bolstering osmoprotectants, rutin reduced MDA and REC (e.g., up to 32.44% MDA decrease in ‘fhth’ on day 2 under rutin + NaCl). PLS-SEM quantified this integration: while antioxidant system I weakly exacerbated membrane damage (positive path 0.166, P<0.05), likely via transient H2O2 buildup, system II’s potent clearance (H2O2 loading 0.942 in membrane stability, R2 = 0.68) and osmotic support negated it, yielding net protection. Correlation analysis reinforced this, with CAT negatively correlated to H2O2, O2•−, MDA, and REC (r = −0.77 to −0.87), illustrating how rutin’s enhancements propagate through these pathways to restore growth [14].

Varietal differences weave into this story as nuances in baseline tolerance and responsiveness. ‘xn9976’ exhibited the most severe salt-induced declines (e.g., 78% germination rate reduction) but the greatest rutin-mediated recovery (258% increase), suggesting weaker inherent defenses. In contrast, ‘kbtq’ showed pronounced root length sensitivity (97% reduction) yet strong responses to rutin. Membership function analysis captured this: ‘kbtq’ had the highest comprehensive salt tolerance under NaCl (0.848), whereas ‘xn9976’ improved from a low baseline to 0.33, narrowing inter-varietal gaps. This suggests rutin can compensate for deficiencies in the defense systems of more sensitive varieties [41], reflecting genotype-specific responses to stress and ameliorative treatments. While this study provides robust physiological evidence that rutin maintains membrane integrity, indicated by reduced lipid peroxidation (MDA) and electrolyte leakage (REC), we acknowledge certain limitations regarding the biophysical mechanism. Specifically, the direct interactions between rutin and the plasma membrane lipid bilayer were not characterized. The mechanical properties of the plasmalemma, such as fluidity and flexibility (which can be analyzed via techniques like Langmuir monolayers), are critical for protein function and stress adaptation. Furthermore, while we observed a significant upregulation of total SOD activity, our current data do not distinguish the specific contributions of different SOD isoforms (e.g., Cu/Zn-SOD and Mn-SOD). Future investigations incorporating membrane biophysics and isoform-specific enzyme analyses are needed to further elucidate the precise molecular targets of rutin. This integrated framework elucidates how rutin’s multifaceted actions counteract salinity’s inhibitory cascade, providing quantitative insights into their synergies in sweet buckwheat.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that exogenous rutin effectively alleviates salt stress during the germination of sweet buckwheat seeds. The primary mechanism involves the orchestrated enhancement of the plant’s antioxidant defense system and its capacity for osmotic adjustment. rutin application amplified the activity of key antioxidant enzymes, which mitigated ROS accumulation and subsequent oxidative damage. Concurrently, it promoted the accumulation of compatible solutes, thereby maintaining cellular water potential. These dual physiological improvements converged to preserve the structural and functional integrity of cell membranes, a critical determinant for successful germination. Our findings provide a quantitative, mechanistic framework for rutin’s protective role and highlight its potential as a natural, effective bio-stimulant for enhancing crop resilience in saline agricultural systems (Fig. 8).

A proposed model depicting the mechanism by which exogenous rutin confers salinity tolerance in germinating sweet buckwheat seeds. Under salinity stress, the combination of osmotic imbalance and ion toxicity triggers an excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as superoxide radicals (O2•−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), leading to oxidative stress. This condition inflicts severe cellular damage, including lipid peroxidation (indicated by increased malondialdehyde, MDA) and loss of membrane integrity (measured as elevated relative electrical conductivity, REC), which ultimately culminates in the inhibition of seed germination and seedling growth. Conversely, the application of exogenous rutin orchestrates a synergistic defense response to counteract these adverse effects. Rutin enhances the plant's antioxidant capacity by upregulating the activities of key ROS-scavenging enzymes, including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and enzymes of the ascorbate-glutathione cycle (APX, DHAR). Concurrently, rutin promotes osmotic adjustment by increasing the biosynthesis and accumulation of compatible solutes, such as proline (Pro), soluble proteins (Sp), and soluble sugars (Ss). The integrated action of these enhanced antioxidant and osmotic regulation systems effectively scavenges ROS, preserves membrane stability, and maintains cellular homeostasis. This comprehensive protective mechanism alleviates the inhibitory effects of salt stress, thereby promoting successful seed germination and seedling establishment
