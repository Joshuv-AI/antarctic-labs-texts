Topology-preserving, diameter-specific framework for conjunctival vessel segmentation and tortuosity analysis in diabetes
Authors: Asma Mohamed Naim, Achintha Iroshan Kondarage, Rukshani Liyanaarachchi, Saroj Jayasinghe, Anjula C. De Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetes is associated with progressive microvascular remodelling, commonly assessed using retinal imaging, yet alternative non-invasive vascular biomarkers remain underexplored. Conjunctival vessels are directly visible and may reflect systemic microvascular changes; however, reliable quantification is challenged by the need to preserve vascular topology during segmentation. In this work, we introduce a curated conjunctival imaging dataset with diameter-specific vessel annotations and demonstrate that topology-preserving vessel extraction is essential for robust tortuosity estimation. We develop a deep learning framework that prioritises vascular continuity and geometric fidelity while selectively segmenting clinically relevant vessel calibres, integrating multi-scale contextual modelling and attention-guided feature fusion to reduce fragmentation without post-processing. Compared with multiple state-of-the-art segmentation methods, the proposed approach achieves substantially lower tortuosity error and centreline deviation, even when pixel-wise accuracy is comparable. Applied to conjunctival images from individuals with diabetes and healthy controls, mean conjunctival vessel tortuosity was significantly elevated in the diabetic group, consistent with established retinal microvascular findings. Together, these results support conjunctival imaging as a non-invasive modality for microvascular phenotyping and suggest its potential utility for scalable, external-eye–based vascular assessment in diabetes research contexts.

Introduction

Early detection of chronic vascular diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and arteriosclerosis is critical to preventing serious complications. According to global estimates1, over 460 million individuals were living with diabetes in 2019, a number expected to exceed 700 million by 2045. Alarmingly, nearly half of it remain undiagnosed. This underscores the urgent need for scalable, screening approaches capable of identifying microvascular dysfunction earlier than traditional biomarkers like blood glucose, which often rise only after disease progression.

Structural abnormalities in blood vessels, particularly increased tortuosity and diameter changes, are among the earliest signs of systemic vascular disease1–4. Importantly, many of these conditions manifest preferentially in vessels within specific diameter ranges, underscoring the clinical value of tools that can extract and analyse diameter-specific vasculature. Developing methods to capture such fine-grained vascular features could support early diagnosis, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring across diverse systemic and genetic disorders.

A particularly well-studied vascular marker is vessel tortuosity, which has been strongly associated with diabetes and other systemic arteriopathies5–7. Sasongko et al. demonstrated that individuals with diabetes exhibit greater retinal tortuosity than healthy controls5, and longitudinal studies have confirmed that tortuosity increases over time in patients who develop diabetic retinopathy8. Despite its diagnostic relevance, routine tortuosity assessment remains impractical due to the need for manual segmentation and subjective grading in retinal images, a process that is time-consuming and increasingly unsustainable given the growing volume of high-resolution ocular imaging data. Moreover, while retinal fundus imaging is widely used for microvascular assessment, its deployment is constrained by high equipment costs and dependence on trained personnel.

In contrast, the bulbar conjunctiva, a transparent, densely vascularised membrane overlying the sclera, offers a more accessible and non-invasive alternative for vascular imaging. Its vascular supply is closely linked to that of the retina, choroid, and optic nerve9, and prior studies have shown that conjunctival microvascular alterations are associated with both retinal and systemic microvascular changes10,11. Quantitative analysis of conjunctival vasculature has demonstrated diagnostic potential in diabetes and diabetic retinopathy, and automated image analysis methods have outperformed manual approaches in these settings12,13.

While deep learning has significantly advanced vascular segmentation, most existing methods prioritise pixel-wise accuracy metrics when evaluating model performance14–16. However, high pixel-level accuracy does not necessarily guarantee preservation of vascular topology, continuity, or curvature, properties that are critical for downstream analyses. Segmentation outputs that break vessels into disconnected fragments or distort their geometric structure can lead to substantial errors in tortuosity calculation, even when conventional segmentation metrics appear favourable.

Most existing approaches extract the full vasculature without explicit consideration of vessel diameter, despite evidence that disease-related changes are often diameter-dependent2,12. Conventional preprocessing pipelines that attempt to isolate vessel subsets often rely on multi-scale vesselness filtering, morphological operations, or post hoc radius estimation, increasing susceptibility to parameter sensitivity and limiting scalability17,18. These approaches are sensitive to parameter selection, image quality, and noise, and do not explicitly preserve vessel topology or isolate vessels by diameter. As a result, they are prone to fragmentation and limited scalability when applied to large datasets or topology-dependent analyses.

To address these challenges, we propose a deep learning framework designed not only to segment conjunctival vessels, but to preserve their topological integrity within clinically relevant diameter ranges. Our architecture integrates dense dilated convolutional blocks to enhance multi-scale feature representation with transformer-based attention mechanisms that promote global contextual awareness and selective feature reconstruction. Multi-Head Self-Attention captures long-range spatial dependencies essential for maintaining vessel continuity, while Multi-Head Cross-Attention in the decoder guides the reconstruction of coherent, diameter-specific vascular structures.

By emphasising topology-preserving, diameter-specific vessel segmentation, the proposed method enables more reliable estimation of vascular tortuosity without requiring additional topology reconstruction algorithms. Leveraging the accessibility of conjunctival imaging, this approach has the potential to support non-invasive vascular assessment and longitudinal monitoring in research settings, while remaining compatible with scalable data acquisition pipelines.

Literature review

Automated extraction of retinal and conjunctival vasculature

Most automated vessel extraction research has focused on retinal rather than conjunctival vasculature. Early retinal vessel segmentation methods primarily relied on hand-crafted features19–23,27. Unsupervised clustering approaches were also employed to extract retinal vessels from pre-processed images24–26.

While these classical approaches can identify vessel-like patterns, they are highly sensitive to noise, illumination variation, and contrast heterogeneity, and frequently produce fragmented or discontinuous vessel representations. Such limitations restrict their suitability for topology-dependent measurements, including vessel tortuosity, which require faithful preservation of vascular continuity and curvature.

A review by Badar et al. evaluated a range of supervised and unsupervised deep learning approaches for retinal vessel segmentation, highlighting the growing reliance on convolutional neural networks (CNNs)28. These methods are typically assessed using pixel-wise metrics29 which quantify segmentation overlap and do not explicitly capture vascular topology, continuity, or geometric fidelity.

More recently, research attention has shifted toward conjunctival vasculature, exploring its potential role in diagnosing systemic diseases such as diabetes. Early conjunctival vessel extraction methods employed Gaussian derivatives to detect vessel edges and identify linear vascular structures of interest30,31. Other studies applied colour clustering and texture-based features to segment conjunctival vessels32,33. However, most conjunctival vessel studies prioritise vessel visibility or density estimation rather than preserving vascular geometry for quantitative morphometric biomarkers.

Recent advances in deep learning have improved vessel segmentation, with CNN-based architectures achieving strong performance in retinal imaging34–36. However, models optimised for pixel-wise overlap often produce fragmented vessels, spurious branches, or curvature distortions, particularly in thin or low-contrast regions. Consequently, many studies still rely on post hoc topology reconstruction methods to restore vascular continuity prior to morphometric analysis37–42.

While effective, these additional processing steps increase pipeline complexity and introduce heuristic assumptions that may bias topology-dependent biomarkers, highlighting the need for segmentation approaches that preserve vascular continuity and geometry directly at the model level.

Research gap

A major challenge in conjunctival vascular analysis is not only the selective extraction of vessels by diameter, but also the preservation of their topological integrity. In diabetes, tortuosity changes are often subtle and vessel-diameter dependent, requiring accurate preservation of continuity, curvature, and branching structure31. However, most existing approaches rely on manual segmentation or full vasculature extraction with heuristic filtering, increasing the risk of fragmentation and geometric distortion40.

Segmenting the entire vasculature without accounting for vessel diameter further limits analysis of biomechanical behaviour and haemodynamics, particularly in regions with overlapping vessels. Additionally, errors that minimally affect pixel-wise metrics can substantially bias tortuosity estimation, as small discontinuities or curvature changes along centrelines lead to unreliable measurements. Despite this, topology preservation is rarely a primary objective in existing methods.

To our knowledge, no approach enables automated, diameter-specific conjunctival vessel segmentation while explicitly preserving topology for reliable tortuosity analysis. To address this gap, we propose a topology-preserving, diameter-specific deep learning framework, described in the following sections.

Methods

Network architecture

Figure 1 presents a hybrid encoder–decoder architecture designed for topology-preserving, diameter-specific segmentation of conjunctival vessels. Rather than optimising pixel-wise classification alone, the network is explicitly structured to preserve vessel continuity, curvature, and spatial coherence, which are critical for reliable computation of topology-dependent biomarkers such as tortuosity.

The architecture extends a conventional U-Net-style encoder–decoder backbone by integrating dilated convolutions and attention mechanisms in a hybrid design43,44. While encoder–decoder networks capture local spatial detail effectively, they often struggle to maintain long-range continuity in thin or fragmented vascular structures. To address this limitation, the proposed design combines the local representational strength of convolutional layers with global contextual reasoning enabled by attention.Fig. 1Overview of the proposed network architecture. To model long-range spatial dependencies, a Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) module is placed at the bottleneck of the network. This targeted placement enables global reasoning over spatially distant but structurally related vessel segments, supporting continuity in thin, tortuous, or partially occluded vessels. Unlike fully transformer-based encoders45,46, selective application of self-attention balances contextual modelling with computational efficiency and spatial interpretability.

Feature fusion between encoder and decoder is performed using a Multi-Head Cross-Attention (MHCA) module, replacing conventional skip connections. In contrast to direct concatenation or scalar attention gates47, MHCA allows the decoder to selectively retrieve spatially and semantically relevant encoder features via query–key–value attention. This decoder-driven selection is particularly advantageous for diameter-specific segmentation, where overlapping vessels of different diameters are common.

Positional encoding is incorporated prior to attention operations to preserve explicit spatial context during global feature aggregation. This ensures that attention-based interactions do not compromise spatial coherence, which is essential for maintaining vessel curvature and connectivity.

By jointly integrating multi-scale convolutional context, global self-attention, selective cross-attention-based fusion, and positional encoding, the proposed architecture reduces vessel fragmentation and preserves geometric consistency across conjunctival vasculature. As a result, the generated segmentations are not only accurate in terms of overlap, but also topologically consistent, enabling reliable centreline extraction and curvature-based analysis without additional post-processing.

Dataset

Publicly available vascular imaging datasets predominantly focus on retinal fundus images and typically provide binary vessel annotations without vessel diameter information. At present, no publicly available dataset provides diameter-specific annotations of conjunctival vasculature in subjects with diabetes and related systemic complications. This limitation restricts investigations into vascular topology and diameter-dependent biomarkers, such as tortuosity, which are known to be sensitive to vessel calibre.

To address this gap, we curated a custom dataset comprising conjunctival images from both healthy individuals and subjects with diabetes, with paired conjunctival and retinal fundus images collected for diabetic subjects. The dataset was specifically designed to support diameter-specific vascular analysis and topology-preserving segmentation, with particular emphasis on tortuosity-based biomarkers relevant to diabetic microvascular disease.

The dataset includes images from subjects with diabetes, and healthy controls. For each subject with diabetes, eight conjunctival images and two retinal fundus images were collected, while healthy controls contributed eight conjunctival images each. Diabetic subjects were further categorised into clinically distinct subgroups (see Participants and Subject Groups subsection) to enable comparative analysis across disease phenotypes and complication status.

The annotated subset represents a small portion of the full image cohort and was randomly selected to capture clinically relevant vascular variations while keeping the annotation effort manageable. These annotations are intended primarily for supervised training and benchmarking of segmentation models.

Because multiple images were acquired per participant (eight conjunctival images per subject capturing both eyes and four gaze directions), the dataset contains several image-level observations originating from the same individual. Accordingly, segmentation model development and benchmarking were performed at the image level, whereas clinical subgroup analyses were conducted at the subject level by aggregating measurements per participant to avoid statistical dependence.

Imaging equipment and acquisition protocol

Conjunctival images were acquired using a Nikon D7200 digital camera under standardised imaging conditions. Participants were seated in Fowler’s position, and camera placement was controlled to ensure consistent magnification and reliable vessel diameter estimation across subjects. The camera-to-eye distance was maintained at approximately 150 mm for all acquisitions.

Uniform illumination and fixation targets were used to minimise motion artefacts and inter-image variability. Eight conjunctival images were captured per subject, comprising four images from each eye with gaze directed in the four cardinal directions to maximise coverage of the bulbar conjunctiva (Fig. 3).

Retinal fundus images (Fig. 4) were acquired separately by a trained medical officer using a standard fundus camera. Blood glucose levels were verified by trained clinical staff to confirm eligibility for inclusion in the diabetic cohort.

Conjunctival image preparation for network training

In addition to the publicly available annotations, a further 5 conjunctival images from healthy controls were manually annotated using the same protocol and included for network training. This resulted in a total of 35 annotated conjunctival images used for model training.

Prior to annotation, images underwent brightness and contrast enhancement to improve vascular visibility. Vessel annotation was performed manually using Fiji (Fiji Is Just ImageJ, version 2.17.0), an open-source distribution of ImageJ, available at https://imagej.net/software/fiji/51. Following established criteria for conjunctival vessel analysis30, only vessels with a diameter greater than or equal to 40 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}m were annotated. This selective annotation strategy was adopted to focus the network on clinically relevant vessel diameters associated with diabetic microvascular changes in tortuosity. During annotation, challenges related to subjective assessment of vessel diameter were mitigated by applying a consistent diameter threshold across all images. Given that vessel diameter naturally tapers along branching structures, this threshold was applied conservatively to maintain annotation consistency and topological continuity.

To preserve the geometric integrity of vessel morphology, no geometry-distorting data augmentation (such as scaling, shearing, or elastic deformation) was applied during training. However, orientation-preserving augmentations, including rotation and horizontal and vertical flipping, were employed to improve model robustness without altering vessel thickness, curvature, or topology. This decision avoided artificial distortions of vessel thickness or curvature that could bias diameter-specific learning and downstream tortuosity estimation.

Training strategy

Due to the high spatial resolution of conjunctival images and hardware memory constraints, network training was performed using image patches rather than full-resolution images. Each annotated conjunctival image was divided into overlapping patches of size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$512 \times 512$$\end{document} pixels, with a fixed overlap (stride of 482 pixels) to reduce boundary artefacts. During inference, overlapping patch predictions were reassembled into full-resolution probability maps by averaging predictions in overlapping regions prior to thresholding.

To minimise the influence of background-only regions during training, a subset of patches containing visible vascular structures was selected from the full set of extracted patches. This resulted in approximately 180 training and validation patches derived from the annotated images.

To improve robustness given the limited dataset size, a five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. The annotated images were partitioned into five folds at the image level. In each iteration, one fold was held out for testing, while the remaining four folds were used for model development. Within these four folds, the data were further divided into training and validation sets using a 4:1 ratio. This procedure was applied consistently across all evaluated networks. Final performance metrics were computed as the mean and standard deviation across all folds.

The network was implemented in PyTorch and trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-3}$$\end{document} and a weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-5}$$\end{document}. A cosine annealing learning rate scheduler was applied, with the learning rate decaying toward a minimum value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-6}$$\end{document}. The model was trained for 10 epochs with a batch size of 4. Early stopping was not employed due to the limited size of the annotated dataset; instead, a fixed training duration was used to minimise bias introduced by validation noise and to ensure consistent and reproducible optimisation across all comparative experiments.

A combined Dice and binary cross-entropy (Dice–BCE) loss function was used to balance pixel-wise classification with region-overlap optimisation, which is beneficial under severe foreground–background imbalance and for thin vascular structures. No class weighting or focal loss terms were applied.

All models were trained from random initialisation. Preliminary development and feasibility experiments were conducted using Google Colab to refine architectural and training choices. All final model training, ablation studies, state-of-the-art comparisons, and computational performance analyses—including FLOPs, inference time, GPU memory usage, and model size—were conducted in a controlled Kaggle environment using a single NVIDIA Tesla P100 GPU. This ensured fair and consistent comparison across all models and experimental settings.

Retinal vessel extraction for comparative analysis

Retinal fundus images were used as a reference modality for comparative tortuosity analysis rather than for training the proposed conjunctival segmentation network. Vessel extraction from retinal images was performed using a pre-trained U-Net architecture, given that retinal vessel segmentation is a well-established task with widely available annotated datasets.

The network was fine-tuned using the publicly available Digital Retinal Images for Vessel Extraction (DRIVE) dataset, which provides expert-annotated ground truth vessel labels52. The trained model was then applied to the retinal fundus images collected in this study to obtain vessel maps suitable for downstream analysis.

Post-processing of the segmented retinal vessels was performed in MATLAB to remove minor artifacts and enhance vessel continuity prior to tortuosity computation53–55. Specifically, morphological operations including opening, dilation, closing, and skeletonisation were applied to suppress noise, remove isolated pixels, and reconnect fragmented vessel segments prior to centreline extraction and quantitative analysis. Such post-processing steps are commonly used following retinal vessel segmentation to refine the binary vessel maps56. The resulting retinal tortuosity measurements were subsequently used as a reference benchmark for comparison with conjunctival tortuosity trends across participant groups.

Tortuosity calculations and evaluation metrics

For clinical comparisons, conjunctival vessel tortuosity was computed per subject by averaging measurements across the eight conjunctival images acquired per participant. Group-wise comparisons were performed between healthy controls and participants with diabetes, as well as across the three diabetic subgroups mentioned in Participants and Subject Groups subsection, using the statistical procedures described in the Statistical Analysis subsection.

Model performance was first assessed using conventional pixel-wise segmentation metrics, including Dice coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), precision, and recall, to provide a baseline measure of segmentation accuracy. As these metrics do not capture vessel continuity or geometric fidelity, additional topology-sensitive measures were employed to assess the model’s ability to preserve vascular topology and spatial coherence.

Vessel tortuosity was quantified using the Arc-over-Chord ratio57, computed on skeletonised vessel centrelines following removal of spurious loops and short isolated components. Tortuosity was aggregated at the image level by averaging across valid vessel segments exceeding a minimum length threshold, and subsequently averaged across images to obtain subject-level conjunctival and retinal tortuosity measures58. All skeletonisation and post-processing steps were applied identically across methods to ensure fair comparison.

To explicitly quantify geometric deviation from ground truth, two complementary topology-sensitive metrics were used: tortuosity error (TE) and centreline distance (CD). Tortuosity error was defined as the absolute difference between predicted and ground-truth mean image-level tortuosity, which avoids bias arising from vessel fragmentation. Images with no valid vessels in either prediction or ground truth were excluded from TE computation.

Centreline distance (CD) was computed as the symmetric mean Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth skeleton pixels, penalising both missing and spurious vessel predictions while remaining independent of vessel thickness. Cases in which vessels were present in only one of the two segmentations were retained, appropriately reflecting false positive or false negative detections.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{CD}=\frac{1}{2}\left( \frac{1}{|\mathcal {C}_{\text {pred}}|}\sum _{p \in \mathcal {C}_{\text {pred}}}\min _{g \in \mathcal {C}_{\text {gt}}}\Vert p - g \Vert _2\;+\;\frac{1}{|\mathcal {C}_{\text {gt}}|}\sum _{g \in \mathcal {C}_{\text {gt}}}\min _{p \in \mathcal {C}_{\text {pred}}} \Vert g - p \Vert _2\right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted separately for method-level evaluation of segmentation networks and clinical comparisons.

Model-level evaluation: For evaluation of segmentation methods, topology-sensitive metrics, including vessel tortuosity, tortuosity error, and centreline distance, were computed at the image level, yielding one aggregated value per image per network. This per-image aggregation avoids pseudo-replication arising from variable vessel counts and fragmentation across methods and ensures fair, paired comparisons.

Paired statistical comparisons between each segmentation method and the ground truth were performed using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test, reflecting the paired nature of the data and the absence of normality assumptions. Effect sizes were reported using the standardised statistic r, derived from the normal approximation of the signed-rank statistic.

To emphasise estimation uncertainty and robustness, 95% confidence intervals for mean differences were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 2000 iterations. Given the limited sample size, interpretation focused primarily on effect magnitude and confidence intervals rather than reliance on p-values alone, providing a more informative assessment of practical differences in topology preservation across segmentation methods.

Clinical comparisons: For clinical analysis, conjunctival and retinal vessel tortuosity were compared between healthy participants and those with diabetes, as well as across diabetic subgroups (diabetes only, diabetic retinopathy, and diabetes-related complications). Normality of tortuosity measurements was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As tortuosity values from diabetic participants violated assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity required for parametric testing, non-parametric statistical methods were employed throughout.

Differences between healthy controls and participants with diabetes were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences among diabetic subgroups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction.

As age can influence vascular morphology, we assessed its association with tortuosity using Spearman’s rank correlation. No significant association was observed; therefore, age was not adjusted for in subsequent analyses.

Most architectures reached stable validation performance within the first 8–10 epochs, indicating that the 10-epoch training schedule used in this study was sufficient for convergence under the unified training protocol. The proposed network exhibited particularly rapid stabilisation, with validation loss plateauing around epochs 5–6.

In contrast, TDCAU-Net continued to exhibit gradually decreasing validation loss beyond epoch 10, suggesting slower optimisation dynamics for this architecture. Importantly, the majority of baseline networks had already reached stable validation performance within the original training budget, indicating that the performance differences reported in Tables 1 and 6 are unlikely to arise from insufficient training of competing models but instead reflect architectural differences in feature representation and topology preservation.

Pixel-wise segmentation performance

Conventional pixel-wise metrics were first used to provide a baseline assessment of segmentation accuracy. For comparison against state-of-the-art methods, evaluation was performed using five-fold cross-validation across all annotated images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 35$$\end{document}). In each fold, four subsets were used for training and validation, while the remaining subset was used for testing. Performance metrics are reported as mean ± standard deviation across all folds.

For controlled architectural comparison, ablation experiments were conducted using a fixed held-out test set (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 10$$\end{document}), ensuring consistent evaluation conditions across all model variants. Unless otherwise stated, results in Table 1 correspond to cross-validation, while Tables 2, 3, 4, and 5 report fixed test set performance.

The proposed method achieved the highest Dice coefficient (0.759 ± 0.028) and Intersection over Union (IoU = 0.613 ± 0.033), indicating strong overall overlap with ground-truth annotations. Several competing architectures achieved high specificity or precision but exhibited reduced recall, reflecting conservative predictions and incomplete vessel extraction. Such behaviour is undesirable for vascular analysis tasks, where continuity and completeness of fine vessels are critical.

Pixel-wise ablation results (Tables 2, 3 and 4) indicate modest sensitivity to attention configuration and architectural design choices. Moderate attention capacity, implemented using four-head multi-head self-attention, provided the most favourable balance between precision and recall.

To further examine whether topology-aware loss functions could improve segmentation performance, we conducted an additional ablation experiment using the recently proposed clDice loss62, which explicitly encourages connectivity preservation by maximising centreline overlap between predicted and ground-truth vessel skeletons. The proposed architecture was retrained using a combined Dice–clDice62 loss while keeping all other training settings identical. The resulting pixel-wise segmentation metrics are summarised in Table 5.

Interestingly, the topology-aware Dice–clDice loss did not improve pixel-wise segmentation performance. Although recall was slightly higher for the Dice–clDice configuration, overall overlap-based metrics were consistently lower than those obtained using the Dice–BCE loss. These results suggest that the architectural design of the proposed network already captures the structural continuity of conjunctival vessels effectively, reducing reliance on explicit topology-preserving loss functions.

Progressive architectural ablation shows that while each component contributes incremental improvement in overlap-based metrics, the full architecture consistently achieved the strongest performance. Topology preservation and tortuosity estimation

To evaluate suitability for topology-dependent analysis, all methods were assessed using mean vessel tortuosity, tortuosity error (TE), and centreline distance (CD) relative to ground truth. These metrics quantify preservation of vessel continuity, curvature, and geometric alignment, which are not captured by pixel-wise measures.

For robust comparison against state-of-the-art segmentation networks, evaluation was performed using five-fold cross-validation across all annotated images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 35$$\end{document}). In each fold, four subsets were used for training and validation, while the remaining subset was used for testing. Performance metrics were aggregated across all folds and reported as mean ± standard deviation, with 95% confidence intervals computed via bootstrapping.

For controlled architectural comparison, ablation experiments were conducted using a fixed held-out test set (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 10$$\end{document}), ensuring consistent evaluation across all model variants.

Discussion

The primary contribution of our work is the demonstration that topology-preserving, diameter-specific vessel extraction is essential for reliable conjunctival tortuosity estimation, and that conventional pixel-optimised segmentation networks are insufficient for this task.

Importantly, this study involves two distinct levels of analysis: (i) image-level segmentation performance evaluated on manually annotated data, and (ii) subject-level clinical analysis of tortuosity across a larger subset of the cohort (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 45$$\end{document}). These two levels differ in sample size, statistical power, and sources of variability, and are therefore interpreted separately throughout this work.

This study presents a topology-aware, diameter-specific framework for conjunctival vessel segmentation that prioritises preservation of vascular geometry rather than pixel-wise accuracy alone. While conventional segmentation metrics such as Dice coefficient and IoU are useful indicators of overlap, they are insufficient when segmented vessels are used as inputs for topology-dependent biomarkers such as tortuosity. Small discontinuities, fragmentation, or spatial misalignment, often inconsequential in pixel-wise evaluation, can introduce substantial bias in curvature-based measurements.

Our results demonstrate that this distinction is critical. Several baseline and ablated architectures achieved segmentation metrics comparable to the proposed model, yet exhibited significantly larger tortuosity errors and centreline deviations. The ablation study confirmed that no single architectural component is sufficient to preserve vascular topology in isolation. Dilated convolutions improved multi-scale feature extraction, while attention mechanisms enhanced feature selectivity; however, only the full hybrid design integrating dilated convolutions, transformer-based contextual modelling, attention-guided feature fusion, and positional encoding achieved faithful reconstruction of vessel continuity and curvature. These findings underscore that topology preservation emerges from coordinated architectural design and that incremental improvements in pixel-wise segmentation accuracy alone are insufficient.

Clinical relevance of conjunctival tortuosity

From a clinical perspective, the observed increase in mean conjunctival vessel tortuosity in individuals with diabetes mirrors well-established findings with retinal vasculature and supports conjunctival imaging as a promising surrogate marker of systemic microvascular remodelling. Importantly, this work does not propose conjunctival tortuosity as a standalone diagnostic criterion. Rather, the results suggest its potential utility as a quantitative biomarker for vascular health assessment and longitudinal monitoring.

Compared to retinal fundus imaging, conjunctival imaging offers several practical advantages: it is non-invasive, low-cost, and compatible with standard digital camera-based acquisition systems. These properties make it particularly suitable for outpatient settings, population-based studies, and deployment in low-resource environments where access to specialised ophthalmic equipment is limited. In addition, by enabling reliable topology-preserving vessel extraction, the proposed framework supports downstream quantitative analyses that may complement existing clinical workflows rather than replace them.

Generalisation and domain shift

Evaluation on external datasets revealed challenges in cross-domain generalisation (Fig. 9). Although data were collected across two institutions, all images were acquired by the same research group using identical imaging equipment and standardised acquisition protocols. Consequently, variability in acquisition conditions and population characteristics remains limited, which may constrain generalisability.

A key limitation arises from the dependency of diameter-specific analysis on spatial calibration. In this study, vessel diameter estimation is tied to a fixed imaging configuration, and accurate pixel-to-micron conversion is required for consistent diameter-aware segmentation. Datasets acquired using different cameras, magnifications, or acquisition geometries may therefore require recalibration before the proposed framework can be applied.

Publicly available datasets such as SBVPI65 differ substantially from the proposed dataset in acquisition protocol, magnification, camera-to-eye distance, and critically, the absence of calibrated vessel diameter information. This lack of consistent spatial calibration limits the direct transferability of models trained for diameter-specific analysis, even when vessel segmentation quality appears visually comparable.Fig. 9Illustration of domain differences between conjunctival vascular datasets. In addition to acquisition-related differences, phenotypic variability between populations further contributes to domain shift. Factors such as conjunctival and scleral pigmentation, iris colour, and vessel contrast influence vessel visibility and segmentation performance. The SBVPI dataset predominantly comprises individuals with lighter sclera and irides, whereas the proposed dataset includes subjects with darker pigmentation typical of the Sri Lankan population. These differences likely contribute to reduced performance when models trained on one domain are applied to another.

Taken together, these findings highlight that both imaging calibration and population characteristics are critical for reliable cross-domain generalisation. External multi-centre validation was therefore beyond the scope of this study and is identified as an important direction for future work.

Topology-aware learning and vascular representation

Accurate segmentation of vascular structures requires not only volumetric overlap but also preservation of topological continuity. Recent studies have therefore proposed topology-aware loss functions, such as clDice, to explicitly encourage connectivity preservation by optimising overlap between predicted and ground-truth vessel centrelines62.

To assess the impact of such losses within our framework, we evaluated the effect of replacing the standard Dice–BCE loss with a Dice–clDice formulation while keeping the network architecture and training protocol unchanged. Interestingly, the performance differences between the two losses were relatively modest.

This observation suggests that the proposed architecture is already effective at capturing the structural continuity of conjunctival vascular networks. While topology-aware losses can provide important inductive biases for preserving connectivity in many segmentation tasks, our results indicate that the architectural design itself plays a substantial role in modelling thin and elongated anatomical structures. While other topology-aware losses such as Betti-number-based formulations exist, clDice provides the most direct optimisation of centreline continuity for vascular segmentation and therefore serves as an appropriate representative baseline.

Beyond loss design, recent studies have also highlighted the importance of representing high-frequency structural information. Studies have demonstrated that improved representation of high-frequency components can significantly enhance the modelling of thin structures in medical images66. Similarly, large-scale vascular segmentation studies have shown that accurate topology-preserving vessel extraction enables quantitative analysis of vascular morphology and demographic variations in pulmonary vasculature67. These works further emphasise the importance of preserving structural integrity when extracting clinically relevant vascular biomarkers.

In this context, our findings suggest that architectural design can play a complementary role to topology-aware loss functions. By integrating dense dilated feature extraction with transformer-based contextual aggregation, the proposed network implicitly promotes structural continuity, enabling reliable tortuosity estimation for downstream microvascular analysis.

Failure cases and limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Despite improved topology preservation relative to baseline models, extremely thin conjunctival vessels located near specular highlights, motion blur, or low-contrast boundaries remain challenging to segment accurately. In these regions, very short capillary segments may be partially or completely under-segmented, even when global vessel continuity is preserved.

Figure 10 illustrates a representative failure case of the proposed network. As shown, residual errors primarily occur in areas affected by glare, motion artefacts, or minimal vessel-to-background contrast. While the proposed method maintains coherent trajectories for larger vessels and preserves overall vascular topology, the visibility of fine capillary segments in such regions is inherently limited by imaging conditions rather than algorithmic instability.Fig. 10A representative failure case of conjunctival vessel segmentation using the proposed network. These failure modes are common to conjunctival imaging and were observed to a greater extent in baseline segmentation models, which frequently exhibited fragmentation or spurious responses under the same conditions. Importantly, the observed errors did not substantially affect global tortuosity estimation, as downstream analysis aggregated measurements across multiple images per subject and excluded isolated, short vessel fragments.

The study is further limited by a relatively small annotated dataset acquired under a single, standardised imaging protocol, and by the absence of external multi-centre validation. While cross-validation mitigates some concerns related to limited sample size at the segmentation level, it does not replace the need for larger and more diverse annotated datasets. Similarly, although the clinical cohort includes 108 participants, subgroup analyses remain underpowered and should be interpreted cautiously. Patch-based training, necessitated by hardware constraints, may also introduce minor boundary artefacts; however, their impact on topology-sensitive metrics such as tortuosity was limited in practice.

Moreover, only a subset of images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 35$$\end{document}) currently includes detailed manual vessel annotations. This is due to the substantial time and expertise required for diameter-specific labelling of fine conjunctival vessels. As a result, segmentation model training and evaluation are performed at the image level using this annotated subset, whereas clinical tortuosity analysis is conducted at the subject level across a larger subset of the cohort (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 45$$\end{document}).

This distinction between segmentation-level and subject-level data introduces important limitations. The annotated subset may not fully capture the variability present in the broader cohort, potentially introducing sampling bias toward images with clearer vessel visibility and higher annotation confidence. Consequently, while the segmentation model demonstrates strong performance on the annotated dataset, its generalisability to more heterogeneous imaging conditions may be constrained.

Due to the limited number of annotated images, segmentation evaluation using a single held-out test set would be statistically underpowered. To address this, we adopted five-fold cross-validation across all annotated images (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 35$$\end{document}) for state-of-the-art comparisons and final model evaluation, providing a more robust estimate of performance variability.

In contrast, ablation studies were conducted on a fixed held-out test set (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 10$$\end{document}) to ensure controlled and consistent comparison across architectural variants. While this introduces a smaller evaluation set for ablation experiments, it avoids confounding effects arising from varying data splits and enables direct assessment of architectural contributions.

Failure modes and architectural sensitivity: Architectures such as nnU-Net, which rely on extensive auto-configuration and prolonged training schedules, were excluded from this analysis as their failure modes primarily reflected optimisation regime mismatch rather than architectural sensitivity under the controlled training budget.

Qualitative inspection revealed that TDCAU-Net struggled to preserve vessel continuity under the limited training budget and dataset size used in this study. TDCAU-Net was originally proposed for large-scale segmentation settings with higher model capacity and attention complexity, which can be challenging to optimise under limited data and constrained training budgets61. Due to GPU memory constraints, the base channel width of TDCAU-Net was reduced from 64 to 32 to enable training at a resolution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$512 \times 512$$\end{document}, which likely contributed to degraded feature representation and fragmented vessel predictions.

While longer training schedules (e.g., 150–200 epochs) may improve TDCAU-Net performance, such optimisation was intentionally avoided to ensure fair comparison under identical computational and training constraints. Importantly, the proposed method demonstrated stable convergence and superior topology preservation under the same conditions, highlighting its robustness in low-data, clinically realistic settings.

Conclusion and future directions

This work demonstrates that accurate vascular phenotyping requires more than high pixel-wise segmentation accuracy. We show that topology-preserving, diameter-specific segmentation is essential for reliable estimation of tortuosity, a clinically meaningful microvascular biomarker. By integrating dilated convolutions, transformer-based contextual modelling, attention-guided feature fusion, and positional encoding, the proposed framework achieves superior preservation of vessel continuity and curvature compared to existing segmentation approaches.

Applied to conjunctival imaging, this method enables non-invasive assessment of microvascular remodelling associated with diabetes, with trends consistent with established retinal findings. While further validation is required, particularly across diverse populations and longitudinal cohorts, this study identifies conjunctival vessel topology as a promising target for quantitative microvascular analysis. Given the accessibility of conjunctival imaging and its compatibility with low-cost acquisition systems, the proposed framework supports scalable investigation of conjunctival tortuosity as a surrogate microvascular marker in clinical and population-level research settings.

Future work will focus on expanding the dataset across multiple institutions, imaging devices, and acquisition protocols to enable more robust evaluation of generalisation and domain shift. Incorporating calibrated acquisition protocols that explicitly encode vessel diameter and magnification will further strengthen diameter-specific analyses. Longitudinal studies tracking conjunctival vascular changes over time may help clarify the temporal relationship between tortuosity progression and systemic disease development. Finally, future work may explore model-specific optimisation for attention-heavy architectures; however, the present study prioritised controlled comparison under identical training budgets to ensure methodological fairness.
