Beyond Nutritional Deficiency: Persistent Rickets as a Manifestation of Vitamin D‐Dependent Rickets Type II in a Toddler
Authors: Abhisek Jha, Anil Yadav, Uma Shankar Jha, Preethuja Shah, Rohan Kumar Shah, Kuldeep Timilsina, Prabha Jha, Aabish Dahal, Saugat Bagale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVitamin D‐dependent rickets type II (VDDR‐II) is a rare hereditary disorder caused by mutations in the vitamin D receptor gene, resulting in resistance to active vitamin D and impaired calcium absorption. We report a 2‐year and 6‐month‐old female toddler presenting with persistent rickets, delayed motor milestones, dental abnormalities, and recurrent hypocalcemia despite standard vitamin D and calcium supplementation. Initial assessment of nutritional rickets was reconsidered due to the development of pathological fractures and persistent biochemical abnormalities—including hypocalcemia, elevated parathyroid hormone, high bone‐specific alkaline phosphatase, and markedly elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3. The patient was managed with high‐dose calcitriol and calcium, resulting in gradual clinical and biochemical improvement. This case emphasizes the importance of recognizing red flags for genetic rickets in children unresponsive to conventional therapy and demonstrates the utility of integrated clinical, radiographic, and biochemical evaluation for early diagnosis, particularly in resource‐limited settings where molecular testing may not be available.

Introduction

Rickets is a metabolic bone disorder characterized by defective mineralization of the growth plate during infancy and childhood, manifesting clinically as bone deformities, growth retardation, and skeletal fragility. It often arises from deficiencies in calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, or from impaired activity of alkaline phosphatase. Vitamin D plays a crucial role in maintaining calcium and phosphate homeostasis, primarily through its interaction with the vitamin D receptor (VDR), a ligand‐activated intranuclear receptor that regulates the transcription of target genes involved in bone mineralization. Genetically determined forms of rickets are broadly classified into two major categories: vitamin D‐dependent rickets—caused by defects in vitamin D synthesis, metabolism, or receptor function; and hereditary hypophosphatemic rickets—resulting from mutations affecting renal phosphate handling that leads to excessive phosphate loss. Vitamin D‐dependent rickets (VDDR) is further subdivided into types I, II, and III. VDDR‐II, also known as vitamin D‐resistant rickets, is an autosomal recessive disorder, resulting due to genetic defect in VDR gene leading to impaired intestinal calcium absorption and consequent hypocalcemia [1, 2, 3].

Children with VDDR‐II typically present to the clinic with bowing of legs often apparent at the onset of walking, accompanied by other characteristic features of rickets such as rachitic rosary, Harrison's groove, waddling gait, genu varum/valgum, coxa vara, short stature, pronounced myopathy, and dental abnormalities [4, 5]. The constellation of laboratory findings—low serum calcium and phosphate, elevated bone‐specific alkaline phosphatase and increased levels of 1,25‐dihydroxy vitamin D—together with typical radiographic changes is highly suggestive of vitamin D‐dependent rickets type II although genetic testing is required for the definitive diagnosis. Management of VDDR‐II involves calcitriol and calcium supplementation, with therapeutic response largely determined by the severity of receptor defect. Early initiation of treatment is crucial, as it facilitates rapid radiographic healing, promotes optimal linear growth, and prevents skeletal deformities—improving overall quality of life [5, 6, 7].

Case History/Examination

An immunized, 2‐year and 6‐month‐old girl weighing 11.2 Kg and measuring 79 cm in height with an unremarkable birth history presented to our clinic with inability to walk. Parents denied any history of preceding trauma or acute illness. Developmental assessment revealed a global delay across multiple domains. Fine motor skills were delayed, with scribbling achieved at 2.5 years (expected 18 months, Developmental Quotient (DQ) = 60%). Gross motor milestones were markedly delayed, as independent sitting occurred at 2 years (expected 6 months, DQ = 25%). Language development was moderately delayed, with two‐ to three‐word sentences produced by 2.5 years (expected 2 years, DQ = 80%). In contrast, social skills were relatively advanced, with sharing behavior noted at 2.5 years (expected 3 years). Past history was notable for hypocalcemic tetany, 6 months back, requiring hospital admission for 2 days. The child was born to non‐consanguineous parents with no history of similarly affected siblings. Her diet was inadequate in both macro and micronutrient intake, reflecting her poor growth status. The review of systems was unremarkable except for gross dental caries involving teeth 52, 51, 61, and 62, as well as occlusal caries in tooth 75, along with poor oral hygiene.

Methods (Differential Diagnoses, Investigations, and Treatment)

Baseline hematological and biochemical investigations were performed. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated markedly elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels at 445 pg/mL (reference range: 6–80 pg/mL), adequate serum vitamin D concentration of 102 ng/mL (30–100 ng/mL)—reflecting prior supplementation, severe hypocalcemia with total serum calcium measuring 4.68 mg/dL (8.4–10.2 mg/dL), and a reduced ionized calcium level of 0.58 mmol/L (1.05–1.27 mmol/L). However, initial 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels could not be assessed due to financial constraints. Radiographic assessment demonstrated splaying and cupping of the distal metaphysis of the right radius and ulna (Figure 1), classical “rachitic rosary” appearance (beaded ribs) at the costochondral junctions accompanied by generalized osteopenia and multiple limb fractures at different stages of healing (Figure 2), widening of distal metaphyses of tibia and fibula with anterior bowing of bilateral lower limbs (Figure 3). Based on the clinical presentation, biochemical profile, and radiographic findings, a diagnosis of nutritional rickets secondary to vitamin D deficiency was established. The patient was started on a combined oral calcium and vitamin D3 supplement, providing approximately 250 mg of elemental calcium and 125 IU of vitamin D3 per dose with two doses being administered daily. In addition, the patient received a therapeutic course of high‐dose cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) at 60,000 IU once weekly for six consecutive weeks to achieve rapid replenishment of vitamin D stores. The caregivers were counseled regarding the importance of adherence to supplementation, adequate dietary calcium intake, and regular exposure to sunlight to optimize bone mineralization and prevent recurrence.

Results and Conclusion (Outcomes and Follow‐Up)

At three‐month follow‐up, the child presented with acute onset pain over the right hip region, aggravated on movement and associated with restricted mobility. Local examination revealed tenderness over the right upper thigh, symmetrical bilateral gluteal folds with restricted active and painful passive movements of the right lower limb. Imaging revealed a healing fracture of the shaft of the right femur (Figure 4). At this visit, the laboratory parameters showcased elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels (which were not measured initially due to financial constraints) along with persistent hypocalcemia despite high‐to‐normal vitamin D—a hallmark of VDDR‐II; high parathyroid hormone (PTH)—suggestive of hyperparathyroidism secondary to low calcium; elevated bone specific alkaline phosphatase—reflecting high bone turnover from defective mineralization and mildly low‐to‐normal serum phosphate level due to PTH‐mediated renal phosphate loss. The presence of fluctuating vitamin D levels suggested end‐organ resistance at the receptor level rather than deficiency, further supporting the diagnosis of vitamin D‐dependent rickets type II. Given the persistence of initial symptoms, occurrence of pathological fractures despite supplementation and elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels, the initial impression of nutritional rickets was revised to VDDR‐II. The patient is currently maintained on oral calcium and vitamin D supplementation, comprising calcium phosphate 300 mg and vitamin D3 150 IU per dose, administered four times daily. In addition, the patient is receiving calcitriol at a dose of 0.25 μg thrice daily (which was increased from the initial dose of 0.25 μg once daily) to enhance intestinal calcium absorption and optimize bone mineralization. The patient has shown gradual clinical improvement in the form of weight bearing and reduced anterior bowing of lower limbs and improved cupping and fraying of wrists (Figures 5 and 6). Regular clinical and biochemical follow‐up has been scheduled to monitor the patient's response to therapy, including assessment of serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, and 25‐hydroxyvitamin D levels, as well as evaluation of symptomatic and radiological improvement. Although optimal therapeutic response has not yet been achieved (persistent elevation of PTH and ALP with low serum calcium)—likely due to poor adherence to treatment—the patient has shown gradual improvement in laboratory parameters, summarized in Table 1 for clarity and ease of reference. Similarly, a diagnostic timeline has been presented in Figure 7 for better comprehension and readability. Caregivers are advised and counseled regarding dietary calcium intake, adherence to medication, the importance of adequate sunlight exposure, the diagnostic value of genetic testing and counseling as well as the prognosis and complications (such as recurrent fractures, sub‐optimal growth) of the given condition. However, due to the poor socioeconomic condition of the family, the follow‐up has been infrequent and irregular.

Discussion

Vitamin D‐dependent rickets type II (VDDR‐II) is a rare genetic form of rickets, most commonly inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, resulting from mutations in the VDR gene. This gene encodes the vitamin D receptor, which is essential for mediating the biological actions of active vitamin D. Mutations disrupt receptor function, rendering tissues resistant to 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D. Consequently, despite elevated levels of the active hormone, intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphorus remains impaired, leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and defective bone mineralization [8].

Patients with VDDR‐II typically present in early infancy or childhood with features like growth retardation, deformed bones, delayed motor milestones, muscle weakness, and hypocalcemic symptoms such as tetany or seizures [9]. In our case, the child presented with delayed motor milestones—progressive worsening of difficulty in walking in the absence of preceding trauma or systemic illness. In nearly two‐thirds of affected cases, alopecia totalis or partialis is observed, which is considered a hallmark feature [10]. The presence of alopecia signifies a more severe form of mutation in the VDR gene affecting keratinocyte differentiation and hair follicle cycling [11]. However, the absence of alopecia in our case might suggest less severe receptor mutation or partial receptor functionality and not necessarily exclude the diagnosis of VDDR‐II. Dental abnormalities, especially affecting the primary dentition, are common in this form of rickets. Characteristic dental findings include dentin defects, enlarged pulp chambers and pulp horns, intraglobular dentin lesions, enamel hypoplasia, poorly defined lamina dura, hypoplastic alveolar ridges, and frequent periapical infections [4]. Our patient presented with gross dental caries involving teeth 52, 51, 61, 62 and occlusal caries in tooth 75. Six months prior, she developed hypocalcemic tetany—which is an early indicator for calcium metabolism disorder [12]. The lack of clinical improvement with emerging new symptoms, early dental caries and healing fractures despite continuous supplementation of adequate vitamin D and calcium further pointed toward VDDR rather than simple nutritional deficiency.

Radiographic findings in VDDR type II are similar to those of nutritional rickets, including cupping, fraying, and splaying of metaphyseal ends of long bones, widening of growth plates, rachitic rosary due to costochondral junction enlargement, and generalized osteopenia. These radiological features occur from unmineralized osteoid accumulation and defective endochondral ossification [8]. Similarly, in our patient, X‐ray images demonstrated cupping and splaying of the distal metaphyses of the radius and ulna, rachitic rosary, multiple pathological fractures, widening of distal metaphyses of tibia and fibula, along with generalized osteopenia. All of these features were consistent with classical changes associated with rickets. In VDDR‐II, biochemical studies demonstrate low serum calcium and phosphate levels with elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 concentrations (ranging from 125 to 2500 pmol/L; normal in children: 75–250 pmol/L) with normal‐to‐elevated serum 25‐hydroxyvitamin D levels as a result of impaired induction of CYP24A1, which is dependent on VDR activity [7]. Biochemical evidence of elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels with persistent hypocalcemia and hypophosphatemia in our patient supported the new diagnosis of VDDR‐II. Although the patient's clinical, radiological, and biochemical features are strong predictors of VDDR‐II, the definitive diagnosis relies on the genetic analysis of the VDR gene. Such testing can identify pathogenic mutations that impair ligand binding, DNA binding, or receptor co‐activator interactions. Genetic confirmation is crucial not only for distinguishing VDDR‐II from VDDR‐I but also for facilitating genetic counseling for families and providing valuable insights into prognosis [13]. Distinguishing between nutritional rickets and Vitamin D resistant rickets can be quite challenging, especially in resource‐limited settings such as ours, where genetic testing and molecular analysis are usually unaffordable and not always feasible. In our case, genetic counseling and testing were suggested; however, due to economic constraints of the family and non‐availability of in‐country molecular analysis, genetic confirmation could not be obtained, representing a major limitation of this study. Therefore, combined evaluation of clinical presentation, radiographic findings, biochemical markers, and therapeutic response becomes essential for early and aggressive management in order to ensure optimal outcome.

Management of VDDR‐II focuses on overcoming end‐organ resistance to 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D and restoring calcium balance. Active metabolites of vitamin D such as calcitriol or alfacalcidol at a dose of 10–400 ng/kg/day are generally administered to improve intestinal calcium absorption, suppress secretion of PTH and enhance residual receptor responsiveness [14]. In partial resistance, supraphysiologic doses may improve calcium absorption and skeletal healing, whereas severe resistance requires additional high‐dose oral or intravenous calcium to correct hypocalcemia and suppress secondary hyperparathyroidism, thereby preventing bony deformities. Cholecalciferol supplementation is recommended to replenish and maintain adequate 25‐hydroxyvitamin D stores, and correction of any nutritional deficiencies is necessary to support residual metabolic activity. Therefore, lifelong combined therapy with calcitriol, vitamin D and calcium is the standard approach in managing VDDR‐II to achieve biochemical normal levels and radiologic healing [15, 16, 17].

The clinical course of VDDR‐II varies depending on the degree of receptor resistance. Patients without alopecia generally respond well to high‐dose vitamin D therapy, often achieving both clinical and radiological remission. In contrast, patients with alopecia have more severe receptor defects and are resistant to very high doses of vitamin D, only showing improvement on exceptionally high supplementation [7]. During and after puberty, intestinal calcium absorption increases, enabling some patients to maintain normal calcium levels with lower doses of supplementation or, in some cases, without additional calcium therapy. However, in few cases, despite adequate treatment, serum 1,25 (OH)2D often remains elevated, reflecting persistent target organ resistance, while normalization of PTH levels and bone mineral density indicates partial metabolic compensation [13]. However, in our case, gradually improving but inadequate biochemical response (elevated PTH and ALP with low serum calcium) to combined high‐dose calcitriol, vitamin D, and calcium therapy likely indicates poor compliance to treatment with partial receptor functionality rather than severe receptor defect. With sustained therapy, catch‐up growth is expected in our patient, although some skeletal deformities might persist. The presence of partial receptor functionality, as evidenced by gradual biochemical improvement and absence of alopecia, suggests a relatively favorable long‐term prognosis with lifelong therapy. However, continuous monitoring of serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, and growth parameters remains essential to optimize treatment, minimize complications, and ensure sustained skeletal health.

Despite gradual clinical improvement, complete biochemical normalization could not be achieved, largely due to irregular follow‐up and poor adherence to treatment. The family's limited socioeconomic resources hindered access to medications, laboratory testing, and consistent medical review, ultimately delaying recovery and obscuring assessment of true treatment efficacy. This case exemplifies how socioeconomic barriers can significantly influence disease course and outcomes, even in genetically favorable variants of VDDR‐II. Strengthening patient counseling, ensuring affordable drug access, and implementing community‐based follow‐up support are essential to improve adherence and prognosis in such settings. This case also highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges faced while differentiating nutritional rickets from vitamin D‐dependent rickets along with the importance of suspecting the genetic form of rickets, especially in the setting of elevated 1,25‐dihydroxyvitamin D3 levels and inadequate clinical response to standard treatment.

Conclusion

Vitamin D‐dependent rickets type II should be suspected in children with persistent rickets, recurrent hypocalcemia, dental abnormalities, or pathological fractures despite adequate supplementation. Early recognition of these clinical and biochemical anomalies allows prompt initiation of high‐dose calcitriol and calcium therapy, improving skeletal outcomes and quality of life. In resource‐limited settings, where genetic testing may be unavailable, careful integration of clinical, radiographic, and biochemical findings remains essential for timely diagnosis and management.
