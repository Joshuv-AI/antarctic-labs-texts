The gendered devaluation of psychotherapy
Authors: Georgios Paslakis
Published: 2026

As of April 1, 2026, psychotherapists in Germany operating within the public healthcare system will face cuts in their compensation by 4.5%. This applies to every session, every initial consultation, and every acute treatment. These fee cuts are affecting a field that is predominantly occupied by women in Germany, thus perpetuating existing gender inequalities in the healthcare system. While women now account for just under half of all physicians in the statutory health insurance system, psychology and psychotherapy have had particularly high proportions of women for years. Among psychologists working as psychotherapists, women make up around three-quarters of the workforce; women are now also in the majority among medical psychotherapists and in psychiatry and psychotherapy.1,2 In contrast, classic high-status and high-income medical specialties such as surgery, urology, or radiology remain predominantly dominated by men, with the proportion of women in some cases well below 40 percent.2,3 These specialties are considered “hard” medicine, are equipped with advanced technology, possess high symbolic capital, and are traditionally significantly better remunerated than communication-intensive, time-consuming specialties. Cuts in these male-dominated fields are not under discussion. Psychotherapy is thus paradigmatic of a feminized field, in which highly qualified, communicative, and relationship-oriented care work is performed, structurally linked to traditional female care work that has historically been poorly paid or unpaid.4 Professional associations and chambers rightly point out that, despite newly introduced structural surcharges, a real reduction in fees of around three percent is to be expected,5 and this in a situation where mental health conditions have been on the rise for years, waiting times are long, and psychotherapeutic care has been shown to reduce follow-up costs.1,6,7

Speaking in my personal academic capacity (and not on behalf of my university or any professional association), I see this decision being formally gender-neutral, but not effectively. It affects a field of care that is not only predominantly provided by women but is also, at the same time, utilized to a higher degree by women.8 Thus, the cuts exacerbate gender inequalities in two ways: by devaluing primarily female career trajectories and worsening care for a group of patients whose needs are already inadequately met. Based on feminist sociology of work and professions, this can be interpreted as a current example of a well-known pattern: the feminization of a professional field being accompanied by a loss of status and wages; “women's fields” are systematically paid less and given lower political priority than “men's fields”. The fee reduction in psychotherapy fits perfectly into this pattern and sends the signal that conversational, emotional, and relational work -that is, activities with “feminized” connotations-are worth less than technical, device-based, and male-coded medicine, despite their high social relevance. In this sense, I think it is justified to speak of system-level gender hierarchies within the health care system. This is an institutional decision that devalues a female-dominated field, even though the data on care needs would suggest the opposite.
