Fiber electrode fabrication from spent graphite for Zn-ion batteries
Authors: Nakarin Subjalearndee, Jirada Chanpriang, Phrutsakorn Thongkrairat, Panpanat Tesatchabut, Priew Eiamlamai, Panida Muangkasem, Zezhao Li, Nattanai Kunanusont, Xiangwu Zhang, Theeranun Siritanon, Pimpa Limthongkul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growth of the electric vehicle industry has been driven by increasing demand for sustainable transportation across various sectors. To fully transition to the era of electric vehicles, minimizing the usage of scarce and expensive metals in Li-ion batteries and developing effective battery recycling processes are two major challenges. Extensive research has focused on recycling cathodic parts of batteries, whereas comparatively little attention has been paid to anodes, primarily because graphite has a much lower value than the metals in cathodes, such as lithium, cobalt, and nickel. In this work, we established and proposed a sustainable alternative by recycling spent graphite from industrial lithium-ion battery recycling processes into fiber electrodes for Zn-ion batteries. The spent graphite was initially characterized and oxidized into graphene oxide (S_GO) before fabricating fibers via a facile and cost-effective wet-spinning technique. After chemical reduction, the reduced graphene oxide fibers were coated with MnO2 and carbon black to obtain a fiber cathode. The fiber cathode is then assembled into a Zn-ion battery along with a Zn wire anode and xanthan gum gel electrolyte with 2 M ZnSO4 and 0.2 M MnSO4. The Zn-ion battery presented a maximum discharge capacity of ∼160 mAh g−1 at 0.2C over 100 cycles.

Introduction

The global demand for energy storage for electric vehicles (EVs), such as Li-ion batteries (LIBs), is continuously increasing due to the rapid growth of the all-electric automobile industry. According to forecasts, more than half of vehicle users will select battery electrics over internal combustion or hybrid engines by 2035.1 This massive industrial conversion from a “fuel-intensive to a material-intensive energy system” has been acknowledged by the International Energy Agency (IEA) and many other multinational automotive companies. For instance, the US automobile giant General Motors declares that the company plans to stop selling petroleum-based models by 2035.2 However, to completely transform into the age of electric cars, overcoming two major challenges is crucial. The first task is to reduce the amounts of scarce and expensive metals such as lithium, nickel, and cobalt used in batteries because their mining involves environmental problems and social costs. Another challenge is to develop effective battery recycling processes, so that most materials in spent batteries can be converted into raw materials for new batteries.

In the LIB recycling industry, battery recycling involves chemical discharge, mechanical shredding, sieving, and separation, which eventually results in “black mass”. High-value transition metals such as lithium (Li), nickel (Ni), and cobalt (Co) are extracted from the black mass via hydrometallurgy and pyrometallurgy.3,4 However, the remaining graphite residue is frequently overlooked due to structural degradation and lower economic incentives, leaving it to be discarded as industrial waste or used as a low-grade fuel.5,6 Finding alternative routes to recycle or upcycle spent graphite is therefore crucial for achieving zero-waste sustainable development goals. Nevertheless, recent research has shown that the defect structures and expanded interlayer spacing of graphite residue mean it can be transformed into functional materials that are suitable for many applications such as catalysts,7,8 adsorption,9,10 and energy storage materials.11,12 Specifically, Khan et al. modified recovered graphite with MnO2via an electrodeless process for supercapacitor applications. The supercapacitor achieved a specific capacity of 28.6 F g−1 at 0.6 A g−1 current density.12 In addition, Bejigo et al. upcycled spent graphite into a nitrogen-doped graphene (NG) catalyst for the cathode in a zinc–air battery (ZAB) and the anode in a lithium-ion battery. The catalyst demonstrated a specific capacity of 732 mAh g−1 as the cathode in a ZAB with remarkable stability, while it presented a reversible capacity of 403 mAh g−1 after 200 cycles at 1 A g−1 in a LIB system.13 Apart from energy storage applications, Xie et al. transformed spent graphite anodes into bifunctional photochemical catalysts for polyethylene terephthalate (PET) depolymerization. The catalyst system demonstrated 95% PET conversion and around 65% BHET yield within 15 minutes.14

Despite these promising developments, most reported studies focused on powder-based materials, where processing requirements were relatively simple and structural heterogeneity could be tolerated. Particularly, the use of spent graphite in fiber-structured electrode architectures remains largely unexplored. Fiber-based electrodes offer distinct advantages, including continuous electron transport pathways, mechanical flexibility, and the ability to form binder-free and self-supported systems.15,16 However, the fabrication of graphene fibers is highly sensitive to the properties of graphene oxide, such as sheet size, defect density, and dispersion behavior. Therefore, utilizing the highly defective structure of spent graphite in graphene fiber fabrication is challenging.17 Among energy storage systems, aqueous-based Zn-ion batteries have emerged as attractive alternatives to conventional energy storage systems due to their intrinsic safety, low cost, and environmental compatibility.18,19 For cathodic active materials, MnO2 has been widely studied because of its favorable redox chemistry and high theoretical capacity. Therefore, integrating MnO2 with conductive and flexible carbon frameworks, such as graphene fibers, has proven to be an effective strategy to improve charge transport and electrochemical utilization.20,21 However, the incorporation of graphene oxide synthesized from spent graphite into such fiber-based Zn-ion systems has not been systematically investigated, particularly in terms of how structural characteristics affect electrode fabrication and electrochemical performance.

In this work, spent graphite obtained from LIB recycling processes was utilized as a precursor for the fabrication of graphene-based fiber electrodes for Zn-ion batteries. Experimentally, spent graphite was oxidized into graphene oxide to prepare a spinning dope. The spinning dope was then mixed with graphene oxide synthesized from pristine graphite to tune spinnability and structural integrity during wet spinning. The resulting fibers were reduced via chemical reduction and subsequently dip-coated in a slurry containing MnO2 and conductive powder. The effects of the content of reduced graphene oxide (rGO) synthesized from spent graphite on fiber morphology, mechanical properties, porosity, and electrochemical performance were systematically investigated. This work demonstrates a viable pathway for converting recycled graphite into structured electrode materials and provides insight into the role of recycled carbon in fiber-based energy storage systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

Graphite flakes with a size of ca. 300 µm (63.84%), hydroiodic acid (HI, 30%), manganese(ii) sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O), ammonium persulfate ((NH4)2S2O8), zinc sulfate heptahydrate (ZnSO4·7H2O), n-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, ACS reagent, ≥99%), and polyamide film tape were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Zinc wire (0.25 mm diameter, 99.99 +% purity) was purchased from Alfa Aesar. Spent graphite powder was received from Thammasat University, Thailand. Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 96%), nitric acid (HNO3, 65%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.5%), potassium permanganate (KMnO4), and hydrogen peroxide solution (H2O2, 50%) were purchased from Krungthep Chemi, Thailand.

Synthesis of graphene oxide

Spent graphite powder was obtained after Li-ion battery recycling via industrial mechanical separation and hydrometallurgy processes. Graphene oxide was synthesized from graphite and spent graphite powder via a modified Hummers' method.22 Briefly, spent graphite powder (10 g) was initially filtered with 300-mesh sieves to separate metal and plastic residues. The sieved spent graphite was purified in a mixture of H2SO4 (300 mL) and HNO3 (60 mL) at room temperature overnight. The mixture was then diluted in 1 L of deionized (DI) water before filtering via vacuum filtration. The dried black solid was treated in a microwave to transform the intercalated graphite into expanded graphite. The expanded graphite was dispersed in H2SO4 (1 L) before adding KMnO4 (50 g) to the mixture. After 24 hours, the mixture was diluted with DI water before adding H2O2 to stop the reaction. The solution was kept for 2 days before centrifuging the supernatant at 5000 rpm. Subsequently, the solid was treated with HCl solution and water before performing dialysis for 2 weeks. The mixture was then dried under a fume hood for several days. The synthesized graphene oxide from pristine graphite flakes was labelled as GO, while the graphene oxide that was synthesized from spent graphite was labelled as S_GO.

Synthesis of alpha- and beta-MnO2

Alpha- and beta-MnO2 were synthesized via hydrothermal reactions according to a previous work.17 For α-MnO2 synthesis, MnSO4·H2O (1.35 g) and KMnO4 (1.26 g) were added to DI water (50 mL) under magnetic stirring for 30 minutes. The solution was then poured into a hydrothermal chamber before performing the reaction at 90 °C for 20 hours. For β-MnO2 synthesis, KMnO4 oxidant was replaced with K2S2O8 (2.16 g) during the solution preparation step. The solution was then subjected to a hydrothermal reaction at 150 °C for 20 hours.

Fabrication of reduced graphene oxide/MnO2 fiber cathodes

Reduced graphene oxide fibers were initially fabricated via a wet-spinning technique (Scheme 1). Four graphene oxide dispersions with different weight ratios of GO to S_GO were prepared to fabricate graphene oxide fibers. GO and S_GO dispersions of 15 mg mL−1 were separately prepared before mixing at different weight ratios of 100 : 0, 75 : 25, 50 : 50, and 25 : 75. For the 100 : 0 weight ratio, only the GO dispersion (10 mL) at 15 mg mL−1 was used to fabricate graphene oxide fibers (GO100 : S_GO0). For the 75 : 25 ratio, the GO dispersion (7.5 mL) was mixed with the S_GO (2.5 mL) dispersion to formulate a spinning dope (GO75 : S_GO25). The 50 : 50 and 25 : 75 weight ratio dispersions were prepared by increasing the ratio of S_GO to 50% (GO50 : S_GO50) and 75% (GO25 : S_GO75) of the spinning dope, respectively. Wet spinning was performed by transferring the formulated dispersion into a 10-mL syringe before connecting with a syringe pump. The dispersion was then injected into a coagulation bath of concentrated acetic acid at a feeding rate of 10 mL h−1 and a fiber collecting speed of 300 rpm (SI, Fig. S1 and S2). The dried fibers (10 fibers) were then twisted into a bundle before immersing in hydriodic solution at 50 °C for 20 hours. After the reduction reaction, the reduced fibers were then washed with ethanol and DI water several times before drying inside a hot air oven. The selected reduced graphene oxide fiber bundle was subsequently dip coated with a fluid slurry containing MnO2, binder, and conductive material to obtain a composite fiber cathode. The slurry was prepared by grinding 70 wt% MnO2 particles (0.7 mg), 20 wt% carbon black (0.2 mg), and 10 wt% PVDF solution (by dissolving 0.2 g of PVDF in 20 mL of NMP) in a mortar for 5 minutes. Dip coating was performed by immersing the rGO bundle into the slurry for 10 seconds before drying inside a vacuum oven at 60 °C overnight. The active MnO2 mass coated onto the fiber bundle was calculated as 70% of the mass increase upon drying the dip-coated fiber bundle.

The Zn-ion battery assembly procedure with a fiber electrode fabricated from pristine and spent graphite.

Physical and chemical characterization

The morphologies of the composite fibers were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM and SEM-EDX; Hitachi, SU 5000). The d-spacings of graphitic materials were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL, Hitachi-HT7800) at 120 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using an X-ray diffractometer over a 2θ range from 10 to 80° with a Cu source (Bruker, D8 ADVANCE). Tensile tests were performed at a loading speed of 5 mm min−1 with a fiber length of 20 mm (Instron 5943 mechanical tester). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area was measured by the N2 adsorption–desorption test (Quantachrome instrument). The pore size distribution was calculated using the BJH method.

Electrochemical characterization

The electrical resistances of MnO2-coated rGO bundle fibers were measured by a four-point probe machine (2450 Source Meter, SMU Instrument and Four-point Collinear Probe, Keithley). Zn-ion battery cells were assembled by aligning 10 cm of the MnO2-coated rGO fiber cathode (∼1.5 mg) and 10 cm of the Zn wire anode (∼30 mg) on a glass slide (∼4.5 g) in a parallel configuration before immersing them in 5 mL of 3.5 wt% xanthan gum gel electrolyte. The gel electrolyte was prepared by dissolving xanthan gum (0.875 g), 2 M ZnSO4·7H2O (14.38 g), and 0.2 M MnSO4·H2O (0.75 g) in 25 mL of DI water. Copper tape was attached to the fiber cathode and used as the current collector (Scheme 1). The total weight of the assembled battery was ∼8 g. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were measured using an electrochemical workstation (Metrohm Autolab, PGSTAT302N). Galvanostatic discharge–charge data were evaluated via a battery testing machine (Novonix High Precision Coulometry cycler at 25 °C) using 1.0–1.8 V cutoffs.

Results and discussion

Characterization of graphite flakes and spent graphite

The physical morphologies of graphite flakes (GF), spent graphite (SG), and purified spent graphite (P_SG) are presented in Fig. 1. GF shows large graphite plates with smooth surfaces and well-defined layered structures, characteristic of highly crystalline graphite (Fig. 1a). In contrast, SG presented irregular and agglomerated particles, with particle sizes ranging from ∼25 to 80 µm (Fig. 1b). After purification, P_SG showed a non-agglomerated and uniform particle distribution with sizes of ∼10–20 µm (Fig. 1c). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to observe impurities in SG and P_SG. The results revealed that spent graphite contained trace amounts of metallic impurities such as cobalt and aluminum, which were left over from electrode materials. In addition, fluorine originating from the binder was also detected (SI, Fig. S3). After the purification process, only fluorine and aluminum were left in spent graphite (SI, Fig. S4).

Physical morphologies of graphite and spent graphite. SEM images of (a) graphite flakes (GF), (b) spent graphite (SG), and (c) spent graphite after purification (P_SG). TEM images and SAED patterns of (d and e) graphite flakes and (f and g) spent graphite after purification.

TEM analysis was performed to further elucidate the structural differences between P_SG and GF (Fig. 1d–g). The TEM images show that P_SG retains a layered structure but exhibits distortion and partial disorder of the graphitic layers, suggesting structural degradation induced by repeated ion insertion/extraction during battery operation. The interlayer spacing (d-spacing) revealed a slight expansion from 0.335 nm for GF to 0.345 nm for P_SG. The increased d-spacing confirms the enlargement of the graphitic interlayer distance, which aligns with ion-induced lattice expansion and structural perturbation.24

XRD patterns of both types of graphite particles (GF and SG) and their oxidized forms are presented in Fig. 2. The diffraction patterns of GF, SG, and P_SG exhibited a sharp and intense characteristic peak at 2θ ≈ 26.5°. The peak corresponds to the (002) reflection of the hexagonal graphite structure with an interlayer spacing (d-spacing) of approximately 0.33–0.34 nm.25 The similar peak profiles and interlayer spacings confirmed that the spent graphite retained high crystallinity and structural integrity equivalent to that of the graphite flakes. After the oxidation reaction, the characteristic graphitic peak at 26.5° decreased, and a new diffraction peak emerged at 2θ ≈ 7.60° and 7.76° for the oxidized forms of graphite flakes (GO) and spent graphite (S_GO), respectively. According to Bragg's law, the shift corresponds to an expansion of the interlayer spacing from about 0.34 nm to 1.16 nm in GO, while the expansion resulted in a spacing of 1.14 nm for S_GO. This lattice expansion is attributed to the intercalation of water molecules and the formation of oxygen-containing functional groups (such as hydroxyl, epoxy, and carboxyl groups).26 The increased interlayer spacing confirmed the successful oxidation of graphite into graphene oxide. In a comparison between GO and S_GO, the trace of the graphitic structure (2θ: ∼26.5°) in S_GO was relatively higher than that in GO due to high purity, ordered crystallinity, and lower porosity in spent synthetic graphite.27 In addition, the oxidation process via the modified Hummers' method could effectively remove fluorine residue from spent graphite (SI, Fig. S5).

XRD patterns of (a) GF, (b) SG, (c) P_SG, (d) GO (synthesized from GF), and (e) S_GO (synthesized from P_SG).

Fabrication and characterization of fiber cathodes

Fig. 3 presents SEM images of wet-spun graphene oxide fibers with different GO : S_GO ratios (from 100 : 0 to 25 : 75). The wet-spun GO fiber from 100% pristine GO showed a smooth and uniform morphology (Fig. 3a and b). At a 75 : 25 ratio of GO and S_GO dispersions, the fiber presented wrinkles on the fiber surface (Fig. 3c and d). After extruding the fiber with a dispersion that replaced 50% of the GO dispersion with the S_GO dispersion (50 : 50 ratio), the fiber showed more wrinkles than that of the spun GO fiber with a 75 : 25 ratio (Fig. 3e and f). The wet-spun GO fibers at the highest ratio of S_GO (GO25 : S_GO75) showed low spinnability with highly wrinkled GO fibers (Fig. 3g and h). It is hypothesized that the observed wrinkles in GO fibers fabricated with a high ratio of S_GO result from disruption of nematic liquid crystal formation from small GO particles within large GO systems.17

The physical morphologies of graphene oxide fibers with ratios of graphene oxide to spent graphene oxide of (a and b) GO100 : S_GO0; (c and d) GO75 : S_GO25; (e and f) GO50 : S_GO50; and (g and h) GO25 : S_GO75.

XRD patterns of GO fibers with different GO : S_GO ratios are shown in SI, Fig. S6. All GO fibers exhibited a characteristic graphene oxide peak at 2θ = ∼10.7°. The GO fibers with higher S_GO ratios resulted in an additional diffraction peak at 2θ = ∼11–14°, which could be attributed to the reflection of a residual or partially oxidized graphene oxide domain. Tensile profiles of the GO fibers are presented in Table 1. The wet-spun GO fibers showed a Young's modulus of 0.95 N per tex, tenacity of ∼1.88 × 10−2 N per tex, and breaking strain of ∼12%. Increasing the ratio of S_GO to 25% resulted in a higher Young's modulus, while both tenacity and breaking strain dropped to ∼1.44 × 10−2 N per tex and ∼5.87%, respectively. Interestingly, the tensile profile of GO that was fabricated with 50% S_GO dispersion showed a higher Young's modulus and tenacity than that of the GO75 : S_GO25 fibers. As the proportion of S_GO reached 75% in the fibers, the tenacity significantly dropped to ∼7.00 × 10−3 N per tex, and the brittleness of the fiber increased sharply, as evidenced by the breaking strain decreasing to 1.91% from ∼12% (GO100 : S_GO0 fibers). When considering the fiber spinnability and overall tensile profiles of the GO fibers, GO 75 : S_GO25 and GO50 : S_GO50 fibers were selected to analyze the differences in surface areas. Comparing the tensile profiles of graphene oxide composite fibers, the GO50 : S_GO50 fibers exhibited a higher Young's modulus and could be continuously fabricated using a pre-pilot-scale wet-spinning system.17

The BET isotherms of GO75 : S_GO25 and GO50 : S_GO50 fibers were evaluated to observe the differences in surface areas and pore characteristics (Fig. 4). Both fibers showed typical type-IV isotherms with distinct hysteresis loops in the relative pressure (P/P0) range of approximately 0.45 to 1.0, which is characteristic of mesoporous materials (Fig. 4a and c). The pore size distribution of GO75 : S_GO25 fibers revealed a primary pore population in the small mesopore range below 10 nm, with some large pores extending up to 100 nm (Fig. 4b). For GO75 : S_GO25 fibers, the pore size showed a high population in the range below 10 nm and a small portion at ∼60 nm (Fig. 4d). The results confirmed that both fibers possessed a hierarchical porous structure comprising mainly mesopores. In terms of specific surface area, the GO75 : S_GO25 and GO50 : S_GO50 fibers showed values of 38.6 and 42.4 m2 g−1, respectively (Fig. 4b and d, insets). It is observed that the highly wrinkled GO fibers present a higher specific surface area than the smooth GO fibers. In contrast, a non-uniform pore size distribution is observed in the wrinkled GO fibers. For fiber electrode fabrication, GO50 : S_GO50 fibers were selected due to their better average tensile profile and higher specific surface area compared with the GO75 : S_GO25 fibers.

N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm plots and pore size distributions of (a and b) GO75 : S_GO25 fibers and (c and d) GO50 : S_GO50 fibers.

Two different crystal structures of MnO2, α-MnO2 and β-MnO2, were selected as active materials in cathodic electrodes (SI, Fig. S7a, b and S8). α-MnO2 showed a crystal tunnel size of 2 × 2 tunnels, which allowed easier H+/Zn2+ insertion, while β-MnO2 had a narrow 1 × 1 tunnel size, which might limit ion insertion.28 Prior to the dip-coating process, the selected GO fiber bundle was assembled by twisting 10 GO fibers into a single bundle before performing chemical reduction with hydriodic acid. After reduction, the XRD patterns showed that the GO fibers were mostly reduced into rGO fibers, showing the characteristic peaks of rGO at 2θ = ∼24.10° (SI, Fig. 7c and d). The rGO50 : rS_GO50 bundle was then dip-coated with PVDF slurry containing active material (MnO2) and carbon black (Fig. 5). After dip-coating the fiber bundle with the slurry containing α-MnO2, the fiber bundle surface was covered with non-uniform MnO2 particles, forming a relatively thick coating layer (Fig. 5a–c). In contrast, the rod-shaped β-MnO2 particles coated on the fiber bundle exhibited a more uniform surface layer (Fig. 5d–f and SI, S9). The phenomenon of agglomerated α-MnO2 could be explained based on high surface energy due to the nanoscale dimensions of α-MnO2, while the thermodynamically stable and compact structure of β-MnO2 showed low surface energy, which reduced its tendency to agglomerate.28 The electrical resistance of the dip-coated fiber bundle is presented in SI, Table S1. The electrical resistance of the fiber bundles showed a similar magnitude of ∼20 Ohms per square. The results confirmed that dip-coating with the slurry formulation is able to provide sufficient electrical conductivity for the fiber cathode.

SEM images of rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers after dip-coating with slurry containing (a–c) α-MnO2 and (d–f) β-MnO2.

XRD patterns of MnO2-coated rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers are presented in Fig. 6. For α-MnO2-coated rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers, the broad diffraction peak at 2θ = ∼10–13° could be attributed to the peaks from the (001) plane of GO and (110) plane of α-MnO2 (Fig. 6a). For the β-MnO2-coated rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers, the XRD pattern showed sharper characteristic peaks corresponding to the (110) and (101) planes of β-MnO2 at 2θ = ∼28.6° and ∼37.2°, respectively (Fig. 6b).29 The β-MnO2-coated rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers generally presented sharper peaks than the α-MnO2-coated rGO50 : rS_GO50 fibers due to the highly crystalline structure of β-MnO2.

Electrochemical performance of Zn-ion batteries

Prototype Zn-ion batteries were assembled on a glass slide using the dip-coated GO50 : S_GO50 fiber bundle as a fiber cathode, Zn wire as an anode, and xanthan gum gel electrolyte containing 2 M ZnSO4 and 0.2 M MnSO4. The cyclic voltammograms of both prototype batteries are presented in Fig. 7. The CV curves were recorded at scan rates of 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, and 10.0 mV s−1 within a potential window of 0.8–2.0 V, corresponding to reversible Zn2+ insertion and extraction within the MnO2 framework. For the α-MnO2-coated fiber cathode, two cathodic peaks appear at ∼1.16 and ∼1.26 V at 0.5 mV s−1, with a corresponding anodic peak at ∼1.7 V. Increasing the scan rate shifts the cathodic peaks to lower potentials, from ∼1.16 to ∼1.10 V and from ∼1.26 to ∼1.24 V at 5.0 mV s−1. At 10.0 mV s−1, the two peaks merge into a single broad peak at ∼1.2 V (Fig. 7a). Notably, the anodic peak disappeared at scan rates from 1 to 10 mV s−1. It could be assumed that non-uniform and thickly coated MnO2 layers resulted in mass-transport limitations and high internal resistance.30 In contrast, the β-MnO2-coated fiber cathode presented two cathodic peaks at ∼1.2 and ∼1.3 V, with an anodic peak near 1.7 V at a 0.5 mV s−1 scan rate. At a scan rate of 1 mV s−1, the cathodic peaks merge into a single broad peak at ∼1.3 V, while the anodic peak was observed at ∼1.7 V. At 5- and 10 mV s−1 scan rates, cathodic and anodic peaks were observed at ∼1.2 and ∼1.8 V, respectively (Fig. 7b). It could be seen that the β-MnO2-coated fiber cathode exhibited a consistently broader current response than the α-MnO2-coated fiber cathode across all scan rates, reflecting higher electrochemical activity, which is beneficial for the fiber cathode-based energy storage system.

Cyclic voltammograms of Zn-ion batteries with (a) an α-MnO2-coated GO50 : S_GO50 fiber cathode and (b) a β-MnO2-coated GO50 : S_GO50 fiber cathode.

Galvanostatic discharge–charge profiles and the cycling stability of a Zn-ion battery employing β-MnO2-coated fibers as a fiber cathode are shown in Fig. 7. The first discharge curve displayed long plateaus around ∼1.2 V, while the second discharge profile presented two plateaus at ∼1.4 V and ∼1.25 V, with a clear turning point at ∼1.25 V (Fig. 8a). These phenomena are explained by a two-phase conversion reaction from coupled proton (H+) and zinc-ion (Zn2+) co-intercalation.31,32 The discharge capacity gradually increased and stabilized, indicating progressive electrode activation and improved electrochemical utilization of the active material. The discharge profiles from the 50th to the 100th cycle also presented long plateaus at ∼1.4 V and ∼1.25 V, which indicated a stable transition and H+/Zn2+ co-intercalation mechanism. The cycling performance and coulombic efficiency of the Zn-ion battery are presented in Fig. 8b. In the first few cycles, the coulombic efficiency was over 100%, which corresponded to parasitic side reactions or irregular ion transportation.33,34 The discharge capacity gradually increased from the 1st to 80th cycle and reached the maximum discharge capacity at the 90th cycle of ∼160 mAh g−1. The discharge capacity then gradually decreased to ∼155 mAh g−1 at the 100th cycle. The average maximum discharge capacity of four battery cells was 167.5 ± 27.2 mAh g−1 with over 99% coulombic efficiency after the 90th cycle. The gradual increase in discharge capacity could be explained by increased ion transportation and insertion into bulk β-MnO2, apart from surface β-MnO2. In comparison with the theoretical capacity, the limited discharge capacity might occur due to many reasons, such as the narrow tunneling pathway characteristics of β-MnO2, low intrinsic conductivity, or by-product formation.35 Although the electrochemical performance of the fiber cathode might not be comparable to those of previously reported fiber-shaped ZIBs, the study demonstrated the use of recycled materials and a more cost-effective fiber cathode fabrication process.36,37

Conclusions

We have successfully established and proposed a viable and sustainable alternative to recycle spent graphite residue from industrial lithium-ion battery recycling processes into functional graphene fiber electrodes for Zn-ion batteries. The spent graphite showed agglomerated particles with sizes of ∼25–80 µm and a slightly expanded interlayer spacing of 0.345 nm. Prior to fiber fabrication via a wet-spinning technique, different ratios of an S_GO dispersion were mixed with a GO dispersion. The optimized ratio for continuously fabricating graphene oxide fibers with the highest percentage of S_GO was 50% (GO50 : S_GO50 fibers), with a mesoporous architecture that showed a specific surface area of 42.4 m2 g−1. The fiber cathode was fabricated by dipping the reduced graphene oxide fiber bundle into a PVDF slurry containing MnO2 and carbon black. Between α-MnO2 and β-MnO2 active materials, it was found that the uniform and non-agglomerated β-MnO2 coating layer could overcome mass-transport limitations and internal resistance in Zn-ion batteries. As a result, the β-MnO2-coated fiber cathode in a Zn-ion battery achieved a maximum discharge capacity of ∼160 mAh g−1 at 0.2C over 100 cycles. These findings highlighted the potential of recycling spent graphite into fiber electrodes for Zn-ion batteries, which could benefit both resource security and the sustainable circular economy.
