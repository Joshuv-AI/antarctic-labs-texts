Plasmodium falciparum sporozoite loads in Anopheles gambiae s.l. across agro-ecological zones of Benin: a cross-sectional field study

Background

Malaria remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths recorded in 2024, mostly in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. In Benin, 2,289,948 cases and 2,450 deaths were reported in 2020, disproportionately affecting children under five and pregnant women [2], and the disease imposes a substantial economic cost on highly endemic countries [3]. Among the eleven Plasmodium species infecting humans [4, 5], Plasmodium falciparum causes the most severe clinical forms of the disease and accounts for the majority of malaria-related deaths [6], transmitted exclusively through the bite of infective female Anopheles mosquitoes, which deliver sporozoites into the bloodstream of a new host during a blood meal [7].

The efficiency of this transmission cycle depends on what is termed the vectorial competence of the mosquito defined as its intrinsic capacity to support the full development of Plasmodium from ingested gametocytes, through the formation of oocysts in the midgut wall, to the production and migration of sporozoites into the salivary glands [8]. This competence is not uniform across species or populations, and understanding its biological determinants has direct implications for predicting transmission intensity and designing targeted control interventions.

A key operational indicator of vectorial competence is the sporozoite load, the quantity of infective P. falciparum sporozoites present in the head–thorax (salivary glands) of a given mosquito at the time of capture. This parameter is directly relevant to malaria transmission, as higher sporozoite burdens are associated with an increased probability that a blood meal will result in successful parasite inoculation into the vertebrate host, although this relationship is nonlinear and constrained by biological thresholds [9, 10]. Despite the routine use of the sporozoite index, the proportion of mosquitoes carrying sporozoites, as an entomological surveillance metric, quantitative sporozoite load at the individual vector level remains comparatively unexplored in natural field populations [10].

In Benin, malaria transmission is sustained primarily by the Anopheles gambiae complex, within which An. gambiae and An. coluzzii are the dominant sibling species [11]. Although morphologically indistinguishable without molecular tools, these two species differ substantially in ecology, behaviour, and larval biology. An. coluzzii shows a marked preference for permanent or semi-permanent anthropogenic breeding sites, including irrigated rice fields, urban drainage channels, peri-domestic pools, which tend to be organically enriched and microbiologically complex. By contrast, An. gambiae breeds predominantly in temporary, sunlit rainwater pools typical of rural and semi-natural landscapes [12]. These contrasting larval environments expose developing mosquitoes to distinct microbial communities and environmental stressors during a period that may critically shape adult immune competence and, by extension, susceptibility to Plasmodium infection.

Benin spans three climatic regimes along its south-to-north axis and is formally subdivided into eight agro-ecological zones, each characterized by distinct rainfall patterns, soil types, vegetation cover, and dominant agricultural practices [13]. This environmental heterogeneity governs not only the geographic distribution of vector species, but also microclimatic parameters, particularly temperature and humidity that directly influence the extrinsic incubation period of P. falciparum. Temperatures below 18 °C arrest sporogonic development entirely, while the optimum for P. falciparum lies between 25 and 30 °C; modest deviations from this range can substantially alter the duration and completeness of sporogony within the mosquito host [14]. It is therefore conceivable that the agro-ecological context in which a vector population lives modulates not only transmission intensity at the population level, but also the individual sporozoite burden carried by infected mosquitoes.

To date, no study has systematically compared P. falciparum sporozoite loads in An. gambiae and An. coluzzii across the full agro-ecological gradient of Benin. The present study addresses this gap by quantifying and comparing sporozoite burdens in natural populations of both species across seven agro-ecological zones in Benin, testing the hypothesis that specific ecological facies favour greater sporozoite accumulation and thereby heightened transmission potential.

Methods

Study areas

The study was conducted in Benin, a West African country located between 6°30' and 12°30' North latitude and 1° and 3°40' East longitude, covering a total land area of 114,763 km2 [15]. Three distinct climatic regimes characterize the country, namely a sub-equatorial climate in the south (four seasons per year), a transitional Sudano-Guinean climate in the centre (four seasons), and a Sudanian climate in the north (two seasons) [15]. Eight agro-ecological zones are formally recognized on the basis of climatic, agropedological, and socioeconomic criteria [16].

Twelve study localities distributed across seven agro-ecological zones were selected for their documented high malaria incidence [2]: Cotonou and Comè (Fisheries zone); Malanville (Far North zone); Djougou and Boukombé (West Atacora zone); Glazoué and Parakou (Central cotton zone); Dogbo and Ifangni (Bar land zone); and Bonou, Toffo, and Zogbodomey (Depression zone) (Fig. 1).

Mosquito collection

Anopheles gambiae s.l. adults were collected by the Human Landing Catch (HLC) technique in sixteen randomly selected houses per commune (eight near the centre, eight at the periphery). Collections were conducted from 21:00 to 06:00 h during the rainy season (August–October 2022), following the protocol of Baird [17]. Two seated volunteers per household used haemolysis tubes and a flashlight to capture mosquitoes landing on their legs. Mosquitoes collected each hour were stored separately. All specimens were transported to the field laboratory for morphological identification and subsequently sent to the Centre de Recherche Entomologique de Cotonou (CREC) for molecular and parasitological assays. Of the total female Anopheles gambiae s.l. collected, 1,355 were selected for molecular species identification and CSP-ELISA testing based on specimen integrity (intact head–thorax preparations) and laboratory processing capacity within the study timeframe; no a priori sample-size or power calculation was performed, consistent with the exploratory design of this cross-sectional survey.

Morphological identification

Adult female mosquitoes were morphologically identified to the species complex level using the standard keys of Gillies and Coetzee [18] and Gillies and De Meillon [19]. Each specimen was then dissected into two fractions, with the head–thorax, preserved individually in a labelled Eppendorf tube with silica gel desiccant at ambient temperature until DNA extraction, for ELISA and quantitative PCR; and the abdomen, wings, and legs, used for molecular species identification.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from the head–thorax and abdomen–wings–legs fractions of each specimen using a 2% CTAB protocol [20]. Briefly, the tissue was ground in 200 µl of 2% CTAB buffer and incubated for 5 min in a water bath at 65 °C. The homogenate was then mixed with 200 µl of chloroform and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. The aqueous supernatant was carefully transferred into a fresh tube containing 200 µl of isopropanol and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min to precipitate the DNA. The resulting pellet was washed with 200 µl of 70% ethanol and centrifuged again at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. After carefully decanting the supernatant and inverting the tube briefly to preserve the pellet, the pellet was air-dried on the bench for a minimum of 3 h, then resuspended in 20 µl of bi-distilled water and allowed to dissolve overnight or for at least half a day at room temperature.

Molecular species identification

Species within the An. gambiae complex were first identified by the SINE-200 diagnostic PCR of Santolamazza et al. [21]. This assay uses four primers, including UN (universal), AG (An. gambiae specific), AA (An. arabiensis-specific), and AM (An. melas-specific), in a single multiplex reaction. The universal primer UN hybridizes to a conserved nucleotide sequence present in all species; in combination with AG, it amplifies a diagnostic fragment in An. gambiae s.l.; paired with AA, it yields a fragment diagnostic of An. arabiensis; and paired with AM, it amplifies a fragment unique to An. melas. To differentiate An. gambiae from An. coluzzii within SINE-200-positive individuals assigned to An. gambiae s.l., a second PCR (SINE-PCR) was performed according to the protocol of Santolamazza et al. [21], using primers F6 (20 bases: 5'-TCG CCT TAG ACC TTG CGT TA-3') and R6 (21 bases: 5'-CGC TTC AAG AAT TCG AGA TAC-3'). Amplification with this primer pair yields a fragment of 249 bp in An. gambiae and 479 bp in An. coluzzii. Following electrophoresis, fragment sizes were estimated by comparison with a molecular size marker under ultraviolet light transillumination (representative gel image provided as Supplementary material 1). Detection of P. falciparum infection

P. falciparum infection in the head–thorax of each mosquito was assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) targeting the circumsporozoite protein (CSP), following the protocol of Wirtz et al. [22]. In this assay, CSP antigen present in the head–thorax homogenate binds to an anti-CSP capture monoclonal antibody (mAb) precoated on the microplate wells. The resulting antigen–antibody complex is then detected by a peroxidase-conjugated anti-CSP detection mAb. Addition of the chromogenic substrate produces a colour reaction, the optical density of which is measured spectrophotometrically. Specimens with optical density values exceeding the predefined threshold (mean optical density of negative controls + 2 × SD) were considered positive for P. falciparum sporozoite antigen. Only CSP-ELISA-positive individuals were subsequently subjected to sporozoite-load quantification.

Quantification of P. falciparum sporozoite load by real-time PCR

Sporozoite load was quantified in the head–thorax preparations of all CSP-ELISA-positive mosquitoes by real-time PCR targeting the plasmepsin gene of P. falciparum. Real-time PCR was then performed using the NZYTech detection kit (reference MD 02411, 150 reactions). This plasmepsin-based quantitative assay was selected over conventional 18S rRNA- or Cox1-based assays because it is commercially validated with a defined linear dynamic range and standardized positive controls, enabling absolute quantification of sporozoite load rather than the qualitative presence–absence detection typically provided by 18S rRNA- or Cox1-based protocols. The primer and probe sequences included in this kit exhibit 100% sequence homology with a comprehensive range of P. falciparum reference sequences, as verified by exhaustive bioinformatic analysis by the manufacturer. The reaction mixture was amplified in a thermocycler equipped with real-time fluorescence detection; fluorescence was recorded at each amplification cycle. The quantity of amplified DNA, and thereby the sporozoite load, was determined by comparing each sample’s fluorescence curve against that of known positive controls of defined concentration. Each amplification run also included a no-template negative control, and samples with ambiguous or borderline amplification curves were re-tested in duplicate before final classification. Results are expressed as picogram (pg) equivalents of P. falciparum DNA.

Statistical analyses

The sporozoite index (SI) was calculated as the number of CSP-ELISA-positive mosquitoes divided by the total number of mosquitoes analyzed, expressed as a percentage. Differences in SI across localities and agro-ecological zones were assessed by chi-square test. Sporozoite loads were compared between agro-ecological zones (within each species) using the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test, and between the two principal species using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test, implemented in R (version 4.4.1). Given the non-normal distribution of sporozoite-load data, results are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Distribution of Anopheles gambiae s.l. species across districts and agro-ecological zones

A total of 3,021 Anopheles gambiae s.l. were collected across twelve districts in seven agro-ecological zones of Benin (Fig. 2). The Depression zone contributed the largest share of specimens (n = 819; 27.11%), followed by the Bar land zone (n = 607; 20.09%) and the West Atacora zone (n = 584; 19.33%). The Fisheries zone (n = 405; 13.41%), the Far North zone (n = 317; 10.49%) and the Central cotton zone (n = 289; 9.57%) accounted for the remaining collections. At district level, Ifangni (n = 318; 10.53%), Malanville (n = 317; 10.49%) and Boukoumbé (n = 300; 9.93%) were the most productive sites, whereas Parakou yielded the fewest specimens (n = 82; 2.71%).

Total number of Anopheles gambiae s.l. collected per locality and agro-ecological zone

Of the 1,355 specimens typed by PCR, An. coluzzii was the most frequent sibling species overall (63.32%; 95% CI 60.97–66.15), followed by An. gambiae (36.09%; 95% CI 33.28–38.43) and An. arabiensis (0.59%; 95% CI 0.25–1.15) (Table 2). Species proportions differed significantly within each district (p < 0.001 in all cases). Anopheles coluzzii reached 100% in Cotonou and Comè, and 90.07% (95% CI 85.89–93.36) across the Bar land zone. Anopheles gambiae predominated in the West Atacora zone (89.96%; 95% CI 85.64–93.34) and the Central cotton zone (79.88%; 95% CI 73.04–85.65). Anopheles arabiensis was detected only in the Central cotton zone (4.73%; 95% CI 2.07–9.11). The overall comparison of species proportions across agro-ecological zones was not statistically significant (p = 0.511).

P. falciparum infection rates and sporozoite index of the Anopheles gambiae complex

A total of 81 CSP-ELISA-positive specimens were detected among 1,355 individuals analyzed, yielding an overall sporozoite index (SI) of 5.98% (95% CI 4.84–7.37%) (Table 3). The SI of An. gambiae was 6.74% (33/489; 95% CI 4.85–9.33%) and that of An. coluzzii was 5.59% (48/858; 95% CI 4.25–7.34%); no positive individual was recorded for An. arabiensis (0/8; 95% CI 0.00–32.44%). At zone level, the highest SI was recorded in the West Atacora zone (11.20%; 95% CI 7.91–15.62%), with a district-level peak of 17.55% (95% CI 11.99–24.97%) for An. gambiae. in Boukoumbé. The lowest zone SI was observed in the Fisheries zone (2.85%; 95% CI 1.23–6.51%) and the Central cotton zone (1.18%; 95% CI 0.33–4.21%). No statistically significant difference in SI between species was detected within any district (all p ≥ 0.114), nor across zones overall (p = 0.512).

P. falciparum sporozoite load across agro-ecological zones

Sporozoite loads quantified in the 33 CSP-positive An. gambiae ranged from 12 to 162,257 pg across the three zones represented (Table 4). Twenty-nine of the 33 positive individuals originated from the West Atacora zone, where the overall median load was 746 pg (SD: 30,062 pg; range: 12–162,257 pg). Within that zone, median loads were 2,402 pg in Djougou (n = 6) and 699 pg in Boukoumbé (n = 23). Two positive specimens were recorded in the Central cotton zone (Glazoué; mean: 650 pg; SD: 763 pg) and two in the Far North zone (Malanville; mean: 267 pg; SD: 360 pg). No significant difference in sporozoite load across zones was detected (Kruskal–Wallis: H = 3.25, df = 2, p = 0.197).

Comparison of sporozoite load between species

When sporozoite loads were compared between the two principal vector species across all zones combined, An. coluzzii exhibited a significantly higher parasite burden than An. gambiae (Mann–Whitney U = 381, p < 0.001; Kruskal–Wallis-equivalent H = 15.61, df = 1, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). The median load was 702 pg (IQR: 502–2,896 pg) in An. gambiae compared with 5,924 pg (IQR: 1,727–10,805 pg) in An. coluzzii. The distribution was markedly right-skewed in both species, a pattern also reflected in the logarithmic scale used in Fig. 3, with the mean substantially inflated in An. gambiae (mean = 8,420 pg, SD = 28,280 pg) by a small number of individuals carrying extreme sporozoite burdens, including a maximum recorded value of 162,257 pg in a single specimen from Djougou.

Discussion

The present study documents the distribution of the An. gambiae complex and the associated P. falciparum sporozoite burden across seven agro-ecological zones of Benin. The observed species distribution, with An. coluzzii predominating in southern communes and An. gambiae in the centre and north, is consistent with the ecological partitioning widely described for these sibling species in West Africa [11, 23, 24]. This pattern reflects their contrasting larval habitat preferences, with An. gambiae is primarily associated with temporary, sunlit rainwater pools prevalent in the less-urbanized savannah-type environments of central and northern Benin, whereas An. coluzzii preferentially exploits the permanent or semi-permanent anthropogenic breeding sites such as irrigated rice paddies and urban drainage pools, more characteristic of the south [11].

The exclusive presence of An. coluzzii in the Fisheries zone (Cotonou and Comè) is consistent with the coastal, highly urbanized character of this zone, where human-modified permanent water bodies abound. More noteworthy is the dominance of An. coluzzii in Malanville, a northern locality climatically more typical of An. gambiae territory. This anomaly may reflect the influence of permanent irrigation infrastructure along the Niger River floodplain, creating year-round larval habitats that favour An. coluzzii, and is consistent with a documented northward range expansion of this species in Benin and elsewhere in West Africa [25].

The detection of An. arabiensis exclusively in Glazoué and Parakou (Central cotton zone) is noteworthy. Although historically associated with the arid savannahs north of Benin, previous studies by Djogbénou et al. [25] and Koukpo et al. [23] have reported a progressive southward migration of this species in Benin. The zoophilic habits of An. arabiensis and the practice of cattle transhumance through the central cotton zone may facilitate its southward dispersal, as livestock movements provide bloodmeal opportunities at increasing distances from historical breeding areas. The ongoing modification of larval biotopes linked to agricultural intensification and climate-driven shifts in rainfall patterns are additional plausible drivers.

The co-occurrence of An. coluzzii and An. gambiae across most study zones underscores the compound vectorial risk they collectively represent for malaria transmission in Benin, as highlighted by Akpodji et al. [11], Koukpo et al. [23] and Gnanguenon et al. [26]. Both species display marked adaptive plasticity that may allow them to exploit novel habitats as environmental conditions shift under climate change.

The absence of significant inter-zonal variation in P. falciparum sporozoite load in either An. coluzzii or An. gambiae suggests that broad agro-ecological categories do not substantially constrain sporogonic development within individual infected mosquitoes. Temperature is the primary determinant of the extrinsic incubation period; however, ambient temperatures during the rainy season transmission peak are likely sufficiently permissive for complete sporogony across all study zones regardless of local microclimatic nuances [27, 28]. A methodological limitation must nonetheless be acknowledged, as ELISA-positive mosquitoes were unevenly distributed across zones, increasing the risk of a type-II error, and replication with larger zone-balanced samples is warranted [29]. Accordingly, the absence of statistical significance in these zone-level comparisons should not be interpreted as evidence of an absence of biological effect, particularly given the modest and s. distributed number of positive specimens per zone.

These findings resonate with other recent West African studies comparing infection dynamics between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii. In Burkina Faso, a direct comparison of oocyst growth between the two species using natural P. falciparum isolates found that parasite development rate depended on both mosquito and parasite genotype, underscoring genuine species-level variation in permissiveness beyond simple infection prevalence [30]. In Benin, molecular-level differences in circumsporozoite antigen carriage between An. gambiae and An. coluzzii have similarly been reported across the same broad geographic setting as the present study [31]. This species-specific pattern has a long history in the West African literature, with early work in Senegal documenting differential P. falciparum susceptibility between the S and M molecular forms, the taxonomic precursors of An. gambiae and An. coluzzii [32], indicating that the divergence observed here is consistent with an established, if still mechanistically unresolved, regional pattern.

The higher sporozoite load recorded in An. coluzzii compared with An. gambiae (p < 0.001) merits particular attention, but should be interpreted with due caution because individual sporozoite load is only one component of vectorial competence, which also depends on infection prevalence, vector survival and the completeness of sporogonic development, none of which were directly measured here [33]. Several, nonmutually exclusive hypotheses may explain this species-level difference and warrant dedicated future investigation. An. coluzzii larvae in this study predominated in peri-urban, organically enriched and anthropogenic breeding sites, and differential larval-stage exposure to environmental microbiota between these habitats and the cleaner, more ephemeral sites typical of An. gambiae could shape adult immune priming and, in turn, permissiveness to Plasmodium sporogony [34]; however, the present cross-sectional design cannot establish the direction of any such effect. Bacterial and microsporidian endosymbionts constitute another plausible, more direct mechanism because both Wolbachia and Microsporidia have been shown to reduce Plasmodium infection intensity in Anopheles, including in a population from Mali [35] and in natural An. gambiae and An. coluzzii populations from Benin [36]. If baseline endosymbiont prevalence or strain composition differs between the two species at our study sites, this could plausibly contribute to the sporozoite-load difference observed here; however, we did not screen for endosymbionts in this study, and this explanation remains a hypothesis generated by, rather than tested in, the present work. Future studies pairing direct endosymbiont screening (Wolbachia and Microsporidia MB) with individual-level sporozoite quantification in field-collected An. gambiae and An. coluzzii from these agro-ecological zones are needed to test these hypotheses directly.

From a public health standpoint, even in the absence of zone-dependent differences in individual sporozoite load, the high sporozoite indices recorded in Boukoumbé (16.2%) and across the Bar land zone (up to 11.22% in Dogbo) confirm the persistence of transmission hotspots that warrant prioritized and sustained vector control interventions. This district-level heterogeneity in sporozoite index, rather than the broader agro-ecological zone categories examined for individual sporozoite load, is likely to represent the most actionable unit for programmatic decision-making. It supports geographically stratified vector control planning, with Boukoumbé and the Dogbo–Ifangni axis of the Bar land zone representing priority areas for intensified indoor residual spraying or supplementary larval source management, whereas lower-transmission districts such as Cotonou and Parakou may be adequately served by standard insecticide-treated net–based interventions. A single transmission-season, cross-sectional survey cannot, however, establish whether these hotspots are stable over time, and repeated sampling would be required before translating this pattern into a firm operational stratification.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Sampling relied exclusively on Human Landing Catch, which carries known ethical and methodological constraints (collector variability, nightly weather effects) that were not formally quantified. Only 1,355 of the 3,021 Anopheles gambiae s.l. collected were processed for molecular and sporozoite analyses, and ELISA-positive specimens were unevenly distributed across zones, particularly for An. gambiae, limiting the power of zone-level comparisons; a nonsignificant zone effect should not be read as evidence of no biological effect. Fine-scale environmental covariates (rainfall, temperature, agricultural practice, insecticide exposure) and direct endosymbiont data were not collected, and the single-season design precludes inference about seasonal variation.

Conclusions

Anopheles gambiae and An. coluzzii co-occur across most study localities in Benin, displaying a well-marked south-to-north distributional gradient. An. arabiensis remains a minor component of the complex, confined to the central cotton zone. The agro-ecological zone in which these vectors develop does not substantially shape the individual P. falciparum sporozoite burden carried by ELISA-positive mosquitoes. Conversely, vector species identity was associated with sporozoite load in this cross-sectional sample, with An. coluzzii harbouring higher parasite burdens than An. gambiae. This association is consistent with possible species-specific differences in vectorial competence, potentially mediated by immune conditioning during larval development and endosymbiont-driven refractoriness, but should be regarded as baseline, hypothesis-generating evidence rather than a causal finding. Future longitudinal investigations should characterize the immunological and microbiome-related mechanisms underlying this differential parasite permissiveness, while employing increased and zone-balanced sample sizes to improve the statistical resolution of zone-level comparisons.
