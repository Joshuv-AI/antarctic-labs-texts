Accuracy of Various Methods for the Surveillance of Hospital-acquired Venous Thromboembolism in a Children’s Hospital
Authors: Robert Marcel T. Huibonhoa, E. Vincent S. Faustino, Kimberly Burkiewicz, Phil Oelkers, Supriya Ravishankar, Sandeep Tripathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Accurate measurement is important in quality improvement. International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes are often used to measure hospital-acquired venous thromboembolism (HA-VTE) rates, but they have high detection error rates. Our objective was to compare HA-VTE rates of various automated calculation methods and their accuracy.Methods:Single-center retrospective study including children younger than 18 years admitted between 2018 and 2023. We developed 4 automated methods to identify potential HA-VTE cases using (1) ICD-10 codes, (2) vascular extremity ultrasound reports (USG), (3) hematology consult reasons (Heme), and (4) receipt of therapeutic-dosing enoxaparin (Meds). We reviewed charts to determine true HA-VTE, defined as the radiographic presence of a thrombus. Additionally, we reviewed all charts with a vascular extremity ultrasound performed during the study period for true HA-VTE. We computed the accuracy of each method.Results:Across 33,430 admissions, we detected 275 (0.82%) cases by ICD, 508 (1.5%) by USG, 151 (0.45%) by Heme, and 176 (0.53%) by Meds. We manually reviewed 869 charts, of which 180 (0.5%) had confirmed HA-VTE. The false-positive rate was highest for USG (70.7%), followed by ICD (52.4%), Heme (50.9%), and Meds (43.8%). The combined false-positive rate was 59.2%. Sensitivity was highest for USG (82.8%), followed by ICD (72.8%), Heme (41.1%), and Meds (55.0%). Specificity was highest for Meds and Heme (88.8%). Discriminative performance was best for Meds (with an area under the precision–recall curve: 44.3%).Conclusions:Combining multiple methods led to greater identification of HA-VTE without significantly increasing false-positive rates, making it useful for quality improvement surveillance. Detection based on Meds is a more accurate alternative to ICD codes.

INTRODUCTION

Hospital-acquired venous thromboembolism (HA-VTE) is a potentially preventable health burden in both adults and children.1,2 Based on database and registry studies that primarily used International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes to estimate prevalence, HA-VTE rates in children have increased over the past 2 decades.3–9 These studies have been pivotal in increasing awareness and promoting quality improvement (QI) initiatives in VTE and thromboprophylaxis among hospitalized children.10–12 However, the use of ICD codes for surveillance purposes has important limitations, as it may lead to inaccurate detection of true HA-VTE admissions. Using ICD codes alone does not distinguish between HA-VTE and community-acquired VTE; rather, it includes codes for both. Observational pediatric studies have shown that the accuracy of using ICD codes alone is suboptimal when determining HA-VTE rates.13–19 Inaccurate measurement of local institutional HA-VTE rates results in inaccurate reporting and detection, ultimately hindering the success and sustainability of QI efforts.20,21 Thus, ensuring accurate detection of HA-VTE rates is an important step when performing QI projects.

The Children’s Hospitals’ Solutions for Patient Safety (SPS) is a national network that aims to reduce harm to patients and employees in hospital settings. They suggest using an HA-VTE surveillance process that includes using 2 or more methods to identify potential HA-VTE admissions. These “automated” diagnostic validation processes include methods such as reviewing discharge ICD codes, radiology reports, hematology consult reasons, and new anticoagulant prescriptions. They suggest confirming true HA-VTE admissions by manual chart review, as documented in radiographic imaging reports, or by direct surgical visualization of a thrombus.22 There is a paucity of literature on the accuracy of these methods. Therefore, the objective of our study was to (1) compare the relative detection rates of these 4 automated diagnostic methods and (2) compute their accuracy.

METHODS

Study Subjects and Design

We conducted a single-center, retrospective study within our medical center. We included all children younger than 18 years old admitted to our hospital between 2018 and 2023. The institutional review board at Peoria, Illinois, approved our study in May 2024 (institutional review board no. 2187274). OSF HealthCare Children’s Hospital of Illinois is a 125-bed children’s hospital in Central Illinois and is part of a larger adult hospital (St. Francis Medical Center). It includes a 32-bed pediatric critical care unit (16 pediatric intensive care beds and 16 pediatric intermediate care beds) and a 61-bed neonatal critical care unit.

Study Procedures

We identified all eligible hospital admissions during the study period and developed 4 rule-based automated methods to search patients’ charts using Structured Query Language. We used each method to identify different cohorts of potential HA-VTE admissions based on the following: (1) ICD-10 code review (ICD-based rule), (2) vascular extremity ultrasound report review (USG), (3) hematology consult reason review (Heme), and (4) receipt of enoxaparin at a therapeutic dose (Meds). Using a rule-based approach, we iteratively revised the linguistic terminology to improve each method’s detection rate. For the ICD-based rule, we included admissions with ICD-10 codes used in recent pediatric VTE database literature and identified admissions that contained these codes within a patient’s diagnosis list. (See Supplementary Digital Content 1, which displays ICD-10 diagnosis codes for venous thromboembolism taken from Raffini et al,3 https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A753.) For USG, we identified admissions with vascular extremity ultrasound radiology reports positive for key terms synonymous with VTE, deep venous thrombosis (DVT), and pulmonary embolism (PE). We excluded other radiographic modalities and specifically used only vascular extremity ultrasound reports because the inclusion of other modalities, such as computed tomography (CT) scan for PE, decreased the accuracy of that individual method (most patients who had CT scans for PE received a vascular ultrasound as part of their work-up, thus, we observed that the addition of CT scans did not identify new cases but led to more false-positive [FP] cases). For Heme, we identified admissions with referral reasons to the hematology service that included key terms synonymous with VTE and physical examination findings such as “swelling” and “discoloration”. For Meds, we identified admissions that received enoxaparin at therapeutic doses (1–1.5 mg/kg, given subcutaneously twice daily) for the first time, as determined using the Lexidrug drug database. We used only enoxaparin and excluded other anticoagulants because we found that inclusion of other drugs made the sensitivity and specificity worse during our exploratory analysis.

We defined HA-VTE using SPS operational definitions. These definitions included any thrombus within a deep vein or PE that occurred after 48 hours of admission or within 1 week of hospital discharge. We included any time frame of occurrence if the thrombus was associated with the insertion of a new central venous catheter during the patient’s admission (Supplementary Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A753).

We then manually reviewed the charts of all admissions flagged by the 4 automated rule-based methods to confirm that the current admission included a true HA-VTE. Additionally, we manually reviewed the charts of all patient admissions in which a vascular extremity ultrasound was performed during the study period to ensure that we did not miss true HA-VTE admissions that automated, rule-based methods failed to capture. Three investigators (R.M.T.H., K.B., and S.R.) independently performed the review. We confirmed true HA-VTE admissions based on evidence of a radiographically or surgically visualized thrombus. We defined a confirmed VTE that did not meet these criteria as a community-acquired VTE.

After manually verifying the charts, we identified true positives [TP] and FP for each method. By manually verifying all admissions identified across all 4 methods and comparing them with the number of admissions performed during the study period, we determined the true and false negatives for each method within this detection cohort. This approach allowed us to create 2 × 2 contingency tables to compute the diagnostic accuracy of each automated method.

Statistical Considerations

We conducted statistical analysis using JMP Pro V 17.0 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC) and the open-source statistical program R (R Core Team).23 We analyzed categorical variables using descriptive statistics, frequency, and percentage. We calculated the diagnostic accuracy of the various methods (sensitivity, specificity, precision, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC], and area under the precision–recall curve [AUPRC]). We used manual verification of HA-VTE on chart review as the gold standard. This analysis was also limited to the charts that were manually reviewed. We calculated exact binomial CIs for sensitivity, specificity, and precision using the Clopper–Pearson method, and the CI for AUROC using the default DeLong method in the R package pROC. Finally, we used bootstrap with 1000 resamples to calculate the CI for AUPRC.

RESULTS

Study Demographics

During the 5-year study period (2018–2023), there were 33,430 admissions among 22,709 unique patients. The age distribution of our cohort was as follows: 6966 (20.8%) were younger than 1 year, 19,177 (57.3%) were aged 1–11 years, and 7287 (21.7%) were aged 12–17 years. A total of 12,701 (37.9%) admissions were to critical care units. There were 1807 (5.4% of all admissions) with a reported central line present at admission or inserted during the admission.

Identification of HA-VTE Using Automated Rule-based Methods and Their TP and FP Rates

Automated rule-based methods flagged potential HA-VTE admissions across 33,430 total admissions. ICD-based rule flagged 275 admissions (0.82%), USG flagged 508 admissions (1.5%), Heme flagged 151 admissions (0.45%), and Meds flagged 176 admissions (0.53%). Overall, the combination of all 4 methods flagged 690 (2.1%) admissions as potential HA-VTE cases. A large majority were flagged by only 1 method (438/690, 63.4%), 17.1% (118/690) by 2 methods, 14.4% (100/690) by 3 methods, and only 4.9% (34/690) by all 4 methods. A review of vascular extremity ultrasounds performed during the study period, in addition to USG, identified an additional 190 admissions not detected by USG. We manually verified 869 admissions. On manual review, we confirmed community-acquired VTE in 235 charts (0.7%) and HA-VTE in 180 charts (0.5%) (Fig. 1).

Despite identifying the greatest number of potential HA-VTE admissions, USG had the highest FP rate (70.7%). Using the sum of FPs across all 4 methods, the combined FP rate was 59.2%.

Accuracy of the Various Methods

The cohort of 869 manually verified admissions was used to calculate the accuracy of each automated rule-based method. We created 2 × 2 contingency tables for each of the 4 automated rule-based methods. Among the 4 methods, USG was the most sensitive for detection of HA-VTE (sensitivity 82.8%; 95% CI, 76.5%–88.0%), but was the least specific (specificity 47.9%; 95% CI, 44.1%–51.7%) and had the lowest positive predictive value (PPV) (29.3%; 95% CI, 25.4%–33.5%). Both Meds and Heme had the highest specificity (88.8%; 95% CI, 86.2%–91.1%). However, Meds had relatively higher sensitivity (sensitivity 55.0%; 95% CI, 47.4%–62.4%) and PPV (56.2%; 95% CI, 48.6%–63.7%) compared with Heme, which was the least sensitive among all 4 methods (sensitivity 41.1%; 95% CI, 33.8%–48.7%) (Tables 1) (Supplementary Digital Content3, https://links.lww.com/PQ9/A752).

Accuracy of Various Diagnostic Methods for Automated HA-VTE Identification (Gold Standard = Manual Verification)

We assessed the diagnostic accuracy of these methods against the gold standard of manual chart verification, yielding AUROC and AUPRC values. Among the 4 rule-based methods, the discriminative performance was highest for the ICD-based rule (AUROC 75.9; 95% CI, 72.3–79.5 and AUPRC 42.6; 95% CI, 37.5–48.5), followed by Meds (AUROC of 71.9; 95% CI, 68.1–75.7 and AUPRC 44.3; 95% CI, 38.3–50.7). USG and Heme showed lower discrimination (Table 1).

Assuming we captured all potential HA-VTE across the 33,430 admissions using automated, rule-based methods and by reviewing all vascular extremity ultrasound reports performed during the study period, the 180 admissions captured represent the true number of HA-VTE in the total population. Combining all 4 automated rule-based methods will yield high sensitivity/specificity (>98%) and negative predictive value (>99%), but at a prevalence of 0.5%, the PPV remains low at 24.3% (95% CI, 22.6–25.9).

DISCUSSION

In this single-center retrospective study, we developed 4 automated, rule-based methods to identify potential pediatric HA-VTE admissions. We found that USG- and ICD-based rules identified the greatest number of potential HA-VTE admissions and, correspondingly, more TP HA-VTE admissions. However, a higher FP rate than the other 3 methods was accompanied by a higher USG detection rate. The combination of all methods detected more potential HA-VTE admissions, with its combined FP rate comparable to the median FP rate across the individual methods. The combined FP rate was also not higher than that of USG, which had the highest individual FP rate. As individual methods, we found that the ICD-based rule and Meds had the best discriminative performance as demonstrated by their AUROC and AUPRC. Meds performed slightly better than ICD-based rules on precision–recall due to its high specificity, potentially making it a more accurate individual alternative to the more common use of ICD codes for HA-VTE identification.

During our manual verification, we found that the low specificity of USG was largely due to the detection of admissions in which suspected VTE, based on limb swelling, prompted appropriate work-up, but resulted in a negative test. Other factors that contributed to the reduced specificity included an old vascular extremity ultrasound result with a positive finding and the presence of an arterial or superficial venous thrombus. For the ICD-based rule, a past diagnosis of resolved VTE that was inaccurately coded during the current admission was a common cause of reduced accuracy. For Heme, the high specificity was likely due to the high likelihood of a true HA-VTE if referred to the hematology service after diagnosis. Likewise, Meds’ high specificity was likely due to enoxaparin being the most prescribed anticoagulant for HA-VTE treatment at our institution, whereas the indications for prescribing unfractionated heparin were not specific to VTE (eg, mechanical circulatory support, anticoagulation, treatment of arterial thrombus, and mechanical/artificial cardiac valves), thus resulting in decreased specificity without improved sensitivity. We also excluded other anticoagulants, apart from unfractionated heparin, such as direct oral anticoagulants, when identifying this cohort, as our standard practice at the time did not include direct oral anticoagulants at the onset of therapy for newly diagnosed VTE. Despite focusing only on vascular extremity ultrasounds to detect HA-VTE in USG, we identified many cases of PE on manual verification. This finding is likely due to providers ordering vascular extremity ultrasounds as part of the work-up for those with confirmed PE. Overall, however, we are unsure how many PE cases were undetected by our methods. Although we did detect cases of nonextremity DVT, such as cerebral sinus vein thrombosis and/or portal vein thrombosis, it is possible that we missed cases due to the exclusion of other radiographic ultrasound locations and imaging modalities in our methods.

Our cohort had 180 unique admissions (0.5%) with true HA-VTE during the 5-year study period from 2018 to 2023. This frequency is similar to that reported in recent literature for pediatric HA-VTE.2,3,9 The Children’s Hospital-Acquired Thrombosis project, a multicenter registry for pediatric HA-VTE, similarly used electronic medical record triggers based on combinations of ICD codes, anticoagulant orders, and hematology consults, with the requirement that radiology reports confirm VTE, to develop a derivation cohort of HA-VTE admissions.6 They found an incidence of 0.12%–0.37%, slightly lower than our observed frequency.6 However, this is likely to be confounded by differences in study periods, with our cohort identifying admissions in more recent years, when HA-VTE rates have been rising. Similarly, our frequency is higher than that reported in database studies specifically examining pediatric VTE using manually verified ICD codes.5 Without manual verification, our automated ICD-10 diagnosis code method yielded a detection rate of 0.8% for all-cause VTE (both HA-VTE and community-acquired VTE), comparable to recent pediatric VTE database studies that used discharge ICD codes only and were not manually verified.3,9 Finally, similar to our methods, a cross-sectional study conducted in Cincinnati that implemented multiple phases and added a new detection method at each phase found improved detection rates for pediatric HA-VTE by the end of the implementation period.24

Retrospective surveillance of HA-VTE relies on manual chart review, which is cumbersome. Prospective surveillance is ideal, but not all hospitals have the resources to do this. Although diagnostic accuracy is limited when automated methods are used without manual chart review, these methods are more practical and feasible. Establishing an automated HA-VTE diagnostic validation process that detects the greatest number of true HA-VTE cases while maintaining a low FP rate balances the limitations of such a process. Our findings suggest that combining multiple methods for detecting HA-VTE is a reasonable approach when the surveillance goal is to identify the greatest number of true HA-VTE admissions without significantly increasing FPs. This possibility needs to be confirmed prospectively, which will also be the next step in our process. The iterative process we applied to the language and terminology of the 4 methods (to improve their individual accuracy) is unique to our hospital. We advocate measuring the validity of VTE detection methods before initiating QI projects related to VTE. Other hospitals would need to determine their own inclusions and exclusions specific to their local practice. The screening methods would also need to be updated periodically to reflect evolving practices in coding, medication use, consulting, and diagnostic testing. Our study has several important limitations. First, a manual review of all eligible charts is not feasible; therefore, determining the true prevalence of HA-VTE is difficult. The performance of the rules and key terms we used determined each method’s accuracy, and we could have improved it with continued iteration. We also did not explore combining terminologies to exclude negative radiographic reports, which could have improved the specificity of the review of vascular extremity ultrasounds. As previously mentioned, we may have underestimated the detection rate for nonextremity DVT because we did not review other ultrasound sites and imaging modalities. PE or nonextremity thrombosis may be missed in centers where ultrasound is not routinely paired with a PE work-up. The current literature uses a wide range of definitions for pediatric HA-VTE. We may therefore have underestimated our true HA-VTE frequency by using SPS definitions that set a 7-day HA-VTE cutoff.5 Finally, we may have missed HA-VTE cases diagnosed after discharge that did not require hospitalization.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, we found that combining all 4 automated, rule-based methods yielded a higher HA-VTE detection rate without significantly increasing the baseline FP rate. Local adaptation of our process could be useful for surveillance in QI initiatives, without requiring manual chart verification. We advocate investigating local measurement validity and standardizing surveillance definitions for pediatric HA-VTE to guide future retrospective studies and QI projects.
