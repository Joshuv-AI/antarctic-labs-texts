Diagnostic Utility of Extracellular Levels of Serum Amyloid P and Proteoglycan 4: A Case-Control Study in Gestational Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Jing Zhong, Heming Wu, Qiuhong Huang, Zhiyuan Zheng, Liubing Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is linked to excessive inflammatory activation during pregnancy. Serum amyloid P component (SAP) and proteoglycan 4 (PRG4) participate in inflammatory and placental regulatory processes; however, their relationships with GDM remain insufficiently clarified. This exploratory single-center retrospective study aimed to preliminarily investigate the associations of circulating SAP and PRG4 concentrations with GDM.MethodsA total of 111 Pregnant women undergoing 24–28 week OGTT screening were enrolled and divided into the GDM group and normal glucose tolerance (NGT) group. Serum SAP and PRG4 levels were measured by ELISA. Baseline characteristics, biomarker concentrations and relevant clinical indices were compared between groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and logistic regression analyses were used to evaluate their associations and predictive value.ResultsThe median serum levels of proteoglycan 4 (PRG4) and serum amyloid P (SAP) were 1.81 (1.11, 2.60) ng/mL and 308.55 (227.06, 437.02) ng/mL, respectively. The serum SAP level in the GDM group [437.66 (366.69, 543.72) ng/mL] was significantly higher than that in the NGT group [232.22 (203.12, 295.86) ng/mL] (p<0.001). The AUC of SAP for predicting GDM was 0.886 (p<0.001). No significant difference in serum PRG4 levels was found between the two groups (p=0.111), with an AUC of 0.588 (p=0.111). Logistic regression analysis showed that serum SAP level ≥386.88 ng/mL was independently associated with GDM (odds ratio (OR)=3.292, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.270–8.528, p=0.014); advanced maternal age (≥30 years), history of induced abortion and history of adverse pregnancy were also independent risk factors for GDM.ConclusionElevated serum SAP may be correlated with GDM, whereas peripheral PRG4 showed no obvious association with gestational hyperglycemia in this cohort.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as any degree of glucose intolerance first identified during pregnancy, which has become one of the most prevalent metabolic complications in the perinatal period worldwide.1 Driven by the growing prevalence of obesity, sedentary lifestyles and delayed childbearing, the incidence of GDM is continuously rising, affecting 10–25% of pregnant women across the globe.2,3 This condition not only poses immediate risks to maternal and fetal health, including gestational hypertension, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and fetal distress,4 but also elevates the lifelong risk of diabetes mellitus in affected mothers and their offspring.5 Although numerous studies have been conducted, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms of GDM remain incompletely elucidated, involving complex interactions among insulin resistance, β-cell dysfunction, placental hormone disorders, inflammatory responses, and metabolic reprogramming.6,7 As GDM frequently occurs without prominent clinical symptoms, timely diagnosis becomes difficult without universal routine glucose tolerance screening among pregnant populations.8 Global researches have extensively explored risk factors for GDM, with several independent influencing factors confirmed, including advanced maternal age, overweight status, a prior history of GDM, and a family history of diabetes mellitus.6,9 Accordingly, discovery of robust serum biomarkers linked to GDM is of great significance for early screening, risk stratification, and formulation of targeted intervention regimens to optimize perinatal outcomes.

Inflammatory responses and metabolic disorders are closely intertwined with the pathogenesis of GDM.10 Pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), can impair insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1) phosphorylation, disrupt insulin-mediated glucose uptake, and thereby exacerbate insulin resistance in GDM patients.11,12 Meanwhile, multiple macroscopic and microscopic placental structural abnormalities are well-documented in GDM, including enlarged placental weight, villous immaturity and excessive angiogenesis, which are direct consequences of sustained inflammatory and metabolic disturbance during pregnancy.13,14 As a member of the pentraxin family, serum amyloid P component (SAP) is a conserved reactant that participates in various physiological and pathological processes, including inflammatory responses,15 tissue repair,16,17 and metabolic homeostasis regulation.18 A proteomic study has identified SAP as a potential predictor of GDM, with its serum levels significantly elevated in pregnant women with GDM compared to those with normal glucose tolerance (NGT).19 Although some studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the correlation between SAP and GDM-related metabolic indicators, accumulating evidence suggests that SAP may be involved in the inflammatory cascade associated with GDM, thereby contributing to the development of insulin resistance and glucose metabolism disorders.20–22 Prior researches observed the relationship of SAP and GDM, yet few clinical cohorts have validated its independent predictive value for GDM.

Proteoglycan 4 (PRG4), also known as lubricin, is a cell surface proteoglycan that plays a key role in maintaining tissue homeostasis, regulating cell adhesion, and inhibiting inflammation in various tissues.23,24 As an adipokine, PRG4 potentiates insulin receptor signaling and facilitates adipocyte differentiation; furthermore, circulating PRG4 levels have been correlated with obesity, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes mellitus.25,26 In addition, its altered local expression may disrupt placental homeostasis. Additionally, dysregulated local PRG4 expression may impair placental homeostasis. Considering its modulatory functions in insulin signaling and metabolic pathways, we hypothesized that PRG4 participates in the pathogenesis of GDM. The study by Kristiansson et al demonstrated that PRG4 is associated with cardiovascular and metabolic health in women after GDM.27 Although existing clinical evidence connects PRG4 to metabolic disorders, the scarcity of clinical data obtained during mid-gestation means its value as a biomarker for gestational inflammation or glycemic abnormalities remains unvalidated.

To date, the differential associations of SAP and PRG4 with GDM remain unclear. Clarifying the specific relationships between these two proteins and GDM can not only enrich our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of GDM but also provide new insights for the development of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Existing literature only offers observations without systematic verification of the two proteins’ diagnostic capacity, and most analyses focus on placental tissue rather than clinically accessible serum samples. This study compares serum SAP and PRG4 levels between women with normal glucose tolerance and GDM, clarifies their divergent correlations with GDM, and assesses their predictive performance.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

This study was a retrospective observational study conducted at Meizhou People’s Hospital, which was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Meizhou People’s Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. Pregnant women who underwent routine prenatal examination and glucose tolerance testing between April 2024 and October 2025 were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) singleton gestation; (2) gestational age of 24–28 weeks (the period for routine GDM screening); (3) absence of pregestational diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, autoimmune diseases, or other metabolic disorders; (4) no administration of medications potentially affecting glucose metabolism or inflammatory markers within 1 month before enrollment; and (5) complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) multiple gestation; (2) fetal malformation or abnormal pregnancy outcomes; (3) incomplete clinical data or failure to complete the follow-up; (4) occurrence of acute infection or inflammation throughout the study period. A total of 111 pregnant women were enrolled in this study.

Diagnosis of GDM was performed in accordance with the criteria established by the International Association for Diabetes in Pregnancy Society Group (IADPSG).28 All enrolled participants received a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation. The diagnostic thresholds were set as follows: fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour postload plasma glucose (1h PG) ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, and 2-hour postload plasma glucose (2h PG) ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. GDM was confirmed when any single threshold above was satisfied.

Collection of Clinical and Laboratory Data

General clinical information of all participants was collected through electronic medical records and face-to-face interviews, including age, gestational age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), parity, history of adverse pregnancy, family history of diabetes mellitus, and first trimester prenatal ultrasound results (including nuchal translucency (NT) (mm), biparietal diameter (mm), and crown-rump length (CRL) (mm)). This retrospective observational study adopted consecutive sampling rather than matched case-control design. All eligible participants were enrolled sequentially and classified into GDM case group and NGT control group solely according to OGTT diagnostic outcomes, without prior matching for maternal age, gestational age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity or other clinical covariates.

A history of adverse pregnancy outcomes mainly encompasses spontaneous abortion, embryonic growth cessation, fetal malformations or structural defects, postpartum hemorrhage, and other related conditions. In accordance with the Chinese national criteria, BMI was categorized into three subgroups: below 18.5 kg/m2, 18.5–23.9 kg/m2, and 24.0 kg/m2 or above.29,30

Venous blood samples (5 mL) were collected from all participants after 8–12 hours of fasting in the morning. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C to separate serum, which was then stored at −80°C until detection to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), 1-hour postload plasma glucose (1h PG), and 2-hour postload plasma glucose (2h PG) levels were detected by the hexokinase method using an automatic biochemical analyzer. Serum SAP and PRG4 levels were detected by commercial ELISA kits [SAP kit: (Renjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China (Catalog Number: RJ14488)); PRG4 kit: (Renjie Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China (Catalog Number: RJ12160))] in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, fasting serum samples were thawed at room temperature and centrifuged before use. All samples were assayed in triplicate to reduce random measurement error. The absorbance values were measured by a microplate reader, and sample concentrations were calculated against the standard curve. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was <8%, and inter-assay CV was <10% for both biomarkers. Laboratory staff performing biomarker testing were blinded to the clinical grouping (GDM or NGT status) of specimens throughout all experimental procedures to minimize detection bias.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., USA) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). The normality of all continuous variables was evaluated via Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots to determine the selection of parametric or non-parametric statistical methods. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared by independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U-test. Pearson correlation was applied for normally distributed variables, while Spearman correlation was used for non-normal data. Categorical data were compared with χ2 test. The predictive performance of SAP and PRG4 for differentiating GDM patients from individuals with NGT was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and corresponding area under the curve (AUC). The optimal cutoff values for these two biomarkers were determined based on the Youden index. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify the independent factors associated with GDM, with GDM status (0 = NGT, 1 = GDM) as the dependent variable, and age, BMI, history of induced abortion, history of cesarean section, history of adverse pregnancy, history of oral contraceptive use, fetal ultrasound abnormality, serum SAP level, and serum PRG4 level as independent variables. All variables were incorporated into multivariate logistic regression alongside serum SAP and PRG4 levels to adjust confounding effects and identify independent risk factors for GDM. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

With respect to obstetric history, the proportion of women with a history of induced abortion was higher in the GDM group (28.6%, 14/49) than in the NGT group (12.9%, 8/62), with a marginally significant difference (χ2= 4.228, p=0.055). No significant intergroup differences were observed regarding previous cesarean delivery (χ2= 0.019, p=1.000) or prior adverse pregnancy outcomes (χ2= 0.426, p=0.643). Notably, none of the participants in the GDM group reported prior oral contraceptive use, which differed significantly from the NGT group (11.3%, 7/62; χ2= 5.905, p=0.017). Fetal ultrasound abnormalities were absent in the GDM group, whereas 4 cases (6.5%) were identified in the NGT group; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (χ2= 3.279, p=0.129).

No significant intergroup differences were observed in fetal biometric parameters, including nuchal translucency, biparietal diameter and crown-rump length (CRL) (all p>0.05). In comparison, significant differences were found in glucose metabolic markers between the two groups. The median FPG, 1h-PG and 2h-PG levels in the GDM group were 4.72 (4.31, 5.25) mmol/L, 10.20 (9.23, 10.79) mmol/L and 8.64 (7.70, 9.04) mmol/L, respectively, which were markedly elevated relative to those in the NGT group [4.27 (4.08, 4.44) mmol/L, 7.72 (6.48, 8.63) mmol/L, 6.43 (5.78, 7.04) mmol/L] (Z = −4.921, −7.590, −7.374; all p<0.001).

ROC Curve Analysis of Serum SAP and PRG4 for Predicting GDM

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive value of serum SAP and PRG4 levels for GDM, and the results are shown in Figure 2. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) of serum SAP level for predicting GDM was 0.929 (95% CI: 0.884–0.974, p<0.001), indicating a good predictive performance. In contrast, the AUC of serum PRG4 level for predicting GDM was 0.588 (95% CI: 0.483–0.694, p=0.111), which was not statistically significant, suggesting that serum PRG4 level has no predictive value for GDM. The optimal cut-off value of serum PRG4 level was 1.36 ng/mL, with a sensitivity of 75.5% and a specificity of 43.5%, further confirming that PRG4 cannot effectively distinguish pregnant women with GDM from those with NGT.

Discussion

This study comprehensively investigated the differential associations between serum SAP, PRG4 levels and GDM, as well as the related risk factors in a cohort of 111 pregnant women. The core findings revealed that: (1) serum SAP level was significantly elevated in GDM patients and showed independently associated with GDM; (2) serum PRG4 level showed no significant difference between GDM and NGT groups, with no association with GDM; (3) advanced maternal age (≥30 years), history of induced abortion and history of adverse pregnancy were associated with GDM. These observations imply a potential role of SAP in identifying GDM and supply new evidence relevant to early detection of GDM. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, only correlational relationships can be identified, and causality cannot be confirmed.

The conclusion that serum SAP level was significantly increased in GDM patients was consistent with part of the existing research. Ravnsborg et al found through proteomics-based analysis that serum SAP levels in peripheral blood of pregnant women with GDM in early pregnancy were significantly higher than those in the control group.19 Can et al reported that SAP levels in patients with metabolic syndrome were significantly higher than those in the control group.31 As a member of the pentraxin family, SAP is an important acute-phase reactant closely related to inflammatory responses.18 Moreover, SAP acts as an inhibitor of the aggregation process of islet amyloid polypeptide (IAPP) associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus.32 Previous studies have shown that SAP can participate in the metabolic regulation process by activating inflammatory signaling pathways, promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and impairing insulin signaling transduction.33,34 In this study, the AUC of serum SAP for predicting GDM reached 0.929, with high sensitivity and specificity, indicating its good clinical application potential as a biomarker. In addition, Scarale MG et al found that SAP has the potential to predict the risk of mortality in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus.35 The relationship between SAP and clinical outcomes in patients with GDM was not assessed in the present study.

The mechanism by which elevated SAP levels contribute to GDM may be related to the regulation of insulin resistance and inflammatory responses.34 SAP participates broadly in modulating systemic inflammatory responses, immune modulation, and metabolic homeostasis.15–18 Its potential link with GDM may be intrinsically associated with inflammatory dysregulation and insulin resistance, which constitute the core pathological mechanisms underlying GDM pathogenesis.36 Accumulating evidence has verified that GDM is not simply a metabolic disorder; its onset is strongly correlated with dysregulated immune responses, and abnormal expression of inflammatory cytokines acts as a key driver of disease progression.37 Elevated SAP facilitates overactivated NF-κB inflammatory signaling and excess release of TNF-α and IL-6, which collectively impair peripheral insulin signal transduction and trigger insulin resistance, the core metabolic defect of GDM.38 Insulin resistance is the core pathological mechanism of GDM, which leads to the decrease of insulin-mediated glucose uptake and utilization, resulting in elevated blood glucose levels.39 Meanwhile, SAP interferes with placental hormone synthesis and nutrient transport at the maternal-fetal interface, disrupting placental homeostasis and aggravating gestational glycemic dysregulation.40 Physiologically, mild inflammatory and metabolic remodeling serves as normal metabolic adaptation for gestation. GDM triggers prominent macroscopic and microscopic placental remodeling, such as increased placental weight, villous maturational defects and disordered angiogenesis, all of which stem from chronic inflammatory activation and disturbed maternal-fetal metabolic crosstalk.13,14 Elevated SAP facilitates the pathological progression of GDM through multiple pathways: it amplifies systemic inflammatory signaling to induce peripheral insulin resistance, and interferes with placental hormone secretion and nutrient transport to aggravate villous developmental abnormalities. Collectively, these multi-pathway biological effects well explain why elevated serum SAP is closely and independently correlated with GDM in our mid-trimester cohort.

However, it remains unclear whether elevated SAP is specifically related to GDM or represents a marker of non-specific inflammatory and metabolic activation in pregnancy. SAP is an acute-phase reactant that can increase under diverse inflammatory stimuli.18 Physiological gestation is accompanied by mild inflammatory remodeling, whereas exaggerated inflammatory responses promote insulin resistance and the onset of GDM.41,42 In the present adjusted regression model, higher SAP maintained an independent association with GDM, suggesting that SAP elevation extends beyond common gestational adaptive changes and correlates with pathological glucose intolerance. Even so, we cannot confirm SAP as a GDM-specific biomarker, given that its concentration may also increase in other pregnancy-related inflammatory disorders.40,43 Future studies incorporating additional groups with gestational inflammatory or metabolic complications will help distinguish whether SAP alterations are unique to GDM or reflect a shared inflammatory phenotype.

The finding that serum PRG4 concentrations were not significantly correlated with GDM was consistent with the hypothesis of the present study and several previous reports. As a multifunctional proteoglycan, PRG4 participates importantly in maintaining tissue homeostasis, modulating cell adhesion and suppressing inflammation.23,24 PRG4 has been shown to be associated with metabolic diseases such as obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus.25 Nevertheless, data regarding the role of PRG4 in GDM under the unique physiological conditions of pregnancy remain scarce. In the current study, PRG4 levels did not differ significantly between the GDM and NGT groups, and ROC curve analysis further revealed that PRG4 lacked predictive value for GDM. It may be due to the complex regulatory network of the maternal-fetal unit during pregnancy, and the expression and function of PRG4 in the maternal body may be affected by multiple factors such as hormones, immune environment, and metabolic changes.44,45 The non-significant association observed for circulating PRG4 should be interpreted with caution and does not exclude potential role of PRG4 in GDM pathophysiology, timing of sampling, biological variation, and assay sensitivity. First, PRG4 is predominantly produced locally within placental tissues, and serum concentrations may not faithfully mirror its expression at the maternal-fetal interface. Complex gestational endocrine regulation could alter PRG4 shedding into peripheral blood, possibly counterbalancing changes triggered by glucose intolerance. Second, biomarker sampling was restricted to a single time window of 24–28 weeks of gestation concurrent with OGTT. Placental function and PRG4 secretion vary progressively throughout gestation, so measurements solely at mid-pregnancy may fail to capture differences that emerge earlier or later in pregnancy. Third, circulating PRG4 undergoes substantial biological variation driven by local placental production, proteolytic cleavage and pregnancy-specific endocrine changes, which could obscure intergroup differences in peripheral blood. In addition, the sensitivity and measurable dynamic range of the immunoassay set a threshold for reliably detecting small effect sizes. Minor alterations in PRG4 related to hyperglycaemia may fall below the resolvable difference of the assay. Taken together, these limitations mean we cannot exclude a biological role of PRG4 in GDM; serial sampling across trimesters and more sensitive detection methods would help further clarify this question.

The expression and function of PRG4 exhibit tissue- and time-dependent specificity.46 In non-pregnant individuals, PRG4 is mainly expressed in tissues such as the joint cavity, synovium, and reproductive system. Its core physiological function is to reduce tissue friction and protect cells from mechanical damage by forming a lubricating layer, while participating in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses.47 A study has shown that Prg4-knockout mice are protected against high-fat diet-induced glucose intolerance.25 Nevertheless, human studies exploring the association of PRG4 with glucose metabolism and insulin resistance remain absent. Several studies have indicated that PRG4, as a cell-surface proteoglycan, is primarily involved in the arthritis,47 modulation of obesity,48 bone development,49 non-alcoholic fatty liver disease,25 and cancers.50 Among pregnant women, pregnancy-associated hormones may alter PRG4 expression within the placenta, uterine decidua and other tissues at the maternal-fetal interface. Such differential local expression may lead to an unchanged circulating PRG4 concentration.

In addition, the identification of advanced maternal age (≥30 years), history of induced abortion and history of adverse pregnancy associated with GDM was consistent with most domestic and foreign research results. Advanced maternal age has been widely recognized as an important risk factor for GDM.51,52 With the increase of age, the function of maternal pancreatic islets decreases, the sensitivity to insulin decreases, and the incidence of insulin resistance increases, which leads to an increase in the risk of GDM. The history of induced abortion may be related to the damage of the endometrium and the change of the internal environment of the uterus, which affects the normal development of the placenta and leads to the occurrence of GDM.53,54 The history of adverse pregnancy may be a reflection of the poor metabolic capacity and immune regulation ability of the maternal body, thus increasing the risk of GDM.55,56 These results further confirmed the importance of comprehensive risk assessment in the early screening of GDM.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, this is a single‑center, retrospective exploratory study with a moderate sample size (n = 111), which may introduce inherent selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings. All participants were enrolled from a single tertiary hospital, and the cohort may not fully represent pregnant populations from different geographical regions, ethnic backgrounds, or different socioeconomic and gestational care conditions. Therefore, our observed associations require further validation in large‑scale, multicenter, and prospective cohorts. Second, due to the cross‑sectional and observational nature of the study design, we can only identify statistical associations rather than establish causal relationships between serum SAP levels and GDM pathogenesis. We cannot definitively determine whether elevated SAP precedes the onset of gestational hyperglycemia and acts as a predisposing biomarker, or whether increased SAP expression merely serves as a secondary inflammatory response to GDM‑induced metabolic and placental dysfunction. Longitudinal prospective studies with serial gestational sampling are necessary to clarify the temporal sequence and potential causal mechanisms. Third, complete unified serum lipid profiles were not available for all participants in this retrospective cohort. Routine mid‑trimester GDM screening in clinical practice does not require mandatory lipid detection, resulting in missing triglyceride and high‑density lipoprotein data. Consequently, we were unable to analyze lipid-related metabolic indices (such as triglyceride/HDL ratio) or thoroughly explore the correlations between PRG4 and gestational lipid metabolic disorders. This deficiency may partially explain the neutral PRG4 findings in our current cohort. Future prospective studies with systematic collection of full metabolic and lipid parameters are warranted to further elucidate the potential metabolic regulatory role of PRG4 in pregnancy. Fourth, all biomarker measurements were performed at a single mid‑gestational time point (24–28 weeks). Dynamic gestational changes in placental function, systemic inflammation, and hormone levels may alter circulating SAP and PRG4 concentrations across different trimesters. Single‑time‑point sampling may fail to capture early or late gestational biomarker variations and may restrict the ability to detect subtle but meaningful metabolic differences between groups. Fifth, this study did not incorporate detailed lifestyle covariates, including dietary structure, physical activity, sleep status, and gestational weight gain, as well as genetic susceptibility factors. These variables are well‑recognized contributors to gestational insulin resistance and inflammatory status and may interact with SAP and PRG4 expression. Unmeasured residual confounding may therefore affect the stability of our association results. Further well‑designed studies integrating behavioral, clinical, and genetic factors will help construct a more comprehensive predictive and mechanistic model for GDM. Finally, this study lacked follow-up data on postpartum metabolic outcomes of enrolled participants. We only focused on mid-trimester biomarker characteristics and GDM diagnosis during pregnancy, without tracking postpartum glucose tolerance recovery, progression to type 2 diabetes, or long-term metabolic complications. Therefore, we cannot evaluate whether mid-gestational SAP alterations have predictive value for long-term postpartum metabolic dysfunction, nor can we clarify the persistent metabolic impact of abnormal inflammatory biomarkers after delivery. Future studies with continuous postpartum follow-up are required to explore the long-term clinical significance of SAP changes in GDM populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that elevated serum SAP concentrations are associated with GDM, whereas serum PRG4 levels show no significant correlation with GDM. Advanced maternal age, a history of induced abortion, and prior adverse pregnancy outcomes are also correlated with GDM. These findings offer new insights into the pathophysiology of GDM and point to a promising biomarker for early screening of GDM. Future multicenter, large-sample prospective investigations and mechanistic research are required to further validate and extend the present findings, thereby facilitating the development of more effective strategies for the prevention and management of GDM.
