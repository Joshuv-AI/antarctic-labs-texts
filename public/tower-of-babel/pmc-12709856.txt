Prevalence and risk factors associated with severe features of pre-eclampsia among women in Thailand
Authors: Nathida Wiriyachoke, Paweena Phaliwong, Sinart Prommas, Buppa Smanchat, Kornkarn Bhamarapravatana, Komsun Suwannarurk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe objective of this study is to investigate the prevalence of and identify risk factors associated with pre-eclampsia with severe features (sPE) and model such data to generate a predictive risk score.Material and methodThis retrospective case-control study was conducted at Bhumibol Adulyadej Hospital (BAH), between January 2017 and December 2024. Participants were pregnant women who delivered at BAH. Subjects who were diagnosed with sPE and normal were classified as the study and control groups. Data was collected from medical records.ResultThere were 30,620 deliveries during the study period. A total of 546 sPE cases were recruited. There were 500 participants in each group. The significant risk factors associated with sPE were BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (aOR: 2.05, 95%CI: 1.43-2.93), age ≥35 years (aOR: 2.33, 95%CI: 1.55 - 3.47), illicit drug use (aOR: 6.47, 95%CI: 2.29 - 18.33), chronic hypertension (CHT, aOR: 10.24 , 95%CI: 4.71, 22.26), diabetes mellitus (DM, aOR: 8.58, 95% CI: 2.46 - 29.93), familial history of hypertension (FHT, aOR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.1- 2.37) and previous history of preeclampsia (PEPP, aOR: 13.42, 95% CI: 2.95 - 61.04).The cut-off was established at a score of ≥ 4, which provided sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value, and accuracy at 54.2, 87.0, 80.7, 65.5 and 70.6 percent respectively.ConclusionThe prevalence of sPE was 17.8 per 1,000 deliveries. PEPP, CHT, DM, illicit drug use, advanced age (≥35 years), BMI ≥25 and FHT were associated with higher risk of sPE development at aOR of 13.42, 10.24, 8.58, 6.47, 2.3, 2.05 and 1.62, respectively. Keywords: preeclampsia with severe features, predictive model, risk factors, preeclampsia

Introduction

According to American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists guidelines (ACOG) 2020; pre-eclampsia with severe features (sPE) is defined as new-onset hypertension in pregnant women with systolic blood pressure of 160 mm Hg or more, or diastolic blood pressure of 110 mm Hg or more on two occasions measured within few minutes [1]. Other signs and symptoms that indicated severe features were thrombocytopenia, impaired liver function test, severe persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain unresponsive to medication, renal insufficiency, pulmonary edema, new-onset headache unresponsive to medication or visual disturbances [1]. Other clinical presentations of severe forms of preeclampsia were HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count) and eclampsia which presented with seizure [1, 2].

Pre-eclampsia with severe features is one of the leading causes of maternal morbidity and mortality. It accounts for 9% of maternal mortality in Asian and African women [1]. The worldwide incidence of pre-eclampsia and eclampsia is 2.16 and 0.28%, respectively. In Thailand, the incidence of preeclampsia is 2.2% and sPE is 10.1 per 1,000 deliveries [3]. Preeclampsia in Thailand also accounted for 7% of maternal death in 2024 [4]. Causes of maternal morbidity include stroke, pulmonary edema, acute kidney failure and coagulopathy resulting from high blood pressure [1]. Not only does sPE affect gravida from severe preeclampsia, but the fetus could also be affected by sPE as well. Negative outcomes for the fetus include non-reassuring fetal status, oligohydramnios and preterm birth [1].

Preventable and unpreventable risk factors of preeclampsia were already established [2]. Aspirin has been given to those high-risk groups, such as those who had history of previous pre-eclampsia (PEPP), multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension (CHT), diabetes mellitus (DM), renal or autoimmune disease [5]. There was limited data on the risk factors of sPE. Risk factors of SPE was investigated by many previous literatures [6–10]. Demographic characteristics, clinical data, hemogram, ultrasound finding and biomarker were reported as the risk factors. Some literature created the predictor model either web-based or AI machine [11].

The aim of this study was to identify the prevalence and risk factors that were associated with sPE in Thailand. A risk score for predicting the occurrence of sPE could be attempted. Predictive model via web-based medium was planned for general use.

Materials and methods

This was an unmatched retrospective case-control study that included all women who delivered in Bhumibol Adulayadej Hospital (BAH), between January 2017 and December 2024. This study was approved by the BAH Ethics Committee (IRB number 8/68) in 2025.

Data gathering from hospitalization records included diagnostic codes using the International Classification of disease [ICD-10] O14.1, O14.2 and O15, namely pre-eclampsia with severe features, HELLP syndrome and eclampsia. All pre-eclampsia on a severe feature spectrum including HELLP syndrome and eclampsia were included in sPE.

Inclusion criteria for the study group included all women who were diagnosed with preeclampsia with severe features before and after delivery (including both stillbirth and live birth). Inclusion criteria for the control group included all patients who delivered at the adjacent time to those in the study group. Exclusion criteria included those with insufficient documentation in medical records for diagnosis of sPE (diagnostic criteria based on ACOG 2020) [1].

This study retrieved the data from hospital data in main frame of BAH database. The data was linked to the other government computer base for accuracy of data. The review of data consists of obstetric and neonatal outcomes, maternal demographic data and route of delivery. Other information such as maternal illicit drug use, smoking, maternal underlying disease, maternal weight and height at delivery, monthly income, history of previous pregnancy or abortion were also collected by reviewing medical and labor records. Illicit drug (cannabis, amphetamine and mitragyna) use was confirmed by laboratory testing in those who self-reported history of substance abuse or who displayed signs and symptoms of substance abuse. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design of the study.

Socioeconomic status (SES) defined in this paper was classified according to Likitpattanakul by dividing average monthly income into 5 groups by using the data survey by National Statistical Official year 2021 [12]. The lowest income (quintile 1) was defined as the lowest SES. In the current study, monthly income was classified into three groups, the lowest, low-high and the highest income.

In Machano’s study, the proportion of sPE and non sPE among DM subjects were 0.047 (5/105) and 0.003 (1/295), respectively [6]. Alpha and beta errors were set at levels of 0.01 and 0.05, respectively. The number of study and control cases was used at a 1:1 ratio. A total sample size of 445 participants per group was calculated. Data on significant factors from previous literature was classified as incomplete data and removed. Missing data was set at 20%, so the total number of patients that were enrolled was 1,068 participants.

Demographic data was analyzed. Categorical data was reported as frequency and percentage. For continuous data, mean, standard deviation, median and interquartile range were used. For comparing between groups, independent t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Logistic regression was done for controlling the possible confounding factors and to assess the association with risk factors with sPE. The results were expressed as an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Risk score for sPE prediction was generated after collecting all significant risk factors. Risk score was calculated by using the lowest value of aOR from all significant risk factors as denominator. Selected significant factors were divided by the lowest significant factors. Risk scores are composed of summation of all significant risk factors. For internal validation, bootstrapping with 10,000 sampling times was performed. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and collinearity analysis was applied as appropriate to verify the adequacy of predictive models.

For easy application, the formula was cited via web-based for easy accessibility. Data analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was considered when p-value was less than 0.05.

Result

One-fourth of the study group and 10.8% of the control group were diagnosed with DM during pregnancy with statistical significance. Lowest socioeconomic status (SES) was defined as the first quintile group at the bottom of the income distribution according to Likitpattanakul’s report [12]. 15% (147/1000) of both groups had the lowest income (p-value = 0.19). The percentage of previous pre-eclampsia, CHT, family history of hypertension (FHT), illicit drug use, twin pregnancy and smoking of the study group were significantly higher than those in the control group.

One-third (385/1000) of participants had no children without clinically significant differences between both groups. The prevalence of sPE in the current study was 1.78% (546/30,620). Half of the study group (290/500) were between 20 and 35 years old and one-third (178/500) of subjects were older than 35 years old. Educational level, the percentage of nulliparity, the percentage of twin pregnancy, and average income of both groups were comparable.

For neonatal outcome, average gestational age, neonatal birthweight, and Apgar score at 5 min of the study group were significantly lower than results from the control group. The cesarean delivery rate (74.4 and 37.8%), estimated blood loss (400 and 300 ml) and NICU admission (6.2 and 0%) of the study group were significantly higher than those of the control group.

From multivariate logistic regression analysis, the significant risk factors associated with developing sPE were BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (aOR: 2.05, 95%CI: 1.43–2.93), age ≥ 35 years (aOR: 2.33, 95%CI: 1.57–3.47), illicit drug use (aOR: 6.47, 95%CI: 2.29–18.33), CHT (aOR: 10.24, 95%CI: 4.71, 22.26), DM (aOR: 8.58, 95% CI: 2.46–29.93), FHT (aOR: 1.62, 95% CI: 1.1–2.37) and PEPP (aOR: 13.42, 95% CI: 2.95–61.04) as described in Table 2. All statistically significant risk factors were analyzed and used to generate the predictive model. The predictive score was made by dividing the aOR of each factor with the lowest aOR in all factors. Numbers were rounded to whole digits at ≥ 0.5 as shown in Table 3.

From Table 3, collinearity was analyzed by using variance inflation factor (VIF) calculation. VIF of risk factors, namely BMI, age, illicit drug use, CHT, DM, FHT and PEPP, ranged between 1.07 and 1.24. Collinearity analysis revealed that none of the above risk factors exhibited collinearity (VIF ≤ 2, tolerance ≥ 0.1). All tolerance values were higher than 0.87, indicating minimal correlation among predictors. The Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated satisfactory model fit (χ² = 4.97, p = 0.55).

Discussion

Pre-eclampsia is one of the leading causes of maternal and neonatal morbidity. Maternal death usually occurs in sequences of pre-eclampsia progressive to sPE that cause multiple end -organ failure. Incidence of sPE in the current study was 17.8 per 1,000 deliveries. Previous study from Thailand by Kongwattanakul in 2018 reported sPE prevalence at 9.5 per 1,000 deliveries [13]. Kongwattanakul’s study was conducted in the Northeast of Thailand. The prevalence of the current study was higher than Kongwattanakul’s study (17.8 vs. 9.5 per 1,000 deliveries). The current study was conducted in the capital city of Thailand. Average age, BMI and maternal underlying disease of subjects in the current study were higher than those of Kongwattanakul’s study [13]. Previous studies from many countries reported the prevalence of sPE ranged from 4.2 to 260 per 1,000 deliveries [6, 7, 14, 15]. Vast differences of prevalence might be caused by many confounders such as criteria for diagnosis sPE, the facility that a given study is conducted in, race, and lack of aspirin prophylaxis.

Lisonkova reported from Canada in 2021 that prevalence of sPE in Canada is 5.2 per 1,000 deliveries. Subjects in Lisonkonva’s study had underlying disease and obesity at a percentage of 5.6 and 1.9, respectively. Underlying diseases such as chronic renal disease, CHT, diabetes mellitus plays a role in stage II (the maternal syndrome) preeclampsia pathogenesis where maternal conditions cause inflammation or endothelial cell activation [2]. The advanced maternal age of Lisonkova’s study was 20% compared to those in the current study 31.2% [7]. The lower percentage of maternal underlying disease, maternal age and maternal BMI might play a role in lower incidence of sPE.

According to Machano’s study done in Tanzania, criteria for diagnosis of sPE was mainly clinical diagnosis by high blood pressure (≥ 160/110 mmHg) and clinical signs of severe features [6]. Machano et al. also stated that there was no supply of aspirin prophylaxis in their country [6]. Subjects in Machano’s study compared to this work had average age (28.8/31.5 years) and underlying disease rates (3.5/38.4%); all numbers were lower than that of the current study. Overall subjects in Machano’s study semed to be at a lower risk of preeclampsia compared to current study. However, the prevalence of sPE in Machano was higher (260 per 1,000 deliveries) than the current study. Well known risk factors of preeclampsia were race (African carry higher risk than Caucasian and Asian), CHT, DM, advanced maternal age (AMA; age more than 35 years), PEPP and nulliparity. However, the risk of sPE development was unclear [2]. However, Machano reported that old spousal age (≥ 45 years), consecutive pregnancies with different partners and household smoking were the risk factors of sPE. The change of new partner antigen explained its risk factor for preeclampsia [2].

The current study found that illicit drugs namely methamphetamine (MA), cannabis and mitragyna (Kratom in Thai language) were the risk factors for sPE. Most pregnant women who used illicit drugs in the current study often consumed multiple illicit drugs due to low cost and availability, especially cannabis and mitragyna. Premchit and Hayer reported that MA was a risk factor for preeclampsia due to its sympathomimetic effects [16, 17]. Prewitt reported that cannabis was a risk factor for gestational hypertension and preeclampsia, likely due to its vasoconstrictive properties [18]. From literature review, Wright reported only the withdrawal symptoms namely diaphoresis, restlessness, anxiety and piloerection in pregnant women who use mitragyna [19]. Mainly, sPE from illicit drugs in this study might be from MA. The effect of mitragyna in sPE merits further study in the future.

Socioeconomic status (SES) was defined in this paper by classification via Likitpattanakul [12]. In the current study, monthly income was classified into three groups, the lowest, low-high and the highest income. There was no statistically significant difference of monthly income between the study and the control groups. Only a large study from Canada in 2021, Lisonkova reported the lowest SES was associated with sPE [7]. Most of Lisonkova’s subjects lived in urban areas and one-fifth of them were classified as the lowest SES [7].

Obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) was associated with sPE [7, 8, 14, 15]. From the current study, Obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) was associated with sPE (aOR 2.05) [20]. Cut point for obesity in Western and Asian countries were different [20]. Other papers from Thailand, Kongwattanakul and Sudjai, reported that obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) was related to late onset sPE [10, 13]. Insulin resistance was mostly presented in obesity and metabolic disease, activated low grade inflammatory, endothelial activation and increased sodium resorption [2]. Pathophysiology of obesity and their result in preeclampsia are caused by low-grade inflammation and endothelial reabsorption [2]. Exaggerated maternal inflammatory response to deport trophoblast induced sPE [1].

Advanced maternal age was an associated risk for sPE in the current study. This was the same result as in Lisonkova’s and Yang’s reports [7, 8]. Machano and Lisonkova reported that mothers of less than 20 and 25 years old were associated with sPE, respectively. These might be from an immature uterus and abnormal placentation from abnormal menstrual cycle which led to defective decidualization [21]. We added this factor even though age less than 20 years old was not associated with sPE in the current study. From the current study, nulliparity, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and twin pregnancy were not the associated risk factors for sPE. Yang reported that nullipara and GDM were the associated risk factors for sPE [8]. Catov and Machano also reported that twin pregnancy was the associated risk factor for sPE [6, 14].

Family history of hypertension (FHT; first degree relative) was already a known risk factor for pre-eclampsia [1]. The current study found that FHT was also a risk factor for sPE. This is consistent with findings from Bezerra and Machano’s study [6, 22]. Silent genetic causes such as genes involved with angiotensin receptors, tumor necrotic factor, the Leiden variant of coagulation factor and endothelial nitric oxide synthase, raised the possible risk for sPE [22].

CHT was an associated risk for sPE from the current study. This finding was in line with Lisonkova’s and Catov’s studies [7, 14]. DM in the present study was associated with risk factors for sPE, in the same manner as in Machano’s, Lisonkova’s and Yang’s papers [6–8]. The mechanism of preeclampsia was associated with placental implantation and cytokines which cause local inflammation [2]. Previous preeclampsia was an associated risk factor for sPE by Machano’s, Catov’s and the current studies [6, 14]. Comparisons of this study to previous literature were summarized and presented in Table 4. Aspirin prescription was mostly applied to high-risk pregnancy women for prevention of preeclampsia. The multivariate study of aspirin did not show the statistical factor. We decided to remove this factor from the risk score.

Associated factors for sPE from the current study were constructed to find the predictive score as shown in Table 3. The total score was summed together as sPE score. The cut-off points of SPE score equal or more than 4 was classified as high risk for developing sPE with area under the curve at 77%. At this point, the sensitivity was rather low (52.4%), but the specificity and positive predictive values (PPV) were 87.0 and 80.7%, respectively. The negative predictive value (NPV) was 65.5%.

This score had a good value if the score was positive (≥ 4). The low sensitivity (54.2%) and the fair NPV (65.5%) of subjects who had negative scores (≤ 3) needed further close evaluation and monitoring, especially during later periods. The web-based predictive model was generated with a QR code as attached in Fig. 2 for easy application. Further study with more subjects with this application might allow more accuracy of this model in the future.

The strength of this study was a population with rather high prevalence of sPE allowing many different risk factors to be examined. A web-based sPE predictive model with easy QR code access was generated. The sensitivity, specificity, NPV and PPV of sPE would be shown after the score was calculated. This is available for public use. Our study had some limitations. Due to the rather low sensitivity of our test, the sPE score might need further refinement before deployment as a screening tool. The predictive score derived from history alone has low sensitivity (54.2%) and moderate PPV (80.7%). The predicting model from the current study had good fitting, no collinearity between factors and minimal bias. Ultrasonography or serum screening are not readily available in low resource or developing countries. Risk scores that use history taking alone may be more applicable. Second, while reviewing our study, we found that the criteria for diagnosing sPE have changed over time, so the risk factors identified in earlier studies might need to be re-evaluated. Lastly, the retrospective design of the current study could not avoid the selection bias. This study showed that some risk factors for sPE can be modified. Our predictive model was generated via web-based access. Additional studies in multicenter were needed to validate the predictive model. Applying this model for health personnel in the hospital or clinic that having low resource might be benefit for parturient in low resource setting.

Conclusion

The prevalence of sPE was 17.8 per 1,000 deliveries. PEPP, CHT, DM, illicit drug use, advanced age (≥ 35 years), BMI ≥ 25 and FHT were associated with higher risk of sPE development at aOR of 13.42, 10.24, 8.58, 6.47, 2.3, 2.05 and 1.62, respectively.
