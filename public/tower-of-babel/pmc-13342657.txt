Comparative research on the influence of incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information on Chinese nulliparous women’s fertility intention
Authors: Hongkun Wang, Zonghai Chen, Qiaolei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines the impact of incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information from personal narratives on the fertility intentions of Chinese unmarried and married women, deploying the Theory of Planned Behavior to explain the subsequent information-processing mechanism. Utilizing data from two group-based surveys (N = 913), results show that incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information significantly reduces fertility intentions in both groups, while the mechanisms differ. For unmarried women, fertility intentions are primarily influenced by perceived internal behavioral control, while for married women, perceived external behavioral control is more significant. This research advances the understanding of health-related information acquisition and processing in the social media environment and offers insights for public policy aimed at addressing the low fertility rate in China and globally.

Introduction

Social media has become a vital space for the public to share health-related information1. The participatory affordances of social media allow users to share personal experiences, insights, and knowledge on various health-related issues, contributing to the widespread presence of health-related personal narratives. These narratives, comprising firsthand or secondhand accounts, personal perspectives, and emotions2, increase the visibility of highly private or culturally sensitive health topics3. In addition, it exerts greater persuasive power than factual information since it captures attention, improves comprehension for risks, and enhances recall of health-related information4,5.

In China, the continued overlap of structural trends – such as delayed marriage and childbearing among young people, a decline in the number of marriages, and a shrinking population of women of childbearing age – poses a significant challenge to maintaining a stable number of new births in recent years. In response, the government has adjusted its family planning policies to address declining fertility rates and negative population growth, which has drawn widespread public and media attention to fertility-related issues. Amid these public discussions, many women share their experiences of childbirth on social media platforms, turning fertility-related personal narratives into an important source of information. Although fertility-related information generally include both positive and negative aspects, personal narratives surrounding fertility in Chinese digital platforms are often framed around ideas such as “the costs of childbearing” or “fertility fear”6, highlighting the burdens and risks associated with childbearing, such as physical health risks, financial pressures, career interruptions, and gender inequality associated with motherhood. This study refers to this type of content as negative fertility-related information, and focuses specifically on it given its prevalence and visibility. Prior research shows that social media disseminates and amplifies negative fertility-related information, thereby undermining individual’s fertility intentions7. However, these studies have primarily focused on relationship between overall social media use and declining fertility intentions, without considering the specific information types and acquisition modes.

Social media not only enables users to actively seek for desired information but be incidentally exposed to content through technological affordances – such as dynamic online networks and algorithmic curation8,9. This acquisition mode – incidental exposure – can significantly influence individual’s cognition and decision-making10. While the relationship between information seeking and individual behavioral outcomes has been well explored in health communication literature11,12, the effects of incidental exposure, especially to personal narratives, remain under-studied. Given that incidental exposure has become a common information acquisition mode in social media13, and that personal narratives have become a key source of health-related content, it is essential to examine how incidental exposure to fertility-related personal narratives affects fertility intentions and the related information processing mechanisms. Understanding this process offers a meso-level explanation for declining fertility intentions in contemporary China and other low-fertility societies globally14, thus contributing to ongoing discussions around the role of media in shaping reproductive behavior.

This study examines the influence of incidental exposure to fertility-related personal narratives on Weibo on Chinese nulliparous women’s fertility intention. It employs the Theory of Planned Behavior – a widely-cited theory in reproductive research – to explore the underlying information-processing mechanisms. Given the prevalence of “fertility fear” sentiments fostered by such narratives, the study focuses specifically on incidental exposure to negative fertility-related content.

Recognizing the distinct cultural context that shapes fertility-related decisions in China, this study refines key variables by emphasizing perceived personal-level social norms and by distinguishing perceived behavioral control into internal and external dimensions. Furthermore, acknowledging the additional complexities that marital status introduces to fertility-related decisions15, the study also examines whether the main effects and underlying mechanisms differ between unmarried and married women.

By anchoring in the concepts of incidental exposure and personal narratives, this study contributes to the growing body of literature on health-related information acquisition and processing in social media contexts. It also responds to recent critiques calling for more culturally grounded investigations of social media incidental exposure beyond Anglo-Saxon settings16. Finally, the study offers practical insights for addressing low fertility rates in China and other low-fertility societies globally.

Literature review

Social media, information acquisition and health-related behaviors

Social media offers two distinct approaches for information acquisition. The first involves purposefully looking for specific information, such as information seeking and selective exposure11. The second approach occurs when users encounter information unintentionally, as exemplified by information scanning and incidental exposure. While information scanning and incidental exposure are often used interchangeably due to their shared element of non-intentionality, these two concepts emphasize different aspects. Information scanning is typically described in terms of breadth (the number of different sources from which information was acquired), depth (the richness of information gleaned), and frequency of exposure17. In contrast, incidental exposure primarily focuses on the frequency of exposure. For example, the measurement of incidental news exposure generally assesses how frequently individuals experience such encounters over a specific period18.

Compared to the effects of purposeful information acquisition on health-related outcomes, the influence of unintentional information acquisition on individuals’ health-related behaviors is less well understood. Few studies have addressed this issue by examining information scanning on social media. However, findings on the effects of information scanning have been inconsistent. For example, Zhu19 suggested that the more pro-smoking information a non-smoking young adult scans on social media, the more likely they are to experiment with smoking. In contrast, Liu, et al.20 found that the effect sizes of information scanning were smaller in promoting intentions to get vaccinated. Moreover, this line of research has largely overlooked the impact of personal narratives.

Incidental exposure has become increasingly prevalent in social media. It has been a subject of research in the mass media era21,22, but it heightened scholarly attention with the emergence of algorithmic recommendation13, online social network sharing23, or just the ubiquitous nature of certain type of information in online space24. Much of the existing literature on social media centers around political news and has demonstrated explicit influence of Incidental exposure on political behavior (Chen et al., 2021; Nanz & Matthes, 2020). However, the effects on non-political content have received limited attention. Goyanes and Demeter24 have realized this limitation, inviting scholars to examine more diverse scope, especially those relating to individuals’ daily life. Exploring how social media Incidental exposure impacts health-related behaviors and decision-making has potential to fill this gap.

Incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information from personal narratives in Chinese social media

In China, fertility-related content in mass media predominantly center on introducing and interpreting national policies25, and reflects idealized representations of motherhood26. In addition, personal experiences of pregnancy and childbirth remained largely hidden due to the cultural stigma associated with these topics27. Therefore, nulliparous individuals often lack access to a holistic understanding of fertility that encompasses both its positive and negative aspects. In recent years, with a heightened sense of individualism and feminist consciousness28,29, an increasing number of women have actively engaged in fertility-related discussions on social media, especially Weibo, sharing their experiences, perspectives, emotions regarding childbearing. Accordingly, fertility-related personal narratives frequently emphasize the negative aspects of fertility, particularly the risks and burdens of childbearing on women25,30, which shape antifertility sentiments31 and construct “fertility fear”32. These studies indicate that negative fertility-related information dominates fertility-related personal narratives on Weibo. Although previous studies have demonstrated that exposure to negative fertility-related information on social media reduces fertility intentions among Chinese women17,25, they have not fully addressed the role of personal narratives in fertility-related information and distinguished between different modes of information acquisition. Therefore, this research examines on incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information (IENFI) on Weibo and its impact on Chinese nulliparous women’s fertility intention.

Incidental exposure, information processing, and the Theory of Planned Behavior

Due to the accidental and unintentional nature of information scanning, research generally suggests that scanning involves less cognitive effort in elaboration and evaluation, making it less likely to alter an individual’s behavioral intentions20. While agreeing with the accidental and unintentional aspects, scholars argue that incidental exposure does not equate to passive information consumption. Rather, it often entails active information processing33,34. Matthes, et al.35 emphasized that when users perceive content as relevant, they begin to intentionally process the information that they incidentally exposed.

Considering the significance of fertility for Chinese women – whether viewed as the “passive following” dictated by traditional cultural norms36 or as a personal-growth experience37, it is evident that they perceive high relevance in fertility-related information. Thus, exploring the information processing mechanisms of IENFI is essential.

This study adopts the Theory of Planned Behavior to explain IENFI-triggered information processing. Extended from the Theory of Reasoned Action, the Theory of Planned Behavior proposed that individual’s behavioral intentions are shaped by three key factors: attitudes, social norms, and perceived behavioral control38. In the field of reproductive and family research, this theory has been partly or entirely applied to better understand fertility-related decision-making at micro-level. Related studies suggest that women are more inclined to have children if they hold positive attitude towards fertility, align with social norms of childbearing, and feel a sense of control over their reproductive behavior39,40.

IENFI, attitude, and fertility intention

Studies have shown that incidental exposure to counter-attitude political information on social media can alter one’s existing attitude. This may be due to the tendency for individuals to allocate fewer cognitive resources to incidentally exposed information, making them more susceptible to persuasion41. An alternative explanation suggests that Incidental exposure prompts active engagement with conflicting information, encouraging thoughtful consideration of diverse perspectives and resulting in attitude change42. However, research on online sexual content suggests that unintentional exposure does not affect adolescents’ attitudes towards engaging in sexual activities, as the varied emotional valences of such content fail to produce a consistent attitude43. Given that negative fertility-related information possesses an explicit emotional valence as counter-attitude information, individual attitude towards fertility is more likely to be influenced with incidental exposure.

IENFI, perceived personal-level social norm, and fertility intention

In some studies, social norms are distinguished at the personal and societal level. At the personal level, norms are reflected in the behaviors of significant others, while at the societal level, they represent behaviors prevalent in broader society44,45. Controversial issues often display discrepancies between personal and societal social norms, consequently leading to different behavioral intentions45. In recent years, fertility has increasingly become a contentious topic in China. While new fertility-related policies and traditional cultural values promote childbearing at the societal level, anti-fertility sentiments are gaining popularity on social media28. As a result, there might be a significant divergence between personal and societal social norms regarding fertility. To get a more nuanced understanding of the impact of social norms, this study focuses on personal level social norm, selecting friends as the significant others group. This choice is based on the idea that personal narratives foster identity-based similarity46, making it more likely for nulliparous women to perceive the prevalence and acceptance of social norms within their circle of friends, rather than among parents or the general public.

Additionally, the distinction between perceived and actual social norms has been well-documented. Unlike actual social norms, which are evidenced by census data and surveys44, perceived social norms arise from individuals’ interpretations and assumptions about the prevalence and acceptance of certain behaviors45,47. Although these perceptions may be inaccurate, they still serve as drivers of behavioral change48,49.

Building on these two lines of research, this study investigates the perception of personal level social norms. Since fertility-related information from personal narratives on Weibo are predominantly negative, incidental exposure to such content may lead to the perception that not giving birth is prevailing among their friends. This is conceptualized as the perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends in this study.

Previous research has shown that unintentional exposure to fragmented individual examples can shape one’s perception of social norms within peer groups, subsequently influencing behavior or behavioral intentions43,44. Given that the negative fertility-related information on Weibo primarily suggests a personal-level norm of not giving birth – albeit in a less explicit manner compared to voluntarily life-long childless individual examples encountered in movies, novels, or real-life interactions, this study examines whether perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends mediate the relationship between IENFI and fertility intentions.

IENFI, perceived internal and external behavioral control, and fertility intention

This study divides perceived behavioral control into two dimensions. Perceived internal behavioral control is used to describe women’s self-assessments of their capacity to manage the consequences of childbearing, and perceived external behavioral control is employed to refer to their evaluations of the effectiveness of external support systems. Theoretically, reason for this refinement is that some scholars have separated perceived behavioral control into two components: perceived self-efficacy and perceived controllability50. Perceived self-efficacy refers to individuals’ self-assessments of their abilities, while perceived controllability relates to their evaluations of situational factors. Practically, different social actors assume varied responsibilities in childbearing and childrearing in China. For instance, intergenerational support plays a significant role in addressing fertility-related costs. Owing to welfare traditions, such as free childcare services at Tuo Er Suo (public kindergarten) and health clinics and subsidized canteens provided by the Dan Wei (organizational working) system, the state is expected to offer resources to alleviate women’s “double burden,” particularly in the face of increasingly popularity of individualism and neoliberalism51. These external factors likely shape women’s perceptions of behavioral control in distinct ways, influencing their fertility intentions. Therefore, differentiating between perceived internal behavioral control and perceived external behavioral control helps to better understand how perceived behavioral control affects the fertility intentions of married and unmarried women in the Chinese context.

Some studies have suggested that influential role models in personal narratives52 and visualized personal experiences portrayed by movie characters53 can affect individuals’ perceived self-efficacy or controllability. However, to the best of our knowledge, no research has directly examined how incidental exposure to such information influences perceived behavioral control.

Fertility intention and marital status

Marital status is closely linked to women’s fertility intentions. Firstly, marriage provides women with a new social role that affects their expectations regarding fertility54. Secondly, factors related to one’s spouse play a role in the fertility decisions of married women, such as partnership stability55 and the husband’s desire for children15. Additionally, some married women view fertility as a symbolic milestone that strengthens their marriage56. In short, the impact of IENFI may vary between married and unmarried cohorts.

Based on the above reviewed literature, this study proposes the following research questions:

RQ1: Does incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information influence women’s fertility intentions?

RQ2: Do women’s own negative attitudes toward fertility mediate the relationship between incidental exposure and fertility intentions?

RQ3: Do perceived personal-level social norms and perceived behavioral control mediate this relationship?

RQ4: Do these mechanisms differ between unmarried and married nulliparous women?

In addition, this study further proposes the following hypotheses regarding the underlying mechanisms:

H1: More incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information is associated with lower fertility intentions among Chinese nulliparous women.

H2: Women’s own negative attitudes towards fertility mediate the relationship between IENFI and fertility intentions.

H2a: More incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information is associated with stronger negative attitudes toward fertility.

H2b: Stronger negative attitudes toward fertility are associated with lower fertility intentions.

Methodology

Weibo was selected as the research platform due to its abundance of fertility-related personal narratives. In recent years, fertility-related topics such as the third-child policy and the rights of single women to access egg-freezing technology have triggered extensive public debate on Weibo57. Many women actively participate in these discussions by creating hashtags and sharing their fertility-related experiences, first-hand knowledge, and emotional reflections. These user-generated posts contribute to a substantial volume of negative fertility-related content. For instance, hashtags such as #FertilityTollonWomen and #SequelasofChildbearing have accumulated approximately 167,000 and 154,000 posts, respectively, and have previously ranked among the Top 10 trending topics. The large volume and high visibility of such content – akin to the prevalence of political news on social media platforms – facilitate frequent incidental exposure among users24.

Data collection

This study employed an online questionnaire to collect data. The design and implementation of the study adhered strictly to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the School of Journalism and Communication at Tsinghua University. All participants were adults aged 18 years or older. At the beginning of the questionnaire, participants were provided with detailed information regarding the purpose of the study, the intended use of the data, potential benefits and risks, the qualifications and contact information of the researchers, and the measures taken to ensure confidentiality and data protection. Informed consent was obtained electronically before participants proceeded to complete the questionnaire.

The data were collected via InsightWorks, a professional Chinese survey platform that includes over 4 million Chinese panel members. The final sample consists of 407 cases from the unmarried cohort and 406 cases from the married cohort. In the unmarried group, ages range from 18 to 45 years old (M = 24.95, SD = 0.65), with 94.8% falling into the 21–30 age category. More than half of the unmarried participants (57.5%) earn a monthly income of 3000–9999 RMB (M = 5289.93, SD = 2.59). Approximately 96% of the sample holds a bachelor’s degree. Among those in the unmarried group, 95.6% live in cities, with around a third (31.7%) residing in first-tier cities. In the married group, almost all participants (99%) fall within the 21–35 age range (M = 27.26, SD = 0.64). About 64.6% of them have a monthly income between 3000 and 9999 RMB (M = 7557.88, SD = 2.61). Among the married participants, 60.1% hold a bachelor’s degree, and 25.1% hold a master’s degree. Around 97.5% of them live in cities, with 29.8% residing in first-tier cities.

Measures

Incidental exposure to negative fertility-related information

It is important to note that individuals who already hold negative views about fertility may be more attentive to negative fertility-related information. To mitigate this concern, the questionnaire explicitly distinguished active information seeking from incidental exposure, emphasizing that the exposure occurred while participants were browsing Weibo rather than intentionally searching for such information.

They were first asked, “How often did you actively search for negative fertility-related information on Weibo in the past year?” (from 1 = Never to 5 = Very often). Following the study of Kim, et al.58, IENFI was measured by asking “When you scroll through Weibo in your everyday life, how often did you come across negative fertility-related information in the past year?” (from 1 = Never to 5 = Very often).

Importantly, the questionnaire did not emphasize “personal narratives.” This omission was intentional, based on the observation that the vast majority of negative fertility-related information on Weibo originates from personal narratives. In contrast, content posted by institutional accounts (e.g., news agencies) typically centers on policy-oriented topics29.

Fertility intention

Fertility intention was measured by combining three components: willingness to have a first child, preferred timing of the first birth, and willingness to have a second child40,59.

For the two items measuring childbearing willingness, responses were coded as 1 = No, 2 = Uncertain, and 3 = Yes. Participants who reported that they did not intend to have a first child were logically coded as “No” for having a second child, as progression to a second birth presupposes the first birth. The item measuring the preferred timing of the first birth was reverse-coded so that higher values represented a stronger and more immediate fertility intention. The three items were then summed to form a composite fertility intention score, with higher values indicating stronger fertility intentions. Thus, the composite score ranges from 3 to 9, with higher scores indicating stronger fertility intentions.

For example, if a respondent reported being uncertain about having a first child (2), preferred to have the first child within 2–3 years (3), and reported no intention to have a second child (1), the resulting fertility intention score would be 6.

One’s own negative attitude towards fertility

Measurements from Billari, et al.59 were adopted in this study, comprising statements that assess the implications of fertility as either “benefits” (5 items, e.g., childbearing brings joy and fulfillment to my life) or “costs” (6 items, e.g., childbearing increases my financial burdens) on a 5-point scale (from 1 = strongly agree to 5 = strongly disagree). The “benefits” statements, which indicate a positive attitude, were reverse-coded. Subsequently, these items were averaged to create a composite measure of an individual’s attitude. The reliability Cronbach’s alpha was 0.849 for unmarried cohort and 0.853 for married cohort, respectively.

Perceived not-giving-birth social norm among friends

The scale used to measure this variable was based on research related to organ donation45, which combined personal-level descriptive norms and injunctive norms. Specifically, participants were asked to rate their agreement with the statements, “I believe that most of my friends have discussed the decision of not having children with their families (from 1 = never to 7 = almost always)” and “I believe that most of my friends support the decision of not having children (from 1 = never to 7 = almost always)”. The two items were averaged for the final analysis. The reliability Cronbach’s alpha was 0.700 for unmarried cohort and 0.789 for married cohort, respectively.

Perceived behavioral control

Drawing on measurements from previous studies39,60 and taking the Chinese social context into consideration, perceived internal behavioral control was assessed by asking participants, “I possess the ability to manage fertility-related consequences in the following areas: (1) family finances, (2) career, and (3) health.” Items related to perceived external behavioral control included statements, “I believe I can receive adequate support from: (1) social welfare, (2) my parents, and (3) my spouse.” Participants responded to these six items using a five-point Likert scale (from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), and the scores were averaged for analysis. The reliability Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.843 for unmarried cohort and 0.778 for married cohort, respectively.

Control variables

Socio-demographic variables, including age, income, education level, and usual residence were controlled in the data analysis. The first three factors are commonly recognized as influencing women’s fertility intentions. Usual residence is particularly influential in shaping the fertility intentions of Chinese women, as it is associated with living costs and cultural environment. For instance, women in rural areas and small cities are often encouraged to have more children due to traditional fertility culture61. Additionally, the frequency of Weibo usage was controlled for, considering the impact of social media usage on individuals’ fertility intentions25,62. The frequency of Weibo usage was measured by the question“How often do you scroll through Weibo on a daily basis in the past year?” (1 = Never, 5 = Very often).

Statistical analysis

Firstly, a correlation analysis was conducted to explore the relationships between variables. Subsequently, hierarchical regression analysis was employed to examine the influence of all predictor variables on fertility intention. Finally, mediation path analysis with multiple parallel mediators was utilized to test the mediating roles of negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, perceived internal behavioral control, and perceived external behavioral control in the relationship between IENFI and fertility intentions.

The mediation path analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 26.0) with the PROCESS macro for Windows. Specifically, PROCESS Model 4 was utilized to examine the mediating relationship, incorporating bootstrap confidence intervals to estimate the indirect effect. The bootstrap procedure involved generating 5,000 samples with replacement to estimate the total and specific indirect effects. Furthermore, bias-corrected and accelerated 95% confidence intervals were applied.

Results

Correlation analyses

To test H1, correlation analyses were conducted, which explored the possible relationships among IENFI, women’s own negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, perceived internal behavioral control, perceived external behavioral control, fertility intention, the frequency of Weibo use, and demographics. The results revealed that the frequency of Weibo use is significantly and positively associated with IENFI in both unmarried cohort and married cohort. Then, IENFI is significantly and negatively correlated with fertility intention among unmarried cohort and married cohort. H1 was supported. In addition, negative attitudes is significantly and negatively associated with fertility intention among unmarried cohort and married cohort. For perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, it displays a negative correlation with fertility intention in both unmarried cohort and married cohort. Moreover, perceived internal behavioral control and perceived external behavioral control are significantly and positively associated with fertility intention in both unmarried cohort and married cohort. Among the demographic variables, educational background was the only factor significantly associated with fertility intention in unmarried cohort. Hierarchical regression analysis

To examine the influence of IENFI, women’s own negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, perceived behavioral control, the frequency of Weibo use, and demographics on fertility intention, hierarchical regression analysis was conducted. Model 1 shows that demographics explained 4.3% of the variance in unmarried cohort and only 0.3% in married cohort. Among the demographic variables, educational background (β = 0.216, p<0.001) and residence (β = 0.150, p<0.01) significantly influenced fertility intention in unmarried cohort. When IENFI and the frequency of Weibo use were included in model 2, IENFI emerged as a significant factor affecting fertility intention in both unmarried cohort (β=-0.261, p<0.001) and married cohort (β=-0.144, p<0.01), while the frequency of Weibo use did not. The joint effect of IENFI and the frequency of Weibo use explained 5.6% of the variance in unmarried cohort and 1.4% in married cohort.

The inclusion of women’s own negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, and perceived behavioral control in the equation in block 3 led to some differences between unmarried cohort and married cohort. In unmarried cohort, fertility intention was significantly and negatively influenced by negative attitudes (β=-0.328, p<0.001) and perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends (β=-0.092, p<0.05), while it was positively influenced by perceived internal behavioral control (β = 0.224, p<0.001). However, perceived external behavioral control (β = 0.087, p>0.05) did not show a significant impact on fertility intention in unmarried cohort. Finally, the model 3 explained 27.4% of the variance in unmarried cohort. Mediating effect analysis

Mediating effect analysis was conducted to examine the mechanism between IENFI and fertility intention while controlling for demographics and the frequency of Weibo use. The total effect of IENFI on fertility intention in unmarried cohort was significant [Effect=-0.327, 95%CI(-0.444, − 0.210)], of which the direct effect [Effect=-0.063, 95%CI(-0.167, 0.042)] was not significant, and the indirect effect [Effect=-0.264, 95%CI(-0.343, − 0.193)] was significant. The total indirect effect consists of four indirect effects. Specifically, indirect effect 1 and its bootstrap 95% confidence interval [Effect=-0.134, 95%CI (-0.198, − 0.079)], indirect effect 2 and its bootstrap 95% confidence interval [Effect=-0.016, 95%CI (-0.037, − 0.001)], and indirect effect 3 and its bootstrap 95% confidence interval [Effect=-0.087, 95%CI (-0.145, − 0.042)] all do not contain 0, indicating that women’s own negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, and perceived internal behavioral control significantly mediates relationship between IENFI and fertility intention of unmarried cohort respectively.

However, the bootstrap 95% confidence interval of indirect effect 4 does indeed contain 0 [Effect=-0.027, 95%CI (-0.063, 0.004)], suggesting that perceived external behavioral control does not have a significant mediating role between IENFI and fertility intention in unmarried cohort. Discussion

This study examined the influence of incidental exposure to fertility-related personal narratives on Weibo on Chinese nulliparous women’s fertility intentions, utilizing the Theory of Planned Behavior to explain the subsequent information-processing mechanism. Overall, this study suggests that incidental exposure to health-related information social media – despite not actively seeking – can meaningfully shape individuals’ behavioral intentions and triggers socio-psychological information processing.

Specifically, IENFI significantly lowers fertility intentions among Chinese nulliparous women. This marked impact of IENFI can be attributed to high perceived personal relevance of fertility-related issues. This perceived relevance can lead to greater engagement with the content after incidental exposure24, such as spending more time browsing these messages63 and seeking additional related information (second-level incidental exposure)35. These engagement patterns are captured by Weibo’s algorithms, creating a feedback loop9 that continuously feeds similar information to nulliparous women, thereby cumulatively lowers fertility intentions. Moreover, while personal narratives do not provide scripted dramatic “stories” in the style of television, social media allows for the weaving together of disparate personally created content64, fostering a sense of authenticity and relatability. This enables nulliparous women to identify more closely with the negative experiences of fertility and then lower their fertility intention.

When women’s own negative attitudes, perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends, perceived internal behavioral control, and perceived external behavioral control are included in the analysis, the direct relationship between IENFI and fertility intentions disappears. This suggests that Incidental exposure is not merely a mode of passive information acquisition that leads to later recall, as described by information scanning17, but rather involves an active socio-psychological information processing mechanism.

The results indicate that both unmarried and married nulliparous women with more IENFI tend to develop more negative attitudes towards fertility, which subsequently lowers their fertility intentions. Since attitudes encompass both cognitive and affective dimensions65, two possible explanations arise. Cognitively, IENFI may prompt active responses, such as expressing similar feelings, seeking more fertility-related information, or engaging in discussions about the pros and cons of fertility in comment sections. This active engagement fosters negative attitudes toward fertility, similar to how exposure to counter-attitudinal information can lead to attitude depolarization42. Affectively, since incidental exposure is less likely to engage sufficient cognitive resources41, the negative emotions embedded in fertility-related personal narratives can be highly contagious and easily elicit negative attitudes. These explanations warrant validation through experimental research.

Additionally, more IENFI is associated with higher level of perceived personal level social norm of not giving birth among friends in both unmarried and married women, which in turn lowers their fertility intentions. Although negative fertility-related information may not explicitly set normative examples, digital social cues such as comments, view counts, and likes act as indicators of personal-level social norms66,67. Notably, whether Chinese nulliparous women would ultimately practice childlessness as a result of perceiving these social norms within their friend circles remains uncertain. Previous study mentioned that anti-fertility slogans such as “no kids, no ring, keep living serene” resonate with many Chinese women37, suggesting that the traditional social norm of childbearing (e.g., more children, more happiness) may have been interrupted by a rising popular perception of not giving birth, particularly among urban, well-educated, middle-class young women. However, even this cohort still places high value on family, making it more likely that they will delay childbearing or prefer nuclear families, rather than opting for lifelong childlessness37. In other words, the perceived shift in personal-level social norms may be insufficient to counterbalance entrenched societal expectations grounded in traditional family values and pro-natalist policies68. Future research should compare the relative influence of personal and societal level norms, especially when they are in conflict.

Another finding is that IENFI decreases both perceived internal behavioral control and perceived external behavioral control among unmarried and married women. Negative fertility-related personal narratives on Weibo offer vivid examples of unfavorable experiences, leading women to underestimate their ability to manage the consequences of childbearing52. Moreover, these narratives rarely offer practical solutions to the challenges of childbearing. Instead, the collective “female complaints”69 reinforce the perception that there are inadequate resources to support women’s needs, which results in underestimated evaluation of external supports.

Notably, the impact of perceived internal behavioral control and perceived external behavioral control on fertility intentions varies between unmarried and married women. Unmarried women’s fertility intentions are influenced by perceived internal behavioral control but not perceived external behavioral control, while the reverse is true for married women, who are influenced only by perceived external behavioral control. This suggests that unmarried women are more focused on their personal abilities to handle the consequences of childbearing, whereas married women prioritize external supports that can mitigate or share the burdens of childbearing.

This difference can be understood within the Chinese cultural context, where childbearing is considered as integral part of marriage70. In this framework, motherhood becomes a socially assigned responsibility for women upon entering marriage. When behavior is driven by other’s demand rather than own motivation (e.g., “I want to achieve X”), individuals’ behavior depends more on external situational factors71. Similarly, married women prioritize external supports to create a suitable environment for childbearing. With increasing privatization and commercialization of childcare and education in China72, married women tend to expect to get more resources from their family. Accordingly, married women’s evaluations of available family resources become pivotal in shaping their fertility intentions. For instance, women with more intergenerational support are more likely to consider having a second child72,73.

For unmarried women, childbearing is more likely to be considered as a personal plan that could be fulfill in the long-run. Therefore, they tend to mitigate the burdens of childbearing by gradually improving their personal capacities, instead of by seeking immediate external support. Additionally, some women, influenced by feminism, view childbearing and motherhood as opportunities for personal growth74, which primarily involves enhancing their own abilities. Furthermore, the popularity of neoliberal feminism has led many unmarried women to emphasize individual capacities in overcoming the challenges of childbearing75. As a result, the influence of perceived internal behavioral control is more significant among unmarried women.

Practical implications

The study offers a meso-level perspective on declining fertility intentions in China and globally within the context of “deep mediatization”76, yielding important practical implications for addressing low fertility rates.

It is important to emphasize that the findings of this study do not imply that individual fertility preferences should be directly manipulated. Instead, the results highlight the importance of providing balanced and comprehensive fertility-related information in the digital media environment, which enables individuals to make more informed reproductive decisions and rational self-choices.

Given that negative attitudes mediates the relationship between IENFI and fertility intentions for both unmarried and married women, addressing women’s negative attitudes toward childbearing is critical. One effective approach is to reaffirm the intrinsic value of childbearing, including the emotional fulfillment and relational rewards associated with raising children. In addition, it is vital to ensure that women’s lived experiences with pregnancy, childbirth, and parenting are portrayed more accurately and comprehensively in public discourse. When women feel that their challenges and contributions are acknowledged and understood, negative attitudes toward fertility are more likely to be mitigated. Influential parenting bloggers could play an important role in providing this type of information.

Considering perceived internal behavioral control is an important mediator in unmarried group, providing them with accurate information and actionable solutions is essential. News agencies and government institutions (e.g., Community Council, Health Resources and Services Administration) should take responsibility for offering accessible, evidence-based content. These efforts can help counterbalance the fear-oriented framing often found in personal narratives, reduce anxiety during information processing, and ultimately strengthen their self-efficacy. In parallel, the significant effect of perceived external behavioral control among married women highlights the need to foster a co-parenting culture and promote greater male involvement in childrearing responsibilities. Transforming the traditional gendered division of labor within families can strengthen intra-family support systems, thereby creating a more enabling environment for married women to meet their fertility needs. At the same time, private social media platforms should contribute by moderating misinformation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study has yet to fully examine the potential issue of reverse causality: individuals who already hold negative views about fertility may be more attentive to negative fertility-related information. Given the cross-sectional nature of the survey data, causal relationships should be interpreted cautiously. Future studies could employ longitudinal or experimental designs to further clarify the causal direction between incidental exposure and fertility intentions. Secondly, the measurement of incidental exposure is relatively simplistic. Future research should employ content analysis techniques to more precisely identify and categorize different types of negative fertility-related information. Thirdly, the algorithmic curation process has not been empirically validated, which remains an area for future online experiments. Finally, the sample lacks representation from women living in less developed areas. Future research should focus on social media platforms, such as Kuaishou, that targets such groups to provide a more comprehensive understanding.
