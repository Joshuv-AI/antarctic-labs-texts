The effect of a protein-rich oat by-product on growth performance, carcass traits, gastrointestinal function and morphology in broiler chickens
Authors: Magdalena Mazur-Kuśnirek, Michał Gesek, Janusz Pomianowski, Agnieszka Wiszniewska-Łaszczych, Marta Sołtysiuk, Marta Borsuk-Stanulewicz, Wiesław Sobotka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of a protein-rich oat by-product obtained during β-glucan extraction, used as a partial substitute for soybean meal (SBM) in broiler chicken diets, on growth performance, carcass traits, and selected gastrointestinal parameters. The experiment involved 320 one-day-old male Ross 308 broilers divided into four groups (10 replicates of eight birds each). Diets contained 0%, 10%, 15%, or 20% oat by-product. Growth performance parameters were monitored throughout the experiment, and on day 42, carcass traits, gastrointestinal function, the pH of cecal digesta, the concentrations of short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), and intestinal histomorphology were evaluated. No significant differences were found in final growth performance or dressing percentage among treatments. The birds that received the oat by-product had a lower proportion of abdominal fat (P < 0.001). Diets containing 10% or 15% oat by-product reduced cecal pH and the concentrations of isobutyric and isovaleric acids (P < 0.001). Histomorphological analysis revealed optimal villus and crypt parameters at 10–15% inclusion levels of the tested product and less favorable values at 20% (P < 0.001). These results indicate that the tested oat by-product may be considered a promising partial substitute for SBM in broiler diets, although its efficacy depends on the inclusion level. The 10%–15% range yielded the most favorable overall response, while the 20% level was associated with suboptimal histomorphological characteristics of the small intestine.

Introduction

Soybean meal (SBM) is the main protein source in poultry diets1. However, soybeans cultivated in Europe, where soil and climatic conditions are less favorable, do not satisfy the high demand for this feed ingredient. In recent years, annual soybean imports to the European Union have reached approximately 13–15 million tons2. It should be noted that the carbon footprint of imported products is several times higher than that of locally sourced ones3.

The above problems could be solved by using by-products from the food processing industry. These by-products are cheaper sources of feed ingredients and align with the principles of sustainable agriculture based on the “zero waste” concept and maximizing the use of locally available resources4. Due to their unique chemical composition, these by-products often have a high nutritional value and can improve the quality of animal-based foods5–7. In addition, the use of agri-food by-products can reduce the overall cost and the negative environmental impact of feed production3. Modern broiler chickens are characterized by efficient feed conversion and rapid growth. Their gastrointestinal tracts are particularly sensitive to dietary composition. Therefore, dietary protein sources and the physicochemical properties of feed ingredients may affect their performance, intestinal function, and gut morphology8–10.

The protein-rich oat by-product analyzed in this study (hereinafter referred to as the oat by-product) is a by-product of the food-processing industry obtained during β-glucan extraction from oat grain. In comparison with whole oat (Avena sativa L.) grain, it is characterized by a higher content of protein (approx. 39%) and crude fat (approx. 8%) and a lower content of crude fiber (approx. 6%). Oat protein is characterized by a favorable amino acid composition and high nutritional value11. Among cereals, oat has the highest lysine content and its amino acid profile is comparable with that of soybeans12. In addition, oat grain and oat-based products are sources of secondary metabolites such as tocopherols, phenolic acids, and avenanthramides, which exhibit antioxidant activity13.

The use of conventional oat grain in broiler nutrition is limited by the relatively high content of fiber and β-glucan, which may increase digesta viscosity and impair nutrient utilization14. β-glucan belongs to the soluble non-starch polysaccharide fraction of oat grain and consists of linear unbranched chains of D-glucose monosaccharides linked by mixed (β-1, 3) and (β-1,4) bonds. It is a major component of endosperm cell walls in various cereal kernels12. The concentration of β-glucan, regarded as an antinutritional factor in poultry nutrition, is reduced in the analyzed by-product, which facilitates its incorporation into broiler diets.

It should also be emphasized that dietary protein sources and their quality may affect the gut environment by modulating the pH of intestinal digesta, microbial activity, and the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). At the same time, the physicochemical properties of dietary fiber may influence gastrointestinal function and gut morphology, thereby contributing to differences in the physiological response of the digestive tract and, consequently, bird performance15,16. Such changes in the intestinal environment may alter the structure of the small intestine, particularly the morphology of intestinal villi and crypts, which are considered sensitive indicators of the gastrointestinal tract’s adaptation to dietary composition17.

However, data regarding the suitability of the tested oat by-product as a partial substitute for SBM in broiler diets remain scarce. The available literature does not clearly indicate its effects on growth performance, intestinal fermentation, or selected functional and histomorphological parameters of the gastrointestinal tract. The optimal dietary inclusion level of the product for fast-growing broiler chickens has not been specified.

The aim of the present study was to determine the effect of a protein-rich oat by-product used as a partial substitute for SBM in broiler chicken diets on the growth performance, carcass traits, and selected functional and histomorphological parameters of the gastrointestinal tract of birds.

It was hypothesized that the partial replacement of SBM with the analyzed oat by-product would not impair the growth performance or carcass traits of broiler chickens and could beneficially modulate the gut environment, as reflected in selected functional, fermentation-related, and histomorphological parameters of the gastrointestinal tract.

Results

Growth performance and carcass traits

In the first week of the experiment, no significant differences in body weight were found between broiler chickens from the control and experimental groups on days 1 and 7 (Table 1). In comparison with the control group, broilers fed diets containing 15% or 20% oat by-product were characterized by lower feed intake and lower feed conversion ratio (FCR) values (P ˂ 0.05).

By day 28, broilers fed a diet containing 20% oat by-product had lower cumulative feed intake than the control group (1.94 vs. 2.07 kg, P ˂ 0.01) (Table 1). Until day 28, the values of FCR were lower in broilers that received diets supplemented with the oat by-product at 10% and 20% than in control group birds (P ˂ 0.001).

The final body weights and weight gains of birds were comparable in all groups. Similarly, the mortality rate and the European Efficiency Index (EEI) did not differ significantly between treatments.

The morphological characteristics of different sections of the small intestine of broiler chickens are presented in Table 5. Duodenal villi were shortest (P < 0.01) in broilers fed diets containing 15% or 20% oat by-product. Villus width was reduced in birds that received a diet with 20% oat by-product, compared with the control group and the group fed 10% oat by-product (P < 0.01). Crypt depth was greatest in broilers fed 20% oat by-product and lowest in those fed 15% oat by-product (P < 0.01). The villus-to-crypt ratio was highest in the 15% group and lowest in the 20% group (P < 0.01). In addition, muscular layer thickness in the duodenum was lower in birds fed a diet containing 15% oat by-product than in the remaining groups (P < 0.01).

Jejunal villi were longer and narrower in broilers that received 10% oat by-product than in the other groups (P < 0.01). Crypt depth was lower in birds fed 15% oat by-product than in the remaining groups (P < 0.01). The villus-to-crypt ratio was higher in broilers fed 10% or 15% oat by-product than in the control and 20% groups (P < 0.01). No significant differences were found in muscular layer thickness in the jejunum (P > 0.05).

Ileal villi were longer in broilers fed 10% or 15% oat by-product than in the control group, whereas the shortest villi were observed in the 20% group (P < 0.01). Crypt depth was greatest in birds fed 10% oat by-product and lowest in those fed 20% oat by-product (P < 0.01). The villus-to-crypt ratio was highest in the 15% group and lowest in the 20% group (P < 0.01). Muscular layer thickness in the ileum was higher in broilers that received 10% oat by-product than in birds fed diets containing 15% or 20% of this component (P < 0.01).

Discussion

The use of oat grain in diets for fast-growing birds is limited by its relatively high content of fiber and β-glucan, which may impair nutrient utilization and growth performance18. The oat by-product evaluated in this study was obtained via β-glucan extraction, which reduced its content to 2.34%. In comparison with whole oat grain, this by-product is characterized by lower crude fiber content (6% vs. approx. 10%) and higher crude protein content (38% vs. 11%-15%)19–21. Furthermore, the product’s detergent fiber profile (28.19% NDF, 20.56% ADF, and 12.01% ADL) indicates a significant proportion of structural cell wall components. These insoluble fiber fractions are associated mainly with structural and mechanical effects in the gastrointestinal tract, including stimulation of gizzard development and gut motility10,22. In the current study, the concentrations of β-glucan and crude fiber in broiler diets ranged from 0.25% to 0.70% and from 4.22% to 5.60%, respectively, depending on the feeding phase and dietary treatment, and were highest in diets with increased inclusion levels of the oat by-product.

In the present experiment, birds fed diets containing 15% or 20% oat by-product were characterized by lower feed intake and a more favorable FCR than the control group during the first week of rearing (P ˂ 0.05). By day 28, a significant decrease in these parameters was recorded only in broilers that received a diet with 20% oat by-product. Similar observations were made by other authors who found that body weight gain and/or feed intake decreased in broiler chickens fed diets supplemented with fibrous ingredients during the first few weeks of life. Jiménez-Moreno et al.23 observed this effect when the crude fiber content of broiler diets was increased (to 7.5%) by adding sugar beet pulp, which contained 18% crude fiber. Hosseini-Vashan et al.24 reported a similar trend in broiler chickens fed diets supplemented with urea-treated pomegranate peel (18% NDF, 14% ADF), at both 10 and 24 days of age. The effects of dietary fiber on gastrointestinal tract development and growth performance depend on both its inclusion level and the relative proportion of soluble and insoluble fiber fractions10,25. Since the β-glucan content of the tested oat by-product was markedly reduced, the lower feed intake observed during the initial weeks of rearing was likely due to the total fiber load and the contribution of structural fiber fractions, rather than the viscosity-related effects typically associated with whole oat grain14. Moderate levels of dietary fiber may support gastrointestinal function in broilers, whereas higher inclusion levels, especially in young birds, may compromise performance16. The absence of significant differences at 42 days of age suggests that the birds gradually adapted to the dietary fiber as their gastrointestinal tracts matured.

The results of the histomorphological analysis suggest that the response to the tested oat by-product depended on both its dietary inclusion level and the intestinal segment examined. Inclusion levels of 10% and 15% were associated with favorable adaptive changes in the small intestine, including longer villi, reduced crypt depth, and a higher villus-to-crypt ratio. In contrast, the 20% inclusion level resulted in less favorable histomorphological characteristics, including shorter villi, deeper crypts, and a lower villus-to-crypt ratio, despite the absence of significant differences in body weight or FCR on day 42. Thus, the absence of differences in final performance should not be interpreted as evidence that the highest inclusion level of the oat by-product had no biological effect on intestinal function. Final performance parameters reflect the cumulative response of birds over the entire rearing period; they may therefore fail to capture transient disturbances in gastrointestinal function during earlier developmental stages. However, the unfavorable intestinal histomorphology observed at the 20% inclusion level suggests that such alterations may still be biologically relevant under environmental or physiological stress conditions26. Similar trends were reported in broiler chickens fed diets containing increasing levels of fibrous by-products, in which villus length decreased and crypt depth increased27–29. A decrease in villus length is associated with a reduction in the absorptive surface area, whereas an increase in crypt depth reflects intensified epithelial turnover and regeneration, potentially raising the energetic cost of intestinal maintenance and epithelial renewal28,29. Dietary fiber fractions may act in a dose-dependent and bidirectional manner: moderate levels, particularly of the insoluble fraction, may improve the villus-to-crypt ratio30, whereas its excessive inclusion may promote epithelial disruption and villus shortening27. Despite the reduced β-glucan content of the tested oat by-product, its fiber profile could still modulate the gut environment and fermentation pattern, thereby affecting intestinal morphology16,31. This may explain the favorable adaptive changes observed at the 10% and 15% inclusion levels and the less desirable intestinal response at the 20% inclusion level.

The fermentation of dietary fiber in the ceca largely depends on the physicochemical properties of its fractions. Soluble fiber fractions are generally fermented more rapidly and contribute more directly to SCFA production, whereas insoluble fractions are usually fermented more slowly. Therefore, their effect on cecal fermentation is more often indirect, involving the modification of digesta passage and substrate availability17,32. Moderate levels of dietary fiber may support gut development and contribute to changes in the cecal environment, including a decrease in digesta pH33. In the present study, the decrease in cecal pH was accompanied by changes in the SCFA profile, specifically lower concentrations of isobutyric and isovaleric acids in the 10% and 15% inclusion groups. As these BCFAs are products of the microbial fermentation of amino acids (mainly valine and leucine), their lower concentrations may indicate reduced protein fermentation or a shift toward carbohydrate fermentation16,31. This may be relevant because the excessive fermentation of amino acids and proteins in the hindgut leads to the formation of nitrogenous metabolites that may impair intestinal integrity34. However, this interpretation remains tentative, as markers of nitrogen metabolism, microbiota composition, and nutrient digestibility were not determined. In broiler chickens, different β-glucan fractions may serve as substrates for the gut microbiome in the distal segments of the gastrointestinal tract. As a result, they may contribute to differences in fermentation processes and the SCFA profile35. According to Petit et al.36, glucans can exert prebiotic effects by enhancing the synthesis of SCFAs. At the same time, the reduced β-glucan content of the tested oat by-product suggests that the observed cecal responses were not caused by the typical effects of whole oat grain associated with β-glucan. Instead, the responses were more likely related to the proportion of soluble and insoluble fiber fractions in the product and their influence on substrate availability for hindgut fermentation10. However, the experimental design did not include a control group supplemented with enzymes, such as β-glucanase. This would have allowed for a more direct verification of the role of residual β-glucan in the observed responses. Total microbial counts in the ceca were similar across all groups, despite the observed changes in cecal pH and SCFA profile37. Nevertheless, the cecal changes may have indirectly contributed to the intestinal morphological response, although their effects on growth performance appeared to be limited or confined mainly to the early rearing period. In the present study, the relative cecal weight decreased significantly in broilers fed diets containing 15% or 20% oat by-product. In other studies investigating fibrous feed ingredients, such as oat hulls and soy hulls, selected gastrointestinal segments increased in response to elevated fiber supply38. The direction of these responses may depend on the fiber profile of the diet and the age of birds16,27,39,40.

In the present study, the carcass dressing percentage was typical of broilers slaughtered at 42 days of age (approx. 74.5%) and was not affected by the dietary treatment. The only carcass trait that changed was the proportion of abdominal fat, which was lower in birds fed diets containing the oat by-product (0.12%–0.13%). Similar reductions in abdominal fat, without changes in dressing percentage, have also been reported in broilers fed alternative high-protein feed ingredients, such as low-fiber sunflower meal or DDGS41,42. A possible explanation is related to the fiber profile of the diet. According to the literature, insoluble fiber fractions can bind bile salts (synthesized from cholesterol) in the intestines, thus limiting their reabsorption, which may reduce abdominal fat deposition16,43. Changes in lipid metabolism lead to lower carcass fatness and are desirable in terms of the quality of the final product and consumer expectations44. Diets with increased crude fiber content may increase the functional load of the gastrointestinal tract (e.g. due to the increased weight or volume of its selected segments). This, in turn, increases the metabolic cost of tissue maintenance and may reduce the pool of energy available for lipogenesis, thus contributing to lower carcass fatness45,46. However, this interpretation should be made with caution, because no significant differences were observed in the weights or proportions of the analyzed organs, and lipid metabolism indicators were not determined in the present study. Therefore, the mechanism underlying the reduced abdominal fat content in broilers cannot be directly confirmed and requires further investigation.

The present findings should also be interpreted with caution, as the experiment was conducted only on male Ross 308 broilers, which may limit the extrapolation of the results to other sexes and genotypes; moreover, no economic evaluation was performed to assess the cost-effectiveness of partial SBM replacement with the tested oat by-product.

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate that the high-protein by-product obtained during β-glucan extraction from oat grain is a promising partial substitute for SBM in broiler diets, although its efficacy depends on the inclusion level. The 10%–15% range yielded the most favorable overall response, with no adverse effects on growth performance or carcass traits; it was also associated with more favorable gastrointestinal function parameters. In contrast, the 20% inclusion level resulted in less desirable values of selected histomorphological parameters of the small intestine, despite the absence of significant differences in final growth performance. These findings suggest that higher dietary inclusion levels of the tested oat by-product should be approached with caution, particularly under commercial farming conditions, where broilers are exposed to additional environmental stressors.

Methods

The animal experiment was approved by the Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experimentation in Olsztyn, Poland (decision No. LKE/04/2025). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the applicable guidelines for animal experimentation and animal care. No procedures involving pain or suffering were carried out, and all analyses were performed on samples collected post mortem. The study adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines, ensuring ethical standards and compliance with all relevant regulations, with detailed reporting of the methods, experimental design, sampling, and data analysis.

Animals and diets

The experimental materials comprised 320 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chickens. The birds were divided into four groups, with 10 replicates per group and eight birds per replicate. The experiment lasted for 42 days.

The broilers were kept in pens on pelleted straw, under standard environmental conditions. The indoor temperature was maintained at 32–33 °C on the first day of rearing. Then, it was gradually decreased by 2–3 °C every week, until it reached 18.5–20 °C in the last week of the experiment. The lighting program was as follows: 24 h of light (L) on day 1, 23 L:1 h of dark (D) on days 2 to 7, 20 L:4D on days 8 to 11, and 18 L:6D on days 12 to 42. Throughout the experiment, all birds had free access to water and feed.

The birds were fed mash diets, in a four-phase system: a starter diet on days 0–10, grower 1 diet on days 11–21, grower 2 diet on days 22–35, and a finisher diet on days 36–42. All diets were isocaloric and isonitrogenous. They were based on wheat grain and maize, and contained SBM as a protein source and soybean oil. The diets were supplemented with minerals (limestone, monocalcium phosphate, fodder salt), amino acids: lysine, methionine, and threonine, and a mineral-vitamin premix. The feed rations were formulated according to the Nutrient Requirements of Poultry47. The composition and nutritional value of the diets are presented in Tables 6, 7, 8 and 9.

The experimental factor was a protein-rich oat by-product from the food-processing industry, obtained during β-glucan extraction from oat grain. Oat (Avena sativa L.) kernels were dehulled and ground to produce oatmeal. The oatmeal was hydrated and carbohydrates were extracted. The fraction containing β-glucan was separated, and the remaining material was placed in a decanter and subjected to heat treatment followed by drying. As a result, an oat by-product with reduced crude fiber was obtained. The chemical composition and nutritional value of the analyzed oat by-product is presented in Table 10. Sample collection and laboratory analyses

The nutrient content of the diets was determined by the Weende method48. The apparent metabolizable energy (AME) content of the diets was calculated based on the Nutrient Requirements of Poultry47. The proportions of neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), and acid detergent lignin (ADL) in the analyzed oat by-product were determined using an ANKOM 2000 fiber analyzer (ANKOM Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA), following the method described by van Soest49. The amino acid content of the diets was analyzed according to the ISO 13903:200550 standard, using ion chromatography with UV detection (IC-UV). β-glucans were determined by an enzymatic spectrophotometric method at Eurofins Food Testing Netherlands (Heerenveen).

Throughout the experiment, the body weights of broiler chickens and feed intake were monitored at weekly intervals. Performance parameters, such as the feed conversion ratio (FCR), mortality rate [%], and the European Efficiency Index (EEI), were calculated using the following formulas:

FCR=(total feed intake (kg))/(total body weight gain (kg)).

Mortality rate (%)=(number of dead birds x 100)/(number of birds used in the experiment).

EEI=(liveability (%)x body weight (kg)x 100)/age (days)x FCR (kg).

At 42 days of age, one bird with a body weight close to the replicate mean was selected from each pen for post-slaughter analyses, resulting in 10 birds per treatment group (n = 10 per group). After slaughter, the carcasses were plucked, the shanks were cut off, and the gastrointestinal tract was removed. Each carcass was weighed. The breast muscles, heart, liver, gizzard, spleen, and abdominal fat were harvested, and their weight and percentage content in the carcass were determined. The carcass dressing percentage was calculated by dividing carcass weight by live weight.

The separated gastrointestinal tract was divided into the following segments: crop, proventriculus, gizzard, small intestine, and ceca, which were weighed. The small intestine and the ceca were measured. Each organ was weighed without the contents. pH was measured with a pH-meter (model 301, Hanna Instruments, Vila do Conde, Portugal) in digesta samples collected from the crop, gizzard, proventriculus, small intestine, and ceca. The concentrations of SCFAs in cecal digesta were determined by gas chromatography (Shimadzu GC 14 A, Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan).

The total microbial count was determined according to the PN-EN ISO 4833-1:2013 − 1251 standard, using the deep plating method on agar medium. From the prepared homogenized sample, tenfold serial dilutions were made, from which 1 ml was applied to sterile Petri dishes and covered with molten PCA medium, cooled to 44–47 °C. After thorough mixing and solidification of the medium, the plates were incubated at 30 °C for 72 ± 3 h. After incubation, all colonies that had grown were counted, and the results were expressed as the number of colony-forming units (CFU) per 1 g or 1 ml of the sample. These values were calculated based on plates containing between 30 and 300 colonies.

A histomorphological analysis was performed to determine villus height and crypt depth. Duodenal samples were fixed in neutral-buffered formalin (10%) at pH 7.4. Microtome Sect. (4 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. The morphological characteristics of the small intestine were determined using an optical microscope (Olympus BX-50, Warsaw, Poland) and a digital camera connected to a personal computer with CellˆB (OLYMPUS) software.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate the normality of data distribution, and Levene’s test was applied to assess the homogeneity of variances. A pen was considered the experimental unit for evaluating growth performance parameters, whereas an individual bird served as the experimental unit for determining carcass traits, gastrointestinal measurements, digesta characteristics, and histomorphological parameters. The results were processed statistically by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s test. The arithmetic mean (x), standard error of the mean (SEM), and the level of significance (P ˂ 0.05) were calculated for all results. All calculations were performed using STATISTICA 13.3 software (StatSoft, Kraków, Poland).
