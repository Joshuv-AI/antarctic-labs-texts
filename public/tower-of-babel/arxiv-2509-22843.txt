Can GRB 250702B be explained as the tidal disruption of a white dwarf by an intermediate mass black hole? Yes. 2026 Can GRB 250702B be explained as the tidal disruption of a white dwarf by an intermediate mass black hole? Yes. – References
Rob A. J. Eyles-Ferris, Andrew King, Rhaana L. C. Starling, Peter G. Jonker, Andrew J. Levan, Antonio Martin-Carrillo, Tanmoy Laskar, Jillian C. Rastinejad, Nikhil Sarin, Nial R. Tanvir, Benjamin P. Gompertz, Nusrin Habeeb, Paul T. O’Brien, Massimiliano De Pasquale

Abstract
GRB 250702B is a unique astrophysical transient characterised by its nature as a repeating gamma-ray trigger. Its properties include possible periodicity in its gamma-ray light curve, an X-ray counterpart that rose prior to the gamma-ray outbursts and faded quickly, and radio and infrared counterparts. These features are difficult to reconcile with most models of high energy transients but we show that they are compatible with a white dwarf bound to an intermediate mass black hole that is tidally stripped over multiple pericentre passages before being fully disrupted. Accretion onto the black hole powers a mildly relativistic jet that produces the X-rays through internal processes and the infrared and radio counterparts through thermal emission and external shocks respectively but is unable to produce the gamma-ray emission on its own. We propose that chaotic debris streams from the multiple stripping episodes can collide with a period roughly the same as the orbital period of the star. These shocks produce hard X-ray photons that are upscattered by the jet to produce the observed gamma-ray emission. Future analysis of the jet properties will allow us to place firmer constraints on our model.

3
A WD-IMBH TDE model of GRB 250702B

Returning to the observed properties of this event, there are five key features that any attempt to explain GRB 250702B must address:

1.

Precursor X-ray emission from
\sim
few 10 ks prior to the gamma-ray flares.

2.

High peak X-ray luminosity that quickly fades as
\sim t^{-1.9}
.

3.

A fast decaying NIR counterpart and long lived radio counterpart.

4.

Gamma-ray flares peaking at hundreds of keV and stretching to MeV energies with fast (
\sim
seconds timescale) variability.

5.

Near constant intervals between the gamma-ray flare times.

We now examine GRB 250702B further in the context of a WD being disrupted by an IMBH and whether these features can be produced by such an event. We make several key assumptions - the periodicity investigated by
Levan et al. 2025a
is both real and related to the orbital period of the WD, and that the conditions, such as the properties of the magnetic field near the IMBH, are sufficient to launch a relativistic jet. Importantly, as shown by
Levan et al. 2025a
, the orbital period eliminates both supermassive black holes and main sequence stars as components of the progenitor system.

3.1
Repeated tidal stripping and accretion of a white dwarf

A key property of WDs is the inverted relationship between their mass and their radius - lower mass WDs have larger radii and vice versa
(
Ambrosino 2020
, e.g.)
. This particular property offers an explanation for the precursor X-ray emission observed in GRB 250702B. Analysis of extreme mass ratio inspirals
(
Amaro-Seoane 2018
, EMRIs,)
show that while gravitational wave emission does result in a decay in the orbital semi-major axis, this effect is slow and therefore the WD’s orbit is, at least initially, relatively stable
(
Peters 1964
, e.g.)
.

However, as the WD undergoes a pericentre passage, while its core may be outside the tidal radius
r_{t}
, it is possible that the outer layers may be sufficiently weakly bound that a small amount of mass is stripped and accreted by the IMBH. There is also a possibility of thermonuclear ignition as the WD is compressed by the tidal force of the IMBH
(
Rosswog et al. 2008
;
Rosswog et al. 2009
;
MacLeod et al. 2016
, e.g.)
although this effect is likely small for the shallow encounter assumed here. Assuming the WD is able to expand quickly enough to quench any thermonuclear burning, i.e.
t_{\rm dyn}<t_{\rm burning}

(
Rosswog et al. 2009
)
, the now lower mass WD will therefore expand in radius, with some impact from tidal distortion also possible
(
Maguire et al. 2020
, e.g.)
. This stripping will be repeated on the subsequent pericentre passages and is a runaway effect - the WD will have an incrementally larger radius on each passage and therefore more matter will be stripped - until the WD has lost sufficient mass that it will lie within
r_{t}
and be fully disrupted. To illustrate this, we show the evolution of
r_{t}
with WD and IMBH mass in Figure
2
. This model is similar to that proposed by
King 2022
to explain quasi-periodic eruptions (QPEs) from galactic nuclei. In fact, it is likely that the early tidal stripping events may resemble QPEs as the accretion rate will decay significantly quicker than a later full disruption.
King 2022
shows that for most systems, the mass transfer is stable and leads to an expanding orbit to conserve angular momentum. However, in this case, the orbital period is significantly shorter than the viscous timescale (of order several thousand seconds) which means that an accretion disk will not be efficiently formed before the WD returns to pericentre. In this case, then, angular momentum cannot be transferred back to the WD and the orbital semi-major axis does not expand. It is important to also note that although the stripping episodes result in fallback rates that evolve quicker than that from the final disruption, they can achieve comparable peak accretion rates
(
Nixon et al. 2021
)
.

The evolution of the tidal radius with IMBH and WD mass derived from the relations between WD mass and radius

. Note the WD will move left along these curves with repeated tidal stripping assuming
t_{\rm dyn}<t_{\rm burning}
.

Due to the repeated stripping events, shocks between the different debris streams are still likely to induce the formation of an accretion flow at early times
(
Rosswog et al. 2009
;
Bonnerot & Stone 2021
)
, although the transfer of angular momentum from a particular debris stream still cannot occur before the next pericentric passage and the runaway effect still occurs. This means that material from later passages will eventually be accreted and, due to the low mass of the IMBH involved, will result in highly super-Eddington accretion. In fact, assuming a
10^{4}~M_{\odot}
IMBH, the Eddington luminosity is of order
10^{42}
erg s
-1
. The vast majority of the stripped material will be accreted over
\lesssim 10^{6}
s so stripping and accreting as little
10^{-5}~M_{\odot}
will result in super-Eddington accretion. The timescale over which the jet formation occurs is unclear and due to the complex nature of the WD’s radius evolution and the amount of matter stripped, we leave this to a future study. However, a jet may only be launched only a few tens of passages prior to the full disruption of the WD as disordered magnetic fields may require the accretion rate to be several orders of magnitude super-Eddington.

The orbit of the WD will naturally differ from that expected in other TDE systems. Typically a parabolic (
e=1
) orbit is assumed but this results in a single encounter as the star is disrupted. Instead, we require the WD to be bound to the IMBH to ensure continuous orbits (i.e.
e<1
). Achieving this likely requires Hills capture where the WD is initially in a binary system that is disrupted by the IMBH leaving only the WD bound
(
Hills 1988
)
and has been suggested in the case of other repeating transients
(
King 2022
;
Cufari et al. 2022
, e.g.)
. If we assume a
0.6~M_{\odot}
WD, close to the peak of the WD mass distribution, orbiting an IMBH with mass
10^{4}~M_{\odot}
and that
r_{p}=r_{t}
, for the inferred period, we require
e\simeq 0.945
. As typically the more massive star is captured, we require either a WD binary or a WD in a binary with a low mass main sequence star such as an M dwarf. The fact that a presumed runaway is observed in GRB 250702B implies that the WD’s initial orbit was very close to its final orbit and it likely was bound to the IMBH only shortly before its disruption. We examine the likelihood of achieving this orbit further in Section
3.4
.

3.2
A wide angle, low Lorentz factor jet

This model of the early accretion and a jet launched through super-Eddington accretion can easily account for the precursor X-ray emission observed by
EP
. In this case, we assume the X-rays originate from some internal process in the jet and this emission dominates over any synchrotron from external shocks. We therefore assume a constant radiative and accretion efficiency in the X-ray band,
\epsilon_{X}
, and jet (half-)opening angle,
\theta_{j}
and the X-ray luminosity should therefore be roughly proportional to the total fallback rate. While we have no knowledge of the observed light curve, our model predicts a stepped or multi-peaked rise with a period approximately equal to the 2825 s identified by
Levan et al. 2025a
and remarkably similar to the behaviour exhibited by XRT 000519
(
Jonker et al. 2013
)
and predicted in
MacLeod et al. 2014
. We note, however, that we have only very weak constraints on the amount of material stripped, and this periodicity could be smoothed out somewhat over the tens of contributing pericentric passages. We therefore do not include it in Figure
1
.

It is difficult to independently predict exactly when the peak in the light curve will occur in this model and we therefore assume it occurs with the third
Fermi
trigger to coincide with the observed X-ray peak. We note that the final disruption could occur earlier, however, assuming the X-rays are primarily powered by accretion, an enhanced accretion rate could still occur coincident with the third trigger. Due to the low mass of the IMBH, and in direct contrast to the prediction from
Levan et al. 2025a
which assumed a low redshift event, the accretion rate is likely to stay highly super-Eddington for months

3.3
Stream induced shocks and gamma-ray emission

There is, however, another possible emission component that we have not yet examined. Following the disruption or stripping of the WD, the disrupted material will be stretched into a long debris stream. This stream will orbit the IMBH and, depending on the exact conditions, may intersect with itself relatively promptly
(
Darbha et al. 2019
;
Lu & Bonnerot 2020
;
Eyles-Ferris et al. 2024
, e.g.)
and can influence formation of the accretion flow
(
Andalman et al. 2022
)
. These collisions are highly energetic and release ‘free’ kinetic energy from material that will still be accreted. They can also enhance the accretion rate as the shocked material is rapidly driven onto the IMBH
(
Andalman et al. 2022
;
Eyles-Ferris et al. 2024
)
. If the final disruption does occur earlier than we assume above, this mechanism may explain why the observed X-ray peak would then be delayed relative to the peak in the fallback rate.

In addition to the stream interacting with itself, the earlier tidal stripping of the WD and the resulting debris streams result in additional and, due to our assumed
\beta=1
curtailing self-intersections, more probable sources of possible interactions. The individual debris streams will behave similarly and in particular, consecutive streams will be almost identical. If, then, enough amount of mass is stripped in a given passage, the debris stream will be long enough that the tip of the debris stream from the subsequent passage will collide with its tail. Such collisions will occur near pericentre and due to the similarity of the debris streams, will be broadly periodic in accordance with the orbital period of the WD. There is also the possibility that remnants from earlier debris streams can be compressed into the same region of space on a somewhat stochastic basis which may boost the mass and therefore energy of these collisions. There is also the possibility of stream-disk collisions that may release energy in a similar fashion to high luminosities
(
Steinberg & Stone 2024
)
.

The energy released by these collisions is likely to vary somewhat but can be roughly constrained using the formalism of
Darbha et al. 2019
. Near the pericentre, the tidal forces exerted by the black hole will compress the streams
(
Luminet & Marck 1985
, e.g.)
to a radius
\sim R_{\rm WD}
and the density at the surface will therefore be comparable to the original WD i.e.
\rho\sim 3M_{\star}/4\pi R_{\rm WD}^{3}
. The kinetic energy of the shocked material is then given by
\rho v_{s}^{2}
where
v_{s}
is the shock velocity which is comparable to the pericentric velocity
v_{s}\sim v_{p}=\left(GM_{\bullet}/r_{p}\right)^{1/2}
. We assume that
r_{p}=r_{t}
for the
M_{\rm WD}=0.6~M_{\odot}
,
M_{\bullet}=10^{4}~M_{\odot}
system described above and therefore
v_{p}\sim 0.26c
. The energy is deposited into an optically thin surface layer with thickness
\Delta R\lesssim(cR_{\rm WD}/\rho\kappa v_{p})^{1/2}
, where
\kappa
is the opacity, and therefore the total energy released thermally is given by

\Delta E\sim 2\pi R_{\rm WD}l_{c}\Delta R\rho v_{s}^{2}\sim 2\pi l_{c}\left(R_{\rm WD}^{3}v_{p}^{3}c\rho/\kappa\right)^{1/2}

(1)

where
l_{c}\sim R_{\rm WD}
. Assuming a standard opacity of
\kappa=0.4
cm
2
g
-1
, we therefore estimate a luminosity of
\sim 6\times 10^{47}
erg s
-1
emitted over a time 0.03 s derived from the light crossing time of the
R_{\rm WD}
. Assuming a perfect blackbody, the spectrum will peak in the hard X-rays at
\sim 50
keV.

However, whether two consecutive streams collide will also be affected by precession of the debris streams. 4
Conclusion

To conclude, we have presented and analysed the results of X-ray observations of GRB 250702B and have also identified several key features from across the electromagnetic spectrum that must be explained in any model of this event. We have shown that all of these features can, withe data currently available to us, be exhibited by the tidal disruption of white dwarf by an intermediate mass black hole. These features and how they can arise from a WD-IMBH TDE are summarised below:

1.

Precursor X-ray emission from
\sim
few 10 ks prior to the gamma-ray flares.
The WD undergoes multiple pericentric passages due to its bound orbit. Material is repeatedly stripped prior to its final disruption and accreted to produce a rising X-ray counterpart.

2.

High peak X-ray luminosity that quickly fades as
\sim t^{-1.9}
.
Super-Eddington accretion rates result in the launch of a wide jet with a modest Lorentz factor. Internal processes produce the X-ray emission and the steeper slope than classically expected from a TDE is a result of the WD’s eccentric orbit.

3.

A fast decaying NIR counterpart and long lived radio counterpart.
NIR emission is likely to be thermal emission from the accretion flow or some other accretion source which will fade quickly following the X-ray. The radio emission is the result of the jet shocking external material to produce a synchrotron afterglow.

4.

Gamma-ray flares peaking at hundreds of keV and stretching to MeV energies with fast (
\sim
seconds timescale) variability.
Stream-stream or stream-disk shocks can produce a flash of hard X-ray photons which are upscattered by the jet. The modest
\Gamma
of the jet is more than sufficient to achieve the MeV photon energies observed.

5.

Near constant intervals between the gamma-ray flare times.
The shocks occur as the stream or disk encounter each other at the time of the WD’s pericentric passages. Such interactions do not occur on each pass due to the precession of one or more of these components.

A thorough rate calculation is beyond the scope of this Letter, however, the expected rate is broadly compatible with that implied by the uniqueness of this event. While there is no conclusive proof that GRB 250702B was indeed a WD-IMBH TDE and a collapsar remains eminently feasible, we have shown that it is possible within the data currently available to us. Future observations and analysis will help determine the exact properties of the jet and allow us to fully evaluate the inferences we have made here.
