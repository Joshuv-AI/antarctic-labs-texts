Prevalence and risk factors of malnutrition in elderly hospitalized patients with post-stroke dysphagia
Authors: Yu’e Wu, Mei Chen, Xilin Liu, Ying Gao, Xiaohuan Li, Jingqin Wang, Shuqing Zhang, Linli Liang, Yaoyang Huo, Jianrong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stroke has emerged as a major public health issue. Dysphagia affects more than half of stroke survivors and is frequently accompanied by malnutrition, particularly in elderly patients. This study investigated the prevalence and risk factors of malnutrition in elderly stroke patients with dysphagia using a large, nationally representative database. Data were extracted from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS) for 2010–2019. Elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with a primary diagnosis of stroke and dysphagia were identified. Descriptive statistics and multivariate logistic regression were used to analyze the association between demographic factors, comorbidities, and malnutrition. The overall incidence of malnutrition in elderly stroke patients with dysphagia was 12.5%. Significant risk factors for malnutrition included fluid and electrolyte disorders and having three or more comorbidities. Malnourished patients had significantly higher mortality rates and longer hospital stays compared to those without malnutrition. Malnourished patients had significantly higher mortality rates (9.9% vs. 6.9%) and longer hospital stays (median 10 vs. 6 days). Malnutrition is prevalent in elderly stroke patients with dysphagia, with a 12.5% incidence. The findings highlight the critical need for early nutritional screening and intervention, particularly in patients with multiple comorbidities, Black race, and those admitted to urban hospitals. Addressing malnutrition in these high-risk patients is essential to improving clinical outcomes, reducing hospital stays, and lowering mortality rates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23448-3.

Introduction

Stroke is a significant health issue in the United States, affecting approximately 795,000 individuals annually1. Between 40% and 95% of stroke survivors develop various complications2, with dysphagia being among the most common, affecting over 50% of stroke survivors3. Swallowing is a complex sensorimotor process requiring coordinated activation of multiple muscle groups and neural pathways across cortical, subcortical, and brainstem regions4. Therefore, damage to any of these regions due to stroke can lead to dysphagia. This condition is often compounded by age-related factors such as decreased sensation, increased muscle stiffness, reduced cortical plasticity, and diminished muscle function, collectively known as geriatric dysphagia5. Although most patients recover swallowing function within one week of the acute phase, some, particularly elderly individuals, experience persistent dysphagia that can significantly impact their recovery6.

The prevalence of malnutrition among elderly post-stroke patients with dysphagia can reach 52%7. Elderly patients with post-stroke dysphagia and malnutrition are at a significantly higher risk of poor clinical outcomes, including increased susceptibility to infections, delayed wound healing, and higher rates of medical complications. Notably, malnutrition has been strongly correlated with increased mortality in elderly stroke patients; one study reported a hazard ratio (HR) of 4.615 for mortality in patients with severe malnutrition8. This underscores the pivotal role of nutritional status in long-term survival following a stroke. Beyond mortality, the combined burden of dysphagia and malnutrition adversely affects key clinical outcomes, including prolonged hospitalization, impaired functional recovery, and diminished overall prognosis9,10. Therefore, assessing nutritional status and identifying risk factors in stroke patients is critical for implementing appropriate nutritional interventions that minimize adverse outcomes and reduce the risk of malnutrition11–14. Risk factors for malnutrition in elderly patients with dysphagia include cognitive impairment, cardiovascular disease, environmental factors, and a history of Parkinson’s disease15–17. Prior studies have identified advanced age, a history of stroke, bilateral hemiparesis, high Rankin scores, and pneumonia as primary contributors to post-stroke malnutrition10,18,19. However, many of these studies were limited by small sample sizes and geographical constraints, which reduced the generalizability of their findings.

This study aims to further explore the incidence of malnutrition and its associated risk factors in elderly stroke patients hospitalized with dysphagia, using a large, nationally representative database. This approach will provide a robust foundation for clinical interventions targeting malnutrition in this high-risk population.

Methods

Data source

This study utilized data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS), the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare database in the United States, sponsored by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). The NIS provides a stratified sample from over 1,000 hospitals across 46 states, representing nearly 20% of all annual U.S. hospital admissions20. The database includes comprehensive patient information, such as demographics, admission status, hospital characteristics, insurance type, diagnoses, procedures coded using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision-Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM), comorbidities, length of stay (LOS), in-hospital mortality, total charges, and discharge disposition. Given that the data used in this cross-sectional study is anonymized and publicly accessible, it is exempt from requiring ethical approval, in accordance with established research guidelines.

Data collection

We identified adult patients (≥ 65 years) hospitalized between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019, with a primary diagnosis of stroke and dysphagia (Supplementary Table S1 & S2) from the NIS database. Exclusion criteria included missing data, patients younger than 65 years, and non-stroke admissions (Fig. 1). After screening, patients were categorized into two groups based on the presence of malnutrition. Demographic variables, such as age, race, and sex, were collected, along with clinical characteristics, including admission mode, LOS, total hospitalization cost, insurance type, and in-hospital mortality (Table 1). Medical complications, such as pneumonia, sepsis, acute renal failure, urinary tract infections, cardiac complications, and respiratory failure, were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision-Clinical Modification (ICD-9)and ICD-10 codes. Additional clinical outcomes, including comorbidities, were also documented to assess their potential influence on the relationship between dysphagia and malnutrition in elderly stroke patients.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25 (IBM SPSS Statistics, United States of America). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographics and clinical characteristics of the study population. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for between-group comparisons, while categorical data were analyzed using chi-square tests. To evaluate the association between dysphagia and malnutrition, both univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were employed, adjusting for confounders such as demographics, comorbidities, stroke characteristics, and hospital characteristics. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated (Table 1). Multiple regression analysis was also used to balance baseline differences between the dysphagia and non-dysphagia groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.001 for all analyses.

Results

Incidence of malnutrition in stroke patients with dysphagia

This study identified 153,983 elderly stroke patients with dysphagia. Of these, 19,317 developed malnutrition during their hospital stay, yielding an overall incidence rate of 12.5%. The malnutrition incidence rate fluctuated between 2010 and 2019, with an overall increase from 11.9% in 2010 to 13.2% in 2019. The highest incidence (13.5%) was recorded in 2018, with minor declines in 2016 (11.5%) and 2013 (11.9%) before subsequent increases (Fig. 2) (Table 2).

Demographic and hospital characteristics of patients in the two groups

The median age of the study population was 80 years. Significant racial disparities were observed (p < 0.001), with white patients constituting 64.9% of the malnourished group and 69.5% of the non-malnourished group. Black patients were more represented in the malnourished group (15.5% vs. 12.0%). Besides, malnourished patients had significantly longer hospital stays (median 10 days vs. 6 days, p < 0.001) and incurred higher total charges ($80,668 vs. $50,364, p < 0.001). The mortality rate was notably higher in the malnourished group (9.9% vs. 6.9%, p < 0.001). Urban hospitals managed a higher proportion of malnourished cases (93.3% vs. 91.7%, p < 0.001), and teaching hospitals had higher rates of malnutrition cases (65.3% vs. 63.0%, p < 0.001) (Table 3) (Supplementary Fig. 3) (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Risk factors associated with the occurrence of malnutrition in post-stroke dysphagia

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several independent risk factors for the development of malnutrition (Table 4), including fluid and electrolyte disorders (OR = 2.157, 95% CI = 2.090–2.225, p < 0.001); deficiency anemia (OR = 1.603, 95% CI = 1.541–1.668, p < 0.001); other neurological disorders (OR = 1.495, 95% CI = 1.442–1.548, p < 0.001); coagulopathy (OR = 1.377, 95% CI = 1.307–1.450, p < 0.001); Asian or Pacific Islander race (OR = 1.286, 95% CI = 1.183–1.398, p < 0.001); Hispanic ethnicity (OR = 1.127, 95% CI = 1.059–1.199, p < 0.001); and urban hospital location (OR = 1.160, 95% CI = 1.086–1.238, p < 0.001). Notably, the cumulative effect of multiple comorbidities was strongly correlated with malnutrition risk. Patients with three or more comorbidities exhibited the highest risk (OR = 3.707, 95% CI = 3.503–3.924, p < 0.001) (Table 3) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Associated complications and malnutrition in post-stroke dysphagia

Several complications were significantly associated with the development of malnutrition (Table 5). Malnourished patients with post-stroke dysphagia may be more susceptible to pneumonia (26.7%), sepsis (15.4%), acute myocardial infarction (7.2%), deep vein thrombosis (4.4%), respiratory failure (12.4%), dehydration (12.6%). Multiple regression analysis suggested that malnutrition was associated with sepsis (OR = 2.072, CI = 1.974–2.175, p < 0.001), pneumonia (OR = 1.765, 95% CI = 1.701–1.832, p < 0.001), dehydration (OR = 1.782, 95% CI = 1.698–1.870, p < 0.001), deep vein thrombosis (OR = 1.607, 95% CI = 1.484–1.739, p < 0.001), respiratory failure (OR = 1.398, 95% CI = 1.331–1.469, p < 0.001), and acute myocardial infarction (OR = 1.222, 95% CI = 1.148–1.299, p < 0.001).

Discussion

Dysphagia, a common complication affecting over half of stroke survivors, significantly increases the risk of malnutrition, particularly in elderly patients due to age-related physiological changes. This study highlights the significant prevalence of malnutrition in elderly stroke patients with dysphagia, reporting a prevalence of 12.5%. The primary contribution of our study lies in validating previously reported associations between malnutrition, comorbidities, and race using an exceptionally large, nationally representative dataset. Importantly, we provide precise quantitative estimates of the magnitude of these risks, thereby substantiating prior findings with more robust evidence. Previous studies have reported malnutrition rates in stroke patients ranging from 8% to 34%12,18,21,22, placing our findings within this established range. Our results offer valuable insights into the considerable burden of malnutrition, especially in patients with dysphagia, a condition often under-recognized and under-managed in stroke care23,24. A 2009 systematic review indicated that patients with dysphagia are at increased risk of malnutrition25. This review identified key factors associated with malnutrition, including race, gender, comorbidity burden, and specific conditions such as ischemic anemia, chronic blood loss anemia, coagulation disorders, and fluid-electrolyte imbalances, consistent with the findings of Sabbouh et al.26,27. Furthermore, our study demonstrated an increased risk of complications, such as pneumonia, sepsis, and respiratory failure, in the malnourished group, emphasizing the critical need to address malnutrition in this vulnerable population. These findings aligned with existing literature that highlights the significant clinical consequences of malnutrition in stroke patients, such as prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and elevated mortality rates19–31.

Our analysis revealed that malnourished patients experienced considerably extended hospital stays (averaging 10 days compared to 6 days for their well-nourished counterparts) and incurred substantially greater hospitalization costs ($80,668 versus $50,364), consistent with previous reports32. The higher prevalence of malnutrition in teaching hospitals (65.3%) compared to urban hospitals (93.3%) suggests potential differences in identification, documentation, or referral patterns. The association between urban hospital location and higher malnutrition risk may reflect multiple factors. First, large urban medical centers often serve as referral hubs for more severe or complex cases, inherently increasing the risk of malnutrition. Second, urban hospitals may have more comprehensive diagnostic practices and specialized nutrition teams, leading to better recognition and documentation of malnutrition.Besides, a significantly higher mortality rate was observed among malnourished patients (9.9% vs. 6.9%). A cohort study found that severely malnourished patients had an adjusted HR of 4.615 for mortality compared to patients with normal nutritional status, corroborating the findings of the present study33. This underscores the importance of early nutritional intervention for this vulnerable group.

Our study also found that certain racial and ethnic groups—specifically Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Pacific Islander individuals—exhibited a higher risk of malnutrition, with ORs ranging from 1.127 to 1.312. These findings highlight the role of socioeconomic and healthcare access factors that may disproportionately affect these groups, leading to delays in the diagnosis and management of malnutrition. This observation aligned with existing literature showing ethnic disparities in health outcomes and underscored the need for targeted interventions for minority groups at higher risk of malnutrition8,34.

Furthermore, the number of coexisting medical conditions emerged as a strong indicator of malnutrition risk. The odds of malnutrition increased with the number of comorbidities, particularly in patients with three or more comorbidities (OR = 3.707, p < 0.001), aligning with previous reports13,35. This finding further emphasizes the complex and multifactorial nature of malnutrition in individuals who have experienced a stroke. Specifically, the presence of comorbidities related to cardiovascular health, renal function, and metabolic processes can initiate a cascade of events that exacerbates nutritional status36–40. These results suggest that managing comorbidities should be a priority in the prevention and treatment of malnutrition, reinforcing the importance of an integrated care model for stroke patients.

Logistic regression analysis identified significant associations between comorbidities—such as deficiency anemia (OR = 1.603), chronic blood loss anemia (OR = 1.551), and fluid-electrolyte disorders (OR = 2.157)—and malnutrition, consistent with previous studies41–43. Fluid and electrolyte disturbances, such as hypernatremia and hyponatremia, are prevalent in the elderly and can exacerbate malnutrition by impairing appetite and nutrient absorption44,45. Iron deficiency and blood loss anemia can exacerbate malnutrition in post-stroke patients by impairing neuromuscular function, depleting iron stores, hindering nutrient absorption, and increasing the body’s demand for essential nutrients46,47. Furthermore, our study suggests that both malnutrition and nutritional imbalances may result from certain neurological disorders (OR = 1.495) and peptic ulcer disease (OR = 1.427), excluding bleeding, consistent with previous findings48,49. Coagulation dysfunction may contribute to the development of malnutrition in post-stroke patients by increasing the risk of aspiration and aspiration pneumonia, impairing neurologic recovery, and exacerbating dysphagia50. Our study also found alcohol abuse (OR = 1.336) to be a risk factor. Existing research has shown that alcohol abuse is associated with deficiencies in essential vitamins and minerals and impaired nutrient absorption, thereby increasing the risk of malnutrition51,52. Chronic pulmonary disease (OR = 1.163) may further hinder recovery in stroke patients by increasing systemic symptoms and energy expenditure, potentially leading to malnutrition, especially due to dysphagia and under-recognition51. While one study found that dysphagia may affect prognosis in patients with congestive heart failure (CHF) through malnutrition, sarcopenia, and cardiac cachexia, there is a lack of literature regarding the effect of congestive heart failure (OR = 1.115) specifically on malnutrition after dysphagia in stroke10. In addition, our study identified peripheral vascular disease as a risk factor for the development of malnutrition in elderly patients with dysphagia after stroke, but there is no relevant literature report, and further studies are needed. Besides, obesity was identified as a protective factor in the logistic regression analysis (Table 4). One possible explanation is that increased energy reserves may mitigate some of the effects of malnutrition42,53,54.In our study, obesity was identified as a protective factor against malnutrition, a phenomenon often referred to as the ‘obesity paradox’ in nutritional epidemiology55,56. This counterintuitive finding may be partly explained by the role of greater energy reserves in obese individuals. These reserves could provide a metabolic buffer during the acute catabolic phase following a stroke, potentially mitigating rapid weight and muscle mass loss57. Furthermore, the clinical diagnosis of malnutrition might be under-recognized in obese patients due to a focus on weight rather than comprehensive nutritional assessment, leading to potential misclassification or detection bias58. While our data did not allow for body composition analysis to explore this further, this observation underscores the complexity of nutritional status assessment in acutely ill, elderly stroke patients. Furthermore, logistic regression analysis of complications in this study showed a higher prevalence of pneumonia, sepsis, acute myocardial infarction, deep vein thrombosis, respiratory failure, and dehydration among malnourished patients compared to those without malnutrition.

While this study offers valuable insights into the prevalence and risk factors of malnutrition in elderly stroke patients with dysphagia, several limitations must be acknowledged. A primary consideration is the reliance on administrative data, which may affect the accuracy of certain variables, such as the diagnosis of malnutrition and specific complications. Second, the identification of dysphagia relied on ICD coding, which is known to significantly under-capture this condition compared to systematic screening protocols59. The low prevalence of dysphagia in our sample (approximately 9.4%) compared to the > 50% prevalence reported in prospective studies that employ standardized swallowing assessments underscores this limitation3. This underdiagnosis likely introduced a selection bias, as our ‘dysphagia’ cohort probably represents only the most severe or clinically evident cases, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to all stroke patients with swallowing difficulties. The use of ICD codes to identify comorbidities and complications may also lead to misclassification or underreporting, potentially limiting the reliability of these findings. Because the identification of malnutrition relied on ICD coding, our results may have been affected by misclassification or underreporting. Milder cases of malnutrition may not be coded, leading to a likely underestimation of the true prevalence and potentially attenuating associations between malnutrition and outcomes. The NIS database lacks certain crucial variables, such as the severity of dysphagia, cognitive function levels, pre-admission nutritional status, and the availability of social support. The absence of these variables limited our ability to fully account for factors that strongly influence nutritional outcomes and may have introduced residual confounding.

Although the large sample size enhances the generalizability of our results, the retrospective design of the study restricts the ability to draw causal inferences. Furthermore, as the NIS is an administrative database for a single hospitalization, it lacks information on patients’ pre-admission nutritional status. Therefore, we could not identify or exclude individuals with pre-existing malnutrition, which may influence the reported incidence and risk factor associations. The reported incidence of malnutrition in our study is likely an underestimate due to significant underdiagnosis in real-world clinical settings. This is a well-documented challenge, particularly for conditions that rely on clinical recognition and specific coding. For instance, a similar phenomenon of underdiagnosis has been reported for chronic respiratory diseases, where a vast number of cases remain undiagnosed due to limitations in diagnostic capacity and primary care recognition60. Furthermore, in the context of malnutrition specifically, the ‘obesity paradox’ — where obesity appears as a protective factor — may further contribute to underdiagnosis, as nutritional deficits can be overlooked in individuals with higher body weight61. This diagnostic gap underscores the need for improved and systematic nutritional screening in elderly hospitalized stroke patients. Consequently, further prospective studies are necessary to validate these findings and to investigate the impact of targeted interventions on malnutrition outcomes in this patient population.

Conclusions

Malnutrition affects 12.5% of elderly stroke patients with dysphagia, with its incidence increasing over the past decade. This study highlights key risk factors, including fluid and electrolyte disorders, deficiency anemia, coagulopathy, and multiple comorbidities, with patients harboring three or more comorbidities at the highest risk. Malnutrition is significantly associated with prolonged hospital stays, higher costs, and increased mortality, as well as complications like pneumonia, sepsis, and respiratory failure. These findings underscore the importance of early nutritional screening and intervention to mitigate adverse outcomes and guide targeted clinical strategies for high-risk populations. Furthermore, while it was suggested to examine whether obesity modifies the risk of complications in patients with malnutrition, our study design and the inherent limitations in the consistent diagnosis of malnutrition in obese individuals within administrative data precluded a reliable analysis of this important interaction, which should be explored in future prospective studies.
