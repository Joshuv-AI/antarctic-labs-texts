The Multi-Query Paradox in Zeroth-Order Optimization
Wei Lin, Qingyu Song, Hong Xu

Abstract
Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization provides a powerful framework for problems where explicit gradients are unavailable and have to be approximated using only queries to function value. The prevalent single-query approach is simple, but suffers from high estimation variance, motivating a multi-query paradigm to improves estimation accuracy. This, however, creates a critical trade-off: under a fixed budget of queries (i.e. cost), queries per iteration and the total number of optimization iterations are inversely proportional to one another. How to best allocate this budget is a fundamental, under-explored question. This work systematically resolves this query allocation problem. We analyze two aggregation methods: the de facto simple averaging (ZO-Avg), and a new Projection Alignment method (ZO-Align) we derive from local surrogate minimization. By deriving convergence rates for both methods that make the dependence on the number of queries explicit across strongly convex, convex, non-convex, and stochastic settings, we uncover a stark dichotomy: For ZO-Avg, we prove that using more than one query per iteration is always query-inefficient, rendering the single-query approach optimal. On the contrary, ZO-Align generally performs better with more queries per iteration, resulting in a full-subspace estimation as the optimal approach. Thus, our work clarifies that the multi-query problem boils down to a choice not about an intermediate query size, but between two classic algorithms, a choice dictated entirely by the aggregation method used. These theoretical findings are also consistently validated by extensive experiments.



1
Introduction

Zeroth-order (ZO) optimization provides a powerful framework for problems where explicit gradient information is unavailable or prohibitively expensive to compute
(
Conn, Scheinberg, and Vicente 2009
)
. Unlike first-order methods that rely on analytical gradients, ZO algorithms guide their search using only function value queries to approximate gradients. Typically, it is achieved by evaluating the objective function along one or more random directions and observing the change in function value
(
Spall 2002
;
Mania, Guy, and Recht 2018
)
. This paradigm has recently gained significant attention due to its applicability to a wide array of challenging, high-dimensional problems, including fine-tuning of LLMs
(
Malladi et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024
)
, policy optimization in reinforcement learning
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
, and hyperparameter tuning
(
Li et al. 2021
)
.

The predominant approach in modern ZO optimization is the single-query method, where a gradient estimate is constructed using one random direction per optimization step
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
. While foundational, these estimators are inherently noisy, with an estimation variance that often scales with the problem’s dimension
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
. A natural extension to mitigate this issue is the multi-query approach, which employs a block of queries at each iteration to construct a more accurate gradient estimate
(
Duchi et al. 2015
)
.
However, this approach introduces a fundamental trade-off. Using multiple queries per iteration inverse proportionally reduces the total number of iteration steps that can be performed under a fixed budget of queries.
A systematic investigation of the multi-query paradigm requires addressing two basic questions: (1) What is the most effective method for combining information from multiple query directions into a single, cohesive gradient estimate? (2) For a fixed total query budget, is it more efficient to perform many iterations with noisy, single-query estimates, or fewer iterations with higher-quality, multi-query estimates by consuming more queries per iteration?

This work systematically addresses these questions. First, to resolve the aggregation problem, we propose a new multi-query estimator called Projection Alignment (ZO-Align), from the first principle of local surrogate minimization. While similar estimators have been proposed from the perspective of sparse gradient recovery
(
Cai et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
, our optimization-centric derivation provides a more fundamental justification for the method, independent of any sparsity assumptions. For a comprehensive comparison, we also analyze the canonical strategy of simply averaging independent estimates (ZO-Avg)
(
Duchi et al. 2015
)
. Our initial analysis of the estimators’ properties reveals that ZO-Align achieves a significantly lower mean squared error, motivating a deeper investigation into its performance within an optimization context.

Second, to resolve the query allocation problem, we derive the convergence rates for algorithms using both ZO-Align and ZO-Avg, making the dependence on the number of queries used per iteration explicit. We then leverage these rates to formally pose and solve an optimization problem: for a fixed total query budget, find the best allocation of queries per iteration that minimizes the final optimization error. To ensure the generality and robustness of our conclusions, this entire theoretical framework is systematically applied across a wide spectrum of problem structures, including strongly convex, convex, non-convex, and stochastic settings.

Our analysis reveals a stark dichotomy where the optimal allocation strategy is critically dependent on the chosen aggregation method. For ZO-Avg, we prove that it cannot effectively utilize information from multiple queries; any attempt to use more than one query per iteration is detrimental, and the optimal strategy is always the single-query approach. In direct contrast, the ZO-Align estimator is capable of efficiently harnessing multi-query information. For ZO-Align, we prove that performance improves with the number of queries, making the optimal strategy a full-subspace estimation where the number of queries matches the problem’s dimension.

To complement our theoretical analysis, we conducted extensive experiments across a suite of optimization problems. The empirical results consistently validate our theoretical findings, demonstrating the practical implications of the query allocation dichotomy. Our work clarifies that the choice is not about finding an optimal intermediate query size, but rather a commitment to an aggregation method that dictates whether the optimal algorithm is a sequential single-query method or a full-subspace estimation.

The primary contributions of this work are as follows:

•

We propose the ZO-Align estimator based on local surrogate minimization, providing a clear optimization-centric perspective that complements existing geometric interpretations.

•

We deliver the first comprehensive convergence analysis that directly compares ZO-Avg and ZO-Align across strongly convex, convex, non-convex, and stochastic settings, revealing a fundamental and consistent dichotomy in their performance.

•

We provide rigorous theoretical proofs that under a fixed query budget, the optimal query allocation for ZO-Avg is always single-query, whereas the optimal allocation for ZO-Align is to use the maximum possible number of queries, resolving the query allocation question for these two classes of estimators.

•

We empirically verify our theoretical findings, demonstrating that ZO-Align with a large query size significantly outperforms both ZO-Avg and single-query baselines across various optimization settings.

Related Work

ZO gradient estimation.

Zeroth-order optimization approximates gradients using finite-difference techniques, a concept explored through various random perturbation strategies. For instance, theoretical convergence has been demonstrated using Gaussian random noise perturbations
(
Nesterov and Spokoiny 2017
)
, while other approaches have utilized uniform sampling
(
Flaxman, Kalai, and McMahan 2004
)
and coordinate-wise perturbations
(
Lian et al. 2016
)
. A distinct line of research focuses on problems where the gradient is assumed to be sparse, framing the estimation task as one of compressed sensing; here, techniques like LASSO
(
Wang et al. 2018
)
or other forms of regularized regression
(
Choromanski et al. 2020
)
enable the reconstruction of a sparse gradient from a limited number of function queries
(
Cai et al. 2022
)
. More recently, methods based on linear interpolation have been investigated as a potentially more accurate alternative
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Research suggests that linear interpolation can yield better gradient estimates than Gaussian smoothing
(
Berahas et al. 2019
)
and may require significantly fewer samples to do so
(
Berahas et al. 2022
)
.

Query-efficient ZO optimizers.

A key challenge in ZOO is its high query complexity, motivating research into more efficient methods. Some methods build more accurate local models to estimate update direction by incorporating second-order Hessian information
(
Ye et al. 2025
)
, or by leveraging problem geometry via Riemannian optimization
(
He et al. 2024
)
. Other approaches optimize the querying process itself, such as using compressed sensing to achieve double-logarithmic query complexity for sparse problems
(
Qiu and Tong 2024
)
, adaptively reusing old queries to reduce variance
(
Xiao, Ling, and Chen 2023
)
, or learning a sampling policy with reinforcement learning
(
Zhai et al. 2024
)
. However, despite these advances, these approaches are all centered on improving the quality of the estimate at each individual step. In contrast, the fundamental problem of how to optimally allocate a fixed query budget to maximize its utility remains a critical yet under-explored question.

3
ZO Estimators: From Single-Query to Multi-Query

This section introduces the core ZO gradient estimators used in our analysis. We begin with the standard single-query estimator before presenting two multi-query approaches: the intuitive Naive Averaging (ZO-Avg) and the principled Projection Alignment (ZO-Align), which we derive from local surrogate minimization. Then we analyze their Mean Squared Error (MSE) to establish a fundamental difference in their accuracy, which underpins the main convergence results in Section
4
.

3.1
Preliminaries and the Single-Query Estimator

We consider the unconstrained optimization problem
\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)
where direct access to the gradient
\nabla f(x)
is unavailable..
Throughout this work, we assume the objective function
f
is differentiable and
L
-smooth:

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

ZO algorithms navigate this problem by iteratively updating a parameter vector
x_{t}
using a gradient estimate
\hat{g}(x_{t})
constructed from function value queries:
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta_{t}\hat{g}(x_{t})
.

The foundational building block for most ZO methods is the single-query estimator. It approximates the gradient by evaluating the function along a single random direction
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A common form is given by:

\hat{g}_{u}(x)=\frac{f(x+\mu u)-f(x)}{\mu}u,

where
\mu>0
is a small smoothing parameter. This form is derived from the first-order Taylor expansion of
f(x)
around
x
:

f(x+\mu u)=f(x)+\mu u^{T}\nabla f(x)+O(\mu^{2}).

For a sufficiently small
\mu
, the estimator approximates the projection of the true gradient onto the direction
u
:
\hat{g}_{u}(x)\approx(u^{T}\nabla f(x))u
. While fundamental, this single-direction estimate is inherently noisy, limiting its effectiveness. A natural extension is to probe the function along multiple directions to construct a more accurate gradient estimate.

3.2
Simple Averaging Estimator (ZO-Avg)

The most direct method for aggregating information from a block of
q
queries is to compute
q
independent single-query estimates and average them. Let
U=[u_{1},\dots,u_{q}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times q}
be a matrix whose columns are random direction vectors drawn independently from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. We let
q\leq d
and assume the columns of
U
are linearly independent. The averaging estimator, which we term ZO-Avg, is defined as:

\hat{g}_{\text{AVG}}(x)=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\frac{f(x+\mu u_{i})-f(x)}{\mu}u_{i}.

Our subsequent theoretical analysis focuses on the idealized estimators where the bias from the finite difference is negligible (i.e., as
\mu\to 0
). This allows us to isolate the error stemming from the geometric properties of the estimators. In this idealized form, the estimator approximates:

\hat{g}_{\text{AVG}}(x)\approx\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}u_{i}u_{i}^{T}\right)\nabla f(x)=\frac{1}{q}UU^{T}\nabla f(x).

This estimator is appealing due to its simplicity and straightforward parallel implementation. A key property of ZO-Avg is that it provides an unbiased estimate of the true gradient.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the columns of
U
be i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The ZO-Avg estimator is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{AVG}(x)]=\nabla f(x)
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

While unbiasedness is a desirable property, it does not guarantee efficiency. As we will show, the high variance of this estimator plays a critical role in its convergence behavior.

3.3
Projection Alignment Method (ZO-Align)

Instead of simply averaging, we adopt a more principled, model-based perspective. The
q
queries provide a snapshot of the function’s local geometry within the column space of
U
. We seek the best update step of the form
-Uy
for some coefficient vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
by minimizing a local surrogate of the objective function. Given that
f
is
L
-smooth, we can form a local quadratic upper bound around the point
x
:

f(x-Uy)\leq f(x)-\nabla f(x)^{T}(Uy)+\frac{L}{2}\|Uy\|^{2}.

Minimizing this quadratic upper bound with respect to
y
is a principled way to find the best local step within the column space. This yields the optimal coefficient vector:

y^{*}=\frac{1}{L}(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}\nabla f(x).

The optimal update direction is therefore proportional to
Uy^{*}=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}\nabla f(x)
. This motivates our second estimator, which replaces the true directional derivatives with their finite-difference approximations:

\hat{g}_{{\text{ALG}}}(x)=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{f(x+\mu u_{1})-f(x)}{\mu}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{f(x+\mu u_{q})-f(x)}{\mu}\end{bmatrix}.

The name “Projection Alignment” comes from the powerful geometric property of this estimator. The matrix
P_{U}=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}
is the orthogonal projection operator onto the column space of
U
. Therefore, assuming
\mu\to 0
,
\hat{g}_{\text{ALG}}
is the orthogonal projection of the true gradient onto the query subspace. This means that, by construction, its own projection onto each query direction
u_{i}
is identical to the estimated projection of the true gradient:
u_{i}^{T}\hat{g}_{\text{ALG}}\approx u_{i}^{T}\nabla f(x)
.

This reveals a crucial difference from ZO-Avg. While ZO-Align is constructed to be geometrically consistent within the sampled subspace, ZO-Avg is not. The projection of the ZO-Avg estimate onto a query direction
u_{i}
does not generally match the true directional derivative, leading to a less accurate representation of the local geometry.

Unlike ZO-Avg, ZO-Align is a biased estimator.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let the columns of
U
be i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The ZO-Align estimator has an expected value of
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{ALG}(x)]=\frac{q}{d}\nabla f(x)
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

3.4
Accuracy Analysis: A Tale of Two Estimators

The two estimators present a classic statistical trade-off. ZO-Avg is unbiased but suffers from high variance. In contrast, ZO-Align accepts a well-structured bias in exchange for a dramatic reduction in estimation error. To formalize this comparison, we analyze the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of each estimator, defined as
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g}(x)-\nabla f(x)\|^{2}]
, which captures the total error from both bias and variance.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
g=\nabla f(x)
and assume the query directions are i.i.d. samples from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. The Mean Squared Error of the idealized estimators are:

1.

For ZO-Avg:
\quad\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{\text{AVG}})=\frac{d+1}{q}\|g\|^{2}
.

2.

For ZO-Align:
\quad\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{\text{ALG}})=\frac{d-q}{d}\|g\|^{2}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

For any
q>1
, it is clear that
\frac{d-q}{d}\ll\frac{d+1}{q}
, indicating that ZO-Align offers a significantly lower estimation error. This fundamental difference in estimator quality is the primary driver of the performance gap we will establish in the following sections. It highlights that the principled, model-based approach of ZO-Align is far more effective at aggregating information from multiple queries than the naive averaging of ZO-Avg.

4
Query Allocation Dichotomy

This section presents our main theoretical results, which establish a fundamental dichotomy in how multi-query ZO estimators should be utilized.
Upon several standard settings, we analyze the convergence behavior of optimization algorithms using the ZO-Avg and ZO-Align estimators under a fixed total query budget. We first consider the deterministic setting, where we have exact function evaluations, for three classes of functions:
\mu
-strongly convex, convex, and non-convex. We then extend our analysis to the stochastic setting for convex functions, where function evaluations are corrupted by noise. Across all these settings, we show that the simple averaging of ZO-Avg is an inefficient mechanism for aggregating query information, making a fully sequential single-query method optimal. In direct contrast, the principled construction of ZO-Align effectively harnesses multi-query information, making the optimal strategy a full-subspace estimation.

4.1
Query Allocation Problem

Suppose a fixed total query budget,
K
, an algorithm performs a series of updates over
T
iterations, and at each iteration
t
, it uses a set of
q_{t}
function queries as stated in Section
3.2
. The total number of queries is therefore constrained by
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}\leq K
. Our central goal is to determine the optimal query allocation strategy—the sequence of queries per iteration,
\{q_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T-1}
—that minimizes the final optimization error.

4.2
Optimal Query Allocation

We begin by analyzing the case where function evaluations are deterministic. The update rule at iteration
t
is
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta_{t}\hat{g}(x_{t})
, where
\hat{g}
is either the ZO-Avg or ZO-Align estimator constructed with a set of
q_{t}
query directions, as illustrated in Section
3
.

Strongly Convex Case

A function is defined as
\mu
-strongly convex if there exists a constant
\mu
such that for all
x
and
y
in its domain:

f(y)\geq f(x)+\nabla f(x)^{T}(y-x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}.

When the objective function
f(x)
is both
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, we can achieve a linear convergence rate
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
)
. However, the rate’s dependence on the number of queries per iteration,
q_{t}
, reveals the core dichotomy of our work. We first analyze the ZO-Avg estimator.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex function. The ZO-Avg algorithm with the optimal step size
\eta_{t}=\frac{q_{t}}{L(q_{t}+d+1)}
achieves the following convergence guarantee:

\begin{split}&\quad\mathbb{E}[f(x_{T})-f(x^{*})]\\
\leq&\left(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(1-\frac{\mu q_{t}}{L(q_{t}+d+1)}\right)\right)(f(x_{0})-f(x^{*})),\end{split}

where
x^{*}
represent the optimum. Under a fixed total query budget
K
, this convergence rate is optimized by choosing
q_{t}=1
for all
t=0,\dots,K-1
, resulting in
T=K
iterations and a rate of:

\mathbb{E}[f(x_{K})-f(x^{*})]\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L(d+2)}\right)^{K}(f(x_{0})-f(x^{*}))

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

The result in Theorem
4.1
is deeply counter-intuitive. It suggests that when using the simple averaging estimator, allocating more than one query per iteration is detrimental to the convergence rate. The core reason lies in the trade-off between variance reduction and the cost of queries. While increasing
q_{t}
does reduce the variance of the gradient estimate, the improvement scales as
1/q_{t}
as shown in Section
3.4
. However, the cost increases linearly with
q_{t}
. The analysis shows that the marginal benefit of reducing variance is not worth the cost of the additional queries. It is always better to spend the query budget on taking more, albeit noisier, steps. This exposes a fundamental flaw in the simple average strategy: it is an inefficient use of a limited query budget.

In stark contrast, the principled structure of the ZO-Align estimator leads to the opposite conclusion.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex function. The ZO-Align algorithm with the optimal step size
\eta_{t}=1/L
achieves the following convergence guarantee:

\mathbb{E}[f(x_{T})-f(x^{*})]\leq\left(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(1-\frac{\mu q_{t}}{Ld}\right)\right)(f(x_{0})-f(x^{*})),

where
x^{*}
represent the optimum. Under a fixed total query budget
K
, this convergence rate is optimized by using as few iterations as possible with the maximum number of queries, i.e.,
q_{t}=d
for
T=K/d
iterations. This yields the rate:

\mathbb{E}[f(x_{K/d})-f(x^{*})]\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu}{L}\right)^{K/d}(f(x_{0})-f(x^{*})).

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Theorem
4.2
presents a picture opposite to that of ZO-Avg. For ZO-Align, the optimal strategy is to use the maximum number of queries per step. The intuition is that ZO-Align does not simply average away noise; it constructs a principled, low-error projection of the gradient onto the query subspace. As shown in Section
3.4
, the quality of this projection improves substantially as the number of query directions increases. Each additional query provides a significant marginal benefit by better aligning the search direction with the true gradient. It is therefore more effective to spend the budget forming a single high-quality, confident step direction than to take many small, uncertain steps. This result highlights the power of a model-based approach: by aggregating information correctly, investing more queries per step becomes a powerful tool for accelerating convergence.

Convex Case

We then analyze the case where the objective function
f(x)
is convex and
L
-smooth, but not necessarily strongly convex. We demonstrate that the optimal query allocation strategy is fundamentally different for the two estimators, reinforcing the core dichotomy of our work.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth and convex function. The ZO-Avg algorithm with step size
\eta_{t}=\frac{q_{t}}{L(q_{t}+d+1)}
achieves the following convergence guarantee after
T
iterations:

\begin{split}E[f(x_{T})-f(x_{*})]\leq\frac{L(\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{L}(f(x_{0})-f(x_{*})))}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{2q_{t}}{q_{t}+d+1}}.\end{split}

Under a fixed total query budget
K
, this convergence rate is optimized by choosing
q_{t}=1
for all
t=0,...,T-1
, resulting in
T=K
iterations and a rate of:

\begin{split}&\quad E[f(x_{K})-f(x_{*})]\\
&\leq\frac{L(d+2)(\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{L}(f(x_{0})-f(x_{*})))}{2K}.\end{split}

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Theorem
4.3
reinforces the conclusion from the strongly convex case: for the ZO-Avg estimator, using multiple queries per step is an inefficient use of the query budget. Next, we show that for the ZO-Align estimator, the conclusion remains the opposite.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth and convex function. The ZO-Align algorithm with step size
\eta_{t}=1/L
achieves the following rate:

\begin{split}&\quad E[f(x_{T})-f(x_{*})]\\
&\leq\frac{d}{2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}}(L\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}+2(f(x_{0})-f(x_{*})))\end{split}

Under a fixed total query budget
K
, ZO-Align yields the rate:

E[f(x_{T})-f(x_{*})]\leq\frac{d}{2K}(L\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}+2(f(x_{0})-f(x_{*})))

under any allocation
\{q_{t}\}
such that
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}=K
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Comparing the rates for ZO-Avg and ZO-Align, we see a factor of
d+2
versus
d
. More importantly, the ZO-Align rate depends only on the total number of queries
K
, not on how they are allocated across iterations. This key distinction stems directly from the mathematical properties of the estimators. The per-iteration progress for ZO-Align is linear in the number of queries. However for ZO-Avg, it is a nonlinear concave function.

Non-Convex Case

Then we proceed to non-convex setting, where the objective is only assumed to be
L
-smooth. In this scenario, the goal is typically to find a first-order stationary point
(
Nocedal and Wright 2006
)
, i.e., a point
x
where
\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\leq\epsilon
. The convergence is measured by the number of queries required to achieve this goal. The analysis reveals the same fundamental dichotomy between the two estimators.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth function. The ZO-Avg algorithm with step size
\eta_{t}=\frac{q_{t}}{L(q_{t}+d+1)}
ensures that:

\min_{t=0,...,T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2L(f(x_{0})-f^{*})}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{q_{t}+d+1}},

where
f^{*}
is the minimum value of
f
. Under a fixed total query budget
K
, this rate is optimized by choosing
q_{t}=1
for all
t=0,...,T-1
, which gives
T=K
iterations and yields:

\min_{t=0,...,K-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2L(d+2)(f(x_{0})-f^{*})}{K}

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
f
be an
L
-smooth function. The ZO-Align algorithm with step size
\eta_{t}=1/L
ensures that:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2Ld(f(x_{0})-f^{*})}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}}

Under a fixed total query budget
K
, any allocation
\{q_{t}\}
that exhausts the budget,
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}=K
, yields the rate:

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{2Ld(f(x_{0})-f^{*})}{K}

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

The conclusions for the non-convex setting mirror those from the convex case, reinforcing the same central dichotomy. While the same fundamental difference in optimal allocation holds, the performance gap between the two methods is less pronounced here than in the strongly convex setting, boiling down to a constant factor difference rather than an exponential one.

Stochastic Case

We now extend our analysis to the stochastic setting, a standard framework in modern machine learning where the objective is an expectation:
f(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[F(x,\xi)]
. At each iteration
t
, the true function
f(x_{t})
is inaccessible. Instead, our zeroth-order estimators must be constructed from a single stochastic realization
F(x_{t},\xi_{t})
using a random sample
\xi_{t}
. Our zeroth-order estimators are then constructed using this stochastic function. We show that the fundamental dichotomy between ZO-Avg and ZO-Align persists even in the presence of this new source of noise.

For this analysis, we adopt standard assumptions:
f(x)
is convex, each component function
F(x,\xi)
is L-smooth, and the stochastic gradients have bounded variance at the optimum,
x^{*}=\arg\min_{x}f(x)
.

Assumption 4.7
.

The variance of the stochastic gradient at the optimum is bounded, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)-\nabla f(x^{*})\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)\|^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}.

This leads to the following standard lemma which bounds the expected squared norm of the stochastic gradient.

Lemma 4.8
.

For a convex and L-smooth function
f(x)
satisfying Assumption
4.7
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x,\xi)\|^{2}]\leq 4L(f(x)-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

This lemma provides the key tool for controlling the error introduced by the stochastic oracle, which we now use to derive the convergence guarantee for two estimators.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
f
be an L-smooth and convex function satisfying Assumption
4.7
. The ZO-Avg algorithm using a diminishing step size
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}/\sqrt{t+1}
, with

4.3
The Multi-Query Paradox

Our theoretical analysis across strongly convex, convex, non-convex, and stochastic functions reveals a consistent and powerful dichotomy. The effective use of multiple queries per step is critically dependent on the method used to aggregate the information from those queries. This suggests that the common pursuit of an optimal intermediate block size

q
where (
1<q<d
) may be misguided. Instead, the landscape of multi-query zeroth-order optimization is defined by two fundamentally different, opposing poles of operation.

A deeper insight emerges when comparing the performance gap between the two estimators across the different function classes. The superiority of ZO-Align is most pronounced in the strongly convex setting and diminishes as the problem structure weakens to convex and non-convex. This is not a coincidence, but rather a reflection of how the problem’s geometry dictates the value of a high-quality gradient estimate. For strongly convex functions, the analysis relies on a multiplicative decrease in error at each step, where the contraction factor is directly improved by a better gradient estimate. The benefit of ZO-Align’s superior update direction compounds exponentially, leading to a vastly different convergence rate.

In contrast, the analyses for convex and non-convex functions rely on weaker potential-based arguments that result in a telescoping sum over the iterations. This framework captures cumulative, additive progress rather than a compounding, multiplicative effect. Within this additive framework, the step-by-step superiority of ZO-Align is averaged out over the entire optimization run. Its advantage manifests only as a slightly better constant factor in the final rate—a factor that becomes marginal for large
d
. The lack of strong geometric structure prevents the algorithm from fully capitalizing on the superior search direction in a compounding manner.

Our analysis of the stochastic convex case further reinforces the central thesis established in the deterministic setting: leveraging parallel queries in ZO methods collapses to a choice between two canonical algorithms. The introduction of noise from the stochastic oracle does not create a new middle ground; it simply reinforces the existing poles.

This trade-off between statistical efficiency and the cost of queries pushes the optimal strategy for each estimator to an extreme. For the ZO-Avg estimator, our theory proves that the optimal strategy is always to set
q_{t}=1
, which reduces the algorithm to a simple, sequential single-query approach. Conversely, for the statistically superior ZO-Align estimator, the optimal strategy is to use the maximum number of queries, setting
q_{t}=d
. In this important special case, the projection matrix
P_{u}=U(U^{T}U)^{-1}U^{T}
becomes the identity matrix
I
for any full rank
U
. The ZO-Align estimator thereby converges to the true gradient, and the method transforms into a classical full-subspace finite-difference method.

Therefore, our analysis suggests that the question of how to leverage multiple queries in ZO methods does not lead to a novel intermediate-query algorithm. Rather, it collapses the problem to a choice between two well-established canonical algorithms: a sequential single-query method (the optimal realization of ZO-Avg) and a full-subspace finite-difference method (the optimal realization of ZO-Align). Our work clarifies that these two poles are the theoretically-grounded optimal choices, and the path taken depends entirely on the chosen aggregation strategy.

6
Conclusions

This work addresses two core questions in zeroth-order optimization: how to aggregate multi-query information and how to allocate a query budget. We analyze two strategies: simple averaging (ZO-Avg) and a principled Projection Alignment (ZO-Align) method derived from local surrogate minimization. Our analysis, validated across numerous theoretical settings and experiments, reveals a stark dichotomy. We prove the optimal strategy for ZO-Avg is always a sequential random search, while ZO-Align’s performance consistently improves as more queries are used, culminating in a full-subspace estimation. This resolves the multi-query paradox, showing the choice is not about an intermediate query size, but about committing to an aggregation method that determines one of these two canonical algorithms.

Proof of Proposition

Proof.

Analysis of ZO-Avg.

Since ZO-Avg is unbiased, its MSE is the trace of its covariance matrix. Let
g=\nabla f(x)
and
\hat{g}_{i}=(g^{T}u_{i})u_{i}
. The ZO-Avg estimator is
\hat{g}_{AVG}=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\hat{g}_{i}
. As the directions
u_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
are i.i.d., the terms
\hat{g}_{i}
are also i.i.d. random vectors. The variance of the average is thus:

\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{AVG})=\frac{1}{q^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{i})=\frac{1}{q}\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{1}).

To compute
\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{1})
, we need the first two moments of
\hat{g}_{1}
. We have
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{1}]=\mathbb{E}[u_{1}u_{1}^{T}]g=Ig=g
. For the second moment, we use the known result for a standard Gaussian vector
u\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
and a fixed vector
v

\mathbb{E}[(v^{T}u)^{2}uu^{T}]=2vv^{T}+\|v\|^{2}I
. Thus:

\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{1}\hat{g}_{1}^{T}]=\mathbb{E}[(g^{T}u_{1})^{2}u_{1}u_{1}^{T}]=2gg^{T}+\|g\|^{2}I.

The covariance matrix of a single estimator is:

\displaystyle\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{1}\hat{g}_{1}^{T}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{1}]\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{1}]^{T}

\displaystyle=(2gg^{T}+\|g\|^{2}I)-gg^{T}=gg^{T}+\|g\|^{2}I.

The covariance of the ZO-Avg estimator is
\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{AVG})=\frac{1}{q}(gg^{T}+\|g\|^{2}I)
. The MSE is the trace of this matrix:

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{AVG})

\displaystyle=\text{tr}(\text{Var}(\hat{g}_{AVG}))=\frac{1}{q}\text{tr}(gg^{T}+\|g\|^{2}I)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q}(\text{tr}(gg^{T})+\|g\|^{2}\text{tr}(I))=\frac{1}{q}(\|g\|^{2}+d\|g\|^{2})=\frac{d+1}{q}\|g\|^{2}.

Analysis of ZO-Align.

The ZO-Align estimator,
\hat{g}_{ALGN}=P_{U}g
, is biased, with
\mathbb{E}[\hat{g}_{ALG}]=\frac{q}{d}g
. Here, we compute the MSE directly. Let
\hat{g}_{U}=P_{U}g
.

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{ALG})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g}_{U}-g\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\|(P_{U}-I)g\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g^{T}(P_{U}-I)^{T}(P_{U}-I)g].

Since
P_{U}
is a projection matrix, it is symmetric (
P_{U}^{T}=P_{U}
) and idempotent (
P_{U}^{2}=P_{U}
). Therefore,
(P_{U}-I)^{2}=P_{U}^{2}-2P_{U}+I=P_{U}-2P_{U}+I=I-P_{U}
.

\displaystyle\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{ALG})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[g^{T}(I-P_{U})g]=g^{T}\mathbb{E}[I-P_{U}]g

\displaystyle=g^{T}(I-\mathbb{E}[P_{U}])g.

Using the known result that the expectation of a projection onto a random
q

\mathbb{E}[P_{U}]=\frac{q}{d}I
, we have:

\text{MSE}(\hat{g}_{ALIGN})=g^{T}(I-\frac{q}{d}I)g=\left(1-\frac{q}{d}\right)\|g\|^{2}=\frac{d-q}{d}\|g\|^{2}.

, the sequence

is non-increasing. Thus, we can lower bound the left side using the final error,
E[f(x_{T})-f(x_{*})]
:

E[f(x_{T})-f(x_{*})]\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{2q_{t}}{Ld}\right)\leq\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{L}(f(x_{0})-f(x_{*}))

Rearranging gives the final bound.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.5

Proof.

From the
L
-smoothness of
f
, we have the one-step progress from the proof of Theorem
4.1
:

E[f(x_{t+1})|x_{t}]\leq f(x_{t})-\frac{q_{t}}{2L(q_{t}+d+1)}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}

Taking the full expectation and rearranging gives:

\frac{q_{t}}{2L(q_{t}+d+1)}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t+1})]

Summing from
t=0
to
T-1
:

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{2L(q_{t}+d+1)}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t+1})])

The right-hand side is a telescoping sum, which simplifies to
\mathbb{E}[f(x_{0})]-\mathbb{E}[f(x_{T})]\leq f(x_{0})-f^{*}
. For the left-hand side, we have:

\left(\min_{t=0,...,T-1}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\right)\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{2L(q_{t}+d+1)}\right)\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{2L(q_{t}+d+1)}\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]

Combining these gives the first result. To optimize the bound under a fixed budget
K
, we must maximize the denominator
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{q_{t}+d+1}
. As established in Theorem
4.1
, this sum is maximized by choosing
q_{t}=1
for
T=K
iterations. Substituting this into the bound yields the final rate.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.6

Proof.

The proof follows the same structure as for Theorem
4.5
. From the one-step analysis in Theorem
4.2
, we have:

E[f(x_{t+1})|x_{t}]\leq f(x_{t})-\frac{q_{t}}{2Ld}\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}

Taking the full expectation, rearranging, and summing from
t=0
to
T-1
:

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}E[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq 2Ld\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(E[f(x_{t})]-E[f(x_{t+1})])\leq 2Ld(f(x_{0})-f^{*})

Dividing by
\sum q_{t}
and using the fact that
\min_{t}E[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum E[\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}]
gives the result. The rate depends directly on the total number of queries,
\sum q_{t}
, which is maximized by using the full budget
K
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.8

Proof.

By the triangle inequality and the property
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
, we have:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x,\xi)\|^{2}]\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x,\xi)-\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)\|^{2}].

Since each
F(x,\xi)
is L-smooth, we have
\|\nabla F(x,\xi)-\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)\|\leq L\|x-x^{*}\|
. Also, from the convexity of
f(x)
, we have the standard result
\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{L}(f(x)-f(x^{*}))
. Combining these gives:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x,\xi)-\nabla F(x^{*},\xi)\|^{2}]\leq L^{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}\leq 2L(f(x)-f(x^{*})).

The second term is bounded by Assumption 1. Substituting both bounds yields the result:

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\|\nabla F(x,\xi)\|^{2}]\leq 2(2L(f(x)-f(x^{*})))+2\sigma^{2}=4L(f(x)-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
4.9

Proof.

We start by analyzing the evolution of the squared distance to the optimum,
\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}
. The update rule is
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta_{t}\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})
.

\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}=\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{t}\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})+\eta_{t}^{2}\|\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})\|^{2}

Let
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|x_{t}]
denote the expectation over the random direction matrix
U_{t}
and the stochastic sample
\xi_{t}
. We have
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})]=\nabla f(x_{t})
. From Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
4.8
, the expected squared norm of the estimator is bounded as:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})\|^{2}]=\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}}[\|\nabla F(x_{t},\xi_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}(4L(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2})

Taking the conditional expectation of the distance expansion and using the convexity of
f
, i.e.,
f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})\leq\nabla f(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{t}\nabla f(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})+\eta_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\hat{g}_{AVG}(x_{t})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{t}(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}(4L(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\left(4L\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}-2\eta_{t}\right)(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\sigma^{2}

Given the step-size condition
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{4L}
, we have
4L\eta_{t}\leq 1
. We can bound the coefficient of the suboptimality gap:

4L\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}-2\eta_{t}=\frac{\eta_{t}}{q_{t}}(4L\eta_{t}(q_{t}+d+1)-2q_{t})\leq\frac{\eta_{t}}{q_{t}}(1\cdot(q_{t}+d+1)-2q_{t})=\frac{\eta_{t}(d+1-q_{t})}{q_{t}}

Substituting this bound back into the inequality for the distance:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}(d+1-q_{t})}{q_{t}}(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\sigma^{2}

Rearranging, taking the full expectation with
\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})]
and
D_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}]
:

\frac{\eta_{t}(d+1-q_{t})}{q_{t}}\delta_{t}\leq D_{t}-D_{t+1}+2\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\sigma^{2}

Summing this inequality from
t=0
to
T-1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\eta_{t}(d+1-q_{t})}{q_{t}}\delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(D_{t}-D_{t+1})+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}2\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq D_{0}-D_{T}+2\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}\leq\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}

Let
\alpha_{t}=\frac{\eta_{t}(d+1-q_{t})}{q_{t}}
. By convexity of
f
and Jensen’s inequality, the suboptimality of the weighted average iterate
\bar{x}_{T}=\frac{1}{\sum\alpha_{t}}\sum\alpha_{t}x_{t}
is bounded by
\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_{T})-f(x^{*})]\leq\frac{\sum\alpha_{t}\delta_{t}}{\sum\alpha_{t}}
. This gives:

\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_{T})-f(x^{*})]\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}+d+1}{q_{t}}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\frac{d+1-q_{t}}{q_{t}}}

Substituting the step size
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}/\sqrt{t+1}
yields the bound stated in the theorem.

To find the optimal query allocation, we analyze this upper bound,
U(\{q_{t}\})
, under the budget constraint
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}\leq K
. We consider a constant query count,
q_{t}=q
, which implies
T=K/q
. For large
K
, we use the asymptotic approximations
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{t+1}\approx\ln(T)
and
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}\approx 2\sqrt{T}
. Substituting
T=K/q
:

U_{asym}(q)\approx\frac{\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+2\eta_{0}^{2}\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{q+d+1}{q}\right)\ln(K/q)}{2\eta_{0}\left(\frac{d+1-q}{q}\right)\sqrt{K/q}}

Rearranging terms to analyze the dependence on
q\in[1,d]
:

U_{asym}(q)\approx\frac{1}{2\eta_{0}\sqrt{K}}\left[\frac{q^{3/2}}{d+1-q}\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+2\eta_{0}^{2}\sigma^{2}\frac{\sqrt{q}(q+d+1)}{d+1-q}\ln\left(\frac{K}{q}\right)\right]

To find the minimum, we analyze the monotonicity of the functions of
q
.
The first term is proportional to
g_{1}(q)=\frac{q^{3/2}}{d+1-q}
. Its derivative is
g_{1}^{\prime}(q)=\frac{q^{1/2}(\frac{3}{2}(d+1)-\frac{1}{2}q)}{(d+1-q)^{2}}
, which is positive for
q\in[1,d]
. Thus, the first term is monotonically increasing in
q
.
The second term is proportional to
g_{2}(q)=\frac{\sqrt{q}(q+d+1)}{d+1-q}(\ln K-\ln q)
. For a sufficiently large budget
K
, this term is also monotonically increasing in
q
.
Since both terms in the asymptotic upper bound are monotonically increasing functions of
q
, the total bound
U(q)
is minimized at the smallest possible integer value for the query count, which is
q=1
. This strategy implies using the full budget for the number of iterations,
T=K
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.10

Proof.

We start by analyzing the evolution of the squared distance to the optimum,
\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}
. The update rule is
x_{t+1}=x_{t}-\eta_{t}\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})
.

\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}=\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{t}\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})+\eta_{t}^{2}\|\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})\|^{2}

Let
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|x_{t}]
denote the expectation over the random direction matrix
U_{t}
and the stochastic sample
\xi_{t}
. Using the properties of the ZO-Align estimator, we have
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})]=\frac{q_{t}}{d}\nabla f(x_{t})
and
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})\|^{2}]=\frac{q_{t}}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}}[\|\nabla F(x_{t},\xi_{t})\|^{2}]
.
Using Lemma
4.8
, this becomes:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})\|^{2}]\leq\frac{q_{t}}{d}(4L(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2})

Taking the conditional expectation of the distance expansion and using the convexity of
f
, i.e.,
f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})\leq\nabla f(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-2\eta_{t}\frac{q_{t}}{d}\nabla f(x_{t})^{T}(x_{t}-x^{*})+\eta_{t}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|\hat{g}_{ALG}(x_{t})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\frac{2\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+\eta_{t}^{2}\frac{q_{t}}{d}(4L(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+2\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\left(\frac{4L\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}-\frac{2\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}\right)(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+\frac{2\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}\sigma^{2}

Given the step-size condition
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{4L}
, we have
4L\eta_{t}\leq 1
. We can bound the coefficient of the suboptimality gap:

\frac{4L\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}-\frac{2\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}=\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}(4L\eta_{t}-2)\leq\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}(1-2)=-\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}

Substituting this bound back into the inequality for the distance:

\mathbb{E}_{t}[\|x_{t+1}-x^{*}\|^{2}]\leq\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}-\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}(f(x_{t})-f(x^{*}))+\frac{2\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}\sigma^{2}

Rearranging, taking the full expectation with
\delta_{t}=\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})-f(x^{*})]
and
D_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\|x_{t}-x^{*}\|^{2}]
:

\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}\delta_{t}\leq D_{t}-D_{t+1}+\frac{2\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}\sigma^{2}

Summing this inequality from
t=0
to
T-1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}\delta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(D_{t}-D_{t+1})+\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{2\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{d}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq D_{0}-D_{T}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}\leq\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}

Let
\alpha_{t}=\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}
. By convexity of
f
and Jensen’s inequality, the suboptimality of the weighted average iterate
\bar{x}_{T}=\frac{1}{\sum\alpha_{t}}\sum\alpha_{t}x_{t}
is bounded by
\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_{T})-f(x^{*})]\leq\frac{\sum\alpha_{t}\delta_{t}}{\sum\alpha_{t}}
. This gives:

\mathbb{E}[f(\bar{x}_{T})-f(x^{*})]\leq\frac{\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{d}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\eta_{t}q_{t}}{d}}=\frac{d\|x_{0}-x^{*}\|^{2}+2\sigma^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}^{2}q_{t}}{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\eta_{t}q_{t}}

Substituting the step size
\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}/\sqrt{t+1}
yields the bound stated in the theorem.

To optimize the bound, we consider the case where the first term in the numerator (the ”bias” term related to the initial distance) dominates the second term (the ”variance” term related to
\sigma^{2}
). This is common in the initial phase of optimization. The goal is to minimize the bound by maximizing the denominator,
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\eta_{0}q_{t}}{\sqrt{t+1}}
, subject to the total budget constraint
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}q_{t}\leq K
.
Let’s analyze the objective
\max\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{q_{t}}{\sqrt{t+1}}
. The term
\frac{1}{\sqrt{t+1}}
represents the ”value” or ”effectiveness” of queries used at iteration
t
. Since this value is a strictly decreasing function of
t
, we should allocate our query budget to the earliest possible iterations to achieve the maximum sum. This implies a greedy strategy: use the maximum allowed queries,
q_{t}=d
, for the first
T=K/d
iterations. This concentrates the entire budget
K
where it is most effective, thus maximizing the denominator and providing the tightest convergence bound.
∎

A.2
Experiment Set Up

Objective Functions

For the strongly convex quadratic function,
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax+b^{T}x
, the positive definite matrix
A
was generated by first creating a random matrix
M
with entries sampled from a standard normal distribution.
A
was then computed as
A=M^{T}M+\epsilon I
, where
\epsilon
is a small positive constant to ensure the matrix is well-conditioned. The vector
b
was also sampled from a standard normal distribution.

For the convex logistic regression task, synthetic data was generated by sampling feature vectors
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1000}
from a standard normal distribution. A ground-truth weight vector
w_{true}
was randomly created, and the binary labels
y_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
were then generated based on the sign of
a_{i}^{T}w_{true}
.

The non-convex objective was the standard Rosenbrock function.

The stochastic convex objective used a logistic regression loss, where mini-batches were sampled at each iteration to compute the function value.

Implementation and Evaluation

We evaluated the ZO-Avg and ZO-Align estimators using various query block sizes
q
. Step sizes
\eta_{t}
were set according to the optimal values derived in our theory: for ZO-Avg,
\eta_{t}=\frac{q_{t}}{L(q_{t}+d+1)}
, and for ZO-Align,
\eta_{t}=1/L
. For the stochastic setting, a standard diminishing step-size rule was applied. Each algorithm was tested under two total query budgets: a large budget of K=20,000 and a constrained budget of K=500. All reported results are an average of 10 independent runs to ensure reliability.

Hardware and Software

Our experiments were conducted using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 1.12. The underlying system was Ubuntu 18.04, equipped with an Intel Xeon Gold 5320 CPU and two NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs.
