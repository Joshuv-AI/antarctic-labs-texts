Undecidability of Finite Orbit Recognition in Polynomial Maps
Gwangyong Gwon

Abstract
. We prove the undecidability of determining whether a Turing machine yields an eventually periodic trajectory. From this, we deduce the undecidability of orbit finiteness in the polynomial dynamical system on infinite tuples of integers.



1.
Introduction

1.1.

This paper concerns several undecidability results in computability and arithmetic dynamics.
By a polynomial map is meant a function from
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
into itself whose components are polynomials in finitely many variables in the usual sense.
Suppose that
S
is a collection of polynomial maps on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, generating a monoid
\langle S\rangle
.
We call that a point
x=(x_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
S\textrm{-}stable
if its
S\textrm{-}
orbit
\mathcal{O}_{S}(x)=\{f(x):f\in\langle S\rangle\}
is a finite set.
For example, if
S
consists of a single map
f
, then a point
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
S
-stable if and only if
f^{k}(x)=f^{l}(x)
for some
k>l\geq 0
.
Also, we say that a trajectory of a Turing machine on a given input is
eventually periodic
if the machine eventually returns to a previously visited configuration.
To induce our main result, we begin with an analogy of the Halting Problem
[
6
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist no algorithms which decide, given a Turing machine
M
and an input
x
, whether or not the trajectory of
M
on
x
is eventually periodic.

Not only does this theorem have a form analogous to the Halting Problem, but its proof does as well, being constructed through diagonalization.

Theorem

1.1
is, in fact, an instance of a general undecidability result;
any nontrivial limit property of Turing machines cannot be determined by an algorithm, such as “Does a Turing machine yield a trajectory which contains a given configuration?” or “Does a Turing machine write a certain symbol infinitely often?”

Meanwhile, as shown by Paul Rendell
[
3
]
, Conway’s Game of Life can simulate Turing machines.
This implies, through reduction, the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

It is undecidable to determine whether or not given a configuration in the Game of Life yields a trajectory which returns to a previously visited configuration.

To push this reduction further, we represent Conway’s Game of Life as an action of a polynomial map on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Each local update rule of the automaton can be expressed as a polynomial map with an argument in finitary tuples of integers.
Then the global transition function can be obtained as a polynomial map on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
by encoding configurations.
This construction allows us to transfer undecidability from the Game of Life to an algebraic setting.
In particular, we reduce the problem of determining whether a configuration in the Game of Life returns to a previous state to the problem of deciding whether the orbit of a point under a polynomial map is finite.
As a consequence, we obtain our main theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

It is undecidable, given a finite set
S
of polynomial maps on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
and a point
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, whether or not the
x
is
S
-stable.

Our proof combines tools from computability and algebra, illustrating that orbit structure questions in polynomial maps are algorithmically intractable in general.

2.
Preliminaries

This section briefly introduces main concepts that appears in this paper, such as Turing machines and Conway’s Game of Life.

2.1.
Turing machines

We follow
[
4
]
for basic definitions.
Informally, a
Turing machine
consists of a tape of infinite length, a read–write head, and a finite table of instructions that determine how the head moves, reads, and writes symbols on the tape.
The machine operates in discrete time steps, at each step updating its state, writing a symbol, and moving the head one cell left or right.

Definition 2.1
.

A (deterministic) Turing machine is a 7-tuple

M=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},q_{\mathrm{accept}},q_{\mathrm{reject}}),

where
Q,\Sigma,\Gamma
are all finite sets and

•

Q
is the set of states,

•

\Sigma
is the input alphabet not containing the blank symbol
\sqcup
,

•

\Gamma
is the tape alphabet, where
\sqcup\in\Gamma
and
\Sigma\subseteq\Gamma
,

•

\delta:Q\times\Gamma\to Q\times\Gamma\times\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{R}\}
is the transition function,

•

q_{0}\in Q
is the start state,

•

q_{\mathrm{accept}},q_{\mathrm{reject}}\in Q
are the halting states, with
q_{\mathrm{accept}}\neq q_{\mathrm{reject}}
.

The machine receives its input word
w
over
\Sigma
, which is placed on the leftmost
|w|
cells of the tape, with all remaining cells containing the blank symbol
\sqcup
.
The head begins on the leftmost square of the tape at the state
q_{0}
.
Computation proceeds according to the transition function
\delta
.
If the head attempts to move left from the first tape cell, it remains in place.
The machine halts when it enters either
q_{\mathrm{accept}}
or
q_{\mathrm{reject}}
; otherwise it continues indefinitely.

A
configuration
of
M
is a triple
(q,w,i)
, where
q\in Q
is the current state,
w\in\Gamma^{\mathbb{N}}
is the current tape contents, and
i\in\mathbb{N}
is the current head location.

Theorem

1.1
concerns the trajectory of configurations of a Turing machine under successive applications of the transition function.

2.2.
The Halting Problem

We now introduce the classical
Halting Problem
, following
[
4
]
and
[
2
]
.
Let

\mathrm{HALT}=\{\langle M,w\rangle:M\text{ is a Turing machine that halts on input }w\}.

Theorem 2.2

(Undecidability of the Halting Problem)
.

The set
\mathrm{HALT}
is undecidable; that is, no Turing machine can decide
\mathrm{HALT}
.

Although Alan Turing is considered to prove the undecidability of the halting problem in
[
6
]
, strictly speaking, he did not actually establish the result.
What he indeed proved is that the Entscheidungsproblem is undecidable, that is, no algorithms can tell us in finite time whether or not a given statement is true.
Nevertheless, Turing also proved the printing problem, which is computably equivalent to the halting problem, in his own way, and he devised the essential ideas for its proof.
The commonly known self-referential proof of the problem is due to Christopher Strachey
[
5
]
, which I imitated in the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

2.3.
Conway’s Game of Life

The
Game of Life
, introduced by Johh H. Conway in the early 1970s and popularized in
[
1
]
, is a two-dimensional cellular automaton defined on the infinite square lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.
Each lattice point, called a
cell
, is in one of two states:
alive
or
dead
.
Time proceeds discretely in steps
t=0,1,2,\dots
, and the state of the system at time
t+1
is determined completely by its configuration at time
t
.

The update rule depends on the
neighborhood
of each cell, consisting of its eight horizontally, vertically, and diagonally adjacent cells.
Formally, if
N_{t}(x)
denotes the number of live neighbors of a cell
x
at time
t
, then the rules are as follows:

•

Birth.
A dead cell at time
t
becomes alive at time
t+1
if
N_{t}(x)=3
.

•

Survival.
A live cell at time
t
remains alive at time
t+1
if
N_{t}(x)\in\{2,3\}
.

•

Death.
A live cell at time
t
dies at time
t+1
if
N_{t}(x)\leq 1
(death by isolation) or
N_{t}(x)\geq 4
(death by overcrowding).

Thus, once an initial configuration is specified at time
t=0
, the entire future evolution is uniquely determined.
Although governed by simple local rules, the Game of Life exhibits extraordinarily rich behavior, including periodic structures, self-replicating patterns, and even universal computation.

Theorem

1.2
addresses the evolution of an initial configuration.

3.1.
The Wandering Problem

Definition 3.1
.

Let
M
be a Turing machine and
x
be an input.
The trajectory of
M
on
x
is a sequence of all its configurations which occur when
M
is run on
x
.
We say that the trajectory of
M
on
x
is eventually periodic in the ordinary sense, i.e.,
M
on
x
returns to a configuration to which has been visited once previously.

We now prove
Theorem

1.1
.
See
1.1

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there exists a Turing machine
E
that takes as input an encoding of a Turing machine and its input. It answers “yes” if the Turing machine on the input yields an eventually periodic trajectory and “no” otherwise.
Fix two Turing machines,
L
and
W
:

L
is any Turing machine that generates an eventually periodic trajectory, while
W
is any Turing machine whose trajectory is not eventually periodic.
We now define a Turing machine
D
as follows: on input
x
, machine
D
simulates
E
on
x
;
if
E
accepts, then
D
runs
W
; otherwise it runs
L
.
Let
d
denote the encoding of
D
, and suppose that
D
receives its own description
d
as input.
We analyze the behavior of
D
on input
d
.
If
D
on input
d
yields an eventually periodic trajectory, then
E
on
d
would output “yes.”
However, this means that
D
would run
W
, whose trajectory is not eventually periodic—a contradiction.
If, on the other hand,
D
does not yield an eventually periodic trajectory, then
E
would output “no.”
In this case,
D
runs
L
, whose trajectory is eventually periodic—again a contradiction.
Therefore, neither case is possible, and we reach a contradiction.
Hence, no such Turing machine
E
can exist.
∎

3.2.
Rendell’s Construction

Rendell’s construction
[
3
]
embeds a universal Turing machine in the Game of Life such that each generation of the cellular automaton corresponds to a single computation step.
This encoding enables us to transfer undecidability results from the abstract machine to the cell configuration.
Since the machine’s state evolution is fully simulated through glider dynamics, the recurrence of a configuration in the Game of Life aligns with the notion of eventual periodicity in Turing machines.
Based on this simulation, we derive the following as a corollary of
Theorem

1.1

3.3.
Simulating Conway’s Game of Life on
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}

Each cell in the Game of Life can be regarded as an element in
\mathbb{N}^{2}
, and we can assign
0
to dead cells and
1
to live cells.
In this way, we shall think of each configuration in the Game of Life as a point in
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
with entries in
\{0,1\}
.
Think about the following polynomial:

(3.1)

(1-x_{0})x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}(1-x_{4})(1-x_{5})(1-x_{6})(1-x_{7})(1-x_{8})

This returns
1
if
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5},x_{6},x_{7},x_{8})=(0,1,1,1,0,0,0,0,0)
and
0
otherwise.
In other words, it captures the case where a dead cell becomes alive in the next generation if exactly three of its neighbors are alive.
Using this kind of polynomials, we can construct, for each possible local pattern of 9 cells (a center cell and its 8 neighbors), a polynomial in variables
x_{(a,b)}
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
which returns
1
if and only if the pattern satisfies one of the update rules of the Game of Life.
By summing these polynomials corresponding to the patterns that produce a live cell in the next generation, we obtain a single polynomial that encodes the update rule for the center cell.
Repeating this construction for every cell yields a global polynomial map
f:\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
such that
f(x)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
gives the next generation of
x
, where
x\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}^{2}}
is a configuration.
Since there is a canonical bijection between
\mathbb{N}^{2}
and
\mathbb{N}
, we may identify configurations of the Game of Life with elements of
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
Hence, the dynamics of the Game of Life can be simulated by a polynomial map
\varphi:\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

3.4.
Undecidability of Eventual Periodicity

We now prove our main result,
Theorem

1.3
.
See
1.3

Proof.

It suffices to consider the case when
S
is a singleton, so we set
S=\{\varphi\}
where
\varphi
is as defined above.
Note that, in this case,
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
is
S
-stable if and only if
\varphi^{k}(x)=\varphi^{l}(x)
for some integers
k>l\geq 0
.
Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that it is decidable whether, given an
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
,
x
is an
S
-stable point.
Then, given a configuration
\alpha\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we can decide whether there exist non-negative integers
k>l
such that
\varphi^{k}(\alpha)=\varphi^{l}(\alpha)
.
This implies that we can decide whether the configuration
\alpha
enters a previously visited configuration as it evolves.
However, this contradicts
Theorem

1.2
, and therefore no algorithm decides whether, given an
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}
and a finite collection of polynomials
S
,
x
is
S
-stable.
∎
