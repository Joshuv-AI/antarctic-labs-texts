Integrative expression profiling and comparative bioinformatics analysis of ALDH7A1 in chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) under salt and ABA treatments
Authors: Serap Demirel, Melike İncetekin, Ahmet Say, Hamdi Özaktan, Kahraman Gürcan, Fatih Demirel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil salinity and abscisic acid (ABA)-related signaling are major constraints affecting chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) productivity by promoting oxidative stress and aldehyde accumulation. Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs), particularly members of the ALDH7 family, are involved in aldehyde detoxification and redox homeostasis under adverse conditions. However, the molecular characteristics, expression behavior, and structural features of ALDH7A1 in chickpea under salt and ABA treatments remain insufficiently understood. Therefore, this study aimed to provide an integrative characterization of CaALDH7A1 through expression profiling, oxidative stress assays, comparative structural analyses, evolutionary assessment, and molecular docking.ResultsQuantitative real-time PCR analyses showed that CaALDH7A1 was induced in both chickpea genotypes under salt and ABA treatments, with a markedly stronger response in the tolerant genotype (Aksu) than in the susceptible genotype (Uzunlu). In Aksu, transcript accumulation peaked at approximately 4.0-fold under 100 mM NaCl, whereas Uzunlu showed a more gradual increase, reaching a maximum of about 2.4-fold under 150 mM NaCl. Overall, salinity was the primary driver of CaALDH7A1 induction, while ABA alone caused comparatively limited variation under non-saline conditions. This transcriptional pattern was accompanied by lower H₂O₂ accumulation and approximately 30–35% lower MDA levels in the tolerant genotype, indicating an association between stronger CaALDH7A1 responsiveness and reduced oxidative damage. Comparative analyses further showed that CaALDH7A1 retains conserved catalytic domains, motifs, and active-site residues across representative orthologs, while molecular docking with MDA and NAD⁺ revealed favorable predicted binding energies and conserved interaction profiles.ConclusionsTogether, these findings indicate that CaALDH7A1 is a salt- and ABA-responsive gene in chickpea, with its induction being driven mainly by salinity and more strongly expressed in the tolerant genotype. The association of higher CaALDH7A1 expression with lower H₂O₂ and MDA levels supports a stress-related role for this gene in aldehyde metabolism and redox-associated responses. The integration of expression, physiological, structural, evolutionary, and docking analyses further suggests that CaALDH7A1 is a conserved stress-associated candidate that may contribute to salinity-related adaptation in chickpea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08912-0.Keywords: Gene expression profiling, Bioinformatics analysis, Molecular docking, Post-translational modifications, Evolutionary conservation

Introduction

Projections of global climate change indicate a 3–5 °C rise in average surface temperature over the next century, intensifying the frequency of extreme climatic events that threaten agricultural productivity and food security worldwide [1]. Among the most serious environmental constraints aggravated by climate change, soil salinity is recognized as a major limiting factor for sustainable crop production. More than 800 million hectares of arable land are currently affected by salinity, accounting for approximately 6% of the world’s total land surface, and this area continues to expand due to inefficient irrigation, overuse of fertilizers, and industrial effluents [2, 3]. The accumulation of soluble salts in the rhizosphere interferes with nutrient uptake, osmotic adjustment, and ion balance, ultimately resulting in severe growth inhibition and yield decline in many plant species [4].

The physiological consequences of salinity stress are largely associated with oxidative imbalance. Excess sodium accumulation disturbs ionic homeostasis and activates secondary signaling cascades, leading to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Elevated ROS not only alters cellular redox status but also induces lipid peroxidation, generating highly reactive aldehydes such as malondialdehyde (MDA), which damage cellular membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids [5, 6]. Effective detoxification of these aldehydes is therefore crucial for maintaining metabolic stability during abiotic stress.

Aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs; EC 1.2.1.3) comprise a conserved superfamily of NAD(P)⁺-dependent oxidoreductases that catalyze the irreversible conversion of reactive aldehydes to carboxylic acids [7, 8]. Beyond their catalytic detoxification function, ALDHs are also involved in maintaining redox homeostasis through NADH/NADPH generation and the regulation of stress-related signaling pathways [9, 10]. Plant genomes encode numerous ALDH family members; to date, different ALDH families with varying copy numbers between species have been identified in different plants: 16 genes in Arabidopsis thaliana, 23 in Vitis vinifera, 29 in Solanum lycopersicum, 22 in Solanum tuberosum, 39 in Malus domestica, 53 in Glycine max, and 27 in Brassica rapa subsp. pekinensis [11–17].

Functional analyses have demonstrated that ALDH gene expression increases markedly under environmental stresses such as drought, salinity, heat, and heavy metal exposure. Overexpression of specific ALDH members enhances tolerance to osmotic and oxidative challenges by reducing ROS and MDA accumulation [18–20]. In Arabidopsis, chloroplastic ALDH3I1 and cytosolic ALDH7B4 are typical stress-inducible genes conferring improved abiotic stress resistance [21]. Similarly, heterologous expression of ALDH genes from soybean and wild grape has been associated with improved salt and drought tolerance by mitigating lipid peroxidation and supporting redox balance [22, 23].

Within this superfamily, the ALDH7 (antiquitin-like) subgroup, particularly ALDH7A1, has attracted attention for its strong responsiveness to multiple abiotic stressors. Members of this subgroup are widely expressed across plant tissues and are highly inducible by salt, drought, and abscisic acid (ABA) stimuli [21, 24]. Overexpression studies in Arabidopsis, tobacco, and soybean have shown that ALDH7A1 genes enhance tolerance through efficient aldehyde detoxification and mitigation of oxidative injury [19, 20, 25].

To our knowledge, despite extensive characterization of ALDH gene families in various crops, the specific role, structural features, and regulatory behavior of ALDH7A1 in chickpea in salt stress and ABA treatment are unknown. This represents a major gap in understanding how aldehyde detoxification contributes to oxidative and ABA-mediated stress tolerance in this important legume. Therefore, this study aimed to provide the first integrative characterization of CaALDH7A1 through expression profiling, structural and comparative bioinformatics analyses, and molecular docking. We hypothesized that CaALDH7A1 would be strongly induced under salt and ABA treatments, particularly in the tolerant genotype, and that its conserved catalytic architecture would support efficient binding of MDA and NAD⁺, reflecting a core role in redox homeostasis. This combined approach was expected to reveal the functional significance of CaALDH7A1 in chickpea stress adaptation.

Materials and methods

Sequence retrieval and analysis

Protein sequences corresponding to the Cicer arietinum ALDH7A1 gene were obtained from the Phytozome database (v13; https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). To identify orthologous sequences, BLASTp searches were conducted against genome assemblies of representative legume and cereal species, including Glycine max, Medicago truncatula, Oryza sativa, Phaseolus vulgaris, Sorghum bicolor, Triticum aestivum, and Vigna unguiculata. Each retrieved sequence was cross-validated through the NCBI Protein database to confirm identity and annotation consistency.

The physicochemical features of all ALDH7A1 proteins such as amino acid composition, molecular weight (Mw), and theoretical isoelectric point (pI) were determined using ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/). Conserved catalytic domains were verified through Pfam v32 (https://pfam.xfam.org/) using hidden Markov model (HMM) profiles to confirm the presence of the aldehyde dehydrogenase superfamily domain. Predicted subcellular localizations were generated by WoLF PSORT v0.2 (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/) [26], while possible transmembrane helices were assessed through TMHMM v2.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/) [27]. The corresponding genomic and coding DNA sequences (CDS) were downloaded from Phytozome and visualized using GSDS v2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) to illustrate exon–intron architecture [28].

Structural and functional characterization

Predictions of secondary structure composition, including α-helices, β-strands, turns, and coils, were obtained via the SOPMA server (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/) [29]. The prediction threshold was adjusted to 8 with a window width of 17 residues. To assess structural alignment and conservation, three-dimensional models were compared using CLICK (http://mspc.bii.a-star.edu.sg/click/) [30], which computes residue overlap scores. Potential intrinsically disordered regions were identified by the PrDOS web tool (http://prdos.hgc.jp/) [31]. Post-translational modification (PTM) predictions included several parameters: potential phosphorylation at serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues was determined using NetPhos 3.1 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetPhos/) [32]; N-glycosylation motifs (Asn-X-Ser/Thr) were identified through NetNGlyc 1.0 (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc/); and potential SUMOylation consensus motifs were predicted with GPS-SUMO v2.0 (http://sumosp.biocuckoo.org/online.php) [33].

Motif architecture, phylogenetic analysis, and gene ontology

The conserved amino acid motifs of all ALDH7A1 proteins were identified using MEME Suite v5.0.3 (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme) [34], setting the maximum number of motifs to 20 and motif width between 6 and 50 residues. Functional associations of the identified motifs were further confirmed using Pfam. Multiple sequence alignments of ALDH7A1 nucleotide sequences were performed with ClustalW implemented in MEGA v11.0 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) [35]. Phylogenetic reconstruction followed the Neighbor-Joining algorithm with 1,000 bootstrap replicates, applying the p-distance substitution model to estimate evolutionary divergence.

Genetic diversity and population differentiation parameters, including nucleotide diversity (π), haplotype diversity (Hd), number of segregating sites (S), average number of pairwise differences (K), and population differentiation and gene flow indices (Kst, Fst, Gst, and Nm), were calculated using DnaSP v6.12 (https://www.ub.edu/dnasp/) [36]. Functional categorization of ALDH7A1 proteins according to Gene Ontology (GO) terms was performed through PANNZER2 (http://ekhidna2.biocenter.helsinki.fi/sanspanz/) [37], which provides molecular function and biological process annotations based on homology.

Protein modeling and molecular docking

Predicted 3D structures of ALDH7A1 proteins were retrieved from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). The stereochemical quality and geometric reliability of the models were validated with VADAR (http://vadar.wishartlab.com/) by analyzing Ramachandran plots [38]. Potential ligand-binding pockets were identified using CASTp 3.0 (http://sts.bioe.uic.edu/castp/index.html) [39]. Energy minimization and docking preparation were conducted in UCSF Chimera v1.15 (https://www.cgl.ucsf.edu/chimera/) [40]. Ligands including malondialdehyde (MDA; PubChem CID: 10964) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺; PubChem CID: 5893) were downloaded from PubChem and geometry-optimized with hydrogen addition prior to docking. Docking simulations were executed using AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 (https://vina.scripps.edu/) with the grid box centered on the predicted catalytic site and dimensions adjusted to include the active region [41]. Default exhaustiveness values were used for reliable sampling. Binding affinities (ΔG, kcal·mol⁻¹) and non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts) were analyzed and visualized in BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer and UCSF Chimera.

Plant material and salt–ABA treatments

Seeds of two chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) cultivars ‘Aksu’, identified as salt-tolerant, and ‘Uzunlu-99’, identified as salt-sensitive were used as experimental material. Certified seeds were obtained from the Department of Field Crops, Erciyes University, Türkiye. Prior to sowing, seeds were surface sterilized and germinated in plastic vials containing a peat: perlite (2:1) mixture under controlled environmental conditions at 22 ± 3 °C with a 16 h photoperiod.

When seedlings reached the two- to four-leaf stage, they were transferred into a hydroponic setup containing Hoagland nutrient solution (Sigma H2395) [42]. After an acclimatization period of several days, the plants were subjected to different salinity and abscisic acid (ABA) treatments in a completely randomized design. Salinity stress was imposed by adding NaCl to the nutrient solution at final concentrations of 50, 100, and 150 mM, along with an untreated control (0 mM NaCl). In parallel, ABA treatments were applied as foliar sprays at concentrations of 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM. ABA was dissolved in a small volume of ethanol and diluted with distilled water for foliar application; control plants received the corresponding solvent without ABA. The final ethanol concentration in the spray solution did not exceed 0.1% (v/v). Each NaCl treatment was combined factorially with each ABA concentration, resulting in 16 treatment groups (Table 1). NaCl (50, 100, and 150 mM) and ABA (0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM) concentrations were selected according to commonly used dose ranges in plant abiotic stress and ABA-response studies [43–46]. For salinity experiments, control plants were maintained in Hoagland nutrient solution without NaCl, while for ABA treatments, control plants were sprayed with the corresponding solvent lacking ABA. Each treatment included five biological replicates with identical growth conditions. The experiment was terminated when clear morphological and physiological differences between the genotypes became evident following the combined stress treatments. Leaf samples from all treatments were harvested for subsequent biochemical and molecular analyses.

Gene expression analysis and oxidative stress assays

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Leaf samples from salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive C. arietinum genotypes were collected after each treatment for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the PureLink™ RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of RNA were verified spectrophotometrically at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop™ 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with oligo(dT) primers, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

The relative expression of CaALDH7A1 was analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each 25 µL reaction contained 12.5 µL of Master Mix, 0.5 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 1 µL of cDNA template, and 10.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Actin gene 1 (GenBank accession no. EU529707) was used as the housekeeping gene for normalization of transcript abundance and comparison of CaALDH7A1 expression among samples. The actin primers were F: 5′-CATCACCCTCGGCATTTC-3′ and R: 5′-CAGCCTTAACCATTCCAGTC-3′, whereas the CaALDH7A1-specific primers described by Carmona-Molero et al. [47] were F: 5′-TGGTTGGGCCTCTGCATAC-3′ and R: 5′-CTGTTAGGATCTTCCCTCCCTG-3′. Relative gene expression was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCt method [48]. Amplification specificity was verified by melting curve analysis and agarose gel electrophoresis, confirming a single specific amplicon.

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification

Hydrogen peroxide levels were measured according to Velikova et al. [49] with minor modifications. Approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue was homogenized in 5 mL of 5% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA) under chilled conditions. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. An aliquot (0.5 mL) of the supernatant was combined with 0.5 mL of 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 mL of 1 M KI. Absorbance was recorded at 390 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800). H₂O₂ concentration was determined from a standard curve and expressed as µmol g⁻¹ FW.

Lipid peroxidation was assessed by estimating malondialdehyde (MDA) content using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction described by Heath and Packer [50], with minor adjustments. Leaf samples (0.5 g) were ground in 5 mL of 0.1% (w/v) TCA and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min. A 1 mL aliquot of the supernatant was mixed with 4 mL of 0.5% (w/v) TBA prepared in 20% TCA, then incubated at 95 °C for 30 min in a water bath and rapidly cooled on ice. The mixture was centrifuged again, and absorbance was measured at 532 nm and 600 nm. MDA concentration was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM⁻¹ cm⁻¹ and expressed as nmol g⁻¹ FW.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using a completely randomized design (CRD). For each NaCl × ABA treatment, five individual plants were used as biological replicates and pooled at harvest to generate a single bulk biological sample. Pooling was applied prior to RNA extraction to minimize within-treatment biological variability and to obtain a representative transcriptional and physiological response for each treatment. This approach resulted in three independent bulk biological replicates per treatment, each derived from a distinct group of five plants grown under identical conditions. For gene expression analysis, total RNA extracted from each bulk biological replicate was reverse-transcribed, and qPCR reactions were performed in three technical replicates. The mean Ct value of the technical replicates was used for subsequent analyses. Relative transcript abundance of CaALDH7A1 was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method [48], with Actin selected as the internal reference gene. Biochemical measurements (H₂O₂ and MDA) were also performed using the same pooled biological replicates. Each measurement was carried out in duplicate and averaged after confirming technical consistency. Before statistical analysis, all datasets were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test). When necessary, data were log-transformed to satisfy ANOVA assumptions. Since all treatment groups were evaluated specifically in comparison to the control group (0 mM NaCl + 0 mM ABA), statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test to compare each treatment mean against the control. Differences were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05. All statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using XLSTAT software (Addinsoft, version 2025.1).

Results

Physicochemical properties and conserved domain analysis

Comparative analysis of primary protein features revealed a high degree of structural uniformity among the examined ALDH7A1 homologs (Table 2). The predicted protein lengths varied from 454 amino acids in wheat (TaALDH7A1) to 516 amino acids in chickpea (CaALDH7A1), while the majority clustered around ~ 508 residues, consistent with typical members of the ALDH family. The corresponding molecular weights (Mw) ranged from 48.7 kDa to 55.2 kDa, aligning with the characteristic size range of plant aldehyde dehydrogenases.

Summary of amino acid composition and stability indices of ALDH7A1 proteins

The theoretical isoelectric points (pI) indicated that most proteins possess slightly acidic properties, with values between 5.27 and 5.87. However, in O. sativa, S. bicolor, and T. aestivum, the pI shifted toward a more neutral range (6.06–6.67), reflecting minor interspecies differences in amino acid composition and charge balance. The instability indices of all proteins were below 40 (30.38–34.98), classifying them as stable under physiological conditions. Furthermore, aliphatic index values were consistently high (94.2–96.8), implying robust thermostability and structural rigidity. The grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) values were slightly positive (0.063–0.140), suggesting that ALDH7A1 proteins are largely hydrophilic but contain limited hydrophobic patches compatible with soluble enzyme function. Collectively, these data indicate that ALDH7A1 proteins are structurally conserved, thermostable, and functionally compatible with NAD(P)⁺-dependent aldehyde oxidation across both legumes and cereals.

Conserved domain assessment using the Pfam and NCBI-CDD databases confirmed the presence of the canonical aldehyde dehydrogenase superfamily (ALDH-SF; cl11961) domain in all sequences (E-values between 1e⁻²⁰ and 1e⁻⁹⁹). This domain is responsible for the oxidation of aldehyde substrates via NAD(P)⁺ reduction, a hallmark of ALDH enzymatic activity. While most proteins contained a single uninterrupted ALDH-SF domain, additional subfamily-related hits were observed in S. bicolor, where weak similarity to retroelement-associated sequences (RNase_HI_RT_Ty1 and RVT_2) appeared, likely representing annotation artifacts or ancient fusion remnants. The universal presence of the ALDH-SF motif across all taxa demonstrates strong conservation of the catalytic core essential for aldehyde detoxification and redox balance in plants.

Transmembrane topology, subcellular localization, and post-translational modifications

Prediction of membrane topology revealed that all ALDH7A1 proteins possess a single transmembrane segment (TMS) located near the N-terminal region, indicating that these enzymes are predominantly soluble proteins anchored by a short hydrophobic helix (Table 3). The TMS generally spanned residues 155–172 in most taxa, whereas a shorter segment positioned between 101 and 117 was observed in T. aestivum (TaALDH7A1). This architecture corresponds to a type 1b topology, suggesting that the proteins are localized mainly in cytosolic or endomembrane compartments rather than integrated membrane complexes.

The motif architecture and WoLF PSORT-based localization of ALDH7A1 proteins

Analysis of subcellular localization by WoLF PSORT predicted that ALDH7A1 proteins are distributed across several intracellular compartments, with the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) being the most frequent targets. Legume representatives (C. arietinum, G. max, M. truncatula, P. vulgaris, and V. unguiculata) exhibited dual localization signals to both ER and plasma membrane, whereas cereal homologs (O. sativa, S. bicolor, and T. aestivum) showed additional probabilities for chloroplastic or vacuolar localization. A distinct mitochondrial targeting peptide (R-2 motif) was detected only in O. sativa, suggesting lineage-specific variation in subcellular routing.

N-terminal N-myristoylation motifs (MG- or MGS-initiated sequences) were present in most legume homologs, supporting potential regulation by lipid modification, whereas monocot proteins largely lacked this feature. Several dileucine (LL) motifs known for their role in vesicular trafficking were detected throughout the sequences, with T. aestivum exhibiting multiple instances (residues 210, 252–253, and 284), possibly enhancing membrane association dynamics.

Evaluation of phosphorylation patterns using NetPhos 3.1 indicated a high frequency of serine phosphorylation sites (70–75%), followed by threonine (20–25%) and tyrosine (3–5%) residues. This serine-dominant pattern was especially pronounced in C. arietinum, consistent with the hypothesis that serine/threonine kinases are key regulators of ALDH7A1 function during abiotic stress signaling. Potential N-glycosylation sites (Asn-X-Ser/Thr sequons) were identified in nearly all proteins, ranging from two to five motifs per sequence. The strongest glycosylation signals were observed at Asn-44 (NQTI) in C. arietinum and Asn-136 (NGSI) in both M. truncatula and O. sativa, suggesting that these residues may contribute to structural stabilization rather than classical secretion. In addition, prediction of SUMOylation and SUMO-interaction motifs (SIMs) revealed multiple conserved lysine residues (K12, K13, K16, K282, and K449–454) across species. These sites were frequently adjacent to hydrophobic SIM cores (IIVMD, IVNVN, VIAEV, or IPLVS), implying coordinated regulation through SUMO attachment and recognition. The coexistence of SUMOylation and phosphorylation hotspots within the same domains suggests that ALDH7A1 proteins are subject to multilayered post-translational control that may modulate enzyme stability and redox responsiveness during salt and ABA stress.

Gene structure organization and secondary structure prediction

Analysis of gene structure revealed that ALDH7A1 loci across the studied species consist of multiple exons and introns arranged in a largely conserved pattern (Fig. 1). Within the Fabaceae members (C. arietinum, G. max, M. truncatula, P. vulgaris, and V. unguiculata), exon–intron composition was nearly identical, with only minor differences in intron length or spacing. In contrast, cereal homologs from Poaceae (O. sativa, S. bicolor, and T. aestivum) displayed noticeably larger genomic regions, mainly due to expanded intronic segments rather than changes in exon count. The overall gene length ranged from approximately 5 kb in P. vulgaris to more than 13 kb in S. bicolor, implying that intron expansion contributed to the increased genome size observed in monocot species. Despite this variability, the exon number remained stable across taxa, suggesting that the core coding framework of ALDH7A1 has been evolutionarily conserved. Such conservation indicates that structural rearrangements within this gene family are more likely driven by lineage-specific intron evolution than by exon gain or loss. These observations support the view that ALDH7A1 plays a fundamental role in aldehyde detoxification processes across divergent plant lineages while maintaining a stable gene architecture.

Gene structure of ALDH7A1 genes showing exon–intron organization

The predicted secondary structure of ALDH7A1 proteins, obtained from SOPMA analysis, showed a uniform distribution of structural elements among all species (Table 4). Alpha helices constituted the major fraction, accounting for approximately 40–43% of residues, followed by extended β-strands (16–17%) and random coils (41–44%). The dominance of α-helical regions reflects the compact folding typical of NAD(P)⁺-dependent dehydrogenases, whereas the moderate proportion of coils indicates flexible segments potentially involved in substrate access or domain movement. Among species, T. aestivum and O. sativa exhibited slightly higher helical content (42.6–42.9%), while C. arietinum displayed the greatest fraction of random coils (44.2%), suggesting enhanced conformational adaptability. The minor variations in secondary structure among taxa likely reflect subtle adjustments that allow functional stability under different physiological or stress conditions. Altogether, the combination of conserved gene architecture and balanced secondary structure elements indicates strong evolutionary constraint and biochemical robustness of ALDH7A1 proteins across both legumes and cereals.

Intrinsic disorder regions and post-translational modification profiles

To further characterize structural flexibility and potential regulatory features of ALDH7A1 orthologs, intrinsically disordered regions and predicted post-translational modification sites were analyzed. PrDOS results showed that ALDH7A1 proteins were predominantly ordered and structurally compact, with disorder probabilities generally below 0.5 and only short flexible regions mainly located at the N- and C-terminal ends and in inter-loop segments (Supplementary Fig. 1). NetPhos analysis identified numerous phosphorylation sites across all orthologs, with serine residues accounting for the majority (70–75%), followed by threonine (19–25%) and tyrosine (3–6%) residues; the highest numbers were detected in C. arietinum (67), P. vulgaris (64), and M. truncatula (61), whereas cereal homologs contained fewer sites (41–51) (Supplementary Fig. 2). NetNGlyc analysis revealed two to five canonical N-glycosylation motifs per protein, with high-confidence sites at Asn-44 in C. arietinum and Asn-136 in both M. truncatula and O. sativa (Supplementary Table S1). In addition, GPS-SUMO prediction identified several conserved lysine residues, including K12, K13, K16, K282, K449–454, and K493–497, as potential SUMOylation or SUMO-interaction sites, frequently adjacent to hydrophobic SIM cores such as IIVMD, IVNVN, VIAEV, and IPLVS (Supplementary Table S2).

Motif architecture, phylogenetic relationships and genetic diversity

To place CaALDH7A1 within a broader structural and evolutionary context relevant to its proposed role in aldehyde detoxification and stress-associated redox metabolism, conserved motifs, functional annotation, and phylogenetic relationships were examined across representative ALDH7A1 orthologs. MEME analysis identified 20 motifs, of which three (Motifs 1–3) were conserved in all orthologs (Supplementary Fig. 3). Motif 2 contained the characteristic catalytic consensus sequence (IIVMDADIKLAVRS) corresponding to the NAD(P)⁺-binding and aldehyde dehydrogenase active region, whereas Motifs 1 and 3 were associated with structurally conserved N-terminal and C-terminal regions. Motif organization was highly similar among legume species, while monocot homologs showed only minor positional shifts, indicating overall conservation with limited lineage-specific divergence. Consistent with these findings, Gene Ontology annotation assigned all proteins to aldehyde dehydrogenase [NAD⁺] activity (GO:0004029) with an average posterior probability of 0.78, supporting functional conservation across orthologs. Phylogenetic analysis further separated the sequences into distinct dicot and monocot clades (Fig. 2), with all Fabaceae members forming a well-supported subclade (> 90% bootstrap) and C. arietinum clustering most closely with G. max. Overall, these results indicate that ALDH7A1 proteins retain conserved catalytic architecture across species while showing modest lineage-dependent variation.

Phylogenetic tree of ALDH7A1 nucleotide sequences from diverse angiosperms, reconstructed using the Neighbor-Joining method

To further place CaALDH7A1 within a broader evolutionary context relevant to the conservation of its catalytic and stress-associated features, genetic diversity and inter-family differentiation analyses were performed using ALDH7A1 sequences from nine plant families. Analysis of 107 sequences revealed substantial variation in nucleotide polymorphism among families (Supplementary Table S3). The highest haplotype diversity was detected in Fabaceae (Hd = 0.97), Poaceae (Hd = 0.96), and Rosaceae (Hd = 0.93), whereas Proteaceae showed no haplotypic variation (Hd = 0.00). Similarly, nucleotide diversity was greatest in Poaceae (π = 0.116) and Fabaceae (π = 0.106), while the lowest values were observed in Fagaceae (π = 0.008), Juglandaceae (π = 0.015), and Proteaceae (π = 0.000). The number of segregating sites and average pairwise nucleotide differences followed a comparable trend, with the highest values in Poaceae (S = 313; K = 75.15) and Fabaceae (S = 247; K = 68.74) and the lowest in Proteaceae (S = 0; K = 0.00).

Pairwise population statistics further revealed pronounced lineage differentiation among plant families (Supplementary Table S4). Fst values were consistently high, ranging from 0.509 for Fabaceae–Rosaceae to 0.970 for Fagaceae–Proteaceae, while Kst values were significant in most comparisons and were especially high in pairings involving Proteaceae. Estimates of gene flow (Nm) varied markedly, with relatively high values between Fabaceae and Poaceae (15.96) and between Fabaceae and Rosaceae (11.55), but much lower values in comparisons involving Proteaceae, such as Betulaceae–Proteaceae (0.25) and Fagaceae–Proteaceae (0.49). These results indicate that ALDH7A1 retains broad evolutionary conservation while displaying substantial lineage-level divergence across plant families.

Molecular docking with MDA and NAD⁺

To relate the conserved active-site features of ALDH7A1 to its proposed role in aldehyde oxidation and redox metabolism, molecular docking analyses were performed for all ALDH7A1 homologs using AutoDock Vina v1.1.2 with malondialdehyde (MDA; PubChem CID: 10964) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺; PubChem CID: 5893) as representative ligands. The obtained binding energies and residue-level interactions indicated favorable ligand accommodation and broadly conserved binding profiles among the analyzed proteins (Table 5). Across all taxa, both MDA and NAD⁺ were positioned within or near the predicted catalytic pocket corresponding to the ALDH active region (IPR029510). Binding free energies ranged from − 6.3 to − 8.6 kcal·mol⁻¹, with C. arietinum showing the strongest predicted affinity for both NAD⁺ (–8.6 kcal·mol⁻¹) and MDA (–7.1 kcal·mol⁻¹), followed closely by O. sativa and T. aestivum for MDA binding (–7.0 kcal·mol⁻¹). These results support the conservation of a ligand-compatible catalytic environment across ALDH7A1 orthologs.

Expression profile of CaALDH7A1 and oxidative stress response

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis revealed a clear induction of CaALDH7A1 expression under salt and ABA treatments compared to the control group (Fig. 6). In the tolerant chickpea genotype Aksu, the expression of CaALDH7A1 remained low and relatively stable under non-saline conditions, ranging from 0.086 to 0.097 across ABA treatments, with the control treatment (0 mM NaCl + 0 mM ABA) showing a value of 0.093. Under 50 mM NaCl, transcript levels increased to 0.133, 0.110, 0.190, and 0.165 in the presence of 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM ABA, respectively, corresponding to approximately 1.43-, 1.18-, 2.04-, and 1.77-fold increases relative to the control. A much stronger induction was observed at 100 mM NaCl, where expression values reached 0.376, 0.376, 0.353, and 0.334, representing about 4.04-, 4.04-, 3.80-, and 3.59-fold increases, respectively. At 150 mM NaCl, expression remained above the control level but declined compared with 100 mM NaCl, with values of 0.244, 0.214, 0.206, and 0.211 under 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM ABA, respectively, corresponding to 2.62-, 2.30-, 2.22-, and 2.27-fold increases. Overall, in Aksu, CaALDH7A1 expression was more strongly associated with increasing NaCl concentration than with ABA treatment alone, and the highest transcript accumulation was recorded at 100 mM NaCl. In the susceptible chickpea genotype Uzunlu, CaALDH7A1 expression under non-saline conditions remained relatively stable across ABA treatments, varying between 0.309 and 0.329, with the control treatment (0 mM NaCl + 0 mM ABA) showing a value of 0.329. Under 50 mM NaCl, transcript levels increased to 0.555, 0.524, 0.503, and 0.501 in the presence of 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM ABA, respectively, corresponding to approximately 1.69-, 1.59-, 1.53-, and 1.52-fold increases relative to the control. At 100 mM NaCl, expression values further rose to 0.656, 0.683, 0.608, and 0.649, representing about 1.99-, 2.08-, 1.85-, and 1.97-fold increases, respectively. The highest expression levels were recorded under 150 mM NaCl, where CaALDH7A1 reached 0.798, 0.767, 0.702, and 0.745 under 0, 0.1, 0.5, and 1 mM ABA, respectively, corresponding to 2.43-, 2.33-, 2.13-, and 2.26-fold increases relative to the control. Overall, in Uzunlu, CaALDH7A1 expression increased progressively with increasing salinity, while the effect of ABA appeared comparatively limited within each NaCl level.

In both genotypes, salt and ABA stress markedly elevated H₂O₂ accumulation relative to the control, but the increase was significantly lower in the tolerant plants. Discussion

Chickpea (C. arietinum L.) is a nutritionally important legume crop whose productivity is constrained by several abiotic stresses, particularly drought, heat, salinity, and osmotic stress, all of which can impair growth and yield [51–54]. Recent studies have increasingly focused on the regulatory genes involved in chickpea stress adaptation [55, 56]. Among these, aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs) constitute a highly conserved enzyme superfamily that contributes to aldehyde detoxification, redox homeostasis, and stress-associated metabolic regulation [9, 11, 13, 16, 57]. Members of the ALDH7 family are of particular interest because they are widely reported to be stress responsive and to participate in oxidative damage mitigation under dehydration, salinity, temperature stress, and ABA treatment [21, 22, 58]. Although ALDH genes have been characterized in several plant species [47, 59, 60], and chickpea ALDH family members have recently been catalogued [47], the specific behavior of ALDH7A1 in chickpea under salt and ABA treatments has not been examined. Therefore, the present study provides the first focused characterization of CaALDH7A1, integrating expression, physiological, structural, and comparative analyses to clarify its potential relevance under stress-related conditions.

Structural and physicochemical characterization

The comparative physicochemical and structural characterization of CaALDH7A1 and its orthologs revealed a high degree of conservation in sequence length, molecular mass, isoelectric point, and stability-related properties across both legume and cereal species. These features are consistent with the general characteristics of the plant ALDH superfamily reported in A. thaliana [11], Gossypium raimondii [61], S. bicolor [62], and S. tuberosum [57]. The chickpea ALDH7A1 protein (55,251.39 Da; pI 5.56) closely matched the profiles reported for orthologs in M. truncatula, Manihot esculenta, and P. vulgaris, in agreement with previous genome-wide studies describing plant ALDH proteins as moderately sized enzymes with generally slightly acidic pI values [47, 60, 63, 64].

The relatively uniform GRAVY, aliphatic index, and instability index values further support the conserved hydrophilic and stable nature of ALDH7A1 proteins. Comparable physicochemical patterns have been reported in Malus × domestica [14], Allium sativum [65], and S. tuberosum [57], where ALDH family members also exhibited high aliphatic index values and low instability coefficients, features commonly associated with structurally stable enzymes functioning under stress-related conditions [66, 67]. Conserved domain analysis identified the canonical ALDH superfamily domain and the Rossmann-fold cofactor-binding region in all examined proteins, in line with earlier reports in Arabidopsis [11] and comparative plant ALDH analyses [13]. Likewise, the presence of at least one ALDH domain (PF00171) in CaALDH7A1 and its orthologs was consistent with findings in apple [14], bamboo [68], and pepper [69]. The conservation of key catalytic residues, including the glutamate and cysteine components of the catalytic framework and the Ser268–Asn165 region in chickpea, further supports the preservation of active-site architecture across species, as also reported for O. sativa [70], S. bicolor [62], and potato [57].

Subcellular localization predictions indicated that CaALDH7A1 is mainly associated with the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum, which is comparable to localization patterns described for ALDH proteins in garlic and potato [57, 65]. In the context of the present study, this conserved structural framework and predicted localization pattern support the broader view that CaALDH7A1 retains core features associated with stress-related aldehyde metabolism. This interpretation is also consistent with the observations of Carmona-Molero et al. [47], who reported that chickpea ALDH family members maintain stable tertiary organization and relatively limited sequence divergence, as well as with comparable structural conservation described for S. bicolor, O. sativa, and T. aestivum [62, 70, 71]. Similar patterns have also been noted in non-crop systems such as Cleistogenes songorica, where ALDH homologs showed high similarity to sorghum sequences [66]. Overall, these findings indicate that ALDH7A1 proteins are subject to strong evolutionary conservation and preserve the major structural features required for their shared enzymatic role.

Gene structure and motif organization

Comparative gene structure analysis revealed that CaALDH7A1 shares a conserved exon–intron organization with its homologs across both legumes and cereals, indicating a common ancestral origin and strong purifying selection. The number of exons and their relative arrangement were remarkably similar among C. arietinum, M. truncatula, G. max, and P. vulgaris, consistent with the pattern described by Carmona-Molero et al. [47], who reported that chickpea ALDH genes exhibit minimal intron loss or gain events across evolutionary time. In contrast, cereals such as O. sativa, S. bicolor, and T. aestivum displayed longer genomic spans mainly due to intron elongation, paralleling the expanded gene lengths (8–14 kb) observed in garlic [65] and potato [57]. These variations suggest that while ALDH7A1 exonic architecture remains highly conserved, differential intron expansion may contribute to lineage-specific gene regulation, transcript stability, or alternative splicing under stress [14, 62]. The conservation of exon number among ALDH7 members aligns with findings from Malus× domestica and G. raimondii, where the structural layout of ALDH genes was shown to be evolutionarily stable despite polyploidy or chromosomal rearrangements [14, 61]. Similarly, Zhang et al. [66] reported that C. songorica ALDH12A1 shares > 90% sequence identity and nearly identical exon boundaries with its Sorghum counterpart, underscoring the evolutionary persistence of ALDH gene modularity in both dicots and monocots.

Motif composition analysis using MEME demonstrated that three highly conserved motifs (Motifs 1–3) were present in nearly all examined ALDH7A1 proteins, each corresponding to functional elements within the aldehyde dehydrogenase catalytic core. Motif 1, localized near the N-terminus, contained hydrophobic residues (e.g., PAFNF, CAYL) that stabilize cofactor-binding loops, whereas Motif 2 included the IIVMDADIKLAVRS sequence representing the catalytic Rossmann-fold domain responsible for NAD(P)⁺ binding. This motif is nearly identical to that reported for Sorghum SbALDH18B1 and potato StALDH7A1, confirming functional conservation of the NAD(P)⁺-binding and aldehyde oxidation domains [57, 62]. Motif 3, enriched in glycine and charged residues (e.g., GGEKATGGREAGSD), was positioned toward the C-terminal region and may contribute to enzymatic flexibility and post-translational regulation, consistent with the observations of Twaij et al. [65] in garlic and Zhang et al. [66] in desert grasses. The alignment of these motifs across diverse taxa suggests that ALDH7A1 proteins have retained a conserved catalytic scaffold despite sequence divergence in peripheral loops or linker regions. Similar motif conservation has been observed in Arabidopsis ALDH7B4 [11] and Oryza ALDH7B6 [10], implying that the enzymatic machinery required for NAD(P)⁺-dependent aldehyde oxidation evolved early and remained under strong functional constraints. Moreover, the stability of motif arrangement across species supports the view that ALDH7A1 may act as a structurally constrained yet functionally adaptable enzyme, enabling plants to respond effectively to abiotic stress conditions such as salinity, dehydration, and oxidative imbalance [13, 47, 67].

Expression, ROS-MDA and post-translational regulation

The present study showed that CaALDH7A1 was induced under both salt and ABA treatments, with a markedly stronger response in the tolerant genotype than in the sensitive genotype. At the same time, the factorial response pattern indicated that salinity was the primary driver of this induction, whereas ABA alone caused comparatively limited variation under non-saline conditions. This distinction is important because it suggests that the observed transcriptional response in chickpea reflects predominantly salt-associated induction, with ABA acting more as a modulatory signal under combined treatment conditions. Comparable stress-responsive expression of ALDH7-type genes has been reported in several plant species, including S. bicolor under PEG and ABA stress [62], S. tuberosum under salinity and ABA treatments [57], and A. thaliana under dehydration- and ABA-related conditions [18, 72]. In chickpea, Carmona-Molero et al. [47] also reported co-expression of ALDH family members with ABA-related regulators, while similar ABA- and salinity-associated induction has been described in garlic [65]. Taken together, these reports support the interpretation that the stronger induction of CaALDH7A1 in Aksu is consistent with a broader pattern of ALDH7 stress responsiveness already observed across both model and crop plants.

The physiological data further strengthen the relevance of this transcriptional pattern. In both genotypes, H₂O₂ and MDA levels increased under stress-related treatments, but their accumulation remained consistently lower in the tolerant genotype. This suggests that the stronger induction of CaALDH7A1 in Aksu occurred together with a more favorable oxidative status. Because salinity promotes ionic imbalance, osmotic stress, and reactive oxygen species generation, the resulting lipid peroxidation products, particularly MDA, are key indicators of oxidative injury [73–76]. In this context, the lower MDA and H₂O₂ levels observed in the tolerant genotype are compatible with a more effective control of oxidative damage. Similar associations between ALDH7-related responses and reduced ROS or lipid peroxidation have been reported in (A) thaliana, (B) rapa, and G. max [16, 18, 25]. More broadly, ALDH enzymes are known to oxidize reactive aldehydes generated during oxidative stress, thereby contributing to detoxification and redox-associated metabolism [73]. Accordingly, the present findings support the view that CaALDH7A1 may participate in stress-associated aldehyde metabolism in chickpea, although the current data should be interpreted as associative rather than directly causal.

A further point to consider is that, under the same NaCl level, exogenous ABA did not produce a uniform directional effect on CaALDH7A1 expression, but rather a limited modulatory response around the salinity-driven induction pattern. This may partly explain why H₂O₂ and MDA did not vary in strict parallel. In plants, H₂O₂ can function not only as an indicator of oxidative load but also as a signaling molecule in ABA-related stress responses, whereas MDA more directly reflects membrane lipid peroxidation. Accordingly, moderate ABA-associated changes in H₂O₂ may coexist with lower MDA accumulation if membrane damage is restrained despite ongoing redox signaling. This interpretation remains consistent with reports showing cultivar-dependent ABA effects on oxidative stress parameters under salinity and with the broader signaling role of H₂O₂ in stress acclimation [77].

This interpretation is also in line with findings from transgenic and comparative studies in other species. For example, overexpression of CsALDH12A1 from C. songorica reduced MDA accumulation and improved drought tolerance [66], whereas VpALDH2B4 from Vitis pseudoreticulata was associated with lower oxidative damage and improved salt stress performance [23]. Likewise, expression of AhALDH3H1-related ALDH activity in soybean has been linked to improved tolerance under saline–alkaline conditions [78]. In addition, ALDH-mediated stress protection has been discussed in relation to ROS homeostasis and antioxidant metabolism in Arabidopsis and other plants [19, 21, 79]. In the context of the present study, these comparisons do not demonstrate the precise mechanism of CaALDH7A1 in chickpea, but they do place our genotype-dependent expression and oxidative stress results within a broader functional framework in which ALDH family members are repeatedly associated with aldehyde detoxification and stress mitigation.

Predicted post-translational modification patterns provide an additional regulatory context for these observations. The predominance of serine phosphorylation sites in CaALDH7A1 agrees with earlier studies emphasizing the importance of serine/threonine phosphorylation in stress-responsive ALDH regulation [13, 67]. Similarly, the predicted N-glycosylation and SUMO-related motifs identified in chickpea resemble motifs described in O. sativa, G. raimondii, and other plant ALDHs [10, 61, 70, 80]. Although these features were inferred in silico, their conservation across orthologs suggests that regulatory complexity accompanies the preserved catalytic framework of ALDH7 proteins. Together with the expression and oxidative stress results, these data support the interpretation that CaALDH7A1 is associated with the broader interface between ABA-related signaling, aldehyde metabolism, and cellular redox status under stress conditions.

Overall, the stronger induction of CaALDH7A1 in the tolerant genotype, together with its association with lower H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation, supports a stress-related role for this gene in chickpea. This interpretation is consistent with observations in (A) thaliana, (B) rapa, G. max, garlic, and chickpea [11, 16, 25, 47, 65], where ALDH7-type genes are recurrently linked to abiotic stress responses and oxidative damage mitigation. At the same time, the present findings more specifically suggest that in chickpea this response is driven mainly by salinity, while ABA likely acts as a secondary modulatory component under combined treatment conditions.

Structural validation and catalytic insights from molecular docking of CaALDH7A1

Molecular docking analyses were included to relate the conserved active-site features of CaALDH7A1 to its proposed role in aldehyde oxidation and redox-associated metabolism. The docking results provided structural support for ligand accommodation by CaALDH7A1, consistent with the conserved catalytic architecture reported for ALDH7-type proteins in higher plants. Simulations with malondialdehyde (MDA) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) showed favorable predicted binding energies (–8.6 to − 6.3 kcal·mol⁻¹) and conserved interaction profiles, in agreement with docking patterns previously reported for other plant ALDHs [62]. In chickpea, the predicted interactions involved conserved residues such as Ser268, Asn165, and Glu396, which are part of the canonical ALDH catalytic region [13, 18]. The dual hydrogen bonding of both ligands to Ser268/Asn165 is therefore consistent with a conserved ligand-recognition environment, as also described for other plant ALDH proteins [61, 71].

The overall docking pattern further indicated that MDA and NAD⁺ were positioned within or near the same conserved catalytic groove, suggesting that the predicted pocket can accommodate both ligands within a shared active-site framework. Comparable dual-ligand binding configurations have been reported for SbALDH7B1 in sorghum [62] and StALDH7A1 in potato [57], while similar residue preferences have also been described for ALDH7 homologs in G. max [22] and A. sativum [65]. Structural comparison by CLICK likewise supported the high tertiary similarity of CaALDH7A1 with soybean and rice orthologs [10, 47]. In the context of the present study, these observations indicate that the chickpea protein retains a structurally conserved catalytic pocket compatible with ligands relevant to aldehyde metabolism. The predicted binding of MDA within this region is also consistent with the broader physiological context of lipid peroxidation-derived aldehydes during oxidative stress [75], while the NAD⁺ interaction pattern supports compatibility with the cofactor-binding framework characteristic of class 7 ALDHs [10, 13, 62].

From an evolutionary perspective, the conservation of the catalytic pocket across species is in agreement with the broader motif, phylogenetic, and diversity analyses presented in this study. Although sequence divergence was observed among plant families, the docking results suggest that the core ligand-binding architecture of ALDH7A1 has remained largely preserved. This conservation supports the broader interpretation that ALDH7A1 proteins share a common structural framework linked to aldehyde metabolism and redox homeostasis across plant lineages.

The docking analyses complement the expression and physiological findings by showing that CaALDH7A1 retains conserved structural features compatible with binding of both MDA and NAD⁺. Together with comparative evidence from Arabidopsis, rice, soybean, and garlic [10, 18, 22, 62, 65], these results support the view that ALDH7A1 is an evolutionarily conserved component of plant stress-associated aldehyde metabolism. A limitation of the present study is that biological replicates were generated from pooled plants, which reduced within-treatment variability but also limited inference at the individual-plant level.

Conclusions

This study provides the first integrative characterization of the ALDH7A1 gene in chickpea by combining expression profiling, structural and comparative bioinformatics, molecular docking, and physiological assays under salt and ABA treatments. Our findings show that CaALDH7A1 is a stress-responsive gene, with markedly higher induction in the salt-tolerant genotype than in the sensitive one. This enhanced transcriptional activation was accompanied by lower H₂O₂ and MDA accumulation, indicating a functional association between ALDH7A1 expression and the mitigation of oxidative damage. Comparative analyses across legumes and cereals revealed high conservation of ALDH7A1 protein structure, including physicochemical features, catalytic motifs, and exon–intron organization, reflecting strong evolutionary pressure to maintain aldehyde detoxification function. The predicted phosphorylation, N-glycosylation, and SUMOylation motifs further suggest that CaALDH7A1 activity is modulated by multilayered post-translational regulation. Molecular docking indicated favorable predicted binding of MDA and NAD⁺ to conserved catalytic residues, particularly Ser268, Asn165, and Glu396, providing supportive structural evidence consistent with NAD⁺-dependent aldehyde metabolism. Together, these results highlight CaALDH7A1 as a promising stress-associated candidate linked to aldehyde detoxification and oxidative stress responses in chickpea. The combined expression, physiological, and structural evidence supports the possibility that CaALDH7A1 contributes to salinity-associated stress responses and may represent a useful candidate for future functional and breeding-oriented studies. Future studies focusing on protein functional validation, gene editing, and regulatory network profiling will further elucidate its role in stress adaptation. Because the present study relied on pooled biological replicates, expression profiling, physiological measurements, and in silico analyses, direct causal inference regarding CaALDH7A1 function will require further validation through enzymatic assays, genetic manipulation, and individual-level physiological analyses.
