Eptifibatide bolus dose postductal stenting intervention: A single-center experience
Authors: Rishika Mehta, Amitabha Chattopadhyay, Aritra Mukherji, Sanjiban Ghosh, Jayita Nandy Das, Pushpanjali Gupta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectives:Stent thrombosis, a potential complication of ductal stenting, is associated with high mortality. This is a catastrophic complication, which can occur acutely (within 24 h), subacutely (within 30 days), or late (≥30 days) after stent implantation, with rates between 0.8% and 25%. Oral antiplatelet drugs like aspirin have erratic and inconsistent absorption and antiplatelet effects in critically ill neonates. Intravenous (IV) glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (GPIs) are antiplatelet agents with rapid effect (84% inhibition of platelet aggregation 15 min after bolus) that may help prevent this catastrophic complication.Materials and Methods:The study was conducted among 127 neonates with a median age of 1 month, of which 48% were male, undergoing ductal stenting procedures between January 2022 and March 2024 at our center who received an IV eptifibatide bolus of 180 µg/kg immediately postprocedure. Dosing simulations were generated based on extrapolation from the adult model. The primary outcome measures were stent thrombosis and bleeding events, whereas the secondary outcomes included platelet count.Results:Stent thrombosis occurred in one of the patients after prophylactic treatment with eptifibatide. Five patients experienced bleeding complications. Eight patients had thrombocytopenia, as thrombosis is prevented via the adenosine diphosphate pathway. The treatment did not affect serum creatinine and liver function.Conclusion:IV GPIs are safe in neonates after a ductal stenting procedure as an adjunct to oral antiplatelet therapy. Dosing considerations should include age and renal function. Randomized trials are warranted to establish efficacy and compare with current anticoagulation practices.

INTRODUCTION

In the current era, ductal stenting is considered an acceptable option with a lower risk of procedural complications, comparable early mortality, and lower medium-term mortality as compared to modified Blalock–Taussig–Thomas shunt (BTTS) for cyanotic newborns with duct-dependent pulmonary circulation.[1] The rate of complications for ductal stenting has significantly dropped from 33% to 57% during the initial implantations to 9%–13% in the current period. Acute complications of the procedure include stent migration, vascular injury or occlusion, ductal spasm, dissection, and perforation. Stent thrombosis is a catastrophic complication with a rate ranging between 0.8% and 25%. It can be acute (within 24 h), subacute (within 30 days), or late (≥30 days) after stent implantation.[2] If acute, it is a potentially fatal complication that can occur shortly after stent expansion or within hours of the procedure. If a guidewire can cross the stent, stent thrombosis can be managed percutaneously through balloon angioplasty and, if possible, subsequent stent reimplantation.

Thrombolytic therapy has been proposed as an additional treatment, but it has a significant risk of bleeding problems, particularly if a surgical shunt is necessary.[3] Although not completely established, the mechanisms of stent thrombosis are mostly multifactorial, including complex patients and lesions, stent choice, multiple stent usage, stent-vessel wall apposition, and type and duration of antiplatelet therapy as extrapolated from adult studies.[4]

There are no established guidelines for thromboprophylaxis, but platelet inhibition with enteral aspirin or a combination of aspirin with clopidogrel is commonly used in most centers. In the clopidogrel study, despite recruiting from 134 sites in 31 countries, the estimated treatment effects had large confidence intervals.[5] Although this study was a multicenter study for using clopidogrel in surgical systemic to pulmonary shunt, the result was no reduction in either mortality from any cause or shunt-related morbidity.[6] Similar results are expected for a ductal stenting procedure. It was our usual practice to maintain activated clotting time (ACT) >250 s during the procedure, followed by a heparin infusion of 15–20 U/kg/h for 24–48 h to prevent acute stent thrombosis, and then switch to 3–5 mg/kg/day of oral aspirin until the subsequent surgical intervention. We did not start an oral antiplatelet dose of aspirin 24 h before the procedure as our neonates were typically sick, were not feeding, or were septic. Institution of aspirin, though, reduces the rate of stent thrombosis; it remains as high as 10%, which may be due to erratic gut absorption due to high inotropic support and muscle paralysis in critically ill neonates.[7]

If stent thrombosis occurs during the procedure, the ductus arteriosus is rewired for balloon dilation to break up the thrombus simultaneously with local alteplase infusion. Treatment with an intravenous (IV) antiplatelet agent before starting aspirin may decrease the occurrence of acute thrombosis and thrombosis during the procedure without incurring the risk of bleeding due to alteplase. These agents are widely used in adults for the prevention of early thrombosis. However, the literature on their use in children is limited.[8] There has been a report where acute intraprocedural thrombosis during arterial ductal stenting was successfully managed using eptifibatide.[9] We used a bolus dose of eptifibatide (an IV glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor [GPI]) to bridge oral antiplatelet therapy in patients undergoing ductal stenting procedures. We report the safety and efficacy profile of eptifibatide in neonates.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

One hundred and twenty-seven neonates undergoing a ductal stenting procedure at Narayana Superspeciality Hospital (NSH) between January 2022 and March 2024 received an IV eptifibatide bolus of 180 μg/kg immediately postprocedure on the table. The primary outcome measures were stent thrombosis and bleeding events. The secondary outcomes included the measurement of platelet count. Using unfractionated heparin, a target ACT was maintained between 200 and 300 s. The results were compared with 32 neonates who underwent ductal stenting procedures in our facility between January 2021 and December 2021. Data collection

Patient demographics, procedure details, and eptifibatide dosing were recorded for the study. Procedural records were reviewed between January 2021 and December 2021 to determine thrombosis, reintervention, or any bleeding episodes following the ductal stenting procedure after ethical clearance from the NSH and Institutional Ethics Committee.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range, and comparisons between groups were performed with the independent Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were reported as percentages (in parentheses) and were analyzed using the Chi-square test to determine statistical significance, represented by the P value. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Out of 127 babies, 61 (48%) were male and 66 (52%) were female, reflecting an equal gender representation overall. The babies weighed from 1.90 kg to 4.20 kg, with a median weight of 2.80 kg. The majority, 74 (58%), of the babies were classified as having a biventricular hemodynamic structure, while 53 (42%) babies were identified as having a single ventricle. The vast majority, comprising 125 (98.4%), of the babies had one duct. In contrast, only 2 (1.6%) babies had two ducts. The majority, 102 (80.3%), of the babies had access through the axillary artery; a smaller portion, 24 (18%), of the babies utilized the femoral artery for access, while only 2 (1.6%) babies had access through the femoral vein. A single stent was used in all cases except for one neonate, who required two stents due to stent migration. All of them received preprocedure prostaglandin. Tortuosity is a major cause of stent thrombosis; hence, all patients were divided according to the ductal tortuosity index (DTI), and the hospital parameters are compared in Table 1. Most (65.5%) cases had complex morphology DTI >Class II and 32.7% DTI III. All stents used were coronary drug-eluting stents with a median stent size of 4 mm × 20 mm. All patients received the eptifibatide bolus dose immediately after the ductal stenting procedure. Stent thrombosis after 6 h of procedure occurred in one patient. It occurred even after heparin was started. The patient was 22 days old, weighing 2.6 kg, had a tortuous ductal anatomy with a DTI of III, and underwent placement of a 3.5 mm × 18 mm drug-eluting stent. The patient started desaturating, and echocardiography detected stent thrombosis. The patient was taken to the laboratory, rewiring was done, and alteplase was given directly to the stent site. Heparin infusion at 20 IU/kg was continued, and thrombosis was resolved. There were two intraprocedural deaths; none of them was thrombosis related. Bleeding was documented in five patients, two of whom had documented bleeding before the procedure. All five patients had hematuria, which resolved within <24 h. None of the patients required fresh frozen plasma or packed cell transfusion. Two patients with predocumented bleeding had self-resolving hematuria. None of the patients had a life-threatening pulmonary hemorrhage or intracranial bleeding.

DISCUSSION

Most studies have used recombinant tissue plasminogen activator as a rescue therapy for acute intraprocedural stent thrombosis.[10] Eptifibatide was previously used in a case report to rescue one neonate intraprocedure.[9] However, large studies and pediatric dosage models are lacking. Eptifibatide is a platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor antagonist used to prevent cardiac ischemic complications of percutaneous coronary intervention. It has been used with bolus dose only or bolus plus infusion in patients who have undergone percutaneous intervention with fewer complications but an increased risk of bleeding.[11] We hypothesized that using an eptifibatide bolus dose following a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) stenting procedure would be associated with lower rates of stent thrombosis. This study describes a single-center experience with a GPI as a bridge to oral antiplatelet therapy in pediatric patients undergoing PDA stenting. The lack of thrombosis after eptifibatide bolus and the low occurrence of life-threatening bleeding events suggest the safety and efficacy of eptifibatide in this population.

Eptifibatide has linear pharmacokinetics, proportional to the bolus drug for bolus doses ranging from 90 to 250 μg/kg. It has a rapid onset of action, with 84% inhibition of platelet aggregation 15 min after a bolus. In adult patients with acute coronary syndrome, it is given immediately after the diagnosis at a loading dose of 180 μg/kg IV.[12] The same dose was used in our patients post-PDA stenting. It is available in strengths of 0.75 mg/mL and 2 mg/mL. Emani et al. have described 52 pediatric patients (<18 years) undergoing surgical aortopulmonary shunt procedure who received tirofiban infusion as a bridge to oral aspirin, which is also an IV antiplatelet agent, and found it to be effective and safe to use.[7]

There is a lack of good evidence to support therapeutic interventions in pediatric cardiology and cardiac surgery, which also applies to our study with eptifibatide. More randomized controlled trial (RCTs) are required to prove its efficacy, as RCTs are the most valid evidence to support therapeutic interventions.[13] Like many low-cost novel innovations from India, eptifibatide has been borrowed from adult studies as a novel approach to preventing stent thrombosis.[14] Such innovations are essential in India, where children often present late and sicker, resulting in suboptimal outcomes. For instance, the recent Indian meta-analysis reported a cumulative mortality of BTTS of 5.61%.[15] Hence, off-label strategies for improving therapeutic interventions in sicker children are needed in India. Our study found that the off-label use of eptifibatide is efficacious in preventing stent thrombosis. Minimal bleeding complications were self-limited and did not require any intervention.

CONCLUSIONS

Platelet inhibition via the GPIIb/IIIa pathway following PDA stenting in neonatal patients results in platelet aggregation inhibition, effectively preventing acute stent thrombosis, and can be used as a bridge to enteral antiplatelet therapy. However, ours is a single-center experience, and more trials are necessary to determine the risks and benefits of this strategy compared with traditional treatment with oral antiplatelet agents alone.
