ME-BYO index as a formative construct for healthy ageing: a cross-sectional PLS-SEM analysis
Authors: Sho Nakamura, Kaname Watanabe, Choy-Lye Chei, Lei Feng, Kaisy Xinhong Ye, Kai Xuan Lim, Ryo Watanabe, Ung-il Chung, Hiroto Narimatsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInnovative health measures are essential for promoting “healthy aging.” The “ME-BYO” index considers health to exist on a continuum scale rather than as a binary state. This investigation sought to validate the ME-BYO index as a formative construct, examining if its constituent domains appropriately reflect overall health status using empirical data from the Kanagawa Prospective “ME-BYO” Cohort Study. We analyzed cross-sectional baseline data from 4,218 participants (aged 18–95 years, mean age, 57.8; 55.5% female) in the ME-BYO cohort. The ME-BYO index was specified as a higher-order formative construct composed of body mass index; blood pressure construct (systolic blood pressure, hypertension history); the 5-item Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale (GLFS-5) for locomotor function; and the 6-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6) for mental health. Its association with subjective well-being, a composite outcome including quality of life and subjective happiness, was examined using structural equation modeling. The GLFS-5 (outer weight: 0.695, t = 35.47) and the K6 (0.617, t = 20.23) were the strongest indicators of the ME-BYO index. Higher scores on the ME-BYO index (showing worse health) had a strong negative relationship with the subjective well-being outcome (path coefficient: -0.676, t = 71.99), accounting for 45.7% of the variance (R²). The ME-BYO index is a structurally valid formative measure that combines indicators from locomotor, mental, and metabolic domains. Introduction

In 2020, the number of people aged 60 years and older surpassed 1 billion, representing 13.5% of the global population, and is projected to nearly double to 2.1 billion by 20501. This demographic shift underscores the urgent need for the healthcare sector to move beyond disease treatment and towards the active promotion of “healthy ageing,” defined as developing and maintaining the functional abilities that support well-being in older age1,2. A key aspect of this perspective is the adoption of a lifespan approach, which views health not as a binary state but as a continuum across all of adulthood. By monitoring health status before the typical onset of age-related diseases, this strategy aims to proactively shift an individual’s entire health trajectory towards a more favorable outcome, making early risk identification and preventive interventions essential for maximizing lifelong quality of life (QOL)2.

Japan’s Kanagawa Prefecture has implemented this perspective through the “ME-BYO” concept. It defines health on a broad spectrum from healthy to sick, focusing on the intermediate “pre-disease” state enabling individuals to actively manage their well-being. The ME-BYO index, central to this initiative, is a public health tool designed for population-based self-monitoring3. Distinct from comprehensive clinical assessments that require administration by healthcare professionals, the ME-BYO index prioritizes indicators that are easily measurable and actionable. This index is based on an integration of key functional domains (locomotor, mental) with major modifiable metabolic risk factors (obesity, hypertension) that are known to shape an individual’s underlying health status and future functional decline.

This approach has led to the development of accessible digital tools, such as the “My ME-BYO Record” smartphone application, enabling users to monitor their healthy status longitudinally through the ME-BYO index4. While such tools hold great promise for fostering personal health awareness and promoting behavioral change, the scientific foundation of the index itself remains to be rigorously validated in a scientific context. Certain components used in the ME-BYO index, such as the Mini-Cog and gait speed, have been previously validated5,6; however, the overall structure of the ME-BYO index requires validation using empirical data. Validating the structure of the index as a formative construct is essential to ensure that it accurately reflects the ME-BYO concept and reliably supports public health programs promoting healthy aging3. The utility of the ME-BYO index as a public health instrument depends on whether its constituent parts collectively form a coherent and meaningful measure of overall health.

To determine whether the ME-BYO index can serve as a reliable tool for promoting healthy aging, the primary objective of this study was to examine, using real-world data, whether the index—conceptualized as a formative construct—is appropriately shaped by its constituent domains. Specifically, we assessed whether indicators representing key domains—metabolic function (body mass index [BMI] and blood pressure), locomotor function (5-item Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale [GLFS-5]), and mental health (6-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale [K6])—contributed significantly to the overall ME-BYO index construct within a structural equation modeling framework. To this end, we used baseline data from the Kanagawa Prospective “ME-BYO” Cohort Study (ME-BYO cohort), a community-based cohort in Japan that collects extensive information on health, lifestyle, and clinical factors relevant to healthy ageing, and applied partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to test a formative measurement model, reflecting the conceptualization of the ME-BYO index as emerging from its underlying domains. The use of PLS-SEM aligns with the theoretical conceptualization of the index as a formative construct and enables evaluation of its empirical validity.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used a cross-sectional approach to analyze baseline data from the ME-BYO cohort. The ME-BYO cohort is a genomic cohort study established in 2016 at the Kanagawa Cancer Center Research Institute and is part of the Japan Multi-Institutional Collaborative Cohort Study (J-MICC Study). The details of these studies have been described previously3,7. In brief, the J-MICC Study is being conducted by 13 research groups in 12 prefectures in Japan using a standardized protocol. Apart from the common standardized processes, each research group is allowed to collect additional data for their own research purposes. As a genomic cohort study, the J-MICC Study and ME-BYO Cohort aims to identify how genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors interact to influence disease risk and generate evidence for lifestyle disease prevention. We included participants from the ME-BYO cohort who were registered at the end of March 2024 (dataset version 2408).

Participants

The ME-BYO cohort enrolled local residents of Kanagawa Prefecture, Japan, aged 18–95 years, who were living or working in the prefecture. Several exclusion criteria were applied in this cross-sectional analysis. First, 681 participants enrolled before 2018 were excluded because the completed questionnaire lacked both the outcome variables essential for this study. Second, an additional 432 participants were excluded from the study. The reasons for these 432 exclusions, which could occur in combination, included unreliable responses to the food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) (affecting 105 participants), defined as either all items left unanswered or the selection of the lowest frequency category for all food items, suggesting potential non-engagement; missing EuroQoL 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5 L) data (affecting 234 participants); or missing single-item subjective happiness scale (SHS-1) data (affecting 394 participants). Dataset version 2408 contained 5,331 individuals. After applying the exclusion criteria detailed above, the final sample comprised 4,218 participants. The ME-BYO cohort and analyses performed in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Kanagawa Cancer Center (Yokohama, Japan; approval no. 28KEN-36). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, requiring them to understand information related to whole-genome sequencing and long-term follow-up.

Variables

The ME-BYO index, the primary construct of interest, is operationally defined using a set of formative health indicators. These included BMI (kg/m²), derived from direct anthropometric measurements of height and weight, and systolic blood pressure (mmHg), measured by trained examiners using established protocols representing the metabolic domain. Locomotor function was assessed with the GLFS-5, a self-administered questionnaire in which the total score (range 0–20, based on a 5-item, 5-point Likert scale) reflects function level, with higher scores indicating greater impairment8. Mental health was evaluated using the K6, another self-administered tool consisting of six items on a 5-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 24, where higher values indicate more severe distress9,10. For clarity, the ME-BYO index developed and validated in this study assigns higher values to poorer health, in contrast to the scoring method used in Kanagawa Prefecture’s “My ME-BYO Record” application, where higher values represent better health states. The scoring mechanism in the “My ME-BYO Record” application is intentionally reversed—higher scores indicating better health—to enhance intuitive comprehension for its primary users, the general public.

To validate the ME-BYO index against a comprehensive measure of an individual’s overall life evaluation, we specified a latent outcome construct of subjective well-being. This construct was formed by two key indicators: health-related quality of life (QOL), quantified using the EQ-5D-5L, and general happiness. This aligns with the ultimate goal of healthy aging, defined as the maintenance of functional ability to enable well-being. This instrument assesses five health dimensions (mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression) at five severity levels, and a Japanese value set is used to calculate a summary utility index (0 for death and 1 for perfect health)11,12. Subjective happiness was also recorded using a single 5-point Likert scale (1=“unhappy,” 5=“happy”).

Finally, the statistical models accounted for several covariates, which included age (years at recruitment); dietary information from a validated FFQ, including variables like total energy intake or specific nutrient consumption (with energy adjustment using the residual method)13,14; daily alcohol intake (g/day)15; and physical activity levels (moderate-to-vigorous physical activity defined as ≥ 3.0 metabolic equivalents) derived according to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire16. Additionally, the medical histories of hypertension and stroke were incorporated into the analysis as dummy variables. Each condition was initially classified into three categories (“no history,” “past history,” “on treatment”), and then represented by two dummy variables where “no history” served as the reference category (coded 0,0), “past history” as (1,0), and “on treatment” as (0,1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)17. The baseline characteristics of the study participants are summarized using descriptive statistics.

The core analysis utilized PLS-SEM to validate the ME-BYO index, operationalized as a formative-formative higher-order construct18,19. This higher-order construct was conceptualized as being formed by lower-order constructs representing the key domains: metabolic function (BMI and blood pressure-related variables), locomotor function (GLFS-5 score), and mental health (K6 score). Sex was coded as male = 0 or female = 1. BMI was treated as the absolute difference from 22.0 kg/m220. PLS-SEM was selected primarily because of its recognized strength in modeling constructs measured by formative indicators (where indicators cause the construct), its objective of maximizing the explained variance of dependent constructs (aligned with predictive research goals) and its suitability for data that may not meet normal distribution assumptions18,19. Following the recommended procedures for higher-order constructs, we employed a two-stage approach to model estimation and evaluation18,19,21. We conducted the analysis using the seminr package, which is designed for variance-based SEM. While the lavaan package is well-suited for covariance-based SEM and reflective measurement models, it does not support higher-order formative constructs according to PLS-SEM conventions. Consequently, seminr offered a suitable and reproducible framework for estimating the formative–formative higher-order structure of the ME-BYO index.

Prior to the main analysis, an initial model, including all theoretically derived formative indicators for each lower-order construct, was estimated (Table 1; Fig. 1). We evaluated the contribution of each formative indicator according to established guidelines18,19,22,23.

Specifically, we examined the indicator’s outer weight and path coefficients using bootstrapping (10,000 re-samples) to obtain t-statistics and 95% confidence intervals (CI) using the estimate_pls and bootstrap_model functions in the seminr package23,24. Indicators with their outer weights’ absolute t-statistic value (t-statistic) above the cutoff of 1.96 were retained. For indicators with t-statistics smaller than the cutoff of 1.96, we further examined their absolute contributions by outer loading. The indicator was retained if the outer loading was ≥ 0.50 or its t-statistic was higher than 1.96. Indicators that failed to meet these statistical criteria were removed unless they were deemed essential for maintaining content validity based on theoretical grounds. This iterative evaluation and refinement process resulted in the final set of indicators used in the measurement models for subsequent analyses (Table 1). The final model incorporating the selected indicators was re-estimated. Multicollinearity among the formative indicators within each construct (both low- and high-order constructs using low-order construct scores) was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), confirming that the highest VIF value was 1.33, indicating no concern for the effect of collinearity22,23. The explanatory power of the final model was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) of the ME-BYO index higher-order construct. The predictive performance of the model was assessed using the PLSpredict procedure implemented as predict_pls function in the seminr package, which follows recent recommendations for assessing prediction-oriented PLS-SEM models24. Because PLSpredict currently faces limitations with direct application to higher-order constructs, prediction was performed without using the high-order construct. We applied 10-fold cross-validation with 10 iterations and compared the prediction errors (root mean squared error [RMSE] and mean absolute error [MAE]) of the PLS-SEM model with those from linear model (LM) benchmark21,24. In addition to the PLSpredict results, we calculated cross-validated Stone–Geisser–type Q² values using the out-of-sample residuals obtained from PLSpredict, as the seminr package does not provide a function to obtain the traditional blindfolding-based Q² value. As our exclusion criteria addressed missing data for key outcome variables and FFQ non-responses, missing data for the remaining variables included in the PLS-SEM model were minimal (i.e., below 5% for any indicator). Accordingly, we handled the remaining missing values using mean replacement, a common approach for such low levels of missing data in PLS-SEM22,23. To evaluate potential confounding, correlations between potential confounders, including lifestyle factors (nutritional intake, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), and the estimated ME-BYO index construct scores were assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. For sensitivity analysis, the main PLS-SEM analysis was repeated using list-wise deletion to address missing data. Furthermore, to specifically assess the validity of the ME-BYO index within the demographic most relevant to healthy aging research, we conducted a subgroup analysis. This analysis replicated the final PLS-SEM model exclusively on the subset of participants aged 55 years and older.

The final PLS-SEM results are shown in Table 3. The GLFS-5 (0.695, t = 35.47) and K6 (0.617, t = 20.23) were the strongest contributors to the ME-BYO index. Age (0.029, t = 2.25) and blood pressure-related constructs (0.047, t = 2.47) also contributed. A strong negative path was observed from the ME-BYO index to the outcome constructs (EQ-5D-5 L and SHS-1), with a coefficient of -0.676 (t=-71.99). Additionally, BMI was positively associated with systolic blood pressure (coefficient: 0.137, t = 8.38). The outcome construct had an R² of 0.457, and the ME-BYO index showed a large effect size (f²=0.842). Q2 values for EQ-5D-5 L, SHS-1, GLFS-5, K6, age, and BMI were, 0.43, 0.08, 0.04, -0.03, 0.05, and 0.01, respectively. The subgroup analysis limited to participants aged 55 years and older (n = 2,584) yielded results consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table S2). The formative indicators, including locomotor function and mental health, remained significant contributors to the ME-BYO index, and the index maintained a strong, negative association with the subjective well-being outcome (path coefficient: -0.697, t=-49.26). This confirms the structural validity of the ME-BYO index specifically within an older population.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to validate the ME-BYO index as a formative construct using real-world data. Analysis of data from the ME-BYO Cohort yielded two key findings. First, the ME-BYO index comprises measures of locomotor function and mental health, along with age and a blood pressure-related construct, confirming that its intended multidimensional structure was successfully captured. Second, the study showed that higher ME-BYO index scores, reflecting a less favorable health status, were strongly associated with lower levels of both QOL and subjective happiness. These findings confirmed the index’s ability to reflect key health domains and its significant association with broader well-being, thereby fulfilling the objective of verifying its structural integrity.

A key finding underscoring the relevance of the ME-BYO index was its strong inverse association with subjective well-being (a composite of QOL and happiness), explaining 45.7% of the variance. This confirms that a decline in the health status captured by the index, correlates with reduced self-reported well-being. This result supports our hypothesis, as promoting well-being is fundamental to healthy aging initiatives globally1,2. While happiness is a broad construct, its inclusion is justified by extensive evidence linking it to positive health behaviors and even longevity, making it a relevant and holistic outcome for an index focused on promoting a healthy lifespan. For instance, physical activity levels associated with locomotor function are strongly linked to improved QOL25. Mental health status, another pillar of the ME-BYO index, is likewise a well-established contributor to overall QOL26. Additionally, metabolic elements such as BMI show a documented, albeit sometimes nonlinear, relationship with QOL through comorbidities like cardiovascular diseases27,28. The ME-BYO index’s capacity to integrate these distinct yet interconnected health domains into a single construct that significantly predicts both QOL and happiness highlights its potential as a practical health evaluation tool. This approach offers particular advantages over restrictive “successful aging” models, which often exclude individuals with chronic health conditions29.

The relatively modest weights for BMI and blood pressure-related constructs in this study warrant careful interpretation, as they may reflect the cross-sectional nature of our analysis and the use of subjective well-being measures as outcomes. These metabolic risk factors’ contributions may emerge more clearly in longitudinal studies using clinical endpoints such as stroke, cardiovascular events, or ADL changes.

Incorporating direct cognitive function assessments is a key consideration for the future refinement of the ME-BYO Index, given the importance of a comprehensive understanding of functional ability30. The participants in this study were part of a genomic cohort; therefore, they needed complex information regarding whole-genome sequencing and long-term follow-up to provide informed consent3. This selection process likely led to a cohort with a low prevalence of cognitive impairment, as individuals with comprehension difficulties may have been underrepresented. While the absence of a cognitive measure in this specific validation may have influenced the findings for this cohort, cognitive function remains critical for assessing functional ability in the broader aging population. Accordingly, to improve the index generalizability and ensure comprehensive health assessment, future studies should prioritize the inclusion of validated cognitive assessments. This is particularly important in research involving individuals with a wide range of cognitive function. Such inclusion is essential to develop an ME-BYO Index that is broadly applicable and accurately reflects the multifaceted nature of healthy aging.

In essence, this study establishes the ME-BYO index as a coherently structured, multidimensional measure of health status that meaningfully correlates with quality of life and subjective happiness, positioning it as a valuable instrument within the “innovation in healthy aging” landscape. However, this study has some limitations. First, global model fit indices were not evaluated because PLS-SEM does not provide established goodness-of-fit measures for formative constructs. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the structural relationships. Second, the voluntary nature of the ME-BYO cohort implies that the study sample is not population-representative, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our specific findings. Consequently, the indicator weights derived from our model should be interpreted as characteristic of this community-based sample rather than definitive national estimates. In addition, PLS-SEM is a variance-based approach that does not aim to reproduce the population covariance structure, meaning that the estimated paths reflect predictive associations rather than population-level structural parameters. Nonetheless, the primary aim of this study was not to establish universal weights, but to test and validate the theoretical structure of the ME-BYO index as a formative construct. PLS-SEM is well-suited for such theory- and prediction-oriented research, even with non-representative samples18,19. We therefore emphasize that the ME-BYO index is primarily designed for intrapersonal health monitoring—allowing individuals to track their own health changes over time—rather than for making direct, normative comparisons between individuals across the general population.

Third, the exclusion of participants with missing data on key variables, although necessary for analysis, may have introduced bias if they systematically differed from those included. Fourth, the ME-BYO index designed for practical self-monitoring, does not encompass all domains of health, such as sensory (vision and hearing) or cognitive functions. While these domains are essential for a comprehensive clinical assessment of an individual’s functional ability, their inclusion exceeded the scope of this public health tool, which prioritizes accessibility and usability. The omission of these factors reflects the pragmatic constraints of the data available in this large-scale community cohort. Additionally, the model’s out-of-sample predictive capacity was limited compared with that of a linear model. While this does not undermine its utility for in-sample individual assessment based on structural properties, it highlights that the ME-BYO index is primarily designed for intrapersonal health monitoring, supporting self-awareness rather than interpersonal comparison or predictive diagnostics. Future research should aim to enhance the ME-BYO index by incorporating sensory function and cognitive assessments and validating it longitudinally against a range of outcomes, including hard endpoints and objective health markers. Importantly, realizing the ME-BYO index’s potential through tools such as the “My ME-BYO Record” app will require further investigation into user engagement and behavioral impact. Despite these limitations, the present study provides a strong foundation for advancing an accessible health index that supports self-monitoring and promotes proactive healthy aging.

The ME-BYO index’s applications extend beyond individual health management. As an accessible, digital self-monitoring tool, it offers opportunities for broader societal and commercial integration. For example, insurers could use the index to develop products that incentivize health maintenance and improvement, supporting preventive, data-driven healthcare models. These applications demonstrate the index value as both a personal health tool and a scalable population health instrument.

In summary, we confirmed that the ME-BYO index is a formative construct, with locomotor function and mental health as its most influential components, alongside age- and blood pressure-related measures. Higher scores on the index, indicating poorer health status, are strongly associated with reduced quality of life and lower levels of subjective happiness. This validation supports the index usefulness in health monitoring and in promoting individual engagement in health management, thereby contributing to healthy aging efforts. Future studies incorporating cognitive function assessments would further enhance the index.
