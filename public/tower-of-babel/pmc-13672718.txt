DNA damage in thyroid cancer patients treated with radioactive iodine in Johannesburg, South Africa
Authors: Shingirai Brenda Kagodora, Ans Baeyens, Anne Vral, Mboyo Di Tamba Willy Vangu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThyroid cancer is the most common endocrine cancer globally. The National Cancer Registry has estimated the prevalence of the disease to be higher in females than in the male population with a risk of 1 in 506 females and 1 in 1035 in males in South Africa. Thyroid cancer mostly develops in the thyroid nodules. If cancerous then the malignant cells can spread to the nearby lymph nodes or by the blood vessels to other organs. The aim of the study was to investigate the correlation between ¹³¹I emitted ionizing radiation and the induced and persistent DNA damage in patients treated with radioactive iodine. Patients suffering from thyroid cancer and treated with radioactive iodine face an increased risk of secondary cancers due to potential chromosomal damage from exposure to ionising radiation. We used the cytokinesis - block micronucleus assay to assess chromosomal damage at four time points.ResultsWe found that therapy induced micronuclei increased with the amount of radioactive iodine activity. There was a significant difference in the mean number of micronuclei in thyroid.ConclusionOur results emphasize the need for ongoing monitoring of chromosomal damage in thyroid cancer patients to understand the long-term implications of radioactive iodine therapy. We anticipate that further research exploring the effects of treatment intervals on genetic stability and the potential risks of retreatment, may elucidate the true impact of this routinely used treatment on DNA damage, particularly in patients who may receive consecutive radioiodine therapies.Keywords: Micronucleus assay, Radioactive iodine, DNA damage, Thyroid cancer

Background

Thyroid cancer is the most common endocrine cancer globally (Shank et al. 2022). The incidence of thyroid cancer has been rising globally, partly due to improved detection methods and increased exposure to risk factors (Drozd et al. 2020). It is more common in women than men, with a higher prevalence in individuals aged 30–50 years (Christofer Juhlin et al. 2023). Several risk factors are associated with thyroid cancer development, including radiation exposure (Xu et al. 2012), particularly during childhood, family history of thyroid cancer (Byun et al. 2020), genetic syndromes (e.g., familial adenomatous polyposis, Cowden syndrome), and environmental factors such as iodine deficiency or excess (Shank et al. 2022; Christofer Juhlin et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2012; Bonnema and Hegedus 2012). Additionally, lifestyle factors such as obesity and diet may also contribute to the risk (Drozd et al. 2020; Bonnema and Hegedus 2012; Matrone 2022).

Thyroid cancer primarily originates in thyroid nodules (Hoang 2010). If these nodules are malignant, the cancerous cells can spread to nearby lymph nodes or through blood vessels to other organs. The most common types of thyroid cancers are differentiated, such as follicular and papillary carcinomas, which generally have a favourable prognosis (Xing 2013; Wang et al. 2023; Santos et al. 2020). Follicular thyroid cancer often results from a low iodine diet, whereas the combination of a low iodine diet and radiation exposure (from medical treatments or nuclear accidents) significantly increases the risk of developing papillary thyroid cancer (Christofer Juhlin and Mete 2023; Sakafu et al. 2018; Knobel and Medeiros-Neto 2007). Conversely, medullary thyroid cancer is primarily associated with hereditary conditions (Christofer Juhlin and Mete 2023; Toraih et al. 2021). Anaplastic thyroid cancer is undifferentiated, highly aggressive, and characterized by rapid growth and poor prognosis (Cabanillas and McFadden 2016; Nagaiah et al. 2011; Sherman 2003). The molecular pathogenesis of thyroid cancer involves genetic alterations such as mutations in the BRAF (B-Raf proto-oncogene), RAS(Rat sarcoma), and RET/PTC (Rearranged during Transfection / Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma) genes, which play crucial roles in cell proliferation and survival (Xing 2013; Nikiforov 2008; Ferrari et al. 2018).

Ionizing radiation can induce chromosomal damage in living tissues (Little 2003). When this damage is unrepaired or mis repaired, it can lead to chromosome damage (Wada et al. 2002). Chromosomal aberrations play a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of thyroid cancer. Research indicates that these genetic alterations are common across various subtypes of thyroid cancer, including papillary, follicular, and anaplastic carcinomas (Wada et al. 2002). Significant inter-individual variation in the number of radiation-induced chromosomal aberrations, are likely correlated with differences in DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) damage repair capacity (Borràs-Fresneda et al. 2016). Initially, an enhanced sensitivity to the chromosome-damaging effects of ionizing radiation was demonstrated in certain hereditary cancer-prone conditions, such as Ataxia Telangiectasia and Nijmegen breakage syndrome (Jeggo and Lavin 2009). Subsequently, this radiation sensitivity became evident in many cancer patients, including those with head and neck cancer, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and breast cancer (Ruyck et al. 2008; Riches et al. 2001; Baeyens et al. 2002).

Chromosomal radiosensitivity of individuals is determined by studying radiation induced damage to the chromosomes (Durante and Formenti 2018). The activation of cells may be delayed by 3–5 days post-RT (Radiation Therapy), with a further delayed response associated with hypoxia occurring approximately two weeks after RT (Barker et al. 2015; Liu and Sun 2022). This delayed activation may contribute to the delayed observation of cell damage (Jarosz-Biej et al. 2019). The cytokinesis-block micronucleus assay (CBMN), a cytogenetic technique used to measure DNA damage, has been instrumental in understanding the genotoxic effects in various cancers, including thyroid cancer (Miszczyk et al. 2019). Micronuclei (MN) are widely recognized as early biomarkers of unstable DNA damage and chromosomal instability. MN assay detects micronuclei in the cytoplasm of interphase cells, which are indicative of chromosomal fragments or whole chromosomes that are not incorporated into the daughter nuclei during cell division (Miszczyk et al. 2019; Fenech et al. 2020; Fenech 2008; Cháves-Campos Andrés and Luisa 2022). Although CBMN is widely used as a sensitive biomarker of chromosome damage and genomic instability, it does not identify the specific chromosomes or distinguish stable from unstable aberrations, so it should be interpreted as a global rather than a locus-specific measure of genotoxicity (Fenech 2008; Luzhna et al. 2013; Sommer et al. 2020).

After exposure to DNA-damaging agents, MN frequency typically peaks and then decreases as cells repair the damage (Dhillon et al. 2011). Recovery to normal levels of MN frequency is generally expected within a week and up to three months post-therapy, depending on the severity of the damage and the repair capacity of the individual (Fenech 2008; Fenech and Bonassi 2011). This timeline aligns with the body’s natural DNA repair mechanisms, which resolve double-strand breaks (DSBs) and other lesions that lead to micronuclei formation (MacDonald Kate et al. 2020).The persistence or delayed resolution of MN may indicate ongoing genomic instability or impaired DNA repair processes, which are commonly observed in cancer and other diseases associated with chromosomal abnormalities (Krupina et al. 2021; Adams et al. 2024).

The use of the MN assay in thyroid cancer research has expanded current understanding of the mechanisms prompting genetic damage and the potential for using this biomarker in clinical practice (Cebulska-Wasilewska et al. 2017; Gkikoudi et al. 2023). It may aid in identifying individuals at higher risk of treatment-related complications and optimizing therapeutic approaches to minimize genetic damage (Cebulska-Wasilewska et al. 2017). Moreover, the MN assay can be integrated with other molecular and cytogenetic techniques to provide a comprehensive evaluation of genetic instability in thyroid cancer (Bonnema and Hegedus 2012; Ferrari et al. 2018). This assay also helps in monitoring the genotoxic effects of radioiodine therapy, providing insights into the long-term risks of secondary malignancies (Luzhna et al. 2013). Thyroid cancer patients exhibit higher frequencies of spontaneous micronuclei compared to healthy controls, indicating increased genetic instability (Santos et al. 2020; Monteiro Gil et al. 2002; Gil 2000).

Current treatments for thyroid cancer include thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine (¹³¹I) (RAI) therapy, and hormone-suppressive treatment (Huang et al. 2019). These regimens are relatively successful for tumours with a good prognosis, increasing survival rates (Huang et al. 2019; Schmidbauer et al. 2017). However, the incidence of secondary cancers has been reported in patients treated with radioactive iodine (Iyer et al. 2011). Treatment guidelines for RAI vary based on the disease’s clinical presentation, with low activity of approximately 1110 MBq (30 mCi) (Hong and Ahn 2018) and high activity ranging from 2960 to 11,100 MBq (80–300 mCi) (Mäenpää et al. 2008). Higher activity of radioactivity can contribute to chromosomal aberrations and increase the predisposition to secondary cancers (Iyer et al. 2011). Acute effects of radioactivity often present as hematologic malignancies, while chronic effects are indicated by the development of solid tumours (Wang and Tepper 2021; Dainiak 2002). Secondary cancers resulting from radioactive iodine therapy include breast cancer, colorectal cancer, bone cancer, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, kidney cancer, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and leukaemia (Pasqual et al. 2022; Kim et al. 2023; Sawka et al. 2009; Teng et al. 2015). Among female thyroid cancer patients treated with radioactive iodine, breast cancer is the most common secondary solid tumor (Bolf et al. 2019). Women with a genetic predisposition are at a higher risk of developing secondary breast cancers following radioactive iodine therapy (Lu et al. 2022). In our manuscript, the statement that breast cancer is the most common secondary tumor in female thyroid cancer patients therefore refers to overall epidemiologic patterns in this population, not exclusively to malignancies directly caused by radioiodine exposure.

The aim of this prospective study was to investigate the correlation between iodine radioactivity and DNA damage in patients treated with radioactive iodine. The primary objective was to evaluate the chromosomal radiosensitivity in blood of thyroid cancer patients at four time points: (a) Time 0 – prior to treatment, (b) Time of discharge – either 3 or 5 days post-treatment depending on the activity, (c) 3 months post-treatment, and (d) 6 months post-treatment. Additionally, the secondary objective was to compare the chromosomal radiosensitivity of thyroid cancer patients with that of hyperthyroid patients (non-cancerous) and healthy individuals, at Time 0 as standard of reference.

Materials and methods

This hospital-based prospective study was done at Charlotte Maxeke Johannesburg Academic Hospital (CMJAH), a referral tertiary academic hospital in Johannesburg that is accessible to the public. The study population comprised 34 thyroid cancer patients, 26 hyperthyroid patients and 30 controls (healthy individuals). They were all invited to enrol and participate in the study in line with the Helsinki Declaration on studies in human subjects. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study. The study received ethics approval from Wits University Human Research ethics committee certificate M1909107.

All 34 thyroid cancer participants received ¹³¹I RAI therapy, with activity ranging from 1110 to 11,100 MBq (30 to 300 mCi). Blood samples were collected in 4 ml lithium-heparin tubes at four key time points: upon admission for radiotherapy, at discharge (3–5 days post-therapy), and at 3 and 6 months after treatment.

The MN assay was initiated by adding heparinised blood to culture medium followed by the immediate stimulation of lymphocytes using phytohemagglutinin (PHA). The culture was incubated for 23 h prior to the addition of cytochalasin B to inhibit cell division. Cells were harvested 70 h post stimulation using an ice-cold hypotonic stock of 5.6 g KCl (Potassium chloride) in 1 L dH2O (distilled water) and washed in a 4:1:5 methanol/acetic acid/ringer solution. The slides were prepared by dropping a cell suspension of 40 µl onto methanol-cleaned slides and stained with acridine orange. Micronuclei were identified according to the following criteria: the micronuclei were in cytoplasm and had a diameter of less than a third of the whole nucleus; they were circular or oval, and their staining and refractivity were in accordance with that of the whole nucleus; and their structures were similar to those of the whole nucleus, with complete separation and no other nearby fragments or impurities. A Zeiss Axioskop fluorescent microscope (Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany) was used for scoring at 40X magnification. All cultures were performed in duplicate and at least 1000 binucleated cells per condition for each patient were analysed as outlined in Fig. 1.

Outline of assay

Descriptive statistics was conducted using Stata 18 software. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentages. The continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation median and interquartile range. Association between demographics and group was assessed using the Pearson’s Chi-Square or Fisher exact test. Kruskal Wallis test with Benjamini-Hochberg ad hoc was used for the analysis of categorical and continuous variables. Statistically significant association was set at p-value < 0.05 and was two sided.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of thyroid cancer and its treatment on DNA damage, as assessed by micronuclei (MN) formation. The data presented provides insights into the demographic characteristics of the study population, risk factors, and the changes in MN frequency following radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy.

The study population comprised control subjects, individuals with hyperthyroidism, and patients with thyroid cancer. A total of 90 participants were included in our study with baseline blood samples for MN assay. Further blood samples were drawn in 34 participants with thyroid cancer at discharge, at 3 and 6 months following discharge after RAI therapy. Literature describes previous studies on potential chromosomal damage and increased risk of secondary cancers for individuals treated with radioactive iodine (Kim et al. 2023). Several studies have evaluated cytogenetic damage after therapeutic ¹³¹I in differentiated thyroid cancer using conventional chromosome aberration analysis or micronucleus-based approaches. M’Kacher et al. reported biological dosimetry in 30 patients treated with 3.7 GBq ¹³¹I, showing bone-marrow doses around 0.5 Gy and highlighting that absorbed doses may exceed MIRD estimates, particularly in hypothyroid patients with reduced renal clearance (M’Kacher et al. 1996). Baugnet-Mahieu et al. found increased chromosome aberrations after ¹³¹I therapy for thyroid cancer, consistent with persistent structural damage in peripheral lymphocytes (Baugnet-Mahieu et al. 1994). Watanabe et al. applied the micronucleus assay after ¹³¹I therapy and demonstrated a significant, dose-dependent rise in MN shortly after treatment, similar to the early peak we observed at discharge in our cohort (Watanabe et al. 1998). More recently, Signore et al. compared short-term and stable DNA damage in patients treated with ¹³¹I either in hypothyroidism or after rhTSH stimulation, showing that thyroid hormone status and stimulation protocol influence both the extent and persistence of DNA lesions (Signore et al. 2022). Our data add to this body of evidence by providing longitudinal MN measurements at baseline, discharge, 3 and 6 months in a Sub-Saharan African population, thereby extending the time-course and demographic context of ¹³¹I-related genotoxicity.

The demographic analysis revealed a predominance of females across all groups (79%), and they comprised the vast majority (88%) in the thyroid cancer group thus aligning with the known higher prevalence of thyroid disorders in women (Meng et al. 2015). In this study, Black participants constituted the majority across all three groups. This predominance may be influenced by country’s distribution of the population as well as the disparities in healthcare access, as the study was conducted in a public hospital setting where Black individuals are more likely to receive care compared to other racial groups (Chen and Yeh 2022; Mhlanga 2020). The age distribution of these patient groups exhibited significant variability. Hyperthyroid patients tended to be older, typically within the 50–59 year age range, which is relatively young compared to high-income countries where the typical age range is 60 years or older (Dedon 2022). In contrast, thyroid cancer patients spanned a broader age range of 40–59 years, reflecting the diverse aetiology of this condition.

Comorbidities like HIV and Hypertension were observed to be more prevalent in patients with hyperthyroidism and thyroid cancer compared to the control group, as noted in a study by Huang et al. (2024). Notably, a substantial proportion of thyroid cancer patients (54%) presented with co-existing chronic illnesses, suggesting a potential association between systemic health and the development of thyroid cancer (Huang et al. 2024). Although younger individuals tended to have slightly higher median MN values, the wide overlap in interquartile ranges indicates that age was not a major determinant of MN induction in this relatively small cohort, and we did not detect a statistically significant difference between age categories. These findings suggest that, within our sample, radioiodine activity and underlying thyroid pathology are more important drivers of MN formation than chronological age alone.

The study utilized the MN assay as a marker of genomic instability. In comparison of the 3 groups, the hyperthyroid group had highest number of spontaneous MN compared to the controls and the thyroid cancer group. Similar results were noted from studies that were done on cytogenetic damage after 131-iodine treatment for hyperthyroidism and thyroid cancer (Cararo Lopes et al. 2023; Gutierrez et al. 1999; Alfaisal et al. 2014).

In keeping with the study’s main objective, there was a significant increase in therapy-induced DNA damage for those thyroid cancer patients treated with radioactive iodine. This transient increase in MN frequency likely reflects the immediate genotoxic effects of RAI on peripheral blood cells (Luzhna et al. 2013; L et al. 2020). RAI emits beta particles that induce DNA damage, leading to chromosomal aberrations and MN formation (Durante and Formenti 2018). While the MN frequency decreased over time, it did not return to baseline levels within the 6-month follow-up, suggesting persistent genomic instability (Özdal et al. 2016).

The median activity for thyroid cancer participants was 5550 MBq (150 mCi), ranging from 3700 to 7400 MBq (100–200 mCi). The MN frequency correlated with the administered activity of RAI, indicating a activity-dependent effect on genomic damage (Huumonen et al. 2014). RAI-induced DNA damage can trigger inflammatory responses within the tumor micro environment (TME), further contributing to genomic instability and potentially promoting tumor recurrence (Kay et al. 2019).Genetic instability, characterized by chromosomal aberrations and mutations, is a hallmark of thyroid cancer (Özdal et al. 2016; Huumonen et al. 2014). These genetic alterations can disrupt critical cellular processes, leading to uncontrolled cell growth, impaired differentiation, and resistance to therapy. Larger additional studies and other FISH techniques for stable aberrations, could enhance understanding of the increase in aberrations even at 3 months after therapy (Gkikoudi et al. 2023). Further studies with planned interval of longer followup including retreatment are required to validate the impact of radioiodine therapy on DNA damage. Longitudinal studies to assess the long-term effects of RAI on genomic stability and cancer recurrence are desirable.

Conclusion

The results of this study are unique in that multiple time points (baseline, discharge, 3 months and 6 months) approach to evaluate DNA damage with Mn technique could not be found in literature. The study is novel as being the first done in Sub-Saharan African population. Since patients in our clinical settings often present with high burden of disease that requires more than one RAI treatment, it would be of interest to investigate cellular radiosensitivity before and after multiple RAI treatment.

This study provides valuable insights into the impact of thyroid cancer and RAI therapy on DNA damage. The data highlights the importance of monitoring MN frequency as a marker of DNA damage and chromosomal instability. Further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and develop strategies to mitigate the genotoxic effects of RAI and improve treatment outcomes in thyroid cancer patients. The findings emphasize the need for a comprehensive approach that considers demographic factors, risk factors, the tumor microenvironment, and genetic stability in the management of thyroid cancer. Of particular interest, a long term assessment of potential impact of DNA damage following RAI therapy may elucidate the course of initial increase of MN number in individuals receiving multiple therapies.
