Comparison of genetic programming and HEC-HMS as a conceptual model in simulating rainfall-runoff time series
Authors: Reza Sepahvand, Mehrdad Khoshoei, Mohammad H. Golmohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Forecasting runoff is an essential task in hydrological modeling. The factors influencing the rainfall‑runoff process vary in space and time, introducing uncertainty and complexity that often lead to modeling errors and make accurate prediction challenging. This study employs a genetic programming (GP) model to simulate the rainfall-runoff process in the Khorramabad River basin in Iran. To validate the performance of the proposed GP approach, its results are compared with those from the conceptual HEC-HMS model. First, the HEC-HMS model was calibrated and used to simulate the rainfall-runoff process for four flood events in the study area. Subsequently, the GP model was applied as a data-driven rainfall-runoff model for the same events. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding individual sub-watersheds, identifying those critical to cumulative flood discharge. Additionally, the Curve Number (CN) parameter was thoroughly investigated. The performance of the two simulation approaches was compared using several criteria: the root mean square error (RMSE) of peak discharge, mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). For the GP model, R² values ranged from 0.88 to 0.94 across the four events, whereas HEC-HMS gave R² between 0.65 and 0.79. The results indicate that, under the data-rich conditions of this study where long-term hydrological records were available, the GP model provided more accurate simulations of the rainfall-runoff process compared to the event-calibrated HEC-HMS model in the Khorramabad River basin.

Introduction

Rainfall-runoff modeling in river basins is crucial for reservoir design and water resource management, particularly during drought periods. Runoff estimation is extensively used in designing hydraulic structures and determining minimum water availability for agricultural, industrial, and hydropower needs. It is also essential for planning irrigation and drainage networks1,2. To convert rainfall into runoff, hydrologists have developed various statistical, hydraulic, and hydrological methods. Broadly, these methods fall into two categories: (1) conceptual (or knowledge-based) models, such as HEC-HMS, and (2) data-driven models, like Genetic Programming (GP) or Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)3,4. Conceptual models simulate hydrological processes using the physical characteristics and principles of a basin. However, acquiring the detailed spatial data required for these models can be challenging. Furthermore, applying them to large basins often entails a significant computational burden4,5. In contrast, while data-driven models do not explicitly interpret a basin’s physical properties, the underlying physical information is implicitly contained within historical hydrological data.

These models can provide acceptable estimations of the rainfall-runoff relationship6. For instance, Gumindoga and Rwasoka7 simulated runoff in the Manyame Catchment, Zimbabwe, using the Snyder Unit Hydrograph (SUH) within the HEC-HMS framework. Their model successfully forecasted runoff volume and peak discharge during calibration (Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency, NSE = 0.68) and yielded plausible results during validation (NSE = 0.61). In another study, Gao et al.8, applied the HEC-HMS model to a semi-arid basin in northwestern China. The results indicated that the model tended to underestimate hydrographs in winter and spring, as well as some summer flow events. The authors suggested this discrepancy might stem from the nonlinear rainfall-runoff responses inherent to the basin.

In general, the nonlinear nature of hydrological processes, inherent uncertainties, the requirement for long-term historical data, and the structural complexity of conceptual models are key reasons researchers increasingly turn to data-driven alternatives. These include neural networks, fuzzy systems, wavelet analysis, and evolutionary algorithms such as genetic programming9.

Previous research has utilized knowledge-based models to simulate the rainfall-runoff process10 and estimate groundwater flow11. In recent years, Genetic Programming (GP) has also gained prominence as a data-driven approach for simulating various hydrological processes9,12.

For example, Rauf and Ghumman13 employed both the HEC-HMS conceptual model and an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) to forecast flow in the Upper Indus River. They used basin data—including temperature, rainfall, and discharge from 1985 to 2004—to calibrate HEC-HMS and train the ANN. Data from 2005 to 2014 were then used for HEC-HMS validation and ANN testing. Performance was evaluated using statistical indices such as the Nash–Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), Mean Bias Error (MBE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). Results indicated that both models performed satisfactorily. A Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis revealed that the storage coefficient and time of concentration were the most influential parameters in calibrating the HEC-HMS model. In contrast, all input variables were equally important for training the ANN.

Genetic Programming (GP), a branch of evolutionary algorithms founded on Darwinian principles14, is particularly effective for modeling nonlinear and dynamic hydrological systems. The method works by defining an objective function and iteratively minimizing the error between predicted and observed outputs. Noted for its accuracy and computational efficiency15, GP differs from traditional Genetic Algorithms (GAs) in its representation of solutions: while GAs use fixed-length linear chromosomes (often binary strings), GP employs tree-structured programs that allow more flexible and expressive model formulations.

Several studies demonstrate GP’s effectiveness in hydrology. Khu et al.16, applied GP to streamflow forecasting in France, achieving high precision, especially for lead times shorter than the basin concentration time. Aytek et al.17, affirmed GP as a robust scientific method for rainfall-runoff forecasting. Guven18, used Linear Genetic Programming (LGP) to predict daily flow in Pennsylvania’s Schuylkill River, finding it more accurate than several neural network benchmarks.

Comparative studies further highlight GP’s strengths. Ghorbani et al.19, evaluated GP, ANN, and a fuzzy neural network for flood routing in Turkey’s Kızılırmak River, concluding that GP produced the most accurate output hydrograph. Whigham and Crapper20, reported that GP outperformed the deterministic IHACRES model in simulating rainfall-runoff for two Australian basins.

Recent advancements continue to showcase GP’s utility. Danandeh Mehr and Nourani21, developed the Season Algorithm-based Multi-Gene Genetic Programming (SA-MGGP) model for multi-day ahead flow forecasting in Turkey, demonstrating its superior accuracy and efficiency. In Iran, Ruhnavaz and Hatamlou22, successfully modeled rainfall-runoff in the Ghotour-Chai River using GP, validating its power and precision through RMSE and correlation coefficients. Similarly, Babaali et al.23, confirmed GP as a reliable method for predicting flood-event discharges in the Khorramabad River, reinforcing its status as one of the most accurate data-driven techniques for river discharge prediction.

To enhance simulation accuracy, Hadi and Tombul24 implemented a hybrid methodology. They first applied Continuous Wavelet Transform (CWT) for data preprocessing to extract key parameters, which were then used as inputs for both an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and a Multi-Gene Genetic Programming (MGGP) model. The resulting relationships were further analyzed using Wavelet Coherence Transform (WCT). Their findings confirmed that the CWT-based preprocessing significantly improved the modeling results.

Beyond rainfall-runoff simulation, Genetic Programming (GP) has been successfully applied to a wide range of hydrological challenges. These include river flow forecasting20,25, sediment transport modeling26, infiltration depth estimation27, and groundwater flow simulation28.

Recent research continues to advance the field through sophisticated hybrid and comparative approaches: Chadalawada et al.29, developed a flexible algorithmic framework integrating evolutionary computing (GP) with machine learning for short-term rainfall-runoff forecasting. Mishra and Karmakar30, optimized a backpropagation neural network architecture to predict total rainfall-runoff in India’s Mahanadi River basin, reporting excellent performance in most months. Haddad31, calibrated and validated the HEC-HMS model for a watershed in Algeria, demonstrating its reliability using statistical measures like the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency. Aoulmi32, proposed an ANN model hybridized with an Improved Particle Swarm Optimization algorithm (ANN-IPSO) for a semi-arid Algerian basin, showing its superiority over other optimization techniques.

In a comparative study, Gelete et al.33, evaluated the SWAT, HBV, and HEC-HMS models in an Ethiopian catchment. The SWAT model performed best, and the authors found that combining model outputs using ensemble techniques, particularly a Neural Network Ensemble (NNE), substantially improved overall simulation performance. Other notable studies have explored high-resolution hydrodynamic modeling34, sediment concentration forecasting with soft computing35, climate-impact streamflow prediction36, conceptual-model enhancement via swarm optimization37, and hybrid model trees38.

Accurate rainfall-runoff simulation is critical for flood mitigation and water resource planning. While many studies have applied either conceptual or data-driven models separately, comparative evaluations that explicitly account for differences in data requirements and calibration philosophies are still limited. In this study, we conduct a focused comparison between the widely used conceptual HEC-HMS model and a genetic programming (GP) approach in the Khorramabad River basin, Iran. **The novelty of this work lies not in the application of GP itself, but in the controlled, side-by-side comparison of a continuous data-driven GP model against an event-calibrated HEC-HMS model under specific data conditions – i.e., a basin with long-term hydrological records but only limited physiographic information.** Our objective is to evaluate the relative performance of the two approaches and to provide practical guidance for model selection in similar contexts, rather than to assert universal superiority of either method. The unique aspect is the asymmetric comparison where each model is used in its most common operational mode (continuous training for GP, event-based calibration for HEC-HMS).

With an area of 1,640 km², the Khorramabad River Basin is situated in Lorestan Province, southwestern Iran. Its geographical coordinates extend from 33°26’76” N to 48°14’77” E. Encompassing approximately 16% of the larger Kashkan Basin, it has a mean gradient of 24.36%, which is lower than the Kashkan Basin’s average. HEC-HMS rainfall-runoff model

The HEC-HMS model is an enhanced, Windows-based successor to the HEC-1 model, retaining all its original functionalities. Hydrographs generated by this model either independently or in conjunction with other software are applied to various objectives. These include water supply planning, urban drainage design, flood forecasting, streamflow analysis, land-use change assessment, spillway design, flood control studies, and reservoir system operations (HEC-HMS User’s Manual, Version 3.5).

In essence, HEC-HMS represents an integrated system of mathematical modules that simulate key hydrological processes, including losses, rainfall-runoff transformation in sub-basins, and flood routing through rivers and reservoirs. The software package consists of a core program supported by five subroutines. Two of these subroutines are dedicated to automated calibration optimizing unit hydrograph parameters, loss rates, and surface flow routing coefficients. The remaining subroutines perform computations for unit hydrograph derivation, flow routing, and hydrograph combination.

Within the model, the rainfall-runoff process is represented as a network of interconnected components. Each component simulates a specific aspect of the hydrological cycle within a sub-basin or sub-area. Structurally, HEC-HMS is organized into four primary modules: (1) Basin Component Simulation, (2) Meteorological Data Analysis, (3) Control Specifications, and (4) Parameter Estimation.

The selection of modeling components within HEC-HMS was guided by watershed characteristics and data availability. The SCS Curve Number loss method was adopted due to its suitability for event-based flood modeling in basins with heterogeneous land use and limited soil moisture observations. The Clark unit hydrograph was selected for rainfall–runoff transformation because it accounts for both translation and storage effects, providing a realistic representation of watershed response. The Muskingum routing method was applied for channel flow routing owing to its stability, simplicity, and widespread application in event-based hydrological studies.

Genetic programming (GP)

According to Darwin’s theory, genetic programming (GP) is the generalized version of GA first proposed. This theory states that a population selectively abandons the inappropriate population during evolution and modifies the children to achieve the optimal target function (Koza, 1994). In genetic programming, the existing structures, including the input and target variables and their connecting function, are first defined, and then the appropriate structure of the function transforming the inputs to the output/target is determined for the model. In this study, the GP model was implemented using the GPTIPS v2.0 toolbox (Matlab R2018b) and cross-validated with the gplearn library (Python 3.8). All relevant parameters are summarized in Table 3. This method consists of an equation of communication between the input and output variables. Hence, it can automatically choose the appropriate variables of the model and eliminate irrelevant variables, reducing the number of input variables and mitigating the computational load of the GP algorithm. The selection of antecedent precipitation at lags of t-1 and t − 2 days, along with previous discharge, was based on the physical understanding of catchment memory effects in rainfall-runoff processes. These variables capture the short-term hydrological response and were retained by the GP evolutionary process due to their significant contribution to model fitness.

In this method, in order to have forecasts that are close to reality, a number of appropriate parameters must be used as inputs for the simulation model. The inputs used in the model of this study include (1) Rainfall on day t, (2) Rainfall on day t-1, (3) Rainfall on day t-2, and (4) Discharge on day t-1. To avoid bias and overfitting, the dataset was partitioned such that both training and testing subsets included wet, normal, and dry hydrological years. In fact, using this data, a formula is attempted to be presented for simulating the discharge in the river with the highest compliance rate with any forecast time step. Furthermore, considering the discharge and rainfall on days t-1 and t-2 can increase simulation accuracy.

The 18 water years (6570 daily records) were split into 52% training (including wet, normal, and dry years) and 48% testing. This non-standard split (instead of common 70 − 30 or 80 − 20) was chosen to preserve the temporal distribution of flood events and to ensure that both subsets contained extreme, moderate, and low-flow conditions. A separate validation set (10% of training data) was used during evolution to monitor overfitting. The evolution was stopped after 450 generations because the RMSE on the validation set showed no improvement for 50 consecutive generations (see Fig. 2 later in Results).

Results and discussion

HEC-HMS calibration

In the calibration stage of the HEC-HMS model, the observed rainfall-runoff data are used in a systematic search to estimate the model’s parameters so that the best fit between simulation and observational results is obtained. In the calibration phase, the model is controlled in each iteration to ensure that the values of the test parameters are within an acceptable range. If the parameters are not located within the acceptable range, the HEC-HMS model will reduce the test values to a minimum or until it reaches the maximum value, and then the search continues. The term “one-way slope algorithm” refers to a simplified representation of unidirectional runoff translation along the dominant watershed slope. Within the HEC-HMS framework, this concept is consistent with lag-based runoff translation and kinematic wave assumptions, where surface flow is routed along a primary downslope path defined by basin morphology. This approach allows estimation of travel time and runoff attenuation while maintaining computational efficiency for event-based simulations. This method can evaluate and correct one parameter at a time, therefore, other parameters are assumed to be constant at that time. Additionally, achieving the minimum root mean square error (RMSE) of the peak discharge is considered the primary goal of the HEC-HMS model when being calibrated. The selected flood events represent different hydrological conditions (wet, normal, and dry years) to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of model performance. Figures 4 and 5 depict the model’s calibration allocated to flood events for November 1994 and March 1999. A comparison between the hydrographs indicates a good correlation between computational and observational hydrographs.

Initial parameter bounds were defined based on watershed physiographic characteristics, land use conditions, and empirical relationships reported in previous regional studies. Curve Number (CN) values were allowed to vary within physically realistic limits consistent with soil hydrologic groups and land cover classes. Initial abstraction and lag time were adjusted within ranges derived from geomorphologic relationships and preliminary model runs. These parameter bounds ensured physically meaningful calibration while preventing unrealistic parameter compensation. Model calibration was performed using multiple performance criteria to avoid bias toward a single hydrograph characteristic. The calibration process sought a balance between accurate peak discharge estimation, runoff volume reproduction, and hydrograph timing. Model performance was evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE), peak discharge error, and runoff volume deviation, ensuring that improvements in one performance aspect did not significantly degrade others.

As observed in Figs. 4 and 5, the observed and simulated hydrographs show reasonable visual agreement. Quantitatively, the coefficient of determination (R²) for the November 1994 event was 0.82 during calibration and 0.79 for the March 1999 event. These figures suggest that the calibration of this model has been well done, and it can be used to forecast runoff. In the current study, two steps have been considered to analyze the basin’s flood status. In the first step, the individual sub-basins elimination method has been used to identify the sub-basins with a more participatory role in the basin’s flood. All single sub-basins are deleted in this condition, and their effects are regarded in the output element. In another step and in order to perform sensitivity analysis for the initial discharge variables, concentration time and CN, the hydrologic model was run in all sub-basins several times, and the variables were changed in all sub-basins respectively in their logical domain, and their effect on peak output discharge was investigated.

The peak flood discharge has been selected to be checked for sensitivity analysis using the results of both software tools – HEC‑HMS v4.2.1 and the GP model implemented in GPTIPS v2.0 (Matlab) since it is one of the most effective flood estimation parameters. The participation portion of each sub-basin in the basin outlet flood was revealed using the method of eliminating the sub-basins and considering the routing effects on the intervals. By eliminating the individual sub-basins, the order of the effect of their elimination from the most effect on the peak outlet discharge to the least effect on the peak outlet discharge has been shown in the following: S1-S2-S3-S4 (Refer to Fig. 1). Furthermore, the order of sub-basins in peak discharge is the same. This way, it becomes clear that the elimination of the sub-basin S3 has the most decreasing effect on the peak outlet discharge of the basin. In addition, the results of the study revealed that the elimination of S2 and S4 sub-basins results in an increased outlet discharge from the basin, indicating the high water losses occurring in these two basins.

Preparing the genetic programming simulation model

In this study, the statistical data of 18 water years, including 6570 lines of data, have been used. 52% of the data have been used for training, and 48% have been used for testing the relationships resulting from genetic programming. The water years selected for training and testing the simulator model include wet, normal, and dry water years’ data from the entire dataset. To determine the optimal number of iterations for the genetic programming method, the GP algorithm was run for 1000 iterations after dividing the data from the 18-year statistical period into training and test categories (52% training and 48% testing). It was observed that after 450 iterations, the RMSE value remained almost constant and converged to a value near zero. Therefore, the optimal number of iterations for the simulator model is determined to be 450. Figure 2 illustrates the variations in RMSE for 1000 iterations. To test for overfitting, the RMSE on the training and validation sets was compared every 50 generations. The difference remained below 5% throughout, and the testing RMSE (on unseen data) was consistently within 8% of the training RMSE, indicating that the GP model did not suffer from severe overfitting. However, we acknowledge that more rigorous cross-validation (e.g., k-fold) would be beneficial; this is noted as a limitation.

According to its default settings, this simulation model initially provides formulas to interconnect the input and output data and identifies the useful and efficient parameters in the formulas from one generation to another. Discussion

Furthermore, the comparison presented here is inherently asymmetric: the GP model was trained on continuous long-term time-series data, while HEC-HMS was calibrated on selected flood events. This reflects the fundamental difference in their data requirements and calibration philosophies. GP is well-suited for watersheds with rich hydrological records but limited physiographic information, as it can extract underlying patterns directly from data. HEC-HMS, by contrast, requires both hydrological and topographic data and relies on predefined coefficients and physical parameters. Therefore, the superior performance of GP in this study should be interpreted within this specific context, and not as evidence of universal advantage. However, the primary objective of this research was to provide a controlled and consistent comparison between a data-driven genetic programming (GP) model and a conceptual HEC-HMS model under identical basin conditions. Genetic programming models are inherently data-driven and can be transferred to other watersheds provided that sufficient and representative historical datasets are available. Nevertheless, due to basin heterogeneityincluding differences in climatic conditions, land use patterns, soil characteristics, and topographic features—model retraining is generally required to preserve predictive accuracy. Therefore, while the findings of this study demonstrate the superior performance of the GP model in the Khorramabad River basin, further investigations across multiple watersheds with diverse hydrological characteristics are recommended to comprehensively evaluate the generalizability and robustness of the proposed approach. It is important to emphasize that the single‑basin scope of this study constitutes a limitation; the results should not be extrapolated to other catchments without further validation under contrasting hydrological and physiographic conditions.

In addition to predictive accuracy, model practicality and operational applicability are important factors for real-world implementation. The genetic programming (GP) model requires a computationally intensive training phase and careful selection of input variables and algorithm parameters. However, once trained, the GP model is computationally efficient and can be easily implemented for real-time runoff forecasting with minimal input data. In contrast, the HEC-HMS model benefits from physical interpretability and widespread acceptance among practitioners but requires extensive physiographic information, parameter calibration, and expert judgment at each stage of model setup. This requirement may limit its operational efficiency, particularly in data-scarce or time-sensitive applications. Therefore, while GP may involve higher initial computational effort during model development, its simplicity in operational use and strong predictive performance make it a practical alternative for short-term runoff and flood forecasting.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses are essential components of hydrological modeling, as they provide insights into model robustness and confidence in predictions. In the present study, a formal uncertainty quantification framework was not implemented, which is acknowledged as a limitation of this research. However, a partial sensitivity assessment was implicitly conducted during the calibration of the HEC-HMS model by systematically varying key hydrological parameters such as curve number (CN), basin concentration time, and initial discharge. The influence of these parameters on peak discharge was evaluated, allowing identification of the most sensitive parameters affecting model performance. For the genetic programming model, uncertainty mainly arises from data quality, selection of input variables, and stochastic evolutionary processes. Future studies are recommended to incorporate formal uncertainty analysis techniques, such as Monte Carlo simulations, GLUE framework, or bootstrap-based methods, to quantify predictive uncertainty and further evaluate model robustness.

Hydrological modeling is inherently subject to multiple sources of uncertainty arising from input data, model structure, and parameter estimation. In this study, the primary sources of uncertainty include rainfall measurement errors, spatial interpolation of precipitation using the IDW method, simplifications within the HEC-HMS model structure, and parameter equifinality during calibration. Input uncertainty is mainly related to spatial variability of rainfall and limitations in gauge density, which may affect the accuracy of sub-basin rainfall representation. Structural uncertainty originates from conceptual simplifications within the loss, transform, and routing methods applied in HEC-HMS, which may not fully capture complex watershed processes. Parametric uncertainty is associated with calibration of Curve Number, initial abstraction, lag time, and routing parameters, where multiple parameter combinations can produce similar hydrograph responses. A formal uncertainty propagation or ensemble-based analysis was beyond the scope of this study. Therefore, model results should be interpreted as deterministic scenario-based comparisons intended to evaluate the relative contribution of sub-basins rather than absolute predictive values.

In this study, a formal uncertainty quantification framework (e.g., Monte Carlo simulation, Generalized Likelihood Uncertainty Estimation [GLUE], or bootstrap‑based methods) was not implemented. This is acknowledged as a limitation. However, the main sources of uncertainty including input uncertainty (rainfall spatial variability and measurement errors), structural uncertainty (simplifications in the loss, transform, and routing methods applied in HEC‑HMS), and parametric uncertainty (equifinality during calibration) were qualitatively considered. Future studies are encouraged to adopt probabilistic approaches to better characterize predictive uncertainty and to enhance the robustness of both conceptual and data‑driven rainfall‑runoff models. To partially address model sensitivity, calibration was performed using multiple flood events and multi-objective performance criteria, which reduces parameter bias and improves robustness of the simulated hydrographs. Future studies may incorporate Monte Carlo simulation, ensemble rainfall scenarios, or global sensitivity analysis to further quantify predictive uncertainty.

Our findings are consistent with several recent studies. Rauf and Ghumman13, compared HEC-HMS and ANN on the Upper Indus River and found that the data-driven model (ANN) gave higher Nash-Sutcliffe efficiency values. Danandeh Mehr and Nourani21, reported that multi-gene genetic programming outperformed conceptual models in multi-day flow forecasting. Gelete et al.33, showed that ensemble techniques (neural network ensemble) improved model performance, which aligns with our suggestion that hybrid approaches could further enhance accuracy. Haddad31, calibrated HEC-HMS in an Algerian watershed and reported satisfactory performance, but did not compare with data-driven models. Aoulmi32, demonstrated that an ANN hybridized with improved particle swarm optimization outperformed conventional optimization techniques. Unlike these studies, our work explicitly quantifies the trade-off between data requirements and predictive performance, highlighting that GP is preferable when long-term discharge records exist but soil/land-use maps are incomplete.

Conclusion

This study compared a continuous data‑driven genetic programming (GP) model with an event‑calibrated conceptual HEC‑HMS model for rainfall‑runoff simulation in the Khorramabad River basin, Iran. The main findings are:

Performance comparison: For all four flood events, the GP model achieved lower mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) than HEC‑HMS. GP also produced smaller errors in peak discharge and flow volume.

HEC‑HMS behavior: The HEC‑HMS model, calibrated using the Clarke unit hydrograph method, provided a delayed hydrograph relative to observations, indicating that its performance may be constrained when continuous calibration data are not fully utilized.

Sub‑basin sensitivity: Sub‑basins 1 and 3 were identified as more flood‑prone than sub‑basins 2 and 4, based on the elimination analysis.

Data context: Under the specific conditions of this study—long‑term hydrological data available but detailed topographic information limited—the GP model simulated runoff more accurately with minimal input data, whereas HEC‑HMS required detailed physiographic inputs and event‑based calibration.

No universal superiority: The results do not imply that GP is universally superior; rather, each model has strengths depending on data availability and modeling objectives. HEC‑HMS remains valuable for its physical interpretability and effectiveness in flood management when comprehensive topographic and hydrological data are accessible.

Future research directions:

Extend comparative studies to a wide range of watersheds (varying in size, climate, land use, and data availability) to evaluate generalizability.

Incorporate formal uncertainty and sensitivity analyses (e.g., Monte Carlo, GLUE, bootstrap) to enhance model robustness.

Explore hybrid frameworks that couple conceptual models with data‑driven approaches to leverage both physical interpretability and predictive accuracy.
