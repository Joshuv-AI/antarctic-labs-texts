Treatment Decision-Making for Individuals with Relapsed/Refractory Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia in the USA
Authors: Catherine C. Coombs, Thomas W. LeBlanc, Katherine B. Winfree, Catherine E. Muehlenbein, Urvi Desai, Noam Y. Kirson, Ashley Holub, Alice Qu, Ellen Sears, Brian Koffman, Meghan C. Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAs available treatment options for relapsed/refractory (R/R) chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) increase, treatment decisions become increasingly complex. This cross-sectional study evaluated patient and physician decision-making when selecting treatment for R/R CLL in the USA.MethodsAfter an initial pilot, 100 individuals with R/R CLL and 100 physicians who treat R/R CLL completed web-based surveys on factors that influenced treatment selection and levels of involvement during treatment selection. Categorical measures were summarized using numbers/proportions, and continuous measures were summarized using means/standard deviations.ResultsIndividuals with R/R CLL and physicians prioritized treatment efficacy (individuals: N = 47 of 78, 60.3%; physicians: N = 79 of 100, 79.0%) and potential side effects (individuals: N = 43 of 78, 55.1%; physicians: N = 50 of 79, 63.3%) when selecting treatment. Differences in the relative importance of specific factors were observed. Financial and cost factors were important for individuals, while medical history was important for physicians. Both cohorts reported a shared decision-making process.ConclusionBuilding upon previous findings in the front-line CLL setting, this study confirms that individuals with R/R CLL and physicians commonly consider efficacy and safety of different treatment options when making their decision. Relative importance of specific factors within these and other categories differs across the samples.

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is the most common leukemia, occurring predominantly in adults over the age of 65. Approximately 23,690 people will be diagnosed with CLL in the USA in 2025 [1]. CLL is a chronic disease typically characterized by an initial indolent course until treatment is indicated [2, 3]. The approval of ibrutinib, a covalent Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (cBTKi) in 2014, followed by approvals of the cBTKi acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, have allowed for safer and more effective treatment options than chemotherapy with or without immunotherapy, but acquired resistance may develop on cBTKi therapy [4, 5]. Pirtobrutinib, a highly selective, non-cBTKi that has demonstrated promising efficacy and safety, received accelerated approval in the USA in December 2023 for the treatment of individuals with relapsed or refractory (R/R) CLL or small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) after the use of at least two prior therapies including a cBTKi and BCL-2 inhibitor [6]. Its mechanism of action addresses the limitations of cBTKi treatments such as cBTKi intolerance and acquired resistance and expands treatment options for people with heavily pretreated CLL [7]. It received full US approval in December 2025 for individuals with R/R CLL/SLL following use of a prior cBTKi [8].

While cBTKi and BCL-2 inhibitors have transformed the management of CLL, many individuals with CLL may require second or later line therapies [3]. National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend cBTKi agents such as acalabrutinib and zanubrutinib, BCL-2i agent venetoclax plus obinutuzumab, and the non-cBTKi, pirtobrutinib, as preferred options for second and subsequent lines of therapy. Other recommended agents for second or subsequent lines include venetoclax, venetoclax plus rituximab, venetoclax/ibrutinib, and ibrutinib. The guidelines note that specific clinical characteristics, such as TP53 mutation or deletion status, as well as therapies received in prior lines of therapy, may impact recommendations [5, 9]. Various dynamic factors specific to individuals with CLL may also play a role in treatment selection and include recommendations from their physician, personal or health-related factors such as comorbidities, experiences along the disease trajectory, and other factors such as disease characteristics, other health considerations, and medications [10].

Prior studies exploring the factors considered in selecting a treatment for individuals with CLL have largely focused on understanding the tradeoffs between efficacy and adverse event profiles of different treatment options [11]. While progression-free survival and overall survival are commonly ranked among the top reasons for treatment selection by both the individuals with CLL and their physicians [12–14], some differences have emerged in the perceptions and preferences related to adverse event profiles [15]. Indeed, one study found that individuals with CLL want to receive more information from physicians on efficacy and quality of life of first-line treatments [16]. Another study found that as many as 46% of physicians were surprised by general patient-physician misalignment, indicating a need to understand and improve the treatment decision-making process [17].

There is relatively little literature about patient-physician involvement in deciding on and selecting a treatment following cBTKi discontinuation for patients with R/R CLL. A recent study suggested that physicians treating CLL would like to engage their patients in shared decision-making more frequently and increase the number of educational materials provided to patients [18]. However, this study only assessed a limited number of factors related to treatment selection overall and did not specifically explore the R/R setting. Furthermore, the perspectives of individuals with R/R CLL are largely unknown with regard to what options they hear, which they consider, and which factors are most important in their decision between treatment options.

With limited evidence regarding treatment selection for patients with R/R CLL, this cross-sectional study was designed to understand the different considerations when selecting therapies for second or later lines of treatment for R/R CLL and to assess the involvement of individuals with R/R CLL in the decision-making process. Both the perspectives of individuals with R/R CLL and physicians who care for patients with CLL were evaluated.

Methods

Study Design and Participant Recruitment

This was a cross-sectional study of 100 individuals with R/R CLL and 100 physicians who treat individuals with R/R CLL. Participants were recruited from market research panels from November to December 2023 and were blinded from information about the study sponsor. The target sample sizes were based on convenience and feasibility, while allowing for an adequate sample size to observe patterns.

Eligible individuals were diagnosed with CLL or SLL and had received at least two lines of CLL therapy (i.e., R/R CLL). Prior treatment with and subsequent discontinuation of at least one cBTKi medication was also required to ensure that the cohort was reflective of the standard of care for patients receiving later lines of therapy. Individuals with R/R CLL were excluded if they were under the age of 18 or primarily resided outside the USA. Eligible physicians included those who primarily practice in the USA, have a medical specialty of hematology and/or oncology, and have cared for at least 5 patients with CLL in second or later lines of therapy during the year before the survey.

To achieve a sample that generally represents the underlying population of individuals with CLL, sampling targets were applied based on findings from Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program with 33% in each of the following age categories: younger than 64 years, 65–74 years, and ≥75 years [19]. Sample targets for race were also defined according to SEER data: 90% White, 8% Black, and 2% Asian/other [20]. As CLL is predominantly diagnosed in male patients, the goal was established to obtain a sample of approximately 60% males [21]. No sampling targets were applied for the physicians. Participants were not part of linked patient-physician dyads and were compensated for their participation in line with fair market value.

Surveys and Data Collection

Data were collected through online surveys which took individuals with R/R CLL and physicians approximately 30 min to complete. Prior to administering the surveys about treatment selection and decision-making process, self-reported demographic and clinical characteristics as well as practice characteristics (physicians only) were collected through brief online screeners to determine eligibility. All eligible participants provided consent to participate in the structured response surveys. For both sets of participants, emphasis was placed on questions designed to help clarify the decision-making process (e.g., who was involved in the process and how; medical, personal, and financial considerations; reasons for selecting a treatment, relative importance of different factors considered). The following domains of questions were explored: current and past treatment information (individuals with R/R CLL only), satisfaction and use of available treatment options (physicians only), factors considered during treatment decisions, levels of involvement in the decision-making process, and discussions with doctors about treatment (individuals with R/R CLL only). Additionally, physicians were asked to select their preferred sequence of treatments for individuals with CLL who do not have a TP53 mutation, as they progress from first-line to third-line therapy from a predefined list of treatments, contingent on their selection for the prior line. To minimize participant burden, conditional logic was used to prompt only relevant follow-up questions. As a result, the denominators for a specific category include only participants who indicated a response to a specific category. The surveys for individuals with R/R CLL and physicians were designed as separate samples and are not standardized across samples. Therefore, this study was not designed to test direct comparisons.

A small pilot investigation was conducted using a preliminary version of the survey with a small group of participants (18 individuals with R/R CLL and 15 physicians who care for individuals with R/R CLL) from May 2023 through June 2023. During the pilot, respondents were asked to complete a preliminary version of the survey using a semi-structured interview format to inform survey response options and other revisions. Interviews lasted approximately 60 min. Data collected during the pilot were used only to inform the survey development and were not considered as part of the final survey report.

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Responses to survey questions were similarly assessed. Continuous variables were summarized using means, standard deviations (SDs), medians, and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Questions which asked participants to rank considerations on a Likert scale from 0 to 10 were treated as continuous values. For the conditional sequencing questions, the denominator for each response category was calculated from the total number of individuals who received the specific question based on branching logic. Rankings of most important factors were calculated as the total proportion of individuals who ranked each item as the topmost important factor (1) to the fourth or beyond most important factor (4). All data were analyzed using R statistical software [22].

Results

Patient and Physician Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 100 individuals with R/R CLL and 100 physicians in the USA met eligibility criteria and completed the online survey (Tables 1, 2). Age, gender, and race targets defined for recruitment were achieved. Individuals with R/R CLL were of median age 68.0 (IQR: 14.3) years at survey completion, and 65.0 (IQR: 16.0) years of age at diagnosis. Individuals with R/R CLL were predominantly White (N = 90; 90.0%) and identified as non-Hispanic or Latino (N = 77; 77.0%). The sample was 60.0% (N = 60) male, and the most reported comorbidities were hypertension (N = 42; 42.0%) and heart disease (N = 20; 20.0%). Most (N = 62; 62%) individuals with R/R CLL reported prior treatment with ibrutinib; 59 (95.2%) indicated they had discontinued its use (Table 1).

Physicians were most commonly 45–54 years old (N = 45; 45.0%) and male (N = 69; 69.0%) (Table 2). The sample was predominantly White (N = 58; 58.0%) or Asian (N = 24; 24.0%); 79.0% (N = 79) were identified as non-Hispanic or Latino. Patient Survey Results

Factors Considered in Selection of Current or Most Recent Treatment for CLL

Among the main domains evaluated in the survey, individuals with R/R CLL were encouraged to select all responses that applied, and most commonly considered treatment-related characteristics of the drug regimen in selecting their current treatment (N = 78; 78%), followed by financial and cost factors (N = 53; 53.0%), other personal, demographic, and health factors (N = 45; 45.0%), and doctor’s recommendation (N = 41; 41.0%). Among those who considered treatment-related characteristics in informing their current treatment selection (N = 78), “how well the drug might work” (N = 47 of 78; 60.3%), “potential side effects” (N = 43; 55.1%), and “how the drug is taken” (N = 37; 47.4%) were the most commonly selected responses (Fig. 1a). Some individuals with R/R CLL also considered past experiences with other medications (N = 24; 24.0%), with over half (N = 13 of 24; 54.2%) considering their past experience with how long the prior medication kept their CLL under control (Fig. 1b).

Number of factors associated with each category considered by individuals with R/R during treatment selection, with rankings of most important factors. a Medication and medical characteristics considered by individuals with R/R CLL, with rankings. b Past experiences with other medications considered by individuals with R/R CLL, with rankings. c Financial and cost factors considered by individuals with R/R CLL, with rankings. d Personal, demographic, and health factors considered by individuals with R/R CLL, with rankings.

Among participants who considered financial and cost factors (N = 53; 53.0%), a majority indicated concerns around out-of-pocket costs each month (N = 42 of 53; 79.2%) (Fig. 1c). Individuals also considered how the total cost would impact their family (N = 38; 71.7%), ranking this as the most important financial factor considered (N = 22; 41.4%). Other personal, demographic, and health factors (N = 45; 45%) considered during the selection of an individual’s current or most recent treatment included age (N = 24 of 45; 53.3%) and impact on their ability to carry out daily activities (N = 19; 42.2%), with individuals ranking impact on daily activities as most important (N = 10; 22.2%) (Fig. 1d).

Involvement during the Decision-Making Process and Discussions with Their Doctor

Overall, most individuals with R/R CLL (N = 85 of 100; 85%) indicated that they would prefer a shared decision-making process with their doctor for treatment selection (Table 3). Only 15.0% (N = 15) noted they wanted to make the final decision. Notably, no individuals responded that they wanted their doctor to make the final decision without their input.

When asked specifically about their most recent treatment selection experience for CLL, individuals indicated that they felt they were involved in the decision-making process as much as they would like to be (mean rating out of 10-point scale: 7.8, SD: 1.6), and described their level of involvement as a collaborative effort between them and their provider. Table 3 describes the actions of the physicians that were most appreciated, which included explaining the reasoning for the preferred treatment as well as details such as side effects and how the treatment is given. Nearly a quarter of individuals (N = 24; 24.0%) noted that discussions around future treatment options could be addressed. Of note 29% of patients reported that physicians “answered all my questions in a way I understood.”

Physician Survey Results

Treatment Selection and Satisfaction with Treatments

A total of 62 unique treatment sequences, highlighting the complexity of the decision-making process, were observed when physicians were asked to describe their commonly selected treatment sequences for individuals with CLL who do not have a TP53 mutation, as they progress from first-line to third-line therapy. The most common class of treatment at first line was cBTKi (N = 39; 39%). Venetoclax plus anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody therapy was the most commonly selected treatment regimen at second line (N = 35; 35%), and single-agent pirtobrutinib was the most common selection for third-line treatment (N = 47; 47%) (Fig. 2). The multiple sequences available highlight the variability and complexity in treatment selection.

Treatment sequences selected by physicians for an individual with CLL without TP53 mutation.

Physicians generally reported satisfaction with current treatment options (N = 76 of 100; 76% “very satisfied” or “satisfied”). They were notably less satisfied with available treatment options following use of cBTKi and venetoclax (N = 50 of 100; 50% “very satisfied” or “satisfied”). Among those who reported dissatisfaction following cBTKi and venetoclax, the top reasons included low efficacy among available options (N = 19 of 22; 86.4%), limited availability of treatment options for next line (N = 10; 45.5%), and poor tolerability of other treatment options (N = 7; 31.8%).

Factors Considered in Treatment Selection

Similar to the individuals with R/R CLL, most physicians (N = 79 of 100; 79.0%) considered “treatment efficacy and safety” as the key factor in selecting treatment after progression on a cBTKi, followed by medical history of individuals with CLL (N = 65; 65.0%), impact on individuals’ quality of life (N = 54; 54.0%), individuals’ financial factors (N = 37; 37.0%), other treatment characteristics (e.g., mode of administration, N = 37; 37.0%), and individuals’ preferences (N = 28; 28.0%) (Fig. 3a–e).

Treatment-Specific Considerations

Among the 79.0% (N = 79) of physicians who indicated they consider treatment efficacy and safety, overall survival (N = 72 of 79; 91.1%) and progression-free survival/time to next treatment (N = 66; 83.5%) were the top selected (Fig. 3a). Likewise, treatment response rates were also commonly considered (N = 57; 72.2%), while potential adverse events (N = 50; 63.3%), symptom reduction or patient-reported outcomes (N = 41; 51.9%), and time to response (N = 25; 31.6%) were considered by fewer physicians. Among the physicians who considered adverse events (N = 50 of 79; 63.3%), arrhythmia (N = 41 of 50; 82.0%), high-grade bleeding risks (N = 36; 72.0%), immunosuppression or infection (N = 33; 66.0%), and cytopenias (N = 32; 64.0%) were of greatest concern.

Among the physicians who indicated they consider additional treatment characteristics when selecting it for individuals following progression on a cBTKi (N = 37; 37.0%), a wider distribution of attributes was reported. Over half of respondents identified frequency of treatment (N = 21 of 37; 56.8%), followed by fixed or limited duration of treatment (N = 18; 48.6%), route of administration (N = 18; 48.6%), and mechanism of action for a new treatment versus prior treatment (N = 16; 43.2%) (Fig. 3b).

Patient-Specific Considerations

Medical history of their patients was also commonly considered by the physician during treatment selection (N = 65; 65.0%), with 78.5% (N = 51 of 65) indicating they consider comorbidities, 70.8% (N = 46) considering del(17p)/TP53 mutation, and 63.1% (N = 41) considering fitness (Fig. 3c). In relative terms, however, more physicians prioritized the del(17p)/TP53 mutation status (N = 13), followed by the disease stage (N = 11) and then comorbidities (N = 9) and disease characteristics (N = 9). Similarly, over half of physicians (N = 54; 54.0%) reported that they consider impact on the individual’s quality of life, with almost all of these physicians considering impact on activities of daily living (N = 50 of 54; 92.6%) (Fig. 3d).

Approximately a third of the physicians reported considering financial factors of individuals with R/R CLL when making treatment decisions (N = 37; 37.0%), primarily the monthly out-of-pocket costs (N = 31 of 37; 83.8%) (Fig. 3e).

Levels of Involvement and Discussions with Patients

Most physicians reported their patients as involved in the decision-making process (N = 85; 85.0%), though less than half (N = 41 of 85; 48.2%) indicated that their patients were “very involved” (Table 4). Physicians believed that their patients generally want their doctor to make decisions about their treatments together (N = 30; 35.3%) or want their doctor to make the final decision while considering their preferences (N = 30; 35.3%), indicating a process aligned with shared decision-making, corroborating with insights provided by individuals with R/R CLL. Additionally, when asked to rate the comfort level of their patients in bringing up treatment-related concerns, physicians provided an average rating of 7.7 (SD = 1.7) on a scale of 0 (not at all comfortable) to 10 (extremely comfortable), further highlighting a collaborative disease management approach.

Discussion

This study provides evidence related to the treatment decision-making process in the post-cBTKi setting from the perspectives of individuals with R/R CLL and physicians who treat CLL. Overall, this study reported that both individuals with R/R CLL and the physicians who care for individuals with R/R CLL dynamically consider a variety of factors in selecting an appropriate treatment following progression on cBTKi therapy.

All participants – individuals with R/R CLL and physicians alike – prioritize treatment efficacy. In addition, both cohorts independently indicated that various treatment-related considerations played a significant role in their decision about selecting the appropriate treatment for R/R CLL. However, there were several non-efficacy treatment-related attributes that emerged in surveys of individuals with R/R CLL and physicians. Individuals with R/R CLL commonly worried about side effects that impact day-to-day life such as fatigue or rashes and burden on caregivers. Physicians noted considering severe side effects such as arrhythmias and high-grade bleeding risks. Over half of individuals with R/R CLL and approximately one-third of physicians also considered financial factors. These findings are in line with other studies that have reported that a wide range of factors play a role in treatment selection including prior treatments and side effects. Preferences of individuals with R/R CLL and comorbidities may also be considered and may be prioritized differently than during front-line selection [3, 23, 24].

All participants also independently highlighted the lack of effective treatment options at later lines of therapy and willingness to try newer therapies. Although physicians were largely satisfied with treatment options available following progression on cBTKi, satisfaction following progression on cBTKi and BCL-2i was notably lower. This suggests the need for additional effective, safe, and accessible treatment options in later lines of therapy. This need was further supported by the selection of hypothetical treatment sequences, where nearly half of all physicians surveyed selected pirtobrutinib, only approved for CLL shortly after study completion, as an option for third-line treatment. These findings are consistent with prior literature highlighting the lack of available treatments for individuals with CLL following cBTKi discontinuation and underscore the need for better treatment options for these individuals [24, 25]. It should be noted that much of the data collection for this study was conducted prior to the approval of pirtobrutinib and liso-cel chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy for use following prior cBTKi and BCL-2i in R/R CLL [26], which may have had an impact on perceived satisfaction and treatment selection in this setting.

With regard to patient-physician interactions, individuals with R/R CLL were satisfied with their physician’s approach to treatment selection. Satisfaction was reported as appreciation for how the physician explained the selection rationale and involved them in the process to the extent they preferred. Prior studies reported only 2% of individuals with CLL preferred their doctor select their treatment, 13.6% of individuals perceived that the physician made final decision, and no physicians perceived making the final decision, thus indicating a potential mismatch in communication during the shared decision-making process [27]. The results of the current study suggest shared decision-making is preferred by patients in this difficult-to-treat population, which may improve overall patient experience. One notable gap highlighted by these data is that only 29% of patients reported that their physician “answered all my questions in a way I understood.” This highlights an unmet need for improved communication during treatment discussions with patients and physicians. Future studies should explore such communication gaps and determine areas for improvement.

This study should be interpreted considering its strengths and limitations. Relatively little is known about the insights and perspectives of individuals with R/R CLL and physicians with regard to their considerations during later lines of treatment selection. No studies have specifically studied the post-cBTKi R/R CLL setting. The current study assessed these views in large samples; however, it enrolled participants from market research panels, which may result in selection bias. For instance, the median age at diagnosis of individuals with R/R CLL included in this study was 65.0 years, while the median age at diagnosis for the overall population of individuals with CLL is 72.0 years [28]. Therefore, insights reflected in these results may not be representative of all individuals with R/R CLL. Additionally, participants enrolled in research panels may have greater awareness of their health or condition relative to the general population, consistent with selection or volunteer bias [29]. Furthermore, the sample may not fully represent the broader R/R CLL population given the inclusion criterion requiring prior exposure to at least one cBTKi. Additionally, the relatively short median interval of approximately 3 years between diagnosis and survey completion may suggest a skew toward individuals with more aggressive disease and not reflect individual patient progression trajectories.

Similarly, physicians who are part of market research panels may be more aware of emerging treatment options. For example, physician responses to hypothetical treatment sequences for an individual with CLL without TP53 mutation indicated that many would select pirtobrutinib as the third-line selection (N = 47; 47.0%). Given FDA approval of pirtobrutinib for CLL occurred in December 2023, this selection indicates that the physician respondents were aware of novel treatment options like pirtobrutinib, which was previously approved for individuals with R/R mantle cell lymphoma in January 2023. Thus, some physicians were potentially considering its use off-label in 3L CLL [30] given the unmet need of treatment options available at the time the survey was conducted. Of note, since the time of survey administration, del(17p) status has become an important clinical characteristic in addition to TP53 mutation; however, it was not detailed for consideration in the hypothetical scenario.

All measures were self-reported; thus, recall (particularly around prior treatments or decisions) or social desirability bias is possible. Furthermore, physician practice patterns in the clinical setting may be driven by patient-specific factors not captured through this survey, which possibly may have emulated hypothetical scenarios rather than real-life decision-making in the absence of complete clinical information for individual patients. Likewise, physician practice may be influenced by their practice setting which may drive treatment selection. For example, this study observed a lower number of physicians from the nonacademic hospital setting who may utilize different treatments or serve different patient populations. Finally, as this study utilized separate surveys and samples for individuals with R/R CLL and physicians, no direct comparisons can be made.

Conclusions

Building upon previous findings in the front-line CLL setting, this study confirms that individuals with R/R CLL and physicians commonly consider efficacy and safety of different treatment options when making their decision. Furthermore, this study identified mode of administration and duration of treatment as well as individual characteristics such as presence of comorbidities, financial considerations, and prior experience with treatments for CLL in treatment decision-making in the R/R setting. Physicians routinely involve individuals with R/R CLL in the decision-making process, resulting in a positive patient experience. Future research evaluating how to further encourage these discussions could be beneficial to everyone involved in the process, including the individual with R/R CLL, the physician, and others involved in the care journey.
