New high-precision b b , c c , and s s masses from pseudoscalar-pseudoscalar correlators in n f = 4 n_{f}=4 lattice QCD
Brian Colquhoun, Christine T. H. Davies, Daniel Hatton, G. Peter Lepage, HPQCD Collaboration

Abstract
We extend an earlier lattice QCD analysis of heavy-quark current-current correlators to obtain new values for the MS ¯ \mathrm{\overline{MS}} masses of the b b , c c , and s s quarks. The analysis uses gluon configurations from the MILC collaboration with vacuum polarization contributions from u u , d d , s s , and c c quarks ( n f = 4 n_{f}=4 ), and lattice spacings down to 0.032 fm. We find that m ¯ b ​ ( m ¯ b , n f = 5 ) = 4.1911 ​ ( 62 ) \overline{m}_{b}(\overline{m}_{b},n_{f}=5)=4.1911(62) GeV, m ¯ c ​ ( 3 ​ GeV , n f = 4 ) = 0.9808 ​ ( 33 ) \overline{m}_{c}(3~\mathrm{GeV},n_{f}=4)=0.9808(33) GeV, and m ¯ s ​ ( 3 ​ GeV , n f = 4 ) = 83.34 ​ ( 26 ) \overline{m}_{s}(3~\mathrm{GeV},n_{f}=4)=83.34(26) MeV. These results are corrected for QED by including (quenched) QED in the simulations. They are among the most accurate values by any method to date. We give a detailed analysis of finite lattice-spacing errors that shows why the HISQ discretization of the quark action is particularly useful for b b -quark simulations even for lattices where a ​ m b ≈ 1 am_{b}\approx 1 . We also calculate QED and isospin corrections to the (fictitious) η s \eta_{s} -meson mass, which is used to tune s s -quark masses in lattice simulations.

Introduction

Accurate values for heavy-quark masses are important for QCD phenomenology and indispensable for high-precision studies of the Higgs particle
Lepage et al. 2014
;
Freitas et al. 2019
;
de Blas et al. 2020

The

High-precision lattice simulations involving

II.2
Finite lattice-spacings errors

There are three sources of finite lattice-spacing errors:
am_{h}
errors coming from the heavy-quark propagators;
ap
errors, where
p
is of order the heavy-quark three-momentum, coming from both quark and gluon propagators; and
a\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}
errors, where
\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}\approx 1
GeV, associated with nonperturbative scales. The
am_{h}
errors are seemingly the most concerning since
am_{h}
gets as large as 1.21 in our analysis (on the coarsest lattice). This suggests lattice-spacing errors of order

(m_{h}/\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}})^{2}=(am_{h}/\pi)^{2}\approx 15\%,

(14)

where
\Lambda_{\mathrm{UV}}=\pi/a
is the ultraviolet cutoff on the lattice. While such errors arise for small
n
,
am_{h}
errors are suppressed by a factor of
(p/m_{h})^{4}\approx(v/c)^{4}
as
n
increases and
p/m_{h}
decreases (Fig.
1
). This factor reduces a 15% error to around 0.5% by
n=14
, at which point
am_{h}
errors are negligible relative to the few-percent
(ap/\pi)^{2}
and
(a\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}/\pi)^{2}
errors. Thus we expect large,
m_{h}
-dependent errors at small
n
to be rapidly replaced as
n
increases by
ap
and
a\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}
errors that are much less dependent on
m_{h}
.

The strong suppression of
am_{h}
errors in the nonrelativistic limit is a feature of the HISQ discretization of the quark action used in the simulations. The nonrelativistic behavior
of any relativistic quark lattice action can be described by a continuum (Euclidean) effective field theory of the form
El-Khadra et al. 1997
;
Lepage 1992

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\psi^{\dagger}\Big(D_{t}+m_{1}-\frac{{\bf D}^{2}}{2m_{2}}+\mathcal{O}\big(m(v/c)^{4}\big)\Big)\psi,

(15)

where usually
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are quite different when
am_{h}
is large. Then the nonrelativistic quark’s dispersion relation, relating its energy to its three-momentum
{\bf p}
(in lowest-order perturbation theory),

E({\bf p})=m_{1}+\frac{{\bf p}^{2}}{2m_{2}}\big(1+\mathcal{O}({\bf p}^{2}/m^{2})\big),

(16)

is wrong. In particular, the quark’s “speed of light”
c({\bf p})
has the wrong
limit as
{\bf p}\to 0
:

c^{2}({\bf p})\equiv\frac{E^{2}({\bf p})-E^{2}(0)}{{{\bf p}^{2}}}\to\frac{m_{1}}{m_{2}}\neq 1.

(17)

The HISQ action is different because it is defined so that the speed of light squared
c^{2}({\bf p})\to 1
as
{\bf p}\to 0
for any value of
am_{h}
. This implies that
m_{1}=m_{2}\equiv m_{h}^{\mathrm{eff}}
for the HISQ action no matter how large
am_{h}
is, and therefore HISQ’s nonrelativistic effective Lagrangian is exact up to corrections suppressed by
(v/c)^{4}
. Consequently the HISQ action is useful for much larger values of
am_{h}
than most other discretizations, particularly for nonrelativistic systems.

II.3
Lattice-spacing uncertainty

Lattice simulations produce quark and meson masses in lattice units:
for example, heavy-quark masses
am_{h}
, and the masses
am_{\eta_{h}}
for the corresponding pseudoscalar mesons. To be useful, these must be converted to physical units (GeV), which is done by multiplying by
1/a
. This suggests that the fractional accuracy of lattice results for masses in physical units is limited by the fractional accuracy with which we can determine the lattice spacing (here, about 0.5%). In fact, however,
b
-quark masses can be far more accurate than the lattice spacing.

We need the lattice
b
mass
m_{b}
in physical units (GeV) to determine the
\mathrm{\overline{MS}}
mass
\overline{m}_{b}(\mu_{n})
using Eq. (
7
). We obtain
m_{b}
by calculating the pseudoscalar-meson masses
am_{\eta_{h}}
in simulations for a variety of heavy-quark masses
am_{h}
in the vicinity of the
b
mass, and then interpolating to find the quark mass that corresponds to the experimental value for the
\eta_{b}
’s mass
Navas et al. 2024
:

m_{\eta_{b}}^{\mathrm{expt}}=9.3987(20)(10)~\mathrm{GeV},

(18)

where we add a second uncertainty to account for the contribution to
m_{\eta_{b}}

from
b\bar{b}
annihilation, which is not included in the simulations
Hatton et al. 2021b
.

The first step in such an analysis is to convert the lattice values
for
am_{h}
and
am_{\eta_{h}}
to physical units by multiplying by the inverse lattice spacing. This introduces fractional errors into
m_{h}
and
m_{\eta_{h}}
that are equal to those of
1/a
, but, for heavy quarks like the
b
, this uncertainty is strongly suppressed by the interpolation used to obtain the final quark mass
Chakraborty et al. 2015
.

The suppression occurs because the
\eta_{b}
mass is twice the
b
-quark mass plus much smaller binding corrections. This means that
m_{\eta_{b}}\approx 2m_{b}
. If the proportionality was exact, such that
m_{\eta_{h}}=Cm_{h}
for some constant
C
, we could determine the
b
-quark mass from lattice data without knowing the lattice spacing:
m_{b}=m_{\eta_{b}}^{\mathrm{expt}}/C
where
C=(am_{\eta_{h}})/(am_{h})
. Insofar as the proportionality is not exact, dependence on the lattice spacing is suppressed, but not removed (see Appendix A.1 in
Chakraborty et al. 2015
). As we shall see, the fractional error in
m_{b}
is about a fifth that of
1/a
. Note that the fractional error in
am_{b}
, therefore, is of order that in the lattice spacing.

The dependence upon the lattice spacing is further reduced by converting the lattice mass to an
\mathrm{\overline{MS}}
mass
using Eq. (
10
), where
\rho_{n}\equiv\mu_{n}/\overline{m}_{h}(\mu)
is given in Table
1
.
We find that uncertainties coming from the lattice spacing are anti-correlated between the mass
\overline{m}_{b}(\mu_{n})
and the scale
\mu_{n}
, which means that they tend to cancel as the mass is evolved to obtain
\overline{m}_{b}(\overline{m}_{b})
. As a result of this second level of suppression, the final value for
\overline{m}_{b}(\overline{m}_{b})
is largely unaffected by the uncertainty in the lattice spacing.

The largest systematic errors that arise when using Eq. (

1.

We first fit the

for each ensemble separately. In doing so we correct for systematic errors in the lattice values for the

and in the perturbation theory for the

values given in Table
2
.

2.

The four

mass used to determine the bare
b

We fit results for the reduced moments from each gluon ensemble

\displaystyle R_{n}^{(s)}(m_{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{b}}{\overline{m}_{b}^{(s)}(\mu_{n})}\,r_{n}\big(\overline{\alpha}_{s}(\xi_{\alpha}\mu_{n}),\rho_{n}\big)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg(1+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{a}}c_{j}^{(n,s)}(m_{h}/m_{b})\,\Big(\frac{a\Lambda_{n}(m_{h})}{\pi}\Big)^{2j}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\times\big(1+d_{n}^{\mathrm{pth}}\,(m_{c}/m_{h})^{2}\big),

(25)

where

\mu_{n}=\rho_{n}\frac{am_{h}}{am_{b}}\,\overline{m}_{b}^{(s)}(\mu_{n})

(26)

We fit

\displaystyle\xi_{\alpha}=1

\displaystyle+g_{\alpha}\frac{\delta m_{uds}^{\mathrm{sea}}}{m_{s}}+g_{a^{2},\alpha}\frac{\delta m_{uds}^{\mathrm{sea}}}{m_{s}}\Big(\frac{m_{c}}{\pi/a}\Big)^{2}

g_{\alpha}=0.082(8)\quad g_{a^{2},\alpha}=0.0(1)\quad g_{c,\alpha}=0.0(1).

\displaystyle\mu^{2}\frac{d\overline{\alpha}_{s}(\mu)}{d\mu^{2}}=-\overline{\alpha}_{s}^{2}(\mu)\sum_{j=0}^{4}\beta_{j}(n_{f})\,\overline{\alpha}_{s}^{j}(\mu).

II.5.2
a\Lambda_{n}
errors

The largest errors in the lattice values for
R_{n}^{(s)}
are from the finite lattice spacings used in the simulations. As discussed in Sec.
II.2
, these are dominated by errors of order
(ap/\pi)^{2j}
where
p
is the typical three momentum of the valence heavy-quarks. We correct for this error by including a factor

1+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{a}}c_{j}^{(n,s)}(m_{h}/m_{b})\,\Big(\frac{a\Lambda_{n}(m_{h})}{\pi}\Big)^{2j}

(35)

in the fit, where

\Lambda_{n}(m_{h})\approx p\approx m_{h}\,\frac{3.15}{n^{0.75}}

(36)

based on the results shown in Fig.
1
.

We write the coefficients as a sum of two functions

c_{j}^{(n,s)}(m_{h}/m_{b})\equiv c_{0j}^{(n)}(m_{h}/m_{b})+\delta c_{j}^{(n,s)}(m_{h}/m_{b}).

(37)

The first function
c_{0j}^{(n)}(m_{h}/m_{b})
is common to all gluon ensembles. The second function allows for non-analytic dependence on the lattice spacing (for example,
\overline{\alpha}_{s}(\pi/a)
corrections) and also such things as small differences in the sea-quark masses; it can differ from gluon ensemble to ensemble. These functions are implemented as monotonic splines
Lepage 2025a
with knots at

x_{k}\in\{0.65,0.85,1.05\}.

(38)

where
x\equiv m_{h}/m_{b}
.
The values of the functions at the knots are fit parameters with (uncorrelated) priors

\displaystyle c_{0j}^{(n)}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=0(1)

(39)

\displaystyle\delta c_{j}^{(n,s)}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=0.0(5).

(40)

The Empirical Bayes criterion suggests priors that are half as wide as these, which results in a good fit and an uncertainty that is reduced by 16%. We will use the wider prior here to be conservative.

The knots are uniformly distributed across the range of values for
m_{h}/m_{b}
in our analysis. Fitting with just two knots does not give good fits (
\chi^{2}
too large). Fitting with four knots gives the same results for the
b
mass as from three knots, but with a much smaller Bayes Factor.

We include only
a^{2}
and
a^{4}
corrections (
N_{a}=2
). Adding further terms has no effect on our final results.

II.5.3
Nonzero
m_{c}

As mentioned above, the perturbative coefficients
r_{n2}
and
r_{n3}
in Table

u

d
, and
s
quarks, there could be appreciable corrections, of order
r_{n2}\,\overline{\alpha}_{s}^{2}(\mu_{n})

, for the
c
quark. We allow for such corrections by including a factor

1+d^{\mathrm{pth}}_{n}(m_{c}/m_{h})^{2}

(41)

in the fit formula, where fit parameter
d^{\mathrm{pth}}_{n}
has prior
0.00(5)
. This factor has no effect on our final results, even if the prior is replaced by
0(1)
.

II.5.4
Nonperturbative and finite-volume effects

There are nonperturbative corrections to perturbative expression for the reduced moments (Eq. (
7
)) coming from gluon and light-quark condensates, but these are suppressed by a factor of
1/m_{h}^{4}
. Condensates were negligible (less than 0.05%) in our earlier determination of the
c
-quark mass
Chakraborty et al. 2015
; they should be much smaller here since the quark masses are 2–3 times larger. We verified this by repeating the present analysis with and without condensates; they make no difference to the final results.

Finite-volume errors can enter through the condensates, but again these are negligible in the
c
-mass analysis (less than 0.01%) and would be much smaller here because of the larger quark masses.

\displaystyle\overline{m}_{b}^{(s)}(\overline{m}_{b}^{(s)})=\,\,

IV
QED Corrections

Lattice simulations show that QED corrections to the
c
-quark’s
\mathrm{\overline{MS}}
mass
\overline{m}_{c}(3\;\mathrm{GeV})
are about
-0.18
%
Hatton et al. 2020a
.
This result represents the dominant QED effect which arises from the electric charge of the valence quarks. It was determined using quenched QED calculations in which sea quarks are taken to be electrically neutral. The QED correction is defined as the shift of the quark mass required to keep the calibration meson mass the same in QCD and QCD+QED. The QED corrections combine self-energy and QED quark-antiquark interaction contributions which have opposite sign for electrically neutral mesons. For charmonium mesons, the self-energy term is larger and the combined effect is to raise the meson mass at fixed quark mass. For bottomonium each QED contribution is reduced by the smaller electric charge and there is a stronger cancellation as the interaction contribution is relatively larger
Hatton et al. 2020b
. We therefore expect a much smaller QED correction to the b-quark mass.

The corrections for the
b
-quark mass were analyzed in
Hatton et al. 2021a
. Eqs. (28) and (33) from that paper can be combined to obtain the shift
\delta\overline{m}_{b}
due to QED in the
b
-quark’s
\mathrm{\overline{MS}}
mass at
\mu=3
GeV:
7
7

7

In the notation of
Hatton et al. 2021a
, this ratio is given by
R(\overline{m}_{b}/\overline{m}_{c},Q=1/3)
times
1+\big(R(\overline{m}_{c}(3),Q_{c}=0\to 2/3)-1\big)/4
.

\frac{\delta\overline{m}_{b}(3~\mathrm{GeV},n_{f}=4)}{\overline{m}_{b}(3~\mathrm{GeV},n_{f}=4)}\Bigg|_{\mathrm{QED}}=-0.00007(10),

(53)

or about
-0.01(1)
%. Combining this result with our pure QCD result from Eq. (

(54)

The only other lattice QCD determination of the

Coming down from the
