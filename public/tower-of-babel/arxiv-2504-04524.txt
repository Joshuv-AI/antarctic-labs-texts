Trust Region Preference Approximation: A simple and stable reinforcement learning algorithm for LLM reasoning
Xuerui Su, Shufang Xie, Guoqing Liu, Yingce Xia, Renqian Luo, Peiran Jin, Zhiming Ma, Yue Wang, Zun Wang, Yuting Liu

Abstract
Inference scaling further accelerates Large Language Models (LLMs) toward Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), with large-scale Reinforcement Learning (RL) to unleash long Chain-of-Thought reasoning. Reward-based optimization algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have achieved significant performance on reasoning tasks, whereas preference-based optimization algorithms such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) significantly improve the performance of LLMs on human alignment. However, despite the strong performance of reward-based optimization methods in alignment tasks , they remain vulnerable to reward hacking. Furthermore, preference-based algorithms (e.g. Online DPO) haven’t yet matched the performance of reward-based optimization algorithms (like PPO) on reasoning tasks, making their exploration in this specific area still a worthwhile pursuit. Motivated by these challenges, we propose the Trust Region Preference Approximation (TRPA) algorithm, which integrates rule-based optimization with preference-based optimization for reasoning tasks. As a preference-based algorithm, TRPA naturally eliminates the reward hacking issue. TRPA constructs preference levels using predefined rules, forms corresponding preference pairs, and leverages a novel optimization algorithm for RL training with a theoretical monotonic improvement guarantee. Experimental results demonstrate that TRPA not only achieves competitive performance on reasoning tasks but also exhibits robust stability. The code of this paper are released and updating on https://github.com/XueruiSu/Trust-Region-Preference-Approximation.git .

3
Method

We propose the Trust Region Preference Approximation (TRPA) algorithm for stable RL training, which leverages the technical routes of rule-based and preference-based optimization.

3.1
Trust Region Preference Approximation (TRPA)

Trust Region Preference Approximation (TRPA) is a preference-based optimization method based on the rule-based optimization scheme. See Figure
1
for the training process of TRPA. The TRPA algorithm utilizes a predefined rule to classify the multiple responses generated by LLM into different preference levels. The preference level represents the degree to which the corresponding response is preferred based on our predefined rule. Then we construct preference pairs between responses which have the same prompt but different preference levels. Finally, these preference pairs are used for optimization under the loss function of the TRPA algorithm. Given the reference policy
\pi_{ref}(y|x)
and the policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
which is the policy before the current optimization step and used for generating data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
, the TRPA algorithm trains the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
under the following loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{TRPA}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }}\right)=-

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,y_{1},y_{2}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta(y_{1})\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{1}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\text{ref }}\left(y_{1}\mid x\right)}-\beta(y_{1})\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{2}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\text{ref }}\left(y_{2}\mid x\right)}\right)\right]

(5)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)||\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x))}\right],

where
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}\triangleq\{(x,y_{1},y_{2})|x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x),(y_{1}\succ y_{2})\sim p^{*}(1|y_{1},y_{2},x)\}
,
\lambda
is a hyper-parameter and
\beta(y_{1})
is the KTPO coefficient which will be discussed in Section
3.2
.➂.

3.2
Technical Details

Building on the principles of preference-based optimization, we develop several key techniques tailored to the TRPA framework to support more effective reasoning and planning in LLMs.

➀
KL Regularization.
Maintaining controlled update steps facilitates smoother convergence during training. TRPA incorporates the constraint

{\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)||\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x))}
to effectively leverage the benefits of trust region principle. By limiting the KL divergence between successive policies, TRPA ensures gradual updates, thereby preventing abrupt changes that could destabilize the training process.

➁
Definition of Rule and Preference Levels.
We adopt the same reward categorization rule as the rule-based optimization algorithms, such as Logic RL
[
14
]
and DeepScaleR
[
26
]
. Specifically, we define four preference levels to evaluate LLM-generated responses, ranging from Level 4 (lowest) to Level 1 (highest), based on both formatting and factual correctness.

Preference Levels

\bullet
Preference Level 4: Responses that violate basic formatting requirements. Specifically, we require responses to follow the standard Chain-of-Thought (CoT) format: the reasoning process and final answer must be enclosed in
<think>...</think>
and
<answer>...</answer>
tags, respectively. For math-related problems, the final answer should be wrapped in
\\boxed{}
.

\bullet
Preference Level 3: Responses that meet formatting requirements but exhibit incomplete or illogical reasoning, indicating a partial understanding of the expected format.

\bullet
Preference Level 2: Responses that are correctly formatted but still contain factual error.

\bullet
Preference Level 1: Responses that are both well-formatted and factually correct.

Here we explain why we choose the BT model
[
23
]
instead of listwise ranking models (e.g.
[
27
]
). In our experiments, we observe that rollouts under the same prompt typically yield outputs falling into two adjacent preference levels, supporting the assumption of binary comparisons of the BT model.

➂
Kahneman-Tversky Preference Optimization (KTPO).
To better guide the optimization process, we introduce the KTPO technique, inspired by Prospect Theory (Kahneman–Tversky model)
[
28
]
, which suggests that humans exhibit asymmetric sensitivity to gains and losses. Motivated by this asymmetry, we design the hyperparameter
\beta(y)
to be anisotropic, assigning larger values when
y
corresponds to a response of higher preference level (e.g., Level 1). Let
\beta
be the base hyperparameter and
N
be the Kahneman–Tversky factor, then

\beta(y)=\begin{cases}N\cdot\beta,&\text{if }y\text{ belongs to Preference Level 1},\\
\beta,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6)

TRPA introduces the adaptive hyperparameter
\beta
(i.e. KTPO) for the first time in the LLMs Reasoning tasks. Experimental results show that this method can significantly improve model performance. It is worth noting that in the Human Alignment tasks, there are a series of preference optimization algorithms (such as WPO
[
29
]
, Adaptive DPO
[
30
]
, NS-DPO
[
31
]
,
\beta
-DPO
[
32
]
, KTO
[
33
]
, etc.) that follow the idea of anisotropy to improve the algorithm performance through the design of
\beta
. Given the focus of this study, we did not conduct a detailed horizontal comparison of existing methods, but their ideas have certain commonalities with this study.

➃
Prompt-wised Optimization Strategy.
In both the early and late stages of optimization, LLMs often generate responses with identical preference levels (or function values) under the same prompt. This typically arises from undertraining in the early phase and overtraining or convergence in the later phase. For example, in GRPO
[
15
]
, this phenomenon leads to normalized rewards being zero, which in turn causes the gradient of the loss function to vanish, rendering the optimization ineffective. In contrast, the TRPA algorithm naturally accommodates such scenarios through a Prompt-wised Optimization Strategy: when all responses have a preference level of 1, they are treated as
y_{1}
and the corresponding
y_{2}
terms are omitted during optimization; conversely, when all responses share a non-1 but identical preference level, they are treated as
y_{2}
, and the
y_{1}
terms are excluded accordingly.

4
Theorem

In this section, we will perform a theoretical analysis of why TRPA ensures a Monotonic Improvement Guarantee towards the target distribution
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
. And in contrast, Online DPO
[
24
]
does not have this guarantee and does not take
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
as the target distribution, even though this algorithm has a very similar loss function to TRPA. In Section
4.1
, we introduce the concept of the Posterior Boltzmann Approximation (PBA) algorithm, which takes
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
as the target distribution, and prove the Online DPO algorithm is not a PBA algorithm, but the PPO and PA algorithms are.
In section
4.2
, we demonstrate that compared to the PA algorithm, which is a PBA algorithm but has a loss function that is difficult to estimate, the TRPA algorithm has both the monotonic improvement guarantee of the PA algorithm and the loss function of the TRPA algorithm is easier to estimate.

4.1
Posterior Boltzmann Approximation Algorithm

Definition 4.1
.

An algorithm qualifies as a Posterior Boltzmann Approximation (PBA) algorithm if and only if its loss function employs
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
as the target distribution:

{\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right),\quad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right).}

(7)

\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
is exactly the optimal solution to the KL-constrained reward maximization objective of PPO
[
12
]
and the DPO objective described in
[
8
]
. However, we prove that the trivial online extension of DPO, namely Online DPO, does not satisfy the definition of a PBA method, indicating that designing an online algorithm for DPO is a non-trivial task. In contrast, we propose that the PA algorithm is a more suitable solution to construct online preference optimization algorithms.

Lemma 4.2
.

The Online DPO algorithm is not a PBA algorithm.

Lemma 4.3
.

The PA algorithm is a PBA algorithm.

Lemma

With straightforward algebraic derivation, we have that the correspondence of the Online DPO and PA algorithms with cross-entropy and KL divergence loss functions, as expressed in Eq.

and Eq.

4.2
Monotonic Improvement Guarantee of TRPA for the PA algorithm

Although as a PBA algorithm, the PA algorithm has good properties on the target distribution, the PA algorithm still faces a challenge: the
M(x,y_{1},y_{2})
term is hard to calculate.
M(x,y_{1},y_{2})=\sum_{z=0,1}p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)\log p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)
is constructed by the ground truth preference distribution
p^{*}
which is usually implicit in the preference data. In practice,
M(x,y_{1},y_{2})
is usually difficult to estimate accurately. Therefore, in this section, we propose to use the TRPA algorithm which has a simpler loss function as a practical algorithm from these theoretical foundations. At the same time, we provide a monotonic improvement guarantee for the PA algorithm to ensure the PBA algorithm properties of the TRPA algorithm by Theorem
4.4
. See Appendix
B.3
for proof.

Theorem 4.4
.

Denote

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{1},y_{2})\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\text{old}}}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}\left(x,y_{1},y_{2}\right)\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}\left[M(x,y_{1},y_{2})\right].

(11)

Then we have the following inequality hold:

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})\leq

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})+\lambda\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}||\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})},

(12)

where
\lambda=4(\bar{U}_{r}+2\log 2)
and
\bar{U}_{r}=\max_{x,y_{1},y_{2}}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||\bar{p}_{\theta_{\text{new}}}(z|x,y_{1},y_{2}))
.

We briefly describe the relationship between the TRPA algorithm and Theorem
4.4
.
First, the
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)}\left[M(x,y_{1},y_{2})\right]
term of
L^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})
is omitted as it is irrelevant to
\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}
. Second, when
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}||\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})\rightarrow 0
, the
\sqrt{\mathrm{D}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}||\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})}
term’s smooth coefficient will tend to
\infty
. Therefore, we use
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\max}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}||\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})
as the trust region regularization item of TRPA. Finally, we get the final loss function of Eq.
5
. The two changes are guaranteed by Theorem
4.4
to ensure the monotonic improvement guarantee of TRPA about
\bar{L}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}})
.

Ablation Study

In the following content, we analyze the effectiveness of the TRPA algorithm and its related techniques through a series of ablation experiments. We mainly focus on the following questions: ➀ Q1: How does TRPA perform differently compared to other works? ➁ Q2: What benefits does the KL divergence used by TRPA bring compared to Online DPO? ➂ Q3: The trends of Response Length and Entropy as RL training progresses.
➃ Q4: The role of KTPO technique. ➄ Q5: Does TRPA have the simultaneous change problem that preference optimization usually faces?

RQ1: How does TRPA perform differently compared to GRPO?
In Figure
3
, the blue and pink solid lines denote the performance metrics of the TRPA and GRPO algorithms, respectively. As shown in Figure
3
(a), TRPA eventually converges to a performance level comparable to
o3-mini-high
(blue dashed line), while GRPO performs worse, reaching only the level of
DeepSeek R1
(orange dashed line). For further analysis of response length and entropy, please refer to
RQ3
.

RQ2: What benefits does the KL divergence used by TRPA bring compared to Online DPO?
Compared to Online DPO, TRPA demonstrates higher efficiency. In Figure
3
, the green solid line represents the results of Online DPO. Throughout training, TRPA consistently maintains a lower response length. This advantage allows TRPA to achieve the same training objectives with fewer tokens and reduced computational overhead. Thus, with monotonic improvement guarantee, TRPA exhibits greater optimization efficiency than Online DPO. Additionally, the lower response length contributes to higher token efficiency in the final deployed TRPA-based LLMs.

Figure 3:
Comparison of TRPA (Blue), TRPA w/oKTPO (Purple), Online DPO (Green) and GRPO (Pink) in terms of (a) Accuracy, (b) Response Length, and (c) Entropy (Response Length and Entropy are averaged by sliding window = 400).

RQ3: The trends of Response Length and Entropy as RL training progresses.

Consistent with
[
26
]
, the response length of GRPO increases first and then gradually decreases. For the preference-based optimization algorithm (TRPA and Online DPO), we observe a more obvious response length decrease in the later stages of training (Figure
3
.(b)). It first performs exploration, increasing the response length, and then executes exploitation to adjust response length into a reasonable range. This suggests that, compared to reward-based optimization method GRPO, TRPA exhibits greater advantages in managing response length without requiring additional technical mechanisms, like length penalty.
Regarding entropy, unlike GRPO, TRPA and Online DPO exhibit greater stability (Figure
3
.(c)). Stable entropy is generally considered a positive indicator for RL training
[
37
]
. In the later stages of training, these two preference-based algorithms maintain entropy fluctuations within the range of 0.1 to 0.01, whereas GRPO exhibits more pronounced oscillations.

RQ4: The role of KTPO technique.
To evaluate the effectiveness of KTPO, we conduct an ablation study by setting the Kahneman-Tversky factor
N
to 1 so that the hyperparameter
\beta
remains uniform across all preference levels. This variant, denoted as TRPA w/o KTPO (purple line in Figure
3
), reveals several notable differences. In Figure
3
(a), TRPA w/o KTPO converges to a lower final accuracy than GRPO, indicating diminished optimization effectiveness. Figure
3
(b) shows that during the mid-training phase (1.5k–3.5k steps), its response length surpasses that of GRPO, while the corresponding accuracy increases only marginally—suggesting that the additional exploration fails to translate into performance gains. After 4.5k steps, its response length declines to a level between that of TRPA and GRPO, indicating a partial retention of TRPA’s length control capability. As shown in Figure
3
(c), the entropy of TRPA w/o KTPO stabilizes in the range
[0,0.3]
after 1k steps—higher than GRPO, indicating improved stability, but still less stable than the full TRPA.

.

RQ5: Does TRPA have the simultaneous change problem that preference optimization usually faces?
In Human Alignment tasks, preference-based optimization methods often suffer from the “simultaneously increase or decrease” problem, where the absolute probabilities of both preferred and dispreferred responses increase or decrease together
[
38
]
. This occurs because minimizing the preference loss encourages a higher relative likelihood for preferred responses, which does not necessarily ensure desirable changes in their absolute probabilities
[
39
,
40
]
.

In contrast, our experiments show that TRPA naturally mitigates this phenomenon. On the K&K logic puzzle dataset (with three participants), we analyze the logit ratios of preferred (winner) and dispreferred (loser) responses, defined as
\log(\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{ref}})
, as shown in Figure
4
(c). Prior to 1.6k training steps, the logit ratios of both winners and losers increase simultaneously. However, after 1.6k steps, the logit ratio of loser responses begins to decline while that of winners continues to rise, indicating a departure from the simultaneous change pattern.

We attribute this behavior to TRPA’s online data sampling mechanism. Theoretically, when samples are drawn from the target preference distribution
p^{*}
, the expected logit ratio of losers decreases while that of winners increases. As training progresses, TRPA’s policy
\pi_{\theta}
converges to the target distribution
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}
, then the resulting preference distribution
p_{\theta}
converges to
p^{*}
, thereby avoiding simultaneous changes. In contrast, static datasets used in conventional Human Alignment methods lack this adaptivity, making them more susceptible to such problems.

6
Conclusion, Discussion and Future Work

In this paper, we introduce the Trust Region Preference Approximation (TRPA) algorithm, an RL framework that integrates rule-based and preference-based optimization to enhance the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. By eliminating the need for reward function, TRPA mitigates reward hacking while ensuring Monotonic Improvement Guarantees toward the target distribution
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}
. Experimental results validate its effectiveness, demonstrating superior reasoning performance and training stability. These findings suggest that TRPA offers a promising direction for improving large-scale RL algorithms in LLM reasoning.

The simplicity and stability of TRPA make it highly promising for applications in scientific research. This paper serves as an early exploration of foundational algorithms in the AI for Science domain. We are actively working on extending this approach to key scientific research areas, such as drug discovery and molecular retrosynthesis. Our future work will conduct a more detailed study of algorithms such as TRPA based on some classic problems that are usually concerned in the RL field:

➀
UTD (Update-to-Data) ratio.
Even if we have already deployed multiple optimization steps on the off-policy data
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
, we still observe that some preference pairs with good enough quality were not fully optimized but were discarded. Therefore, introducing technologies such as the Replay Buffer
[
41
]
, Prioritized Experience Replay (PER)
[
42
]
and Model-Based Methods
[
43
]
to enhance data utilization is helpful for enhancing the ability of LLM reasoning and planning. In other words, the current optimization of LLMs’ policy on existing data is still insufficient, which may also be the reason why the current use of RL algorithms does not trigger the Plasticity Loss phenomenon
[
44
]
, which is often encountered in Deep RL and Continual Learning.

➁
Exploration-Exploitation.
About RL exploration, the increase in response length can be understood as the exploration of the policy in RL. For wrong questions, more exploration should be conducted so that LLM can use more tokens for exploration. For correct questions, exploration should focus on how to solve the problem more efficiently. Regarding exploitation, the utilization patterns of low-variance data and high-variance data should be different. Low-variance data means that LLM tends to get it right most of the time or get it wrong most of the time. Such data is less useful for exploitation of RL training. Our TRPA processing at the response level is to more directly use the comparison of logits between different responses. The logit of the correct answer should become larger, otherwise it will become smaller. In this process, the logits of different answers will participate in each other’s gradient calculations, while in the GRPO algorithm, only different advantages (not logits) will participate in the gradient calculations.

➂
Learning Signal Sparsity.
For current large-scale RL algorithms, the learning signal comes from the evaluation of responses based on reward function or preference levels defined by rules. Therefore, the sequence-level learning signal is still sparse for token-level optimization. How to design richer token-level or reasoning-step-level learning signals is the key idea to enhance algorithm performance.

➃
RL Cold Start.
The performance of the base model determines the upper limit of post-training technology such as large-scale RL. However, it is still possible to further break through the original performance upper limit through the design of post-training technology, such as designing a multi-stage RL algorithm to alleviate the phenomenon of language mixing or enhancing the necessary knowledge mastery and logical thinking ability when LLMs reasoning.

Appendix A
Discussion of Preference-based Optimization, Reward-based Optimization and Rule-based Optimization methods

The basic process of the three methods is shown in Figure
1
. Here we focus on the relationship and difference among these methods. These methods have overlaps with each other but are different from each other. Firstly, the recently proposed DeepSeek R1
[
9
]
proposed that using Reward-based Optimization method (such as GRPO) for Rule-based optimization, where the reward function comes from predefined rules (such as whether the correct answer is output). But not all reward functions can be abstracted from a set of predefined rules. For example,
[
45
]
proposed that in complex games, the reward functions to optimize the intelligent agent to reach the human level should be automatically evolutionary. Secondly, in addition to Preference-based and Reward-based Optimization, Rule-based Optimization also includes other types of methods, such as model-based optimization, evolution-based optimization (e.g. Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)
[
46
]
) methods, etc. These methods optimize model behavior through other mechanisms (implicit rules). For example, model-based policy optimization methods
[
43
,
47
,
48
]
optimize by building an environmental model to predict future rewards. Thirdly, Preference-based Optimization such as human preference alignment cannot usually be modeled as Rule-based Optimization. This is because human preferences are unclear, changing, and have individual differences, making it difficult to abstract them into unified rules. Only some specific tasks such as safety can be designed as rule-based optimization-like methods
[
49
]
, but they still need to use artificial intelligence models to model complex safety guidelines. Our paper intends to design a new type of Preference-based Optimization method based on the idea of Rule-based Optimization to enhance the reasoning capability of LLMs.

Here we introduce several RL algorithms for Reward-based Optimization that are commonly used in LLMs Reasoning tasks.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

In the response generation process, a language model is prompted with an input question
x
to generate an output
y
, where both
x
and
y
are sequences of tokens. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[
12
]
algorithm incorporates a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to constrain the updated policy
\pi_{\theta}
from deviating too far from a reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
. The optimization objective for the policy
\pi_{\theta}
can be formulated as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim D,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)}\left[r_{\phi}(x,y)\right]-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\right)\,,

(13)

where
\beta>0
is a hyperparameter controlling the regularization strength.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

GRPO
[
15
]
is a reinforcement learning algorithm designed to optimize policies by leveraging group-wise relative preference information. Instead of relying on absolute reward values, GRPO updates policies based on relative comparisons within predefined groups, improving stability and sample efficiency.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x\sim\mathcal{D},\left\{y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)\right]

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}

\displaystyle\left(\min\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{i}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\left(y_{i}\mid x\right)}A_{i},\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{i}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\left(y_{i}\mid x\right)},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)A_{i}\right)-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}\left(\pi_{\theta}||\pi_{ref}\right)\right),

where the advantage
A_{i}=\frac{r_{i}-\operatorname{mean}\left(\left\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{G}\right\}\right)}{\operatorname{std}\left(\left\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{G}\right\}\right)}
is computed using a group of rewards.

Proof of Lemma

Lemma
4.2
: The Online DPO algorithm is not Posterior Boltzmann Approximation (PBA) algorithm.

Proof: Recall that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Online\ DPO}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }}\right)=-\mathbb{E}_{\left(x,y_{1},y_{2}\right)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}\left(x,y_{1},y_{2}\right)\right)\right]

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}[p^{*}(z=1|y_{1},y_{2},x)\log\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}(x,y_{1},y_{2})\right)

\displaystyle+p^{*}(z=0|y_{1},y_{2},x)\log\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}(x,y_{2},y_{1})\right)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\left[\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))\right].

Calculate the gradient of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Online\ DPO}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }}\right)
about
\theta
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Online\ DPO}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }}\right)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\left[\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}(x)(\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))\nabla_{\theta}(\pi_{\theta}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{2}|x))

\displaystyle+\pi_{\theta}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{2}|x)\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)))dy_{1}dy_{2}dx.

As for
\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}{p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}\right.

(17)

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{1-p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}{1-p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}\right)\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x).

Consider using the BT model
[
23
]
, we have that when
\pi_{\theta}
is
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
, because
p_{\theta}(z=1|y_{1},y_{2},x)=\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}(x,y_{1},y_{2})\right)
and
p_{\theta}(z=0|y_{1},y_{2},x)=\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}(x,y_{2},y_{1})\right)
, then

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(z=1|y_{1},y_{2},x)=\sigma\left(\bar{h}_{\theta}(x,y_{1},y_{2})\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{1}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\text{ref }}\left(y_{1}\mid x\right)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{2}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{\text{ref }}\left(y_{2}\mid x\right)}\right)=\sigma\left(r(x,y_{1})-r(x,y_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(r\left(x,y_{1}\right)\right)}{\exp\left(r\left(x,y_{1}\right)\right)+\exp\left(r\left(x,y_{2}\right)\right)}=p^{*}(z=1|y_{1},y_{2},x).

And similarly,
p_{\theta}(z=0|y_{1},y_{2},x)=p^{*}(z=0|y_{1},y_{2},x)
. Then for Eq.
17
, we have

\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))=0.

(19)

But for
\mathrm{H}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))
, as shown in Figure
2
(a), (c), it will not be 0 except
p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)
,
p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)
are equal to 0 or 1 which usually does not appear in comparison models such as BT model. Then for
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Online\ DPO}}\left(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }}\right)
, it would not be zero all the time when
\pi_{\theta}
is
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
, i.e.
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
is not the target distribution. Therefore, the Online DPO algorithm is not Posterior Boltzmann Approximation (PBA) algorithm. Proof finished.
\hfill\qed

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }})
about
\theta
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }})=\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},y_{1},y_{2}\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\left[\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))\right]

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}(x)(\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))\nabla_{\theta}(\pi_{\theta}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{2}|x))

\displaystyle+\pi_{\theta}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{2}|x)\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)))dy_{1}dy_{2}dx.

Because
\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p||q)=\mathrm{E}_{p}[\log p-\log q]
, we have that:

\nabla_{q}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p||q)=\nabla_{q}\mathrm{E}_{p}[\log p-\log q]=-\nabla_{q}\mathrm{E}_{p}[\log q]=\nabla_{q}\mathrm{H}(p||q).

(22)

Consider using the BT model
[
23
]
, we have that when
\pi_{\theta}
is
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
, based on Eq.
17
, then

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}{p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}\right.

(23)

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{1-p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}{1-p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)}\right)\nabla_{\theta}p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x).

Based on Eq.
18
, we have
p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)=p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)
. Then

\nabla_{\theta}\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))=0.

(24)

\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(p^{*}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x)||p_{\theta}(z|y_{1},y_{2},x))=0
when

. Then we get that
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\pi_{\theta};\pi_{\text{ref }})=0
when
\pi_{\theta}
is
\bar{\pi}^{\tau}(y|x)
. Therefore, the PA algorithm is Posterior Boltzmann Approximation (PBA) algorithm. Proof finished.

Theorem
