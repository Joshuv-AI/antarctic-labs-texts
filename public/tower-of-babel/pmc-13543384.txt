Effect of Angulated Screw Channel (ASC) design on the fracture resistance of implant-supported monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate crowns: an in vitro study
Authors: Bedia Gökten Uçar, Özlem Çölgeçen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ti-base abutments with angulated screw channel (ASC) designs have been developed to address the esthetic challenges associated with labial screw access in anterior implant-supported single crowns, as well as the ergonomic limitations caused by restricted mouth opening in posterior crowns. Despite their increasing clinical use, there is limited research evaluating the impact of this design on the fracture resistance of various monolithic restorative materials across different anatomical regions, particularly after aging. This study aims to investigate the effect of angled screw channel design on the fracture resistance of implant-supported single crown restorations made of different materials in different anatomical regions after long-term thermal aging. In the study, a total of 80 implant-supported single crown restorations were prepared and then divided into subgroups (n = 10) considering anatomical region (anterior/posterior), restoration material (monolithic zirconia/lithium disilicate), and screw channel design (straight 0°/angled 20°). Monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate crowns were fabricated in the form of maxillary central incisors for anterior, and mandibular first molars for posterior specimens. After cementation on Ti-base abutments, the specimens were aged with 40,000 thermal cycles (5°C/55°C), and the fracture resistance of the crowns was subsequently determined using a universal testing machine under compressive loading. Fracture patterns were examined under a stereomicroscope. Three-way analysis of variance followed by a Bonferroni post hoc test was used in data analysis (α = 0.05). According to the results, anatomical region, restorative material, screw channel design, and the interactions between these variables had a significant effect on the fracture resistance of implant-supported single crown restorations (p < 0.001). The use of ASC design reduced the fracture resistance of the restoration material in all groups, regardless of anatomical region and material type (p < 0.001). In the anterior region, using the ASC design resulted in a statistically significant strength loss, which was similar across all materials. In the posterior region, although zirconia material showed significantly higher fracture strength than lithium disilicate, the strength loss was more pronounced. The fracture loads observed in all specimens remained within reported physiological bite force ranges under the present experimental conditions. In conclusion, although the ASC design offers clinical advantages for implant-supported restorations, its effect on reducing the mechanical strength of the restorative material should be taken into account in clinical practice. While the fracture loads observed in this study remained within physiological limits, further investigations under long-term and in vivo conditions are warranted.

Introduction

In modern dentistry, implant-supported restorations can now be used in the rehabilitation of almost all types of tooth loss due to their high success rates [1, 2]. While these restorations can be classified in many different ways, they are divided into two main groups according to the type of retention of the superstructure: cement-retained and screw-retained [3]. Although cement-retained restorations have many advantages such as ease of application, the risk of peri-implantitis observed due to residual cement has increased the interest in screw-retained restorations, which can be removed and reinserted without damaging the restoration and have better marginal fit [4]. Today, Ti-base abutments integrated with CAD/CAM are becoming increasingly popular in single-unit implant-supported restorations. Combining the mechanical advantages of titanium with the aesthetic advantages of porcelain material, this intermediate piece is used in hybrid abutment designs as well as in monolithic restorations. Since monolithic restorations are cemented to Ti-base abutments outside the mouth and screwed in the mouth, the risk of peri-implantitis observed due to cement has been eliminated [5] At the same time, the homogeneous structure of monolithic restorations, being a single piece, ensures a more balanced load distribution, thus increasing mechanical strength [6]. Monolithic restorations, which can be produced from many different materials, are frequently produced from monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate [7]. Monolithic zirconia is frequently used in implant-supported restorations due to its high biocompatibility, favorable esthetic properties, and superior mechanical characteristics associated with its polycrystalline structure and favorable mechanical properties. Studies in the literature report a 5-year survival rate of 100% for this material in implant-supported single crown restorations [8]. On the other hand, lithium disilicate, with a flexural strength ranging from 360 to 450 MPa and high translucency, has traditionally been used as a glass ceramic material in the anterior region. However, with the increasing aesthetic demands, the range of indications for this material has expanded. Current literature indicates that, due to its improved mechanical properties, lithium disilicate can be safely used in the posterior region under appropriate indications [7, 9, 10].

When dental implants are placed in ideal positions, esthetic outcomes can be achieved with monolithic restorations. However, particularly in the anterior maxilla, anatomical factors such as bone resorption patterns and the presence of buccal concavities may influence implant positioning, leading to unfavorable screw access locations and compromised esthetics [11]. In such situations, the angulated screw channel (ASC) system enables the use of screw-retained restorations instead of cement-retained alternatives by redirecting the screw channel at specific angles [12]. While conventional straight screw channel (SSC) systems align the screw access with the implant axis, ASC systems allow the screw access to be redirected at various angles independently of implant position. This approach improves esthetics in the anterior region by correcting the location of the screw access opening. Another advantage of the ASC system is that it facilitates access to posterior regions in cases with limited mouth opening, such as temporomandibular joint disorders, muscular dysfunctions, and trismus. Restricted mouth opening can present challenges for both the clinician and the patient, and ASC designs improve clinical handling and ergonomics [13]. Limited studies in the literature have suggested that angulated screw channel designs may alter crown morphology, reduce material thickness in specific areas, and consequently decrease mechanical strength [14]. Additionally, studies have reported that intraoral temperature fluctuations may induce molecular changes in ceramic materials, leading to reduced fracture resistance [15, 16]. A review of the literature reveals that studies evaluating the fracture resistance of crowns fabricated from different restorative materials using ASC systems in anatomically distinct regions after thermal aging are scarce. Most studies investigating the effects of angulated screw channel design on fracture resistance have focused on a single anatomical region and a single restorative material. Consequently, data regarding the combined and comparative effects of these variables on fracture resistance following long-term thermal aging under different clinical scenarios remain limited. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of angulated screw channel design on the fracture resistance and fracture patterns of single-crown restorations fabricated from monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate materials after thermal aging, separately in anterior and posterior regions. The null hypotheses of the present study were that the angulated screw channel design would not affect the fracture resistance of monolithic zirconia or lithium disilicate restorations in the anterior region, nor would it affect the fracture resistance of monolithic zirconia or lithium disilicate restorations in the posterior region.

Preparation of the crowns

In this study, each experimental specimen consisted of an implant analog, a Ti-base abutment, and a superstructure. For the anterior specimens, 40 narrow-diameter implant analogs (BİLimplant®, Istanbul, Türkiye) and Ti-base abutments with a height of 5.5 mm and a gingival height (gh) of 1 mm (BİLimplant®, Istanbul, Türkiye) were used. For the posterior specimens, 40 standard-diameter implant analogs (BİLimplant®, Istanbul, Türkiye) and Ti-base abutments with a height of 3.5 mm and a gingival height (gh) of 1 mm (BİLimplant®, Istanbul, Türkiye) were utilized. Ti-base abutment forms used in this study (BİLimplant®). A Ti-base abutment used for posterior angulated specimens, B Ti-base abutment used for posterior straight specimens, C Ti-base abutment used for anterior angulated specimens, and D Ti-base abutment used for anterior straight specimens

In the present study, a circular rigid plastic plate with a thickness of 2 mm was prepared, and a hole corresponding to the diameter of the implant analog was created on its surface. The implant analog was then positioned in an inverted orientation, and its coronal portion was tightly fitted into the prepared hole to achieve stable fixation. The perpendicular alignment of the analog was verified from all directions using a protractor ruler. Subsequently, a plastic mold was positioned around the plate, and autopolymerizing acrylic resin (SC Imicryl Dental, Türkiye) was poured into the mold. After the polymerization was completed, the mold was removed. Thus, the apical portions of the implant analogs remained embedded within the acrylic resin, while the coronal 2 mm portion remained exposed above the acrylic resin surface. After securing the scan bodies to the implant analogs, digital impressions were obtained using a laboratory scanner (Medit T500, Medit Corp., South Korea).

Crown designs were created to simulate a right maxillary central incisor for the anterior specimens and a right mandibular first molar for the posterior specimens. The incisal–gingival height and mesiodistal width of the anterior crowns were set at 11 mm and 8.5 mm, respectively, in accordance with the literature [17]. For the posterior crowns, the mesiodistal width was set at 11 mm, the buccolingual width at 10.5 mm [18], and the occlusal thickness at 1.5 mm [19, 20]. All crowns were designed using identical CAD parameters. A standardized cement space of 50 μm was applied to all restorations, while the remaining internal surfaces were designed using the software’s default internal relief settings. These parameters were kept constant for all specimens throughout the study.

Initially, crowns were designed with a straight screw channel. Subsequently, without altering the overall crown morphology, a 20° angulation was applied to the screw access channel; accordingly, the screw access opening was relocated from the incisal edge to the palatal surface in the anterior crowns and from the central fossa to the mesial surface in the posterior crowns. Figure 3-C illustrates the angulation of the screw access channel in the specimens.

The designed monolithic zirconia crowns were milled from partially sintered blocks (Duradent, ERK Dental, Türkiye) and subsequently sintered at 1500°C for 9 h to achieve full densification. For the fabrication of lithium disilicate crowns, resin patterns were first produced according to the digital designs using a three-dimensional printing device (SprintRay Midas Dental 3D Resin Printer, USA). These patterns were then subjected to a burn-out procedure, followed by a heat-pressing process to fabricate the lithium disilicate crowns. All zirconia and lithium disilicate specimens were mechanically polished.

Cementation of the crowns to the Ti-base abutments

The Ti-base abutments were screwed into the implant analogs using a specially designed driver for the angulated screw channel specimens and a conventional driver for the straight screw channel specimens. The abutment screws in both narrow and standard groups were tightened to 30 Ncm in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommended torque protocol. The internal surfaces of the monolithic zirconia crowns and the external surfaces of the Ti-base abutments were airborne-particle abraded with 50 μm aluminum oxide particles at a pressure of 2 bar from a distance of 15 mm for 20 s. Subsequently, the specimens were cleaned ultrasonically in 80% ethyl alcohol. The internal surfaces of the lithium disilicate crowns were etched with 9.5% hydrofluoric acid for 20 s, then thoroughly rinsed. A bonding agent (Clearfil Ceramic Primer, Kuraray Co., Japan) was applied to the internal surfaces of all crowns and to the external surfaces of the Ti-base abutments. Resin cement (Panavia V5, Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan) was applied to the Ti-base abutments, and the crowns were seated with finger pressure by the same operator. Then, a constant load of 50 N was applied to the crowns for 5 min using an analog dynamometer (NK 50 Analog Dynamometer, Loyka, Türkiye). Excess cement was removed, and polymerization was performed for 20 s at the crown margins using an LED curing unit (VALO Grand, Ultradent Products, USA). The screw access holes were sealed with polytetrafluoroethylene tape and flowable composite resin (Filtek Supreme Flowable Restorative, 3 M ESPE, USA). A total of 80 specimens were prepared, including both anterior and posterior crowns, with each region incorporating monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate crowns in both straight and angulated screw channel (ASC) designs, as shown in Fig. 4. All specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 ± 1°C in an incubator (FN 120, Nüve, Türkiye) for 24 h before the aging procedure.

Aging procedure

For assessing long-term thermal aging, all specimens were subjected to 40,000 thermal cycles, corresponding to approximately four years of clinical service, using a thermocycling device specifically designed for this purpose (Gökçeler Makine, Türkiye). The thermocycling procedure was conducted between water baths at 5°C and 55°C, with a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 10 s.

Fracture resistance test

The anterior specimens were mounted on the testing platform at a 30° angle to simulate a Class I occlusal relationship and clinically relevant oblique loading conditions. A loading tip with a diameter of 2.5 mm was positioned 2 mm below the incisal edge of the anterior specimens. The posterior specimens were mounted vertically on the testing platform, and a loading tip with a diameter of 5 mm was positioned perpendicularly to the central fossa of the specimens. Figure 5 shows the application of the fracture test to the anterior and posterior specimens. Fracture testing was performed using a universal testing machine (Model AGS-X 5 kN, Shimadzu, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. The sudden drop in the force–deformation curve was considered the point of fracture, and the fracture load values were recorded in Newtons. During mechanical loading, a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tape was interposed between the loading tip and the specimen in the anterior groups to simulate point contact conditions, while a silicone interlayer material was used in the posterior groups to simulate a broader occlusal contact area.

Analysis of fracture patterns

All crowns and Ti-base abutments were examined under a stereomicroscope (AxioCam ERc 5s; Zeiss, Germany) from five different aspects-buccal, lingual, mesial, distal, and occlusal-at various magnifications (×10, ×20, and ×30). In the stereomicroscopic images, crack lines extending from the vestibular surface toward the interfaces in posterior specimens and those extending from the interface toward the cingulum in anterior specimens were classified as oblique fracture lines. In addition to fracture morphology, the number of fragments generated after fracture was recorded for each specimen. Fracture morphology (e.g., bulk fracture or oblique fracture line) and fragmentation characteristics were evaluated separately, and the frequency of each fracture characteristic was determined for every experimental group.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User, version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. A three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to investigate the individual, two-way, and three-way effects of anatomical region, restorative material, and screw channel design on fracture resistance. When significant main effects or interaction effects were detected in the three-way ANOVA, Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparison tests were performed to enable pairwise comparisons between groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The sample size was determined during the study design by reviewing previous in-vitro studies evaluating the fracture resistance of implant-supported ceramic restorations, in which sample sizes typically ranged from 5 to 10 specimens per experimental group [21–23]. Based on this evidence, 10 specimens were included in each experimental group. Following completion of the experiment, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power version 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) to verify the adequacy of the selected sample size.

Among the anterior groups, the lowest fracture resistance was observed in the AL-A group, followed by the AL-S, AZ-A and AZ-S groups, respectively. In the posterior region, all groups demonstrated higher fracture resistance values compared with the anterior specimens. In the posterior lithium disilicate groups, the fracture resistance value decreased from 1978 ± 158 N in the PL-S group to 1613 ± 184 N in the PL-A group, corresponding to a reduction of 19%.

Accordingly, the mean fracture resistance value observed in the AL-A group (660 ± 128 N) was found to be statistically significantly lower than that observed in the AL-S group (1050 ± 110 N) (p < 0.001). Similarly, the mean fracture resistance value observed in the AZ-A group (1225 ± 45 N) was statistically significantly lower than that observed in the AZ-S group (1878 ± 183 N) (p < 0.001). The mean fracture resistance value observed in the PL-A group (1613 ± 184 N) was also statistically significantly lower than that observed in the PL-S group (1978 ± 158 N) (p < 0.001). Likewise, the mean fracture resistance value observed in the PZ-A group (2902 ± 640 N) was statistically significantly lower than that observed in the PZ-S group (4423 ± 574 N) (p < 0.001). In the inter-material comparison, the mean fracture resistance values observed in zirconia restorations were significantly higher than those observed in lithium disilicate restorations, regardless of screw channel design and anatomical region (p < 0.001).

Based on the observed effect sizes obtained from the three-way ANOVA results, the achieved statistical power of the study exceeded the minimum recommended threshold of 0.80 for all evaluated effects. Even for the lowest interaction effect (ηp² = 0.103), the achieved statistical power was calculated as approximately 0.85, whereas the primary main effects demonstrated statistical power values greater than 0.99.

Fracture pattern analysis results

Regardless of anatomical region and restorative material, fractures in all specimens occurred at the crown level, and no fractures or any form of deformation were observed in the implant analogs, screws, or abutments. All crowns exhibited fractures of a catastrophic nature. Accordingly, all fracture patterns observed in the present study were classified as non-repairable due to the extensive loss of structural integrity and fragmentation of the restorations. In the anterior specimens, irrespective of the restorative material, straight screw channel designs demonstrated fracture lines that followed the screw access channel, resulting in separation of the crown into two fragments (20/20, 100%). In both straight screw channel groups, fracture initiation consistently originated from the incisal region (20/20, 100%), with fracture propagation occurring vertically in 16/20 specimens (80%) and obliquely in 4/20 specimens (20%). In contrast, specimens with angulated screw channel design exhibited fracture initiation in the cingulum region on the palatal surface (20/20, 100%), characterized by oblique fracture lines (20/20, 100%). The fracture patterns observed in the anterior specimens are presented in Figure 7, including both straight and ASC designs. Bulk-type fractures were observed in all posterior specimens (40/40, 100%). In posterior monolithic zirconia specimens, three-fragment fractures were identified regardless of screw channel design, occurring in 12/20 specimens (60%), whereas the remaining specimens fractured into four fragments (8/20, 40%). However, differences related to screw channel design were observed in lithium disilicate specimens. While specimens in the PLS-D group predominantly fractured into three fragments (9/10, 90%) characterized by oblique fracture lines, only one specimen fractured into two fragments (1/10, 10%). In contrast, specimens in the PLS-A group predominantly fractured into two fragments (9/10, 90%) characterized by mesiodistally oriented fracture lines, whereas only one specimen fractured into three fragments (1/10, 10%). Discussion

In this study, the effect of ASC design on the fracture resistance of implant-supported single-crown restorations after thermal aging was investigated by considering different anatomical regions and two different restoration materials. According to the findings, the use of the ASC design reduced fracture resistance in both anterior and posterior regions, regardless of the restorative material.

Crown fractures represent one of the most significant complications observed in ceramic restorations, because the fracture resistance of the restorative ceramics is a critical factor that substantially influences the long-term success of restorative treatments [24, 25]. It is well established that hydrothermal aging reduces the fracture resistance of ceramic materials [16]. In a study by Liebermann et al. [26], in which mechanical strength was evaluated after 220, 1,500, 10,000, 20,000, and 40,000 thermal cycles, a progressive decrease in fracture resistance was reported with increasing numbers of thermal cycles. In previous in vitro studies [16, 42], thermocycling protocols of approximately 5,000 cycles or 10,000 cycles have generally been applied, with 10,000 cycles being reported to simulate nearly one year of clinical service [27]. In the present study, 40,000 thermocycles were performed in order to represent a more extended artificial aging condition and a long-term clinical scenario. Furthermore, limited data are currently available regarding the long-term thermocycling behavior of implant-supported restorations with angulated screw channel designs. Therefore, the extended thermocycling protocol applied in the present study may be considered one of the distinctive methodological aspects of this study.

In implant-supported restorative treatments, another important success criterion is biological success [28]. The use of (ASC) design options increases the preference for screw-retained restorations rather than cement-retained restorations, thereby eliminating the risk of cement-related biological complications [29]. However, an important issue in these restorations is screw stability. Previous studies have reported that as angulation of ASC increases, torque loss of the screw also increases [30, 31]. According to the manufacturer’s recommendations for the prosthetic system investigated in the present study, the maximum screw channel angulation is limited to 20º, representing the upper clinically applicable angulation while maintaining acceptable screw stability. Therefore, a 20º screw channel angulation was selected to evaluate the mechanical effect of the ASC design under the most demanding clinically relevant conditions permitted for this prosthetic system.

The results of the present study demonstrated that the incorporation of ASC designs was associated with decreased fracture resistance in all groups. In this respect, the null hypotheses of the present study were rejected. Previous studies have reported that increasing screw-channel angulation reduces the volume of restorative material within the crown, thereby negatively affecting fracture resistance [3, 32]. In an in vitro study conducted by Drew et al. [13] simulating a maxillary tooth, the fracture resistance of ASC crowns was found to be lower than that of straight screw channel designs; although this difference was not statistically significant, the authors reported that the straight screw channel design was mechanically more favorable. In an in vitro study conducted by Garcia-Hammaker et al. [33] the fracture resistance of restorations fabricated from monolithic zirconia was evaluated using ten specimens. The authors reported that straight screw channel designs exhibited significantly higher fracture resistance values compared with angulated designs. These findings are consistent with the results of the present study. Moreover, extending the scope of previous studies, the present study provides a comprehensive evaluation by demonstrating that angulated screw channel designs reduce fracture resistance not only in anterior but also in posterior regions, and in both lithium disilicate and zirconia restorations, thereby offering a novel contribution to the current literature.

Another finding of the present study was that, based on material comparison, zirconia restorations exhibited absolutely higher fracture resistance than lithium disilicate restorations, regardless of anatomical region and screw channel design. The higher fracture resistance of zirconia restorations compared with lithium disilicate is an already known finding in the literature [7, 18]. In the present study, lithium disilicate restorations were fabricated using the heat-pressing technique rather than CAD/CAM milling. Although CAD/CAM systems provide standardized and accurate production workflows, previous studies have suggested that milling procedures in brittle ceramic materials may influence marginal quality, particularly in thin or geometrically complex regions [34–36] Since the present study involved restorations with altered screw channel geometries and potentially reduced localized ceramic thicknesses, the heat-pressing technique was considered an appropriate and standardized fabrication approach for the lithium disilicate specimens.

Nevertheless, the incorporation of ASC designs produced region-specific effects in both materials. In anterior specimens, changing from straight to angulated screw channel designs led to a pronounced and similar reduction in mechanical resistance for both restorative materials. Furthermore, the analysis of fracture patterns in anterior restorations was consistent with this mechanical response. The literature has reported an increased risk of fracture occurrence at the palatal region, particularly on the cingulum surface, in ASC crowns [14, 37]. In specimens with ASC, the observation of this fracture pattern may be attributed to the angulation-induced displacement of the load application point away from the center of the restoration, resulting in a concentration of tensile stresses in the palatal region. The fact that the cingulum represents the thinnest area of the restoration, together with alterations in the balance between tensile and compressive stresses, may explain the similar magnitude of fracture resistance reduction and the common fracture pattern observed in both materials when transitioning to angulated designs.

A different pattern was observed in the posterior region. The loading protocols differed between the anterior and posterior specimens because the primary objective of the present study was to reproduce the predominant functional loading conditions of each anatomical region. Accordingly, the anterior specimens were loaded at a 30° angle to simulate clinically relevant oblique incisal forces [38, 39], whereas the posterior specimens were subjected to vertical loading through the central fossa to better reproduce the predominantly axial occlusal forces generated during mastication [40]. Similar loading configurations have been adopted in previous in vitro studies, allowing a more clinically relevant assessment of the fracture resistance of implant-supported monolithic crowns [21, 41]. Although zirconia restorations exhibited absolutely higher fracture resistance than lithium disilicate restorations for both screw channel designs, the reduction in mechanical resistance associated with the angulated design was more pronounced in zirconia restorations. This finding may be attributed to differences in crack propagation behavior related to the distinct material compositions of the two ceramics, as well as differences in their elastic moduli. In a study conducted by Özdemir Dogan et al. [20] in the posterior region, it was reported that microstructural and physical differences among ceramic materials alter crack growth mechanisms within the material, thereby affecting fatigue resistance. Donmez et al. [42] have conducted an in vitro study in which posterior implant-supported restorations have been fabricated using different ceramic and hybrid restorative materials. Their findings have revealed statistically significant differences in fracture resistance among the tested materials. In addition, each material has exhibited distinct fracture patterns, and these differences have been attributed to the structural characteristics of the materials. Ceramic restorations are generally more resistant to compressive stresses than to tensile stresses [43]. In the posterior experimental setup, the vertically applied load emphasizes the compressive force–resistance mechanisms of both materials. Furthermore, these restorations are volumetrically larger than anterior restorations. Based on this information, unlike in the anterior region, the applied forces in the posterior region are not concentrated in a single area but are distributed throughout the crown. Accordingly, in the posterior region, the structural properties of the materials may have influenced the fracture behavior, resulting in differences in mechanical resistance loss and fracture patterns between monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate materials.

In a study conducted by Elshiyab et al. [15], bulk-type and three-fragment fractures have been observed in posterior monolithic zirconia restorations. This finding is consistent with the fracture pattern results of the present study. In another in vitro study conducted by Elshiyab et al. [44], bulk-type fracture patterns have been observed in monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate restorations, and this fracture mode has been reported as the most frequently observed fracture pattern in lithium disilicate in both in vivo and in vitro studies. In the present study, bulk-type fractures were observed in both materials; however, these fractures occurred with different numbers of fragments.

In lithium disilicate specimens, the ASC design altered the fracture pattern, resulting in separation into two fragments. This finding suggests that the stress generated in lithium disilicate restorations may have altered crack propagation behavior within the material structure, whereas the observation of similar fracture patterns in zirconia restorations with both screw channel designs may be associated with the higher stiffness and different microstructural characteristics of zirconia materials. In zirconia crowns, the load may have been transferred more uniformly throughout the restoration, whereas in lithium disilicate restorations, crack initiation and propagation within the material structure may have occurred at earlier stages. Stereomicroscope examinations of posterior crowns in the present study also revealed distinct radial fracture lines in zirconia restorations, supporting differences in fracture behavior between the tested materials.

The maximum anterior bite force measured in healthy young individuals ranges between 90 N and 743 N and is most frequently reported to be between 120 N and 370 N; this wide range is known to be associated with parafunctional habits [45, 46]. In this study, the fracture resistance values observed in anterior crowns with straight screw channels exceeded the reported physiological bite force ranges under static in vitro conditions. Although the fracture resistance values of lithium disilicate ASC restorations were lower than those of zirconia restorations, all tested groups remained within the reported physiological bite force ranges under static in vitro conditions. Additionally, another finding of the present study was that, in the anterior specimens, fractures associated with the angulated design predominantly occurred in the cingulum region, while the labial surface remained intact. In such a clinical scenario, temporary restorative management may help preserve esthetics for a limited period, since the labial surface generally remains intact.

In a study conducted by Calderon et al. [47], the maximum bite forces measured in the posterior region were reported as 834 N in women and 893 N in men. Additionally, there are studies reporting posterior masticatory forces ranging between 600 and 900 N [48, 49]. When the findings of the present study are evaluated, the fracture resistance values observed in posterior crowns for both screw-channel designs exceeded the reported posterior bite force ranges under the present experimental conditions. Although zirconia restorations exhibited a pronounced reduction in resistance following the transition from SSC to ASC design, they demonstrated significantly higher fracture resistance values than lithium disilicate restorations.

This study has several limitations. The in vitro nature of the study does not fully simulate the clinical environment, including the biological, thermal, and functional load variables of the oral cavity. The use of a single implant–abutment system and single-crown designs, the application of a single aging protocol and the use of only one screw-channel angulation can be considered limitations of this study. In addition, all implant analogs were positioned perpendicular to the test base in order to standardize the loading conditions and minimize confounding variables related to implant angulation. Therefore, the present experimental setup may not fully represent all clinical scenarios involving angled implant placement. Although thermocycling was performed to simulate long-term thermal aging, the absence of thermo-mechanical aging and dynamic cyclic loading may also be considered a limitation of the present study, since masticatory forces and lateral movements could influence the long-term clinical behavior of restorations. In addition, different crown geometries and dimensions, different implant–abutment systems, and variations in the local ceramic thickness surrounding the screw access channel may also influence the outcomes.

Future studies may further investigate the relationship between local material thickness around the screw access channel and fracture resistance behavior using STL-based digital analyses, three-dimensional thickness mapping techniques, and thermo-mechanical aging protocols incorporating dynamic mechanical loading conditions. Such approaches may provide additional information regarding stress distribution patterns and the biomechanical behavior of restorations with different screw channel designs.
