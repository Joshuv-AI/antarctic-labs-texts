Co-occurrence of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance gene, mcr-1 and blaCTX−M in multidrug-resistant Escherichia coli isolates from calves in Iran
Authors: Mehri Haeili, Seyyed Mohammad Reza Naghibi Biji, Fatemeh Alsahlani, Kiarash Moghaddasi, Maryam Omrani, Arash Ghodousi, Daniela Maria Cirillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colistin, a “last-resort” antibiotic for treatment of human infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria, has been widely used in veterinary medicine for decades. Farm animals have been identified as a significant reservoir of mcr-1-carrying bacteria with potential zoonotic transmission. This study aimed to characterize mcr-1-positive Escherichia coli isolates obtained from calves in Iran. Rectal swab samples were collected from both diarrhetic and healthy calves and screened for colistin-resistant bacteria. The presence of colistin resistance genes mcr-1 to 5 was examined using PCR. The genomic features of 15 out of 82 colistin resistant isolates were further studied by whole -genome sequencing. All isolates carried mcr-1.1 variant and belonged to ST224 (n = 3), ST162 (n = 2), ST167 (n = 2), ST1642 (n = 2), ST949 (n = 2), ST617, ST5229, ST88 and ST2345 (n = 1 each). These isolates exhibited high rates of resistance to ceftazidime, cefepime, and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (100%), followed by tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, gentamicin, ciprofloxacin (≥ 80%) and amikacin (60%) mediated by the presence of blaCTX−M, sul and dfrA variants, tetA/B/M, floR/cmlA1, aac(3)-IId/a or ant(2’’)-Ia, qnrS, aac(6’)-Ib-cr, gyrA (S83L, D87N), and parC (S80I) mutations and rmtB gene. All isolates were susceptible to tigecycline, nitrofurantoin and imipenem. The IncI2 (delta) replicon was the most prevalent plasmid type among the studied isolates, suggesting it may play a key role in mcr-1 dissemination. The mcr-1- associated elements and colistin resistance phenotype remained stable following 10 days of passage in the absence of colistin pressure. This study reports isolation of mcr-1-bearing-MDR E. coli isolates co-harboring blaCTX−M and rmtB genes from animal sources. The observed clonal diversity among mcr-1-positive-isolates suggests a potential role for epidemic plasmids including IncI2, in the dissemination of mcr-1 gene in farm animals.

Introduction

The global rise of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) bacteria, in particular carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), has necessitated the re-introduction of polymyxins to the therapeutic arsenal as last-resort antibiotics1. However, the extensive use of colistin in livestock, primarily for the prevention and treatment of Enterobacteriaceae infections2 has facilitated the emergence and dissemination of colistin-resistant bacterial strains. Colistin, a polycationic antimicrobial peptide, acts by interacting with the negatively charged lipid A component of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria. Resistance to colistin arises due to lipid A modifications through cationic substitutions. This resistance mechanism was initially found to be merely conferred by chromosomal mutations in PmrAB and PhoPQ regulatory systems, which control the expression of LPS-modifying enzymes PmrC (EptA) and PmrK (ArnT)3. In 2015, however, the first plasmid-encoded colistin resistance gene mcr-1, was discovered in Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates from both animal and human sources in China4. Since then, mcr-1 has been detected in a range of Enterobacteriaceae species including E. coli, Klebsiella and Salmonella spp. from human, animals and the environment, across numerous countries5–7. The mcr-1 gene encodes for a phosphoethanolamine transferase that catalyzes the addition of phosphoethanolamine to lipid A, thereby decreasing binding affinity of cationic colistin to its anionic LPS target. As of March, 2025, ten major mcr gene variants, (mcr-1 to mcr-10) have been identified8–15, each exhibiting a nearly identical mechanism of resistance to colistin despite subtle amino acid variations. Among these, mcr-1 remains the most widely distributed and clinically significant variant.

E. coli is a commensal bacterium that can act as an opportunistic pathogen, causing a wide range of diseases in both humans and animals including intestinal and extraintestinal infections. Several studies have identified farm animals as reservoir of mcr-1-carrying E. coli raising concerns about zoonotic transmission to humans16–18. In Iran, colistin is used in veterinary medicine for the treatment of bacterial infections in poultry and cattle, and in human medicine as a last-line antibiotic for treatment of infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria such as CRE, Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, particularly in critically ill patients in intensive care units19. Beyond Iran, colistin has been used in veterinary medicine in multiple countries, including China, European countries, and parts of South America, primarily to treat and prevent infections in livestock20. In some regions, it was historically used as a growth promoter, contributing to selection pressure for resistance21. The global spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance has prompted policy interventions such as bans or restrictions on veterinary colistin use in China and the European Union, highlighting the international significance of this resistance problem20,22. Colistin resistance, particularly plasmid-mediated mcr genes, has been reported in humans, livestock, food products, and the environment across Asia, Europe, Africa, and the America, highlighting a global dissemination of resistance and raising serious concerns for public health and veterinary medicine4,23.

Colistin resistant Enterobacterales have been reported in Iran from both human and animal sources with resistance mechanisms predominantly associated with chromosomal mutations3,24. However, data on plasmid-mediated mcr-1 in food-producing animals in Iran remain limited and largely rely on PCR screening, with no whole-genome sequencing (WGS)-based genomic analysis of mcr-1-positive E. coli reported to date.

Given that the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified colistin as a “highest priority critically important antimicrobial for human medicine”25, surveillance of transferable colistin resistance, particularly in food-producing animals is crucial. Therefore, this study aimed to provide the first WGS-based characterization of mcr-1-positive MDR E. coli from calves in Iran, including detailed analysis of their resistome, plasmid replicon types, virulence factors, and genetic context.

Results and discussion

Bacterial isolation and mcr gene variants conferring colistin resistance

Out of 500 rectal swab samples collected from a single farm, 82 colistin resistant bacterial isolates (20 from healthy (13.3%) and 62 from diarrheic calves (17.7%) were recovered (isolates with known intrinsic resistance to colistin (e.g., Proteus spp.) were excluded from analysis). All colistin-resistant enteric isolates were identified as E. coli according to conventional biochemical tests. These isolates exhibited colistin MIC values of 4 to16 mg/L (MIC50 = 8 mg/L, MIC90 = 16 mg/L) as determined by broth dilution assay and all tested positive for mcr-1 genes by PCR. In contrast to our previous study on poultry fecal samples24, no colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae isolates were identified in the current study. This observation is consistent with epidemiological data indicating a higher prevalence of mcr-1 in E. coli compared to K. pneumoniae. The difference may be explained by factors such as variation in conjugation efficiencies, different plasmid stability, and fitness costs associated with mcr-1-bearing elements in these two species26.

Genetic diversity of mcr-1-positive E. coli isolates

Fifteen mcr-positive Escherichia coli isolates were selected for WGS to represent the diversity of colistin-resistant isolates in our study. Selection was based on (i) colistin MIC values, covering the full observed range (4–16 mg/L) to capture variation in resistance levels, and (ii) host health status, including isolates from both healthy and diarrheic calves, reflecting potential differences in antimicrobial exposure. This approach ensured that the sequenced isolates were representative of the range of phenotypes observed among the colistin-resistant population. WGS analysis confirmed that these 15 isolates carried the mcr-1.1 variant and were assigned to 9 distinct sequence types (STs): ST224 (n = 3), ST162 (n = 2), ST167 (n = 2), ST1642 (n = 2), ST949 (n = 2), ST617, ST5229, ST88 and ST2345 (n = 1 each) (Table 1). The mcr-1-bearing E. coli isolates have been assigned to several clonally different lineages. For instance, E. coli ST162, and ST224 have been identified in mcr-1-positive isolates recovered from retail raw meat in Czech Republic27. Additionally, E. coli ST162 and ST88 co-harboring extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) and mcr-1 have been detected in food, food-producing animals, and companion animals in China28. Notably, mcr-1-positive isolates belonging to ST617 and ST167-both members of the ST10 clonal complex- were identified in this study. The ST10 clonal complex (including ST10, ST167 and ST617 and others) is widely distributed in humans and food animals and often carries multiple antimicrobial resistance determinants, including mcr-129,30. These lineages are commonly found in commensal intestinal flora and are also associated with human clinical infections worldwide31,32, highlighting their role as reservoirs and potential vectors for zoonotic transmission of colistin resistance across diverse ecological niches.

Genotypic and phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profile

Analysis using ResFinder and KmerResistance revealed that, all mcr-1-positive isolates co-harbored CTX-M-type ESBLs (blaCTX−M−55 (n = 10) and blaCTX−M−15 (n = 5)), sulfonamide/trimethoprim resistance gene (sul/dfrA variant), aminoglycoside adenyltransferases (aadA variants) and phosphotransferases, (aph variants). Furthermore, 80%(n = 12) of mcr-1-positive isolates carried at least one of the genes conferring resistance to chloramphenicol (floR, cmlA1, catB3, catA1) and tetracycline (tetA/B/M). Notably, 60% (n = 9) of the isolates harbored the 16S rRNA methyltransferase (RMTase) RmtB conferring resistance to nearly all clinically important aminoglycosides. Analysis of the genetic background of quinolones resistance in mcr-positive isolates showed chromosomal mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region (QRDR) of topoisomerase encoding genes gyrA and parC in 93% of isolates (n = 14). The most frequent mutation pattern involved simultaneous substitutions at GyrA (S83L and D87N), and ParC (S80I). In addition, plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) genes were identified, including qnrS1 (n = 6) and aac(6’)-Ib-cr (n = 1) (Table 1). These findings align with previous studies reporting co-carriage of ESBLs and mcr genes in E. coli strains from animal sources. For example, co-occurrence of ESBLs and mcr-1 and mcr-4 was reported in strains from a Portuguese Farrow-to-Finish Swine Herd33. Similarly, Liu et al. described co-occurrence of mcr-1 and ESBL genes in E.coli from Bovine Mastitis Milk in China, implicating dairy cattle as a potential reservoir of mcr-1-carrying and ESBL-producing strains34. Furthermore, co-localization of mcr-1 and bla CTX−M−55 on IncI2 plasmids has been documented35, highlighting the potential for simultaneous dissemination of resistance to colistin and ESBLs. In our study, mcr-1 and IncI2 replicons were found on the same contig in 4 isolates, but blaCTX−M was on separate contigs that could not be confidently assigned to a plasmid; therefore, we could not determine whether the two genes were co-located. Future long-read sequencing would be required to clarify the genetic context and co-mobilization potential of these resistance determinants. A particularly concerning finding in our study was detection of the plasmid-mediated fosfomycin resistance gene fosA6 (n = 1 isolate) encoding a glutathione transferase inactivating fosfomycin in one isolate. Given the recent reintroduction of fosfomycin into clinical practice for the treatment of infections sustained by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae36 in human medicine, the co-occurrence of mcr-1 in association with blaCTX−M, rmtB and foA6 is worrisome issue. Similarly, mcr and blaCTX−M−55 producing E. coli resistant to fosfomycin and aminoglycosides have been reported from various animal species in France37. In addition to the plasmid-encoded colistin resistance determinant, mcr-1 gene, several isolates also carried amino acid substitutions in pmrA and pmrB genes. These included pmrB D283G and Y358N, (co-harbored by 11 isolates), P220S (1 isolate) or V161M (1 isolate) and pmrA G144S (2 isolates) changes (Table 1). Although the mcr gene-related resistance is the most well-documented mechanism in E. coli, chromosomal resistance mechanisms are less common and frequently restricted to pmrA and pmrB alterations38. Among the mutations detected, D283G, Y358N, V161M/G changes in PmrB, and G144S in PmrA, have been previously reported in both colistin- susceptible and -resistant isolates, suggesting a limited contribution to resistance individually38–40. The pmrB P220S substitution, however, has not been previously documented in association with colistin resistance, and its role remains uncertain. These findings support the conclusion that colistin resistance in our isolates is primarily mediated by plasmid-borne mcr-1 gene. According to phenotypic AST results all mcr-positive isolates were characterized as MDR strains showing resistance to, ceftazidime/ceftriaxone/cefepime (100%), sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (100%), ciprofloxacin (93%), tetracycline (86.6%), gentamicin (86.6%), chloramphenicol (80%) and amikacin (60%). However, all isolates remained susceptible to imipenem, nitrofurantoin, and tigecycline (Table 2; Fig. 1). The multidrug-resistant profiles observed may reflect farm-level antimicrobial practices. Diarrheic calves were routinely treated with cephalosporins, tetracyclines, quinolones, sulfonamides, and colistin, which can select for ESBL-producing E. coli carrying blaCTX−M and plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes such as mcr-141.

Comparison between phenotypic and WGS-based genotypic antimicrobial susceptibility profile demonstrated a high correlation between the two AST approaches with 194 out of 195 test results (99.48%,) being in agreement across 13 tested antibiotics, including colistin. The only discrepancy was found in a single isolate that exhibited phenotypic resistance to tetracycline despite lacking a known tetracycline resistance gene in its genome.

Plasmid incompatibility groups and stability of mcr-1-mediated colistin resistance

Plasmid incompatibility (Inc) group analysis using PlasmidFinder revealed that, the mcr-1-positive MDR E. coli isolates carried a diverse range of plasmid replicons, with each strain harboring between two and eight different replicons (Table 3). The detected incompatibility groups included IncI2(delta), IncF family, IncI1(alpha), IncH, IncB/O/K/Z, IncX, IncY, IncP, IncQ, Col156, and Col440II. In four isolates (F12, M34, C12, and S98) each belonging to distinct sequence types (ST162, ST88, ST2345, and ST5229 respectively), Illumina sequencing data showed that mcr-1 gene and IncI2 replicon were located on the same contig. This strongly suggests that the mcr-1 gene was carried on IncI2 plasmids in these strains. Figure 2 shows comparative analysis of mcr-1-bearing elements in the above mentioned four isolates with a mcr-1-bearing IncI2 plasmid (pECO-POL-29_mcr1) which was reported to carry mcr-1 in clinical E. coli isolates in China42. The result exhibited that mcr-1 carrying elements of E. coli stains from calves in our study showed high similarity with that of mcr-1-positive human isolates highlighting the risk of mcr-1-bearing-IncI2 plasmid dissemination among various hosts. While analysis of the reconstructed IncI2 plasmid backbone did not reveal additional resistance genes within this plasmid, the conjugation-associated genes, such as virB/virD4 were detected which allow plasmid transfer between various bacterial hosts through a type IV secretion system (T4SS) as conjugative machinery. Additionally a BLAST search of mcr-1-harboring contig against the NCBI database in the remaining 11 bacteria, showed high identity (> 99%) to other IncI2-type mcr-1-harboring plasmids (accession numbers: MK542639.1, CP127302, AP027580.1, KY471315.1, CP029184.1) characterized worldwide (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi; accessed on 7 June 2025). Following the initial discovery of mcr-1 on an IncI2 plasmid, by Liu et al.4, mcr-1 has been detected on a wide range of conjugative plasmids with IncI2, IncX4 and IncHI2 being the most prevalent replicon types facilitating the global dissemination of colistin resistance in both human and animal sources43–46. While IncX4 plasmids are largely characterized by carrying only the mcr-1 gene, IncI2 plasmids occasionally harbor additional resistance determinants, and IncHI2 plasmids frequently carry multiple antimicrobial resistance genes including ESBLs, enhancing their clinical relevance and potential for co-selection47–49. The mcr-1 was found to be harbored by IncI2 and IncX4-type plasmids among mcr-1-positive E. coli from human and animal sources in Switzerland, with the food chain being proposed as a major transmission route for mcr-1-carrying plasmids to human46. Similarly, Ding et al. demonstrated high sequence similarity between the mcr-1-bearing IncI2 (delta) plasmids from human fecal isolates and raw meat isolates reinforcing the hypothesis that IncI2 (delta) plasmids are key vectors facilitating dissemination of mcr-1 along the food chain and humans50. Considering the higher prevalence of IncI2 (100%, n = 15 isolates) compared to IncX4 (20%, n = 3 isolates) and IncHI2 plasmid types (6.6%, n = 1 isolate) among the mcr-1-positive isolates in our study, IncI2 plasmid appears to be the most common replicon type associated with mcr-1 among the studied bacterial population.

Plasmid stability assay was performed by continuous subculturing of mcr-1-positive isolates in colistin-free EMB agar media for 10 consecutive days. Throughout the experiment, no changes were observed in colistin MIC values and the mcr-1 gene remained detectable by PCR in all isolates. These findings indicated that the mcr-1-carrying elements exhibited high stability in their respective host strains, even in the absence of antibiotic selective pressure. This observation align with previous reports demonstrating high stability of mcr-bearing IncI2 plasmids51. Wang et al. reported a high stability for mcr-1-carrying IncHI2 plasmid in their host strains across multiple time points over 96 h52. Similarly, a recent study showed that IncX4- and IncI2-type mcr-1 plasmids were stably maintained in transconjugants following 35 days of serial passage in the absence qof colistin selection pressure26. Collectively, these findings highlight the epidemiological significance of stable mcr-1-bearing plasmids. The predominance of IncI2 plasmids together with the demonstrated stability of mcr-1-mediated resistance in the absence of colistin selection suggests that these resistance elements may persist in bacterial populations within dairy farm environments even without direct antimicrobial pressure. Such persistence in animal microbiota, manure, and farm surroundings can facilitate long-term maintenance and horizontal transfer of mcr-1 among Enterobacteriaceae and increase the risk of dissemination along the food chain in a One Health context.

Characterization of virulence-associated genes

Virulence gene analysis using VirulenceFinder revealed that mcr-1-positive E. coli isolates possessed numerous virulence-associated genes. These included genes related to serum resistance (iss), adhesins (fimH, fdeC, csgA, lpfA, faeC, yehABCD fimbrial operon, papC, papA), iron acquisition systems (sitA, fyuA, iroN, iucC, iutA, irp2), toxins (hlyE, hlyF, astA) and bacteroicins, including colicin-encoding genes (cib, cea). Additional elements included the tellurium ion resistance protein (terC), the conjugal transfer activator gene (traJ) typically associated with F-like plasmid, and several other virulence factors with varied prevalence, as indicated in Table 3. Of particular note, the astA gene, encoding the enteroaggregative heat-stable enterotoxin (EAST1), was detected in six isolates, including two from healthy and four from diarrheic calves. This gene constitutes an important virulence determinant in the enteric E. coli strains and has been associated with diarrheal disease in animals, contributing to economic losses in livestock industries53. Moreover, EAST1-producing E. coli isolates have been implicated in outbreaks of diarrhea in humans, affecting both children and adults in multiple countries54–56. Specifically, the ST167 isolates A85 and A21 carried multiple virulence-associated genes, including astA and several adhesins and iron acquisition genes, suggesting potential extraintestinal and enteric pathogenic traits. The presence of these strains highlights the potential to cause human infections through zoonotic transmission and food-chain contamination, representing a significant public health concern.

While these findings provide important insights into the virulence and resistance profiles of E. coli in calves, this study has several limitations. First, samples were collected from a single farm, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other livestock systems in Iran, as differences in farm management and antimicrobial use can influence the prevalence and co-occurrence of resistance genes such as mcr-1 and blaCTX−M. Second, although short-read sequencing allowed the identification of mcr-1 and associated plasmid replicons, long-read sequencing was not performed, preventing full resolution of plasmid structures. Third, conjugation assays were not conducted, limiting direct assessment of the transferability of mcr-1-bearing plasmids. Finally, detailed quantitative information on antimicrobial usage at the farm was not available, which limits our ability to correlate resistance patterns with specific antimicrobial exposure.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first WGS-based genomic characterization of mcr-1-positive MDR E. coli isolates from calves in Iran. Notably, these isolates also co-harboring blaCTX-M and rmtB, along with multiple virulence-associated genes such as astA, and were recovered from both healthy and diarrheic calves. The majority of these mcr-1-harbouring isolates exhibited resistance to a broad range of clinically important antimicrobials including cephalosporins, quinolones, tetracyclines, phenicols, sulfonamides and several aminoglycosides leaving very limited therapeutic options for treatment. The presence of these MDR and potentially virulent E. coli strains in calves from the investigated farm has One health implications, as they may pose a risk of zoonotic transmission to farmworkers and contribute to environmental contamination via manure and water, facilitating further dissemination across the farm ecosystem. These findings underscore the need for broader surveillance across different livestock production systems in the country. Furthermore, the detection of mcr-1 among clonally diverse isolates suggests that the dissemination of this gene is likely driven by “epidemic” vectors (particularly those of the IncI2 type) rather than expansion of specific bacterial clones. Together, these results emphasize the importance of effective antimicrobial stewardship in food-producing animals and ongoing monitoring of mobile resistance determinants along the food chain.

Methods and material

Bacterial isolation and screening for colistin resistant enteric bacteria

Fecal samples were collected as rectal swabs from diarrheic (n = 350) and healthy (n = 150) calves aged 1 to 9 months on a commercial cattle farm located in the northwest of Iran. The farm was selected due to its large herd size and intensive production system, which provides an epidemiologically relevant setting for investigating the occurrence and potential dissemination of antimicrobial resistance. Written informed consent was obtained from the farm owner prior to sample collection. Diarrheic calves had received routine veterinary treatment, including antibiotics commonly used in Iran such as tetracyclines, cephalosporins, quinolones, sulfonamides, and colistin. Healthy calves had not received any medical interventions and were maintained under routine farm feeding. Rectal swab samples were collected from standing animals using gentle physical restraint without anesthesia, as the procedure is minimally invasive and does not cause significant discomfort. The collected fecal samples were cultured on Eosin methylene Blue (EMB) Agar to isolate enteric bacteria. To screen for colistin resistance, bacterial colonies grown on EMB agar were inoculated in to Mueller Hinton broth (MHB) supplemented with 2 mg/L colistin, at a final concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/ml in 96- well microtiter plates and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. A 10 µl sample was transferred from microtiter wells with visible bacterial growth to EMB agar supplemented with 2 mg/L colistin. Recovered colonies were subjected to phenotypic identification using standard biochemical tests57 and colistin MIC determination.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

Colistin MICs were determined by the broth microdilution technique using MHB from Difco and colistin sulfate from Glentham life sciences (UK). MICs were measured in manually prepared 96-well microtiter plates according to CLSI guidelines. The Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method was used for testing susceptibility to the following 12 antimicrobial agents: ceftazidime (CZ), ceftriaxone (CR), cefepime (CF), imipenem (IM), gentamicin (GN), amikacin (AK), tetracycline (TE), tigecycline (TGC), ciprofloxacin (CP), sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SXT), chloramphenicol (CH) and nitrofurantoin (NT). Interpretation of the results was based on CLSI breakpoints (M100-ED34) for most antibiotics. For tigecycline and colistin, breakpoints specified by European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, V14.0) for Enterobacteriaceae were used: tigecycline MIC ≤ 0.5 mg/L was considered susceptible and > 0.5 mg/L resistant; colistin MIC ≤ 2 mg/L was considered susceptible and >2 mg/L resistant. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as a quality-control strain for antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Detection of mcr genes by PCR

DNA was extracted from colistin resistant bacteria using boiling method as described previously58. Detection of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance genes mcr-1 to mcr-5 was performed using the commercially available Taq DNA Polymerase Master Mix RED (Ampliqon, Denmark) with previously published primers4,8–11. PCR was carried out under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 35 s (denaturation), 51–56 °C for 35 s (annealing, depending on the primer), and 72 °C for 45 s (extension), with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. A negative control (nuclease-free water) was included in each PCR run. The amplified PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel in 1X TBE buffer and visualized under UV light.

Whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on mcr-1-positive Enterobacteriaceae isolates using the Illumina platform (Illumina, CA, USA) as described previously59. Briefly, genomic DNA was extracted using the Maxwell® RSC Pathogen Total Nucleic Acid Kit (Promega), supplemented with 5 µL of lysozyme solution, following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quantification was conducted using the Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). High-quality DNA samples were used for library preparation with the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform with paired-end reads (2 × 150 bp). Raw reads were quality-trimmed and filtered prior to de novo assembly, and the genome assemblies were generated using Unicycler v0.4.8. The resulting assemblies, including both chromosomal and plasmid-derived contigs, were annotated using Prokka (v1.14) with default parameters60. Plasmid replicons were identified using PlasmidFinder, and contigs carrying antimicrobial resistance genes were inspected to determine plasmid association. In 4 out of 15 isolates, the mcr-1 gene and the IncI2 plasmid replicon were detected on the same assembled contig, confirming plasmid localization of the resistance determinant. To further characterize mcr-1-bearing plasmids, putative plasmid contigs were compared against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTn to identify the closest reference plasmids. These references were subsequently used for comparative genomic analysis and visualization with BRIG v0.95 to assess sequence homology with IncI2(delta)-type mcr-1-bearing plasmids, including the reference plasmid available in GenBank (accession no. PP721192).

Whole genome sequence data were additionally analyzed using online tools available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology website (http://www.genomicepidemiology.org/services/), to determine multilocus sequence types (MLST 2.0), antimicrobial resistance genes and chromosomal mutations associated with antimicrobial resistance (ResFinder v4.6.0 as the primary tool, with KmerResistance v2.2 used for confirmation), plasmid replicon types (PlasmidFinde v2.1), and virulence genes (VirulenceFinder 2.0). Raw FASTQ files for all strains were deposited in the NCBI sequence read archive with BioProject accession number PRJNA1262825.

Evaluation of stability of mcr-1-mediated colistin resistance

To assess the stability of the mcr-1-harboring plasmids in the absence of antibiotic selective pressure, serial passages of nine mcr-1- positive strains each representing a distinct sequence type were performed on antibiotic-free EMB agar every 24 h for 10 consecutive days. Following the final passage, 10 colonies were randomly selected from each culture and tested for colistin susceptibility using MIC determination, alongside PCR amplification of the mcr-1 gene to confirm gene retention.
