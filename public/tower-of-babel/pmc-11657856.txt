Transcriptomic alterations in the ovine caruncular endometrium due to imbalanced nutrition and FSH-induced ovarian hyperstimulation
Authors: Özlem Bedir, Miguel Tavares Pereira, Hubert Rehrauer, Anna Grazul-Bilska, Mariusz P. Kowalewski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImbalanced diet and exogenous gonadotrophins affect uterine function and morphology. In sheep, FSH-induced superovulation alters implantation-related gene expression, influenced by both treatment and diet. In this study, we used deep RNA sequencing (NGS, RNA-Seq) to expand our understanding of these effects on the caruncular endometrium.MethodsEwes (n = 3–5/group) were separated into control fed (CF), overfed (OF), and underfed (UF) groups, with each group subdivided between FSH (superovulated; SOV) or saline (negative controls; CONT) treatment. Caruncular samples were collected on day 10 of diestrus of the subsequent estrous cycle, with samples from CF_CONT also collected on day 5 to assess time-dependent changes.ResultsThe 1484 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05) identified between CF_CONT animals at days 5 and 10 were predominantly associated with increased immune activity and cellular metabolic processes and cellular proliferation. In CONT animals, imbalanced nutrition (i.e., both OF and UF) was associated with enrichment of terms associated with cell adhesion and differentiation, immune response and angiogenesis. The FSH carry-over effects resulted in a higher number of DEGs in CF animals (1374), than in OF (168) or UF (18), mostly associated with dysregulation of cell cycle and hormonal sensitivity.ConclusionThe absence of genes concurrently affected by superovulation (SOV) in all feeding regimes indicates that the effects of FSH on the caruncular transcriptome are multidirectional and dependent upon body condition. Therefore, the homeostasis of ovine caruncles is influenced by both body condition and superovulation (SOV), potentially affecting uterine receptivity.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10799-w.

Background

Early pregnancy loss is a common obstetric complication observed in both humans and domestic animals. Studies have reported varying rates of early pregnancy loss, with ranges of 10 to 28% in humans [1, 2], approximately 35% in cattle, and 20 to 30% in pigs [3]. A common cause of early pregnancy loss is the failure of implantation [4], i.e., the inability of the blastocyst to attach to the uterine wall, which is a critical step for embryo-maternal interaction and the establishment of the placenta. The success of implantation relies on a coordinated molecular dialogue between the blastocyst and maternal endometrium, mediated by a plethora of factors, i.e., cytokines, growth factors, prostaglandins, extracellular matrix-modulators and adhesion molecules [5, 6]. The impairment of this crosstalk can be associated with various factors, of both the maternal and embryonal origin, also involving infectious agents [3, 7–9]. With regard to maternal factors, feeding regimens and hormonal manipulation have been the target of intensive research, not only in humans, but also in domestic mammals [10–13]. The associated effects exerted upon the uterus are of particular importance and create a foundation for the current study. The impact of imbalanced nutrition on fertility can be related to its disruptive effects on the endocrine system. In undernutrition, there is a negative energy balance that can result in impaired or decreased production of gonadotropins and other reproductive hormones [14–17]. On the other hand, obesity-related reproductive dysfunction in humans, mice, and sheep is characterized by metabolic and endocrine disturbances, including insulin and leptin resistance, impaired adipokines, secretion of insulin-like growth factors, changes in steroidogenesis and gonadotropin response, which are associated with complications including ovarian and uterine dysfunction [18–20]. Similarly, sheep under extreme feeding regimes, i.e., both under dietary excess (overfed) or restriction (underfed), present delayed puberty, reduced pregnancy rates, and decreased efficiency of in vitro fertilization (IVF) [21, 22]. Imbalanced nutrition in sheep is associated with alterations in the uterine expression of hormonal receptors (ESR1, ESR2, and PGR), as well as insulin-like growth factors (IGF-1 and − 2) and VEGFA, in caruncles, potentially affecting the responsiveness of the uterus to implantation [23]. Recently, we have demonstrated an upregulation of various integrins and mucin 1 in the caruncles of overfed and underfed ewes on day 10 of the estrous cycle, suggesting a potential disruption of implantation due to diet-related changes in uterine receptivity [24].

FSH-mediated superovulation has been widely employed in assisted reproductive technologies and research in various mammalian species to facilitate oocyte collection and embryo production [25, 26]. However, hormonal manipulations can also have unpredictable effects on reproductive function [27–29]. Thus, an acute increase in FSH activity during the later stages of cumulus-oocyte complex (COC) maturation has the potential to restrict oocyte developmental competence in both humans and animals [12]. Furthermore, changes in uterine morphology were observed in FSH-superovulated sheep, including increased inclusions of lipid droplets in the cytoplasm of both epithelial endometrial surface and glandular epithelial cells [30]. Superovulation is also associated with an apparent modulation of PGR protein expression in ovine inter-caruncular uterine compartments under different feeding regimes [31]. At the mRNA level, the administration of FSH results in increased expression of PGR, as well as ESR1 and IGF1 in the caruncles of ewes that are on a maintenance diet. This effect was shown to be most prominent during mid-estrus of the subsequent cycle, indicating carry-over effects [23]. Importantly, the impact of FSH treatment on uterine functionality seems to be modulated, to some extent, by the body condition [23]. Accordingly, the expression of ESR1 is upregulated in the caruncles of FSH-treated underfed ewes, when compared with untreated animals, whereas ESR1 is downregulated by superovulation in overfed animals [23]. Furthermore, the caruncular expression of several subunits of integrins, e.g., ITGA4, ITGA5, ITGAV and ITGB5, but also of fibronectin 1 (FN1) increased after superovulation in overfed ewes at mid-estrous cycle [24]. In contrast, in underfed animals, the expression of ITGB1 and FN1 decreases in the uterine caruncles after FHS treatment [24].

Cumulatively, considering the possible impact that nutritional and hormonal imbalance appears to have on uterine receptivity, the aim of this follow-up study was to expand our knowledge by performing a genome-wide analysis of uterine caruncles of sheep using next generation RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). Caruncles are an integral part of the uterine endometrium in ruminants. They represent specialized aglandular parts of the uterus which, during pregnancy, interdigitate with fetal cotyledons, creating together placentomes, and thereby becoming the maternal part of the placenta (placenta materna) [32]. Their functionality thus determines the outcome of superficial implantation and placentation in sheep, as in other ruminants. The impact of nutritional imbalance was assessed using samples from nonpregnant animals kept under different feeding regimes, including maintenance/control diet, overfed and underfed, collected on day 10 of the estrous cycle (i.e. 10 days after ovulation). This time point marks the initiation of embryo-maternal communication and recognition of pregnancy. The FSH-induced effects were assessed in animals that had undergone superovulation during the previous estrous cycle. Furthermore, the effects of time were evaluated by comparing samples from non-superovulated, control fed animals at days 5 and 10 following ovulation.

Materials and methods

Animals, tissue samples and experimental design

This follow-up study used caruncular samples from 29 non-pregnant, non-lactating, 3 to 5 year-old Rambouillet ewes, derived from previous studies [23, 24, 33, 34]. The animals belonged to the North Dakota State University (NDSU) farm. The study has been approved by the NDSU’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (permit number A12013). The animals were randomly divided into three feeding groups: the maintenance/control fed (CF, n = 13) group received 100% of National Research Council (NRC) requirements, i.e., 2.4 Mcal of metabolizable energy (ME) / kg body weight (BW); overfed (OF, n = 9) animals received 200% of NRC requirements; whereas underfed (UF, n = 7) animals received 60% of the calories required by NRC. All sheep were in good health throughout the duration of the experiment. Sheep were fed twice daily (at 8:00 and 15:00), and they consumed all feed during the day.

More details regarding estrus synchronization, feeding process and diet composition were provided previously [23]. Briefly, estrus was synchronized in all animals using intravaginal controlled internal drug release (CIDR) devices for 14 days, and the day of estrus was recorded as day 0. To induce superovulation (SOV), the animals were then treated twice daily with decreasing amounts of FSH (FSH-P; Sioux Biochemical, Sioux Center, IA, USA) for three consecutive days of the estorus cycle, i.e., 5 mg/injection on day 13, 4 mg/injection on day 14 and 3 mg/injection on day 15. Animals from the control groups (CONT) received the same volume of saline (10 ml, 0.9% NaCl in water) twice daily on the same three days of the estrous cycle. Caruncular endometrial samples were collected on day 10 of the subsequent estrous cycle (i.e., on day 10 after ovulation of the first estrous cycle after superovulation/saline). Tissue samples were immediately dissected, frozen on dry ice and stored at -80ºC until use. Prior to collecting the samples, ewes were restrained with a head harness and slaughtered by using captive bolt gun to the forehead followed by immediate exsanguination in the surgery room at the Animal Nutrition and Physiology Center at NDSU. Overall, the present work included 6 groups of animals with samples collected on day 10 of the estrous cycle, divided between the following groups: maintenance/control fed and non-superovulated (n = 4, CF_CONT_D10), maintenance/control fed superovulated (n = 4, CF_SOV_D10), overfed not superovulated (n = 5, OF_CONT_D10), overfed and superovulated (n = 4, OF_SOV_D10), underfed not superovulated (n = 4, UF_CONT_D10) and underfed superovulated (n = 3, UF_SOV_D10). Additionally, to investigate time-dependent effects during early luteal phase, caruncular samples from control fed, non-treated animals were collected on day 5 of the subsequent estrous cycle (CF_CONT_D5, n = 5).

Total RNA isolation and purification

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific AG, Reinach, Switzerland) was used to measure the RNA purity and concentration. Further purification of RNA was achieved using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) according to the RNA cleanup protocol provided by the manufacturer. The integrity of RNA was then evaluated with the Agilent 2200 TapeStation System (Agilent, Waldbronn, Germany), with RNA integrity numbers (RIN) of the samples submitted for sequencing ranging between 8.0 and 9.8.

RNA-Sequencing (NGS, RNA-Seq)

Genome-wide quantitative evaluation of gene expression was performed by sequencing the total isolated RNA using next generation sequencing (NGS) methodology. Library preparation, cluster generation and sequencing were performed following the methodology previously described [35], with differences in the equipment and kits used. Briefly, the quality and quantity of the purified total RNA of the 29 samples was further verified with a Fragment Analyzer (Agilent). Library preparation was conducted for each RNA sample (100–1000 ng) with the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold kit (Illumina, Inc., California, USA). All RNA samples were enriched by ribosomal depletion and underwent reverse-transcription. Following this, cDNA samples were randomly fragmented, end-repaired, polyadenylated and ligated to TruSeq adapters for multiplexing. PCR was then performed to selectively amplify fragments containing adapters on both ends. Finally, the Fragment Analyzer (Agilent) was used to evaluate the quality and quantity of enriched libraries. The product was a smear with an average fragment size of approximately 260 bp. Libraries were then normalized to 10 nM in 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH = 8.5) with 0.1% Tween 20. Cluster generation and sequencing were conducted in a Novaseq 6000 (Illumina, Inc.), with sequencing being single-end 100 bp. The obtained fastq files, used for downstream analysis, were also deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information under the GEO Series accession number GSE229048.

Data analysis

The SUSHI framework, developed by the Functional Genomics Center of Zurich (FGCZ ETH/UZH, Zurich, Switzerland), was used for the analysis of data [36, 37].

Raw data was trimmed with the fastp tool. The options that were explicitely set for fastp were “--trim_front1 2 --trim_tail1 5 --average_qual 10 --adapter_sequence = AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCA–length_required 20”, which translates into: trim the adapter, trim 2 bases from the read start, trim 5 bases from the read end, filter all reads with an average quality below 10, and require a minimum length of 20nt after trimming. The alignment was done using Spliced Transcripts Alignment to a Reference (STAR) mapper with the Ensembl genome build Oar_rambouillet_v1.0 as reference. The function featureCounts from the R package Rsubread was used to compute gene expression levels [38], followed by quality control with the CountQC app provided in the SUSHI framework. The CountQC provided visualization and was used to confirm the consistency of the data without changing the data. Details on sequencing depth, expressed as mio reads per sample, are provided in the Additional Table 1. A gene was identified as expressed when presenting a minimum average of 10 reads in at least one batch of replicates. Pairwise comparison was performed by defining contrasts between different groups, and gene differential expression for each contrast was evaluated using the generalized linear model approach from the Bioconductor package DESeq2 [39]. The Wald test was used to determine the significance of differential expression, followed by a correction of multiple testing with the Benjamin-Hochberg algorithm, that computes the false discovery rate (FDR, adjusted P-value), following previously described methodologies [35]. The determination of differentially expressed genes (DEG) was defined using P < 0.01 and FDR < 0.05 as thresholds. The contrasts, i.e. pairwise comparisons, were established to assess: (1) the time dependent effects, by comparing day 5 and day 10 CF; (2) the feeding effects, by comparing CF animals with their OF and UF counterparts; and finally (3) the impact of FSH treatment within feeding groups (CF, UF and OF with/without FSH). The entire lists of DEG identified for each contrast were used for downstream analyses and are provided in Additional Table 2 (for time-dependent effects), Additional Table 3 (for feeding effects) and Additional Table 4 (for superovulation effects under different feeding regimes). The determination of enriched gene ontologies (GOs) was performed with the online tool DAVID (Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery, https://david.ncifcrf.gov) and further validated with Enrichr (http://amp.pharm.mssm.edu/Enrichr/). Enriched functional biological networks were identified and visualized using the software ClueGO v2.5.1 [40] on the Cytoscape v3.6 software platform [41]. Finally, the most significantly affected canonical pathways and potential upstream regulators were predicted using the software Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA, Qiagen, Redwood City, CA, USA). Additional Table 5 lists up to 20 representative genes associated with specific functional terms (gene ontologies, functional networks, canonical pathways, and upstream regulators) from each of the comparisons. To identify DEGs (P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05) concomitantly affected by FSH treatment in different feeding regimens, a Venn diagram was generated with Venny 2.0 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index2.0.2.html). Additional Table 6 provides complete lists of the DEGs present at each group and intersections of the Venn diagram.

Semi-quantitative real-time TaqMan PCR

The relative mRNA abundance of 19 selected genes was evaluated by TaqMan PCR as described previously [23]. All primers and TaqMan probes are listed in Table 1. If not commercially available, the 6-carboxyfluorescein (6-FAM)- and 6- carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) labeled probes and primers were designed with the Primer Express software version 2.0 (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific), based on publicly available sequences, and were ordered from Microsynth AG (Balgach, Switzerland). The commercially available TaqMan systems were purchased from Applied Biosystems. The efficiency of systems was evaluated using the CT slope, which was set at approximately 100%. To eliminate possible genomic DNA contamination, the RQ1 RNase-free DNase kit (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, CH) was used, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Using random hexamers as primers and reagents from Applied Biosystems, reverse transcription was performed in an Eppendorf Mastercycler (Vaudaux-Eppendorf AG, Basel, CH). For each sample and reaction, cDNA corresponding to 100 ng total RNA was used for semi-quantitative PCR, using an automated ABI PRISM 7500 Sequence Detection System cycler (Applied Biosystems). All samples were analyzed in duplicate in 96-well optical plates. Autoclaved water was used as a non-template control instead of cDNA. To detect possible genomic DNA contamination, non-reverse transcribed samples were also evaluated (RT-minus control). The comparative CT (∆∆CT) method was used to calculate relative gene expression according to the ABI 7500 manufacturer and as described previously [42, 43]. The sample with the lowest expression served as a calibrator for calculating relative gene expression. As reference genes GAPDH and ACTB were used to evaluate target gene relative expression [44]. Stability of reference genes was assessed by using online tool RefFinder (https://www.ciidirsinaloa.com.mx/RefFinder-master/), that incorporates several major computational programs (BestKeeper, Normfinder and geNorm). Both reference genes were found suitable for evaluation of data. Possible changes in relative gene expression between the groups were evaluated with an unpaired two-tailed Student`s t-test using GraphPad 3.06 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA); P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Initial evaluation of sequencing results

The CountQC app provided in the SUSHI framework was used to perform the quality assessment of sequencing data. The initial evaluation of intra-group homogeneity, using a samples correlation matrix (Fig. 1A), identified samples with divergent transcriptional profiles. These included samples UF_SOV_142, OF_SOV_149, and CF_CONT_6, which had low correlation coefficients when compared with the other samples in their treatment groups (Fig. 1A). Thus, to improve homogeneity (Fig. 1B), these outliers were removed from downstream analyses. In the heatmap containing the 2,000 genes with higher variation among all analyzed samples, here appeared to be a marked difference between the samples collected on days 5 and 10 (Fig. 1C, yellow and blue rectangles). Furthermore, nutrition appeared to have a greater impact than FSH treatment upon the transcriptome, as differences within some clusters (Fig. 1C, green rectangles) could be observed for non-superovulated animals under different feeding regimes.

Pairwise comparisons, including functional annotations

Time-, feeding- and superovulation-dependent effects were evaluated through a differential expression analysis (pairwise). The DESeq2 package for Bioconductor was used to evaluate the following contrasts (Table 2): “CF_CONT_D10 over CF_CONT_D5”, “OF_CONT over CF_CONT”, “UF_CONT over CF_CONT”, “CF_SOV over CF_CONT”, “OF_SOV over OF_CONT”, “UF_SOV over UF_CONT”. A total of 20,921 genes were detected in the transcriptomic analysis, but only those genes with counts greater than the threshold of 10 reads in at least one group were considered as expressed, with values ranging between 13,792 and 14,371 expressed genes within the different contrasts. Genes were assumed to be differentially expressed if P < 0.01 and false discovery rate (FDR, adjusted P-value) < 0.05. The numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for all contrasts are presented in Table 2, while all of the DEGs are listed in Additional Tables 2, 3 and 4. The functional characterization of DEGs from each contrast was performed by evaluating enriched gene ontology (GO) terms related to the domain biological process (BP) with the DAVID online tool, further corroborated with Enrichr. Furthermore, overrepresented functional networks were visualized with the ClueGO plug-in for the Cytoscape platform. Finally, IPA software was used for prediction of affected canonical pathways and upstream regulators. Lists of representative genes and statistical details for each term are presented in Additional Table 5.

Time-dependent effects

Time-dependent effects were assessed by comparing control fed untreated (CF_CONT) samples collected on days 5 and 10 of the estrous cycle. A total of 1484 DEGs (P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05) could be identified, with 651 being up- and 834 downregulated at day 10, when compared with day 5 (Table 2; Fig. 2, Additional Table 2).

Gene ontologies associated with biological processes overrepresented on day 5 included cell division (P = 4.91 × 10− 10), cell adhesion (P = 1.87 × 10− 5), integrin-mediated signaling pathways (P = 7.05 × 10− 4), epithelial cell differentiation (P = 1.9 × 10− 3) and cell cycle (P = 4.7 × 10− 3) (Fig. 2, Additional Table 5). On the other hand, genes more highly represented at day 10 were mainly related to functional terms including immune system process (P = 3.14 × 10− 2), immune response (P = 7.59 × 10− 2), lipid biosynthetic process (P = 8.05 × 10− 2), positive regulation of cell cycle (P = 1.9 × 10− 2), protein autophosphorylation (P = 2.2 × 10− 2) and intracellular signal transduction (P = 3.5 × 10− 2) (Fig. 2, Additional Table 5).

Feeding effect

The effects of unbalanced nutrition on the caruncular transcriptome at day 10 of the estrous cycle were evaluated by comparing samples from overfed and underfed animals with maintenance/control fed animals.

A total of 158 DEGs (P < 0.01 and FDR < 0.05) were identified for the contrast “OF_CONT over CF_CONT”. From these, 19 DEGs were downregulated and 139 DEGs were upregulated (Table 2; Fig. 4A, Additional Table 3). Biological processes overrepresented for genes upregulated in OF animals included epithelial cell-cell adhesion (P = 1.67 × 10− 3), epithelial cell differentiation (P = 2.44 × 10− 2), activation of GTPase mediated activity (P = 1.56 × 10− 2) and negative regulation of transforming growth factor beta (TGFB) 1 production (P = 2.79 × 10− 2), while Rap protein signal transduction (P = 9.1 × 10− 3) was highly represented in CF (Fig. 4A, Additional Table 5). The functional networks enriched for these upregulated DEGs were associated with epithelial cell differentiation, negative regulation of T cell proliferation and cell-cell junction assembly (Additional Fig. 1, Additional Table 5). Due to the low number of DEGs, no functional networks could be identified for the downregulated genes, and analysis of canonical pathways was not possible for this contrast.

Superovulation (FSH treatment) effect in different feeding regimes

Samples from treated and untreated/control animals under the same feeding regime were compared to evaluate any carry-over changes in the caruncular transcriptome signature on day 10 of the estrous cycle after FSH-induced superovulation. Under maintenance feeding conditions, FSH treatment was associated with the differential expression of 1374 genes (P < 0.01 and FDR < 0.05). From those, 799 DEGs were upregulated while 575 DEGs were downregulated (Fig. 6A; Table 2, Additional Table 4). DEGs upregulated in superovulated animals were associated with translation (P = 2.57 × 10− 23), negative regulation of the apoptotic signaling pathway (P = 6.18 × 10− 3), response to oxidative stress (P = 1.4 × 10− 2), cellular response to transforming growth factor beta stimulus (P = 2.76 × 10− 2), cell migration (P = 3.1 × 10− 2), and steroid biosynthesis (P = 4.81 × 10− 2) (Fig. 6A, Additional Table 5). As for the DEGs downregulated in the contrast “CF_SOV over CF_CONT”, enriched biological processes included cell cycle regulation (P = 4.36 × 10− 3), cytoskeleton organization (P = 6.51 × 10− 3), negative regulation of apoptotic process (P = 6.17 × 10− 3), and regulation of epithelial cell proliferation (P = 1.78 × 10− 3), gene expression (P = 4.39 × 10− 2), and the transforming growth factor beta receptor signaling pathway (P = 5.07 × 10− 2) (Fig. 6A, Additional Table 5).

Next, the identification of genes simultaneously modulated in the estrous cycle after FSH treatment under different feeding conditions was attempted. For this, the intersection of DEGs (i.e., P < 0.01 and FDR < 0.05, up- and downregulated) obtained from the three contrasts (control fed, overfed, or underfed) in superovulated animals was visualized with a Venn diagram (Fig. 6B, Additional Table 6). No gene that was concomitantly affected in all feeding regimens could be identified (Fig. 6B). However, twenty-six genes were commonly affected after FSH treatment in control fed and overfed animals, including IGF1R, NFIL3, IRS4 and, KCNJ8 (Fig. 6B, Additional Table 6). Furthermore, four genes could be identified in both animals that were underfed and those under maintenance feeding conditions, including CST6, SAA4 and PTGS2 (Fig. 6B, Additional Table 6).

The mRNA availability of all factors could be detected in all samples. Significant changes are shown in Table 3, whereas all mRNA evaluation results are presented in Additional Table 7. In general, mRNA quantification followed the expression pattern observed in transcriptomics analysis.

Discussion

Adversely modified maternal nutritional and hormonal status have been associated with negative effects on uterine receptivity in different animal species [14, 45, 46], and in humans [47–49]. Disruption of endometrial function during maternal recognition of pregnancy can potentially result in embryo loss, as the uterine environment plays a crucial role in the establishment of pregnancy and early conceptus development [3]. Here, our aim was to gain a deeper understanding of the molecular signals associated with the maternal body condition and modified hormonal milieu on the caruncular tissue transcriptome using deep RNA sequencing in an ovine model.

Time-dependent effect

Access to early diestrus samples allowed the investigation of the time-dependent changes in the caruncular transcriptome in control fed, but not superovulated, animals using tissue collected at days 5 and 10 of the estrous cycle. We found a predicted deactivation of estrogen receptor signaling-associated pathways, with ESR1 and beta-estradiol identified among the top upstream regulators for this contrast. While consistent with the reduction in ESR1 expression observed on day 10 of the estrous cycle [23], the concurrent decline in uterine ESR1 levels observed during that phase, along with the suppression of the oxytocin receptor (OXTR) and PGR, are considered necessary for establishing uterine receptivity in sheep [50]. Thus, the anticipated reduction in estradiol signaling serves to reinforce the accuracy of this transcriptomics analysis.

Traditionally, the induction of uterine tissue proliferation has been linked to estradiol signaling [51–53]. Activation of this signaling pathway was also observed in vitro in ovine endometrial epithelial cells [52]. In this regard, functional terms associated with cell growth and proliferation were enriched on day 5 of the estrous cycle and less represented at day 10. Conversely, on day 10, corresponding to the time point of the initiation of embryo-maternal recognition of pregnancy, we found an increased expression of FOXO1 and FOXO4. FOXOs, forkhead box O, are transcriptional factors involved in the regulation of the cell cycle and differentiation, growth and survival, but are also associated with cellular metabolism and apoptosis [54]. While the role of FOXO family members in the uterus of ewes is still poorly understood, FOXO1 was associated with the inhibition of sheep granulosa cell proliferation in vitro [55]. Thus, although still requiring further investigation, it is possible that FOXO family members play a role in the modulation of proliferative activity in the ovine endometrium.

Functional terms associated with integrins, and cell adhesion were enriched on day 5 of the estrous cycle. In agreement with these observations, the expression of ITGAV and ITGB3 was reported to decrease between days 5 and 10 of the estrous cycle [24], and the expression of ITGA2 was also observed to be decreased with the passage of time in the current study. The immunolocalization of a variety of integrin subunits, including ITGAV and ITGB3, was observed in the apical surface of the luminal epithelium and trophectoderm in different stages of pregnancy, suggesting their involvement in implantation and placentation [56]. Interestingly, however, the immunodetection of different subunits could be observed in the ovine uterus on day 11 of pregnancy, but not on day 9 [57]. Thus, it appears that there is a transitional decrease in the expression of different integrin subunits in the early estrous cycle, with localized increased expression around the time of implantation. However, it is important to note that since the current analysis was conducted in cyclic animals, the presence of an embryo could potentially impact the observed results.

On day 10 of the estrous cycle, functional terms associated with immune function and cellular metabolism were enriched. The modulation in immune response was further associated with an increased mRNA availability of IL1R1, and the presence of different immune-related factors, i.a., toll-like receptors, chemokines and interleukin (IL) 25 and − 6 receptors, among the upregulated DEGs. While not extensively studied in sheep, it is possible that some of these factors contribute to changes in the uterine immune environment during the peri-implantation period, which is crucial for embryo-maternal communication and successful implantation [58]. The expression of PTGS2, which is also associated with immune function, increased from day 5 to day 10. The availability of this prostaglandin synthase has previously been reported in the sheep uterus starting on day 12, but not on day 9 [59]. Prostaglandins play diverse roles in the ovine uterus, including mediating the responsiveness to interferon tau, which is crucial for maternal recognition in ruminants [60, 61]. Additionally, prostaglandins are involved in the processes of conceptus elongation and survival [60, 61].

Feeding effect

The impact of feeding regimes and, consequently, body condition, on the transcriptome of the caruncular endometrium on day 10 of diestrus was investigated by comparing samples collected from overfed and underfed animals with those from maintenance/control fed ewes. When compared with maintenance fed animals, a higher number of DEGs was identified in underfed (520 DEGs, P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05), than in overfed (158 DEGs, P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05), animals.

Altered nutrition, i.e., underfeeding and overfeeding, resulted in decreased expression of FOXO1 and FOXO4. Additionally, FOXO3 was among the predicted top upstream regulators in underfed animals. Furthermore, nutritional imbalance upregulated IHH and MSX1. IHH is a member of the hedgehog family and plays a crucial role in the regulation of cell differentiation, particularly during embryonic development [62]. In the murine uterus, IHH is expressed in the epithelial cells and its knockdown results in implantation failure and decreased cell cycle progression, associated with increased estrogen signaling [63–65]. Furthermore, it is regulated by P4 signaling, as shown in the mouse uterus [66]. Given this, the reported upregulation of PGR in the uterus of overfed and underfed ewes, as well as the increased availability of ESR1 [23] could be related to the increased presence of IHH observed in the current study. Moreover, the upregulation of IHH was associated with the enrichment of epithelial cell-cell adhesion and differentiation among the more highly represented functional networks in overfed animals.

A homeobox gene and HOX family member, MSX1, is linked to enhanced mouse uterine receptivity [67], although its expression in the uterus is decreased following implantation in humans and in mice [68, 69]. Therefore, dysregulation of cell proliferation and differentiation and, subsequently, uterine receptivity may be linked to the modification of forkhead box and homebox genes after imbalanced nutrition. This, however, remains to be addressed.

The possible involvement of hypoxia-related HIF1A attracted our attention, as by mediating cellular responses to hypoxia, HIF1A is involved in regulating placental morphogenesis, cell migration, angiogenesis, erythropoiesis, and cell metabolism [70, 71]. Its involvement in regulating placental function includes placental growth, remodeling, differentiation, transport and vascularization [72]. The transcriptional availability of HIF1A on day 10 of the estrous cycle was decreased in both over- and underfed animals, when compared with control animals. Concomitantly, the enrichment of terms associated with angiogenesis in downregulated genes has been associated with underfeeding. However, the VEGF signaling pathway was predicted to be activated in underfed animals, in agreement with a previous report of caruncular upregulation of VEGFA in both overfed and underfed ewes [23]. The heightened VEGF signaling could potentially serve as a compensatory mechanism in response to diet-induced vascular disturbances. In fact, in cows, nutritional restriction has been associated with modulation of cotyledon vascularity, early placentome differentiation, or elevation of the expression of angiogenic proteins [45, 46, 73].

When compared with control fed animals, the functional terms associated with immune function were included in the downregulated DEGs in the modified dietary groups, and were associated with the decreased availability of IL1R, TGFB1 and PTGS2. Furthermore, NF-κB signaling was predicted to be activated in underfed animals. NF-κB plays a central role in regulating both the innate and adaptive immune response [74]. Dysregulation of uterine cytokines in different mammals is associated with implantation failure and abnormal placental development [75]. Therefore, it is possible that the projected NF-κB activation could be related to a local immunological imbalance as a result of inadequate food intake, which might have negative consequences on maternal-conceptus communication.

With regard to IL1 signaling, the expression of IL1R1 has been detected in bovine uterine luminal and glandular epithelium and stroma during the estrous cycle and early pregnancy [76, 77], as well as in porcine, human, and murine endometrium [78–80]. Furthermore, its ligand, IL1β, is expressed in bovine, human, murine, porcine and canine embryos [81–84]. Besides its role as a proinflammatory interleukin, IL1β is associated with the regulation of trophoblast adhesion and invasion in humans by regulating, e.g., ITGB3 and matrix metalloproteinases [85, 86]. Although requiring further investigation, it is possible that there might be a similar role in the promotion of blastocyst adhesion in the sheep. Thus, the increased expression of IL1R observed on day 10 might be associated with increased uterine receptivity that could be disrupted by nutritional imbalance. Furthermore, as IL1β can modulate the expression of different prostaglandins [87], the decreased sensitivity of the uterus to IL1β might be associated with the observed downregulation of PTGS2, and, taking into consideration the reported role of this factor in concepto-maternal communication, have further detrimental effects in the establishment of pregnancy.

Finally, the leukemia inhibitory factor receptor (LIFR) was among the DEGs downregulated in underfed animals. This suggests the disruption of LIF signaling, a cytokine considered crucial for embryo implantation [88, 89], in underfed animals, further supporting the possible decrease of uterine receptivity.

Decreased nutritional intake was also associated with the activation of the integrin signaling pathway. This was supported by the previous observation of increased expression of ITGA2 in underfed animals when compared with control fed animals [24]. In the same study, increased expression of integrin subunits was also observed in underfed ewes compared with control fed animals on day 10 [24]. The increased availability of integrins on day 10 in underfed animals appears to conflict with the decreased expression of different integrins observed with the passage of time and might affect embryo-maternal interaction. Furthermore, in the present study, estradiol and ERBB2 were among the top upstream regulators in response to decreased feeding. Similarly, changes in the basic capability of caruncles to respond to hormones were observed previously, mirrored in the transcriptional changes of steroidogenic receptors (ESR1, ESR2 and PGR) in underfed animals [23].

Superovulation effects in different feeding regimes

Numerous studies have investigated the long-term impact of superovulation upon uterine physiology in different species, including humans [25, 90]. In light of the adverse effects observed, such as changes in fetal and placental weight, endometrial receptivity, and alterations in the expression of E2 and P4 receptors, some authors have proposed the transfer of cryopreserved embryos instead of fresh embryos [91–95]. Moreover, supraphysiological FSH concentrations may impair cumulus oocyte complex (COC) maturation and endometrial developmental competence [96]. Similarly, as shown in humans [97], the ovarian reserve gradually depletes, and age-related excessive intrafollicular FSH levels may further impair oocyte quality and, consequently, implantation. This impairment occurs by interfering with the transzonal delivery of molecules derived from cumulus cells, which are necessary for energy production, oxidative stress control, meiotic accuracy, DNA damage repair, and gene expression [12, 98]. These studies [98] demonstrated that basal FSH concentrations affect IVF/ICSI success in relation to maternal age.

The current study explored the potential adverse effects of administering supraphysiological levels of FSH on uterine health and its potential carry-over effects on the uterine transcriptome in a sheep model. Therefore, the caruncles were collected during the subsequent estrous cycle following hormonal treatment with FSH. Additionally, the potential influence of diet on these long-lasting effects was assessed in samples from both overfed and underfed animals.

The strongest effects of superovulation on the caruncular transcriptome were observed in control fed animals, as suggested by the increased number of DEGs (1374), when compared with overfed (168 DEGs) or underfed animals (18 DEGs).

Previous findings revealed a correlation between superovulation and elevated levels of ESR1 and PGR expression in sheep, as well as reduced ESR2 transcriptional availability. These changes were observed in ewes subjected to normal feeding conditions on day 10 of the cycle after treatment, apparently changing the physiological ratio in the expression of steroidogenic receptors [23]. The adverse effect of hyperstimulation with FSH on endometrial functionality was further implied by the increased expression of apoptosis-associated genes BAD and CASP3 after superovulation in control fed animals.

The Wnt/B-catenin signaling pathway has an important role in coordinating the uterus-embryo contact necessary for implantation [99, 100], and its involvement in regulating cell cycle progression and uterine gland development in sheep has been demonstrated [101]. Similarly, numerous Wnt-related factors are expressed in the neonatal sheep uterus, contributing to the regulation of postnatal uterine development and endometrial adenogenesis [99, 100]. In the present study, Wnt/B-catenin signaling was among the pathways predicted to be activated in control fed superovulated animals. Concomitantly, Wnt5B and Wnt4 were among the DEGs upregulated in control fed animals in response to superovulation. Wnt signaling has been shown to be modulated by estradiol in various organs and species, such as the murine uterus [102], where inhibition of the Wnt/B catenin signaling pathway led to reduced embryo implantation rates [99]. Interestingly, disruptions in Wnt signaling in the uterus have also been observed in women undergoing superovulation protocols [29, 91]. The modulation of Wnt signaling in FSH-treated animals, in addition to the effects exerted on growth factors and FOXOs signaling, appears to further support the presence of FSH carry-over effects on cell cycle disruption in ovine caruncles, potentially affecting endometrial development.

Growth factors encompass a range of functions, including the regulation of mitogenic and secretory activities, immunomodulation, and control over the expression of adhesion molecules [50, 75, 103]. The possible involvement of superovulation and the feeding scheme in modulating the production of stromal-derived growth factors (e.g. HGF, FGF10), insulin-like growth factors (IGF1 and IGF2) and VEGFA, in the ovine uterus during implantation was previously implied [23, 24]. Thus, FGF10 expression was increased in the ovine caruncular endometrium on day 10 of the estrous cycle following superovulation [24]. The current analysis appears to support these findings by showing an increase in FGF signaling in response to FSH treatment. In contrast, the signaling of other growth factors, such as EGF, IGF1, and HGF, was predicted to decrease following superovulation. Together with the previously demonstrated upregulated expression of IGF1 [23], FSH treatment led to an increase in TGFB1 expression in animals receiving control feed. However, concurrently, terms related to TGF receptor signaling were enriched in downregulated DEGs, and TGF signaling was expected to be deactivated following superovulation. Additionally, superovulation appeared to exhibit local immunomodulatory activity, as indicated by the higher expression of PTGS2 and downregulation of IL1R1.

The difference in the numbers of DEGs found after superovulation in different feeding regimes was striking (1374 DEGs in control fed, 168 DEGs in overfed and 18 DEGs in underfed animals, P < 0.01, FDR < 0.05). Furthermore, no gene was commonly affected by treatment across all three feeding regimens. Nonetheless, the transcriptome signature observed in caruncles indicates that feeding has a notable impact on the carry-over effects of FSH.

The lower number of DEGs additionally identified in the groups subjected to an imbalanced diet, as opposed to those that were control fed, could potentially imply a reduction in the influence of superovulation in both overfed and underfed animals, in which the transcriptomic profile is already affected by the altered diet. Another possible reason for the low number of DEGs could be associated with the high variation observed within the FSH-treated groups. This could imply an impaired response to hormonal treatment itself.

The lower effects of superovulation in underfed and overfed animals also limited functional analysis, although some overlaps between control fed and overfed or underfed animals could be observed. The 26 DEGs modulated after superovulation both in control fed and overfed animals included factors related to proliferation and cell growth such as BTG2, RNF157, NFIL3 and IGF1R. Furthermore, the expression of THBS1 was decreased in both groups. THBS1 is recognized as an anti-angiogenic factor, as it acts directly on endothelial cell migration, proliferation, survival, and apoptosis [104, 105]. Furthermore, the increased expression of integrin ITGA2 observed here added to the previously described upregulation of integrin subunits ITGA4, ITGA5, ITGAV and ITGB5 after superovulation of overfed animals [24].

As for superovulated underfed animals, several genes were downregulated when compared to non-stimulated animals, including the growth factor IGFBP2, the cell adhesion molecule CADM1, and PTGS2. Conversely, PTGS1 was upregulated in underfed animals. This suggests possible dysregulation of prostaglandin synthesis in the caruncles following FSH treatment, depending on the body condition.

Conclusions

This study presents an overview of the caruncular transcriptome signature in sheep during the early luteal phase and in response to feeding regime and/or FSH treatment. Some of the main findings from this and previous studies [23, 24] are schematically summarized in Fig. 7 (and in graphical abstract). The progression of diestrus from day 5 to 10 was mainly associated with decreased cellular proliferation and adhesion, and estrogen signaling, whereas functional terms associated with the immune system and prostaglandin synthesis became enriched. Generally, underfeeding appeared to affect the expression of more factors than overfeeding. Yet, an impaired nutritional plan, i.e., both over- and underfeeding, was associated with the modulation of different factors, possibly involved in regulating uterine receptivity. Strikingly, when trying to associate body condition with response to hormonal treatments, huge differences in the amount of DEGs could be observed when comparing control fed with under- or overfed animals. Furthermore, no gene was concomitantly affected by superovulation under different feeding conditions, suggesting the presence of multidirectional effects of FSH depending on different nutritional regimes. Thus, body condition and carry-over effects of hormonal treatments should be taken into consideration in the sheep, as well as in other species, when evaluating reproductive performance or applying ART technologies. Although the administration of FSH for superovulation is not a standard protocol in sheep, the results of our study serve as a model to enhance the effectiveness of IVF/ICSI by drawing attention to other external factors (e.g., maternal diet) that should be considered before ART treatment, including in humans.
