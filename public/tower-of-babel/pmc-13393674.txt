Long-term survival of divers cohort compared to the general population in the Czech Republic

Background

Diving is a globally practiced activity, with an estimated 6 million active divers worldwide, including approximately 3.1 million in the United States [1]. Understanding the long-term effects of diving on survival is essential, as the sport involves exposure to hyperbaric conditions, physical exertion, and unique physiological adaptations.

Diving is associated with unique physiological stressors and environmental exposures that may influence both acute and long-term health outcomes. Fatal accidents can occur during a dive or shortly after surfacing. The Divers Alert Network (DAN) has estimated the annual fatality rate among recreational divers to be approximately 16.4 deaths per 100,000 divers [2]. Lower rates have been reported in Australia (8.73 deaths per 100,000 divers) [3] and Japan (3.4–5.6 deaths per 100,000 divers) [4].

While the epidemiology of diving-related fatalities is well described, the long-term effects of diving on overall mortality remain insufficiently characterized. Diving may affect longevity through multiple, and at times opposing, mechanisms involving physical fitness, socioeconomic factors, mental health, and physiological stress.

Divers typically maintain higher levels of physical activity, which are associated with improved cardiovascular health, reduced metabolic risk, and greater longevity [5]. Cross-sectional studies have shown a lower prevalence of asthma, angina, diabetes, and hypertension among divers compared with the general U.S. population [6]. In contrast, former Norwegian professional divers demonstrated an increased prevalence of certain cardiovascular diseases (high blood pressure, myocardial failure) compared with the general population [7].

Recreational diving is more prevalent among individuals from higher socioeconomic and educational backgrounds, which may confer indirect health benefits through healthier lifestyles, improved access to healthcare, and reduced occupational risk exposures [6].

Regular diving has been associated with psychological benefits, including stress reduction, mindfulness, and enhanced well-being. However, repeated exposure to hyperbaric and hyperoxic conditions has been hypothesized to contribute to neurocognitive impairment or accelerated cognitive decline in some divers [8–10].

Diving-related pathophysiology may also affect long-term survival. Endothelial dysfunction resulting from intravascular microbubble formation, as well as oxidative stress induced by oxygen-rich breathing mixtures, have been proposed as potential mechanisms for vascular injury [11, 12]. Health risks may vary among professional diving sectors, such as rescue, instructional, aquaculture, and construction diving, reflecting differences in exposure intensity and occupational hazards [13].

Thus, long-term survival among divers likely reflects a complex interplay between harmful exposures related to hyperbaric and oxidative stress, diving-related accidents, and protective factors such as physical fitness, psychological resilience, and socioeconomic advantage.

To date, few studies have examined all-cause mortality among divers. In a cohort of Norwegian professional male divers, overall mortality was slightly lower than that of the general population (23 vs. 24 per 1,000), with a hazard ratio (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.63–0.997). However, divers exhibited higher mortality from occupational accidents and suicide [14].

The present study aims to assess long-term mortality among divers in the Czech Republic compared with an age- and sex-matched general population, in order to elucidate potential differences in survival and cause-specific mortality.

Methods

Study design and data sources

Data from two main sources were used for the analysis. The first is the DIVE-PFO Registry, which has been collecting data on divers screened for the presence of a patent foramen ovale (PFO) since 2005. The screening for PFO presence is mandatory for rescue divers in the Czech Republic. The registry includes individual identifiers along with demographic data such as age, sex, height, weight, comorbidities, and chronic treatments. Diving-related information is also collected, including the number of dives and details of decompression sickness episodes, if any. The registry is one of the largest databases of divers worldwide, containing data from approximately 1,300 individuals (as of May 2025), and is conducted by two of the authors (MS, JH).

The second data source is the National Health Information System (NZIS), which serves as the central authority for healthcare data collection and analysis in the Czech Republic. Information about the patient cohort was obtained from the NZIS upon institutional request by the Military University Hospital and linked using personal identifiers to hospitalization and mortality records to to determine survival status and causes of death among divers. Relative survival was estimated from general population mortality tables matched by calendar year, age, and sex.

Demographic and epidemiological data on the general population were obtained from the 2021 Czech Census and the 2019 European Health Interview Survey (EHIS).

Statistical analysis

For the comparison of demographic characteristics, a one-sample, for continuous variables one-sample t-test and for categorical variables one-sample proportion Z-test. Survival time was calculated from study entry (May 2005) until death or the end of follow-up (December 31, 2024). Death during follow-up was treated as the event, and alive individuals at the end of follow-up were censored. Observed survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Expected survival was calculated based on national mortality tables for the Czech Republic stratified by calendar year, age and sex. Relative survival was assessed using the Pohar-Perme estimator, a recommended method for unbiased estimation of net survival that accounts for background mortality in the general population. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05. Statistical computations were performed using R 4.4.2 with libraries readxl, dplyr, tidyr, survival, lubridate, relsurv.

Data from 1,076 divers enrolled in the DIVE-PFO registry between 2005 and 2024 were analyzed and compared to data from the general population. Among the 1,076 divers, 15 deaths (1.4% of the cohort) were recorded in the NZIS during the follow-up period. Five of them were due to cancer, none of the recorded deaths were due to cardiovascular causes, such as stroke or myocardial infarction. Four deaths were classified as possible diving-related fatalities: three due to asphyxiation (T71) and one due to air/water pressure effects (T709). No detailed records about fatalities were available, so the connection with diving remains uncertain. These four deaths represent 26.7% of all deceased (15 divers) in the cohort and correspond to a rate of 38.9 deaths per 100,000 divers annually (95% CI 10.6–99.5), based on 10,294 person-years in follow-up (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that divers in our cohort are less obese and overweight (females), have lower rates of hypertension, and smoke less (males) than the general population. In summary, these divers exhibit fewer risk factors for lifestyle-related diseases, consistent with previously published data. One possible explanation for the generally better health status of divers is the exclusion of applicants with asthma, diabetes, and certain neurological disorders during the initial fitness-to-dive assessment. Another contributing factor may be the “healthy worker” effect, as divers are generally expected to have fewer medical conditions and lower levels of sedentary behavior than the general population.

We recorded four deaths presumed to be diving-related, representing a substantial proportion of all deaths in the cohort (15 participants) during the follow-up period. Based on these four deaths, the annual mortality rate in our cohort is 38.9 per 100,000 divers with a wide confidence interval (95% CI 10.6–99.5). The result is more than twice that reported by the Divers Alert Network and considerably higher than rates in other regions (Australia, Japan). This discrepancy should be interpreted with caution due to the low absolute number of recorded deaths and uncertainty about their relation to diving (for example, a diver can drown from other reasons than diving). We speculate that the higher annual mortality rate observed in our cohort, compared with previous studies, may reflect riskier diving practices or, alternatively, more comprehensive national registration of fatalities through the NZIS in the Czech Republic.

Despite this, our findings suggest a positive association between diving status and long-term survival, as divers in our cohort demonstrated significantly higher 10-year relative survival (101.9%) than the general population. This result aligns with previously published data from professional male divers in Norway, though our cohort also included women and recreational divers [14].

Limitations of the study

Data from the DIVE-PFO registry should be generalized to the overall diver population with caution. The main limitation is selection bias related to enrollment in the registry. Rescue divers in the Czech Republic must be screened for PFO presence, and there is also a higher interest in PFO screening among divers after non-deserved decompression sickness, traditionally connected with PFO. The proportion of occupational divers (33.2%) and divers with PFO (39.9%) is higher in our cohort compared to that expected from the population of Czech divers, suggesting a significant inclusion bias. The cohort of divers comprises both occupational and recreational divers, so the results can be applied to either subgroup only with caution.

The comparator for our cohort of divers was the general population (mortality tables, NZIS data, EHIS survey), but not a truly matched non-diving population. Greater confidence in the findings would have been achieved if the diver cohort had been compared with a more closely matched non-diving reference group at the time of entry into diving activity.

A small number of deaths during follow-up and uncertain classification of the four deaths as diving-related provide results with a low level of precision and confidence in conclusions regarding the annual mortality rate.

Another limitation is the relatively young age of the participants, resulting in a low number of age-related conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease, and no deaths attributable to these causes. These diseases are known to be mitigated by regular physical activity, including scuba diving. Therefore, any potential preventive effects associated with diving may become more apparent as the cohort ages. A follow-up analysis is planned as the participants age.

Uncertainty regarding diving activity during follow-up also represents a limitation. Although all participants were active divers at inclusion, it is unknown whether they continued diving throughout the observation period. However, a related study using DIVE-PFO data found that approximately 94% of participants remained active divers during follow-up (median 6.5 years). Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the majority of our cohort remained active during the analysis period [15].

Conclusion

The DIVE-PFO diver cohort exhibited fewer major cardiovascular risk factors, including obesity, hypertension, and smoking, together with better long-term relative survival compared with the general population.
