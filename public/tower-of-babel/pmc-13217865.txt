Functional diversity patterns of urban and forest breeding birds across four city–forest pairs in China
Authors: Sidan Lin, Wei Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urbanisation has led to rapid declines in species richness, contributing to urban biotic homogenisation. Nonetheless, our understanding of the effects of urbanisation on functional community structures remains limited. During the 2023 breeding season, we employed a standardised point-count method to survey bird communities across four cities and their corresponding forest habitats in China, and compared the functional structural characteristics of urban and forest avian communities. Our results revealed that urban birds showed greater functional trait differences, which partially compensated for reduced species richness. While forest bird communities showed declines in species and functional richness with increasing latitude, urban communities displayed a weaker latitudinal trend, which we tentatively interpreted as a possible, but unconfirmed, buffering effect on the urban environment. However, we observed a decrease in functional redundancy and an imbalance within urban avian communities along the latitudinal gradient, suggesting potential shifts in the abundance-based community structure that merit further investigation. We caution that all latitudinal inferences are exploratory, as they are based on only four city–forest pairs, which severely limits statistical power and generalisability. Therefore, our findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. They provide preliminary evidence that the functional structure of urban avian communities may differ from that of forest communities; however, broader sampling across more cities and latitudes is needed to validate these patterns and draw firm conclusions about their stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-026-02535-7.

Introduction

Urbanisation is defined as the effect of human aggregation in residential and industrial areas [37]. It has been projected that urban coverage may reach up to 1.2 million km² by 2030, thereby becoming a major driver of land-use change [65]. Urbanisation, through mechanisms such as habitat loss or degradation, the urban heat island effect, and pollution [25, 42], has led to global reductions in biodiversity and biotic homogenisation [20, 41].

Urban biotic homogenisation refers to the increasing similarity in species composition among different cities as a consequence of urbanisation [26, 41]. Taxonomic homogenisation increases species similarity [47]. It can occur in a range of taxa [5], including insects [7], fish [55], amphibians, reptiles [79], and birds [34]. Homogenisation is evident at the species level and manifests in functional aspects (functional homogenization) [12, 16, 47]. Functional homogenisation may occur irrespective of taxonomic homogenisation. For instance, habitat fragmentation and conversion can lead to functional homogenisation in plant communities without any evidence of taxonomic homogenisation [1]. Certain functional traits of plant communities are highly similar in disturbed landscapes. This indicates that disturbances filter out species with particular adaptive traits, leading to a convergent functional composition. While disturbances may eliminate certain sensitive species, new colonisers may not necessarily belong to the same taxonomic groups but may instead be species with similar functional characteristics [1]. However, there is a lack of exploration of changes within communities, specifically the ecological consequences of homogenisation, particularly with regard to functional diversity [47]. Urbanisation involves a filtering effect based on biological functional traits, enabling specific organisms to survive in urban environments. This phenomenon is known as environmental filtering [4]. This filtering effect leads to biotic homogenisation in urban areas. For example, birds in urban environments tend to exhibit smaller body size, larger clutch size, increased dispersal ability, and broader dietary preferences than rural birds [46]. Urban environmental pressures affect functional traits across diverse biological groups. Studies on beetles, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and bats have demonstrated that urbanisation drives the selection of specific functional traits. This process leads to the formation of trait syndromes in different taxa [27]. Urban biotic homogenisation replaces specialists with generalists, thereby increasing functional trait similarity among the remaining species. However, this hypothesis has not yet been tested.

Urban biotic homogenisation increases species similarity, suggesting that urban biological communities exhibit greater functional trait redundancy [16]. Functional redundancy is often considered one of the mechanisms utilised by communities to resist and rapidly recover from environmental changes [19]. When the environment is disturbed, similar species can replace those lost due to the disturbance, a phenomenon known as the insurance effect. This mechanism enables a community to maintain dynamic stability [48]. For example, after an early spring season or a fire event, generalist pollinator species can replace specialist pollinators, thereby maintaining the ecological function of pollen dissemination within the ecosystem [24]. As mentioned above, species that survive in urban environments are expected to possess stronger resistance to disturbance and rapid recovery capabilities owing to greater functional redundancy [16]. This contradicts the intuition that high-biodiversity ecosystems, such as forests, are more resistant to disturbance. The diversity-stability debate centres on whether biodiversity enhances stability [39]. Thus, whether urban homogenisation increases redundancy and stability remains unknown.

A classical pattern of biodiversity is the latitudinal diversity gradient (LDG), in which taxonomic diversity decreases with increasing latitude [22, 29]. Numerous hypotheses have been proposed to explain LDG, including geographical [62], historical [21, 43, 77, 78] and climate [2, 21, 28, 29, 78] hypotheses. Moreover, the latitudinal gradient influences the filtering effect of urbanisation on birds [33, 46, 81]. The intensity of the city-filtering effect varies with latitude, and cities may support more bird species than the surrounding cold and harsh natural habitats. Thus, the LDG anticipates a decline in biodiversity from the equator towards the poles. However, urbanisation may paradoxically support higher biodiversity in high-latitude regions. Our understanding of how this contradictory pattern might influence changes in urban biodiversity under the LDG remains limited.

Based on this pattern, we addressed three questions: (1) Does urban homogenisation increase functional trait similarity within communities? (2) Does this increase functional redundancy? (3) Does higher redundancy confer greater stability under environmental changes (i.e. latitudinal variation)? To address these three questions, we selected bird communities from four cities along different latitudinal gradients across China and their adjacent natural forests as the study subjects. We compared taxonomic and functional diversity metrics between homogenous urban bird communities (homogenous) and non-homogenous forest bird communities (non-homogenous) to explore the ecological consequences of urban biotic homogenisation. Using these metrics, we explored whether urban bird communities might exhibit different patterns under environmental changes (i.e. latitudinal gradients) compared to forest communities, and whether such differences could be related to functional redundancy. Our exploratory analysis was based on a limited number of sites, which precluded strong statistical inferences regarding latitudinal trends or ecosystem stability.

Methods

Study area and data collection

We conducted our research at eight sites in China, including four cities (Haikou City, C1, 20.0351°N, 110.3130°E; Guangzhou City, C2, 23.1421°N, 113.2489°E; Wuhan City, C3, 30.6130°N, 114.3141°E; and Beijing City, C4, 40.0263°N, 116.3710°E) and four forests (Limu Mountain, F1, 19.1546°N, 109.7707°E; Dinghu Mountain, F2, 23.1667°N, 112.6483°E; Jigong Mountain, F3, 31.8037°N, 114.0814°E; and Xiaolongmen Mountain, F4, 39.9645°N, 115.4343°E). They were distributed along a latitudinal gradient, with each latitudinal gradient having closely located pairs of cities and forests as controls (Fig. 1). The proportions of the urban population were approximately 84% in Haikou (C1), 87% in Guangzhou (C2), 85% in Wuhan (C3), and 84% in Beijing (C4). The distances between cities and forests were approximately 102 km (C1–F1), 74 km (C2–F2), 137 km (C3–F3), and 82.4 km (C4–F4). The average distances of sampling points from the city centre in each urban area were 4.3 km (C1), 2.0 km (C2), 2.4 km (C3), and 9.4 km (C4). These distances were considerably smaller than the separation between the city and its corresponding forests, ensuring that the urban sampling points truly represented urbanised habitats.

C1 and F1 are located in the tropics and have a tropical monsoon climate. The representative vegetation is tropical rainforest. C2 and F2 are located in the southern subtropical zone and have a subtropical monsoon climate. The representative vegetation is an evergreen broad-leaved forest. C3 and F3 are located in the northern subtropical zone and contain mixed subtropical evergreen and deciduous broad-leaved forests. C4 and F4 are located in temperate zones and have temperate monsoon climates. The representative vegetation is broad-leaved deciduous forests. To quantify habitat heterogeneity, we classified each survey point into habitat type according to the urban habitat classification scheme of Morelli et al., [44]. The habitat types included (1) tree, (2) shrub, (3) grass, (4) water, and (5) grey area. For each site, habitat diversity was calculated using Shannon’s diversity index based on the proportion of habitat types at each point. Given that forest habitats consist almost entirely of trees, we set habitat heterogeneity to 0. The Wilcoxon test was used to determine whether urban habitat heterogeneity was greater than that of forest habitats. We recognise that the five habitat types we used, while commonly employed in urban bird studies (e.g., [44]), do not fully capture the complexity of habitat structure. For example, forest sites may contain considerable vertical stratification (canopy, understory, shrub layer, and ground cover) and fine-scale microhabitat variation that our classification system failed to capture. Therefore, the habitat heterogeneity referred to here should be understood as land-cover/land-use heterogeneity.

Bird species composition in cities across China is similar due to the homogeneous urban environment [70]. We defined urban green areas as areas including wetlands, open water, grass, shrubs/scrubs, forests, and other mixed habitats, overlaying urbanisation, that are potentially managed through human aesthetic landscape planning. Urban survey points were established systematically in green areas, such as schools and parks, within cities, whereas forest survey points were set in locations accessible via forest trails. We used systematic sampling as the design, and survey points were evenly spaced in urban green areas or along forest trails. The number of survey points varied with green area size in the cities, and forests had more survey points to ensure adequate sampling (supplementary material Figure S1). During the 2023 bird breeding season (June–July), bird surveys were conducted using the standard point-count method [6]. Point surveys were conducted in the morning when bird activity was high. Each survey at a designated point lasted for 10 min, during which observers recorded sightings or vocalisations of birds within a 50-m radius. Most adjacent survey points were separated by at least 200 m to avoid duplicate recordings. Each survey point was surveyed only once, under suitable weather conditions (sunny or cloudy), to ensure consistent bird detection rates. The same observer (S. Lin) surveyed all the city and forest points.

Functional traits

To measure functional diversity among birds, we used morphological features from Tobias et al., [72]. These features included beak length, beak width, and beak depth (describing bird resource utilisation), wing length, secondary length, hand wing index (describing bird flight and dispersal abilities), and tarsus and tail lengths (related to bird microhabitats and foraging strategies). These morphological features have been shown to accurately reflect birds’ ecological niches [52]. All morphological features were log-transformed and scaled to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 prior to analysis. To eliminate biased analyses caused mostly by body size, Trisos et al., [73] proposed a two-step PCA that outperformed PCA pooling of all traits. To quantify the primary components of the morphological features, we used the two-step principal component analysis (PCA) process proposed by Trisos et al., [73]. Functional traits were positively correlated because most traits (such as beak and tarsus lengths) were associated with body size. All traits were categorised into foraging traits (beak length, width, and depth) and locomotory traits (tail, wing, and tarsus lengths, as well as the hand-wing index). PCA was conducted separately for foraging and locomotory traits to obtain foraging PC1, foraging PC2, locomotory PC1, and locomotory PC2 traits. Foraging PC1 explained most of the variance (76.5%) in the foraging traits and was strongly correlated with beak length, width, and depth (supplementary material Table S1). However, locomotory PC1 and locomotory PC2 explained 60.2% and 22.2% of the variance in locomotory traits, respectively, and PC2 was strongly correlated with the hand wing index (supplementary material Table S1). For trait information loss, a second PCA was performed on foraging PC1, locomotory PC1, and locomotory PC2 to obtain the body size PC1 and PC2, which were used for subsequent functional diversity calculations.

Functional diversity

We evaluated the variations in bird functional diversity across different habitats (urban and forest) and latitudinal gradients using various measures, including pairwise functional dissimilarities between species (Functional dist), functional richness (FRic), functional redundancy (Redundancy), and functional imbalance (Imbalance). Among them, richness and FRic were used to measure differences in diversity between forests and urban areas and to examine their variation along the latitudinal gradient. Stability includes two mechanisms: compensatory dynamics and the insurance effect [19]. The former emphasises the functional differences between species within a community, whereas the latter highlights the functional redundancy of species within the community [19]. Functional dist and Imbalance capture the functional differences between species within a community; the former does not account for the number of individuals per species, whereas the latter does [58, 69]. On the other hand, Redundancy measured the functional redundancy of the community [59].

Functional dist measures the functional distance between every pair of species within a community. The larger the differences between species, the greater the functional distance. In the case of urban homogenisation, where species within a community become more similar, one would expect a decrease in functional distance. Neither Functional dist nor FRic account for the influence of species abundance. However, urban homogenisation can result in some species having much higher population densities than others, without increasing diversity, leading to an urban density-diversity paradox [63]. The density-diversity paradox describes urban biological communities which are characterised by high population density but low biodiversity (Schochat et al., 2010). The site-level Functional dist was calculated using the dsimFun() function in the R package adiv [50, 51]. To compare differences across habitats, we computed point-level FRic using the RNED() function in the R package TPD [8], and Redundancy and Imbalance using the functions uniqueness() and FunImbalance() in the R package adiv [50].

Analysis of breeding bird diversity changes between habitats and across latitudinal gradients

Owing to inconsistent survey efforts leading to variations in species diversity (γ) at each site, we utilised Hill numbers to extrapolate species diversity under the same survey effort using the iNEXT function in the R package iNEXT [10, 30]. We computed rarefaction and extrapolation sampling curves for species richness (one of the Hill number families), and compared them with equal sample sizes [11]. We compared site-level Functional dist and point-level species richness (Richness), FRic, Redundancy, and Imbalance using the Wilcoxon rank sum test with continuity correction. Since Richness follows a negative binomial distribution, and Functional dist and Redundancy follow beta distributions, we fitted separate generalised linear models for each using the manyglm() function in the R package mvabund [76] and betareg() in the R package betareg [15]. As FRic and Imbalance deviated from a normal distribution, they were log-transformed and scaled to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 before being modelled with a Gaussian error structure.

All data analyses were conducted using the R software version 4.2.3 (R Core Team, [54]).

Results

We set 432 points in total across all sites and habitats (85 points for F1, 46 points for F2, 70 points for F3, 61 points for F4, 44 points for C1, 46 points for C2, 40 points for C3, and 40 points for C4; supplementary material Figure S1, Table S2). Urban habitat heterogeneity (1.08 ± 0.22) was significantly greater than forest habitat heterogeneity (Wilcoxon test, W = 170, P = 0.04).

We calculated the mean and standard deviation of each diversity index at the site level (supplementary material Table S2). For Richness, γ diversity was generally higher in forests, indicating a greater number of bird species in forested areas (Estimate ± SD: γF1 = 52.3 ± 1.8 > γC1 = 37.0 ± 11.6, γF2 = 54.1 ± 5.9 > γC2 = 49.1 ± 6.1, γF3 = 45.1 ± 3.7 > γC3 = 36.0 ± 6.5, γF4 = 39.1 ± 8.3 > γC4 = 28.4 ± 0.9). At the point level, urban bird richness was slightly higher than forest bird species overall (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P > 0.05), with significant differences only observed between sites at the highest latitude (Richness: C4 = 5.8 ± 1.4 > F4 = 3.0 ± 1.7; Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). For Functional dist, urban bird species were significantly larger than forest bird species (Wilcoxon rank sum test, PC1_F1 < 0.01, PC2_F2 < 0.001, PC3_F3 < 0.01, and PC4_F4 < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). For FRic, there were no significant differences between forest bird species and urban bird species (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P > 0.05), except for one group (FRic: C2 = 7.0 ± 3.1 and F2 = 8.9 ± 3.4; Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.01) (Fig. 2E). A comparison of redundancy at different latitudes yielded varying results. C1 was significantly higher than F1 (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.05), C2 was significantly lower than F2 (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001), and C3 was significantly higher than F3 (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001), and no significant difference was observed between C4 and F4 (Wilcoxon rank sum test, P > 0.05) (Fig. 2G). Imbalance was significantly lower in urban areas than in forests at higher latitudes (Imbalance: C3 = -0.49 ± 0.50 < F3 = 0.36 ± 0.84, C4 = -0.73 ± 0.54 < F4 = 0.05 ± 0.85; Wilcoxon rank sum test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2I) (Supplementary material Table S3).

The bird diversity indices exhibited distinct latitudinal gradients across habitats. Richness in forests showed a decreasing trend with increasing latitude (slope = -0.022, P < 0.001), whereas in urban areas, although there was a decreasing trend, it was not significant (slope = -0.003, P > 0.05). In contrast to Richness, Redundancy and Imbalance in urban bird species decreased with increasing latitude, whereas in forest bird species, although there was a decreasing trend, it was not significant. The relationships among Functional dist, FRic, and latitude were not significant (Fig. 2, supplementary material Table S4).

Discussion

In this study, we compared the taxonomic and functional aspects of bird diversity in urban and forest communities across four cities in China along a latitudinal gradient. Given our small sample size (n = 4 city–forest pairs), all findings should be interpreted as preliminary patterns rather than generalisable conclusions. Our main observation is that urban bird assemblages, despite having lower site-level γ diversity, maintained comparable species and functional richness to forest assemblages, and exhibited greater functional divergence among species. This pattern suggests that urbanisation does not necessarily lead to functional homogenisation, which is a common assumption in urban ecology [41, 69]. Instead, it may promote distinct functional strategies; however, replication across more cities is required. Notably, while forest communities showed a significant decline in richness with increasing latitude, urban communities remained stable, which we tentatively describe as a possible buffering effect of the urban environment. The relationship between functional redundancy and latitude varied across habitats; however, given our limited site-level replication, we refrained from drawing strong ecological inferences from this pattern. We caution that inferences regarding latitudinal trends are constrained by the limited number of city–forest pairs (n = 4) at the site level, and future studies with broader geographic coverage are needed to validate these patterns. These findings contribute to the growing body of research on how urbanisation reshapes biodiversity patterns and highlight the need to consider multiple dimensions of diversity when assessing urban ecological impacts.

Compensatory dynamics and functional divergence in urban bird communities One mechanism of ecosystem stability is compensatory dynamics, in which species with similar functional traits within a community respond differently to environmental changes. One species can compensate for the reduced ecological function of another disturbed species [19, 35]. Compensatory dynamics require asynchronous responses of species to environmental disturbances, and empirical evidence suggests that species asynchrony is related to functional differences [14, 64]. Specifically, species with similar traits exhibit greater synchrony [31, 32, 61, 74]. Our finding that the functional differences among species within urban bird communities were larger than those in forest communities supports the idea that urbanisation may enhance functional asynchrony. This finding aligns with those of several urban ecological studies, showing that urban environments, through habitat heterogeneity and resource supplementation, can host species with diverse functional strategies [71]. However, this contrasts with other studies that reported functional homogenisation in highly urbanised areas [17], suggesting that the direction of urbanisation effects on functional diversity may depend on the intensity of urbanisation, regional species pools, and spatial scale of analysis. The greater functional divergence explains why site-level γ diversity was lower for urban bird species, even though urban FRic was comparable to that of forests. This diversity can reduce the number of species required to maintain the ecological stability of community functions [80]. Therefore, larger functional differences among urban bird species may compensate for the lower functional richness caused by fewer species. In addition, our results show that forests support higher γ diversity than cities, but the mean taxonomic diversity of forests is comparable to that of cities, indicating that avian individuals in cities are concentrated in a few species. This pattern of taxonomic diversity loss and uneven abundance distribution corroborates previous studies on little-urbanised environments worldwide (referring to urban green spaces in our study), where functional diversity (quadratic entropy, Q) did not differ significantly from that of natural vegetation [69]. The true cause of decreased functional diversity (quadratic entropy, Q) and reduced redundancy in cities appears to be a decrease in species richness and uneven distribution of abundance, rather than functional dissimilarity among species [69].

Functional redundancy and the insurance effect

Another mechanism for ecosystem stability is the insurance effect, which requires redundant species within a community to replace lost species and fulfil ecological functions [19]. For example, research on global fish communities has shown that communities with low functional redundancy are more susceptible to environmental changes [45]. Similarly, studies on plant communities have shown that high redundancy buffers species loss under climate change [57]. Our results indicate that in two out of the four cities (C1 and C3), bird community redundancy was significantly higher than that in the corresponding forests, whereas it was significantly lower in one city (C2). Thus, the differences in redundancy between urban and forest areas lacked a consistent pattern. Theoretically, functional redundancy reflects the number of species contributing to a specific ecological function. However, the existing quantitative metrics are strongly associated with both species and functional diversity, which raises uncertainties regarding their true efficacy [23, 59]. Therefore, the lack of consistent patterns in redundancy variations between urban and forest bird communities could be attributed to limitations in the currently available quantification metrics.

Despite potential biases in quantifying functional redundancy metrics, both functional redundancy and imbalance in urban bird communities showed a significant decrease with increasing latitude. This is in contrast to the trend observed in urban areas, where Richness and FRic remained stable or increased with latitude. Compared with Richness and FRic, Redundancy and Imbalance consider the individual abundance of each species within the community, reflecting more refined structural characteristics related to population dynamics [19, 58]. This facilitates the connection between population dynamics and ecosystem stability, which is often overlooked in diversity-stability research [19]. One of the best-known patterns of biodiversity is the LDG, in which taxonomic diversity decreases with increasing latitude [29, 36, 78]. In this study, bird taxonomic and functional richness followed the LDG pattern in forests, but not in cities. One possible interpretation is that urbanisation may disrupt typical LDGs. However, given the limited number of city–forest pairs (n = 4), this pattern should be viewed as preliminary and requires validation with broader spatial sampling.

Latitudinal patterns and potential mechanisms

Two mechanisms may produce these ecological consequences: habitat alteration and changes in food availability. Urbanisation involves the conversion of natural landscapes into built environments, resulting in habitat loss or fragmentation [40, 65, 66]. Unlike forests, which are relatively homogeneous in structure and resource availability, cities contain a mosaic of habitat types (e.g. wetlands, green parks, and residential areas) that support different bird assemblages [3]. This habitat heterogeneity may explain why the functional distances between bird species in cities are larger than those in forests, as heterogeneous environments can support species with diverse functional traits [71]. Food supplementation and stabilisation are the most common mechanisms responsible for the increase in urban bird population density. [38, 60]. Exotic vegetation [67] and garbage [49, 53] may provide stable food sources for urban birds, altering bird community compositions and abundances. Bird species that exploit generalist food resources may benefit from urbanisation and maintain stable populations, whereas specialist populations may decline [9, 68]. To incorporate population size into our functional analyses, we used Imbalance to measure the distribution of abundance among species with different functional dissimilarities [58]. The imbalance is greater when most individuals are concentrated in species with large functional trait differences [58]. Therefore, the decrease in the imbalance in urban bird communities with increasing latitude suggests that individuals within the community are increasingly clustered around functionally similar species at higher latitudes. As functional traits become more similar, the synchrony of species’ responses to disturbances increases, rendering the ecosystem more vulnerable to perturbations [19, 35]. If this pattern holds with additional data, it suggests that vulnerability may manifest at the population level rather than at the species level. This has important implications for urban conservation planning, as it suggests that maintaining population evenness across functionally distinct species may be as important as preserving species richness. One limitation of this study is that the survey was conducted only once, which introduced substantial randomness and may not have fully captured the true abundance of the species. Therefore, the abundance estimates presented here should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should include repeated surveys to obtain more robust and reliable estimates.

Limitations and future directions

In summary, our study provides preliminary evidence that greater interspecific functional differences among urban bird species may partially compensate for reduced functional richness due to lower species numbers. Determining whether urban bird species exhibit greater functional redundancy than their forest counterparts requires the development of more robust metrics for quantifying larger datasets. We found that forest bird communities showed a clearer latitudinal decline in richness than urban communities, which we tentatively described as a potential pattern rather than evidence of buffering or stability. Given our limited number of city–forest pairs, we explicitly refrained from interpreting this pattern as indicating that urban environments buffer against climatic drivers or confer greater ecosystem stability. The simultaneous decline in redundancy and imbalance with latitude in urban communities is an intriguing observation, but requires further testing with expanded spatial and temporal sampling. Although our findings raise interesting questions about how urbanisation alters functional diversity patterns, strong inferences about latitudinal trends, stability, or buffering effects are not possible from this dataset alone.
