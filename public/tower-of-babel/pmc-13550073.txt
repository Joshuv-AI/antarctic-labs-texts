Low-level laser irradiation stimulated tumoricidal impacts and dose-dependent modulation of Wnt signaling in three-dimensional human colorectal cancer microspheres
Authors: Hesam Saghaei Bagheri, Cigir Biray Avci, Nafiseh Didar Khosrowshahi, Fereshteh Vaziri Nezamdoust, Somayyeh Rashidi, Ali Baradar Khoshfetrat, Habib Tajalli, Reza Rahbarghazi, Asghar Khalilnezhad, Elham Shahriyari, Fereshteh Valipour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe high incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) has been a big challenge in human medicine. Along with several available therapies, specific modalities, e.g., low-level laser irradiation (LLLI), have been introduced to achieve proper regenerative outcomes. Unfortunately, most studies have investigated the tumoricidal effects of LLLI on cancer cells using a 2D culture system with less comparable capacity to in vivo conditions. Here, the detrimental impacts of LLLI were studied in human CRC cells encapsulated inside alginate-gelatin (Alg-Gel) microspheres after 48 h. Alg (1% w/v)-Gel (1% w/v) microspheres were developed using CaCl₂-mediated ionic cross-linking. Physicochemical properties, including swelling and degradation rates, gel fraction, FTIR spectra, and SEM morphology, were examined. The mean diameter and cell distribution of HT-29 cell-laden microspheres were evaluated by bright-field microscopy. Cell membrane integrity and nitric oxide (NO) production were assessed using LDH-release and Griess assays 48 h after LLLI at fluences of 2 and 80 J/cm². Cell density was evaluated by hematoxylin-eosin staining, and the expression of Wnt-related genes was assessed by RT-qPCR. FTIR findings supported integration of Alg and Gel, and the composite showed measurable swelling, degradation, and gel-fraction characteristics together with a porous interconnected ultrastructure. The mean microsphere diameter was 305.85 ± 19.07 μm, and cells were distributed throughout the microspheres. The 80 J/cm² exposure was associated with increased LDH release, higher NO production, and lower HT-29 cell density (p < 0.05), while less LLLI intensity, 2 J/cm², showed a modest, assay-dependent cytoprotective pattern. RT-qPCR demonstrated a mixed transcriptional response involving both up- and down-regulation of Wnt-related genes; these data do not by themselves establish activation of a Wnt signaling pathway. LLLI can alter the dynamic growth of human CRC cells depending on laser intensity by influencing survival rate, nitrosative stress, and certain Wnt signaling genes. These data can help in the development of an accurate therapeutic protocol in the clinical setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10103-026-05023-y.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Laser therapy, Human, Dynamic growth, Wnt signaling pathway

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), with high morbidity and mortality in developing and industrialized countries, is the third most fatal tumor-associated death in humans [1]. In the clinical setting, colonoscopy is suggested to diagnose CRC cases at early stages and increase the efficiency of therapeutic regimens [2]. Surgical excision, chemotherapy alone or in combination with radiotherapy, and immunotherapy are conventional approaches to alleviate CRC conditions [3]. Unfortunately, in most cases, these modalities cannot guarantee complete tumor cell removal and satisfactory outcomes [4]. Therefore, attempts have been made to find and develop novel therapeutic approaches to yield better regenerative outcomes in CRC patients.

In recent decades, photobiomodulation has been used for different regenerative purposes, especially cancer therapy [5]. In phototherapy-based modalities, various therapeutic options such as photodynamic, photoablation, and low-level laser therapies (LLLT) are available [6]. The LLL irradiation (LLLI) is based on the application of near-infrared light or a light-emitting diode (LED) with low intensity [7]. Calling attention, the LLLI approach has been extensively applied in various medical disciplines, including the alleviation of neurodegenerative disorders [8], musculoskeletal [9], cutaneous diseases [10], oral-dental health [11], and diabetic ulcers [12]. Using photobiomodulation in tumor cells, the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane is influenced, leading to the release of cytochrome C oxidase into the cytosol, with the intracellular nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels also increased [13]. These features can sensitize the irradiated tumor cells to prominent oxidative stress and apoptotic changes [14]. Practically, LLLI is the main choice for the alleviation of oral mucositis post-chemotherapy, breast cancer-related lymphedema, and relief of pain after the surgery of patients with oropharyngeal cancers [15]. Various studies have indicated both tumor cell killing and protection of LLLI in different conditions [16]. On one hand, LLLI can exert synergistic effects on anti-tumor modalities, although these effects are primarily associated with the applied laser intensity, energy, wavelength, and tumor type [17]. Of note, the laser-based tumoricidal effects are mediated via the activation of apoptotic and autophagic cell death following oxidative stress [18]. While at lower intensities, LLLI can increase the survival rate of malignant cells via the promotion of mitochondrial activity and glycolysis [19]. Commensurate with these descriptions, standard protocols should be developed for determining significant tumoricidal properties in cancer patients.

In most of the previously conducted studies, LLLI has been used alone or in combination with other anti-cancer modalities for monitoring tumoricidal effects cultured on a two-dimensional (2D) culture system [20]. In this system, all tumor cells are exposed to LLLI evenly without any complexities described for in vivo-like conditions. It is believed that the cultured tumor cells in a monolayer 2D system are morphologically flattened and have poor cell-to-cell communication [21]. These features can alter the metabolic profile, leading to poor differentiation capacity, drug sensitivity, and loss of anti-tumor resistance mechanisms [22]. In this regard, data from various 2D in vitro studies using LLLI cannot recapitulate in vivo-like conditions compared to real tumor conditions due to the lack of in vivo complexity and cell heterogeneity [23]. The previous studies have only investigated the tumoricidal properties of light- or laser-based modalities such as droplet-microfluidic-generated tumor spheroids used for photothermal therapy and delivery in 3D tumor models [24, 25]. Therefore, the advent and development of sophisticated three-dimensional (3D) culture systems using various biomimetic substances have revolutionized the understanding of tumor cell biology and their response to therapeutic protocols [23]. The incorporation of cells into the supporting matrices could mimic the primary site-like niche, which can help us acquire comparable data about the efficiency of therapeutic protocols inside the body [26].

Novel tissue engineering techniques and fabrication of various hydrogel types facilitate the development of alternative culture systems such as spheroids and tumoroids in cancer biology [27]. The application of natural, synthetic, and semi-synthetic hydrogels with specific formulation and distinct physicochemical properties, along with single and multiple cell types, is ideal for monitoring tumor cell biology [28]. These platforms are accurate and high-throughput drug screening tools for the precise evaluation of tumor cell responses within the hydrogels [27].

Among various 3D platforms, Alg-Gel-based hydrogels have been extensively used for evaluating phototherapeutic responses because their physicochemical properties can be tuned to approximate key features of the tumor microenvironment [29]. It is thought that ionic cross-linking of Alg with Gel can provide a reliable, stable hydrogel with interconnected porosity, supporting the attachment, morphological adaptation, and suitable motility of encapsulated cells [30]. These features ensure the easy and facile diffusion of gases and nutrients into the hydrogel structure [31]. Along with these comments, it is assumed that the exposure of cell-laden Alg-Gel hydrogel to LLLI can give us valid data related to tumor cell dynamic growth and bioactivity in a 3D culture system.

Here, in this study, we aimed to investigate the tumoricidal effects of LLLI on the human CRC HT29 cells within the alginate-gelatin (Alg-Gel) microspheres induced by ionic cross-linking. It is assumed that this system can efficiently reflect the potential tumor cell-killing activity of LLLI in human CRC cells and help in the development of new modalities in the clinical setting.

Materials and methods

Culture protocol

Human CRC HT-29 cells were obtained from the Iranian National Cell Bank (NCBI Code: C466; Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran) and expanded in DMEM/F12 (Ref No: 32500-035; Lot no: 2839284; Gibco; USA). To improve HT-29 cell growth, the culture medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat No: FB-100/100; Lot No; F772119631; Cholet; France). 1% Pen-Strep solution (Cat no: XC-A4122; Biosera; Cholet, France) was added to the medium to reduce the possibility of bacterial growth. Cells were detached using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Ref: 25200; Lot no: 2786672; Gibco; Paisley; UK) and passaged. For various analyses, HT-29 cells at passages 3–6 were used.

LLLI

In the present study, the effects of LLLI were evaluated in HT-29 cells maintained either as conventional 2D cultures or within Alg-Gel microspheres. Irradiation was delivered in continuous-wave mode using a 632.2-nm diode laser (Shenzhen Tai Yong Technology, China). Two irradiation geometries were used. For 2D cultures in 96-well plates (SPL; Gyeonggi-do; South Korea), the beam-spot radius recorded at the culture plane was 0.33 cm, corresponding to an irradiated area of approximately 0.342 cm². The handpiece was placed in direct contact with the outer surface of each well, and output powers of 30 mW and 150 mW were used to deliver fluences of 2 J/cm² (22.79 s) and 80 J/cm² (182.37 s), respectively. For cell-laden microspheres in 24-well plates, the handpiece was fixed 5 cm above the well surface; the recorded spot radius at the sample plane was 0.81 cm, and the corresponding irradiated area was approximately 2.06 cm². An output power of 150 mW was used to deliver 2 J/cm² (27.64 s) and 80 J/cm² (1098.74 s). The spot radii were obtained from the laboratory irradiation record for each geometry. The beam footprint was treated as approximately circular, and area was derived from A = πr². Exposure time was calculated using T(s) = E(J/cm²) × A(cm²)/P(W), where E is fluence, A is irradiated area, and P is output power. Unrounded recorded values were used in the calculations, whereas rounded radii and areas are reported here. The reporting of dose and beam parameters followed established recommendations for laboratory photomedicine studies [32]. After irradiation, the supernatant was replaced with freshly prepared DMEM/HG containing 1% FBS, and cells were maintained for 48 h. Reduced-serum medium was applied identically to control and irradiated groups to minimize confounding by serum-derived growth factors and antioxidants. Non-irradiated controls were handled identically without laser exposure.

MTT assay

To this end, HT-29 cells were seeded at a density of 3 × 104 cells per well in 96-well culture plates (. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, HT-29 cells were randomly allocated into two main groups: non-treated Control and LLLI cells. After cell irradiation, cells were incubated with 2 mg/ml MTT solution (Cat no: M5665; lot no: MKCS4540; Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louise; USA) for 3–4 hours at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with relative humidity of 95%. Dimethyl sulfoxide (Cat no: D4540; Lot no: BCCD8942; St. Louise; Mo; USA) was used to dissolve the formed formazan crystals and generate a blue-to-purple color appearance. The absorbance of each well was measured at 570 and 630 nm using an ELISA reader (LabSystem, MultiScan MS, serial RS_232C) [33].

Alg-Gel hydrogel preparation

Human HT-29 cells were encapsulated within Alg-Gel microspheres to establish a 3D platform for evaluation of LLLI-associated effects. High-guluronic-acid alginate with an approximate molecular weight of 70 kDa (I-1G, high content of guluronic acid; Kimica, Japan) and porcine-skin Gel (Cat no: G2500; Sigma-Aldrich) were selected because Ca²⁺-cross-linked alginate provides structural support, whereas gelatin supplies cell-adhesive motifs and improves cytocompatibility. The use of Alg-Gel matrices for 3D cell culture and microsphere-based cell encapsulation has been demonstrated in directly relevant primary studies [34]. Alg (2% w/v) and Gel (2% w/v) were mixed and cross-linked with 0.2 M CaCl₂ at 37 °C for 2 h to obtain a solidified composite. Before polymerization, the Alg solution was disinfected with 70% ethanol and washed repeatedly with sterile Ca²⁺-free Krebs-Ringer HEPES-buffered saline (CF-KRH; pH 7.2–7.4). For physicochemical characterization, the mixture was transferred to cylindrical molds, ionically cross-linked, removed gently after polymerization, and washed with buffer.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM)

The prepared Alg-Gel hydrogels were frozen using liquid nitrogen and lyophilized at -80 °C for 48 h. Alg-Gel hydrogels were imaged at cross-sections after gold sputtering using an FE-SEM system (MIRA3, TESCAN) at 15 kV.

FTIR

To confirm the appropriate ionic cross-linking of Alg with Gel, FTIR analysis was performed. For this purpose, the powder of freeze-dried Alg-Gel hydrogel was analyzed in the range of 500–4000 cm− 1 with 4 cm− 1 resolution and 24 scans using a Bruker Tensor 27 FTIR spectrometer, compared to the Alg and Gel groups.

Fabrication of Alg-Gel microspheres

To provide a 3D culture system, about 1 × 106 HT-29 cells were mixed with 1 mL sterile Alg-Gel solution (1% w/v) and transferred to a sterile syringe connected to a syringe pump (YSP-101, USA). The hydrogel solution was extruded through a 25G needle at a flow rate of 0.1 ml/min at 8 kV (Vita Teb, Iran). The hydrogel was dripped into 0.2 M CaCl2 (0.2 M; Merck) solution. Here, the distance between the tip of the needle and the cross-linking bath was adjusted to 5 cm. After that, HT-29 cell-encapsulated microspheres were removed from the ionic solution and washed with fresh medium to eliminate unbound Ca2+ cations After ionic gelation, cell-laden microspheres were collected and washed with fresh culture medium to remove unbound Ca²⁺ [35].

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay

To monitor the integrity of the HT-29 cell membrane, HT-29-laden microspheres were randomly allocated into three groups as follows: Control, 2 J/cm2, and 80 J/cm2 groups. For this purpose, microspheres were transferred into each well of 96-well plates and exposed to LLLI at the above-mentioned intensities. After the completion of LLLI, the microspheres were maintained at standard conditions in culture medium containing 1% FBS, and the content of released LDH was determined using the appropriate LDH Cytotoxicity Assay kit (SKU: KLDH96; Pars Azmun Co. Ltd, Iran), and OD values were read using a microplate ELISA reader (BioTek) at 340 nm [36].

Histological examination

To assess the localization and number of encapsulated HT-29 cells inside the Alg-Gel microspheres following exposure to LLLI, H & E staining was done. For this purpose, microspheres were exposed to LLLI at power intensities of 2 and 80 J/cm2 and kept for 48 h under standard conditions. After that, microspheres were collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm for 20 min) and fixed in 10% buffered formalin solution. Thereafter, paraffin-embedded blocks were cut into 5 μm thick sections and stained with H & E solution.

Griess assay

Nitrosative stress was monitored in irradiated HT-29 cells inside microspheres in terms of NO production using the Griess assay. Briefly, supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 400 g to remove cellular debris. After that, 200 µl of each sample was mixed with Griess reagent (20 µl) A in a 96-well plate and kept at 37 °C for 10 min. The procedure was continued with the addition of Griess B reagent (20 µl) and incubation at RT for 10 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The final NO levels were measured based on standard nitrite concentrations generated by certain concentrations of sodium nitrite (CAS No: 7632-00-0; Sigma-Aldrich).

Real-time PCR analysis

To monitor the status of the Wnt signaling pathway in HT-29 cell-laden Alg-Gel microspheres after being exposed to LLLI, the expression of various related genes, including DAAM1, DKK1, DKK3, FZD1, FZD2, GSK3B, MMP7, TCF7, WNT1, WNT3A, and WNT5B, was assessed using real-time PCR analysis (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). After completion of the treatment protocol, the HT-29 cells were released from microspheres using 0.1 M trisodium citrate dihydrate, followed by washing with PBS. Then, RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Cat. No: LB38055; Life Biolab, Germany) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA by the Easy™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Ref. No. A101161; Parstous Co., Iran). Using LightCycler 480 Instrument II (Roche) and RT2 SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Qiagen), the expression of each gene was calculated after normalization to GAPDH using the 2−ΔΔCT method [37]. Fold-changes greater than 2 and less than − 2 were considered statistically significant.

Results

FTIR revealed the successful integration of Alg with Gel

FITR analysis was done to prove the successful ionic integration (Fig. 1A). In this regard, the presence of functional groups of Alg, Gel, and Alg-Gel composite was analyzed. The characteristic peaks in Alg alone were observed at 3417 cm− 1 broad (-O-H) stretching vibration, 2924 cm− 1 stretching vibration related to alkane (-CH2) groups, 1616 cm− 1 (-C = O) stretching, and 1418 cm− 1 (-COO−) symmetric stretching. Of note, a broad absorption band was detected at approximately 3416 cm− 1, corresponding to the N-H stretching vibration of Gel. The amide I band, observed at 1648 cm− 1, is attributed to (-C = O) stretching vibrations. Besides, the amide II band at a wavelength of 1540 cm− 1 correlates with (-N-H) bending and (-C-N) stretching. We found the amide III band near 1237 cm− 1 and the bands related to (-CH2) and (-C-O) groups in the 1454 cm− 1 and 1237 cm− 1 regions, respectively, in the Gel structure (Fig. 1A). FTIR spectrum of Alg-Gel hydrogel exhibits a stretching frequency for the carboxylic acid group at 1618 cm− 1. A broad absorption band around 3416 cm− 1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of (-O-H) groups, indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups from both Alg and Gel substrates. A peak observed near 2920 cm− 1 is attributed to (-C-H) stretching vibrations. Notably, the peak in the 1420 cm− 1 region is associated with the symmetric stretching of the (-COO−) groups from Alg. The peak at a wavelength of 1224 cm− 1 is identical to amide III, indicating the presence of Gel in the final composite after ionic cross-linking with Ca2+ ions (Fig. 1A). According to these descriptions, it is noteworthy to declare that the present protocol can promote ionic polymerization of Alg with Gel in the presence of an ionic cross-linker.

Physicochemical properties

Based on the obtained data, the Alg-Gel hydrogel exhibited an upward trend in terms of swelling rate, in which these values were 286.37 ± 89.20% after 1 h and reached 800.94 ± 76.67% during three days (Fig. 1B). It is assumed that Alg-Gel hydrogel has the potential to absorb a noticeable amount of water and make this substrate an eligible niche for regenerative medicine. We also monitored the degradation of Alg-Gel hydrogel in PBS containing lysozyme for 12 days (Fig. 1C). Similar to the swelling ratio, the developed Alg-Gel hydrogel showed an enhanced degradation rate over time, with the maximum values on day 12 compared to day 3 (62.56 ± 1.53% versus 25.19 ± 2.64%). It is thought that the existence of an ionic connection between the Gel and Alg can potentiate this composite to be cleared over time, which is consistent with, though not direct proof of, a gradual replacement of the synthetic matrix by cell-secreted extracellular matrix (ECM) components; confirming this hypothesis would require direct immunostaining for ECM proteins in future work. Of note, the gel fraction, defined as the proportion of the initial hydrogel mass that remains as an insoluble, cross-linked polymeric network after** prolonged aqueous immersion (i.e., the complement of the soluble/leachable, non-cross-linked fraction), reached 54.57 ± 2.53% in the present study for Alg-Gel hydrogel (Fig. 1D), indicating that roughly half of the initial polymer mass is retained within a stable network capable of supporting encapsulated cells over the experimental timeframe.

Cell incorporation into Alg-Gel microspheres

Bright-field images indicated the relatively homogeneous size of Alg-Gel microspheres induced by ionic cross-linking (Fig. 2A). Data showed that the distribution of HT-29 cells inside the spherical Alg-Gel microspheres was even. Based on the calculation, the mean diameter of Alg-Gel microspheres reached 305.85 ± 19.07 μm (Fig. 2B). These bright-field images and diameter measurements were obtained on day 0, immediately following ionic cross-linking, to characterize the as-fabricated microsphere population before LLLI. Given that substantial swelling and degradation of the Alg-Gel matrix occur on the scale of days rather than hours (Fig. 1B-C: swelling plateauing near 72 h; degradation progressing over 3–12 days), the microspheres were expected to remain structurally stable over the shorter 48 h biological assay window used in the present LLLI experiments.

SEM imaging

SEM data revealed the unique ultrastructural features in Alg-Gel microspheres induced by Ca2+ ion cross-linking (Fig. 2C). Monitoring the external surface of Alg-Gel microspheres revealed the Gel strands randomly distributed in the Alg matrix (Fig. 2C; yellow arrows). Monitoring the cross-section of microspheres indicated the existence of lamellae with interconnected spaces, which can foster cell distribution into the deep layers of microspheres. Incubation for 48 h led to morphological adaptation, flattening, and attachment of cells into the backbone of the supporting matrix (Fig. 2C). Taken together, it is noteworthy to mention that the development of Alg-Gel microspheres using ionic cross-linking contributes to the generation of a porous microenvironment that facilitates cell attachment and distribution.

LLLI exerted fluence-dependent changes in 2D- and 3D-cultured HT-29 cells

In the present study, the cytoprotective and tumoricidal properties of LLLI were assessed on human CRC HT-29 cells cultured on a plastic surface and incorporated into Alg-Gel microspheres (Fig. 3A-B). The exposure of 2D cultured HT-29 cells to LLLI at 2 and 80 J/cm2 intensities led to a dose-dependent survival rate (Fig. 3A), in which HT-29 cells exhibited a significant increase in viability after being exposed to 2 J/cm2 when compared to 80 J/cm2 irradiated cells (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were obtained between the control and 80 J/cm2 groups (p < 0.05). Despite a slight increase in the viability of HT-29 cells exposed to 2 J/cm2, no significant increase was found in terms of survival rate as compared to the non-treated control cells. To monitor the impact of LLLI with different intensities on incorporating HT-29 cells within the Alg-Gel microspheres, the LDH leakage assay was performed (Fig. 3B). Data showed suitable cell membrane integrity in the control and 2 J/cm2 groups, indicated by minimum released LDH contents in the supernatants. Of note, in the 80 J/cm2 group, the levels of LDH reached maximum levels as compared to the control and 2 J/cm2 groups (p < 0.05). These features indicate that LLLI with certain intensities can yield cytoprotective and tumoricidal properties on human CRC HT-29 cells.

LLLI increased NO levels at higher intensities in 2D and 3D culture systems

The tumoricidal properties of LLLI were studied in cultured HT-29 cells in 2D and 3D culture systems by monitoring the NO levels (Fig. 3C-D). Based on the obtained data, exposure of cultured HT-29 cells to LLLI at 2 J/cm2 on a plastic surface did not significantly alter NO production compared with the control group (p > 0.05; Fig. 3C). Whereas LLLI at 80 J/cm2 promoted significant NO production as compared to the control HT-29 cells, indicating the induction of nitrosative stress (p < 0.05). Similar to data from a 2D culture system, the exposure of HT-29 cell-laden Alg-Gel microspheres to 80 J/cm2 LLLI led to a statistically significant increase in NO compared to the control and 2 J/cm2 groups (p < 0.05; Fig. 3D). Very interestingly, it was shown that the NO levels decreased in the 2 J/cm2 group even below the basal levels in comparison with the control group (p < 0.05). These data confirmed that the toxic effects of LLLI on HT-29 cells, either in a 2D culture system or 3D Alg-Gel microspheres, at higher intensities could be in part related to nitrosative stress.

Histological examination revealed the toxic effects of high LLLI

H & E staining was also done to assess HT-29 cell densities within the Alg-Gel hydrogel after being exposed to LLLI at densities of 2 and 80 J/cm2 (Fig. 4A-B). It was shown that higher laser intensities (80 J/cm2) led to reduced cell densities after 48 h in comparison with non-treated HT-29 cells (p < 0.05; Fig. 4A-B, arrow heads). The highest cell number per high-power field of Alg-Gel microspheres was found in the 2 J/cm2 group when compared to the control and 80 J/cm2 groups (p < 0.05). Taken together, lower laser intensities can stimulate the survival rate of CRC cells either in a 2D culture system or inside the 3D Alg-Gel microspheres, while higher doses can exert tumoricidal effects. These data highlight the selection of suitable laser intensities for therapeutic approaches.

LLLI altered the expression of Wnt signaling pathway genes

To monitor the effect of LLLI on the Wnt signaling axis, the expression of various genes was studied using real-time PCR analysis (Fig. 5; Table 2). It was indicated that LLLI at 2 J/cm² increased the expression of canonical Wnt genes such as TCF7 (14.19-fold), GSK3β (6.06-fold), and DKK1 (3.12-fold), while the expression of WNT3A (-3.10-fold) was reduced. In this group, the expression of genes related to planar Cell polarity, such as DAAM1 and WNT5B, was 4.33-fold and − 2.89-fold, respectively. These changes were accompanied by the down-regulation of MMP7 (-2.76-fold) after irradiation with 2J/cm2 dose of LLLI. MMP7 and WNT3A are related to cell fate, cell growth, and proliferation signaling transduction pathways and were inhibited by the low LLLI (Fig. 5; Table 2). Despite the down-regulation of MMP7, the transcription of DKK1 (3.12-fold) was stimulated, which can increase cell migration and motility inside the Alg-Gel microspheres. Calling attention, it can be said that low intensity of LLLI can stimulate the Wnt signaling pathway in incorporated HT-29 cells within the Alg-Gel microspheres. The strong expression of canonical Wnt genes such as DKK1 (7.58-fold), DKK3 (2.22-fold), GSK3β (15.86-fold), TCF7 (6.93-fold), WNT1 (2.36-fold), and WNT3A (4.81-fold) was evident in cells exposed to an 80 J/cm2 dose of LLLI. Of note, LLLI at 80 J/cm2 led to prominent expression of DAAM1 (16.12-fold) related to planar cell polarity as compared to the control and 2 J/cm2 groups. The expression of WNT1 and WNT3A reached 2.36- and 4.81-fold in 80 J/cm2, respectively, indicating the activation of a compensatory response in irradiated cells to maintain cell viability and fate. In contrast to the 2 J/cm2 group, the expression of MMP-9 (2.32-fold) was stimulated in cells exposed to 80 J/cm2 LLLI as compared to the 2 J/cm2 group. This gene can regulate cell proliferation, growth, and some of the Wnt signaling target genes. It seems that the up-regulation of WNT3A (4.81-fold) can help the cells to restore their proliferation properties in this group. The expression of genes related to migration capacity, including DKK1 (3.12-fold) and WNT1 (2.36-fold), was also stimulated in high LLLI doses. These data show that LLLI at high intensities can dysregulate the normal function of the Wnt signaling pathway via strong inhibition or stimulation of certain effectors involved in cell survival and dynamic growth.

Discussion

The prevalence of human CRC, with high-rate mortality and morbidity, is one of the most challenging issues in the clinical setting [38]. A cell culture system is thought to be an efficient screening tool for assessing tumoricidal properties of different compounds [39]. Unfortunately, data obtained from 2D culture systems cannot be applied directly to in vivo conditions because of inherent limitations related to cell behavior, lack of heterogeneity, and treatment-response patterns [40]. In this regard, development of sophisticated culture systems that better approximate in vivo conditions is recommended. In the present study, human HT-29 cells were encapsulated within ionically cross-linked Alg-Gel microspheres to establish a 3D CRC model.

FTIR findings supported integration of Alg and Gel in the presence of CaCl₂. The Alg-Gel hydrogel showed swelling and degradation characteristics relevant to exchange of water and solutes within the matrix [41]. Hydrogel swelling can facilitate water uptake and diffusion of soluble compounds [42], although pore size, porosity, gel fraction, and bond characteristics influence this behavior [43]. In calcium-cross-linked alginate, exchange of Ca²⁺ with monovalent ions can promote electrostatic repulsion of carboxyl groups and network relaxation [44]. Gel fraction and degradation behavior are interrelated [45], and highly porous structures may degrade more rapidly in biological fluids [46]. The interconnected spaces observed by SEM were consistent with the distribution of HT-29 cells throughout the microspheres. Alg-Gel matrices can provide cell-adhesive motifs and support morphological adaptation [47], while maintaining a three-dimensional environment for cell-cell interactions [48].

Based on the obtained data, the biological responses differed depending on experimental conditions. The 2 J/cm² exposure showed a modest, assay-dependent cytoprotective pattern, whereas 80 J/cm² was associated with increased LDH release, higher NO production, and lower cell density. Differences between MTT and LDH findings may reflect the distinct biological endpoints measured by these assays and the altered metabolic and transport environment of 3D matrices [49]. A previous endothelial-cell study using related laser conditions reported preserved mitochondrial ultrastructure [50]; however, those observations cannot exclude heating in the present experiment because the cell type, culture geometry, irradiated area, irradiance, medium volume, and exposure duration differed. In particular, in the 80 J/cm² group the microsphere exposure time lasted 1098.74 s (approximately 18.3 min), so cumulative thermal effects remain plausible. Accordingly, the reduced survival at 80 J/cm² should not be attributed exclusively to a non-thermal photochemical mechanism. The increase in NO is consistent with nitrosative stress, and bidirectional interactions between NO and Wnt/β-catenin signaling have been reported [35, 51]; nevertheless, the present data establish association rather than causality. Excess reactive species can impair tumor-cell viability [52], and 3D culture can modify nutrient diffusion, proliferation, and treatment sensitivity [53]. Therefore, the Wnt-related RT-qPCR data require particularly cautious interpretation. Wnt signaling is regulated at multiple levels, including ligand-receptor interactions, inhibitory proteins, GSK3β activity, β-catenin stability and localization, and TCF/LEF-dependent transcription [54]. At 2 J/cm², increased TCF7 and DAAM1 expression occurred together with increased DKK1 and GSK3β and decreased WNT3A, WNT5B, and MMP7. Because DKK1 and GSK3B can oppose canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling, this mixed pattern does not support a unidirectional activation claim. At 80 J/cm², up-regulation of WNT1, WNT3A, TCF7, and MMP7 was accompanied by marked increases in DKK1, DKK3, and GSK3B, again indicating a complex transcriptional stress or feedback response rather than confirmed pathway activation. Related studies have reported PBM-associated changes in cell viability and Wnt-related signaling [55], and stress-responsive gene expression has been described in HT-29 cells after phototherapy [56]; however, correlations between these transcriptional changes, NO production, and survival remain hypothesis-generating. Protein-level measurements of total and active β-catenin, phospho-GSK3β, DKK proteins and Wnt ligands, together with β-catenin nuclear-localization analysis, TCF/LEF reporter assays, and pathway-specific functional perturbation, are required before activation or inhibition of a Wnt module can be concluded. This interpretation is consistent with mechanistic reviews emphasizing the context- and dose-dependent complexity of photobiomodulation in cancer [57].

The present study faces some limitations that need further consideration in future experiments. A real-time PCR panel of the Wnt signaling pathway monitored the changes in transcriptional levels of target genes. To ascertain the activation/inhibtion of Wnt signaling pathway modules, monitoring the protein levels with some functional assays is mandatory. The addition of other natural substrates to mimic a tumor-like microenvironment can efficiently reflect the in vivo-like molecular and cellular changes on a laboratory scale. Surface temperature fluctuation was not directly measured during irradiation, and monitoring surface temperature values can give us possible thermal and non-thermal effects in LLLI-irradiated cells.

Conclusion

In the present study, LLLI exerted dose-dependent, bimodal effects on human CRC HT-29 cells encapsulated within Alg-Gel hydrogel microspheres. It was found that a low-intensity dose (2 J/cm²) was associated with a modest, assay-dependent cytoprotective trend, while a high-intensity dose (80 J/cm²) reduced cell survival by increasing nitrosative stress. It is assumed that these changes were accompanied by transcriptional modulation of multiple Wnt signaling pathway effectors. The current data are preliminary, hypothesis-generating from in vitro observations using a single CRC cell line. Monitoring certain items related to precise evaluation of LLLI, such as direct temperature verification, different laser dose effects, protein changes of Wnt signaling pathway components, with other relevant cell lines can help us in a deep understanding of LLLI impacts on 3D culture systems. Considering some limitations, the applied Alg-Gel microsphere system seems to be a feasible and valid 3D platform for future dosimetric and mechanistic studies of laser-based phototherapy in CRC studies. The findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating and should be validated using real-time thermal monitoring, protein and functional pathway assays, additional fluences, and other colorectal-cancer models.
