The effect of air pollution on chronic liver disease and the modifying effect of physical activity: a nationwide study from CHARLS
Authors: Wei Xu, Mingjie Li, Xiang Ma, Kai Lei, Wanping Shi, Zuojin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe relationship between ambient air pollution and chronic liver disease (CLD), and whether physical activity (PA) modifies this association, remains unclear.Materials and methodsWe analyzed 17,708 middle-aged and older adults from the 2013 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Individual-level exposures to CO, O3, and NO2 were estimated from the China High Concentration Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (0.1°resolution). CLD status was assessed by structured questionnaire. PA was measured using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and categorized as light (LPA), moderate (MPA), or vigorous (VPA). Multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for demographic and health covariates were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) for CLD; effect modification by PA was tested via interaction terms and stratified analyses.ResultsIn fully adjusted models, higher pollutant levels were associated with increased CLD risk: CO (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.04–1.19, p = 0.025), O3 (OR 1.15, 95% CI 1.08–1.32, p = 0.014), and NO₂ (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.47, p = 0.032). Stratified analyses showed attenuation of pollutant–CLD associations with increasing PA. For CO: LPA 1.15 (0.97–1.46), MPA 0.89 (0.78–1.02), VPA 0.81 (0.61–1.21). For O3: LPA 1.14 (0.93–1.36), MPA 1.09 (0.98–1.21), VPA 0.99 (0.68–1.14). For NO2: LPA 1.05 (0.89–1.21), MPA 0.91 (0.59–1.17), VPA 0.85 (0.61–0.98). Interaction tests were significant for CO (p = 0.021), O3 (p = 0.023), and NO2 (p = 0.028).ConclusionsAmbient CO, O3, and NO2 are associated with higher CLD risk, and higher PA levels appear to mitigate these associations. Promoting appropriate PA may help reduce pollution-related liver disease burden.

Introduction

As industrialization and urbanization have accelerated, air pollution has emerged as a progressively severe global challenge [1]. Research indicates that air pollution is linked to the risk of numerous systemic diseases, including respiratory conditions [2, 3], cardiovascular diseases [4, 5], dementia [6], and depressive symptoms [7, 8]. Additionally, air pollution may even impact fertility [9]. Several studies suggest that exposure to air pollutants can induce oxidative stress, impair immune function, and disrupt hormone levels, which could contribute to the development of various diseases [10–13].

The connection between air pollution and CLD has garnered significant attention. CLD, which includes conditions such as chronic hepatitis B, alcoholic hepatitis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, and other forms of hepatitis, has become a major public health concern with an increasing incidence rate [14, 15]. Globally, CLD ranks as the 11th leading cause of death, accounting for 4% of all fatalities. In China, more than one-fifth of the population suffers from CLD, underscoring the substantial burden it places on public health [16, 17]. Identifying and addressing modifiable risk factors is crucial for effectively tackling this public health issue. Recent epidemiological studies have shown that both short-term and long-term exposure to ambient air pollution is associated with a higher risk of CLD. Long-term exposure to outdoor pollutants like particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, and PM10) is believed to increase the likelihood of developing CLD. Moreover, indoor air pollution, such as that caused by household solid fuel use, has also been linked to an elevated risk of liver disease [18–20]. However, evidence regarding the association between other air pollutants—such as carbon monoxide (CO), ozone (O3), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2)—and the risk of CLD remains limited.

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as a protective factor against multiple chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, obesity, and some cancers [21–23]. Emerging evidence suggests that PA may also mitigate some of the adverse health effects of air pollution [24–28]. Mechanistically, PA can improve antioxidant capacity, enhance immune regulation, and promote metabolic homeostasis, which may counteract oxidative stress and inflammation induced by air pollutants [29–31]. For example, cohort studies have reported that higher levels of PA attenuate the associations between air pollution exposure and risks of cardiovascular events and respiratory symptoms [25, 27]. Nevertheless, the role of PA in modulating the effects of air pollution on liver health, particularly in relation to CLD risk, has not been thoroughly investigated.

Given these gaps, this study aims to investigate the relationship between specific air pollutants (CO, O3, and NO2) and the risk of CLD among middle-aged and older adults. In addition, we aim to assess whether PA can mitigate the detrimental effects of air pollution on liver health. By examining both environmental and behavioral factors, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of modifiable determinants of CLD risk and offer evidence-based strategies for disease prevention.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationwide, community-based cohort of residents aged ≥ 45 years. CHARLS covers ~ 150 county-level regions in 28 provinces and employs multistage, stratified, probability-proportional-to-size sampling; waves were conducted in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Because high-resolution air pollution data in China are available from 2013 onward, we used CHARLS data from 2013–2020. Initially 17,708 participants who underwent physical examinations were considered; after excluding participants with missing air-pollution exposure data or incomplete CLD information and those with prevalent CLD at the 2013 baseline, the analytic sample comprised 9,632 participants from 27 provinces (Fig. 1). CLD status was ascertained at each wave using a structured questionnaire (self-reported physician diagnosis). To account for the longitudinal/repeated-measures structure, we fitted mixed-effects logistic regression models with a participant-level random intercept to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between pollutant exposure and CLD. Pollutants were modeled continuously; PA was categorized by IPAQ into light, moderate, and vigorous and was included as both a main effect and as an interaction term with pollutant exposure to evaluate effect modification. Models adjusted for age, sex, education, urban/rural residence, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, and major comorbidities. Robustness was evaluated via sensitivity analyses using generalized estimating equations (exchangeable correlation) and alternative model specifications; results were consistent. Statistical analyses were performed in R, and all participants provided written informed consent; the study was approved by the Peking University Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-11015).Fig. 1Flowchart of participants’ selection process

Air pollution assessment

Full-coverage ground-level concentrations of NO2, O3, and CO were obtained from the China High Concentration Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (0.1°spatial resolution, ≈10 km; https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). The CHAP product integrates multiple information sources — including ground-based monitoring, satellite remote sensing, atmospheric reanalysis, and chemical transport/meteorological model outputs, and applies a machine-learning framework to generate daily pollutant estimates. Specifically, the Space–Time Extremely Randomized Trees (STET) algorithm was used to predict daily ambient NO2, O3, and CO concentrations on the 0.1° grid. Detailed descriptions of data inputs, model training, and validation procedures are provided in the CHAP documentation and previous publications [21, 32–35]. For linkage to individual participants, daily grid-level concentrations were aggregated to wave-specific exposure metrics: for the primary analysis we calculated the mean concentration during the 12 months preceding each CHARLS interview (1-year average, time-updated). Grid cells were matched to participants by residential location (community/township or county/district); for participants without precise community coordinates the county/district centroid was used. Units and model performance metrics are reported in the CHAP documentation and cited references above.

Chronic liver disease assessment

Trained investigators carried out face-to-face interviews by means of structured questionnaires to gather data concerning cases of CLD. The initial determination of whether an individual had CLD was made based on the following question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with CLD (excluding fatty liver, tumors, and cancer) by a healthcare professional?” To minimize potential recall bias, the disease status of participants was further evaluated by asking, “Are you currently undergoing any treatments (such as traditional Chinese medicine, Western medicine, or other therapies) for CLD or its associated complications?” Subsequently, the CLD status was categorized as either “yes” or “no” in accordance with the combined answers to these two questions.

Physical activity assessment

PA information was gathered by employing a standardized questionnaire, namely the revised International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [36]. Specifically, participants were asked about the duration, frequency (in days), and intensity (vigorous, moderate, or light) of PA for at least 10 min per week. The daily PA time was divided into five groups: < 10 min, 10–29 min, 30–119 min, 120–239 min, and ≥ 240 min. To facilitate the calculation and address the lack of specific PA durations in CHARLS, we used the median durations for each category. For < 10 min and ≥ 240 min of PA, 0 and 240 min were assigned, respectively, referring to the previous literature [37, 38]. The weekly total PA time was calculated by multiplying the days per week by the duration per day. Metabolic equivalents (MET) were used to characterize PA intensity [39]. According to previous studies [37, 38], MET values were 8.0, 4.0, and 3.3 for vigorous, moderate, and mild PA, respectively. PA = 8.0 × weekly vigorous exercise time + 4.0 × weekly moderate intensity exercise time + 3.3 × weekly exercise time. Finally, according to the previous study [40, 41], PA was divided into three levels (light PA < P33.3, P33.3 < = moderate PA < P66.7, vigorous PA >= P66.7).

Covariates

In accordance with previous literature, potential covariates were incorporated and adjusted within the models. These covariates encompassed gender (male or female), age (years), Location (Village or City/Town), Education (Below primary school, Primary school, Middle School, and High school and above), marital status (non-married, or married), smoking status (Never, Former, and Current), alcohol drinking status (Never, Former, and Current), and other chronic diseases (0, 1, or ≥ 2). Chronic diseases, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic lung disease, heart attack, stroke, kidney disease, digestive disease, arthritis or rheumatism, and asthma, were ascertained based on self-reported physician diagnoses. For all missing values of the covariates, multiple imputations were employed to address the data gaps.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were carried out to scrutinize the baseline characteristics of both the CLD group and the control group. Parametric or nonparametric tests, as well as chi-square tests, were respectively utilized to contrast the disparities in continuous and categorical variables between the two cohorts.

Logistic regression was employed to appraise the associations between air pollution and CLD. The effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported in the form of the OR. Initially, a crude model devoid of any adjustments was constructed. Subsequently, Model 1 (and later Model 2) was implemented to make allowances for meteorological factors (such as temperature and relative humidity), sociodemographic elements (age and sex), socioeconomic variables (residence, education level, marital status), and the history of other chronic diseases. Eventually, health behaviors and lifestyles (encompassing smoking status, drinking status, and physical activity) were also factored in and adjusted for in the adjusted Model 2 (subsequently renamed Model 3). Moreover, subgroup analysis was further conducted to stratify the study populations based on gender, age, education, residence, and smoking, with the aim of exploring possible modifying impacts among diverse vulnerable populations.

We included multiplicative pollutant × PA interaction terms in multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression models (participant-level random intercept) to assess effect modification by PA (three categories: LPA, MPA, VPA), and used a likelihood-ratio test (LRT) comparing the full model with interaction to the nested model without interaction to obtain the reported “p for interaction” (joint test of the two dummy interaction coefficients). Stratified ORs (95% CIs) for each PA subgroup were estimated from the full model, and pairwise post-hoc contrasts were computed where needed using estimated marginal means. A sensitivity analysis was performed on the unimputed dataset to replicate the primary results. All analyses were two-sided, used an α of 0.05, and were conducted in R (version 3.6.0) with the lme4 and emmeans packages.

Associations of air pollution on CLD

Table 2 shows the associations between air pollutants and the presence of chronic liver disease (CLD). In the unadjusted models, the ORs (per unit increase as defined in the main analysis) were 1.06 (95% CI: 1.01–1.11, p = 0.008) for CO, 1.08 (95% CI: 1.03–1.33, p = 0.021) for O3, and 1.11 (95% CI: 1.02–1.19, p = 0.011) for NO2. After sequential adjustment for meteorological, sociodemographic, and socioeconomic factors, and further adjustment for health behaviors (smoking, drinking, and physical activity), the associations remained and were slightly strengthened: CO (fully adjusted OR 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04–1.19, p = 0.025), O3 (fully adjusted OR 1.15, 95% CI: 1.08–1.32, p = 0.014), and NO2 (fully adjusted OR 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09–1.47, p = 0.032). Thus, in the fully adjusted models each unit increase in CO, O3 and NO2 was associated with 13%, 15% and 18% higher odds of CLD, respectively.

To assess effect modification by physical activity (PA), we fitted pollutant × PA interaction terms in multivariable mixed-effects logistic regression models. The likelihood-ratio tests for interaction were significant for CO (p = 0.021), O3(p = 0.023), and NO2 (p = 0.028), indicating that the pollutant–CLD associations differed across PA levels. From the full interaction models, stratified ORs (95% CIs) by PA level were as follows. For CO: LPA 1.15 (0.97–1.46), MPA 0.89 (0.78–1.02), VPA 0.81 (0.61–1.21). For O3: LPA 1.14 (0.93–1.36), MPA 1.09 (0.98–1.21), VPA 0.99 (0.68–1.14). For NO2: LPA 1.05 (0.89–1.21), MPA 0.91 (0.59–1.17), VPA 0.85 (0.61–0.98). These patterns indicate stronger pollutant–CLD associations in the light-PA group and attenuated—or in the case of NO2 among VPA participants, statistically significant protective—associations in higher PA groups, suggesting that higher PA levels may mitigate the adverse effects of ambient air pollution on liver health.

Subgroup analysis of the association between air pollution and CLD

The subgroup analysis demonstrated that the associations between CO, O₃, and NO₂ exposure and CLD risk varied across different populations (Figs. 2, 3, 4). For CO, urban residents exhibited a stronger association than rural residents (OR: 1.38 vs. 1.14, P for interaction = 0.015), and current smokers showed the highest vulnerability (OR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.05–1.77). For O₃, urban residents were also more affected compared to rural residents (OR: 1.31 vs. 1.15, P for interaction = 0.021), while other subgroups did not exhibit significant interaction effects. Regarding NO₂, urban populations again displayed a heightened risk compared to rural populations (OR: 1.13 vs. 0.88, P for interaction = 0.040), with higher education appearing to confer a protective effect (OR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83–0.99 for high school and above). The modifying role of PA

Figure 5 presents the effect of air pollution on CLD stratified by PA levels. The results of this analysis indicate that PA level significantly modifies the relationship between air pollution and CLD risk. For CO exposure, the association with CLD risk was stronger in individuals with light physical activity (LPA), with an OR of 1.15 (95% CI: 0.97–1.46), while moderate and vigorous physical activity (MPA and VPA) showed weaker associations, suggesting that lower PA levels increase the susceptibility to CO-related CLD. Similarly, for O₃ exposure, the association was stronger in the LPA group (OR: 1.14, 95% CI: 0.93–1.36), but the effect was less pronounced for MPA and VPA. For NO₂ exposure, the strongest protective effect was seen in individuals with high PA levels (VPA: OR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.61–0.98), indicating that higher physical activity may reduce the negative effects of NO₂ exposure on CLD risk. Discussion

The air pollution problem caused by the development of industrialization is becoming more and more serious. A large amount of waste gas, waste water, and waste residue discharged during industrial production have caused great damage to the environment. Previous studies have found that some air pollutants, such as particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10, etc.), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and sulfur dioxide (SO2), have adverse effects on the respiratory system and cardiovascular system, and may also affect liver health through a variety of ways [42–44]. This study used the data of CHARLS to analyze the significant correlation between ambient air pollution exposure and the occurrence of CLD in middle-aged and elderly people in China, mainly through exposure to CO, O3, and NO2. After adjusting for potential confounders and fully adjusting the model, the odds of CLD increased by 13% for each unit increase in CO level (OR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.04–1.19, p = 0.025). For each unit increase in O3 level, the odds of CLD increased by 15% (OR = 1.15; 95% CI: 1.08–1.32, p = 0.014). For each unit increase in NO2 level, the odds of CLD increased by 18% (OR = 1.18; (95%CI: 1.09–1.47, p = 0.032). Subgroup analysis revealed that the association between CO, O₃, and NO₂ exposure and the risk of CLD differed among different populations. Subgroup analyses of the effects of demographic and lifestyle factors on air pollution exposure revealed a greater risk of CLD among urban residents and smokers. This study also examined the effect of PA on the association between air pollution and the risk of CLD and found that higher levels of PA may play a protective role in the occurrence of CLD. This suggests that levels of PA substantially modify the association between air pollution and CLD risk.

The association between air pollutants and the risk of liver disease has also been confirmed in some previous studies. Higher ambient PM2.5 and NO2 are linked with higher odds of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and advanced fibrosis [20, 45]. Long-term exposure to outdoor air pollution, such as ambient particulate matter (PM1, PM2.5, and PM10), is thought to be associated with an increased risk of CLD incidence. It can even lead to the occurrence of liver cancer [46]. In addition to outdoor air pollution, indoor air pollution, such as household solid fuel use, has also been linked to an increased risk of liver disease. Harmful substances in solid fuels and tobacco smoke are thought to enter the bloodstream through inhalation and be metabolized in the liver, leading to chronic liver injury [18, 19, 47]. Some animal experiments further confirmed the relevant conclusions. Liver oxidative stress and inflammation caused by PM2.5 inhalation lead to abnormal liver function, which further promotes lipid accumulation in the liver, thus aggravating normal lipid metabolism and leading to liver damage [48]. Animal models of infection have shown that fine particulate matter in the environment facilitates the penetration of the H1N1 virus into the distal lungs and its transport to extrapulmonary organs, including the liver, leading to severe local and systemic disease [49].

The possible mechanisms of air pollution affecting CLD include inflammatory response and oxidative stress, immune dysfunction, and changes in liver metabolic function. Various pollutants in the atmosphere, such as PM2.5, sulfur dioxide, carbon monoxide, and ozone, can trigger inflammatory stress and oxidative stress responses in the human body, which have adverse effects on the liver [32, 50, 51]. The immune dysfunction caused by air pollution may lead to the decline of the immune surveillance and defense function of the liver, which makes the liver more vulnerable to viral infection, alcohol intake, obesity, and other factors, thereby increasing the risk of CLD. Liver metabolic disorders caused by air pollution are closely related to the occurrence and development of CLD. Liver lipid overload can induce the overproduction of oxidators by affecting the generation mechanisms of several reactive oxygen species, and increase the risk of liver injury [52–55].

High levels of PA confer a beneficial effect on the risk of CLD. High levels of PA can reduce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, and exercise helps to increase anti-inflammatory markers and enhance the antioxidant defenses of the body. These factors can reduce adverse effects on the liver [56]. Higher levels of PA can lead to better body mass index (BMI), and better BMI can reduce the occurrence of NAFLD [57, 58]. PA also reduces the levels of stress hormones (such as cortisol), and the decrease in stress hormone levels reduces the metabolic burden on the liver, which has a positive effect on the risk of CLD [59, 60].

Efforts have been made to reduce air pollution in China and even in the world, but air pollution is still a major problem for global health. Air pollution is significantly associated with the risk of a variety of diseases. Air pollutants are significantly associated with the risk of CLD, and high levels of PA can reduce the effect of air pollution on the risk of CLD. Based on these findings, we should recognize the important role of air pollution in our daily life and health. Governments should enact and enforce stricter air quality regulations to further reduce pollution levels through higher emission standards, improved monitoring systems, and stringent compliance enforcement. In addition, public health campaigns should be conducted to raise awareness of the risk of PA for CLD and to encourage individuals to incorporate regular exercise into their daily life.

This study has several limitations. First, we estimated ambient pollutant concentrations at participants’ residential locations, which may not accurately reflect personal exposure because individuals spend substantial time indoors and in other microenvironments (workplaces, transit, etc.). Indoor sources and time–activity patterns were not captured, potentially leading to exposure misclassification; such non-differential measurement error would likely attenuate true associations. Second, we did not evaluate all possible air pollutants and, owing to collinearity among pollutants, our primary analyses focused on single-pollutant models; although sensitivity and stratified analyses were performed, residual confounding by co-pollutants cannot be excluded. Third, CLD status was based on self-reported physician diagnosis from questionnaire data and may be subject to recall or reporting bias; objective clinical measures would strengthen causal inference. Finally, unmeasured or imperfectly measured individual-level factors (e.g., occupational exposures, indoor air quality, and detailed socioeconomic variables) may have influenced the results despite multivariable adjustment. Future studies using personal exposure monitoring, medical-record–verified outcomes, and multi-pollutant exposure assessment are warranted to confirm and refine our findings.

Conclusions

This study proves that air pollution significantly affects the risk of CLD, and higher PA levels mitigate the effect of air pollution on the risk of CLD. This provides evidence for the development of better public health policies.
