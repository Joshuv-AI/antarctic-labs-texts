Sequential Monte Carlo Squared for Online Inference in Stochastic Epidemic Models
Dhorasso Temfack and Jason Wyse School of Computer Science and Statistics, Trinity College Dublin, Ireland

Abstract
Effective epidemic modeling and surveillance require computationally efficient methods that can continuously update estimates as new data becomes available. This paper explores the application of an online variant of Sequential Monte Carlo Squared (O-SMC 2 ) to the stochastic Susceptible-Exposed-Infectious-Removed (SEIR) model for real-time epidemic tracking. The particularity of O-SMC 2 lies in its ability to update the parameters using a particle Metropolis-Hastings kernel, ensuring that the target distribution remains invariant while only utilizing a fixed window of recent observations. This feature enables timely parameter updates and significantly enhances computational efficiency compared to the standard SMC 2 , which processes the entire dataset. First, we demonstrate the efficiency of O-SMC 2 on simulated data, where both the parameters and the observation process are known. We then apply the method to a real-world COVID-19 dataset from Ireland, successfully tracking the epidemic trajectory and estimating the time-dependent reproduction number of the disease. Our results show that O-SMC 2 provides highly accurate online estimates of both static and dynamic epidemiological parameters while substantially reducing computational costs. These findings highlight the potential of O-SMC 2 for real-time epidemic monitoring and supporting adaptive public health interventions.

Disease modeling, Stochastic model, Sequential Monte Carlo, Online inference

Introduction

Disease modeling plays a crucial role in public health by providing insights into the mechanisms behind the spread of infectious diseases. Such understanding is vital for designing effective intervention strategies and supporting evidence-based policy-making. As the global landscape of infectious diseases evolves and the risk of novel outbreaks increases
(
Marani et al. 2021
)
, there is a pressing need for robust modeling frameworks that can adapt to changing data, update parameter values as new information becomes available, and deliver accurate assessments and predictions of epidemic trajectories
(
Birrell et al. 2017
)
.

Bayesian approaches have emerged as powerful tools for tackling these challenges, particularly in sampling from the posterior distribution of model parameters. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods have been widely utilized in epidemic analysis
(
O’Neill and Roberts 1999
;
Jewell et al. 2009
;
Wang and Walker 2022
)
, enabling exact inference but requiring an explicit expression for the complete-data likelihood. Furthermore, these methods can be computationally intensive and inefficient for real-time applications, as they necessitate reanalyzing the entire dataset with each new data point
(
Birrell et al. 2017
)
. An alternative approach is Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC), which has gained popularity for analyzing epidemic processes due to its ability to bypass the need for explicit likelihood formulations
(
Kypraios et al. 2017
)
. While ABC offers flexibility, it relies on summary statistics, which can introduce bias and limit its precision
(
Kypraios et al. 2017
)
. Furthermore, ABC does not inherently address the challenge of sequentially updating estimates as new data are collected.

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods, also known as particle filtering, offer an attractive solution to update the posterior distribution of a stochastic process as new data become available
(
Doucet et al. 2001
)
. These methods have seen increasing application in epidemiology, with early studies demonstrating their effectiveness in calibrating nonlinear compartmental infectious disease models
(
Yang et al. 2014
;
Camacho et al. 2015
)
. For instance,
Welding and Neal 2019
employed a sequential Bayesian framework to the 2001 UK Foot-and-Mouth epidemic, utilizing MCMC algorithms to rejuvenate particles and facilitate real-time outbreak analysis through data augmentation. Additionally,
Birrell et al. 2020
proposed an age-structured SEIR model for influenza outbreaks, showcasing the advantages of SMC over MCMC for online prediction, particularly in integrating multiple data sources for parameter inference.

However, it is important to recognize that traditional SMC algorithms are not well suited for estimating static parameters
(
Kantas et al. 2015
)
. Although extensions like particle MCMC (PMCMC)
(
Andrieu et al. 2010
)
have been developed to achieve exact sampling from the posterior by creating unbiased estimators for the likelihood through simulation and subsequently applying traditional MCMC methods, they remain computationally demanding. Each MCMC iteration typically requires a full SMC run, making these methods less suitable for real-time applications
(
Dureau et al. 2013
;
Funk et al. 2018
;
Endo et al. 2019
;
Cazelles et al. 2021
)
. In their recent study,
Storvik et al. 2023
propose an SMC framework for inferring a time-varying reproduction number of COVID-19 in Norway. It is essential to highlight that this SMC algorithm relies on a set of sufficient statistics to sequentially estimate hyperparameters that control the evolution of the reproduction number. However, the estimation of the posterior parameter relies on the assumption that sufficient statistics-based filters are analytically tractable, which will only hold for particular dynamical system setups.

A promising solution to these challenges is the SMC
2
method
(
Chopin et al. 2013
;
Jacob 2015
)
, which enhances traditional particle filtering by integrating a nested filtering mechanism. This approach simultaneously estimates model states and parameters, improving both the accuracy and adaptability of the estimation process. A key feature of the SMC
2
algorithm is the “rejuvenation” step, which uses a PMCMC kernel to mitigate particle impoverishment. However, this step traditionally requires computing the likelihood using all past data points, which may limit its online applicability.

In this paper, we implement an online variant of the SMC
2
method (O-SMC
2
), which reduces computational costs per iteration by targeting an approximate distribution and evaluating the PMCMC over a fixed window of recent observations
(
Vieira 2018
)
. This adaptation preserves the sequential and online attributes of the method, emphasizing its practical application in real-time state and parameter estimation for epidemic models, specifically the stochastic SEIR model.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section
2
describes the state-space framework for the SEIR compartmental model and provides a brief overview of the SMC
2
/O-SMC
2
methodology. In Section
3
, we apply the O-SMC
2
algorithm to both simulated data and real-world COVID-19 data from Ireland, demonstrating its potential for real-time epidemiological surveillance and for inferring the time-dependent reproduction number. Finally, Section
4
concludes the paper by summarizing the main findings and discussing the potential challenges and limitations associated with our model.

In the context of the SEIR model, the system can be formulated as a state-space model (SSM), which consists of two stochastic processes: the first process governs the evolution of the disease within the population, while the second process represents the observation process. State-space models are particularly useful for modeling complex dynamical systems such as disease transmission where the true states are not directly observable, and observations are subject to noise or other forms of uncertainty. By explicitly modeling the hidden states and their relationship to observed data, we can perform inference on both the state variables and the model parameters.

2.3.1
Particle filtering

For a given value of the model parameters
\theta
, we aim to infer the latent variables
x_{t}
at each time point, given the observations
y_{1:t}
by means of the filtering distribution
p(x_{t}|y_{1:t},\theta)

\{x_{t}^{i},w_{t}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{x}}

\theta

\displaystyle p(x_{t}|y_{1:t},\theta)\approx\dfrac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{x}}w_{t}^{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}w_{t}^{i}\delta_{x_{t}^{i}}(x_{t}).

(6)

Here,

\displaystyle w_{t}^{i}=w_{t-1}^{i}\dfrac{g(y_{t}|x_{t}^{i},\theta)f(x_{t}^{i}|x_{t-1}^{i},\theta)}{q(x_{t}^{i}|x_{t-1}^{i},y_{t},\theta)},\quad i=1,\ldots,N_{x},

2.4
Online SMC
2

While SMC
2
allows for sequential estimation of parameters as new observations arrive, it is not a truly online method. The PMMH step, which has a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(tN_{\theta}N_{x})
, involves a calculation of the total likelihood. This complexity increases with the number of observations
t
, making it computationally expensive as more data becomes available. Although
Chopin et al. 2013
demonstrates that, in theory, the frequency at which the rejuvenation step is performed will decrease over time, the algorithm may still be impractical for some real-time applications. A possible solution suggested by
Vieira 2018
to implement SMC
2
in an online manner (O-SMC
2
) is to restrict the likelihood calculation set to a fixed-size subset of past observations, keeping the computational cost bounded. By considering a fixed window of observations of size
t_{k}>0
in the PMMH, we can ensure that the online part of SMC
2
will not be altered, and the online approximation will target the posterior of the parameters given the observation window
p(\theta|y_{t-t_{k}:t})
. This approach changes the cost from
\mathcal{O}(tN_{\theta}N_{x})
to
\mathcal{O}(t_{k}N_{\theta}N_{x})
.

Hence, in step
8
of Algorithm
2
, once we have reached
t>t_{k}
, the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability will now take the form:

\displaystyle\min\left\{1,\dfrac{\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta^{*})p(\theta^{*})}{\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta^{m}_{t})p(\theta^{m}_{t})}\times\dfrac{q(\theta^{m}_{t}|\theta^{*})}{q(\theta^{*}|\theta^{m}_{t})}\right\},

(13)

where the estimate of the marginal likelihood within the time window is computed similarly to (
9
), but this time using the restricted particle set
x_{t-t_{k}:t}^{1:N_{x}}
( see
A
for further details) :

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)=\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}w_{s}^{i}(x_{s-1}^{i},x_{s}^{i})\right).

(14)

This restriction is possible under the assumption that static parameters will remain constant indefinitely
(
Vieira 2018
)
. The O-SMC
2
scheme appears to be particularly amenable to parallelization over parameter particles, as the rejuvenation step can be performed separately for each parameter value.

3.2
Real case study: Analysis of COVID-19 in Ireland

Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in December 2019 in Wuhan, China, the World Health Organization (WHO) has reported over 772 million confirmed cases globally and more than 69 million deaths
(
World Health Organisation (2024) WHO
)
. The rapid transmission and widespread nature of the virus led the WHO to declare it a pandemic. In
(
Cazelles et al. 2021
)
, particle MCMC has been employed to reconstruct the COVID-19 epidemic’s trajectory in Ireland. However, it is worth noting that while this method efficiently tracks observed data, it remains an offline approach and may not be suitable for real-time decision-making when rapid responses are required.

3.2.1
Model

We present a fully discrete stochastic extended-SEIR model to study the spread of COVID-19, with a structure tailored to focus on the change in transmission dynamics during the pandemic. In this model, individuals transition through compartments representing different stages of infection. The model comprises the following compartments:
S_{t}
for susceptible individuals,
E_{t}
for exposed but not yet infectious individuals,
A_{t}
for asymptomatic infected individuals,
I_{t}
for symptomatic infected individuals, and finally,
R_{t}
is the count of individuals recovering or succumbing to the virus. The following discrete equations describe the dynamics of the model:

\displaystyle S_{t+\delta t}

\displaystyle=S_{t}-Y_{SE}(t),

\displaystyle E_{t+\delta t}

\displaystyle=E_{t}+Y_{SE}(t)-Y_{E}(t),

\displaystyle A_{t+\delta t}

\displaystyle=A_{t}+Y_{EA}(t)-Y_{AR}(t),

\displaystyle I_{t+\delta t}

\displaystyle=I_{t}+Y_{E}(t)-Y_{EA}(t)-Y_{IR}(t),

(17)

\displaystyle R_{t+\delta t}

\displaystyle=R_{t}+Y_{AR}(t)+Y_{IR}(t),

where
Y_{AB}(t)
represents the number of individuals moving from compartment
A
to compartment
B
at time
t
and defined by the following binomial distributions:

\displaystyle Y_{SE}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(S_{t},1-\exp\left(-\beta_{t}\frac{(I_{t}+r_{A}A_{t})}{N}\cdot\delta t\right)\right),

\displaystyle Y_{E}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(E_{t},1-e^{-\sigma\cdot\delta t}\right),\quad Y_{EA}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(Y_{E}(t),p_{A}\right),

(18)

\displaystyle Y_{AR}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(A_{t},1-e^{-\gamma\cdot\delta t}\right),\quad Y_{IR}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(I_{t},1-e^{-\gamma\cdot\delta t}\right).

The model assumes a homogeneously mixing population of constant size:

N=S_{t}+E_{t}+A_{t}+I_{t}+R_{t}.

(19)

The models presented in (
3.2.1
)-(
3.2.1
), do not explicitly incorporate vaccination. This is because they are primarily designed to focus on the spread of the virus and the impact of various non-pharmaceutical interventions during a period when vaccines are either unavailable or not yet widely deployed. However, the effect of vaccination can easily be introduced by adding a compartment for vaccinated individuals and adjusting the transmission dynamics to reflect partial or complete immunity. Allowing the transmission rate to be time-varying seems reasonable since, throughout the outbreak, the intensity of disease transmission will tend to vary due to individuals’ behavioral changes or government lockdown measures. Hence, we consider a geometric random walk (RW) model for the transmission rate:

\beta_{t}=e^{\log(\beta_{t-1})+w_{t}},\quad w_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\nu_{\beta}^{2}),

(20)

where
\nu_{\beta}
is the parameter controlling the innovation in the transmission rate, consistent with previous study of COVID-19 in Ireland
(
Cazelles et al. 2021
)
. Giving the distribution of
\beta_{t}
, we can sequentially estimate the distribution of the effective reproduction number (
R_{\text{eff}}(t)
) that refers to the average number of secondary infections caused by a single infected individual during their infectious period in a population where some individuals are immune or other interventions have been implemented:

\displaystyle R_{\text{eff}}(t)=\beta_{t}\left(\frac{(1-p_{A})+p_{A}\cdot r_{A}}{\gamma}\right).\frac{S_{t}}{N}.

(21)

In general, when
R_{\text{eff}}(t)>1
, the number of observed cases will increase, while it will decrease if
R_{\text{eff}}(t)<1
. Consequently, policymakers can determine whether to relax or strengthen control measures based on whether
R_{\text{eff}}
falls below the self-sustaining threshold of
1
.

3.2.2
Data and model fitting

The data source used to make inferences was restricted to daily confirmed cases counts extracted from the updated database of the Health Protection Surveillance Centre (HPSC) (
https://COVID19ireland-geohive.hub.arcgis.com/
). To integrate these observations into our model, we define two variables:
C^{I}_{t}
representing the cumulative number of infectious cases. These are given by the following equation:

C^{I}_{t+1}=C^{I}_{t}+Y_{E}(t)-Y_{EA}(t).

(22)

Hence,
\mu_{t}=C^{I}_{t+1}-C^{I}_{t}
represents the daily expected number of cases. Since daily reported case counts are subject to observation errors and typically exhibit overdispersion (where the conditional variance exceeds the conditional mean), we model the observed daily cases,
Z^{I}_{t}
, using a normal approximation (chosen for computational
efficiency,
Funk et al. 2018
) of the negative binomial distribution. The negative binomial distribution is a generalization of the Poisson distribution that accounts for overdispersion in the observation. Specifically, for the negative binomial distribution, the mean is
\mu_{t}
and the variance is
\mu_{t}(1+\phi\mu_{t})
, where
\phi
is the overdispersion parameter. To avoid singularities when
\mu_{t}=0
, we followed the approach of
Funk et al. 2018
, rounding variances smaller than 1 up to 1. Thus, the observed daily case counts follow a negative binomial distribution:

Z^{I}_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{t},\mu_{t}(1+\phi\mu_{t})).

(23)

We estimate the spread of COVID-19 in Ireland from February 29th, 2020, to February 28th, 2021, using the daily new infected count data of 365 days. The Irish population in 2020 was estimated to be
N=4,965,439
. The O-SMC
2
uses
N_{\theta}=1000
parameter particles,
N_{x}=500
state particles,
M=5
successive PMMH moves and a window size of
t_{k}=80
days. Table
C.1
gives the description and prior of the inferred parameters used in the model (
3.2.1
). These priors are based on previous studies of COVID-19 dynamics in Ireland
(
Irish Department of Health 2020
;
Cazelles et al. 2021
)
. For the choice of prior distributions for the hyperparameters,
\nu_{\beta}
(which governs the variability in the transmission rate) and
\phi
(the overdispersion parameter), we adopt settings that allow for rapid changes in the transmission rate, ensuring the model can adapt to sudden shifts in disease dynamics, as discussed in
(
Funk et al. 2018
)
. Regarding initial states, we set:
S_{0}=N-E_{0}-A_{0}
,
E_{0}=1
,
A_{0}\sim\mathcal{U}(\{10,\dots,50\})
and
I_{0}=R_{0}=0
. The execution time for the 365 days considered here was less than 5 hours, making it suitable for practical real-time applications.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we explored the application of an online variant of the Sequential Monte Carlo Squared (O-SMC
2
) method for real-time inference in stochastic epidemic models. This approach differs from standard SMC
2
by integrating a mechanism that approximates the posterior distribution using a fixed window of recent observations, thereby reducing computational demands while maintaining parameter estimation accuracy. This adaptation makes O-SMC
2
particularly suitable for dynamic disease modeling, where timely responses are critical. We demonstrated the effectiveness of O-SMC
2
in the stochastic SEIR compartment model, validating its performance on both synthetic simulations and real-world COVID-19 data from Ireland. Our results show that O-SMC
2
, even with a modest window size, effectively tracks epidemic progression and generates robust online estimates of both static and dynamic epidemiological parameters. Moreover, the sequential nature of this method enables the efficient updating of estimates by using results from previous runs as prior information, eliminating the need to rerun the model from scratch as new data batches become available. This feature underscores the practicality of O-SMC
2
for real-time epidemiological surveillance, enabling public health authorities to adapt interventions based on the latest information. However, we also acknowledge certain challenges, particularly in the early stages of disease transmission when available data may be sparse and less informative. This can hinder accurate parameter estimation during the onset of an outbreak. To address these limitations, future work will focus on refining our approach by integrating diverse modeling techniques that can better handle early-stage uncertainties and improve prediction accuracy.

Appendix A
Approximation of the acceptance probability

During the rejuvenation step in the algorithm
2
, when a new parameter
\theta^{*}
is proposed at time
t
, an unbiased estimate of the marginal likelihood is computed by running Algorithm
1
over observations from time 1 to
t
. However, as time progresses, the computational cost of this procedure grows, making it less feasible for long-time series data. This limitation can be alleviated by considering only the most recent observations
(
Vieira 2018
)
.

Let the window size be denoted by
t_{k}>0
. By the law of total probability, the marginal likelihood at time
t
can be factorized as:

\displaystyle p(y_{1:t}|\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{s=1}^{t-t_{k}-1}p(y_{s}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)\right)\times\left(\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}p(y_{s}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=p(y_{1:t-t_{k}-1}|\theta)\times\left(\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}p(y_{s}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)\right).

(24)

For
s\geq t-t_{k}
, if we assume that for a sufficiently large
t_{k}
, the older observations
y_{1:t-t_{k}-1}
have minimal impact on the filtering estimate of the latent state at time
s-1
, i.e.,
p(x_{s-1}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)\approx p(x_{s-1}|y_{t-t_{k}:s-1},\theta)
, then, from the result in
(
Kantas et al. 2015
)
, we have:

\displaystyle p(y_{s}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)

\displaystyle=\int g(y_{s}|x_{s},\theta)f(x_{s}|x_{s-1},\theta)p(x_{s-1}|y_{1:s-1},\theta)\,dx_{s-1:s}

\displaystyle\approx\int g(y_{s}|x_{s},\theta)f(x_{s}|x_{s-1},\theta)p(x_{s-1}|y_{t-t_{k}:s-1},\theta)\,dx_{s-1:s}

\displaystyle=p(y_{s}|y_{t-t_{k}:s-1},\theta).

(25)

This approximation yields to:

\displaystyle p(y_{1:t}|\theta)\approx p(y_{1:t-t_{k}-1}|\theta)\times p(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta),

(26)

where

\displaystyle p(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)=\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}p(y_{s}|y_{t-t_{k}:s-1},\theta)\approx\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}p(y_{s}|y_{1:s-1},\theta).

(27)

Given the current
\theta
-particle, the new parameter
\theta^{*}
at time
t
is drawn from the region where most of the probability mass is located
(
Chopin 2002
)
. Under the assumption that the target parameter vector remains constant over time, and provided that the window size
t_{k}
is large enough to capture the necessary temporal variability in the observations
y_{t-t_{k}:t}
, it is reasonable to assume that the historical likelihoods
p(y_{1:t-t_{k}-1}|\theta)
and
p(y_{1:t-t_{k}-1}|\theta^{*})
are approximately equal. Consequently, their contribution to the likelihood ratio becomes negligible. Under this assumption, the full likelihood ratio simplifies to:

\displaystyle\dfrac{p(y_{1:t}|\theta^{*})}{p(y_{1:t}|\theta)}\approx\dfrac{p(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta^{*})}{p(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)}.

(28)

Thus, using the SMC estimate of the incremental likelihood for a fixed number of particles
N_{x}
(see Equation (
8
)),
we estimate the windowed likelihood
p(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)
as:

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)=\prod_{s=t-t_{k}}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{N_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{x}}w_{s}^{i}(x_{s-1}^{i},x_{s}^{i})\right),

(29)

and the acceptance probability for the PMMH kernel is given by:

\displaystyle\alpha=\min\left\{1,\dfrac{\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta^{*})p(\theta^{*})}{\hat{p}_{N_{x}}(y_{t-t_{k}:t}|\theta)p(\theta)}\times\dfrac{q(\theta|\theta^{*})}{q(\theta^{*}|\theta)}\right\}.

(30)

Appendix D
Application in real-time surveillance

The methodology developed in this paper is applied to a real-time surveillance setting, demonstrating how our model can track evolving dynamics in sequential data. By integrating new observations over time, the model provides insights into the system’s behavior. The focus is on an adaptive framework that updates the posterior distribution, conditioned on incoming data, while utilizing results from previous O-SMC
2
runs as prior information. The results are shown in Figure
D.1
