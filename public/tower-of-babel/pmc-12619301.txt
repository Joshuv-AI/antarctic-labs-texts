Identification of prognostic indicators for early recurrence of gastric cancer following laparoscopic gastrectomy
Authors: Mingze Wu, Ningbo Liu, Shilong Li, Bang Liu, Zexuan Shen, Hengbin Zhao, Guozheng Ding, Weihua Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postoperative recurrence of gastric cancer, especially early recurrence, significantly impacts the prognosis of patients. We aimed to identify factors affecting patient post-recurrence survival and identify patients who would experience early postoperative recurrence, facilitating prompt preoperative intervention.MethodsClinical and pathological data from 295 gastric cancer patients who underwent gastrectomy and D2 lymphadenectomy were retrospectively analyzed. The classification of tumor and nodal status was updated on the basis of the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer’s tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) system for gastric cancer. Survival data were acquired from medical records and follow-up phone calls. Recurrence was diagnosed based on radiological imaging, endoscopic biopsy, and intraoperative findings during reoperation. Categorical variables were analyzed for differences via Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s χ² test. And for survival analysis, we applied Kaplan‒Meier method curves, which were compared with the log-rank test. Further univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were employed to identify variables associated with post-recurrence survival and early recurrence. Time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) analysis was performed to evaluate and compare the predictive performance of these factors.ResultsA total of 295 patients were included in this study, with a mean age of 65.6 years. K‒M survival curves revealed that the overall survival rate (OS) and post-recurrence survival (PRS) rate in the late recurrence group were significantly higher than those in the early recurrence group (P < 0.05). Cox regression analyses identified early recurrence (HR = 2.136, 95%CI: 1.291-3.534) and a postoperative albumin (ALB) level < 35 g/L (HR = 2.107, 95%CI: 1.187-3.741) as independent risk factors and a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25kg/m2 (HR= 0.544, 95%CI: 0.297-0.997) as a protective factor for PRS. Further Cox regression analyses indicated that higher T stage (HR = 6.589, 95%CI: 1.513-28.696), higher N stage (HR = 2.305, 95%CI: 1.185-4.480) and serum carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level > 37U/mL (HR = 1.889, 95%CI: 1.023-3.489) were independent risk factors for early recurrence.ConclusionsPatients with early recurrence had a poorer prognosis than those with late recurrence. Decreased postoperative albumin was the other independent risk factor for PRS, with high BMI being an independent protective factor for PRS. Higher T stage, higher N stage and elevated serum CA19-9 were independent risk factors for early recurrence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03305-w.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Recurrence, Prognostic factors, Tumor makers

Background

Gastric cancer ranks fifth in both the mortality and incidence of cancers worldwide [1]. The incidence of gastric cancer has demonstrated marked regional disparities worldwide, with approximately two-thirds of cases arising in Asia. Currently, surgical resection remains the fundamental strategy for treating advanced gastric cancer. Furthermore, D2 lymphadenectomy has become the standard operative procedure for treating advanced gastric cancer, and research has indicated that D2 lymphadenectomy reduces recurrence rates and improves overall survival for patients with gastric cancer [2]. Nevertheless, even after radical resection, distant metastasis and recurrence continue to occur.

Perioperative treatment has significantly improved the prognosis of gastric cancer patients. However, approaches to neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy vary globally, reflecting the results of clinical trials conducted in different regions [3]. Adjuvant chemotherapy based on oral fluoropyrimidine has received favorable recognition in East Asia. The CLASSIC trial conducted in China, South Korea, and Japan demonstrated that adjuvant treatment with capecitabine plus oxaliplatin (XELOX) significantly improved disease-free survival and was feasible for patients following D2 gastrectomy [4, 5]. The ACTS-GC trial (Adjuvant Chemotherapy Trial of S-1 for Gastric Cancer) demonstrated that the S-1(an oral fluoropyrimidine derivative) significantly improved both disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) in gastric cancer patients [6]. In the United States and Europe, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is the standard approach for locally advanced gastric cancer, primarily on the basis of findings from the Medical Research Council Adjuvant Gastric Infusional Chemotherapy (MAGIC) trial conducted in the UK. The UK-led MAGIC trial demonstrated that patients receiving perioperative epirubicin, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil (ECF) treatment had a significantly improved 5-year OS rate compared with those receiving surgery alone (35% vs. 23%, P = 0.009) [7]. Similarly, the FNCLCC/FFCD trial highlighted the survival benefits associated with perioperative 5-FU and cisplatin treatment [8]. Recently, while European countries have shown enthusiasm for neoadjuvant chemotherapy, some Eastern countries such as South Korea and Japan, have also made significant attempts to adopt this new chemotherapy approach. The RESOLVE trial, a randomized phase III clinical study led by Chinese researchers, aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of adjuvant XELOX or adjuvant SOX against neoadjuvant SOX followed by five cycles of adjuvant SOX and three cycles of S-1 in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer [9]. The study revealed that perioperative SOX was superior to adjuvant XELOX for patients with locally advanced gastric cancer at stage cT4a/NxM0 or cT4a/N + M0, with achieving a higher three-year DFS rate (62.0% vs. 54.8%, P = 0.045). On the basis of these findings, the neoadjuvant SOX regimen is now a Grade I recommendation for resectable gastric cancer in the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) guidelines [10].

Despite advances in diagnosis and treatment, gastric cancer still carries high incidence and mortality, with recurrence after curative treatment being the leading cause of postoperative death. Moreover, recurrence is often associated with poor survival, and patients and their families frequently express concern about survival after recurrence. Most previous studies have focused on prognostic factors for OS and DFS in patients after gastrectomy, with relatively few investigations into risk factors affecting survival after recurrence. Recently, a multicenter Japanese cohort study showed that patients who experienced recurrence shortly after surgery and adjuvant S-1 chemotherapy had poor prognosis [11]. One study reported that recurrence within one year after surgery was a risk factor for post-recurrence survival (PRS) [12]. Approximately 50% of recurrences occur within the first year following surgery as reported in several studies [13–15]. Although the term “early recurrence” is commonly employed in academic and clinical contexts, a strict definition is currently lacking. Xu et al. identified 12 months as the optimal cutoff for defining early recurrence on the basis of differences in post-recurrence survival [16].

Previous studies on the prognosis of patients with postoperative gastric cancer have explored primarily the factors contributing to recurrence, while research focusing specifically on populations with recurrence soon after surgery is limited. Intensive follow-up and aggressive treatment for all gastric cancer patients after surgery are neither practical nor cost-effective [17]. Therefore, it is essential to concentrate on patients who are at risk of early recurrence.

Tumor markers are frequently used for postoperative surveillance of gastrointestinal tumors and are routinely monitored by most institutions [18–21]. Several studies have also demonstrated the predictive value of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) in assessing gastric cancer recurrence [20, 22]. Additionally, inflammation markers derived from routine blood tests, such as the platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) and neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), have been studied for their prognostic significance in varied cancers, including gastric cancer [23–26].

Here, we integrated clinical information, perioperative laboratory results, and pathological data from gastric cancer patients to investigate the factors influencing PRS and identify the risk factors for early recurrence.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of Tianjin Medical University General Hospital (NO. IRB2024-YX-469-01).

Patients

This single-center exploratory study was conducted at the Department of General Surgery, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. Between January 2018 and December 2021, a total of 372 patients diagnosed with gastric adenocarcinoma underwent laparoscopic gastrectomy combined with D2 lymphadenectomy. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) early or advanced gastric adenocarcinoma, (2) no neoadjuvant therapy, (3) D2 lymph node dissection. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) distant metastasis at diagnosis, (2) positive surgical margins, (3) incomplete medical records, and (4) comorbidities of other tumors. Patients with non-R0 resections (n = 12), distant metastasis of gastric cancer (n = 10), incomplete medical records (n = 11, including 5 with missing laboratory data, 2 with missing perioperative treatment information, and 4 with missing medical history details), gastric stump cancer (n = 3), comorbidities of other tumors (n = 6) and those lost to follow-up (n = 35) were excluded from the study. The missing data, resulting from improper data storage, were considered to be Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) and were therefore excluded from the study. The pathological staging of the tumor, including both the T stage and the N stage, was performed according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [27]. Consequently, this study included 295 patients, all of whom were histologically confirmed to have gastric adenocarcinoma (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of this study

Variables selection

Variables such as body mass index (BMI), serum albumin (ALB), and other clinical indicators were selected for their established clinical relevance and prognostic significance in gastric cancer. Previous studies have shown that nutritional status markers like BMI and ALB reflect patients’ immune and metabolic status and are closely associated with gastric cancer outcomes [28–31]. Additionally, tumor markers and inflammatory indicators were chosen due to their routine use in clinical practice and established value in prognosis prediction.

Treatment

All patients underwent curative resection and D2 lymphadenectomy. In total, 192 patients underwent distal gastrectomy, 17 underwent proximal gastrectomy, and 86 underwent total gastrectomy. The main postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy regimens were as follows: SOX, with an oxaliplatin infusion (130 mg/m² over 2 h on days 1) and oral S-1 (1000 mg/m² twice daily for 14 days), repeated every 21 days; XELOX, with an oxaliplatin infusion (130 mg/m² over 2 h on days 1) and oral capecitabine (1000 mg/m² twice daily for 14 days), repeated every 21 days; and FOLFOX4, with an oxaliplatin infusion (85 mg/m² on days 1), leucovorin (200 mg/m² over 2 h), followed by a 5-FU bolus (400 mg/m² on days 1), plus a continuous 22-hour intravenous 5-FU infusion (600 mg/m² on days 1 and 2), repeated every 2 weeks for at least six cycles. A total of 88 patients received the XELOX regimen postoperatively, 35 received the SOX regimen, 19 received the FOLFOX4 regimen, 9 received oral S-1 only, and 144 did not undergo postoperative chemotherapy.

Follow-up

All patients were regularly followed up, and overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death or the follow-up cutoff date of April 3, 2024, whichever occurred first. Post-recurrence survival (PRS) was defined as the time from recurrence to death or the follow-up cutoff date of April 3, 2024, whichever occurred first. Survival data were acquired from patients’ medical records and follow-up phone calls. Recurrence was diagnosed on the basis of radiological imaging, endoscopic biopsy, and intraoperative findings observed during reoperation.

Laboratory tests were conducted every three months for the first 2 years, then every six months from 2 to 5 years, and annually after 5 years. Radiology imaging was performed every 6 months for the first 2 years and then annually thereafter. If possible, endoscopic examinations were conducted annually, depending on the patient’s preference.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were analyzed for differences via Pearson’s χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, via SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) software. Univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to identify variables associated with post-recurrence survival and early recurrence. Significant variables identified through univariate analysis were subsequently included in a multivariate Cox regression analysis to identify independent prognostic factors. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted via R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, 2024) [32, 33]. Survival analysis was performed using the survival package (Therneau, 2024) in R, and Kaplan‒Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were generated via GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 for windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com) [33]. Forest plots were created using the forestploter package (Dayimu, 2024), and data cleaning were carried out via the dplyr package (Wickham et al., 2023) [34, 35]. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to evaluate the predictive performance of key variables for PRS and early recurrence over different time points. These analyses were conducted using the timeROC package in R, with bootstrap resampling (n = 1000) applied to estimate 95% confidence intervals for the area under the curve (AUC). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using an alternative cutoff of recurrence within 18 months after surgery. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed for the 18-month cutoff, and the results were compared to assess the robustness of the findings. All the statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. The predictive capacity of covariates was represented by calculating hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Of the 295 patients included, 69.8% were male and 30.2% were female. In terms of age, 73.2% were older than 60 years and 26.8% were 60 years or younger. A total of 101 patients (34.2%) experienced recurrence, whereas 194 (65.8%) did not. The median follow-up duration for these 295 patients was 41.37 months, with a range spanning from 3 to 75 months. Among the patients who experienced recurrence, the median disease-free survival time was 12.13 months, with a range of 1.57 to 62.20 months. Fifty patients (49.5%) experienced early recurrence within 12 months after surgery, whereas the remaining 51 patients (50.5%) experienced late recurrence after 12 months (Table 1). Furthermore, chemotherapy regimens (XELOX, SOX, FOLFOX4, and S-1) were similarly distributed among the early recurrence, late recurrence, and non-recurrence groups (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Factors associated with post-recurrence survival

Kaplan–Meier curves showed a significant survival difference; patients with early recurrence had poorer outcomes than those with late recurrence (log-rank test, P < 0.001; Fig. 2). Univariable Cox regression analysis of the prognosis of patients with recurrence revealed that early recurrence, postoperative ALB level, adjuvant chemotherapy, BMI, and Ferritin (FER) were significantly associated with PRS (Table 3). Further multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that early recurrence (P = 0.004) and postoperative ALB level < 35 g/L (P = 0.011) were independent risk factors for PRS (Table 3; Fig. 3). Furthermore, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 (P = 0.049) was an independent protective factor for PRS. We further compared the predictive performance of these factors using time-dependent ROC analysis and time-dependent AUC analysis. The AUCs (95% CI) at 12 months were 0.637 (0.533–0.741) for early recurrence, 0.643 (0.536–0.751) for postoperative ALB level < 35 g/L, and 0.618 (0.513–0.722) for BMI < 25 kg/m2. At 24 months, the AUCs were 0.609 (0.492–0.725), 0.655 (0.536–0.773), and 0.602 (0.486–0.717), respectively. At 36 months, the AUCs were 0.523 (0.359–0.687), 0.646 (0.481–0.811), and 0.626 (0.466–0.786), indicating that postoperative ALB level had slightly higher discriminatory ability for PRS during longer-term follow-up (Fig. 4).

Factors associated with early recurrence

Among the 295 patients, 50 (16.9%) experienced recurrence within 12 months after surgery, with a median survival time of 4.73 months. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the clinicopathological data of all postoperative patients, with recurrence within 12 months used as the endpoint. The results indicated that serum CEA, serum CA19-9, preoperative ALB, tumor size, T stage, N stage, differentiation, and lymphovascular invasion were associated with early recurrence (Table 4). These significant variables were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, and the results indicated that serum CA19-9 level > 37 U/mL (P = 0.042), higher T stage (P = 0.012), and higher N stage (P = 0.014) were independent risk factors for early recurrence (Table 4; Fig. 5). Time-dependent ROC analysis and time-dependent AUC analysis that showed at 3 months post-surgery, higher N stage had the highest predictive accuracy for early recurrence with an AUC of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.662–0.842), followed by CA19-9 > 37 U/mL (AUC = 0.739, 95% CI: 0.588–0.890) and higher T stage (AUC = 0.658, 95% CI: 0.631–0.685). The AUCs of these factors remained relatively stable over 6 to 12 months, with CA19-9 showing a slight decline. These results suggest moderate predictive ability of these factors for early recurrence (Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analysis using the 18-month cutoff

A sensitivity analysis using the 18-month cutoff was conducted, and the results were consistent with those obtained at 12 months. Early recurrence (within 18 months), BMI < 25 kg/m², and preoperative ALB level < 35 g/L remained significantly associated with poor PRS (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, serum CA19-9 level > 37 U/mL, higher T stage, and higher N stage continued to be significant independent risk factors for recurrence within 18 months (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, CEA and FER were identified as additional significant risk factors for recurrence within 18 months. These findings reinforce the robustness of the identified prognostic factors.

Discussion

Despite advancements in diagnostic techniques and multimodal treatment approaches, the prognosis for patients with gastric cancer remains challenging, primarily due to local recurrence and distant metastasis after surgery. Post-recurrence survival for patients with gastric cancer has received limited attention, although it is a primary concern for patients’ families. Furthermore, early recurrence following gastric surgery may impact survival, but factors influencing early recurrence remain uncertain. To address this, we investigated factors affecting patients’ PRS and early recurrence.

In alignment with earlier studies, we found that approximately half of the recurrences occurred within the first year after surgery. Our findings demonstrated that early recurrence served as an independent risk factor influencing PRS in patients with gastric cancer. The median PRS among all recurrent gastric cancer patients was 4.7 months, with a significantly poorer median PRS in the early recurrence group than in the late recurrence group. Additionally, decreased postoperative ALB level emerged as the other risk factor impacting PRS. Serum albumin serves as a crucial indicator of nutritional status and is strongly associated with unfavorable prognoses across various diseases. It is also an effective factor for predicting the prognosis of various cancers. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain its anticancer effects, including neutralizing and binding to carcinogens, stabilizing cell growth and DNA replication, and maintaining calcium and sex hormone homeostasis to prevent hormone-induced cancers [36]. Moreover, it plays a significant role in the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapy agents by facilitating their transport through binding [37]. For patients with recurrent gastric cancer, chemotherapy represents the primary treatment modality. We hypothesized that the substantial impact of ALB on the pharmacokinetics of chemotherapy agents may significantly influence PRS in this patient population. Based on this, nutritional intervention and ALB supplementation in patients with low preoperative ALB level may potentially improve their prognosis. Further evaluation is needed to assess the impact of serum ALB level on PRS.

Interestingly, we found that overweight (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) an independent protective factor for PRS. The obesity paradox is used to describe the phenomenon whereby a higher BMI is associated with better survival outcomes in several cancers [38, 39]. The relationship between BMI and long-term survival in patients with gastric cancer remains unclear and controversial. Retrospective studies have shown that overweight is associated with better long-term survival in gastric cancer patients after gastrectomy [40, 41]. Some studies have suggested that high BMI in gastric cancer patients contributes to poor prognosis due to increased complication rates and difficulties in lymph node dissection [42, 43]. Park et al. reported that patients with a low preoperative BMI were more likely to experience severe BMI loss, and both postoperative low BMI and severe BMI loss were associated with poorer prognosis [31]. Our findings demonstrated that a higher preoperative BMI was associated with improved PRS in gastric cancer patients. However, a limitation of this study is the lack of data on postoperative BMI changes and sarcopenia. Future studies should consider perioperative body composition, including BMI changes and sarcopenia, to better understand their impact on recurrence and survival after recurrence.

Lymphatic metastasis and serosal infiltration have long been recognized as major determinants of recurrence in gastric cancer [44, 45]. Radical gastrectomy combined with D2 lymphadenectomy is the standard curative procedure in East Asia, as it effectively reduces regional recurrence and cancer-specific mortality compared to limited lymph node dissection [46, 47]. Our findings emphasize the continued importance of adequate lymph node dissection and vigilant postoperative surveillance in patients with high T and N stage to reduce early recurrence. However, a limitation of our study is that the T stage hazard ratio has a wide confidence interval, suggesting potential instability due to small subgroup sizes. This may affect the precision of our estimates, and further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the findings regarding T stage and its role in early recurrence.

Several studies have shown that neoadjuvant chemotherapy can shrink tumors, increase the rate of R0 resection, eliminate micro-metastases to prevent systemic spread, and prolong survival without increasing mortality in gastric cancer patients. Identifying patients who could benefit from preoperative treatment, those at high risk for recurrence, particularly early recurrence, to receive neoadjuvant chemotherapy could improve their outcomes and reduce costs. Therefore, biomarkers indicating early recurrence are needed to guide neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Our data suggest that elevated serum CA19-9 level are an independent risk factor for early recurrence in patients with gastric cancer. Owing to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness, serum tumor markers play a vital role in the routine monitoring of postoperative patients with gastric cancer. Among these, CEA and CA19-9 are particularly significant, as they are commonly used as markers for postoperative monitoring of gastric cancer in many institutions. Studies have indicated that patients with serum CEA level exceeding 5 ng/ml tend to have larger tumors, more significant serosal infiltration, and a higher frequency of lymphovascular involvement, as well as an increased incidence of lymph node and liver metastases [48]. Furthermore, early stage gastric cancer patients with serum CA19-9 level below 37 U/ml exhibited significantly better survival rates than those with level exceeding 37 U/ml [49]. Kochi et al. reported that the concurrent elevation of serum CEA and CA19-9 level did not act as an independent prognostic marker for gastric cancer, with serum CEA offering more significant prognostic insights [20]. Consistently, a multicenter Japanese cohort study reported that postoperative CEA ≥ 5 ng/mL was an independent risk factor for early recurrence following radical gastrectomy and adjuvant chemotherapy [50]. However, Shibata et al. reported that preoperative abnormality in CA19-9, rather than CEA, was a risk factor for postoperative recurrence in patients with gastric cancer, underscoring the importance of CA19-9 [51]. While numerous studies have investigated the prognostic significance of serum CEA and CA19-9 in gastric cancer, with some debate surrounding their implications, there is a notable lack of research focusing specifically on their role in early recurrence. Toiyama et al. reported that elevated serum levels of CEA and CA19-9 were associated with shorter postoperative disease-free survival (DFS) in patients with gastric cancer [52]. Based on our findings, patients with elevated CA19-9 level should be considered for intraperitoneal hyperthermic perfusion therapy to reduce the risk of recurrence. Additionally, a more intensive surveillance strategy and aggressive adjuvant treatment approach should be implemented for these patients. In the future, more extensive research is needed to evaluate the prognostic value of serum tumor markers in the early recurrence of gastric cancer.

Different recurrence patterns, such as local recurrence, peritoneal metastasis, and distant metastasis, have distinct prognostic implications. Future large-scale studies are needed to investigate the temporal patterns of these recurrence types. In our study, no significant differences in early recurrence rates were observed among the different chemotherapy regimens. The early recurrence rates were lower for S-1 (11.1%) and XELOX (14.7%), followed by FOLFOX4 (15.8%) and SOX (17.1%), while the non-chemotherapy group had the highest rate at 18.6%. Further evaluation is warranted to assess the impact of these regimens on early recurrence.

However, our study has some limitations due to its retrospective nature, single-center design, and relatively small sample size. Additionally, patients who received neoadjuvant therapy were not included in the study, with only five cases available. This may limit the generalizability of our findings, as neoadjuvant therapy has been shown to improve outcomes by reducing tumor size, enhancing R0 resection rates, and potentially lowering recurrence. Furthermore, due to the low proportion of HER2-positive patients and the absence of routine immunotherapy at our center during the study period, the prognostic impact of HER2 status and immunotherapy was not assessed. Future prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts should incorporate these factors to more comprehensively investigate determinants of recurrence.

Conclusion

Patients with early recurrence have a poorer prognosis than those with late recurrence. Decreased postoperative ALB level were identified as an independent risk factor for PRS, while elevated BMI acted as a protective factor for post-recurrence survival. Higher T stage, higher N stage and elevated preoperative serum CA19-9 level were independent risk factors for early recurrence in gastric cancer patients.
