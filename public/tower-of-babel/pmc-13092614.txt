Development and validation of the Clinical Nursing Knowledge Questionnaire-15 (CNKQ-15) for assessing contraceptive knowledge among nurses
Authors: Jose David Urchaga-Litago, Cristian Martín-Vázquez, Laura Rosón-Matilla, María Cristina Martínez-Fernández, Marta Valencia-Prieto, María Cortés-Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDeficiencies in contraceptive knowledge among healthcare professionals persist worldwide, while existing assessment tools lack rigorous psychometric validation and standardized interpretation. Given nurses’ central role in contraceptive counseling, a robust, profession-specific instrument is needed to accurately identify knowledge gaps and guide targeted training to improve clinical practice.ObjectiveTo develop and psychometrically validate the Contraception Nursing Knowledge Questionnaire (CNKQ-15) in order to provide a standardized tool for assessing contraceptive knowledge and identifying training needs among nursing professionals.DesignA methodological study conducted in two phases: qualitative instrument development and cross-sectional psychometric evaluation.Setting(s)A nationwide study conducted in Spain between February and April 2025. Data collection was distributed digitally through the General Council of Nursing and provincial official nursing colleges.ParticipantsA total of 1123 actively practicing nursing professionals (including generalist nurses and specialized nurses).MethodsContent validity was assessed by a multidisciplinary expert panel using Aiken’s V index. Psychometric evaluation included item difficulty and discrimination analysis, known-groups discriminant validity (comparing specialists in Obstetrics and Gynecology with non-specialized nurses), and test-retest reliability evaluated in a subsample of 100 participants over a 14-day interval. Standardized cut-off points were also established.ResultsThe final sample included 1123 nursing professionals, predominantly women (90.5%), with a mean age of 38.3 years (SD = 10.3). Most participants reported no specialization (58.2%), while 23.5% specialized in Obstetrics and Gynecology. The CNKQ-15 showed adequate item difficulty indices (0.30–0.94) and demonstrated strong discriminant validity, effectively distinguishing between professionals specialized in Obstetrics and Gynecology and those without specialization (mean scores 12.7 vs. 9.9; p < 0.001). Test–retest reliability was excellent, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.85 (95% CI [0.77–0.91]). According to the established cut-off points, 48.3% of participants exhibited null or insufficient knowledge of contraception, 30.1% achieved only a minimal level, and fewer than one in five demonstrated good or very good knowledge. This proportion of inadequate knowledge increased to 59.3% among nurses without specialization in Obstetrics and Gynecology.ConclusionsThe CNKQ-15 shows adequate content validity, discriminant validity, and reliability. Its standardization allows the accurate identification of knowledge gaps among nursing professionals, including midwives, and offers a practical tool for evaluating contraceptive training and guiding targeted educational interventions.Social media abstractThe newly validated CNKQ-15 robustly assesses contraceptive knowledge in nurses and midwives, identifying critical gaps. @cris90due

Background

Contraception is defined as the set of methods aimed at preventing or reducing the likelihood of pregnancy (Bansode et al., 2023). Adolescent sexual health is a key component of global health and is directly linked to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, which include reducing maternal mortality, ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive health services, and eradicating gender-based violence and child marriage. These interconnected goals have a direct impact on sexual and reproductive well-being (Cadena et al., 2022).

The choice of contraceptive method is a complex process that requires individualized professional counseling, tailored to the preferences and circumstances of each individual. Dissatisfaction with such counseling may affect adherence to the method, underscoring the importance of adequate training in contraception among nursing professionals (Reyes-Martí et al., 2021). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that in 2023, around 257 million women of reproductive age were unable to meet their contraceptive needs, largely due to gaps in knowledge that could be addressed by healthcare professionals (World Health Organization, 2022). Several reviews have reported that between 40% and 80% of adolescents and young adults show insufficient knowledge in this field (Kirubarajan et al., 2022; Munakampe et al., 2018), highlighting the need for comprehensive training of nursing professionals (Dehlendorf et al., 2014). In this regard, specialization in obstetric-gynecological nursing is particularly relevant, equipping professionals to provide high-quality counseling and follow-up (Ministerio de Sanidad Consumo y Bienestar., 2009)

Numerous studies have long documented deficiencies in the training and knowledge of healthcare providers (Dehlendorf et al., 2010; Landry et al., 2008; Yang et al., 2018). More recent research confirms that these gaps persist in diverse contexts, including Ecuador (Espinoza et al., 2025), Spain (Leon-Larios et al., 2022), Nigeria (Adigwe and Onavbavba, 2025), Ethiopia (Deribew, 2024), the United States (Brown et al., 2025), and Australia (James et al., 2026). These deficits are often rooted in the early stages of academic training, where nursing and medical students demonstrate critical gaps in areas such as the mechanism of action of emergency contraception and medical eligibility criteria (Addo and Tagoe-Darko, 2009; Asut et al., 2018; Azzam et al., 2025; Veloso et al., 2014).

Despite the ubiquity of these deficits, the scientific literature reveals a significant methodological void. Given that nursing professionals are on the frontline of reproductive health, the absence of a standardized and robust tool tailored to their specific scope of practice is a major concern. To date, no validated instrument exists in this field that meets the necessary psychometric standards for professional use. Consequently, the CNKQ-15 was developed to fill this void, providing a psychometrically sound instrument capable of identifying real knowledge gaps and guiding targeted professional training to ensure safe and effective contraceptive counseling.

Study aims

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to develop and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Contraception Nursing Knowledge Questionnaire (CNKQ-15). Specifically, this research sought to: (1) establish evidence of content validity through expert consensus; (2) analyze the instrument's internal structure and item performance using both Classical Test Theory and Item Response Theory; and (3) determine the reliability and temporal stability of the scale. Ultimately, this study aims to provide a validated and standardized tool to accurately assess contraceptive knowledge among nursing professionals, facilitating the identification of clinical training needs.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection procedures

The process of designing and validating the questionnaire was conducted in two phases (Fig. 1). In the first, which was qualitative in nature, an initial version of the instrument was developed. The initial version was developed based on a literature review and expert consultation, followed by a content validity study through expert judgment. In the second phase, a psychometric evaluation of the scale was conducted using a nationally representative sample of nursing professionals. This included test–retest reliability, criterion validity, and norming procedures.Fig. 1Development and validation process of the CNKQ-15.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Study I: questionnaire design and content validity

Literature review and item generation

A systematic literature review was performed to construct the preliminary version of the questionnaire. The databases PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (WOS) were consulted, with the search limited to the period 2014–2024. The following descriptors were used: “contraception,” “nursing,” “knowledge,” “questionnaire,” and “validation studies.” Based on the evidence retrieved, the research team (comprising health professionals, nurses, and nurse-midwives with expertise and training in contraception) developed a 30-item preliminary version with dichotomous response options (True/False). These items covered six thematic categories: oral hormonal methods, non-oral hormonal methods, intrauterine device (IUD), emergency contraception, natural methods, and barrier methods. Expert judgment and content validity

To ensure content adequacy, the expert judgment technique was applied, following established methodological guidelines (Fernández-Gómez et al., 2020; Torres-Malca et al., 2022). Seeking multidisciplinarity, the panel consisted of five professionals with expertise in sexual and reproductive health and with legal competencies in contraceptive counseling in Spain: two gynecologists, two nurse-midwives, and one community health nurse.

In the first round, the panel received the preliminary 30-item questionnaire. Each item was rated for clarity, representativeness, and relevance using a 5-point Likert scale, and Aiken’s V was calculated for each criterion (Aiken, 1985), with correction applied considering the number of judges. Additionally, item essentiality was assessed on a three-point scale: 1 = essential, 2 = useful but not essential, 3 = not necessary. Experts also provided qualitative feedback regarding item wording, as well as recommendations for the inclusion, modification, or elimination of items to optimize content representativeness. Both quantitative and qualitative evidence informed the refinement of the item pool, which served as the basis for the final CNKQ-15 instrument. In the second phase, using a nationally representative sample of nursing professionals, the psychometric properties of the questionnaire were assessed, including test–retest reliability, criterion validity, and norming of the scale. Analytical procedures and detailed results are reported in the results section.

A total of 20 items obtained mean scores above 4 for representativeness and relevance and were therefore preselected for inclusion in the questionnaire. These items achieved Aiken’s V values greater than 0.80, indicating a high level of agreement among judges and, consequently, strong content validity (Table 2). Based on the feedback received, several statements were reformulated, technical concepts were refined, and the structure of the questionnaire was reorganized.

Subsequently, a second version of the questionnaire was sent to the experts. In this round, minor additional suggestions were provided, mainly related to wording clarity and clinical precision (e.g., simplification of sentences and clarification of ambiguous expressions). These refinements were incorporated into the instrument, resulting in the final version of the questionnaire, which was later administered to a representative sample of nursing professionals.

Study 2: analysis with a sample of nursing professionals. discriminant validity, reliability, and standardization

Sample and sampling procedure

The sample size calculation was based on a population of 353.635 registered nursing professionals in Spain (Instituto Nacional de Estadística, 2025), with a 95% confidence level and a maximum margin of error of 3%. An expected proportion of 50% (p = 0.5) was applied to ensure the most conservative sample size estimate, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 1.065 participants.

The inclusion criteria were: a) actively practicing nursing professionals (generalists or nurse-midwives), b) age ≥ 21 years, c) residence in Spain, and d) academic degree obtained from a Spanish university. The exclusion criteria were: a) absence of informed consent and b) failure to meet any of the inclusion criteria.

Following approval from the Ethics Committee of the University and validation of the questionnaire by subject-matter experts, the instrument was distributed in digital format. Dissemination was conducted through the General Council of Nursing, the Official Nursing Colleges of all Spanish provinces, and additionally via digital platforms and social media (Instagram, WhatsApp). Participants were informed about the study objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, and the guarantee of anonymity in completing the questionnaire. Data collection was carried out between February and April 2025.

Discriminant validity, reliability, and standardization

The psychometric robustness of the CNKQ-15 was rigorously evaluated through three interconnected methodological processes:

Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity was ascertained by examining the instrument’s sensitivity in distinguishing between cohorts with theoretically divergent levels of expertise. It was hypothesized that nursing professionals specializing in Obstetrics and Gynecology (midwives) would exhibit significantly higher proficiency levels compared to generalist nurses. This "known-groups" paradigm provides robust evidence of criterion-related validity, attesting to the questionnaire’s capacity to accurately reflect specialized clinical competencies.

Reliability

Reliability was evaluated through the lens of temporal stability. A subsample of 100 participants completed the instrument on two separate occasions, with a 14-day washout period between administrations. This interval was strategically selected to mitigate potential recall bias while ensuring that no confounding formal training in contraception was undertaken by the participants during the interim.

Standardization and cut-off points

To standardize the interpretative framework of the CNKQ-15, cut-off points were established through a dual approach integrating expert consensus and a correction-for-guessing formula. Given the dichotomous (True/False) architecture of the questionnaire, where the probability of success by chance is 0.5, a score of 50% (7.5 points) was formally defined as "null knowledge." Consequently, the threshold for "minimal knowledge" was stringently set at 11 correct responses. This ensures that passing scores represent a level of mastery substantially superior to what could be achieved through random conjecture.

Instruments

Information was collected on several sociodemographic variables, including age, sex, academic level, specialization, year of graduation, and workplace setting.

The level of contraceptive knowledge was assessed using the preliminar version of the CNKQ, consisting of 20 dichotomous items (True/False). Scores were calculated by assigning 1 point for each correct answer, with no penalty for incorrect responses.

In the interpretation of results, it was considered that the probability of guessing an item correctly at random is 0.5; therefore, answering half of the items correctly would correspond to a null level of knowledge. This methodological decision is grounded in the recommendations of American Educational Research Association (AERA), American Educational Research Association (APA) and National Council on Measurement in Education (NCME) (2014), who argue that, in educational or formative assessment contexts, the interpretation of test results should be clear, transparent, and consistent with the test’s objectives.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies, were computed to delineate the sample characteristics, while skewness and kurtosis were scrutinized to assess distributional properties. For the psychometric appraisal of the CNKQ-15 (study 2), item difficulty was quantified via the proportion of correct responses (p-value). To establish discriminant validity, a 'known-groups' validation approach was implemented, comparing mean scores between midwives (specialists) and non-specialized nursing professionals. Underpinned by the robust sample size (N = 1123) and the observed data distribution, an independent samples t-test was utilized to identify statistically significant inter-group disparities. Furthermore, temporal stability was evaluated through test-retest reliability, employing an Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) based on a two-way mixed-effects model. For all primary validation outcomes, the threshold for statistical significance was stringently set at p < 0.001.

Discriminant validity

The sample was divided into two groups according to specialization in Obstetrics and Gynecology. One item was removed because, contrary to expectations, the percentage of correct responses was lower among specialized professionals (46.6%) than among those without specialization (70%). Content analysis indicated that the wording could lead to misinterpretation. Consequently, the decision to remove this item was based both on the response pattern and on the content review carried out by the panel of experts and researchers. After eliminating this item, the finalised questionnaire compromised 15 items. Total scores revealed that the Obstetrics and Gynecology group obtained a significantly higher mean (12.7) than the non-specialized group (9.9), thus confirming the instrument’s discriminant validity (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Reliability

The reliability of the CNKQ-15 was primarily established through temporal stability (ICC = 0.847; 95% CI: 0.77–0.91), which is the preferred metric for knowledge-based tools. Although internal consistency was calculated (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.557; 95% CI: 0.521- 0.593), this was considered a secondary indicator due to the heterogeneous nature of the items (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014; Streiner et al., 2015).

Standardization

In order to establish a consensual and interpretable framework for score interpretation, a criterion-referenced approach was adopted. This process integrated expert judgment regarding the number of essential items and the dichotomous response format of the questionnaire (true/false). Based on expert evaluations of item essentiality (see Table 2), eight items with mean essentiality ratings ≤ 1.2 were identified as core content reflecting basic contraceptive knowledge.

Considering this expert-based evidence and the total number of items, a cutoff point of 11 correct responses was established. A minimum score of 11 was agreed to represent a very basic or minimal level of contraceptive knowledge, whereas scores below this threshold were interpreted as indicative of insufficient or clearly erroneous knowledge. Scores at or above the cutoff were considered to reflect at least a fundamental level of knowledge.

Based on the total number of correct responses, the following ordinal knowledge categories and cut-off points were defined: no knowledge (0–8), insufficient knowledge (9–10), minimal knowledge (11–12), good knowledge (13–14), and very good knowledge (15) (Table 5). Scores in the 0–8 range are compatible with performance expected by chance alone in a true/false format and therefore indicate an absence of demonstrable contraceptive knowledge. In the overall sample, 48.3% of participants demonstrated null or insufficient knowledge, 30.1% achieved only a minimal level of knowledge, 17.9% showed good knowledge, and only 3.7% reached a very good level. Among specialists in Obstetrics and Gynecology (midwifes), only 12.1% exhibited null or insufficient knowledge, while 60.3% demonstrated good or very good knowledge. In contrast, among the remaining nursing staff, 59.3% showed insufficient knowledge of contraception, and only 9.6% achieved good or very good knowledge levels. These differences support the practical interpretability and external validity of the proposed cut-off points, as they distinguish between groups with expected differences in domain-specific training.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Contraception Nursing Knowledge Questionnaire (CNKQ-15) as a valid and reliable instrument for assessing contraceptive knowledge among nursing professionals. The results confirm that the CNKQ-15 meets fundamental psychometric standards, demonstrating content validity based on expert consensus, appropriate item functioning assessed through Classical Test Theory and Item Response Theory, high temporal stability (ICC = 0.847), and standardized cut-off scores that allow for meaningful clinical interpretation. These findings support the use of the CNKQ-15 as a robust tool for evaluating contraceptive knowledge in professional settings. From a clinical perspective, the descriptive results reveal a structural deficit in contraceptive knowledge among nursing staff. Nearly half of the sample (48.3%) demonstrated null or insufficient knowledge, while only 21.6% achieved good or very good levels. Observed in a large and heterogeneous sample of 1123 professionals, these findings cannot be attributed to isolated individual shortcomings but rather indicate a widespread educational gap. In this context, the CNKQ-15 not only quantifies these deficiencies but also provides a valuable framework for identifying training needs and guiding targeted educational interventions to improve the quality of contraceptive counseling in clinical practice.

Methodological rigor and contribution of the CNKQ-15

The comparative review of existing instruments (Table 1) reveals important methodological limitations in most studies assessing contraceptive knowledge among healthcare professionals. Many rely on ad hoc questionnaires without formal quantitative validation, item difficulty or discrimination analyses, and without evidence of temporal stability or discriminant validity (Gothwal et al., 2020; James et al., 2026; Nivedita and Shanthini, 2014; Thapa, 2013). Even recent studies that include expert panels, such as Dur et al. (2025), continue to omit the reporting of essential psyhometric indices. This lack of rigor limits both comparability and clinical utility.

In contrast, the CNKQ-15 was developed through a comprehensive psychometric process, including qualitative and quantitative content validity (Aiken’s V > 0.80), item analysis using Classical Test Theory, discriminant validity between groups with different training levels (p < 0.001), high test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.85), and standardized cut-off scores. Moreover, unlike most previous tools, it was validated in a large, nursing-only sample (n = 1123), strengthening its external validity. Only the study by Jusoh et al. (2025) presents a partially comparable psychometric approach; however, it is based on a much smaller sample (n = 190) and does not assess temporal stability. In addition, this instrument focuses exclusively on long-acting reversible contraceptives and does not examine discriminant validity, reliability, or standardized score interpretation, which limits its clinical and comparative usefulness.

Comparison of contraceptive knowledge levels

Over the past decade, numerous studies have revealed substantial gaps in contraceptive knowledge among healthcare professionals, underscoring the urgent need for targeted training in this area (Dehlendorf et al., 2010, 2014). Alarmingly, these deficits persist across different contexts. For example, Espinoza et al. (2025) reported a 47% error rate among healthcare professionals in Ecuador, while Azzam et al. (2025) found high levels of insufficient knowledge and inappropriate attitudes toward family planning among clinical professionals. Similarly, Usha et al. (2023) observed that a significant proportion of healthcare professionals in India demonstrated inadequate contraceptive knowledge, likely related to deficiencies in specific training. In contrast, Carlón López et al. (2000) found that only 30% of primary care physicians adhered to World Health Organization recommendations for postcoital hormonal contraception.

Consistent with this international evidence, the results of the present study show that 48.3% of participants demonstrated null or insufficient knowledge, while only 21.6% achieved good or very good levels. These findings align with previous reports of high error rates in critical areas. Nivedita and Shanthini (2014) described error rates of up to 75% in the administration window for emergency contraception, and Thapa (2013) identified frequent mistakes in dosing and side effects. Likewise, Espinoza et al. (2025) found substantial deficits in knowledge regarding oral contraceptive initiation, diaphragm use, and post-ligation abstinence.

Comparable patterns have been observed in other professional populations. Gothwal et al. (2020) reported that only 42.3% of nursing staff reached a “good” knowledge level, and James et al. (2026) identified significant gaps in long-acting reversible contraception provision among primary care nurses.

Beyond knowledge deficits, the absence of formal education in contraceptive counseling may also influence clinical practice. Healthcare professionals who acquire these competencies informally during professional practice may adopt approaches that are not patient-centered and, in some cases, coercive. Therefore, training should incorporate the recognition and prevention of contraceptive coercion, as well as promote person-centered counseling that respects individual values and supports autonomous decision-making. Such training should be delivered through structured curricula integrating theoretical instruction with supervised clinical experience (Brown et al., 2025).

Limitations and strengths

The limitations of the study include its cross-sectional nature, which precludes the establishment of causal relationships between knowledge levels and contextual factors. Despite the validation of the questionnaire in a national sample, its development was specifically for the Spanish context, which may limit its direct applicability to other countries without prior cultural adaptation. The employment of a self-administered digital questionnaire may have engendered biases associated with participant self-selection and the precision of responses. Additionally, the questionnaire may limit discrimination at higher knowledge levels.

One of the main strengths of this study is the rigorous design of the CNKQ-15, which integrates a literature review, expert judgment, and comprehensive psychometric testing, thereby ensuring its validity, reliability, and standardization. The use of a large and nationally representative sample of nursing professionals in Spain, including specialists in Obstetrics and Gynecology (midwives), further enhances the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the implementation of a correction for chance in score interpretation ensures methodological robustness by averting the overestimation of knowledge levels.

A limitation of this study is that the CNKQ-15 was developed and validated within the Spanish healthcare context, where contraceptive practices, clinical guidelines, and regulatory frameworks are well defined, and where nurses, particularly those in primary care and in the specialty of Obstetrics and Gynecology (midwives), have professional autonomy to provide contraceptive counseling to the population. As a knowledge-based instrument, its content is inherently context-dependent; therefore, direct application in other countries requires a structured cross-cultural adaptation process rather than simple linguistic translation. Future use of the CNKQ-15 in other settings should follow established guidelines in health research, including forward and back translation, expert panel review in the target country to ensure conceptual and idiomatic equivalence, and a reassessment of content validity to align with local clinical and legal frameworks (Benlidayi and Gupta, 2024; Cruchinho et al., 2024). Subsequent evaluation of item performance and reliability in the new context will be essential to ensure the validity and interpretability of the results.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the CNKQ-15 is a valid, reliable, and standardized instrument for assessing contraceptive knowledge among healthcare professionals, with a specific focus on the nursing workforce. Its development was grounded in a rigorous methodological process that integrated a systematic literature review, expert consensus, and a comprehensive psychometric evaluation. The findings reveal significant structural gaps in contraceptive knowledge among general nursing staff, underscoring the critical need for objective and methodologically sound assessment tools. In this context, the CNKQ-15 provides a practical, evidence-based resource to evaluate training outcomes, identify educational needs, and guide targeted interventions aimed at improving the quality of contraceptive counseling in clinical practice.
