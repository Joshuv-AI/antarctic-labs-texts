Updated safety evaluation of the food enzyme catalase from the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus tubingensis strain AE‐CN
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize L M Solano, Henk Van Loveren, Laurence Vernis, Simone Lunardi, Magdalena Andryszkiewicz, Daniele Cavanna, Ana Criado, Yi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme catalase (hydrogen‐peroxide:hydrogen‐peroxide oxidoreductase; EC 1.11.1.6) is produced with the non‐genetically modified Aspergillus tubingensis strain AE‐CN by Amano Enzyme Inc. The food enzyme is intended to be used in seven food manufacturing processes. In a previous evaluation, the Panel could not conclude on the safety of this food enzyme due to uncertainties about the absence of viable cells of the production organism in the food enzyme and to the clastogenic effects observed in the in vitro genotoxicity studies, which could not be dismissed due to limitations in the in vivo studies. In this assessment, the Panel evaluated new data provided by the applicant and completed the safety evaluation of this food enzyme. The new data demonstrated the absence of viable cells of the production organism in the food enzyme and showed no concerns on genotoxicity. Based on the new data provided and the previous evaluation, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.Keywords: Aspergillus tubingensis, catalase, EC 1.11.1.6, EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00449, Food enzyme, hydrogen‐peroxide:hydrogen‐peroxide oxidoreductase

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or micro‐organisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using micro‐organisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that:

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;

there is a reasonable technological need;

there is a reasonable technological need;

its use does not mislead the consumer.

its use does not mislead the consumer.

All food enzymes currently on the European Union market and intended to remain on that market, as well as all new food enzymes, shall be subjected to a safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and approval via an EU Community list.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes1.

On 31 March 2025, a new application was submitted by “Amano Enzyme Inc.” seeking authorisation for the food enzyme Catalase from non‐genetically modified Aspergillus tubingensis (strain AE‐CN).

This application is a follow‐up of an inconclusive opinion adopted by EFSA on 25 October 2023 (EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00449). The new application contains additional studies to address the data gaps identified in the published opinion.

In accordance with the requirements of Article 12(1) of Regulation (EC) No 234/2011 implementing Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008, the Commission has verified that the application falls within the scope of the food enzyme Regulation and contains all the elements required under Chapter II of that Regulation.

Terms of Reference

The Commission requests the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) to verify the requirements of Article 32b of Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, whether the suitability of the new data is in line with the requirements of Article 12(2) of Regulation (EU) No 234/2011. Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

The applicant submitted previously an application for catalase from the non‐genetically A. tubingensis strain AE‐CN, for which EFSA could not conclude on the safety, due to uncertainties (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

As a follow‐up, the applicant submitted new data. The present opinion assesses the new studies provided and updates the safety evaluation of the food enzyme. Whenever previous evaluation remains unchanged, reference to the previous EFSA opinion is made.

ASSESSMENT

Systematic nameHydrogen‐peroxide:hydrogen‐peroxide oxidoreductaseSynonymsCatalase‐peroxidaseIUBMB NoEC 1.11.1.6CAS No9001‐05‐2EINECS No232‐577‐1

Catalases catalyse the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide, converting it to water and oxygen. The food enzyme is intended to be used in seven food manufacturing processes as defined in the EFSA guidance (EFA CEP Panel, 2023b): (1) processing of dairy products for the production of cheese; (2) processing of egg and egg products; processing of meat and fish products for the production of (3) modified meat and fish products (fish roe only) and (4) protein hydrolysates from meat and fish (gelatine only); processing of grains for the production of (5) baked products and (6) cereal‐based products other than baked; (7) processing of fruit and vegetables for the production of fruit and vegetable products other than juices.

In a previous application (EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00449), the absence of viable cells of the production strain in the food enzyme was not demonstrated due to limitations in the study provided. Furthermore, in vitro genotoxicity studies indicated the possible presence of a clastogenic agent in the food enzyme which could not be dismissed due to limitations in the in vivo studies. Consequently, the Panel could not conclude on the safety of the food enzyme (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

As a follow‐up, the applicant provided new studies to test the possible presence of viable cells of the production strain in the food enzyme and new genotoxicity studies. New analyses were also conducted to characterise the purity of the food enzyme batch used for toxicological studies. Furthermore, the applicant updated the intended uses of the food enzyme. The present opinion assessed these new data provided in the application EFSA‐Q‐2025‐00275 and reaches a conclusion on the safety of the food enzyme.

Purity

For batches 1–3 (Table 1), see previous evaluation (EFSA‐Q‐2015‐00449).

The lead content in batch 4 (Table 1) was below 5 mg/kg 6 , 7 which complies with the specification for lead as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006).

The food enzyme preparation complies with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). 8 No antimicrobial activity was detected in any of the tested batches. 9

Strains of the Aspergillus species, in common with most filamentous fungi, have the capacity to produce a range of secondary metabolites (Frisvad et al., 2018). The presence of aflatoxins (B1, B2, G1, G2), fumonisins (B1, B2), deoxynivalenol, ochratoxin A, sterigmatocystin, T2‐toxin and HT2‐toxin, and zearalenone was examined in the batch used for toxicological testing and all were below the limit of detection (LoD) of the applied methods. 10 , 11 Adverse effects caused by the possible presence of other secondary metabolites are addressed by the toxicological examination of the food enzyme.

The Panel considered that the information provided on the purity of the food enzyme was sufficient.

Viable cells of the production strain

Previous data on the absence of viable cells of the production strain in the food enzyme showed the presence of some colonies that were not discriminated from the production strain. Furthermore, no adequate positive control was included in the analysis. Based on this, the Panel concluded that the absence of viable cells from the production strain was not demonstrated (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

The applicant has now provided new studies to demonstrate the absence of viable cells of the production strain in the food enzyme in three independent batches analysed in triplicate. ■■■■■ No colonies were detected. A positive control was included. 12

Toxicological data

The EFSA CEP Panel (2023a) evaluated in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity studies submitted for the safety evaluation of the food enzyme catalase from the non‐genetically modified A. tubingensis strain AE‐CN.

The food enzyme did not induce gene mutations in bacteria in the presence or absence of metabolic activation (S9‐mix). However, in two in vitro studies, an increased frequency of structural chromosomal aberrations and of bi‐nucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) were observed in the presence of S9‐mix. Follow‐up in vivo genotoxicity studies were inconclusive, because the exposure of target organs was not demonstrated and the highest dose administered was not a maximum tolerated dose (MTD) or achievable dose.

Based on this, the Panel concluded that the possible presence of a clastogenic agent in the food enzyme could not be excluded (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

In 2025, the applicant provided two new in vitro genotoxicity tests, including a bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) and an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test.

The batch used in these studies (batch 4, Table 1) has lower enzyme activity/TOS ratio than the batches intended for commercialisation reported in the original opinion (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a) and thus is considered suitable as a test item.

Genotoxicity

The present assessment supersedes Section 3.4.1 of the previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

Bacterial reverse mutation test

A bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 471 (OECD, 2020) and following Good Laboratory Practice (GLP). 13 Four strains of Salmonella Typhimurium (TA98, TA100, TA1535 and TA1537) and Escherichia coli WP2uvrA(pKM101) were used with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix), applying the pre‐incubation method. A range‐finding and two main experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Based on the results from the range‐finding test, the main experiments were carried out using five concentrations of the food enzyme of 313, 625, 1250, 2500 and 5000 μg TOS/plate.

No cytotoxicity was observed at any concentration of the test substance. Upon treatment with the food enzyme, there was no biologically relevant increase in the number of revertant colonies above the control values, in any strain tested, with or without S9‐mix.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme catalase did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2016) and following GLP. 14 A range‐finding test and two separate experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of a Chinese hamster lung (CHL/IU) fibroblast cell line. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

Based on the results from the range‐finding test, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of MNBN at concentrations of 750, 1500 and 3000 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (6‐h exposure and 24‐h recovery period) with S9‐mix, or at concentrations of 500, 1000 and 2000 μg TOS/mL without S9‐mix.

In the second experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for MNBN at concentrations of 650, 1300 and 2600 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (24‐h exposure without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

In the short‐term treatment, cytotoxicity of 67% (based on a cytokinesis‐block proliferation index (CBPI) was observed at 3000 μg TOS/mL with S9‐mix and of 16% at 2000 μg TOS/mL), without S9‐mix.

In the long‐term treatment, cytotoxicity of 33% (based on CBPI) was observed at concentration of 2600 μg TOS/mL.

The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at any concentration tested.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme catalase did not induce an increase in the frequency of MNBN under the test conditions applied in this study.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rodents

The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 323 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested. See previous evaluation (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

The Panel noted a substantial decrease in the use levels for five food manufacturing processes, when compared to the previously recommended levels. The applicant ascribes these changes to the availability of actual use levels from food manufacturers. 18

In the production of cheese, fish roes, gelatine and fruit peel fibres, following a treatment with hydrogen peroxide, the catalase is added to the raw materials (milk, 19 fish roes, 20 side streams from meat and fish 21 and fruit peel slurry 22 respectively) to remove residual amounts of hydrogen peroxide.

In the processing of eggs and egg products, 23 and in the production of baked products, 24 cereal‐based products other than baked 25 and fruit and vegetable products other than juices (e.g. concentrates), 26 the raw materials are treated with glucose oxidase and the food enzyme is added to remove the residual hydrogen peroxide. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final foods.

The food enzyme has a temperature optimum between 40°C and 60°C (pH 7.0) and a pH optimum between pH 6.0 and 8.0 (30°C). After pre‐incubation of the food enzyme for 5 min at different temperatures (pH 7.0), no residual activity was detected above 80°C (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a). Based on these data and the downstream processing within the respective food manufacturing processes, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme is inactivated in most of the food manufacturing processes listed in Table 2. However, it may remain in its active form in baked products, fish roes and cheeses, depending on the heat treatment conditions.

Dietary exposure estimation

Chronic exposure to the food enzyme–TOS was calculated using the FEIM webtool 27 by combining the maximum recommended use level with individual consumption data (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). The estimation involved selection of relevant food categories and application of technical conversion factors (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023b). The applicant provided a list of the FoodEx categories that should be considered for the exposure estimation to the production of fish roe and production of gelatine, together with their respective technical conversion factors. 28 This list has been revised by the Panel and the final outcome that was used for the exposure calculation is presented in Appendix C.

Table 3 provides an overview of the derived exposure estimates across all surveys. Detailed mean and 95th percentile exposure to the food enzyme–TOS per age class, country and survey, as well as contribution from each FoodEx category to the total dietary exposure are reported in Appendix A – Tables 1 and 2. For the present assessment, food consumption data were available from 51 dietary surveys (covering infants, toddlers, children, adolescents, adults and the elderly), carried out in 27 European countries (Appendix B). The highest dietary exposure was estimated to be 0.030 mg TOS/kg bw per day in toddlers at the 95th percentile.

The conservative approach applied to estimate the dietary exposure to the food enzyme–TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

Margin of exposure

In the previous evaluation, a margin of exposure was not calculated because of the uncertainty over genotoxicity (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a).

In the current evaluation, a comparison of the NOAEL (323 mg TOS/kg bw per day) identified from the 90‐day rat study (EFSA CEP Panel, 2023a) with the derived exposure estimates of 0.001–0.018 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.002 to 0.030 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 10,767.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the new data on absence of viable cells, the new genotoxicity studies and the evaluation of the data previously submitted, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme catalase produced with the non‐genetically modified A. tubingensis strain AE‐CN does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.
