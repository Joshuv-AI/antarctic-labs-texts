Agentic Workflows for Economic Research: Design, Implementation, and Evaluation
Herbert Dawid 1 , Philipp Harting 2 , Hankui Wang 2,* , Zhongli Wang 1,3 , Jiachen Yi 4 1 Faculty of Business Administration and Economics, Universität Bielefeld, Germany 2 Université Côte d’Azur, CNRS, GREDEG, France 3 Department of Economics & Finance, Università Cattolica del Sacro Cuore, Italy 4 School of Economics, University of Bristol, United Kingdom

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) can now assist across the economic research lifecycle, yet general-purpose LLMs and agent systems give no assurance that their outputs meet the discipline’s bar. We present an agentic-workflow methodology that carries a research question through the full lifecycle, from ideation and literature review through modeling, data, implementation, estimation, and reporting, across seven specialized teams under a cross-team orchestrator, with human-in-the-loop review at each stage. The methodology incorporates two commitments. The first, trustworthy execution , is a shared infrastructure that verifies outputs, records the provenance of every step, bounds each agent’s authority, addresses agent-specific threats, and serves every LLM locally on open weights. The second, reasoning as an economist , makes disciplinary checks explicit in the workflow: agents state identification strategies for causal claims, check structural models against stylized facts by simulation, and search for counterexamples to theoretical claims. Together they produce a first research prototype in hours, with every step’s provenance and verification verdict recorded. A two-tier framework evaluates the workflows across fourteen dimensions, combining execution-log metrics with a dual-judge LLM-as-reviewer ensemble across all seven teams.

Introduction

As computational capabilities expand and Large Language Models (LLMs) become more powerful, economists can use LLM agents in
agentic workflows
, AI-assisted sequences of research steps with varying degrees of autonomy, to carry out parts of their research. Every stage of economic research, from ideation and literature review to data processing and empirical validation, involves time-consuming routine work, and researchers must propose novel questions while keeping up with a growing literature and increasingly complex datasets. Agentic workflows respond to these demands by automating routine tasks, integrating information from multiple online sources, and supporting complex analyses. The potential benefits for economists include proposing and refining research questions grounded in the existing literature, conducting literature reviews at a scale that would be infeasible through manual methods alone, and spending less time on data preprocessing. Furthermore, by documenting research steps in executable formats, these workflows enhance transparency and reproducibility.

A growing ecosystem of commercial and open-source AI research tools now exists, but few of these tools are built around the methodological needs of economics. General-purpose research agents generate hypotheses and run experiments end to end
(
Yamada et al.,, 2025
;
Agarwal et al., 2025a,
;
Swanson et al.,, 2025
)
. Within economics, LLM use has moved from a catalog of assistant-style use cases across the research lifecycle, covering ideation, background research, coding, data analysis, and writing
(
Korinek,, 2023
)
, to guidance for economists building agents that plan, call tools, and carry out multi-step research tasks
(
Korinek,, 2025
)
. The open question is no longer whether LLMs can help with individual research tasks but whether their output can be trusted, both within a single stage and when stages are chained into a pipeline. Within a stage, an LLM can produce an answer that reads plausibly but is wrong; across stages, an error in one becomes an input to the next, so that a fabricated citation can become a modeling assumption and a misread data series can become an estimate. A workflow therefore needs checks at every step and every hand-off, a record of which choices the agent made and which the economist made, and outputs held to the discipline’s standards.

Economic research moves through a sequence of stages, from formulating a question and reviewing the literature to specifying a model, assembling data, implementing the model, estimating, and reporting, and each stage carries its own methodological standards, such as causal identification and the calibration of models against data. The general-purpose research agents above are organized around hypothesis search and experiment execution, not around these stages. The first version of this paper introduced agentic workflows to economic research and illustrated them with worked examples for ideation, literature review, modeling, and data
(
Dawid et al.,, 2025
)
. We have since built the
Agentic Econ Lab
(AEL), an open-source implementation of such a workflow
1

.
that can run any single stage on its own or all of them in sequence, integrates the practice of human economists at each one, and is served on open-weight models. AEL covers seven applications, one for each stage: (i) proposing and refining research questions; (ii) reviewing and synthesizing the literature; (iii) specifying and calibrating economic models; (iv) collecting and preparing data; (v) translating models into verifiable code; (vi) estimating and validating empirical relationships; and (vii) interpreting and reporting results.

The contribution we claim for this framework is workflow-structural: it lies in how the workflow that produces research outputs is organized, checked, and recorded. Domain experts remain responsible for assessing the substantive validity of the models and interpretations it produces. Beyond the seven applications listed above, the paper makes four contributions to that structure. (viii) The architecture organizes the teams around the methodological dependencies of economic research, with a cross-team orchestrator, a bounded Model–Data feasibility cycle, and economist review in every stage. Unmet data requirements return to the modeler as revision requests or are recorded as limitations. (ix) The trustworthy-execution layer addresses reliability through output verification, accountability through provenance records and bounds on authority and budgets, safety through signed artifacts and sandboxed code execution, and privacy through local serving of every LLM on open weights. (x) Reasoning as an economist is built into the workflow through explicit steps: stating identification strategies for causal claims, checking structural models against stylized facts by simulation, reasoning within the theoretical frame the economist adopts, and searching for counterexamples with an adversarial Falsifier paired with the Theorist agent. (xi) A two-tier, fourteen-dimension evaluation framework adds Decision Quality, Economic Rigor, Simulation Fidelity, and Causal Validity to standard AI evaluation criteria. Six content dimensions are scored by two open-weight judges from model families different from the model that produced the outputs.

The framework serves two uses. The first is rapid research prototyping. Here a research prototype comprises a model specification, a dataset assembled for the research question, and a reduced-form estimate or a recorded explanation of why the proposed specification could not be estimated. The model carries a calibration verdict against cited targets, or a recorded reason why it could not be calibrated, and verification results and unresolved limitations accompany these outputs. The chained runs reported here completed in hours. The second use is onboarding: a researcher entering an unfamiliar subfield of economics can examine these outputs, their cited sources, and their verification records to study the subfield’s methodological standards. Onboarding is the use we designed the framework for: a first prototype in an unfamiliar field can support research across the subfields of economics, between economics and the other social sciences, and in interdisciplinary work more broadly, and the workflow records the provenance, cited empirical targets, and calibration verdicts that others need before trusting such work.

2
Literature Review

An agentic workflow for economic research is useful only if economists can trust what it produces. AI assistance can help on some tasks and hurt on others within a knowledge workflow. Access to an LLM raised the speed and quality of professional writing
(
Noy and Zhang,, 2023
)
. On 18 consulting tasks within GPT-4’s capability frontier, consultants given access to it completed 12.2% more tasks and completed them 25.1% more quickly on average, with higher-quality solutions, than consultants without AI. On a complex managerial task selected to lie outside that frontier, however, consultants with GPT-4 access were 19% less likely to produce a correct solution
(
Dell’Acqua et al.,, 2026
)
. AI can add more value when it executes contiguous chains of tasks than when it executes isolated ones
(
Demirer et al.,, 2026
)
, and which tasks to delegate and which to reserve for human judgment can be set by a sufficient-statistic rule
(
Agarwal et al., 2025b,
)
. Applied to research, these results favor a workflow that chains the stages of a project and places human judgment at the points of delegation.

This section reads the literature for the conditions under which that trust is warranted. We first set out the four requirements that any research agent must meet, then follow the research lifecycle stage by stage, from ideation to reporting, turn to how such systems are evaluated, and close with the gaps the rest of the paper addresses.

2.1
Reliability, Accountability, Safety, and Privacy

An agent that handles confidential data, makes analytical decisions, and shapes a scholarly output has to meet four requirements before it is deployed: its outputs must be reliable, its use must be accountable, its actions must be safe, and the data it touches must stay private.

Reliability comes from the structure around the model more than from the model itself: hallucination falls when generation is grounded in observable outcomes and subjected to verification and external feedback
(
Durante et al.,, 2024
;
Schneider,, 2025
)
. The components of such structure are well studied. Eliciting intermediate reasoning steps improves multi-step performance
(
Wei et al.,, 2022
)
; interleaving reasoning with actions lets a model consult its environment while keeping a reasoning trace
(
Yao et al.,, 2023
)
; tool use ties outputs to external evidence and computation, through a browser
(
Nakano et al.,, 2021
)
or a modular set of tools
(
Karpas et al.,, 2022
)
; and multi-agent debate subjects claims to adversarial scrutiny before they are accepted
(
Du et al.,, 2024
)
. Surveys of agentic LLMs identify decomposition, role specialization, tool use, and critique as the means by which a task is broken into checkable steps
(
Plaat et al.,, 2025
;
Wang et al., 2024a,
;
Guo et al.,, 2024
;
Xi et al.,, 2025
)
. General frameworks implement iterative refinement, self-correction, and code-grounded verification as workflow components
(
Wu et al.,, 2024
;
Tang et al.,, 2025
;
Wang et al., 2024b,
)
, and domain deployments such as agentic auditing follow the same design
(
Schreyer et al.,, 2024
)
. A research workflow built from these components makes each step checkable.

Accountability matters because socio-economic settings magnify the consequences of bias: biased systems lose user trust and with it the benefit of the technology
(
Ferrara,, 2024
)
, and generative AI may widen socioeconomic inequality and change how policy is made
(
Capraro et al.,, 2024
)
. Such biases are documented in LLMs themselves: their outputs carry systematic decision and gender biases
(
Corazzini et al.,, 2025
)
, political bias and value misalignment appear in generative systems
(
Motoki et al.,, 2025
)
, and LLM assistance can enable bidder-side manipulation in fair-division settings
(
Verma and Unnikrishnan,, 2025
)
. When an output passes through a pipeline of interconnected services, an “AI supply chain”, responsibility for it becomes diffuse
(
Hopkins et al.,, 2025
)
, and effective use may require contracting and governance structures that reconcile productivity gains with control
(
Bastani and Cachon,, 2025
)
. A workflow that delegates analytical choices to an agent must therefore record where each choice was made and by whom. The instruments for such a record exist: production agent platforms expose traces, token usage, tool invocations, and memory operations as inspectable records
(
Amazon Web Services,, 2026
;
NVIDIA,, 2026
)
, and system-level observability links an agent’s semantic trace to its process, filesystem, network, and runtime behavior
(
Zheng et al.,, 2025
)
. For research, this telemetry belongs to the methodological record: it shows how evidence was retrieved, which code ran, and where a human intervened.

Safety evaluation has moved from static prompts to agents acting on real tools: on realistic multi-turn tasks with browsers, code execution, shells, messaging, and filesystems, frontier systems still show substantial safety failures
(
Vijayvargiya et al.,, 2026
)
. Evaluation must therefore cover the full action loop of memory, tool invocation, and collaboration, and its failure modes of prompt injection, action hallucination, and runaway execution
(
Arunkumar V et al.,, 2026
)
. Standards bodies have begun to codify these threats. The Top 10 for Agentic Applications of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project lists ten threat categories specific to agents, among them goal hijacking, tool misuse, memory poisoning, and cascading failures
(
OWASP GenAI Security Project,, 2025
)
, and the US National Institute of Standards and Technology is developing standards for agent identity, authorization, and inter-agent communication
(
National Institute of Standards and Technology,, 2026
)
. The risk compounds for workflows that accumulate retrieved material across sessions: one poisoned document in a retrieval database can spread through an ecosystem connected through retrieval-augmented generation and extract data from it
(
Cohen et al.,, 2024
)
. The infrastructure through which agents reach tools and one another has developed alongside these evaluations. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) specifies how an LLM application connects to tools and data sources and is supported by the OpenAI Agents SDK
(
OpenAI,, 2026
)
and the Microsoft Agent Framework
(
Microsoft,, 2026
)
. The Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol specifies communication between agents across process boundaries
(
A2A Project,, 2025
)
. Each such channel, to a tool, a memory store, or another agent, is a route by which the threats above reach a workflow.

Privacy is the requirement that most directly shapes deployment, and three layers of protection are available. The first is contractual: providers publish data-use and retention policies
(
OpenAI,, 2025
;
Anthropic,, 2025
)
within broader frameworks for accountability and ethical oversight
(
Floridi and Cowls,, 2019
;
Leslie et al.,, 2021
;
Jobin et al.,, 2019
)
, yet under any such policy, prompts and outputs sent to a hosted model are processed, and may be retained, on the provider’s infrastructure on terms the provider sets. The second is API isolation, which separates inference from training; hosted agents nonetheless remain exposed to prompt injection
(
Kumar et al.,, 2024
)
, which the agent-level evaluations above observe in practice
(
Vijayvargiya et al.,, 2026
;
Zheng et al.,, 2025
)
. The third is local deployment: on-device computation, as studied in edge intelligence and federated learning, keeps sensitive data from leaving the local environment
(
Bonawitz et al.,, 2022
;
Wang et al.,, 2025
)
. A workflow that serves its models locally keeps both the data and the model under the researcher’s control.

2.2
Across the Research Lifecycle

Ideation.

Literatures are fragmented, time is scarce, and synthesis across fields is costly. LLMs can generate, refine, and screen candidate questions, and tools exist for parts of the stage: instruments for survey design and content analysis
(
Bail,, 2024
)
, JEL-code classification
(
Heikkilä,, 2025
)
, and a human-in-the-loop system that carries an economic intuition to literature-grounded hypotheses and executable computational experiments
(
Chen et al.,, 2026
)
. For creative work the evidence cuts both ways: model-generated ideas raise the quality of individual output, most for the least creative writers, but reduce the diversity of what a group produces
(
Doshi and Hauser,, 2024
)
. For research, a field converging on model-suggested questions would lose variety, so ideation inside a workflow needs a human editor.

Literature review.

A review is only as good as its retrieval and its citations. Without retrieval, GPT-4o hallucinated 78–90% of its citations in a large literature-synthesis benchmark; a retrieval-augmented model over 45 million open-access papers reached citation accuracy on par with human experts, and experts preferred its answers to expert-written ones in half or more of comparisons
(
Asai et al.,, 2026
)
. A literature agent optimized for factuality matched or exceeded subject-matter experts on retrieval, summarization, and contradiction-detection tasks
(
Skarlinski et al.,, 2024
)
, and multi-perspective question asking produces grounded, outline-first survey articles
(
Shao et al.,, 2024
)
. Two consequences follow: every claim in an automated review should be tied to a retrieved source, and the review can be no more complete than the corpus it retrieves from.

Modeling and simulation.

Human-subject experiments are costly and their samples narrow. LLMs reproduce economic behavior across risk, time, and social preferences
(
Chen et al.,, 2023
)
, mimic strategic reasoning in games
(
Akata et al.,, 2025
;
Brookins and DeBacker,, 2024
)
, replicate established behavioral results at low cost
(
Horton et al.,, 2023
)
, and have been used to develop theory
(
Tranchero et al.,, 2024
;
Zheng et al.,, 2024
)
and to synthesize policy evaluations
(
Asatryan et al.,, 2025
)
. Simulation is moving from prompted mimicry to theory-grounded agents: competitive multi-agent environments let market dynamics among LLM agents be studied
(
Zhao et al.,, 2024
)
; “general social agents” built from social-science theory, seed-game data, and pretrained knowledge forecast human play in new settings better than behavioral and game-theoretic baselines
(
Manning and Horton,, 2025
)
; and “virtual agent economies” turn agent interactions into measurable counterfactual outcomes
(
Tomasev et al.,, 2025
)
. Related pipelines that combine structured data, news retrieval, and LLM reasoning forecast international events
(
Ye et al.,, 2024
)
. An LLM can propose and populate a model, but its parameters and its fit to data must be established by the methods an economist would use without it.

Data.

LLMs reduce the cost of turning unstructured material into measures that can enter an econometric model. From text they have produced indices of corporate sustainability commitments
(
Bauer et al.,, 2024
)
, economic policy uncertainty narratives
(
Hartley,, 2025
)
, inflation sentiment that marginally improves a Federal Reserve nowcast
(
Allard et al.,, 2024
)
, central-bank mandates and stances
(
Bertsch et al.,, 2025
)
, efficiency–equity framing in economics research
(
Galiani et al.,, 2026
)
, the content of categorical labels
(
Chen et al.,, 2024
)
, AI-related products in trade data
(
Waugh,, 2026
)
, and structured records from 3.3 million Chinese policy documents
(
Fang et al.,, 2025
)
. Survey-style responses generated by LLMs can probe preference heterogeneity, although willingness-to-pay estimates are often inaccurate unless the model is fine-tuned on prior human data
(
Brand et al.,, 2023
)
. Agents given a fixed research instrument, explicit evidence policies, and quality tracking construct datasets from public primary sources
(
Afonso et al.,, 2026
)
. Other modalities are measured too, each with its own pipeline: night-time satellite imagery for growth
(
Henderson et al.,, 2012
)
and poverty
(
Jean et al.,, 2016
)
, the Federal Reserve chair’s voice
(
Gorodnichenko et al.,, 2023
)
and facial expressions
(
Curti and Kazinnik,, 2023
)
at press conferences, image-based investor sentiment
(
Gu et al.,, 2025
)
, and multimodal inputs to investment research
(
Li et al.,, 2023
)
. Each constructed measure is only as credible as its validation against the source material, so a workflow must record how every variable it constructs was produced.

Coding.

Specialized econometric languages and hard-to-reproduce code make implementation costly. Programmers with GitHub Copilot completed a task 56% faster than a control group
(
Peng et al.,, 2023
)
, and a field experiment at a large technology firm found gains of similar size, concentrated among less experienced programmers
(
Gambacorta et al.,, 2024
)
; a dataset of agent-authored pull requests lets adoption and review of coding agents be studied in real repositories
(
Li et al.,, 2026
)
. Agents carry specific tasks to completion: database interaction
(
Xue et al.,, 2024
)
and quantitative factor discovery through hypothesis generation, code, backtesting, and feedback
(
Li et al.,, 2025
)
. A second pattern treats expert-built software as a tool to be called rather than code to be written: an agent given eighteen expert-designed chemistry tools plans and executes multi-step syntheses without writing chemistry code
(
Bran et al.,, 2024
)
, and a guide exists for economists building tool-using agents
(
Korinek,, 2025
)
. An agent that calls an established economic toolbox runs code that economists have already tested and used; code that an agent writes anew has to be verified before its results are relied on.

Estimation.

LLM outputs used as data can contaminate inference: training-data leakage threatens identification, and an econometric framework incorporates LLM components into estimation while accounting for the leakage
(
Ludwig et al.,, 2026
)
. LLMs can recover a decision maker’s preferences from choice data
(
Kim et al.,, 2024
)
and have been benchmarked against utility theory
(
Ross et al.,, 2024
)
. An econometrics agent that plans an analysis, writes and executes code, and reflects on errors outperforms general-purpose LLMs on expert-level econometric tasks
(
Chen et al.,, 2025
)
. The estimator itself remains the economist’s: the agent’s role is to run established estimators and report their diagnostics.

Reporting.

LLMs draft policy advice
(
Biancotti and Camassa,, 2023
)
, generate finance studies that reviewers rate as acceptable
(
Dowling and Lucey,, 2023
)
, and assist economic research from idea to write-up
(
Leigh,, 2024
)
. Agentic systems have produced whole manuscripts: one passed peer review at a workshop of the International Conference on Learning Representations
(
Yamada et al.,, 2025
)
, and another spans literature review, experimentation, and report drafting
(
Schmidgall et al.,, 2025
)
. When such manuscripts were examined by human reviewers, however, they contained hallucinated results and reproducibility failures, and most of those an automated reviewer accepted contained fabricated or unsupported claims
(
Gaddipati et al.,, 2026
)
. A report produced by a workflow should therefore trace every number to a computed result.

2.3
Evaluation of LLMs and Research Workflows

A trusted workflow needs an evaluation as structured as the workflow itself. LLM performance is multidimensional (capability, robustness, bias and safety, efficiency) and sensitive to task and measurement choices. Surveys organize evaluation around what, where, and how to evaluate and identify leakage and missing coverage as recurring pitfalls
(
Chang et al.,, 2024
)
, and a holistic evaluation framework reports trade-offs beyond accuracy, scenario by metric, which argues for multi-metric reporting over a headline score
(
Liang et al.,, 2022
)
.

General capability is measured on standardized suites such as MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding)
(
Hendrycks et al.,, 2020
)
. Research assistants produce open-ended output, and for that the field uses preference- and rubric-based assessment: LLM judges agree substantially with human preference but show verbosity, position, and self-enhancement biases
(
Zheng et al.,, 2023
)
, and learned metrics such as BERTScore and BLEURT match human judgments of generated text better than surface overlap
(
Zhang et al.,, 2019
;
Sellam et al.,, 2020
)
. Factuality has its own instruments: TruthfulQA tests whether a model avoids common misconceptions
(
Lin et al.,, 2022
)
, FactScore verifies claim-level facts against trusted sources
(
Min et al.,, 2023
)
, SelfCheckGPT detects hallucination by self-consistency sampling
(
Manakul et al.,, 2023
)
, and RAGAS scores retrieval and grounded generation without reference answers
(
Es et al.,, 2024
)
. Given the self-enhancement bias of LLM judges, the judges of a workflow’s output should come from model families other than the one that produced it.

A research workflow can be evaluated at three further levels. At the domain level, FinQA tests numerical reasoning over financial reports
(
Chen et al.,, 2021
)
. At the agent level, AgentBench assesses tool use and sequential decision-making
(
Liu et al.,, 2023
)
, and practical guidance covers what to measure in agentic systems
(
Grace et al.,, 2026
)
and how to build agents that audit alignment
(
Bricken et al.,, 2025
)
. At the workflow level, human–AI collaboration in the science of science has been measured as a workflow
(
Shao et al.,, 2026
)
, expert-level scientific tasks frame the evaluation of general scientific agents
(
Chai et al.,, 2025
)
, and a protocol for networks of autonomous scientific agents raises system-level evaluation questions
(
Jiang et al.,, 2025
)
. An economics workflow needs, in addition, criteria specific to the discipline, such as whether an effect is identified and whether a model is calibrated against data.

Single-run benchmarks miss reliability. Measured along consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety with twelve metrics across fifteen models, agent reliability has improved only slightly as capability has grown
(
Rabanser et al.,, 2026
)
. Running twelve multi-agent systems under repeated runs, backend models, and tool configurations shows substantial run-to-run variance, with architecture the main driver of resource use and reproducibility
(
Ma et al.,, 2026
)
. A research workflow must therefore be run more than once, with the variance reported alongside the mean.

2.4
Research Gaps

Agentic workflows that operate across several research stages with bounded autonomy are rare in economics. Elsewhere, end-to-end systems exist: agentic tree search carries machine-learning research from hypothesis through experiments
(
Yamada et al.,, 2025
)
, and a six-agent platform covers chemical synthesis from literature search through screening, kinetics, optimization, scale-up, and purification
(
Ruan et al.,, 2024
)
. Two gaps follow from the reading above.

The first gap is the absence of an implemented, open workflow spanning the lifecycle. The first version of this paper proposed such a workflow for economics
(
Dawid et al.,, 2025
)
. The systems that have appeared since each cover part of it: data construction
(
Afonso et al.,, 2026
)
and the path from an economic intuition to a simulated experiment
(
Chen et al.,, 2026
)
. As of this writing, none spans ideation, literature, modeling, data, code, estimation, and reporting with a public, runnable implementation. Whether such a workflow is reproducible, auditable, and methodologically valid under realistic constraints is the question the rest of this paper takes up.

The second gap concerns reliability, accountability, safety, and privacy (Section
2.1
) in systems that produce research. When six autonomous research systems, among them AI Scientist-v1 and v2 and Agent Laboratory, were run on papers from the 2025 International Conference on Machine Learning, human reviewers found methodological flaws, hallucinated experimental results, and reproducibility failures in all of them, and 59% of the manuscripts an automated reviewer accepted contained fabricated or unsupported claims
(
Gaddipati et al.,, 2026
)
. Frontier coding agents reproduce a large share of published social-science findings from released materials, yet subtle prompt framing can nudge them toward confirmatory specification search
(
Alizadeh et al.,, 2026
)
. A workflow that produces research must therefore make its outputs verifiable, record who made each choice and how each result can be reproduced, defend its infrastructure against the threats set out there, and keep its data under the researcher’s control.

The lifecycle review above reads each stage on its own, but the stages also depend on one another: a literature review supplies the evidence for a modeling assumption, a model specification states what the data must measure, and an estimate carries identifying assumptions that limit its interpretation. A research workflow therefore needs checks on what passes between stages as well as on each stage’s output, and economist review where a question, specification, or interpretation must be revised.

3
Methodology

We introduce a methodology for economic research built on agentic workflows: autonomous, iterative processes in which LLMs carry out research tasks under human-in-the-loop oversight. Spanning the full lifecycle, from ideation and literature review through model specification, data collection, implementation, and empirical estimation, to interpretation and reporting, it lets agents act with substantial autonomy while economists retain control at the decisions that matter. The following subsections describe the five pillars of this methodology: agent design, human-in-the-loop oversight, workflow coordination, reasoning as an economist, and trustworthy execution.

3.1
Agent Design

Each agent in the system is engineered to manage a clearly defined responsibility within the economic research lifecycle.
The design is based on the following core principles:

Defined Responsibilities
: Each agent is assigned a specific scope of focus within the economic research pipeline. For example,
Ideator
surfaces candidate research concepts from economic phenomena documented in published research and emerging web discussions;
GreyScout
searches grey literature such as working papers, policy reports, and preprints;
DataCleaner
turns economic data from different sources into a validated, aligned, analysis-ready dataset;
ModelDesigner
formulates a chosen theoretical framework into precise mathematical form;

Estimator
proposes econometric specifications for a deterministic harness, a fixed rule-based procedure rather than an agent, to estimate and verify;
and
Proofreader
suggests wording and clarity improvements against disciplinary conventions on an already-verified draft.

Inter-Agent Communication
: Agents exchange structured, typed artifacts in a fixed handoff sequence that mirrors the economic research workflow. Each agent’s output becomes a defined input for the next, rather than open-ended conversation between LLMs.
For instance,
Estimator
does not describe its regression to
Validator
in prose. It hands over an
EstimationSpec
that names the dependent variable, the regressors, their transformations, and the sign the theory predicts for each coefficient. The deterministic harness estimates exactly that specification, and
Validator
and
HypothesisTester
receive the coefficients, standard errors, and diagnostic statistics as fields of the same record, so every check is run on the specification that was estimated rather than on a restatement of it.

Error and Escalation Pathways
: Each agent incorporates mechanisms to detect issues within its domain and either resolve them internally or escalate them.
For example, when one of the literature databases the workflow searches stops responding, the search continues with the others and records which database failed; the problem is resolved within the stage. When a live data fetch fails, by contrast,
DataCollector
cannot recover the missing series itself, so it escalates: it flags the series as simulated, the estimation excludes it, and the report discloses it.

Adaptive Mechanisms
: Agents are designed to revise a strategy or specification if the initial attempt proves inadequate, and this adaptation is not confined to a single agent; it can span stages.
For instance, when
ModelDesigner
specifies what data a model needs, the data agents report back which requirements the available data can actually support, which can only be approximated with a proxy, and which cannot be met at all.
Where the data fall short,
ModelDesigner
revises the specification accordingly.
This exchange is bounded, and each revision must make progress toward resolving the shortfall or the cycle stops, with the remaining gap documented as a limitation.

The design and implementation of these agents are fundamentally guided by human expertise.
Economists define each agent’s responsibilities, knowledge boundaries,
and interaction protocols based on established research methodologies and domain-specific requirements.
Each agent operates only within the boundaries so defined.
Economists carefully structure the communication pathways between agents, whether sequential or parallel, to mirror the logical progression of economic research workflows.
What the system can do therefore depends on the economists’ input at this design phase.
Furthermore, ethical considerations such as data privacy, citation integrity, and methodological transparency are embedded into agent protocols through human oversight.

3.2
Human in the Loop

Human economists are embedded at specific checkpoints throughout the workflow to guide and validate the autonomous processes.
As with the agents’ responsibilities and communication pathways, the economists themselves decide where these checkpoints sit when they design the workflow.
Their roles include:

Initial Review and Prioritization
:
As raw research ideas first emerge, economists review and prioritize them,
and only the questions they select proceed. At this filter, economists weigh three things: whether a question is new relative to the literature, whether it can be answered with the data and identification strategies available, and what an answer would contribute, to economic understanding or to policy.

Iterative Feedback
:
At every stage, economists annotate and correct agent outputs before the next stage builds on them, steering the work toward their own standards of rigor and the economic debates they judge relevant.
During ideation, for example, they narrow a research question’s scope, sharpen its theoretical grounding, and check that the available data can answer it; during modeling and estimation, they revise the assumptions and specifications the agents propose.

Methodological Validation
:
Economists judge whether the chosen methods suit the research question and the evidence, by the standards of the subfield concerned: for example, whether a theoretical model’s assumptions are consistent and its equilibrium well defined, whether an identification strategy supports a causal claim, whether a calibrated or simulated model reproduces the facts it targets, or whether an experiment has the power to detect the effect it seeks.

Quality Control and Verification
:
Throughout analysis, interpretation, and documentation, economists check that each result is correct and that its interpretation stays within what was shown: for example, that a derivation holds, that a simulated result survives changes in its parameters, that an estimate is read within the limits of its identification, and that a welfare or policy statement follows from the model behind it. These checks target results that are correctly computed but wrongly interpreted or overstated.

Ethical Oversight and Bias Mitigation
:
Throughout all stages, economists monitor for ethical concerns: data privacy in sensitive economic datasets, the distributional implications of policy recommendations, and algorithmic biases that might skew economic analyses along socioeconomic, racial, or gender lines.
This oversight matters most when research involves vulnerable populations or carries significant policy implications.

Final Approval and Archiving
:
In the final stages of formatting, replication, and version control, economists review and sign off each component against disciplinary standards, ethical guidelines, and replication requirements.
The review includes examining data documentation, assessing code accessibility and clarity, and evaluating the comprehensiveness of sensitivity analyses.

The quality of these human checkpoints determines the value of the research output.
This human-in-the-loop (HITL) framework thus serves as both a quality assurance mechanism and an ethical safeguard against potential misuse or misapplication of AI in economic research.

3.3
Workflow Coordination

This methodology coordinates AI agents and human economists across an interconnected, multi-phase workflow that mirrors traditional economic research, assigning the work to agents and the judgment calls to economists.
Key phases include:

Research Question Formulation and Initialization
: Various specialized agents work together to generate, enrich, and refine research questions within economic paradigms. The workflow supports both autonomous ideation, where agents discover research questions from scratch by analyzing economic trends and literature gaps, and human-initiated ideation, where economists provide initial ideas that are systematically enriched and developed by the agents. Human economists judge the proposed questions for novelty, answerability, and contribution before they proceed.

Automated Literature Exploration
: Multiple agents systematically discover and synthesize relevant economic literature, while management agents keep the collection up to date as new work appears. However, these agents may occasionally hallucinate non-existent sources or misinterpret economic theories. Therefore, specialized supervision agents with conservative verification thresholds check citations and theoretical interpretations, with human economists providing the final validation of the literature foundation.

Model Specification
: Agents translate the chosen theoretical framework into a formal model, specifying its equations, variables, functional forms, and the structure that identifies its parameters (e.g., DSGE systems, structural equations, or reduced-form instrumental-variable designs), and set out the data the model will require. A deterministic harness then estimates the model’s parameters against cited external targets and returns a calibration verdict, including uncalibratable where the evidence does not identify them. Human economists check the specification for internal coherence and for whether its parameters are identifiable before any data are gathered.

Data Collection and Preprocessing
: Specialized agents gather and prepare economic datasets (e.g., panel data, time series, cross-sectional surveys), followed by quality-assurance agents that check each dataset and record how it was produced. Human economists collaborate with these agents to address complex data issues, such as structural breaks in macroeconomic time series or selection bias in microeconomic surveys, that call for domain judgment.

Model Implementation and Coding
: Agents derive runnable code from the model’s equations and test it by running it; a component that cannot be translated is declined and reported, not approximated. Human economists review the code for fidelity to the specification, since an implementation that runs is not yet one that faithfully encodes the model; a discrepancy goes back to the agents for correction, or to the model specification when the equations themselves must change, and the stage ends when code and specification agree or the remaining gap is recorded as a limitation.

Empirical Estimation
: Agents propose an estimation specification, reduced-form or structural, which a deterministic harness estimates; they then run the diagnostics the method calls for (e.g., residual tests for a regression, over-identification tests for a moment-based estimator) and carry out robustness and sensitivity checks. Human economists judge whether the identifying assumptions hold before the estimates are interpreted.

Interpretation and Reporting
: Agents derive economic insights from the validated results (e.g., elasticity interpretations, standardized effect sizes, policy-relevant magnitudes), assemble supporting exhibits, proofread the draft, and format outputs to economic-journal conventions; they also package, track, and archive the project’s assets so that every step can be traced to its inputs and its deterministic steps re-run. The economist owns the interpretation and the write-up, treats the agents’ outputs as provisional (aware that both humans and AI can misread statistical results or overstate causal claims), and determines which version constitutes the authoritative research record.

Across these phases, the agents carry out the work and the economists make the judgment calls: a phase’s output moves on to the next phase only after passing the checkpoint the economists have set for it, and what they return there, a correction, a revised question or specification, or an accepted limitation, directs the agents’ next round of work.

3.4
Reasoning as an Economist

Economic reasoning draws on a wide range of methods, from optimization and equilibrium analysis to causal inference and structural modeling, among many others. Whatever the technique, a claim must still meet standards general enough to build directly into a workflow: an empirical effect must be identified, the mechanism behind it must be structurally coherent, and the theory it rests on must survive deliberate refutation. Integrating economics into an agentic workflow means making these standards explicit and checkable at the point in the pipeline where they apply. The following practices implement these standards, drawn from the broader universe of economic reasoning.

Causal identification
: Identification comes first, and the workflow makes it an explicit step in its own right: when an agent proposes an empirical relationship, it must state the strategy that identifies it (an instrumental-variable, natural-experiment, or selection-on-observables argument) and put it up for scrutiny; a result that fits the data well but rests on no such argument is not allowed to stand as causal. Under the stated strategy the workflow then obtains a second estimate by a different estimation method and compares it with the agent’s estimate; any divergence between the two estimates is reported rather than assumed away.

Structural coherence and simulation
: Some claims rest on a model of an economic mechanism, such as how the economy responds to a policy or a shock. For those, the workflow runs the model as a simulation and checks its behavior against known stylized facts before any counterfactual is drawn from it: for example, it compares a macroeconomic model’s simulated moments and impulse responses, or a market model’s simulated price and entry responses, with their empirical counterparts, so a model whose behavior misses the stylized facts is flagged before its counterfactuals are used, testing the mechanism itself rather than a reduced-form summary of the data.

Theory-guided reasoning
: The agents should reason within the theoretical frame the economist chooses to adopt, whether general equilibrium, game theory, or a particular econometric tradition. The workflow lets the economists ground the reasoning layer in the theory their study assumes, so that the agents’ judgments start from economic argument and stay consistent with the modeling choices already made.

Adversarial falsification
: When an agent advances a theoretical claim, such as that a policy raises welfare or that an effect is monotone in a parameter, the workflow searches the agreed model space for a counterexample: a parameterization or agent composition, admissible under the model’s assumptions, in which the claim fails. A claim that survives the search is retained, with the search recorded beside it; one for which a counterexample is found is returned for revision. Refutation is thus a routine, documented step in building theory.

3.5
Trustworthy Execution

An autonomous workflow can be trusted in economic research only if its own conduct meets the same standard of accountability as the research it produces. Trustworthy execution addresses reliability, accountability, safety, and privacy (Section
2.1
) with safeguards that apply at every stage.

Reliability through verification
: The workflow verifies each agent’s output through a step independent of the agent that produced it. Every citation is checked against the source it points to before it reaches the report: a verification step flags references it cannot confirm, and the economist judges whether each source supports the claim it is attached to. The data passed between stages must conform to an agreed structure before the receiving agent acts on them. Where reasoning as an economist tests whether a claim meets the field’s methodological standards, verification tests whether it is supported by its sources and inputs.

Accountability through provenance and bounded authority
: Every model call, tool invocation, and stage transition is logged, and every artifact carries the provenance of the inputs and decisions that shaped it. From these records a result can be traced back to the process that produced it, and its deterministic steps re-run. Authority is bounded as well as recorded: each agent is given only what its role needs, stays within its declared responsibilities, and may use only the tools it has been granted; anything else is denied by default. Budgets on cost, tokens, and retries bound each stage and the workflow as a whole. These limits confine each agent to its own task and cap the resources a run can consume.

Safety through an explicit threat model
: Agents that read untrusted material, write to shared memory, and call external tools face risks an ordinary prompt does not, so the workflow is designed against an explicit threat model for agentic systems. Agent memory is isolated and allowed to expire, which limits how long a poisoned input persists; messages between agents are signed, so an alteration in transit is detected; generated code runs in a sandbox; and human approval is required at the steps that would be hard to undo. The authority limits above bound what an agent can do by accident or over-reach; these safeguards bound what an adversary can make it do.

Privacy through local serving
: Every LLM in the workflow can be served on open weights on hardware the researcher controls, so prompts, intermediate artifacts, and outputs need not leave the researcher’s environment. Incoming data are screened for personal information, and credentials are kept out of the logs.

Together, these safeguards record each run, so a result can be traced to its recorded process and the human checkpoints it passed. As AI systems and the risks they pose continue to develop, economists may extend or adapt these safeguards to match.

Workflow Design and Implementation

This section presents a sample design and implementation of an agentic workflow system for economic research that follows the methodology outlined in Section
3
.
We describe a typical architecture, components, and operational mechanisms that enable collaborative research between AI agents and human economists,
noting that actual implementations can vary based on economists’ goals and design preferences.
The following subsections elaborate on each stage of this example workflow.

4.1
Ideation and Research Question Formulation

The ideation process in economic research presents challenges, including the need to navigate vast and rapidly expanding literature,
identify meaningful gaps in knowledge, connect different theoretical frameworks, and formulate questions with both academic precision and policy relevance.
To address these difficulties, we employ agentic workflows for research question formulation.
Semi-autonomous agents identify emerging trends, scan the literature, and synthesize what they find,
and HITL checkpoints let economists review and redirect the output at each stage.
The following subsections describe the agents and outline their respective responsibilities.
They also explain how these agents work together to generate and refine candidate research questions.

4.1.1
Agents

The ideation phase uses eight agents, each responsible for one task in formulating research questions.
These agents operate in a sequential-parallel architecture, sharing information through standardized data structures while maintaining independent processing capabilities.
Together, they form a system that spans from broad trend identification to precise research question formulation, with human oversight integrated at strategic checkpoints.

TrendSurfer
identifies emerging or trending topics in real time by searching recent arXiv economics and finance preprints and an open news search.
It runs in each search round of the sourcing stage.

CorpusScout
gathers preliminary academic literature from Semantic Scholar (with Crossref as the fallback) and, in the web-crawl modes, from an open web search restricted to academic domains, to build an initial knowledge base.
It generates a structured literature database with basic metadata that serves as the foundation for deeper analysis.
This agent runs in each search round of the sourcing stage.

CorpusScout
works in close coordination with
ScholarSearcher
and provides its initial literature findings for concept generation.

ScholarSearcher
surfaces foundational and highly cited papers by querying open bibliometric indices, ranking candidates by citation count and filtering for seminal, field-defining contributions. It produces standardized research data with removed duplicates, rankings, and organized tags. For economists or institutions with subscription access, the connector layer is designed to be extended with premier licensed databases such as Web of Science and Elsevier Scopus, broadening coverage beyond what open indices alone provide. The agent is triggered when detailed research is needed following
CorpusScout

ScholarSearcher
collaborates with
CorpusScout
and feeds organized scholarly literature, enabling better coverage of peer-reviewed research.

GreyScout
expands the search beyond peer-reviewed publications to grey literature: working papers, preprints, policy reports, and conference proceedings from institutions and think tanks. It creates a grey-literature database with relevance scoring that captures emerging ideas not yet formalized in academic journals. This agent runs in each search round of the sourcing stage.
GreyScout
complements
ScholarSearcher
’s academic focus and provides alternative perspectives, aiming for a balanced view of both established and emerging knowledge.

Ideator
aggregates insights from both human economists and the sources discovered by the above agents to produce initial ideas, hypotheses, and key concepts. It generates potential research directions based on the information gathered throughout the system. The agent runs on the sourcing stage’s output and the economists’ inputs.
Ideator
receives data from
CorpusScout
,
ScholarSearcher
, and
GreyScout

Refiner
evaluates the ideas generated by

Ideator
, often incorporating human feedback or similarity checks during its refinement process.
Refiner
processes
Ideator
’s output and forwards its refined questions to
Contextualizer
for theoretical framing.

Contextualizer
adds depth to emerging questions by mapping them against established economic theories and highlighting relevant policy debates. It creates contextualized research questions with clear theoretical and policy relevance that situate the inquiry within the broader academic discourse. This agent is triggered after gap analyses, debate-detection outputs, or by economist request.
Contextualizer
processes
Refiner
’s output and passes its contextualized questions to
Finalizer
for prioritization; it frames the questions it is given and does not add, drop, or reword them.

Finalizer
ranks the contextualized questions and writes, for each, its rationale, methodology, expected impact, and feasibility, checking alignment with project objectives and related literature. Each of its outputs must cite the identifier of the question it ranks and reproduce that question verbatim; a deterministic check replaces any reworded or uncited output with the source question. It produces a prioritized shortlist of research questions ready for final selection that represents the main idea of the entire ideation process. The agent is generally initiated by economists seeking to finalize the research question set after iterative refinements.
Finalizer
receives input from
Contextualizer
and presents its final questions to human economists for ultimate decision-making, completing the ideation cycle.

4.2
Automated Literature Review and Synthesis

This phase systematically finds, examines, and combines relevant literature using a network of specialized agents that retrieve targeted papers, summarize findings, detect research gaps, and verify citations.
At HITL checkpoints, economists align the literature base with the research agenda.

4.2.1
Agents

A set of specialized agents (

,

,

TopicCrawler

serves as the field-pattern analyst for the batch

It takes input from processed summaries and paper decompositions, as well as citation networks and publication metadata. The agent produces gap analyses highlighting promising research directions, methodological innovations needed, and potential contributions to economic theory.
GapFinder
activates after

CiteKeeper
acts as the bibliographic management system for economic research. Its role involves maintaining reference information and facilitating proper attribution practices. The agent ingests metadata from published economic papers, working papers, and conference proceedings, including author affiliations, journal impact factors, and citation counts. It produces standardized citation entries in a consistent bibliographic format, manages DOI information, and generates formatted bibliographies.
CiteKeeper
updates continuously as new economic literature enters the system, with particular attention to version tracking for working papers. It connects with all other agents by providing normalized reference data, particularly supporting
TrendTracker
with citation metrics and feeding bibliographic relationships into the team’s synthesis step.

KnowledgeWeaver
functions as the integrative synthesis engine for economic literature. Its role is to construct maps of economic knowledge domains, showing theoretical lineages and empirical contradictions. The agent processes all outputs from other agents, including summaries, decompositions, gap analyses, citation data, and trend reports. It produces interactive literature graphs visualizing schools of economic thought, methodology clusters, and conceptual relationships, while maintaining research notes.
KnowledgeWeaver
activates on demand for literature reviews or automatically as new agent outputs accumulate. It integrates with all other agents by consolidating their outputs into one knowledge structure, particularly informing
GapFinder
about system-level patterns that might reveal new research opportunities.

acts as the factual-grounding gate for the

4.3
Model Specification and Calibration

This phase turns a research question into a working model: a theoretical framework is developed, formalized mathematically, and calibrated against real-world data and established economic principles.

Theorist
,
ModelDesigner
, and
Calibrator
carry out these three steps under continuous human oversight, joined by an optional

and

4.3.1
Agents

From theoretical formulation to mathematical implementation and calibration, each agent handles conceptual soundness, logical consistency, and empirical relevance.

Theorist
functions as the conceptual architect for economic models. Its primary role is to establish the theoretical foundation by selecting appropriate economic paradigms, defining assumptions, and outlining functional relationships between variables. The agent processes inputs from research questions, literature reviews, and human-specified constraints about economic phenomena being studied. It produces theoretical frameworks with clearly articulated economic assumptions, behavioral mechanisms, and expected relationships among variables.
Theorist
activates when preliminary research questions are sufficiently refined or when economists initiate the model development phase. It integrates closely with
KnowledgeWeaver
from the literature review phase by incorporating synthesized theoretical insights, and provides its theoretical framework directly to

for mathematical formalization, establishing a conceptual foundation for subsequent model development.

ModelDesigner
serves as the mathematical formalizer for economic theories. Its role involves translating theoretical constructs into precise mathematical structures such as utility functions and market equilibrium conditions. The agent processes the theoretical framework from
Theorist
, along with model specification requirements and methodological preferences from economists. It outputs formal mathematical models including equations (e.g., difference/differential, recursive, or simultaneous), parameter definitions, and computational implementation guidelines.
ModelDesigner
activates immediately after

finalizes the theoretical framework or when economists request alternative mathematical formulations of existing theories. It collaborates both with
Theorist
, by translating its conceptual insights, and with

Calibrator
functions as the empirical alignment specialist for economic models. Its role involves determining parameter values intended to reproduce key empirical facts or stylized economic phenomena. The agent takes inputs from the formal model structure produced by
ModelDesigner
, empirical targets from the literature, and preliminary datasets provided by economists. It proposes candidate parameter sets, each with a qualitative sensitivity rating; the deterministic harness produces the goodness-of-fit metrics against the cited targets, and the
CodeTeam
’s comparative-statics sweep computes how parameter variations move the steady state.
Calibrator
activates once

completes the formal model structure and preliminary data or benchmarks become available. It integrates with
ModelDesigner
by operationalizing its mathematical framework, and its calibrated parameters later serve as a baseline the empirical estimation phase can compare its own estimated values against. Given observed data, the
Calibrator
can additionally produce a zero-shot causal estimate from a causal foundation model (under an instrumental-variable, natural-experiment, or selection-on-observables strategy, scored for identification soundness) as an independent cross-check on the classical parameter estimates; without observations it records a refusal instead of an estimate.

falsified

Falsifier

Simulator
(optional) executes the calibrated model through a pluggable backend to obtain simulation trajectories. It consumes the model specification from
ModelDesigner
, calibrated parameters from
Calibrator
, and scenario definitions from economists or from a library of built-in scenarios covering settings such as fiscal-policy transmission, monetary-policy counterfactuals, and strategic pricing under information asymmetry. It produces scenario-level trajectories, impulse responses, and macro-moment matching statistics.
Simulator
is invoked only when the optional simulation stage is enabled (below); it receives calibrated parameters from
Calibrator
and emits artifacts scored under the Simulation Fidelity dimension.

4.3.2
Workflow and Human Checkpoints

The model development process consists of three key stages: formulating the theoretical foundation, designing the model structure, and calibrating parameters. At each stage, specialized agents collaborate with human economists. Figure
3
shows the process.

Theoretical Foundation Stage

In the initial stage,
Theorist
drafts the theoretical foundation. Economists then review the proposed framework for coherence with existing theories and project objectives, refining assumptions or selecting alternative theoretical constructs where the draft does not fit the intended research direction.

Model Design Stage

Building on the theoretical framework,
ModelDesigner
produces the structured model. Human economists validate this theory-to-model translation for consistency and mathematical soundness, annotating components, refining variable definitions, and proposing alternative formulations where the representation of economic mechanisms falls short.

Calibration Stage

With the model structure in place,
Calibrator
proposes parameter values, which the deterministic harness estimates and scores against cited targets. Economists review the verdict and the targets behind it and may send the targets or the model back for revision, but cannot change the verdict.

Calibration is deliberately split so that the language model only proposes parameters while a deterministic harness independently estimates and scores them. Against cited external targets, the harness returns a verdict:
calibrated
,
point-calibrated
, or
partially calibrated
from the closed-form steady-state identities of the textbook structures it recognizes in the model’s own parsed equations (an RBC or New Keynesian core, Cobb–Douglas or CES production, a hand-to-mouth household share);
simulation-calibrated
by simulated method of moments on a simulator of the model’s own equations, a path available when such a simulator is supplied, which the pipeline does not yet do;
archetype-calibrated
by simulated method of moments on a registered canonical model that shares a mechanism with it, in which case only the parameters that model estimates are harness values; or
uncalibratable
, marked non-applicable. An estimate counts only when it lies inside the parameter’s declared range and off its bounds; a model is
point-calibrated
only when every pinned moment lies within two standard errors of its target, and
calibrated
only when its over-identifying restrictions are not rejected (Hansen’s J test at 5%). A registered canonical model is used only for a parameter whose name states the matching role and whose declared range overlaps the canonical model’s admissible range. It is this verdict, not the model’s narration, that the pipeline reports. The harness’s tiered design, and a concrete run exhibiting these verdicts, are given in Appendix
A
.

Simulation Stage (optional)

An optional simulation stage runs after calibration when enabled. The human role here is to choose the scenario and judge the result: an economist selects a scenario (built-in or their own), and once
Simulator
has executed the calibrated model, they review the results against known stylized facts and decide whether the simulated behavior is credible enough to draw counterfactuals from. Outputs that miss the stylized facts are sent back for re-specification rather than carried forward. The stage is disabled by default.

4.4
Data Collection and Preprocessing

This phase systematically gathers, cleans, and prepares economic data for economists’ empirical work. These data include time series, panel data, unstructured text, and data from alternative sources (e.g., satellite imagery, social media feeds). Because the agents are LLM-driven, the pipeline can ingest multilingual and multimodal data (non-English sources, tables, and figures) that would otherwise be costly to process at scale. Specialized agents with distinct roles retrieve, clean, validate, and document the data, and human economists remain in the loop through interactive feedback, guiding and validating each step of the process.

The
DataTeam
draws on open-source APIs by default, escalating to premium subscribed sources (e.g., Bloomberg, Refinitiv) or user-uploaded datasets only when needed, and never silently substitutes a simulated series for a real one: a series from a source without a live connector enters the dataset only as a disclosed simulation. After its first retrieval, the
DataTeam
reconciles what the model needs against what the data can supply: the
ModelTeam
emits a
Data Requirements Specification
(DRS), the
DataTeam
answers with a
Data Availability Report
(DAR) and a feasibility verdict, and where a requirement cannot be met, a
Model Revision Request
(MRR) carries the constraint back to the
ModelTeam
for re-specification. The full mechanics of this Model–Data feasibility exchange are described in the Feasibility Review Stage below.

4.4.1
Agents

The
DataTeam
’s agents form an automated data processing pipeline, facilitating dataset discovery, retrieval, cleaning, integration, validation, and documentation. Each agent has a specific function, from identifying relevant data sources to maintaining reproducibility.

AvailabilityScout
functions as the feasibility-search specialist. Its role is to determine, for each requirement in the DRS, whether a satisfying series exists and at what cost, searching in priority order across the three source tiers: open-source APIs (default), then premium subscribed sources, then user-uploaded data. For each requirement it searches the retrieved series and the series the open connectors can fetch, maps the requirement to the best-matching one, and grades it feasible, proxy-only, or unavailable; a feasible match outside the retrieved series is fetched in a supplemental retrieval. It consumes the DRS from the
ModelTeam
and passes its graded matches to
AvailabilityReporter
.

AvailabilityReporter
assembles the DAR from
AvailabilityScout
’s graded matches. For each requirement it records the best match and its source tier, the coverage gap relative to the DRS requirement, and a ranked list of candidate proxies, each annotated with an explicit bias caveat (what the proxy over- or under-measures), and it summarizes the share of essential requirements met alongside the overall feasibility verdict. It leaves the choice between a proxy and the ideal measure to the economist: it presents the options and their consequences for the economist to weigh at the Feasibility Review.

DataScout
functions as the economic data discovery specialist. Its primary role is to identify and evaluate relevant datasets that match the research requirements for economic analysis. The agent scans academic databases, APIs, and specialized repositories to locate both conventional economic indicators and alternative data sources. It processes inputs from research questions, model specifications, and economist-defined data requirements.
DataScout
produces dataset inventories with relevance scores, source reliability ratings, access requirements, and update frequencies for economic time series, cross-sectional, and panel data. It activates either through scheduled periodic scans of data repositories or when manually initiated by economists exploring new economic topics or requiring updated information. It integrates with
DataCollector
by providing prioritized dataset lists for acquisition, and its metadata on source characteristics later documents the data sources in economic research.

DataCollector
serves as the acquisition and standardization system for economic datasets. Its role involves retrieving identified datasets while maintaining access compliance and standardizing formats across sources. The agent processes targeted dataset URLs, API endpoints, and access credentials from
DataScout
, along with economist-specified collection settings. It outputs standardized raw datasets with harmonized formats addressing international differences in time zones, currencies, units of measurement, and periodicity commonly found in economic data.
DataCollector
activates immediately after
DataScout
compiles its inventory of relevant sources or upon direct economist request for specific economic series. It works in coordination with
DataScout
by executing its acquisition recommendations, and with
DataCleaner
by delivering consistently formatted raw datasets, establishing the foundation for subsequent cleaning operations in the economic data pipeline.

DataCleaner
functions as the quality control specialist for economic datasets. Its role involves identifying and resolving data quality issues while preserving the integrity of economic information. The agent processes raw datasets from
DataCollector
, along with domain-specific cleaning rules and economist-specified quality thresholds.
It produces processed datasets with documentation of all modifications, including missing value treatments, outlier handling, and duplicate removal.

DataCleaner
activates following successful data collection or when economists identify quality issues in existing datasets. It integrates with
DataCollector
by processing its raw outputs, and with
DataIntegrator
by providing cleaned, individual datasets ready for merging.

DataIntegrator
serves as the dataset consolidation engine. Its role involves merging data from different sources into analysis-ready datasets while reconciling their formats, units, and frequencies. The agent processes multiple cleaned datasets from
DataCleaner
, along with economist specifications for desired integration outcomes. It produces unified datasets with harmonized variable names, time indices aligned deterministically (each series resampled to the coarsest common frequency by period mean, incomplete periods dropped), and consistent cross-sectional identifiers.
DataIntegrator
activates once
DataCleaner
completes processing individual datasets or when economists require a combination of disparate economic data sources. It works with
DataCleaner
by building upon its quality-assured outputs, and with
IndicatorBuilder
by providing integrated datasets ready for indicator construction, facilitating economic analyses that require multiple data sources.

IndicatorBuilder
functions as the variable creation specialist for economic analysis.
Its role involves developing theoretically grounded derived variables the analysis requires.
The agent processes integrated datasets from
DataIntegrator
, along with economic theory specifications and economist requirements for analytical indicators.
It outputs enriched datasets containing derived economic indicators, transformed variables for statistical compliance, and dimensionality reductions for high-dimensional economic data.

IndicatorBuilder
activates upon economist request for specific data analysis or when model specifications require particular variable forms not present in the raw data.
It integrates with
DataIntegrator
by enhancing its consolidated datasets, and with
DataAuditor
by providing indicator-rich datasets ready for quality verification, supporting econometric modeling with appropriately constructed variables.

DataAuditor
serves as the statistical verification system for economic datasets.
Its role is to check prepared data against quality and statistical requirements.
The agent processes indicator-enhanced datasets from
IndicatorBuilder
, along with predefined validation rules and economic benchmarks. The dataset’s quality verdict comes from deterministic checks (constant series, no numeric values, duplicate dates, too few observations, simulated series), which the agent narrates; the estimation stage reads the same archived observations and excludes series that failed them.
It produces validation reports detailing statistical properties (stationarity, normality, heteroskedasticity), variable correlations, benchmark comparisons against established economic facts, and recommendations for additional data processing if needed.

DataAuditor
activates once data preprocessing is complete or periodically during updates. It works closely with
IndicatorBuilder
by validating its derived variables, and with
MetadataKeeper
by providing statistical assessment reports.

MetadataKeeper
functions as the documentation specialist. Its role involves creating metadata and maintaining processing records.
The agent collects information from all preceding data agents, including source characteristics, processing steps, and quality assessments.
It generates data dictionaries with economic variable definitions, units, and sources; transformation logs documenting all data analyses; and quality reports highlighting potential limitations or special considerations for economic interpretation.
MetadataKeeper
runs continuously throughout the data processing workflow, updating documentation with each modification or on scheduled intervals. It integrates with all other agents by centralizing their metadata and processing information, and its transformation logs and quality reports are the documentation
Archivist
(below) draws on for the replication manifest.

Archivist
is the replication-assurance agent for the
DataTeam
. It is deterministic (no language model call) and runs once the dataset is certified, consolidating what the pipeline already tracked into one artifact. The artifact holds a fingerprint of the Python environment (interpreter version, platform, and the installed versions of the packages the pipeline depends on), a content hash of the exact stage scripts that ran, a content hash of the certified dataset’s record (its metadata, provenance, alignment summary, and last five complete-case rows), and a per-dataset audit trail assembled from the retrieval source, the merge and frequency-alignment methods applied, the certification verdict, and the sandbox-validation outcome. The result is a
replication manifest
: given the same pipeline code and the same inputs, an economist can check whether a rerun reproduces the same stage code and dataset record, and which hashed component differs if it does not.
Archivist
activates automatically after
MetadataKeeper
completes documentation, drawing on its transformation logs and quality reports as the audit trail’s source material.

4.4.2
Workflow and Human Checkpoints

The data preparation process consists of four key stages: discovering and retrieving data sources, preprocessing and cleaning, quality assurance and documentation, and reconciling model requirements with data availability (the Model–Data feasibility review), which the pipeline runs after the first retrieval. Figure
4
shows the process.
At each step, economists review what the agents propose before it carries forward to the next stage.

Feasibility Review Stage

After the first retrieval, the economist reviews the DAR at a dedicated feasibility review checkpoint, the human trade-off gate. When the feasibility verdict is not feasible, the reviewer is shown the unmet essential requirements and their candidate proxies and chooses, per requirement, from a fixed menu: accept a named proxy (whose bias caveat is then recorded in provenance); escalate the source (authorize a premium subscription or request a user upload, re-running availability on that tier); revise the model (emit an MRR that loops back to the
ModelTeam
to drop, replace, or re-specify the variable); narrow the research question (the decision is recorded with the run’s limitations; the run does not return to the
IdeationTeam
); or accept the specification as-is with a documented limitation. Because trading a proxy against a change to the model determines what the study can ultimately claim, this is a mandatory human checkpoint: the agents propose and the economist disposes. When the review revises the model or adds a fetchable series, the
DataTeam
retrieves, cleans, and validates the data once more; the downstream teams proceed even when the cycle ends without a feasible model–data pair, with the unmet requirements recorded.

Data Source Integration Stage

The open-source tier retrieves real observations through live connectors to seven services: FRED and Yahoo Finance via their native clients, plus five keyless open REST APIs (World Bank, DBnomics,

Economists review identified and collected datasets, manually adjusting source priorities, verifying access protocols, and flagging data sources requiring special handling or additional review.

4.5
Code Implementation and Testing

This phase derives an executable module from each model specification, validates it, runs a comparative-statics sweep on it, and versions and documents the result. Human oversight is embedded throughout to maintain quality, traceability, and alignment with the intended model.

4.5.1
Agents

The
CodeTeam
’s agents translate a model’s equations into working code without ever letting a language model write that code itself: a fixed, rule-based procedure, not an agent, converts each equation directly into its corresponding piece of the program, much as a spreadsheet formula converts directly into a calculation. The agents’ only job is to explain, check, time, version, or document what that procedure produced, never to write or edit the code. This guarantee is scoped to models whose equations reduce to a closed-form, steady-state-solvable system, such as DSGE or static equilibrium models; large agent-based simulations, which have no such system to parse, instead run through
ModelTeam
’s optional simulation stage on an established, pluggable backend. A likely medium-term path beyond a single custom parser is to wrap established, economist-trusted toolboxes such as Dynare, Stata, or MATLAB as callable tools once they expose programmatic APIs, rather than having an LLM re-author their functionality from scratch: economists already know these tools, and reimplementing what they already do well is slower and costlier than calling them directly.

Coder
reports on the executable module that the rule-based procedure derives for each formal model from the model’s parsed equation system. The agent processes the parsed equations from
ModelDesigner
and the parameter values proposed by
Calibrator
, and narrates the result: which variables and parameters the module solves for, and, for any equation that could not be parsed into closed form, the parser’s specific reason rather than a silent omission.
Coder
activates upon receiving finalized model specifications and proposed parameter values. It integrates with
ModelDesigner
and
Calibrator
by consuming their outputs, and with
Debugger
by passing the derived module forward for the executed check battery.

Debugger
narrates the executed, deterministic check battery run against each derived module: compilation, parameter completeness, residual finiteness, steady-state solvability, parameter sensitivity, and system completeness (whether the module is built from every equation of the model). Because the module is derived rather than hand-written, there is no free-hand code to debug: a failing check is a refusal to certify the module, not a bug the agent patches, and
Debugger
’s narration states what a failure implies about the model specification rather than proposing a code fix.
Debugger
activates whenever
Coder
delivers a new module. It works with
Coder
by reporting on its output, and certifies which modules are eligible for the comparative-statics sweep that follows.

BatchRunner
serves as the systematic experimentation engine for economic models. Its role involves sweeping every parameter of a certified module around its proposed value and reporting the resulting steady-state response, generating comparative statics for analysis.
BatchRunner
activates once
Debugger
certifies a module. It interfaces with
SweepProfiler
, which profiles its sweep’s real execution time; with the versioning step, whose identifiers let its comparative-statics results be matched back to the exact module and parameters that produced them; and it provides structured simulation outputs to the empirical analysis phase downstream.

SweepProfiler
profiles the comparative-statics sweep
BatchRunner
runs: it times each model’s sweep in wall-clock seconds and reports the total, mean, and slowest sweep time, alongside the share of individual parameter perturbations that failed to converge. This is diagnostic, not corrective:
SweepProfiler
identifies where computational cost concentrates; it does not rewrite the derived module, since LLM-authored edits to the derived module would break the rule that no LLM-authored code enters the pipeline.
SweepProfiler
activates automatically once
BatchRunner
’s sweep completes, and its findings are reported alongside the rest of that stage’s output.

VersionManager
is deterministic and content-hash based: it fingerprints each derived module’s exact source together with its exact parameter values into a single model version identifier, so identical code with different parameter values, or vice versa, is always a different, traceable version. It also fingerprints the pipeline’s own stage code, the same technique the
DataTeam
’s
Archivist
uses, so the exact code version that produced a result is independently verifiable.
VersionManager
activates once
BatchRunner
’s sweep completes, versioning every module generated in that run. It connects with
BatchRunner
by identifying which version produced which comparative-statics results, and supports the
DataTeam
’s
Archivist
component by making exact model versions recreatable.

DocuAgent
narrates the combined record from generation, validation, experimentation, and versioning into a single technical-documentation summary (what was derived and from how many equations, what the validation checks found, what the comparative-statics sweep showed and how long it took, and each module’s version identifier), under the same guardrail as
Coder
,
Debugger
, and
BatchRunner
: describe only what the pipeline actually produced, never invent capabilities the run did not exercise.
DocuAgent
activates once
VersionManager
completes versioning, drawing on all three stages’ outputs plus the version manifest.

Logger
is the shared instrumentation layer used by every team in the pipeline, not an agent unique to
CodeTeam
. It records stage-level timing, item counts, errors, and status for each of the three
CodeTeam
stages into a structured execution log: a fixed, uniform audit trail rather than one an agent dynamically designs.
Logger
runs automatically at the start and end of every stage; economists consult its execution log to verify milestone completion and diagnose where a run failed.

4.5.2
Workflow and Human Checkpoints

The code development and validation process runs across three stages (deterministic code generation, testing and validation, and experimentation with versioning and documentation), with

4.6
Empirical Estimation and Validation

This phase applies statistical and econometric techniques to estimate model parameters, then runs diagnostics and robustness checks on those estimates. Economic-magnitude interpretation is deliberately left to the next phase, Interpretation and Reporting, rather than duplicated here.

4.6.1
Agents

Four agents run this estimation and validation pipeline: an LLM proposes a specification or narrates already-computed statistics, while the reported verdict, diagnostics, and effects are always the deterministic numerical core’s, never the LLM’s.

Estimator
functions as the econometric specification specialist for economic models. Its role is to translate a model specification into one estimable reduced-form regression on the series actually available. The agent processes the model specification produced upstream (e.g., by
ModelDesigner
/
Calibrator
) and the validated dataset from
DataTeam
, proposing dependent/regressor variables, transforms, and theory-derived sign hypotheses as an
EstimationSpec
. The deterministic harness then estimates it and issues the verdict (
estimated
,
fragile
, or
inestimable
with a machine-readable reason), never a substituted or fabricated fit.
Estimator
activates once a model specification and a prepared dataset are both available, and again for one bounded revision round after reviewer feedback.

Validator
serves as the empirical performance evaluator. It narrates the diagnostics battery the harness runs on the stored analysis panel: Breusch–Pagan (heteroskedasticity), Durbin–Watson and Ljung–Box (autocorrelation), Jarque–Bera (normality), a residual augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test as a spurious-regression guard, Ramsey’s RESET (functional form), variance inflation factors (VIF; multicollinearity), and a sample-size check. Severe failures (a residual AR(1) coefficient of at least 0.9, a Durbin–Watson statistic below 0.5, or a residual-ADF
p
-value of at least 0.10) downgrade the verdict from estimated to fragile, a harness decision the agent reports but cannot override.
Validator
activates immediately after an estimable specification is produced.

HypothesisTester
functions as the formal inference and stability specialist. It deterministically tests every theory-derived sign/zero restriction carried in the specification, classifying a sign restriction as
supported
(one-sided
p<0.05
in the predicted direction),
contradicted
(two-sided
p<0.05
in the opposite direction), or
inconclusive
(neither), and a zero restriction as
consistent
or
rejected
at the 5% level; it then runs robustness sweeps (e.g., drop-one-regressor, split-sample halves, and covariance-estimator swap), all re-estimated on the same stored analysis panel, reporting a sign-stability score. It narrates the computed hypothesis and robustness results; it does not run additional statistics beyond what the harness already computed.

LatencyProfiler
is a deterministic performance profiler: it aggregates the real wall-clock timing recorded across all three stages, split into deterministic-compute time (data loading, estimation, diagnostics, robustness sweeps) versus LLM proposal/narration time, so an economist can see where the workflow’s actual bottleneck sits: at this pipeline’s scale, LLM latency typically dominates the (comparatively cheap) econometric computation.

4.6.2
Workflow and Human Checkpoints

The empirical estimation workflow runs in three stages, each pairing deterministic harness computation with LLM proposal or narration and, in the with-HITL configuration, a review checkpoint. Figure
6
shows the process.

Parameter Estimation

Estimator
proposes a specification. Before anything is estimated, economists review it against the available series and the observation budget, without seeing any estimate; a rejected proposal gets one revision, which is reviewed again, and if the revision is also rejected the original is estimated with the objections recorded. The harness then estimates the reviewed specification; it refuses any specification in which a series that builds the dependent variable enters a regressor in the same period. If the harness refuses the reviewed specification, one repair may be proposed: it must keep the reviewed dependent variable and hypotheses, it is reviewed before it is fitted, and it replaces the refused specification only if its verdict is better. Every fitted specification is recorded with its verdict and listed in the report. This HITL checkpoint reviews the specification against economic theory and institutional context before any estimate is seen.

Validation & Diagnostics

The harness runs the full diagnostics battery on the stored analysis panel and applies any resulting verdict downgrade;
Validator
reports the results. Economists examine the battery results and the (possibly downgraded) verdict, determining whether the specification needs revision or the caveats can be carried forward into the final narrative.

Inference, Robustness & Performance Profiling

HypothesisTester
tests theory-derived restrictions and runs the robustness sweeps;
LatencyProfiler
aggregates the run’s performance profile across all three stages. Economists conduct a final review of the hypothesis-test outcomes and stability results, recorded alongside the outputs, before the estimation artifact moves to
ReportingTeam
for economic interpretation and reporting.

4.7
Interpretation and Reporting

This stage converts technical model estimations and empirical data into interpretable economic knowledge, alongside the preparation of the final academic document.
The stage computes economic magnitudes from the estimates, assembles the draft report, and checks the numbers in the draft against the upstream artifacts before the wording is polished.

4.7.1
Agents

Five agents run an automated report-writing pipeline built around a deterministic reporting harness: every number in the final report is either computed from the upstream artifacts or, unless exempt, looked up in them, and every exhibit is assembled directly from those artifacts. The LLM’s narration is confined to captions, labels, and structural summaries; where the report calls for argumentative prose the pipeline cannot generate deterministically, such as the introduction and discussion, it hands the economist a bounded, numerically checked reference summary rather than inserting its own text.

ResultsInterpreter
functions as the economic significance translator for empirical findings. Its role is to convert statistical results into meaningful economic magnitudes. The agent processes the estimation artifact from
EstimationTeam
(coefficients, the stored analysis panel, and the fitted specification’s transforms), computing one-standard-deviation effects, standardized betas, and elasticity-at-means for each regressor (deterministic; the interpretation kind follows the transform pair, as in
EstimationTeam
), and accompanying figures (e.g., a time-series plot and a coefficient plot) rendered directly from the same data.
ResultsInterpreter
then narrates the computed magnitudes into economic (not merely statistical) significance; it may not compute or invent a new number. An inestimable upstream verdict passes through with no invented magnitudes.
ResultsInterpreter
activates once
EstimationTeam
’s output is available.

Reporter
functions as the research narrative architect. It specializes in organizing empirical outcomes and their analyses into a research report. The agent deterministically assembles a structured report: a fixed section skeleton (research question, literature context, model, data, estimation results, discussion, limitations, references, provenance), with every table and figure formatted directly from the pipeline’s own artifacts and a References list sourced from the literature corpus
LiteratureTeam
gathered. The model section tabulates each parameter’s proposed value next to the harness’s estimate, naming the stand-in simulator where one was used; the estimation section lists the module variables of the executable model that each series was linked to; and the limitations section is compiled from the artifacts: hypotheses that were inconclusive or contradicted, the scope of each calibration verdict, and every series used as a proxy. Where the skeleton calls for prose the pipeline cannot generate deterministically, such as the introduction and discussion, it leaves the slot marked for the economist and attaches a short, bounded reference summary of the relevant artifacts, grounded and numerically checked, for the economist to draw on while writing that section.
Reporter
activates once
ResultsInterpreter
’s magnitudes and figures are available, and again for one bounded revision round after reviewer feedback.

Proofreader
narrates wording and academic-convention suggestions on the checked draft; it has no authority over numbers, citations, or verdicts, and does not itself check them; that is the deterministic consistency check described next.
Proofreader
activates once the number-consistency check has run.

JournalAdvisor
serves as the publication strategy specialist; its shortlist is computed deterministically rather than freely recommended: it scores candidate journals by keyword overlap between the report’s own content (research question, estimated dependent variable) and a small, static, illustrative reference table of journal scopes, explicitly not live database intelligence, so it never claims acceptance rates, impact factors, or current editorial preferences it cannot actually observe. For the top-ranked candidates it narrates an adaptation strategy (framing, emphasis) grounded only in the shortlist and the report’s own content.
JournalAdvisor
activates after the number-consistency check has run.

Formatter
performs a deterministic, no-LLM re-render of the checked report into a specific journal reference style (AEA by default) and appends a Data & Code Availability statement built from real pipeline facts (how many series were retrieved live versus disclosed as simulated; whether a code-implementation section is present). It writes a separate file and leaves the checked original unchanged.
Formatter
activates alongside
JournalAdvisor
, after quality verification.

4.7.2
Workflow and Human Checkpoints

The research dissemination process runs in three stages: each pairs deterministic harness computation with either LLM narration or, where the report calls for argumentative prose, the economist’s own writing. Figure
7
shows the process.

Interpretation & Figures

ResultsInterpreter
converts
EstimationTeam
’s statistical results into economic magnitudes and the accompanying publication figures, narrating what each effect means in economic terms. An inestimable upstream verdict carries through unchanged. This stage runs without a dedicated human checkpoint; its output feeds directly into drafting, where
Reporter
tabulates it alongside the raw estimates and economists review the assembled draft.

Drafting & Tables

Reporter
assembles the report skeleton, tables, and figure placements, and attaches reference summaries for the sections the economist still needs to write. Economists draft the introduction and discussion themselves, informed by those summaries, and review the skeleton’s framing, emphasis, and any missing caveats; substantive feedback triggers one bounded revision round before the draft is finalized. This is the workflow’s HITL checkpoint: the point at which a human economist’s judgment about narrative and emphasis is incorporated before the numbers are locked in for verification.

Quality, Journal Targeting & Formatting

The number-consistency checker looks up the numbers in the draft among the numeric fields of the named upstream artifacts and the numbers the report template derives from them; numbers inside generated prose are not sources. Small integers used as counts, ordinals, or list labels, the confidence levels 90, 95, and 99 printed next to “CI” or “confidence”, and digits inside URLs and DOIs are exempt. For each number, the checker records the artifact it was found in and the kind of match (exact, rounded, same significant figures, or a percentage rescaling), and it annotates the report with a verified/total count; it establishes that a number occurs upstream, not that it is used in the right place.
Proofreader
then narrates wording suggestions on the annotated draft,
JournalAdvisor
shortlists candidate journals and narrates an adaptation strategy, and
Formatter
writes a separately formatted, submission-styled copy. Economists receive the annotated report, the journal shortlist, and the formatted copy together as the pipeline’s final output.

4.8
Cross-Team Pipeline Integration

While each team described above can run on its own, a full study requires them to execute in dependency order: a research question is formulated, the literature is reviewed, a model is specified and reconciled against the data that actually exists, and the reconciled model is then implemented, estimated, and reported. A cross-team orchestrator formalizes this dependency chain into an automated, resumable pipeline over the seven teams.

The orchestrator resolves the team dependencies into an execution order and runs in two phases. A linear pass carries the research topic through the
IdeationTeam
(prioritized research questions), the
LiteratureTeam
(a review, gap analysis, and knowledge graph), the
ModelTeam
(a model specification), and the
DataTeam
(a validated dataset). The three analytic teams are held back until the model and data have been reconciled: the
CodeTeam
turns the specification into an executable model, the
EstimationTeam
produces estimation results on the assembled data, and the
ReportingTeam
assembles the research report; the
CodeTeam
is optional and off by default. When it runs, it precedes the
EstimationTeam
, which reads the lead model’s executable module and records which of its variables each estimated series measures. Each of these outputs is registered as a named artifact in a shared store, alongside the feasibility contracts introduced below. Each artifact is signed with a hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) when it is registered. Before a team starts, the orchestrator retrieves the upstream artifacts it depends on and checks each against the signature and a content fingerprint recorded when it was produced; an artifact that fails the signature check fails the team that reads it, a fingerprint mismatch is logged as a warning, and the run manifest records the number of verified reads and every signature failure.

The Model–Data boundary is a bounded
feasibility cycle
rather than a one-way hand-off. The
ModelTeam
emits the DRS; the
DataTeam
answers with the DAR, grading each requirement and issuing a feasibility verdict; and where the data cannot support the specification, an MRR carries the binding constraints back to the
ModelTeam
for re-specification (Section
4.4
). These three contracts are themselves provenance-stamped artifacts, so the final study records why each variable is a proxy or why the model was narrowed. The cycle is bounded (at most three reconciliations by default), stops when a revision does not shrink the set of unmet essential requirements, and on non-convergence it terminates by proceeding with a documented data limitation rather than failing the run. The cycle’s decisions are not checkpointed, so a resumed run repeats the cycle from its start. Escalation to premium or user-uploaded data is off by default, so the reconciliation turns on the substantive choice between accepting a proxy and revising the model. That choice is made at the Feasibility Review, a human-in-the-loop checkpoint owned by an economist.

Resource use and failures are handled as the pipeline runs. Each team’s cost is metered separately and rolled up into a running total; budget limits, per team and overall, are checked before each team and halt the run if an allocation would be exceeded. After each team completes, its outputs and timing are saved, so the pipeline can be resumed from the last completed team rather than restarted. This matters for long research campaigns, where a failure late in the pipeline should not force re-execution of the earlier, more expensive teams.

LLM selection is configured per stage. The same configuration the standalone workflows use binds a chosen LLM to each team and stage, supporting cost-aware deployment: a stronger reasoning LLM for the
ModelTeam
’s theory stage, a lighter one for routine extraction in the
DataTeam
. The whole sequence runs from a single command, given a research-topic seed, an optional global LLM override, a choice of data workflow (open-source APIs, premium subscriptions, or user-uploaded data), and a resume option for continuing an interrupted run.

The LLM-Economist Committee.

The methodology keeps a real economist at each human-in-the-loop checkpoint the workflow defines. A real economist cannot sit an autonomous batch run, so for this demonstration those decisions were made by an
LLM-Economist committee
standing in for the reviewer: a testing convenience that changes only who answers a checkpoint, never whether it exists. Following the same “no model grades its own work” principle as the dual-judge evaluation, the committee is three open-weight models from distinct families (OpenAI’s gpt-oss, Microsoft’s Phi-4-mini, and AllenAI’s Olmo), none shared with the generating model or the two evaluation judges, each given the decision packet a human reviewer would see together with a shared economist rubric; in the chained runs the Model team’s theory and calibration checkpoints are logged without a vote. Each member additionally reviews through a distinct economist lens (theoretical soundness, empirical realism, policy relevance), and across the 26 runs of Section
6.3.4

Every yes/no proposition is carried on a two-of-three majority, and in a list selection (which questions to merge or discard) a merge or discard takes effect only if two of the three members chose it; a multi-option gate such as the Feasibility Review is resolved as a priority-ordered cascade of such votes with a documented-limitation default that guarantees resolution. Checkpoints that ask for free-text feedback are not voted on: the first member (gpt-oss) consolidates the three members’ comments as chair. Every ballot is logged with each member’s vote and rationale, with its prompt recorded in its first 500 characters, and tagged as committee-resolved, so these runs stay separable from a real reviewer’s calls.

6
Evaluation Framework

Evaluating agentic workflows for economic research presents methodological challenges distinct from standard AI benchmarking. Unlike classification or generation tasks amenable to controlled experiments, research workflows involve extended, context-dependent processes where output quality depends on interactions between system design, domain knowledge, and researcher judgment.
Following established frameworks for evaluating complex socio-technical systems
(
Hollnagel et al.,, 2006
;
Woods,, 2017
)
, we develop an evaluation framework that assesses agentic workflows across twelve dimensions applicable to economic research generally, together with subfield-specific dimensions illustrated here by
Simulation Fidelity
and
Causal Validity
, which cover the optional agent-based simulation and causal-foundation-model stages and are reported as not evaluated when those stages do not run; further such dimensions can be developed as a study requires.
The framework relates architectural choices to research outcomes, and Section
6.3
applies it to workflow executions.

6.1
Theoretical Foundations

Our evaluation framework integrates three theoretical perspectives that inform how workflow design relates to research quality.

Distributed cognition theory
conceptualizes research workflows as systems where cognitive labor is allocated across human and artificial agents
(
Hutchins,, 1995
;
Hollan et al.,, 2000
)
.
This perspective generates specific predictions for workflow evaluation: task decomposition across specialized agents should improve
Reliability
through role-constrained behavior and
Correctness
through domain-focused verification, while coordination overhead creates
Efficiency
trade-offs that depend on the degree of task interdependence and communication protocol design.
The same allocation of labor across modular agents governs
Scalability
, since performance under a growing workload depends on how far components execute independently, and
Innovation Potential
, since exploration distributed over agents with different sources and vantage points is the mechanism through which the architecture pursues the cross-domain recombination that accounts of scientific innovation emphasize
(
Kuhn,, 1962
;
Thagard,, 1992
)
.

Epistemic reliability theory
analyzes workflows as inference chains where each step transforms inputs into outputs for subsequent steps
(
Goldman,, 1999
;
Kitcher,, 1993
)
.
This framework predicts that sequential pipeline stages can either amplify or attenuate errors depending on inter-stage verification mechanisms, directly informing
Soundness
(whether reasoning chains preserve logical validity across stages) and
Robustness
(whether error detection and recovery mechanisms prevent cascading failures).
The same lens covers the domain-specific dimensions that turn on the validity of a single inference step:
Decision Quality
(whether the method chosen at a stage is a reliable process for the question asked),
Economic Rigor
(whether identification and calibration steps are valid in the discipline’s own terms), and
Causal Validity
(whether a causal claim rests on a sound identification strategy).

Scientific norm theory
emphasizes transparency, replicability, and scrutability as essential properties for knowledge production
(
Merton,, 1973
;
Popper,, 1959
)
.
Merton’s norms of communalism and organized skepticism map directly to
Transparency
(making operations inspectable for scrutiny) and
Reproducibility
(enabling independent replication), while the norm of universalism, that claims should be evaluated on merit regardless of source, motivates
Traceability
(preserving provenance so that AI-generated outputs can be verified against their inputs).
Popper’s falsificationism grounds
Simulation Fidelity
: a simulated economy is held to the stylized facts of its empirical counterpart and actively searched for counterexamples.

These theoretical foundations map to twelve dimensions for general economic research, organized into five clusters:
Quality
dimensions (Reliability, Correctness, Soundness) address output dependability;
Operational
dimensions (Efficiency, Scalability, Robustness) address system performance;
Epistemic
dimensions (Transparency, Traceability, Reproducibility) address scientific norms; the
Innovation
dimension addresses generative capability; and the general
Domain-Specific
dimensions (Decision Quality, Economic Rigor) address the appropriateness of methodological choices and the domain validity of reasoning. Within the Domain-Specific cluster, two further, subfield-specific dimensions (
Simulation Fidelity
and
Causal Validity
) illustrate how the framework extends to a particular subfield (here agent-based macroeconomics), capturing whether simulation outputs’ macro-moments match stylized facts and whether causal claims rest on sound identification; additional such dimensions can be developed as a study requires.

6.2
Evaluation Dimensions

The evaluation dimensions, with their definitions, supporting design features, and target metrics, are summarized in Table
11
(Appendix
B
).
The following subsections provide integrated discussion of each cluster.

Quality Dimensions: Reliability, Correctness, and Soundness.

The quality dimensions address the fundamental requirement that workflow outputs meet research standards.

Reliability
concerns consistency of outputs under varying conditions; it is supported through explicit agent specifications that constrain behavior within defined limits, structured communication protocols that standardize information exchange, and version control that tracks system state.

Correctness
addresses accuracy relative to domain standards. It is supported through specialized agents with domain-focused capabilities (e.g.,
TopicCrawler
for literature discovery,
Estimator
for econometric analysis), verification agents that check outputs against specified criteria, and human-in-the-loop checkpoints at critical decision points.

Soundness
concerns logical and methodological validity, and is supported through staged workflows that enforce appropriate sequencing (e.g., theoretical grounding before model specification) and explicit reasoning chains maintained across agent interactions.

These dimensions share common design features: multi-layer verification, explicit error escalation, and human oversight at critical junctures.
However, they face distinct limitations: Reliability (output consistency) is bounded by LLM stochasticity; Correctness is limited by the completeness of verification criteria and the possibility of plausible but incorrect LLM outputs; and Soundness is constrained by the inherent opacity of agent reasoning processes.
The architecture provides multiple layers of quality assurance without guaranteeing error elimination.

Operational Dimensions: Efficiency, Scalability, and Robustness.

The operational dimensions address system performance characteristics essential for practical deployment.

Efficiency
concerns the relationship between resources consumed and outputs produced; it is supported through parallelization capabilities enabling concurrent agent execution, task automation for routine operations, and caching mechanisms for previously computed results.

Scalability
concerns performance maintenance as workload increases. It is supported through modular agent design enabling independent component scaling, distributed execution support, and incremental processing strategies for large datasets.

Robustness
concerns the ability to handle errors and unexpected conditions without catastrophic degradation. It is supported through error escalation pathways that route anomalies to appropriate resolution mechanisms, validation checkpoints that detect invalid inputs early, and fallback mechanisms that enable alternative execution paths when primary components fail.

The operational dimensions involve inherent trade-offs.
Parallelization provides efficiency gains only when tasks are genuinely independent; coordination overhead increases with system complexity.
Scalability in research workflows must be evaluated not only in terms of computational performance but also in terms of maintaining research quality.
Robustness mechanisms may not detect all errors, and graceful degradation may produce outputs of uncertain quality.

Epistemic Dimensions: Transparency, Traceability, and Reproducibility.

The epistemic dimensions directly address scientific norms essential for trustworthy research.

Transparency
refers to the inspectability of system operations; it is supported through explicit agent definitions specifying roles and constraints, logged inter-agent communications, and documented decision points with associated rationales.
Transparency enables real-time or post-hoc inspection of system behavior at multiple levels: architectural (what agents exist), execution (what happened during a run), and decision (why particular choices were made).

Traceability
concerns the ability to track output provenance. It is supported through logging, version control for all artifacts, explicit provenance metadata, and checkpoint preservation enabling workflow replay.
Traceability enables reconstruction of workflow history when outputs are questioned.

Reproducibility
concerns obtaining consistent results when repeating identical procedures, and is supported through deterministic workflow orchestration, configuration documentation, version-controlled dependencies, and automated generation of replication packages.

The epistemic dimensions face bounded completeness.
Transparency is limited by the interpretability of underlying LLM operations; while we can log agent outputs, internal reasoning remains largely opaque.
Traceability depends on logging comprehensiveness; external operations may not be fully captured.
Complete reproducibility faces inherent challenges: LLM outputs exhibit stochasticity, external resources may change, and proprietary model access may vary.

Innovation Dimension.

Innovation Potential
(the generation of novel, valuable ideas) represents a particularly challenging dimension for automated systems.
Theoretical accounts of scientific innovation emphasize analogical reasoning, cross-domain transfer, and creative recombination
(
Kuhn,, 1962
;
Thagard,, 1992
)
.
The architecture includes features potentially relevant to innovation: multi-agent exploration of diverse information sources, explicit gap identification agents (e.g.,
GapFinder
), and cross-domain integration capabilities.

We measure Innovation Potential through a two-tier approach (see Appendix
B.2
).
The Tier-1 structural component captures output diversity (normalized Shannon entropy of item distribution across pipeline stages) and coverage breadth (total item count relative to a reference threshold). These are proxies for the range of exploration, not for novelty per se.
The Tier-2 content quality component uses LLM-as-reviewer assessment against four sub-criteria:
Novelty
(whether outputs identify genuinely under-explored research directions),
Cross-Domain Integration
(whether outputs connect concepts across economic subfields),
Research Significance
(whether identified questions address substantive gaps), and
Specificity and Actionability
.
These sub-criteria operationalize specific facets of innovation potential, but the resulting scores are preliminary indicators that have not been calibrated against human expert judgments or benchmarked against human-generated research outputs.
Without such calibration, the absolute magnitude of innovation scores (e.g., 0.70 vs. 0.45) should not be interpreted as reflecting a known level of innovativeness; only relative comparisons across configurations within the same evaluation framework are meaningful.

We explicitly refrain from claiming that agentic workflows enhance breakthrough innovation.
The design supports systematic exploration and comprehensive coverage, potentially valuable for identifying incremental research opportunities, while recognizing that transformative innovation likely depends on human insight, serendipity, and factors beyond architectural control.

Domain-Specific Cluster.

The four domain-specific dimensions (Decision Quality, Economic Rigor, Simulation Fidelity, and Causal Validity) extend the framework beyond generic AI evaluation to capture economics-discipline requirements.
Decision Quality assesses whether agents select appropriate methodological approaches given the research question (e.g., choosing instrumental variables over OLS when endogeneity is present, or selecting DSGE over reduced-form models when general equilibrium effects matter). This is evaluated through LLM-as-reviewer scoring against sub-criteria for decision coherence, information utilization, and scope management.
Economic Rigor assesses domain-specific validity: whether identification strategies are sound, whether calibration follows established discipline (e.g., matching steady-state ratios, using Bayesian priors from the literature), and whether robustness checks cover standard sensitivity analyses.
Simulation Fidelity applies only when the Model team’s optional Simulation Stage is active; it is the share of the scenario’s benchmark moments that the simulation reproduces within
5\%
of the target, or within
0.05
when the target is smaller than one in absolute value (Appendix
B.2
); impulse-response coverage remains a target metric and the Falsifier cross-check a design feature that is not yet scored (Table
11
).
Causal Validity applies when
CausalFMClient
emits a
causal_estimate
artifact; it compares the estimate with a known effect or, failing that, a prior mean (Appendix
B.2
); the soundness of the identification strategy (back-door, front-door, or instrumental-variable strategies), the divergence between the zero-shot and classical estimates, and the sensitivity to unobserved confounding remain target metrics (Table
11
).
Decision Quality and Economic Rigor are scored exclusively through Tier-2 LLM-as-reviewer assessment; Simulation Fidelity and Causal Validity are computed structurally from the simulation output and the causal estimate (Appendix
B.2
); no LLM judge scores them.
Absolute values on Simulation Fidelity and Causal Validity have not yet been calibrated against human-expert judgment and should be interpreted relatively across configurations rather than as standalone grades.

Agent-Level Metrics.

In addition to the fourteen workflow-level dimensions, the code defines four Tier-1 diagnostics, computed from per-call observability records without further LLM calls:

Tool correctness
combines whether a team called the tools expected for its stages, avoided the tools declared inappropriate for it, and had its calls succeed;

Step efficiency
combines the number of LLM calls against an expected range per stage, the share of output tokens, and the balance of stage durations;

Plan adherence
combines stage completion, stage ordering, and the presence of each stage’s expected output;
and
Argument correctness
is the share of individual argument checks passed (required arguments present and non-empty, typed arguments of the declared type).
They locate execution problems within a stage; they do not assess the quality of reasoning (Appendix
B.2
). The execution logs of the runs reported here store aggregated rather than per-call observability, so these diagnostics were not computed for them.

6.3.1
Two-Tier Evaluation

Evaluating workflow quality requires assessment at two distinct levels: (i)
structural compliance
: whether the system executes correctly, produces expected outputs, and maintains execution traces; and (ii)
content quality
: whether the outputs are factually accurate, logically sound, and scientifically valuable.
Structural properties can be measured automatically from execution logs, but content quality inherently requires domain judgment.
Since human expert evaluation at scale is infeasible prior to peer review, we employ an
LLM-as-reviewer
approach
(
Zheng et al.,, 2023
)
as a systematic proxy for expert assessment.

Tier 1: Structural Assessment.

We analyze the execution log of the seven-stage chained run.
The workflows are instrumented using the shared
WorkflowLogger
module, which captures per-stage timing information, item counts, error occurrences, and output file tracking.
A companion
MetricsCollector
observability layer records every LLM API call (model, token counts, latency, cost) and external tool invocation (HTTP requests, database queries, search-API calls) with per-agent and per-stage attribution.
The orchestrator hands the same collector to every agent it creates, and the collector writes an
observability
section into each execution log that records resource use by agent and stage without changes to the agents’ own code.
Each of the seven stages generates a structured
execution_log.json
file containing execution metadata, and the pipeline-level log tracks the chain across stages.
Tier-1 metrics assess six dimensions automatically: Reliability (stage completion rate, output file coverage, and error penalty), Efficiency (normalized execution time), Scalability (throughput relative to a reference rate), Robustness (error recovery rate), Traceability (provenance metadata completeness), and Reproducibility (configuration documentation coverage).
Importantly, these structural metrics measure
design-level compliance
(whether the pipeline executes as specified) rather than empirical properties that require repeated trials (e.g., output consistency across runs for Reliability) or external validation (e.g., expert-rated correctness).
Across the 26 runs, stage completion, stage ordering, output coverage, provenance completeness, and the presence of configuration metadata are all
1.0
, with no stage error and no failed artifact-signature check (996 verified reads); Table
3
reports these values with execution time and efficiency.

Tier 2: Content Quality via LLM-as-Reviewer Ensemble.

For six dimensions that require domain judgment (Correctness, Soundness, Innovation Potential, Transparency, Decision Quality, and Economic Rigor), we employ a multi-model ensemble evaluation.
Each workflow’s actual output files (research questions, literature reviews, theoretical frameworks, model specifications, data requirements) are evaluated against rubrics with 3–4 sub-criteria per dimension, scored on a 1–5 Likert scale; the Correctness, Soundness, and Economic Rigor rubrics are team-specific.
To mitigate evaluator stochasticity identified in preliminary single-pass analyses, we use a two-judge ensemble of open-weight reviewers drawn from different model families, Mistral-Small-3.2-24B and Gemma-3-27B, each at temperature
=0.0
with structured output in JavaScript Object Notation (JSON).
Both reviewers are distinct from the Qwen3.6-27B-FP8 model that produced the workflow outputs, and neither sits on the checkpoint committee (Section
5
), so no model grades its own work or its own checkpoint decisions.
Each judge’s scores are reported separately; where a single figure is given (Table
6
), it is the arithmetic mean of the two judges’ normalized dimension scores.
This design keeps the entire evaluation on local open-weight inference and surfaces between-judge scoring bias: Gemma scores systematically higher than Mistral on the chained run’s reasoning stages. This is why we report both judges rather than a single conflated figure.
Sub-criterion scores are averaged and normalized to a 0–1 scale.
The reported score for each of these six dimensions is its Tier-2 score. Tier 1 also computes a structural proxy for each of them; in each of the 26 runs five of the six proxies equal
1.0
and the sixth (Innovation Potential) equals
0.584
, so they are not blended in by default.
Exact formulae for all metric computations are provided in Appendix
B.2
.

This two-tier approach addresses a fundamental limitation: structural metrics (stage completion rates, file existence) confirm that a workflow ran successfully but cannot assess whether its outputs are correct, logically sound, or novel.
Using open-weight reviewers from families distinct from the subject model (Mistral and Gemma vs. Qwen) as the Tier-2 evaluators provides a degree of evaluator independence.
Reporting both judges makes between-judge disagreement visible, which a single reviewer would conceal; the inter-evaluator analysis of Section
6.3.4
quantifies this agreement per stage.
However, LLM-as-reviewer evaluation serves as a systematic proxy for, not a replacement of, human expert review, and does not by itself constitute the independent external validation that would require domain experts, standardized benchmarks, or blind evaluation protocols; three co-author raters completed the preliminary expert check reported in Section
6.3.4
, and independent external validation remains outstanding (see Section
6.5
).

6.3.4

To probe the stability of the primary results, we conducted three supplementary analyses: a multi-run study, a multi-evaluator agreement assessment, and a human-expert validation of the LLM-as-reviewer scores.
All three are preliminary and limited in scope; they are reported here to characterize the evaluation infrastructure and identify directions for future work, not to establish definitive conclusions.

Pipeline Outcomes Across Runs.

reports what the deterministic components decided in each of the 26 runs, stage by stage. 6.4
Extended Evaluation Capabilities

Beyond the fourteen-dimension framework and the two-judge evaluator ensemble, AEL provides additional evaluation infrastructure.

Enhanced Observability.

The runtime observability layer has been extended beyond the
WorkflowLogger
and
MetricsCollector
described above.
All LLM calls, tool invocations, and API requests are recorded with per-agent attribution and can be exported in the OpenTelemetry tracing format, with 12 model providers recognized.
The
evaluation_observability
key in result JSONs captures evaluation-specific metrics including evaluator model, temperature, token counts, and per-dimension latency, enabling meta-evaluation of the evaluation process itself.

Princeton Reliability Dimensions.

Complementing the fourteen primary dimensions, AEL implements a four-dimension reliability framework
(
Rabanser et al.,, 2026
)
:
consistency
(variance of quality scores across
K
runs of the same input),
robustness
(quality maintenance under prompt paraphrasing),
predictability
(correlation between agent confidence and actual quality), and
safety
(absence of hallucinations, harmful content, and factual errors).
These dimensions provide complementary reliability diagnostics for deployment decisions.

Judge Calibration.

A
JudgeCalibratorGLM
module can calibrate LLM-as-reviewer scores against a reference dataset of human-rated outputs using generalized linear models, producing calibrated probability estimates in place of raw Likert scores, as a response to the systematic biases documented in LLM evaluation
(
Zheng et al.,, 2023
)
.
It requires a human-rated reference set larger than the one available here, so the scores in this paper are uncalibrated and its effect on bias was not evaluated.

6.5
Limitations and Future Directions

This evaluation framework has important limitations.
First, the Tier-2 LLM-as-reviewer approach is a proxy for human expert evaluation rather than a substitute.
LLM evaluators exhibit known biases including verbosity preference, position effects, and limited ability to verify factual claims against ground truth
(
Zheng et al.,, 2023
)
.
The evaluation uses a two-judge ensemble from different model families (Mistral-Small-3.2-24B and Gemma-3-27B), deterministic settings (temperature
=0.0
), structured rubrics with explicit scoring criteria, and multi-criterion averaging.
The ensemble partially addresses between-model bias but does not eliminate systematic biases.
Future work should validate LLM-based scores against human expert panels to establish calibration.
Second, the analysis assumes design features operate as intended; implementation gaps and emergent behaviors may cause actual performance to diverge from specifications.
Third, the value of different dimensions varies across research contexts: reproducibility may be paramount for empirical research but less critical for theoretical exploration.
Fourth, LLM capabilities evolve rapidly; design features appropriate for current capabilities may become unnecessary or insufficient as underlying technologies develop.
Fifth, the Tier-1 structural metrics for Reliability, Robustness, Traceability, and Reproducibility confirm design-level compliance (that stages complete, outputs are produced, errors are recovered, provenance is recorded, and configurations are documented) but do not empirically demonstrate the full scope of these dimensions.
In particular, Reliability (as defined in Table
11
) encompasses output consistency under varying conditions, which would require repeated executions with identical inputs and measurement of output similarity; our structural metric captures only the necessary condition of successful execution, not the sufficient condition of consistent outputs.
The multi-run study (
n=26
, Section
6.3.4
) measures structural consistency at the pipeline level and reports the distribution of the deterministic outcomes; it does not measure whether repeated runs reach the same research question, model, or estimate. With the same seed topic, the final research questions differed across runs (24 distinct leading questions in 26 runs), because the agents sample at temperatures between 0.2 and 0.7 without a fixed sampling seed, the literature and trend searches are run live, and committee votes vary; the downstream models, data, and estimates differ accordingly (Table
4
).
Content quality scores use a two-judge ensemble to reduce scoring variance; Table
5
reports per-stage means with confidence intervals across the 25 replicates, which describe the precision of each mean across runs, not whether the runs reach the same content.
Similarly, Reproducibility is assessed through the presence of configuration metadata rather than actual replication studies.
Sixth, 26 runs of one configuration on one seed topic remain a small sample for confirmatory analysis.
The Tier-2 content quality scores use a two-judge ensemble, which reduces but does not eliminate scoring stochasticity.
Small between-judge differences may fall within the natural variation of LLM evaluation rather than reflecting a genuine dimension effect.
Consequently, the empirical analysis should be interpreted as an exploratory characterization rather than a confirmatory study.
Seventh, the evaluation is
self-referential
: the same research team designed both the workflows and the evaluation framework, and the evaluation was conducted on the team’s own system without large-scale independent external validation.
While the Tier-2 LLM-as-reviewer introduces partial evaluator independence (the open-weight judge models were involved neither in generating workflow outputs nor in the checkpoint committee, and belong to different model families than both), this does not substitute for the rigorous external validation protocols recommended in the evaluation methodology literature, including blind evaluation, independent expert panels, and comparison against standardized benchmarks with known ground-truth answers.
The inter-evaluator analysis (Section
6.3.4
, Table
5
) characterizes agreement across stages; absolute score magnitudes are evaluator-dependent and should be interpreted cautiously.
The small-sample human-expert check described there has been run (Table
6
); its raters are co-authors, so it is a preliminary domain-expert check, not the blind, full-scale external validation described above, which remains outstanding.
The absence of large-scale human expert validation means that systematic biases in framework design (e.g., dimensions defined to favor the system’s strengths) or in rubric construction (e.g., criteria that the system is designed to satisfy) cannot be ruled out.

Future work should pursue several directions:
(i)
external validation
: independent expert panels conducting blind evaluation of workflow outputs to validate both the LLM-based scores and the evaluation framework itself (the preliminary multi-evaluator study and the small-sample human-expert validation in Section
6.3.4
are first steps toward evaluator triangulation, but scaling the expert panel to a blind, full-sample study remains essential);
(ii)
comparative benchmarking
: evaluating the workflows against prompt-based human–LLM interaction and traditional manual research on identical tasks, with multiple executions per condition, to characterize where multi-agent orchestration adds the most value;
(iii)
larger-scale multi-run studies
: the 26-run study in Section
6.3.4
covers one configuration and one seed topic; runs across several seed topics and configurations, with significance tests between them, remain outstanding;
(iv)
standardized benchmarks
: developing task suites with known ground-truth answers (e.g., literature review tasks with pre-established corpora) to measure absolute rather than relative performance;
(v) longitudinal studies tracking workflow performance in production research settings;
(vi) development of automated evaluation tools for real-time quality monitoring;
(vii) investigation of how evaluation dimension trade-offs vary across economic research subfields;
and (viii) calibration of the two subfield-specific dimensions (Simulation Fidelity and Causal Validity) against human-expert judgment, since absolute magnitudes on these dimensions have not been validated against domain-expert ratings and should be interpreted only as within-framework relative indicators.

7
Conclusion

This paper has presented a methodology for agentic workflows in economic research and an implementation of it, the Agentic Econ Lab (AEL). Seven teams, from ideation to reporting, run in the order of the methodological dependencies of economic research under a cross-team orchestrator. A bounded feasibility cycle connects the Model and Data teams, and the design places economist review at checkpoints in every stage. Every LLM used in the reported evaluation, including the workflow, committee, and judge models, was served locally on open weights.

All 26 executions of the chained pipeline completed the seven stages. Completion, ordering, and provenance metrics were
1.0
in every run, and mean wall-clock time was
5.38
hours (standard deviation
1.10
). An LLM-Economist committee provided checkpoint review in place of economists; the Model team’s theory and calibration checkpoints were logged without a vote. The harnesses declared 43 of the 78 candidate models uncalibratable and refused 3 of the 26 estimation specifications. Of the 35 theory-derived hypotheses tested, 24 were inconclusive. An offline recheck with the corrected number-consistency checker found at least one unmatched, non-exempt number in 12 of the 26 reports.

The two-tier, fourteen-dimension evaluation combined execution-log metrics with Tier-2 content scores from two LLM judges. In a preliminary human check, three raters scored a 28-item sample drawn from the 26 runs at a pooled mean of
0.440
, compared with
0.645
for the LLM ensemble. The ensemble scored higher in all seven stages; the pooled Spearman correlation was
\rho=0.46
over 168 item–dimension pairs. Three limits apply to these results (Section
6.5
): the runs share one configuration and one seed topic, the judges are themselves LLMs, and all three human raters are co-authors rather than independent external economists.

The reported workflow automates the production of specifications, datasets, estimates, and reports, while recording refusals and unresolved limitations for economist review. These runs do not measure how human economists would revise the outputs or how much of their work the workflow would replace.

Future research should pursue six directions:
(i) blind validation of the Tier-2 scores by an independent panel of external economists, which would also allow the judge-calibration step to be fit on human-rated data;
(ii) calibration of the Simulation Fidelity and Causal Validity dimensions against expert judgment, so that their absolute magnitudes can be interpreted rather than only compared within the framework;
(iii) comparative benchmarks against prompt-based use of LLMs and manual research on identical tasks, together with task suites that have known ground-truth answers;
(iv) adaptive orchestration that selects between sequential, parallel, and hierarchical team execution according to task dependencies and resource constraints;
(v) application of the workflow in other fields of economics, such as public policy evaluation, financial markets, and development economics;
and (vi) bringing the inputs that economics already measures through purpose-built pipelines, such as satellite imagery, audio, video, and large document collections, into the agentic workflow.

The contribution claimed here is workflow-structural: the framework organizes and records the production of a first research prototype. Domain experts remain responsible for assessing the substantive validity of its models and interpretations.
The use we designed it for is entry into an unfamiliar field: a researcher crossing a boundary, whether between subfields of economics, between economics and the other social sciences, or into interdisciplinary work, begins from a prototype whose sources, calibration verdict, and provenance are recorded.
