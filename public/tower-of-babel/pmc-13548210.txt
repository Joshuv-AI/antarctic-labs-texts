Perspectives of practitioners providing weight management support for young adults with obesity in an urban setting in Sri Lanka: a qualitative study
Authors: Gayani Priyangika Gamage, Priyadarshika Hettiarachchi, Thamara Dilhani Amarasekara, Ranil Jayawardena, Sudharshani Wasalathanthri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study explored perspectives of practitioners providing weight management support for urban-dwelling young adults (18–35 years) with obesity in Sri Lanka, addressing an important evidence gap in understanding obesity management from a provider perspective.Study designThis descriptive qualitative study employed face-to-face semi-structured interviews, and the data were analysed using a framework approach within thematic analysis.Study setting and participantsEight purposively selected practitioners involved in weight management in the Colombo District, Sri Lanka, were interviewed. Participants represented key domains of comprehensive obesity management, including dietary, physical activity, behaviour and medical management. Recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved.ResultsTwo key themes emerged from the data: (1) Barriers to effective weight management and (2) Strategies to improve weight management. Within the first theme, five subthemes emerged, including limited knowledge and confidence among practitioners, provider attitudes towards clients, resource and service limitations, client motivation and adherence challenges and cultural and religious influences and myths. The second theme comprised four subthemes: early detection with a proper referral system, comprehensive and individualised care approach, continuous follow-up and self-monitoring and supportive social environment.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that obesity management is hindered by fragmented care, with provider, client and system-level factors constraining practitioners’ weight management efforts and limiting opportunities for improved care. These findings suggest the potential value of strengthening interdisciplinary referral pathways and targeted training to support more coordinated care.Keywords: Health Workforce, Obesity, PUBLIC HEALTH, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Obesity has become a global public health concern, with its prevalence continuing to rise across all age groups and regions.1 It is characterised by excessive body fat accumulation that impairs health and is considered a major risk factor for non-communicable diseases such as type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases and certain cancers.2 In recent years, obesity has increased markedly among younger populations, a group once considered less susceptible to obesity-related morbidity.1 This population is particularly vulnerable due to a combination of lifestyle transitions, including increased consumption of high-calorie, low-nutrient foods, sedentary behaviours and psychosocial stressors associated with academic, occupational and social changes.3 4 Furthermore, young adulthood often represents a period of reduced health-seeking behaviour and limited access to preventive health services, which may exacerbate weight gain and hinder early intervention.3 Given these growing risks among young adults, the role of practitioners involved in weight management becomes crucial in promoting early intervention and long-term weight management.

Practitioners involved in weight management play an important role in the prevention, early detection and management of obesity.5 Their knowledge, attitudes and communication skills can greatly influence client motivation, engagement and adherence to weight management strategies.6 7 Evidence suggests that empathetic, non-judgmental counselling can improve client engagement and outcomes.8 Conversely, negative attitudes and limited competence in obesity management may contribute to missed opportunities for intervention.8 Given the multifactorial nature of obesity, practitioners from different disciplines can support client-centred weight management approaches that integrate lifestyle modification, behavioural therapy and, where appropriate, pharmacological or surgical treatment options.9 10 Continuous professional education and institutional support are important to strengthen practitioners’ capacity to address the complex determinants of obesity.11 However, the effectiveness of such approaches is influenced by local health system capacities and sociocultural contexts.7 These factors may differ considerably between resource-limited settings such as Sri Lanka and high-income countries where most existing evidence has been produced.

In Sri Lanka, obesity has emerged as a growing public health concern, reflecting broader global trends.12 National surveys indicate a steady increase in overweight and obesity among both urban and rural populations, with particularly high rates among young adults.13 In this study, young adults are defined as individuals aged 18–35 years, consistent with previous literature.3 14 Rapid urbanisation, increasing consumption of energy-dense foods and declining physical activity have contributed to the rising trend of obesity in this population.15 Additionally, cultural perceptions that associate larger body size with social status may hinder recognition of obesity as a health concern.16 Recent studies among Sri Lankan young adults have highlighted critical gaps in obesity-related knowledge, perceptions and behaviours.17 18 However, limited research has explored the perspectives of practitioners involved in weight management. Understanding their perspectives may guide the development of targeted educational programmes, strengthen clinical policies and support the design of culturally and contextually appropriate interventions for young adults. This is particularly important in Sri Lanka, where weight management services remain fragmented and comprehensive programmes are limited. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the perspectives and experiences of practitioners involved in weight management of young adults with obesity in an urban setting in Sri Lanka.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design, which allowed researchers to stay close to the data and describe a phenomenon that is not well understood.19 Its reporting reflects the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines20 (online supplemental file 1).

Participants were practitioners from different disciplines involved in supporting weight management among young adults with obesity in the Colombo District, Sri Lanka. The Colombo District is the most populated district in the country and has the highest reported prevalence of obesity (31.2% by Asian Body Mass Index (BMI) criteria).12 Practitioners working in this urban setting were therefore expected to have substantial experience in supporting adults with obesity. In Sri Lanka, weight management services are largely fragmented, with limited availability of comprehensive multidisciplinary programmes. Existing services primarily focus on dietary management of individuals with obesity related comorbidities, such as diabetes, rather than those without comorbidities.

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling with maximum variation21 to ensure representation from key domains of comprehensive obesity management, including dietary, physical activity, behaviour change and medical management. Potential participants were identified through professional contacts within public and private hospitals in the Colombo District and referrals from practitioners involved in obesity management. A total of 12 eligible practitioners were invited via email or telephone; 10 consented to participate and two did not respond. Eligible participants had at least 2 years of experience in practice and were practising in the Colombo District, Sri Lanka. Sample size was determined by data saturation, which was reached through iterative review of the transcripts and coding, with no new concepts or perspectives emerging.22

Data collection

A semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental file 2), developed based on a review of relevant literature and refined after pilot testing with two practitioners, was used to ensure consistency across interviews. Open-ended questions and probes were used to facilitate in-depth discussion of participants’ experiences in managing young adults with obesity. A demographic and professional information sheet was also used to collect details, including age, sex, area of specialisation and years of experience before each interview.

Data collection was conducted between February and March 2022. Eight individual, face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted by the first author (GPG), who was trained in qualitative interviewing techniques. A trained note-taker documented relevant verbal and non-verbal expressions. Each interview lasted approximately 30–40 min and was conducted in a comfortable private space at the participant’s workplace to minimise disruptions.

Data collection and coding occurred in parallel, allowing emerging patterns to be identified early. The first author and the second author, who is an expert in qualitative research, undertook the coding. As no new concepts or perspectives appeared in the later transcripts, the team agreed that a sample of eight participants adequately represented the perspectives needed to address the study aim.

Data analysis

All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author. Data analysis was guided by the Framework approach as outlined by Gale et al.23 All transcripts were checked for accuracy by reading them while listening to the audio recordings. After familiarisation with the transcripts, the two coders (GPG and TDA) independently coded transcripts to minimise potential bias. Codes were generated inductively from transcripts and compared across participants to identify patterns of similarity and difference. Coding decisions, including both common and unique code categories, were discussed with the team, and differences were compared and resolved. The coding framework was refined iteratively until no new codes were identified. Following coding, data were extracted from the transcripts and organised verbatim in a Microsoft Excel matrix, with each row representing an interview and each column representing a code. The matrix was reviewed, and related codes were clustered into subthemes, which were further developed into themes.

Validity, rigour and reflexivity

Several strategies were used to enhance rigour and trustworthiness. Dependability was supported by keeping detailed analytic notes and documenting key decisions throughout the analysis. Credibility was strengthened through the use of a pilot-tested interview guide and interviews conducted by a trained interviewer. Investigator bias was minimised by involving two researchers experienced in qualitative analysis who independently coded the transcripts. Regular team meetings were held to refine codes, sub-themes and themes, contributing to the credibility, dependability and confirmability of the findings. Confirmability was further enhanced by summarising key points at the end of each interview and asking participants to verify the accuracy of the summary. Participants generally agreed with the summaries provided and no substantial corrections or changes were suggested. Transferability was supported through clear reporting of the study context and adherence to the SRQR checklist (online supplemental file 1).

Reflexivity was maintained through ongoing reflection on how the researchers’ backgrounds and assumptions shaped data collection and analysis. Interviews were conducted by the first author, a female nursing academic in a recognised university in Sri Lanka, with training in qualitative research methods. The interviewer had no direct relationship with the participants. Independent coding and team discussions ensured that interpretations stayed grounded in participants’ accounts.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Of the eight practitioners interviewed, two each were in the field of Nutrition and Clinical Psychology and one each in the fields of Sports Medicine, Physiotherapy, Endocrinology and Yoga. The years of experience of the practitioners varied from 2 to 23 years. Most of the participants (n=6) were women.

Emerging themes related to practitioners’ perspectives on weight management of young adults

Two key themes emerged from the analysis: (1) Barriers to effective weight management and (2) Strategies to improve weight management. Although individual interviews did not capture all subthemes, the identified themes emerged repeatedly across multiple participant narratives and across participants representing different domains of comprehensive weight management. Themes and subthemes identified based on the perspectives of practitioners

Theme 1: barriers to effective weight management

This theme captured the challenges faced by providers themselves, their clients and the broader healthcare system and is discussed across five sub-themes.

Limited knowledge and confidence among practitioners

Participants frequently reported gaps in cross-disciplinary knowledge that limited their confidence in delivering comprehensive obesity care. While many felt competent within their own specialty, they were less certain when addressing other essential components, such as exercise prescription or behavioural counselling. For example, nutritionists acknowledged uncertainty around physical activity recommendations but still encouraged clients to incorporate some form of exercise. Psychologists similarly described limited knowledge of exercise and dietary guidance, relying on referrals where needed:

I focus on dietary guidance, and am less confident in prescribing exercises (P2, Nutritionist 2)

I am not familiar with exercise prescriptions, so I advise walking and refer to a dietitian when needed. (P7, Clinical psychologist 2)

This lack of confidence led some practitioners to rely heavily on their own discipline and sometimes offer inconsistent or overly general advice. While nutritionists described structured dietary assessment processes, they recognised insufficient emphasis on behaviour change strategies:

We are not discussing behaviour change as a separate topic… it would be good if we could focus on it properly. (P1, Nutritionist 1)

Participants also highlighted the importance of building client trust, noting that obesity management requires a sustained therapeutic relationship.

If the clients see we are not confident, they lose trust in us. (P3, Sports Medicine Expert)

It takes time for clients to open up, but once trust is established, we can work towards a shared goal. (P1, Nutritionist 1)

These findings highlight that limited cross-disciplinary knowledge and confidence among practitioners can restrict the delivery of comprehensive obesity care and weaken the therapeutic relationship that is essential for sustained behaviour change.

Provider attitudes towards clients

Practitioners expressed a range of attitudes towards clients with obesity, reflecting contrasting views on motivation, capacity for lifestyle change and responsibility in weight management. Some participants viewed clients as lacking willpower, attributing limited engagement in healthy behaviours to laziness rather than real constraints:

I think they are lazy, even though they talk about time limitations. Finding time for exercise is not the real problem (P2, Nutritionist 2)

In contrast, others recognised the substantial socio-economic and daily life challenges that hinder clients’ adherence to recommended lifestyle changes:

Healthy food is often too expensive, and many of my clients can’t afford. (P1, Nutritionist 1)

Some clinicians further emphasised the practical limitations clients face in maintaining exercise routines:

We need to recognise that exercise plans may fail because clients face many day-to-day challenges (P3, Sports Medicine expert)

These different viewpoints also extended to how practitioners perceive responsibility in weight management. Some emphasised client autonomy, viewing weight reduction primarily as the individual’s responsibility:

We can advise patients, but weight loss ultimately depends on their determination and effort. (P1, Nutritionist 1)

Others stressed the providers’ role, asserting that structured guidance and consistent support are essential for successful outcomes:

Clinicians carry a major responsibility; with proper guidance, clients tend to follow well. (P3, Sports Medicine expert)

These contrasting attitudes illustrate the complexity of provider perceptions and how they influence approaches to client support and shared responsibility in obesity care.

Resource and service limitations

Participants highlighted several healthcare system-related barriers that hinder effective obesity management, including overcrowded clinics, limited staff, insufficient consultation time and limited follow-up opportunities. They also pointed out the absence of supportive systems for the clients.

The clinic space is very small… it gets crowded, and we have to send clients out quickly. (P 1, Nutritionist 1)

A lack of multidisciplinary collaboration, particularly the limited recognition and integration of psychological services, was also noted.

Our health system still does not formally recognise psychological services; we are confined to the clinical setting. (P 6, Clinical Psychologist 1)

Practitioners further emphasised the need for community-based support mechanisms to assist clients in maintaining long-term lifestyle changes:

A social support group to help clients work towards their goals would be valuable, but we do not have such a link. (P 1, Nutritionist 1)

Overall, these system-level constraints highlight the need for better infrastructure, multidisciplinary integration and community support networks to strengthen obesity care delivery.

Client motivation and adherence challenges

Participants consistently reported low motivation and poor adherence to treatment plans. Many clients were perceived as seeking advice for short-term or appearance-related reasons rather than for long-term health benefits.

Young adults, particularly females, are often focused on their appearance. (P3, expert in Sports Medicine)

Many clients come for other illnesses rather than obesity itself. (P2, Nutritionist 2)

Poor compliance with treatment recommendations was frequently highlighted.

Very few patients bring the diet records. Only about 10% follow such requests (P2, Nutritionist 2).

A tendency to disengage from care as monitoring increased was also noted:

After two or three sessions, if they feel we are monitoring them closely, they skip appointments and eventually stop coming. (P7, Clinical Psychologist 2).

Participants further emphasised the impact of external pressures and competing priorities on clients’ motivation and adherence to lifestyle changes:

With busy lives and competing commitments, many people report having little time for exercise and rarely choose active transport (P 3, Sports Medicine expert)

Taken together, these insights illustrate how low motivation, poor adherence and competing life demands significantly hinder consistent engagement in obesity management.

Cultural and religious influences and myths

Participants described several cultural and religious practices that posed challenges to obesity management. They emphasised that cultural eating patterns, fasting rituals and long-standing beliefs about health often limit clients’ ability to adopt healthier routines.

Some practitioners highlighted how religious practices influenced eating behaviours:

During fasting periods, Muslims often take very heavy meals when they break the fast, apart from high physical inactivity. (P2, Nutritionist 2)

Hindus are mostly vegetarian, so alternative protein sources need to be identified. (P1, Nutritionist 1).

Traditional Sri Lankan food habits were also seen as contributing to excessive calorie intake:

Sri Lankan people are used to eating a large plate of rice, a habit rooted in the physically demanding work of the past, even though such strenuous activity is no longer common today. (P7, Clinical Psychologist 2)

Cultural perceptions of health further complicated interventions, especially parental feeding practices:

Parents overfeed their children because a fuller appearance is considered healthy (P4, Endocrinologist).

Participants also noted that misconceptions during pregnancy and breastfeeding hinder weight management efforts among young adults.

Many women believe they must ‘eat for two’ during pregnancy and think that reducing food intake while breastfeeding will affect milk supply. (P6, Clinical Psychologist 1)

Taken together, these cultural influences and long-held beliefs influence clients’ engagement in effective dietary behaviours, creating barriers that require culturally sensitive strategies in obesity care.

Theme 2: strategies to improve weight management

The participants proposed several strategies to strengthen obesity management among young adults. These strategies spanned over four sub-themes.

Early detection with a proper referral system

Participants emphasised the importance of identifying obesity early and creating structured referral pathways to facilitate timely intervention. Routine BMI monitoring in both community and school settings was strongly recommended:

It is better if all adults can be monitored for their BMI routinely at the community level. (P2, Nutritionist 2)

Obesity should be detected at a young age… BMI of schoolchildren should be monitored annually, and parents informed. (P6, Clinical Psychologist 1)

These insights reflect a shared view that early identification and systematic referral can improve engagement and outcomes in weight management.

Comprehensive and individualised care approach

Participants stressed the need for comprehensive, multidisciplinary programmes that integrate dietary, physical activity and behavioural components. They highlighted the absence of such coordinated approaches in current services:

For successful weight management, it is important to focus on three components: diet, exercise, and a proper mindset. (P3, Sports Medicine expert)

Personalisation was seen as central to success, with several participants advocating for ongoing behavioural assessment and use of psychological models, such as the Transtheoretical Model, to tailor interventions:

Weight management is very individualistic, and we must identify our clients’ needs and cater to them accordingly. (P3, Sports Medicine expert)

We should vary the plan depending on each person’s stage of change. (P8, Physiotherapist)

Together, these perspectives underline the necessity of integrated, client-centred programmes that address multiple dimensions of obesity.

Continuous follow-up and self-monitoring

Regular follow-up and consistent self-monitoring were viewed as essential for maintaining adherence and preventing relapse. Participants proposed setting short-term goals within a long-term plan and offering flexible follow-up options:

Clients should be monitored at short intervals in the initial period… it doesn’t always need to be face-to-face. (P3, Sports Medicine expert)

Additionally, identifying the barriers was also emphasised as an integral part of effective follow-up:

During follow-up, we should assess whether patients face barriers when adhering to the plan and offer alternatives when needed. (P7, Clinical Psychologist 2)

Self-tracking tools were also considered powerful motivators:

I ask clients to keep a journal of eating habits, emotions, achievements, and failures… it helps them recognise when they drift from the plan. (P5, Yoga practitioner)

These views illustrate the importance of structured monitoring in supporting sustained behaviour change.

Supportive social environment

Participants emphasised the crucial role of family, peers and broader community structures in supporting lifestyle change. Support mechanisms such as exercise partners and family involvement were frequently recommended.

If we buddy two people with similar needs, it can greatly support both to continue. (P6, Clinical Psychologist 1)

I encourage monitoring by a family member between sessions; having someone interested helps clients stay consistent. (P7, Clinical Psychologist 2)

Policy-level actions were also viewed as essential for enabling healthier environments:

Policy-level changes are important, such as educating parents through schools, regulating food advertisements, and controlling canteen food. (P4, Endocrinologist)

Taken together, these reflections highlight the need for multi-layered support that extends beyond clinical settings to strengthen long-term engagement in healthy behaviours.

Discussion

This study identified multiple barriers that the participants perceived as undermining their ability to effectively manage weight among young adults with obesity. In addition, participants articulated a range of strategies aimed at improving weight management practices. Together, these findings highlight the multifaceted and complex nature of delivering effective obesity care within this population.

Barriers to effective weight management

A key finding of this study is that barriers to weight management occur across provider, client and system levels. Participants highlighted limited knowledge, low client motivation and resource constraints as major challenges, consistent with the ecological model of health behaviour, which emphasises multi-level influences on health outcomes.24 Young adults similarly reported intrapersonal difficulties with self-control and emotional regulation, limited social support and structural barriers such as time constraints and lack of safe spaces for physical activity.18 Together, these findings suggest that weight management efforts in this context may benefit from approaches that address multiple-level determinants rather than focus solely on individual behaviour change.

Practitioners in our study demonstrated strong discipline-specific expertise but reported limited confidence when working beyond their primary scope, echoing evidence of widespread gaps in obesity-related competencies across professional groups.25 Such limitations may contribute to fragmented care and reduce comprehensiveness of weight management support.7 Consistent with this, previous research indicates that young adults often have variable knowledge about obesity and its health risks,17 suggesting that both providers and clients may experience knowledge gaps. While some participants demonstrate empathy and understanding, others expressed frustration or attributed obesity to poor willpower, reflecting persistent weight stigma in healthcare settings.7 26 Such bias can undermine trust and adherence.7 Previous studies similarly reported that young adults with obesity often feel judged or blamed in healthcare encounters, leading to avoidance of care and reduced openness in discussions about weight.18 These findings suggest that addressing stigma is an important component of effective obesity care. Incorporating structured anti-stigma and empathy-focused training into professional education may help reduce bias and support more constructive client-provider relationships.

Practitioners frequently perceive low motivation and poor adherence to weight management practices among young adults. These perceptions align with evidence suggesting that motivation and self-efficacy are important factors for long-term weight management.27 A Sri Lankan cross-sectional study further highlighted that cultural factors such as religious fasting practices, traditional diets and body size norms influence both obesity risk and provision of care.16 Taken together, these findings highlight culturally sensitive, theory-informed interventions that incorporate readiness-to-change frameworks may be particularly relevant in this population.

Participants also highlighted structural barriers such as overcrowded clinics, inadequate staffing and limited access to multidisciplinary services. These concerns are consistent with previous reviews describing organisational challenges in obesity care.7 In low- and middle-income settings, obesity often remains under-prioritised relative to other chronic conditions.16 Strengthening health systems through multidisciplinary teams, protected consultation time and integration of dietitians, psychologists and exercise specialists may help improve continuity and quality of care.

Strategies to improve weight management

A notable and contradictory finding was that, despite acknowledging limited multidisciplinary knowledge and confidence beyond their own professional scope, participants consistently identified comprehensive and multidisciplinary team-based care consistent with international guidelines as a key approach for improving obesity management.10 28 Participants’ endorsement of multidisciplinary care may reflect an awareness that obesity is a complex condition requiring expertise beyond that of any single discipline. Social and environmental support such as family involvement, peer networks and community engagement were also viewed as influential, alongside broader policy initiatives, including school-based health education and healthier food environments.29 This tension suggests a distinction between practitioners’ recognition of an ideal model of care and their capacity to enact it within the existing system.7 In the Sri Lankan context, limited formal training opportunities, unclear referral pathways and the absence of coordinated obesity services may restrict practitioners’ ability to operationalise collaborative care, even when its value is clearly understood. This gap highlights the potential value of system-level mechanisms, such as interdisciplinary training and structured referral frameworks that may facilitate the implementation of coordinated care in practice.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The inclusion of practitioners representing key disciplines of comprehensive weight management enabled a broader range of perspectives on obesity management. It facilitated exploration of barriers and strategies across provider, client and system levels within a resource-limited, South Asian context. At the same time, this practitioner diversity represents a limitation, as perspectives may reflect discipline-specific priorities rather than shared experiences, and findings should therefore be interpreted as contextual and exploratory rather than uniform across providers. The qualitative design and focus on a single regional setting further limit transferability to other contexts. In addition, reliance on self-reported data may have introduced social desirability bias, particularly in relation to attitudes toward clients with obesity. Despite these limitations, the study offers valuable insights into perceived challenges and strategies for improving obesity care among young adults in resource-limited settings, where care is often fragmented and comprehensive approaches for this vulnerable population are not widely available.

Implications for practice

Within the context of this study, the findings suggest a need for more systemic and culturally tailored approaches to obesity management among young adults. Interventions should move beyond client behaviour change to address barriers at provider and organisational levels, incorporating family and peer support, community engagement and policy-level strategies. The variation in perspectives across professional groups highlights the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration and communication in delivering coherent and effective care. Training initiatives that integrate nutrition, physical activity, behavioural counselling and anti-stigma education may help reduce fragmented care. At system level, prioritising multidisciplinary teamwork, protected consultation time and clearer referral and follow-up pathways may improve continuity and quality of care in similar settings. Additionally, culturally sensitive, theory-informed approaches that incorporate readiness-to-change frameworks and acknowledge sociocultural determinants appear particularly relevant for engaging young adults in this context. Future research should examine how such integrated models function in practice, assess their effectiveness and explore their adaptability and scalability across different resource-limited healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights that obesity management for young adults is hindered by fragmented care, despite practitioners recognising the complex, multi-level challenges involved. Addressing these challenges may require broader system reforms. The introduction of an interdisciplinary referral system along with targeted training may provide a practical approach to improve coordination, reduce stigma and strengthen care delivery.
