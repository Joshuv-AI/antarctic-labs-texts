A Brush With Danger
Authors: Christopher W Jones, Emily K Damuth, Austin R Chang, Salina Wydo, Maggie E Jones, Dhaval Rana, Catherine L Kennedy
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 10-year-old girl presented to the emergency department with a toothbrush embedded in her soft palate after she was struck by a sibling while brushing her teeth. She was in severe pain and could not swallow. Physical examination revealed a plastic toothbrush embedded in the posterior oropharynx with blood-tinged saliva present, but no frank hemorrhage or respiratory distress (Fig 1). Computed tomography (CT) angiography showed the toothbrush lodged in the right mucosal parapharyngeal space abutting the internal carotid artery (Figs 2, 3).

Toothbrush embedded in the right soft palate medial to the mandibular ramus.

An axial computed tomography (CT) angiography image showing the head of the toothbrush (blue arrow) adjacent to the right internal carotid artery (red arrow), with parapharyngeal emphysema.

A sagittal computed tomography (CT) angiography image showing the toothbrush (blue arrow) abutting the internal carotid artery (red arrow).

Diagnosis

Penetrating Injury of the Palate

The patient was taken to the operating room, where a nasotracheal tube was placed via fiberoptic scope. Otolaryngology dissected adherent soft tissue to mobilize and remove the toothbrush. The internal carotid artery was visualized in the wound bed, though no vascular injury was apparent. The wound was irrigated and repaired in a multilayer fashion (Fig 4). Following a brief admission for neurological checks, the patient was discharged home on prophylactic amoxicillin/clavulanate and a liquid diet for one week. She recovered without complication.

Wound after removal of the toothbrush. The black arrow identifies the pharyngeal constrictor muscle.

Oral toothbrush injuries account for several thousand annual visits to United States emergency departments (EDs), though most involve penetrating soft tissue injuries to the cheek with a low risk of serious complications.1 Conversely, penetrating injuries medial to the mandibular ramus are rare but may result in life-threatening complications, including carotid artery dissection or thrombosis, retropharyngeal abscess, and pneumomediastinum/mediastinitis.2 Carotid injuries have also been reported after blunt trauma, emphasizing the importance of CT angiography in evaluating patients with intraoral trauma medial to the mandibular ramus.
