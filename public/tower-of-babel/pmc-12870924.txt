Association of endocrine autoantibodies (anti-TPO, anti-GAD, anti-insulin) with histopathological severity in pediatric Celiac disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Büşra Tetik Dinçer, Nafiye Urgancı, Aybike Koç, Hatice Kup, Merve Usta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeCeliac disease (CD) is an autoimmune disorder triggered by gluten ingestion, characterized by the development of specific autoantibodies. An increased incidence of type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) and autoimmune thyroid disease (ATD) has been reported in patients with CD. However, there is a gap in the literature regarding the association between the severity of CD and the levels of these autoantibodies. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between the severity of CD and autoantibody levels associated with T1DM and ATD.MethodsPatients diagnosed with CD between 2010 and 2023 and followed for at least two years were included, provided that endocrine autoantibody levels were available. Patients were evaluated based on their demographic, clinical, biochemical, and pathological data.ResultsA total of 248 patients were analyzed, with a median age of 11 years (Range: 3–18); 159 (64.1%) were female. Endocrine autoantibodies were found to be positive in 51 patients (20.6%). All patients with positive autoantibodies were aged 10 years or older (p < 0.001). Total villous atrophy was observed in 94 patients (37.9%) and 94 patients (37.9%) had findings consistent with Marsh 3 C. In multivariate analysis, the presence of endocrine autoantibodies emerged as the only independent factor associated with an increased Marsh grade (p < 0.001).ConclusionOur study demonstrated that the presence of autoantibodies associated with T1DM and ATD was correlated with more severe histopathological findings in patients with CD. There is a need for prospective studies investigating the role of these autoantibodies in predicting the prognosis of CD.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic autoimmune disorder triggered by gluten intake in genetically predisposed individuals, characterized by the development of specific autoantibodies [1]. Once considered rare, the incidence of CD has been increasing, likely due to improved awareness and diagnostic methods. Current epidemiological studies estimate its prevalence at approximately 1% of the general population [2]. In a study conducted on school children in Türkiye, the prevalence of celiac disease was found to be 0.47% [3].

The severity of CD symptoms varies widely among patients. Although several studies have examined the relationship between disease symptoms (such as anemia, growth retardation, and histopathological findings) and disease severity, the findings remain inconclusive [4–6].

CD is known to be associated with other autoimmune conditions, particularly type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), autoimmune thyroid disease (ATD), Addison’s disease, skin disorders and multiple endocrine syndromes [7]. In children with celiac disease, the prevalence of ATD ranges from 10.5% to 20% in different studies and the prevalence of T1DM ranges from 4.5% to 7.4% [8–10]. The only proven treatment for CD is a lifelong strict gluten-free diet, which also appears to reduce the risk of developing other autoimmune diseases [11, 12]. Additionally, some studies have reported that in children with both CD and T1DM, adherence to a gluten-free diet facilitates glycemic control and improves quality of life [13–15].

There are publications recommending routine screening for CD in patients with T1DM [16]. In patients with CD, there are publications investigating T1DM and ATD autoantibody levels [17]. However, there is very limited data in the literature regarding the association between T1DM and ATD autoantibody levels and the histopathological severity of CD. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between CD severity and autoantibodies associated with T1DM and ATD.

Materials and methods

This single-center, retrospective study included patients aged 3–18 years who were diagnosed with CD between 2010 and 2023 and had a minimum follow-up period of 2 years. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Şişli Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (25.06.2024-No: 2678).

Patients were eligible if they were diagnosed with CD according to the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) criteria [18], had been followed for at least two years in our clinic, and had laboratory evaluation for autoimmune antibodies associated with T1DM and ATD (anti-TPO, anti-thyroglobulin, anti-GAD, and anti-insulin antibodies). Patients those with incomplete follow-up data, missing antibody measurements, existing diagnoses of T1DM or ATD or related clinical findings were excluded. (Fig. 1)

Patients were analyzed based on age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), CD-related symptoms, biochemical and histopathological data, and levels of autoimmune antibodies associated with T1DM and ATD. The ferritin threshold value for iron deficiency was determined as 30 µg/l [19]. In determining the presence of anemia, specific hemoglobin concentration threshold values determined by the World Health Organization according to age groups and gender were used.

The primary outcome was the frequency of endocrine autoantibody positivity and its relationship with CD severity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS® (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Numbers, percentages, means, medians, etc., were used to summarize the results. The normal distribution of numerical data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported, while median and interquartile range (IQR) were used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical data were compared using Pearson Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact Test. Variables that were statistically significant or showed a trend toward significance (p < 0.1) in univariate analysis were included in an ordinal logistic regression model to identify independent predictors associated with higher Marsh grades. All p-values are two-tailed and results were considered significant at p < 0.05, with a confidence interval of 95%.

Results

A total of 248 patients were included. The median age was 11 years (range: 3–18), and 159 (64.1%) were female. Endocrine autoantibodies were found to be positive in 51 patients (20.6%). The median weight SDS was − 0.3 (IQR: -1.5 to 0.5), height SDS − 0.4 (IQR: -1.1 to 0.2), and BMI SDS − 0.1 (IQR: -1.1 to 0.6). Abdominal pain was reported in 54% of the patients, constipation in 21.8%, dyspeptic symptoms in 43.1%, and diarrhea in 59.3%. Iron deficiency anemia was detected in 54% of the patients. The age of patients with positive endocrine autoantibodies was found to be significantly higher (p < 0.001), while no significant differences were observed in other demographic or clinical parameters. Biochemical parameters showed a median hemoglobin level of 12 mg/dL (IQR: 11–13), median MCV of 78 fL (IQR: 71–82), and median ferritin of 16 ng/mL (IQR: 8–31). Histopathological evaluation revealed total villous atrophy in 94 patients (37.9%). Marsh 3 A was observed in 66 patients (26.6%), Marsh 3B in 88 (35.5%), and Marsh 3 C in 94 (37.9%). In patients with positive endocrine autoantibodies, the presence of villous atrophy (p < 0.001) and higher Marsh grades (p < 0.001) were detected, while no significant differences were observed in other parameters. Among the 248 patients, 51 (20.6%) had at least one positive autoimmune antibody. Anti-TPO positivity was observed in 6.5%, anti-thyroglobulin (anti-Tg) in 8.5%, anti-GAD in 8.9%, and anti-insulin in 8.9%. Twelve patients had both anti-GAD and anti-insulin positivity; three had anti-Tg, anti-GAD, and anti-insulin positivity; two had anti-Tg, anti-TPO, and anti-GAD positivity; and two had anti-TPO, anti-GAD, and anti-insulin positivity. Higher Marsh scores were significantly associated with the presence of anti-TPO (p = 0.003), anti-thyroglobulin (p < 0.001), anti-GAD (p < 0.001), and anti-insulin antibodies (p < 0.001). (Table 3)

Discussion

CD is an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten intake, with no current treatment beyond lifelong adherence to a gluten-free diet. Genetic studies have demonstrated a link between specific HLA types and CD. The co-occurrence of other autoimmune diseases may be explained by a shared genetic predisposition to autoimmunity. Only a limited number of studies have examined the prevalence of endocrine autoantibodies and their clinical implications in CD [17]. A review by Troncone et al. noted that over 60% of genes associated with CD are also implicated in other autoimmune diseases [20].

Abdominal pain and diarrhea are the most common symptoms in CD due to malabsorption [21], but a wide range of gastrointestinal and extraintestinal manifestations may be seen. Approximately 30–40% of CD patients report dyspeptic symptoms. Bardella et al. [22] found the prevalence of CD in dyspeptic patients to be twice that of the general population and suggested that serological testing could aid in early diagnosis. Conversely, Lasa et al. [23] reported no significant difference in CD prevalence among dyspeptic patients. Singh et al. [24] found a 1.5% prevalence in a meta-analysis, similar to the general population. Our study found dyspeptic symptoms in 43.1% of patients, consistent with the literature.

Constipation is a less common symptom in CD compared to diarrhea and abdominal pain [21]. However, studies have shown higher CD prevalence in patients referred for persistent constipation unresponsive to laxatives [25], especially when accompanied by growth failure [26, 27]. In our study, constipation was observed in 21.8% of patients, aligning with previous findings.

In the literature, the relationship between CD symptoms and histopathological findings has been investigated in only a limited number of studies. In the study by Galli G et al. [28], 102 patients with persistent symptoms after a gluten-free diet were evaluated, and no association was found between the presence of persistent symptoms and histopathological findings. Similarly, in our study, although patients with positive autoantibodies had higher Marsh grades, the presence of autoantibodies was not associated with differences in symptoms compared to those who were autoantibody-negative. Our findings are consistent with the existing literature [29].

Iron deficiency anemia, the most common extraintestinal manifestation of CD, is often due to duodenal involvement. Reports indicate that 12–82% of newly diagnosed CD patients have iron deficiency anemia. Even with dietary and iron supplementation, some patients exhibit persistent anemia, likely due to gluten-induced molecular and ultrastructural changes affecting absorption [30]. In our study, 54% of patients had iron deficiency anemia, consistent with published data.

In this study, the relationship between the histopathological stage of CD in children and the presence of autoantibodies associated with ATD and T1DM was investigated. In our study, autoantibody positivity was found to be associated with a higher Marsh stage, and in multivariate analysis, it was identified as the only independent variable associated with advanced Marsh staging. This suggests that increased mucosal damage may reflect shared pathways of immune activation. In the review article by Akdis CA et al. [31], the association between mucosal damage and systemic autoimmune diseases is discussed. The authors highlight the rapid increase in the prevalence of these diseases in Western societies and their potential link with the gut microbiota. Another study investigating the relationship between CD and IL-21 levels reported a positive correlation between celiac autoantibody levels, IL-21 levels, and duodenal mucosal damage [32]. However, given the gap in the literature regarding the association between T1DM and ATD autoantibodies and mucosal damage in patients with CD, as well as the retrospective design and limited sample size of our study, analyses in larger series are needed to further clarify this issue.

HLA-DQ2 and HLA-DQ8 haplotypes are recognized as the strongest genetic risk factors for both CD and T1DM, and this shared genetic predisposition provides a biological basis for autoimmune responses simultaneously targeting multiple organs [33, 34]. In addition, immunological cross-reactivity may explain why CD patients with endocrine autoantibodies tend to exhibit more severe histopathological alterations. It has been demonstrated that microbial peptides can be recognized by the same HLA-DQ2.5–restricted T-cell receptors that are reactive to gluten (gliadin) epitopes, thereby suggesting that gluten-reactive T cells may also be activated by environmental stimuli independent of gluten exposure; this supports a potential role of extrinsic triggers in the pathogenesis of CD beyond gluten-dependent immune activation [35]. These findings imply that CD-specific autoantibodies—such as anti-tTG, anti-gliadin, or anti-endomysium—may not be exclusively directed against intestinal mucosal targets but could also exert cross-reactivity toward structurally similar epitopes in endocrine organs including the pancreas and thyroid. Consequently, the observed correlation between elevated T1DM/ATD-related autoantibodies and the severity of intestinal mucosal injury in our study may also have a biologically plausible immunological explanation. Considering these mechanisms, the interplay of shared genetic predisposition, non-HLA immunogenetic factors, and environmental triggers should be evaluated collectively when interpreting the clinical linkage among CD, T1DM, and ATD, as it appears to be crucial for both diagnostic and pathogenetic perspectives.

The primary limitations of our study are its retrospective design, the relatively small sample size, and the absence of measurements for zinc transporter 8 antibodies and insulinoma-associated protein 2. Additionally, as autoimmune antibody testing was not performed routinely in all patients, only those with available data were analyzed, which may have introduced selection bias. Larger prospective studies are needed to better elucidate the relationship between endocrine autoantibodies and CD severity.

Conclusions

Our study indicates a statistically significant relationship between the presence of autoimmune antibodies related to T1DM and ATD and the histopathological severity of celiac disease. Future studies investigating whether there is a causal relationship between these autoantibodies and mucosal damage in patients with CD, as well as evaluating the utility of screening for these autoantibodies in predicting disease prognosis, will provide valuable contributions to the literature.
