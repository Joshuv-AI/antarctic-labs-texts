Species-specific allometric models for estimating aboveground biomass and carbon stocks of plantation forests in northcentral Ethiopia
Authors: Getabalew Teshome Reta, Motuma Tolera, Mulugeta Mokria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plantation forests are crucial for landscape rehabilitation and carbon sequestration. However, the efficacy of these plantations in carbon sequestration in Ethiopia remains uncertain due to a lack of robust, species- and site-specific biomass estimation models. This study addresses this gap by developing and validating allometric models for estimating aboveground biomass (AGB) and carbon (C) stocks of plantation forests. Allometric models were developed using data from 69 harvested trees of three species: Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula. AGB was regressed against diameter at breast height (DBH) as the sole predictor, with stepwise inclusion of height (H), crown area and wood density. Model performance was evaluated using fit statistics, including Pseudo-R2 and mean prediction error (MPE). The model that incorporated both DBH and H as predictors provided the best fit (Pseudo-R2 > 0.90, p < 0.001) and achieved the lowest MPE (1.47–5.67%) across all species. The findings indicated a mean C stock of 121.6 Mg C ha−1 in the plantations studied. Our models provide valuable insights for forest management and improve the accuracy of AGB and C stock estimations in the study area and similar ecosystems. Introduction

Global forest coverage has been steadily declining, primarily due to human-driven deforestation, leading to a shortage of forest products and significant changes in forest ecosystem functions1,2. Forest resources in Ethiopia have also experienced deforestation and forest degradation over the past century due to the growing demand for wood products and agricultural expansion3. These activities affect the carbon cycle by increasing the amount of carbon dioxide (CO2) in the atmosphere4,5. In this context, there are numerous visible symptoms of such adverse effects on ecosystems and society today6.

These challenges can be addressed through targeted measures, such as establishing tree plantations across degraded landscapes7. Tree plantations could significantly contribute to rehabilitating degraded lands, conserving natural forests, and regulating climate by capturing atmospheric CO28,9. In Ethiopia, trees have been planted for over a century to meet the increasing demand for wood products and reduce the pressure on natural forests10. Most plantations are composed of monocultures of exotic species, particularly those from four genera: Eucalyptus, Cupressus, Pinus, and Acacia11. Currently, the expansion of fast-growing tree species, such as Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula through afforestation and reforestation is becoming a government priority for restoring degraded landscapes and creating climate-resilient landscapes11,12. This is linked to the global “Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation” (REDD+) initiative to mitigate climate change13.

Accurate measurements of the aboveground biomass (AGB) and carbon stocks (C-stocks) of plantation forests are crucial for understanding their contributions to national and global carbon (C) budgets and supporting sustainable forest management14,15. This, in turn, requires a robust allometric model to obtain reliable forest biomass and C-stock information for the development of successful forest strategies and policies, as well as for claiming performance-based carbon credits under REDD + 16. Both species-specific and general allometric models have been developed for estimating forest biomass and carbon dynamics17,18. Although the general allometric model is commonly applied, it might not accurately estimate local biomass due to differences in species composition, stand density, age, site conditions, and management practices15,19. Thus, modes tailored to specific species are preferred for their accurate prediction for a given species16,20. Currently, there is strong national and global interest in developing species-specific models to reduce uncertainty in biomass and C-stock estimation21,22.

In Ethiopia, although there is a wide range of ecosystems and a rich variety of plant species, the country has developed only a limited number of allometric models for its forests23–26. Most existing models are limited to a small number of tree species from natural forests and fail to reflect the country’s extensive species and agroecological diversity26,27. This is becoming a concern with respect to the reliability of emission factor estimations, especially for plantation biomass. Recently, certain researchers have developed local species-specific allometric models to estimate tree AGB in different parts of Ethiopia22,28,29. Nevertheless, many of the allometric equations previously developed rely on the destructive method, which is both expensive and labor-intensive to apply across extensive forest areas30. Consequently, achieving precise estimates in these assessments remains challenging. This difficulty is partly due to the significant uncertainty associated with the allometric models used for biomass estimation. Additionally, measurement errors, sample size and range, and model development techniques significantly add to the uncertainty in efforts to assess AGB31. For example, the models by Zewidie et al.32 were derived primarily from relatively young E. globulus plantations in the central highlands of Ethiopia. These models are based on a limited tree diameter range (2–16 cm), exclude large, mature trees, and utilize log-transformed linear regression, which can introduce systematic bias when converted back into arithmetic units. Therefore, it is uncertain whether these models are applicable to mature Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula plantations in northcentral Ethiopia.

Based on the Amhara Region Bureau of Agriculture and Forestry Strategic Plans (2020–2024), planted forests in north-central Ethiopia span approximately 64,000 hectares (ha). The three species focused on in this study, Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula, are the dominant species in these plantings, accounting for approximately 70% of the total planted area in the region. The plantation sites selected for this study are part of a government-driven afforestation project that encompasses more than 1740 ha of degraded land within the study districts. These figures highlight the vital need to accurately measure the aboveground biomass and carbon stocks of these species, as they are central to regional carbon sequestration initiatives and restoration monitoring. More importantly, the practice of enrichment planting in the degraded natural forests of northcentral Ethiopia creates hybrid ecosystems, justifying a model that accurately reflects their unique biomass characteristics. The development of species- and site-specific biomass estimation models will fill a crucial gap, enabling precise AGB and carbon estimation in these transitional forests, which is vital for informing forest management decisions, including REDD+ initiatives and climate change mitigation. This may also encourage a shift in focus towards landscape restoration with enrichment planting, moving beyond the sole emphasis on the values of remaining degraded natural forests. Thus, in Ethiopia, there is a pressing need to develop species- and site-specific allometric models tailored to particular species and forest types. In response, this study aimed to derive and evaluate species-specific allometric models for estimating aboveground biomass and carbon stocks in Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula plantations in northcentral Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in Wof-Washa dry Afromontane forests, which are situated between latitudes 9° 44′ and 9°46′ N and longitudes 39° 44′ and 39° 47′ E. The elevation in this area varies from 1700 to 3700 m above sea level33 (Fig. 1). Annually, the area experiences an average rainfall of 1400 mm, characterized by a bimodal pattern. The longer rainy season occurs between July and September, whereas the shorter rainy season occurs from March to May34. The soil is generally classified as black clay or compact clay, along with reddish-brown heavy loam soil.

The Wof-Washa natural forest is among the few remaining dry Afromontane forests in Ethiopia35,36. It offers numerous ecological, economic, and social benefits to the local community. However, forests face significant threats due to widespread deforestation, fires, and overgrazing. Additionally, climate-related challenges accelerate the degradation of forests, leading to a decrease in and alteration of forest cover over time35,36. As a result, the forest cover has decreased from 9200 hectares (ha) in 1994 to 8200 ha in 2010, with the current extent being 6604.4 ha of severely disturbed natural forest33. In the early 1980s, plantations were established around the periphery of the Wof-Washa natural forest to alleviate human pressure on the forest, meet the rising demand for wood products, and address climate change. Today, these plantation forests cover 1740 ha and are predominantly composed of monocultures of exotic species, including E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula. These species are commonly used in Ethiopian afforestation and reforestation projects because of their rapid growth, adaptability, high yield, and positive impacts on rural livelihoods and ecological services11,12.

The management practices of plantation stands

In the study area, plantations were set up with tree densities ranging from approximately 1600 to 2500 trees per hectare37. Although the management strategies for these species vary greatly, effective forest management practices have not been sufficiently implemented to increase plantation productivity in the study area. In this context, the implementation of appropriate forest management practices for each species, such as tending operations, choosing tree diameter sizes for harvesting, and determining rotation ages, is crucial for increasing plantation productivity.

Sampling techniques and data collection

Tree allometry is influenced by factors such as location, species, and size. Therefore, when selecting trees for destructive sampling and biomass modelling, it is important to account for these variations to reduce the need for extrapolation in the model applications. To choose the right trees, a ground-based forest inventory that accurately represents the target population should be conducted38. Accordingly, we conducted a reconnaissance survey of Wof-Washa forests, guided by information from the North Shewa Agricultural Office, to identify a landscape that included plantation forests. We then selected plantation forests with stands of E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula. We employed a systematic random sampling procedure to collect forest inventory data from the three forest stands9,38. The initial sample plot was randomly placed, and subsequent plots were systematically arranged at equal intervals along each transect within each stand9. The number of plots per transect and transect lines per landscape were determined by the size and shape of the landscape. In total, we collected forest inventory data from 40 temporally established sample plots (20 m × 20 m). The sample plots included E. globulus (14 plots), C. lusitanica (14 plots), and P. patula (12 plots).

All trees with a DBH ≥ 2.5 cm and > 2 m height within each plot were identified. Next, we measured the diameter at breast height (DBH), total height (H), widest crown diameter (CD1), and perpendicular crown diameter (CD2) for all the trees. The stem density and basal area in each forest stand were assessed to ensure that the sample trees selected represented a wide range of diameters (Table 1). We stratified the vegetation inventory data into six diameter classes for E. globulus trees, whereas C. lusitanica and P. patula trees were grouped into five diameter classes to analyze the distribution of tree sizes within the study population.

Selection of sample trees for destructive sampling

Based on information from the sample plot inventories, 1–8 tree samples were randomly selected from each DBH class for each species. In total, 69 sample trees, 25 from E. globulus, 23 from C. lusitanica, and 21 from P. patula, were taken in proportion to the size-class distribution for AGB modelling (Table 2). In selecting trees, the aim was to achieve an even distribution across various diameter classes, with a particular emphasis on including a significant number of larger trees. This is because a small number of larger trees typically account for a substantial portion of the forest’s biomass. Additionally, during fieldwork, we aimed to select trees that accurately represented the population in terms of form and spatial distribution.

The allometric models described are empirically valid for E. globulus trees, with DBHs ranging from 8.8 to 68.4 cm and heights between 11.3 and 42 m; C. lusitanica trees, with DBHs ranging from 10 to 44.6 cm and heights ranging from 9 to 34.4 m; and P. patula trees, with DBHs ranging from 8.4 to 46.3 cm and heights ranging from 12 to 36.5 m (Table 2), which aligns with the calibration data range. Predicting trees beyond these specified ranges involves extrapolation and carries a higher degree of uncertainty.

Destructive sampling and determination of tree biomass

Before the selected sample trees were harvested, various biometric parameters were recorded for each tree in the field. These included diameter at breast height (DBH, measured at 1.3 m above the ground), diameter at stump height (DSH, taken 0.3 m above the ground), total height (H), largest crown diameter (CD1), and crown diameter measured perpendicularly (CD2). The measurements of DBH and DSH were taken using a calliper. The crown diameters (CD1 and CD2) were converted into the crown area (CA)25. All the sampled trees were felled with a chainsaw at the stump height (0.3 m above ground), and their heights were measured via a meter tape. Additionally, the heights and diameters of the stumps were measured to determine their volume and dry weight16. For sampling purposes, the felled tree samples were dissected into three parts: merchantable stems (stems with a diameter of ≥ 10 cm), large branches (branches with diameters of ≥ 2 cm and stem tops with diameters < 10 cm), and foliage (small branches with diameters < 2 cm, along with twigs and leaves). For further processing of local wood, the merchantable stems were monitored to a minimum commercial length of 2.1 m intervals, maintaining a minimum top diameter of 10 cm, while those with diameters ranging from ≥ 2 cm to 9.9 cm were considered branches. All the fresh logs, branches, and foliage were then weighed separately in the field using a spring balance with capacities of 50 and 100 kg.

Subsamples for dry-weight biomass and basic wood density

To determine the dry weight, we initially collected three subsamples from each section: stems (including discs from the base, middle and top parts of the stem), branches (categorized as small, medium and large) and foliage (comprising small, medium, and large twigs) from destructive sample trees. We then weighed, labelled, and transported these subsamples to the Debere Birhan Agricultural Research Centre laboratory for drying. The subsamples were oven-dried for a period of 2–3 days at 105 °C. We monitored and weighed the subsamples every 24 h until they reached a stable weight39. After all the oven-dried subsamples were weighed, the ratios of the fresh weight to the oven-dry weight of the corresponding tree sections were calculated. These ratios were subsequently used to convert the total fresh weights of sample trees measured in the field into total oven-dry weights22,26. Ultimately, the total aboveground biomass of a tree was calculated as the sum of the merchantable stem, branch, and foliage dry weights.

To determine the basic wood density of the trees, we collected separate wood subsamples from four locations along the tree’s height: the base (0.3 m above the ground), the diameter at breast height (DBH), the midpoint between the DBH and the upper stem, and the top of the stem40. We employed water displacement methods41 to determine the wood density for each species. We first converted the fresh weights of the subsamples into their corresponding green volumes. The subsamples were subsequently oven-dried in the laboratory for 72 h at 105 °C to determine their dry mass. Finally, we calculated wood density from the average ratio of dry weight to fresh volume41.

Model fitting and evaluation

All the data were analyzed using R software version 4.3.2. The study considered several potential predictor variables to explain biomass variation, including stem diameter (DBH in cm), total tree height (H in m), basic wood density (ρ in g cm− 3), and crown area (CA in m2). The dependent variable, the total aboveground biomass (AGB in kg), was calculated as the sum of the stem, branch, and foliage dry weights of the harvested trees. Scatter plots of the AGB against the potential independent variables showed nonlinear relationships (S. Fig. 1j–l). To address this nonlinearity, power functions have been commonly used in biomass modeling25,26,38. Accordingly, the study developed AGB allometric models for each species using nonlinear regression based on DBH, H, ρ, and CA. Notably, the simultaneous use of multiple predictors can lead to collinearity problems, which might adversely affect the accuracy of the regression coefficients42,43. To address this issue, a multicollinearity assessment was performed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF = 1/(1 − r2)), where a VIF value over 10 indicates significant collinearity, suggesting that both variables should not be used together. Consequently, separate predictive allometric models were developed for each species. Generally, eight different model forms were tested to determine the optimal predictor combination, with DBH used alone and in combination with a stepwise inclusion of H, ρ, and CA (Table 3).

The models were fitted using a weighted nonlinear least-squares regression with the ‘nls’ function in R44, and the plot diagnostics were evaluated using the ggplot2 package in R45. To address heteroscedasticity in the residuals, weighting by 1/(DBH)2δ was implemented, with δ being a weighting factor determined according to the methods outlined by Picard et al.46.

Model diagnostics were conducted using various goodness-of-fit statistics. These included the percent relative standard error (PRSE) (Eq. 1), Akaike information criterion (AIC) (Eq. 2), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) (Eq. 3), and Pseudo-R2 (Eq. 4)47. In principle, the optimal model would exhibit the lowest PRSE, AIC, and MAPE values while concurrently achieving the highest Pseudo-R2 value48. As the number of parameters increased, the model typically exhibited a lower RMSE and higher Pseudo-R2 values, regardless of their contribution to explaining the variation in the response variable49. In this case, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was generally employed to select the final models, as it considers the number of parameters and corrects them accordingly50. A model is considered unreliable when the PRSE exceeds 25% for any of its parameters51. Thus, we assigned a rank to each model based on individual goodness-of-fit statistics; these ranks were summed to determine the overall model performance52. Model validation was conducted using a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) technique to validate and select the best-fitting model for each studied species53. This method offers the benefit of delivering an impartial estimate of the prediction error, thereby enhancing the reliability of the results54. It is particularly crucial when a small dataset exists28. The LOOCV method involves excluding a single data point for validation while constructing the calibrated model using the rest of the data points (n-1 observations) at a time. The excluded observation is then predicted, and the error related to the model prediction is computed. This process is repeated n times until every data point has been included in the procedure53. The model’s performance was assessed using the root mean square error (RMSE) (Eq. 5) and the mean prediction error (MPE) (Eq. 6)38. The resulting residuals (the difference between the observed and predicted values) were used to determine the model performance indicators, RMSE and MPE. Models with non-significant parameter estimates were ignored from the tests for homoscedasticity of residuals and the cross-validation process.

The mean prediction error (MPE %) is a key indicator of whether a model satisfies the expected accuracy requirements. This is a measure of the systematic deviation of the model estimation from the observed data. It is an important and widely used statistical parameter for model evaluation, in addition to the 95% confidence interval of the predictions49. A relative mean prediction error (MPE) < ± 10% at the 95% confidence level was considered acceptable. Accordingly, we calculated the MPE values for all candidate models. After that, one-sample t-test was performed to assess whether the Mean Prediction Error (MPE) significantly differed from zero. The test statistic was derived by dividing the MPE by the standard error of the errors22. A significant result (p < 0.05) would suggest that the model systematically over- or under-predicts55. We also compared the RMSE (%) and MPE (%) from the LOOCV test models with those derived from the complete dataset to evaluate their effectiveness.

Model comparison

We assessed the performance of our best-performing species-specific models by comparing them with existing biomass estimation models26. To do this, we applied two widely used pantropical models, developed by Brown18 and Chave et al.17, to our datasets for comparison. The performance of the models was assessed using MPE56, and a simple t-test was performed to test whether the MPE significantly deviated from zero.

Estimation of aboveground biomass and carbon stocks in plantation forests

We converted the forest inventory data to tree biomass carbon stocks using our best species-specific models. To determine the carbon content of the trees, the AGB values were multiplied by the IPCC’s default carbon fraction of 0.4757. This value is consistent with the IPCC (2006)57 default carbon fraction for forest biomass, which represents a conservative global average for tropical tree species. The plot-level C-stocks were then converted to megagrams per hectare (MgCha− 1). Finally, we determined the weight of carbon dioxide sequestered per hectare (Mg CO2−equivalent ha− 1) by multiplying the carbon weight in AGB (Mg C ha− 1) by 3.6722.

Statistical analysis

Pearson’s correlation tests were conducted to explore the relationships between AGB and independent biometric parameters. One-way ANOVA was performed to assess the differences in carbon accumulation among the plantation stands. The significance of the mean differences was tested using the least significant difference test (LSD), with P < 0.0526.

Results

Sampled species and their biometric relationships

The harvested tree species, their dendrometry information (DBH, H, ρ, and CA), and the range of oven-dry biomass per species are presented in Table 4. The mean DBH ranged from 29.2 cm for C. lusitanica to 37.7 cm for E. globulus in the study area. The height (H) varied from 21.6 m for C. lusitanica to 28.2 m for E. globulus. The mean AGB observed within the study forests was 514.5 kg/tree. The mean AGB values for E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula were 953.1, 224.1, and 366.2 kg/tree, respectively. AGB was strongly correlated (P < 0.001) with DBH (r = 0.91–0.96), H (r = 0.90–0.93), and CA (r = 0.67–0.88) (S. Fig. 1a–i) in all the studied species. In contrast, the correlation between AGB and ρ was relatively weak (r = 0.28–0.67) compared with the other parameters (S. Fig. 1j–l).

Developed species-specific allometric models

The allometric models developed for each species and their performance statistics are presented in Table 5. The model using DBH as the sole predictor (M1) explained 94%, 81%, and 90% of the variation in the AGB of E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula, respectively. However, a more accurate AGB estimate was observed for each species when H was included in the DBH-alone model. The parameter estimates for the model using DBH and H together as predictors (M2) were highly significant (p < 0.001), with acceptable PRSE values (PRSE < 25%) for each species. Model performance analysis also showed that M2 had the lowest RMSE, MAPE, and AIC values for each species among the given set of candidate models. The addition of ρ in any form of combination in the AGB models resulted in nonsignificant model parameters with inflated PRSE values (Table 5). On the other hand, including CA with DBH and H in the model (M6) improved the model performance for each species. Despite M6 having the lowest values of fit statistics, the parameter estimates of this model were inflated (PRSE > 25%) for all species. Therefore, model-fitting statistical analysis revealed that model M2 was the best model for each species.

Validation of biomass estimation models

The predictive accuracies of our species-specific candidate models over the test datasets are presented in Table 6. The MPEs did not significantly deviate from zero in any model for all species. Adding H to the DBH-alone model increased the prediction accuracy, reducing the MPE from 8.9 to 1.5% for E. globulus, from 5.8% to 4.3% for C. lusitanica, and from 2.9 to 0.5% for P. patula. The RMSEs derived from the cross-validation test statistics are nearly equivalent to those obtained using the full data (Tables 5 and 6). Furthermore, the standard errors of the coefficients of model M2 across all species were not inflated, with a PRSE of 15.4–24.9%. Thus, our optimal model M2 for each species is stable and reliable for capturing variation in AGB. Based on the model performance and cross-validation test results, model M2, which uses DBH and H, was generally ranked as the top model for each studied species.

Model comparison

We plotted the AGB predicted using our best species-specific models and generalized pantropical models against the observed tree biomass, as shown in Fig. 3. The observed AGB reflected the trend of the predicted AGB from the best species-specific models developed in this study (Fig. 3a,d,g). Our best models resulted in minimal errors, with MPEs ranging from − 0.2% to 3.8%, and the MPE for each species did not significantly differ from zero. When Brown’s18 model was applied to our species-specific dataset, we observed a large error (MPE varying from − 22.2 to 79.7%), which was significantly different from zero (Fig. 3). The model markedly over-predicted the AGB by 179. 7% and 122.2% for C. lusitanica and P. patula, respectively, whereas it was underpredicted by 81.6% for E. globulus. Similarly, the model developed by Chave et al.17 produced large errors, with MPE% ranging from − 73.7% to -249.6% for all species. This model over-predicted the AGB by 173.7%, 349.5%, and 210.6% for E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula, respectively. Overall, our results indicate that species- and site-specific allometric models provide more accurate AGB estimates than do generalized models.

Estimated aboveground biomass and carbon stocks in plantation forests

The study plantations had an average biomass accumulation of 258.7 Mg ha− 1 and stored 121.6 Mg C ha− 1 in their AGB (Table 7). Significant differences (P < 0.001) in aboveground carbon (AGC) estimates were observed among plantations (Fig. 4). The highest C-stocks were recorded in E. globulus, which stored 176.1-± 17.7 Mg C ha− 1 in its AGB. This equates to 646.3 ± 64.9 Mg. ha− 1 of CO2 equivalents stored in E. globulus plantations (Table 7). Our findings indicated that E. globulus (48.3%) and P. patula (38.6%) were the primary contributors to biomass carbon accumulation, whereas C. lusitanica accounted for 13.1% of the total carbon accumulation in the study area.

Discussion

Species-specific allometric models and their performance

This paper presents species- and site-specific allometric models based on a destructive approach and data collected from three dominant tree species in plantation dry Afromontane forests in northcentral Ethiopia. Our findings revealed that the tested models accounted for 94–99% of the variation in AGB for E. globulus, 81–90% for C. lusitanica, and 90–95% for P. patula. This difference could be attributed to variations in the type and number of predictors included in the model, and their allometric variations22,26,43. Cross-validation and performance statistics testing indicated that the newly developed model M2, which uses DBH and H as predictors, was the best for each species compared with the other sets of available model forms. This model accounted for 98%, 90%, and 94% of the variation in the AGB of E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula, respectively, with the lowest bias. This implies that using DBH and H together as predictors may improve the robustness of the model. The PRSE values further demonstrated that the parameter estimates of our best model, M2, were consistent and reliable40,43. This approach can partially help capture site-specific height‒diameter allometry effects in biomass estimation models26,58. Temesgen et al.59 also highlighted the importance of adapting height–diameter relationships and considering tree height variations within diameter classes to improve the predictive power of biomass models.

Moreover, the predictive performances of our best models were evaluated over the testing dataset to ensure their accuracy60,61. The RMSEs obtained from the cross-validation test are nearly equal to those obtained using the complete dataset (Tables 5 and 6), indicating that our best models are not overfitting. This means that the performance of model M2 in producing accurate AGB estimations is realistic and not artificial22,26,43. Our residual diagnostics analyses also confirmed a more reliable assessment of the model’s performance. This model assumption is satisfied by the residuals’ absence of pattern, which verifies that the model’s prediction is impartial and the error variance is constant. This strengthens our findings that the model (M2) is robust for estimating the AGB of the species studied in the study area.

In this study, the inclusion of wood density (ρ) or crown area (CA) in the DBH-alone model did not significantly improve the model performance for any of the species (Tables 5 and 6). This indicates that other factors captured in the models were the main drivers of AGB. However, combining CA with DBH and H in the model (M6) explained 99%, 91% and 95% of the variation in AGB for E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula, respectively, indicating that including CA may increase the robustness of the models. Similar findings have also been reported for mixed-species models in Ethiopia25,38. Although model M6 exhibited the lowest fit statistics in this research, the parameter estimates for all species were exaggerated (PRSE > 25%), suggesting that the models might be inconsistent and unreliable for use as final models40,43. In agreement with our findings, adding more predictors to the model can increase collinearity effects43,62 and increase the expenses associated with the data collection59. In light of this, it is important to evaluate carefully the tradeoffs between the significance of predictor variables and their effectiveness in model development to prevent the inclusion of superficial variables in the final models47. In this context, this study suggests that locally developed biomass estimation models that use DBH and H as predictors may be preferable for each species in the study area.

Comparison of species-specific and pantropical models

The estimated AGB values derived from our best species-specific models were nearly similar to the measured AGB values (Fig. 3). However, estimates using the pantropical models developed by Brown18 and Chave et al.17 demonstrated high variability in the predictive performance for the study area. This biomass variability may be partially attributed to the fact that the tree characteristics at the study site differ from those of the models of Brown18 and Chave et al.17 development ecosystems15. Worku and Soromessa63 also reported the overestimation of biomass when the model of Brown18 was applied to their observed data for Juniperus procera (Hochst. Ex. Endl.) and Podocarpus falcatus (Thunb) in Ethiopia. Moreover, our models produced relatively low estimation errors compared with those of the pan-tropical models for the study area (Fig. 3). Our results further confirmed that species- and site-specific allometric models are more robust and reliable in converting forest inventory data to AGB and C-stocks20,21,56,58. Therefore, our best models have the potential to significantly reduce uncertainties in biomass estimation, which arises from the lack of robust models for plantation trees in the study area. This further highlights the importance of expanding the number of allometric models tailored to specific species and locations, as this could increase the accuracy of national emission data and aid in developing a generalized model for the Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) region.

Previously developed models, such as those by Zewidie et al.32, were developed primarily from relatively young Eucalyptus globulus plantations located in the central highlands of Ethiopia. These models are based on a limited DBH range, exclude large mature trees, and utilize log-transformed linear regression, which can lead to systematic bias when converting back to arithmetic units. In contrast, we used weighted nonlinear models, which are better suited to handle the inherent heteroscedasticity of biomass data and provide unbiased estimates across all tree sizes. Our models are empirically valid for E. globulus trees with DBHs ranging from 8.8 to 68.4 cm and heights between 11.3 and 42 m; C. lusitanica trees, with DBHs ranging from 10 to 44.6 cm and heights ranging from 9 to 34.4 m; and P. patula trees, with DBHs ranging from 8.4 to 46.3 cm and heights ranging from 12 to 36.5 m (Table 2). Predicting trees beyond these specified ranges involves extrapolation and carries a higher degree of uncertainty. Our research offers the first locally calibrated models for this species, considering regional differences in wood density and tree architecture, thereby enhancing the accuracy of carbon accounting in north-central Ethiopia.

Generally, the site-and species-specific models developed in this study could significantly contribute to efforts to obtain reliable biomass and C-stock information on plantation forests in north-central Ethiopia. Our models have potential applications in other similar ecosystems, particularly in data-limited areas with a scarcity of robust models. However, the performance of these models in different ecosystems may require additional verification. Future studies could explore the impact of environmental factors, such as climate and topography, on the effectiveness of various biomass models and examine how different predictor variables influence forest biomass and carbon storage.

Although Model M2 showed a strong overall performance, notable residual patterns linked to specific species were still observed, such as the consistent overestimation observed for C. lusitanica and P. patula at larger diameters than expected. We assert that although a single model serves as a practical approximation, it does not fully capture species-specific allometry. We provide recommendations for when users should consider employing separate models for different species. Future research with larger sample sizes in these categories may improve the model by incorporating variance functions. Moreover, the residual patterns for C. lusitanica and P. patula suggest that exploring more flexible model structures (e.g. nonlinear mixed-effects models with species-specific parameters) could be a promising direction for future research.

Estimated aboveground biomass and carbon stocks in plantation forests

The estimated AGC stocks varied significantly among the plantations studied (Fig. 4). E. globulus and P. patula plantations were the main contributors to biomass carbon accumulation, storing 176.1 and 140.7 Mg C ha− 1 in their AGB, respectively for 42 years old stands. This contributed to climate change mitigation by sequestering 646.3 Mg. ha− 1 of CO2 equivalents with E. globulus and 516.4 Mg. ha− 1 of CO2 equivalents with P. patula. These C-stocks were comparable to the biomass carbon accumulation reported for 38 years old Eucalyptus plantations in the central highlands of Ethiopia9. Our result, however, was higher than that of Mada et al.64 in a previous study of the same Eucalyptus plantations in southern Ethiopia. The use of biomass estimation models, the age of trees, the management of forests, and the environmental differences between the studied and those ecosystems might have partially contributed to this variation. Overall, our results imply that expanding tree plantations with E. globulus and P. patula could play a significant role in mitigating climate change and reducing anthropogenic pressures from the remaining dry Afromontane forests in Ethiopia. Planting such species may also contribute to earning carbon credits, further strengthening the resilience of rural communities by providing rewards and support for environmental conservation.

Moreover, the estimated C-stock of the C. lusitanica in our study area was significantly lower than those of E. globulus and P. patula (Table 7). The variation in this estimate can be attributed to ontogeny and functional trait differences among species. This may be partly linked to a balance between the survival strategy of the species and its resource availability uptake rate6,65, which leads to significant differences in branch structure and stem form, as observed in the field in the studied species. The AGB-DBH scaling exponent analysis of our results further confirmed that the tree allometry of C. lusitanica was lower than that of the other studied species. This highlights the need to assess carbon pools across different species and forest types to obtain more accurate landscape-level C-stock estimates. Such information is crucial for understanding carbon fluxes after afforestation or reforestation.

More importantly, when integrated with remote sensing data, the predicted C-stocks per hectare could offer a significant opportunity to monitor AGB and C-stock changes in rehabilitating landscapes on a regional or national scale26,66. To enhance the comparison of carbon pools among various monoculture tree plantations, future research should aim to increase the number of observations from diverse locations and tree species. It is also crucial to note that wood carbon content can differ among species, influenced by factors such as wood density, extractives, and structural compounds. Relying solely on a global average (default conversion factor) might lead to a source of systematic error. Therefore, we suggest that future studies in this field should include direct measurements or use species-specific data to enhance the accuracy of these estimates. This approach will help assess how effectively these plantations can restore degraded landscapes.

Conclusions

Accurate estimation of biomass and carbon stocks in plantation forests is essential for climate change mitigation and sustainable-forest management. In this study, we developed species-specific allometric models for key plantation species (Eucalyptus globulus, Cupressus lusitanica, and Pinus patula) in north-central Ethiopia using a semi-destructive sampling approach. The diameter at breast height (DBH) combined with tree height (H) was identified as a strong predictor of aboveground biomass (AGB), explaining 98%, 90%, and 94% of the variation in measured AGB for E. globulus, C. lusitanica, and P. patula, respectively. Compared with generalized pantropical models, our locally developed models showed substantially lower bias when applied to the study dataset, demonstrating the value of species- and site-specific calibrations for Ethiopian plantation systems.

The applicability of these models should, however, be interpreted within the ecological and structural conditions under which they were developed. The limited sample size and geographic focus on plantations surrounding the Wef-Washa dry Afromontane forest restrict direct extrapolation to other agroecological zones, stand structures, and management regimes. The models are most reliably applicable to monoculture plantations under site conditions comparable to those represented in the present study. Applications beyond these contexts, including mixed-species stands or markedly different climatic zones, may introduce additional uncertainty and therefore require local validation. Although wood density did not significantly improve the model performance in this study, this result should not be generalized to forest types with greater interspecific variation in wood properties.

In the studied landscapes, E. globulus and P. patula were the main contributors to biomass carbon accumulation, averaging 176.1 Mg C ha−1 and 140.7 Mg C ha−1, respectively. These estimates provide improved baseline information for plantation carbon accounting in northcentral Ethiopia and can inform national greenhouse gas inventories and REDD+ reporting frameworks. Nevertheless, uncertainties associated with model selection, sampling constraints, and the use of a default carbon fraction (0.47) should be considered when these values are integrated into broader carbon budgeting assessments.

Overall, well-managed plantations of E. globulus and P. patula under comparable dry Afromontane conditions can substantially contribute to carbon sequestration and land restoration. However, decisions regarding species expansion should incorporate ecological trade-offs, biodiversity considerations, and site suitability assessments, rather than relying solely on carbon accumulation potential. Future research should focus on independent validation using larger and geographically broader datasets, the inclusion of belowground biomass components, the evaluation of model transferability across ecological gradients, and the assessment of how allometric relationships respond to management interventions such as thinning and coppicing. These steps will improve confidence in model generalization and support the development of robust forest carbon monitoring systems in Ethiopia in the future.
