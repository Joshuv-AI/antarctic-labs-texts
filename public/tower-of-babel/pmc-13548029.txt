Treatment of acute calcific tendinopathy of the hip using focused shockwave
Authors: Jennifer R Arthurs, Christine Hunt, Shane A Shapiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCalcific tendinopathy (CT) is a painful condition characterised by the formation of deposits of calcium hydroxyapatite within tendon insertions. This most often occurs in the shoulder but may occur at other sites such as the gluteus medius or minimus tendons. While extracorporeal shockwave treatment has been used in the management of shoulder CT, limited data exist for CT of the hip. Here we describe a man who developed sudden onset of right gluteal/posterior thigh pain. Imaging revealed prominent CT at the insertion of the gluteus maximus at the linea aspera. He was treated with focused shockwave; treatments were administered once per week for 5 weeks with significant improvement in symptoms of pain and function. Repeat imaging obtained 1 month after completion of shockwave showed resolution of previously noted calcium hydroxyapatite.

Background

Calcific tendinopathy (CT) is a painful condition of unknown aetiology characterised by the formation of deposits of calcium hydroxyapatite within tendon insertions.1–4 This most often occurs in the shoulder but may occur at other sites such as the gluteus medius or minimus tendons.5–7 Treatment options for CT of the hip can include conservative management of the tendinopathy with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), activity modification and physical therapy. Injection therapy using ultrasound-guided lavage and local glucocorticoid injection or even platelet rich plasma can be considered to break up the calcium deposit through barbotage. Surgical decompression and removal have also been described.2 4–6 8 9 Figure 1 provides an overview of the diagnostic and management algorithm.

Extracorporeal shockwave treatment (ESWT) may also be beneficial; using sound waves through mechanotransduction, theorising the energy delivered into the tissue leads to destruction of calcifications in the targeted tissue.3–5 10–13 Systematic reviews assessing ESWT for CT of the shoulder have shown benefit in pain and function over placebo with some reports of calcification resorption.3 14–16 ESWT treatment can further be categorised into radial pressure wave and focused shockwave therapy. The delivery of energy varies greatly with radial pressure waves generating the greatest amount of energy at the applicator surface followed by decreasing energy at subsequent depths inside the body compared with focused shockwave that delivers energy that converges at a particular depth inside the body. For radial devices, the amount of maximal pressure that is reached during treatment is measured in bars. Focused devices record the amount of energy delivered into the tissue, as energy flux density (EFD), documented in mJ/mm2.17–20 While both technologies have been studied for CT of the shoulder, focused shockwave appears to offer an advantage for calcium resorption with reports of EFD >0.28 mJ/mm2 used during treatment.21–23 For treatment of CT of the shoulder, the International Society for Medical Shockwave Treatment recommends focused treatment using an EFD of 10–0.32 mJ/mm², as tolerated based on the patient’s pain.24

ESWT has been used for management of greater trochanteric pain syndrome with studies noting improvement in symptoms of pain and function.7 25–30 Carlisi et al studied 50 patients with greater trochanteric pain syndrome, with 80% of patients noted to have CT in a randomised controlled trial comparing focused shockwave to ultrasound therapy. There was a clinically significant decrease in pain scores noted at 2 and 6 months in the ESWT group compared with the ultrasound therapy group although the authors did not provide a breakdown of CT in each treatment group or report any follow-up specifically assessing for resorption of calcium.27 A retrospective review conducted by Seo et al examined 18 patients with greater trochanteric pain syndrome, 5 of whom had documented CT, who underwent treatment with focused shockwave (0.10 mJ/mm2, 600 impulses per treatment) and found statistically significant decreased pain scores at immediate (83.3%) and long-term follow ups (55.6%), although there are no reports of follow-up imaging to assess for calcium resorption.30

To date, there are five published case reports using ESWT for CT of the hip, with 4 cases noting improvement or resolution of the calcium deposit.31–34 Details of treatment are provided in table 1. Case presentation

The patient is a male in his 40s with a complex history of superficial siderosis in the setting of chronic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) venous fistula and CSF leak with signs and symptoms of balance deficits, muscle weakness and limited range of motion at the spine with muscle guarding related to multiple surgeries from CSF leak. He developed sudden onset of right gluteal/posterior thigh pain aggravated by weight-bearing activity, going from seated to standing position and laying on the affected side with a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score of 10/10. Differential diagnoses for his acute lateral hip pain included gluteal tendon tearing, greater trochanteric pain syndrome or referred lumbar radiculopathy given his history of limited range of motion at the spine with baseline balance deficits. Symptoms were less likely to be from a proximal femoral fracture or pelvic fracture as there was no trauma or recent overuse of the hip. X-ray images obtained within 1 week of symptoms onset showed a 1 cm curvilinear radiodensity in the soft tissues lateral to the right femur of uncertain aetiology (figure 2A). MRI thereafter demonstrated a 2.6×1×1.8 cm multilobular deposit felt to be consistent with calcium hydroxyapatite posterior to the subtrochanteric right femur at the site of insertion of the gluteus maximus at the linea aspera, with severe adjacent oedema (figure 3). After review of treatment options, the patient was referred for discussion of focused shockwave treatment as he declined ultrasound-guided needle tenotomy. Given his history of recent CSF leak, it was his preference to avoid injection therapy. Due to his history of superficial siderosis, he could not tolerate NSAIDs due to risk of bleeding.

Treatment

He elected to proceed with focused shockwave treatment; treatments were administered once per week for 5 weeks. Patient was positioned side lying with a pillow between his knees while slightly flexed for comfort. Focused shockwave treatment was performed using Storz Medical DUOLITH SD1 ultra (Tagerwilen, Switzerland) with second standoff (30 mm). Treatment was applied using clinical focusing technique with moderate pressure; a total of 3000 shocks were delivered at 5 Hz using 0.30–0.35 mJ/mm2. He began physical therapy within 2 weeks of initiation of shockwave, starting with gentle strength training and to address personal impairments and improve overall function with the goal of returning to independent walking and general strength training.

Outcome and follow-up

During weekly treatment, he noted significant improvement in symptoms of pain and function decreasing from a VAS pain score of 10/10 to 3/10. By his fifth and final treatment, he denied any symptoms of pain when going from seated to standing position and laying on the affected side and was able to resume exercise such as walking and cycling. Repeat X-ray obtained 1 month after completion of shockwave showed complete resolution of previously noted calcification overlying the right subtrochanteric femur (figure 2B). Post-treatment diagnostic ultrasound was also performed and did not reveal any remaining calcium deposits (figure 4).

Discussion

CT is most reported in the shoulder but has been reported within other tendons, including the hip.5–7 Extracorporeal shockwave therapy appears to be safe and can successfully treat CT of the gluteal region, although there is limited published literature. Here we describe a case of resolution of CT involving the gluteus maximus insertion at the linea aspera (figure 5) using 5 sessions of focused shockwave treatment.

Illustration of calcific tendinopathy of the gluteus maximus insertion at the linea aspera.

Of note, this patient was treated using focused shockwave with clinical focusing technique without anaesthesia, in which shockwave is delivered over the area of maximal pain reported by the patient.10 19 In a recent international modified Delphi study, clinical focusing has been recommended over the use of imaging guidance for treatment of tendinopathies and fasciopathy, but there was failure of consensus when discussing clinical focusing compared with image guidance for bone pathologies.10 In the management of CT of the shoulder, Ilia et al found that ultrasound guidance was more effective than clinical focusing techniques using radial pressure wave at managing shoulder pain and function along with increased calcific deposit resorption.35 In the published case studies, image guidance was reported in 2 out of 5 case reports. When targeting areas of calcification, ultrasound guidance, if available and in the hands of a trained provider, seems reasonable to effectively direct shockwave treatment. Additionally, patient positioning may also play a role.36 Future investigations are needed to better understand the use of focused shockwave and radial pressure wave and the role of image guidance in the treatment of CT of the hip.
