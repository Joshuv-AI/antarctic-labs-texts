Likelihood landscape of binary latent model on a tree
David Clancy Jr, Hanbaek Lyu, Sebastien Roch

Abstract
We investigate the optimization landscape of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) for the Cavender-Farris-Neyman (CFN) model, a two-state latent tree model fundamental to statistical phylogenetics and the ferromagnetic Ising model. Although the log-likelihood function is non-concave and may admit many critical points, simple coordinate maximization algorithms are remarkably effective in practice. We provide the first theoretical justification for this success. We prove that sufficiently deep inside the reconstruction regime, the population log-likelihood is strongly concave and smooth within a box around the true parameter, whose size is independent of tree topology and number of leaves. This fundamental result implies that the empirical landscape shares these regularity properties with high probability given polynomial sample complexity and also that coordinate maximization converges exponentially fast to an O ⁡ ( 1 / m ) O(1/\sqrt{m}) -consistent MLE. Our analysis centers on a novel decay property of the population Hessian: diagonal entries remain large while off-diagonal entries decay exponentially with graph distance. These results provide rigorous theoretical evidence for the efficacy of likelihood-based tree inference and suggest broader principles for latent variable models.

Introduction

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) for estimating an unknown model parameter is a fundamental technique in statistics and machine learning. In this framework, one considers a parametric probabilistic model
(\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}};\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a dataset
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
assumed to be i.i.d. observations from
\mathbb{P}_{\theta^{*}}
for some unknown
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. The goal is to find an estimate
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
such that

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\textup{MLE}}

\displaystyle\in\arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\,\,\left[\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{1},\dots,x_{m}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(x_{j})\right],

(1)

thereby maximizing the likelihood of the observed data.

Classical theory
Cra (46)
;
Wal (49)
establishes that whenever the population landscape (i.e., the
m\to\infty
limit)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})]=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(X),

is concave and maximized at a unique point coinciding with the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
, then
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\textup{MLE}}
serves as a consistent estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
. Much of the rigorous theoretical foundation of MLE assumes that the properties of this population landscape extend to the empirical landscape
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
. This extension relies on the ability to approximate
\mathbb{E}[\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
by
\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{1},\dots,x_{m})
such that optimizing the latter approximately recovers
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}

LB (19)
. However, many natural MLE problems—such as those arising from mixture models
RW (84)
;
Mur (12)
, errors-in-variables regression
VHV (91)
, or blind deconvolution
ARR (14)
—exhibit highly non-concave likelihood landscapes in both the population and the empirical levels, which significantly complicates the analysis of MLEs.

In this paper, we study a fundamental parameter estimation problem for two-state latent models on broadcasting trees known as
branch-length estimation

GG (03)
under the Cavender-Farris-Neyman (CFN) model
Ney (71)
;
Far (73)
;
Cav (78)
. This model is widely used to study molecular evolution along phylogenetic trees. Broadly speaking, the goal is to estimate the flip probabilities in noisy channels along the edges of a broadcasting tree, given signals observed only at the tips (leaves) of the tree. While arising in phylogenetics, such models and their various inference problems have applications in theoretical computer science, signal processing, and statistical physics
Mos (22)
.

The associated MLE problem is challenging. Marginalizing hidden variables makes the likelihood non-concave, and even for closely related latent-tree models the sharpest known landscape guarantees are population-level: for Gaussian latent tree models,
DKD (22)
proves that the true parameter is the unique non-trivial stationary point of the population log-likelihood, where “non-trivial” excludes boundary edge correlations equal to 0 or 1. Thus, in that setting, spurious interior stationary points are absent. By contrast, empirical likelihoods for discrete phylogenetic models can have substantial algebraic complexity. A standard measure of this complexity is the maximum-likelihood degree (ML degree): for a generic data vector, it is the number of complex solutions to the likelihood critical equations, divided by the generic fiber cardinality of the model parametrization, i.e., the number of parameter values that generically map to the same model distribution. García Puente, Garrote-López, and Shehu
GGS (23)
compute ML degrees for small group-based phylogenetic models, including the CFN model, and show that these counts can already be large; importantly, ML degree counts complex generic-data critical points, not real or biologically admissible stationary points, and it does not address the population landscape. The real likelihood landscape is nevertheless known to be nontrivial even on small trees: Steel
Ste (94)
gives a four-leaf CFN example with two global maximizers on the boundary of the parameter space, and related work exhibits multiple optima or local maxima for phylogenetic likelihoods
RS (99)
;
CHHP (00)
. These difficulties motivate our semi-global approach: rather than characterizing the entire non-concave landscape, we identify a dimension-independent neighborhood of the true parameter on which the population–and, with enough samples, empirical–likelihood is well conditioned.

Despite these theoretical hurdles, likelihood maximization has proven remarkably effective in practice. For instance, Guindon and Gascuel
GG (03)
;
GDL+ (10)
developed PHYML, a coordinate-ascent algorithm that performs well empirically even with a few coordinate updates. Other widely used likelihood-based methods include RAxML
Sta (14)
and IQ-TREE
NSvHM (15)
. However, a rigorous theoretical explanation for the success of these methods in the discrete setting has remained elusive. Recent advances in statistical estimation theory have emphasized the importance of analyzing the geometric structure of likelihood landscapes to bridge this gap between practice and theory
MWCC (20)
;
CFMY (19)
;
CLC (19)
.

In this work, we establish a condition, regardless of the tree’s size, under which the population likelihood landscape becomes smooth and well-behaved within a box centered at the true parameter. This fundamental likelihood landscape result, combined with our recent work on its finite-sample applications
CLR (25)
, shows that a simple coordinate maximization algorithm converges quickly and reliably to the correct estimate if initialized in this box. These results provide the first theoretical guarantees for the optimization methods commonly used in phylogenetic and machine learning analyses of discrete latent tree models. Importantly, our semi-global landscape analysis is distinct from standard local analyses, as it establishes regularity within a region whose size is independent of the problem dimensions.

1.1
The CFN model

Let
T=(V,E)
be an unrooted tree where all vertices have degree 1 or 3. The vertices with degree 1, called leaves and denoted by the set
L
, represent the observed variables, while the remaining vertices represent latent variables. Let
n=|L|
denote the number of leaves. A counting argument shows that such a tree must have
2n-2
total vertices.

At each vertex
v\in V
, we have a binary state
\sigma_{v}\in\{\pm 1\}
which starts from its stationary distribution and evolves along the branches, i.e., edges, of the tree according to a reversible Markov chain with unknown transition matrices. Specifically, there is an unknown “true” parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}=(\theta^{*}_{e};e\in E)
such that the transition matrices
(P^{*}_{e};e\in E)
are given by

P^{*}_{e}=\begin{bmatrix}1-p^{*}_{e}&p^{*}_{e}\\
p^{*}_{e}&1-p^{*}_{e}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1+\theta^{*}_{e}}{2}&\frac{1-\theta^{*}_{e}}{2}\\
\frac{1-\theta^{*}_{e}}{2}&\frac{1+\theta^{*}_{e}}{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Here,
p^{*}_{e}=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\sigma_{u}\neq\sigma_{v})
represents the transition probability along edge
e=\{u,v\}
, and the stationary distribution is uniform:
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\sigma_{v}=1)=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(\sigma_{v}=-1)=\frac{1}{2}
. The relationship between the transition probabilities and edge parameters is given by

p^{*}_{e}=\frac{1-\theta^{*}_{e}}{2},\qquad\theta^{*}_{e}=1-2p^{*}_{e}.

(2)

While
\theta_{e}
can theoretically take values in
[-1,1]
, we focus on the regime
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in[0,1]^{E}
where neighboring variables are positively correlated (i.e., the “ferromagnetic regime”). This constraint appears naturally in applications, including in phylogenetics.

The central problem we study is the recovery of the true parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
from repeated, independent observations of the states at the leaves of the tree. Formally, let
\sigma^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(m)}
be independent samples from the model described above with true parameter
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
. We only observe the leaf states, denoted by
\sigma^{(j)}|_{L}=(\sigma_{v}^{(j)};v\in L)
for
j=1,\dots,m
. Our goal is to estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
from these partial observations.
This estimation problem arises naturally in statistical phylogenetics, where it is known as “branch length estimation”. In that context,
p^{*}_{e}
can be interpreted as a monotone function of the evolutionary distance, or branch length, along edge
e
assuming a constant-rate mutation process
1
1

1

More precisely,
\theta^{*}_{e}=e^{-2l^{*}_{e}}
and
p^{*}_{e}=\frac{1-\exp{(-2l^{*}_{e})}}{2}
where
l^{*}_{e}
represents the “evolutionary distance” (i.e., time scaled by mutation rate). We work directly with the
\theta^{*}_{e}
parameterization throughout this paper.
.
See, e.g.,
Fel (04)
;
Yan (14)
for background on statistical phylogenetics.

1.2
The maximum likelihood landscape

It has long been an open problem
whether standard coordinate ascent algorithms for maximum likelihood can solve the branch length estimation problem. Namely, the log-likelihood of the leaf observations
\sigma^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(m)}
under the model with parameter
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is given by

\displaystyle\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}):=\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma^{(1)}|_{L},\dotsm,\sigma^{(m)}|_{L}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(\sigma_{v}=\sigma^{(j)}_{v},\,\,\forall v\in L).

(3)

We then seek to find the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) as

\displaystyle{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}\in\argmax_{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in[0,1]^{E}}\,\,\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma^{(1)}|_{L},\dotsm,\sigma^{(m)}|_{L}).

(4)

As with many latent variable or mixture models
BWY (17)
;
DKD (22)
, the objective function in (
4
) is non-concave and may admit multiple critical points for generic data. See also
GGS (23)
and references therein. Steel
Ste (94)
provided an explicit example, reproduced in Figure
2
below, where there are multiple global maximizers to (
4
). The likelihood function
L({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma^{(1)},\sigma^{(2)})
for Figure
2
has exactly two maximum values

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{1}=(0,1,0,1,1)\qquad\textup{and}\qquad\boldsymbol{\theta}^{2}=(1,0,1,0,1),

which are both on the boundary
\partial[0,1]^{E}
.

1.3
The one-dimensional likelihood landscape

Several results have been obtained about the shape of the one-dimensional likelihood landscape, i.e., the coordinate-wise optimization problem. Fukami and Tateno
FT (89)
showed that, under a four-state model known in phylogenetics as F81
Fel (81)
, when restricted to a single edge parameter
\hat{\theta}_{e}
, the log-likelihood
\ell
is strictly concave and attains a unique maximizer. For more general discrete models, Dinh and Matsen
DM (17)
provide conditions under which the one-dimensional likelihood is guaranteed to have at most one stationary point, a condition satisfied by the CFN model in particular.

Recently, with Sly
CLRS (26)
, we showed that under the CFN model, the gradient of the population log-likelihood function is characterized by
the following approximation:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma|_{L})\right]=\frac{\theta^{*}_{e}-\hat{\theta}_{e}}{1-\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}}+O(\delta),

(5)

where the parameter
\delta>0
controls the scale of the transition probabilities along the edges.
From this result, it follows for instance that standard coordinate maximization for solving (
4
) achieves an estimation of the true parameter within
L_{\infty}
-error of
O(\delta^{2})
in a single round in the population limit.
A key tool developed in
CLRS (26)
is a sensitivity analysis of ancestral reconstruction, i.e., hidden state estimation (see Theorem
10
). Roughly speaking, it says that the posterior mean (or “magnetization”; see (
20
)) of the state of a node (expressed as a spin value), remains a good estimate for the actual state there even under approximate parameters. This technical result is key to our analysis in this paper as well.

1.4
Contribution.

Our work contributes to this line of research by providing a fundamental population likelihood landscape result for the CFN model.

1. (
Population likelihood landscape
; Thm.
1
, Cor.
2
) There exists a box
\mathtt{B}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Theta}
with constant
L_{\infty}
-norm side lengths around the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
such that the population likelihood
\mathbb{E}[\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta})]
is strongly concave and smooth on
\mathtt{B}
. (See the illustration in Fig.
1
.)

In our recent work
CLR (25)
, we have already established that such a population likelihood landscape result can be used to establish sample complexity and consistency of the MLE and also obtain computational guarantees of coordinate maximization for computing the MLE. We summarize these applications below.

2. (
Empirical likelihood landscape
; Thm.
3
) With enough samples
m
(polynomial in the size of the tree in the balanced case), the empirical log-likelihood is strongly concave and smooth on the same universal box
\mathtt{B}
with high probability.

3. (
Statistical estimation guarantee
; Thm.
4
) For any fixed problem size, the MLE is
O(1/\sqrt{m})
-consistent with the true parameter with arbitrarily large probability.

4. (
Computational guarantee of coordinate maximization
; Thm.
5
) The iterates of the coordinate maximization algorithm converge exponentially fast to the confined MLE
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
with a rate independent of the tree, provided a sufficiently close initial point.

The overarching goal in this work is to understand the structure of the optimization landscape of the maximum likelihood estimation problem in (
4
). In particular, we seek to understand the structure of the Hessian of the log-likelihood function in (
3
). Our likelihood landscape results above show that, even though the population and empirical landscapes may be non-concave and contain numerous local (or even global) maximizers, it is very well conditioned inside the universal box
\mathtt{B}
where there is a unique maximizer and all other local maximizers lie strictly outside this region. It is important that this region does not shrink as the sample size or the problem dimension grows large, which is typically the case in standard local landscape analysis, relying on positive definiteness of the Fisher information and continuity of the Hessian around the true parameter. Our analysis is semi-global in its nature.

By applying our semi-global likelihood landscape analysis, we show that one can recover the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
using standard likelihood maximization techniques and assess the incurred statistical and computational errors rigorously.

Organization

where
\lambda_{\min}(\cdot)
and
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denote the minimum and the maximum eigenvalues of a matrix. In particular,
in the population limit
m\rightarrow\infty
, the log-likelihood function
\ell
in (
4
) is

(\frac{C_{\ref{eqn:HessianDiag}}}{\delta}-26)
- strongly concave and
(\frac{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eqn:HessianDiag}}}{\delta}+26)
-smooth. In particular, the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
is the unique maximizer of
\ell
over
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}
.

2.4
Applications to finite-sample likelihood landscape and coordinate maximization

The population landscape results stated in the previous section have applications to finite-sample likelihood landscapes, consistency of maximum-likelihood estimators, and convergence of the coordinate maximization algorithm. These applications have recently been published in
CLR (25)
, which were obtained assuming the population landscape result in Theorem
1
and Corollary
2
. We summarize the main results in
CLR (25)
. We emphasize that the results stated in this section are not sharp and we believe our techniques will lead to further improvements.

First, the result in Corollary
2
continues to hold for the empirical likelihood landscape as long as the number
m
of observed samples is large enough. To establish such a result, we use a uniform version of matrix Bernstein’s inequality
(
CLR, 25
, Lem. 4.5)
to show that the Hessian of the empirical log-likelihood function is concentrated near its expectation
uniformly
over the box
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}(\delta)
with high probability. Then the assertion will follow from the population landscape result in Corollary
2
.

Theorem 3

(Finite-sample log-likelihood landscape: strong concavity and smoothness; Thm. 3.2 in
CLR (25)
)
.

Let
\delta\leq\delta_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}
and let
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
denote the Hessian of the
m
-sample log-likelihood function
\ell
in (
3
).
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\ref{eq:sample_complexity}}>0
such that if

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq(C_{\ref{eq:sample_complexity}}/\delta)^{\textup{diam}(T)+8}\,\log(\varepsilon^{-1}),

(12)

then for any binary tree
T
, and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}(\delta)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\Bigg(-\frac{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}{\delta}-27\leq\inf_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}(\delta)}\lambda_{\min}(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}(\delta)}\lambda_{\max}(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\leq-\frac{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}{\delta}+27\Bigg)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

(13)

The required sample complexity for Theorem
3
grows exponentially in the diameter of the tree
T
, which can be as small as
O(\log n)
when the tree is ‘well-balanced’. In the latter case, Theorem
3
below establishes a polynomial sample complexity to obtain a strongly concave and smooth optimization landscape for the MLE problem with high probability. However, we suspect this sample complexity can be improved to depend logarithmically in the number of leaves rather than exponentially in the diameter. We leave this important direction for future work.

Next, denote a generic global maximizer of the empirical log-likelihood function
\ell(\cdot)
(in (
3
)) over
{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}(\delta)
(which always exists) by
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
. A particular consequence of Theorem
3
is that
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
is uniquely determined with high probability and enough samples. Specifically, Theorem
4
below states that the MLE
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
is a
1/\sqrt{m}
-consistent estimator of the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
with high probability.
This consistency (up to a constant depending on
T
) does not depend on our choice of norm on
\mathbb{R}^{E}
for any fixed tree. We write
\|\cdot\|
for the
L^{2}
-norm of a vector.

Theorem 4

(Statistical estimation guarantee; Thm. 3.3 in
CLR (25)
)
.

Assume the hypothesis of Theorem
3
holds. Let
E_{\ref{eq:finite_sample_ML_landscape_thm}}
denote the event in (
13
). Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and denote
\rho:=\frac{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}{\delta}-27
and

C_{\ref{eq:MLE_estimation_error}}:=\frac{16\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}

\displaystyle\left(E_{\ref{eq:finite_sample_ML_landscape_thm}}\cap\left\{\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}\rVert\leq C_{\ref{eq:MLE_estimation_error}}\sqrt{|E|/m}\log(|E|/\varepsilon)\right\}\right)\geq 1-3\varepsilon

(14)

provided that
m
satisfies (
12
) and
m\geq\frac{|E|^{2}}{4C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}^{6}c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}^{6}\delta^{3}\varepsilon}
.

Now that we know the MLE
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
is close to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
with high probability, we turn our attention to how we can compute the
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
from the observed samples
\sigma^{(1)},\dots,\sigma^{(m)}
restricted to the leaves. While the log-likelihood function
\ell
in (
3
) is non-concave, it has the nice structure of being strictly concave when restricted to a single branch length
\hat{\theta}_{e}
for
e\in E
(see Lem.
6
). Thus, it is natural to cycle through the branch lengths and optimize one at a time, maximizing the one-dimensional restricted likelihood function. This yields the following “cyclic coordinate maximization” algorithm for computing the MLE. Namely, given our estimate
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k}=(\hat{\theta}_{k;e};e\in E)
after
k
iterations, the algorithm proceeds by optimizing for a single branch length
\theta_{k;e}
by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&\hat{\theta}_{k+1;e}\leftarrow\argmax_{\hat{\theta}\in[-1,1]}\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta}),\,\,\textup{where}\,\,\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta}):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k+1;1:e-1},\hat{\theta},{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k;e+1:|E|};\sigma^{(i)}),\\
&{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k;i:j}:=(\hat{\theta}_{k;i},\hat{\theta}_{k;i+1},\dotsm,\hat{\theta}_{k;j})\end{cases}

(15)

assuming that we label the edge set
E
as integers from 1 through
|E|
. The one-dimensional objectives
\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta})
in (
15
) are known to be strictly concave
FT (89)
and they have a unique maximizer in
(-1,1)
at a unique critical point:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\overline{f}_{k;e}(\hat{\theta})=0.

(16)

The unique zero of the above critical-point equation can be found rapidly by using standard zero-finding algorithms (e.g.,
Bre (13)
). See e.g.
GG (03)
for a practical implementation of this type of algorithm.

Despite the popularity and the empirical success of the coordinate maximization algorithm above, however, due to the non-concavity of the optimization landscape, there has been no guarantee about the convergence of this algorithm to the maximizer of
\ell
or the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
. Theorem
5
below establishes that the coordinate maximization algorithm above (
15
) converges exponentially fast to the MLE
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}
, which is within
C(T,\varepsilon,\delta)m^{-1/2}
from the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
, provided the initial estimate
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}
is within
O(\delta)
from the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
in
L_{2}
norm.

Theorem 5

(Statistical and computational estimation guarantee; Thm. 3.4 in
CLR (25)
)
.

Suppose the hypothesis of Theorem
4
holds.
Let
({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
denote the sequence of estimated parameters generated by the coordinate maximization algorithm with the initial estimate
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}
satisfying

\displaystyle\lVert{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\rVert\leq\frac{(C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}-27\delta)C_{\ref{eq:coordinate_max_initialization}}}{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}+\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}}\frac{\delta}{2},

(17)

where
C_{\ref{eq:coordinate_max_initialization}}:=(C_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}-C_{\ref{eqn:pBounds}})\land(c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}-c_{\ref{eqn:pBounds}})>0
.
Then with probability at least
1-3\varepsilon
, for all
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\lVert

\displaystyle{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k}\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}-27\delta}{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}-27\delta}\left(1-\frac{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}\delta^{-1}-27}{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}\delta^{-1}-27}\right)^{k-1}\lVert{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}\rVert^{2}.

(18)

In particular,

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092pt\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{k}\rVert\leq\underbrace{C_{\ref{eq:MLE_estimation_error}}\frac{|E|}{m}\log(\frac{|E|}{\varepsilon})}_{=\textup{statistical error}}+\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}-27\delta}{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}-27\delta}}\left(1-\frac{C_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}\delta^{-1}-27}{\widetilde{C}_{\ref{eq:expected_Hessian_eval_range}}\delta^{-1}-27}\right)^{(k-1)/2}\lVert{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{\textup{MLE}}-{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}\rVert}_{=\textup{computational error}}.

(19)

It is important to note that the exponential rate of convergence of the coordinate maximization in Theorem
5
is a universal constant that does not depend on the tree
T
and also the parameter
\delta
(as long as it is less than some universal constant in Thm.
3
). This means that the computational error for computing the MLE can be made to be less than a desired tolerance
\varepsilon
within
C\log\varepsilon^{-1}
iterations for some universal constant
C
.
This gives some theoretical support for the empirical fact that coordinate maximization algorithm performs well empirically, even with a small number of coordinate updates

;
GDL+ (10)
.

It would be of interest to show that the assumption that
\|{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\|=O(\delta)
can be dropped; however, our proof needs the initial iterate to be sufficiently close to
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
in order to know that the empirical Hessian is smooth and strongly concave with high probability (Thm.
3
) and that the subsequent iterates also lie in this “good” region.

Sketch of analysis for the population landscape

2.5.1
Magnetization

We first discuss how to conveniently express the Hessian of the log-likelihood function (
3
) using an observable called the ‘magnetization’.
We begin with some important definitions.

Fix two distinct nodes
u,v
in
T
. We call a node
w
a
descendant
of
u
with respect to node
v
if the shortest path between
w
and
v
contains
u
. The
descendant subtree at
u
with respect to
v
is the subtree
T_{u}
rooted at
u
consisting of all descendants of
u
with respect to
v
. A subtree of
T
rooted at
u
is a
descendant subtree of
u
if it is a descendant subtree of
u
with respect to some node
v
.

The following notion of ‘magnetization’ is central to the overall analysis in this work. Roughly speaking, the magnetization
Z_{u}
of a node
u
with respect to a descendant subtree
T_{u}
rooted at
u
is the ‘bias’ on its spin after observing all spins at the leaves of the descendant subtree
T_{u}
. For instance, if all spins on the leaves of
T_{u}
are
+
, then
u
will be quite likely to have
+
spin as well. The formal definition of magnetization is given below.

Definition 3

(Magnetization)
.

Let
T_{u}
be a descendant
subtree of
T
rooted at a node
u
. Let
L_{u}
denote the set of all leaves in
T_{u}
. For a generic parameter
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in[-1,1]^{E(T_{u})}
and fixed spin configuration
\sigma_{L_{u}}\in\{\pm 1\}^{L_{u}}

on the leaves of
T_{u}
, define the magnetization at the root
u
of
T_{u}
under
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
as

\displaystyle Z^{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}}_{u}(\sigma_{L_{u}}):=\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\hat{\sigma}_{u}=+1\,|\,\hat{\sigma}_{L_{u}}=\sigma_{L_{u}})-\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\hat{\sigma}_{u}=-1\,|\,\hat{\sigma}_{L_{u}}=\sigma_{L_{u}}),

(20)

where
\hat{\sigma}
is a random spin configuration on
T
sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
.
Furthermore, if
\sigma
is a random spin configuration sampled from
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}
, we consider the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{u}^{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}}:=Z_{u}^{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}}(\sigma_{L_{u}}).

We write this random variable simply as
Z_{u}
when
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}
are clear from the context.

If
T_{u}
consists of a single node
u
, then
Z_{u}=X_{u}
as we get to observe the spin at
u
. In general,
Z_{u}
is a random variable determined by the spin configuration
X_{L_{u}}
on the leaves of
T_{u}
and takes values in
[-1,1]
. In fact, there is a recursive structure of magnetization, first established in Borgs, Chayes, Mossel, and Roch
BCMR (06)
which we now recall.

Suppose
T_{u}
is a descendant subtree of a node
u
and let
v,w
be its two children in
T_{u}
. There are corresponding descendant subtrees rooted at these nodes with respect to
u
, which defines the magnetization at these nodes, say,
Z_{v}
and
Z_{w}
.
Then
(
BCMR, 06
, Lemma 4 and 5)
imply that, under
\mathbb{P}_{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}
,

Z_{u}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{v}Z_{v}+\hat{\theta}_{w}Z_{w}}{1+\hat{\theta}_{v}\hat{\theta}_{w}Z_{v}Z_{w}},

(21)

where
\hat{\theta}_{v}=\hat{\theta}_{\{u,v\}}
for the edge
\{u,v\}
and similarly for
w
.
Throughout the paper, we reserve

\displaystyle q(x,y):=\frac{x+y}{1+xy},

(22)

so that (
21
) reads as

Z_{u}=q(\hat{\theta}_{v}Z_{v},\hat{\theta}_{w}Z_{w}).

(23)

The magnetization
Z_{u}
at a node
u
depends implicitly on the choice of the descendant subtree
T_{u}
. As already observed in
CLRS (26)
, magnetizations can be used to describe the gradient of the log-likelihood function in the mutation probabilities on the edges. To describe the Hessian, we introduce canonical decompositions of the tree
T
with respect to either a single or two edges, which yields unambiguous choice of the descendant subtrees of all nodes.

Lemma 6

(Derivatives of the log-likelihood and magnetization)
.

The following formulas hold.

(i)

(Gradient) For edge
e=\{x,y\}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma|_{L})

\displaystyle=\frac{Z_{x}Z_{y}}{1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e}},

(24)

(ii)

(Hessian)
For edges
e=\{x,y\}
and

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{f}}Z_{y}=Z_{v}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},y_{j-1}\}}\prod_{j=0}^{N}\frac{1-(\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}Z_{w_{j}})^{2}}{\left(1+\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},y_{j-1}\}}Z_{w_{j}}Z_{y_{j-1}}\right)^{2}}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}\partial\hat{\theta}_{f}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma|_{L})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{v}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},y_{j-1}\}}\right)\prod_{j=0}^{N}\frac{\left(1-(\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}Z_{w_{j}})^{2}\right)}{\left(1+\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},y_{j-1}\}}Z_{w_{j}}Z_{y_{j-1}}\right)^{2}}.

(26)

(iii)

(Third-order derivatives) If

2.5.2
Sketch of proof of Theorem
1

As we mentioned before, the proof of Theorem

The off-diagonal entries are written as the product of strongly correlated random variables with large variances and the length of the product is proportional to the shortest-path distance between the two edges indexing the off-diagonal entry. To be more precise, first observe that, under (

) in Assumption

of the magnetization
Z_{y_{j}}=q(\xi_{j},\eta_{j})
at
y_{j}
.

Using the notation introduced above and bounding
(1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta)^{N}\leq 1
, we can rewrite the bound (
29
) on the off-diagonal entries of the Hessian as

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}\partial\hat{\theta}_{f}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma|_{L})\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{(1+\xi_{N+1}\eta_{N+1})^{2}}\prod_{{j=0}}^{N}\frac{1-\eta_{j}^{2}}{(1+\eta_{j}\xi_{j})^{2}}.

(31)

Due to the recursion in (
30
) that
\xi_{j+1}
satisfies, the
N
-fold product on the right-hand side above is in fact a function only of
\xi_{1},\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{N}
. Note that by Claim
11
,
\sigma_{w_{1}}\eta_{1},\dots,\sigma_{w_{N}}\eta_{N}
are independent of each other and of the signals
\sigma_{y}
for
y\in\gamma
. However, the terms in the product are far from being independent since the chain of recursions mixes up these independent variables. This is the key difficulty that we face in analyzing the off-diagonal entries in the Hessian. We will handle this issue by grouping four consecutive terms in the product, while regarding the first
\xi_{j}
term in each subgroup as a non-random ‘adversarial input’
\xi_{j}^{\circ}
for the four-step recursion. We now make this sketch more precise.

Note that, under (
7
) in Assumption
2
, we have
\hat{\theta}_{\{a,b\}}\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta
for all edges
\{a,b\}
and
Z_{s}\in[-1,1]
for any vertex
s
, so we have the
bounds

\displaystyle|\xi_{j}|,|\eta_{j}|\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta.

(32)

Now consider the following product of four consecutive terms in the product in (
31
):

\displaystyle\prod_{j=i-3}^{i}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}}\qquad\textup{for $3\leq i\leq N$}.

(33)

The above is a function of random variables
\xi_{i-3},\eta_{i-3},\eta_{i-2},\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i}
since they determine the value of
\xi_{i-2},\xi_{i-1},\xi_{i}
through the recursion
\xi_{j+1}:=\hat{\theta}_{j}q(\eta_{j},\xi_{j})
. Since
|\xi_{i-3}|\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta
, if we fix a deterministic value
x
with absolute value
\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta
and define random variables
\xi_{i-2}^{\circ},\xi_{i-1}^{\circ},\xi_{i}^{\circ}
using the recursion
\xi_{j+1}^{\circ}:=\hat{\theta}_{j}q(\eta_{j},\xi_{j}^{\circ})
for
j=i-3,i-2,i-1
with
\xi_{i-3}^{\circ}=x
, then

\displaystyle W_{i}=W_{i}(\eta_{i-3},\eta_{i-2},\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i})

\displaystyle:=\sup_{|x|\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta}\prod_{j=i-3}^{i}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi_{j}^{\circ}\eta_{j})^{2}}

(34)

\displaystyle\geq\prod_{j=i-3}^{i}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}}\quad\textup{for $3\leq i\leq N$},

(35)

where the inequality above holds almost surely with respect to the randomness of
\xi_{i-3},\eta_{i-3},\eta_{i-2},\eta_{i-1},\eta_{i}
. In the above we have defined random variables
W_{i}
for
3\leq i\leq N
. Similarly, define

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{N}

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{W}_{i}(\eta_{N-3},\eta_{N-2},\eta_{N-1},\eta_{N},\eta_{N+1})

(36)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|x|\leq 1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta}\frac{1}{(1+\xi^{\circ}_{N+1}\eta_{N+1})^{2}}\prod_{j=N-3}^{N}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi^{\circ}_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}},

(37)

\displaystyle\qquad\textup{where $\xi_{j+1}^{\circ}:=\hat{\theta}_{j}q(\eta_{j},\xi^{\circ}_{j})$ for $j=N-3,N-2,N-1$ with $\xi^{\circ}_{N-3}=x$}.

Then the following inequality holds almost surely:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+\xi_{N+1}\eta_{N+1})^{2}}\prod_{j=N-3}^{N}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}}\leq\widetilde{W}_{N}.

Lastly, denote

\displaystyle R_{r}

\displaystyle:=R_{r}(\xi_{0},\eta_{0},\dotsm,\eta_{r-1})=\prod_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{(1-\eta_{j}^{2})}{(1+\xi_{j}\eta_{j})^{2}}\quad\textup{for }0\leq r\leq 3,

(38)

where we set
R_{0}\equiv 1
using the convention of setting the empty product to one. Note that
in the definition of
R_{r}
, we have not replaced the random variable
\xi_{0}
with a deterministic adversarial input as we did in the definition of
W_{i}
and
\widetilde{W}_{i}
above.

With (
31
) and the notations introduced above, we deduce the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

For each edge
e\neq f
, and the notation in (
31
)–(
37
), with probability 1 it holds that

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}\partial\hat{\theta}_{f}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma|_{L})\bigg|\leq\widetilde{W}_{N}W_{N-4}W_{N-8}\dotsm W_{r+3}R_{r}\qquad\textup{ where }r:=(N+1)\mod 4.

(39)

Note that
N\equiv r+3\,(\textup{mod 4})
and so each term in the product on the right-most term in (
31
) is accounted for in the bound exactly once. See the illustration in Figure
6
.

The significance of the bound in (
39
) is that the constituent random variables are all independent from each other as stated in Lemma
9
. This is in contrast to the long-range correlations (through chains of recursions) between terms in the upper bound in (
31
). Its proof can be found in Section
4.2
.

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that
N\geq 4
and write
r=r_{N}=(N+1)\mod 4
.
Then the random variables
\widetilde{W}_{N},W_{N-4},\dotsm,W_{r+3}
and
R_{r}
are independent of each other.

It is important to note that the random variables that appear in the expression on the right-hand side of (
39
) above are independent by Lemma
9
so that we can easily bound its moments.
In other words, we have decoupled the long chain of dependence into independent blocks by “adversarializing” the input to each block of recursions. In the following sections, we will show that these variables have small expectation and finite variance. This will imply that their product has mean exponentially small in
N
and finite variance. The former is enough to deduce the population level result (Theorem
2
), while the latter is used to deduce finite-sample results.

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{j=0}^{N}\frac{\left(1-(\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}Z_{w_{j}})^{2}\right)}{\left(1+\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},w_{j}\}}\hat{\theta}_{\{y_{j},y_{j-1}\}}Z_{w_{j}}Z_{y_{j-1}}\right)^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq(C/\delta)^{N-1/2}\frac{1}{(2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta)^{2}}\textup{ when }N\textup{ is even}.

(46)

The desired statement follows from

.
∎

Next, we recall a key result from Clancy, Lyu, Roch, and Sly

Theorem 10

(Insensitivity of magnetization to parameters)
.

There exist constants
\delta_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}},c_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}},C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}},c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}},C_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}>0
depending only on the constants in

such that the following holds for any unrooted binary tree

and
\delta\leq\delta_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}
.
Fix a descendant subtree

of a node
u

denote the set of all leaves in

and suppose that
{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\in{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}_{0}(\delta)
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{0}(\delta)

(i)

(Upper tail)

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left(\sigma_{u}Z_{u}^{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}}(\sigma_{L_{u}})\geq 1-C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2}\right)\geq 1-c_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta.

(47)

(ii)

(Lower tail)

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left(\sigma_{u}Z_{u}^{{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},T_{u}}(\sigma_{L_{u}})\leq-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}\right)\leq C_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}\delta^{2}.

To conceptualize Thm.

This result is a crucial ingredient for our analysis in Sections

Suppose the two children of

4.1
Bounding the diagonal entries of the Hessian

Using Theorem
10
, we prove the estimate for the diagonal entries of the Hessian given in (
9
) in Theorem
1
.

Proof of (
9
) in Theorem
1
.

Let us fix an edge
e=\{x,y\}
. Note that, by Lemma
6
,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}}\frac{Z_{x}Z_{y}}{1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e}}=-\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}.

Heuristically, the magnetizations
Z_{x}
and
Z_{y}
are likely to be close to the spins
\sigma_{x}
and
\sigma_{y}
respectively. Hence we should have the following approximation

\displaystyle\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+Z_{x}Z_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}\approx\frac{(\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y})^{2}}{(1+\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}.

(52)

The expectation of the right-hand side above can be easily computed. Indeed, considering whether there is a flip or not on the edge
e=\{x,y\}
and recalling (
2
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y})^{2}}{(1+\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}\frac{1+\theta_{e}}{2}}_{\Theta(1)}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{(1-\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}\frac{1-\theta_{e}}{2}}_{\Theta(\delta^{-1})}=\Theta(\delta^{-1}).

In order to rigorously justify the above heuristic calculation, we need to control the error of the approximation in (
52
).
To do so, we use the trichotomy of magnetizations in (
49
) due to the reconstruction theorem (Thm.
10
) and the independence properties of unsigned magnetizations in Claim
11
.
Define events

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\quad F&:=\{\textup{flip on the edge $e=\{x,y\}$}\}=\{\sigma_{x}\neq\sigma_{y}\}\\
\quad A&:=\{\textup{good reconstruction at both $x$ and $y$}\}\\
\quad M&:=\{\textup{one moderate failure and one good reconstruction among $x$ and $y$}\}\\
\quad E&:=A\cup(F^{c}\cap M).\end{cases}

(53)

Upper bound.
Observe that the function
t\mapsto\frac{t^{2}}{(1+t\hat{\theta}_{e})^{2}}
is maximized at
t=-1
and attains the value
\frac{1}{(2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta)^{2}}=\Theta(\delta^{-2})
at
t=-1
. Therefore, extreme negative values of
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\hat{\theta}_{e}^{2}}\ell({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}};\sigma)
should occur when
Z_{x}Z_{y}
is close to
-1
. This is precisely what happens on the event
F\cap A
.
In order to show the upper bound in (
9
), note that it suffices to show that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[F\cap A]}\right]=\Omega(\delta^{-1}),

(54)

since then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[F\cap A]}\right]=\Omega(\delta^{-1}).

To show (
54
), note that on the event
F\cap A
we have
Z_{x}Z_{y}\leq-1+2C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2}
since

\displaystyle-Z_{x}Z_{y}=(\sigma_{x}Z_{x})(\sigma_{y}Z_{y})\geq(1-C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2})^{2}=1-2C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2}\geq 0.

(55)

So we get

2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta\leq 1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y}\leq 1+(1-2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta)(-1+2C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2})\leq 2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta+2C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2},

where the lower bound above holds as
Z_{x}Z_{y}\geq-1
and
2
. That is, on
F\cap A
the term
1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y}=\Theta(\delta)
. Similarly,
(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}=(-Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}\geq(1-2C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2})^{2}=\Omega(1)
on the event
F\cap A
again. Moreover, by the reconstruction theorem (Thm.
10
) and the independence property (Claim
11
),
\mathbb{P}(F\cap A)=\mathbb{P}(F)\,\mathbb{P}(A)\geq c_{\ref{eqn:pBounds}}\delta(1-C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta)^{2}=\Omega(\delta)
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[F\cap A]}\right]=\frac{\Omega(1)}{\Theta(\delta^{2})}\mathbb{P}(F\cap A)=\Omega(\delta^{-1}).

Lower bound.
To establish the lower bound in (
9
), we need a comparable upper bound for
\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}
. We do this by partitioning the sample space into
F^{c}\cap E
and
F\cup E^{c}
. Note that under Assumption
2
(in particular (
7
)) we have always
1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y}\geq 2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta
and
(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}\leq 1
.
This gives the upper bound

\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{(2c_{\ref{eqn:pHatBounds}}\delta)^{2}}

(56)

We claim that on the event
F^{c}\cap E=(F^{c}\cap A)\cup(F^{c}\cap M)
, the following stronger upper bounds of
O(1)
hold:

\displaystyle\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[F^{c}\cap A]}

\displaystyle\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[F^{c}\cap M]}\leq\frac{1}{(1-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}})^{2}}.

(57)

Indeed, the fraction in front of the indicators in the above display will be large only when
Z_{x}Z_{y}
is close to
-1
. This does not happen on the event
F^{c}\cap A
, since then
Z_{x}Z_{y}=\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}Z_{x}Z_{y}\geq 0
. Since magnetizations are between
-1
and
1
and since
\hat{\theta}_{e}\geq 0
by
2
, it follows that
Z_{x}Z_{y}\leq 1\leq 1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y}
. This yields the first inequality.
For the second inequality, note that on the event
F^{c}
we have
\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}=1
, and on the event
M
, the smallest possible value for
Z_{x}Z_{y}=\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}Z_{x}Z_{y}
is at least
-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}(1-C_{\ref{eqn:Reconstruct}}\delta^{2})\geq-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}

so we also have the trivial upper bound of
1
for the numerator. This shows the claim.

Now note that
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(E^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(F)+\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(M)=O(\delta)
by
2
and Thm.
10
. Hence
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}(F\cup E^{c})=O(\delta)
.
Combining this with (
56
) and (
57
), we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{F^{c}\cap E}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left[\frac{(Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}{(1+\hat{\theta}_{e}Z_{x}Z_{y})^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{F\cup E^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle=O(1)+O(\delta^{-2})\times O(\delta)=O(\delta^{-1})

This establishes the corresponding lower bound in (
9
).
∎

5.3
Decomposition of the sample space

We decompose the sample space into a collection of disjoint events depending on the tiers of the signals.

1.

We say that there is a
flip

at two neighboring vertices
u,v\in\{y_{1},\dots,y_{4},w_{1},\dots,w_{4}\}
if

\sigma_{u}\neq\sigma_{v}
;

2.

We say that reconstruction fails
moderately at

w_{j}
if
\sigma_{w_{j}}\eta_{j}\in[-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}},1-C_{\ref{eqn:etareconstruct}}\delta]
;

3.

We say that reconstruction fails
severely at

w_{j}
if
\sigma_{w_{j}}\eta_{j}<-c_{\ref{eqn:antiReconstruction}}
.

Using Corollary
22
, the above events are closely related to whether or not the magnetizations in Thm.
10
reconstruct the true signal well. By Claim
11
, the above events are independent for
j=1,\dots,4
.

In the remainder of the proof, when we say that there is a
flip
(resp.
failure
) we will implicitly refer to a flip happening between two of the vertices involved in equation (
59
) (resp. at one of the vertices in (
59
)). Define the events

\displaystyle M_{j}

\displaystyle:=\{\textup{there is a moderate failure at }w_{j}\},

\displaystyle S_{j}

\displaystyle:=\{\textup{there is a severe failure at }w_{j}\}

\displaystyle A_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\begin{matrix}\textup{there are exactly }i\textup{ flips,}\\
\textup{exactly }j\textup{ moderate failures, and exactly }k\textup{ severe failures}\end{matrix}\right\}.

These events

\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left(\bigcup_{i=0}^{7}\bigcup_{j=0}^{4}\bigcup_{k=0}^{4}A_{i,j,k}\right)=1.

Now by the reconstruction theorem (Thm.

) or Corollary

,

Together with (
