Development of an Evaluation Indicator System for Discharge Preparation Services for First-Time Insulin Injection Patients Based on the Delphi Method
Authors: Yaru Li, Yao Yu, Fenglian Zhang, Meng Wang, Zhiqiang Kang, Haixia Han, Xiyu Wang, Zhigang Shi, Shiyi Zhang, Caixia Xing, Yaojie Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo establish a scientific, systematic, and operational assessment framework for discharge preparation services of patients initiating insulin therapy, providing a standardised tool for regulating and evaluating transitional care quality for this patient group.MethodsBased on the “structure-process-outcome” three-dimensional quality model, drawing on literature, and after two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, a preliminary Delphi-based indicator system was established for the evaluation of the nursing quality of discharge preparation services for patients with first-time insulin injections.ResultsBoth rounds of expert questionnaire responses achieved 100% valid return rates. Expert authority coefficients were 0.953 and 0.934, with Kendall’s W values of 0.101 and 0.096. Coefficients of variation ranged from 0.048 to 0.320 and 0.066 to 0.269 (all p < 0.05). The study ultimately established three primary indicators, ten secondary indicators, and thirty tertiary indicators.ConclusionGrounded in the “structure-process-outcome” theoretical framework, this framework comprehensively covers service foundations, implementation processes, and health outcomes. It offers a practical reference for evaluating and continuously improving discharge preparation services for first-time insulin injection patients in clinical endocrinology settings, thereby supporting the enhancement of transitional care quality.

Introduction

Diabetes is a metabolic disease characterized by chronic hyperglycemia, which is caused by defects in insulin secretion and/or action1 and is accompanied by disorders of carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism.2 This impairs numerous bodily organs,1 leading to disability and life-threatening complications such as cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy.3

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is the most common form of diabetes, accounting for approximately 85–90% of all diabetes cases worldwide,3,4 placing a huge burden on individuals, families, and society. As diabetes progresses, pancreatic β-cell function gradually declines, and insulin becomes an important therapeutic tool for controlling hyperglycemia in T2DM patients. However, patients often have misunderstandings about insulin, lack awareness of standard discharge procedures, and may even refuse treatment.5 These challenges are especially apparent for people starting to take insulin for the first time. Therefore, education and management that provide scientific basis for initiating insulin therapy for discharged patients are crucial to correct understanding, improve treatment effects, and improve compliance.

As articulated by the China Elderly Care Alliance,6 discharge preparation services adopt a patient-centered multidisciplinary approach to ensure continuity of care across settings. Discharge readiness is a key indicator in determining whether a patient can safely return home.7 However, its core dimensions (eg, general disease knowledge, self-management, and psychosocial support8–11) fail to capture insulin-specific competencies, including injection technique, dose adjustment, hypoglycemia recognition and management, and psychological acceptance of lifelong injectable therapy. A recent international review further confirmed that existing tools are insufficient to meet the transition needs of insulin-naïve patients.12 Although some foreign regions have established mature discharge service systems, our country is still in the exploratory stage.8 This gap highlights the urgent need for specialized multidimensional indicator frameworks tailored for first-time insulin patients.

However, my country currently lacks unified and standardized evaluation standards for discharge preparation services for this special patient group. Although tools exist to assess hospital readiness for patients with diabetes in general or specific subtypes, structured and comprehensive assessment systems specifically designed for first-time insulin patients are still lacking. Existing general assessment tools are mainly aimed at ordinary patients with type 2 diabetes,8 elderly patients with type 2 diabetes9 and patients with gestational diabetes.10 Its core dimensions focus on general aspects such as disease knowledge,8 self-management skills and psychosocial support,11 and fail to cover specific functional aspects of initiating insulin therapy. International research on discharge preparation has been relatively mature, and some regions have established relatively complete discharge service systems, but our country is still in the exploratory stage.8

The “structure-process-outcome” (SPO) model13 provides a suitable theoretical foundation for such a framework, as it allows comprehensive evaluation of the institutional conditions (structure), care delivery processes (process), and ultimate patient health outcomes (outcome) that together define discharge preparation quality. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a preliminary Delphi-based indicator system for evaluating discharge preparation services for first-time insulin injection patients, guided by the SPO model and Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) weighting, in order to standardise quality assessment and ultimately enhance transitional care continuity.

Methods

Establishment of the Research Team

The research team comprises one senior-level head nurse from the Endocrinology Department, two physicians of intermediate level or above, two diabetes specialist nurses, and two postgraduate nursing students. The head nurse serves as team leader. Primary responsibilities of research members include: conducting literature reviews and analyses, drafting initial intervention protocols; developing expert consultation questionnaires; selecting experts and conducting consultations; synthesising expert feedback and revising protocols; performing statistical data analysis to finalise the assessment indicator system. This study has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ordos Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2025051401). All participating experts provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Experts were informed that their responses would be kept confidential, that data would be anonymized before analysis, and that participation was voluntary with the right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

Literature Review

Search Strategy

The literature review method involves consulting, collating, and analysing literature to select information relevant to the present study, thereby identifying content or perspectives consistent with the research objectives. Computerised systems were employed to search databases including China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, VIP, the Chinese Medical Association series databases, the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), the National Guidelines Resource Centre, MedLink, the Chinese Guidelines Repository, and other guideline portals, alongside PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. Searches employed a combination of subject headings and free-text terms. Chinese search terms included: “Diabetes mellitus”, “Insulin”, “Insulin injection”, “First insulin injection”, “Nursing”, “Discharge nursing”, “Discharge plan”, “Discharge preparation service”, “Discharge preparation”. English search terms: “Diabetes”, “Insulin”, “Insulin injection”, “First insulin injection”, “Nursing”, “Discharge nursing”, “Discharge plan”, “Discharge preparation service”, “Discharge preparation”. The search period was from the database establishment to August 2025. Through literature review, the current status and measures of nursing care for patients receiving their first insulin injection both domestically and internationally were identified and summarised. Additionally, relevant guidelines and expert consensus documents concerning discharge preparation services were searched for and synthesised. Search strategy using CNKI as an example: (Subject: Diabetes + Insulin + Insulin injection + First insulin injection) AND (Subject: Nursing + Discharge nursing + Discharge plan + Discharge preparation service + Discharge preparation).

Literature Screening and Data Extraction

Two researchers independently conducted literature screening and data extraction. Database search results were imported into EndNote X9. After removing duplicates, both researchers screened titles and abstracts against inclusion criteria, reading full texts where necessary to preliminarily select eligible literature. Literature initially screened by both researchers was compared; identical selections were retained, while disputed items were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher.

The “Structure-Process-Outcome” three-dimensional quality framework, proposed by Donabedian in 1966,13,14 comprehensively evaluates the structural conditions, service delivery processes, and final efficacy of healthcare interventions. Delphi Expert Consultation

Expert Selection

The selection of experts was rigorously guided by the principles of representativeness and authority. To be included, experts had to meet all of the following criteria: (1) more than 10 years of clinical, nursing, or management experience in endocrinology or diabetes specialty; (2) an in-depth understanding of diabetes medical treatment or nursing; (3) an intermediate professional title or above; (4) a bachelor’s degree or above; and (5) voluntary participation with informed consent. To ensure that diverse perspectives were represented, experts were purposively recruited from multiple professional backgrounds, including endocrinology physicians, diabetes specialist nurses, and nursing managers.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Being unable to complete two rounds of consultation; (2) Experts who self-rated as “not very familiar”, “very unfamiliar” or “generally familiar” in the first round will be excluded.

A convenience sampling strategy was used to identify potential participants from a tertiary first-class hospital in Inner Mongolia. Initially, individuals who satisfied all inclusion criteria were invited to take part. After the first round of consultation, the exclusion criteria were applied: those who did not meet the familiarity threshold or could not commit to both rounds were withdrawn. Ultimately, a panel of 18 experts, who fully completed the two-round consultation and demonstrated adequate familiarity, constituted the final sample. The sample size of 18 falls within the recommended range of 15–30 experts for Delphi studies, which balances feasibility with sufficient heterogeneity of expert opinions.24

Implementation of Expert Consultation

The questionnaire was developed based on the literature review and team discussions, consisting of three sections: (1) instructions for completion, (2) indicator importance ratings using a 5-level Likert scale (1=extremely unimportant, 5=extremely important), and indicator relevance ratings using a 4-level Likert scale (1=irrelevant, 4=highly relevant), and (3) expert background, familiarity, and judgment basis.

The consultation was conducted via Email or paper questionnaires from August to December 2025. Before the formal distribution, 3 diabetes care experts who did not participate in the formal consultation were invited to conduct a pre-test to evaluate the clarity and content validity of the questionnaire. The pre-test results showed that the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) ranged from 0.83 to 1.00, and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/UA) was 0.90, indicating that the questionnaire had good content validity.

First Round of Consultation

Experts used a 5-level Likert scale (1=extremely unimportant, 5=extremely important) to rate the importance of each indicator, and used a 4-level Likert scale (1=irrelevant, 4=highly relevant) to evaluate the correlation between indicators and preset dimensions. Meanwhile, experts’ judgment basis and self-rated familiarity information were collected.

Second Round of Consultation

A second round of questionnaire was distributed to experts who completed the first round and rated themselves as “relatively familiar” or “extremely familiar”. Statistical results of the first round (mean value, coefficient of variation) were provided for reference, and experts were invited to rate again and put forward modification suggestions.

Termination Criterion for Consultation

When more than 70% of the indicators have a mean importance score ≥ 4.0 and a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25, and experts’ opinions tend to be stable between the two rounds without new modification suggestions, consensus is considered to be reached. Consensus was defined a priori as more than 70% of indicators having a mean importance score ≥ 4.0 and a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25, with stability of expert opinions between rounds.25 Indicator deletion criterion: indicators with an importance score < 4.0 or a coefficient of variation > 0.2 will be deleted.25 To clearly demonstrate the Delphi process, this study uses a flow chart to summarize it (Figure 1). Although some indicators had CV values exceeding 0.2 in the second round, they were retained if their importance mean remained ≥ 4.0 and the research team determined, based on qualitative expert feedback and clinical relevance, that the indicator addressed a critical aspect of discharge preparation not captured by other indicators. This approach prioritizes content validity over strict statistical cutoffs while maintaining transparency in decision-making.

Statistical Analysis

Two researchers conducted data entry and database construction independently, and performed statistical analysis using SPSS 27.0. The expert enthusiasm was expressed by the effective recovery rate of the questionnaire, and ≥70% indicated high enthusiasm. The authority coefficient (Cr) was used to measure the degree of expert authority, Cr = (Judgment coefficient Ca + familiarity coefficient Cs)/2. Among them, Ca reflects the weight of theoretical analysis, practical experience, domestic and foreign data and personal intuition that experts rely on when making judgments; Cs reflects the experts’ subjective familiarity with the consultation content. Usually Cr ≥ 0.7 is considered as high expert authority.26,27 The coefficient of variation (CV) and Kendall’s harmony coefficient (W) were used to express the coordination degree of expert opinions. CV ≤ 0.25 and W-test is statistically significant (P < 0.05) indicates good coordination. The self-assessment criteria and familiarity assignments are detailed in Table 2 and Table 3.27 Based on the mean of index importance in the second round, the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was used to construct pairwise comparison judgment matrices following the Saaty 1–9 scale (where 1 indicates equal importance and 9 indicates extreme importance). For each matrix, we calculated the local weight (eigenvector), combined weight, and consistency ratio (CR = CI/RI, where CI is the consistency index and RI is the random index). Results

Expert Profile

Eighteen experts participated in the consultation, aged 29–65 years with over 10 years of professional experience. The average age of the 18 experts involved in both rounds was (42.3 ± 5.7) years, with an average professional tenure of (18.5 ± 2.1) years. Professional Titles: Chief Physician: 2 (11.1%) Chief Nurse: 5 (27.8%) Deputy Chief Nurse: 11 (61.1%) Educational Background: Master’s Degree: 2 (11.1%) Bachelor’s Degree: 16 (88.9%).

Expert Engagement and Authority

During both rounds of correspondence, 18 expert questionnaires were dispatched and 18 were received in each instance, yielding a 100% valid response rate. This indicates a high level of engagement among the consulted experts. In the first round of expert correspondence for this study, the Cs was 0.938, Ca was 0.969, and Cr was 0.953; The second round yielded Cs = 0.950, Ca = 0.919, and Cr = 0.934. Typically, a Cr ≥ 0.7 indicates high expert authority and reliable consultation outcomes. The expert authority coefficients from both rounds of consultation in this study significantly exceeded this threshold, indicating that the consulted expert group possesses high authority. Results of Expert Consultation and Revision of the Indicator System

Based on expert feedback, several indicators were revised to enhance their clarity, practical relevance, and comprehensiveness. Under “1.2 Institutional and Regulatory Framework”, the indicator for integrity of the insulin injection education system was expanded to include the frequency of content updates, ensuring that educational materials remain current. Under “2.1 Assessment”, the patient acceptance indicator was refined to explicitly cover psychological acceptance and willingness, providing a more holistic assessment. In the “3.1 Knowledge Acquisition” dimension, the indicator on insulin carrying knowledge was broadened to encompass transportation and carrying, better capturing real‑world scenarios such as travel; the indicator for hypoglycemia management was revised to emphasise recognition and prevention as prerequisites for effective management. One indicator, “2.3.3 Clarity of Personalised Follow-up Plan Development and Communication”, was removed because its content overlapped with “2.3.2 Proportion of Discharge Plans Developed by Multidisciplinary Teams”. Additionally, a new indicator, “3.4.4 Satisfaction Score of Patient Family Members Regarding Discharge Guidance”, was introduced under “3.4 Psychological and Satisfaction” to reflect the important role of family members in post‑discharge care.

Following these revisions, the finalized discharge preparation service assessment indicator system for first-time insulin injection patients comprises three primary indicators, ten secondary indicators (three for structural quality, three for process quality, and four for outcome quality), and thirty tertiary indicators. In accordance with predefined indicator screening criteria (importance score < 4 points or coefficient of variation > 0.2), this study prioritized review of indicators exhibiting a coefficient of variation exceeding 0.2 across both rounds of consultation. Analysis by the research team revealed that although expert opinions on these indicators varied, their importance scores were all significantly higher than the 4.0 deletion threshold (the mean importance scores and standard deviations for all thirty tertiary indicators are presented in Table 6). This indicates broad expert recognition of their core value. To ensure the integrity of the core content of the indicator system and respect the collective opinion of the experts, these indicators were retained and optimized in this study. The weights for each indicator were calculated using SPSSAU, and consistency tests were performed. Results showed that the CR values for all indicator levels were below 0.100, confirming the reasonableness of the weighting. As shown in Table 6. Discussion

Despite the availability of discharge readiness instruments for general diabetes populations, a critical gap persists for the specific subgroup of patients initiating insulin therapy for the first time. These individuals face distinct challenges—heightened psychological distress, intensive knowledge demands, and complex psychomotor skill acquisition—that existing tools do not adequately capture. For example, the instruments reported by Shang Zhili11 and Wu Jiawei8 assess general diabetes self-care but overlook the unique transitional needs of insulin-naïve patients, such as injection technique mastery and hypoglycaemia-related anxiety. This omission may lead to incomplete discharge preparation evaluations and compromise early home safety. The present indicator system was therefore explicitly designed to incorporate these neglected domains, including psychological readiness and skill competency, which moves beyond a purely descriptive comparison by identifying why prior tools are insufficient.

To address this gap, a Delphi-based framework was constructed around the Donabedian structure-process-outcome model, yielding three primary, ten secondary, and thirty tertiary indicators. The results showed that expert consensus of our model was robust. Although these statistics confirm strong agreement among panellists, it is essential to recognise that the indicators represent expert-derived consensus, not proven clinical effectiveness. The multidimensional structure enables a comprehensive evaluation that addresses previously fragmented and subjective assessments, yet its real-world utility, predictive validity, and impact on care processes have not been empirically tested.

The explicit metrics within the system—such as the rate of evaluation for insulin injection skill proficiency, the rate of assessment for hypoglycaemia recognition and management knowledge, and the completion rate of structured insulin education programmes—offer a standardised template that, if adopted, may help streamline service delivery and focus attention on patients’ core competencies. Nevertheless, any assertion that these indicators will directly enhance healthcare quality or patient safety must remain tentative, as the current evidence is limited to content validity derived from expert opinion.

From the patient’s perspective, the framework’s emphasis on knowledge, practical skills, psychological well-being, and satisfaction signals a deliberate shift toward patient-centred, proactive health management. Structured assessment and education hold the potential to increase the involvement and self-management capacity of patients and their families, which could, in turn, foster greater treatment confidence and possibly improve quality of life. However, claims that post-discharge treatment adherence and health outcomes improve significantly, thereby reducing disease burden, are aspirational at this stage; such benefits need to be substantiated through controlled longitudinal studies. The movement from “traditional care” to “health promotion” thus represents the intended direction of the system rather than a documented achievement.

Limitations

First, the expert panel was recruited exclusively from a single tertiary hospital in Inner Mongolia, which severely restricts geographic and institutional representativeness and limits the generalisability of the findings. Second, the indicator system relies entirely on expert consensus; no patient or caregiver input was incorporated, and no multicentre clinical testing has been performed. Consequently, the patient-centred relevance, feasibility, and acceptability of the indicators remain unvalidated. Third, the number of consulted experts was limited, and the statistical methodologies employed were basic. In addition, the research process lacked timely interim synthesis, and the theoretical underpinnings could be deepened. These limitations underscore that the current framework is at an early stage of development and requires rigorous field evaluation before it can be recommended for routine practice.

Future research should broaden the scope of expert consultation across diverse regions and healthcare settings to enhance the breadth and cultural applicability of the system. Collaboration with external statistical experts should be intensified to enable more sophisticated data mining, such as indicator weighting and latent structure analysis. Most importantly, multicentre prospective validation studies and pilot testing in real-world discharge planning are urgently needed to examine the system’s reliability, validity, and impact on clinical and patient-reported outcomes. Incorporating qualitative feedback from patients and nurses during pilot implementation would further strengthen the tool’s practical utility and help translate this expert-driven consensus into an evidence-based, actionable instrument.

Conclusion

Grounded in the “structure-process-outcome” model, this study developed a preliminary evaluation framework for discharge preparation services targeting first-time insulin injection patients. Through a systematic literature review and two rounds of Delphi consultation, an initial indicator system comprising 3 primary, 10 secondary, and 30 tertiary indicators was formulated. While high expert engagement (100% response rate) and authority coefficients (>0.93) suggest a degree of scientific rigour, the system should be regarded as preliminary. Future multi-centre empirical validations are needed to refine indicator weightings, incorporate clinical feedback, and test the system’s universality across diverse healthcare settings. The indicator system presented here is preliminary and requires further validation before broader application.
