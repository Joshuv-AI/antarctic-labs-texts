SML-YOLO: a lightweight insulator defect detection algorithm for transmission lines under harsh weather conditions
Authors: Guangyang Ren, Haosen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient defect detection of insulators in transmission lines is critical to ensuring the reliability and safety of power systems. However, adverse outdoor conditions such as rain, fog, and low illumination can reduce the contrast between targets and background, resulting in blurred defect details. Meanwhile, insulators often coexist with towers, conductors, and other elements within complex backgrounds, which can lead to inaccurate target segmentation and subsequently cause missed or false detections. This study introduces a lightweight insulator defect detection algorithm, SML-YOLO, designed for harsh weather scenarios. Built upon the YOLO11 framework, the algorithm achieves performance optimization through three core innovations. Specifically, StarNet-nano is proposed as the backbone network, thereby improving the perception of features without compromising the model’s lightweight architecture to improve the detection of small-scale defects. In addition, a multi-branch modulated feature pyramid network (MMFPN) is developed to optimize feature fusion while adhering to lightweight objectives, and an LSCD detection head is employed to balance model compactness with high detection accuracy. Experimental results demonstrate that SML-YOLO reduces model size by 21.1% and parameter count by 20.9%, while achieving a 2.4% improvement in detection accuracy and a 23.8% increase in detection speed. Moreover, it exhibits strong adaptability under adverse weather conditions. These findings confirm that the proposed algorithm combines lightweight design, high detection performance, and robust generalization capability. It not only provides an efficient solution for automated inspection of transmission lines, reducing manual inspection workload and minimizing maintenance downtime, but is also well-suited for deployment in resource-constrained UAV applications.

Introduction

The power grid, as the core infrastructure for national energy transmission and distribution, plays a vital role in sustaining social productivity and ensuring uninterrupted public services. Its safe and stable operation constitutes a critical foundation for the sustainable development of the economy and society1. Insulators serve a crucial role in high-voltage power transmission systems and high-speed railway catenary systems, not only provide electrical insulation and isolation to prevent leakage currents but also deliver mechanical support to withstand complex external forces such as wind load and ice accretion. They serving as an essential safety barrier ensuring reliable electric power transmission2. Accurate detection of insulator defects is an effective measure to prevent serious accidents such as line trips and large-scale blackouts, while also providing indispensable technical support for the rapid restoration of energy supply and transportation following extreme weather events such as heavy rain and ice accretion3. Therefore, timely identification and precise localization of insulator defects are crucial for ensuring the normal operation of transmission lines.

Conventional manual inspection suffers from low efficiency, harsh environmental conditions, difficult accessibility, and significant safety risks, rendering it inadequate for the demands of smart grid development. With the rapid development of smart grids and the widespread application of computing technologies and intelligent devices, vision-based inspection of transmission lines has gradually become the dominant method4. Among these methods, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been widely adopted owing to their advantages of low cost, short inspection cycles, and high mobility5. By processing UAV-acquired inspection images, potential hazards can be effectively identified, thereby reducing accident risks and enhancing grid reliability6. Furthermore, image processing and analysis not only enable the detection and localization of electrical components but also provide technical support for UAV autonomous inspection, navigation control, automatic camera focusing, and defect recognition. However, UAV-based inspection inevitably generates massive amounts of image data, which poses increasing challenges for subsequent processing and analysis.

To further advance the automation and intelligence of transmission line inspection, numerous deep learning–based approaches have been proposed for electrical equipment recognition and insulator defect detection. For instance, Wang et al.7 adopted ResNeSt as the backbone and optimized the enhanced Region Proposal Network (RPN) to improve the feature capture efficiency for small defects. Yang et al.8 modified YOLOv3 by introducing the EIoU loss function, which enhanced the overlap between predicted bounding boxes and ground-truth boxes while accelerating model convergence. Wang et al.9 proposed ML-YOLOv5, which combined depthwise separable convolution with an enhanced feature fusion module, achieving a significant improvement in detection accuracy.

Similarly, Qiang et al.10 extended YOLOv8s to detect not only insulator defects but also external hazards (e.g., bird nests), thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of its application. Focusing on small-object detection, Tao et al.11 proposed SnakeNet under the YOLOv8 framework, which notably improved localization accuracy in complex scenarios. Deng et al.12 proposed RML-YOLO, which incorporated hierarchical scale fusion features and cross-layer feature fusion strategies, thus achieving higher detection precision in multi-scale scenarios. Likewise, Lu et al.13 introduced a context conversion module into the YOLOv8 backbone to better capture contextual information, and added partial convolutions to reduce redundant computation. Scapinello et al.14 integrated YOLOv8 with an adaptive context refinement (ACR) mechanism: YOLOv8 enabled real-time detection, while the ACR mechanism leveraged multi-scale contextual information around detected objects to refine classification and localization results.

Meanwhile, Transformer-based methods have also demonstrated strong potential in insulator defect detection. For example, Liu et al.15 developed a high-resolution cross-modal translation module (HICT) and a multi-domain multi-scale enhancement module (MMA) to improve the model’s generalization ability in complex environments. Lu et al.16 proposed an enhanced deformable attention mechanism (EDAM), which incorporates gated activation functions to improve the capture of key feature points. Lu et al.17 designed a multi-scale feature fusion Transformer (MSFFT), significantly boosting the recognition accuracy of small defects. Finally, Zhang et al.18 introduced EFENet, an edge feature–enhanced Transformer network, which achieves high detection precision through frequency-varied multi-scale feature fusion.

Most of the aforementioned studies primarily focus on improving model accuracy, while paying insufficient attention to the design requirements and processing constraints of mobile platforms. For instance, Lu et al.19 combined GhostNet with a lightweight channel–spatial attention (LCSA) module, which reduces model redundancy while preserving feature extraction capability. Li et al.20 proposed LiteYOLO-ID by improving YOLOv5s, constructing a lightweight defect detection model that effectively lowers computational costs. Shen et al.21 integrated a channel–spatial attention module into the YOLOv5 backbone to enhance its representation learning ability. An improved YOLOv8 model based on bidirectional weighted feature fusion was presented in22, which maintains high detection efficiency under complex environments. In23, LDDFSF-YOLO11 was introduced to target small-scale features, achieving enhanced small-object detection performance while retaining a lightweight design. YOLOv5s-TC and LiteYOLO-ID simplify network structures but sacrifice feature representation capability, while LDDFSF-YOLO11 enhances small-object detection at the cost of increased architectural complexity. Transformer-based lightweight solutions have also emerged, such as AdIn-DETR24, which employs an adapter-enhanced architecture to detect shed defects of composite and glass insulators, and it even achieves competitive results on relatively small datasets. Moreover, Li et al.25 proposed A2MADA-YOLO, an attention-aligned multi-scale adversarial domain adaptation model tailored for foggy scenarios, which improves model robustness while controlling its size.

Overall, most existing research tends to prioritize either improving accuracy or pursuing lightweight design, but often overlooks the balance between these two aspects. As a result, models optimized solely for accuracy usually become overly complex, whereas lightweight models frequently compromise detection precision.

In this study, we propose a lightweight object detection model dedicated to insulator defect recognition. The model achieves 94.6% mAP with only 2.04 M parameters, 6.3GFLOPs, and a model size of 4.1 MB, rendering it well-suited for deployment on mobile and resource-constrained devices. This work advances insulator defect detection research by balancing accuracy and efficiency. This study makes the following key contributions:

1) A lightweight and high-performance insulator defect detection framework, SML-YOLO, is proposed to address the challenges of small-object detection and complex backgrounds under adverse weather conditions.

2) A novel StarNet-nano backbone and a multi-branch modulated feature pyramid network (MMFPN) are introduced to enhance fine-grained feature representation and adaptive multi-scale feature fusion while maintaining model compactness.

3) A lightweight shared convolutional detection (LSCD) head is developed to reduce parameter redundancy and computational cost, enabling efficient deployment on resource-constrained platforms while preserving detection accuracy.

Materials and methods

YOLO11 basic model

YOLO11 represents an efficient single-stage object detection framework, released in five configurations: n, s, m, l, and x. These versions share the same overall architecture, while their depth and width progressively increase. The architectural diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1YOLO11 network structure.

YOLO11 network structure.

The backbone of YOLO11 primarily relies on the Conv module and the C3k2 module to extract multi-scale features, thereby achieving a lightweight design while maintaining rich gradient flow. The Conv module progressively downsamples feature maps, reducing their spatial resolution while preserving key information across multiple scales (large, medium, and small). The C3k2 module, derived from the C2f module in YOLOv8, adopts a more compact dual-convolution structure combined with optimized cross-stage residual connections. This design not only accelerates inference speed but also enhances the model’s applicability in real-world deployment. The compressed feature maps are then processed by the SPPF module, which employs multi-scale pooling operations to comprehensively capture contextual information, enabling more effective feature representation. The extracted features are then forwarded to the C2PSA module, which integrates two convolutional layers with a multi-head self-attention mechanism to further strengthen feature modeling capability. The refined features are passed to the neck network for multi-level feature fusion and upsampling. Finally, the detection head employs a decoupled design, where classification and regression branches are separated to improve detection flexibility. However, while this design enhances overall performance, it inevitably introduces additional parameters and computational cost.

A lightweight design not only accelerates inference but also reduces hardware dependency and power consumption, while alleviating storage overhead, thereby facilitating deployment and updates. StarNet-nano

In YOLO11, the CSPDarknet backbone leverages a feature-splitting and merging mechanism to effectively extract low-level features such as edges, textures, and gradients, while reducing redundancy. However, its ability to preserve fine-grained information is limited in small-object detection, which constrains detection accuracy. To address this limitation, we propose StarNet-nano as a lightweight backbone alternative to YOLO11. The design is derived from the core Star operation introduced in StarNet26, which maps input features into a high-dimensional nonlinear space, thereby enhancing feature representation without significantly increasing parameter count. Essentially, the Star operation functions as a cross-channel feature pairwise product kernel, closely related to polynomial kernel functions. Through inter-layer stacking, the Star operation implicitly expands feature dimensions in an exponential manner, enabling efficient transformation from low-dimensional computation to high-dimensional representation. Its general form can be expressed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}_{1}^{T}x*{w}_{2}^{T}x.$$\end{document} This property makes the Star operation inherently well-suited for small-object detection tasks in resource-constrained environments.

StarNet-nano is constructed as a lightweight variant of StarNet-s1 to s4, retaining the same four-stage hierarchical structure as the YOLO11 backbone but with fewer convolutional layers and reduced module complexity. The overall architecture consists of four stages: one Star block in the first stage, two in the second, four in the third, and one in the fourth. Compared with StarNet-s1, StarNet-nano eliminates seven Star blocks, thereby reducing parameter count while improving inference accuracy.

The Star block, serving as the core unit of StarNet-nano, is composed of depthwise convolution, pointwise convolution, element-wise multiplication, nonlinear activation, residual connection, and a final 1 × 1 convolution. It adopts a dual-branch design: one branch extracts spatial details, while the other captures semantic information. The two branches are dynamically fused via element-wise multiplication, highlighting target-relevant features “like a magnifying glass.” Meanwhile, residual connections preserve the original features, mitigating the loss of fine details in deeper layers. Specifically, the input feature is first processed by depthwise convolution and batch normalization, then split into two branches. Each branch undergoes channel expansion and nonlinear transformation through a 1 × 1 convolution and ReLU6 activation27. Their outputs are fused via element-wise multiplication, followed by refinement through pointwise and depthwise convolutions. This mechanism introduces dynamic channel-wise feature control at the end of the Star block: the output feature is split into two parts along the channel dimension, where one part is passed through a Sigmoid activation to generate gating weights, which are then applied to the other part via element-wise multiplication to achieve channel selection. CGLU enhances feature representation by emphasizing salient features while suppressing redundant information with minimal computational overhead. Moreover, its dynamic regulation allows the network to adaptively adjust to input features, thereby improving its ability to handle complex cross-transformations. The combination of residual connections and CGLU further alleviates the vanishing gradient problem and ensures stable convergence during training.

Overall, StarNet-nano, building upon the efficient mapping capability of the Star operation, integrates Star blocks with the CGLU dynamic gating mechanism to achieve a favorable balance between feature representation and computational cost. This design not only enhances the accuracy of YOLO11 in small-object detection but also preserves lightweight characteristics and efficient inference, demonstrating strong potential for practical applications. Multi-branch modulated feature pyramid network

In the original YOLO11 architecture, the neck network employs Path Aggregation Network (PANet) as its core, which achieves multi-level feature fusion through upsampling and concatenation, while the C3k2 module is used to enhance cross-scale interaction. However, this structure exhibits two major limitations in complex scenarios. First, PANet processes all feature maps equally, lacking targeted enhancement for critical features; shallow-layer noise can thus be incorrectly introduced, leading to a higher false detection rate. Second, feature reuse efficiency remains limited, as cross-layer transmission lacks adaptive regulation. Consequently, shallow details (e.g., fine crack textures) are often overwhelmed by deep semantic features, restricting the accuracy of small-object detection.

To overcome these bottlenecks, this study proposes a Multi-branch Modulated Feature Pyramid Network (MMFPN), which integrates the Multi-branch Auxiliary Feature Pyramid Network (MAFPN)29 with the Modulated Fusion Module (MFM)30 to replace the original PANet and construct an enhanced neck structure. Specifically, MAFPN aggregates shallow detail features and deep semantic features through both bottom-up and top-down pathways, thereby providing a strong foundation for refined representation of small and medium-scale objects. MFM, in turn, substitutes the traditional concatenation operation with an attention-based adaptive weighting mechanism, avoiding the redundancy and background interference associated with equal channel treatment. The synergy of MAFPN and MFM forms an intelligent multi-branch modulated neck architecture that not only preserves the multi-scale fusion advantages of PANet but also significantly improves feature utilization and fusion effectiveness.

In implementation, MAFPN consists of two core modules: Shallow Auxiliary Fusion (SAF) and Advanced Auxiliary Fusion (AAF). The SAF module focuses on shallow-layer features, employing skip connections to deliver fine-grained details to deeper layers, thereby mitigating the weakening or loss of small-object features during transmission. The AAF module, designed for medium-object detection, integrates multi-level feature sources to generate semantically enriched deep features, strengthening both object boundary delineation and class discrimination. Notably, both modules employ the MFM to replace the conventional concatenation mechanism. MFM first extracts global contextual information to generate channel weights, then dynamically highlights critical channels while suppressing redundant ones. This adaptive fusion strategy enhances feature selectivity and computational efficiency, leading to more effective and discriminative feature representations.

Modulated fusion module

The MFM is designed to replace the conventional concatenation mechanism with a dynamic weighting strategy, as shown in Fig. 4c. Its inputs are F1 (the original neck feature) and F2 (the auxiliary feature). Specifically, the fusion process operates as follows: The combined features F1 + F2 are first processed via global average pooling (GAP) to extract contextual information. The resulting vector is passed through a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) and a Softmax function to generate a channel weight matrix A. The weight matrix is applied through element-wise multiplication (⊙) with the input features, adaptively enhancing critical channels while suppressing redundant ones. Finally, a convolutional layer aggregates the weighted features to produce the fused representation. Compared with the naive concatenation operation, MFM significantly reduces redundant information, improves computational efficiency, and maintains sensitivity to small objects even in cluttered backgrounds.

In summary, the improved MMFPN neck architecture achieves optimization on three levels: SAF ensures stable transmission of shallow details and enhances small-object detection; AAF strengthens cross-scale integration and improves the representation of medium-sized objects; and MFM dynamically assigns channel weights to reduce redundancy and increase fusion efficiency. Through their synergy, the proposed neck not only improves detection accuracy for small and medium-scale insulator defects but also maintains high inference efficiency, thereby providing high-quality input features for the detection head.

Lightweight shared convolutional detection head

The detection head is a critical component of object detection models, responsible for both classification and localization. In YOLO11, a decoupled head architecture is utilized, enabling independent processing of classification and regression tasks. While this improves detection flexibility and stability, prior research has shown that the detection head is also the primary source of computational overhead31. Excessive parameters not only increase memory consumption but also slow down inference, which limits deployment in lightweight-oriented scenarios such as UAV-based inspections.

To address this issue, we introduce a Lightweight Shared Convolutional Detection Head (LSCD), which balances accuracy and efficiency while reducing parameter size and computational cost. The LSCD is composed of three key steps. First, the input multi-scale feature maps P3, P4, and P5 (corresponding to small, medium, and large defects, respectively) are compressed using 1 × 1 convolutions, providing a foundation for lightweight processing. Second, shared convolution is applied, where a single convolutional kernel is used across P3, P4, and P5, thereby significantly reducing the number of parameters and FLOPs. However, this sharing may result in a loss of accuracy. To compensate, we integrate Group Normalization (GN): the convolved features are divided into groups along the channel dimension and normalized within each group32. This not only enhances classification and localization performance but also avoids the training instability of Batch Normalization (BN) under small batch sizes. Experiments and result analysis

Dataset

In this study, we employed the Insulator Defect Image Dataset (IDID) constructed by Lewis et al.33, which contains 1,596 high-resolution images of insulator strings across three categories: intact insulators, shell damage, and flashover damage. The dataset is publicly available on IEEE DataPort. To enhance the model’s learning capability and improve its representation of insulator features, multiple data augmentation strategies were applied. First, adjustments in brightness, color, and contrast were performed, expanding the dataset to 2,800 images. Second, to simulate complex illumination conditions encountered in real inspection scenarios—such as direct sunlight, metallic reflection, backlighting, and low-light environments—an additional 2,800 images were generated. Furthermore, to increase scene diversity, 2,100 images representing adverse weather conditions, including fog, rain, and snow, were synthesized. As a result, the final dataset consists of 7,300 images. Within the training set, the class distribution is 34.5% intact insulators, 19.5% shell-damaged insulators, and 50% flashover-damaged insulators. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and test subsets, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2, with a ratio of 7:2:1 (Table 1). It should be noted that Table 1 reports the number of images, whereas Table 2 presents the number of annotated instances. Evaluation metrics

In this study, we adopted widely used evaluation metrics for object detection, including precision, recall, mean Average Precision (mAP), number of parameters, and model size. Interpretability and failure case analysis

To provide a more rigorous understanding of model behavior, we further analyze the interpretability of the proposed SML-YOLO using gradient-based visualization techniques. Specifically, Grad-CAM and LayerCAM are employed to examine the spatial attention distribution of the model.

Grad-CAM is widely used for visual explanation; however, it tends to produce relatively coarse activation maps due to its reliance on high-level feature maps and global gradient information. Consequently, the highlighted regions may span the entire object or even surrounding context, rather than precisely localizing small defect areas. This phenomenon explains why activations sometimes appear distributed over large regions instead of focusing strictly on defect locations. It should be noted that such activations cannot be solely attributed to visualization limitations, as they may also reflect biases in the learned feature representations.

To achieve more precise localization, LayerCAM is additionally introduced46. Compared with Grad-CAM, LayerCAM utilizes pixel-wise gradient information from multiple layers, enabling finer spatial resolution and more accurate identification of salient regions.

As illustrated in Fig. 12, Grad-CAM visualizations for both YOLO11 and SML-YOLO exhibit a certain degree of background activation. These failure cases can be broadly categorized into three types: (1) structural edge bias, where prominent contour features (e.g., insulator boundaries) are incorrectly emphasized; (2) contextual co-occurrence bias, where background elements frequently associated with defects are mistakenly highlighted; and (3) insufficient response to small or low-contrast defects. Representative failure cases are visualized in Fig. 12 to support the above analysis.

As illustrated in Fig. 13, compared with YOLO11, the proposed SML-YOLO exhibits noticeably fewer irrelevant activations, indicating improved feature discrimination capability. Furthermore, LayerCAM results show that the attention of SML-YOLO is primarily concentrated on defect regions, while still revealing minor residual bias in more challenging scenarios.

These observations suggest that, although the proposed model enhances defect-oriented feature learning, it remains partially sensitive to contextual bias and structural distractions.

Overall, the combined use of Grad-CAM and LayerCAM provides complementary insights, demonstrating that SML-YOLO achieves more accurate and robust feature localization than the baseline model.

Conclusions

In this study, a lightweight and high-accuracy insulator defect detection model, SML-YOLO, is proposed to address the challenges of small-object detection and complex background interference under adverse weather conditions. By integrating the StarNet-nano backbone, MMFPN feature fusion neck, and LSCD detection head, the proposed model achieves an effective balance between detection accuracy and computational efficiency.

Experimental results demonstrate that SML-YOLO consistently outperforms the baseline YOLO11 and other lightweight YOLO-based models in terms of mAP, inference speed, and model compactness, while maintaining strong robustness across diverse environmental scenarios. These results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed architecture in enhancing small-defect representation, improving feature discrimination, and enabling real-time deployment on resource-constrained platforms such as UAV-based inspection systems.

Furthermore, interpretability analysis based on Grad-CAM and LayerCAM reveals that the proposed model is capable of focusing more accurately on defect regions, with reduced irrelevant background activations compared to the baseline. However, residual activation in non-defect regions is still observed in certain challenging cases. Rather than being solely caused by texture similarity, these phenomena are more closely related to structural edge bias, contextual co-occurrence bias, and limited sensitivity to small or low-contrast defects, as discussed in the failure case analysis.

These observations indicate that, while SML-YOLO improves defect-oriented feature learning, it still exhibits partial sensitivity to contextual interference and structural distractions.

In future work, a more systematic failure case analysis will be conducted to further investigate these limitations. Additionally, more discriminative attention mechanisms and spatial regularization strategies will be explored to suppress irrelevant activations. The framework will also be extended to broader power equipment inspection tasks, incorporating multimodal data (e.g., infrared or depth information) to enhance robustness. Finally, improving cross-domain generalization under unseen environmental conditions remains an important direction for further research.
