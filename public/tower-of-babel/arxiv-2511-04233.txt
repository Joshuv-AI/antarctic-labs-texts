Introduction

In many cases in combinatorial geometry, counting questions involving distances, slopes, collinearity, etc., can be reformulated as analogous counting questions involving grid points lying on certain algebraic varieties. A unified study of such problems began with a question of Elekes
[
2
]
about expansion of bivariate real polynomials
f(x,y)
.
Specifically, he asked: For a bivariate polynomial
f\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
and given finite sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, how small can be the image set

f(A,B)=\{f(a,b)\mid a\in A,b\in B\}.

Elekes conjectured that the image of
f
on a
n\times n
Cartesian product must be of cardinality superlinear in
n
, unless
f
has a very concrete
special form
. This was confirmed in 2000 by Elekes and Rónyai
[
3
]
who proved the following dichotomy:
Either
f
has one of the forms

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(p(x)+q(y))\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle f(x,y)

\displaystyle=h(p(x)q(y)),

(3)

for some univariate real polynomials
p,q,h
, or, otherwise, for every finite
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}
, each of size
n
, we have

|f(A,B)|=\omega(n).

(4)

In case
f
is not one of the forms in (
3
), the lower bound on
|f(A,B)|
was improved in
[
6
]
to be
\Omega(n^{4/3})
, and further improved in
[
11
]
to be
\Omega(n^{3/2})
, which is currently the best known lower bound for bivariate polynomials that are not special.

An analogue of the Elekes–Rónyai problem can be formulated for polynomials in more than two variables. The trivariate case was studied by Raz, Sharir, and De Zeeuw
[
7
]
, and the general
k
-variate case was established by Raz and Shem Tov
[
8
]
. They obtain the following result.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
7
,
8
]
).

Let
k\geq 3
and let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
. Then one of the following holds:

(i) For every
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
each of size
n
one has

f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})|=\Omega\left(n^{3/2}\right)

(ii)
f
is of one of the forms:

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=h(p_{1}(x_{1})+\cdots+p_{k}(x_{k}))

(5)

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=h(p_{1}(x_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{k}(x_{k}))

Note that the bound in Theorem
1.1
, for non-special polynomials
f
, is independent of
k
, and in particular coincides with the bound for
k=3
.
At first glance this may appear to be merely a consequence of the proof. Indeed, the argument in
[
8
]
reduces the
k
-variate case for
k\geq 4
to the trivariate case by fixing values for
k-3
of the variables. They then show that if fixing any such subset of
k-3
variables yields a special trivariate polynomial, then
f
itself, as a
k
-variate polynomial, must be special in the sense of (
5
).

It is natural to expect that increasing the number of variables should force the image of
f
to grow faster. However, certain polynomials in many variables can in effect behave like polynomials in fewer variables. For example, consider the
(k+2)
-variate polynomial

f(x,y,z_{1}\ldots,z_{k})=xy+z_{1}+z_{2}+\cdots+z_{k}.

Let
A,B,C_{1},\ldots,C_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}
, where
A,B
are arbitrary finite sets of size
n
and
C_{1}=\cdots=C_{k}=[n]
. Let

C:=C_{1}+\cdots+C_{k}=\{k,k+1,\ldots,kn\}.

Then
|C|=\Theta(n)
, and letting
g(x,y,z):=xy+z
, we have

f(A,B,C_{1},\ldots,C_{k})=g(A,B,C).

In this case, with the current techniques, it is unclear how to obtain a bound on the expansion of
f
that improves upon the trivariate result for
g
.

Our results.

In this paper, we recognize
k
-variate polynomials that are, in a precise sense, truly
k
-variate, and we improve the corresponding expansion bounds for them. More precisely, for a
k
-variate polynomial
f
, we introduce the notion of the
rank
of
f
. If
f
has rank
r
, then, in a rigorous sense, it is essentially
(r+1)
-variate, and the bound on the size of its image can be improved with an exponent that grows with
r
.

We now define the rank of a polynomial and then state our main result.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right]
and let
d_{x_{1}}
stand for the degree of
f
with respect to the variable
x_{1}
.
Write

f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d_{x_{1}}}\alpha_{i}\left(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right)x_{1}^{i}.

We consider the
coefficient map

T=T_{f,x_{1}}:\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{x_{1}}+1}
given by

\left(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right)\mapsto\left(\alpha_{0}\left(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right),\ldots,\alpha_{d_{x_{1}}}\left(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right)\right).

We define the rank of
f
with respect to the variable
x_{1}
to be

{\rm rank}_{x_{1}}(f):={\rm rank}(J_{T}),

where
J_{T}
stands for the Jacobian matrix of
T
.
Note that

0\leq{\rm rank}_{x_{1}}(f)\leq k-1.

Similarly, define
{\rm rank}_{x_{i}}(f)
, for every
i=2,\ldots,k
, where
x_{i}
plays the role of
x_{1}
.

Finally, define the
rank
of the polynomial
f
to be

{\rm rank}(f):=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}{\rm rank}_{x_{i}}(f).

Example 1.2
.

Let

f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right)=x_{1}x_{k}+x_{2}x_{k}^{2}\dots+x_{k-1}x_{k}^{k-1}.

Then
\text{rank}(f)=\text{rank}_{x_{k}}(f)=k-1
.

Example 1.3
.

Let

f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=p_{1}(x_{1})x_{k}+p_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})x_{k}^{2}+\dots+p_{k-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})x_{k}^{k-1},

where
p_{i}
is an
i
-variate polynomial that depends non-trivially on
x_{i}
.
Then
\text{rank}(f)=\text{rank}_{x_{k}}(f)=k-1
. Indeed, in this case the matrix
J_{T_{f,x_{k}}}
is upper-triangular.

We prove the following main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
and let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1}\ldots,x_{k}]
.
Assume that
{\rm rank}(f)=r\geq 2
.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds:
Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be finite, each of size
n
. Then

\lvert f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})\rvert=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{5r-4}{2r}-\varepsilon}\right),

where the constant of proportionality depends on
\deg(f)
, on
r
, and on
\varepsilon
.

We observe that
{\rm rank}(f)=1
corresponds to the special forms from Theorem
1.1
. Indeed, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
k\geq 3
and let
f\in\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1}\ldots,x_{k}\right]
. Assume that
f
depends non-trivially on each of its variables and that

{\rm rank}(f)=1.

Then
f
has one of the forms

\displaystyle f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=h\left(p_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)+\cdots+p_{k}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\ \ \text{ or}

\displaystyle f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=h\left(p_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\cdot\ldots\cdot p_{k}\left(x_{k}\right)\right),

(6)

for some univariate real polynomials
h(x),p_{1}(x),\ldots,p_{k}(x)
.

Finally,
we present an application of our results to the following Erdős-type combinatorial geometric problem.
Let
\nu
denote the moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, parameterized by

\nu(t)=(t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{d}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
P\subset\nu
be a finite set of
n
points. For any distinct
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}\in\nu
, let
\sigma=\sigma(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1})
denote the
d
-simplex which is the convex hull of
p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
{\rm vol}(\sigma)
denote its
d
-dimensional volume.
Define

\Delta(P)=\left\{{\rm vol}(\sigma(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}))\mid p_{1},\ldots,p_{d+1}\in P\right\}.

We have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\nu
be the moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
P\subset\nu
be any finite set of size
n
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

|\Delta(P)|=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{5d-4}{2d}-\varepsilon}\right),

where the implicit constant depends only on
\varepsilon
and on
d
.

Theorem
1.6
is obtained by identifying a
(d+1)
-variate polynomial
f
whose expansion over a certain
n\times\cdots\times n
grid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
corresponds to the number of distinct volumes of
d
-simplices spanned by
P
. We then show that
f
has rank
d
and apply our main Theorem
1.4
.

2
Incidences between points and algebraic curves

For a finite set of points
{\cal P}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a finite set of planar curves
{\cal C}
, we let
I({\cal P},{\cal C})
denote the set of point-curve incidences; that is

I({\cal P},{\cal C})=\{(p,\gamma)\in{\cal P}\times{\cal C}\mid p\in\gamma\}.

The classical Szemerédi–Trotter theorem
[
12
]
asserts that, for the special case where
{\cal C}
is a set of lines, and putting
m:=|{\cal P}|
and
n:=|{\cal C}|
, one has

\left|I({\cal P},{\cal C})\right|=O\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right).

Since the Szemerédi–Trotter result, many alternative proofs and analogue problems have been studied. Today incidence problems play a fundamental role in combinatorial geometry.
For our result we will need an extension of the Szemerédi–Trotter theorem to point-curve incidence problems, where the curves are algebraic and come from an
s
-dimensional family of curves. We now present the definition from Sharir–Zahl
[
9
]
.

A bivariate polynomial
h\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
of degree at most
D
is a linear combination of the form

h(x,y)=\sum_{0\leq i+j\leq D}c_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}.

Note that the number of monomials
x^{i}y^{j}
such that
0\leq i+j\leq D
is
\binom{D+2}{2}
.
In this sense, every point
\vec{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
(other than the all-zero vector) can be associated with a curve in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, given by the zeroset of the bivariate polynomial whose coefficients are the entries of
\vec{c}
.
If
\lambda\neq 0
, then
f
and
\lambda f
have the same zero-set.
Thus, the set
of algebraic curves that can be defined by a polynomial of degree at most
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
can
be identified with the points in the projective space

{\bf P}\mathbb{R}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
.

In
[
9
]
, Sharir and Zahl defined an
s
-dimensional family of plane curves of degree at most
D
to be an algebraic variety
F\subset{\bf P}\mathbf{\mathbb{R}}^{\binom{D+2}{2}}
such that
\dim(F)=s
. We will call the degree of the variety
F
the
complexity of the family
.

They then proved the following incidence bound:

Theorem 2.1

(
Sharir–Zahl
[
9
]
).

Let
\cal{F}
be an
s
-dimensional family of plane curves of degree at most
D
and complexity at most
K
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of
m
points in the plane and let
{\cal C}\subset{\cal F}
be a set of
n
plane curves.
Suppose that no two of the curves in
{\cal C}
share a common irreducible component. Then
for every
\varepsilon>0
,
we have

I\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right)=O_{\varepsilon}\left(m^{\frac{2s}{5s-4}}n^{\frac{5s-6}{5s-4}+\varepsilon}\right)+O\left(m^{2/3}n^{2/3}+m+n\right),

where the constant of proportionality depends on
s,K,D
and in the first term also on
\varepsilon
.

A special case

In this section we prove the special case of Theorem
1.4
with
r=k-1
. This will serve as a key ingredient for the proof of the general result.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
,
f\in\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1}\ldots,x_{k}\right]
, and assume that
{\rm{rank}}(f)=k-1
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0

\deg(f)
, on
k
, and on
\varepsilon

Proof.

Let
f,A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
be as in the statement. Up to renaming of the variables, we may assume without loss of generality that
{\rm rank}_{x_{1}}(f)=k-1
.
Put

B=f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})

We aim to lower bound
|B|
.
For this, consider:

S=\left\{\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k},y\right)\in A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots\times A_{k}\times B\mid y=f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)\right\}.

Notice that, for every
(a_{1}\ldots,a_{k})\in A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{k}

(a_{1}\ldots,a_{k},f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}))\in S

\lvert S\rvert=n^{k}.

(7)

We claim that, for every
\varepsilon>0

\lvert S\rvert=O\left(\lvert B\rvert^{\frac{2(k-1)}{5(k-1)-4}}n^{(k-1)(1+\varepsilon)}\right),

(8)

where the constant of proportionality depends on
\varepsilon
,

, and
\deg(f)
.
Combining (
7

n^{k}=O\left(\lvert B\rvert^{\frac{2(k-1)}{5(k-1)-4}}n^{(k-1)(1+\varepsilon)}\right)

where

, which proves the proposition.

So in order to complete the proof of Proposition
3.1

we only need to prove (
8
). Let
T=T_{f,x_{1}}
be the coefficient map defined in the introduction. By assumption,
{\rm rank}(J_{T})=k-1

such that for

\hat{T}:(x_{2}\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto(\alpha_{i_{1}}(x_{2}\ldots,x_{k}),\ldots,\alpha_{i_{k-1}}(x_{2}\ldots,x_{k})),

we have

\det J_{\hat{T}}\not\equiv 0.

Define

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b\right)\in S\mid\det J_{\hat{T}}\left(a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\right)=0\right\},

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle=S\setminus S_{0}.

Clearly

Observe that,

(9)

where the constant of proportionality depends on

We now bound

. For this, we reduce the problem into a point-curve incidence problem in the plane as follows.
With each

Note that

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=A_{0}\times B,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\left\{\gamma_{a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}}\mid\det J_{\hat{T}}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\neq 0\right\};

Let

Claim 3.2
.

We have

\lvert S^{\prime}\rvert=\Theta\left(\lvert I(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C})\rvert\right),

(10)

Proof.

By definition, if
(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k},b)\in S^{\prime}

So, to prove the claim, it suffices to show that every

For

We need to prove that

and on

By the definition of the set

\displaystyle\alpha_{i_{1}}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=c_{i_{1}}

\displaystyle\alpha_{i_{2}}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle=c_{i_{2}}

\displaystyle\alpha_{i_{k-1}}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle(a_{2},\ldots,a_{k})=(c_{i_{1}},\ldots,c_{i_{k-1}})

\displaystyle\lvert I\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right)\rvert

\displaystyle=O\left(\lvert B\rvert^{\frac{2(k-1)}{5(k-1)-4}}n^{(k-1)\left(1+\varepsilon\right)}+|B|^{\frac{2}{3}}n^{\frac{2k}{3}}+\lvert B\rvert n+n^{k-1}\right).

Note that we may assume without loss of generality that the first summand is dominant. Indeed,
the second summand is dominant if

|B|^{\frac{2(k-1)}{5(k-1)-4}}n^{(k-1)(1+\varepsilon)}\leq|B|^{\frac{2}{3}}n^{\frac{2k}{3}}

or

|B|\geq n^{\frac{5(k-1)-4}{4}+O(\varepsilon)},

which is stronger than the lower bound we wish to prove on
|B|
, for every
k\geq 3
.
Similarly, the third summand is dominant
if

|B|^{\frac{2r}{5r-4}}n^{r(1+\varepsilon)}\leq|B|n

or

|B|\geq n^{\frac{(k-2)(5(k-1)-4)}{3(k-1)-4}+\varepsilon\frac{(k-1)(5(k-1)-4)}{3(k-1)-4}},

which is better than the lower we want to prove on
|B|
for every
k
, as is easy to verify. Finally, the fourth summand is always subsumed by the first one.

Hence, either the conclusion of Proposition
3.1
holds, or we obtain

\lvert I\left(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{C}\right)\rvert=O\left(\lvert B\rvert^{\frac{2(k-1)}{5(k-1)-4}}n^{(k-1)\left(1+\varepsilon\right)}\right).

In view of Claim
3.2
and combined with (
9
), the inequality (
8
) follows. This completes the proof Proposition
3.1
.
∎

4
Proof of Theorem
1.4

The following lemma shows that the main Theorem
1.4
can in fact be reduced to the statement of Proposition
3.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1}\ldots,x_{k}\right]
and assume that
{\rm{rank}}_{x_{1}}(f)=r<k-1
.
Then, up to renaming of the variables
x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}
, we have that

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1}):=f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}\right)

is a
(r+1)
-variate polynomial in
\mathbb{R}\left[x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1}\right]
with
{\rm rank}_{x_{1}}(g)=r
,
for all
\left(y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{k-r-1}\setminus Z_{0}
, where
Z_{0}
is some subvariety of
\mathbb{R}^{k-r-1}
of codimension at least 1.

Proof.

Let
f
be as in the statement. Let
T=T_{f,x_{1}}
be the corresponding coefficient map. By assumption

{\rm rank}\left(J_{T}\right)=r<k-1.

(13)

Up to renaming the variables, we may assume without the loss of generality, that the first
r
columns of
J_{T}
, corresponding to the variables
x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1}
, are independent.
Observe that the matrix composed of the first
r
columns of
J_{T}
, is in fact the Jacobian matrix of the coefficient map
T_{g,x_{1}}
, where
g
is the
(r+1)
-variate polynomial given by

g\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1}\right):=f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}\right);

here
y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}
are regarded as constant parameters.
More concretely, we have that
{\rm rank}(J_{T_{g,x_{1}}})=r
, for every generic

(x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Let
\Delta
denote the polynomial corresponding to the sum of squares of the determinants of all the
r\times r
submatrices of
J_{T_{g,x_{1}}}
. So
\Delta
is a multivariate polynomial, and we can write

\Delta(x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})=\sum\limits_{i}\beta_{i}(y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})g_{i}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1}),

with
g_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1}]
and
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}[y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}]
.
By the definition of
\Delta
we have

{\rm rank}(J_{T_{g,x_{1}}}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}))<r
if and only if
\Delta(x_{2},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})=0
.

Thus, in view of (
13
), we have
\Delta\not\equiv 0
.
In particular,
the polynomials
\beta_{i}
are not all zero. Thus, letting

Z_{0}:=\left\{\left(y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k}\right)\mid\forall i~\beta_{i}(y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})=0\right\},

we see that
Z_{0}
has codimension at least 1.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We can now complete the proof of our main Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
and assume without loss of generality that
\deg_{x_{1}}(f)=r\leq k-2
. Apply Lemma
4.1
to
f
. Then, up to renaming of the variables, there exists an algebraic variety
Z_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{k-r-1}
, of codimension at least one, and of degree
O(1)
, such that for every
(y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k-r-1}\setminus Z_{0}
we have that the polynomial

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1})\mapsto f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1},y_{r+2},\ldots,y_{k})

is an
(r+1)
-variate polynomial of rank
r
.

Observe that, by the Schwartz–Zippel lemma, there exists
(a_{r+2},\ldots,a_{k})\in\left(A_{r+2}\times\ldots\times A_{k}\right)\setminus Z_{0}
. Thus

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1}):=f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r+1},a_{r+2},\ldots,a_{k})

satisfies
{\rm rank}_{x_{1}}(g)=r
. Thus, by Proposition
3.1
, we have that

|g(A_{1},\ldots,A_{r+1})|=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{5r-4}{2r}-\varepsilon}\right).

Noting that

g(A_{1},\ldots,A_{r+1})=f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{r+1},\{a_{r+2}\},\ldots,\{a_{k}\})\subset f(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}),

this completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

5
Characterization of rank-1 polynomials

In this section we prove Theorem
1.5
. For the proof we will use the following lemma from Raz and Shem Tov
[
8
]
.

Lemma 5.1

(
Raz–Shem Tov
[
8
, Lemma 2.3]
).

Let
f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
. Assume
that

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}{r_{1}(x_{1})}=\cdots=\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{k}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}{r_{k}(x_{k})},

(14)

for some univariate real polynomials
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
.
Then,
f
is one of the forms

\displaystyle f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=h\left(p_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)+\cdots+p_{k}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\ \ \text{ or}

\displaystyle f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=h\left(p_{1}\left(x_{1}\right)\cdot\cdots\cdot p_{k}\left(x_{k}\right)\right),

for some univariate real polynomials
h(x),p_{1}(x),\ldots,p_{k}(x)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Let
f
be as in the statement.
Since
f
depends non-trivially on each of its variables, we have in particular that

{\rm rank}_{x_{i}}(f)=1,\quad\text{ for each $i=1,\ldots,k$.}

(15)

In view of Lemma
5.1
, it suffices to show that
f
satisfies the differential equation (
14
), for some univariate polynomials
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
. By symmetry, it suffices to prove that

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}{r_{1}(x_{1})}=\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}{r_{2}(x_{2})}

(16)

We write

f\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{x_{1}}}\alpha_{i}^{(1)}\left(x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\right)x_{1}^{i}=\cdots=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{x_{k}}}\alpha_{i}^{(k)}\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1}\right)x_{k}^{i},

(17)

where
d_{x_{j}}
stands for the degree of
f
as a univariate polynomial in the variable
x_{j}
.
Then for every
j,\ell\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
, such that
j\neq\ell
, we have

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{j}}\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{x_{\ell}}}\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}^{(\ell)}\left(x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x_{\ell}},\ldots,x_{k}\right)}{\partial x_{j}}x_{\ell}^{i}.

(18)

We next show that

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}}{{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}}=u(x_{1},x_{2}),

(19)

where
u
is some rational function over
\mathbb{R}
.

In other words, we need to show that

\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}/{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}
is independent of
x_{i}
, for every
i\neq 1,2
.
By symmetry it suffices to show that this ratio is independent of the variable
x_{3}
.
Consider the first two columns of
J_{T_{f,x_{3}}}
, corresponding to derivatives with respect to
x_{1}
and to
x_{2}
. Namely,

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{2}}\\
\frac{\partial\alpha_{1}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial\alpha_{1}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial\alpha_{d_{x_{3}}}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{1}}&\frac{\partial\alpha_{d_{x_{3}}}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{2}}\end{array}\right).

Note that neither of the columns is zero, since
f
depends non-trivially in each of its variables. Moreover, since
{\rm rank}(J_{T_{f,x_{3}}})=1
, by (
15
), and using the fact that the entries of
J_{T_{f,x_{3}}}
are independent of
x_{3}
, we get that there exists a rational function
u(x_{1},x_{2},x_{4},\ldots,x_{k})
such that

\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{1}}=u(x_{1},x_{2},x_{4},\ldots,x_{k})\frac{\partial\alpha_{i}^{(3)}}{\partial x_{2}},\quad\text{for $i=0,\ldots,d_{x_{3}}$.}

Using the identity (
18
), this implies that

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}=u(x_{1},x_{2},x_{4},\ldots,x_{k});

that is, this ratio is independent of
x_{3}
.
This proves (
19
).

Repeating the analysis symmetrically for the pair
x_{1},x_{3}
and then to the pair
x_{2},x_{3}
we conclude that there exist real rational functions
v(x_{1},x_{3})
and
w(x_{2},x_{3})
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{3}}}

\displaystyle=v(x_{1},x_{3})

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{3}}}

\displaystyle=w(x_{2},x_{3}).

But then

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}=\frac{v(x_{1},x_{3})}{w(x_{2},x_{3})}=u(x_{1},x_{2}),

meaning in particular that the ratio of
v,w
is independent of
x_{3}
. Thus, setting an arbitrary value for
x_{3}
, we get that

\frac{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}}}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}}}=\frac{v(x_{1},0)}{w(x_{2},0)}.

Thus, letting
r_{1}(x_{1}):=v(x_{1},0)
and
r_{2}(x_{2}):=w(x_{2},0)
, this proves (
16
), and hence completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

6
Distinct
d
-volumes on the moment curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this section we prove Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

The
moment curve
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by
\nu(x)=(x,x^{2},\ldots,x^{d})
. Consider
d+1
point on
\nu
given by the parameters
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let

\sigma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})
denote the simplex spanned by these points. Define

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}):={\rm vol}\sigma(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}).

It suffices to prove that

{\rm rank}_{x_{d+1}}(f)=d.

(20)

Indeed, assume that (
20
) is true and let
P\subset\nu
be a set of size
n
. Let
A
denote the
x_{1}
-coordinate of the points in
P
. Then

\Delta(P)=f(A,\ldots,A)
. By Theorem
1.4
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we have

|\Delta(P)|=|f(A,\ldots,A)|=\Omega\left(n^{\frac{5d-4}{2d}-\varepsilon}\right),

as needed.

So we only need to prove (
20
). We have

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\det\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{1}^{2}&\ldots&x_{1}^{d}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&\ldots&x_{2}^{d}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&x_{d+1}&x_{d+1}^{2}&\ldots&x_{d+1}^{d}\\
\end{pmatrix},

which is the determinant of the
(d+1)\times(d+1)
-
Vandermonde matrix
.
Thus

\displaystyle f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d+1}(x_{j}-x_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{d}(x_{d+1}-x_{k})

\displaystyle=g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\hat{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}),

where

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\frac{1}{d!}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i})

is independent of
x_{d+1}
and

\hat{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1}):=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(x_{d+1}-x_{k}).

Note that
\hat{f}
can be written as

\hat{f}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}s_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})x_{d+1}^{\ell},

where

\displaystyle s_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d}x_{1}\cdots x_{d}

\displaystyle s_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{d-1}(x_{2}\cdots x_{d}+x_{1}x_{3}\cdots x_{d}+\cdots+x_{1}\cdots x_{d-1})

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle s_{d-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=-x_{1}-\cdots-x_{d}

\displaystyle s_{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=1,

are the symmetric polynomials.

Claim 6.1
.

Let

M
be the
d\times d
matrix

M=(M_{i,j})_{0\leq i\leq d-1,\;1\leq j\leq d}
given by

M_{i,j}=\frac{\partial s_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\qquad(0\leq i\leq d-1,\;1\leq j\leq d).

Then
{\rm rank}(M)=d
.

The strategy for this proof is adapted from the proof of
[
15
, Section 4]
.

Proof.

We prove that

\det M
is not the zero polynomial.
Consider the function

P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d},t)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(t-x_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{d}s_{k}\,t^{k},

which is a monic polynomial in
t
.
For
1\leq j\leq d
we have

\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{j}}(t)=-\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}(t-x_{k})=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\,t^{k}

Evaluating at
t=x_{i}
yields

\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{j}}(x_{i})=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\frac{\partial s_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}\,x_{i}^{k}=\begin{cases}0,&i\neq j,\\[4.0pt]
-\displaystyle\prod_{k\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{k}),&i=j.\end{cases}

(21)

The identity (
21
) implies

\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&x_{1}^{2}&\ldots&x_{1}^{d-1}\\
1&x_{2}&x_{2}^{2}&\ldots&x_{2}^{d-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
1&x_{d}&x_{d}^{2}&\ldots&x_{d}^{d-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}M=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{1}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{d}}(x_{d})\right).

Thus

\displaystyle\det(M)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{d}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\prod_{i\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{i})}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{d}\prod_{1\leq i\neq j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i})}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq d}(x_{j}-x_{i}).

This completes the proof of Claim
6.1
.
∎

We are now ready to prove (
20
).
Write

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d+1})=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\alpha_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})x_{d+1}^{i}.

By the above we have

\alpha_{i}=g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})s_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}),\text{ for $i=0,\ldots,d$}

Let
T=T_{f,x_{d+1}}
. We have

J_{T}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{x_{1}}s_{0}+g{s_{0}}_{x_{1}}&g_{x_{2}}s_{0}+g{s_{0}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g_{x_{d}}s_{0}+g{s_{0}}_{x_{d}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
g_{x_{1}}s_{d-1}+g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{1}}&g_{x_{2}}s_{d-1}+g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g_{x_{d}}s_{d-1}+g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{d-1}}\\
g_{x_{1}}&g_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g_{x_{d}}\end{pmatrix}.

Applying row operation to the first
d
rows, namely,
R_{i}\leftarrow R_{i}-s_{i}R_{d}
we get

\displaystyle{\rm rank}J_{T}

\displaystyle={\rm rank}\begin{pmatrix}g{s_{0}}_{x_{1}}&g{s_{0}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g{s_{0}}_{x_{d}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{1}}&g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g{s_{d-1}}_{x_{d-1}}\\
g_{x_{1}}&g_{x_{2}}&\cdots&g_{x_{d}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle={\rm rank}\begin{pmatrix}{s_{0}}_{x_{1}}&{s_{0}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&{s_{0}}_{x_{d}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
{s_{d-1}}_{x_{1}}&{s_{d-1}}_{x_{2}}&\cdots&{s_{d-1}}_{x_{d-1}}\\
g_{x_{1}}/g&g_{x_{2}}/g&\cdots&g_{x_{d}}/g\end{pmatrix}.

Note that first
d
rows of the above matrix are exactly the matrix
M
from Claim
6.1
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.6

∎
