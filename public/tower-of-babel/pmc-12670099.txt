Whole-exome ctDNA sequencing reveals intratumoral heterogeneity and drivers of relapse in locally advanced head and neck cancer
Authors: G Bruixola, J Martín-Arana, F Gimeno-Valiente, JA Carbonell-Asins, B García-Micó, B Martínez-Castedo, D González-Camblor, N Grimalt, M García-Bartolomé, C Alfaro-Cervelló, V Escorihuela, ME Iglesias, M Maroñas, D Dualde, A Cervantes, N Tarazona
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mechanisms underlying tumor evolution and treatment resistance in locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (LA-HNSCC) are not fully understood. This study used whole-exome sequencing (WES) of paired tumor tissue and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) to analyze intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) and clonal dynamics at diagnosis and relapse.Patients and methodsBetween January 2017 and September 2022, we enrolled 152 patients with LA-HNSCC treated with radical chemoradiotherapy. Tumor tissue and plasma samples were collected at baseline and at relapse. WES was carried out on DNA extracted from tissue, plasma, and germline samples. Oncogenic variants were analyzed to evaluate tumor evolution and ITH. Concordance, pathway alterations, and associations with survival were examined.ResultsOut of the 152 patients selected, 81 were excluded based on clinical criteria. Of the 71 remaining patients, 37 had the complete set of samples of adequate quality for analysis. The most frequent mutations involved TP53 (48%), KMT2D (34%), and NOTCH1 (34%) at diagnosis. Pathogenic germline variants, including actionable mutations in BRCA2, CHK2, and KIT, were identified in 13.5% of cases. Concordance between tissue and plasma was low (median 11.8% at baseline, 12.4% at relapse), with up to 67% of oncogenic variants found only in ctDNA. Longitudinal analysis revealed limited overlap (10.3%) between baseline and relapse ctDNA, with some emerging resistance-associated mutations in PI3K-mTORC2 and ATM-CHK2 pathways. Alterations in IL6-JAK-STAT3 and RHO GTPase pathways were enriched in locoregional relapses (adjusted P < 0.001). Mutations in MMP15 and PTPRE were linked to shorter locoregional progression-free survival, whereas PDE2A mutations were associated with prolonged progression-free survival.ConclusionsWES of ctDNA uncovers extensive ITH and identifies molecular drivers of resistance not captured by tissue biopsies. These findings support the use of plasma ctDNA analysis to monitor tumor evolution and personalize follow-up in LA-HNSCC, especially given the anatomical complexity that often limits repeated tissue sampling.Key words: LA-HNSCC, ctDNA, precision medicine, intratumor heterogeneity, biomarkers

Introduction

Locally advanced head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (LA-HNSCC) accounts for ∼60% of new head and neck cancer diagnoses.1 Despite advances in treatment, including multimodal approaches such as surgery with adjuvant radiotherapy (RT) with or without chemotherapy, definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT), and RT with cetuximab in patients unfit for CRT,2, 3, 4 the rates of locoregional recurrence and distant metastases remain high, reaching 60% and 30% at 5 years, respectively.5,6

The prognosis for recurrent or metastatic (R/M) HNSCC is poor,2 with a median overall survival (OS) of 12-15 months even in the era of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed cell death protein 1 in first-line7 or platinum-refractory settings.8 Although these therapies can achieve durable responses in a subset of patients, subgroup analyses from pivotal trials indicate limited benefit in those with isolated locoregional recurrence. This has led to trials investigating the addition of ICIs to curative-intent treatment strategies in LA-HNSCC. However, randomized studies combining ICIs with CRT have so far failed to demonstrate significant improvements in efficacy.9,10 Nevertheless, early results from the KEYNOTE-689 trial11 suggest a potential benefit in selected operable patients. No targeted therapy has yet shown survival advantage in either LA-HNSCC or R/M settings.3,4

Intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH), defined by the coexistence of genetically or phenotypically distinct subpopulations within a single tumor, plays a critical role in cancer progression and resistance to therapy.12 In head and neck cancers, high ITH complicates diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment planning, as resistant subclones can evade detection and expand under therapeutic pressure.13 Single-site biopsies often fail to capture this diversity, limiting their ability to identify subclonal mutations that drive relapse.14

Liquid biopsy based on ctDNA analysis offers a minimally invasive, dynamic approach to assess both minimal residual disease and ITH.15 By capturing spatial and temporal subclonal evolution, ctDNA sequencing enables early detection of resistance-associated mutations, providing opportunities for real-time adjustment of therapy and personalized surveillance strategies.16, 17, 18 Although previous studies have shown that ctDNA reflects clinically relevant heterogeneity in head and neck cancers, the application of whole-exome sequencing (WES) to ctDNA for comprehensive characterization of genomic drivers of locoregional progression in LA-HNSCC has not been explored.

To address this gap, we carried out WES on paired tumor tissue and plasma samples collected at diagnosis and relapse from patients with LA-HNSCC treated with definitive CRT. Our objectives were to (i) evaluate the concordance between tissue and plasma, (ii) identify actionable mutations detectable only in ctDNA, (iii) map clonal dynamics and pathway shifts associated with treatment resistance, and (iv) define molecular drivers of locoregional relapse. These findings may provide a basis for improving risk stratification and tailoring follow-up in patients with LA-HNSCC.

Patients and methods

Study design and patient selection

The inclusion of patients and the collection of samples were carried out prospectively between January 2017 and September 2022, whereas the analysis was subsequently conducted retrospectively at Hospital Clínico Universitario de Valencia, Spain. Eligible participants were patients diagnosed with LA-HNSCC deemed suitable for definitive chemoradiation by the multidisciplinary tumor board. Inclusion criteria required availability of tumor tissue and plasma samples at baseline and at relapse (Figure 1). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample collection, DNA extraction, quality control and whole-exome sequencing

A detailed description of sample collection, DNA extraction, quality control, and WES is presented in Supplementary Materials and Methods 1 and the workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904.

Clinical variables and data storage

Clinical and histopathological data collected at diagnosis included age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Group (ECOG) performance status, tumor site, human papillomavirus (HPV)/p16 status, smoking and alcohol history, and TNM (tumor–node–metastasis) stage according to the eighth edition classification. Treatment-related variables, such as radiotherapy dates, concurrent systemic therapy, type of recurrence (locoregional or distant), and date of relapse, were also recorded. All data were extracted from electronic medical records and entered into a secure, anonymized database in accordance with applicable data protection regulations.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Detailed procedures about bioinformatics, somatic variant and copy number analysis, tumor mutational burden (TMB), clonality and tropism analysis are described in Supplementary Materials and Methods S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904.

Endpoints and statistical methods

This study was exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The exploratory endpoints were: (i) concordance of mutations between tumor tissue and plasma; (ii) identification of actionable alterations; (iii) pathway enrichment analyses; and (iv) associations of specific mutations and TMB with progression-free survival (PFS) and OS. Survival outcomes were estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to explore associations with genomic alterations. To identify mutations associated with survival, regularization methods were applied to address the high number of potential variables relative to the sample size. Cross-validation was used to determine the optimal lambda parameter for variable selection. Significant mutations were subsequently analyzed using Cox regression, and those with P values < 0.05 were retained in the final survival models.

Comparisons of TMB between tissue and plasma, and between baseline and relapse, were carried out using a mixed-effects model with patient identity as a random effect and sample type/timepoint as fixed effects. Pathway enrichment was tested with a one-sided hypergeometric test and false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Clinical and demographic variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test for categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables.

At first response evaluation, 6 patients (16.2%) achieved complete response, 17 (45.9%) partial response, 1 (2.7%) had stable disease, and 13 (35.1%) showed disease progression. Most patients were heavy smokers (86.5% with ≥10 pack-years), and 51.4% reported regular alcohol consumption.

Primary tumor sites were distributed as follows: oral cavity (21/37, 56.8%), larynx (8/37, 21.6%), oropharynx (7/37, 18.9%), and hypopharynx (1/37, 2.7%). According to the American Joint Committee on Cancer, eighth edition staging system, 5 patients (13.5%) had stage III, 20 (54.1%) stage IVA, and 12 (32.4%) stage IVB disease.

Over a median follow-up of 29.5 months (range 2.0-99.1 months), the median PFS was 9.2 months (range 0.6-67.6 months) and the median OS was 17.5 months (range 2.3-99.0 months). Patterns of relapse included locoregional only in 20 patients (54.1%), distant only in 5 (13.5%), and combined locoregional plus distant recurrence in 12 (32.4%). HPV status was negative in 25 cases (67.6%), positive in 2 (5.4%), and undetermined in 10 (27.0%). Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904 shows the Kaplan–Meier curves for PFS of the 37 included cases and the remaining patients. Importantly, there were no significant differences in survival outcomes between patients excluded due to sample quality issues and those retained for analysis.

Plasma ctDNA profiling uncovers intratumoral heterogeneity

To delineate the genomic landscape of LA-HNSCC and evaluate how comprehensively plasma ctDNA reflects tumor complexity, we carried out paired analysis of single nucleotide variant (SNVs) and insertions/deletions in tumor tissue and plasma samples from 30 patients at diagnosis and 13 patients at relapse.

All 30 baseline and 13 relapse patients exhibited at least one somatic mutation detectable in plasma. The average tissue-ctDNA concordance was only 11.8%, underscoring limited overlap (Figure 2A). Despite this, up to 67% of oncogenic variants were identified exclusively in plasma, including mutations in BRCA2 (13%), EP400 (13%), ANKRD11 (13%), and SMARCA4 (13%), suggesting that ctDNA captures non-ubiquitous alterations present in unsampled tumor regions (n = 30, Figure 2B). These plasma-exclusive mutations likely reflect genetic diversity across spatially distinct tumor areas or emerging subclones.

To further investigate treatment-induced late-emerging mutations, we analyzed paired tumor tissue and ctDNA from 13 patients at relapse. Tumor samples at relapse showed a predominance of KMT2D mutations (54%), followed by alterations in BCL10 (23%) and EP400 (23%) (Supplementary Figure S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904). A core set of genes, including TP53 (85%), KMT2D (54%), NOTCH1 (38%), BCL10 (23%), EP400 (23%), KMT2C (23%), MED12 (23%), MSH6 (23%), harbored mutations in both tumor tissue and matching plasma ctDNA. However, the differing mutation frequencies between these two sample types highlight ongoing clonal evolution and demonstrate the superiority of liquid biopsy over single-site tissue sampling in capturing the full subclonal diversity of the tumor.

In contrast, ctDNA samples obtained at relapse revealed distinct mutational profiles, with mutations in genes such as PTPR (11%), and SOX9 (11%) detected exclusively in ctDNA, underscoring its capacity to capture genetic events from tumor clones not sampled by tissue biopsy and the emergence of subclones driven by treatment pressure. Concordant tissue-plasma mutations exhibited significantly higher variant allele frequency than plasma-exclusive variants at baseline (n = 37, P = 0.0001 t-test) and at relapse (n = 10, P = 4.187e-05; t-test).

Notably, no distinct mutational clustering was associated with smoking status, pack-years, HPV status, sex, age, TMB, or type of concurrent systemic therapy (cisplatin versus cetuximab) (Supplementary Figures S4-S6, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904), suggesting a broad distribution of genetic alterations across tumor sites and underscoring the intrinsic heterogeneity of LA-HNSCC tumors.

Finally, primary tumor DNA harbored both clonal and subclonal variants, with significant enrichment of RND3/RHO GTPase pathway mutations (CKAP4, CPD, NISCH, PLEKHG5, ROCK1, SCRIB, TNFAIP; adjusted P = 0.041), pointing to early functional diversification through RHO signaling, a pathway involved in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell polarity, and migration (Figure 2C).

Molecular mechanisms underlying progression after CRT relapse

To investigate temporal clonal shifts, we compared ctDNA profiles obtained at diagnosis and relapse (Figure 3A). As shown in Figure 2A, concordance between baseline and relapse plasma samples was notably low (10.3%), with 50% of oncogenic mutations detected exclusively in relapse plasma. A core set of genes, including KMT2D, NOTCH1, TP53, KMT2C, MED12, STAG2, BRCA2, RASA1, EP400, MLH1, SMARCA4, and DNMT3, were recurrently altered across both timepoints, with mutation frequencies ranging from 22%-49% (Figure 3B).

Interestingly, TP53 mutations were detected in baseline ctDNA from 14 of 37 patients (37.8%), of whom 4 (28.6% of the 14 patients; 10.8% of the cohort) maintained the mutation at relapse, whereas it was no longer detectable in 10 (71.4% of those 14; 27.0% of the cohort). Conversely, TP53 mutations emerged de novo at relapse in one (2.7%) patient who was wild-type at baseline. By contrast, ARID1B, PTPR, and SOX9 mutations appeared de novo at relapse in four patients each (10.8%), all of whom were wild-type for these genes at baseline; notably, ARID1B had been mutated in one patient (2.7%) at baseline. Four patients (10.8%) had no longer detectable TP53 mutation at relapse and acquired at least one ARID1B, PTPR, or SOX9 mutation at relapse. These findings shed light on the dynamics of clonal evolution and highlight the utility of serial ctDNA analysis for monitoring treatment resistance and disease recurrence (Supplementary Figure S7, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904). Additionally, Supplementary Figure S8, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904 illustrates the comparative mutational landscape between relapse and baseline tumor tissues.

To gain insight into the biological processes contributing to tumor progression following standard CRT in LA-HNSCC, we implemented three integrative analytical strategies focusing on relapse-specific genomic alterations.

Firstly, pathway enrichment analysis of ctDNA from relapse samples revealed significant overrepresentation of genes involved in serine/threonine kinase (STK) signaling (adjusted P = 0.0073). Notably, this enrichment included components of the PI3K-mTORC2, ATM, and CHK2 pathways, none of which were altered at baseline. These findings suggest that in response to therapy, tumors may undergo adaptive reprogramming of signaling networks, potentially contributing to treatment resistance and clonal outgrowth at relapse.

Secondly, stratification of relapse cases by recurrence pattern revealed somatic mutations specifically enriched in locoregional progression. In particular, the RHOU GTPase and IL6-JAK-STAT3 pathways, both implicated in cell migration, immune evasion, and lymphatic dissemination, showed substantial mutation rates. Among genes within these pathways, HGS, PAK2, and PTK2B were significantly mutated (adjusted P = 0.00027), as were FAS, SOCS, and ITGB3 (adjusted P = 0.0023). These alterations suggest a molecular basis for nodal relapse. In contrast, not statistically significant changes were identified in cases of distant metastasis, likely due to the limited number of samples analyzed, suggesting that distinct molecular mechanisms may underlie locoregional versus metastatic progression (Figure 3C).

Thirdly, to determine whether the observed pathway enrichments were influenced by treatment-induced mutational processes, we carried out a comprehensive mutational signature analysis on relapse samples. This analysis sought to detect known COSMIC mutational signatures associated with therapeutic exposure, such as those linked to platinum chemotherapy or radiation-induced DNA damage. However, no treatment-related signatures were identified (Supplementary Figure S9, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904), suggesting that the enriched pathways observed in relapse ctDNA are unlikely to result directly from therapy-induced genotoxicity. Instead, these findings support a model in which treatment exerts selective pressure that promotes expansion of resistant subclones harboring pre-existing or newly acquired alterations.

Finally, to assess the prognostic value of these genomic alterations, we analyzed associations between somatic mutations and disease-free survival in patients with locoregional relapse. Recurrent mutations in MMP15 and in PTPRE were significantly associated with a shorter PFS. On the contrary, a significant prolongation of PFS was found among patients presenting PDE2A mutations. These genes are involved in extracellular matrix remodeling, signal transduction, and cAMP metabolism, mechanisms that may facilitate tumor regrowth in the irradiated microenvironment. Kaplan–Meier survival curves further confirmed the prognostic relevance of these mutations, underscoring their potential as biomarkers for predicting relapse. Detailed data and survival analysis results are presented in Supplementary Table S1 and Figure S10, respectively, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904.

Tumor mutational burden analyses

In contrast to these gene-specific associations, analysis of TMB did not reveal prognostic value in our cohort. TMB was calculated in both tumor tissue (tTMB) and plasma (bTMB) using whole-exome sequencing. Across the cohort, bTMB was significantly higher than tTMB, with a mean reduction of −34.95 mutations/Mb when comparing plasma with tissue samples (P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in TMB between baseline and relapse samples (P = 0.061). Survival analyses showed no significant association between TMB and either OS (P = 0.159) or PFS (P = 0.485). A mixed-effects model confirmed that the higher TMB values observed in plasma compared with tissue were consistent across timepoints, independently of whether samples were collected at baseline or relapse (Supplementary Figure S11, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904).

In relapse tumor tissue, eight actionable mutations were detected, all of which were potentially amenable to approved or investigational therapies. Notably, EZH2 and ARID1 mutations were observed in 15.3% and 7.6% of patients, respectively, representing candidates for treatment with the EZH2 inhibitor tazemetostat. Additionally, alterations in homologous recombination repair (HRR) genes, including BRCA2, ATM, CHEK2, and PALB2, suggest potential sensitivity to poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors such as olaparib and niraparib.

By contrast, ctDNA analysis at relapse revealed a substantially broader and more diverse repertoire of actionable alterations. Specifically, 67.57% (25/37) and 78.38% (29/37) of patients had clinically actionable mutations detected in plasma at baseline and relapse, respectively. At relapse, a total of 58 actionable mutations spanning 28 different genes were identified, expanding the landscape of potential therapeutic targets considerably. This enhanced detection highlights the added value of ctDNA profiling for capturing intratumoral heterogeneity and emergent resistance mechanisms. Indeed, if treatment decisions had relied solely on tumor tissue analysis, therapeutic options would have been limited to tazemetostat and PARP inhibitors, whereas ctDNA profiling revealed a wider spectrum of actionable mutations.

Importantly, 72.9% of patients harbored mutations potentially responsive to PARP inhibitors, as indicated by alterations in key DNA repair genes. Furthermore, 27.1% of patients presented mutations in the TSC2, MTOR, or TSC1 genes, supporting eligibility for mTOR inhibition with agents such as everolimus. Additional alterations in PIK3CA, EGFR, HER2, and PDGFRA further expanded the range of possible targeted interventions. These findings collectively highlight the clinical relevance of ctDNA profiling in uncovering treatment opportunities that may be overlooked by conventional tissue biopsy alone.

Finally, WES analysis of white blood cells identified pathogenic germline variants in five patients (5/37, 13.5%), involving genes related to HRR (BRCA2, CHECK2), proliferative signaling (KIT), RNA splicing regulation (RBM10), and angiogenesis (KDR), all of which may have therapeutic or surveillance implications (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105904).

Discussion

LA-HNSCC remains a major clinical challenge due to high rates of recurrence despite aggressive multimodal treatment. Our study provides novel insights into the molecular landscape and evolutionary dynamics of LA-HNSCC, with a focus on ITH, clonal selection, and resistance mechanisms emerging after CRT.

The analytic cohort was enriched for patients with progression, including many early progressors. This design explains the relatively high rate of progression at first evaluation (35.1%) and the shorter median PFS (9.2 months) and OS (17.5 months) compared with unselected series. Although this limits direct comparability with trial outcomes, it allowed us to specifically investigate aggressive disease courses and early resistance, which represent the greatest unmet need.

Patients received either cisplatin-based CRT (48.6%) or radiotherapy with cetuximab (51.4%), reflecting real-world practice during the COVID-19 pandemic. Many individuals were cisplatin-ineligible due to comorbidities, and cetuximab was sometimes chosen to reduce infection risk. Stratified analyses did not reveal significant molecular differences by regimen, suggesting resistance mechanisms are not primarily treatment driven. Treatment heterogeneity remains a limitation, but it enhances representativeness for contemporary practice.

A relatively high proportion of oral cavity tumors were treated with definitive CRT. Although surgery is the standard for resectable oral cavity disease, many cases were unresectable or inoperable, often exacerbated by pandemic-related delays. All decisions were made within a multidisciplinary board, supporting the validity of the selection process. Although this limits generalizability, it provides insight into a high-risk subgroup with considerable unmet need.

A key finding was the extensive ITH observed at both diagnosis and relapse, highlighted by the low concordance between mutations in tumor tissue and plasma ctDNA (11.8% at baseline and 12.4% at relapse). Similar low tissue-plasma concordance has been reported in other tumors with high heterogeneity, such as pancreatic cancer, where single-site biopsies fail to capture subclonal diversity.19 Our data underscore the limitations of relying solely on tissue sampling and emphasize the complementary value of ctDNA in detecting mutations from spatially distinct tumor regions or emergent subclones.

Longitudinal ctDNA analysis revealed substantial genomic evolution, with only 10.3% of mutations shared between baseline and relapse. Over half of oncogenic alterations detected at relapse were absent at diagnosis, emphasizing adaptive clonal dynamics under therapeutic pressure. These findings align with previous studies in HNSCC by De Roest et al.13 and Pierik et al.,14 which described significant clonal shifts in recurrent tumors after CRT, although neither incorporated longitudinal ctDNA-WES as in our work. Importantly, the scale of our ctDNA-WES analysis captures a wider mutational range than prior targeted panels, enabling the discovery of novel alterations relevant to treatment resistance. Consistent with this dynamic, we observed that TP53 mutations present at baseline but no longer detectable at relapse may reflect either biological evolution or technical limitations, in line with recent ctDNA studies.20, 21, 22

Our study extends findings from colorectal cancer research,15 and more recently ctDNA-WES data,16 which demonstrated that plasma sequencing captures subclonal evolution and minimal residual disease undetectable by conventional imaging or tissue biopsy. Translating these insights to LA-HNSCC, our ctDNA profiling identifies dynamic molecular changes with potential for real-time monitoring and personalized adaptation of therapy.

We found that ctDNA at relapse harbors a far richer repertoire of actionable mutations than tissue analysis alone. Although tumor biopsies identified only eight actionable alterations, limited mainly to EZH2 and HRR genes, plasma ctDNA revealed 58 actionable mutations across 28 genes, including PIK3CA, EGFR, HER2, PDGFRA, and multiple mTOR pathway components. This broader landscape substantially expands potential therapeutic opportunities and underscores the clinical value of ctDNA for uncovering emergent resistance mechanisms.17

Pathway-level analyses identified significant enrichment of serine/threonine kinase signaling (PI3K-mTORC2, ATM, CHK2; adjusted P = 0.0073) and persistent activation of IL6-JAK-STAT3, both known mediators of resistance to radiotherapy and platinum-based chemotherapy.23 These findings echo preclinical and clinical studies implicating PI3K/AKT/mTOR activation and STAT3 signaling in treatment failure.23, 24, 25, 26 Trials of PI3K/mTOR inhibitors (e.g. buparlisib, duvelisib) and emerging STAT3 inhibitors may offer therapeutic strategies to counter these pathways in recurrent disease.27, 28, 29

Our stratified analyses revealed mutations enriched in locoregional relapses, particularly in genes modulating cell adhesion, lymphatic dissemination, and immune evasion (HGS, PAK2, PTK2B, SOCS, ITGB3), suggesting that distinct molecular mechanisms underpin nodal progression. Conversely, no clear drivers of distant metastasis were identified, likely reflecting limited sample size. From a prognostic standpoint, mutations in MMP15, PTPRE, PDE2A were associated with different locoregional PFS, highlighting their potential as biomarkers to guide risk stratification and surveillance strategies.30,31

In our study, TMB was higher in plasma than in tissue, likely reflecting broader tumor heterogeneity captured by ctDNA. TMB remained stable between baseline and relapse and showed no association with survival, indicating limited prognostic value in LA-HNSCC.

Furthermore, the value of ctDNA as a tool for monitoring disease dynamics and minimal residual disease (MRD) in LA-HNSCC is increasingly supported by recent studies. Honoré et al. developed a tumor-independent ctDNA assay for MRD detection using a 26-gene panel, demonstrating a correlation of after treatment ctDNA positivity with significantly worse PFS and OS; importantly, this method circumvents the need for prior tumor sequencing, enhancing its feasibility for routine clinical implementation.32 Similarly, Hanna et al. assessed a personalized ctDNA assay in HPV-negative LA-HNSCC, showing that after treatment ctDNA positivity strongly predicted poor outcomes.33 Sim et al. demonstrated that early post-operative MRD detection using the MAESTRO assay was accurately associated with recurrence and worse OS and event-free survival, even at ultralow tumor fractions, highlighting the need for ultrasensitive methods to optimize adjuvant therapy decision-making in HNSCC.34 Together, these findings reinforce the prognostic value of ctDNA and also suggest that serial plasma monitoring could play a critical role in guiding therapeutic decisions and early intervention strategies.

Despite these strengths, our study has limitations, including the relatively small cohort size, the heterogeneity of systemic treatment (cisplatin versus cetuximab)—partly influenced by clinical constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic—and the lack of external validation, which together limit the strength of definitive conclusions about the predictive value of individual alterations. Moreover, as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating analysis, no formal statistical hypothesis testing or pre-specified statistical analysis plan was defined. Nonetheless, the rarity of comprehensive longitudinal ctDNA-WES studies in LA-HNSCC positions our work as an important exploratory step that lays the groundwork for future multicenter confirmatory studies.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ctDNA profiling reveals extensive ITH, captures real-time tumor evolution, and identifies actionable alterations that may inform therapeutic decisions. Integrating ctDNA analysis into routine management could enhance risk stratification, enable biomarker-driven trial enrollment, and personalize treatment strategies in LA-HNSCC.

Conclusion

In summary, our study highlights the dynamic and heterogeneous molecular landscape of LA-HNSCC, demonstrating the utility of integrated tissue and ctDNA sequencing for comprehensive profiling of tumor evolution and resistance mechanisms. By identifying key pathways such as IL6-JAK-STAT3 and PI3K/AKT/mTOR, along with novel prognostic mutations like MMP15, PTPRE, and PDE2A, our findings provide a rationale for biomarker-driven strategies to improve outcomes in recurrent LA-HNSCC. Prospective validation in larger, independent cohorts will be essential to confirm these findings and support their integration into clinical practice.
