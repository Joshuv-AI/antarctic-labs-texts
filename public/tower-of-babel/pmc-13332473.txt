Biological applications and future perspectives of bioactive compounds as pig feed additives
Authors: Yang Hao, Yuqiang Qian, Da Liu, Fuhou Li, Hengzong Zhou, Minjun Jin, Chengcheng Li, Tianjia Liu, Dongxu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Restrictions or prohibitions on the use of antibiotics in pig feed for growth promotion have been introduced in many countries. Bioactive compounds (BCs) as functional additives are emerging as an alternative to overcome these restrictions. These compounds include natural extracts from animals, plants, and microorganisms, as well as their chemically synthesized counterparts. Their growing popularity is due to their abundant sources, safety, and multiple biological functions, including antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and intestinal health-promoting effects. This review summarizes the various types of BCs developed to date, including polyphenols, polysaccharides, aldehydes, terpenes, and polypeptides, and their use in pig production. It also discusses how these BCs can be used in pig farming, particularly to improve sows' reproductive performance, reduce diarrhea in piglets, and enhance the meat quality of finishing pigs. Despite these functions, the broader application of BCs remains limited due to the challenges such as poor water solubility, low stability, intestinal malabsorption, and low bioavailability. These challenges can potentially be addressed through emerging technologies, particularly nanotechnology, genetic engineering, and artificial intelligence. This review also highlights how these technologies may facilitate the translation of BCs into practical applications within commercial pig production. Finally, this review focuses on future perspectives of BCs in pig feed by exploring how advances in cell biology, materials science, and genetic engineering may interact to shape the future trajectory of BCs use in the pig industry.

Introduction

Pork is the most widely consumed meat globally, and the demand for it continues to grow. Intensive pig farming has markedly improved production efficiency, but it also faces challenges (Martinez-Miro et al., 2016). Major issues that requiring resolution include declining sow fertility, high incidence of diarrhea in piglets, and reduced pork quality during the fattening period (Wang and Li, 2024). Bioactive compounds (BCs), such as polyphenols, polysaccharides, aldehydes, terpenes, and polypeptides, are a class of chemical substances that are not considered essential nutrients but can modulate bioactive functions and participate in physiological processes (Song et al., 2025). These bioactive molecules are derived from diverse sources, and include natural products generated through the secondary metabolic processes of animals, plants, and microorganisms, as well as structurally analogous molecules produced via chemical synthesis or bioengineering techniques (Camara et al., 2020). In the livestock industry, BCs have emerged as promising additives for improving pig health, growth performance, and feed quality, especially in the context of increasing restrictions on the use of antibiotics in pig feed (Gadde et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018). However, the low concentrations of target BCs in their natural sources lead to high extraction costs, and their overharvesting can harm the environment. In addition, the composition of naturally derived BCs varies considerably due to factors such as geographical origin, seasonal changes, cultivation practices, developmental stage of the organisms producing them, and their exposure to environmental contaminants. These variations can lead to notable inconsistencies in the composition and concentration of BCs across different extracts (Figueiredo et al., 2008). Moreover, differences in extraction, processing, and characterization techniques further contribute to batch-to-batch heterogeneity (Osorio-Tobon, 2020). In the face of this range of challenges, expectations are growing regarding the potential to apply innovative strategies integrating biotechnology approaches such as genetic engineering, metabolic engineering, and synthetic biology. Most BCs also have some inherent drawbacks in terms of their pharmacokinetics, including low water solubility, poor stability during storage, susceptibility to degradation, and poor bioavailability (Kurien and Scofield, 2009). To overcome these limitations and achieve consistent improvements in animal performance, various BC delivery systems have been developed. These include organic nanoparticles (NPs), such as liposomes, lipid NPs, polymeric NPs, and exosomes, as well as inorganic NPs, including those consisting of silver, silica, and zinc oxide (ZnO) (Siddiqui et al., 2022). The choice of BC delivery system depends on the particular BCs to be delivered and the route of administration.

This review summarizes the major classes of BCs and highlights their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and gut-modulatory functions and mechanisms relevant to pig nutrition. Special emphasis is placed on BCs with well-defined compositions that have been shown to improve sow productivity, prevent diarrhea in piglets, and enhance meat quality in finishing pigs. By also discussing the roles of emerging biotechnologies in improving the production, bioavailability, and cost-effectiveness of BCs, this review aims to provide novel insights into BC development for the production of high-quality pigs.

Bioactive compounds as feed additives for pigs

The BCs are classified into 18 major categories, including essential oils, polyphenols, polysaccharides, saponins, carotenes, terpenoids, polypeptides, and alkaloids (Fig. 1). These compounds are primarily extracted from plants, animals, and microorganisms. Terpenoids, also known as isoprenoids, represent a large family of natural substances predominantly derived from plants, for which over 60,000 structures have been identified (Christianson, 2017). Major subclasses include monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, diterpenes, triterpenes, and their derivatives, many of which exhibit immunomodulatory, antiviral, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-ulcer activities (Masyita et al., 2022). Limonene, a type of monoterpene, imparts the characteristic aroma of citrus fruits, while artemisinin, a sesquiterpene, is widely used to treat malaria (Eddin et al., 2021). Triterpenoids represent a structurally diverse subgroup of terpenoids with at least 18 distinct subclasses, among which members of the lupane, oleanane, and ursane groups are particularly noted for their anticancer properties (Masyita et al., 2022). Phytosterols, which are oxidized triterpenes, function in plant cell membrane stabilization in a manner analogous to cholesterol in animals (Ruscica et al., 2025). Among these compounds, clinical studies have demonstrated that plant stanols and sterols from sources such as rapeseed and legume can reduce cardiovascular risk by competitively inhibiting intestinal cholesterol absorption and significantly reducing the levels of low-density-lipoprotein cholesterol (Poli et al., 2021).Fig. 1Classification and functional properties of major bioactive compounds in pig dietary supplements. Another important class of BCs, alkaloids, are nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds known for their antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, making them valuable feed additives in animal production (Dey et al., 2020). Berberine, an alkaloid, is found in Phellodendron amurense and exhibits broad-spectrum inhibitory activity against intestinal pathogens (Jael Teresa de Jesús et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023b).

Essential oils, a further category of BCs, are volatile extracts obtained from various plant parts. Their biological activities are largely attributed to terpenes and phenylpropenes (Brenes and Roura, 2010). They exhibit antibacterial effects because of the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups and delocalized electrons, which disrupt microbial membrane integrity and inhibit enzymatic activity (Hyldgaard et al., 2012). Extensive research has indicated that essential oils possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, including carvacrol, thymol, citral, eugenol, and cinnamaldehyde. They also make feed more palatable and support intestinal health in livestock (Omonijo et al., 2018).

Polyphenols are characterized by the presence of one or more benzene rings with multiple hydroxyl groups. They are abundant in vegetables, fruits, grains, herbs, legumes, and spices (Lin et al., 2016). Key polyphenol subclasses include flavonoids (e.g., baicalein, quercetin, naringenin, silymarin), phenolic acids (e.g., ferulic acid, caffeic acid, and butyric acid), lignans (e.g., magnolol and honokiol), and stilbenes such as resveratrol (Brglez Mojzer et al., 2016). Numerous studies have indicated that polyphenols improve the growth of animals and the quality of animal-derived products by modulating intestinal microbiota, enhancing immune responses, and elevating antioxidant capacity (Mei et al., 2024).

Another BC category is polysaccharides, which are widely distributed in nature and consist of repeating monosaccharide units. They can be obtained from plants, animals, and microorganisms, and exhibit multiple physiological functions (Zhang et al., 2022b). For example, marine polysaccharides are derived from seaweed with antioxidative, immunomodulatory, and gastrointestinal protective effects (O'Doherty et al., 2021). Commercial yeast cell wall polysaccharides (e.g. Bio-Mos and MTB100) are capable of maintaining animal health and improving growth performance via immune-enhancing and anti-pathogenic effects (Kogan and Kocher, 2007). However, their physiological functions are highly influenced by the composition and molecular weights of their constituent monosaccharides (Lovegrove et al., 2017).

The BCs derived from animal and microbial sources include polypeptides (composed of 2-50 α-amino acids), saponins, and carotenoids, which primarily function via antimicrobial activity, immunomodulatory effects, and metabolic regulation. Antimicrobial peptides are a major group of polypeptides that are widely distributed in bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals. They are typically cationic and amphiphilic, enabling them to bind to and disrupt negatively charged microbial membranes. Given their structural diversity, they exhibit a wide range of antimicrobial activities and levels of stability (Zhang et al., 2021b). Specific antimicrobial polypeptides, such as colicins and cecropin have demonstrated effectiveness in improving the performance and reducing the incidence of diarrhea in weaned pigs (Silveira et al., 2021).

Saponins are secondary metabolites produced by plants, consisting of hydrophilic sugar chains attached to lipophilic glycosidic moieties (Moses et al., 2014). For example, saponins isolated from sea cucumbers have been shown to act as BCs by exhibiting selective anticancer effects by inducing the apoptosis of tumor cells via the mitochondrial pathway, attributed to their unique sulfated triterpene structure (Mondol et al., 2017). In addition to cytotoxicity, the amphiphilic nature of saponins allows membrane penetration, contributing to their anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, and antiparasitic properties (Timilsena et al., 2023).

Finally, carotenoids are BCs in the form of lipid-soluble tetraterpenoid pigments, including beta-carotene, astaxanthin, and lycopene (Milani et al., 2017). More than 1000 distinct carotenoids have been identified in fungi, bacteria, algae, and plants, which produce red, yellow, and orange pigmentation (Maoka, 2020). These compounds are known for their antioxidant, anticancer, and immunoregulatory functions, highlighting their value as BCs (Fiedor and Burda, 2014).

Biological functions and challenges of bioactive compounds

Antioxidant function of BCs as pig feed additives

Oxidative stress is a state characterized by an imbalance between oxidative reactions and antioxidant reactions within the body, leading to an excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). The most common forms of ROS include superoxide anion (·O2–), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and hydroxyl radical (·OH), which can cause irreversible damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids (Schieber and Chandel, 2014). In pig production, various challenges (e.g., long-distance transportation, vaccination and disease, temperature changes, and pig house management) during production can induce severe oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions in pigs, and even lead to mortality in severe cases. Moreover, oxidative stress is closely linked to reproductive processes in sows, particularly during farrowing, late gestation, and lactation (Pereira and Martel, 2014). The elevated metabolic demands required to support fetal growth and milk production result in high levels of ROS, which may contribute to pregnancy complications, including abortion, preeclampsia, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (Berchieri-Ronchi et al., 2011).

Dietary supplementation with BCs presents a promising strategy to overcome oxidative challenge by enhancing the endogenous antioxidant defenses and immune functions in pigs (Fig. 2). The BCs can directly scavenge free radicals or indirectly enhance antioxidant defense by modulating total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) as well as the activity and expression of key oxidoreductases such as GSH-Px, catalase CAT, and SOD. The significant antioxidant effects of BCs are also closely associated with their regulation of endogenous defense pathways, including PPARγ/Nrf2 (The full names of the abbreviations of all genes and proteins can be found in Table S1), AMPK, and NF-κB signaling pathways, among others (Mei et al., 2024). They augment the endogenous antioxidant and immune system functions of sows, thus significantly enhancing their reproductive performance and lactation quality (Mei et al., 2024). For example, dietary supplementation with grape seed proanthocyanidins (GSPE) significantly decreased the incidence of fetal growth restriction and stillbirths, while increasing the number of live-born piglets (Wang et al., 2019c). The placenta is a primary site of ROS production during late gestation, owing to its high metabolic rate and elevated mitochondrial activity (Chiarello et al., 2020). Dietary BCs also have the potential to mitigate heat-stress-induced oxidative damage. Heat stress often occurs during hot summers, long-distance transportation, and farrowing, leading to reduced breeding and farrowing rates, higher mortality, decreased weaning weights, and increased feed conversion ratios in growing-finishing pigs (Liu et al., 2022b). This is largely due to that pigs struggle to dissipate body heat through evaporation, a result of their underdeveloped sweat glands and substantial subcutaneous fat layer (Gourdine et al., 2021). One study examined the effects of Macleaya cordata extracts (MCE) on heat stress-induced impairment of sow reproduction and IUGR piglets. Sows were supplemented at 500 mg/kg MCE from gestation d 90 until farrowing. The MCE significantly increased the activities of SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px, and decreased MDA levels in the serum of both sows and IUGR piglets. Additionally, MCE significantly reduced the mRNA levels of heat shock protein (Hsp) 70 and increased Hsp27 mRNA expression in the serum of sows and piglets. Although MCE and/or nano-selenium improved antioxidant and immune functions in sows and IUGR piglets, they had no impact on the reproductive performance of sows (Liu et al., 2022a). In IUGR piglets, dietary curcumin supplementation at 400 mg/kg significantly improved average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and overall feed efficiency, suggesting that curcumin may alleviate metabolic disorders associated with IUGR through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties via Nrf2 pathway (Zhang et al., 2022a, 2020b). In another study, IUGR piglets were fed with resveratrol at 80 mg/kg during lactation (d 7–21) and 300 mg/kg post-weaning (d 21–150). Resveratrol supplementation enhanced antioxidant activity, altered muscle fiber composition, and reduced lipid accumulation, thereby improving meat quality (Cheng et al., 2020). It also attenuated liver injury and promoted skeletal muscle growth and maturation, primarily by increasing mitochondrial biogenesis, elevating antioxidant levels, enhancing IL-10 gene expression, and inducing macrophage repolarization (Cheng et al., 2021). Pterostilbene is a dimethyl ether analog of resveratrol found primarily in blueberries and rosewood. It exhibits superior absorption and metabolic stability compared to resveratrol (Choo et al., 2014). Similarly, pterostilbene was found to reduce liver damage and oxidative stress in IUGR piglets. However, neither pterostilbene nor resveratrol significantly improved overall growth and development of these animals (Chen et al., 2021c).

Weaned piglets frequently experience diarrhea and suboptimal growth due to the immaturity of their gastrointestinal tract and immune system (Rhouma et al., 2017). During this transition, they often undergo systemic and intestinal oxidative stress, which contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammatory responses, and impaired intestinal barrier function (Tang et al., 2021b). Dietary supplementation with BCs can help alleviate the adverse effects of weaned stress. For example, malic acid has been shown to improve the antioxidant capacity of nursery piglets by increasing the activities of SDH, T-AOC, and GSH-Px (Zhang et al., 2021d). Dietary resveratrol and curcumin improve antioxidant status and intestinal barrier function in piglets by reducing N6-methyladenosine methylation levels of tight junction proteins and updating the expression of genes encoding antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px). These changes help maintain microbial homeostasis and prevent the invasion of toxins and pathogens (Gan et al., 2019). Dietary pterostilbene increased the expression of genes related to intestinal tight junction proteins, enhanced SOD activity, and elevated levels of NQO1 in the jejunum of weaned piglets (Zhang et al., 2020a). Wang et al. (2024) reported that the survival rate of weaned piglets significantly increased to 91% when fed with Guizhi Li-Zhong (GLZ) extract-supplemented diets from the 4th to the 10th weeks, compared to 71% in the control group and 81% in the group receiving diets with 0.2%. Additionally, GLZ notably enhanced antioxidant capacity, reduced inflammation, and promoted beneficial gut bacteria, whereas antibiotic treatments resulted in a decline in beneficial microbes (Wang et al., 2024a). Future studies should focus on elucidating the synergistic effects of these BCs to develop holistic dietary strategies that enhance resilience to weaning stress and support sustainable pig production.

Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory function of BCs as pig feed additives

Environmental influences, pathogenic infections, and variations in feeding conditions can induce inflammation and infections that adversely affect pig performance (Grzinic et al., 2023). In sows, systemic inflammatory cascades triggered by pregnancy and parturition increase the risk of uncontrolled inflammation and reproductive disorders (Bjorkman et al., 2023). The BCs have been shown to modulate inflammatory pathways to mitigate these effects (Fig. 2). They reduce the production and expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Habtemariam, 2023). For instance, supplementing sows with Forsythia suspensa extract (FSE) during late gestation reduced inflammation by decreasing IL-6 and TNF-α while increasing IL-10 in the serum and colostrum of the sows. The FSE also improved the oxidative status in colostrum and serum of sows (elevated T-AOC and SOD activities while reducing MDA levels), increased the levels of CAT, GSH-Px, and immunoglobulin A (IgA) in the serum of piglets, as well as increased the birth weight of litters (Long et al., 2021). Furthermore, colostrum serves as a vital source of energy and nutrients for newborn piglets, which are born with limited brown adipose tissue and glycogen reserves (Theil et al., 2014). Enhancing the quality of colostrum and milk with improved antioxidant status, elevated immunoglobulins, and inflammatory factors can significantly promote growth performance and reduce disease susceptibility in piglets. Compared to basic diets, a diet containing 2% olive oil during gestation and lactation does not increase fat deposition in sows. Instead, it enhances the nutritional quality and energy density of the milk, resulting in higher milk fat content. This dietary intervention is also associated with reduced inflammation in piglets, increased birth weights, and enhanced survival rates (Shen et al., 2015). Dietary supplementation with seaweed extracts (rich in algal polysaccharides [SDP], laminarin, and fucoidan) during late gestation and lactation significantly elevated the concentrations of immunoglobulin G (IgG) and IgA in both colostrum and piglet serum. A reduction in Enterobacteriaceae in sows at farrowing and Escherichia coli in weaned piglets was also observed (Leonard et al., 2010a). Fish oil from gestational supplementation from gestational d 84 until lactation d 16 significantly increased IgG levels in colostrum and IL-10 in milk, while also elevating high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels, AKP, and TNF-α in their plasma (Luo et al., 2020). Additionally, sows fed GSPE exhibited the highest serum progesterone and estradiol levels, along with immunoglobulin M (IgM) and IgG in colostrum, outperforming both basal diets and vitamin E-supplied diets (Wang et al., 2019c). These studies demonstrate that dietary BCs can enhance antioxidant capacity, immune status, and intestinal health of sows, thereby improving lactation quality and yield, which in turn supports piglet growth and health.

While optimizing colostrum and milk composition through maternal nutrition is essential for early piglet development, effective management of pathogen exposure remains critical throughout the weaning and growing phases. Enteric infections caused by bacteria and viruses represent major threats to intestinal health and overall productivity in piglets. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a strong endotoxin that leads to negative alterations in the structure of the intestines, the function of the barrier, the permeability of the mucosa, and the bacterial translocation (Mohr et al., 2022). Dietary supplementation with 500 mg/kg caffeic acid ameliorated LPS-induced inflammation in weaned piglets. This diet leads to a significant rise in body weight (BW) on d 28, and ADG between d 21 and 28 improves antioxidant status while inhibiting inflammation (Wen et al., 2023). Similarly, β-glucan supplementation alleviated inflammatory responses and metabolic disturbances in LPS-challenged piglets, as evidenced by increased serum levels of complement 3 (C3) and decreased serum Pig-MAP (major acute-phase protein), haptoglobin (HP), and IL-6 (Hahn et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2018). Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV), transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV), and rotavirus are common viral agents that are primarily transmitted via fecal-oral and fecal-nasal routes (Duan et al., 2022). After being consumed, the viral particles invade undifferentiated mature intestinal epithelial cells, provoking local inflammation, disrupting immune homeostasis, and ultimately leading to viral diarrhea in weanling piglets. Several BCs have exhibited promising antiviral effects. Cepharanthine (CEP), Homoharringtonine (HHT), puerarin, and Buddlejasaponin IVb have demonstrated significant anti-PEDV activity, alleviating clinical symptoms and intestinal damage in PEDV-infected piglets (Dong et al., 2018, 2022; Sun et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020a). In TGEV-infected piglets, Eugenol supplementation elevated serum IgG, decreased TNF-α levels, alleviated intestinal injury, improved clinical outcomes, and enhanced feed efficiency (Wang et al., 2022b). Similarly, lentinan administration improved growth performance and feed conversion ratios in weaned piglets suffering from rotavirus, reduced diarrhea incidence, and strengthened intestinal barrier function. This was accompanied by increased serum immunoglobulins (IgA, IgM, and IgG), elevated interferon-beta (IFN-β) in the jejunal mucosa, and inhibited invasion of rotavirus in the intestines (Fan et al., 2021).

Beyond infectious agents, mycotoxins represent another significant threat to pig health. The predominant mycotoxins found in cereal-based foods and animal feeds include deoxynivalenol (DON), aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), ochratoxin (OTA), T-2 toxin (T-2), zearalenone (ZEN), and fumonisin (FUM) (Marin et al., 2013). For example, even low intake of DON via placenta, colostrum, or feed can compromise intestinal function, induce oxidative stress, and dysregulate immunity (Liu et al., 2020). High doses of DON can provoke severe clinical symptoms, including diarrhea, vomiting, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage (Yao and Long, 2020). Several BCs have demonstrated efficacy in counteracting mycotoxin-related toxicity. For example, Baicalin (1000 mg/kg) effectively suppressed the NF-κB pathway while simultaneously activating mTOR signaling in DON-infected piglets, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation (Liao et al., 2020). Berberine (BBR) is a compound derived from traditional Chinese herbal remedies, including Coptis chinensis and Phellodendron bark. A 21-d regimen of BBR resulted in a significant rise in the ADG, ADFI, and feed conversion efficiency in weanling piglets under DON challenge through modulation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways and intestinal tight junction proteins (Tang et al., 2021a). Combinations of glycyrrhizic acid and probiotics as feed additives also improved the growth performance of piglets consuming DON-contaminated diets and reduced DON residues by modulating gut microbiota and enhancing barrier function (Xu et al., 2022b, 2023a). Other compounds such as hesperidin, quercetin, baicalin, vitamins C, D, and E have been reported to reduce DON-induced oxidative stress and intestinal damage (Li et al., 2024b; Liu et al., 2023; Sauve et al., 2023). The organ-specific toxicity of mycotoxins is also notable; AFB1 primarily targets the liver, while OTA damages the kidneys (Zain, 2011). After four weeks of feeding weanling piglets with diets containing grape seed and sea buckthorn meal, the potential compensatory effects on AFB1 and OTA damage on the liver and kidney were analyzed. Proteomics results indicated that grape seed and sea buckthorn powder as dietary alleviated AFB1- and OTA-induced hepatic and renal damage by restored protein levels involved in fatty acid and amino acid metabolism, antioxidative defense, and mitochondrial function, and may be used as a novel nutritional intervention to reduce deleterious effects of mycotoxins (Popescu et al., 2021, 2023). The GSPE supplemented meal also reduced gene and protein expression of MAPK and NF-κB signaling activation and remodeled gut microbiota by increasing Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria, and reducing Firmicutes (Grosu et al., 2019; Taranu et al., 2020). Vitamin C (150 mg/kg) counteracted the effects of ZEN on immune function, serum biochemistry, and hormone levels in weanling piglets (Su et al., 2018). For T-2 toxin, which causes severe gastrointestinal lesions in pigs, melatonin (5 mg/kg) inhibited oxidative stress via Nrf2 activation, suppressed inflammatory cytokine expression by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway, and reduced Caspase-3-mediated apoptosis (Ning et al., 2024). Herbicide residues such as diquat can also retard growth performance (Siroka et al., 2019). Supplementation with puerarin or chlorogenic acid (CGA) improved growth metrics, including BW, ADG, and ADFI in diquat-exposed piglets (Chen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020b). In summary, BCs offer a promising nutritional strategy to alleviate mycotoxin toxicity in pigs, thereby supporting growth and health under challenging conditions. Therefore, the appropriate use of BCs represents a promising strategy to mitigate viral infections and support intestinal health in pig production.

Intestinal epithelial barrier protective function of BCs as pig feed additives

The intestinal barrier is composed of a tightly linked monolayer of columnar epithelial cells. It serves not only as the primary site of nutrient absorption in pigs, but also the first line of defense against pathogens and toxins (Hall et al., 2001). Under challenging conditions, various stressors can lead to intestinal dysfunction, which is primarily characterized by intestinal villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and impaired integrity of critical tight and adherent junctions between intestinal enteric cells (He et al., 2019, 2020). Such intestinal damage further disrupts the gut microbial community and metabolic function, disturbs intestinal homeostasis, exacerbates inflammatory responses, and ultimately results in reduces production performance and impaired growth.

In this context, several classes of BCs have been reported to enhance both the general health status and intestinal health in pigs. A key mechanism through which BCs exert their beneficial effects is by promoting the expression of tight junction proteins such as claudin-1, occludin, and ZO-1 in intestinal epithelial cells, while also elevating the levels of goblet cell-derived MUC2. These actions collectively reduce mucosal permeability and inhibit pathogen invasion. For example, pterostilbene, as a dietary supplement in weaned piglets, significantly improved mucosal integrity, as evidenced by a reduction in apoptotic cells within intestinal villi and increased proliferation of crypt cells (Zhang et al., 2020a). The number of mature intestinal cells directly influences digestive efficiency and susceptibility to diarrhea and E. coli infection. Furthermore, pterostilbene upregulated the expression of genes encoding intestinal tight junction proteins in weaned piglets (Zhang et al., 2020a). Similarly, caffeic acid supplementation was shown to effectively repair the colonic barrier in LPS-challenged piglets, improving colon morphology and integrity while reducing the apoptosis of goblet cells (Wen et al., 2023).

In addition to reinforcing the physical integrity of the intestinal barrier, BCs also exert protective effects by enhancing the innate immune response and promoting immunoglobulin production, thereby strengthening overall intestinal immunity of pigs (Takiishi and Fenero, 2017). The supplementation of chitosan or mannan oligosaccharides in the diets of gestating and lactating sows elevates antibody levels in milk, reduces intestinal inflammation, and modulates the gut microbiota, thereby improving sow reproductive performance and enhancing immune function in piglets (Duan et al., 2020; Ho et al., 2020). Moreover, BCs contribute to intestinal health by stimulating the production of microbial metabolites such as butyric acid, accelerating the renewal of intestinal epithelial cells, and promoting structural improvements. These changes expand the absorptive surface area and enhance nutrient utilization efficiency (Zhang et al., 2025). Che et al. (2016) indicated that adding 2% soybean oil to the diet of lactating sows significantly improved the villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) in the jejunum and colon of their piglets. Dietary supplementation with 0.02% xylooligosaccharide (XOS) was also shown to increase VH, decrease CD, and improve intestinal mucosal integrity via increasing disaccharidase activity and claudin-1 protein. Furthermore, XOS ameliorated LPS-induced intestinal damage and flora dysbiosis by regulating inflammation-related mediators and increasing the content of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) in intestinal flora, particularly by enriching beneficial bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus and Prevotella, etc.) (Wang et al., 2021b). Elevated SCFA levels have been linked to improved farrowing performance in sows and enhanced gut health in piglets (Xu et al., 2025). Metabolic regulation function of BCs as pig feed additives

Animal products constitute an essential component of human diets; however, persistent emphasis on lean meat yield has often compromised meat quality (Drewnowski, 2024). As living standards improve, consumers are increasingly concerned with the sensory attributes and nutritional value of meat (de Araujo et al., 2022). Meat color, tenderness, juiciness, and flavor are core organoleptic properties, largely determined by biochemical and microbiological changes in muscle postmortem. Muscle from finishing pigs with high lean meat and low-fat content frequently exhibits undesirable characteristics (e.g., pale or dark coloration, moisture leakage, firm texture, and lack of flavor) (Ma et al., 2017). The BCs present in animal diets offer a promising approach to improve meat sensory quality. Dihydromyricetin (DHM), a natural flavonoid, supplemented diet at doses ranging from 100 to 500 mg/kg increased the redness (a∗) at 45 min and 48 h while decreasing lightness (L∗) at 24 h in the longissimus dorsi (LD) muscle (Guo et al., 2022). The a∗ value indicates deoxymyoglobin content, the b∗ value reflects metmyoglobin formation, and the L∗ value represents myoglobin oxidation. Meat with higher a∗ and lower b∗ and L∗ values generally represents better sensory quality and is associated with better consumer acceptance (Chouikhi et al., 2023). Betaine supplementation over 119 d also improved a∗45 min and marbling of longissimus psoas muscle, while reducing b∗24 h and cooking loss (Fu et al., 2023). Yeast β-glucan, a polysaccharide derived from yeast cell walls, is known for its immune-enhancing, antioxidant, and gut-health-promoting properties. Dietary yeast β-glucan at 200 mg/kg was shown to inhibit postmortem muscle glycolysis, improve muscle pH, and enhance water-holding capacity in finishing pigs (Caseiro et al., 2022; He et al., 2022). Postmortem glycolysis and lactic acid accumulation often lead to low pH, poor water-holding capacity, and pale color in muscle. Modulating glycolysis is therefore critical for improving meat quality (Van de Perre et al., 2010). Another study reported elevated antioxidant capacity in muscle induced by a yeast β-glucan diet, which helps mitigate oxidative deterioration that leads to discoloration and nutrient loss (He et al., 2022). It is worth noting that not all BCs effectively improve meat quality traits, even when they possess antioxidant properties (Araujo et al., 2021; Hanczakowska et al., 2015; Ranucci et al., 2015).

Beyond sensory attributes, the nutritional value of meat is equally important in defining overall meat quality. It is determined by multiple factors, with crude protein content, amino acid profile, and fatty acid composition serving as primary nutritional indicators (Imran et al., 2018). Crude protein content reflects the basic nutritional quality of pork, while the amino acid profile influences its flavor, and the fatty acid composition determines both the health-related properties and culinary characteristics of the fat. Optimizing these indicators can significantly improve pork quality to meet modern consumer demands (Alfaia et al., 2019). The BCs have demonstrated considerable potential in improving the nutritional profile of pork. The DHM supplementation has been shown to increase crude protein content and optimize amino acid composition (Guo et al., 2022). Chlorogenic acid enhances lean mass and alters profiles of amino acids by modulating mTOR signaling. The expression of amino acid transporters SNAT2 and LAT1 in the skeletal muscle, thereby facilitating protein synthesis in the LD muscle (Wang et al., 2022d). It is important to note that the benefits of CGA are dose-dependent: 0.02% and 0.04% being effective, while 0.08% cannot further enhance these benefits and may even negate these effects (Wang et al., 2022d, 2021a). Flavonoids from Fructus aurantii immaturus (FFAI) increased the content of flavor amino acids (e.g., glutamic acid, serine, threonine, etc.) and flavor substances (e.g., rich benzene and octanal) in pork (Yang et al., 2024). Dietary supplementation of GSPE increased pH at 24 h, redness, and crude protein content of LD, while decreasing shear force, drip loss at 48 h, lactic acid content, and glycolytic potential. Specifically, GSPE enhanced total polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), n-3 PUFA, and the ratio of PUFA/saturated fatty acids (SFA) in LD, thereby significantly improving the fatty acid profile of meat (Xu et al., 2022a). Clinical studies have shown that SFA and mega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFA; especially eicosapentaenoic acid and docosahexaenoic acid) are associated with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects beneficial to human health (Minelli et al., 2023). Similarly, 400 mg/kg malic acid decreased total SFA and total monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) levels in the LD muscle of finishing pigs, and betaine (1200 mg/kg) modulated lipid metabolism by up-regulating lipogenesis and down-regulating genes associated with lipolysis (Zhang et al., 2021d). Diets with 25% alfalfa leaf meal (ALM) can increase the intramuscular fat (IMF) content, cooked meat percentage, a∗ value, and antioxidant capacity of LD, optimize amino acid composition, and increase MUFA in LD (Guo et al., 2023). These results provide a theoretical basis for BCs in improving pork quality through lipid metabolism and antioxidant pathways.

Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that BCs can facilitate the conversion of fiber types and metabolic activity in fattening pigs through multiple mechanisms, including epigenetic modifications, signaling pathways, mitochondrial functionality, and gut microbiota composition. Differences in metabolic properties, contraction speeds, and energy utilization result in slow-twitch fibers being generally associated with enhanced elasticity, tenderness, and nutritional quality, whereas fast-twitch fibers contribute to firmer texture and influence meat coloration (Joo et al., 2013). The GSPE and malic acid as feed additives throughout the nursery and fattening stages increased the percentage of fast-twitch fiber in the longissimus thoracis (LT), enhanced antioxidant capacity in both serum and LT, and positively influenced the color, texture, and nutritional value of the LT muscle (Li et al., 2024c; Xu et al., 2022a; Zhang et al., 2021d). Wen et al. (2022) found that dietary lycopene at 200 mg/kg significantly increased the protein level of slow myosin heavy chain (MyHC) and decreased fast MyHC in finishing pigs’ muscle, promoting a shift from fast-twitch to slow-twitch fiber types. Apple polyphenols were found to enhance the activity of SDH and MDH, while decreasing LDH activity, thereby encouraging a transition from glycolytic muscle fibers to oxidative muscle fibers (Chen et al., 2021a). Compounds such as resveratrol, DHM and GSPE enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism of skeletal muscle via the activation of AMPK signaling, promoting slow-switch myofiber formation (Guo et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024c). Mangosterol alters the postmortem glycolysis and antioxidant capacity of finishing pig muscle by decreasing acetylation levels and activities of PGK1, GAPDH, and PFKFB3 (Wang et al., 2020). Dietary supplementation of 30% alfalfa meal improved the amino acid composition of LD by up-regulating the relative abundance of Bacteroides and Faecalicatena in intestinal flora (Li et al., 2022b). In addition, yeast β-glucan diets have also been shown to reduce WPS-2 (Eremiobacterota) abundance in the gut of finishing pigs, indirectly improving pork quality.

The potential of BCs to influence the performance of finishing pigs has also garnered research interest, albeit with varying outcomes. Some BCs have been reported to partially improve the productivity of finishing pigs. For example, DHM diets were shown to reduce the feed-to-meat ratio in growing-finishing pigs. Similarly, betaine significantly decreased the feed conversion ratio while increasing ADG and ADFI (Zhong et al., 2021). The addition of mangosterol improved the productivity of finishing pigs, while simultaneously improving meat quality parameters such as drip loss, shear force and backfat thickness, indicating its potential to enhance growth performance and carcass traits (Wang et al., 2020). However, some studies report neutral or even adverse effects of BCs to finishing pigs at certain inclusion levels. For instance, dietary supplements including herbal extract mixtures (e.g., sage, nettle, lemon balm and coneflower), oregano oil, sweet chestnut extract mix, vitamin E, GSPE, resveratrol, apple polyphenols, red branch dogwood polyphenol extract and other active substances did not yield significant improvements in production performance (Hanczakowska et al., 2015; Ranucci et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2022a; Xu et al., 2022a, 2019; Zhang et al., 2015; Zheng et al., 2021). A notable example is mulberry leaves powder (MLP), which consists of diverse BCs such as polysaccharides, flavonoids, and polyphenols. It has been indicated as a safe dietary supplement with good palatability and minimal side effects, effectively reducing diarrhea incidence in weaned piglets. Importantly, diets containing 1% and 3% MLP did not adversely affect the growth of weanling pigs, whereas a 5% inclusion led to growth retardation, likely due to altered lipid metabolism (Du et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022). Similarly, in finishing pigs, a 9% dietary inclusion of MLP was optimal for improving carcass quality, while concentrations of 6% or 12% negatively affected growth performance, potentially due to shifts in cellulose-degrading microbial populations (Ma et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2021). These findings underscore the importance of optimizing the type, composition, and dosage of BCs to maximize their benefits in pig production, highlighting that precise application is essential for improving productivity without compromising animal growth or health.

Additionally, some attempts yielded limited success, such as using 15% sainfoin to reduce boar odor (Seoni et al., 2021). In another interesting study, MP reduces fecal odor in pig farming environments by regulating intestinal flora and reducing the production of skatole and its accumulation in adipose tissue of finishing pigs. This may be because MP can stimulate the expression of CYP1A1 in porcine hepatocytes (Sun et al., 2024). Likewise, the inclusion of 0.2% isomalto-oligosaccharides (IMO) significantly improved their gut health, decreased the frequency of diarrhea, and diminished the generation of indole and skatole in weaned piglets (Ma et al., 2025). Overall, these findings highlight the multi-mechanistic potential of BCs to enhance meat quality in pigs, although further studies are needed to fully elucidate the direct causal relationships between gut microbial changes and muscle biology.

Challenges facing the use of BCs

Despite the ability of BC supplementation of pig feed to meet nutritional and functional demands while promoting health and preventing disease (Fig. 4), the manufacturing of BCs faces challenges. For example, a lack of raw materials, insufficient pharmacologically active substances, and complications in boosting the levels of these active ingredients. The main conventional methods for obtaining natural BCs include Soxhlet extraction and maceration (Zhang et al., 2018). Although these methods present advantages such as their simple equipment, ease of application, and low cost, they also involve lengthy extraction times and high temperatures, which may lead to the deterioration of certain compounds (Zhang et al., 2018). In particular, Soxhlet extraction necessitates extended heating, which can result in the breakdown of phenolic compounds. In contrast, maceration operates at lower temperatures but requires more time and is less efficient (Aires, 2017). Recent advances have led to the adoption of methods such as supercritical fluid extraction, ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE), and microwave-assisted extraction (MAE), which are progressively supplanting conventional extraction techniques (Osorio-Tobon, 2020). Employing MAE and UAE allows superior extraction yields to be achieved in a shorter period, making them particularly appropriate for phenolic compounds (Islam et al., 2023). Moreover, the unique properties of extraction with supercritical carbon dioxide, characterized by a relatively low critical temperature of 31.1 °C and adjustable solvent polarity, facilitate the targeted extraction of fat-soluble terpenes and alkaloids while preventing the thermal degradation of polyphenols (De Aguiar et al., 2023). Moreover, the high variability of naturally extracted BCs creates significant obstacles to ensuring the quality of feed additives. For numerous BCs with intricate structures, the method of total chemical synthesis is challenging; it is time-consuming, poses difficulties in selectivity management, and results in low yields, which complicates the ability to achieve large-scale production.Fig. 4Main benefits after supplementation with bioactive compounds across the pig life stage. ↑, increase; ↓, decrease. The inherent characteristics also obstruct the application of BCs. Specifically, BCs are characterized by low water solubility, unpalatability, low oral bioavailability, and susceptibility to degradation (Rezaei et al., 2019). For example, resveratrol, despite its ability to improve diarrhea in piglets and promote reproductive performance in sows, can be easily eliminated from the body due to its extensive phase II metabolism (glucuronic acid or sulfate binding) and low bioavailability (Walle et al., 2004). These inherent biological characteristics limit the dispersibility, storage stability, and efficacy of BCs in pig feed formulations. Appropriate strategies are needed to ensure the efficiency and activity of dietary BCs from storage to consumption, digestion, and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract (Singh et al., 2023). Therefore, improving the production of bioactive counterparts with defined compositions is essential for consistent and effective application in pig feed.

Approaches to improve the production of natural BCs

Synthetic biology provides innovative approaches to overcome the aforementioned challenges and enable the sustainable generation of biocompatible compounds (Cravens et al., 2019). Upon characterizing the processes by which intricate natural compounds are biosynthesized, microbial cell factories for the heterologous expression of clusters of genes involved in BC synthesis can facilitate the large-scale production of terpenes, polyphenols, and various other substances (Liu et al., 2021b; Patra et al., 2021; Rodriguez et al., 2017). The model microorganisms currently widely used in such research include E. coli, Corynebacterium glutamicum, Bacillus subtilis, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Xu et al., 2020a). Significant findings in this field include the development of a semisynthetic route for artemisinin as anti-malarial medications” or “for artemisinin as an anti-malarial medication. Specifically, the biosynthetic pathway of artemisinic acid in Artemisia annua was elucidated, followed by its integration into S. cerevisiae, utilizing a combination of glucose and ethanol as a carbon source in a chemical photocatalytic process (Ro et al., 2006). In another synthetic biology-based study, Wang et al. (2019a) created a chassis for protopanaxadiol (PPD) production by modularly adjusting the mevalonate pathway and fine-tuning cytochrome P450 expression, which led to the generation of 179.3 mg/L PPD in shake flasks and 2.25 g/L in 10 L bioreactors of ginsenoside Rh2. To precisely regulate the molecular composition of multicomponent BCs, such as ginsenoside complexes and icariin derivatives, researchers can develop individual cell factories for each component and then combine them in designated proportions. The BC production capacity of these cell factories can be further enhanced by applying various types of metabolic engineering (fine-tuning of gene copy number, overexpression of pathway-related genes, promoter engineering and codon optimization, and enzyme engineering strategies [targeted evolution and rational design of key enzymes]) (Ko et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). It has also been reported that optimizing the flow of the mevalonate pathway via promoter engineering may help mitigate the excess accumulation and metabolic disturbances of toxic intermediates, such as isopentenyl pyrophosphate (IPP) and 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA), during the biosynthesis of BCs (Shen et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019b). Dong et al. (2025) implemented a comprehensive metabolic engineering approach to elevate the levels of kaempferol and quercetin in shaking flask cultures of the yeast Yarrowia lipolytica, reaching 194.30 ± 7.69 and 278.92 ± 11.58 mg/L, respectively. Key metabolic strategies that contributed to these enhancements included the fusion of the F3H-FLS genes, the integration of genomic multitargeting for knock-in, promoter engineering for FMOCPR, and an increase in glucose concentration (Dong et al., 2025).

The establishment of cell factories and the activation of gene clusters necessitate a robust genetic manipulation system (Ran et al., 2013). The Clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/Cas system is valuable in this context as it enables precise genome editing by targeting specific DNA sequences with single-guide RNA. It overcomes the challenges posed by conventional gene editing techniques, like those involving zinc-finger nucleases and transcription activator-like effector nucleases, by resolving problems related to sequence homology and site specificity. Base editors and prime editors have also been developed to introduce site-specific mutations and small insertions and deletions in bacteria (Li et al., 2020a). Beyond genome editing, precise regulation of bacterial gene expression can be achieved by fusing inactivated dCas-9 with transcription repressors or activators (Wu et al., 2020b). The use of CRISPR technology to modify engineered bacteria enhances the efficiency of strain alterations and reduces the iterative process involved in developing industrial strains, while allowing scientists to achieve greater precision in designing and refining microbial cell factories (Hussain et al., 2023). Various strategies, including blocking competitive pathways via intermediate compounds, introducing novel metabolic pathways, increasing precursor flux, and regulating the overexpression of genes or transcription factors, constitute recommended approaches for achieving the biosynthesis of BCs in bacterial or fungal species (Sharma et al., 2019). For example, researchers have employed CRISPR/Cas9 technology to genetically engineer plants to overexpress key genes encoding dammarane synthase and amyrin synthase, achieving approximately 3-fold increases compared with the levels in the control group (Bulgakov et al., 1998). Jakociunas et al. (2015) applied CRISPR/Cas9 to disrupt multiple genes in S. cerevisiae, achieving alterations in metabolic pathways that significantly enhanced mevalonate production, all without the need to overexpress any genes. Moreover, Li et al. (2015) employed CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing to specifically target mutant strains of E. coli, aiming to optimize both the methylerythritol–phosphate (MEP) pathway and central metabolic pathways. This approach yielded 2.0 g/L β-carotene, with glucose serving as the sole carbon source. Furthermore, the application of CRISPR/Cas9 technology facilitated the disruption of lactate deactivation in lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and other probiotics by silencing LDH and altering its metabolic pathway towards the production of antimicrobial compounds (Haryani et al., 2024). These beneficial compounds not only served as alternatives to antibiotics but also improved the ability of the engineered bacteria to colonize and maintain stability within complex intestinal environments by either eliminating their virulent genes or incorporating properties that resist acid and bile salts. In summary, CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing technology has greatly expedited the development of microbial cell factories.

Recent advances in genome sequencing and bioinformatics have led to the discovery of numerous functionally uncharacterized gene clusters involved in the biosynthesis of natural products (Jensen, 2016). The use of deep genome mining methods alongside bioinformatic platforms such as the antibiotics and Secondary Metabolites Analysis Shell (antiSMASH) has become crucial for predicting the active natural products that may arise from these clusters, helping to identify new biosynthetic clusters (Blin et al., 2023). In parallel with this, metabolomic investigations have provided more in-depth assessments of metabolites in plants, animals, and microorganisms, thereby identifying variations across BCs extracted from extraction sites, harvest periods and extraction methods (Shen et al., 2023). Thus, researchers can predict the chemical skeletons or key functional groups potentially produced by biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) through bioinformatics analysis, thereby establishing direct links between genes and metabolites in natural extracts using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics. Conversely, specific structural fragments resolved by NMR can also serve as “probes” to engineer the BGCs responsible for their biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2025). A genome-scale metabolic network model serves as a tool to inform the design and development of strains, facilitating systematic approaches to metabolic engineering. It enables comprehensive characterization of an organism's metabolic network of and predicts its metabolic behavior under varying conditions (Passi et al., 2021). For instance, in microbial production of L-methionine, integrating metabolomics with genome-scale metabolic network modeling helps identify challenges in metabolic pathways as metabolite inhibition, degradation, and precursor availability (Cai et al., 2023). It has also been reported that researchers can pinpoint active natural compounds with distinct chemical configurations or targeted mechanisms of action by employing specialized techniques in active natural product mining that focus on structural or resistance genes, yielding potential candidates for the discovery and development of new pharmaceuticals (Atanasov et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2025). These investigations have established a technical basis for the heterologous production of these compounds and the exploration of novel bioactive natural products.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine-learning technologies are also significantly enhancing the efficiency of conventional experimental processes for screening novel BCs by improving structural analysis, automating identification, and predicting bioactivity from various perspectives. The prediction of natural product backbone structures from biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) sequences has been facilitated by tools such as ClusterFinder, which is based on hidden Markov models (HMMs), and DeepBGC, which applies deep learning approaches (Hannigan et al., 2019). Advanced deep learning modeling platforms, exemplified by tools such as simple modular architecture research tool (SMART) and class assignment and ontology prediction using mass spectrometry (CANOPUS), enable researchers to annotate the intricate structures of complex natural products by using data from sophisticated nuclear magnetic resonance and mass spectrometry analyses (Gangwal and Lavecchia, 2025). Researchers can also use machine learning algorithms to combine pangenomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic datasets (Ahmed et al., 2024). Additionally, strategies for virtual screening grounded in machine learning, such as deep neural networks and transfer learning algorithms, have been implemented to predict the pharmacological properties and targets of natural substances, enhancing the efficiency of natural product discovery and drug development (Gangwal and Lavecchia, 2025). Finally, automated experimental platforms, including droplet microfluidic systems, facilitate the parallel evaluation of a large-scale strain variants, thereby shortening the typical duration of research from months to days (Gantz et al., 2023). These studies have greatly expanded the breadth and accuracy of BGC prediction and accelerated the high-throughput screening and precise design of structurally novel BCs.

The NPs are mainly divided into organic NPs (polymer NPs, liposomes, hydrogels, etc.) and inorganic NPs (gold NPs [Au NPs], silver NPs [AgNPs], zinc oxide NPs [ZnO NPs], etc.). They are widely used in the delivery of therapeutics and in various fields such as medical diagnosis and treatment, cosmetics, and agriculture (Fig. 5) (Khan et al., 2019). Many articles exploring the application of nanotechnology in the pig industry have been published, with some describing that NPs can be used to deliver alternatives to antibiotics for the supplementation of pig feed (Movahedi et al., 2024). Furthermore, NPs are expected to be suitable as delivery systems for BCs with excellent biocompatibility, safety, and drug loading capacity. They can improve the water solubility of fat-soluble BCs, improve their stability during feed processing and transportation, and prevent the destruction of components caused by high temperatures or oxidation. After ingestion, nanocarriers prevent the premature degradation of BCs in the acidic environment of the gastrointestinal tract, ensuring that they reach their effective site in the gut (Puttasiddaiah et al., 2022). More importantly, owing to their special size effect and transcellular pathway (e.g., clathrin-mediated endocytosis), they can efficiently penetrate the intestinal mucosal barrier and thereby improve the bioavailability of BCs (Zheng et al., 2024). In addition, NPs can be co-loaded with multiple active substances such as probiotics and prebiotics, or antioxidants and anti-inflammatory agents, to exert synergistic effects (Zhang et al., 2024c). Furthermore, by appropriately designing NPs to respond to environmental stimuli (enzymes, pH, temperature, light, oxygen, etc.), they can release encapsulated compounds in a spatially and temporally controlled manner (Patra et al., 2018). This spatiotemporally precise delivery strategy not only enhances the targeting and local concentration of BCs at specific locations in the intestine, reducing the waste of active substances, but also avoids the disturbance of intestinal flora caused by the transient release of high BC concentrations (Zhang et al., 2024c). To date, numerous studies have explored the antioxidant and antibacterial effects of NP-encapsulated BCs. However, the application of engineered NPs as an effective alternative to antibiotics and antimicrobials in pig feed is still in its infancy. Further, studies are needed to explore the potential of nanotechnology to enhance pig production.

Inorganic nanoformulations to improve the bioavailability of bioactive compounds

Inorganic NPs are composed of inorganic substances, such as metals, metal oxides, and ceramics (Burlec et al., 2023). They have significant potential for agricultural applications due to their antibacterial properties and ability to deliver drugs. Among the metal nanomaterials with antimicrobial functions, AgNPs have the most pronounced antibacterial and antiviral effects. For example, it has been reported that spraying AgNP solution in pigsties significantly reduced microbial contamination while inhibiting African swine fever virus (ASFV) infections (Duan et al., 2020). In addition, Fondevila et al. (2009) showed that AgNPs at low concentrations (20–40 mg/kg) exhibited antidiarrheal effects similar to those of antibiotics, and improved feed intake and growth in weaning piglets. Although few studies have been conducted on AgNPs as a feed additive for pigs, experimental results in a large number of other species (e.g., birds and mice) have confirmed the inhibitory effects of AgNPs on bacteria such as Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus, as well as their potential to improve animal growth performance and intestinal flora (Michalak et al., 2022). Moreover, it has been reported that copper NPs can be added to animal feed as growth promoters, immunostimulants, and antibacterial and antifungal agents (Morsy et al., 2021). For example, Chang et al. (2018) increased the antioxidant level/activity in the serum of weaning piglets by adding 100 mg/kg CuNPs to the diet of weaning piglets. In addition, nano-chromium tripicolinate (nCrPic) has been found to significantly improve the growth performance and reproduction as a dietary supplement for summer fattening sows (Hung et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2009).

High doses of ZnO (2000–4000 mg/kg) are commonly used to prevent diarrhea in piglets (Hu et al., 2012). However, excessive residual Zn in the body has the potential to induce oxidative stress and DNA damage, and excessive Zn in pig excreta is an environmental pollutant (Alengebawy et al., 2021). The ZnO NPs have been explored as an alternative to the direct application of ZnO to combat the risks posed by multidrug-resistant strains, given their excellent antimicrobial activity at low levels (<1000 mg/kg). Indeed, it has been reported that these NPs can prevent diarrhea in piglets and effectively reduce the damage caused by orally administered Zn to the main viscera of pigs (Kociova et al., 2020; Pei et al., 2019). The ZnO NPs are 30 to 150 nm in size and have a controllable shape. They are usually synthesized by physical or chemical methods and can also be produced by prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms such as bacteria, fungi, and yeast (Fig. 6). A study showed that dietary ZnO enhanced the absorption and deposition of Zn in IUGR pigs, and had no obvious side effects on the development of the major organs, liver function, or the metabolism of Cu, Fe, and Mn (Zhou et al., 2023a). Interestingly, that paper described that, unlike the common mode of absorption of NPs (active uptake via phagocytosis and pinocytosis, passive diffusion, or transcytosis), Zn absorption was promoted in IUGR pigs via regulation of the expression of genes encoding the jejunal transporters metallothionein (MT), ZIP, and ZnT families (Horky et al., 2019). Notably, optimizing the synthesis conditions and methods of ZnO NPs also enabled the manipulation of their size and shape, which in turn improved their performance (Li et al., 2016; Oh et al., 2020). In another study, Ouyang et al. (2021) synthesized ZnO NPs with a porous microsphere structure by a chemical hydrothermal method using β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) as an organic small-molecule template. The porous structure and high specific surface area enhanced the efficiency with which ZnO contacted bacteria, and significantly inhibited the growth of E. coli and S. aureus; the study also showed that 500 and 1000 mg/kg dietary porous ZnO NPs had better anti-diarrhea and growth-promoting effects than 3000 mg/kg dietary ZnO (Ouyang et al., 2021). Additionally, several studies have explored the regulation of growth performance and diarrhea in piglets using commercially available ZnO NPs in an experimental setting (Chen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018). For example, 800 mg/kg of commercially available ZnO NPs increased the average daily weight gain in weaning piglets, decreased the incidence of diarrhea, altered the intestinal morphology of piglets, and promoted the expression of mRNA encoding tight junction proteins (occludin and ZO-1), cytokines (IL-1β, IL-10, and TNF-α), and the cell proliferation marker Ki67 in the ileal mucosa. This is a concern that elevated levels of tight junction proteins during inflammatory states may trigger structural disorders of the intestine, leading to impaired barrier function, increase permeability and ultimately lead to diarrhea (Wang et al., 2018). It was assume that the conflicting findings in that study resulted from variations in ZnO dose, the severity of stress, and the growth stage of the animals studied. Further studies on the dynamic balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory factors in this process should deepen the understanding of the mechanism by which ZnO acts on the intestinal barrier and inflammatory homeostasis. Meanwhile, another study showed that the addition of commercially available ZnO NPs (600 mg/kg) modified the water holding capacity, drip loss, cooking loss, shear force, and redness (a∗) of muscle in IUGR piglets, improving the organoleptic properties of pork. The findings showed that the Nrf2-GCL signaling pathway was activated to relieve the oxidative state, increase the abundance of UCG-005, hoa5-07d05, and Rikenellaceae RC9 intestinal flora in the cecum, and improve the decline in meat quality caused by IUGR (Chen et al., 2023). The ZnO NPs also reportedly improved the antioxidant capacity and reduce the incidence of diarrhea in finishing pigs. Together, these findings suggest that different concentrations of ZnO NPs in feed, different synthesis methods, and the manufacturer may affect the efficacy of ZnO NPs in improving the intestinal health, growth performance, and immune capacity of pigs. The further use of metallic nanomaterials as delivery platforms not only enhances the solubility of BCs but also protects the encapsulated cargo from degradation and aggregation. For example, Dinga et al. (2024) prepared N150MA by loading Melia azedarach seed extracted into ZnO NPs to ensure uniform distribution in feed and thermal stability in pellets. Piglets were fed with N150MA at a concentration of 150 mg/L for 7 weeks, after which their growth performance, protein utilization efficiency, and hematological and nutritional status were evaluated. The results showed that N150MA enabled effective utilization of the active ingredients of M. azedarach seed extract in vivo, and improved piglets’ ADFI, protein consumption, and growth efficiency, among others, indicating the importance of the delivery system for maximizing the efficacy of BCs in animal production (Dinga et al., 2024).

Organic nanoformulations to improve the bioavailability of bioactive compounds

Organic NPs are usually prepared from natural or synthetic organic molecules, such as lipids, proteins, sugars, and degradable biopolymers, by self-assembly or chemical synthesis (Patra et al., 2018; Samir et al., 2022). Compared with inorganic nanomaterials, they have unique advantages in the biomedical field including simple formulation, high biocompatibility, high safety, and strong drug-carrying capacity (Patra et al., 2018). Chitosan NPs (CNPs) are a class of NPs prepared from natural polysaccharides with antibacterial properties, usually synthesized by ionic gelation using polyanionic tripolyphosphate (TPP) (Shih et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2021; Yanat and Schroën, 2021). They have high loading efficiency and low toxicity, can be produced on a large scale, and are cost-effective, achieving significant success in the pig feed industry (Yanat and Schroën, 2021). Xu et al. (2023b) reported that feeding on 400 mg/kg CNP effectively alleviated LPS-induced intestinal damage in weaning piglets, especially by reducing neutrophil invasion and inflammatory marker secretion in jejunal mucosa via inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway. It has also been reported that CNPs may form a protein corona (PC) due to protein adsorption in the physiological environment to which they are exposed upon oral administration, which enhances oral drug delivery through lymphatic transport (Lin et al., 2024). The delivery of BCs, especially essential oils, has been extensively studied, and CNPs have been found to enhance not only the protective effects of essential oils in the harsh environment of the gastrointestinal tract but also their mucosal delivery and intestinal adsorption (Astutiningsih et al., 2022; Hadidi et al., 2020; Mondejar-Lopez et al., 2022). However, there is a need for further research into how CNPs-delivered BCs as feed additives can improve pig health and alleviate disease. Furthermore, NPs offer the possibility of delivering multiple compounds simultaneously. For example, the use of a nano-chitosan-zinc-zinc complex was shown to combine the advantages of Zn and chitosan, effectively improving the gastrointestinal digestion, absorption, and antioxidant stress in weaning piglets. This, in turn, significantly reduced the incidence of diarrhea in the piglets and alleviated weaning-associated stress (Zhang et al., 2021a). Copper-loaded CNPs (100 mg/kg per d) also showed the potential to enhance the growth and intestinal microflora of weaning piglets; these NPs are expected to replace aureomycin as an antibacterial agent in feed supplements (Wang et al., 2012a). Studies have also evaluated the effects of chromium-loaded CNPs on finishing pigs, indicating that their dietary supplementation regulates the recruitment of glucose transporter 4 and relevant mRNA and proteins of the AMPK pathway, thereby promoting glucose uptake and increasing levels of serum IgA/IgM and C4 (Liu et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2012b).

As another form of nanoformulation, nanoemulsions (NEs) are homogeneous systems formed by two immiscible liquids (usually oil and water) with the addition of a suitable emulsifier (Preeti et al., 2023). The particle size of its dispersed phase droplets usually ranges from 20 to 200 nm. The NEs can be synthesized through a range of methods, including high-pressure homogenization, ultrasonic emulsification, microfluidic technology, nanoprecipitation, and solvent evaporation-emulsification (Koroleva and Yurtov, 2012). Appropriate selection of the oil phase, surfactant, and cosurfactant at the correct ratio is vital for the successful formulation of NEs, as these factors directly influence their physicochemical properties and stability, thereby impacting encapsulation efficiency and functional performance (Dokovic et al., 2022). In a designed juglone-encapsulating NE, hydrophobic juglone was stably dispersed in the aqueous phase by surfactants to form a colloidal system of nano-droplets (Wan et al., 2023). This NE exhibited a mean particle size distribution of approximately 120 nm, along with a zeta potential of −57.3 mV. These micro-sized nanodroplets effectively avoided aggregation and delamination, facilitating prolonged stability during storage (Wan et al., 2023). Upon its release, the hydrophobic juglone within this NE significantly inhibited S. aureus and lowered total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), total viable count, thiobarbituric acid reactive substances, and pH when applied to pork surfaces (Wan et al., 2023). Meanwhile, Wang et al. (2022c) prepared a chitosan NE encapsulating thymol or thyme essential oil (TEO-CS/T-CS) with a dispersed particle size of approximately 120 to 140 nm and a positive charge (about 30 mV). The TEO-CS/T-CS retained the antibacterial activity of chitosan and BCs, and had a strong inhibitory effect on spoilage organisms, while maintaining the diversity of microbiota (Wang et al., 2022c). Moreover, after nanoemulsification through high-power ultrasonic emulsification technology, essential oils (e.g., lemon, oregano, garlic, and cinnamon oils) were found to exhibit greater thermal stability, ionic stability, and antioxidant capacity, which could reduce TVB-N and total bacterial count in pork. In conclusion, these studies indicated that NEs significantly enhance the antibacterial effect of BCs, effectively extend the shelf life of pork, and offer a viable alternative for reducing bacterial contamination in livestock products (Liu and Liu, 2020).

As a further form of nanoformulation, nanomicelles are stable micellar structures formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules such as surfactants, natural phospholipids, and amphiphilic polymers (Bose et al., 2021). Upon reaching the critical micelle concentration in a solution, the hydrophobic segments converge to form an inner core that effectively encapsulates water-insoluble BCs, while the hydrophilic regions form the shell that enhances their stability in aqueous environments (Bose et al., 2021). Owing to their smaller particle size, usually 10 to 100 nm, good biocompatibility, and ability to protect drugs or nutrients from degradation, nanomicelles serve as efficient delivery vehicles (Mushtaq et al., 2023). For example, Zhang et al. (2021c) investigated the effects of commercially available silymarin-containing micelles on the reproductive performance and health status of sows. Extracted from the milk thistle plant, silymarin is a natural flavonoid mixture that has poor water solubility and low in vivo biological activity (Surai, 2015). Encapsulation of silymarin in micelles improved its solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Silymarin-containing micelles were added to the sows’ diets from the 109th d prenatally to the 21st d postnatally. The findings revealed significantly increased antioxidant capacity (SOD and GSH-Px activity) and decreased cortisol levels (oxidized glutathione [GSSG] concentrations and GSSG/GSH ratio) in the serum of the sows, resulting in increased feed intake, reduced BW loss, improved milk production, and increased litter weaning weight (Zhang et al., 2021c).

Nanospheres are solid NPs consisting of either natural or synthetic materials, including polymers, lipids, and inorganic compounds. Unlike NEs and micelles, nanospheres have a solid core and thus higher structural stability and drug loading flexibility. Various methods for preparing nanospheres are available, with commonly used ones including the emulsion solvent evaporation method, nano-precipitation, high-pressure homogenization, self-assembly technology, and spray drying. Additionally, cargo such as BCs can be encapsulated into nanospheres via physical embedding, adsorption, or chemical bonding, and the synthesized nanospheres are usually 10 to 1000 nm in diameter (He et al., 2015). The synthetic formula can be flexibly adjusted according to the properties of the active ingredients (hydrophilicity/hydrophobicity) and delivery requirements (sustained release/targeting) (Masotti and Ortaggi, 2009). One application of nanospheres involved curcumin, a polyphenolic compound derived from Curcuma longa that exhibits various biological activities, including antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immune-enhancing properties. However, curcumin has limitations, including low solubility in water and rapid metabolic breakdown, which obstruct its widespread application in pig feed (Moniruzzaman and Min, 2020). To overcome these issues, Kim et al. (2020) prepared 100 nm curcumin nanospheres using lecithin, which enhanced the water solubility of curcumin by approximately threefold, thereby increasing its bioavailability and allowing it to exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic regulatory effects more effectively. It was also found that a diet containing these curcumin nanospheres improved the growth performance and health status of pigs by improving lipid metabolism, enhancing the intestinal barrier, regulating immune responses, and inhibiting pathogenic microorganisms (Moniruzzaman et al., 2023).

Future prospects and conclusion

Owing to their diverse biological functions, non-toxicity, environmental friendliness, and cost-effectiveness, BCs have achieved great progress in improving pig health and growth performance. Even under challenging conditions, such as stress and infections, dietary BCs mitigate the negative impacts of these challenges and help to prevent diseases by improving antioxidant status and exerting immunoregulatory effects, reducing inflammation and enhancing gut health, thereby reducing the incidence of diarrhea, IUGR, and mortality, among others. Despite these benefits, the use of BCs in the pig industry faces various challenges, including issues related to poor stability during storage, low bioavailability, and difficulty in scaling up production. Nonetheless, this review also highlights that advances in synthetic biology, gene editing, and AI provide new opportunities to promote the industrial use of BCs as feed additives. This review has also explored the use of nanoparticle delivery systems to improve the stability, targeting, and bioavailability of BCs, although such work is still in its infancy. The range of achievements mentioned above has made BCs increasingly attractive as additives to pig diets. Regarding the challenges facing BCs in the pig industry, these are summarized below based on five points that require further work to promote the application of BCs for pig feed.1)In certain instances, BCs can have harmful effects, particularly for natural extracts containing various bioactive molecules. For example, a high-fat diet supplemented with fish oil may induce significant obesity in sows, which can in turn negatively impact litter size and piglet health. Thus, there is a need for comprehensive investigations to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying specific BCs and their potential toxic effects. Additionally, BCs need to undergo systematic analyses regarding taxonomic identification, safety, and toxicities to be approved as feed additives under legislation such as the European Regulatory Framework and Safety Assessment.2)The significant differences among species and the changing conditions that pigs are in intensive farming are also major challenges that need to be overcome. While dietary BCs have been shown to ameliorate growth impairment in IUGR piglets, their effects on growth performance in finishing pigs are often less pronounced in the context of maturation of the pigs' digestive and immune systems, along with their increased resilience to internal and external challenges. It would thus be informative to rationally optimize the formulation of diets supplemented with appropriate BCs or combinations thereof, considering pigs' physiological condition and growth stage, while carefully attending to safety, dosage, and dietary patterns.3)There is an urgent and ongoing need to identify new BCs and optimize the yield and physicochemical properties of existing ones. Establishing a structural database of BCs and a library of engineered microbial strains targeting specific biochemical pathways, along with the use of machine learning and AI, should accelerate the discovery and refinement of BCs. In addition, programmed preparation processes and standardized quality control technologies remain crucial aspects to be resolved. By enabling the appropriate design of BCs, nanotechnology provides a promising platform for improving BC stability and safety, as well as specific or sustained delivery in targeted intestinal regions, thereby enhancing bioavailability and biological functions in animals. Notably, there is a need to evaluate the balance between production gains and overall cost-effectiveness, including the preparation of BCs and their maintenance during storage.5)NPs possess charged surfaces, highly specific surface areas, and strong adsorption capacities, making them prone to interacting with ions and proteins during circulation. This can potentially diminish their intended efficacy and trigger inflammatory responses in animals. Key research challenges include obtaining a detailed understanding of the regulation of toxicity and inflammatory responses of NPs in the animal gut and elsewhere in the body, as well as elucidating the potential long-term toxicity of NPs to humans due to their residues in pork products. Another point to be considered is the potential synergistic effects and interaction mechanisms between antimicrobial NPs and BCs.

In conclusion, research on BCs as feed additives in the pig industry is flourishing, although challenges related to industrialization and large-scale implementation remain. This review provides an overview of the application of BCs and the obstacles limiting their wider and more effective use. This paper underscores the importance of research aimed at elucidating and refining the functional mechanisms of BCs, as well as at developing methods for preparing BCs as pig feed additives. Ongoing efforts are needed to effectively employ novel technologies to expand the sources and yields of BCs, along with the use of NPs to improve their bioavailability.
