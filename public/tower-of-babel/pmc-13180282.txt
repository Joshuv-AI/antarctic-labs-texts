VV-ECMO without anticoagulation in trauma patients: balancing bleeding and thrombosis
Authors: Jingyi Wu, Yu Gan, Hongbin Hu, Lihan Shen, Zhenhua Zeng
Published: 2026

Severe hypoxemic respiratory failure is a major cause of early mortality in severe trauma patients. Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) offers life-saving support when conventional mechanical ventilation fails [1, 2], with its use expanded in trauma populations in recent years [3, 4]. Systemic anticoagulation is traditionally recommended during ECMO to prevent circuit thrombosis [5], but it increases bleeding risk, a particular challenge in trauma patients with acute traumatic coagulopathy and ongoing hemorrhage risk. We report our experience with 14 trauma patients with severe hypoxemic respiratory failure who received VV-ECMO without systemic anticoagulation in a tertiary intensive care unit.

The median age was 47 (35–62) years, and 78.6% were male. Additional baseline characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The median duration of VV-ECMO support was 227.3 (110.6–293.6) hours. Before ECMO initiation, the median PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio was 58.0 (49.8–77.1), indicating profound hypoxemia. Detailed ECMO management parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

During ECMO support, thrombotic complications were observed but remained limited. Oxygenator thrombosis occurred in 2 patients (14.3%), and oxygenator exchange due to thrombosis was required in 1 patients (7.1%). No circuit exchange was necessary. A total of 5 patients (35.7%) had deep vein thrombosis, and 1 patient (7.1%) had suspected pulmonary embolism on CT angiography without deep vein thrombosis. These findings suggest that, in this context, thrombotic complications may be manageable with careful monitoring.

Major bleeding occurred in 4 patients (28.6%), reflecting the intrinsically high hemorrhagic risk of trauma patients requiring ECMO support. However, no catastrophic bleeding event directly attributable to the absence of anticoagulation was identified. Transfusion requirements and laboratory data are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

ICU mortality was 21.4% (3/14), and 28-day survival reached 78.6% (11/14). No deaths were directly related to ECMO-related complications. Key clinical characteristics and outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

This experience reflects real-world clinical decision-making in a particularly high-risk population in whom standard anticoagulation may not be feasible. The balance between bleeding and thrombosis is especially challenging in trauma patients, and our observations suggest that VV-ECMO without systemic anticoagulation may be a reasonable option in carefully selected cases.

These findings should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of patients. Nevertheless, they support the feasibility of an anticoagulation-free approach in selected trauma patients, provided that close monitoring of the ECMO circuit and patient condition is ensured.
