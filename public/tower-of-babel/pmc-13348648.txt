Multimodal perioperative monitoring of intracranial pressure surrogates and cerebral oxygenation during laparoscopic surgery: implications for early cognitive outcomes
Authors: Ozkan Aba, Gulbin Yalçın Sezen, Ozlem Ersoy Karka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate perioperative changes in optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD) and regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) using noninvasive monitoring techniques during laparoscopic abdominal surgery, and to assess their association with postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD).MethodsThis prospective observational study included 50 ASA I–III patients aged 20–60 years undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery under general anesthesia. Bilateral ONSD and rSO₂ were measured at five time points: before induction (T0), 5 min after induction (T1), 5 min after CO₂ pneumoperitoneum (T2), 30 min after pneumoperitoneum (T3), and 5 min after desufflation (T4). IAP was assessed using intravesical measurements and insufflator readings. Cognitive function was evaluated with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) preoperatively and 24 h postoperatively. Hemodynamic and airway parameters were recorded concurrently.ResultsONSD increased significantly following pneumoperitoneum, peaking at 30 min, and returned toward baseline after desufflation. In contrast, rSO₂ demonstrated a non-synchronous pattern, increasing after induction, decreasing transiently after pneumoperitoneum, and subsequently recovering toward post-induction levels, suggesting preserved cerebral oxygenation despite intraoperative physiological changes. No significant correlation was observed between ONSD and rSO₂. Intravesical intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) measurements were consistently higher than insufflator-derived values. No significant differences were found between preoperative and postoperative MMSE scores.ConclusionsCO₂ pneumoperitoneum induces transient increases in intracranial pressure (ICP) surrogates and reversible changes in cerebral oxygenation without affecting early postoperative cognitive function. These findings support the use of multimodal monitoring rather than reliance on single parameters.Trial registrationThe study was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov.ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT07264764. Introduction

Laparoscopic surgery has become the preferred approach for many abdominal procedures because of its minimally invasive nature, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter recovery times compared with open surgery [1]. However, carbon dioxide (CO2) pneumoperitoneum, which is required to create the operative field, induces an acute elevation in IAP that can influence respiratory mechanics, cardiovascular dynamics, and cerebral physiology [2, 3]. Elevated IAP can increase ICP via transmission through the valveless vertebral venous system and impaired jugular venous drainage, potentially altering cerebral perfusion and oxygen delivery [4]. These physiological changes may contribute to POCD, a complication characterized by impairments in memory, attention, and executive function, affecting a substantial proportion of surgical patients. Reported incidence rates vary depending on patient characteristics and timing of assessment, ranging from approximately 10% to 40%, particularly in high-risk populations such as older adults, individuals with obesity, and those with low baseline cognitive reserve [5, 6].

Noninvasive monitoring tools, such as ultrasonographic measurement of the ONSD and NIRS based cerebral oximetry, provide clinicians with valuable insights into perioperative cerebral dynamics. ONSD correlates with invasively measured ICP and reflects rapid, reversible changes during anesthetic and surgical interventions, including pneumoperitoneum [7, 8]. NIRS allows continuous monitoring of rSO2, which serves as a surrogate marker for cerebral perfusion and oxygen balance [9]. While both modalities have demonstrated utility in neurocritical care and major surgeries, their combined application during routine laparoscopic procedures remains underexplored.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of CO2 pneumoperitoneum on ONSD, rSO2, and early postoperative cognitive function in adults undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery under general anesthesia. We hypothesized that pneumoperitoneum would induce transient changes in ONSD and rSO₂ and explored whether these changes might be associated with early postoperative cognitive alterations. By integrating multimodal monitoring, this study seeks to clarify perioperative cerebral responses and their clinical relevance, providing evidence for optimized anesthetic strategies in laparoscopic surgery.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Duzce University Faculty of Medicine, Department of Anesthesiology and Reanimation between July 2020 and February 2021. The protocol was approved by the Duzce University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. 2020/149, July 6, 2020) and registered with the Duzce University Scientific Research Coordination Office under project number 2020.04.02.10140. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT07264764.

Participants

Fifty adult patients (aged 20–60 years; American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] I–III) scheduled for laparoscopic abdominal surgery (e.g., cholecystectomy, appendectomy, hernia repair) under general anesthesia were enrolled. The exclusion criteria were pregnancy, known neurological or ocular disease, prior ocular surgery, significant cardiovascular disease (moderate to severe), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and conversion from laparoscopy to open laparotomy.

Anesthesia and monitoring protocol

All patients underwent standardized anesthetic management. Following preoxygenation with 100% oxygen for 2 min, anesthesia induction consisted of intravenous thiopental sodium (5–7 mg·kg⁻¹), fentanyl (1 µg·kg⁻¹), rocuronium (0.6 mg·kg⁻¹), and lidocaine (1 mg·kg⁻¹). Tracheal intubation was performed by an experienced anesthesiologist, and the laryngoscopy duration was recorded. Anesthesia was maintained with 1 minimum alveolar concentration sevoflurane in a 50% O2/air mixture using volume-controlled ventilation (tidal volume 6–8 mL·kg⁻¹, PEEP 5 cmH2O, respiratory rate adjusted to maintain end-tidal CO2 [EtCO2] 33–40 mmHg). Hemodynamic variables, including systolic (SBP), diastolic (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), and peak inspiratory airway pressure (Ppeak), were continuously recorded. All measurements were performed in the supine position, and the Trendelenburg position was not used.

Optic nerve sheath diameter measurement

ONSD was measured by a single trained anesthesiologist using a high-frequency (18 MHz) linear ultrasound probe (MyLab 5, Esaote, Genoa, Italy). With the patients in the supine position and eyelids closed, the probe was gently placed on the upper eyelid in the horizontal and vertical planes without exerting pressure. The ONSD was defined as the distance between the outer edges of the optic nerve sheath 3 mm posterior to the optic disc. For each eye, two measurements (horizontal and vertical) were averaged, and the mean bilateral ONSD values were calculated for each time point.

Cerebral oxygenation monitoring

rSO2 was continuously measured using NIRS (INVOS 4100, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with bilateral frontal sensors. The rSO2 values were recorded at the same five perioperative time points as the ONSD: (T0) before induction, (T1) 5 min after induction, (T2) 5 min after CO2 pneumoperitoneum, (T3) 30 min after pneumoperitoneum, and (T4) 5 min after desufflation.

Intra-abdominal pressure measurement

The IAP was assessed using two methods: intravesical pressure measurement and direct insufflator readings. For intravesical measurements, a Foley catheter was placed post-induction, the bladder was drained, and 25 mL of sterile saline was instilled. A pressure transducer was connected via a catheter and zeroed at the mid-axillary line. The IAP values were recorded at T1–T4. Insufflator IAP was recorded at T2 and T3 (when pneumoperitoneum was active).

Cognitive function assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE, administered in a face-to-face format by a trained researcher on the day before surgery (baseline) and at 24 h postoperatively. The MMSE was selected as a brief bedside screening tool to detect early postoperative cognitive change, rather than to establish a definitive diagnosis of POCD.

In line with previous perioperative studies, a decrease of ≥ 2 points from the baseline MMSE score at 24 h was used as an operational definition of early POCD for screening purposes. This threshold was applied to facilitate standardized comparison across patients and to identify individuals with clinically apparent short-term cognitive decline [10, 11].

Given the known limitations of MMSE in detecting subtle cognitive impairment and its susceptibility to ceiling effects, the assessment was intentionally restricted to early postoperative screening. The results were therefore interpreted as indicators of early cognitive vulnerability, and not as comprehensive neuropsychological outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was applied to evaluate time-dependent changes. Between-group differences (e.g., POCD vs. non-POCD) were analyzed using independent sample t-tests or chi-square tests, as appropriate. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationships between ONSD, rSO2, IAP, and hemodynamic variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Power analysis, based on prior studies reporting ONSD changes, determined that 48 subjects were sufficient to detect clinically significant differences (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size = 0.16), with 50 patients enrolled to account for potential dropouts.

Perioperative hemodynamic and respiratory changes

A significant effect of time was observed on systolic blood pressure (SBP) (F(3,37) = 20.91, p < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni analysis showed no significant differences between T0–T1 (p = 0.082) and T1–T2 (p = 1.000), nor between T3–T4 (p = 0.267). Despite the lack of differences between adjacent time points, SBP demonstrated a gradual decline from induction onwards.

Diastolic blood pressure (DBP) also showed a significant time-dependent change (F(2,69) = 9.42, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed among T0, T1, and T2 (p = 1.000), whereas DBP values at T4 were significantly lower compared to earlier time points (p = 0.001–0.030). Additionally, DBP at T3 was significantly lower than T2 (p = 0.001) but higher than T4 (p = 0.030), indicating a delayed decline after pneumoperitoneum.

Similarly, mean arterial pressure (MAP) varied significantly over time (F(2,84) = 9.06, p < 0.001). No differences were found among T0, T1, and T2 (p = 1.000), while MAP at T3 and T4 was significantly lower than earlier measurements (p = 0.001–0.030), with no difference between T3 and T4 (p = 0.067), suggesting a sustained decrease after pneumoperitoneum.

Heart rate (HR) also differed significantly across time points (F(3,36.40) = 32.21, p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between T0–T2 (p = 1.000) or T3–T4 (p = 1.000), whereas all other comparisons were significant (p = 0.001–0.008). HR peaked at 5 min after induction (T1) and subsequently declined, with T0 and T2 values remaining significantly higher than T3 and T4. Optic nerve sheath diameter and cerebral oxygenation changes

ONSD showed a significant time-dependent change (F(3,10) = 111.42, p < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni analysis revealed no significant difference between post-induction (5.67 ± 0,06 mm, T1) and post-desufflation (5.59 ± 0.04 mm, T4) values (p = 1.000). However, ONSD increased significantly following pneumoperitoneum initiation (5.96 ± 0.05 mm, T2) and at 30 min (6.15 ± 0.06 mm, T3) compared to other time points (p < 0.001), and returned to near post-induction levels after desufflation.

rSO₂ also demonstrated significant variation over time (F(3.26) = 7.44, p < 0.001). rSO₂ at baseline (67.47 ± 1.07, T0) was significantly lower than both T1 (72.6 ± 1.4; p < 0.001) and T4 (71.43 ± 1.09; p = 0.015). Additionally, rSO₂ decreased significantly at T2 (69.2 ± 1.2) compared to T1 (p = 0.014). Overall, rSO₂ increased after induction, exhibited a transient decline shortly after pneumoperitoneum, and subsequently showed an increasing trend. As illustrated in Fig. 2, ONSD and rSO₂ exhibited distinct temporal patterns. While ONSD progressively increased following pneumoperitoneum and peaked at T3, rSO₂ initially increased after induction, then transiently decreased at T2 before recovering to near post-induction levels after desufflation. Intra-abdominal pressure changes

Intravesical IAP measurements were initiated 5 min after induction, whereas insufflator-derived IAP values were available only during pneumoperitoneum (T2 and T3). Therefore, comparisons between the two measurement methods were limited to these time points. Paired samples t-test analysis demonstrated significantly higher intravesical IAP values compared to insufflator measurements at both T2 (t(49) = 4.44, mean difference = 1.2 mmHg, p < 0.001) and T3 (t(49) = 2.60, mean difference 0.8 mmHg, p = 0.012).

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between the two measurement methods, indicating that although both methods varied in parallel, intravesical IAP values were consistently higher than insufflator-derived measurements. POCD and risk factors

Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE preoperatively and 24 h postoperatively. The mean preoperative MMSE score was 26.06 ± 2.52, while the postoperative score was 24.74 ± 3.09. Paired samples t-test analysis revealed no significant difference between the two measurements (t(49) = 1.25, p = 0.218), although a strong positive correlation was observed (r = 0.81, p < 0.001).

Further subgroup analyses based on gender, education level, body mass index, and ASA status showed no significant differences between preoperative and postoperative MMSE scores across these variables (p = 0.178–0.729). Detailed results based on subgroup differences are presented in Table 6.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of perioperative cerebral dynamics during laparoscopic abdominal surgery using a multimodal monitoring approach. Importantly, our findings offer novel evidence demonstrating a clear dissociation between ICP surrogates and cerebral oxygenation during pneumoperitoneum. Furthermore, it shows that these physiological alterations are transient and are not associated with early postoperative cognitive performance. Collectively, these findings contribute to a more nuanced understanding of perioperative cerebral physiology and support the concept that isolated monitoring parameters may not adequately reflect overall cerebral status.

ONSD evaluation

The marked increase in ONSD during pneumoperitoneum, peaking at 30 min and returning toward post-induction levels after desufflation, supports the presence of a transient ICP-related effect. ONSD is widely recognized as a reliable noninvasive surrogate of ICP and has been shown to correlate with invasive measurements across various clinical settings [12–14].

However, despite similar temporal trends observed between ONSD and IAP, no significant correlation was identified. This finding suggests that intracranial dynamics during pneumoperitoneum cannot be explained solely by increased abdominal pressure but rather reflect a complex interaction between intrathoracic pressure transmission, venous congestion, and cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, as described in previous physiological models [15, 16].

The association between ONSD and peak inspiratory pressure (Ppeak), particularly during pneumoperitoneum, further supports the role of respiratory mechanics in ICP modulation. Increased intrathoracic pressure may impair cerebral venous drainage and contribute to transient elevations in ICP, a mechanism highlighted in laparoscopic surgery studies [17].

The absence of consistent correlations between ONSD and MAP or IAP reinforces the concept that ONSD reflects a multifactorial physiological response rather than a single pressure-dependent variable.

rSO2 evaluation

In contrast to ONSD, rSO₂ demonstrated a distinct and non-synchronous pattern, characterized by an increase following induction, a transient decrease after pneumoperitoneum, and subsequent recovery toward post-induction levels. This dissociation represents a key and clinically relevant finding, indicating that increases in ICP surrogates do not necessarily translate into impaired cerebral oxygenation.

This finding supports the concept that cerebral oxygenation is more closely related to autoregulatory capacity than to absolute ICP changes. Under controlled anesthetic conditions, cerebral autoregulation appears to be preserved, allowing maintenance of adequate oxygen delivery despite transient physiological perturbations.

Similar patterns have been reported in recent studies evaluating cerebral oxygenation during laparoscopic procedures, where autoregulatory mechanisms maintained stable cerebral oxygenation despite increases in IAP and associated hemodynamic changes [18, 19]. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of normocapnic ventilation strategies, which are known to play a crucial role in preserving cerebral perfusion.

Overall, the observed dissociation between ONSD and rSO₂ underscores the importance of interpreting cerebral monitoring parameters within a broader physiological framework, rather than relying on single-modality measurements.

It should be noted that all measurements were performed in the supine position, and procedures requiring steep Trendelenburg positioning were not included. The Trendelenburg position is known to increase ICP by impairing venous drainage and increasing central venous pressure. Therefore, the absence of this positioning may have contributed to the relatively modest changes observed in ONSD and the lack of association with early cognitive outcomes.

IAP evaluation

The comparison of IAP techniques revealed that intravesical IAP values were consistently higher than insufflator-derived measurements, despite a moderate correlation between the two methods. This suggests that insufflator readings may underestimate the effective IAP transmitted to the patient.

Previous studies have similarly emphasized variability between measurement techniques and the importance of appropriate method selection when evaluating pressure-related physiological effects [20, 21].

MMSE assessment for POCD

Importantly, no significant differences were observed between preoperative and postoperative MMSE scores, either in the overall cohort or within stratified subgroups.

The absence of significant within-group differences suggests that the observed intraoperative changes in ONSD and rSO₂ were not associated with clinically detectable short-term cognitive decline at 24 h. This finding indicates that transient intraoperative physiological alterations may not be sufficient to induce early measurable cognitive impairment, particularly in relatively low-risk patient populations.

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have emphasized that perioperative neurocognitive disorders are influenced by a complex interplay of factors, including baseline cognitive status, age, metabolic conditions, inflammation, and perioperative variables, rather than single intraoperative parameters alone [5, 22–24]. In this context, the lack of association between intraoperative physiological changes and early cognitive outcomes in the present study is consistent with current evidence.

Taken together, these findings highlight that early postoperative cognitive outcomes cannot be reliably predicted by isolated intraoperative cerebral monitoring parameters. This underscores the need for a more comprehensive and multidimensional approach when evaluating perioperative neurocognitive risk.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE, which is a practical screening tool but has limited sensitivity for detecting subtle cognitive impairment [25, 26]. Cognitive assessment was restricted to the early postoperative period, and delayed cognitive outcomes were not evaluated.

The relatively small sample size may have limited the ability to detect weaker associations. Additionally, the single-center design may restrict the generalizability of the findings.

Another important limitation is that all procedures were performed in the supine position, and surgeries requiring steep Trendelenburg positioning were not included. Since the Trendelenburg position is known to significantly increase ICP, our findings may not be generalizable to procedures involving head-down positioning, where more pronounced cerebral physiological alterations may occur [27].

Finally, the absence of a universally accepted ONSD threshold remains a limitation when interpreting absolute values [28, 29].

Conclusion

CO₂ pneumoperitoneum during laparoscopic abdominal surgery results in significant but reversible increases in ONSD and transient alterations in rSO₂, without a direct association with early postoperative cognitive outcomes.

These findings highlight the complex and multifactorial nature of perioperative cerebral physiology and suggest that intraoperative changes in cerebral monitoring parameters should be interpreted within a broader, integrative clinical context rather than as isolated predictors of cognitive outcomes.

Overall, this study supports the concept that multimodal neuromonitoring provides a more comprehensive understanding of cerebral dynamics and may be more informative than single-parameter assessment in the perioperative setting.
