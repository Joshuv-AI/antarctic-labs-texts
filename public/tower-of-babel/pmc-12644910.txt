Mathematical modeling and analysis for the transmission dynamics of wheat yellow rust disease
Authors: Abayneh Kebede Fantaye, Mohammed Yiha Dawed, Kassahun Getnet Mekonen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Wheat is one of the most significant crops grown worldwide and serves as both an important source of food for consumers and a means of income for farmers. However, the fungus that causes wheat yellow rust (WYR) seriously damages the world’s wheat production, especially in developing countries. In this study, we proposed and analyzed a compartmental mathematical model to describe the transmission dynamics of WYR disease on wheat crops. For the proposed model, the existence and uniqueness, the positivity and boundedness of the solutions are explained. The two equilibrium points, namely disease-free and endemic equilibrium points are demonstrated, and the basic reproduction number is evaluated using the next-generation matrix approach. The Routh Hurwitz stability criteria and center manifold theory are used to analyze the local stability of the equilibrium points and the existence of forward bifurcation, respectively. The global stability of the equilibrium points are also established using Lyapunov’s approach. A sensitivity analysis of the model is also carried out. To validate our analytical results, MATLAB software is utilized to conduct several numerical simulations and generate graphical representations of the results. The findings of this study suggest that effective use of fungicides can significantly prevent the spread of WYR disease.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is the earliest cereal known to have been cultivated, and it played a role in human history in addition to being a significant crop today1. It is a widely grown and traded cereal crop, accounting for 15% of total planting areas worldwide. It is the world’s second most significant cereal crop, next to rice, and is widely traded globally2. Wheat is an important crop in Sub-Saharan African nations, contributing to farm income and food security3,4. Ethiopia, Africa’s second-largest wheat producer, produces over 6 million tons annually from 2.1 million hectares, accounting for 21.7% of Africa’s total wheat production5. It is the fourth most widely grown crop in Ethiopia. Wheat grain is a crucial cereal crop for both traditional and contemporary processed foods like injera and macaroni and pasta, the straw is used as animal fodder and roof tacking material, making it a priority for production and marketing6.

However, rust diseases(stem, yellow/strip, and leaf rust) pose a threat to wheat production7. Among these, wheat yellow rust (WYR), often known as wheat stripe rust, is a severe infection of wheat generated by the biotrophic fungal pathogen8. Disease has the potential to happen at all stages from the one-leaf stage to plant maturity as long as the plants remain green9. WYR is primarily dispersed by wind and have the potential to spread far and wide10. Massive amounts of spores can be observed in pustules on the upper surface of the leaves. Whenever the spores become airborne, they propagate at random. Some of them will land on soil or other plants, but most will remain airborne until they are killed by sunlight in a few days. The disease is most common on leaves, but head tissue also can develop symptoms when disease is severe. On susceptible crops, yellow rust occurs as a swarm of yellow to orange urediniospores emerging from pustules arrayed in long, narrow stripes on leaves, leaf sheaths, glumes, and awns11. The pathogen’s alternative host was undiscovered until 2010,12 discovered that sensitive Berberis spp. was the pathogen’s alternative host and demonstrated that the berberry serves a significant role for the emergence of new strains.

WYR is a historical occurrence that still causes significant damage to vulnerable wheat varieties all over the world13. Currently, the most significant commercial wheat rust disease in the world, with yield losses approaching 100% in vulnerable cultivars14. It was originally reported in Europe by Gadd in 1777 and in Sweden in 1794, however, it has most likely persisted for a long period15. WYR epidemics have occurred in over 60 countries worldwide at the moment, especially in central and western Asia and North Africa. WYR has lead to loss of crop production that ranged from 40 to 100% in Ethiopia, primarily due to cultivar vulnerability, time of initial infection, and the environmental conditions during epidemic growth16. Significant outbreaks of WYR happened in Ethiopia in the 1970s, 1980s, 1988, 2000s, and 2010s17. More than 400,000 hectares of wheat were impacted in 2010, resulting in significant output losses that are impossible to measure. In spite of the fact that the occurrence of WYR varies seasonally in the country’s Arsi and Bale areas, the primary and long period of rainfall is suitable for stripe rust formation18.

WYR needs green plants to live from one growing season to the next, hence the term green bridge. Eliminating volunteer plants that assist stripe rust growth (the Green Bridge) is an efficient treatment method for epidemics caused by endogenous infection19. Other crop management that includes cultivar selection, sowing time, and variety resistance could lead to effective yellow rust control20. Furthermore, to protect crop production from possible losses brought on by infections, fungi management entails timely chemical treatment. When fungicides are used early in infections, they can successfully prevent its transmission20. Yet, detection must continue after fungicide application to prevent infection reappearance since there is the short period between infection and spore growth. Because of fungicide resistance, inadequate treatment coverage, and natural breakdown, fungicides are frequently reapplied during crop growth.

Mathematical modeling has an important role in understanding, predicting, and managing the propagation dynamics of pathogens. Important crop diseases caused by fungal infections are dispersed mostly through wind and rain splash. By including a factor that contributes to spore dispersion from rain splash and modifying the infection term in response to meteorological factors like air temperature and relative humidity, Tabonglek et al.21 developed a mathematical model for spore dispersion, specifically a non-local diffusion equation in terms of the dispersal kernel. The finite difference method was employed by the model to answer the problems after evaluating their existence and uniqueness using Banach’s fixed point theorem. The research areas experienced a broad pandemic of rice blast illness due to the successful use of climate data in the development of the disease.

Ullah et al.22 suggested a mathematical model for describing wheat production, which includes three variables: wheat spike length, total wheat biomass, and pest, represented by S(t), B(t), and P(t), respectively. Researchers eventually illustrated what could lead to wheat yield if the winter season in Bangladesh’s southwestern region was extended a few more days for these temperature-dependent crops. According to the authors’ findings, optimal temperature and low salinity are required for good production and spike length. The overall amount of wheat output will increase if the biomass and spike length are good. As a result, low temperature and salinity will yield more wheat than high temperature and salinity. The dynamical behavior of an eco-epidemiological model including a wheat field (Host species) and predator species (Locust insect) was studied and analyzed by23. The author classified species as follows: prey X(t) (host species (wheat)) and predator individuals Y (t) (locust bug). A system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations of first order characterizes the interaction of susceptible and infected species with assailant species.

An integrated model-based forecasting strategy for WYR disease risk at the regional scale, involving meteorological, airborne inoculum, and satellite-based data, was produced and tested by24. Weather data such as maximum, minimum, and average temperature; total and frequency of precipitation; consecutive days with and without precipitation; accumulation of negative and positive degree-days; and number of days with extreme temperature events were used to develop probabilistic models to assess WYR severity in the United States. A threshold-based weather model was proposed by25 in Luxembourg and is currently being used operationally in a warning system for timely and optimal fungicide treatments for WYR. Although the findings were region-specific, the authors stated that the fundamental premise and approach are likely applicable in other geographical regions. These methods have been proposed to increase fungicide application timeliness and effectiveness while minimizing economic expenses, environmental impacts, and loss of productivity. Zhang et al.26 introduced hyperspectral remote sensing as a novel method for nondestructive monitoring of wheat stripe rust. Their study demonstrated that fractional order differential (FOD) equations in the sense of Grunwald–Letnikov (G–L) combined with Gaussian process regression improved the accuracy of disease detection and reduced the background noise in spectral data, thus enabling more precise field management strategies.

However, all of the previous works did not incorporate a compartmental mathematical model with qualitative analysis for the WYR transmission dynamics. The suggested model takes into account a compartmental mathematical model for the WYR transmission dynamics with two interacting populations: the wheat leaf population and the fungal pathogen in the environment. To create realistic scenarios, most of the values of the parameters of model Eq. (1) are estimated and the remaining are taken from the literature. Symptoms of WYR appearing on the surface of leaves, leaf sheaths, glumes, or awns) commences approximately 12–14 days under favorable conditions8. Conclusion

In this work, we formulated and investigated a deterministic compartmental mathematical model for the transmission dynamics of wheat yellow rust disease. Some basic properties of the models such as existence and uniqueness, positivity and boundedness of solution of the model, disease-free (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{0}$$\end{document}) and endemic equilibrium points (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{1}$$\end{document}) was calculated. Also, using next-generation matrix, the basic reproduction number (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}$$\end{document}) where obtained. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}<1$$\end{document}, the system has just the disease-free equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{0}$$\end{document}, which is asymptotically stable both locally and globally, while unstable when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}>1$$\end{document}. The system has a unique endemic equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{1}$$\end{document}, which is locally stable. According to our findings, the disease is endemic if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}>1$$\end{document} and disappears when it reaches \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}<1$$\end{document}. The model’s forward bifurcation at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}=1$$\end{document} was demonstrated by bifurcation analysis, which was proved through center manifold theory. The normalized forward sensitivity index was employed as a tool in the sensitivity analysis. The value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}$$\end{document} decreases as the transmission rates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{s}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{p}$$\end{document} rise, and the numerical findings confirm that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{0}$$\end{document} eventually becomes less than one at a given level of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{s}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _{p}$$\end{document}. Decreasing the susceptible class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_{w}$$\end{document} results from an increasing protection rate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}). The numerical simulation is performed using MATLAB ode45 and agrees with the analytical findings. In subsequent research, we plan to expand the model in various biologically significant ways. These include taking into account weather variability and seasonal climate, which have significant effects on the persistence and extinction of diseases. Furthermore, because these social dynamics can be crucial to the efficacy of disease management and control, we intend to look into the role of farmer awareness and behavioral responses. Furthermore, our goal is to expand the current model to a fractional optimal control framework, which will include a variety of management techniques in a spatial context, including the use of fungicides, resistant crop varieties, and cultural practices.
