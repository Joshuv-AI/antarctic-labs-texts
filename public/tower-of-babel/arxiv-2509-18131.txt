Two ways to knowledge?
Jean-Michel Tucny, Abhisek Ganguly, Santosh Ansumali, Sauro Succi

Abstract
It is shown that the weight matrices of transformer-based machine learning applications to the solution of two representative physical applications show a random-like character which bears no directly recognizable link to the physical and mathematical structure of the physical problem under study. This suggests that machine learning and the scientific method may represent two distinct and potentially complementary paths to knowledge, even though a strict notion of explainability in terms of direct correspondence between network parameters and physical structures may remain out of reach. It is also observed that drawing a parallel between transformer operation and (generalized) path-integration techniques may account for the random-like nature of the weights, but still does not resolve the tension with explainability. We conclude with some general comments on the hazards of gleaning knowledge without the benefit of Insight.

Burgers problem, physics-informed neural networks, xAI

1
Introduction

In the last decade AI and most notably machine learning (ML) applications
have taken science and society by storm, with relentless progresses
especially in the context of Large Language Models (LLM) and their
alleged and often hyped capability of boosting text elaboration
and speech abilities beyond the realm of human reach.
Leaving aside for the moment the paramount societal implications, such
developments put forward a very different approach to scientific
problem solving, with alleged potential of handling peaks of
complexity beyond the reach of our best theoretical and computational
models based on the Scientific Method,
e.g. models rooted upon a deep
Insight
into the basic
mechanisms underneath the phenomenon
under inspection (recently called World Model
Vafa et al. 2025
).

Ultimately, transformers, the hard-core architecture powering LLMs
and chatbots, are based on a mathematical black-box procedure
largely unaware of the physics underlying the material world, although adding
physics-informed constraints helps convergence and extrapolation capabilities for a large class of problems

Raissi et al. 2019
.
ML success stories are talk of the day: chess and GO victories, self-driving cars,
DeepFold mapping of protein structure, stand out
as some the most spectacular cases in point
Jumper et al. 2021
, lately
even conquering the Stockholm podium both in chemistry and physics
2025 2025
.
Given the current state of affairs, it appears necessary to go beyond
the dismissal ML as a “glorified interpolators” or LLMs as
mere “stochastic parrots”
Bender et al. 2021
.
This opens up a very intriguing scenario, both in terms of fundamental science
and practical technological fallouts alike.
The latter are making daily headlines and here we will not comment any
further on whether and to what extent they convey the real story.
For this, the reader is directed to the very informative paper
Liang et al. 2025
.

On the former front, the ultimate question is whether ML is here
to enable the so called “
End of Insight
” (EoI), an inspiring expression
first put forward by Steven Strogatz some twenty years ago,
Strogatz 2007
.
Basically, Strogatz’s point is that Insight, as we know it since
Galileo, namely the ultimate understanding of the basic principles
and mechanism which rule the systems under study, may show the
end of the rope when confronted with the most complex problems in science.
Examples include pandemics, weather forecast, most biological
processes and generally all phenomena which host the coexistence and mutual
interaction between mechanisms which straddle across many decades in space and time.

Perhaps, the complexity of such phenomena exceeds the capability, beauty
and elegance of a thorough understanding, and AI might be here
to keep us pushing ahead the frontier of knowledge without the
perk of Insight and the intimate pleasure of “finding things out”.

This is plausible, fascinating and perilous as well, since it
may distract us from noting that, besides an exuberant interest driven by
virtually unlimited financial big-tech power, ML is subject to a increasing
number of limitations when it
comes to put its achievements on the quantitative bench of solid scientific
and societal progress.
Hence, before we declare the EoI, it is imperative
to pursue more economic routes to knowledge, if not for
anything else, just because the current high-tech trend towards
ever bigger networks with trillions of parameters is simply
energetically unsustainable for the rest of the world, as recently
detailed in
Coveney and Succi 2025
(see also https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mjB6HDot1Uk).

Before unfolding our line of thinking, let us recollect
a few basic facts about the main complexity barriers faced
by the scientific method.

2
The complexity barriers

The basic philosophy behind ML is that with sufficient training data,
any
target can be reached, which often lead to claims of the scientific method’s demise.
The dominant view suggests that models and theories are unnecessary, as “data speak for themselves” and “correlation supersedes causation”.
However, when framed in such grandiose terms, this idea is fragile and easily refuted by general considerations from the physics of complex systems (see for example
Coveney et al. 2016
;
Succi and Coveney 2019
).

In recent years, LLMs have been celebrated as magical and revolutionary, yet
an increasing number of research papers are now exposing a different reality.
For instance, some of these studies highlight “Machine Bullshit” behavior, namely the strong tendency of
Reinforced Learning Human Feedback to promote the worst out
of humans, namely the attitude at ignoring factual truth in favor
of various forms of cheating, such as empty rhetoric, paltering,
weasel words and unverified claims
Liang et al. 2025
.

In this paper, we adopt a liberal stance toward large language models, by assuming that they can indeed achieve superhuman performance in text and speech generation. A few important questions then arise: What is the source of this apparent magic, and at what cost for science, society and humankind fabric altogether? These questions span many aspects of ML, but here we focus on just one, namely the notion of “Explainability”, whose absence is often seen as the royal path towards the End of Insight.

Before elaborating further, we briefly discuss the three pillars of complexity:

Nonlinearity
,
Nonlocality
and
Hyperdimensionality
.

2.1
Nonlinearity

Nonlinear systems exhibit two distinguishing and far-reaching
features: i) they do not respond in direct proportion to the solicitations
they receive, ii) they transfer information (energy) across scales.
It is not hard to grasp the enormous freedom which results from the
two features above, the loss of the linear superposition
principle being possibly the most significant consequence.
Such freedom makes nonlinear systems hard to predict, not only
in terms of erratic behavior (chaos), but also with regard to
their constructive power (emergent phenomena), which is particularly
apparent in the biological world.
The scientific method though has forged very powerful
mathematical methods to handle non-linearity, except that these
methods fall short of capturing the vastity of scales generated by
very strong nonlinearities, weather forecasting being a prominent example in point.

2.2
Non-locality

The key feature of non-local systems is that the behavior in one spatial region
being influenced by the system’s state everywhere else.
This interaction usually decays with the distance between the
two regions, but in non-local systems it cannot be ignored, no matter
how far apart the interacting components.
A typical example from physics is classical gravitation, which is controlled
by a potential decaying with inverse power of the distance.
The peculiarity of these systems is that they hardly reach a state of dynamic
order known as “local equilibrium”, usually controlled by a subset
of variables (termed “slow”) living in a lower-dimensional manifold.
The local equilibrium arises from a clear scale separation between slow and fast
variables, a feature which greatly simplifies the dynamics. Capturing dynamics is notoriously more challenging than statistics, which is why statistical mechanics is so effective for describing complex systems.
With non-locality in play, even the statistical mechanics may remain hard
to capture because of the aforementioned lack of scale separation.

2.3
Hyper-dimensionality

The human brain struggles to visualize phenomena beyond three dimensions. However, mathematics faces no such limitation, but high-dimensional systems become rapidly computationally untractable, due to
the notorious “Curse of Dimensionality” (CoD). This phenomenon of CoD
refers to the fact that
the information contained in a
d
-dimensional system grows
exponentially with the number of dimensions
Bellman 1966
.
As a matter of fact, the point is subtler than that.
Indeed, due to non-linearities, heterogeneities
and other factors, hyperdimensional systems are usually very sparse, i.e
the “important” phenomena occur in ultrasmall corners of their state space.
Reaching to the hidden places where the “golden nuggets” are located
is an exponentially difficult task, and this is possibly where ML can
indeed come to rescue to the scientific method and extend its scope.
To appreciate the above matters in some more detail, let us recollect
a few basic facts about transformers operation, the hard-core engine
of LLMs and chatbots.

3
The basic transformer procedure

The basic idea of transformers, foundational architecture of large language models, is to represent a given
D
-dimensional output
y
(target) through the recursive application of a simple non-linear map
LeCun et al. 2015
. For a neural network (NN) consisting of an input layer
x
,
L
hidden layers
z_{1}\dots z_{L}
, each containing
N
neurons, and an output layer
y
, the update chain
x\to z_{1}\dots\to z_{L}\to y
reads symbolically as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}x&\equiv z_{0},\\
z_{1}&=f[W_{0}x-b_{0}],\\
&\vdots\\
z_{L}&=f[W_{L-1}z_{L-1}-b_{L-1}],\\
y&\equiv z_{L+1}=f\left[W_{L}z_{L}-b_{L}\right].\end{split}

(1)

where
W_{l}
are
N\times N
weight matrices,
b_{l}
are N-dimensional bias arrays, and
f
is a nonlinear activation function (for example ReLU or GELU), which can be chosen from a large palette of options. At each layer a normalization condition is generally imposed:

||z_{l}||=||z_{0}||,

(2)

where
||.||
denotes some suitable norm.

The output
y
is then compared with a given target
y_{T}
(Truth) and the weights are recursively
updated in such a way as to minimize the distance between
y
and
y_{T}
(Loss function), up to the desired tolerance.
This error back-propagation step is usually performed via a steepest descent minimization:

{W^{\prime}}_{l}=W_{l}-\alpha\frac{\partial E[W_{0}\dots W_{L}]}{\partial W_{l}},

(3)

where
E[W]=||y_{T}-y[W]||
is the error (Loss Function) and
\alpha
is a relaxation parameter controlling the learning rate. Clearly there is no guarantee that steepest descent would systematically converge to the global minimum, and in fact, in general it does not since the Loss landscape can be utterly corrugated (see Fig.1). However, empirical evidence shows that the neural network can reach its target to a reasonable degree of accuracy also upon partial optimization, i.e. by attaining local minima instead of the global one. Understanding this kind of robustness is still one of the most active research areas in modern machine learning theory
He et al. 2016
.

It is worth noting that the above description essentially corresponds to a standard feed-forward neural network, often employed in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs). Transformers extend this basic structure by incorporating additional architectural ingredients (self-attention, multi-head attention, layer normalization, and positional encodings) that enable the efficient processing of sequential data and make them the backbone of large language models. These features are not central to the present work, but they are essential to the broader context of explainability in modern ML.

4
Transformers as discrete dynamical systems

In a recent paper
Succi 2025
it was pointed out that the ML
procedure described in the previous section presents an exact mapping
to discrete dynamical systems in relaxation form.
More precisely they can be regarded as a discretized
version of “neural” integro-differential equations of the form:

\partial_{t}z=-\gamma(z-z^{att}),

(4)

where
z=z(q,t)
is the physical signal and the local attractor
is given by:

z^{att}=f(Z),

(5)

where
f
is a local activation function(al) and the
symbol
Z
is a shorthand for the shifted linear convolution

Z(q,t)=\left(\int W(q,q^{\prime})z(q^{\prime})dq^{\prime}\right)-b(q)\equiv\bar{z}(q)-b(q),

(6)

where
b(q)
is a linear bias.

The procedure is mathematically elegant and conceptually transparent:
the solution
z(q,t)
relaxes to the local attractor
z^{att}

on a fast timescale
\gamma^{-1}
, while the local attractor also
moves in time, but usually on a slower pace.
It is evident that the preparation of the local attractor is the key ingredient of the procedure.

This is the result of two basic and complementary steps: first the signal
z(q,t)
is
non-locally and linearly convoluted to produce a “weighted average”
\bar{z}(q,t)
.
This latter is then shifted via the bias to yield the input
Z(q,t)=\bar{z}(q,t)-b(q)

to the second step, namely a local and nonlinear “deformation” finally yielding the
attractor
z^{att}=f(Z)
.
The linear convolution couples scales but leaves amplitudes unaffected, the local nonlinear
activation does just the opposite (note that they do not commute, hence the order matters).
This sequence holds the key for the universality of transformers as
interpolators, especially in high-dimensional spaces where it is key to form
sparse attractors in the ultra-small corners of state-space where the solution resides.

The analogy is completed by moving to discrete time: a
simple Euler time marching of eq. (
4
) as combined with
a suitable discretization of the “space” variable
q
into
a set of
N
discrete nodes
q_{i}
, delivers

z_{i}(t+1)=(1-\omega)z_{i}(t)+\omega z_{i}^{att}(t),

(7)

where
\omega=\gamma\Delta t
.

Direct comparison with (
1
) shows that, with
\omega=1
, this is precisely the forward step of the ML procedure with
(L+1)=T/\Delta t
layers and
N
neurons per layer, with the initial condition
z(0)=x
and output
y=z(T)
,
T
being
the time span of the evolution.
Clearly, the result is crucially dependent on the structure of the convolution kernel
W(q,q^{\prime})
, whose discrete
version is basically the weight matrix
W_{ij}
. Such matrix is the result of the minimization
problem eq. (
3
), hence strictly related to the topography of the Loss function
landscape
E=E[W]
, where
W
denotes the full set of weights.
Note that here and throughout we have assumed that each layer contains the same number of neurons
N
, a
condition which characterizes the transformer architecture.
One could of course generalize the formalism by allowing a different number of neurons at
each layers, which would correspond to coarsening (encoding) or refinement
(decoding) of the spatial representation of the signal
z(q,t)
.
Likewise one may occasionally jump across layers, thereby realizing the analogue
of an adaptive time-marching procedure. Both variants would affect the the discrete
dynamics of the signal in non-trivial ways, but in this paper we confine our
attention to the simplest of uniform number of neurons and timesteps across the entire network.

So far, ML sits fully within the math framework of the
scientific method, the “only” difference being that it
makes no use of any foreknowledge of the problem at hand.

5
Neural PDEs

In
Succi 2025
it was noted that each kernel gives rise to a corresponding PDE
and perhaps even low order PDEs, such as advection-diffusion-reaction, with
inhomogeneous, possibly time-dependent or even nonlinear coefficients, can
give rise to pretty complex spatio-temporal patterns.
Clearly, most common PDEs lead to highly structured sparse kernels
(narrow banded matrices), hence it was (kind of naively) argued
that inspection of real-life machine learning
weights might show signatures of such underlying structure.
For instance, a simple advection-diffusion-equation in one spatial
dimension would give rise to a tridiagonal-dominant weight matrix.
The detection of such structural regularity in the weight matrices
would offer a great benefit for their explainability in the first place,
let alone the energy savings resulting from a much smaller set of weights.
Since this is a cornerstone of our discussion, let us expand the idea in
some more detail with specific reference to
the Burgers equation, to be analyzed in depth in the next section:

To this purpose, let us introduce the gaussian kernel

g_{h}(r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}h}\exp{\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right)}.

and recall the integral representation of the
first order derivative

\frac{du}{dx}=\int W_{1}(r)u(x+r)dr,

where

W_{1}(r)=-\lim_{h\to 0}\left(\frac{r}{h^{2}}g_{h}(r)\right),

W_{2}(r)=\lim_{h\to 0}\;\left(\frac{r^{2}-h^{2}}{h^{4}}\right)g_{h}(r).

Hence the weight kernel for the Burgers equation reads as:

W_{Bur}(x,r)=-u(x)W_{1}(r)+\nu W_{2}(r),

where we have treated
u(x)
as an external field. This is
obviously an approximation (think of an iterative scheme to handle
the non-linearity), with no effect on the structure of the kernel.

This kernel is highly peaked around
r=0

and decays rapidly to zero as
r/h\gg 1
, reflecting
the local and deterministic structure of the Burgers equation.

6
Inspecting the weights of PINN solutions

The above considerations emphasize the key structural role played by the
weight kernel (matrix) in controlling the specific input-output
relation implemented by the transformer architecture.
In the following we inspect such information for the PINN solution of
two physics problems, rarefied gas dynamics described by the Boltzmann equation
and the Burgers equation.

6.1
PINN solution of the Boltzmann equation

In a previous work, we have inspected the weight matrices of a PINN
application to the solution of the Boltzmann equation for rarefied
gas dynamics
Tucny et al. 2025a
;
Tucny et al. 2025b
.
Just to fix idea, let us remind that the Boltzmann kinetic equation
reads as follows (in one spatial dimension for simplicity):

\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f=\frac{1}{\tau}C(f,f),

(8)

where
f(x,v;t)
is the probability density of finding a particle around position

x
and time
t
with velocity
v
.
The left hand side represents the free streaming of particles
(no force is assumed for simplicity), while the right-hand-side encodes binary collisions,
occuring on a collisional timescale
\tau
. The collision term
C(f,f)
is local
in space and time but quadratic and fully non-local in velocity space.
Conversely, the streaming term is non-local and linear.
On a spatial grid with
N_{x}
grid points in space and
N_{v}
in velocity space, the
Boltzmann equation gives rise to a fairly structured block tridiagonal
matrix, each diagonal block consisting of a full
N_{v}\times N_{v}
collision matrix,
while upper and lower diaghonal blocks have sparsity
N_{v}
.
In general, the Boltzmann equation lives in a
2d
-dimensional phase space,
d

being the number of spatial dimensions. As a result, in three-dimensional space
is generally solved by stochastic particle methods, typicall Direct Simulation Monte Carlo.

Full details are provided in the original paper, here we just remind
the main upshot, namely that such weights are pretty
close to random Gaussian matrices, with no directly recognizable link
to the underlying structure of the Boltzmann equation.

(9)

Note that at variance with the Boltzmann equation, nonlinearity and nonlocality
are tangled in the same
u\partial_{x}u
term.

The ML procedure employs 9 layers with 100 neurons each
and
tanh
as an activation function.
Here, the viscosity
\nu
is taken as
0.01/\pi
and the equation is
solved for

The boundary conditions are kept as periodic, i.e,
u(0,t)=u(1,t)

The problem is initialized as:

u(x,0)=sin(2\pi x).

(10)

Since the problem is exquisitely physical, one may expect

all layers (Fig.

).
We see that the eigenvalues of the weight matrices follow the

The radii of the non-normalized circles for each layer vary less than

of the weight matrices, if only with a few outliers.

We investigate this further by plotting the singular values of the weight
matrices, compared with those of the random Gaussian matrix given
by an SVD decomposition,
W=U\,\Sigma\,V
.
Here,
U
and

are the left and right singular vectors, and
\Sigma
represents the

This said, the random-like nature of the PINN matrix invites a further observation:
namely, that PINN may represent a kind of (generalized) path integral
solution of the PDE in point. Even though this may seem to score a point
in favor of explainability, in the following we shall argue that this is not the case.

7
Transformers and path integrals

We have already commented on the way that transformers combine nonlocality and nonlinearity to form universal interpolators. Regarding hyperdimensionality, the transformer basically sinks the problem into an ocean of weights, with the backward step in charge of fishing out the solution by relentlessly chasing the
golden nuggets, no matter how well hidden into the ocean of weights.
By selective focus on “important” regions of hyperdimensional
state space, the transformer manages (when it does)
to circumvent the curse of dimensionality.

In more technical terms, the point is that a
DNN (deep neural net) of depth
L

(number of layers) and width
N
(numbers of neurons
per layer), deploys
N_{P}=N^{L}
possible paths connecting any
single item
x_{i}
in the input layer to any another
single item
y_{j}
in the output layer.
For instance, a DNN with
N=10^{3}
and
L=10^{2}
features

N^{2}L=10^{8}
weights and
N_{P}=10^{30}
paths.
Current leading edge ML applications, such as DeepFold or
Large Language Models motoring the most powerful “ask-me-anything” chatbots
are using up to hundreds of billions weights, basically the number
of neurons in our brain. Except that our brain works at 20 Watt
while the largest ML models are now sucking up at least
ten million times more, a point to which we shall return shortly.

These numbers unveil the “magic” behind ML: deep networks duel the
CoD face up, by unleashing an exponential number of paths, and
adjust their weights thru backward error propagation in such
a way as to populate the ultra-small corners of state space where
the golden nuggets are to be found.

This is very reminiscent of path-integral techniques, hence
we may speculate that transformers solve a stochastic version of the PDE
in point and reproduce the deterministic solution by focusing the weights
around the paths that most contribute to it, pretty much in the spirit
of Feynman’s path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics.
Of course, at low dimensionality there is little need for such path-integral
procedure, but in hyper-dimensional space this might well be the only way to go.
Yet, the transformer procedure is the same regardless of the dimensionality, and
this might explain the random-like nature of the weight matrices discussed in the
previous section.

The analogy between ML and path-integrals has been noted before
Iyengar and Kais 2023

in the context of Boltzmann machines.
However, to the best of our knowledge, its specific connection to the
transformer operation remains largely uncharted.
In principle, random-looking matrix solutions for the kernels

W_{l}(q,q^{\prime})
should result from highly corrugated loss function landscapes, although
much work is still needed to place this matter on a solid mathematical basis.

This said, the fact that the deterministic structure of the
PDE problem remains “buried” under the ocean of random-like weights, still
raises concerns regard explainability of ML.
A similar conclusion is reached even if we take the perspective of path-integration
techniques currently used in computational statistical physics, where the weights
bear a well-defined mathematical expression and physical meaning.
They are the Boltzmann factors of the corresponding Lagrangian action, and the contribution
of each path to the final solution is precisely the (Boltzmann) probability of
this path to occur, namely:

W_{ij}=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta S_{ij}/\tau},

(12)

where
S_{ij}
is the action of the path connecting item
i
in the input
layer (
t=0
) to item
j
in the output layer (
t=\tau
),
Z
is the partition function
and
\beta=1/k_{B}T
.
Note that, for a path going from
i
to
j
via
L
intermediate layers
(k_{1},k_{2}\dots k_{L})

the action is additive, namely

S_{ij}=S_{i,k_{1}}+S_{k_{1},k_{2}}+\dots S_{k_{L},j},

(13)

corresponding to the direct matrix product (repeated indices are summed upon):

P_{ij}=P_{i,k_{1}}P_{k_{1},k_{2}}\dots P_{k_{L},j}.

(14)

None of the relations above carries on to transformers because i) weights are
both positive and negative, often in near-equal proportion, ii) they get “deformed” by
the activation function before being passed to the next layer.
These two points of departure are essential and may well
hold the key for the learning power of ML, but they are also
likely to be responsible for obscuring its explainability.

Summarizing, no matter whether we take the viewpoint of standard numerical integration
of PDEs, or the stochastic perspective of path-integration, the scientific method
is explainable, while the transformer operation remains obscure at best.
Of course, it is always possible to argue that weights are not the best observables
to decide about explainability and that more informative quantities could be identified
to extract interpretable information from the procedure.
Yet, these procedures are far less direct than the
mathematical methods inspired by physical insight.

The matter is open and exciting and the next few years will surely
illuminate the ground. Till then, explainability of ML remains highly debatable.

Finally, we conclude with a few general considerations which are
worth keeping in mind regardless of the scientific verdict on explainability.

8
Outlook

Modern neuroscience seems to converge on the idea that the human brain is a “machine” optimized to make sense of the natural world
Friston 2010
. In the context of human beings, explainability assumes that our mind/brain is manifestly able to make sense of its own doing. Since we, scientists, commit to perform our work under the scientific method, we’d like to expect that the output of ML tools be intelligible in terms of human concepts developed through our human scientific insight.

However, modern LLM applications show an increasing departure from the brain architecture, hence what they are “solving” bears less and less relevance to natural intelligence. In this respect, there is no real reason to expect explainability, in fact the opposite might be true: the more ML departs from the human brain architecture, the more it is likely to become inherently unexplainable to humans. Conversely, the scientific method might be simply unexplainable to LLMs.

Machine learning offers a distinct path to knowledge acquisition, yet one that differs in important ways from Insight. In the representative example we showed here, namely the Burger’s equation, neural networks successfully approximated solutions, but without yielding clear insight into the physical meaning of their internal representation. The difficulty lies not in the solvability of the PDEs themselves, but in the lack of a transparent correspondence between learned weights and the governing structures of the underlying dynamics. This raises important questions about the scope and limitations of current approaches to interpretability in physics-informed machine learning.

We showed (generalized) Gaussian distributions of weights and biases, which are known to maximize entropy
under given mean and variance constraints. This suggests that the network organizes its internal state
in a way that is close to maximal entropy, spreading information broadly rather
than concentrating it into a structured, interpretable form.
This ”democratic” way of operation may well favor robustness but at the price of obscuring
the process of extracting physical meaning from the parameters.
The fact that this difficulty appears already in relatively simple systems such as the Burgers
equation suggests that interpretability, at least in this direct sense, may remain elusive even
before reaching the higher levels of complexity envisioned by Strogatz.

This challenge should not be seen as discouraging, but as motivating careful reflection on how to deploy ML responsibly in scientific practice. A reliance on ever-larger, energy-intensive models with little concern for interpretability may yield short-term empirical control but risks long-term inefficiency and unsustainable societal cost.
At the same time, the alternative is not to reject ML, but to explore synergies where ML complements rather than supplants the scientific method. Hybrid strategies (multiscale modeling, effective models, physics-informed ML, etc.) point toward a constructive integration. In such approaches, ML can act as a tool for discovery, acceleration, or closure, while physical insight ensures parsimony, transparency, and interpretability.

In this light, the role of ML in science should not be judged solely by its capacity to reproduce outcomes, but also by how it enriches understanding and preserves scientific integrity. Aligning ML research with outstanding scientific and societal challenges, rather than with profit-driven scale-up, offers a path where efficiency, interpretability, and responsibility are mutually reinforcing. Far from being catastrophic, this integration could provide a new virtuous cycle between ML and the scientific method, one that benefits both science and society.

Although no general conclusions can be drawn from the two representative examples discussed in this paper, they provide nonetheless a clearcut example of knowledge gained without Insight. Not because the math apparatus itself would not be explainable a priori, but because there is no straightforward procedure to interpret the meaning of the weights and biases which control the success/failure of the ML procedure. Of course, one could retort that such meaning could be extracted by a deeper analysis, but the fact remains that this is a very indirect, opaque and laborious procedure. The fact that this lack of direct correspondence pops out already at the level of comparatively simple physical problems such as the two equations discussed in this paper, lends weight to Strogatz’s scenario, namely the possibility that beyond a given threshold of complexity, “Insight” might be a luxury we can no longer afford.

This appears to be sad at first glance and also scary in many respects. However, making the most of it is basically up to us. Advancing knowledge without the Insight of the scientific method is unquestionably hazardous, but needs not turn into a degenerative AI catastrophe, as long as the lack of Insight is managed responsibly. That is; first, that it does not translate into a reckless and potentially toxic use of ever larger and energy-devouring machines exclusively geared towards empirical control; second, that researchers using these tools are aware of their limitations.

And here is where we reconnect to the leitmotif of the impact on society. Even assuming that LLMs can reach higher peaks of complexity beyond the power of the scientific method, the question still remains:
What for
? What is the price of losing sight of Insight for science, society and ultimately, for the very fabric of humankind? If ML outperforms the scientific method in a way that
must
sacrifice our understanding, it is crucial to realize that two dangerous genies are immediately out of the bottle, and need to be handled with lots of extra-caution.

First, lack of understanding is the golden route to highly non-parsimonious representations with associated unsustainable energy costs
Coveney and Succi 2025
. Big-tech can afford the luxury of ignoring this plain reality and build nuclear plants to power their trillion-dollar AI centers, but the bill for the rest of us, and ultimately our planet, still remains unsustainable. Second, and no less alarming, LLMs have recently been shown to significantly enhance cheating attitudes, by disregarding factual truth in favor of deceiving practices
Liang et al. 2025
. If (double f) this feature is going to be confirmed, it means that chatbots and the like may literally poison the very fabric of human integrity.
The same question then returns: do we really have to sacrifice the energy budget of the planet and possibly even the best part of the human fabric, to the altar of financial interests of the high-fintech super-elite?

The answer is a no-brainer, yet our point here is all but a catastrophic one: alternatives are available and can/should be pursued if only ML could be steered back in the direction of solving outstanding scientific and social challenges through a virtuous circle between the scientific method and ML. Science and Society are littered with extremely hard problems where ML can significantly benefit and augment the scientific method and AI for Science is all but an empty mantra
Jung et al. 2023
. Pursuing this goal is unlikely to add the 13th digit to the profits of the high-tech giants, but it will definitely benefit the rest of us. And quite possibly, them as well.
