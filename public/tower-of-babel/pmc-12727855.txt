Association between bruxism and inflammatory rheumatic diseases: is there a possible bidirectional relationship?
Authors: Zofia Korkosz-Wróbel, Ulyana Telishevska, Mariusz Korkosz, Joanna Zarzecka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBruxism has increasingly been recognised as associated with temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) in chronic inflammatory rheumatic diseases (IRDs), which are conditions that may cause pain, synovitis and damage of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ). The goals of this article are two-fold. First, to present condensed knowledge on bruxism for the rheumatology community, and second, to find evidence on the possible links between bruxism and IRDs – rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA) and axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA). A review of articles that address bruxism was initially performed. A search of the MEDLINE/PubMed and SCOPUS bibliography databases was then conducted to identify articles related to bruxism and IRD. Data extraction was performed on May 20, 2025. The investigators screened the identified articles to determine their relevance and whether they met the inclusion criteria. Following a bibliography search, eight papers were included, and a full-text analysis focused on the relationship between bruxism and IRD was performed. TMD pain was associated with a higher frequency of sleep bruxism (SB) in patients at risk of RA and also in patients with seronegative early RA and seronegative RA. TMDs and SB were more prevalent in patients with PsA than with psoriasis. Bruxism in females was a strong predictor of TMD occurrence in axSpA. Bruxism in IRDs may be secondary to existing TMDs and could also be a potential risk factor for TMD development. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify bidirectional causality.Keywords: Bruxism, Temporomandibular disorders, Temporomandibular joint, Rheumatoid arthritis, Psoriatic arthritis, Axial spondyloarthritis

Introduction

Bruxism

Over the last decades, bruxism has received significant attention from various specialists and researchers as an important element of comprehensive patient assessment and management. Bruxism is now being seen as an involuntary masticatory muscle activity and should no longer be considered a pathology or disease. A recent update on the definition of bruxism reported by the International Consensus Meeting proposed that unspecified bruxism is a repetitive jaw muscle activity characterised by clenching or grinding of the teeth and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible [1]. The Consensus also pointed out that bruxism has two distinct circadian manifestations, during sleep (sleep bruxism, SB) or during wakefulness (awake bruxism, AB) [1]. SB is defined as a masticatory muscle activity during sleep that is characterised as rhythmic (phasic) or non-rhythmic (tonic), and is not considered a movement disorder or a sleep disorder. AB occurs as a masticatory muscle activity during wakefulness that is characterised by repetitive or sustained tooth contact and/or by bracing or thrusting of the mandible, and is not a movement disorder [1]. SB and AB are coded under the ICD as G47.63 (nervous system) and F45.8 (mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental), respectively, but not under musculoskeletal conditions [2].

Studies on bruxism have focused on adverse dental consequences, in particular tooth wear and prosthodontic complications, without examining the relationship with inflammatory rheumatic disease (IRD). Therefore, it is important from the rheumatologist’s perspective whether bruxism as a motor behaviour could be a primary risk factor for developing temporomandibular disorder (TMD)-related pain in IRD, or could it be secondary to an already diseased temporomandibular joint (TMJ) in IRD. Of note, bruxism also has several positive effects, such as reducing chemical damage to the tooth surface in patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease and interrupting episodes of disturbed breathing during sleep in patients with sleep apnoea [3], and finally, bruxism can also appear neutral [1].

The prevalence of SB in the general population is estimated to be between 8% [4] and 13% [5]. Bruxism prevalence in IRD isunderstudied. Khalayi et al. [6] found the frequency of probable SB to be 30.55% among rheumatoid arthritis (RA) patients, not statistically significantly different from controls (34.28%). Based on a questionnaire survey of 400 patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA), Könönen et al. [7] found the bruxism (pooled SB and AB) rate to be 43% and the frequency of SB and AB to be 25% and 34%, respectively. In axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), self-reported bruxism was present in 62.2% of patients [8].

The temporomandibular joint

The TMJ is a synovial, condylar and hinge-type joint. The regular movement of the mandible depends on the correct functioning of the TMJ, including opening and closing of the mouth, chewing, speaking and yawning. Bruxism appears to be clinically relevant in chronic IRD, which may involve the TMJ. Clinical symptoms of TMJ involvement include pain during mastication, joint sounds such as clicking or crepitus and restricted mandibular mobility or limited mouth opening, all of which may also be found in bruxism. In IRD, synovial inflammation and articular damage can disrupt normal occlusion and mandibular motion, potentially leading to compensatory muscle activity. These altered biomechanics may serve as a trigger for the development or exacerbation of bruxism. Conversely, existing bruxism may promote or aggravate TMJ damage by increasing intra-articular load and parafunctional strain. Therefore, the rheumatologist should always consider bruxism in the differential diagnosis of TMJ complaints in IRD.

The prevalence of TMJ involvement in IRD has been reported as 45–92.8% for RA [9] and 25% for PsA [10]. However, no data have been reported for TMJ involvement in IRD for axSpA.

Temporomandibular disorders

TMDs describe a group of conditions that cause pain, dysfunction and structural abnormalities in the TMJ, the masticatory muscles and associated structures. The aetiology of TMDs is multifactorial and includes biological, environmental, social, emotional and cognitive triggers. Of note, IRDs are included as a possible cause of TMDs in the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) [11]. The most common features of TMDs include pain in the form of myalgia, headache attributed to a TMD and arthralgia in the face, limitation in lower jaw movements, noise during jaw function, disc displacement and degenerative diseases in the TMJ [12]. According to the standardised and validated diagnostic criteria for TMDs, the diagnosis is based on five categories: myalgia, arthralgia, articular disc displacement, degenerative joint disease and headache attributed to a TMD. Also, the term ‘TMD pain’ is used in clinical research, where a TMD is responsible for myalgia and/or arthralgia. The diagnostic criteria for TMDs do not classify bruxism as a TMD. Instead, bruxism is considered to be a parafunctional habit, which is a potential risk factor for the development or aggravation of an already established TMD. Indeed, the presence of bruxism has been found to increase the risk of developing a TMD; a recent systematic review with a meta-analysis of 20 observational studies found a rise in TMDs in people with bruxism, with SB and AB increasing the risk of TMDs two-fold [12]. Despite some studies showing a positive correlation between bruxism and TMDs [12], no correlations have also been obtained when different methodologies, interpretations of data and diagnostic tools were utilised [12]. Therefore, the causal relationship between these entities is still unclear. Nevertheless, it seems important to recognise TMDs since studies often use TMDs as a proxy for bruxism, because TMD symptoms may mimic bruxism.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of papers comprising 2,518 subjects found the prevalence of TMDs to be 25.2–34.9% [13]. Others found TMD prevalence in the general population to range from 5 to 12% [11] and 22 to 30% [14]. In IRD, the prevalence of TMD diagnoses was 44.44% in RA [6], 80% in PsA [15] and 39% [16] and 59.2% in axSpA [8].

Diagnosis

Diagnosing bruxism remains complex and relies on a combination of patient-reported history, clinical signs such as tooth wear or masticatory muscle hypertrophy and instrumental assessments. Electromyography (EMG) or polysomnography (PSG) may provide objective evidence of masticatory muscle activity [17], particularly in SB, but their routine use is limited by availability and cost.

A diagnostic grading system for bruxism was developed to make a distinction between possible, probable and definite forms (Table 1) [18]. Possible bruxism is diagnosed based solely on the patient’s self-reporting, probable bruxism is determined through clinical examination, and definitive bruxism is identified using instrumental devices such as EMG and PSG.

TMJ involvement poses additional diagnostic difficulties in RA, PsA and axSpA since TMJ inflammation often remains subclinical and may not be detected without imaging, despite its potential impact on function. MRI is the gold standard for detecting active inflammation, while CT can also reveal structural damage.

From a rheumatologist’s perspective, bruxism may confound a proper diagnosis and explain pain in the TMJ and surrounding tissues, which is secondary to TMJ inflammation and damage. This can worsen sleep and quality of life, and lead to misassessment of disease activity and treatment efficacy.

Aetiology of bruxism

The aetiology of bruxism has not been fully elucidated but includes biological, psychological and exogenous factors. Biological factors such as neurotransmitters (serotonin, dopamine and gamma-aminobutyric acid) and genetic factors [19] may be involved, particularly with the SB form. Stress and anxiety may also be responsible for bruxism development and progression [20]. The exogenous factors consist of nicotine, caffeine [21], alcohol, medications (serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [SNRIs], selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs] and amphetamine) [22]and prolonged screen time. A selection of morphological and occlusive reasons has been associated with bruxism, such as occlusal abnormalities, parafunctions (e.g., biting objects or biting nails) and improperly made fillings or dentures.

Possible links between rheumatic disease and bruxism

The potential association between IRD and bruxism can be sought through several pathways.

Chronic pain and central sensitisation

Central sensitisation (CS) can be defined as an amplified response of the central nervous system to sensory stimuli and peripheral nociception [23].

In rheumatology, frequently reported widespread and chronic pain, including nociplastic pain, may contribute toCS in patients with IRD and non-inflammatory diseases like fibromyalgia [24]. CS was present in almost half of the IRD patients (41% of RA, 45% of axSpA) and in 94% of fibromyalgia patients [24]. CS was also shown to have a role in the pathophysiology of TMDs [25]. Thus, CS could represent the basis of chronic pain in patients with TMDs [26]. Clauw and Calabrese [27] proposed that several defined entities, including TMD and fibromyalgia, could be pathophysiologically interrelated and share a nociplastic pain phenotype. Fibromyalgia frequently coexists with IRD and is also associated with increased rates of bruxism. The prevalence of comorbid fibromyalgia among populations with RA, PsA and axSpA is substantially higher than among the general population, with pooled prevalence estimates of 18–24% in RA, 18% in PsA and 14–16% in axSpA [28]. Patients with fibromyalgia had a greater prevalence than the control group of TMJ pain, headache due to a TMD, jaw locking, headache attributed to bruxism, SB and AB [29]. Although high fibromyalgia rates could partially explain increased rates of bruxism in the IRD cohort, and concomitant fibromyalgia could be considered as a proxy indicator for bruxism risk, the direct link between IRD and bruxism based on CS and high fibromyalgia rates in IRD has not been proven. Therefore, it is only a hypothesis that chronic inflammatory pain in IRD acts as a promoting factor for bruxism through shared CS mechanisms.

Psychological comorbidity

Psychological factors are strongly implicated in the pathophysiology of both IRD and bruxism. Depression and anxiety are frequently associated with IRD, and approximately 20–40% of patients have either depression, anxiety or a combination of both [30]. Psychological stress may trigger or perpetuate bruxism. Stress and anxiety were found to be responsible for bruxism development and progression [20]. On the other hand, psychological distress in IRD may be exacerbated by SB and orofacial discomfort [20]. Furthermore, disturbed sleep architecture, commonly observed in IRD, may interfere with inhibitory pathways that regulate motor muscle activity during sleep, facilitating the emergence of SB.

Inflammation pathways

Inflammatory markers and cytokines are commonly elevated in RA, PsA and axSpA – such as C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) – and are responsible for disease pathogenesis and progression [31].

Michałek-Zrabkowska et al. [32] sought to determine inflammatory markers in 74 patients with probable SB and found that the PSG bruxism episode index positively correlated with the concentrations of CRP and fibrinogen in blood. In addition, they observed a statistically significantly higher bruxism episode index in patients with increased CRP than those with normal values.

Louca Jounger et al. [33] examined patients with TMDs and controls to investigate cytokine levels in the masseter muscle in response to experimental tooth-clenching. The manifestation of clenching in patients with TMDs can be a reflection of bruxism [34]. IL-6 and IL-8 levels were increased in response to tooth clenching in both groups, while IL-7, IL-13 and TNF levels rose only in patients with TMD-related myalgia. In masseter muscle microdialysis, levels of IL-6, IL-7, IL-8 and IL-13 were higher in patients with TMD-related myalgia than in controls. Lago-Rizzardi et al. [35] sought to assess blood parameters associated with stress in 24 women with chronic facial musculoskeletal pain (CFMP). Their study revealed that CRP, IgE, C3 and C4 blood concentrations, along with white blood cell count, tended to be higher in CFMP patients than in controls (not statistically significant). Of note, bruxism was found to be significantly more frequent a complaint in patients (62.5%) than in controls (0.0%).

Taken together, these potentially overlapping pain CS mechanisms, psychological distress and inflammatory pathways suggest a possible pathogenic and pathophysiologic link between signs and symptoms of IRD and bruxism.

Clinical implications for the rheumatologist

Impact on quality of life

The overlap of bruxism and inflammatory arthropathies may be responsible for substantial deterioration in function and pain. Patients may experience facial pain, limitations in mandibular movement, chewing difficulties and sleep disturbances. Bruxism-related parafunctional and inflammatory TMJ pain poses a diagnostic challenge and underscores the need for comprehensive research to distinguish between CS, biomechanical strain and immunologically driven inflammation.

Management considerations

Optimal care for patients with potentially overlapping bruxism and TMJ involvement in IRD requires a multidisciplinary approach. Rheumatologists, dental specialists, orofacial pain experts and physiotherapists should collaborate in individualised management plans.

Treatment strategies should address both inflammatory and mechanical contributors to symptoms [36]. Anti-inflammatory therapy, including a wide range of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs), corticosteroids (systemic and/or local) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), is essential in controlling overall joint inflammation. Arthrocentesis of the TMJ, also used in rheumatological practice, has been studied with promising results. This procedure enables the lavage of a joint cavity and installation of hyaluronic acid or corticosteroid to alleviate symptoms and improve the function of the TMJ [37–39]. For bruxism, behavioural interventions (e.g., cognitive–behavioural therapy, biofeedback), stress reduction and sleep hygiene are recommended along with pharmacological treatment, e.g., gabapentin [40]. Occlusal splints may be used in cases of severe parafunction, but should be carefully selected in the context of joint instability. A recent systematic review suggested that oral splints have no significant effect on pain in TMDs or bruxism [41]. Physical therapy targeting TMJ mobility and stability may also be beneficial. Local application of botulinum toxin into the masseter and/or temporalis muscles may be an effective alternative [42].

Literature review

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the methodology guidelines of Gasparyan et al. [43]. PubMed and Scopus bibliographic databases were searched. Search terms included MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) terminology and additional terms to obtain the maximum number of results. Specific search queries utilised in the process are provided in Fig. 1. The initial data extraction, including article titles and authors’ names, was performed on May 20, 2025. The articles were then screened by two authors (ZKW, MK) between May 20 and June 30, 2025, according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Data extraction and analysis

Key data extracted included study design, population size, bruxism type, TMD evaluation, and outcomes related to joint pain, function or dysfunction. Findings were synthesised qualitatively and organised into thematic categories, such as bruxism subtypes, rheumatic diagnoses, and TMJ/TMD involvement.

This approach allowed for integrating mechanistic, epidemiological and clinical perspectives to support a comprehensive synthesis.

Results

The initial database search found 17 articles in PubMed and 29 in Scopus. After removing duplicates, 37 articles remained. Title and abstract screening left 11 articles that qualified for full-text analysis. After full-text analysis, 8 eligible articles remained. The search was further expanded through citation and reference screening, with no additional articles included in the final analysis.

Despite searching two major bibliographic databases, using liberal search criteria and conducting searches by two team members, to the authors’ best knowledge, at the time of the final search on June 27, 2025, no more studies assessing bruxism in IRD were available.

Conclusive summary

In a search of the magnitude of bruxism occurrence in IRD, the comparison of bruxism rates in IRD and controls was only performed in one RA study assessing unspecified bruxism and the rates were found not to be statistically different [44]. Interestingly, Crincoli et al. [15] found two important signs of bruxism to be less frequent in ERA than controls. In PsA, the rates of bruxism, pooled and separate SB and AB, were compared to the rates in psoriasis cohorts and were found to be higher in PsA [7]. In axSpA patients fulfilling the criteria for AS, only possible, self-reported bruxism was reported to be 62.2% [8].

Since bruxism in IRD is assumed to be linked with the presence of TMDs, the authors sought to determine the rates of TMDs in IRD and controls and also compared the rates of bruxism between patients with TMD diagnoses and those without. Kroese et al. [45] found no difference in the prevalence of TMD diagnosis between ERA, RA at-risk and controls and no difference in probable SB between patients with a TMD and those without. Others noted a significantly higher rate of TMDs in RA compared to controls, but there was no difference in rates of probable SB between RA with TMDs and without [6]. In PsA, the frequency of both TMD and SB was substantially higher than in controls, but the association between TMDs and bruxism was not studied [46]. In axSpA patients, Bilgin et al. [9] diagnosed TMDs in almost 60% and possible bruxism in 62.2% and found a significantly higher rate of bruxism in patients with TMDs.

In the analysed papers, interesting observations and repeatability emerge regarding the analysis of TMD pain, where a TMD is diagnosed as either myalgia or arthralgia or both. In RA, TMD pain was more prevalent in RA at-risk than in controls and in seronegative than in seropositive ERA. These authors also found that the rate of probable SB was significantly higher in patients with TMD pain than in those without [45]. Khalayli et al. [6] reported a higher prevalence of TMD pain in seronegative rather than seropositive RA patients with a mean disease duration of 3.7 ± 1.4 years and low disease activity as measured by DAS28, and participants with a TMD pain diagnosis had a probable SB diagnosis more often than those without. They reported no difference in TMD pain rate between those with a disease activity DAS28 score of < 2.6 and those with a score of > 2.6. However, when the DAS28 score of both groups was compared, seronegative patients were more often in remission. On the other hand, when considering TMJ involvement, which is more clinically palpable to the rheumatologist than TMD pain diagnosis, involvement of the TMJ in RA was more frequently associated with higher disease activity and worse health status [47]. Application of the TMD pain variable in PsA and axSpA studies on bruxism has not been reported so far.

As summarised above, the majority of papers presented descriptive statistics of rates of TMDs, TMD pain, and bruxism, mainly SB, and their associations in RA, PsA and axSpA. Authors reported that in a certain subgroup of patients, the presence of TMD pain was higher than in other subgroups or controls and was associated with a higher rate of bruxism, e.g., more frequent SB in patients with TMD pain in seronegative RA and ERA in comparison to seropositive patients and in RA at-risk individuals versus controls. Nevertheless, since bruxism was found to be substantially more common in individuals with TMD pain in RA, and with TMDs in PsA and axSpA, the link between bruxism and TMJ involvement is possible. However, further systematic recording of a larger amount of data is mandatory to see whether these associations are plausible.

Bruxism in females with axSpA was a strong predictor for TMD development, which was, in fact, the only evidence across the analysed papers describing bruxism as a variable that may forecast TMDs [8]. However, the study’s cross-sectional design limits its predictive power to statistical rather than causal or temporal insights, and thus, we cannot determine which comes first. Also, most of the data cited here are derived from relatively small cohorts, often lack standardised diagnostic criteria and are methodologically different. Notably, no studies employed instrumental assessment (EMG, PSG) to establish a definitive bruxism diagnosis, and most were designed by dentists rather than rheumatology investigators. Therefore, we need large, well-controlled and well-designed longitudinal cooperative studies that may examine not only the association between bruxism and IRD but also bidirectional causes and predictors. Thus, the question for the rheumatology community is still open as to whether bruxism is secondary to already diseased TMJs and could also be a risk factor for the development of TMJ pathology in IRDs.

In conclusion, bruxism is quite prevalent in IRD and in specific subpopulations (e.g., ERA with TMD pain). A possible bidirectional relationship may exist between bruxism and IRDs, mediated by TMJ pathology but also by CS, psychological factors and inflammatory pathways. Understanding this interplay is critical for accurate diagnosis, improving patient outcomes and tailoring interdisciplinary management strategies.
